A Dynamic Energy-Based Hysteresis Model for Pulsed-Operated Fast-Ramping Magnets Manuscript received July, 2025; Corresponding author: D. Moll (email: dominik.moll1@tu-darmstadt.de).
Dominik Moll 1 , Laura A. M. D’Angelo 1 , Herbert De Gersem 1 , Fulvio Boattini 2 , Luca Bottura 2 , Marco Gast 3

Abstract
Due to the strongly nonlinear behavior of ferromagnetic yokes, the numerical analysis of fast-ramping magnets is highly cumbersome and, therefore, in practice overly simplified by means of anhysteretic material descriptions and a posteriori loss formulae. This paper establishes the use of a dynamic ferromagnetic model combining a preconditioned energy-based hysteresis description and a thin-sheet eddy-current model in time-domain. The model was successfully employed in the analysis of a normal-conducting bending magnet in order to precisely calculate losses and fields.



I
Introduction

Fast-ramping normal-conducting magnets delivering rise rates of several
\mathrm{k}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}

receive increasing attention as they fill various key roles in
particle accelerator lattices.
A synchrotron-based muon collider will make extensive use
of such magnets in the intermediate acceleration stages
[
1
,
2
]
.
The magnets will be excited by periodically pulsed currents
with very short duty cycles
[
3
]
,
in order to minimize resistive loss
while reliably enforcing the requested ramp-up of the magnetic field.

The ramped excitation and the locally strong saturation of the ferromagnetic yoke
prohibit the application of frequency-domain approaches.
Moreover, calculating the hysteresis loss by post-processing an anhysteretic solution,
e.g. by the Steinmetz-Bertotti formulae
[
4
]
, yields inaccurate results
as overall energy conservation and the time-lag between exciting current
and generated magnetic field are neglected.
In order to precisely resolve the ferromagnetic behavior
of hysteresis and eddy-current phenomena,
an adequate dynamic material model as in
[
5
,
6
]
will be formulated and applied.

In the following sections, the numerical representations of Maxwell’s equation
and the applied dynamic energy-based hysteresis model are introduced.
The proposed method is then successfully applied on one of the current designs of a fast-ramping muon-collider magnet.
Lastly, the computational costs are compared and discussed.

II
Formulation

The magnetic vector potential approach resolves the magnetic law of Gauss and the law of Faraday-Lenz as

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}=-\dot{\mathbf{A}}-\nabla\varphi,

(2)

with magnetic flux density
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r},t)
, electric field strength
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)
,
magnetic vector potential
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)

and electric scalar potential
\mathbf{\varphi}(\mathbf{r},t)
.
With current density
\mathbf{J}=\sigma\mathbf{E}
we formulate the law of Amp
{\grave{\text{e}}}
re as

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}+\sigma\dot{\mathbf{A}}=-\sigma\nabla\varphi,

(3)

with

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r},t)
the magnetic field strength
and
\sigma(\mathbf{r})
the conductivity.
A so-called field-circuit coupling is needed to account
for the external circuitry creating the pulse.
Every conductor is given a distribution function
\mathbf{x}_{m}(\mathbf{r})

[
7
]
such that

\displaystyle-\sigma\nabla\varphi=\sum_{m}u_{m}\sigma\mathbf{x}_{m},

(4)

where
u_{m}(t)
are the voltages along the conductors.
The currents through the solid conductors are given by

\displaystyle i_{m}=G_{m}u_{m}-(\mathbf{x}_{m},\sigma\dot{\mathbf{A}})_{\Omega},

(5)

where
G_{m}=(\mathbf{x}_{m},\sigma\mathbf{x}_{m})_{\Omega}
is
the conductance of conductor
m

and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega}
denotes the
L^{2}
scalar product over the computational domain
\Omega
.
The variational form of (
3
) is

\displaystyle(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{H})_{\Omega}+\cancelto{0}{(\mathbf{H}\times\mathbf{v},\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}}+(\mathbf{w},\sigma\dot{\mathbf{A}})_{\Omega}=\sum_{m}u_{m}(\mathbf{w},\sigma\mathbf{x}_{m})_{\Omega},

(6)

where the test function
\mathbf{w}
belongs to the space
H(\text{curl},\Omega)

of tangentially continuous vector fields with vanishing tangential components at the Dirichlet boundaries, and

\mathbf{v}=\nabla\times\mathbf{w}
.

II-A

Discretization

Adopting the Ritz-Galerkin approach, i.e., using the same functions
\mathbf{w}_{n}
for discretizing

\mathbf{A}\approx\sum_{n}a_{n}(t)\mathbf{w}_{n}

and testing one obtains

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{a},\dot{\mathbf{a}})+\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}\dot{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{u},

(7)

with
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{h}_{j}=(\mathbf{v}_{j},\mathbf{H})_{\Omega}
,
\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}|_{j,k}=(\mathbf{w}_{j},\sigma\mathbf{w}_{k})_{\Omega}
,

\mathbf{X}|_{j,m}=(\mathbf{w}_{j},\sigma\mathbf{x}_{m})_{\Omega}
.
In case of known current excitations the system can be reformulated by incorporating (
5
)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{a},\dot{\mathbf{a}})+(\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\dot{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{i},

(8)

with
\mathbf{R}|_{j,m}=R_{m}\delta_{jm}
as diagonal matrix of all resistances.

The right hand side of (
7
) (or selectively (
8
)) is known for all times.
A Newton based solving scheme expands the left hand side locally around a guess
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
,
\dot{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{a},\dot{\mathbf{a}})+\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}\dot{\mathbf{a}}\approx

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\dot{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})+\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}\dot{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}}+\frac{\partial\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{a}}\delta\mathbf{a}+\left(\frac{\partial\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{a}}}+\mathbf{M}_{\sigma}\right)\delta\dot{\mathbf{a}},

(9)

with
\delta\dot{\mathbf{a}}=\dot{\mathbf{a}}-\dot{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}
,
\delta{\mathbf{a}}={\mathbf{a}}-{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{h}_{j}}{\partial a_{k}}=\left(\mathbf{v}_{j},\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial a_{k}}\right)_{\Omega}=\left(\mathbf{v}_{j},\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)_{\Omega},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{h}_{j}}{\partial\dot{a}_{k}}=\left(\mathbf{v}_{j},\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial\dot{a}_{k}}\right)_{\Omega}=\left(\mathbf{v}_{j},\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{B}}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)_{\Omega}.

Within the scope of this paper we will expect
\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{B}}}

to be constant, such that the system of differential equations is given in explicit form
\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{a})

and standard time-integration routines can be applied.
We use the Python package
Pyrit

[
8
]

to assemble all finite element systems.

III
Magnetic Constitutive Laws

On the macroscopic scale the magnetic flux density and the magnetic field strength
are related via the magnetization
\mathcal{M}:\mathbf{H}\mapsto\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\mu_{0}(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{H}))=(\mu_{0}+\mu_{0}\mathcal{M})\mathbf{H}.

(10)

In this section we characterize the ferromagnetic response of the
M235-35A soft-magnetic alloy, which will serve as
iron yoke material in the accelerator magnet design.
Extensive measurement data on this material can be found
and was used in the frame of this work
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

III-A

Anhysteretic Material Response

The
an
hysteretic magnetization shall be given by an isotropic relationship as in
[
9
]

\displaystyle|\mu_{0}\mathbf{M}_{\text{an}}|=\mu_{0}M_{a}L(\frac{|\mathbf{H}|}{h_{a}})+\mu_{0}M_{b}L(\frac{|\mathbf{H}|}{h_{b}}),

(11)

with Langevin function
L(x)=\coth(x)-x^{-1}
,
\mu_{0}M_{a}=$1.39\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
,
h_{a}=$18.18\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$
,
\mu_{0}M_{b}=$0.56\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
,
h_{b}=$3.91\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$
.
The maximum susceptibility of
\chi_{\max}={20.32}\,\,10^{3}

is found for very small excitations
|\mu_{0}\mathbf{M}_{\text{an}}|=\mu_{0}\chi_{\max}|\mathbf{H}|+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{H}|^{3})
.

Anhysteretic models are commonly applied as they offer a rapid evaluation of
\mathbf{H}\mapsto\mathbf{B}

and also
\mathbf{B}\mapsto\mathbf{H}
, whereby the latter may require the use of look-up-tables
as analytical inverses often can not be provided.
Similarly straight forward is the construction of the differential tensors

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}=\frac{|\mu_{0}\mathbf{M}_{\text{an}}|}{|\mathbf{H}|}[\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{e}^{\top}_{\mathbf{H}}]+\frac{\partial|\mu_{0}\mathbf{M}_{\text{an}}|}{\partial|\mathbf{H}|}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{e}^{\top}_{\mathbf{H}}.

(12)

By construction, this type of material model is incapable of
reproducing the hysteretic behavior of ferromagnetic alloys.
Accordingly, the hysteresis loss can only be approximated using a posteriori formulae
for which we here assume the principle of loss separation
[
4
]

combined with signal-independent generalized loss expressions
[
12
]
.
The time-averaged hysteresis and eddy current loss densities are

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\text{hyst}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\gamma k_{\text{hyst}}|\dot{\mathbf{B}}|\hat{B}\text{d}t/T}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}|2\pi\cos(\theta)|\text{d}\theta/2\pi},

(13)

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\text{eddy}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\gamma k_{\text{eddy}}|\dot{\mathbf{B}}|^{2}\text{d}t/T}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}|2\pi\cos(\theta)|^{2}\text{d}\theta/2\pi},

(14)

with mass-density
\gamma
, empirically obtained parameters
k_{\text{hyst}}
,
k_{\text{eddy}}

and
\hat{B}
as one half of the peak to peak value of the local magnetic flux density.
The values
k_{\text{hyst}}=$13.88\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}\mathrm{T}^{2}\mathrm{)}$
and
k_{\text{eddy}}=$44.77\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$

have been reported in
[
10
]
and are used here.
The so-called excess loss will not be discussed in this work.

III-B

Hysteretic Material Response – Forward

The energy-based hysteresis model of Bergqvist
[
13
]
and refined by Henrotte
[
14
]

expresses the magnetization as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{H})=\sum_{k}\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{H})=\sum_{k}{w_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{\text{an}}\mathcal{H}_{\text{r}}^{k}\mathbf{H},

(15)

for which the weights
w_{k}
fulfill
\sum_{k}{w_{k}}=1
.
The operator
\mathcal{M}_{\text{an}}
realizes the anhysteretic mapping as defined in
III-A
. A history dependence is included in the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\text{r}}^{k}

which can be approximated by a vector-play model with pinning forces
\kappa_{k}\geq 0
(Tab.
I
)
and Heaviside function
\Theta

[
14
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{r}^{k}=\mathcal{H}_{r}^{k}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}_{r,\text{prev}}^{k}+f(|\delta\mathbf{H}_{k}|,\kappa_{k})\mathbf{e}_{\delta\mathbf{H}_{k}},

(16)

\displaystyle f(x,y)=(x-y)\,\Theta(x-y),

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{H}_{k}=\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}_{r,\text{prev}}^{k}.

For arbitrary fields, errors are introduced by this model which can only

For both approaches the computation of the differential susceptibility tensor
\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}

and differential permeability tensor
\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}=\mu_{0}+\mu_{0}\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}

p_{\text{hyst}}

(18)

\displaystyle p_{\text{hyst}}=\sum_{m}\left(\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}_{r}^{m}\right)\cdot\mu_{0}\dot{\mathbf{M}}_{m}\geq 0.

(19)

Within the scope of this work the vector-play model was selected,
as the impact of the thereby introduced errors has been observed to be marginal
for our intended application of accelerator magnets.

III-C

Hysteretic Material Response – Inverse

The introduced vector-potential based solving algorithm (
7
)
requires the inverse mapping
(\mu_{0}+\mu_{0}\mathcal{M})^{-1}:\mathbf{B}\mapsto\mathbf{H}
.
A direct realization of this operation has been recently proposed
[
17
]
.
State-of-the-art implementations rely on fixed-point iteration
schemes based upon the forward model
[
16
]
.

The fixed-point iteration schemes for a given
\mathbf{B}^{*}
are
characterized by
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*}\mapsto\mathbf{Q}
such that
\mathbf{H}_{\text{n+1}}=\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{H}_{n},\mathbf{B}^{*})

constructs a converging series with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{H}_{n}=\mathbf{H}^{*}
and
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{H}^{*})
.
Root finding algorithms applied on the residual

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{H}^{*},\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{H}^{*})-\mathbf{B}^{*}=\mathbf{0}

(20)

lead to the following iterative schemes

Direct Iteration

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{H}-(\mu_{0}\mu_{r,\max})^{-1}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*}),

(21)

Newton Iteration

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{H}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*}).

(22)

The direct iteration has ensured convergence for all initial guesses
\mathbf{H}_{0}
with
\mu_{0}\mu_{r,\max}
being the maximum differential permeability
but converges slowly.
The Newton iteration is able to provide a much faster convergence if the initial guess is
well justified for example by using the previous result in combination with a very fine time stepping.
As the validity of the initial guess is not a priori known
the Newton method generally needs to be stabilized by additional success criteria and relaxation factors.
Furthermore, the differential tensors must be constructed for every iteration step.

To overcome the disadvantages of both of these well studied schemes
we introduce the preconditioned iteration scheme.
The preconditioned residual is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{p}(\mathbf{H}^{*},\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{B}_{\text{an}}^{-1}(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{H}^{*}))-\mathbf{B}_{\text{an}}^{-1}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{0}

(23)

yielding the

Preconditioned Iteration

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*})=\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{g}_{p}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B}^{*})

(24)

The preconditioned iteration thus actively exploits information on the anhysteretic magnetization curve
\mathbf{B}_{\text{an}}

for which the inverse operation can be cheaply performed using look-up-tables.
It yields ensured convergence for the ferromagnetic material
and drastically reduces the amount of iteration steps as opposed to the direct iteration.

III-D

Dynamic Ferromagnetic Response

The magnetic response of soft-magnetic alloys
is further governed by
the eddy current problem
which in case of purely transversal fields with
\partial_{x}=\partial_{y}=0
reduces to the so-called
one-dimensional lamination problem

\displaystyle-\partial_{z}^{2}\mathbf{H}=-\sigma_{\text{Fe}}\dot{\mathbf{B}}.

(25)

It was shown that the dynamic model

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{surf}}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{B})+\frac{\sigma_{\text{Fe}}d^{2}}{12}\dot{\mathbf{B}}

(26)

is able to approximate the magnetic field strength
\mathbf{H}_{\text{surf}}
at the iron-insulation-interface
very well for low frequent periodic excitations
[
5
,
18
]
.
The eddy current loss is then given by

\displaystyle{p}_{\text{eddy}}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{Fe}}d^{2}}{12}|\dot{\mathbf{B}}|^{2}\geq 0.

(27)

Further refinements of this dynamic model including excess terms have been suggested
but are not considered in this scope
[
6
]
.

In the following section we will apply the anhysteretic and hysteretic material responses
with and without the eddy-current contribution of (
26
).

IV-C

Triangular Pulsed Excitation

We will now assume an excitation
of repeating triangular pulses (Fig.
6

obtained from a switched resonance circuit
[
3
]

In the simulations the intended repetition rate of

The hysteresis loss of the pulsed excitation is identical to the
result obtained for the sinusoidal excitation (Tab.

V
Runtime Comparison

The simulation runtime
increases linearly
with the timesteps
N_{t}
,
nonlinear iteration steps
N_{\text{Newton}}

and degrees of freedom
N_{\text{Nodes}}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{O}(N_{t}N_{\text{Newton}}N_{\text{Nodes}})
.
The calculations with anhysteretic and hysteretic model
only differ in the ferromagnetic material evaluation,
which is required in all
N_{\text{int}}^{\text{Fe}}
integration points of the
iron yoke.
The additional time consumption related to
the serialized material
evaluation scales as

\mathcal{O}(N_{t}N_{\text{Newton}}N_{\text{int}}^{\text{Fe}})
.

The calculation with sinusoidal excitation
was repeated for various uniformly resolved meshes using a standard desktop computer with
10 cores and 32 GB of RAM.
The amount of time steps and Newton iterations was
fixed as
N_{t}=600
and
N_{\text{Newton}}=5
.
The simulation with a mesh of
N_{\text{int}}^{\text{Fe}}=11898
integration points
required
32.7\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}
with anhysteretic materials
and
43.5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}
with hysteretic materials (Fig.
10
).
The difference of
10.8\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}
translates into an additional
individual
evaluation time of
18.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}

per integration point which is in a good agreement
with the results reported in Tab.
II
.

Since the material evaluations for each
integration point are fully independent of another,
a parallelization
is easily implementable.
A dual thread material evaluation
reduced the simulation time to
38.3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}

and thus almost halved the time difference.
For coarser meshes the
speed-up was lower due to the necessarily
introduced overhead of the parallelized implementation.

Under idealized circumstances the runtime can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(N_{t}N_{\text{Newton}})

and thus a constant in
N_{\text{int}}^{\text{Fe}}
by performing all
N_{\text{int}}^{\text{Fe}}

material evaluations on distinct threads.
In this example the additional simulation time would be only
55\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a new stable
and quickly evaluable inversion scheme of the energy-based hysteresis model.
The static ferromagnetic model has been extended by the thin sheet eddy-current description
to obtain a dynamic hysteresis model.
It enabled an accurate simulation of a fast-ramping accelerator magnet
for sinusoidal and triangular pulsed current excitations.
The resulting differences in fields and loss as compared to
a static anhysteretic model were comparably small, granted correctly adjusted loss parameters
of the a posteriori formulae.
However, the dynamic hysteresis model is the only analyzed option
actively incorporating eddy current and hysteresis loss in the time domain simulation.
This property is crucial when analyzing the behavior of switched resonance circuits
consisting of capacitor banks and magnets.
We have shown that the added computational complexity in terms of material evaluation time is manageable and
can even on standard machines be reduced by exhausting parallelization.
