Background

Stroke is a chronic, non-communicable disease characterized by high morbidity, disability, and recurrence, and ranks as the second leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Meanwhile, stroke affects 17.8 million Chinese adults; among survivors, 2.2 million live with stroke-related long-term disability, imposing substantial economic and caregiving burdens on families [3, 4].

More than half of stroke survivors are discharged directly to home- and community-based rehabilitation after acute hospital care [5, 6]. Consequently, the home and community have become the primary settings for rehabilitation and physical activity (PA). However, survivors encounter substantial barriers to PA in these environments. A shortage of professional guidance and social support leaves them without evidence-based exercise plans or effective supervision [7], impeding functional recovery and the restoration of self-care capacity while increasing the risk of recurrent stroke [8].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines physical activity as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure, including that undertaken during leisure, transportation, work, household tasks, or recreational pursuits [9]. Exercise, a subcomponent of physical activity, denotes planned, structured, repetitive, and purposeful movements intended to improve or maintain physical fitness [10, 11]. Owing to multifactorial barriers such as fear of movement and post-stroke fatigue [12], stroke survivors remain largely inactive after returning to community and home settings [13, 14]. Quantitative evidence indicates that higher physical-activity levels significantly lower the risk of recurrent stroke [15] and related complications [16], improve self-care capacity [17, 18], and enhance community reintegration [19].

Qualitative inquiry into physical activity among community-dwelling stroke survivors is expanding [20–23]. Emphasis differs across studies, reflecting distinct sociocultural contexts, and single qualitative studies seldom capture the full continuum of survivors’ experiences. A qualitative systematic review—now an established method in nursing and health-care research—can synthesize these narratives and yield a comprehensive understanding of participants’ perspectives [24].

To our knowledge, no systematic synthesis has yet integrated the physical-activity experiences of stroke survivors after they return to home and community settings. This qualitative systematic review will synthesize the available evidence on stroke survivors’ physical activity experiences and construct a theoretically grounded framework to inform future community-based interventions.

Methods and design

Design

This qualitative systematic review and meta-aggregation protocol was registered with PROSPERO on July 5, 2024 (registration number CRD42024561586); any subsequent amendments will be documented in the same record. This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) [25]; the completed PRISMA-P 2015 checklist is provided (details in Supplementary Material 1) [26]. The forthcoming qualitative systematic review will be carried out in accordance with the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [27].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants (P)

(1) Adults ≥ 18 years with a clinician-confirmed diagnosis of stroke (first-ever or recurrent). (2) Explicit documentation of stroke subtype: intracerebral hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Other etiologies will be excluded. (3) Stroke survivors who are able to ambulate independently, with or without assistive devices, will be eligible; those who are bedridden or unable to stand and mobilize for any reason will be excluded.

Phenomenon of interest (I)

Stroke survivors’ experiences with physical activity in community and family settings (e.g., needs, attitudes, experiences, perceptions, feelings).

Context (Co)

Studies conducted in either community or home-based settings will be included. (1) Community settings: the environments in which stroke survivors customarily lived before stroke, encompassing familiar social networks, physical surroundings, and sociocultural milieus. Stroke survivors residing in community-based rehabilitation facilities, nursing homes, or any other institutionalized settings will be excluded, as their daily activities are likely to be uniformly scheduled and spatially constrained. (2) Home-based setting: the survivor’s usual place of residence before stroke, comprising familiar family members, household physical infrastructure, and informal caregivers. Other domestic arrangements will be excluded.

Types of studies

Qualitative studies that use individual interviews, focus groups, phenomenology, grounded theory, narrative inquiry, or ethnography will be included. Mixed-methods articles will be eligible if they explicitly report stroke survivors’ physical activity experiences in community or home settings. Conference abstracts, duplicates, and unobtainable full texts will be excluded.

Information sources

To ensure comprehensive coverage, we will search ten databases: six English-language (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Scopus) and four Chinese-language (WanFang Data, CNKI, Chongqing VIP, CBM). Gray literature will be retrieved from Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD), Google Scholar, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses.

Search strategy

We will use the JBI-recommended three-phase search process [16]. Phase 1: Build a logic grid of keywords from pilot searches in PubMed and CNKI; iteratively add relevant terms and controlled vocabulary. Phase 2: Translate and run the refined strategies in all ten databases. Phase 3: Screen reference lists of included studies to identify additional citations; repeat cycles until saturation. No publication-date limit will be applied; only English or Chinese articles will be eligible. The PubMed strategy is provided in Table 1. Gray-literature searches will be tailored to each platform’s syntax and filters.

PubMed strategy

Data selection

After the search, all records will be exported to a secure drive and de-duplicated with EndNote (version X9). Two reviewers will then independently screen titles and abstracts; disagreements will be resolved by consensus, with a third reviewer available if needed. A senior team member will make final decisions when consensus cannot be reached. Reasons for exclusion will be logged for every record. Full texts of potentially eligible articles will be retrieved, and their reference lists hand-searched for additional studies. A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram documenting the entire process will be provided in the final report (details in Supplementary Material 2) [28].

Assessment of eligible studies

Two reviewers will independently appraise each included study using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research (10 items; response options: yes, no, unclear, not applicable). The checklist is provided (details in Supplementary Material 3) [29, 30]. Each study will receive one point for every “yes” on the JBI checklist (range 0–10). Studies will ultimately be classified as low, moderate, or high quality on the basis of their scores: low quality (score ≤ 6), moderate quality (7–8), high quality (9–10). Only moderate- and high-quality studies (score ≥ 7) will be retained for data extraction and synthesis [31]. Two reviewers will independently appraise each study; disagreements will be resolved by consensus, with a third reviewer available if needed. Clarification will be sought from authors via e-mail when necessary. Ultimately, studies will be classified as high, moderate, or low quality on the basis of their scores, and the results will be tabulated and described in the final qualitative systematic review.

Data extraction

To ensure rigor and standardization, two independent reviewers will extract data using the JBI QARI qualitative data-extraction tool for studies that have passed methodological appraisal [32]. Each finding and its accompanying illustration will be assigned a credibility level: (1) Unequivocal (U): finding supported by a rich, detailed, and clearly linked illustration. (2) Credible (C): finding accompanied by an illustration that lacks depth, detail, or clear linkage. (3) Not supported (NS): finding not supported by the data or no data are provided. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion; if consensus is not reached, a third reviewer will adjudicate. Missing information will be clarified by e-mailing the authors. For each study we will extract author(s), country, design, aim, participants, setting, stroke stage, data-collection and analysis methods, sample size, and key results. Extracted data will be tabulated and described in the final qualitative systematic review (details in Supplementary Materials 4 and 5) [29].

Data synthesis

Findings will be integrated using the JBI meta-aggregation approach [30]. Similar findings will be assembled and categorized on the basis of meaning, and these categories will be synthesized into a coherent set of recommendations that can inform evidence-based practice. Only unequivocal and credible findings will enter the aggregation; unsupported findings will be reported separately. If textual pooling is not possible, results will be presented narratively.

Assessing the confidence of results

Evidence credibility and dependability will be graded with the JBI ConQual approach, yielding high, moderate, low, or very low ratings [33]. Two independent reviewers will conduct the assessment; disagreements will be resolved by consensus (details in Supplementary Material 6).

Discussion

This protocol describes a qualitative systematic review designed to synthesize the physical activity experiences of community-dwelling stroke survivors. The review will address the central question: What are the experiences, perceptions, and needs of stroke survivors engaging in physical activity after returning to home and community settings? By meta-aggregating qualitative evidence, we will generate coherent, up-to-date findings that can inform individualized, evidence-based rehabilitation programs.

Strengths of this protocol include prospective registration in PROSPERO, which enhances transparency and minimizes selective reporting. Consistent with JBI methodology, the search strategy will be refined iteratively through hand-searching and team discussion, maximizing sensitivity and relevance. Limitations are acknowledged: resource constraints restrict inclusion to English and Chinese publications, potentially limiting the cultural breadth and generalizability of the synthesized evidence.
