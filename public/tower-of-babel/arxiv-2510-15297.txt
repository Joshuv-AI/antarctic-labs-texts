Abstract
. We introduce VERA-MH (Validation of Ethical and Responsible AI in Mental Health), an automated evaluation of the safety of AI chatbots used in mental health contexts, with an initial focus on suicide risk. Practicing clinicians and academic experts developed a rubric informed by best practices for suicide risk management for the evaluation. To fully automate the process, we used two ancillary AI agents. A user-agent model simulates users engaging in a mental health-based conversation with the chatbot under evaluation. The user-agent role-plays specific personas with pre-defined risk levels and other features. Simulated conversations are then passed to a judge-agent who scores them based on the rubric. The final evaluation of the chatbot being tested is obtained by aggregating the scoring of each conversation. VERA-MH is actively under development and undergoing rigorous validation by mental health clinicians to ensure user-agents realistically act as patients and that the judge-agent accurately scores the AI chatbot. To date we have conducted preliminary evaluation of GPT-5, Claude Opus and Claude Sonnet using initial versions of the VERA-MH rubric and used the findings for further design development. Next steps will include more robust clinical validation and iteration, as well as refining actionable scoring. We are seeking feedback from the community on both the technical and clinical aspects of our evaluation.



1.
Background

AI is rapidly reshaping access to, and the experience of, mental healthcare, ranging from chatbots that offer support to models that aid in clinical triage and care navigation. Yet the pace of innovation and enthusiasm about its potential has far outpaced the establishment of ethical, clinical, regulatory, and safety standards. Today, most AI systems applied to mental health are not designed for healthcare, operate without clinical oversight, and lack consistent standards to evaluate the inherent risks.
To help the industry safely and responsibly unlock the potential of AI in mental healthcare, we developed VERA-MH (Validation of Ethical and Responsible AI in Mental Health), a first-of-its-kind evaluation system for the safety of AI chatbots used for mental healthcare. This model combines clinical expertise and technical rigor to assess how AI performs across critical dimensions such as empathy, validation, and risk detection.
In accordance with the Hippocratic oath of “do no harm”, the first phase of VERA-MH focuses on clinical safety related to suicide risk. Suicidal thoughts and behaviors affect people of all ages and backgrounds. In 2023, 1.5M
people in the US alone
attempted suicide. It’s critical that suicide risk is handled safely in AI tools, with strong safeguards and clear pathways to human help. Depending on feedback from the community, future iterations of VERA-MH may focus on how AI tools handle other areas of concern (such as harm to others or psychosis).

This paper discusses our approach to building a clinically validated evaluation tool to reflect the safety and appropriateness of AI-generated responses to chatbot conversations related to suicide risk.

2.
Principles

We believe that for an evaluation of clinically related AI to be
meaningful and effective, it should satisfy the following criteria:

1.

Clinically Informed.
Experienced clinicians should be
included at
every
stage of the design and validation process.

2.

Narrowly Scoped.
Because safety in mental health is difficult to quantify, the evaluation should focus on clear, well-defined concerns.

3.

Multi-turn.
Single-turn conversation evaluations, which involve sending a single prompt to the AI system and assessing its response, are not enough to evaluate for clinical safety. Each individual response may appear benign, but the overall interaction can pose risks when evaluated in its entirety.

4.

Automated
. To keep pace with AI model rates of change, the evaluation should be fully automated.

5.

Model Agnostic
. The evaluation should be agnostic of the specific AI system. The only requirement is the generation of a text output (‘system output’), given a text as input (‘user input’).

6.

Multi-metric
. Given the complexity and nuance of mental health, the safety of a system can’t be defined by a single metric.

3.
Current Design

It’s common for evaluations of Large Language Models (LLMs) to focus on static datasets and single-turn conversations. In this scenario, a list of prompts is manually curated and the LLM agent’s responses are evaluated.

In mental health, however, this approach proves insufficient. Therapeutic interactions are dynamic and, therefore, meaning and context evolve over multiple turns. These nuances pose a significant challenge to static, pre-scripted evaluations. As a result, evaluating mental health LLMs based on static datasets and single-turn conversations can lead to an incomplete or even misleading assessment of capabilities and safety in a clinical context.

To ensure chatbot evaluations reflect realistic mental health use cases, we propose analyzing complete conversations rather than single exchanges. VERA-MH was developed for this purpose, enabling scalable evaluation of chatbot–user interactions, informed by practicing clinicians and suicide prevention experts. This methodology provides a structured and consistent approach to assessing the safety and quality of AI systems in mental health contexts.

There were two equally important components of this structured approach. The first was the development of a rubric, a scoring guide with judgment criteria relevant to the safety, empathy, and appropriateness of chatbot responses. The second was the creation of an automated evaluation system.

5.
Testing Scenarios: The role of user-agents and
judge-agents

It is critical to evaluate how newly developed AI tools for mental health interact with users prior to release. To achieve this, we introduced a user-agent to simulate realistic conversations. Drawing on their clinical experience, practicing clinicians developed system prompts, or user personas, to construct user-agents that role-play individuals engaging with a chatbot about their mental health. This approach enables fine-grained control over user behavior by defining precise parameters, such as presenting concerns, demographic characteristics, and communication styles. The role-playing agents span a continuum of suicide risk levels: some represent individuals with acute suicidal ideation, others exhibit no current indicators of risk, and some express risk indirectly through language or tone rather than explicit disclosure.

After the user-agent completes a simulated, mental health–focused conversation with the chatbot under evaluation, the interaction is assessed using the predefined rubric. To automate this evaluation, we introduced a second agent: the judge-agent. The judge-agent receives both the simulated conversation and the rubric as inputs and scores each criterion, selecting the appropriate response category: best practice, missed opportunity, actively damaging, or not relevant.

5.1.
Ongoing Human Validation

In VERA-MH, a user-agent has a conversation with a chatbot, which is in turn evaluated by a judge-agent using the rubric. This methodology raises questions on construct validity. Are user-agents capable of simulating realistic human behavior? Are judge-agents capable of providing credible ratings based on simulated conversations?

To ensure the validity of our approach, partnership with practicing clinicians has been foundational to answering three critical questions:

1.

How well can user-agents simulate real chatbot users?
It’s essential that the user-agents are credible representations of actual mental health chatbot users. We believe the best way to achieve this is through continuous iteration on the user persona prompts. For example, a previous version of our user-agents was too direct and forthcoming in their communication style to be realistic. As part of our validation process, we have clinicians systematically rate how realistic our user-agents are so we can iterate.

2.

How well does the judge-agent do at evaluating safety?

Clinicians also rate the simulated conversations using the same rubric given to the judge-agent. This step is critical to understanding how well the judge-agent matches human clinicians when tasked with rating the same simulated conversations.

3.

Are ratings consistent across clinicians?
A valid comparison of the judge-agent to human clinician ratings hinges on an acceptable rating consensus among clinicians. This ensures that the judge-agent is evaluated against a consistent standard.

In our initial human validation assessment, licensed mental health clinicians rated a small set of early simulated conversations. At least three clinicians evaluated each conversation, using the same rubric given to the judge-agent. We note that this effort used an earlier version of the rubric, which has since been modified, and an earlier version of the judge-agent. We had three main results, the details of which can be found in the Appendix:

1.

Clinicians generally perceived the simulated user-agents as highly
realistic.

2.

The earlier judge-agent tended to evaluate chatbot safety more
leniently than clinicians, prompting our ongoing work to calibrate the
judge-agent toward more rigorous evaluation.

3.

Clinicians’ ratings of chatbot safety showed inconsistency,
highlighting the need for more structured training and calibration to
improve inter-rater reliability.

These initial results informed our subsequent and ongoing iterations of the rubric, as well as the simulated user-agents and judge-agent. In the ‘Next Steps’ section below, we share more about the planned next steps in our ongoing human validation and iteration efforts.

6.
Known Limitation and Risks

The current framework has the following known limitations.

6.1.
Output and Metrics

Mental health is inherently complex and nuanced, and it would be unwise to reduce safety to a single score. Doing so would compress the multidimensional nature of the data and obscure important context. However, the current 5x4 matrix output format (see Appendix for examples from early experiments) can make it challenging to compare different models or fully capture the trade-offs among dimensions or models. We are therefore exploring several approaches to make the results more interpretable and actionable, and consistent and we specifically invite feedback on this aspect of the framework.

6.2.
Saturation

Saturation is the phenomenon in which models are able to easily obtain the highest score in an evaluation. In our preliminary evaluations (reported in the Appendix), the tested models’ scores are close to the top. Furthermore, with the refinement of model capabilities, it’s possible that the evaluation will completely saturate in the near future. One mitigation strategy is to iteratively refine the personas and rubric leveraging expert clinical feedback and, through prompt adjustments, guide the judge-agent to apply a more rigorous evaluation standard.

6.3.
Emulation of users

This evaluation is only as good as the agent is successful at role-playing a user seeking mental health support. It is possible that models are unable to capture the complexity (and too often, the shame and internalized stigma) of someone seeking help. As discussed above, we are performing human validation to understand to what extent agents are good at simulating users.

It’s also important to note that the way users interact with chatbots is different from the way patients interact with human therapists. Chatbot interactions lack the pleasantries and social norms of human-to-human interactions and are often much more direct and to the point. These differences represent a challenge when evaluating the realism of generated conversations against naturalistic human conversation.

6.4.
Personas

VERA-MH currently has 10 personas that serve as system prompts for the user-agents. Only 10 personas, of course, cannot fully reflect the full range of individuals seeking mental health support from chatbots. We intentionally did not include youth in this first iteration to reduce complexity given the many specific considerations when evaluating safety for that demographic. We also do not cover all demographic and other characteristics that may be important to vary across user-agents to maximize evaluation robustness. As part of this request for feedback we are seeking input on additional key user-agents or their characteristics that we should include in VERA-MH’s next iteration of the personas.
Furthermore, adding more personas will increase the computational costs of running each evaluation.

6.5.
Computational Costs

VERA-MH uses two extra agents to evaluate a single model. To reduce variance, ideally, all possible combinations of models should be considered for the two agents, and their scores aggregated. However, this creates a computationally prohibitive scenario: with a single model, there is only one option. However, by adding a second model, then there are four combinations for all the pairs of (user-agent, judge-agent). With three models, there are nine combinations, and so forth. This requires making choices of which models are used to power the user- and judge-agent.

7.
Next steps

VERA-MH is an iterative evaluation, continuously updated to keep pace with the ever-evolving capabilities of models while upholding rigorous clinical standards.

In addition to collecting feedback from the community, we also plan to:

1.

Rate a larger set of simulated user-agents for not only how realistic they are, but also how well they cover a wide range of suicide risk levels and presentations, from direct disclosures to more subtle risk indicators.

2.

Conduct a larger, more systematic, and rigorous head-to-head comparison of our updated judge-agent and clinicians on a new set of conversations. We’ll use training and calibration sessions to strengthen rating consistency among clinicians and continue to refine our rubric and scoring guide based on feedback. This will ensure that both the judge-agent and clinicians have clear rating guidance and consistent scoring.

3.

Iterate on the judge-agent prompts to make them even more aligned with expert human raters.

4.

Experiment with multiple judge-agents to evaluate a single conversation.

We are excited to introduce VERA-MH to the public for review. Many individuals still face significant barriers to receiving quality mental healthcare. When AI is deployed in a way that is ethical, safe, and clinically appropriate, it can eliminate many of those barriers. Those developing mental health support AI chatbots must demonstrate that they are safe; provide appropriate risk assessments and responses; and respond with empathy, compassion, and support.
