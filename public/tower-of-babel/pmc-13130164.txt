Development of Magnetic Microbeads Equipped with Temperature-Responsive Artificial Antibodies for Efficient Anticancer Drug Administration
Authors: Shuyi Sun, Satohiro Itagaki, Ryo Nishii, Xueling Shan, Genki Ogata, Yojiro Yamamoto, Yasuaki Einaga, Hiroshi Hibino, Zhidong Chen, Hiroshi Shiigi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, micrometer-sized magnetic beads equipped with artificial antibodies whose reactivity could be controlled by temperature tuning were developed. An artificial antibody that can specifically bind to doxorubicin (Dox) was fabricated on an N-isopropylacrylamide copolymer matrix on the surface of the magnetic beads. The growth reaction of gold nanoparticles (NPs) was effectively used to promote the selective polymerization reaction on the beads. To develop magnetism and polymerization via the growth of gold NPs, plastic beads were coated with both magnetic iron oxide and gold NPs. The fluorescence microscopy evaluation of the fabricated artificial antibody revealed that the temperature-dependent affinity change in the copolymer reversibly changed its binding properties upon temperature control. This engineered antibody is effective against anthracyclines with complex molecular structures and exhibits excellent selectivity (>50-fold). This development makes the efficient separation and recovery of Dox contained in human plasma samples using an external magnetic force and temperature control feasible. The results of this study could guide the development of highly efficient artificial antibodies that could revolutionize cancer treatment.

According to data provided by the World Cancer Research Fund and World Health Organization, approximately 20 million cancer cases have occurred worldwide with nearly 10 million deaths in 2020. Cancer prevention has become one of the most important public health challenges of the 21st century. Cancer treatment is most effective when combined with clinical surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. The efficacy of chemotherapy was first confirmed in the 1940s; however, studies confirm that an overdose of anticancer drugs increases its side effects. Despite the high efficacy of cancer treatments combining clinical surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, these approaches are associated with a risk of side effects that may adversely affect daily life. Therefore, new therapeutic agents are being developed to improve the effectiveness of antitumor treatments while reducing their side effects. Measuring the amount of anticancer drug that acts on the tumor in a living body can reduce side effects by enabling the administration of an appropriate amount of the drug for chemotherapy and enhance the effect of anticancer drug treatment.

The anthracycline drug doxorubicin (Dox) is one of the most widely used anticancer drugs that has been used to treat blood and solid malignancies for over 40 years. Similar to most other anthracyclines, it can cause chronic toxicity to the heart. − Therefore, the cumulative cardiotoxic dose of Dox is set at a body surface area of 550 mg m–2. Numerous studies on Dox concentration measurements have been conducted worldwide to ensure patient safety and reduce side effects and costs. − Various assays have been developed to determine the appropriate dose that acts on cancer cells in each patient; however, maximizing the usefulness of these assays requires the separation and recovery of target substances from human samples. − Analytical techniques, including spectrophotometry, , gas chromatography, − high-performance liquid chromatography, − resonance light scattering technique, and voltammetry, have been used to identify and quantify paraquat in biological samples over the past few decades. Most of these techniques are costly and frequently require specialized operators and expensive equipment, thereby exhibiting limited clinical applicability.

Magnetic beads are used for protein purification, peptide separation, and cell separation because of their advantages such as easy handling, rapid separation, and the lack of the need for equipment. Generally, magnetic beads with a specific binding ligand introduced onto their surface are dispersed in a solution containing the target substance. Target substances bind to these magnetic beads via ligands such as DNA/RNA, antibodies, and enzymes. , Target-bearing magnetic beads can be rapidly collected by approaching the magnet from outside the vessel. Immunization-based antibody production is not only limited to substances with antigenicity but can require a total of several months to half a year. Artificial antibodies can be synthesized within a few days and express the specific bonding ability of an antigen-free substance. − Consequently, from an industrial perspective, artificial antibodies for molecular recognition are attracting increasing attention.

In our previous study, we developed an artificial antibody for Escherichia coli O157 using polypyrrole as a matrix polymer formed on plastic microbeads using molecular imprinting polymer (MIP) techniques. The artificial antibody obtained by half-embedding bacterial cells in a matrix polymer followed by lysis treatment can recognize target cells with high selectivity (>10 times). − Matrix polypyrrole can be formed by chemical or electrochemical oxidative polymerization on plastic-based materials, such as microbeads and microplates, or on metal-based materials, making it useful for analytical applications such as separation, recovery, and detection. The binding of artificial antibodies to polypyrrole is a one-way process, and peroxidation and/or alkali treatments are required to remove the template and target. These treatments deteriorate the polymer matrix, making the maintenance of the artificial-antibody function challenging. Centrifugal recovery also causes physical stress on the beads and can adversely affect the performance of artificial antibodies. Consequently, these treatments make the maintenance of bead function challenging. Therefore, developing artificial antibody beads that can be manipulated under mild chemical and physical conditions is vital.

In this study, we attempted to develop artificial antibodies on magnetic microbeads with a temperature-sensitive polymer. We investigated the selectivity and reversible bonding ability of the artificial antibodies and evaluated the Dox collection ability of the beads in human plasma.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

All chemicals used were of reagent grade. Ultrapure water (>18 MΩ cm) sterilized by UV light was used for all experiments. Magnetic beads were purchased from GreenChem Inc. (Japan). Dox, mitomycin C, sodium dodecyl sulfate, N-t-butyl acrylamide (nTBA), N,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) (MBAm), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAm), ammonium persulfate, N,N,N′,N′-tetrametylethylenediamine (TMEDA), fluorescein, and rhodamine (B and 6G) were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries Co. (Japan). Acrylic acid (AAc) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. (Japan). Chloroauric acid was purchased from Tanaka Kikinzoku Kogyo, Inc. (Japan).

Fabrication of Dox-Imprinted Microbeads

Using Dox (0.29 mg) as the template, NIPAm (20 mg), AAc (1.1 μL), nTBA (17 mg), and MBAm (1.0 mg) were added as copolymerization monomers into ultrapure water (50 mL). Sodium dodecyl sulfate (10 mg) and chloroauric acid (1 wt %, 0.30 mL) were added to the comonomer solution. Magnetic microbeads (100 mg) were then added to the mixture. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was degassed in a sonication bath under vacuum for 10 min and subjected to nitrogen stripping for 30 min. Copolymerization was carried out by the addition of ammonium persulfate (0.26 μM, 0.5 mL) and TMEDA (6.7 μM, 15 μL). The solution was stirred for 18 h at 298 K to complete copolymerization. After copolymerization, the beads were collected using an external magnetic field and dispersed in ultrapure water for 30 min at 323 K. This procedure was repeated thrice to eliminate nonspecific template adsorption and remove the templates. Subsequently, the beads were dried for 3 h at 343 K. The obtained powder, labeled Dox-imprinted microbeads (DIP beads), was stored at 298 K. Beads using fluorescein as a template (FIP beads) were also fabricated using the same procedure. The bead surfaces were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Miniscope TM3030, HITACHI, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV.

Microscopic Observation

The FIP beads were incubated in an aqueous fluorescein solution (0.010 mM, 2.0 mL) at 298 K for 10 min. After incubation, the beads were collected using an external magnetic field and dispersed in ultrapure water. The suspension (10 μL) was pipetted onto a glass slide and allowed to dry naturally in the dark at 298 K for 60 min. The resulting slides were observed using a dark-field microscope (DFM) equipped with a fluorescence unit (ECLIPSE Ni–U; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to acquire fluorescent and dark-field images of the beads. DFM detects only the light scattered by a sample; the directly transmitted light is blocked by a dark-field condenser. DFM images were acquired using an optical microscope (Eclipse 80i, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a dark-field condenser, 100 W halogen lamp, and charge-coupled device camera. Light-scattering spectra were acquired using a miniature grating spectrometer (USB4000, Ocean Optics, Orlando, FL) connected to the DFM using an optical fiber (core diameter: 400 μm). The position of the beads was confirmed in the dark-field mode, and the fluorescence images and fluorescence intensity of the beads were acquired over a typical acquisition time of 40 ms.

Adsorption/Desorption Operation of Targets with Beads

DIP beads (20 mg) were added to a Dox solution (10 μM, 2.0 mL) adjusted to pH 8.0 with phosphate buffer (0.01 M). The mixture was incubated at 298 K for 1 h with gentle shaking. The supernatant was collected, and the spectrum was measured using a spectrophotometer (V-750, JASCO, Japan). After adsorption, the beads were dispersed in phosphate buffer (2.0 mL) and allowed to stand at 323 K for 2 h to remove Dox. After bead collection using an external magnetic force, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured. To determine the rates of adsorption/desorption, this process was repeated 5 times.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Evaluation of the Reversible Binding Properties of FIP Beads

Magnetic beads synthesized by the nanoplating technique show rough nanometer-sized surfaces because of the formation of two-dimensional films of Fe3O4 NPs with a mean diameter of 10 nm on plastic microbeads (Figure S1). − Here, gold NPs with a mean diameter of 30 nm were deposited on the surfaces of the magnetic beads by nanoplating with bis­(2-benzamidophenyl) disulfide as a binder (Figure A). A distinct contrast based on the difference in electrical conductivity between Fe3O4 and the gold NPs on the magnetic beads was observed using SEM (Figure B). After polymerization using fluorescein as a template, the beads were magnetically collected from the polymerization solution and dispersed in ultrapure water. The growth of gold NPs on the bead surface improved the electrical conductivity of the beads, while concurrent polymerization formed an insulating N-isopropylacrylamide copolymer (poly-NIPAm) layer, which led to a slightly blurred contrast in the acquired SEM images.

(C) Dark-field (left) and fluorescence images (right) of FIP beads with a fluorescein template (a) before and (b) after template removal at 323 K. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that the fluorescein template was incorporated into the poly-NIPAm matrix covering the bead surface (Figure C). The position of the beads on the glass slide was confirmed by dark-field observation, and the same location was observed in the fluorescence mode. After incubating in ultrapure water at 323 K for 30 min, no fluorescein-based fluorescence was observed on the bead surface, confirming the complete removal of the template from the poly-NIPAm matrix. This is due to a phase transition at the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of the copolymer (305 K). , No distinct differences in the bead surface were observed in the SEM images before and after template removal. These results indicate the formation of a copolymer with template molecules on the bead surface. The molecular recognition and binding properties of the FIP beads were investigated at controlled temperatures.

The FIP beads were placed on a glass slide and observed under fluorescence (Figure A). Prior to dispersion in fluorescein solution, no fluorescence was observed at the bead positions on the glass slide in the dark. The FIP-coated beads were dispersed in an aqueous fluorescein solution and incubated for 10 min with gentle shaking at 298 K. Subsequently, the beads were collected using an external magnetic force and dispersed in ultrapure water for washing. After incubation for 10 min, the beads were clearly visible in the fluorescence field and the spectrum showed a strong peak due to fluorescein fluorescence at 520 nm (Figure B), indicating that the beads were recognized and bound to the target fluorescein molecules in the first cycle. To repeat this cycle of molecular recognition, the fluorescein-bound beads collected by magnetic force were dispersed in ultrapure water at 323 K. No fluorescence was observed after 1 h of gentle shaking at 323 K. The beads were collected using an external magnetic field and dispersed in a fluorescein solution again. After incubation, the beads were clearly visible in the fluorescence field, and the spectrum showed a strong peak in the second cycle. During repeated temperature-cycling experiments, no differences were observed in the fluorescence intensity of the beads at 298 and 323 K. This indicates that the cavities in the matrix were reproducibly reformed during the repeated phase transitions of poly-NIPAm. Furthermore, the absence of fluorescence after incubation in rhodamine B and 6G solutions indicated that the cavities formed in the poly-NIPAm matrix showed excellent selectivity (Figure S2). The experimental results support the disappearance and regeneration of cavities due to the temperature-induced shrinkage-swelling cycle of the copolymer. However, the details are extremely difficult to explain at the molecular level and remain conjecture based on a combination of experimental results and theoretical predictions.

Evaluation of the Binding Capacity of DIP Beads

This copolymer used NIPAm as the polymer backbone to impart temperature-responsive phase transfer, AAc to enhance specific binding and exclusion efficiency through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with functional groups, nTBA to optimize the polymer’s hydrophobicity, and MBAm as a cross-linker to construct a three-dimensional network. The molar ratio of each monomer (NIPAm:nTBA:AAc:MBAm = 53:40:5:2) was optimized from the perspectives of molecular recognition function and temperature responsiveness. As described above, the binding properties of elaborate cavities with complementary shapes to the target substances fabricated using poly-NIPAm as a matrix could be controlled by temperature tuning. We applied this technique to Dox, an anthracycline-based compound with a complex chemical structure. The chemical structure of Dox was affected by the surrounding pH (Figure S3). Because the absorbance of Dox (10 mM) at 475 nm (I 0) depends on pH, the recovery rate of the beads (r b = [I 0 – I a]/I 0) was calculated based on the absorbance of the supernatant at each pH after the addition of 5 mg of beads (I a) (Figure A). To improve the binding efficiency of the DIP beads, we conducted experiments across a wide pH range (3–9) with 1 mg of DIP beads added to each system (Figure B). In acidic media (pH < 5), the binding rate (r b) remained consistently below 10%; however, it increased sharply above pH 5 and reached 30% at pH 8. Notably, the absorbance of Dox decreased drastically at pH 9 (Figure S4), which interfered with r b calculations. Based on these observations, pH 8 was selected as the optimal condition for subsequent Dox recovery experiments. As the amount of added DIP beads increased, the absorbance of the supernatant (I a) gradually decreased (Figure C); at a bead dosage of 20 mg, the r b reached 95%. To quantify Dox binding per bead, Dox solution (10 μM, 2.0 mL) containing 2.0 × 10–8 mol of Dox with a bead density of 1.1 × 10–7 mg per bead was used; the amount of Dox bound per bead was estimated to be 1.1 × 10–16 mol. Additionally, when we fabricated DIP beads using Dox solution (10 μM, 50 mL, containing 5.0 × 10–7 mol of Dox), the Dox binding per bead was estimated to be 5.5 × 10–16 mol, indicating that only 20% of the imprinted cavities on the beads were occupied by Dox during adsorption (Figure S5).

(A) Absorption spectrum of a Dox aqueous solution (10 μM) (a) before and (b) after the addition of DIP beads (1 mg) at pH 8. (B) The r b vs pH of the Dox solution at 298 K. (C) Dependence of the I a on the amount of beads at pH 8. (D) The r d vs pH.

Subsequently, we investigated the optimal pH for Dox desorption from the DIP beads (Figure D). DIP beads (20 mg) were added to a Dox solution (10 μM, 2.0 mL, pH 8) at 298 K; after incubation, the supernatant absorbance (I a) was measured. The DIP beads were collected via an external magnetic force and dispersed in acidic, neutral, or alkaline media at 323 K. After reincubation and magnetic collection, the absorbance of the supernatant (I d), which includes the detached Dox from DIP beads, was recorded and the detaching rate (r d = I d/[I 0 – I a]) was calculated. The highest r d was observed at pH 4, indicating the most efficient Dox desorption (Figure D); ∼1.2 × 10–8 mol of Dox was desorbed at pH 4, corresponding to the r d of 65%.

The reversible binding of Dox to DIP beads is driven by the phase transition of poly-NIPAm at a LCST of 305 K. Poly-NIPAm forms a swollen hydrogel at temperatures below 323 K, and the cavity matches the chemical structure of Dox for specific binding. Conversely, above 323 K, the water molecules hydrated in the polymer are desorbed, leading to polymer chain aggregation, which breaks the hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl groups of Dox and the carboxyl groups of AAc, facilitating Dox desorption. Notably, pH may affect this process. Under alkaline conditions, the carboxyl group derived from acrylic acid carries a negative charge, whereas Dox is positively charged; consequently, absorption is facilitated at pH 8 (Figure ). , Under acidic conditions, Dox retains its positive charge, and because of electrostatic repulsion between the DIP surface and the Dox cation, desorption is facilitated at pH 4. ,

Molecular Recognition Properties of DIP Beads

To investigate the reversibility and repeatability of molecular recognition reactions, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured after alternately dispersing the DIP beads in Dox solution at 298 K and in phosphate buffer (pH 4) at 323 K. During repeated temperature cycling experiments, the r b remained nearly constant at 95%, while the r d remained 65% with a variation of <5% (Figure ). As calculated above, only 20% of the imprinted cavities on the DIP beads were occupied by Dox during binding. Despite a 30% difference between the binding and detachment rates (r b > r d), unremovable Dox occupied only ∼8% of the total cavities. This low occupancy of unremovable Dox minimized irreversible cavity blocking, ensuring the structural integrity and reusability of the beads. As a result, the Dox binding–desorption cycle could be repeated stably up to five times without a significant loss of performance.

The r d is calculated from the I d and I 0 obtained before and after removing Dox. Adsorption was performed using 20 mg DIP beads in 2.0 mL of 10 μM Dox solution at pH 8, 298 K, followed by desorption in PB at pH 4, 323 K. This cycle was repeated five times using the same DIP beads.

To examine the selectivity of DIP beads, we performed recovery tests using mitomycin C (Figure S6). To examine the nonspecific adsorption of these drugs on the beads, we conducted the same experiment using beads coated with a non-imprinted polymer (NIP). The DIP beads clearly distinguished Dox from mitomycin C, whereas the NIP beads did not adsorb Dox or mitomycin C. The DIP beads likely recognize Dox through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds between the carboxyl group derived from AAc in the cavity and Dox, which contains an anthracycline skeleton with two hydroxyl groups (Figure ). Interestingly, mitomycin C, a small molecule with a chemical structure similar to that of Dox, was unable to form a specific bond, even after entering the cavity (Figures S6 and S7). Furthermore, we evaluated the Dox adsorption behavior of the unmodified gold NP-coated magnetic beads. The beads exhibited a high Dox binding capacity, showing 75% recovery with 5 mg of beads and 100% recovery with 10 mg of beads (Figures S8). This high recovery was caused by nonspecific adsorption rather than molecular recognition. This result, combined with the negligible adsorption of NIP beads and the high-specific adsorption of DIP beads, confirms that the specific recognition of Dox is not derived from the nonspecific binding of the magnetic bead core or the polymer matrix, but is entirely dependent on the shape-complementary imprinted cavities and directional intermolecular interactions (electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding) constructed by the molecular imprinting process. The stark contrast among DIP, NIP, and unmodified gold NP-coated magnetic beads clearly illustrates the unique advantage of using a molecularly imprinted artificial antibody in the targeted enrichment of anticancer drugs.

Molecular Recognition Properties of DIP Beads in Plasma

Human plasma consists of a large amount of water and other components, including proteins, − glucose, − and enzymes. − These components may interfere with the adsorption performance of DIP beads; thus, we evaluated the Dox-binding ability of DIP beads in Dox-spiked human plasma by separately adding DIP beads to Dox-spiked plasma solutions at 298 K. After 1 h of incubation, the DIP beads were collected using an external magnetic force and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured. Although the presence of various components in human plasma elevated the spectral baseline (Figure A), the characteristic absorbance peak of Dox at 475 nm could be clearly identified. Therefore, r b was calculated using the difference between the absorbance at 475 nm and the baseline. The r b increased with the number of DIP beads added. When 20 mg of DIP beads were used, the r b reached nearly 100%, similar to the results observed in the aqueous system, and remained constant, even with changing Dox concentrations. Dox binds to plasma proteins for transport. − Plasma proteins, including albumin, exhibit a high affinity for gold NPs. − Gold NPs are coated with proteins to form a protein “corona,” and there are concerns regarding the impact of this phenomenon on the performance of DIP beads in in vitro experiments using plasma. However, in this study, the r b was in good agreement with the value obtained in aqueous systems, and no distinct impact of this phenomenon was observed. This suggests that the poly-NIPAm coating on the gold NPs or the cavities formed therein may inhibit the permeation of these proteins and suppress their adsorption onto the gold NPs.

Conclusion

In summary, we fabricated an MIP on the surface of microbeads that exhibited an excellent adsorption/desorption performance and selectivity as an artificial antibody. In both aqueous and human plasma Dox solutions, 20 mg of DIP beads showed a binding rate of >90%, confirming the Dox-binding ability of the DIP beads. By increasing the temperature to 323 K, both the FIP and DIP beads could remove the target, and the removal rate of the DIP beads was calculated to be >60%, indicating temperature-controllable binding and removal and the formation of a temperature-responsive artificial antibody. On repeating the temperature-control cycle, both FIP and DIP beads showed stable binding and removal properties, confirming that the surface was not damaged by temperature changes. Finally, both FIP and DIP beads showed selectivity, distinguishing between the target and other medicines, showing high potential for utilization as artificial antibodies. These findings indicate that MIP beads can be used as temperature-responsive artificial antibodies in medicine control. Thus, the results of this study contribute significantly toward research on artificial antibodies and could guide the design and development of advanced systems for efficient therapy.
