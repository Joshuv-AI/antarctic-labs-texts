A qualitative study exploring concepts and attitudes of guinea pig producers in rural parishes of Pichincha, Ecuador: The need for a one health-one welfare approach to enhance production practices
Authors: Heather Fowler, Grace Faulkner, Gustavo Donoso, Oscar Fuentes, Monique Pairis-Garcia, Sarah Rhea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith its compact size, resilience at high altitudes, and high feed efficiency, the guinea pig (Cavia porcellus) is a sustainable and high-quality protein option for people in resource restrictive areas of South America's Andean region. Each year in Ecuador, 47 million guinea pigs, destined for sale or household consumption, are produced. Despite the reported presence and anticipated growth of guinea pig production in the region, husbandry standards in this industry have not been fully documented. A qualitative study was conducted to explore the thoughts, attitudes, and practices of guinea pig producers in three rural parishes of Pichincha, Ecuador regarding guinea pig production, health, and welfare. During October–December 2024, a focus group was held in each parish with discussion including the basic principles of animal production and husbandry (i.e., breeding, feeding, treatment, preventative activities, euthanasia). Focus group transcripts underwent thematic analysis. Inductive codes were identified via an iterative open coding process with two independent coders, until a unifying theme was identified. Nineteen guinea pig producers participated across the three focus groups. A One Health approach was identified as the primary domain under which the identified themes fell. The main unifying theme mapped back to the concepts of traditional and modern medicine. The rural Ecuadorian guinea pig producers included in this study implement a combination of traditional and modern medicine and production practices to ensure the humane production of this species. When developing and implementing future studies and educational programs, researchers, veterinarians, and agricultural educators should consider blending these two strategies to ensure the industry can improve in a One Health-One Welfare way.Keywords: Guinea pig, Food animal, Animal welfare, One health, One welfare, Animal production, Traditional medicine

Introduction

Cavia porcellus, more commonly known as the guinea pig, is a species of cavy reared throughout the world for companionship, research, and human consumption [1]. Production of these animals for food dates back centuries in the Andean region, primarily in northeastern South America, including Bolivia, Colombia, Peru, and Ecuador [2]. The ubiquitous nature of the guinea pig in this region, along with its adaptability and resilience in various climates, contribute to its viability as a high-quality protein source that rivals that of more conventional food animal production in other regions of the world [2,3].

In the Andean region, guinea pig production can be placed in three different categories: family owned and operated (family-type), family owned and commercially operated (family-commercial) and commercially owned and operated (commercial-type). Among these, family-type is the most common, and is typically managed by household members, particularly women [2]. In Ecuador, specifically, guinea pig production is quite common. Though the exact number is unknown, data from Ecuador's Instituto Nacional Autónomo de Investigaciones Agropecuarias suggest that 47 million guinea pigs, destined for sale or household consumption, are produced annually in Ecuador [1,4,5]. Farm sizes range from just a few animals, which producers use to sustain themselves and their families (family-type productions), to larger more commercial production that provides animal protein for neighboring communities (family-commercial and commercial type productions) [1,6].

Common herd health issues affecting guinea pigs include the zoonotic conditions Salmonellosis, Campylobacteriosis, and ectoparasitosis due to mites, lice, and fleas. These conditions highlight the importance of the interconnections between animal, human and environmental health, known as the “One Health” approach, in guinea pig production settings [7,8]. The holistic nature of One Health has been identified as synergistic with traditional knowledge held by indigenous communities; however, gaps in the scientific literature remain in this regard [9].

One Health and animal welfare are also linked, as improving health in all three sectors of the One Health paradigm results in better welfare outcomes and vice versa [10,11]. Additionally, improving animal health and welfare can contribute to food security and sustainability for producers, ultimately enhancing the wellbeing of both animals and humans. The relationship between health and welfare, which is relevant to guinea pig production in Ecuador, can be framed under another comprehensive theme known as One Welfare [10]. Guinea pigs have been recognized as important for food security, rural development, and women's empowerment in South American countries that participate in guinea pig production. Therefore, understanding how guinea pig producers in Ecuador consider and address both health and welfare within their productions proves crucial for the country and the progress of its rural communities [12,13]. However, information in this realm is lacking.

Considering these points, it is important to document and understand what steps Ecuadorian guinea pig producers are taking to ethically, responsibly and sustainably produce and raise these animals. This qualitative study aimed to explore the thoughts, attitudes, and practices of guinea pig producers in rural Ecuador regarding guinea pig production, health and welfare using focus groups. Results could inform future educational initiatives to enhance safe and sustainable guinea pig production practices in this region of Ecuador through a One Health-One Welfare approach.

Materials and methods

Study design

A qualitative study of guinea pig producers was conducted, using focus groups in three rural parishes of Quito canton (Ilalo-Guangopolo, Conocoto and Amaguaña) in Pichincha, Ecuador during October–December 2024 (Fig. 1). Guinea pig producers were recruited by referral sampling to participate in a focus group session lasting approximately 1 h, or until saturation was reached [14]. To qualify for enrollment, participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria at the time of the focus group session: 1) were > 17 years of age; 2) were raising, had previously raised, or planned to raise in the near future, at least one guinea pig for consumption. Participants were offered an educational seminar hosted by one of the study veterinarians (O.F.) as an incentive. The study team had planned to host three focus groups at the same location and on the same day. However, the number of participants was lower than expected and only a single focus group was initially held. Study team members revisited the area approximately 2 months after the initial focus group. During this second visit, recruitment efforts were intensified and expanded to obtain enrollment numbers that would ensure saturation [14]. Ultimately, the team's referral sampling strategy successfully facilitated study recruitment from this otherwise difficult-to-reach population.

Ethics, positionality, and reflexivity statements

Study protocols were reviewed and approved by the North Carolina State University Institutional Review Board (Study #20629). Participants' names were masked to protect their identities. However, general locations of the production sites and the sites where the focus groups were conducted were disclosed for transparency.

As researchers' background and experiences can influence data collection and interpretation, the team considered how their positionality could impact and inform qualitative research activities in the study communities. Both G.D. (focus group facilitator, transcript translator) and O.F. (educational seminar facilitator, community organizer) are native Ecuadorian, Spanish-speaking veterinarians who had been working with communities in the Quito canton area for more 2 years at the time of the focus group sessions. Considering the long-standing insider/outsider debate, G.D. and O.F. have reflected on their perceived status as insiders, as well as how their backgrounds could have influenced focus group session dynamics [15]. G.D. and O.F. acknowledge that their perceived insider status, ability to speak Spanish in the regional dialect, and long-standing community interest and engagement were assets to the study. Specifically, these factors likely aided participant recruitment from the hard-to-reach areas where the study was conducted and facilitated conversation during focus group sessions. However, G.D. and O.F. also acknowledge that their perceived insider status could influence interpretation, possibly favoring community narratives.

To capture the diverse range of perspectives that participants disclosed during focus group sessions, construct a comprehensive end product that accurately portrays participant views, and draw on the qualitative analysis expertise of H.F. and G.F., the study included outsider perspectives, as well. H.F. (qualitative analyst/coder) is a veterinarian and native English-speaker with expertise in production medicine, preventative veterinary medicine, animal welfare, One Health, public health, and qualitative analysis. G.F. (qualitative analyst/coder) is a public health professional and native English-speaker with expertise in surveillance, data analysis, One Health, and public health. H.F., G.F., and the rest of the study team provided the outsider perspective to this work [15].

Data collection

Focus groups were facilitated by a member of the research team (G.D.), a native Spanish speaker from Ecuador, in the language (i.e., Spanish) and dialect of the region. A total of three focus group sessions, each including four to nine guinea pig producers, were conducted for this study to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices in guinea pig production. All focus groups were held in community houses of the rural parishes near the participants' residences. Focus groups were recorded and transcribed in Spanish before being translated into English by one of our native Spanish-speaking team members (D.G.) to facilitate qualitative analysis and theme identification by the native English-speaking research team members. At the commencement of each focus group, producers were asked about the number of people or employees working on their farm as well as a complete animal inventory including other species that may be raised on site. All participants were from Pichincha province, born and raised in their respective parishes. Additional demographic information (e.g., age, gender) of respondents was not recorded, though participants were encouraged to introduce themselves and describe their production characteristics at the inception of the focus group session (Table 1). Subsequent questions covered the basic principles of animal production and husbandry, including breeding, feeding, treatment, preventative activities, and euthanasia.

Qualitative analysis

Participant characteristics, including animal inventory, were summarized. Given the novel, exploratory nature of this work, a thematic analysis was performed. Recognizing the diversity in thematic analysis, the study was centered in reflexive thematic analysis and guided by a critical realist paradigm [[16], [17], [18]]. An iterative and inductive approach was applied, with the analysis process following the six steps described by Braun and Clark [19]. Succinctly, inductive codes were identified via an iterative open coding process until a unifying theme was determined. The quality of the transcription was verified and/or corrected by H.F. and G.F. before data analysis. After the first focus group, the full study team met to refine the survey tool and methods. This reassessment and survey tool update aimed to strengthen the study team's ability to identify and describe unifying themes and to ensure robust data collection during the second and third focus group sessions without influencing the study findings [19].

Each transcript was reviewed by two coders (H.F. and G.F.) at least three times and independently coded using open coding methodology [19]. An independent readthrough of the transcript was conducted the first round, with inductive codes independently assigned when each coder read through the transcript a second time. Themes and codes assigned represented either a discipline or topic evidenced by the response directly, or the discipline required to address the issue via the authors' future educational interventions.

Subsequently, the two coders convened to identify an initial unifying theme, or concept, that connected the others identified in the analysis. The following taxonomy was used to code data: primary themes were defined as recurring patterns associated with inductive codes identified during thematic analysis. Domains referred to the sections of an existing theory under which the themes reside. More than one domain could be present in an existing theme.

During the third, and final, review of the transcripts, the two coders met once again to discuss the identified codes and facilitate additional reflexivity. The identified themes were then finalized and a single code agreed upon. Results were subsequently reviewed to identify an overarching, unifying theme that effectively connected the identified domains and themes in the study based on existing theory in the food animal space [19].

Results

Nineteen guinea pig producers participated in this study across three focus groups (nine (9) participants in Conocoto, six (6) participants in Amaguaña and four (4) participants in Ilalo-Guangoplo) (Fig. 1). Producers either worked the farm alone or with a spouse or other family member(s) to raise animals on their farms. Due to the discussion style of the focus groups not all participants responded to each question. However, among the 14 participants who reported their guinea pig inventory at the time of the focus group, the average number per farm was 20 guinea pigs with a range of 0–60 guinea pigs per farm. Among the 17 participants who answered the question regarding raising multiple species on their farms, all indicated raising other species besides guinea pigs, including poultry, small and large ruminants, camelids, and canines. Producers reported raising guinea pigs either indoors, especially in the case of younger animals, or near the home outdoors (e.g., under the house or in nearby pens). Among the 15 participants who answered the question on housing style, 10 participants mentioned keeping their guinea pigs outside the home. Participants primarily reported rearing animals for household consumption as opposed to producing for commercial sale. Specifically, among the 14 participants who answered the question on consumption and production practices, 13 stated that they raised their guinea pigs for internal household consumption.

Given the animal husbandry focus of the interviews, identified themes closely followed the themes of the questions themselves. For instance, questions related to animal feeding practices elicited responses tied to animal health and nutrition (Table 1). Upon initial coding, connections to existing frameworks, One Health and One Welfare, arose. And, ultimately, the One Health approach emerged as the primary domain under which the identified themes fell. The main unifying theme mapped back to the concepts of traditional and modern medicine, highlighting the co-existence of these two philosophies and the need for a holistic, or integrated, approach when identifying solutions that enable continued industry improvement. The three levels of code were as follows: 1) primary themes of Animal Health, Husbandry and Biosecurity, Animal Nutrition and Welfare, Animal Welfare and Euthanasia, and Valuation; 2) traditional and/or modern medicine practices unifying themes; and 3) One Health domains (Table 2). Though no direct animal welfare assessments were collected as part of this study, the discipline of animal welfare emerged as a study theme in several areas of the work given its presence in animal production and husbandry.

Animal health, husbandry and biosecurity

Questions related to animal health and disease invoked responses that fell into the categories of animal husbandry, genetics, biosecurity, and valuation, highlighting commonly held beliefs by study participants on the basics of animal production. Producers across the focus groups outlined a mixed traditional and modern medicine practice to maintain good genetics in the herd, with an aim to prevent disease and/or effectively treat animals in their herds. For example, many producers noted the importance of genetic diversity to ensure the integrity of the herd. One producer stated, “…you can't mate them with their father or brother because the breed deteriorates,” suggesting a recognition that inbreeding could result in the decline in overall health and well-being within smaller or more isolated animal populations. Others reinforced this point, mentioning that they “exchange breeders among the neighbors”. Finally, one producer noted utilizing the expertise of their veterinarian in sourcing the best genetics in the quote “we've bought them from specific places recommended by the doctor.”

Similar points were made by participants when discussing infectious diseases. As one producer discussed a case of parasitism in their herd, they described both the traditional processes for addressing a potentially contaminated environment as well as working with a veterinarian to treat specific issues:

“For lice, I use the knowledge that has been passed down from grandparents to their children. I give them chilco to deworm them; there's a weed called ‘la purga.’ I give them that once a month. I also sprinkle a bit of rue or marigold on the floor from time to time to help disinfect. They eat a little bit, and then they get dewormed.”

Another producer described using chemically disinfecting the cage in between herds:

“…when I finish a batch, I actually buy a chemical, or my husband buys a chemical to put in the new guinea pigs or when changing their cages, we always fumigate and disinfect all the cages.”

The unifying theme of traditional and modern medicine was evident when participants discussed basic animal husbandry.

Animal nutrition and welfare

When discussing animal feeding and watering protocols, producers described approaches that either leaned solely on traditional practices or included a blend of traditional and modern practices. One producer invoked traditional practices noting “I was told not to give water to guinea pigs as they might get bloated and die.” as the reason for not providing drinking water to guinea pigs in their care. While another stated “With grass, they don't need water.” A third producer made it clear why water was not always provided, and that hydration was dependent on the feed source in the following statement:

“… the way I manage their food is with herbs….They eat cutul, sugarcane, and ground corn, and they also get balanced feed. I don't give them water because the herbs are often very tender, or sometimes they're wet, so I give them that, and they haven't needed water because the herbs are already moist…”

One producer made a similar argument but, contrary to others, noted that water is needed in certain instances in their statement:

“…, they don't need water, but when I give them balanced feed, then I do put water in little bottle jars….All day, they eat and drink water, eat, drink water, eat.”

Animal feed and nutrition often followed traditional practices with animals receiving traditional herbs and receiving hydration from their feed; however, instances were noted where water was needed to supplement the animal's feed source.

Animal welfare and euthanasia

Humane endings relevant to disease incidence and slaughter were also covered at multiple points in the focus groups with the unifying theme of animal welfare and euthanasia emerging as the identifying characteristic. Producers discussed a variety of methods for euthanizing guinea pigs, such as exsanguination, blunt force trauma, and cervical dislocation “Like with the chicken”. Some mentioned allowing the animal to die from its ailments, citing it as a natural process. Producers at times took this a step further, stating in their opposition to utilizing medications and/or other preventative strategies that these were unnatural in the quote “No chemicals, if it dies, it dies. I raise them naturally, and that's it”.

Beyond the physical act of euthanizing these animals, producers described how euthanasia emotionally impacted them due to their closeness to the animals, referring to the guinea pigs as “my little children”. In addition, the traditional medicine aspect was present here when producers described beliefs around existing methods for handling and euthanizing their guinea pigs, while simultaneously acknowledging a discomfort with an existing practice, as shared in the quote: “I was always taught to sacrifice guinea pigs by crushing their heads, but I don't sacrifice them myself—I love them.”

Valuation

In addition to the animal health specific responses tied to disease prevention and treatment, producers often discussed household valuation of guinea pigs and the economic impacts of their decisions, especially as related to the involvement of veterinarians. Multiple producers reiterated the perceived high cost of the guinea pigs being produced in relation to the cost of veterinary care in quotes such as “…they charge too much”, “More than what the guinea pig is worth…”. Thus, in instances where a health condition required treatment producers noted using traditional methods, such as:

“…my grandmother, instead of buying DDT for fleas, would clean the area where the guinea pigs were and spread ash to prevent fleas.”

or purchasing products, such as topic or oral antiparasitics, at the “veterinary store” as noted in the following quote:

“So, I would go [to the veterinary store] to buy the powder, take it, and apply it myself. If, by God's will, it recovered, great and if not, well, that was it. Calling the vet at that moment would cost more than the guinea pig itself, so we just leave it there.”

Other producers, confident in identifying and treating conditions in their herd, reiterated they saw no need to include veterinarians in promoting the health and welfare of the animals in their care:

“No, because I can usually tell what's wrong and I give them celery water when I detect it in time.”

Beyond the cost of the guinea pigs themselves, the available market for production also determined the profitability of this production type. Some producers suggested an overall decrease in production in the country following the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, participants also discussed that exchanging the meat for other goods was a relatively common practice in rural communities:

“I think this is seen more in rural communities… I think it's more common in the countryside. In the city, they don't do this as much. But in the countryside…this was very common during the pandemic. You had something, I had something, and we traded especially for food.”

In combination, these findings suggest a valuation, or economic, component influencing animal health and welfare in this study population.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify guinea pig producers' key knowledge, attitudes and practices related to guinea pig production in three rural parishes of Pichincha, Ecuador while considering a One Health, One Welfare model. This work drives home the crucial role that guinea pigs play in the culture and health of individuals in the study population. Additionally, this work found that producers often balanced and, at times, blended modern and traditional medicine and production practices when raising guinea pigs for food, to safeguard guinea pig welfare through preventative care and disease management. Traditional practices cited in the study (e.g., feeding guinea pigs fresh herbs, thereby eliminating the need to provide access to water; using marigold and other herbs to treat environments plagued by ectoparasites) are often rooted in science. For instance, it is well recognized that the moisture content in feed influences the fluid water intake of animals raised for consumption; however, feed and water intake standards in the US are often derived from the use of guinea pigs in lab animal studies [3]. The “balanced feed” product referenced in the study is a dry, pelleted feed. Pelleted feeds are typically 10–12 % moisture, so feed alone cannot meet the animal's daily water requirement [2,20]. Therefore, ad libitum access to clean water is vital for overall health, welfare and growth of animals used for meat production [2,3,20]. Additionally, naturally occurring toxins in marigolds are known to repel certain pests [21,22]. In fact, the synthesis of botanical pesticides like those derived from marigolds and chrysanthemums are currently ubiquitous in commercially available pesticide products [23].

Traditional and modern medicine practices, though reflective of the interdependence between humans and animals, do not formally constitute a One Health approach. Yet, our results also suggest that producers often worked in a One Health way, simultaneously addressing human, animal, and environmental health and welfare topics including nutrition, infectious diseases, the economics of veterinary care, and euthanasia. The application of the One Health approach is not unique to this form of production [7,24,25]. The animal agriculture industry in the United States often takes a One Health approach to their production by implementing practices that span all three sectors of the approach [24]. For instance, the swine industry in the United States signals the One Health approach in their mantra of doing what is right for “People, pigs, and the planet” which is reiterated and emulated in other sectors of animal production across the globe [24]. Additionally, researchers encourage a One Health approach to food animal production globally which positively impacts human, animal, and environmental health and ensures the sustainability of these systems [7,25]. This overarching theme also emerged from our data, with animal welfare as a core theme among producers.

The term biosecurity refers to those practices that prevent the introduction and/or spread of infectious agents in animal production and veterinary medicine. Participants in this study described deploying both traditional and modern practices to positively impact the animal housing environments by reducing disease burden. Washing and “treating” the environment with boiling water and/or disinfectants can effectively reduce disease burden, depending on the pathogen or parasite present [1,4]. References to these practices were coded as mapping back to both the animal and environmental health aspects of the One Health framework, as discussed in previous work [26]. Furthermore, the positioning of animal housing units or pens outside the house may help minimize zoonotic pathogen contamination, subsequently reducing zoonotic transmission risk to guinea pig producers and their families [1,4,20].

Animal nutrition in this study involved a combination of traditional herbal sources as well as pelleted “balanced feed”. In various animal production species, animal feed, genetics, and other key husbandry practices directly determine the rate of growth of the animal [2,20]. Thus, the importance of a nutritionally balanced food source and access to clean water cannot be understated when aiming for efficient growth of a production animal and providing animals with the opportunity to consume complex forages that result in positive affective states of satiety [2,3,27]. As such it is important for researchers to understand the nutritional and moisture content of the products traditionally offered to guinea pigs in this study population.

Animal welfare and euthanasia are key components of animal production, as ensuring good animal welfare requires producers to prioritize the animal's affective state from birth to death. In this study population, participants described multiple methods of euthanasia including blunt force trauma, exsanguination, and cervical dislocation. Some participants also mentioned simply allowing the animal to die of its affliction which is not considered an approved method in any area of animal welfare science. Though acceptable standards of slaughter and/or euthanasia are much debated worldwide, in the United States where the research team resides, methods for humane euthanasia are described by the American Veterinary Medical Association [[28], [29], [30]]. According to these references, asphyxiation via CO2 exposure and/or exsanguination or decapitation under anesthesia are the preferred methods of euthanasia [28,31]. It is important to note that in the United States guinea pigs are not produced for food. Instead, guinea pigs are described in the humane endings document as laboratory animal species; they are also kept as pets [30]. Furthermore, guinea pig meat known as “cuy” is not commonly available in the United States, though statistics on its consumption are not readily available [32].

The caring-killing paradox is well noted in the scientific literature and present in this study, with participants expressing discomfort in performing euthanasia given the close ties to their animals [33]. Participants described the guinea pigs as their “…little children” and expressed sadness tied to their death, suggesting an emotional connection to the animals they raise for meat. Previous studies have noted farm workers' reluctance to complete the task of euthanasia due to an emotional connection with the animals they work with daily [[34], [35], [36]]. Thus, educational activities aimed at improving the health and welfare of these animals can utilize One Welfare techniques, leaning on this compassion to improve welfare-related outcomes for these animals.

Connections in this study were noted between common production practices, One Health, One Welfare, and traditional and modern medicine. This underscores the influences, balance, and at times opposition of traditional beliefs and modern practices in the production of guinea pigs for food. Both One Health and One Welfare approaches are utilized in modern animal production systems, considering the overlapping aspects in these systems of human health, animal health, animal welfare, and environmental health [7,24]. Researchers and veterinarians working in this space should consider this perspective to support producers in the region to continuously enhance their practices and improve the overall health and welfare of their animals. For instance, this study identified economic drivers, or valuation, that influenced consultation with a veterinarian, as traditional medicine provided producers the confidence to act independently to positively influence guinea pig health. Thus, veterinarians aiming to actively improve the health and welfare of these animals must be grounded in the factors that influence producer behaviors to remove any remaining barriers to their involvement. Furthermore, researchers must acknowledge when traditional practices are grounded in science and, where appropriate, work to fold them into herd health and response plans tied to animal health and welfare. These professionals must also simultaneously work to identify and improve upon traditional practices that may negatively impact guinea pig health and welfare. With the acceptance and inclusion of the two influencing factors, traditional and modern medicine, in a One Health-One Welfare framework, production system improvements can be achieved through education and outreach.

Our survey tool covered a wide variety of topics making it difficult at times to reach saturation. In addition, basic sociodemographic questions were not captured as part of the focus group interviews, leading to incomplete collection of these data across all three focus groups, and not all participants answered each question. This lack of data prohibits comparative analysis of results by group and is an important limitation in terms of contextualizing the producers' experiences. The authors, however, worked to summarize key findings based on those comments available for analysis in this work. Additionally, the referral sampling approach for participant recruitment use in this study has limitations compared to population-based sampling (e.g., potential selection bias). However, this approach was selected given the small, hard-to-reach population and considering practicality and culturally appropriate recruitment [19]. Future projects will likely include a short-written or orally administered survey capturing key demographic information prior to the focus group inception and aim for broader recruitment. Additionally, inclusion of direct observations on participant properties (i.e., farms) regarding management practices and animal welfare approaches in future studies could also increase robustness of this work.

Kappa scores were not calculated in this work [19]. The authors acknowledge that the use of quantitative measures to validate qualitative work is much discussed in the literature [37,38]. Given the novel and exploratory nature of this early work in guinea pig production, the authors elected to forgo kappa scores remaining true to the roots and value of qualitative research studies.

In conclusion, this focus group-based, qualitative study aimed to characterize the knowledge, attitudes and practices of guinea pig producers with a focus on animal health and welfare in the Andean region of Ecuador. These results suggest that the One Health, One Welfare-based approach, which recognizes producers' reliance on a combination of traditional and modern medicine and production practices, adequately describes these influences. This approach along with an acknowledgment of the cultural history and reliance of this population on the guinea pig species for health and prosperity must be recognized and incorporated into future studies and educational programs when engaging with these communities and beyond.
