The relationship between dyadic coping and post-traumatic growth in breast cancer patients and spouses: based on potential profile analysis
Authors: Yefan Zhang, Yuqi Gao, Ning Zhang, Kaiyan Xu, Shuo Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe disease impact of breast cancer is to view the couple as a whole, breast cancer is a disease shared by the couple. Cancer coping has evolved from an individual perspective to a dyadic coping perspective for couples. The purpose of this study was to identify and describe the dyadic coping categories of couples with breast cancer and to analyze the relationship between dyadic coping categories and post-traumatic growth (PTG) in couples with breast cancer.MethodIn this study, breast cancer patients and their spouses were selected as the study population from several tertiary hospitals in Jinzhou City using convenience sampling method in 2023. A general information questionnaire, Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI), and Post-traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) were used to survey 254 couples with breast cancer. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed using Mplus (version 8.3). SPSS 26.0 was used for data entry, and data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance and multifactor logistic regression. Significance level α = 0.05.ResultThe study examines the influence of various factors such as educational attainment, age, income, residence, medical insurance, surgical procedure type, disease stage, and breast cancer recurrence on patients' coping strategies. Multiple logistic regression analysis showed that education level, age at marriage, place of residence, form of health care coverage, stage of the disease, and whether or not the disease recurred were significant predictors of each indicator (P < 0.05). The LPA yielded four dyadic coping subgroups, with high relative entropy (0.942), respectively, each accounting for 7.4%, 17.1%, 24.3%, and 51.2% of the total. The study found that the high-level coping group scored significantly higher in all dimensions of binary coping with post-traumatic growth among different patient and spouse subgroups.ConclusionCouple dyadic coping in breast cancer patients was categorized into four groups. The low-level coping group is the one that needs focused observation and intervention. This is a better reference for caregivers to provide more targeted coping programs based on the different dyadic coping categories of couples of breast cancer patients.

Background

Breast cancer has surpassed lung cancer as the most prevalent form of cancer among women globally and is showing an upward trend [1].The year 2020 saw the ascension of female breast cancer to the top of both the worldwide cancer incidence and mortality spectrums, with the disease accounting for roughly 12.50% and 6.92% of all malignant tumor incidence and deaths, respectively [2].The absence of breasts resulting from breast cancer surgery and the significant discomfort caused by chemotherapy have had profound negative physical and psychological effects on patients. Additionally, as the primary source of social support for these patients, spouses experience immense pressure in providing care and face heightened anxiety about disease progression, leading to a strong stress reaction [3, 4].

Breast cancer incidence and mortality in China are projected to rise to 35.95/100,000 and 11.94/100,000, respectively, by 2030 due to the aging population and lifestyle changes [5].Although the survival rate of breast cancer patients has been increasing year by year with the popularization of cancer screening and advances in diagnostic and treatment technologies [6], however, it has caused a series of problems such as bleeding and ulceration of the breasts, wasting and fatigue due to the depletion of body functions, missing breasts and scars caused by the surgery and hair loss and sexual dysfunction produced by chemotherapy, and it still puts a great deal of pressure on the patients themselves [7]. Meanwhile, Breast cancer patients' spouses face emotional and financial stress, leading to psychological problems and a decline in their relationship [7]. Therefore, breast cancer can be considered as a unit that affects both partners in a relationship. In other words, it is a common stressor for couples.

The way that people cope with cancer occurrences has shifted throughout time from the perspective of the individual patient to that of the patient and spouse, or dyadic coping [8]. Dyadic coping refers to the couple's joint coping responses and strategies in the face of a stressful event [9]. Vigorous spousal encouragement alleviates the stress of the illness and has a beneficial consequence on the physical and psychological well-being of both spouses [10]. Positive dyadic coping involves patients and spouses providing support and joint coping for a disease, promoting communication, and interdependence, and establishing a strong relationship, which can alleviate stress and enhance intimacy [11, 12]. However, the bidirectional coping of couples with breast cancer in the face of their condition is staggeringly complex, with more pronounced group heterogeneity, that is, different couples with breast cancer will be characterized by bidirectional coping under the influence of different psychological, social, cultural, and other factors. An Individual-Centered Approach Considers Couples of Breast Cancer Patients as a Unit of Analysis and Identifies the Interactive Effects of Coping Styles [13]. When employing a person-centered approach instead of a more variable-centered methodology, it is more informative to identify variability in dyadic coping in couples dealing with breast cancer [14]. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a human-centered method for constructing typologies or clusters based on response patterns generated by observed variables [15, 16]. LPA allows couples with the same dyadic coping profile to be grouped into the same subgroup and couples with different dyadic coping profiles to be grouped into another category, thus ensuring that between-group heterogeneity is maximized and within-group heterogeneity is minimized [17].

According to positive psychology, people who have experienced a traumatic incident can also undergo some good psychological changes, a process known as post-traumatic growth (PTG), in addition to negative psychological experiences. PTG refers to the positive changes in psychological aspects experienced by an individual through growth and endeavor during a traumatic negative life incident [18]. It is a multidimensional concept that manifests itself in a deep understanding of the value of life, a reordering of what is essential in life, personal empowerment, the emergence of new possibilities, intimacy with others, and a positive change in spirituality after experiencing a life event or catastrophe that threatens life, bodily integrity, or inflicts serious harm [19].Research has indicated [20] that there is a reciprocal effect on posttraumatic growth (PTG) in couples dealing with breast cancer. Increased intimacy among breast cancer patients and their partners is significantly associated with both individuals experiencing more positive psychological changes. Furthermore, these associations are linked to the overall quality of the couple's relationship. Nevertheless, existing studies can only demonstrate the correlation between the stage of breast cancer patients and couples, and cannot confirm the correlation between the level of heterogeneity of couples and PTG of breast cancer patients in different populations. Therefore, this study will examine differences in PTG across dyadic coping types.

Object and method

The cross-sectional investigation was utilized in this research. The convenience sampling method was used to select breast cancer patients and their spouses from several tertiary hospitals in Jinzhou City as the study population. Inclusion Criteria: Patients with breast cancer: (1) Diagnosed with breast cancer by histopathologic examination. (2) Aged ≥ 18 years. (3) Married and able to participate in this study. (4) Aware of the disease and diagnosis, and agreed to participate in this study. Spouse: (1) Legal spouse of the patient. (2) Age ≥ 18 years. (3) Married and able to participate in this study. (4) Aware of the disease and diagnosis and agree to participate in the study. Exclusion Criteria: Patients with breast cancer: (1) Cognitive impairment.(Measured using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale.) [21, 22] (2) Suffering from severe heart, lung, liver, kidney disease or other malignant tumors. (3) Having severe visual, hearing, and speech impairments that prevented them from completing the survey. (4) Having a history of psychiatric illnesses, psychotic disorders, and disorders of consciousness. Spouses: (1) Those with cognitive impairment. (2) Those with comorbidities such as severe heart, lung, liver, kidney, or other malignant tumors. (3) Those with severe visual, auditory, and speech impairments that prevented them from completing the survey. (4) Those with a history of prior psychiatric illnesses as well as psychiatric disorders and disorders of consciousness. (5) Those who have suffered other major life events (for example, bereavement, separations from the workforce, and so forth) or traumatic events (for example, natural disasters and manmade disasters).

According to Kendall's rough estimation of sample size: Sample size = [number of variables × (5–10) × (1 + (10–15%)] [23], the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of variables. It considers a 20% sample attrition rate and potential errors due to convenience sampling. Three questionnaires were used in this study with dimensions of 11 items from the General Information Questionnaire, 6 items from the Dyadic Coping Scale, and 5 items from the Post-Traumatic Growth Scale, for a combined total of 22 dimensions and a sample size of 132 to 264 participants. A total of 270 pairs of breast cancer patients and their spouses were interviewed, and 254 valid questionnaires were finally returned, providing an effective response rate of 94.07%.

The study was authorized by the Jinzhou Medical University Third Affiliated Hospital's Institutional Review Board. The ethical approval number is KX2023023. To determine which patients were eligible for breast surgery, nurses assisted patients in filling out questionnaires during their hospital stay and analyzing hospital medical data. A qualified nurse researcher informed patients and their spouses about the goal and methodology of the study before inviting them to participate. They were also made aware of the survey's voluntary nature as well as the data's confidentiality and anonymity. Every volunteer who consented to take part in the study signed an informed consent form before they were enrolled.

Survey tools

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire collected information on age, education, occupation, age at marriage, per capita monthly household income, place of residence, current form of health care coverage, type of surgery, affected side, stage of disease, and whether or not the breast cancer was recurrent. While participants and their spouses self-reported demographic data, clinical data was extracted by qualified nurse researchers from their medical records.

Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI)

The Chinese version of the Dyadic Coping Scale, developed by Bodenmann [24] and others and later culturally adapted by Xu [25], was used in this study to measure couples' joint responses and coping styles in response to stressful events. The scale consists of 37 items and includes six dimensions: stress communication, supportive coping, negative coping, empowering coping, coping together, and quality appraisal (not counted in the total score). Except for the coping and quality evaluation dimensions, the other four dimensions can be categorized into perceived self and perceived partner coping strategies. The higher the total score, the better the ability of both partners to cope with stress. The DCI has been extensively tested on couples with multiple chronic illnesses and has excellent reliability and validity [26–28]. Cronbach's were α = 0.83 (patients) and α = 0.82 (spouses), respectively [26].

Post-traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI)

Tedeschi [25] and others developed this scale in their 1996 study. The Post-traumatic Growth Inventory consists of 20 items, encompassing 5 dimensions: relationships with others, new possibilities, personal strength, spiritual change, and appreciation of life. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of post-traumatic growth (PTG). The PTGI has been used extensively in couples with chronic illnesses, with good reliability and validity [29–31]. Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.90 [29]was reported.

Survey methods

In this study, after obtaining the consent of patients and their spouses, face-to-face surveys, on-the-spot distribution, and retrieval were used, problems were identified and corrected on-site on time, and unqualified and incomplete questionnaires were excluded to improve the validity of the questionnaires. Patiently explaining and answering coherently when study participants have questions. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were strictly mastered, and the principles of voluntary participation, informed consent, and strict confidentiality were followed, so that the research subjects actively cooperated in participating in the study.

Statistical methods

To aid in improving dyadic coping in couples with breast cancer, the current study used LPA to objectively identify several dyadic coping subgroups for further quantitative investigation.LPA using Mplus 8.3 for dyadic coping of couples with breast cancer was employed to identify the best model. We explored models that could recognize 1—7 categories and used the following fit metrics to determine the optimal number of potential contours. LPA model fit metrics, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the sample corrected BIC (aBIC), indicate that a smaller value of these three metrics corresponds to a better model fit [32]. Entropy takes values from 0–1, the larger the entropy value, the higher the accuracy rate [33]. When the corresponding p-values of the Bootstrap method based on the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) and Lo Mendell Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR) [34] are significant (p < 0.05), it indicates that the k-class model is superior to the k-1-class model [35]. The results show that the best number of models is 4 and the overall trend is consistent across the four categories. SPSS 26.0 was employed for data processing, a normality test was implemented, quantitative information conforming to normal distribution was represented by mean ± standard deviation, categorical variables were represented by frequency and constitutive ratio, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparison between multiple groups. Variables with statistically significant comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Discussion

This research used latent profile analysis to identify four different potential dyadic coping categories within each of the five dimensions of couples of breast cancer patients: low-level coping group, medium–high common coping group, medium–high supportive coping group, and high-level coping group. Significant differences in couple dyadic coping and scores on each dimension were found in different subgroups of breast cancer patients, indicating heterogeneity in couple dyadic coping in breast cancer patients. In addition, the results of the LPA in this study yielded good fit indices and good mean attribution probabilities, providing some evidence of the accuracy of the fit and categorization of the four latent categories. In terms of identification type, the low-level coping group accounted for 7.4% of the total sample, the medium–high common coping group accounted for 17.1%, the medium–high supportive coping group accounted for 24.3%, and the high-level coping group accounted for 51.2%, which is lower than what has been found in existing studies [36–38]. Most breast cancer couples chose high levels of dyadic coping, reflecting their predisposition to face the disease [36]. It has been established that patients with gynecologic cancers with dyadic coping mostly select a low level of coping, and environmental and disease-related reasons can interpret this difference, which is associated with a relatively higher survival rate for breast cancer than for gynecologic oncology cancers [39, 40].

The results of the research provide a literature rationale for studying dyadic coping in couples of breast cancer patients and provide evidence of between-group differences. In this study, most of the variables were directly and significantly correlated with different dyadic coping subgroups in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics. This is inconsistent with previous research, which found no correlation between medical variables and dyadic coping types [41], and the reason for the discrepancy in results may be due to inconsistencies in the demographic characteristics of the study population and setting. Nevertheless, the present study, in conjunction with previous research [36], showed that couples with low levels of education were more likely to be in the low-level coping subgroup, and couples with higher levels of education were more inclined to be in the medium–high coping group and high-level coping group, concerning educational attainment. The explanation for this result may be associated with the socio-economic burden caused by low educational attainment. Breast cancer burdens couples, especially those with less education, resulting in limited access to disease knowledge and social support.

According to existing studies [42–44], the current study is the first to explore and analyze the relationship between different dyadic coping subgroups and PTG. Couples with high post-traumatic growth scores are more likely to belong to the high-level coping group, couples with moderate post-traumatic growth scores are more likely to belong to the medium–high group, and couples with low scores are more likely to belong to the low-level coping group. The outcome of the present research was lower compared to the PTG scores of existing studies [30], and the reason for the difference may be due to the different demographic characteristics of the study population. PTG occurs as a result of a shift in the individual's fundamental beliefs about themselves and their surroundings in the face of something drastic [45]. It has been established that women with breast cancer experience PTG after a disease event, and that increased expression of these values can counteract the negative consequences of the disease as they adapt to the disease [29]. Up till now, research has demonstrated [30] that some patients have PTG as a result of the trauma connected to their breast cancer diagnosis, treatment, and recovery. Face-to-face encounters with mortality set off stress and mechanisms that result in PTG, a process in which the sufferer transforms their suffering into something positive. We may conclude that PTG is a common outcome that is caused by both patients and spouses based on the research that is currently available on PTG in cancer patients [46]. The better growth in this present study for PTG for breast cancer patients and spouses seems to confirm the theoretical model of post-traumatic growth in oncological diseases [47].Arriving at this finding demonstrates that traumatic events associated with breast cancer promote growth in breast cancer patients and spouses. This ending is adaptive and a result of cognitive processing of the traumatic episode. Breast cancer patients who receive chemotherapy or radiation show growth that is significantly associated with appreciation of life after a radical change, changes in self-perception, and relationships with others, according to the results of a study [48]. The results of the current study clearly show that patients who have undergone a traumatic experience (breast cancer) show higher positive changes in self-perception and relationships with others. Therefore, this study provides that healthcare workers as well as patients' families who face dyadic coping and PTG with extreme situations can improve constructive solution coping, which motivates them to show high levels of dyadic coping.

Research significance and clinical guidance

In this research, latent profile analysis identified four different latent categories in the five dimensions of patient and spouse dyadic coping: low-level coping group, medium–high common coping group, medium–high supportive coping group, and high-level coping group. Through our study, we have indicated that breast cancer patients and partners overall use similar dyadic coping styles, which should be used as a community intervention. Also, the results indicated a higher-than-average level of dyadic coping in breast cancer patients and spouses. More importantly, the present study is the first to analyze and compare the heterogeneity of dyadic coping subgroups with the variability in PTG, with dyadic coping and post-traumatic growth levels positively correlated. Moreover, this research uses a scientific approach to classify dyadic coping into four subgroups and is not traditionally centered on variables, thus overcoming some of the limitations in the research methodology.

For clinical practice guidance, it can first enable clinical health care workers to distinguish the expected distribution of dyadic coping in breast cancer patients and spouses and to consider the coping styles of couples of each type of patient, as well as to consider coping styles and PTG and to provide a scientific reference basis for effectively identifying the objects that need to be focused on in clinical practice and to carry out relevant research and formulate interventions in a more targeted manner. Secondly, couples with breast cancer who can cope with good outcomes through dyadic coping, couples with breast cancer are encouraged to formalize the inadequacies of their coping styles, accept their shortcomings, and be sensitive to identifying the problems that exist so that they can increase their coping skills and achieve better outcomes.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study restricts our ability to comprehend the trajectories of dyadic coping in couples coping with breast cancer. For dyadic coping there are fewer research variables and some limitations, more research variables and populations can be added in the future. Research tools that address dyadic coping and PTG are evaluative tools that are somewhat subjective and may lead to confidence bias.

Conclusion

Dyadic coping has important implications for couples with breast cancer. This research adopted LPA and found that there are four potential categories of dyadic coping in couples with breast cancer, namely“low-level coping group”, “medium–high common coping group”,“medium–high supportive coping group”, and “high-level coping group”, and identified the post-traumatic growth characteristics of couples with different types of dyadic coping. It also clarified the PTG characteristics of couples with distinct dyadic coping groups, which provides a new intervention perspective for caregivers to fully consider the potential categories of couples' dyadic coping in breast cancer patients, to formulate a more targeted intervention strategy accordingly.
