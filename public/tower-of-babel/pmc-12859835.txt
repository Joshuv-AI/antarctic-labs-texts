Monoclonal antibody preparation and linear epitope identification for the ASFV A151R protein
Authors: Zhibo Liu, Jianyi You, WenBo Zhang, Chenyang Gao, Shaohong Huang, Lang Gong, Xiongnan Chen, Guihong Zhang, Yiwei Huang, Zhao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfrican Swine fever (ASF) is an acute, hemorrhagic infectious disease caused by the African swine fever virus (ASFV), posing a major threat to the global pig farming industry. ASFV is a complex DNA virus. Its non-structural protein, A151R, is a thioredoxin crucial for the replication and morphogenesis of ASFV. In this study, a monoclonal antibody (mAb) 3E5D6 against pA151R (A151R Protein) was prepared using cell fusion technology to gain deeper insight into the function of A151R during ASFV infection. Immunolabeling of pA151R with 3E5D6 mAb revealed its involvement in viral factory formation and its characteristic early expression during infection. However, the antibody exhibited no detectable neutralizing activity. Subsequently, the antigenic epitope 84KIWSGEPR91 of 3E5D6 was identified using bioinformatic analysis and molecular biology techniques. Homology and structural analyses indicated that this amino acid sequence was highly conserved across pA151R of different strains and was located on the surface of the protein. Furthermore, the 87 S residue is a key site for 3E5D6 recognition of pA151R. This antibody will allow us to better characterize host-virus interactions and further our understanding of the pathophysiology of ASFV infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05252-3.Keywords: ASFV pA151R, Monoclonal antibody, B cell epitope

Introduction

African swine fever virus (ASFV) is the causative agent of African swine fever (ASF). Pigs of all ages are susceptible to ASF, with mortality rates reaching up to 100% following infection [1]. ASFV has severely impacted the pig industry, with increasing implications for global public health. Importantly, the current prevalence of ASF is complex, characterized by the coexistence of virulent genotype II strains, attenuated genotype I strains, and chimeric genotype I and II strains [2, 3], presenting significant challenges for the prevention and control of ASF.

ASFV is a large icosahedral double-stranded DNA virus containing 150–167 open reading frames (orfs) [4]. Encoding 68 structural proteins and more than 100 non-structural proteins, the length of the genome varies mainly because of an increase or decrease in the number of orfs in the multi-gene family (MGF) encoded by different ASFV strains [5]. Shorter genome lengths were caused by variations in the number of short repeats within or between genes [6]. Although the B cell epitopes of various ASFV proteins, such as p72, p30, CD2v, and pCP312R have been systematically identified [7–10], significant gaps remain in the field of epitope research for many other ASFV proteins.

The ASFV non-structural protein A151R is a thioredoxin. Recent studies have shown that this protein significantly inhibits type I interferon (IFN-β) production promoting the degradation of E3 ligase TRAF6 and inhibiting K63 ubiquitination of TBK1. This immune escape mechanism is a key regulator of viral pathogenicity [11]. Furthermore, thioredoxin is primarily responsible for reducing disulfide bonds, which play important roles in other large-scale DNA assembly processes. For example, in vaccinia virus, the E10R protein, a viral-encoded sulfhydryl oxidase, catalyzes the formation of correct disulfide bonds in key viral membrane proteins—essential for viral infectivity [12]. In herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), the UL6 portal protein forms intra- and intermolecular disulfide bonds during assembly [13]. These bonds are critical for maintaining the stable 12-mer ring structure required for viral DNA packaging. In our previous work, we also studied the mechanism of various ASFV immunosuppressive proteins [14–16], and during these studies, the acquisition of monoclonal antibodies proved crucial for in-depth research. Notably, pA151R has a unique WCTKC motif that differs from the structure of thioredoxin, and the unique spatial conformation formed by pA151R may affect the exposure status of its epitopes [17]. Currently, immunological research on pA151R has mainly focused on its functional mechanisms, and the antigenic characteristics of PA151R have not been systematically analyzed.

To further investigate the biological function of ASFV pA151R, we prepared a mAb against ASFV pA151R and identified the antigenic epitope of pA151R, for the first time, by shortening overlapping peptides. This works lays a foundation for the development of serological diagnostic reagents for ASFV infection and for further studies on the functional role of pA151R.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

The A151R gene was synthesized by Zhongding Biotechnology (Nanjing). HeLa cells, human embryonic kidney cells (HEK 293T), porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs), myeloma cells (SP2/0), and the pEGFP-C1 eukaryotic expression vector and pET-30a prokaryotic expression vector were stored in our laboratory. The WSL cells was presented by Professor Han Jun from China Agricultural University. Five-week-old SPF BALB/c mice were purchased from Yangzhou University. DH5α competent cells and BL21(DE3) competent cells were purchased from Tolo Port Biotechnology (Shanghai). The ASFV-GZ201801 strain (Genebank accession number: MT496893.1) was also preserved in our laboratory.

Amplification of A151R gene and construction of Recombinant plasmid

The A151R gene was amplified by PCR, and the empty pET-30a vector was digested with EcoRI and SacI and ligated by homologous recombination. The ligation product was transformed into DH5α competent cells and coated on LB plates containing ampicillin. After culturing at 37℃ for 12 h, single colonies were picked, and positive clones were selected for PCR identification to verify whether the target gene was correctly inserted into the vector. The positively cloned plasmid was extracted using a small plasmid extraction kit and sent to Sangon Bioengineering (Shanghai) for sequencing. The recombinant expression plasmid was identified as pET-30a-A151R by sequencing.

Prokaryotic expression and purification of pA151R

The pET-30a-A151R plasmid was transformed into BL21 competent cells, and monoclonal clones were selected and inoculated into LB medium containing ampicillin. The expression was induced by IPTG at a final concentration of 0.2 mmol/L− 1. After expression, bacteria were collected, resuspended in PBS, and sonicated in an ice bath. The precipitates of the inclusion bodies were collected by centrifugation. Finally, the target protein was purified using nickel affinity chromatography and refolded.

Monoclonal antibody production

Five BALB/c mice were immunized with 50 µg pA151R during the second and third weeks. The pA151R was mixed with Freund’s adjuvant and administered via subcutaneous injection. Freund’s incomplete adjuvant was used for the second and third immunizations. After three immunization cycles, blood samples were collected from the tail vein, and sera were prepared. Indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (IELISA) was used to determine antiserum titers. Mice whose serum ELISA titers exceeded 1:50,000 were selected and immunized with 100 µg of the antigen intraperitoneally three days before fusion. Mouse spleen cells were fused with SP2/0 cells in the presence of PEG1500, and the fused cells were spread into 96-well plates at 100 µL per well. After fusion, appropriate HAT selective medium was added, and the cells were cultured at 37 ℃, 5% CO2 for 7 to 10 days for fusion screening. Hybridoma cell lines stably secreting the pA151R mAb were screened according to IELISA results. Positive hybridoma cells were further subcloned until the positivity rate reached 100% and the monoclonal cell lines were expanded and cultured.

Epitope identification of monoclonal antibodies

The antigenic epitopes of pA151R were identified using a peptide truncation approach. The truncated A151R gene fragments were cloned into the eukaryotic expression vector pEGFP-C1. The primers are shown in Table 1. Western blotting and IFA were performed using the pA151R mAb and murine GFP-tagged antibody, respectively, as primary antibodies to determine the epitope regions recognized by the pA151R mAb.

Primers for B-cell epitope identification

Indirect ELISA

Proteins were coated onto 96-well ELISA plates at 4 °C for 12 h. Plates were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST). Blocking solution containing 5% skimmed milk was added, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing 3× with PBST, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Solarbio, Beijing) was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Plates were washed 3× with PBST, then TMB substrate (TIANGEN, Beijing) was added to each well. The enzymatic reaction proceeded for 15 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped using ELISA stop solution (Solarbio, Beijing). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Homology analysis of minimal epitopes of pA151R among different ASFV genotypes

Using the GenBank database in NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene), we retrieved the ASFV pA151R amino acid sequence of 20 ASFV strains. Based on the GZ201801 reference strains, MegAlign (Version 11.0), and JalView software were used to analyze the conservation of the identified epitopes.

Bioinformatics analysis and display of pA151R

The 3D structure of pA151R was predicted using the I-TASSER online prediction tool. The predicted 3D structure of the pA151R was displayed using PyMOL, and its minimum antigen epitope was marked. The biological function and activity of pA151R were predicted using the I-TASSER online prediction tool. Protean software was used to predict the two-dimensional structure of the minimal epitope region of the pA151R and to analyze its function.

Results

Prokaryotic expression, purification, and identification of pA151R

Recombinant pET-30a-A151R/BL21 was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG, and the whole bacteria, supernatants, and precipitates were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. A target protein band of approximately 16 kDa was observed, primarily localized in the precipitate (Fig. 1A), consistent with the expected molecular weight. A specific band at approximately 16 kDa was detected by nickel column affinity chromatography, refolding, SDS-PAGE, and western blot identification. This indicates that the pA151R was successfully purified (Fig. 1B and C). Moreover, pA151R reacted well with ASFV-positive standard serum (Fig. 1D).

Preparation of pA151R monoclonal antibody

To generate hybridoma cells for pA151R, BALB/c mice were immunized by subcutaneous injection of purified pA151R with complete Freund’s adjuvant for primary immunization and incomplete Freund’s adjuvant for booster immunization (Fig. 2A). The mouse with the highest serum titer after triple immunization (number 4), determined by ELISA, was used for cell fusion (Fig. 2B). Hybridomas were generated by PEG-mediated cell fusion. Positive cell lines screened by IELISA were sub-cloned using the limited dilution method. After three rounds of subcloning, the hybridoma cell line 3E5D6 was obtained. The selected positive hybridoma cells were expanded and cultured, and BALB/mice ascites was prepared. Purified 3E5D6 was obtained by purifying ascites using Protein Agarose. The heavy-chain subclass of purified 3E5D6 was identified as IgG2a and the light-chain subclass as κ (Fig. 2C).

Characterization of the pA151R monoclonal antibody

To examine the reacitivity of 3E5D6, an indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) and laser confocal microscopy were used to detect the subcellular localization of pA151R during ASFV infection. IFA was performed using 3E5D6 and rabbit p30 as the primary antibody. The results showed that pA151R (green) co-localized with p30 (red) in the cytoplasm and pA151R localized to the virus factory (Fig. 3A). Western blotting showed that 3E5D6 specifically detected pA151R bands in virus-inoculated samples (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that the antibody in mouse serum could specifically recognize pA151R expressed by PAMs after ASFV infection. We then examined the expression phase of pA151R during ASFV infection and found that the change in the expression level of pA151R was consistent with that of p30, which is an early expression protein (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, 3E5D6 exhibits no reactivity with common swine viruses such as PEDV, PRV, PCV2, or PRRSV, demonstrating excellent specificity (Fig. 3D). In conclusion, 3E5D6 generated in this study reacted well with pA151R in ASFV-infected cells. pA151R is an early expressed protein during ASFV infection and participates in the formation of its ASFV viral factory.

Determination of neutralizing activity of pA151R monoclonal antibody

The capacity of ASFV-positive serum to neutralize the virus and confer protective immunity in pigs remains controversial [18]. While antibodies targeting ASFV structural proteins have demonstrated neutralizing potential [19], incomplete viral neutralization persists even with high-titer immune sera, leaving a residual fraction of infectious virus unneutralized [20]. The mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain unclear. To evaluate the neutralizing activity of 3E5D6, we combined ASFV (MOI = 0.1) with serially diluted 3E5D6 in a 1:1 ratio and inoculated the mixture into porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs). Subsequent erythrocyte adsorption assays and qPCR analysis revealed no significant differences in viral infectivity or viral mRNA transcription levels compared to controls (Fig. 4A, B). To further assess whether 3E5D6 possesses neutralizing activity, we evaluated the HAD50 of ASFV after incubation with varying concentrations of 3E5D6. As shown in Figure C, no significant difference in ASFV infectivity was observed (Fig. 4C). Collectively, the 3E5D6 mAb demonstrates no neutralizing activity against ASFV. The inability of 3E5D6 to neutralize ASFV may be attributed to steric hindrance within the virion architecture that limits epitope accessibility.

Identification of antigenic epitopes of monoclonal antibodies to pA151R

To identify the epitopes of the mAbs against pA151R, systematic identification of epitopes was performed using a multistep strategy. Based on the results of the epitope Prediction Tool (IEDB), truncated mutations were performed on pA151R. (Fig. 5A and B) The three protein fragments were recombined with the pEGFP-C1 eukaryotic expression vectors GFP-A151R-1, GFP-A151R-2 and GFP-A151R-3, respectively. Epitope identification by western blotting using the GFP antibody and 3E5D6 as the primary antibody showed that the antibody specifically recognized the region from AA 42 to 107 (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, truncation of the pA151R from AA 42 to 107 revealed that 3E5D6 recognizes AA 77 to 92 (Fig. 5D and E). To map the minimal epitope recognized by mAb 3E5D6, we performed sequential truncation of the candidate peptide spanning residues 77 to 92. Initial N-terminal deletions narrowed the core epitope to residues 77 to 91. Subsequent C-terminal truncation of this region further refined the minimal binding sequence to 84KIWSGEPR91 within the pA151R (Fig. 5F). To further verify these results, 26 recombinant eukaryotic expression plasmids of short peptides were transfected into HeLa cells, and IFA was performed using an mAb. The results showed that 3E5D6 specifically recognized GFP-A151R and GFP-A151R-2, 6, 8, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, and 22, which was consistent with the western blotting results (Fig. 6). In conclusion, the minimum epitope region recognized by the mAb against pA151R prepared in this study was the amino acid sequence 84KIWSGEPR91.

Homology analysis of B cell epitopes

Next, we performed multiple sequence alignment of pA151R homologs. The analysis revealed that epitope regions recognized by the mAb generated in this study are highly conserved across Asian, European, and most African ASFV isolates (Black wireframe marks). However, two residue substitutions (I85 and S87) were identified in the African isolate Uvira_B53 (Fig. 7A). To assess the functional impact of I85 and S87 substitutions on 3E5D6 binding, we introduced I85V and S87V mutations into the pA151R-expressing plasmid and evaluated antigen recognition. Immunoassays demonstrated that 3E5D6 failed to recognize the S87V mutant in both HeLa and WSL, while retaining binding to the I85V variant (Fig. 7B). To further validate the critical role of S87 in 3E5D6 recognition of pA151R, we synthesized a short peptide containing the S87V mutation and performed epitope mapping via ELISA. As shown in Fig. 7C, consistent with our hypothesis, substitution of serine 87 with valine (S87V) abolished antibody binding to pA151R. Collectively, these findings indicate that the epitopes recognized by the mAbs are relatively conserved across geographic regions and genotypes, with residue S87 serving as an essential molecular determinant for 3E5D6-mediated recognition of pA151R.

Biological characteristics and Spatial structure prediction of pA151R

To elucidate the spatial localization of epitopes recognized by the pA151R mAb within the ASFV pA151R, we employed the I-TASSER computational prediction platform to model its tertiary structure. Using PyMOL software, we mapped the epitope region (AA 84 to 91) onto the predicted structure, highlighting it as a surface-exposed domain (red, Fig. 8A). Functional annotation of pA151R via I-TASSER suggested potential roles in membrane lipid metabolism, organic acid metabolism, phosphorylation, and DNA replication. Further structural analysis using the Protean tool revealed that the 84KIWSGEPR91 epitope resides predominantly within a β-sheet conformation, stabilized by reverse-turn motifs. This region exhibited high antigenic potential, as evidenced by its elevated antigenic index, hydrophilicity, and surface accessibility (Fig. 8B). Collectively, these structural insights underscore the surface-exposed nature of the mAb-binding site and provide critical functional clues for understanding pA151R’s role in ASFV biology.

Discussion

pA151R is a key virulence factor of ASFV, and deletion of the A151R gene has been shown to confer protection in pigs against challenge with the parental strain [21]. The preparation of pA151R mAb is essential for further studies on the functions of pA151R. In this study, we generated a pA151R-specific mAb, 3E5D6, using immunization and hybridoma techniques.

In characterizing 3E5D6, we found that pA151R was expressed 3 h after ASFV infection, classifying it as an early protein. In the later stage of ASFV infection, pA151R localizes to the viral factory, consistent with previous reports on A151R mRNA levels following ASFV infection [22, 23]. Moreover, structural mapping reveals that the epitope recognized by the 3E5D6 resides on the surface of the pA151R. Subsequently, to further characterize the function of 3E5D6, the neutralizing activity of pA151R was investigated. The results showed that 3E5D6 did not have neutralizing activity. This can be related to several aspects, such as the effectiveness of neutralizing antibodies, which generally depends on blocking virus binding to host cells or invasion; [24] pA151R has only been reported to be involved in virus assembly, therefore, pA151R may not be involved in viral invasion, limiting the neutralizing potential of 3E5D6. Furthermore, 3E5D6 cannot function early in the infection, which may be related to the complex structure and immune escape mechanisms of ASFV. ASFV possesses a multilayered capsule and a complex architecture [25, 26], which may potentially hinder 3E5D6 from reaching pA151R. Therefore, even though 3E5D6 has virus-neutralizing capabilities, it may be unable to penetrate the capsid to exert a neutralizing effect.

B-cell epitopes, defined as the antigenic regions recognized and bound by antibodies [27], serve as critical targets for vaccine development and antibody engineering [28]. Utilizing pathogen-specific B-cell epitopes as capture antigens offers high specificity, effectively avoiding cross-reactivity caused by non-specific epitopes present in whole antigens—such as in the differentiation between Dengue and Zika viruses [29]. Furthermore, identifying B-cell epitopes on viral proteins is pivotal for elucidating virus-host interactions and developing effective diagnostic tools and vaccines. Epitope-based vaccines exhibit enhanced safety profiles and demonstrate superior efficacy against viral variants compared to traditional vaccines.

In this study, we characterized the B-cell epitope of the ASFV non-structural protein pA151R, a key virulence factor involved in host immune regulation. Despite its functional significance, no active sites or epitopes had previously been mapped on pA151R. To address this gap, we generated 26 recombinant eukaryotic expression plasmids encoding truncated pA151R variants. Through systematic western blot and immunofluorescence assay (IFA) screening, we identified a linear epitope spanning residues 84KIWSGEPR91 in pA151R. Sequence alignment revealed high conservation of this epitope across European and Asian ASFV isolates. This finding demonstrates that the mAb elicits antibody responses against multiple distinct ASFV variants. Notably, the African strain Uvira_B53 harbored two substitutions (I85V and S87V), with functional assays confirming that the S87 residue is essential for 3E5D6 antibody binding (Fig. 7B). These findings demonstrate that the pA151R mAb exhibits broad reactivity against multiple ASFV genotypes, with the exception of the Uvira_B53 variant carrying the s87 mutation. This provides valuable guidance for developing ASFV vaccines and surveillance strategies.

This study also has several limitations. For instance, due to technical constraints, we did not elucidate how the S87 residue precisely regulates subunit interactions, which may require cryo-EM structural analysis coupled with molecular dynamics simulations. Furthermore, the primary cause underlying the loss of pA151R neutralizing activity remains unclear. Deeper investigations are needed to establish direct structural evidence demonstrating that viral structural barriers lead to the loss of pA151R’s neutralizing activity—for example, by assessing whether biomolecular mimics can penetrate the viral architecture.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a mAb against ASFV pA151R was successfully generated using hybridoma technology and B cell epitopes recognized by the mAb were systematically identified. This study not only expands the epitope information available for pA151R but also lays a foundation for further understanding the biological function of ASFV pA151R and the development of epitope-based serological detection methods.
