The performance of a novel, diabetes-specific cognitive screening tool against the MoCA in older adults with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Muzzammil H. Tayob, Caitlyn N. Goncalves, Mbali N. Musundwa, Azeemah Thokan, Husnaa Choonara, Zainab Modan, Wakhile P. Mabuza, Mashienyane P. Mapatlare, Farzahna Mohamed, Anu Mathew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are at a significantly greater risk of cognitive impairment than those without T2DM. Existing screening tools, such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), are not tailored to the vascular cognitive profile characteristic of T2DM. To address this gap, we designed the Diabetic Cognitive Assessment Tool (DCAT), a simplified instrument for efficient screening of cognitive impairment in older adults with T2DM, and assessed its performance against the MoCA. The associations of glycaemic control, frailty, depression, education, age, and T2DM duration with cognitive performance were also assessed.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted among 103 adults aged 65 years and older with T2DM attending a tertiary hospital diabetic clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa. Additionally, 55 age- and sex-comparable controls without T2DM were recruited. Cognitive function was assessed using the DCAT and MoCA, whereas frailty and depression were assessed using the FRAIL scale and Geriatric Depression Scale. DCAT performance relative to the MoCA was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Youden’s index identified the optimal cut-off. Associations between clinical and cognitive measures were analysed using Spearman’s correlation, and Cohen’s d quantified domain-specific effect sizes.ResultsThe DCAT’s area under the curve was 0.81. Using a cut-off score of 13, the DCAT achieved a sensitivity of 78.4% and specificity of 78.7%, with positive and negative predictive values of 89.2%, and 56.9%, respectively. Among participants with T2DM, HbA1c levels correlated negatively with MoCA scores, and depression scores correlated negatively with both DCAT and MoCA scores. In both groups, years of education correlated positively with both cognitive measures. Age and T2DM duration did not correlate significantly with cognitive measures in either group. The executive function and memory domains exhibited large and medium effect sizes, respectively.ConclusionThe DCAT demonstrated good discriminative ability in screening for cognitive impairment among older adults with T2DM. Poorer glycaemic control and depressive symptoms were associated with lower cognitive performance, whereas greater educational attainment was associated with better outcomes. The DCAT effectively assesses key cognitive domains, particularly executive function and memory. Further validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07498-4.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) affects approximately 462 million people globally, and diabetes as a whole affects almost 1 in 5 adults over the age of 65 [1, 2]. Cognitive impairment is a significant and increasingly recognised complication of T2DM [3]. Elevated glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, in particular, are associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment in older adults with diabetes [4].

T2DM and cognitive impairment share a bidirectional self-reinforcing relationship: T2DM contributes to cognitive decline through mechanisms such as cerebrovascular injury, whereas cognitive impairment, in turn, compromises an individual’s ability to self-manage T2DM effectively [5, 6].

Individuals with diabetes also face a greater risk of developing physical frailty, characterised by a decreased physiological reserve and resistance to stressors, increasing their vulnerability to adverse outcomes [7, 8]. Frailty has been shown to be associated with poorer processing speed, attention and memory in older adults; therefore, incorporating validated screening tools, such as the FRAIL scale, alongside neurocognitive testing, supports a more comprehensive evaluation of older adults with diabetes [9, 10].

Additionally, individuals with both diabetes and depression tend to have poorer glycaemic control and a greater risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications which further exacerbates cognitive decline [11]. Validated screening tools, such as the Geriatric Depression Scale, help identify this common but often neglected disorder that impacts optimal diabetes control [11, 12].

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) are among the tools recommended by the American Diabetes Association for cognitive screening in older adults with diabetes [13]. Both are single-page, 30-point screening tools that test the various domains implicated in cognitive impairment. The MMSE assesses orientation, memory, attention, language, and visuospatial ability, whereas the MoCA assesses the same domains in addition to executive function [14, 15]. Multiple studies suggest that the MoCA is more sensitive than the MMSE in detecting cognitive impairment [16–18].

Although these tools provide broad cognitive coverage, they were not designed to detect the specific cognitive profile associated with T2DM. Whilst there is overlap with vascular cognitive impairment, diabetes is characterised by a distinct metabolic-vascular phenotype driven by chronic hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation, resulting in structural and functional brain changes beyond classical vascular injury [5]. These mechanisms are associated with slowed processing speed and impaired psychomotor efficiency in addition to executive dysfunction and memory deficits [19]. This distinction highlights the need for screening tools that are not only sensitive to vascular-type deficits but also aligned with the broader metabolic and functional consequences of T2DM.

Additionally, unlike the MoCA, the MMSE requires paid licensing for clinical use, which may limit its feasibility in resource-constrained settings [20]. Furthermore, demographic and sociocultural factors, including age, educational attainment, and language proficiency, can potentially influence performance on these tests and should be considered when interpreting results [21, 22]. Finally, each test takes approximately 10 to 15 minutes to complete, which can be time intensive in busy clinical settings [23].

To address these gaps, we designed a novel screening tool termed the Diabetic Cognitive Assessment Tool (DCAT), which takes approximately 5 minutes to complete and assesses the areas of cognition commonly affected by T2DM. In contrast to a general vascular cognitive screen, the DCAT was designed to incorporate the domains of psychomotor efficiency and processing speed, alongside executive function, memory and attention to better reflect the functional and metabolic cognitive profile associated with T2DM [19, 24].

In this study, we examined the performance of the DCAT in screening for cognitive impairment in older adults with T2DM, using the MoCA as the reference standard. We also assessed the associations of glycaemic control, frailty, depression, education, age, and T2DM duration with cognitive function, and identified the specific cognitive domains most affected in these individuals.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional comparative analysis of cognitive tests was used to assess the DCAT’s concurrent performance relative to the MoCA. The study was conducted at the diabetic clinic of Charlotte Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital (CMJAH). CMJAH is a public-sector tertiary academic hospital located in Johannesburg, South Africa. Ethics approval to conduct the study was obtained from the University of the Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (reference R14/49).

Participant recruitment

Patients aged 65 years and older with T2DM and a documented HbA1c measurement within the preceding 6 months were recruited between 27 March 2025 and 11 September 2025. Recruitment was conducted by interviewers who were not the attending physicians, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The exclusion criteria included the absence of an HbA1c record within the last six months, significant hearing impairment, or a language barrier between the assessor and the patient. Patients were also excluded if they were experiencing a hypoglycaemic episode at the time of assessment; had a diagnosis of major depressive or psychotic disorder; had overt hypothyroidism (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone [TSH] > 10 mIU/L with a low thyroxine [T4]); were acutely delirious; had established dementia; had a disabling stroke; or other neurological deficits that impair language comprehension. Finally, wheelchair-bound patients were also excluded because their psychomotor function could not be assessed reliably.

The sample size was calculated pragmatically based on the average number of T2DM patients attending the clinic in over a six-month period, reflecting feasibility within the study setting. On average, 50–60 older adult T2DM patients visit the clinic each month. Using a 95% confidence interval with a 5% margin of error and a 50% proportion of the population, a target of 110 participants was calculated.

A healthy control group of participants aged 65 years and above without DM who passed the same exclusion criteria as the study cohort was recruited at a 2:1 ratio (110:55) to the target diabetic sample. They were recruited, with informed consent, through social networks from the same geographical location as the study cohort. Following recruitment, the age and sex characteristics of the two cohorts were statistically compared to ensure comparability.

Methods

Two data collection booklets were created for the diabetic and control groups, respectively. Both booklets recorded patients’ background information, consisting of sex, age, and years of education, as well as a checklist to ensure that they did not meet the exclusion criteria. In addition, the diabetic booklet recorded the following information retrospectively: T2DM duration; presence of target organ damage (TOD); most recent HbA1c; type of medication prescribed for DM; presence of polypharmacy; and incidence of hypoglycaemic events in the last month. Both booklets included the DCAT, MoCA, FRAIL Scale, and Geriatric Depression Scale. Four additional PDF files show these in more detail [Additional files 1, 2, 3, and 4].

The DCAT utilises validated bedside tests to assess each of the five domains implicated in diabetes producing a total score out of 15 (Table 1). The MoCA assesses seven areas of cognition: visuospatial and executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction and orientation [14]. It produces a score out of 30, where cognitive impairment is suggested by a score of less than 26 [14]. Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale, which is a questionnaire on fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illnesses, and loss of weight [10]. It produces a score out of 5, where 1–2 is considered “pre-frail” and 3–5 is considered “frail” [10]. Depression was assessed using the short form of the Geriatric Depression Scale, which is a yes-or-no questionnaire producing a score out of 15, where a score of 5 or higher suggests depression [30].

Data collection

To minimise information bias and ensure standardised, accurate assessments, all study investigators were trained by a qualified geriatrician with experience in conducting the cognitive tests. Training on ethical and respectful patient interactions was given and monitored. Additionally, all the investigators received formal MoCA training and certification.

Eligible T2DM patients were identified at the CMJAH diabetic clinic. Once informed consent to participate in the study was obtained, the data was recorded directly in the data collection booklet. Information regarding the patient’s most recent HbA1c, T2DM duration, presence of target organ damage, type of diabetic medication prescribed, and presence of polypharmacy was obtained from the patient’s clinic file and recorded anonymously in the data collection booklet. Incidents of hypoglycaemia and other information not recorded in the files were obtained from the patients themselves. The data was then entered into a secure Microsoft Excel Sheet and subjected to quality control before data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Python version 3.11. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the distribution of quantitative variables for the diabetic and control groups [see Additional file 5]. These variables are presented as the means and standard deviations, or medians and interquartile ranges, depending on the distribution of the data, and were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Cohen’s d was used to calculate the effect size of each cognitive domain with thresholds of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), and 0.8 (large). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-squared test.

The performance of the DCAT as a screening tool was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the MoCA as the reference standard for cognitive impairment. An optimal cut-off score for DCAT-defined cognitive impairment was then determined using Youden’s index to provide the best balance between sensitivity and specificity. Positive and negative predictive values were also calculated with 95% confidence intervals established using the Wilson score method.

Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s rank coefficients to assess the relationships between HbA1c levels and cognitive scores; depression scores and cognitive scores; frailty scores and cognitive scores; years of education and cognitive scores; age and cognitive scores; and T2DM duration and cognitive scores.

Missing data was minimal because assessments were conducted in person and data were verified at the time of collection. All the cognitive and clinical variables were complete for the participants in the analysis. No imputation methods were needed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with Bonferroni corrections applied for multiple comparisons to reduce the risk of Type I error.

Analysis of the cognitive domains revealed that participants with T2DM performed significantly worse across most DCAT domains (Table 5). The largest effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were observed for executive function (d = -0.89) and memory (d = -0.61), indicating large and medium effects, respectively. Smaller yet significant differences were observed in processing speed (d = -0.53) and attention (d = -0.55). Similarly, MoCA domain analysis revealed that the diabetic group scored significantly lower across most domains, particularly visuospatial/executive function (d = -0.88), memory (d = -0.72), attention (d = -0.64), and language (d = -0.59) (Table 6).

To account for multiple comparisons, a post-hoc Bonferroni correction was applied. The adjusted significant thresholds were determined based on the number of domain-level comparisons, resulting in p < 0.01 for DCAT domain analysis (Table 5) and p < 0.007 for MoCA domain analysis (Table 6).

ROC curve analysis of DCAT performance against the MoCA revealed an AUC of 0.81 (Fig. 2). Youden’s index identified an optimal cut-off score of 13 for the DCAT, providing a balanced sensitivity of 78.4% (95% CI [66.0, 81.9]) and specificity of 78.7% (95% CI [65.1, 88.0]). The PPV was then calculated to be 89.2% (95% CI: 81.3–94.1), and the NPV was 56.9% (95% CI: 44.8–68.2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of the DCAT against the MoCA for screening cognitive impairment among older adults with T2DM. The DCAT demonstrated good discriminative ability, suggesting it can reliably identify individuals who screen positive for cognitive impairment in this population. However, its modest NPV suggests that it is better suited for screening rather than exclusion. In practice, this supports a two-step screening approach in which a positive DCAT screen result should prompt further evaluation with a standardised screening tool, such as the MoCA, followed, where appropriate, by formal neuropsychological assessment. Importantly, a positive screen on either instrument does not equate to a clinical diagnosis of cognitive impairment and should be interpreted cautiously. Conversely, in patients with a negative DCAT screen but a high level of clinical concern, further assessment should be considered rather than relying on a single screening outcome. This approach may enhance the clinical utility of the DCAT while minimising the risk of misclassification.

The high proportion of participants screening positive for cognitive impairment using both the MoCA (83.5%) and the DCAT (74.8%) is consistent with existing evidence that older adults with T2DM are at substantially increased risk of cognitive decline compared to their non-diabetic peers [31, 32]. Using the MoCA, cognitive impairment was 1.8 times more prevalent among older adults with T2DM than among non-diabetic controls. When assessed using the DCAT, the proportion screening positive for cognitive impairment was increased approximately 2.6-fold, highlighting the substantial cognitive burden associated with diabetes in this population.

Our study is among the first in South Africa to assess the proportion of older adults with T2DM who screen positive for cognitive impairment using both a validated tool (MoCA) and a novel diabetes-specific instrument (DCAT) in a tertiary-care setting. These findings align with those of regional studies from sub-Saharan Africa, which reported high prevalence rates ranging from 27% to 88% among diabetic populations [33, 34].

While these high rates of impairment may reflect the cognitive vulnerability associated with T2DM, it is important to acknowledge the role of sociocultural factors, such as language and educational attainment, in cognitive performance which may account for the high proportion of MoCA-defined cognitive impairment observed in both the T2DM (83.5%) and control (45.5%) groups [21, 22]. These results likely represent a combination of true underlying cognitive burden and a high sensitivity of the MoCA when applied to a diverse South African population.

Both the DCAT and the MoCA identified executive function and memory as the most affected cognitive domains, consistent with the cognitive profile associated with diabetes-related microvascular and metabolic brain injury [19, 35, 36]. These findings align with prior South African data linking executive dysfunction to poor glycaemic control in T2DM [37]. Deficits in these domains can impair key aspects of diabetes self-management, including medication adherence, glucose monitoring, and dietary regulation [38, 39]. The DCAT’s ability to capture these domains, alongside processing speed and psychomotor function, supports its utility as a brief, clinically relevant screening tool in busy outpatient settings.

The observed negative correlation between HbA1c and MoCA scores supports the established link between chronic hyperglycaemia and neurocognitive decline as described in large cohort studies [40, 41]. Although the correlation between HbA1c and DCAT scores did not reach statistical significance, the directional trend was consistent with previous research, suggesting a potential link between poor glycaemic control and neurodegenerative processes [40]. This highlights the importance of early and routine cognitive screening in all patients aged 65 years and older with T2DM.

A significant negative correlation between frailty scores and cognitive scores was observed in the control group but not in the diabetic group. In the diabetic group, the correlation between frailty and cognition was attenuated. This may suggest that the relationship between diabetes and cognition involves more complex mechanisms, such as chronic metabolic dysregulations, inflammation, and depression, rather than exclusively physical components captured by frailty indices [42]. Depression significantly correlated with poorer cognitive performance among diabetic participants, which is consistent with existing evidence that the coexistence of diabetes and depression is associated with worse cognitive outcomes [11].

Age and T2DM duration did not significantly correlate with cognitive performance in our study group, in contrast to the findings of previous studies [21, 22, 43]. However, more years of education significantly correlated with better cognitive performance in both groups, with a stronger effect observed among diabetic participants. This finding aligns with evidence that differences in education are reflected in cognitive performance on screening tests and should thus be accounted for when screening tools such as the DCAT are designed [21, 22].

Recommendations

Future research should focus on validating the DCAT in larger, multicentre, and longitudinal studies across culturally diverse populations to strengthen generalisability and improve accuracy. ROC curve analysis can also be performed on larger samples or by subgroups such as age, education, and language to refine cut-off scores. Evaluation of the tool in diabetic patients with established vascular cognitive impairment, compared with those without, would provide additional insight into its screening performance.

Further work in also needed to establish key psychometric properties, including inter-rater reliability, test-retest reliability and internal consistency, as well as to explore its predictive value for long-term cognitive decline. Finally, adaptations for low literacy and multilingual populations may strengthen the DCAT’s applicability and scalability across diverse healthcare settings.

Strengths, limitations, and sources of bias

The study achieved high recruitment rates with 95.8% of the target sample size obtained. However, as the sample size was determined based on the average number of patients attending the clinic over a 6-month period, the precision of the estimates for area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity may be limited. Furthermore, given the modest sample size of 158 participants, the power to detect smaller effect sizes may also be limited.

It is also important to acknowledge that this study represents an early stage in the clinical validation of the DCAT. Consequently, comprehensive data regarding the tool’s initial development process, such as pilot testing or baseline psychometric evaluations, were limited prior to this application, highlighting the need for ongoing methodological refinement.

A further limitation is the use of the MoCA as the reference standard. While the MoCA is a validated and widely used screening tool, it is not a diagnostic test for cognitive impairment. Consequently, the high proportion of cognitive impairment observed in this study, particularly the 83.5% in the T2DM group, may represent an overestimation of true clinical impairment. Although standard MoCA scoring procedures were applied, including the addition of one point for participants with ≤ 12 years of formal education, this adjustment may not fully account for the influence of broader sociocultural and demographic factors on cognitive performance. These factors may contribute to misclassification when fixed cut-offs are used, particularly in diverse populations such as ours.

The study’s recruitment strategy may have introduced a selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings. T2DM participants were recruited from a tertiary academic hospital diabetic clinic, where patients are more likely to have complex or poorly controlled disease and higher comorbidity burdens, including polypharmacy and diabetes-related complications. This high-acuity setting, together with unmeasured vascular comorbidities, such as hypertension and dyslipidaemia, may have also contributed to an overestimation of the proportion of participants screening positive for cognitive impairment, as well as its apparent severity, in this cohort.

In contrast, the control participants were recruited through convenience sampling from social networks within the researchers’ communities, which may not be fully representative of the general population. These differing recruitment approaches may limit the comparability between the groups and introduce systematic differences beyond diabetes status alone. Consequently, the findings may not be generalisable to broader community-based populations, and the results should be interpreted within the context of a tertiary-care setting.

Furthermore, the exclusion of non-English-speaking participants may introduce a sampling bias, further restricting the generalisability of the findings. This may have resulted in a study population that does not fully represent the linguistic and cultural diversity of the broader population of older adults with T2DM. As cognitive assessments, such as the DCAT rely on language comprehension, restricting inclusion to English speakers may have inadvertently excluded individuals with different cognitive profiles or health behaviours. Additionally, patient-reported information was subject to recall bias.

Moreover, wheelchair-bound patients were excluded from this study because psychomotor testing could not be reliably performed. This exclusion restricts the generalisability of the findings, as mobility impairment is common among older adults with T2DM. Consequently, the DCAT may not be applicable to this clinically relevant subgroup who are at a high risk of cognitive and functional decline. Future studies should evaluate alternative or adapted screening strategies for individuals with limited mobility to ensure the DCAT’s applicability across the full spectrum of older adults with diabetes.

Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for causal relationships to be established between clinical and cognitive variables. Since multivariable regression modelling was not performed in the assessment of the relationships between clinical and cognitive variables, independent associations cannot be definitively established, and the influence of confounding variables cannot be ruled out. Consequently, these findings represent unadjusted associations and must be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal studies with targeted interventions are needed to confirm such relationships.

Conclusion

While the DCAT is not intended to replace comprehensive neuropsychological evaluations, it offers a pragmatic, diabetes-specific option for preliminary cognitive screening. Its brevity, ease of use, and focus on relevant cognitive domains make it particularly suitable for implementation in resource-limited settings, where time and staffing constraints limit the use of more extensive tools such as the MoCA. In addition, the inclusion of the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test allows for the assessment of psychomotor function, a domain frequently affected in T2DM but less frequently captured by conventional screening tools.

By integrating cognitive and functional elements, the DCAT may provide a clinically relevant assessment of individuals at risk of cognitive decline in diabetes. As such, it represents a practical approach to identifying individuals who may benefit from further evaluation within routine diabetes care pathways.
