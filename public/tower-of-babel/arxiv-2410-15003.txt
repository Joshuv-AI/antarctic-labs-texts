Introduction

The Restless Multi-Armed Bandit (RMAB) problem is an important framework in sequential decision-making, where a decision maker selects a subset of tasks (arms) to work on (pull) at each time step to maximize cumulative rewards, under known model parameters
[
27
]
. Unlike the classical (restful) bandit
[
9
]
, in the restless variant, the state of each arm evolves stochastically regardless of whether it is pulled. RMABs have been widely applied in domains such as machine maintenance
[
10
,
7
]
, healthcare resource allocation
[
18
,
19
]
, and target tracking
[
17
,
15
]
, to name a few, where optimal decision-making under uncertainty is critical.
A general RMAB is PSPACE-hard
[
22
]
, and finding optimal policies is computationally challenging, especially as the number of arms grows.
Recently, there have also been efforts to use deep learning and reinforcement learning to learn heuristic policies for RMABs, such as
[
20
,
29
,
14
,
28
,
1
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the finite-horizon version of the RMAB problem with
N
homogeneous arms and horizon
H
, where each arm follows the same (time-dependent, known) state transition and reward function. While computing the exact optimal policy is impractical, the homogeneity of the model allows for the design of computationally efficient policies. One such class of policies is based on fluid approximation, which transforms the original
N
-armed RMAB problem into a Linear Program (LP), and an LP-based policy can be efficiently computed based on the solution to the LP.

Optimality gap.
In
[
11
]
, an LP-based index policy was proposed and achieves an
o(1)
optimality gap. This gap was later improved to
\mathcal{O}(\log N/\sqrt{N})
in
[
31
]
, and subsequently to
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
in
[
5
]
. In the numerical experiments of
[
5
]
, it was observed that while this
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
gap appears tight for certain problems, in others the gap converges to zero more rapidly. This empirical observation was theoretically confirmed in
[
33
]
, where it was shown that under a
non-degenerate
condition (formally defined in Definition
3.1
), the gap is at a smaller order of
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
.

Non-degenerate condition.
This non-degenerate condition has since become a key assumption in subsequent works.
[
8
]
shows that, under this condition, the optimality gap becomes exponentially small when the rounding error induced by scaling the fluid approximation by
N
is eliminated. Further generalizations of the non-degenerate condition have been made in
[
6
]
, extending it to multi-action and multi-constraint RMABs (also known as weakly coupled Markov decision processes). In
[
32
]
, the non-degenerate condition was further extended to settings with heterogeneous arms.

Prevalence of degenerant RMABs.
Despite the central role played by the non-degenerate condition, many RMAB problems are
degenerate
. Notable examples, which originate from real-world applications, have been discussed in
[
5
,
33
,
6
]
. Moreover, a numerical study presented in Appendix
F.1
revealed that a significant proportion (about 50% for some cases) of randomly generated RMABs are degenerate, and almost all of them satisfy the Uniqueness Assumption
4.1
. This highlights the practical importance of addressing degenerate RMABs.

However, to the best of our knowledge, in all previous works, when an RMAB is
degenerate
, the best known optimality gap is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
. It is widely believed that this upper bound is also order-wise tight under LP-based policies, making it
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
. We will formally prove this result in Theorem
4.2
by using an example.

These results led us to ask the following central question of this paper:

Does there exist a computationally efficient algorithm for degenerate RMABs with an optimality gap order-wise smaller than
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
?

Contributions.
This paper answers the question affirmatively and includes the following results:

•

We construct a Gaussian stochastic system (see (
16
)) that more accurately captures the behavior of the
N
-system than the fluid system. Unlike the fluid system, which serves as a first-order approximation to the stochastic
N
-system, the Gaussian stochastic system incorporates both the mean and variance of the
N
-system.

•

We demonstrate that the SP-based policy obtained from the Gaussian stochastic system (see Algorithm (
1
)) achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
optimality gap for degenerate RMABs that satisfy the Uniqueness Assumption
4.1
(see Theorem
4.1
). We further prove that for degenerate RMABs without the Uniqueness Assumption, SP-based policy results in
\Omega(1/\sqrt{N})
improvement per arm compared with a large class of LP-based policies (see Theorem
4.3
).

•

We further compliment our main result by presenting a
degenerate
example in Section
3

4.2
that not only the LP-based policy has
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap, but also the LP upper bound has
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
gap from the optimal value. This indicates that the LP upper bound, which is a widely used baseline, itself is not tight for degenerate RMABs.

Additional related work.

Gaussian approximation, based on the Central Limit Theorem, is a popular approach for approximating stochastic processes. For example, in the heavy-traffic analysis of continuous-time queue theory, such an approximation is called the diffusion approximation because the limiting process, after centering and scaling, becomes a diffusion process. The early works in this line include
[
3
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
. Diffusion approximation in the queueing theory primarily focuses on understanding the convergence or the rate of convergence of the centered and scaled queueing system to the diffusion limit, while this paper uses the Gaussian stochastic system to obtain a near-optimal policy for a decision problem with
N
homogenous components. Therefore, both the application of the approximation and its analysis are different.

Notational convention.

Vectors are row vectors by default.

N

\tilde{\quad}

Problem formulation

RMAB model.
We consider the
H
-horizon Restless Multi-Armed Bandit (RMAB) problem with
N
homogeneous arms. Each arm is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with state space
\mathcal{S}:=\{1,2,\dots,S\}
and action space
\mathcal{A}:=\{0,1\}
. At each time step
h
(
1\leq h\leq H
), the decision maker decides which arms to take action
1
, also referred to as the pulling action, subject to the budget constraint that exactly
\alpha N
arms should be pulled.
Here
0<\alpha<1
and we assume
\alpha N
is an integer.
After the actions are applied, the
N
arms evolve independently.
Specifically, the state transitions from
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}
to
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}
with probability
\mathbf{P}_{h}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}_{n}\mid s_{n},a_{n})
,
where
(s_{n},a_{n})
denotes the state-action pair for the
n
-th arm, and
\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid\cdot,a)
is the transition kernel under action
a
.
For convenience, we refer to this RMAB system with
N
arms as the
N
-system
.

At each time step
h
, the decision maker collects an additive reward
\sum_{n=1}^{N}r_{h}(s_{n},a_{n})
, where
r_{h}(s,a)
denotes the reward for the state-action pair
(s,a)
at time
h
and is assumed to be nonnegative without loss of generality. The objective is to find a policy
\pi^{N}
, which maps the bandit state vector
\mathbf{s}_{h}=(s_{1,h},s_{2,h},\dots,s_{N,h})
to an action vector
\mathbf{a}_{h}=(a_{1,h},a_{2,h},\dots,a_{N,h})
for each
h
, that maximizes the total expected reward per arm over the horizon:

\displaystyle\quad V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}:=\underset{\pi^{N}}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{N}}\left[r_{h}(s_{n,h},a_{n,h})\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n,h}=\alpha N,\quad 1\leq h\leq H.

(2)

To reduce the computational complexity, we leverage the fact that the
N
arms are homogeneous and aggregate the states of individual arms.
This gives us a simplified representation of the bandit state, which facilitates subsequent analysis and policy design.
Specifically, we represent the bandit state at time
h
as a vector
\mathbf{X}_{h}=(X_{h}(s))_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\in\Delta^{S}_{N}
, where each
X_{h}(s)
denotes the fraction of arms in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Here,
\Delta^{S}_{N}
is a discrete subset of
\Delta^{S}
, the probability simplex over
\mathcal{S}
, such that each
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{S}_{N}
satisfies that
N\mathbf{x}
has all integer entries.
Similarly, we represent the action at time
h
as a vector
\mathbf{Y}_{h}=(Y_{h}(s,a))_{s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}\in\Delta^{2S}_{N}
, where each
Y_{h}(s,a)
denotes the fraction of arms in state
s
taking action
a
.
We treat
\mathbf{Y}_{h}
as a row vector of length
2S
.
We also write
\mathbf{Y}_{h}(\cdot,0)=(Y_{h}(s,0))_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{h}(\cdot,1)=(Y_{h}(s,0))_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
, which are both row vectors of length
S
.

Under the new state and action representation, given an action
\mathbf{Y}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h}
, the state evolves as

\mathbf{X}_{h+1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s,a}\mathbf{U}^{(s,a)}_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h}),

(3)

where
\mathbf{U}^{(s,a)}_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h})\in\mathbb{N}^{S}
follows a multinomial distribution
\mathrm{multi}(Ny_{h}(s,a),\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a))
,
and the
\mathbf{U}^{(s,a)}_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h})
’s for different
(s,a)
’s are
independent
.

Under the new state and action representation, a policy
\pi^{N}
maps the state
\mathbf{X}_{h}
to an action vector
\mathbf{Y}_{h}
for each
h
.
We also rewrite the reward function as a vector
\mathbf{r}_{h}=(r_{h}(s,a))_{s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2S}
.
Suppose the initial state of the
N
-system is
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}}\in\Delta^{S}_{N}
.
Then the objective of the RMAB problem can be reformulated as:

\displaystyle\quad V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)=\ \underset{\pi^{N}}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{N}}\left[\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{Y}_{h}^{\top}\right]

(4)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s}Y_{h}(s,1)=\alpha,\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

(5)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{Y}_{h}(\cdot,0)+\mathbf{Y}_{h}(\cdot,1)=\mathbf{X}_{h},\quad\mathbf{Y}_{h}\in\Delta^{2S}_{N},\quad 1\leq h\leq H.

(6)

Here (
5
) corresponds to the budget constraint, and (
6
) ensures that
\mathbf{Y}_{h}
is a valid action for state
\mathbf{X}_{h}
.

Fluid approximation.

One classical approach to address the complexity of RMAB is to consider a fluid approximation as in
[
11
,
31
,
5
,
33
,
8
]
, where the stochastic state transition is replaced by its expected value, leading to a deterministic transition at each step:

\mathbf{x}_{h+1}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a).

(7)

This fluid system smooths out the stochastic fluctuations in the
N
-system and leads to the following Linear Program (LP), which we refer to as the
fluid LP
:

\displaystyle\quad\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1):=\ \underset{(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h})_{h\in\{1,2,\dots,H\}}}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{y}_{h}^{\top}

(8)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s}y_{h}(s,1)=\alpha,\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

(9)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{y}_{h}(\cdot,0)+\mathbf{y}_{h}(\cdot,1)=\mathbf{x}_{h}(\cdot),\quad\mathbf{y}_{h}\geq\mathbf{0},\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

(10)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},\quad\mathbf{x}_{h+1}(\cdot)=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}\left(\cdot\mid s,a\right),\quad 1\leq h\leq H-1.

(11)

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*})_{h\in\{1,2,\dots,H\}}
and
\mathbf{y}^{*}=(\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*})_{h\in\{1,2,\dots,H\}}
be an optimal solution to this fluid LP.
Note that this LP can be efficiently solved.
Furthermore, it has been shown that
V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)\leq\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)
; see, for instance,
[
33
, Lemma 1]
or
[
30
, Lemma 3]
.
That is, the optimal value of the LP provides an upper bound on the optimal value of the RMAB problem.
Based on the solution to this LP, LP-based policies can be obtained but have
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap per arm in degenerate RMABs, as we pointed out in the Introduction.

3
Gaussian approximation and SP-based policy

3.1
Gaussian stochastic system

The performance of LP-based policies is inherently limited by how accurately the fluid system approximates the original
N
-system.
To design policies that outperform the LP-based policies, we construct a Gaussian stochastic system that better approximates the original
N
-system by capturing not only the mean but also the variance of the system.
This Gaussian stochastic system is centered around
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, an optimal solution to the fluid LP.
We then search for an optimal policy for the Gaussian stochastic system in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, which adjusts
\mathbf{y}^{*}
to account for the stochasticity.
This policy is then applied to the
N
-system after properly handling the integer effect.

Specifically, we construct a Gaussian stochastic system with state space
\Delta^{S}
and action space
\Delta^{2S}
.
The system has the same initial state
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}}
as the
N
-system.
Under action vector
\mathbf{y}_{h}
, the state of the Gaussian stochastic system at the next time step,
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}
, is a random vector of the following form:

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\Delta^{S}}\left(\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\mathbf{Z}_{h}/\sqrt{N}\right),

(12)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
is a Gaussian random vector with distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}))
, and
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
’s are independent across steps.
The covariance matrix
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h})
is a constant matrix independent of
N
, with its explicit expression given below.
The projection,
\mathrm{Proj}_{\Delta^{S}}\left(\cdot\right)
, is onto the simplex
\Delta^{S}
under the
\ell_{2}
-distance.
This projection will not be used with a high probability.
Indeed, we will show in Lemma
C.8
in the appendix that
\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\mathbf{Z}_{h}/\sqrt{N}\in\Delta^{S}
occurs with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(1/N^{\log N})
.

We now explain how the Gaussian stochastic system captures the mean and variance of the
N
-system.
Suppose the action at time
h
is
\mathbf{y}_{h}
.
Ignoring the projection, we have

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\mathbf{Z}_{h}/\sqrt{N}.

The term
\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)
is the expectation of the state
\mathbf{X}_{h+1}
in the
N
-system, which is the same as the state
\mathbf{x}_{h+1}
in the fluid system under action
\mathbf{y}_{h}
.
The additional term,
\mathbf{Z}_{h}/\sqrt{N}
, is random, and we construct it in such a way that its covariance matrix matches that of
\mathbf{X}_{h+1}
in the
N
-system if
\mathbf{y}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*}
. In other words, for
(h,s,a)\in[1,H]\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, denote the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{h}(s,a)
of size
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\times\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
, for which the
(i,j)
-th entry is:

\begin{cases}-\mathbf{P}_{h}(i\mid s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(j\mid s,a),&\mbox{if }i\neq j\\
\mathbf{P}_{h}(i\mid s,a)(1-\mathbf{P}_{h}(i\mid s,a)),&\mbox{if }i=j.\end{cases}

(13)

Then

\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}):=\sum_{s,a}y^{*}_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{\Sigma}_{h}(s,a).

(14)

Note that by construction,
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h})/N
is the covariance matrix of the state
\mathbf{X}_{h+1}
in the
N
-system if the action at time
h
is
\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*}
.
Furthermore, this term captures the variance of the
N
-system when
\mathbf{y}_{h}
is close to the optimal solution
\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*}
of the fluid LP. We remark that it is important to define the covariance matrix based on
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}
instead of
\mathbf{y}_{h}.
Using
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h})
in (
14
) would make
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}
a more accurate approximation of
\mathbf{X}_{h+1}
but would make
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
action-dependent. The problem becomes much more difficult if
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
’s distributions need to be optimized instead of being pre-determined

3.2
Stochastic-Programming-based (SP-based) policy

After constructing the Gaussian stochastic system, we search for an optimal policy in the Gaussian stochastic system.
However, we restrict the search to a
neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, since the Gaussian stochastic system closely approximates the
N
-system when the state and action of the
N
-system stay close to
\mathbf{y}^{*}
.
In particular, given a fixed parameter
\delta_{N}:=2\log N/\sqrt{N}=\tilde{\Theta}(1/\sqrt{N})
, we define the following policy class:

\begin{split}\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}):=\big\{&\pi\colon\forall 1\leq h\leq H,\;\|\pi(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N},\;\hbox{if }\|\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq z_{h}\delta_{N};\\
&\mspace{25mu}\pi=\pi_{\text{pre}}\hbox{ for a predefined policy }\pi_{\text{pre}},\;\hbox{if }\|\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}>z_{h}\delta_{N}\big\}.\end{split}

(15)

Here
\kappa
is a positive constant with value
\kappa:=\max\left\{2+6S,\,3+2r_{\mathrm{max}}HS/\sigma\right\}
, where
r_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{s,a,h}r_{h}(s,a)
;
\sigma
is a constant depending on the fluid LP;

for

is a recursive sequence. The explicit expressions for these constants are collected in Appendix

.
We note that all of them are independent of

We then formulate the following Gaussian stochastic program (SP):

\displaystyle\quad\ \underset{\pi\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}^{\top}\right]

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s}\tilde{Y}_{h}(s,1)=\alpha,\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}(\cdot,0)+\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}(\cdot,1)=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}\geq{\bf 0},\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

We now present our SP-based policy, formally described in Algorithm
1
.
The core idea of this policy is to solve the Gaussian stochastic program and obtain an optimal policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
.
This policy is then applied to the
N
-system through a simple rounding procedure, which maps the action given by
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
to a feasible action in the
N
-system with
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
error; see Appendix
B

We remark that we only use the policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
when the RMAB is degenerate.
When the RMAB is non-degenerate, our policy defaults to a LP-based policy, which prior work (see Introduction) has shown to achieve an exponentially small optimality gap (in terms of
N
) relative to
\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}
.
The definition of non-degeneracy is given below, and it is easy for an algorithm to check whether an RMAB is non-degenerate or not.

Definition 3.1

(Non-degeneracy
[
33
,
8
,
6
]
)
.

An RMAB is
non-degenerate
if, its corresponding fluid LP (
8
) admits an optimal solution
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, such that for each
h
with
1\leq h\leq H
, there exists at least one state
s\in\mathcal{S}
such that
y^{*}_{h}(s,0)>0
and
y^{*}_{h}(s,1)>0
.

3.3
Illustration of the SP-based policy on a degenerate example

We illustrate the SP-based policy via a two-state RMAB with horizon
H=2
and pulling budget
\alpha=0.5
.
The rewards are given by
r_{1}(1,1)=r_{2}(1,1)=1
, with all other
(h,s,a)
-tuples being
r_{h}(s,a)=0
.
The transition probabilities at
h=1
are
\mathbf{P}_{1}(1\mid 1,1)=0.2
,
\mathbf{P}_{1}(1\mid 1,0)=0.9
,
\mathbf{P}_{1}(1\mid 2,1)=0.7
,
\mathbf{P}_{1}(1\mid 2,0)=0.25
.
Assume at
h=1
there are
N/2
arms in state
1
, and the other
N/2
arms are in state
2
.

N
-system problem.

Note that at time
h=2
, only
r_{2}(1,1)=1,
so the optimal action at
h=2
is to pull as many arms in state
1
as possible, i.e.,
Y_{2}(1,1)=\min\{0.5,X_{2}(1)\}.

Therefore, we only need to decide the optimal action at
h=1
.
We further notice that since
X_{1}(1)=X_{1}(2)=\alpha=0.5,
the entries of
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
are all determined by
Y_{1}(1,1)
as follows

Y_{1}(1,0)=Y_{1}(2,1)=0.5-Y_{1}(1,1),\quad Y_{1}(2,0)=Y_{1}(1,1).

Therefore, we only need to optimize
Y_{1}(1,1)
.
The
N
-system optimal policy is given by the solution of the following problem

\max_{0\leq Y_{1}(1,1)\leq 0.5}\ Y_{1}(1,1)+\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0.5,X_{2}(1)\right\}\right].

(20)

Fluid LP and its optimal solution.

In the fluid system, we replace
X_{2}(1)
with its mean
x_{2}(1)=\sum_{s,a}y_{1}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{1}(1\mid s,a)=0.8-1.15\times y_{1}(1,1)
.
Then the fluid LP is

\displaystyle\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}=

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq y_{1}(1,1)\leq 0.5}y_{1}(1,1)+\min\big\{0.5,\;0.8-1.15\times y_{1}(1,1)\big\},

(21)

which exchanges the expectation and the min operator in the
N
-system problem (
20
).
The optimal solution is
y^{*}_{1}(1,1)=0.2609.

One can verify that this problem is degenerate (see Definition
3.1
).

SP-based policy.

Given the fluid solution
\mathbf{y}^{*}
above, the corresponding Gaussian stochastic program is

\max_{0\leq\tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)\leq 0.5}\ \tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)+\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0.5,\;0.8-1.15\times\tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)+\frac{Z_{1}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\right],

(22)

where
Z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\,0.1624).
Since we are searching for an optimal solution around
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{1},
let
\tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)=y_{1}^{*}(1,1)+\frac{c}{\sqrt{N}}.

Then the stochastic program can be written as

\mathbf{y}^{*}_{1}(1,1)+0.5+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\max_{c}\left(c+\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0,Z_{1}-1.15c\right\}\right]\right),

which is equivalent to the following problem

\max_{c}\ c+\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0,Z_{1}-1.15c\right\}\right].

There exists an explicit and unique solution to the problem above, and the solution can be numerically computed and is
c^{*}_{\text{d}}=0.3940
(see details in Appendix
D
). Therefore, the SP-based policy is given by
\mathrm{round}(\tilde{Y}^{*}_{1}(1,1))=\mathrm{round}(0.2609+0.3940/\sqrt{N})
.
This SP-based policy outperforms LP-based policies, as illustrated in Figure
1
in the Introduction.

Some insights.

We compare the fluid approximation in (
21
) with the Gaussian approximation in (
22
) when they both take an action
y_{1}(1,1)=\tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)=y_{1}^{*}(1,1)+c/\sqrt{N}
for a positive constant
c
.
In the fluid system, the reward for
h=2
is
\min\big\{0.5,\;0.8-1.15\times y_{1}(1,1)\big\}
, which is capped at
0.5
.
One can verify that this deviates from the value
\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0.5,X_{2}(1)\right\}\right]
in the
N
-system by
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
, caused by the exchange of the expectation and the min operator.
In contrast, in the Gaussian stochastic system,

\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0.5,0.8-1.15\times\tilde{Y}_{1}(1,1)+\frac{Z_{1}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\right]=0.5+\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0,\frac{Z_{1}-1.15c}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\right],

which can be verified to be
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
away from the value
\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{0.5,X_{2}(1)\right\}\right]
in the
N
-system and thus giving a better approximation.
This better approximation allows us to find a better policy near
y_{1}^{*}(1,1)
.
The inaccuracy of the fluid approximation is in fact a fundamental reason that LP-based policies have a
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap for some degenerate RMABs,
whereas the correction in our SP-based policy reduces the
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
inaccuracy to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
.

4
Main theoretical results

In this section, we present our main theoretical results.
We will frequently use the following quantities.
Let
V^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)
and
{Q}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)
denote the value function and Q-function of policy
\pi
evaluated in the
N
-system; and
\tilde{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)
and
\tilde{Q}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)
denote the value function and Q-function of a policy
\pi
evaluated in the Gaussian stochastic system.

Further, we consider the following optimal policies within the policy class
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
:

\displaystyle\quad{\pi}^{N,*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})}{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})}\tilde{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{h},h),

which are referred to as the
locally-optimal
policy and the
locally-SP-optimal
policy, respectively.
We sometimes say that we apply the locally-SP-optimal policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
to the
N
-system and denote its value function as
V^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)
, with the understanding that we apply
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
with the rounding procedure.

There is a minor technical subtlety in the optimization within the policy class
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
.
By the definition of
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, a policy in this class follows a predefined policy outside the
\tilde{\Theta}(1/\sqrt{N})
-neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}
, which is not affected by the optimization.
With a slight abuse of terminology, we choose not to distinguish these locally optimal policies with the ones that also optimize outside this neighborhood. This simplification is justified for the following reason. The smallest optimality gap of interest in this paper is of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
.
However, according to the high probability bound established in Lemma
C.8
, the state of the
N
-system’s state falls out of this neighborhood with probability
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{\log N})
, and consequently has only this order of influence in the value function.

4.1
Global optimality

Assumption 4.1

(Uniqueness)
.

The fluid LP in (
8
) has a unique optimal solution
\mathbf{y}^{*}
.

Note that once we obtain an optimal solution to the LP, verifying uniqueness is straightforward
[
2
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Global optimality)
.

Consider an RMAB that satisfies the Uniqueness Assumption
4.1
.
Then the locally-SP-optimal policy,
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
, when applied to the
N
-system (with rounding), achieves an optimality gap of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
; i.e.,

V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)-V^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N),

where
V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is the optimal value function, and
V^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}
is the value function of
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
, both in the
N
-system.

A detailed proof of Theorem
4.1
is presented in Appendix
C
.
Below, we provide an outline and highlight some novel and technically interesting components of the proof.

:
Proof structure of Theorem
4.1
.

Our proof of Theorem
4.1
consists of proving the following statements, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

•

Prove that
V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}=V^{N}_{\pi^{N,*}}
(Lemma
C.1
in the appendix), where
\pi^{N,*}
is the locally optimal policy within the policy class
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
.

This is proved by showing that a (globally) optimal policy for the
N
-system belongs to the policy class
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
under Assumption
4.1
.

•

Prove that
|V^{N}_{\pi^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}^{N}_{{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}}(\mathbf{x},h)|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
(Lemma
C.2
in the appendix) and
|\tilde{V}^{N}_{{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}}(\mathbf{x},h)-V^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x},h)|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
(Lemma
C.3
in the appendix).

These two lemmas are enabled by the fact that we restrict the policies
\pi^{N,*}
and
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
to
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, i.e., a
\tilde{\Theta}(1/\sqrt{N})
-neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, which translates into a Wasserstein distance of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
between the respective next-state distributions in the
N
-system and in the Gaussian stochastic system.

We next highlight two interesting components in the proofs.

•

Characterization of optimal policies in the
N
-system.

Lemma
C.1
in the appendix establishes that under Assumption
4.1
, there exists an optimal policy of the
N
-system whose actions are close to the optimal fluid solution
\mathbf{y}^{*}
.
This result is noteworthy because optimal policies of the
N
-system are not well-understood in the literature.
Prior work often circumvents this by only studying the LP upper bound
\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}
, which can be loose as shown in Theorem
4.2
.

•

Approximate Lipschitz continuity in the Gaussian stochastic system.

A key step in proving Lemmas
C.2
and
C.3
in the appendix is to establish an approximate local Lipschitz property of the value function
\tilde{V}^{N}_{{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}}
in the Gaussian stochastic system, where restricting the policy to
{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}
introduces technical challenges.
We overcome these challenges through the careful construction of an action mapping.

4.2
The
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap of LP-based policies

To complement Theorem
4.1
, we next present a result showing that the
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap is fundamental to a large class of LP-based policies.
Specifically, consider the following policy class, which includes a large class of LP-based policies such as those in
[
5
,
33
,
8
]
:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}):=\big\{\pi\colon\|\pi(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa\|\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty},\;\forall 1\leq h\leq H\big\},

(23)

where
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is an optimal solution of the fluid LP in (
8
).
It has been shown in
[
8
, Theorem 1]
that any policy in
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
has an
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap.
We now show that there exist RMAB instances where this optimality gap order is tight. The result is established based on the example given in Section
3.3
, and the detailed proof is presented in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 4.2

(Fluid gap)
.

There exist RMAB instances for which all LP-based policies in
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
have an
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap; i.e.,

V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)-V^{N}_{{\mathrm{fluid}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)=\Theta(1/\sqrt{N}),

where
V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is the optimal value function, and
V^{N}_{{\mathrm{fluid}}}
is the value function of the optimal policy within the policy class
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
.
Moreover, there is an
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
gap between the optimal value of the
N
-system and the optimal value of the fluid LP in (
8
); i.e.,
\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)-V^{N}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)=\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
.

We remark that although this theorem is proved via a specific example, we believe the result to hold more broadly for many degenerate RMABs.
The
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap of LP-based policies stems from the
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
approximation error in the fluid approximation, which arises when exchanging the expectation and the minimum operator, as shown in the example in Section
3.3
.
This phenomenon is common in degenerate RMABs.

4.3
Performance improvement

Under the Uniqueness Assumption
4.1
, we have shown that our SP-based policy achieves an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
optimality gap.
When this assumption does not hold, the same optimality gap may not apply.
However, Theorem
4.3
below shows that the SP-based policy can still yield improvement over LP-based policies.

Let
\mathbf{y}^{*}
be any optimal solution to the fluid LP in (
8
), and let
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
be the corresponding SP-based policy.
Recall that
\tilde{V}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)
and
\tilde{Q}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},\mathbf{y}_{1},1)
denote the value function and the Q-function of the policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
in the Gaussian stochastic system, where in the Q-function action
\mathbf{y}_{1}
is applied at time
1
.
Theorem
4.3
states that if the action given by the SP-based policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
at time
1
is “strictly” better than the optimal LP-based action
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{*}
in the Gaussian stochastic system, then the SP-based policy improves over any LP-based policy in
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
by
\Omega(1/\sqrt{N})
in the
N
-system.

Theorem 4.3

(Performance improvement)
.

If there exists a positive constant
\epsilon
independent of
N
such that
\tilde{V}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)-\tilde{Q}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{1},1)\geq\epsilon/\sqrt{N}
, then for any
\pi\in\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, we have
V^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)-V_{\pi}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)=\Omega(1/\sqrt{N})
.

Note that a key strength of this result is that we only need to evaluate the first step action
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{*}
in the Gaussian stochastic system, and the result then holds for all policies in
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. We refer to Appendix
E
for a detailed proof of Theorem
4.3
.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, we first present the details of the numerical method we used to solve the Gaussian stochastic program (
16
), which is an essential part of Algorithm
1
.
For the SP (
16
), we introduce the vectors
(\mathbf{c}_{h},\mathbf{d}_{h})_{h\in\left\{1,2,\dots,H\right\}}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}}_{h}:=

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}+\frac{\mathbf{c}_{h}}{\sqrt{N}},

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{h+1}:=

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{Proj}_{\sqrt{N}(\Delta^{S}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h+1})}\left(\sum_{s,a}c_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+{\mathbf{Z}_{h}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}:=

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{x}^{*}_{h+1}+\frac{\mathbf{d}_{h+1}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(25)

With these vectors, we can rewrite the SP (
16
) as follows:

\displaystyle\quad\underset{\pi_{c}\in{\Pi}^{c}_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{c}_{h}^{\top}\right]

(26)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s}c_{h}(s,1)=0\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{c}_{h}(\cdot,0)+\mathbf{c}_{h}(\cdot,1)=\mathbf{d}_{h},\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

\displaystyle\quad c_{h}(s,a)\geq-\sqrt{N}y_{h}^{*}(s,a),\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

(27)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{d}_{1}=\mathbf{0},\quad\quad\mathbf{d}_{h+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\sqrt{N}(\Delta^{S}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h+1})}\left(\sum_{s,a}c_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+{\mathbf{Z}_{h}}\right),\quad 1\leq h\leq H-1.

(28)

Here
{\Pi}^{c}_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a set of policies where each policy maps
\mathbf{d}_{h}
to
\mathbf{c}_{h}
such that
\|\mathbf{c}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}\sqrt{N}
if
\|\mathbf{d}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq z_{h}\delta_{N}\sqrt{N}
, and it follows a predefined policy otherwise; i.e.,

\displaystyle{\Pi}^{c}_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}):=\big\{\pi_{c}\colon

\displaystyle\|\pi_{c}(\mathbf{d}_{h},h)\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}\sqrt{N},\ \hbox{ if }\|\mathbf{d}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq z_{h}\delta_{N}\sqrt{N};

\displaystyle\pi_{c}\hbox{ follows a predefined policy},\ \hbox{ if }\|\mathbf{d}_{h}\|_{\infty}>z_{h}\delta_{N}\sqrt{N}\big\}.

Note that there is a one-to-one correspondence between
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
and
{\Pi}^{c}_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. Hence solving the SP (
16
) is equivalent to solving the SP (
26
).
We further simplify the SP (
26
) to the following
N
-independent and projection-free SP:

\displaystyle\quad\underset{\pi_{c}\in{\Pi}^{c}_{\tilde{D}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{c}_{h}^{\top}\right]

(29)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{s}c_{h}(s,1)=0\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{c}_{h}(\cdot,0)+\mathbf{c}_{h}(\cdot,1)=\mathbf{d}_{h},\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

\displaystyle\quad c_{h}(s,a)\geq 0\ \text{ if $y^{*}_{h}(s,a)=0$},\quad 1\leq h\leq H,

(30)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{d}_{1}=\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbf{d}_{h+1}=\sum_{s,a}c_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\mathbf{Z}_{h},\quad 1\leq h\leq H-1,

(31)

where the policy class
{\Pi}^{c}_{\tilde{D}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
is

\displaystyle{\Pi}^{c}_{\tilde{D}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}):=\big\{\pi_{c}\colon

\displaystyle\|\pi_{c}(\mathbf{d}_{h},h)\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa\tilde{D},\ \hbox{ if }\|\mathbf{d}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\tilde{D};

\displaystyle\pi_{c}\hbox{ follows a predefined policy},\ \hbox{ if }\|\mathbf{d}_{h}\|_{\infty}>\tilde{D}\big\}.

In our numerical experiments, we have chosen
\tilde{D}
to be
20
.

Let
{\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{c}}
be an optimal policy in
{\Pi}^{c}_{\tilde{D}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
for the SP (
29
). For the
N
-system, given state
\mathbf{x}_{h}
at step
h,
we apply action

\mathbf{y}_{h}=\mathrm{round}\left(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}+\frac{\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{c}\left(\sqrt{N}(\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h})\right)}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

In other words, the solution to the SP (
29
) provides an SP-based policy that can be applied to all
N.

The SP (
29
) is a stochastic linear program, which is significantly easier to solve than the original
N
-armed RMAB Problem (
4
) because the “noise”
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
’s are predefined Gaussian random vectors whose distributions are independent of the state and action of the system.
For such a problem, there exist computationally efficient methods. In our numerical examples, we numerically solved the
1/\sqrt{N}
-scale SP (
29
) using the standard Sample Average Approximation (SAA) approach
[
25
, Chapter 5]
and the Explorative Dual Dynamic Programming (EDDP) algorithm
[
16
, Algorithm 3]
, which is an enhancement of the classical Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP)
[
23
,
24
]
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the finite-horizon Restless Multi-Armed Bandit (RMAB) problem and proposed a Stochastic-Programming-based (SP-based) policy, which is based on a carefully constructed Gaussian stochastic system that approximates the original system.
Our SP-based policy achieves an
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/N)
optimality gap for degenerate RMABs under a uniqueness assumption, while existing LP-based policies based on the fluid approximation only guarantee a
\Theta(1/\sqrt{N})
optimality gap.
Further, our SP-based policy can achieve an
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
improvement over a large class of LP-based policies even without the uniqueness assumption.

Appendix B
Rounding procedure

Throughout this paper, we represent systems and controls as “fractions” of the total population
N
of arms, using vectors such as
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
. In the unnormalized-scale system, the coordinates of
N\mathbf{x}
and
N\mathbf{y}
therefore need to be integers. If these quantities are not integers, they are implicitly understood to be appropriately rounded via some
rounding procedure
. For example, after obtaining
\mathbf{y}
from the SP-based policy, we need to applying rounding before using it for the
N
-system.

Formally, a rounding procedure is to solve the following problem: Given an integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{X}\in\Delta^{S}_{N}
so that
N\mathbf{X}
have integer coordinates, then, for any
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{X}}
,
we need to construct
\mathbf{Y}^{N}=\mathrm{round}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{X}}
for which
N\mathbf{Y}^{N}
have integer coordinates.

This can be accomplished as follows. Set

Y^{N}(s,1):=\frac{\lfloor NY(s,1)\rfloor}{N},\ Y^{N}(s,0):=\frac{\lceil NY(s,0)\rceil}{N},\mbox{ for $1\leq s\leq S-1$};

(32)

Y^{N}(S,1):=\alpha-\sum_{s=1}^{S-1}Y^{N}(s,1),\ Y^{N}(S,0):=1-\alpha-\sum_{s=1}^{S-1}Y^{N}(s,0).

(33)

It is then easy to verify that,
\mathbf{Y}^{N}:=\mathrm{round}(\mathbf{Y})
defined in (
32
)-(
33
) indeed satisfies all these required conditions. Furthermore, by construction, it holds true that

\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathrm{round}(\mathbf{Y})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{N}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{N}),

(34)

independently of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{*,^{\prime}}_{h},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)=\Theta\left(\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
, i.e., the action
\mathbf{y}^{*,^{\prime}}_{h}
improves the LP solution from
\mathbf{y}_{h}
.

Using the established properties in Claim
C.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mspace{23mu}\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{*,^{\prime}}_{h},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)

\displaystyle\geq\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{+}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{*,+}_{h},h)-r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{+}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{+}_{h},h)

\displaystyle\geq\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h},h)-r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}-\left(\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)+r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)-2r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}.

To further lower bound
\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)
, we utilize the following claim, which establishes an “anti-Lipschitz” property of the function
\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\cdot,h)
.
The proof of this claim is given in Section

.

Claim C.2
.

For any
h
with
1\leq h\leq H
, there exists a positive constant
\sigma>0
that depends on
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}
but not on
N
, such that for any
\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{h}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}}
, it holds that

\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{h},h)\geq\sigma\|\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}-\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{h}\|_{\infty}.

(47)

Applying Claim
C.2
to
\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}}
, we have

\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)\geq\sigma\|\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\|_{\infty}\geq\sigma(\kappa-2)z_{h}\delta_{N}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mspace{23mu}\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{*,^{\prime}}_{h},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)

\displaystyle\geq\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h}^{*},h)-\overline{Q}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)-2r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma(\kappa-2)z_{h}\delta_{N}-2r_{\max}HSz_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\sigma\kappa-2\sigma-2r_{\max}HS)z_{h}\log N}{\sqrt{N}}=\Theta\left(\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

(48)

as long as

Improved policy for

-system and contradiction.

We now prove that for the

at time

To ease the discussion, let us write the LP in (

) in the following standard form:

\displaystyle\text{LP}:\quad

\displaystyle\max\{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{y}^{\top}:\;\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b},\;\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{0}\}.

where

•

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a given matrix with
m\leq n
, and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given r.h.s. vector;

•

\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reward vector;

•

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the decision variable vector.

Define the
feasible set
of
LP
by

\mathcal{U}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b},\;\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{0}\}.

(60)

By
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
, the set
\mathcal{U}
written in the above form is a polyhedron. In our case it is further a polytope (i.e., a bounded polyhedron) since any element in
\mathcal{U}
has
\|\cdot\|_{1}
-norm bounded by
H
.

We suppose that the feasible set
\mathcal{U}
is
not
a singleton. Note that under some extremal and trivial cases
\mathcal{U}
can reduce to a singleton, e.g. if the initial condition has
x(s)=1
and
x(s^{\prime})=0
for all
s^{\prime}\neq s
, and the transition kernels are all identity matrices. But then Equation (
47
) holds trivially.

From the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming (see, e.g. By
[
21
, Theorem 13.2]
), which states that
if an optimal solution of an LP exists, at least one optimal solution is a vertex; consequently, whenever the optimum is unique it must be that vertex
, we deduce that
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, being the unique optimal solution by assumption, must be a vertex of
\mathcal{U}
.

Let
V(\mathbf{y}):=\mathbf{r}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
be the objective value for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}
. Because
\mathbf{y}^{*}
is the unique maximizer, for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}
with
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{y}^{*}
we have
V(\mathbf{y})<V(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. We then define the
smallest (nontrivial) descent
at
\mathbf{y}^{*}
as

\sigma:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{y}^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{\infty}}.

(61)

Note that since
\mathcal{U}
is not a singleton,
\sigma
is well defined.

Now the Minkowski–Weyl Theorem
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
states that every polytope is the convex hull of a
finite
set of its vertices.
Hence we write
\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}
where
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\in\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the
finite

\mathcal{U}
. Note that
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
and we may write
\mathbf{y}^{*}=\mathbf{v}^{*}
.
Therefore, we also have

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}\\
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{v}^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\right\|_{\infty}}.

To see this, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{y}^{*}
, write
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda_{i}\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\top}}{\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{j})\right\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda_{i}\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\top}}{\sum_{i=j}^{r}\left\|\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{j})\right\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}}{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{j}\right\|_{\infty}}\right)\frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}\\
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{v}^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\right\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sigma.

The above holds for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{y}^{*}
, we deduce that

\sigma=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}\\
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{y}^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{\infty}}\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}\\
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{v}^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbf{r}\bigl(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\bigr)^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v}\right\|_{\infty}}\geq\sigma,

so all the inequalities above must be equalities.

Now
\sigma>0
because of the uniqueness assumption and the fact that there is only a finite number of vertices in
\mathcal{V}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}(s^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}(s^{\prime})\right]\right)^{2}+\mathrm{Var}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}(s^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\left(\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{h}(s^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{s,a}y_{h}^{*}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)(1-\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a))

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{s,a}y_{h}^{*}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{N}.

Combining the bounds above yields

\displaystyle d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}},\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{S\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)}=\mathcal{O}\left(N^{-\frac{\log N}{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right).

Upper bound on
d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}},\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}}\right)
.

Note that
\mathbf{Z}_{h}
can be written as
\mathbf{Z}_{h}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}^{*}(s,a)\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)
, where each
\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma_{h}(s,a))
and the
\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)
’s across different
(s,a)
’s are independent.
We couple
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}
by letting
\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}},\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(1)}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{Z}_{h}-\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{s,a}\left|y_{h}(s,a)-y^{*}_{h}(s,a)\right|\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{s,a}\left|y_{h}(s,a)-y^{*}_{h}(s,a)\right|\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{N}_{h}(s,a)\|^{2}_{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{s,a}\left|y_{h}(s,a)-y^{*}_{h}(s,a)\right|\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)(1-\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{S}}{\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{y}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2S\sqrt{S}}{\sqrt{N}}\left\|\mathbf{y}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right),

where the last step has used the assumption that
\mathbf{y}_{h}\in{\cal Y}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}}
.

Upper bound on
d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}},\mu_{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{h+1}}\right)
.

We first rewrite
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{h+1}
using the sum of
N
independent random vectors on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.
Let
\mathbf{e}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be the vector whose
s
th entry is equal to
1
and all other entries are equal to
0
.
Since each
\mathbf{U}_{h}^{(s,a)}\sim\mathrm{multi}(Ny_{h}(s,a),\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a))
, we can write it as

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{h}^{(s,a)}=\sum_{i=1}^{Ny_{h}(s,a)}\psi^{(s,a)}_{i},

where the
\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}
’s are i.i.d. random vectors on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that
\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{s^{\prime}}
with probability
\mathbf{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{N}_{h+1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s,a}\sum_{i=1}^{Ny_{h}(s,a)}\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s,a}\sum_{i=1}^{Ny_{h}(s,a)}\left(\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}-\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)\right).

Let

\Psi=\sum_{s,a}\sum_{i=1}^{Ny_{h}(s,a)}\left(\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}-\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)\right).

Note that
\Psi
is the sum of
N
independent random vectors
\left(\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}-\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)\right)
’s with
\left\|\psi^{(s,a)}_{i}-\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)\right\|_{1}\leq 2
and
\mathrm{Cov}\left(\Psi\right)=N\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h})
.

Recall that

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N}}=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{N}\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime},

where
\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{y}_{h}))
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{h+1}^{(2)}},\mu_{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{h+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\sqrt{N}\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}},\mu_{\Psi}\right).

By
[
26
, Theorem 2]
,

\displaystyle d_{W}^{(1)}\left(\mu_{\sqrt{N}\mathbf{Z}_{h}^{\prime}},\mu_{\Psi}\right)\leq 2S(2.7+0.93\log N).

Therefore,

This completes the proof.

C.2.2
Almost Locally Lipschitz value function

Lemma C.5

(Almost Locally Lipschitz value function)
.

For all
1\leq h\leq H
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h}\delta_{N}}
, it holds true that

|\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)|\leq u_{h}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right),

where
u_{h}
is a recursive sequence defined via
u_{H}=r_{\max}AS(S+1)
, and for
H-1\geq h\geq 1
,

u_{h}=r_{\max}AS(S+1)+\sqrt{S}u_{h+1}L_{h}(S+1),

(75)

with
L_{h}>0
being the Lipschitz constant of the linear function
\phi_{h}(\cdot)
defined in (
62
).

Proof of Lemma
C.5
.

We use backward induction. Starting with
h=H
, consider
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{H\delta_{N}}.
Without loss of generality, assume
\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},H)>\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},H)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},H)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},H)=\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x},H),H)-\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},H),H)>0.

Define
\mathbf{y}_{H}=\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x},H)
, and let
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{H}
be the control constructed using the action mapping algorithm defined in Section
C.2.3
. From the definition of the value function, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},H)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},H)\leq

\displaystyle\ \tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{H},H)-\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{H},H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{r}_{H}\mathbf{y}^{\top}_{H}-\mathbf{r}_{H}\mathbf{y}^{\prime\top}_{H}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ r_{\max}AS\|\mathbf{y}_{H}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{H}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ r_{\max}AS(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ u_{H}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where the last inequality follows from the Locally Lipschitz action mapping Lemma
C.6
.

Now we prove the induction step. Consider
h<H
and suppose that for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h+1)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h+1)|\leq u_{h+1}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right).

(76)

Now consider
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h}\delta_{N}}
. Without loss of generality, assume
\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)>\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x},h),h)-\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h),h).

Define
\mathbf{y}_{h}=\tilde{\pi}^{*}(\mathbf{x},h)
, and let
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
be the control constructed using the action mapping algorithm in Section
C.2.3
. From the definition of the value function, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{h},h)-\tilde{Q}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h},h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{y}^{\top}_{h}+\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{V}(\mathbf{X},h+1)\mid\mathbf{y}_{h}\right]-\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{y}^{\prime\top}_{h}-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{V}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},h+1)\mid\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right].

Here
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
are the states at stage
h+1
in the two Gaussian stochastic systems, respectively, under action
\mathbf{y}_{h}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
respectively.
We now couple
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
via the same Gaussian term
\mathbf{Z}_{h}/\sqrt{N},
i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{\Delta^{S}}\left(\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{h}}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{\Delta^{S}}\left(\sum_{s,a}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)+\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{h}}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

and define

\triangle\mathbf{x}:=\sum_{s,a}y_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)-\sum_{s,a}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,a)\mathbf{P}_{h}(\cdot\mid s,a)=\phi_{h}(\mathbf{y})-\phi_{h}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}).

Then

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)

(77)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{y}^{\top}_{h}-\mathbf{r}_{h}\mathbf{y}^{\prime\top}_{h}

(78)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{V}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},h+1)-\tilde{V}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime},h+1)\right)1_{\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\;\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\right\}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right]

(79)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{V}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},h+1)-\tilde{V}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime},h+1)\right)1_{\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\;\mathrm{or}\;\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\right\}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right].

(80)

Note that according to the Locally Lipschitz action mapping Lemma
C.6
:

\eqref{eq:aa}\leq\ r_{\max}AS(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

From the induction assumption (
76
), we further have

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:ab}\leq

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left(u_{h+1}\|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)\right)1_{\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\;\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\right\}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle\leq_{(a)}

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{S}u_{h+1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\triangle\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}1_{\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\;\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\right\}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq_{(b)}

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{S}u_{h+1}L_{h}\|\mathbf{y}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)

where inequality (a) holds due to the coupling we defined and the fact that the projection mapping
\mathbf{x}\to\mathrm{Proj}_{\Delta^{S}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
is 1-Lipschitz under the
\|\cdot\|_{2}
norm, the additional
\sqrt{S}
factor is due to the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
norm that we use here (see the deduction of Equation (
93
) for a similar situation); and inequality (b) holds because
L_{h}>0
is the Lipschitz constant of the function
\phi_{h}(\cdot)
. Finally,

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:ac}\leq

\displaystyle\ Hr_{\max}\mathbb{E}\left[1_{\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\;\mathrm{or}\;\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}\right\}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h},\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ Hr_{\max}\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}|\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}=\mathbf{y}_{h}\in{\cal Y}_{\mathbf{x}}\cap{\cal Y}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h}\delta_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\ +Hr_{\max}\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\notin{\cal X}_{z_{h+1}\delta_{N}}|\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}_{h}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\in{\cal Y}_{\mathbf{x}}\cap{\cal Y}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h}\delta_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right),

where the last equality follows from (
88
).

Summarizing the results above, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)-\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},h)\leq

\displaystyle\ r_{\max}AS(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{S}u_{h+1}L_{h}\|\mathbf{y}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq_{(a)}

\displaystyle\ r_{\max}AS(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{S}u_{h+1}L_{h}(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle=_{(b)}

\displaystyle\ u_{h}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right),

where inequality (a) follows from the Locally Lipschitz action mapping Lemma
C.6
, and equality (b) holds by definition (
75
) of the sequence
u_{h}
.
∎

C.2.3
Locally Lipschitz action mapping

To complete the proof of
\tilde{V}(\mathbf{x},h)
being locally Lipschitz, we first present an action mapping algorithm, which is locally Lipschitz, maps an action
\mathbf{y}_{h}
for state
\mathbf{x}
to an action
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.

The following algorithm maps an action
\mathbf{y}_{h}
for
\mathbf{x}
to an action
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}.

Action mapping algorithm:

•

Initialization:
Given
\mathbf{x},

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{y}_{h}
, initialize
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
such that

y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)=\min\{y_{h}(s,1),x^{\prime}(s)\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)=x^{\prime}(s)-y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1).

•

Adjustment:
Iterate each state
1\leq s\leq S
once as follows:

–

Case 1:
If
y_{h}(s,1)\leq x^{\prime}(s)
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}(s,\cdot)\in{\cal Y}^{s}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}},
then skip.

–

Case 2:
If
y_{h}(s,1)\leq x^{\prime}(s)
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}(s,\cdot)\notin{\cal Y}^{s}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}},
then consider two sub-cases:

*

Sub-case 1:
If
y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)>y^{*}_{h}(s,0)+\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N},
then let
\epsilon=y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)-y^{*}_{h}(s,0)-\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}>0
and find state
\hat{s}
such that
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)>y^{*}_{h}(\hat{s},1)+2z_{h}\delta_{N}.

*

Sub-case 2:
If
y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)<y^{*}_{h}(s,0)-\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N},
then let
\epsilon=y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)-y^{*}_{h}(s,0)+\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}<0
and find state
\hat{s}
such that
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)<y^{*}_{h}(\hat{s},1)-3z_{h}\delta_{N}

Adjust
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}(s,\cdot)
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},\cdot)
as follows:

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)-\epsilon\quad\hbox{and}\quad y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)+\epsilon

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)+\epsilon\quad\hbox{and}\quad y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)-\epsilon.

–

Case 3:
If
y_{h}(s,1)>x^{\prime}(s),
let
\epsilon=y_{h}(s,1)-x^{\prime}(s)
and find
\hat{s}
that satisfies one of two conditions: (1)
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)\geq 2z_{h}\delta_{N}
and
y^{*}_{h}(\hat{s},0)=0
or (2)
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\leq y^{*}_{h}(\hat{s},1)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}
and
y_{h}^{*}(\hat{s},0)>0.
Then make the following change:

y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)-\epsilon\quad\hbox{and}\quad y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\leftarrow y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)+\epsilon.

Lemma C.6

(Locally Lipschitz action mapping)
.

Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}_{z_{h}\delta_{N}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{h}\in{\cal Y}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}}\bigcap{\cal Y}_{\mathbf{x}}
, the vector
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}
obtained by the action mapping algorithm above satisfies
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\in{\cal Y}_{\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}}\bigcap{\cal Y}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}
and

\|\mathbf{y}_{h}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq(S+1)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

i.e. the action mapping is locally Lipschitz.

Proof.

The first step is to show that the algorithm is feasible, i.e.
\hat{s}
defined in Case 2 and Case 3 always exists. To prove this, we first observe that after initialization,

\alpha-2Sz_{h}\delta_{N}\leq\sum_{s}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)\leq\alpha

(81)

because

\displaystyle 0\leq\sum_{s}y_{h}(s,1)-\sum_{s}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{s}y_{h}(s,1)-\sum_{s}\min\{y_{h}(s,1),x^{\prime}(s)\}

\displaystyle\ \leq\sum_{s:y_{h}(s,1)>x^{\prime}(s)}x(s)-x^{\prime}(s)\leq S\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\ \leq 2Sz_{h}\delta_{N}.

We now analyze the feasibility of each case:

•

Case 2.1
: Note that
y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)>y^{*}_{h}(s,0)+\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}
implies

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)=x^{\prime}(s)-y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)

\displaystyle\ \leq x^{*}_{h}(s)+z_{h}\delta_{N}-y^{*}_{h}(s,0)-\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\ =y^{*}_{h}(s,1)-(\kappa-1)z_{h}\delta_{N}.

Now suppose
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\leq y_{h}^{*}(\hat{s},1)+2z_{h}\delta_{N}
for all
\hat{s}\not=s
then

\sum_{s}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)\leq\alpha+2Sz_{h}\delta_{N}-(\kappa-1)z_{h}\delta_{N},

which contradicts (
81
) since
\kappa>1+6S
. We note that

y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)>2z_{h}\delta_{N}

and
\epsilon=\Delta(s,0)\leq 2z_{h}\delta_{N}
according to Lemma
C.7
stated and proven below, so
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},\cdot)\geq{\bf 0}
after the adjustment.

•

Case 2.2
: Note that
y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)<y^{*}_{h}(s,0)-\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}
implies

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)=x^{\prime}(s)-y^{\prime}_{h}(s,0)

\displaystyle\ \geq x^{*}_{h}(s)-z_{h}\delta_{N}-y^{*}_{h}(s,0)+\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\ =y^{*}_{h}(s,1)+(\kappa-1)z_{h}\delta_{N}.

Now suppose
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\geq y_{h}^{*}(\hat{s},1)-3z_{h}\delta_{N}
for all
\hat{s}\not=s,
then

\sum_{s}y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)\geq\alpha+(\kappa-1)z_{h}\delta_{N}-3Sz_{h}\delta_{N},

which contradicts (
81
). We note that

y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},0)=x^{\prime}(\hat{s})-y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},1)\geq x^{*}_{h}(\hat{s})-z_{h}\delta_{N}-y_{h}^{*}(\hat{s},1)+3\delta_{N}h=y_{h}^{*}(\hat{s},0)+2z_{h}\delta_{N}

and
\epsilon=\Delta(s,0)\leq 2z_{h}\delta_{N}
according to Lemma
C.7
, so
y^{\prime}_{h}(\hat{s},\cdot)\geq{\bf 0}
after the adjustment.

•

Case 3:
Note that in this case
y^{*}_{h}(s,0)=0
because otherwise,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(s)\geq x_{h}^{*}(s)-z_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\ =y_{h}^{*}(s,1)+y_{h}^{*}(s,0)-z_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\ \geq y_{h}(s,1)-\kappa z_{h}\delta_{N}+y_{h}^{*}(s,0)-z_{h}\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\ >y_{h}(s,1),

which is a contradiction. Therefore given
\alpha<1,
there exists
\hat{s}\not=s
such that
y^{*}_{h}(\hat{s},0)>0.
Note that
y^{\prime}_{h}(s,1)=x^{\prime}(s)\geq x_{h}^{*}(s)-z_{h}\delta_{N}\geq y_{h}^{*}(s,1)-z_{h}\delta_{N}
. Now Suppose no
\hat{s}=s
satisfies condition (1) or condition (2). Then for
\hat{s}
such that

Proof of Claim (

Since events

and

are independent,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\mathcal{E}_{h}}\mid\mathcal{D}_{h}\right)=

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{h}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\|_{\infty}>\delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq s^{\prime}\leq S}|Z_{h}(s^{\prime})|>\sqrt{N}\delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{h}(s^{\prime})|>\sqrt{N}\delta_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\log N}}\right),

Recall the definition of the policy class
\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})=\big\{\pi:\|\pi(\mathbf{x}_{h},h)-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa\|\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty},\;\forall h\geq 1\big\}.

Note that from the definition of
v^{N}_{\pi_{c}},
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)=\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)+\frac{{v}^{{N}}_{\tilde{\pi}_{c}^{{N},*}}(\mathbf{0},1)}{\sqrt{N}},

and for
\pi\in\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)=\overline{V}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)+\frac{v^{N}_{\pi_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)}{\sqrt{N}}.

Now given
\pi,
we construct a modified policy
\pi^{\prime}
such that
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},h)=\pi(\mathbf{x},h)
for
\mathbf{x}
such that
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq z_{h}\delta_{N}
and
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},h)=\pi_{\text{pre}}(\mathbf{x},h),
where
\pi_{\text{pre}}
is the predetermined policy when defining
\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. We therefore have
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
and
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
coincide when their trajectories satisfy
\|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}=\|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\pi}_{h}-\mathbf{x}^{*}_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq z_{h}\delta_{N}
for all
h.
Therefore, from Item
2
of Lemma
C.8
, we have

v^{N}_{\pi_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)-v^{N}_{\pi^{\prime}_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)\leq 2r_{\max}H\sqrt{N}\frac{\tilde{C}}{N^{\log N}}.

(106)

Let
{\tilde{\pi}^{{N},*}_{c}}
be an optimal policy in
{\Pi}^{c}_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
for Problem (
26
) that corresponds to the locally-SP-optimal policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
for Problem (
16
). Since
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{ini})=\pi(\mathbf{x}_{ini})=\mathbf{y}^{*}_{1}
and by construction
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\delta_{N}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, we have

v^{N}_{\pi^{\prime}_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)\leq{q}^{N}_{{\tilde{\pi}_{c}^{{N},*}}}({\bf 0},{\bf 0},1),

which from (
106
) implies that

v^{N}_{\pi_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)\leq{q}^{N}_{{\tilde{\pi}_{c}^{{N},*}}}({\bf 0},{\bf 0},1)+\frac{2r_{\max}H\tilde{C}}{N^{\log N-0.5}}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)-\tilde{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)=\frac{{v}^{{N}}_{\tilde{\pi}_{c}^{{N},*}}(\mathbf{0},1)-v^{N}_{\pi_{c}}(\mathbf{0},1)}{\sqrt{N}}\geq\frac{\epsilon-\frac{2r_{\max}H\tilde{C}}{N^{\log N-0.5}}}{\sqrt{N}}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{N}},

(107)

where the last inequality holds for any
N
such that

N^{\log N-0.5}\geq\frac{4r_{\max}H\tilde{C}}{\epsilon}.

We now invoke Lemma
C.3
, which is proved for policy
\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}
, and the observation that the same result holds for any policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\text{fluid}}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, because by definition
\pi
is a Lipschitz policy so its value function is Lipschitz. We then have

\left|\tilde{V}^{N}_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)-V_{\tilde{\pi}^{N,*}}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)\right|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

(108)

as well as

\left|\tilde{V}^{N}_{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{ini},1)-V_{\pi}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ini}},1)\right|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(109)

The claim of the theorem is then established by combining Equations (
107
), (
108
) and (
109
).
