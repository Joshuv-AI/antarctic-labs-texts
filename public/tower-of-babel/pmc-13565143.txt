Is there evidence for additive neurocognitive deficits in adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and co-occurring conditions?
Authors: Allyson G Harrison, Nathaniel Davin, Dylan Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) frequently present with co-occurring anxiety and depressive symptoms, raising questions about whether such comorbidity is associated with additive neurocognitive impairment. Prior findings have been mixed and rarely account for symptom exaggeration or noncredible task engagement. This study examined whether adults with ADHD and elevated internalizing symptoms demonstrate additive neurocognitive deficits when symptom and performance validity are controlled.MethodArchival data were analyzed from 752 postsecondary students referred for psychoeducational/neuropsychological evaluation. Participants were classified into ADHD only, ADHD with elevated anxiety and/or depressive symptoms, anxiety only, depression only, and assessment-seeking clinical controls without formal diagnoses. Analyses were restricted to individuals who passed established presentation validity criteria. Neurocognitive performance was examined across intellectual functioning, executive functioning, memory, and academic achievement using mean-level comparisons and rates of scores at or below the 16th percentile.ResultsAcross domains, group means generally fell within the average range with substantial overlap among groups. Adults with ADHD and co-occurring anxiety and/or depressive symptoms did not demonstrate poorer neurocognitive performance than adults with ADHD alone. Statistically significant group differences emerged on select measures but were small, inconsistent, and not specific to comorbid ADHD groups. Rates of low scores (e.g., ≤ 16th percentile) were comparable across groups.ConclusionsWhen presentation validity is established, co-occurring anxiety and/or depressive symptoms were not associated with consistent or clinically meaningful additive neurocognitive deficits in adults with ADHD. These findings underscore the importance of validity-controlled assessment and caution against inferring objective cognitive impairment solely from diagnostic comorbidity.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most commonly diagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders of childhood and is characterized by developmentally inappropriate levels of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Worldwide prevalence estimates suggest that ~7.2% of children meet diagnostic criteria for ADHD (Thomas et al., 2015), with more recent estimates in the United States approaching 10.47% (Li et al., 2023). Although ADHD was once conceptualized primarily as a childhood condition, substantial evidence now indicates that symptoms frequently persist into adulthood (Sibley et al., 2017). Longitudinal studies suggest that between 50% and 78% of individuals diagnosed in childhood continue to experience full or partial symptom persistence into adulthood (Biederman et al., 2011; Faraone et al., 2005), with more recent work demonstrating heterogeneous developmental trajectories that include stable, remitting, and fluctuating symptom courses (Van Meter et al., 2024). Meta-analytic estimates place the global prevalence of adult ADHD at ~2.58%, with rates continuing to rise in recent decades (Abdelnour et al., 2022; Song et al., 2021; Vasiliadis et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2018).

The clinical presentation of ADHD often changes across development. While hyperactivity and impulsivity tend to diminish with age, inattentive symptoms frequently persist into adulthood (Biederman et al., 2000; Martel et al., 2012; Targum & Adler, 2014; Wilens et al., 2009). In adults, these symptoms are commonly associated with difficulties in academic, occupational, and interpersonal functioning, including problems with task completion, time management, planning, and meeting deadlines (Adler et al., 2008; Riccio et al., 2005). Neurocognitively, ADHD has been most consistently linked to weaknesses in executive functions, particularly so-called “cool” executive processes such as response inhibition, working memory, sustained attention, cognitive flexibility, and temporal processing, which are often attributed to atypical frontostriatal development (Rubia, 2018; Rubia et al., 2013). Empirical studies of adults with ADHD frequently report poorer performance on tasks emphasizing processing speed, working memory, reaction time, and cognitive flexibility relative to healthy controls (Somogyi et al., 2023).

Diagnostic complexity increases substantially in adulthood, largely due to the high prevalence of psychiatric comorbidity. Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most common co-occurring conditions in adults with ADHD (Harrison & Pollock, 2024; Katzman et al., 2017). Epidemiological studies suggest that nearly half of adults with ADHD meet criteria for an anxiety disorder (Kessler et al., 2005), and lifetime rates of major depressive disorder have been reported in more than 60% of adults with persistent ADHD (McGough et al., 2005). These rates exceed those observed in child and adolescent populations (Souza et al., 2005) and contribute to challenges in diagnostic differentiation and neurocognitive interpretation.

Symptom overlap further complicates this picture. Many diagnostic criteria for mood and anxiety disorders include cognitive complaints that closely resemble ADHD symptoms, such as difficulties with concentration, restlessness, psychomotor agitation, and diminished cognitive efficiency (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Harrison & Pollock, 2024). Elevated self-reported ADHD symptoms have been observed in individuals with high levels of depression, anxiety, or stress, even in the absence of a developmental ADHD history (Harrison et al., 2013). Similarly, ADHD symptom endorsement is substantially more prevalent among individuals with chronic major depressive disorder than in the general population, with rates increasing as depression becomes more persistent or severe (Bron et al., 2016). Emotional dysregulation, often discussed as a prominent feature of adult ADHD, is likewise common in mood disorders, borderline personality disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder (Barkley & Brown, 2008; Carpenter & Trull, 2013; Conti et al., 2023; Katzman et al., 2017).

Consistent with this symptom overlap, neurocognitive deficits have been reported not only in ADHD (Pievsky & McGrath, 2018) but also in major depressive disorder and certain anxiety disorders (Castaneda et al., 2008; Lam et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2020). Individuals with depression often demonstrate reduced performance in processing speed, attention, executive functioning, learning, and memory relative to healthy controls (Hasselbalch et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2012; Murrough et al., 2011; Talarowska et al., 2015), and young adults with major depressive disorder have been shown to perform similarly to adults with ADHD on measures such as the Trail Making Test and verbal fluency tasks (Mahurin et al., 2006). Anxiety disorders have likewise been associated with deficits in executive functioning, attention, and memory (Castaneda et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2015). When depression and anxiety co-occur, impairments in processing speed and executive functioning may be more pronounced than in depression alone (Basso et al., 2007; Crane et al., 2016; He et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020).

Given these findings, it has been proposed that co-occurring internalizing conditions may exert an additive or synergistic effect on neurocognitive functioning in individuals with ADHD. Supporting this possibility, Bloemsma et al. (2013) found that anxiety symptoms in children with ADHD were associated with slower motor and response speed, while Larochette et al. (2011) reported poorer processing speed and delayed verbal recall in young adults with ADHD and elevated depressive symptoms relative to those with either condition alone. These observations have led to the hypothesis that disorders sharing genetic, neurochemical, or neurobiological substrates (such as dysregulation of dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems) may jointly exacerbate cognitive impairment (Dunlop & Nemeroff, 2007; Klassen et al., 2010; Van der Meer et al., 2018).

At the same time, an emerging literature has raised concerns that many reported neurocognitive deficits in psychiatric populations may be substantially influenced by test-taking engagement and symptom exaggeration rather than by disorder-related cognitive dysfunction per se. Across clinical contexts, performance validity has been shown to account for a large proportion of variance in neuropsychological test scores, often exceeding that explained by disease severity (Constantinou et al., 2005; Green et al., 2001; Stevens et al., 2008). Despite widespread claims of cognitive impairment in depression, a recent systematic review found that few studies controlled for performance validity, and those that did reported limited or absent objective deficits (Legemaat et al., 2023). Similar concerns have been raised in ADHD research, where inconsistent findings may partly reflect the inclusion of individuals providing noncredible symptom reports or suboptimal test performance (Harrison et al., 2021; Sherman et al., 2020).

Notably, no prior study examining additive neurocognitive deficits in adults with ADHD and co-occurring internalizing symptoms (e.g., anxiety, depression) has first excluded individuals with evidence of symptom exaggeration or noncredible performance before evaluating cognitive outcomes. This methodological gap raises questions about the validity of previously reported additive effects. Accordingly, the present study examined neurocognitive performance in a large sample of postsecondary students diagnosed with ADHD, with and without clinically significant anxiety and/or depressive symptoms, as well as assessment-seeking students without formal diagnoses. A defining feature of the present study is the exclusion of individuals who failed established performance or symptom validity criteria, allowing for a more conservative and clinically interpretable evaluation of whether co-occurring internalizing symptoms confer meaningful incremental neurocognitive impairments beyond ADHD alone.

Building on prior research on adult ADHD, internalizing psychopathology, and neurocognitive performance, and addressing concerns regarding symptom overlap and assessment validity, the present study examined whether co-occurring anxiety and depressive symptoms are associated with additive objective neurocognitive deficits in adults with ADHD when symptom and performance validity are established. The following hypotheses guided the analyses:

Hypothesis 1: Additive impairment hypothesis

Adults with ADHD and co-occurring clinically elevated anxiety and/or depressive symptoms were not expected to demonstrate broadly worse neurocognitive performance than adults with ADHD alone once symptom and performance validity criteria were met.

Hypothesis 2: Specificity of observed group differences

If statistically significant group differences emerged, they were expected to be limited in scope and small in magnitude, rather than reflecting a consistent or generalized pattern of impairment attributable to diagnostic comorbidity.

Hypothesis 3: Internalizing symptoms without ADHD

Individuals with elevated anxiety and/or depressive symptoms in the absence of ADHD were not expected to exhibit a distinct or systematically below average neurocognitive profile when validity criteria were met.

Hypothesis 4: Assessment-seeking clinical controls

Assessment-seeking adults who did not meet criteria for any DSM diagnosis were expected to demonstrate neurocognitive performance comparable to that of the other diagnostic groups. Any observed overlap was anticipated to reflect common referral pathways and assessment demands, rather than the presence or absence of a clinical diagnosis.

Exploratory analyses

Given ongoing debate regarding base rates of low scores (e.g., ≤16th percentile) in clinical samples, exploratory analyses examined whether the frequency and distribution of low scores varied meaningfully by diagnostic group once validity was established.

Methods

Participants

Data for the current study were drawn from a database of community college and four-year university students who completed a psychoeducational assessment at a university-affiliated postsecondary assessment clinic between 2008 and 2025 for reported difficulties with learning, attention, and/or mental health. All students provided informed consent permitting the use of their de-identified assessment data for research purposes.

To quantify current psychological distress, we reviewed archival data from 1433 students whose assessment included the Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI; Morey, 1991). While some students had been given a mental health diagnosis or were referred on for further psychiatric evaluation, we defined the mental health symptom groups using elevations on the PAI Depression (DEP) and/or Anxiety (ANX) clinical scales, with T-scores ≥70 (≥ 2 SD above the mean) indicating that these students reported experiencing clinically significant symptom severity on the date of testing. This threshold reflects endorsement of a broad range of clinically meaningful symptoms associated with functional impairment across multiple life domains (Keiski et al., 2007; Morey, 1991, 1996).

Participant selection and exclusion procedures are summarized in Fig. 1. Briefly, cases were sequentially excluded based on (a) uninterpretable PAI protocols (inconsistency or infrequency above recommended cutoff or data for these variables were missing), (b) evidence of marked negative response bias or mental health malingering on PAI validity indicators, or data missing for these variables, (c) missing or invalid performance validity data (≥2 PVT failures), and (d) diagnoses associated with known neurocognitive impairment (specific learning disorder or acquired brain injury). This stepwise approach was used to ensure that group classification was based on interpretable self-report data and that neurocognitive outcomes reflected credible test performance. Rates of exclusion did not differ meaningfully across diagnostic groups.

While some students in the database had been given a co-morbid mental health diagnosis or referred for a more intensive psychiatric evaluation, the anxiety and depression groups in the present study were defined dimensionally using PAI clinical scale elevations to capture symptom severity at the time of assessment rather than lifetime DSM diagnoses, ensuring the presence of clinically significant symptoms on the test date. Groups did not differ significantly in age, F(6, 735) = 0.962, p = .450. Gender distribution differed significantly across groups, χ2(12) = 59.89, p < .001; post hoc analyses indicated that the ADHD-only group included a higher proportion of males, whereas the ANX-only group included a higher proportion of females. No other groups differed significantly in gender composition.

Information regarding ethnicity was not collected at the time of assessment, however, an evaluation of all the referrals made to the assessment center during a portion of this time period revealed the following distribution of ethnic background: 72% White, 12% Asian, 6% Black, 3% Middle Eastern, and 7% Other/Not Specified. We therefore estimate a similar ethnic distribution in the current sample.

Materials

Clinicians administered a flexible battery of tests, depending on the referral question and clinician’s preference. Each battery assessed general intellectual ability, processing speed, speed of retrieval from long-term memory, and academic fluency, as well as psychological functioning; however, not all tests reviewed in the present study were given to all subjects. Notably, any subtest for which more than three-quarters of students in any group were missing data was not included in the present study to avoid unstable estimates due to very small group sizes. When tests measuring similar constructs were administered (e.g., tests of written expression, mathematical calculation, etc.), we chose to pool the results in order to evaluate whether demonstrable neurocognitive deficits could be identified in the standardized scores obtained by different subject groups.

Measures of interest in the current study included the following

The PAI (Morey, 1991) is a self-report measure used to assess domains of psychological functioning, interpersonal functioning, receptivity to psychological treatment, and impression management. Written at a 4th grade reading level, the PAI consists of 344 items endorsed on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from False to Very True. Items in each clinical scale independently assess breadth and depth of the specific construct in question. In addition to a DSM diagnosis, extreme scores from the Anxiety (ANX) and Depression (DEP) clinical scales of the PAI were employed for the current study because of their diagnostic relevance for anxiety and/or mood disorders (Morey, 1996) and to demonstrate that students were currently experiencing significantly high levels of symptoms on the date of the assessment.

The validity of each PAI protocol was evaluated using established indicators of inconsistency, infrequency, and response bias (Musso et al., 2016). Profiles demonstrating excessive inconsistency or infrequency were considered uninterpretable and excluded. In addition, because internalizing groups were defined based on elevated self-reported symptoms, profiles showing marked negative response bias or likely malingering of mental health symptoms (e.g., elevated NIM or MAL) were excluded to reduce the likelihood that extreme scores reflected exaggerated reporting rather than current symptom severity. Positive Impression Management (PIM) elevations were not used as exclusion criteria, as these reflect symptom minimization.

Finally, cases were included only if clinicians administered at least two minimally related performance validity tests (PVTs) as part of the assessment. Measures were selected from a standard set that included the Word Memory Test (WMT; Green, 2003/2005) or the Medical Symptom Validity Test (MSVT; Green, 2004), the Test of Memory Malingering (TOMM; Tombaugh, 1996; cutoffs per Martin et al., 2020), the Victoria Symptom Validity Test (VSVT; Slick et al., 1997; cutoffs per Resch et al., 2021), Reliable Digit Span (RDS; Greiffenstein & Baker, 1994), the b Test (Boone et al., 2000), and the Symptom Exaggeration Index (SEI) from the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA; Leark et al., 2002). Consistent with best-practice recommendations, individuals who failed two or more PVTs were excluded (Odland et al., 2015; Sherman et al., 2020). Failure classifications were based on established cutoffs from the respective validation literature for each measure.

Neurocognitive performance measures

The following neurocognitive performance measures were included in the current study: The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale – Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV; Wechsler, 2008) provides a Full Scale IQ index (FSIQ) and a General Abilities Index (GAI), and also provides various index scores including Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Perceptual Reasoning (PRI), Working Memory (WMI), and Processing Speed (PSI); this latter index measures “the speed and fluency at which very simple repetitive tasks…can be performed” (Schneider, 2013, p. 312).

The Wechsler Memory Scale – Fourth Edition (WMS-IV; Wechsler, 2009a) includes indices of a composite of subtest scaled scores of Auditory Memory (AMI), Visual Memory (VMI), Visual Working Memory (VWMI), Immediate Memory (IMI), and Delayed Memory (DMI).

The Woodcock-Johnson III (WJ-III; Woodcock et al., 2001a, 2001b) and the Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ-IV, Schrank et al., 2014a; Schrank et al., 2014b) include subtests that measure Sentence Reading Fluency and Writing Fluency; given that the two versions of these subtests are almost identical in content and produce a scaled score based on normative data, we combined scores from both versions of the test to achieve a higher sample size. Moreover, we combined the Wechsler Individual Achievement Test III (WIAT-III; Wechsler, 2009b), WJ-III, and WJ-IV subtests that measure Math Fluency, Basic Reading, Math Problem Solving/Applied Problems, and Numerical Operations/Calculation.

The Delis Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS, Delis et al., 2001) assesses processing speed from the Trails Tests (Letter Sequencing, Number Sequencing, and Visual Scanning), speed of accessing information from verbal long-term memory by means of the Color-Word Interference Tests (Color Naming and Word Reading tests) and the Verbal Fluency Tests (Letter Fluency and Category Fluency), and overall achievement from the Tower Test. Performance on this latter test was determined by a single score which was calculated using the scoring system provided by the D-KEFS manual. Each individual item received a score based on whether or not the tower was correct, how many moves the participant made, and how long the participant took to complete the item. Once the score for each item was determined, all nine numbers were added together. The sum was the overall achievement score for the participant’s performance on the Tower Test.

For all measures, standard scores at or below 85 (or scale scores below 7 on subtests, i.e., at or below the 16th percentile) were taken to indicate relatively low or below-expected performance for the purposes of categorical analyses. This threshold was selected because performance at or below the 16th percentile is commonly used in clinical, educational, and disability-related contexts as a marker of meaningful functional difficulty (APA, 2013).

Procedure

Postsecondary students in this study had been referred for either an updated assessment of a previously-diagnosed disorder or to investigate the cause for currently-reported academic difficulties. Students were assessed by either a registered psychologist or a supervised graduate student. Prior to the beginning of the assessment, students were informed that their effort would be tested throughout the day and were advised to put forth their best effort. Additionally, subjects were instructed to work as quickly and as accurately as possible on all timed tests, as per individual test instructions. As such, time pressure was a factor present for any speeded test.

Evaluations for suspected ADHD conducted at this center included a multi-method, multi-informant procedure that included all of the following: (a) a review of all available historical records (medical, mental health, academic history including elementary school report cards, and any prior psychoeducational or neuropsychological evaluations); (b) semi-structured clinical interview based on the DSM-IV-TR (APA, 2000) or DSM-5 (APA, 2013) diagnostic criteria for ADHD; (c) retrospective evaluation by parents or caregivers of ADHD symptoms displayed by the client prior to age 12; (d) both self-and observer ratings of current ADHD symptoms; (e) performance and symptom validity tests; (f) assessment of general psychological difficulties using the Personality Assessment Inventory (Morey, 1991) to help rule out other potential causes for reported symptoms; (g) evidence of historical and current functional impairment in two or more major life areas; and (h) a comprehensive neuropsychological battery of tests. The overall procedure for diagnosis of ADHD followed closely the steps recommended by Sibley (2021). In cases where significant internalizing symptoms were reported, additional information was obtained to determine if the client met diagnostic criteria for another psychological disorder, and to investigate whether or not the symptoms objectively predated the reported attention complaints. All diagnoses were made by a licensed psychologist (or a graduate student being supervised by a licensed psychologist).

All research procedures were approved by an institutional research ethics board.

Data analysis

The relationship between depressive or anxiety-related symptoms and neurocognitive performance in ADHD was examined using categorical analyses. Group differences were analyzed using Fisher’s Analysis of Variance with Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance and Tukey’s post hoc pairwise analysis. Where assumptions for Fisher’s ANOVA were not met, Welch’s ANOVA was substituted, and pairwise post hoc analysis was done using Tamhane’s T2 which allows for unequal sample size and heterogeneity of variance. Group differences were also analyzed categorically by assessing differences in group proportions of those who scored below average across the domains as indicated by percentages of participants that scored below a scaled score of 85 or seven depending on the subtest.

Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the high degree of correlation among measures, no additional correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

Intellectual functioning

Table 1 presents WAIS-IV composite scores for each group. Across groups, mean scores generally fell within the average range, with the exception of the ADHD+DEP group, whose mean GAI and VCI scores were in the above-average range. Significant omnibus group differences were observed on FSIQ, GAI, VCI, PRI, and WMI, but not on Processing Speed (see Table 1). Post hoc analyses indicated that these differences were limited in scope and primarily reflected slightly higher scores among the ADHD-only group relative to ASC group rather than poorer performance among participants with ADHD and co-occurring anxiety and/or depressive symptoms. Overall, the pattern of findings did not support additive intellectual or working memory impairment associated with internalizing comorbidity.

Executive functioning

Performance on executive functioning and processing speed measures from the D-KEFS is presented in Table 2, along with the percentage of participants who scored in the below-average range (i.e., at or below the 16th percentile) on each subtest. Across groups, mean scores consistently fell within the average range, and no significant differences were observed between groups on any D-KEFS measure. Rates of below-average performance were likewise similar across groups, with the exception of the Color Naming subtest, where the DEP group demonstrated a higher proportion of low scores than the ASC group. Overall, the pattern of findings did not support additive executive dysfunction associated with co-occurring anxiety and/or depressive symptoms.

Discussion

The purpose of this investigation was to evaluate whether additive neurocognitive deficits are present when ADHD co-occurs with severe internalizing symptoms (anxiety and/or depression), relative to when each condition occurs alone, and to compare performance to assessment-seeking clinical controls without a formal diagnosis. All mental health groups were characterized by elevated self-reported symptom severity at the time of assessment, reflecting periods of heightened stress and academic demand (Barker et al., 2018). Because all speeded tasks explicitly emphasize time limits and accuracy, some degree of performance anxiety was expected across groups.

As anticipated, results from this large postsecondary sample indicate that, when analyses are restricted to individuals demonstrating credible symptom reporting and task engagement, young adults with ADHD and co-occurring anxiety and/or depression do not show consistently identifiable neurocognitive or functional academic deficits relative to non-diagnosed clinical controls or to those with a single condition. Although statistically significant group differences emerged on several WAIS-IV indices, effect sizes were small and mean scores for all groups remained within broadly average normative ranges. Notably, these differences did not reflect additive impairment associated with comorbid internalizing symptoms. Instead, the ADHD-only group frequently obtained the highest scores, whereas assessment-seeking clinical controls often demonstrated the lowest performance.

An interesting finding was that assessment-seeking clinical controls frequently performed comparably to, and in some cases less well than, participants diagnosed with ADHD. These individuals were referred for evaluation because of perceived academic, cognitive, or emotional difficulties, or to determine whether previously established diagnoses and accommodations remained supported by current evidence. Although they did not meet criteria for ADHD, a learning disorder, a traumatic brain injury, or the internalizing symptom groups examined in the present study, they nevertheless represent a clinically relevant population commonly encountered in postsecondary assessment settings. This finding highlights the importance of using appropriately selected clinical comparison groups when evaluating the specificity of neurocognitive findings associated with ADHD and internalizing symptoms. Had comparisons been restricted to asymptomatic controls, group differences may have appeared larger despite having less relevance to real-world diagnostic decision-making.

Across the remaining neurocognitive and achievement measures, performance was broadly comparable across groups and generally remained within average normative limits. If additive deficits associated with internalizing symptoms meaningfully affect performance on time-limited academic or processing speed tasks, they would be expected to emerge on at least some of these commonly used fluency and processing-speed measures. Notably, on relatively short measures of these time-based skills, no significant additive neurocognitive deficits were found. These findings converge with prior work suggesting that ADHD, whether alone or co-occurring with internalizing disorders, does not generally confer disadvantage on shorter, time-limited academic tasks (Jansen et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2015; Pritchard et al., 2016). Importantly, the measures used in the present study closely resemble those commonly relied upon in clinical practice when making recommendations for extra test-taking time in academic settings. Within this context, no consistent or meaningful group differences were observed.

At the same time, these findings should not be interpreted as directly addressing performance under prolonged, high-stakes testing conditions, which may involve additional demands on sustained attention, fatigue, and test anxiety. However, the neuropsychological and psychoeducational measures used in the present study closely resemble the standardized, time-limited tasks commonly relied upon by clinicians when evaluating the need for extended-time accommodations. Within this context, adults with ADHD, with or without co-occurring internalizing symptoms, performed comparably to other assessment-seeking individuals when performance validity was established. Accordingly, assumptions that diagnostic status alone implies objective impairment on these commonly used clinical measures warrant careful scrutiny.

These null or limited findings may appear inconsistent with portions of the existing literature reporting cognitive deficits in ADHD and internalizing disorders. A key distinction, however, is that most prior studies did not systematically control for presentation validity. This omission is particularly consequential in postsecondary and high-stakes assessment contexts, where estimates of symptom exaggeration or noncredible performance range from 25% to 50% (Sagar et al., 2017). Failure to account for validity introduces uncertainty in the interpretation of both symptom reports and test performance (Green et al., 2001; Ruiz et al., 2020), raising the possibility that some previously reported neurocognitive differences may, in part, reflect unrecognized response bias rather than disorder-specific impairment. Alternatively, individuals with ADHD and internalizing symptoms may experience amplified subjective distress without corresponding additive cognitive performance deficits. Future research and clinical assessment should therefore incorporate rigorous symptom and performance validity measures to accurately characterize functional impairment.

Interpretation of low scores and base rates in an assessment-seeking sample

In addition to mean-level comparisons, interpretation of individual low scores must be grounded in well-established base-rate findings. Extensive research demonstrates that most non-disabled adults obtain one or more scores at or below the 16th percentile when administered comprehensive neuropsychological or psychoeducational batteries (Binder et al., 2009; Brooks et al., 2011; Jeffay et al., 2021; Lovett & Schaberg, 2026; Maddocks, 2018; Schretlen et al., 2008). Accordingly, isolated low scores are common in normative samples and lack diagnostic specificity, particularly in assessment-seeking samples.

Consistent with these base-rate findings, a substantial proportion of students across all groups in the present study (including assessment-seeking clinical controls) obtained at least one score at or below the 16th percentile. Significant group differences in low-score frequency were observed primarily on WAIS-IV subtests, where, with the exception of processing speed, the assessment-seeking clinical control group showed the highest proportion of low scores and the ADHD-only group the lowest. This pattern further underscores the limited diagnostic specificity of isolated low scores.

One isolated difference was observed on the D-KEFS Color Naming task. Otherwise, executive functioning measures showed substantial overlap across groups and did not support additive dysfunction associated with internalizing comorbidity.

Across all remaining measures, no group differences were observed in the proportion of low scores. One notable exception was the consistently high rate of low performance on measures of mathematical fluency and calculation across all groups, including assessment-seeking controls. These findings likely reflect broader educational factors rather than disorder-specific cognitive deficits. As noted previously (e.g., Harrison et al., 2022), the general difficulties with basic math skills demonstrated by all of the current groups may reflect longstanding concerns regarding mathematics instruction in Ontario, particularly the adoption of discovery-based curricula that deemphasized automatization of basic math skills (CMEC, 2012; Stokke, 2015; Zwaagstra, 2023) rather than disorder-specific cognitive deficits. These results highlight the importance of interpreting academic test performance within educational and base-rate contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Sample sizes varied across diagnoses and measures, which may have reduced statistical power for some comparisons. The exclusive focus on postsecondary students enhances relevance to this population but limits generalizability to individuals with lower educational attainment. The retrospective design also introduced variability in diagnostic practices across clinicians and diagnostic eras. Not all participants completed measures of sustained or divided attention, which may warrant further study, although prior research suggests that attentional deficits alone do not justify extended-time accommodations (Harrison et al., 2022; Jansen et al., 2019; Lewandowski et al., 2013; Lovett & Leja, 2015; Miller et al., 2015; Pritchard et al., 2016). Medication status was not systematically documented; however, recent evidence suggests minimal cognitive differences between medicated and unmedicated postsecondary students with ADHD on most neurocognitive and achievement measures (blinded for review, in preparation).

The exclusion of individuals with specific learning disorders or acquired brain injuries reduced heterogeneity in neurocognitive profiles but limits generalizability to more clinically complex populations in which such comorbidities are common. In addition, internalizing groups were defined using dimensional self-report criteria rather than standardized diagnostic interviews, which may not fully capture formally diagnosed anxiety or depressive disorders. Although comprehensive clinical evaluations were conducted, diagnostic classification necessarily reflects information available at the time of assessment. Symptom expression may fluctuate over time, particularly for anxiety and depression, and some individuals may have experienced clinically significant symptoms outside the assessment period that were not captured by the grouping criteria used in the present study. However, because the primary objective was to examine neurocognitive performance in relation to symptom severity at the time of testing, participants were intentionally classified according to their contemporaneous symptom presentation rather than lifetime diagnostic history.

The use of assessment-seeking controls may limit generalizability to non-referred populations. However, this comparison group provides a clinically relevant reference point because differential diagnosis in practice typically occurs among individuals presenting with subjective academic, cognitive, or emotional concerns rather than among asymptomatic postsecondary students. Participants in this group underwent comprehensive evaluation but did not meet criteria for ADHD, a specific learning disorder, traumatic brain injury, or the internalizing symptom groups examined in the present study.

Finally, although multiple performance validity indicators were used, most participants did not receive more than two PVTs, which may reduce sensitivity to certain forms of noncredible responding. In addition, currently available validity measures are not uniformly sensitive to all forms of noncredible presentation, particularly when difficulties are selectively exaggerated rather than broadly fabricated.

Clinical considerations and conclusion

The present findings have important implications for clinical assessment. Neuropsychological testing alone did not meaningfully differentiate ADHD from anxiety, depression, or assessment-seeking controls, consistent with prior research indicating limited diagnostic specificity of cognitive test profiles in psychiatric conditions (Barkley, 2019; Harvey, 2012; Zakzanis et al., 1999). Accurate diagnosis therefore requires careful consideration of developmental history, symptom trajectory, collateral information, and age of onset, which reliably distinguishes ADHD from internalizing disorders (Kessler et al., 2007; Lijster et al., 2017; Solmi et al., 2022).

While neuropsychological and academic tests may not differentiate diagnoses, credible test results can still document functional impairments relevant to intervention and academic accommodation decisions. Such interpretations, however, require careful consideration of symptom and performance validity and the base rate of low scores in non-disabled populations (Suhr & Johnson, 2022; Weis & Waters, 2023). Consistent with prior work, the present findings raise questions regarding the assumptions that ADHD and internalizing disorders, alone or combined, necessarily imply the presence of objective deficits on standard time-limited clinical measures commonly used to support extended-time accommodation recommendations. Decisions regarding the need for academic accommodations should therefore be based on demonstrated functional impairment and contextual evidence rather than diagnosis alone (Roberts, 2012).

In summary, after controlling for credible performance, no consistent or clinically meaningful evidence for additive neurocognitive deficits was observed in adults with ADHD and high levels of co-occurring anxiety or depression symptoms. Although comorbid conditions may exacerbate subjective distress or functional difficulties in other domains, they did not result in additive cognitive impairment on objective measures. These findings underscore the necessity of validity-controlled assessment and cautious interpretation when evaluating cognitive complaints in individuals with overlapping psychiatric presentations.
