Retrospective study on the correlation between biochemical and blood routine indexes and prognosis in elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1)
Authors: Xiaoxu Li, Minmin Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Comprehensive assessment of elderly patients with H1N1.•D-dimer is associated with longer hospital stays and lung pathology.•Lymphocyte is linked to the development of severe H1N1.•Blood markers indicate an increased risk of progression of H1N1.Keywords: Influenza A (H1N1), d-dimer, Inflammatory response, Elderly patients, Prognosis assessment

Introduction

As a respiratory infectious disease caused by the influenza A virus, influenza A (H1N1) is one of the seasonal influenza viruses. Currently, the number of deaths caused by seasonal influenza globally each year is estimated to be between 290,000 and 650,000, which poses a significant threat to global public health security (Lampejo, 2020; Iuliano et al., 2018). The sources of infection of influenza A (H1N1) include symptomatic and asymptomatic infected individuals, and it is mainly transmitted through respiratory droplets. The incubation period of this virus is generally 1 to 7 days, and people of all ages are susceptible. However, children, pregnant women, the elderly, and those with underlying respiratory diseases are at a higher risk of infection (Wang et al., 2024). As an acute respiratory infectious disease, influenza A (H1N1) has a rapid onset and can easily trigger various complications, such as pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), bronchial asthma, etc., and may further lead to renal failure, sepsis, and multiple organ failure, seriously threatening human life (Dou et al., 2022). It is particularly noteworthy that due to the decline in immune function and the presence of underlying diseases in the elderly population, the symptoms and complications after infection with influenza A (H1N1) are more severe, which has become one of the main reasons for the significant increase in the mortality rate of elderly patients with severe pneumonia caused by influenza A (Okahashi et al., 2022).

Currently, blood routine tests and antibody tests are commonly used in clinical practice to diagnose influenza A (H1N1). However, due to the strong variability of the influenza A virus, the accuracy of blood routine tests is low, and antibody tests are time - consuming. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find a simple, rapid, and accurate serological indicator to improve the diagnostic accuracy and efficiency of influenza A (H1N1). Especially for elderly patients, further analysis combined with serological indicators to clarify the risk factors related to the development of pulmonary diseases complicated by influenza A (H1N1) and the malignant progression of the disease is of great significance for predicting and identifying high - risk patients with severe illness, formulating targeted intervention measures, and improving the prognosis of patients. In view of this, this study will focus on analyzing the serological indicator data of elderly patients with influenza A, aiming to provide more data support for clinical diagnosis and to provide strong evidence and a basis for the prognosis prediction of influenza A and the selection of treatment plans.

Experimental methods

General methods

This study was a retrospective cohort study. The data are from the relevant case records of elderly patients hospitalized in the Second People’s Hospital of Wuhu City from November 2023 to February 2024. The total number of influenza-like cases covered by the study was 179, and among them, there were 97 elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1) aged over 60 years old. The research protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second People’s Hospital of Wuhu City (approval number: 2025-KY-023). Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived during the research process.

Research subjects

All patients included in the study had a clear history of contact with influenza A (H1N1) patients. The initial symptoms of the patients were mainly fever, cough, and sore throat, and the typical manifestations were high fever, headache, muscle soreness, and fatigue. During the physical examination, pharyngeal congestion and tonsillar enlargement were the most common signs.

Data collection

The clinical data of the patients included age, gender, fever (body temperature >39 °C), cough, shortness of breath and other clinical manifestations. All patients underwent laboratory index tests after admission. The test items include:

Blood routine indexes: Monocytes (MONO), neutrophils (NE), white blood cells (WBC), lymphocytes (LYM), platelets (PLT), and hemoglobin (HGB) were detected using a fully automatic blood cell analyzer.

Inflammatory indexes: C- reactive protein (CRP), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).

Coagulation function index:d-dimer.

Renal function indexes: Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cr).

At the same time, the research team observed and recorded the acute physiological state and organ failure of the patients.

Imaging examinations

All patients underwent chest imaging examinations (X-ray or CT) immediately after admission, and professional physicians summarized and analyzed the imaging features.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Diagnostic criteria

The patients included in this study met the diagnostic criteria of the "Diagnosis and Treatment Plan for Influenza A (H1N1) (2010 Edition)" and had complete clinical data (Zhong et al., 2011).

Inclusion criteria

Patients included in the study needed to meet the following conditions: 1. Age ≥60 years old; 2. Diagnosed as H1N1 according to the above-mentioned diagnostic criteria; 3. No co- infected with other types of influenza viruses.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with the following characteristics needed to be excluded: 1. Co- infected with other types of influenza viruses; 2. Vaccinated against influenza within the past 1 month; 3. Poor compliance; 4. Bronchial foreign body; 5. Abnormal mental state.

Criteria for judging the severity of the disease

According to the severity of the disease, this study divided patients with H1N1 into mild patients and critically ill patients (Chudasama et al., 2013). The specific grouping criteria are as follows: Criteria for Inclusion in the Critically Ill Patient Group: patients with severe H1N1 were defined as those requiring ICU admission or who died. ICU admission criteria included the presence of one or more of the following features: (1) oxygen saturation (SpO₂) < 60 mmHg; (2) failure to maintain adequate SpO₂ despite oxygen mask therapy; (3) tachypnea and dyspnea; (4) respiratory rate > 40 breaths/min; (5) altered sensorium; (6) patchy consolidation on chest X-ray. Criteria for Judging Mild Patients: Patients who did not meet the above- mentioned severe criteria were classified into the mild patient group.

Statistical analysis

This study used GraphPad Prism 8.0 statistical software for data analysis. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). For comparisons between two groups, the t-test was used; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. In addition, through Pearson correlation analysis, the expression changes and correlations of serum and blood routine indicator levels in different patients were explored, and a linear regression equation was used to further analyze the correlations between different serum and blood routine indicators.In order to more intuitively present the expression spectrum of various indicators of elderly patients with H1N1, this study used Origin 2019b software to draw heat maps. In all statistical analyses, P < 0.05 was used as the criterion for determining that the difference was statistically significant.

Correlation analysis among serum biochemical indicators and blood routine indicators

This study used Origin software to conduct Pearson correlation analysis to explore the internal relationships among the serum and blood routine indicators of elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1). The analysis results were presented in the form of heat maps, and the specific correlations were as follows (Fig. 1):

C-reactive protein was significantly correlated with high-sensitivity CRP (R2) = 0.4849), lymphocyte count (R2) = 0.0777), and neutrophil count (R2) = 0.2723) (P < 0.05); high - sensitivity CRP was significantly correlated with hemoglobin (R2) = 0.1007) (P < 0.05); hemoglobin was significantly correlated with monocyte count (R2) = 0.0418) (P < 0.05); monocyte count was significantly correlated with white blood cell count (R2) = 0.3332), neutrophil count (R2) = 0.2402), d- dimer (R2) = 0.1975), and blood urea nitrogen (R2) = 0.0867) (P < 0.01); platelet count was significantly correlated with lymphocyte count (R2) = 0.1943) and white blood cell count (R2) = 0.0411) (P < 0.05); erythrocyte sedimentation rate was significantly correlated with blood urea nitrogen (R2) = 0.2747) and creatinine (R2) = 0.2869) (P < 0.05); white blood cell count was significantly correlated with neutrophils (R2) = 0.9636), d-dimer (R2) = 0.1821), and blood urea nitrogen (R2) = 0.2129)(P < 0.01); neutrophils were significantly correlated with d-dimer (R2) = 0.1929), blood urea nitrogen (R2)= 0.2266), and creatinine (R2) = 0.0774) (P < 0.05); PCT was significantly correlated with d-dimer (R2) = 0.1112) and creatinine (R2) = 0.0882) (P < 0.01); d-dimer was significantly correlated with blood urea nitrogen (R2) = 0.1223) and creatinine (R2) = 0.1126) (P < 0.01); blood urea nitrogen was significantly correlated with creatinine (R2) = 0.6533) (P < 0.01).

The above-mentioned correlation analyses were separately verified by linear regression equations. For repeated relevant indicators, this study did not describe them separately. The specific analysis data are shown in Fig. 2. Through the above analysis, this study found that the d-dimer was significantly correlated with the expression changes of 6 indicators (monocyte count, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, PCT, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine). This result suggests that d-dimer may play an important role in the inflammatory response and disease progression of elderly patients with influenza A, and can be used as a key indicator for evaluating the severity of the disease and prognosis.

Analysis of potential risk factors for disease progression, hospital stay duration, and pulmonary pathological changes in elderly patients with influenza A

Through a global heat map analysis of the distribution of the expression intensity spectra of various indicators of all patients (Fig. 3), the results showed that the changes in the serum and blood routine indicators of elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1) were mainly concentrated in the following aspects: C-reactive protein, high - sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, monocyte count, platelet count, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), d-dimer, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine (P < 0.05). The changes in the expression intensity of these indicators showed significant differences in the patient population, suggesting that they may have important implications for disease progression, hospital stay duration, and pulmonary pathological changes.

Heat map analysis of the expression of biochemical indices and blood routine indices in elderly patients with influenza A.

It is worth noting that procalcitonin (PCT) did not show significant differences within the group in this study (P > 0.05). This result indicates that PCT has a relatively low contribution in indicating the malignant progression of the disease, hospital stay duration, and pulmonary pathological changes. Therefore, it can be excluded from the statistical scope in subsequent analyses. The significant changes of these indicators suggest that they may play a key role in disease progression, and the exclusion of PCT helps to further optimize the selection of indicators in research and clinical practice.

Red lines mark the indicators with statistically significant differences within the group, P < 0.05. The differences within the group were tested by one sample t - test.

Through the analysis of potential risk factors for the hospital stay duration of elderly patients and various indicators (Fig. 4), the research results showed that the d-dimer level of patients with a hospital stay of >14 days was significantly higher than that of patients with a hospital stay of no >14 days, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P = 0.0413). This finding indicates that the d-dimer level may be an important indicator for predicting the hospital stay duration of elderly patients with influenza A.

Analysis of potential risk factors between biochemical indices, blood routine tests and the length of hospital stay (P < 0.05 indicates that the inter - group difference is statistically significant, marked in red).

This study explored the potential risk factors related to disease progression by comprehensively analyzing the biochemical test indicators and blood routine indicators of elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1). The research results showed that the lymphocyte count level was significantly decreased in critically ill and deceased patients (Fig. 5, P = 0.0355), while the d-dimer level was significantly increased in these patients (P = 0.0107). The comparisons between the two groups showed that these differences were all statistically significant. This finding indicates that a decrease in lymphocyte count and an increase in d-dimer level may be important early warning indicators for the progression of the disease in elderly patients with influenza A to a critical condition or even death. A decrease in lymphocyte count may reflect the impairment of the patient's immune function, while an increase in d- dimer level indicates the activation of the inflammatory response and the coagulation system in the body. The changes of these indicators provide important reference for clinicians, helping to identify high-risk patients early and take targeted intervention measures in a timely manner, thus improving the prognosis of patients.

Analysis of potential risk factors for disease progression in elderly patients with influenza A (P < 0.05 represents a significant difference in the inter - group comparison, No group (mild cases, n = 55), CIP-MORT (severe cases, n = 42, including deaths, n = 6); presented as Mean±SD).

In this study, the levels of pulmonary inflammation, calcification, fibrosis, and nodules in elderly patients with influenza A (H1N1) were analyzed in detail through pulmonary X-ray or CT imaging. The research results showed that there was a significant correlation between the changes in d-dimer level and pulmonary pathological changes (including inflammation, calcification, fibrosis, and nodules) (Fig. 6, F = 2.623, P = 0.0437). Specifically, compared with patients with only pulmonary inflammation, the d-dimer level was significantly lower in the following patient groups: patients with only pulmonary nodules (P = 0.0340); patients with two or more pathological changes (such as fibrosis, nodules, and calcification) at the same time (P = 0.0454). The d-dimer level is not only closely related to pulmonary inflammation but may also play an important role in the formation of pulmonary fibrosis, calcification, and nodules. A decrease in the d-dimer level may indicate a transition of pulmonary pathological changes from acute inflammation to chronic repair or fibrosis. This result provides important reference for clinicians, helping to identify the complexity and severity of pulmonary pathological changes early through the changes in d-dimer level, thus optimizing the treatment strategy and improving the long - term prognosis of patients.

P < 0.05 represents a significant difference).

Discussion

With the relaxation of mitigation measures from 2022 to 2023, the detection rate of influenza viruses in China increased significantly (Song et al., 2025). The pathogenesis of influenza A (H1N1) is closely related to a decline in immunity and the inflammatory response. The disease progresses rapidly, often causing pneumonia and inducing cardiopulmonary function damage, which significantly increases the difficulty of treatment. After the virus invades, it can weaken the body's immune system and trigger an inflammatory cascade reaction. In addition, the neuraminidase and hemagglutinin on the surface of the virus are prone to mutation, further enhancing the pathogenicity of the virus (Ge and Ross, 2021; Badar et al., 2020). Therefore, finding biomarkers related to influenza A (H1N1) to monitor the disease progression is of great significance for the formulation of clinical treatment plans.

D-dimer is a fibrinolytic degradation product of cross- linked fibrin in the blood after the decomposition of blood clots. After vascular injury, the coagulation system restricts blood loss by forming blood clots, and after plasminogen is converted into active plasmin, the blood clots are decomposed into fibrin degradation products, and the dimer of fragment D is d-dimer (Tortolini et al., 2022). d-dimer is not only a useful biomarker for diagnosing pulmonary embolism but also reflects the severity of the infection and is closely related to the inflammatory response (Cerda-Mancillas et al., 2020; Ge et al., 2019). In COVID-19, there have been studies on the correlation between d-dimer and monocytes, but research on their interaction is scarce. Moreover, relevant studies from HIN1 are rarely reported (Saber et al., 2022; Azarfar et al., 2023).

This study explored the relationship between d-dimer levels and the disease progression of patients with influenza A (H1N1) through Pearson correlation analysis and clinical disease progression correlation analysis. The results showed that the high expression of d-dimer significantly prolonged the hospital stay of patients and was closely related to the malignant progression of the disease. In addition, the d-dimer level was significantly correlated with the hospital stay duration from the onset of the disease to admission (Wang et al., 2011). Previous studies have also shown that the d-dimer level is significantly positively correlated with the prognosis of patients with influenza A (H1N1) (Jr et al., 2013; Nguyen et al., 2012). However, most of these studies were based on the North American population and lacked analysis of elderly patients. Therefore, this study focusing on the Asian elderly patient population has important reference significance. It particularly echoes the epidemiological characteristics of the 2023–2024 influenza epidemic in China. During this epidemic, the A (H1N1) pdm09 subtype was absolutely dominant, and the elderly, due to low immunity, became a high-risk group for severe cases. The biomarker analysis in this study just fills the gap in the clinical assessment of this population (Xie et al., 2024; Zhu and Gu, 2024).

It is worth noting that the d-dimer level was significantly elevated in patients with pulmonary inflammation, while it was generally at a relatively low level in patients with influenza A who only showed fibrosis, calcification, or nodules. This difference may be closely related to the promoting effect of d-dimer in the inflammatory response. When nodules, fibrosis, or calcification occur in the lungs, the lung tissue is usually dominated by anti-inflammatory reactions or chronic inflammation. At this time, the tissue will release more profibrotic factors, further promoting the parenchymalization process of the lungs (Lucas et al., 2020). This process is a legacy of tissue repair. Although it progresses slowly and usually does not cause sudden death, close follow - up and observation are still required in the long-term prognosis. In addition, timely anti-fibrosis treatment and regular monitoring of the changes of nodules and calcified foci are of great importance for improving the prognosis of patients.

In this study, the d-dimer level was significantly positively correlated with the counts of pro - inflammatory response cells such as monocytes and white blood cells. Monocytes and white blood cells are usually abnormally activated in patients with influenza A and are accompanied by a strong inflammatory cytokine storm, which not only aggravates the damage to lung tissue but may also lead to the further deterioration of the disease. The increase in white blood cell count usually occurs simultaneously with the increase in monocytes because monocytes are a subset of white blood cells. In addition, the increase in white blood cell count is closely related to the severity of the disease, which may indicate a stronger inflammatory response. This study also observed that patients with pulmonary inflammatory reactions after admission had a more rapid disease progression, which is also an important potential risk factor.

At present, no study has clearly confirmed the direct relationship between d- dimer and monocytes in patients with influenza A. However, in the study of COVID- 19, d-dimer has been reported to bind to the surface receptors of monocytes, further activating monocytes and releasing more inflammatory factors, thus forming a vicious cycle (Park et al., 2022). This mechanism may also exist in patients with influenza A, but further research is needed to verify it. Therefore, an increase in the d-dimer level in elderly patients may indicate a stronger inflammatory response. At the time of admission, special attention should be paid to patients with an increase in the d-dimer level accompanied by an increase in monocyte and white blood cell counts. The changes of these indicators have important reference value for subsequent control of disease progression.

In addition, this study also observed a significant association between a decrease in lymphocyte count and the severe condition of patients. The study found that the lymphocyte count of severe influenza A patients was significantly lower than that of mild patients, and the degree of lymphocytopenia was closely related to the disease progression and prognosis. In addition, lymphocytopenia may be related to the systemic inflammatory response caused by viral infection, which inhibits the proliferation and function of lymphocytes (Cheng et al., 2019). The influenza A virus (such as H1N1) can induce apoptosis of host cells through its PB1 - F2 protein, and this apoptotic effect is particularly significant in lymphocytes (GK et al., 2018). After viral infection, the body's immune response may lead to excessive consumption or functional inhibition of lymphocytes (Cheng et al., 2019). This immune consumption and inhibition response is more common in elderly patients with low immunity.

Although this study failed to directly prove that the D - dimer level can replace the classic and well - validated scoring systems as a single biomarker, the rapid quantification characteristic of the d-dimer level makes it a valuable auxiliary indicator. The comprehensive application of the d-dimer level, combined with lymphocyte, monocyte, and white blood cell counts, as well as pulmonary X- ray/CT imaging examinations, may help predict the hospitalization treatment needs of elderly patients and the pathological changes of influenza A, thus more accurately and scientifically optimizing the treatment and management strategies of the disease.

Furthermore, the core value of vaccination lies in the indirect inhibition of the "inflammation-coagulation" axis. Studies have mentioned that the percentage of specimens positive for A(H1N1)pdm09 began to increase at the end of March. In week 15 of 2024, A(H1N1)pdm09 was the most detected virus strain, showing an increasing trend. Timely and accurate influenza vaccination can effectively reduce the risk of infection. For patients infected with low-reactivity strains, the regulatory effect of the vaccine on d-dimer is weakened (with the intervention effect reduced by 35 %), suggesting that such patients need to rely on indicators such as d-dimer and lymphocyte count to strengthen disease monitoring (Gào et al., 2024). In addition, the decreased rate of severe cases in the elderly after vaccination indicates that vaccines can break the "coagulation-inflammation" vicious cycle by improving immune disorders in elderly patients, which provides laboratory evidence for prioritizing influenza vaccination in the elderly.

This study has the following limitations: (1) As a retrospective study, this study only focused on the initial levels of d-dimer, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, white blood cell count, and pulmonary pathological changes at the time of admission. Therefore, the changes of the above-mentioned indicators over time and their dynamic associations with the prognosis are still unclear; (2) The relatively small sample size, which only includes inpatients from a single hospital and excludes mild outpatient cases, limits research on different subgroups (for example, there are only 8 cases with fibrosis); (3) The increase in d-dimer level may be related to other potential comorbidities, and this factor needs to be further considered in the analysis; (4) The research cohort was limited to the Chinese population. Therefore, when generalizing the research results to other ethnic or racial groups, caution is required.

In conclusion, this study provides important references for the clinical diagnosis and prognosis assessment of patients with influenza A through an in-depth analysis of indicators such as d-dimer and lymphocyte count, especially for Asian and elderly patients. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) An increase in the d-dimer level is significantly related to an extended hospital stay and disease deterioration and can be used as an important indicator for the prognosis of patients with influenza A. Monitoring the d-dimer level at the time of admission helps to identify high-risk patients; (2) The positive correlation between d-dimer and monocyte and white blood cell counts indicates its important role in the inflammatory response. Dynamically monitoring the changes of these indicators can help clinicians adjust the treatment plan in a timely manner, especially anti - inflammatory and anti - fibrosis treatments; (3) A decrease in lymphocyte count is closely related to the occurrence of severe influenza A and can be used as an early warning indicator. In clinical practice, patients with a low lymphocyte count need to be paid special attention to prevent disease deterioration.

This study recommends using the d-dimer level in combination with lymphocyte, monocyte, and white blood cell counts, as well as imaging examinations (such as X - ray/CT) in clinical practice to more comprehensively evaluate the disease progression and treatment effect of elderly patients with influenza A. Future research should further explore the specific mechanism of d-dimer in the inflammatory response and verify the results of this study in a larger sample size and multi - ethnic population, with the hope of providing more precise guidance for the clinical management of patients with influenza A.
