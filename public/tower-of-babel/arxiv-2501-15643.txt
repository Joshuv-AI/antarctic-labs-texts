Abstract
. In recent works by L. Drewnowski and I. Labuda [ 7 ] and J. Martínez et al. [ 27 ] , non-pathological analytic P P -ideals and non-pathological F σ F_{\sigma} -ideals have been characterized and studied in terms of their representations by a sequence ( x n ) n (x_{n})_{n} in a Banach space, as 𝒞 ⁡ ( ( x n ) n ) \mathcal{C}((x_{n})_{n}) and ℬ ⁡ ( ( x n ) n ) \mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n}) . The ideal 𝒞 ⁡ ( ( x n ) n ) \mathcal{C}((x_{n})_{n}) consists of sets where the series ∑ n ∈ A x n \sum_{n\in A}x_{n} is unconditionally convergent, while ℬ ⁡ ( ( x n ) n ) \mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n}) involves weak unconditional convergence. In this paper, we further study these representations and provide effective descriptions of ℬ \mathcal{B} - and 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -ideals in the universal spaces C ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) C([0,1]) and C ⁡ ( 2 ℕ ) C(2^{\mathbb{N}}) , addressing a question posed by Borodulin-Nadzieja et al. [ 4 ] . A key aspect of our study is the central role of the space c 0 c_{0} in these representations. We focus particularly on ℬ \mathcal{B} -representations in spaces containing many copies of c 0 c_{0} , such as c 0 c_{0} -saturated spaces of continuous functions. A central tool in our analysis is the concept of c c -coloring ideals, which arise from homogeneous sets of continuous colorings. These ideals, generated by homogeneous sets of 2-colorings, exhibit a rich combinatorial structure. Among our results, we prove that for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , the random d d -homogeneous ideal is pathological, we construct hereditarily non-pathological universal c c -coloring ideals, and we show that every ℬ \mathcal{B} -ideal represented in C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) , for K K countable, contains a c c -coloring ideal. Furthermore, by leveraging c c -coloring ideals, we provide examples of ℬ \mathcal{B} -ideals that are not ℬ \mathcal{B} -representable in c 0 c_{0} . These findings highlight the interplay between combinatorial properties of ideals and their representations in Banach spaces.

Introduction

(Set-theoretic) Ideals of sets are a way to
describe “smallness” or “negligibility” of subsets in mathematical analysis.
Classical examples include the
Fréchet ideal
of finite sets
\mathrm{FIN}
and the ideal of measure-zero sets in a measure space. Definable, in particular, analytic ideals have been vastly investigated (see for example,
[
10
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
35
]
,
[
36
]
). In this paper we will focus on ideals over a countable set
X
, and of simple definition, that is,
F_{\sigma}
-ideals –as subsets of
\{0,1\}^{X}
via characteristic functions–. The first example of them are the summable ideals
\fin(\mu)
, collections of finitely
\mu
-measured subsets of
X
for some total (
\sigma
-additive) measure
\mu
on
X
. Although not all
F_{\sigma}
-ideals are summable ideals, they are structurally similar, as it was proved by K. Mazur
[
29
]
that every
F_{\sigma}
-ideal is of the form
\fin(\varphi)
for some total lower semicontinuous
submeasure

\varphi
on
X
. So, the simplest ones are those defined from measures, then the ones that use lower semicontinuous submeasures that are supremum of measures, called
non-pathological
, and finally the pathological ones.

The second sort of ideals we are interested in are the
P
-ideals, again somehow extending the summable ones. Recall that a
P
-ideal is defined as an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
\mathbb{N}
such that for every sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{I}
such that
A_{n}\setminus A
is finite for all
n
. Again, the simplest
P
-ideals are the summable ones, and, in general, they are a kind of summable: S. Solecki
[
35
]
demonstrated that every analytic
P
-ideal can be expressed as
\mathrm{Exh}(\varphi):=\{{A\subseteq X}\,:\,{\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(A\setminus\{0,1,\dots,n\})=0}\}
for some lower semicontinuous submeasure
\varphi
on
X
. Once more, we have the same simple classification of analytic
P
-ideals: the summable ones
\mathrm{Exh}(\mu)=\fin(\mu)
for a measure
\mu
, the non-pathological ones,
\mathrm{Exh}(\mu)
for a non-pathological submeasure
\mu
, and finally the pathological ones.

In recent years, some interesting ideals on
\mathbb{N}
related to series in Banach spaces have been studied
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
]
. Given a sequence
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{n})_{n}
in a Banach space, L. Drewnowski and I. Labuda
[
7
]
defined an ideal on

as follows:

\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}}):=\left\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\sum_{n\in A}x_{n}\text{ is unconditionally convergent}\right\},

where the sum
\sum_{n\in A}x_{n}
is

when it converges under every permutation of the index set. As a consequence of a classical Bessaga-Pełczyński’s theorem
[
3

, Drewnowski and Labuda showed that a Banach space does not contain an isomorphic copy of
c_{0}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})
is
F_{\sigma}
(as a subset of
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
via characteristic functions) for every
{\mathbf{x}}
. Later, P. Borodulin-Nadzieja, B. Farkas, and G. Plebanek conducted a thorough analysis of the ideals
\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})
in
[
4
]
and established a tight connection with the well-known class of analytic P-ideals. The ideal
\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})
can be naturally interpreted as the generalization of summable ideals, a prototype being
\mathcal{I}_{1/n}
, which consists of all
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty
; it plays a central role in the classification of ideals on countable sets.

In the aforementioned work
[
7
]
, the authors introduced a new family of ideals associated with a different notion of summability in Banach spaces:

\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{x}}):=\left\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\sum_{n\in A}x_{n}\text{ is weakly unconditionally convergent}\right\}.

We recall that
\sum_{n\in A}x_{n}
is
weakly unconditionally convergent
(or
perfectly bounded
) when the numerical series
\sum_{n\in A}x^{*}(x_{n})
is unconditionally convergent for every
x^{*}\in X^{*}
. It is well-known that this is equivalent to saying that
\sup_{F\subseteq A\text{ finite}}\left\|\sum_{n\in F}x_{n}\right\|
is finite. Observe that
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x})

[
7
]
it is shown that a Banach space does not contain an isomorphic copy of
c_{0}
exactly when
\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{x}})
for every
{\mathbf{x}}
. Following the work developed in
[
4
]
, Martínez et al.
[
27
]
characterized the ideals
\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{x}})
as the
non-pathological

F_{\sigma}
ideals, that is to say, ideals of the form
\fin(\varphi):=\{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}:\varphi(A)<\infty\}
for
\varphi
a non-pathological lower semicontinuous submeasure on
\mathbb{N}
(i.e., a lscsm which is a supremum of measures).
However, this characterizations are somewhat inefficient as they are realized in the universal space
\ell_{\infty}
. In the Section
4

\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
-ideals in the universal spaces

and

, addressing a question posed in
[
4

The representation of ideals on
C[0,1]
that we present has the advantage of being done in a Polish space. This opens up the possibility of properly analyze classes of ideals from a descriptive set-theoretic perspective, thereby extending the study of the complexity of the codes of families of
F_{\sigma}
ideals (
[
17

18

) to classes of non-pathological ideals.

In general, it is natural to investigate the consequences on ideals that can be
\mathcal{B}
- or
\mathcal{C}
-represented in a particular class of Banach spaces. One example of them is the result by Drewnowski and Labuda of
\mathcal{B}
-representation in spaces without copies of

, and conversely, P. Borodulin-Nadzieja, B. Farkas, and G. Plebanek showed in

that the only

\mathcal{C}
-representable in

are the summable ones. Similarly, ideals that are

-representable in a finite-dimensional space are also summable.

Analogous to the representation of non-pathological

-ideals as
\mathrm{Fin}(\varphi)

\mathcal{B}
-representation is also non-unique. It is straightforward to observe that any
\mathcal{B}
-ideal can be represented using a 1-unconditional basis (see Proposition

.
More generally, when the underlying space satisfies a slightly weaker version of this identity, specifically possessing
non-trivial cotype

q\geq 2
, we can prove that the
\mathcal{B}
-ideal
\mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n})
is included in the summable ideal
\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|^{q})_{n})
. However, it remains an open question whether one can choose the
\mathcal{B}
-representation such that this summable ideal is non-trivial.

A classical reformulation of the property of having non-trivial cotype is that
c_{0}
cannot be
finitely representable
, highlighting once again the importance of the space
c_{0}
in the theory of
\mathcal{B}
-representation.

As every ideal
\mathcal{B}
-representable in a space without isomorphic copies of
c_{0}
is automatically a
P
-ideal, it is natural to closely study the relationship between
\mathcal{B}
-representation and the presence of
c_{0}
in a space to understand, in particular,
\mathcal{B}
-representable non-
P
-ideals. Recall that an ideal
\mathcal{I}
is

when every infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
has a further infinite subset belonging to
\mathcal{I}
.
We prove in Theorem
3.2
that a
\mathcal{B}
-ideal is tall exactly when it can be
\mathcal{B}
-represented by a seminormalized unconditional basis
(x_{n})_{n}
that is sequentially saturated by equivalent copies of the unit basis of
c_{0}
; that is, every subsequence of
(x_{n})_{n}
has a further subsequence
(y_{n})_{n}
such that
\left\|\sum_{n}a_{n}y_{n}\right\|\approx\max_{n}|a_{n}|
. We do not know if this can be strengthened to assert that tall
\mathcal{B}
-ideals can be
\mathcal{B}
-represented in spaces that are saturated by isomorphic copies of

, i.e. such that every infinite-dimensional subspace has a subspace isomorphic to

.

A representative family of
c_{0}
-saturated spaces consists of the spaces of continuous functions on a countably infinite compact space, which will be the focus of Subsection
6.3

c-coloring ideals

These ideals are generated by the homogeneous sets of a given 2-coloring of
[\mathbb{N}]^{2}
, or more generally, by a

on
\mathbb{N}
—a natural generalization introduced by C. St. J. A. Nash-Williams
[
31

for the families

that every ideal
\mathcal{B}
-represented in some

-coloring ideal. Moreover, if
K
has finite Cantor-Bendixson rank, it contains a
c
-coloring ideal generated by a coloring of
[\mathbb{N}]^{2}

, we provide an example of a
\mathcal{B}
-ideal that is not
\mathcal{B}
-representable in

.

The study of

-coloring ideals, presented in Section

-coloring ideals are particularly interesting for two main reasons.

First, they provide a natural source of examples of

ideals that can exhibit pathological behavior. For instance, based on the work in
[
27
]
on Mazur’s pathological ideal, we demonstrate that the ideals of cliques and anticliques in the random

Second, the techniques employed in their study are of significant interest. In particular, we draw on ideas from measure concentration to analyze certain hypergraphs and on the work of F. Galvin
[

and D. C. Devlin
[
6

in Ramsey theory for canonical colorings of

Finally, we employ another combinatorial tool—Dilworth’s theorem—to construct examples of

3.
Tallness,

-saturation and summability

In this section we explore the relationship between the tallness of a
\mathcal{B}
-ideal and the sequential
c_{0}
-saturation of the sequence that represents it. This relationship is established in Theorem
3.2
, whose proof employs standard techniques from Banach space theory. The theorem highlights the central role of the space
c_{0}
in the
\mathcal{B}
-representation of ideals. We demonstrate that whenever an ideal
\mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n})
is tall and represented by a seminormalized sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
, then this sequence must be saturated by sequences equivalent to the unit basis of
c_{0}
. It is natural to then ask if the space spanned by
(x_{n})_{n}
is saturated by isomorphic copies of
c_{0}
. In Proposition
3.4
, we show that a natural class of spaces
\mathcal{I}(X,\varphi)
, with

not containing copies of
c_{0}
, contradicts this. Specifically, these examples can be
\mathcal{B}
-represented in a space that does not contain copies of
c_{0}
and consequently are simultaneously
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
-represented by the same sequence.

Interestingly, if we strengthen the hypothesis from not containing “full” isomorphic copies of
c_{0}
to not containing “asymptotic” finite-dimensional copies of

, we find that the corresponding ideal
\mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n})
satisfies the inclusion

\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|)_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|^{q})_{n}),

for some
2\leq q<\infty
.
The proof of this extension theorem relies on the local theory of Banach spaces, more precisely on the notion of cotype of a Banach space, which was primarily developed by B. Maurey and G. Pisier
[
28
]
. A particular case of independent interest is the
\mathcal{B}
-representability in a Hilbert space.

The following is a classical characterization that uses the uniform boundedness principle.

Proposition 3.1
.

A series
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x_{n}
in a Banach space
X
is weakly unconditionally convergent exactly when the numerical series
\sum_{n}x^{*}(x_{n})
converge unconditionally for every

. Consequently, if the ideal
\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{x}})
is tall, then the sequence
{\mathbf{x}}
is weakly null. ∎

Recall that an ideal of the form
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})
is tall if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is norm-null (i.e. norm-converging to zero). Moreover, if
c_{0}
does not embed in

for every sequence, hence we have the same characterization of tallness. However, using the well-known fact that weakly unconditional bases are precisely the sequences equivalent to the unit basis of
c_{0}
, we can show a general characterization which explicitly takes

into account.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{n})_{n}

(1)

\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{x}})
is tall.

(2)

Every subsequence of
{\mathbf{x}}

Consequently,

(3)

\mathbf{x}

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)

(1)

(iii)

3.1.
\mathcal{B}
-representability vs summability

R. Filipów and J. Tryba recently proved in

The extension of ideals to summable ones has led to intriguing characterizations, including connections to Riemann summability (see
[

[

Proof.

]
we obtain that
\mathcal{B}(\bf x)=\mathcal{C}(\bf x)
. This can be easily seen directly: without loss of generality, we assume that
X=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Assume that
M\notin\mathcal{C}(\bf x)
for some sequence
\bf x
in
X
, does not converge unconditionally, i.e. there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
F\subseteq M
finite there is
G
finite and disjoint from

such that
\left\|\sum_{n\in G}x_{n}\right\|\geq\varepsilon
. By recursion we can construct a sequence of finite subsets
G_{0}<G_{1}<\cdots
such that
\left\|\sum_{n\in G_{k}}x_{n}\right\|\geq\varepsilon
for all
k
. For each

there is a coordinate

such that
|\sum_{n\in G_{k}}x_{n}(j)|\geq\varepsilon/N
, and passing to a subsequence we can assume that

is the same for all

we can now find

and a sign

\theta_{k}\sum_{n\in G_{k}^{\prime}}x_{n}(j)\geq\varepsilon/2N
. Again passing to a subsequence we can assume that

for all

\sup\left\{{\left\|\sum_{n\in F}x_{n}\right\|}\,:\,{\text{$F\subseteq M$ finite}}\right\}\geq\left\|\sum_{n\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}G_{k}^{\prime}}x_{n}\right\|\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{n\in G_{k}^{\prime}}x_{n}(j)\geq\frac{k\varepsilon}{2N}.

Since

Now, since

is finite dimensional, a series is unconditionally convergent exactly when it is absolutely convergent, that is,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|)_{n})
.

We have mentioned before that every
\mathcal{B}
-ideal can be represented by an unconditional Schauder basis (see Proposition
2.5
). If this can be done in a Hilbert space, we have the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

An ideal can be
\mathcal{B}
-represented in a Hilbert space by an unconditional basic sequence exactly when it is summable.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{B}(\bf x)
with
\bf x
an unconditional basic sequence in a Hilbert space
H
. It is well known –see after this proof– that unconditional bases of Hilbert space are equivalent to an orthogonal basis. Hence, there is some constant
K\geq 1
such that
(1/K)\sum_{n\in A}\|x_{n}\|^{2}\leq\|\sum_{n\in A}x_{n}\|^{2}\leq K\sum_{n\in A}\|x_{n}\|^{2}
for every
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. This means that
\mathcal{B}({\bf x})=\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|^{2})_{n})
.
∎

The proof of the uniqueness of unconditional basic sequences in a Hilbert space follows directly from the
generalized parallelogram identity
: for every sequence
(y_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
in a Hilbert space we have that

(3)

\mathbb{E}_{(\theta_{k})_{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\!\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}y_{k}\right\|^{2}\right):=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{(\theta_{k})_{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}y_{k}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|y_{k}\|^{2},

that is, the average
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\|\sum_{k}\theta_{k}y_{k}\|^{2}
of the square of the norm of perturbation by signs is the sum of the square of the norms. Hence, if
(x_{k})_{k}
is a
C
-unconditional basic sequence, then for every sequence of scalars
(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
we have that

C^{-2}\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}x_{k}\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{(\theta_{k})_{k}}\left(\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}a_{k}x_{k}\|^{2}\right)\leq{C}^{2}\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}x_{k}\|^{2}
, and since
\mathbb{E}_{(\theta_{k})_{k}}\left(\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}a_{k}x_{k}\|^{2}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\|x_{k}\|^{2}
, we conclude that
(x_{k})_{k}
is
C
-equivalent to an orthogonal sequence.

It is natural to ask for properties similar the generalized parallelogram identity in a given Banach space. Recall that a Banach space
X
has
cotype

1\leq q\leq\infty
if there is a constant

such that for every finite sequence
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
one has that

(4)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|x_{k}\|^{q}\leq C\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}x_{k}\right\|^{q}\right),

where when for
q=\infty
the previous inequality has to be interpreted as
\max_{k=1}^{n}\|x_{k}\|\leq C\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}x_{k}\|
. This means, by a simple use of the triangle inequality, that every space has cotype
\infty

are always at least 2.
A Banach space

has
non trivial cotype
when it has cotype
q
for some

has non trivial cotype
2\leq q<\infty
when there is a constant

such that for every finite sequence
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
in
X
such that

(5)

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|x_{k}\|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}x_{k}\right\|\right).

We have the following.

Proposition 3.7
.

For every sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n}
in a space
X
with non-trivial cotype
q
one has that

\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|)_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\mathrm{Sum}((\|x_{n}\|^{q})_{n}).

Proof.

The first inclusion follows trivially from the triangle inequality.
Suppose now that
A\in\mathcal{B}(\bf x)
, that is
\sup_{F\in[A]^{<\infty}}\|\sum_{k\in F}x_{k}\|=K<\infty
. Then, by the
q
-cotype of
X
, for every finite set
F\subseteq A
one
that

\left(\sum_{k\in F}\|x_{k}\|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\{-1,1\}^{F}}\left\|\sum_{k\in F}\theta_{k}x_{k}\right\|\leq 2C\mathbb{E}_{G\subseteq F}\left\|\sum_{k\in G}x_{k}\right\|\leq 2CK,

where we have used that it follows from the triangle inequality that

(6)

\mathbb{E}_{(\theta_{k})_{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}y_{k}\right\|\right)\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}\left(\left\|\sum_{k\in A}y_{k}\right\|\right).

This implies that
\sum_{k\in A}\|x_{k}\|^{q}<\infty
, that is

.

4.

In this section we see how to represent effectively

-ideals in a space of continuous functions. The effectiveness will come from the fact that we will use natural Schauder bases of certain spaces of continuous functions.

Definition 4.1

(Evaluation sequence)
.

For
K\subseteq[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
compact we define the corresponding evaluation sequence

In particular, when

Let

For the next result, let
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{n})_{n}

The following three conditions are equivalent, and they hold if

We see the equivalence between those three conditions.

Proposition 4.3

Remark 4.6

5.
Coloring ideals

Given an ideal
\mathcal{I}
its characteristic function
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}
can be seen as a coloring
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to 2
. If in addition
\mathcal{I}
is tall, then
\mathcal{I}\cap[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
coincides with the collection of
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}
-homogeneous sets. Recall that
M\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
is
c
-homogeneous for a given finite coloring
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to r\in\mathbb{N}
, when
c
is constant on the cube
[M]^{\omega}
. In this section we study what we call
coloring ideals
. The
continuous
coloring ideals are especially relevant as they provide new examples of pathological and non-pathological
F_{\sigma}
ideals, and exhibit interesting connections with graph theory.

Definition 5.1

(Homogeneous sets, coloring ideal)
.

Let
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to r\in\mathbb{N}
. For
i<r
we define
\Hom_{i}(c)
as the collection of
c
-homogeneous subsets of
\mathbb{N}
with constant value
i
. Let
\langle\Hom(c)\rangle
be the ideal generated by
\Hom(c):=\bigcup_{i<r}\Hom_{i}(c)
.
A coloring ideal is an ideal of the form
\langle\Hom(c)\rangle
for some coloring
c
.

Observe that when
c
is an analytic coloring (i.e.
c^{-1}(i)
is an analytic set), then it follows from the Ellentuck-Silver Theorem that
\langle\Hom(c)\rangle
is a tall ideal. Reciprocally, as we have observed above, if
\mathcal{I}
is a tall ideal, then
\mathcal{I}=\langle\Hom(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}})\rangle
is a coloring ideal, and its complexity obviously coincides with that of the coloring
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

One particular case is that of
open
colorings, those
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to 2
where
c^{-1}(1)
is an open set. These colorings are always induced by families of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. More precisely, let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all finite subsets
s
of
\mathbb{N}
such that
\langle s\rangle:=\{{M\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}}\,:\,{s\sqsubset M}\}\subseteq c^{-1}(1)
. Then
c(M)=1
if and only if
M
has an initial segment in
\mathcal{F}
, so the Ramsey property of the coloring
c
is in this case equivalent to the following result. We denote by
K\upharpoonright A:=K\cap\mathcal{P}(A)
the
restriction
of a set
K\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
to a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 5.2

(Galvin
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
. For every infinite
M\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, there is an infinite
N\subseteq M
such that one of the following holds:

(0)

\mathcal{F}\upharpoonright N=\emptyset
,

(1)

For all
P\in[N]^{\omega}
there is
s\in\mathcal{F}
such that
s\sqsubset P
.

For a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
let
c_{\mathcal{F}}:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to 2
be the corresponding Galvin coloring, that is, given by
c_{\mathcal{F}}(M)=1
exactly when
M
has an initial segment in
\mathcal{F}
. Thus every open coloring is of the form
c_{\mathcal{F}}
for some family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\fin
, and
\Hom_{i}(c_{\mathcal{F}})
,

, is the set of all
N\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
satisfying

in Galvin’s Theorem. It is easy to see that, if
\mathcal{F}
is a compact family (as a subset of
2^{\mathbb{N}}
), each

is closed in
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. This gives a characterization of tall
F_{\sigma}
ideals.

Proposition 5.3
.

[
18
]
Every tall
F_{\sigma}
ideal is an open coloring ideal.

Proof.

Recall first that for any
F_{\sigma}
ideal
\mathcal{I}
(containing
\fin
) there is a closed hereditary set
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{I}=\langle\mathcal{K}\rangle
. In fact, we assume that
\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{F}_{n}
where each
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is closed and hereditary and
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
for all
n
. Now consider

\mathcal{K}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{A\setminus n:\;A\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\}.

Then
\mathcal{K}
is closed and hereditary. Clearly
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
and given
A\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
since
A=(A\setminus n)\cup(A\cap n)
and
\langle\mathcal{K}\rangle
contains all
\{n\}
,
A\in\langle\mathcal{K}\rangle
.

Define
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}=\fin\setminus\mathcal{K}=\{s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}:s\not\in\mathcal{K}\}
. We claim that
\Hom_{1}(c_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}})=\emptyset
.
Indeed assume for a contradiction that
Y\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
satisfies the second condition in the conclusion of Galvin’s theorem. Since
\mathcal{K}
is tall there is an infinite

such that
Z\in\mathcal{K}
. As
Y
satisfies the second condition with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}
, there is
s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}
such that
s\sqsubset Z
, but since
\mathcal{K}
is hereditary we have

and this contradicts the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

It remains to check that
\mathcal{K}\cap[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}=\Hom_{0}(c_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}})
.
Clearly
\subseteq
holds.
Conversely, let
X\not\in\mathcal{K}
be infinite. Since
\mathcal{K}
is hereditary and closed there must be some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X\cap n\not\in\mathcal{K}
. Thus
X\cap n\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}
and we get
X\not\in\Hom_{0}(c_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}})
.
∎

In the case of
continuous
colorings
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to r
, i.e., colorings where all colors are open, these are induced by colorings of
fronts
on
\mathbb{N}
, a notion introduced by Nash-Williams to characterize families of finite sets with the Ramsey property. Recall that a family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of some infinite set

is a front on
M
if it is thin—no element of
\mathcal{F}
is a proper initial segment of another—and satisfies that every infinite subset of
M
has a necessarily unique initial segment that belongs to
\mathcal{F}
.
Now, given such a coloring, define for each color
i<r
, the family
\mathcal{F}_{i}
of all finite subsets
s\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\langle s\rangle\subseteq c^{-1}(i)
. Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\sqsubseteq-\min}
the minimal elements of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
with respect to initial segments, and define

\mathcal{G}:=\bigcup_{i<r}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\sqsubseteq-\min},

along with a coloring
c^{*}:\mathcal{G}\to r
defined by
c^{*}(s)=i
if
s\in\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\sqsubseteq-\min}
. It is easily seen that
\mathcal{G}
is a front on
\mathbb{N}
and that
c(M)=c^{*}(s_{M})
, where
s_{M}
is the unique initial segment of
M
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Conversely, every coloring
{c}:\mathcal{G}\to r
of a front
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathbb{N}
induces a continuous coloring
{c}_{*}
on
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
in the same way. These are called
front (or continuous) colorings
, and we define
\hom_{i}({c})
as the set of all
{c}
-homogeneous sets of color
i
, i.e., those
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
{c}
is constant with value
i
on

\mathcal{G}\restriction A:=\mathcal{G}\cap[A]^{<\omega}.

Those sets
A
with
\mathcal{G}\restriction A=\emptyset
are considered
{c}
-homogeneous for all colors, so that
\hom_{i}({c})
is an hereditary and compact family. This is also logically consistent, since all elements of
\mathcal{G}\upharpoonright A
have color
i
.

It is important to observe that, in contrast to
\Hom_{i}(c)
,
\hom_{i}({c})
may include both finite and infinite sets. It follows that each
\hom_{i}({c})
is hereditary and a compact subset of
2^{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore,
\langle\hom({c})\rangle
, the ideal generated by
\bigcup_{i<r}\hom_{i}({c})
, is
F_{\sigma}
. Recall that
\langle\hom({c})\rangle
contains, by our definition of ideal, all finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 5.4

(c-coloring ideal)
.

An ideal is a continuous coloring ideal (c-coloring ideal in short) when it is of the form
\langle\Hom(c)\rangle
for some continuous coloring
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to r\in\mathbb{N}
or, equivalently, of the form
\langle\hom({c})\rangle
for some
{c}:\mathcal{F}\to r
defined on a front
\mathcal{F}
.

A coloring
{c}:\mathcal{F}\to r
on some family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of a set
X
induces
r
hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{0}({c}),\dots,\mathcal{H}_{r-1}({c})
over
X
, where the hyperedges of
\mathcal{H}_{i}({c})
are defined as the sets of elements in
\mathcal{F}
that are colored by
{c}
with color
i
. In particular, when the family is a subset of
[X]^{d}
, the associated hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{i}
are
d
-uniform. Furthermore, when
\mathcal{F}=[\mathbb{N}]^{d}
and
r=2
, the hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
are complements of one another.

Within this framework, the
c
-coloring ideal
\langle\hom({c})\rangle
for a 2-coloring coincides with the ideal on the vertex set of the hypergraph generated by the cliques and anticliques of the hyperedges.
More generally, for an arbitrary
r
-coloring, the corresponding
c
-coloring ideal is the ideal generated by the complete sub-hypergraphs of each hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
This interpretation of
c
-coloring ideals will be useful for establishing connections between structural properties of these ideals and those of hypergraphs.

5.1.
Examples of pathological c-coloring ideals

A positive aspect of c-coloring ideals is their effective tallness, as highlighted in
[
18
]
. However, despite their utility, c-coloring ideals can exhibit pathological behavior. These ideals, rooted in the Mazur ideal, are intricately linked to Ramsey theory. Furthermore, their interplay with the concentration of measure phenomenon introduces an additional layer of complexity. As a significant consequence of these interactions, we establish that multidimensional random ideals inherently exhibit pathological behavior.

Definition 5.5

(Super-pathology)
.

We say that an ideal is super-pathological if every non-trivial
F_{\sigma}
ideal that covers it is pathological.

Mazur’s ideal
\mathcal{M}

[
29
]
was the first example of an
F_{\sigma}
-ideal not extended by a summable one. Later, J. Martínez, D. Meza-Alcántara and C. Uzcátegui proved in
[
27
]
that in fact
\mathcal{M}
is the first example of a super-pathological
F_{\sigma}
-ideal. The proof of this uses a simple but fundamental property of measures in relation to coverings, explicitly defined by the
Kelley’s covering number
. This number
\delta(X,\mathcal{S})
is defined for a covering
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
of a finite set
X
as

\delta(X,\mathcal{S}):=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{S}}\min_{x\in X}\#\{{S\in\mathcal{S}}\,:\,{x\in S}\}.

It is proved in
[
27
, Lemma 3.7]
(see also
[
26
, Corollary 6]
) that if
\mu
is a measure on a finite set
X
then every covering
\mathcal{S}
of
X
has some element
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\mu(S)\geq\delta(X,\mathcal{S})\cdot\mu(X)
. In contrast, there are submeasures that do not satisfy this. The failure of Mazur’s submeasure to follow this principle is what makes
\mathcal{M}
pathological. We shall refine Mazur’s example to construct new super-pathological ideals, among them c-coloring ones.

Definition 5.6

(Cardinal intervals of sets)
.

Let
X
be a finite set. For
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq 1
we define the cardinal interval
X^{[\alpha,\beta]}:=\{{A\subseteq X}\,:\,{\alpha\#X\leq\#A\leq\beta\#X}\}
. A symmetric cardinal interval is a cardinal interval of the form
X^{[(1-\delta)/p,(1+\delta)/p]}
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
and some
0\leq\delta\leq 1
.

Roughly speaking, the symmetric cardinal interval
X^{[(1-\delta)/p,(1+\delta)/p]}
is the
\delta/p
-fattening of
[X]^{n/p}
by the
normalized Hamming distance

d_{X}
. Recall that this metric counts the (normalized) cardinality of the symmetric difference of two subsets
A,B
of
X
, that is,
d_{X}(A,B):=\#(A\triangle B)/\#X
.

For each
x\in X
, define

\widehat{x}:=\widehat{x}^{X}:=\{A\in X^{[\alpha,\beta]}:x\notin A\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{C}_{X}:=\{\widehat{x}\}_{x\in X}
is a covering of
X^{[\alpha,\beta]}
exactly when
\beta<1
, which we assume from now on. Its
Kelley’s covering number

\delta(X^{[\alpha,\beta]},\mathcal{C}_{X})
is

(11)

\delta(X^{[\alpha,\beta]},\mathcal{C}_{X})=\frac{\min_{A\in X^{[\alpha,\beta]}}\#\{{x\in X}\,:\,{A\in\widehat{x}}\}}{\lvert\mathcal{C}_{X}\rvert}=\frac{\min_{A\in X^{[\alpha,\beta]}}\#(X\setminus A)}{\#X}\geq 1-\beta.

Define the covering submeasure for
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
as

(12)

\psi_{X}(\mathcal{A}):=\min\left\{\#F:F\subseteq X\text{ and }\mathcal{A}\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in F}\widehat{x}\right\}.

For example, if
\mathcal{A}
covers
X
, then
\psi_{X}(\mathcal{A})>1
. More generally, it is easy to see that

\psi_{X}(\mathcal{A})=\min\{{r\in\mathbb{N}}\,:\,{\mathcal{A}\text{ is not an $r$-covering}}\},

where
\mathcal{A}
is an
r
-covering if for every
s\in[X]^{r}
there exists some
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
s\subseteq A
.

Next, we freely amalgamate all these submeasures. Suppose now that
(X_{n})_{n}
is an unbounded sequence of disjoint finite sets,
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta<1
, and let
\mathfrak{X}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{X}_{n}
, where each
\mathfrak{X}_{n}:=X_{n}^{[\alpha,\beta]}
. On
\mathfrak{X}
we define the submeasure

\psi(\mathcal{A}):=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\psi_{X_{n}}(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{X}_{n}).

In this setting we can prove the super-pathology of
\fin(\psi)
using the following result:

Theorem 5.7

(
[
27
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

Let
\psi
be a lscsm on a countable set
\mathfrak{X}
such that there is
M>0
and a partition
\{\mathfrak{X}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
\mathfrak{X}
in finite sets satisfying:

•

The family
\mathcal{C}:=\{\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{X}:\psi(\mathcal{A})\leq M\}
covers
\mathfrak{X}
.

•

\sup_{n}\psi(\mathfrak{X}_{n})=\infty
.

•

\psi(\mathcal{A})=\sup_{n}\psi(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{X}_{n})
for all
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{X}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
be a subfamily of
\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{X}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{n}
covers

Proposition 5.11

Let

Suppose

In this section we analyze tall

6.1.
The block sequence coloring and
c_{0}

Recall that a
basic sequence
in a Banach space is a sequence which is a (Schauder) basis of the closed subspace it generates. Throughout this part we assume that every basic sequence is seminormalized. This implies, in particular, that the sequence of
coordinate functionals

\mathbf{f}=(f_{n})_{n}
, which map each
x\in X
to its
n
-th coefficient in terms of the basis, is uniformly bounded. Given
x\in X
, its
support
(with respect to the basis) is the set
\supp x:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:f_{n}(x)\neq 0\}
. Finally, a
block subsequence
of
\mathbf{e}
is a finitely supported sequence
\mathbf{x}
satisfying
\max\supp x_{n}<\min\supp x_{n+1}
for all
n
. Vectors with finite support are clearly dense in
X
, thus in order to study ideals of the form
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x})
we can always assume that every
x_{n}
has finite support. Note that in
C(K)
spaces we can also assume that the
x_{n}
’s are non-negative functions by Proposition
4.3
, but this is not the case in arbitrary function spaces, as shown by Example
4.4
.

Definition 6.1

(The block sequence coloring)
.

Let
\mathbf{e}
be a basis for a Banach space
X
and let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n}
be a finitely supported sequence in
X
. We define the block sequence (
\mathfrak{bs}
) coloring (with respect to
\mathbf{f}
)
\mathfrak{bs}=\mathfrak{bs}_{\mathbf{x}}:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to\{0,1\}
for
m<n
by
\mathfrak{bs}(\{m,n\}_{<})=1
exactly when

(17)

\max\supp x_{m}\leq\max\supp x_{n}
and for all
k\leq\max\supp x_{m}
one has that
|f_{k}^{*}(x_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{k+1}\,2^{m+1}}
.

Clearly
\mathfrak{bs}
is a
1
-comparability coloring. Fix now a Banach space
X
with a normalized basis
\mathbf{e}
and a finitely supported sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n}
in
X
. For each
F\subseteq\mathbb{N}
define
(x_{n}^{F})_{n\in F}
by

(18)

x_{n}^{F}:=\sum_{k\in\supp x_{n}\setminus[0,\max_{m\in F\cap n}\max\supp x_{m}]}f_{k}(x_{n})e_{k}.

Observe that
(x_{n}^{F})_{n\in F,x_{n}^{F}\neq 0}
is a block sequence.

For 1-homogeneous sets
H
of
\mathfrak{bs}
the sequence
(x_{n}^{H})_{n\in H}
are equivalent:

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose that
H\in\hom_{1}(\mathfrak{bs})
. Then for every sequence of scalars
(a_{n})_{n\in H}
with at most finitely many non-zero we have

(19)

\left|\left\|\sum_{n\in H}a_{n}x_{n}\right\|-\left\|\sum_{n\in H}a_{n}x_{n}^{H}\right\|\right|\leq\frac{\max_{n\in H}|a_{n}|}{2^{\min H}}.

Consequently, if
H
is infinite,
\mathcal{B}((x_{n})_{n\in H})=\mathcal{B}((x_{n}^{H})_{n\in H})
and, if in addition
(x_{n})_{n\in H}
is a basic sequence, then
(x_{n})_{n\in H}
and
(x_{n}^{H})_{n\in H}
are equivalent.

Proof.

Enumerate
H=\{n_{i}\}_{i}
in an increasing way. Then
(\max\supp x_{n_{i}})_{i}
is increasing and for every
i
one has that

\displaystyle\|x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i+1}}^{H}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k\in\supp x_{n_{i+1}}\cap[0,\max\supp x_{n_{i}}]}f_{k}(x_{n_{i+1}})e_{k}\right\|\leq\sum_{k\in\supp x_{n_{i+1}}\cap[0,\max\supp x_{n_{i}}]}|f_{k}(x_{n_{i+1}})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\supp x_{n_{i+1}}\cap[0,\max\supp x_{n_{i}}]}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}\,2^{n_{i}+1}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n_{i}+1}},

hence

\left|\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{n_{i}}x_{n_{i}}\right\|-\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{n_{i}}x_{n_{i}}^{H}\right\|\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|a_{n_{i}}|\left\|x_{n_{i}}-x_{n_{i}}^{H}\right\|=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{n_{i}}|\left\|x_{n_{i}}-x_{n_{i}}^{H}\right\|\leq\max_{i}|a_{n_{i}}|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n_{i}+1}}\leq\frac{\max_{n\in H}|a_{n}|}{2^{\min H}}.

∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}
is weakly null. Then

\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{bs}_{\mathbf{x}})\subseteq\fin
, and consequently
\mathfrak{bs}_{\mathbf{x}}
is a 0-asymmetric 1-comparability coloring and

\langle\hom(\mathfrak{bs}_{\mathbf{x}})\rangle=\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{p}^{\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{bs}_{\mathbf{x}})}).

Proof.

Set

Remark 6.10
.

In contrast with what we have seen, there is a important difference between the global and local version of the problem of finding colorings for tall ideals. Indeed, it is shown in
[
23
]
that every tall
F_{\sigma}
ideal contains some restriction
\hom(c)\upharpoonright A
of a coloring
c:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
. To see this, recall that an ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
p^{+}
, if for every decreasing sequence
(A_{n})_{n}
of sets in
\mathcal{I}^{+}
, there is
A\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that
A\setminus A_{n}
is finite for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
. It is well known that every
F_{\sigma}
ideal is
p^{+}
(see
[
23
, Lemma 3.3]
for a proof).
An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
q^{+}
, if for every
A\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
and every
partition
(F_{n})_{n}
of
A
into finite sets, there is
S\in\mathcal{I}^{+}

such that
S\subseteq A
and
S\cap F_{n}
has at most one element for each
n
. Equivalently, if
c
is the coloring on
A
associated to the partition, that is,
c\{i,j\}=0
if
i,j
are in the same piece of the partition, then
\hom(c)\not\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. An ideal is selective if it is
p^{+}
and
q^{+}
(this is an equivalent formulation of this notion, see
[
37
, Lemma 7.4]
).
Analytic selective ideal have the following Ramsey property: For any partition of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
into two sets one of which is analytic, there is
A\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that
[A]^{\omega}
is a subset of one of the pieces of the partition (for a proof see
[
37
, Lemma 7.23]
). Now suppose
\mathcal{I}
is an analytic selective ideal. By using
\mathcal{I}
as one of the pieces of the partition, we conclude that there is
A\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that
[A]^{\omega}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{+}
. In particular,
\mathcal{I}
is not tall. Thus, if
\mathcal{I}
is a tall
F_{\sigma}
ideal, then it is not selective and necessarely is not
q^{+}
and from above we have a coloring

and an infinite set

such that
\hom(c)\upharpoonright A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.

\mathcal{B}
-representable in

In particular, the spaces

Let

Take a sequence

Lemma 6.11
.

\mathcal{G}
is a compact and hereditary family of finite sets and, if

is weakly null, then its Cantor-Bendixson rank is at most that of

.

Proof.

We first show that if

and

witnesses it, then

, so in particular

does not contain infinite sets. To see this write
s=\{n_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k}
in increasing order. For each

there exists

such that

but

for all

. In particular
u_{j}\neq u_{k}
for

and they are all initial segments of

, thus

.

Now fix a sequence
(s_{k})_{k}
in
\mathcal{G}
converging to some

, and let us show that

. For each

let

be a witness that

. Passing to a subsequence, we can assume that

is a
\Delta
-sequence with root

, i.e.

and
u_{k}\setminus t<u_{k+1}\setminus t
for all

. Fix

and

such that
s_{k}\cap[0,n]=A\cap[0,n]
for all

. For each such

, since

, we can find

such that

but
f_{v_{n,k}}^{*}(x_{m})=0
for

. For each

we can have either
v_{n,k}\sqsubseteq t
or

. If all

with respect to the basis

, thus contradicting the fact that

has finite support. Hence at least one

is an initial segment of

, so

witnesses

, and in particular

is finite.

Finally, for each ordinal
\alpha<\omega_{1}
denote by
\mathcal{F}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha)}
the

\mathcal{F}
and

respectively. We claim that, if

is weakly null, for each

there is

witnessing it. Fix a countable ordinal

and assume that the result holds for all smaller ones. Let
(\alpha_{k})_{k}
be a sequence satisfying

for all

if

is a successor, or increasing to

if it is limit. Pick

and a non-trivial

-sequence

with

converging to it. For each

pick

witnessing it and, passing to a subsequence, assume that
(u_{k})_{k}
is a
\Delta
-sequence with root

. The argument in the previous paragraph shows that

is a witness for

, so it remains to show that

. If this was not the case then

would be a witness of

for all but finitely many

. Since the sequence

is non-trivial, set

for each

, and find an initial segment

such that, in particular,

. Passing again to a subsequence we can assume that

for all

is weakly null. ∎

We want to use the
Ramsey property
of the family
\mathcal{G}^{\max}
of
\sqsubset
-maximal elements of
\mathcal{G}
, but there might be an infinite set

such that

. This can be solved with the help of the following notion, introduced by Pudlák and Rödl

Definition 6.12
.

Given a countable ordinal
\alpha<\omega_{1}
and
M\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, we say that a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\fin
is an
\alpha
-uniform front on
M
if
\alpha=0
and
\mathcal{A}=\{\emptyset\}
, or
\alpha>0
,
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{A}
, and for each
n\in M
the family

\mathcal{A}_{\{n\}}:=\{s\in\fin:\text{$n<s$ and $\{n\}\cup s\in\mathcal{A}$}\}

is an
\alpha_{n}
-uniform front on
M/n
, where
\alpha_{n}+1=\alpha
for all
n
if
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, or
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in M}
is a sequence increasing to
\alpha
, if it is limit. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is uniform on
M
if it is
\alpha
-uniform on
M
for some
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, in which case
\alpha
is called its uniform rank.

Uniform fronts on
M
are indeed fronts on
M
, thus they possess the Ramsey property. Conversely, every Ramsey family has a restriction that forms a uniform front. Moreover, uniformity is preserved under restrictions, and the topological closure of a uniform front coincides with its
\sqsubseteq
-closure,
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{\sqsubseteq}
. An easy inductive argument shows that the Cantor-Bendixson rank of an
\alpha
-uniform front is
\alpha+1
. Therefore, closures of uniform fronts exhaust, up to homeomorphism, all countable compact Hausdorff spaces.

A classical example of an
\omega
-uniform front is the Schreier barrier
\mathfrak{S}:=\{s\in\mathrm{Fin}:\#s=\min s+1\}
, which can be used to construct uniform fronts of arbitrarily high rank. For example, if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are
\alpha
- and
\beta
-uniform fronts on
M
, respectively, then

\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}:=\{s\cup t:\text{$s\in\mathcal{B}$, $t\in\mathcal{A}$, and $s<t$}\}

is
(\alpha+\beta)
-uniform. One can also define a product

\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}:=\{s_{1}\cup\cdots\cup s_{n}:\text{$\{s_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}$ block, and $\{\min s_{i}\}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}$}\}
.
Note also that if
\alpha<\beta
and we are given an
\alpha
-uniform front
\mathcal{A}
, then we can always find a
\beta
-uniform front
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{B}}
: indeed, just take
\gamma
such that
\alpha+\gamma=\beta
and let
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is
\gamma
-uniform. For each non-empty
s\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we denote
{}_{*}s:=s\setminus\{\min s\}
.

Proposition 6.13
.

Every compact family
\mathcal{G}
on
M
is included in the closure of a uniform front
\mathcal{A}
on
M
.

Proof.

We further claim that the uniform rank of
\mathcal{A}
can be taken equal to the rank
\rho
of the well-founded tree
(\mathcal{G},\sqsubset)
. The proof goes by induction on
\rho
. Define
\eta_{n}+1=\rho
for
n\in M
if
\rho
is a successor ordinal, or let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in M}
be a sequence increasing to
\rho
if it is limit. For each
n\in M
the rank
\rho_{n}
of the tree
(\mathcal{G}_{\{n\}},\sqsubset)
is strictly smaller than
\rho
, so by the induction hypothesis there is a
\rho_{n}
-uniform front
\mathcal{A}_{n}
on
M/n
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\{n\}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}}
. By the remark made above, replacing
\mathcal{A}_{n}
if necessary we can assume that
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
\max\{\rho_{n},\eta_{n}\}
-uniform. Let

\mathcal{A}:=\{s\in[M]^{<\omega}\setminus\{\emptyset\}:{}_{*}s\in\mathcal{A}_{\min s}\}.

It easily follows that
\mathcal{A}_{\{n\}}=\mathcal{A}_{n}
for all
n
, thus
\mathcal{A}
is a
\rho
-uniform front on

. Moreover, given
s\in\mathcal{G}
non-empty, for

we have
{}_{*}s\in\mathcal{G}_{\{n\}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}_{n}}=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\{n\}}}^{\sqsubseteq}
, thus there is

such that

, and hence

.
∎

Definition 6.14

(The bounded coloring)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a uniform front of rank at least
1
such that
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{A}
. Each element
s\in\mathcal{V}
has a unique decomposition
s=s[0]\cup s[1]
with

s[0],s[1]\in\mathcal{A}
and
s[0]<s[1]
.
We define the bounded coloring
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{b}_{\mathbf{x}}:\mathcal{V}\to\{0,1\}
by declaring that
\mathfrak{b}(s)=1
exactly when

\left\|\sum_{n\in s[0]}x_{n}^{s[0]}\right\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{n\in s[1]}x_{n}^{s[1]}\right\|.

Observe that
\mathfrak{b}
is a 0-comparability coloring.

Proposition 6.15
.

If
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x})
is tall, then
\hom_{1}(\mathfrak{bs})\cap\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{b})\subseteq\fin
.

Proof.

For suppose otherwise that
H\in\hom_{1}(\mathfrak{bs})\cap\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{b})
is infinite. Then there is a unique partition
(s_{n})_{n}
of
H
consisting of
<
-consecutive elements of
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathfrak{b}(s_{n}\cup s_{n+1})=0
for every
n
, it follows that
\|\sum_{k\in s_{n+1}}x_{k}^{s_{n+1}}\|>2\|\sum_{k\in s_{n}}x_{k}^{s_{n}}\|
. In particular
\|\sum_{k\in s_{1}}x_{k}^{s_{1}}\|>2\|\sum_{k\in s_{0}}x_{k}^{s_{0}}\|\geq 0
, so

, and by induction

\left\|\sum_{k\in s_{n+1}}x_{k}^{s_{n+1}}\right\|\geq 2^{n}C\text{ for every $n$.}

Since

, it follows from this and (

Claim 6.17.1

For every

\left\|\sum_{n\in R}x_{n}\right\|\leq 1+\sup_{s\in\mathcal{G}\upharpoonright R}\left\|\sum_{n\in s}x_{n}\right\|.

Proof of claim:

Fix
t\in\mathcal{F}
and set

Given
n\in s

, because otherwise

, so using (

\sum_{n\in R}x_{n}(t)=\sum_{n\in s}x_{n}(t)+\sum_{n\in R\setminus s}x_{n}(t)\leq\sup_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\upharpoonright R}\left\|\sum_{n\in s^{\prime}}x_{n}\right\|+\sum_{n\in R\setminus s}\sum_{u\sqsubseteq t}f_{u}^{*}(x_{n}).

To bound the second term enumerate
R\setminus s=\{n_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k}
in increasing order and fix

be the minimum

Since

Consequently, using Proposition

and the fact that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{n\in F_{1}}x_{n}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left\|\sum_{n\in s}x_{n}\right\|\leq 1+\left\|\sum_{n\in s_{1}}x_{n}\right\|\leq 1+\left\|\sum_{n\in s_{1}}x_{n}^{s_{1}}\right\|+\frac{1}{2^{\min s_{1}}}\leq 2\left\|\sum_{n\in s_{0}}x_{n}^{s_{0}}\right\|+2.

The last expression does not depend on

, so

.
∎

\mathfrak{t}
then
\hom_{(i,j)}(\mathfrak{t})\subseteq\fin
for
(i,j)\neq(1,1)
, whereas if

then
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{p}^{\hom_{(1,0)}(\mathfrak{t})})\cap\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{p}^{\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{bs})})

For the reverse inclusion, suppose that
M\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{p}^{K})

M\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{p}^{\hom_{0}(\mathfrak{bs})})=\langle\hom_{1}(\mathfrak{bs})\rangle
, so

of

for if

and this means that

Theorem 6.19

If a tall ideal can be

,

This result gives a necessary condition that aids in comprehending the following fundamental question.

Question 6.20
.

Which
\mathcal{B}
-ideals can be represented in

for a countable compactum

A question similar to the previous one could yield a negative answer when no simple characterization is possible. This might be the case here, as the Cantor-Bendixson rank of a countable compactum can take any countable ordinal value. It is a classical result that the collection of codes for countable ordinals is a co-analytic, non-Borel set.

Since all
\mathcal{B}
-ideals are represented in the Polish space

Question 6.21
.

Find the (Borel or projective) complexity of the following set:

\{{\bf g}\in C(2^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}:\mathcal{B}({\bf g})\mbox{ is representable in $C(K)$ for some countable compactum $K$}\}.

An instance of the previous question, which is of particular interest, is to determine the complexity of

\{{\bf g}\in C(2^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}:\mathcal{B}({\bf g})\mbox{ is representable in $c_{0}$}\}.

An ideal characterization of

Recall that the isomorphic classification of the spaces

and

. For more information, see
[

It follows from here that when the Cantor-Bendixson rank of

is finite, being

isomorphic to

, the ideal

, and consequently, either norm-null or equivalent to the unit basis of

. Theorem
3.2
guarantees that
\mathcal{B}({\mathbf{g}})
is tall. We have the following characterization.

.

.
