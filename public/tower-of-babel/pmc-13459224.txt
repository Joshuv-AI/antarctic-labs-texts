Indicators, frameworks, and influencing factors for evaluating the performance of mental health programs in primary health care: a scoping review
Authors: Salman Shojaei, Soad Mahfoozpour, Iravan Masoudi Asl, Somayeh Hessam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMental disorders represent a major challenge for global health systems due to their substantial disease burden. With the integration of mental health services into the primary health care system, evaluating the effectiveness of these programs is essential. This study aimed to conduct a systematic scoping review to examine the indicators, frameworks, and influencing factors related to performance evaluation of mental health programs in primary health care.Materials and methodsThis study was a systematic scoping review conducted based on the PRISMA-ScR framework. A comprehensive search was performed in international databases including PubMed and Scopus, as well as supplementary sources such as Google Scholar, national databases (Magiran and SID), and relevant institutional websites including the World Health Organization (WHO). A total of 541 initial records were identified. The search strategy used combinations of main keywords “mental health”, “primary health care”, and “performance evaluation”. In total, 49 studies were included in the final analysis.ResultsThe study identified eight categories of performance evaluation frameworks, including macro-level health system frameworks, primary care frameworks, performance management tools, specialized mental health tools, indigenous Iranian frameworks, rapid evaluation methods, hybrid frameworks, and community-based frameworks. Factors affecting performance were categorized into four dimensions: contextual-policy, organizational-managerial, human resources, and infrastructure-technology. In addition, 117 indicators were identified across six domains: efficiency, effectiveness, equity, quality and responsiveness, access, and outcomes.ConclusionThis scoping review synthesizes existing evidence on performance evaluation of mental health programs in primary health care and highlights gaps in integrated evaluation frameworks. By mapping key indicators, frameworks, and influencing factors, the findings can inform the development of more coherent evaluation approaches in different health systems, including countries such as Iran.

Introduction

Mental disorders, accounting for 13% of the global disease burden and 32.2% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), represent a major challenge to health systems worldwide [1]. This issue is more severe in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, where Iran is located. According to World Health Organization reports, over 20% of the population in this region faces mental health-related problems [2]. The Mental Health Atlas report also highlights significant gaps in mental health resources and services globally [3]. Iran’s National Health Survey indicated that approximately one-quarter of the population (23.6%) met the diagnostic criteria for at least one mental disorder over a 12-month period [4]. These disorders have been identified as the leading cause of disability among the 10–40 age group in Iran [5]. More than half of individuals with mental disorders lack access to necessary care and appropriate interventions, and over 30% of the household income of families with patients suffering from severe psychiatric disorders is spent on direct treatment costs [4].

To address mental health challenges, Iran has developed programs to integrate mental health services into the primary health care system. These programs include interventions such as screening and identification of disorders across different age groups, psychosocial empowerment, self-care during disasters and emergencies, community-based preventive programs, prevention of child and spousal abuse, prevention of addiction and substance use disorders, and suicide prevention [6]. However, significant challenges in implementing these programs underscore the critical need for systematic performance evaluation in this area.

Achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), which is included in the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), requires strengthening Primary Health Care (PHC). However, a World Health Organization report indicated that at least half of the global population still lacks access to essential health services [7]. This underscores the necessity of strengthening primary care systems and developing effective performance evaluation methods by comparing outcomes with expected targets [8]. These efforts align with international mental health objectives, such as reducing the treatment gap (mhGAP) [9] and improving access to essential mental health services [10]. This process not only assesses the efficiency and effectiveness of programs but also ensures public accountability and transparency [11].

Health system performance evaluation serves two main purposes: first, to establish an evidence-based foundation regarding performance, and second, to empower managers for evidence-based decision-making and to monitor and compare outcomes with targets for continuous improvement over time [12]. The benefits of this evaluation include health system reform, organizing efforts toward goals, encouraging policymakers to make desirable changes, strengthening the scientific basis of health policies, improving the quality of management decisions, and enhancing resource management [13].

Various conceptual frameworks have been developed to evaluate the performance of health systems [14–20], and several studies have assessed these programs [21–25]. Studies conducted in Iran have generally fallen into two categories: general health system performance evaluation [19, 26] and specific evaluation of mental health programs [18, 27, 28]. Although these studies are valuable, they have not provided a comprehensive model for evaluating the performance of mental health programs.

Internationally, frameworks such as the model by Murray and Frenk (2000) [29] and the study by Barry (2003) [30] have been proposed. Additionally, specialized tools like the WHO-AIMS for assessing mental health systems [31], and frameworks such as the Balanced Scorecard [32] and the rapid assessment method by Macinko et al. (2007) have been developed [33]. Despite their significance, these studies lack the necessary compatibility with the context, structure, and resources of the Iranian health system.

Significance and innovation of the research

Mental disorders represent a major component of the global burden of disease and pose significant challenges for health systems. In many countries, mental health services have been increasingly integrated into primary health care as a strategy to improve accessibility, continuity of care, and population coverage. As a result, evaluating the performance of mental health programs at the primary care level has become an important issue for health system management and policy-making.

Although several studies and frameworks have addressed health system performance and mental health evaluation, many of them have either focused on specific components of the system or examined mental health programs in isolation. Consequently, a comprehensive synthesis of indicators, influencing factors, and evaluation frameworks specifically related to mental health programs in primary health care remains limited.

Therefore, the present scoping review aims to systematically identify and synthesize performance indicators, influencing factors, and evaluation frameworks related to mental health programs at the primary health care level, thereby helping to address this knowledge gap. This review adds new knowledge by providing the first integrated synthesis of performance indicators, influencing factors, and evaluation frameworks specifically focused on mental health programs delivered at the primary health care level.

The innovation of this research lies in several distinct areas. Unlike previous reviews that mainly focus on general health system performance or single evaluation approaches, this study integrates three key elements, performance indicators, influencing factors, and evaluation frameworks, into a single analytical perspective. In addition, it specifically examines mental health programs at the primary level of service delivery, which represents the population’s first point of contact with the health system. Another innovative aspect is the use of international experiences and their adaptation to the local context of Iran. These experiences include internationally recognized mental health system assessment approaches, such as WHO‑AIMS and other global performance evaluation frameworks, which provide methodological guidance for evaluating mental health programs across different health systems. Although the contextual implications of the findings are discussed with reference to Iran, the evidence synthesized in this review is primarily derived from international literature, ensuring broader relevance beyond a single national context.

Finally, the study simultaneously considers multiple performance dimensions, including efficiency, effectiveness, equity, responsiveness, and quality.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a systematic scoping review based on the PCC (Population–Concept–Context) framework and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA‑ScR guidelines [34] and the methodological approach proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [35] and implemented in the following five stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results.

This methodology is appropriate for the present research given the exploratory nature of the topic [36].

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in major electronic databases, including PubMed and Scopus, as well as supplementary sources such as Google Scholar, national databases (Magiran and SID), and relevant institutional websites, including the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Ministry of Health. In addition, organizational reports and policy documents were reviewed as part of the grey literature search. Search keywords were developed based on the PCC (Population–Concept–Context) framework, which is recommended for scoping reviews [37]. The final search was completed in June 2025 across all databases and supplementary sources.

In designing this study, a broad time range was intentionally selected. Although the main focus was on contemporary literature, several foundational sources from the early 21st century were retained because they introduced key conceptual frameworks, reported pioneering pilot studies, and allowed for the analysis of historical trends and scientific continuity in the development of mental health programs in primary health care.

The complete search strategy, including all keywords and Boolean operators used for the PubMed database, is provided in Appendix 1. The initial draft search string that included the restrictive line “OR (‘mental health’[tiab] AND ‘primary care’[tiab] AND ‘performance evaluation’[tiab])” was reconsidered and replaced with a broader PCC‑aligned structure to increase search sensitivity and avoid missing relevant studies. The search strategy was tailored to the specific features of each database to optimize both sensitivity and precision. Given PubMed’s central role in medical literature and the researchers’ full access to it, a maximum‑sensitivity (broad) search strategy was applied to ensure comprehensive coverage. For other databases, including Scopus and Google Scholar, more precise (narrower) strategies were used to manage the volume of results while maintaining relevance.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they were published between 2000 and 2025, addressed the performance evaluation of mental health programs in primary health care, presented evaluation indicators, models, or conceptual or evaluation frameworks, were conducted at the primary care level, and were published in either Persian or English.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on the clinical evaluation of individual patients rather than program performance, provided insufficient data for extraction, or consisted solely of theoretical discussions without an empirical research basis.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into reference management software, and duplicate records were identified and removed. The study selection process was conducted in two stages. First, the titles and abstracts of the identified records were screened for eligibility and relevance to the review objectives. In the second stage, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The screening process at both stages was conducted independently by two reviewers. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion at the full‑text assessment stage included lack of relevance to mental health programs in primary health care, absence of performance evaluation content, and insufficient methodological information. The overall study selection process is presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third researcher. The extracted information included study characteristics (authors, publication year, country, and study design), type of mental health program, evaluation indicators, influencing factors, and any reported conceptual or evaluation frameworks. To ensure consistency, the extraction form was pilot‑tested on a small sample of studies and refined accordingly before full implementation.

Quality assessment of sources

Quality appraisal was conducted to provide an overview of the methodological characteristics of the included studies and to support the interpretation of the findings.

The quality of the selected sources was assessed using tools appropriate to the study type:

Quantitative studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool [38], qualitative studies using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist [39], and mixed‑methods studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [40].

In addition, practical program evaluation guidelines [41] were used as a complementary reference to support the organization and interpretation of the extracted performance indicators. The results of the quality appraisal were not used as exclusion criteria but were considered during the interpretation of the findings and are summarized in the Results section.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis and thematic synthesis [42].

The analysis involved familiarization with the data, initial coding, identification of themes, review and refinement of themes, and final synthesis. To ensure the relevance and applicability of the extracted indicators, the World Health Organization’s practical guide for program evaluation was consulted as an additional reference [41]. MAXQDA software was used to organize and code the data.

Ethical considerations

Since this review was based exclusively on secondary data obtained from previously published studies and did not involve human participants, formal ethical approval was not required. Nevertheless, the principles of ensuring accuracy and transparency in reporting, respecting authors’ rights, citing all used sources, and reporting findings impartially were strictly observed [43].

Results

A total of 541 records were initially identified through database searching (n = 393) and additional sources including Magiran, SID, organizational websites, and manual searching (n = 148). After removing duplicates, 477 records remained and were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Of these, 399 records were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria.

In the full-text screening stage, 78 articles were assessed for eligibility. A total of 29 full-text articles were excluded for the following reasons: (a) lack of relevance to mental health programs in primary health care settings, (b) absence of content related to performance evaluation, and (c) insufficient methodological information. Ultimately, 49 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

The final sample consisted of 49 studies, conducted between 2000 and 2025, focusing on various aspects of mental health programs implemented within primary health care (PHC) settings. The included sources encompassed peer‑reviewed articles, national reports, program documents, and policy papers, all aligned with the Population–Concept–Context framework. Most studies were conducted at the national level, with a smaller number addressing provincial or district-level program implementation.

Across the included studies, variations were observed in scope, objectives, and methodological approaches. Several studies examined the structure and processes of PHC-based mental health services, while others assessed specific indicators, program performance dimensions, implementation challenges, or contextual determinants. A number of documents specifically addressed workforce functions, referral pathways, integration of services, and monitoring mechanisms within PHC networks. Findings Related to Objective One: Key Performance Evaluation Indicators.

Across the included studies, a total of 117 performance evaluation indicators were identified. These indicators were classified into six main domains and 14 sub-domains, reflecting different dimensions of performance evaluation within primary mental health care systems. The six domains consisted of effectiveness, efficiency, equity, quality and responsiveness, access, and health outcomes (Table 2). Although some conceptual proximity may exist between certain domains (for example, access and equity), they were retained as separate analytical categories because they represent distinct dimensions commonly used in health system performance evaluation frameworks. These domains encompassed sub-domains related to service coverage, clinical service quality, service diversity, resource allocation, productivity, equitable access, client experience, safety, physical and financial access, and clinical, social, and community-level outcomes. Findings Related to Objective Two: Factors Influencing Performance.

The identified factors influencing performance were categorized into the following four themes:

Contextual and Policy Factors included transparent and supportive mental health laws and regulations [6], priority level of mental health in upstream policy documents [51] and level of social stigma towards mental illness [2].

Organizational and Managerial Factors included management stability and leadership strength [13, 19], existence of an integrated data-driven information system [28] and performance-based payment mechanisms [15].

Human Resource Factors included equitable distribution of specialized staff [22, 23], skill level and knowledge of the workforce [23] and rate of burnout among service providers [25].

Infrastructure and Technology Factors included availability of essential medicines [7, 21], existence of screening and diagnostic tools [4] and presence of an integrated health information system for patient registration and follow-up [28].

In summary, these four main themes represent the key factors influencing performance identified in this review.

Identified performance evaluation framework categories

Based on the frameworks presented in Table 3, the identified performance evaluation frameworks varied in their scope and application. Some frameworks were designed for macro-level performance evaluation of health systems, focusing on dimensions such as efficiency, effectiveness, equity, and responsiveness. Others were specialized tools developed specifically for performance evaluation of mental health systems, emphasizing components such as service organization, governance, and human resources. Several studies used frameworks tailored to primary health care settings, which defined operational indicators relevant to service delivery. In addition, a number of context-specific or community-based frameworks had been developed or adapted for use in local settings, reflecting organizational structures and community engagement approaches.

Findings related to objective four: gaps and challenges

The reviewed studies reported several gaps and challenges related to the performance evaluation of mental health programs at the primary healthcare level. One commonly reported gap was the absence of a validated indigenous framework specifically designed for evaluating the performance of mental health programs within primary healthcare settings in Iran, despite the availability of general international and national frameworks [19, 26, 29]. Another reported gap was the limited incorporation of perspectives from all relevant stakeholders in the processes of indicator collection, review, and analysis [21–23]. Additionally, some studies described challenges related to the lack of an integrated system for monitoring indicators [28], frequent management turnover [6], and constraints in financial resources [14].

Summary of results

This scoping review identified the main components related to the performance evaluation of mental health programs at the primary healthcare level. The findings showed that a wide range of performance evaluation indicators were reported across the reviewed studies, which were organized into several key domains and sub-domains. In addition, multiple factors influencing the performance of mental health programs were identified and categorized into four main thematic groups. The review also identified several categories of performance evaluation frameworks used in previous studies, including international health system frameworks, quality management models, and specialized mental health assessment tools. Furthermore, the reviewed studies reported several gaps and operational challenges related to performance evaluation, including the absence of a validated indigenous framework, limited stakeholder involvement in indicator processes, and challenges related to monitoring systems, management continuity, and financial resources.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes diverse approaches to performance evaluation in PHC mental health and highlights substantial variation in the selection, organization, and application of indicators across studies. Rather than representing a unified measurement tradition, this variability indicates the absence of shared priorities and a common measurement logic across studies. This variability reflects both contextual differences and the absence of an accepted, context‑appropriate evaluation framework.

The findings of this review align with previous literature that has examined performance evaluation in mental health and broader health system settings. Evidence from Iran also reflects the use of multi‑domain structures for organizing mental health performance indicators, indicating comparable areas of measurement across different contexts [44]. Furthermore, review studies on health system performance assessment have described analogous domain‑based classifications and the use of diverse conceptual frameworks to guide evaluation efforts [54]. These consistencies across the literature suggest that the domains and structures identified in the present review are not unique to a single setting but reflect broader trends in performance evaluation research.

Existing literature on PHC mental health performance appears broad yet fragmented, which may complicate coherent performance measurement. The dispersion of indicators across multiple domains suggests the potential need for greater integration and prioritization. Macinko et al. [33] similarly argued that effective performance evaluation depends on using a limited number of practical indicators.

Structured prioritization methods, such as the Importance–Performance Analysis (IPA) applied by Masalehi et al. [25], may help organize wide indicator sets into more usable decision-oriented groups.

At the international level, Murray and Frenk [29] proposed the ‘golden square’, efficiency, effectiveness, equity, and responsiveness, as a conceptual guide for balanced performance evaluation. Related literature also stresses the importance of incorporating client outcomes and service experiences when developing indicator sets [17, 45]. Taken together, these points may underscore the value of emphasizing indicator quality and ensuring a balanced approach to performance assessment.

The distribution of influencing factors across individual, organizational, and contextual levels suggests that performance in PHC mental health is shaped by interacting system components rather than isolated elements [29, 54]. The emphasis on contextual factors, such as enacting supportive laws [6] and eliminating stigma [2], suggests attention to cross-sectoral challenges beyond the direct control of the health sector and indicates the relevance of broader social determinants of health. Recent evidence also underscores the importance of institutionalizing psychosocial support within routine occupational health programs, strengthening organizational policies for continuous mental health support, and adapting interventions to local socio-cultural contexts to improve acceptability and sustainability [61]. Certain factors, such as management stability [13, 19] and the existence of an integrated information system [28], appear to function as prerequisites for performance evaluation, pointing to the influence of organizational factors. These patterns highlight that improving PHC mental health performance requires system‑level interventions rather than isolated program-level adjustments.

The identified performance evaluation frameworks, although thematically diverse, remain individually insufficient for assessing mental health programs within Iran’s primary healthcare system, as none of them offers a comprehensive structure that aligns with the operational realities of PHC mental health services. A stronger focus is needed on outcome‑oriented indicators that capture meaningful improvements in service users’ lives. Priority domains may include enhanced functional status and quality of life, strengthened social relationships, reduced stigma, and equity‑driven indicators such as financial accessibility and expanded service coverage for vulnerable populations. Emphasizing these areas may help align performance evaluation with the core objectives of PHC mental health services and ensure that measurement extends beyond structural and process indicators. Macro-level frameworks [29], specialized mental health assessment tools [46], and existing indigenous frameworks [13, 19, 26] provide valuable foundations by offering structured domains and system-level guidance. Evidence from the broader health‑system literature also emphasizes the importance of governance structures, coordination among diverse stakeholders, and system‑level enabling factors in advancing universal health coverage, suggesting that performance frameworks should integrate these multi‑level system interactions [62]. However, their applicability to PHC mental health remains limited. Macro‑level and primary care frameworks tend to overlook mental‑health‑specific outcome indicators, specialized tools often require substantial contextual adaptation, and indigenous models primarily emphasize structures and processes rather than client outcomes and experiential dimensions. Developing an integrated model that synthesizes elements from international frameworks together with locally grounded evidence, such as the Seraj program [5] and the established role of Behvarzes in community‑based service delivery [22], could help bridge these gaps by aligning global principles with contextual and operational realities. In this context, the criteria proposed by Zaadoud and Chbab [47] for adapting performance evaluation frameworks provide a scientifically grounded basis for selecting, modifying, and integrating relevant components into a coherent and context‑sensitive model. According to their framework, an effective model for evaluating mental health programs in Iran should ensure contextual compatibility, feasibility under current PHC conditions, comprehensive coverage across performance dimensions, and practical usability, all of which closely correspond with the findings of this review emphasizing the need to simultaneously address structural, process-based, outcome, and experiential indicators within Iran’s health system. The present review also indicates that, despite sufficient knowledge about evaluation indicators and influencing factors, a noticeable gap remains regarding the availability of validated and context‑appropriate indigenous frameworks. Addressing systemic challenges such as inefficiencies in the information system [28] and frequent managerial turnover [6] could further contribute to improving the performance of mental health programs. Resolving these issues requires sustained political commitment and managerial stability, particularly at the primary healthcare level. A study from Brazil similarly highlights managerial instability and system‑level constraints as key barriers to effective performance evaluation [47].

Implications for policy and practice

A key policy priority for the Ministry of Health should be the development of a national guideline for evaluating the performance of mental health programs at the primary healthcare level. Such a guideline can be informed by upstream documents, including the Seraj manual [63], to ensure alignment with national mental health policies. Furthermore, factors identified in domestic research as influencing the performance of mental health programs [64] should be incorporated into the guideline to ensure contextual relevance and feasibility. Establishing a functional system for monitoring mental health indicators [28] and ensuring sustainable and predictable financing [14] are among the highest operational priorities for strengthening PHC mental health programs. The use of simple rapid assessment frameworks [25, 33] may support managers at lower levels of the health system in conducting timely internal performance reviews. The design and validation of a native performance evaluation model remains an essential research priority and should be pursued through methodologically rigorous studies. Future research directions.

Future research should focus on the design, development, and empirical validation of an integrated and context‑appropriate performance evaluation model for PHC mental health services in Iran. Longitudinal and multi‑site studies are needed to assess the feasibility, reliability, and sensitivity of outcome‑based and experiential indicators, including functional improvement, service accessibility, and equity in service provision. Additionally, studies that examine effective strategies for stakeholder engagement, intersectoral collaboration, and data utilization across different levels of the health system can generate valuable evidence to strengthen implementation and ensure the sustainability of performance evaluation frameworks.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that although substantial literature exists on the components of performance evaluation in the field of mental health, this body of knowledge does not yet translate into a practical and applicable model for managers and service providers at the primary healthcare level in Iran. The findings carry clear implications: for policymakers, they underscore the urgent need to develop a standardized and indigenous evaluation framework; for health managers, they offer a consolidated set of indicators and influencing factors to support local performance monitoring; and for researchers, they provide a comprehensive roadmap while identifying critical gaps for future studies focused on model development and validation.

Considering the criteria for adapting evaluation frameworks proposed by Zaadoud and Chbab [60], the design of a native model should ensure compatibility with the specific conditions of Iran’s health system, feasibility at the primary care level, and sufficient comprehensiveness to cover the essential dimensions of performance. The dispersion of indicators, the generic nature of existing frameworks, and the identification of multiple implementation challenges collectively underscore the need to design and implement a native, simple, and valid model for evaluating the performance of mental health programs in Iran. Future research can build upon this roadmap to design, refine, and validate such a model through rigorous methodological approaches. It is also recommended that future studies prioritize the developing practical frameworks that emphasize stakeholder participation and fully account for Iran’s local contextual realities.

Limitations of the study

This scoping review had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although a systematic and comprehensive search was conducted, some relevant studies, particularly those published in languages other than Persian and English, may not have been captured. Second, although a basic methodological appraisal was conducted to support the interpretation of findings, the review did not aim to exclude studies based on quality scores. This approach is consistent with scoping review guidelines, which emphasize mapping the evidence rather than performing strict quality-based exclusion. Third, some indicators or influencing factors that were mentioned only implicitly or without clear operational definitions may have been overlooked during data extraction, despite efforts to apply a rigorous and iterative coding process.
