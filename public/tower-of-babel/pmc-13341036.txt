Analysis of Trajectories of Anxiety Behaviors During Induction of Anesthesia in Children With Multiple Encounters: A Secondary Analysis of a Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Annie Xin, Allan F. Simpao, Timothy Liversedge, Abby V. Winterberg, Ari Y. Weintraub, James J. Thomas, Clyde T. Matava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPreoperative anxiety is a significant stressor for children and is associated with negative postoperative outcomes. Although the incidence of difficult inductions during a single anesthetic encounter is well documented, the longitudinal trajectory of anxiety behaviors in children undergoing repeated anesthesia remains poorly characterized. It is unclear whether repeated inductions lead to habituation (reduced difficult inductions) or sensitization (increased difficult inductions).MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of a large multicenter retrospective observational study involving data from six pediatric hospitals between 2019 and 2022. The cohort consisted of children under 18 years of age. The primary outcome was the trajectory of difficult induction, defined as a Child Induction Behavioral Assessment (CIBA) score of 3. Secondary outcomes included mask acceptance and trends in anxiolytic interventions. We employed mixed effects logistic regression models to analyze anxiety trajectories, adjusting for age, parental presence, and behavioral diagnoses. Lorenz curves were used to assess the concentration of anesthetic burden within the population.ResultsThe study included 102 017 unique patients, of whom 24 564 (24%) underwent multiple encounters. The prevalence of difficult induction remained stable during the initial visits but decreased significantly after the fifth encounter, with the odds of difficult induction decreasing by at least 30% compared to the index visit. This “learning effect” was setting‐dependent: children aged 1–12 years undergoing Nonoperating room anesthesia (NORA) demonstrated significant habituation, whereas difficult induction rates in the operating room (OR) remained static regardless of visit frequency. Additionally, high‐frequency utilizers in NORA settings exhibited a pragmatic shift in anxiolytic strategy, transitioning from pharmacological premedication to increased incidence of parental presence at induction of anesthesia.ConclusionsIn this secondary analysis, we found that repeated anesthetic exposure did not inherently lead to sensitization. Instead, children—particularly in NORA settings—exhibited habituation, characterized by decreasing anxiety behaviors over time. This divergence suggests that the less hostile physical environment and absence of surgical pain in NORA facilitate desensitization, whereas the OR environment maintains a higher baseline threat level. Clinicians should consider these distinct trajectories and prioritize environmental adaptations or parental involvement for high‐frequency patients.

Introduction

Preoperative anxiety remains a challenge for children undergoing surgery [1, 2, 3, 4]. Anesthetic induction is reported as the most distressing time point of their perioperative journey [5, 6]. Preoperative anxiety in younger children is strongly associated with increased postoperative negative emotions and behaviors including sleep disturbances, enuresis, temper tantrums, separation anxiety, and defiance toward authority in the short term [7, 8, 9].

Alleviating preoperative anxiety has long been recognized as an important goal for pediatric anesthesiologists, especially for those children who require multiple anesthetic encounters. A strong association between increased anxiety at anesthetic induction and previous surgeries and hospital admissions has been demonstrated in multiple studies [2, 3, 8], particularly when the prior hospital experiences were negative [10] [11]. Information on anxiety behavior over multiple inductions can provide anesthesiologists with opportunities to finetune strategies for reducing the individual child's preinduction anxiety, which may also result in positive learning effects for both the patient and their caregivers for their future encounters. However, longitudinal studies specifically measuring changes in anxiety behaviors in the same child over multiple anesthetic encounters are lacking.

We recently published an international multicenter retrospective observation study over a 3‐year period that described the incidences of anxiety‐related behaviors during anesthetic inductions as well as real‐world evidence for the efficacy of anxiolytic interventions [12]. The dataset from this population‐based study included participants with multiple exposures to general anesthesia and presented an opportunity for examining anxiety trajectories in those patients. The aims of this secondary analysis [12] are to describe the trajectories of difficult induction and significant anxiety behaviors in children over multiple anesthetic encounters.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective study was approved by the Hospital for Sick Children Research Ethics Board (1000065224). Detailed methods for the original study have been previously published [12]. Electronic health record data on anesthetic inductions were retrospectively collected from six tertiary or quaternary pediatric hospitals between January 2019 and March 2022. Institutional review board approvals or waivers were obtained as per local policies. Patients under 18 presenting for general anesthesia at each center were eligible, excluding patients who were intubated prior to anesthetic induction. Data were de‐identified and securely transmitted to a central study database at the Hospital for Sick Children.

Variables

As detailed in the original study, standardized clinical information were recorded as a routine part of anesthesia care including patient demographics (e.g., age, sex, ASA physical status) and procedural details (e.g., date and time of encounter, type of procedure, location, anesthesia provider ID and route of induction) at each encounter. Outcome data were recorded using the Standardized Anesthesia InductioN Tool (SAINT) [13], an electronic health record tool used by all participating sites for documenting anesthesia inductions as part of their routine clinical care. Components of SAINT included the Child Induction Behavioral Assessment (CIBA) tool [14] and the Mask Acceptance Scale (MAS). The CIBA is a validated instrument designed for documenting induction behaviors in electronic health records [15]. It consists of a 3‐point Likert scale: 1, smooth (e.g., calm and controlled, cooperative with induction, does not exhibit negative behaviors); 2, moderate (e.g., cries, pulls away from mask/i.v., verbalizes refusal); and 3, difficult (e.g., covers face/limb, pushes away, screams, kicks/flails, appears rigid, or limp) [11]. Difficult induction was defined as a CIBA score of 3, and significant anxiety behavior was defined as a CIBA score of 2 or 3 [11]. The MAS is a 4‐point Likert scale describing mask acceptance: 1, excellent (unafraid, cooperative, accepts mask readily); 2, good (slight fear of mask, easily reassured), 3 fair (moderate fear of mask, not calmed with reassurance), and 4 poor (terrified, crying, and combative). Easy mask acceptance was defined as an MAS score of 1 or 2, and difficult mask acceptance was defined as an MAS score of 3 or 4.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the trajectory of difficult induction over multiple anesthetic inductions in children. The secondary outcomes included the trajectory of difficult mask acceptance over multiple inductions and factors associated with these.

Statistical Methods

Power and Sample Size Calculation

We conducted an a priori power and sample size calculation for this study. Because we performed a secondary analysis of an existing dataset containing 155 604 anesthesia encounters from 101 957 unique patients, the available data determined our sample size rather than prospective recruitment. We further estimated that roughly 30% of these patients underwent multiple anesthesia encounters, resulting in an anticipated subset of 30 587 patients for our secondary analysis.

This sample size of patients with multiple anesthesia encounters would provide 80% power for detecting small and meaningful differences over time in CIBA anxiety scores and MAS scores (standardized effect size = 0.02) based on a paired t‐test and assuming a two‐tailed 5% alpha. Power calculations were performed using PASS software (v23, NCSS LLC, Kaysville, Utah).

Patients with more than one anesthesia induction visit were included in trend analyses. Baseline patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous data were presented using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Denominators were presented for variables with missing data. The number of observations at each anesthesia encounter was displayed.

The distribution of difficult induction across encounters was summarized using frequencies and percentages. This was quantified in the full analysis cohort and stratified by age category (< 1 year, 1–3 years, 4–12 years, and 13–18 years). To address the possibility of large variations in subjective CIBA scoring among different anesthesia providers, we examined difficult inductions by provider ID across the entire cohort.

Multivariable mixed effects logistic regression modeling was implemented to determine the independent association between time to anesthesia encounter or number of encounters and difficult induction, adjusting for age, parental presence, and autism diagnosis as fixed effects. Patient ID and provider ID were included as random effects to account for multiple encounters per patient and provider. For logistic regression analysis, CIBA anxiety score was dichotomized as Difficult (versus Smooth/Moderate), as well as Difficult/Moderate (versus Smooth). Three multivariable models (Model A, Model B, and Model C) were fit for each binary outcome: Model A included time in months as linear, quadratic, and cubic terms to evaluate polynomial nonlinear trends over time; Model B included number of encounters as a categorical fixed effect; and Model C included number of encounters as a linear fixed effect. Each of the three models included age, parental presence, and autism diagnosis as fixed effects. Results from multivariable models were presented as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values. For the secondary outcome, the MAS score was summarized across encounters using frequencies and percentages, and multivariable mixed effects logistic regression was performed like the analysis of CIBA above, where the MAS score was dichotomized as terrified (versus unafraid/slightly/moderate). We also analyzed location, the operating room (OR) versus non‐operating room anesthesia (NORA). NORA is defined as anesthesia care provided outside operating room suites in locations including but not limited to endoscopy, radiology, catheterization lab, and medical imaging.

To evaluate the concentration of anesthesia encounters and difficult inductions within the patient population, we performed a post hoc analysis using Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients. Lorenz curves were constructed to visualize the cumulative proportion of total anesthetic encounters and difficult inductions against the cumulative proportion of the patient population. The Gini coefficient was calculated to quantify the degree of inequality in these distributions, ranging from 0 (perfect equality, where every patient has the same number of outcomes) to 1 (maximal inequality), thereby identifying the extent to which anesthetic volume and difficult behaviors were concentrated within specific patient subgroups.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (v18.0, StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas). A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We identified 24 564 patients with more than one anesthetic induction. Among these, the mean number of inductions was 3.2 (range 2–173), with a median of 2 (IQR 2–3). The majority (60.9%; 14 953/24564) of these patients underwent two inductions, whereas 18.4% (4526/24564) underwent three. Primary Outcome—Difficult induction

In the total cohort of 102 017 unique patients, 91.8% (93662) experienced exclusively smooth inductions. Only 8.2% (8355) of patients had at least one difficult induction encounter. Among this subgroup, the burden of difficult induction was predominantly low: 7.3% (7492) of the total cohort experienced a single difficult induction. Recurrent difficult inductions were rare, occurring in only 0.63% (647/102017) of patients for two episodes and 0.13% (132/102017) for three. One patient experienced 22 difficult inductions. The Lorenz curve for difficult visits demonstrated that the cumulative proportion of difficult inductions remained near zero for most of the population, with the burden of difficult inductions concentrated in 15% of the cohort (Figure 2B).

The prevalence of difficult induction was 6.3% (6359/155783) at the index visit and remained stable during initial anesthetic experiences. However, a reduction in difficult induction rates was observed after five anesthesia encounters (Figure 3).

Difficult mask acceptance (MAS score of 3–4) during all anesthetic inductions overall (A), OR inductions (B), and NORA inductions (C).

In OR settings, difficult induction rates did not decline with increased encounters. Conversely, in NORA settings, rates fell following five inductions (Figure S1), particularly among children aged 1–12 years. Children aged 1–3 years and 4–12 years demonstrated the most significant reduction in difficult inductions over multiple encounters, primarily within the NORA setting.

Secondary Outcomes

Trends in significant anxiety mirrored those of difficult induction (Figure 3). Difficult mask acceptance (MAS scores of 3 or 4) occurred in 20%–30% of anesthetic inductions, with consistent rates observed across encounters and settings (OR and NORA) (Figure 4).

Anxiolytic Interventions

The use of premedication steadily decreased over multiple encounters, while the proportion of inductions involving parental presence at induction of anesthesia (PPIA) increased (Figure 5). The premedication rates remained stable for OR inductions but gradually declined over multiple encounters for NORA inductions. The overall shift toward increasing PPIA and decreasing premedication corresponded with the predominance of NORA inductions in the higher encounter numbers.

Unique patient count with trends for significant anxiety (CIBA score > 1) and difficult induction (CIBA score 3) across sequential visits.

For children over 1 year of age, PPIA was infrequent in the OR (< 20%) but prevalent in NORA (> 40%) (Figure S2) in the overall cohort.

Induction Techniques

In the overall cohort, inhalational induction was the predominant technique during the initial visit, accounting for approximately 60% of cases. However, an inverse relationship was observed over subsequent encounters: the prevalence of inhalational induction steadily declined, whereas the use of intravenous induction increased, crossing over to become the majority method by the third to fourth anesthesia induction encounter. Among those with > 20 visits, intravenous induction was the dominant technique, used in over 80% of anesthesia inductions in the NORA setting compared to 40% in the OR (Figure S3).

Multivariable Analysis

We adjusted for potential confounders, including age, parental presence, and behavioral diagnoses, using mixed effect models (Table S1). No association was found between the time interval between encounters and difficult induction rates. After five or more anesthetic encounters, the odds of difficult induction were reduced by at least 30% compared to the first encounter, with continued improvement thereafter (p < 0.001). These findings were consistent for significant anxiety behavior (p < 0.01) (Table S2).

Using mixed effects modeling with the same fixed and random effects described in the primary outcome analysis, we found no significant association between the number of anesthetic encounters and difficult mask acceptance scores (Table S3). We did not identify any latent group of children whose inductions became progressively more difficult over repeated encounters.

In a post hoc analysis of NORA inhalational inductions, gradual reductions in difficult inductions were observed over time, though those effects were less pronounced in the children aged 1–12 years. Within this group, those with inhalation induction continued to experience stable rates of difficult induction (Figure S4).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter retrospective analysis, we characterized the longitudinal trajectories of anxiety‐related behaviors in children undergoing repeated anesthetics. Our results challenge the assumption that repeated medical exposure inevitably leads to sensitization and escalating distress. Instead, we observed a pattern of stabilization, with a significant reduction in difficult induction rates after the fifth encounter. However, this “learning effect” was not universal; habituation was more likely to occur outside the OR, while difficult induction rates in the OR did not change over multiple visits. This divergence could be attributed to multiple factors including environment, complexity of procedure, and postoperative pain that may influence a child's ability to habituate to repeated anesthesia.

Consistent with previous findings, younger age, autism diagnosis, and PPIA were linked to higher odds of difficult inductions [16]. Children aged 1–12 years showed the greatest reduction in difficult inductions over multiple visits in NORA but not OR environment. One possible reason is that the OR is typically a more intimidating setting and thus is less conducive to desensitization. In comparison, NORA locations like imaging suites are less sterile and may become more familiar due to frequent visits and consistent staff, thereby leading to habituation [17, 18, 19, 20]. Additionally, NORA imaging studies and procedures are typically less painful, which can foster positive NORA associations. Meanwhile, painful OR procedures can reinforce anxiety and hinder adaptation for some patients and caregivers [20, 21].

Our findings suggest a pragmatic shift in clinical management of these patients: as children undergo more frequent procedures, particularly in NORA, clinicians often rely less on pharmacological premedication and more on parental presence and the child's own habituation. Because the reduction in difficult induction persisted in our multivariable model after adjusting for premedication, we conclude that this improvement represents a genuine behavioral habituation rather than a result of intensified anxiolytic therapy.

We also found changes in the use of anxiolytic interventions over repeated encounters. In the NORA setting, the rate of premedication in the 1–3 years and 4–12 years age groups declined while the rate of PPIA increased over time in the NORA setting. In the OR, the use of premedication was unchanged along with the incidence of difficult induction. Parents and caregivers were present at only 14% of the OR inductions but were present at 67% of NORA inductions. This finding was consistent from previous North American surveys reporting low rates of PPIA during OR inductions [22, 23]. Inside the OR, patients with difficult induction and PPIA are associated with both anxious children and anxious parents, and several studies including systematic reviews have not demonstrated objective benefit in PPIA [24, 25, 26]. Conversely, the reduction of difficult induction in NORA may be reflective of improvements in the child's coping ability moreso because of the more friendly NORA environment regardless of parental presence [27]. Induction rooms have been shown to reduce perioperative anxiety in children [20, 28]. Those factors alone might have helped alleviate the child's anxiety over multiple visits through training effects. Although PPIA alone may not significantly influence the child's anxiety or distress [25], reduced parental anxiety as well as active participation during induction have been positively correlated with a reduction in the child's anxiety [11, 26, 29]. The increased rates of PPIA during NORA inductions over time might also have facilitated such training of parents.

Interestingly, mask acceptance scores did not significantly change over multiple visits even during NORA inductions, with difficult mask acceptance occurring at 20%–30% of encounters. We also found increased rates of intravenous induction over multiple NORA encounters. The use of mask inductions has been considered a trigger for difficult inductions, in part due to the smell of the inhalational agents and possible claustrophobic effects in children [30]. However, there was a mild reduction in difficult NORA inhalational inductions among the high‐frequency encounters, suggesting a possible small contribution by learning effects.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, “index encounter” refers to a patient's first record in our 3‐year dataset, rather than their first‐ever anesthetic experience; therefore, any previous anesthesia history is unknown. However, our large sample size may help mitigate this issue. Second, our binary classification of location (OR vs. NORA) did not account for the architectural heterogeneity of surgical suites. Specifically, we could not distinguish between inductions occurring directly inside the OR and those occurring in dedicated induction rooms, which may offer a quieter, “NORA‐like” environment. However, objective evaluations of the anxiolytic benefit of induction rooms are limited, and participating hospitals equipped with induction rooms in our study still reported high rates of difficult inductions, suggesting that the “OR” categorization remains a valid proxy for a higher stress environment. We also acknowledge that induction practices including the utilization of PPIA inside OR vary from country to country. Third, using only 3 years of data restricts our ability to analyze long‐term anxiety trends, and since only a few patients had more than 10 visits, percentage estimates for these subgroups can be unreliable. Last, as a retrospective descriptive study, we can identify associations but cannot establish causality or fully eliminate selection bias stemming from protocol variations that were not documented in our data.

Conclusions

We describe population‐level anxiety trajectories in children undergoing repeated anesthetics. Contrary to sensitization concerns, difficult inductions did not escalate; instead, rates decreased significantly after five encounters. This “learning effect” was observed only in the NORA setting, whereas anxiety in the OR remained static regardless of visit frequency. Although these findings highlight the critical role of the induction environment, future research must validate these trajectories at the patient level to establish causality regarding individual behavioral changes.
