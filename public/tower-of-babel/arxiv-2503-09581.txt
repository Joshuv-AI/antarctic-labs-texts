On a Cahn–Hilliard equation for the growth and division of chemically active droplets modeling protocells
Harald Garcke, Kei Fong Lam, Robert Nürnberg, Andrea Signori

Abstract
The Cahn–Hilliard model with reaction terms can lead to situations in which no coarsening is taking place and, in contrast, growth and division of droplets occur which all do not grow larger than a certain size. This phenomenon has been suggested as a model for protocells, and a model based on the modified Cahn–Hilliard equation has been formulated. We introduce this equation and show the existence and uniqueness of solutions. Then formally matched asymptotic expansions are used to identify a sharp interface limit using a scaling of the reaction term which becomes singular when the interfacial thickness tends to zero. We compute planar solutions and study their stability under non-planar perturbations. Numerical computations for the suggested model are used to validate the sharp interface asymptotics. In addition, the numerical simulations show that the reaction terms lead to diverse phenomena such as growth and division of droplets in the obtained solutions, as well as the formation of shell-like structures.



Keywords
: Cahn–Hilliard equation, chemical reactions, pattern formation, active droplets, protocells

35B25, 35K55, 35K61, 35R35, 35Q92.

1
Introduction

It has been proposed recently that chemical reactions in phase separating systems can lead to a suppression of Ostwald ripening and to the growth and division of droplets
[
32
,
31
,
6
]
.
These systems are away from thermodynamic equilibrium with an external supply of energy enhancing chemical reactions.
In
[
31
]
it was even demonstrated that droplets in the presence of chemical reactions can grow and spontaneously split. Then a further growth of divided droplets, using up the fuel from chemical reactions, is possible, leading eventually to further splitting. The model studied in
[
32
,
31
,
6
]
involves a Cahn–Hilliard model with chemical reactions, and in general, does not fulfill a free energy inequality. This is due to the fact that energy is supplied, and such systems are called active systems.
In
[
31
]
the authors argue that such active systems can play an important role in the transition
between nonliving and living systems. Initially, featureless aggregates of abiotic matter evolve and form protocells which can be the basis for systems that gain the structure and functions necessary to fulfill
the criteria of life.

It was also shown in subsequent studies that synthetic analogues of such chemically active systems can be developed, see

[
16
]
. In such system not only Ostwald ripening is suppressed but also stable liquid shells can form, see
[
6
,
8
]
. Here, a shell of one phase forms with an inside and an outside of a second phase. In fact, it was observed experimentally that spherical, active droplets can undergo a morphological transition into a spherical shell. In
[
8
]
it was shown that the mechanism is related to gradients of the droplet material, and the authors also identify how much chemical energy is necessary to sustain the spherical drop.
The non-conservative Cahn–Hilliard system the authors of
[
31
]
introduced is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(m({{\varphi}})\nabla\mu)+S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}}),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\beta(-\varepsilon\Delta{{\varphi}}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\psi^{\prime}({{\varphi}})),

where in this paper we take
{{\varphi}}
to be the normalized concentration difference between the droplet material and
the background material, which is scaled such that the two phases are at
\pm 1
. Besides,
\beta
and
\varepsilon
are constants, and
m
and
S_{\varepsilon}
are phase-dependent functions that will be introduced later. The variable
\mu
is the chemical potential given as the first variation of a
Ginzburg–Landau
free energy
given by

\displaystyle{\cal E}({{\varphi}})=\beta\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{{\varphi}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\psi({{\varphi}})\Big),

(1.2)

where
\psi
represents a suitable double-well free energy density.
Compared to the classical Cahn–Hilliard model the main new term is the reaction term
S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}})
which in

[
31
]

was taken to be affine linear outside of the interfacial region that separates the two phases given by the droplet regions
and the background region. Within the interfacial region an interpolation between these two affine linear functions is
chosen. In the droplet phase
\{{{\varphi}}=1\}
,
S_{\varepsilon}(1)
will be negative, which reflects the fact that the droplet material degrades chemically. In the background phase
\{{{\varphi}}=-1\}
,
S_{\varepsilon}(-1)
will be positive, which takes into account that the material making up the
droplet phase is produced in the background phase by chemical reactions involving a fuel which powers its production, see

[
31
]
for details.

In the case without chemical reactions, i.e.,
S_{\varepsilon}=0
, the Cahn–Hilliard model was first formulated
in
[
11
]
using the free energy
(
1.2
) introduced in
[
12
]
.
Since its introduction the Cahn–Hilliard equation has been the subject of many studies and has found many
applications. We refer to
[
27
,
26
,
4
]
for detailed overviews.
In particular, it
can be shown that the Cahn–Hilliard model is the
H^{-1}
gradient flow of the energy (
1.2
), see e.g.,
[
19
,
4
]
. Furthermore, it was also shown, first formally by Pego
[
28
]
and later rigorously by
Alikakos, Bates and Chen
[
3
]
, that the Cahn–Hilliard model converges to the Mullins–Sekerka sharp interface model as the interfacial thickness converges to zero.
It was also demonstrated that the Cahn–Hilliard model can be
used to describe the Ostwald ripening process, where small particles dissolve and larger ones grow, see

[
22
]
. There are numerous analytical results on the Cahn–Hilliard equation and we here only refer to the existence results in
[
18
,
17
]
and to
[
2
,
26
]
who, in particular, discuss the Cahn–Hilliard equation from a semi-group perspective and also study the physically relevant
logarithmic potential.

Several models have been proposed in which a reaction type term
S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}})
appears.
The simplest one is the Cahn–Hilliard–Oono model in which

S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}})=-\omega({{\varphi}}-c^{*})
with a positive constant
\omega
, and
c^{*}\in(-1,1)
is given.
This term accounts for nonlocal interactions in phase separation, see
[
26
]
. A proliferation term

S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}})=-\lambda{{\varphi}}(1-{{\varphi}})
with a constant
\lambda>0
has been introduced in
[
25
]
. For analytical results in this case we refer to
[
26
]
.
A source term which depends on
{{\varphi}}
but also depends on the spatial variable
x

has been proposed in
[
9
]
for applications in binary image inpainting and was subsequently analysed in

[
10
,
21
]
, see also
[
30
]
for an application to image segmentation. In addition, in several tumor growth models, Cahn–Hilliard type models with source terms appear and are coupled to other equations, see, e.g.,
[
14
,
24
,
20
]
.

In this paper, we mathematically analyze the Cahn–Hilliard model introduced in

[
31
]
. We will first carefully introduce the model and then show a well-posedness result for the system.
We use formally matched asymptotic expansions to relate the diffuse interface Cahn–Hilliard model to a new sharp interface model, which differs from the sharp interface model proposed in
[
31
]
.
In particular, we will show that asymptotic expansions lead to a quasi-static diffusion problem also possibly involving
source terms stemming from reactions at the interface.
For the sharp interface model, we derive planar stationary solutions.
Finally, we will use finite element computations for the Cahn–Hilliard model with reactions to
numerically verify the matched asymptotics and to illustrate the stability and instability behavior of solutions. In particular,
we will show several splitting scenarios as well as the formation of shell-like structures.

Mathematical models

2.1
The Cahn–Hilliard model

Let
\Omega

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(m({{\varphi}})\nabla\mu)+S_{\varepsilon}({{\varphi}})\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle=\beta(-\varepsilon\Delta{{\varphi}}+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\psi^{\prime}({{\varphi}}))\hskip 20.00003pt

in

\displaystyle\partial_{{\bm{n}}}\mu

\displaystyle=\partial_{{\bm{n}}}{{\varphi}}=0\hskip 20.00003pt

(2.1c)

\displaystyle{{\varphi}}(0)

\displaystyle={{\varphi}}_{0}\hskip 20.00003pt

in

(2.1d)

Here,

The source term

S_{\varepsilon}(r)=S_{1}(r)+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}S_{2}(r){,\hskip 10.00002ptr\in{\mathbb{R}}}.

(2.2)

Here, the term

will later lead to a fast reaction in the interfacial region.
On choosing
r_{c}\in(0,1]

S_{+}
,
S_{-}
,

,

, and
L

\displaystyle S_{1}(r)=\begin{cases}{S_{+}}&\quad\text{if $r\geq{r_{c}}$,}\\
{S_{-}}+G_{1}(r)({S_{+}}-{S_{-}})&\quad\text{if $r\in(-{r_{c}},{r_{c}})$,}\\
{S_{-}}&\quad\text{if $r\leq-{r_{c}}$,}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle S_{2}(r)=\begin{cases}-{K_{+}}(r-1)&\quad\text{if $r\geq{r_{c}}$,}\\
\widehat{S}_{2}(r)&\quad\text{if $r\in(-{r_{c}},{r_{c}})$,}\\

where we define for

Here,

are suitable differentiable interpolation functions, to be introduced below, satisfying

\displaystyle G_{1}({r_{c}})=1,\hskip 10.00002ptG_{1}(-{r_{c}})=0,\hskip 10.00002ptG_{2}(\pm{r_{c}})=G_{3}(\pm{r_{c}})=G_{4}(\pm{r_{c}})=0,

(2.5)

\displaystyle G_{1}^{\prime}(\pm{r_{c}})=G_{4}^{\prime}(\pm{r_{c}})=0,\hskip 10.00002ptG_{2}^{\prime}({r_{c}})=G_{3}^{\prime}(-{r_{c}})=0,\hskip 10.00002ptG_{2}^{\prime}(-{r_{c}})=G_{3}^{\prime}({r_{c}})=1,

so that the source term

.
We often use

which considerably simplifies the expression for

and the matched asymptotic expansions which we use later

The system (
2.1
) is related to the following
free energy

\displaystyle{\cal E}({{\varphi}})=\beta\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{{\varphi}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\psi({{\varphi}})\Big).

In what follows we assume that
\psi
is even, that is
\psi(r)=\psi(-r)
for
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and satisfies
\psi(\pm 1)=0

and
\psi^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)\neq 0
.
Typically, we will choose the
quartic potential

\displaystyle\psi(r)=\tfrac{1}{4}(1-r^{2})^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptr\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.7)

Possible choice of the interpolation functions

As mentioned above, in the theoretical analysis to follow, we consider any interpolation functions

,

,

and

\displaystyle G_{1}(r){=\widehat{G}_{1}(\tfrac{r}{r_{c}}),\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{1}(r)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{3}{4}(r+1)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}(r+1)^{3},

\displaystyle G_{2}(r){=r_{c}\widehat{G}_{2}(\tfrac{r}{r_{c}}),\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{2}(r)}

\displaystyle=-{\tfrac{1}{2}}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\psi^{\prime\prime}(-1)}}(r-1)\sqrt{2\psi(r)},\hskip 10.00002ptG_{3}(r)=-G_{2}(-r),

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{1}(1)=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{1}(-1)=G_{1}^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{2}(\pm 1)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{2}^{\prime}(1)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{G}_{2}^{\prime}(-1)=1.

2.3
The sharp interface model

We now present the corresponding sharp interface system related to the Cahn–Hilliard model above.
We will later use the method of formally matched asymptotic expansions, see, e.g.,

\displaystyle={S_{+}}-\rho_{+}\mu\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle-m_{-}\Delta\mu

\displaystyle={S_{-}}-\rho_{-}\mu\hskip 10.00002pt

in
\Omega^{-}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\beta\kappa}{2}\hskip 20.00003pt

on
\Sigma
,

(2.11c)

\displaystyle[\mu]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0\hskip 20.00003pt

on
\Sigma
,

(2.11d)

\displaystyle-2{\cal V}

\displaystyle=[m\nabla\mu]^{+}_{-}\cdot{{\bm{\nu}}}+S_{I}\hskip 20.00003pt

on
\Sigma
,

(2.11e)

\displaystyle\partial_{{\bm{n}}}\mu

\displaystyle=0\hskip 20.00003pt

on
\partial\Omega
,

(2.11f)

where

\rho_{\pm}=\frac{K_{\pm}}{\beta\psi^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)}
,

is a constant depending on the choice of the double well potential

and

is the interface reaction term which depends on the choice of the interpolation functions

,

and

(cf. (

\rho_{\pm}=\frac{K_{\pm}}{2\beta},\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3},\hskip 10.00002ptS_{I}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(K_{+}-K_{-}+\frac{4}{3}L\Big).

In the above, we also use

to denote the mean curvature of

{\bm{\nu}}
is the unit normal to the interface, and

In addition,
for

[u]^{+}_{-}(t,x):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to x\\

Further information about the notation used can be found in

4

Notice that the above system is connected to the well-known

2.4

We now perform a nondimensionalization argument in order to identify important dimensionless parameters.
Choosing units

and

\displaystyle=\frac{(\widetilde{x})^{2}}{m_{-}\widetilde{\mu}}{S_{-}}-\frac{(\widetilde{x})^{2}\rho_{-}}{m_{-}}\widehat{\mu}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle[\widehat{\mu}]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0\hskip 20.00003pt

on

\displaystyle=0\hskip 20.00003pt

on
{\partial\widehat{\Omega}}
,

with

being the normal velocity of the interface

in the direction of the normal

and on assuming that

3
Well-posedness

In this section, we address the well-posedness of the system (
2.1
), aiming to cover a wide spectrum of scenarios. Specifically, we aim to accommodate various configurations without relying on the specific structure of the source term
S_{\varepsilon}
, as long as its growth is under control. Furthermore, in our analysis we can include rather general potentials, provided they are regular, nonsingular and exhibit polynomial growth.
Let us first specify the notation we need for the well-posedness result.

3.1
Notation

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
. The Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
is denoted by
|\Omega|
, while the Hausdorff measure of
\partial\Omega
is denoted by
|\partial\Omega|
.

For any Banach space
X
, its norm is represented as
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, its dual space as
X^{*}
, and the duality pairing between
X^{*}
and
X
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X}
. In the case where
X
is a Hilbert space, the inner product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
.

For each
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
k\geq 0
the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces defined on
\Omega
are denoted as
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
, with their respective norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}
. For simplicity we may often use
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
, and employ similar shorthand notation for other norms. We adopt the convention
H^{k}(\Omega):=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and denote the mean value of a functional
h\in{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}
as

h_{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\langle h,1\rangle_{H^{1}}.

We now introduce a tool commonly employed in the investigation of problems associated with equations of Cahn–Hilliard type. Given
\phi\in{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}
, we seek
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=\langle\phi,v\rangle_{H^{1}},\hskip 10.00002ptv\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(3.1)

This corresponds to the standard weak formulation of the homogeneous Neumann problem for the Poisson equation
-\Delta u=\phi
for
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. The solvability of (
3.1
) for
\phi\in{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}
relies on the condition that
\phi
possesses a zero mean value, that is,
\phi_{\Omega}=0

this entails
\Phi_{0}
is just a function of
z
, resulting in the ODE relation

\displaystyle\partial_{zz}\Phi_{0}(z)-\psi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0}(z))=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\Phi_{0}(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\Phi_{0}(\pm\infty)=\pm 1.

(4.7)

Upon testing (
4.7
) with
\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}
, we obtain the so-called
equipartition of energy
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)|^{2}=\psi(\Phi_{0}(z)),\hskip 10.00002ptz\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.8)

Hence, we find the identity

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)|^{2}\,dz=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2\psi(s)}\,ds=:\gamma.

(4.9)

From
\psi^{\prime}(-z)=-\psi^{\prime}(z)
, we see that
-\Phi_{0}(z)=\Phi_{0}(-z)
.
Let us point out that, in the special case of
\psi
being the quartic potential (
2.7
),
i.e.,
\psi(r)=\tfrac{1}{4}(1-r^{2})^{2},
we obtain

\gamma={\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-s^{2})\,ds=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}.

Next, considering (
2.1a
) to order
\varepsilon^{-2}
produces

\displaystyle\partial_{z}(m(\Phi_{0})\partial_{z}\Lambda_{0})=0.

Upon integration and using the matching condition (
4.5
) to
\Lambda_{0}
, we infer that

\displaystyle m(\Phi_{0})\partial_{z}\Lambda_{0}(t,s,z)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt{z\in{\mathbb{R}}}.

(4.10)

Since
m(\Phi_{0})>0
, (
4.10
) implies
\partial_{z}\Lambda_{0}(t,s,z)=0
. We integrate once more and use the matching condition (
4.4
) to obtain the jump condition

[\mu_{0}]^{+}_{-}=0.

4.4
Higher order expansions in the interfacial region

Moving to
\varepsilon^{0}
order in (
2.1b
), we derive that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}=\beta\psi^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{0})\Phi_{1}-\beta\partial_{zz}\Phi_{1}+\beta\kappa\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}.

(4.11)

Testing the above by
\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}
and integrating from
-\infty
to
{+\infty}
produces

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Lambda_{0}(t,s)\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)\,dz=\beta\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\big(\partial_{z}(\psi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0}(z)))\Phi_{1}-\partial_{zz}\Phi_{1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)+\kappa|\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)|^{2}\big)\,dz.

Using (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) for
\Phi_{0}
, integrating by parts and using
{\psi^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0}
, we infer

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\partial_{z}(\psi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0}(z)))\Phi_{1}-\partial_{zz}\Phi_{1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\big[\psi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0})\Phi_{1}-\partial_{z}\Phi_{1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}\big]^{+\infty}_{-\infty}-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\partial_{z}\Phi_{1}(\psi^{\prime}(\Phi_{0}(z))-\partial_{zz}\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz=0,

as both terms on the right-hand side vanish,
whence, recalling (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), this entails that

2\mu_{0}=\gamma\beta\kappa.

In order to utilize the equations to order
\varepsilon^{-1}
, we now
exploit the preliminary assumption

r_{c}=1
. From (
2.1a
), we obtain

\displaystyle-{\cal V}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}=\partial_{z}(m(\Phi_{0})\partial_{z}\Lambda_{1})-[{K_{-}}G_{2}(\Phi_{0})-{K_{+}}G_{2}(-\Phi_{0})]+LG_{4}(\Phi_{0}).

Integrating from
-\infty
to
{+\infty}
and using (
4.6
) for
\Lambda_{1}
leads to

\displaystyle-2{\cal V}

\displaystyle=[m({{\varphi}}_{0})\nabla\mu_{0}]^{+}_{-}\cdot{\bm{\nu}}+\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}{K_{+}}G_{2}(-\Phi_{0}(z))-{K_{-}}G_{2}(\Phi_{0}(z))+LG_{4}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz.

As
\Phi_{0}
is a fixed function, the integral on the right-hand side yields a constant
S_{I}
depending on
K_{\pm}
,
L
and the double well potential
\psi
.
By

-\Phi_{0}(z)=\Phi_{0}(-z)
, it holds that

\displaystyle S_{I}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}{K_{+}}G_{2}(-\Phi_{0}(z))-{K_{-}}G_{2}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz+L\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}G_{4}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz

(4.12)

\displaystyle={({K_{+}}-{K_{-}})}\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}G_{2}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz+L\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}G_{4}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz,

where, upon setting
\widetilde{c}:=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\psi^{\prime\prime}(-1)}}
, we realize with the help of the equipartition of energy (
4.8
) that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}G_{2}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz=-\widetilde{c}\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}(\Phi_{0}(z)-1)\sqrt{2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))}\,dz

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\widetilde{c}\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}(\Phi_{0}(z)-1)\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)\,dz=-\widetilde{c}\int_{-1}^{1}(s-1)\,ds=2\widetilde{c}.

In addition, we notice that

G_{4}(\Phi_{0}(z))=2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))=\sqrt{2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))}\sqrt{2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))}=\sqrt{2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z)

and compute

\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}G_{4}(\Phi_{0}(z))\,dz=\int_{-\infty}^{{+\infty}}\sqrt{2\psi(\Phi_{0}(z))}\partial_{z}\Phi_{0}(z){\,dz}=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2\psi(s)}\,ds=\gamma.

Thus, recalling the definition of
\widetilde{c}
, we find that

\displaystyle S_{I}=\frac{{{K_{+}}-{K_{-}}}}{\sqrt{\psi^{\prime\prime}(-1)}}+\gamma L.

(4.13)

This concludes our analysis concerning the sharp interface system (
2.11
). Let us point out that, for the quartic potential (
2.7
), it holds that
\widetilde{c}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
, and so

\displaystyle S_{I}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}({{K_{+}}-{K_{-}}})+\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}L.

(4.14)

We remark that in the case that
r_{c}\in(0,1)
the term
S_{I}
can be computed
if we insert
\Phi_{0}
in the definition of the source term, see (
2.2
), and integrate from
-\infty
to
\infty
.
This would then replace the integrals in (
4.12
).

Combining the calculations above, we have demonstrated that as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the phase-field system (
2.1
) formally converges to the limit described by the free-boundary problem (
2.11
).

5
Planar solutions and their stability

5.1
Setting

A simple solution can be computed in a planar geometry. In particular, we will later use planar solutions to validate
the asymptotic analysis from the previous section. More precisely, we will compare numerical computations for the phase field model with planar solutions of the sharp interface limit.
We now consider the free boundary problem (
2.11
) in the special domain

\Omega=(0,\mathcal{L})\times(0,\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}})^{d-1}{,}

for
\mathcal{L},\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}>0
and look for a planar solution under the geometry

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}(t)=(0,q(t))\times(0,\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}})^{d-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega_{-}(t)=(q(t),\mathcal{L})\times(0,\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}})^{d-1},

where
q(t)
encodes the location of the moving interface
\Sigma_{0}
. In addition, we require a
90^{\circ}
degree boundary condition at points where the interface meets the
external boundary. For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we write
x=(z,\widehat{x})
with
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\widehat{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
.
As
\bm{\nu}=(-1,{\bm{0}})^{\top}
, we get

{\cal V}=-\frac{dq(t)}{dt}{=-\dot{q}}{,}

with the dot denoting the time derivative.
For this planar setting we make the ansatz

\mu_{\pm}(t,x)=\mu_{\pm}(t,(z,\widehat{x}))=\widehat{\mu}_{\pm}(t,z),\hskip 10.00002ptx=(z,\widehat{x})\in(0,\mathcal{L})\times(0,\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}})^{d-1}.

On the interface we obtain

\nabla\mu_{\pm}(t,x)\cdot\bm{\nu}=-\widehat{\mu}_{\pm}^{\prime}(t,z),

where prime denotes the partial derivative with respect to
z
.
In what follows, we drop the hat-notation for convenience. Due to the planar setting, we have
\kappa=0
and hence (
2.11c
) and (
2.11d
) can be replaced by

\mu_{+}(t,q(t))=\mu_{-}(t,q(t))=0,

(5.1)

while (
2.11e
) can be reformulated as

2{\dot{q}}=-[m\mu^{\prime}]^{+}_{-}+S_{I}.

(5.2)

Equation (
2.11f
) can be replaced by

\mu_{+}^{\prime}(t,0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\mu_{-}^{\prime}(t,\mathcal{L})=0.

(5.3)

In addition, (
2.11a
) and (
2.11b
) become

\displaystyle-m_{+}\mu^{\prime\prime}_{+}

\displaystyle=S_{+}-\rho_{+}\mu_{+}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }z\in(0,q(t)),

(5.4)

\displaystyle-m_{-}\mu^{\prime\prime}_{-}

\displaystyle=S_{-}-\rho_{-}\mu_{-}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }z\in(q(t),\mathcal{L}).

(5.5)

5.2
Solution formula and evolution equation for the interface position

From now on, we restrict ourselves to the case
\rho_{\pm}>0
which is relevant for applications, as shown in
[
31
]
.
We obtain the following solutions

\displaystyle\mu_{+}(t,z)

\displaystyle=d_{+}\Big(1-\frac{\cosh{({\lambda}_{+}z)}}{\cosh{({\lambda}_{+}q{(t)})}}\Big),

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{-}(t,z)

\displaystyle=d_{-}\Big(1-\frac{\cosh{({\lambda}_{-}(\mathcal{L}-z))}}{\cosh{({\lambda}_{-}(\mathcal{L}-q{(t)}))}}\Big){,}

(5.7)

where the constants
d_{\pm}
and
{\lambda_{\pm}}
are defined as

d_{\pm}:=\frac{S_{\pm}}{\rho_{\pm}}=\beta\frac{S_{\pm}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\pm 1)}{K_{\pm}},\hskip 10.00002pt{\lambda_{\pm}}:=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\pm}}{m_{\pm}}}.

It can be readily verified that (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) solve the ordinary differential equations (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), and fulfill the boundary conditions (
5.1
) and (
5.3
).

Meanwhile, the evolution equation (
5.2
) for the interface position
{q=}q(t)
becomes

\displaystyle{\dot{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(-m_{+}\mu^{\prime}_{+}(q)+m_{-}\mu^{\prime}_{-}(q)+S_{I}\Big)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(d_{+}m_{+}{\lambda}_{+}\tanh{({\lambda}_{+}q)}+d_{-}m_{-}{\lambda}_{-}\tanh{({\lambda}_{-}(\mathcal{L}-q))}+S_{I}\Big)

\displaystyle=:{\cal H}(q).

We notice that

\displaystyle{\cal H}(0)=\frac{1}{2}[d_{-}m_{-}{\lambda}_{-}\tanh({\lambda}_{-}\mathcal{L})+S_{I}],\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal H}(\mathcal{L})=\frac{1}{2}[d_{+}m_{+}{\lambda}_{+}\tanh({\lambda}_{+}\mathcal{L})+S_{I}].

In the case where
S_{I}=0
, by having
d_{+}<0
and since the other parameters
m_{\pm}
,
{\lambda_{\pm}}
and
d_{-}
are positive, it holds that
{\cal H}(0)>0
,
{\cal H}(\mathcal{L})<0
and
{\cal H}^{\prime}(q)<0
. Due to the continuity of
{\cal H}(q)
, we are guaranteed the existence of exactly one root
q^{*}
where
{\cal H}(q^{*})=0
. For example, setting
S_{+}=-1
and all other parameters equal to 1, we find that
q^{*}=\frac{\mathcal{L}}{2}
is a root of
{\cal H}
. Note that when
S_{I}\neq 0
it is possible that roots of
{\cal H}
may not exist at all. However, after fixing parameters
d_{\pm}
and
{\lambda_{\pm}}
(hence fixing also
K_{\pm})
, one can adjust the parameter
L
in
S_{I}
to help ensure the existence of a root for
{\cal H}(q)
.

5.3
Linear stability of planar solutions

We now aim to analyze the stability of the planar solutions, with their position denoted by
q^{*}
and the corresponding chemical potentials denoted by
\mu_{\pm}^{*}
. Specifically, we consider a perturbed interface of the form
w:=q^{*}+\epsilon Y
, where
0<\epsilon<1
and
Y=Y(t,\widehat{x})
. The idea is to start from the stationary front characterized by
q^{*}
, as identified above, and proceed with a stability analysis around this equilibrium.
Thus, we define perturbed domains as

\displaystyle\Omega_{Y,\epsilon}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\Omega\,\,:\,\,{z}<w(t,\widehat{x})\},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega_{Y,\epsilon}^{-}:=\{x\in\Omega\,\,:\,\,{z}>w(t,\widehat{x})\}.

We make the ansatz

\mu_{\pm}(t,x)=\mu_{\pm}^{*}({z})+\epsilon u_{\pm}(t,x),

and demand that those solve the free boundary problem on the perturbed domains, given by

\displaystyle-m_{+}\Delta(\mu_{+}^{*}+\epsilon u_{+})=S_{+}-\rho_{+}(\mu_{+}^{*}+\epsilon u_{+})\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega_{Y,\epsilon}^{+},

(5.9a)

\displaystyle-m_{-}\Delta(\mu_{-}^{*}+\epsilon u_{-})=S_{-}-\rho_{-}(\mu_{-}^{*}+\epsilon u_{-})\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega_{Y,\epsilon}^{-},

(5.9b)

\displaystyle\mu_{+}^{*}+\epsilon u_{+}=\mu_{-}^{*}+\epsilon u_{-}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=w\},

(5.9c)

\displaystyle 2(\mu_{\pm}^{*}+\epsilon u_{\pm})=\gamma\beta\kappa\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=w\},

(5.9d)

\displaystyle-2{\cal V}=[m\nabla(\mu^{*}+\epsilon u)]_{-}^{+}\cdot\bm{\nu}+S_{I}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=w\},

(5.9e)

\displaystyle(\mu_{+}^{*}+\epsilon u_{+})^{\prime}(t,0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(\mu_{-}^{*}+\epsilon u_{-})^{\prime}(t,{\cal L})=0.

(5.9f)

Linearizing the above equations about the original interface
\{{z}=q^{*}\}
, while using (
5.1
)–(
5.5
), as well as

\mu_{\pm}^{*}(w)=\mu_{\pm}^{*}(q^{*})+(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}(w-q^{*})+\text{ h.o.t.}=0+\epsilon(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+\text{ h.o.t.},

(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}(w)=(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}(q^{*})+(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}(w-q^{*})+\text{ h.o.t.}=(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}(q^{*})+\epsilon(\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+\text{ h.o.t.},

we obtain the following system for
Y
and
u_{\pm}
:

\displaystyle-m_{+}\Delta u_{+}=-\rho_{+}u_{+}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ in }\{{z}<q^{*}\},

(5.10a)

\displaystyle-m_{-}\Delta u_{-}=-\rho_{-}u_{-}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ in }\{{z}>q^{*}\},

(5.10b)

\displaystyle(\mu_{+}^{*})^{\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+u_{+}=(\mu_{-}^{*})^{\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+u_{-}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=q^{*}\},

(5.10c)

\displaystyle 2((\mu_{\pm}^{*})^{\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+u_{\pm})=-\gamma\beta\Delta_{\widehat{x}}Y\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=q^{*}\},

(5.10d)

\displaystyle 2{\dot{Y}}=-m_{+}((\mu_{+}^{*})^{\prime\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+u_{+}^{\prime})+m_{-}((\mu_{-}^{*})^{\prime\prime}|_{{z}=q^{*}}Y+u_{-}^{\prime})\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{ on }\{{z}=q^{*}\},

(5.10e)

\displaystyle(u_{+})^{\prime}(t,0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(u_{-})^{\prime}(t,{\cal L})=0,

(5.10f)

where the linearization of the mean curvature operator gives

\kappa(t,\widehat{x})\approx-\Delta_{\widehat{x}}(\epsilon Y(t,{\widehat{x}})){,}

with
\Delta_{\widehat{x}}
denoting the Laplace operator with respect to the
(d-1)
-dimensional coordinates
\widehat{x}
. Suppressing the explicit dependence on
t
, for each
t
we make the ansatz

u_{\pm}(x)=v_{\pm}(z)W(\widehat{x})

and choose
W
as an eigenfunction of the
\Delta_{\widehat{x}}
-operator with Neumann boundary conditions such that for
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d-1}
,

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Delta_{\widehat{x}}W=\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}W&\text{ in }(0,\widetilde{\cal L})^{d-1},\\
\partial_{{\bm{n}}}W=0&\text{ on }\partial(0,\widetilde{\cal L})^{d-1},\end{cases}

where
\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}
serves as a corresponding eigenvalue. A possible eigenfunction is

W(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})=\cos\Big(\frac{\pi}{\widetilde{\cal L}}\ell_{2}x_{2}\Big)\times\cdots\times\cos\Big(\frac{\pi}{\widetilde{\cal L}}\ell_{d}x_{d}\Big)

with

\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}=-\pi^{2}(\ell_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d}^{2}).

With this ansatz for
u_{\pm}
we find that (
5.10a
)–(
5.10b
) reduces to

-m_{\pm}(v_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}W+v_{\pm}\Delta_{\widehat{x}}W)=-\rho_{\pm}v_{\pm}W

and this yields

v_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}=\Big({\lambda_{\pm}^{2}}-\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}\Big)v_{\pm}=(\Gamma_{\pm}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}})^{2}v_{\pm}{,}

where we recall
{\lambda_{\pm}^{2}}=\frac{\rho_{\pm}}{m_{\pm}}
and set
\Gamma_{\pm}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}=\sqrt{{\lambda_{\pm}^{2}}-\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}}
. We then consider

\displaystyle v_{+}(z)=a_{+}\cosh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}z),\hskip 10.00002ptv_{-}(z)=a_{-}\cosh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-z)),

for some unknown functions
a_{+}(t)
and
a_{-}(t)
to be determined. Similarly, we make the ansatz

Y(t,\widehat{x})=y(t)W(\widehat{x})

and get

\displaystyle\Delta_{\widehat{x}}Y=y(t)\Delta_{\widehat{x}}W(\widehat{x})=y(t)\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}W(\widehat{x})=\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}Y,

which also leads to perturbed interfaces that fulfil a
90^{\circ}
angle condition at points where the interface intersects the outer boundary. We now need to ensure that the boundary conditions (
5.10c
)–(
5.10f
) are fulfilled. On recalling (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) we see that

\displaystyle(\mu_{+}^{*})^{\prime}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=-d_{+}{\lambda}_{+}\tanh({\lambda}_{+}q^{*}),\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle(\mu_{+}^{*})^{\prime\prime}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=-d_{+}{\lambda}_{+}^{2},

\displaystyle(\mu_{-}^{*})^{\prime}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=d_{-}{\lambda}_{-}\tanh({\lambda}_{-}({\cal L}-q^{*})),\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle(\mu_{-}^{*})^{\prime\prime}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=-d_{-}{\lambda}_{-}^{2},

and so (
5.10c
) and (
5.10d
) become

\displaystyle-d_{+}{\lambda}_{+}\tanh({\lambda}_{+}q^{*})+a_{+}\cosh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}q^{*})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=d_{-}{\lambda}_{-}\tanh({\lambda}_{-}({\cal L}-q^{*}))+a_{-}\cosh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-q^{*}))=-\frac{\gamma\beta}{2}\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}.

Hence, we derive the following:

\displaystyle a_{+}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}q^{*})}\Big(d_{+}{\lambda}_{+}\tanh({\lambda}_{+}q^{*})-\frac{\gamma\beta}{2}\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}\Big),

\displaystyle a_{-}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\cosh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-q^{*}))}\Big(d_{-}{\lambda}_{-}\tanh({\lambda}_{-}({\cal L}-q^{*}))+\frac{\gamma\beta}{2}\frac{\zeta_{\widehat{\bm{\ell}},d}}{(\widetilde{\cal L})^{2}}\Big).

Meanwhile, for the evolution equation (
5.10e
), we have

\displaystyle 2{\dot{Y}}

\displaystyle=m_{+}d_{+}{\lambda}_{+}^{2}Y-m_{-}d_{-}{\lambda}_{-}^{2}Y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-m_{+}a_{+}(q^{*})\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}q^{*})Y-m_{-}a_{-}(q^{*})\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-q^{*}))Y

\displaystyle=(S_{+}-S_{-}-m_{+}a_{+}(q^{*})\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}q^{*})-m_{-}a_{-}(q^{*})\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-q^{*})))Y,

where we recall the definition
d_{\pm}=\frac{S_{\pm}}{\rho_{\pm}}=\frac{S_{\pm}}{{\lambda_{\pm}^{2}}m_{\pm}}
. The amplification factor is the prefactor coefficient on the right-hand side of the above identity

\displaystyle S_{+}-S_{-}-m_{+}a_{+}(q^{*})\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{+}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}q^{*})-m_{-}a_{-}(q^{*})\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}\sinh(\Gamma_{-}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}({\cal L}-q^{*})).

(5.11)

For the rest of this section, we consider specific parameters ensuring the amplification factor (
5.11
) is positive so as to induce the development of instabilities from interface perturbations. We fix

\widehat{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{2},...,\ell_{d})
, and

S_{+}=-1,\hskip 10.00002ptS_{-}=m_{\pm}=\rho_{\pm}=1{,}

so that the stationary state is
q^{*}=\frac{{\cal L}}{2}
, along with
d_{+}=-1
,
d_{-}=1
,
{\lambda_{\pm}}=1
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pm}^{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}}=\sqrt{1+\frac{|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}}{\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}},\hskip 10.00002pta_{+}(q^{*})=\frac{-\tanh(\frac{{\cal L}}{2})+\frac{\gamma\beta}{2}|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{{\cal L}}^{2}}{\cosh{\Big(}\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\sqrt{1+|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}{\Big)}},\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle a_{-}(q^{*})=\frac{-\tanh(\frac{{\cal L}}{2})+\frac{\gamma\beta}{2}|\bm{\ell}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{{\cal L}}^{2}}{\cosh{\Big(}\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\sqrt{1+|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}{\Big)}},

and the amplification factor (
5.11
) reduces in this setting to

\displaystyle-2+\sqrt{1+\tfrac{|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}}{\widetilde{{\cal L}}^{2}}}\tanh\left(\tfrac{{\cal L}}{2}\sqrt{1+\tfrac{|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}}{\widetilde{{\cal L}}^{2}}}\right)\left(2\tanh\left(\tfrac{{\cal L}}{2}\right)-{\gamma\beta}\tfrac{|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}}{\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}\right).

(5.12)

In the case
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}=(0,...,0)
, this corresponds to interface perturbations of the form
w=q^{*}+\epsilon
, i.e., translational perturbations. We immediately see that the amplification factor becomes

-2+2\tanh^{2}\Big(\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\Big)=-2\mathrm{sech}^{2}\Big(\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\Big)<0.

Thus, we obtain stability with respect to translational perturbations. For
|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|>0
, we obtain interface perturbations of the form
w=q^{*}+\epsilon\cos(\pi\ell_{2}x_{2}/\widetilde{{\cal L}})\times\cdots\times\cos(\pi\ell_{d}x_{d}/\widetilde{{\cal L}})
. We see that (
5.12
), for a given perturbation whose wave length is related to
\widehat{\bm{\ell}}
, can be positive if
\beta<\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\widehat{\bm{\ell}})
, where

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\widehat{\bm{\ell}})=\frac{2}{\gamma}\frac{\widetilde{{\cal L}}^{2}}{|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}}\left(\tanh\Big(\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\Big)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}\tanh\big(\frac{{\cal L}}{2}\sqrt{1+|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\pi^{2}/\widetilde{\cal L}^{2}}\big)}\right).

(5.13)

We remark that in this setting
\beta
is the modified capillary length
c_{l}
introduced in (
2.13
) in Subsection
2.4
, i.e.,
we obtain instability in cases where the modified capillary length is small enough.
As
|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}=(\ell_{2})^{2}+\cdots+(\ell_{d})^{2}
with
{\widehat{\bm{\ell}}=(}\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d-1}
, we obtain that
the possible perturbations lead to amplification factors via sum of squares, namely:

|\widehat{\bm{\ell}}|^{2}\in\begin{cases}\{0,1,4,9,16,\dots\}&\text{ if }d=2,\\

The numerical simulations also support the sharp interface asymptotics, as we get a good agreement between phase field computations and exact solutions of the sharp interface problem.

6.1
Finite element method

We assume that
\Omega
is a polyhedral domain and let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a regular triangulation of
\Omega
into disjoint open simplices. Associated with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the piecewise linear finite element space

S^{h}=\{\zeta\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\,:\,\zeta|_{o}\in P_{1}(o)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall o\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

where we denote by
P_{1}(o)
the set of all affine linear functions on
o
,
see
[
13
]
.
Let
(\cdot,\cdot)^{h}
be the usual mass lumped
L^{2}
-inner product on
\Omega
associated with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, and let
\pi^{h}:C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\to S^{h}

be the standard interpolation operator.
In addition, let
\tau
denote a chosen uniform time step size. Then our finite element approximation of (
2.1
)
is given as follows. Let
\phi_{h}^{0}\in S^{h}
, e.g.,
\phi_{h}^{0}=\pi^{h}\varphi_{0}

if
\varphi_{0}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
.
Then, for
n\geq 0
, find
(\phi_{h}^{n+1},\mu_{h}^{n+1})\in S^{h}\times S^{h}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}(\phi_{h}^{n+1}-\phi_{h}^{n},\chi_{h})^{h}+(m(\phi_{h}^{n})\nabla\mu_{h}^{n+1},\nabla\chi_{h})^{h}

\displaystyle=(S_{\varepsilon}(\phi_{h}^{n}),\chi_{h})^{h}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall\chi_{h}\in S^{h},

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\beta\varepsilon(\nabla\phi_{h}^{n+1},\nabla\eta_{h})+\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}(\psi^{\prime}(\phi_{h}^{n+1}),\eta_{h})^{h}-(\mu_{h}^{n+1},\eta_{h})^{h}

\displaystyle=0\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall\eta_{h}\in S^{h}.

(6.1b)

We implemented the scheme (
6.1
) with the help of the finite element
toolbox ALBERTA, see
[
29
]
.
To increase computational efficiency, we employ adaptive meshes, which have a
finer mesh size
h_{f}
within the diffuse interfacial regions and a coarser
mesh size
h_{c}
away from them. In particular, we use the
strategy from
[
7
,
5
]
, and refine an element
o
if

\eta_{o}=|\max_{o}|\phi_{h}^{n}|-1|>0.5
, unless it is already of size
h_{f}
,
and similarly coarsen an element
o
if
\eta_{o}<0.1
, unless it is already of
size
h_{c}
. For simplicity we assume from now on that

\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(0,L_{i})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with

L_{1}\geq L_{2}\geq{\dots\geq}L_{d}
, and then let
h_{f}=\frac{L_{d}}{N_{f}}
,

h_{c}=\frac{L_{d}}{N_{c}}
for two chosen integer parameters
N_{f}>N_{c}
.
Here, unless otherwise specified, for the computations with a phase field
parameter
\varepsilon=(2^{k}\pi)^{-1}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we choose

N_{f}=8N_{c}=2^{3+k}L_{d}
. This ensures that the interfacial regions are
accurately resolved, while using a relatively coarser mesh in the pure
regions. For the time discretization we choose
\tau=10^{-3}
, unless stated
otherwise.

The nonlinear system of equations arising at each time level of
(
6.1
) are solved with the help of Newton’s method.
The resulting linear systems at each iteration in two spatial dimensions
are solved by direct factorization using the package UMFPACK,
see
[
15
]
,
and in three spatial dimensions with a
V
-cycle multigrid solver using a block Gauss–Seidel smoother.

For the initial data
\varphi_{0}
we in general choose a diffuse interface
representation of a desired sharp interface, with signed distance function

d_{0}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular, unless otherwise stated,
we let

\varphi_{0}(x)=\tanh\Big(\frac{d_{0}(x)}{\varepsilon\sqrt{2}}\Big).

(6.2)

For the definition of
S_{2}
in (
2.2
), recall (
2.4
),
we need to specify
K_{\pm}
and
L
.
For convenience, for the numerical simulations to follow, we will define the
relations
\rho_{\pm}=\frac{K_{\pm}}{2\beta}
, from which
K_{\pm}
can then be
inferred.
Unless otherwise stated, we set
m_{\pm}=\rho_{\pm}=r_{c}=1
and
L=0
throughout.
