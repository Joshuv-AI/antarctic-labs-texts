Solving Inverse Problems using Diffusion with Iterative Colored Renoising
Matthew C. Bendel bendel.8@buckeyemail.osu.edu, Saurav K. Shastri shastri.19@buckeyemail.osu.edu, Rizwan Ahmad rizwan.ahmad@osumc.edu, Philip Schniter schniter.1@osu.edu

Abstract
Imaging inverse problems can be solved in an unsupervised manner using pre-trained diffusion models, but doing so requires approximating the gradient of the measurement-conditional score function in the diffusion reverse process. We show that the approximations produced by existing methods are relatively poor, especially early in the reverse process, and so we propose a new approach that iteratively reestimates and “renoises” the estimate several times per diffusion step. This iterative approach, which we call Fast Iterative REnoising (FIRE), injects colored noise that is shaped to ensure that the pre-trained diffusion model always sees white noise, in accordance with how it was trained. We then embed FIRE into the DDIM reverse process and show that the resulting “DDfire” offers state-of-the-art accuracy and runtime on several linear inverse problems, as well as phase retrieval. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/matt-bendel/DDfire .

Introduction

Diffusion has emerged as a powerful approach to generate samples from a complex distribution
p_{0}

(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Song & Ermon, 2019
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021b
;
Song et al., 2021a
)
.
Recently, diffusion has also been used to solve inverse problems
(
Daras et al., 2024
)
, where the goal is to recover
\bm{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}
from incomplete, distorted, and/or noisy measurements
\bm{y}
in an unsupervised manner.
There, a diffusion model is trained to generate samples from
p_{0}
and, at test time, the reverse process is modified to incorporate knowledge of the measurements
\bm{y}
, with the goal of sampling from the posterior distribution
p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{y})
.

When implementing the reverse process, the main challenge is approximating the conditional score function
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
at each step
t
, where
\bm{x}_{t}
is an additive-white-Gaussian-noise (AWGN) corrupted and scaled version of
\bm{x}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\bm{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is treated as a draw from a likelihood function
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{0})
.
(See Sec.
2
for more details.)
Many existing approaches fall into one of two categories.
The first uses Bayes rule to write
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
, where an approximation of
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
is readily available from the
p_{0}
-trained diffusion model, and then approximates
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
(e.g.,
Chung et al. (2023a)
;
Song et al. (2023)
).
The second approach uses Tweedie’s formula
(
Efron, 2011
)
to write

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}-\bm{x}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

(1)

and then approximates the conditional denoiser
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
(e.g.,
Kawar et al. (2022a)
;
Wang et al. (2023)
;
Zhu et al. (2023)
;
Chung et al. (2024a)
).

A key shortcoming of the aforementioned approaches is that their conditional-score approximations are not very accurate, especially early in the reverse process.
For the methods that approximate
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
, we can assess the approximation quality both visually and via mean-square error (MSE) or PSNR, since the exact
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
minimizes MSE given
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}
.
For the methods that approximate
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
, we can compute their equivalent conditional-denoiser approximations using

\displaystyle\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y}),

(2)

which follows from (
1
).

.

In this paper, we aim to improve the approximation of
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
at each step
t
.
Since we have observed that methods using a single neural function evaluation (NFE) to approximate
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
or
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
perform poorly, we consider using several NFEs.
In particular, we propose an iterative approach to approximating
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
that we call Fast Iterative REnoising (FIRE).
FIRE is like a plug-and-play (PnP) algorithm (see the PnP survey
Ahmad et al. (2020)
) in that it iterates unconditional denoising with linear estimation from
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{y}
.
But unlike traditional PnP algorithms, which aim to minimize an implicit loss function, FIRE is based on
colored renoising
, where carefully designed colored Gaussian noise is added to the linear-estimation output so that the denoiser’s input error approximates AWGN.
Since the denoiser is trained to remove AWGN, renoising aims to minimize the distribution shift experienced during inference.

for noisy box inpainting, which is relatively free of artifacts.
We propose two versions of FIRE: one that handles AWGN-corrupted linear measurement models like (
6
), and one that handles generalized-linear inverse problems such as phase retrieval
(
Shechtman et al., 2015
)
, Poisson regression
(
Figueiredo & Bioucas-Dias, 2010
)
, and dequantization
(
Zymnis et al., 2010
)
.
The latter is based on expectation propagation (EP)
(
Minka, 2001
;
Bishop, 2007
)
.

We then embed FIRE into the DDIM diffusion reverse process
(
Song et al., 2021a
)
, yielding the “DDfire” posterior sampler.
Figure
1
shows examples of DDfire’s
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
approximation when run on noisy box inpainting.
DDfire’s
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
approximations have fewer structural artifacts and higher PSNR than its competitors.

The contributions of this work are as follows:

1.

We propose linear FIRE, an iterative approach to solving AWGN-corrupted linear inverse problems that injects carefully designed colored Gaussian noise in order to whiten the denoiser’s input error.

2.

We theoretically analyze the convergence of linear FIRE.

3.

We use expectation propagation (EP) to extend linear FIRE to generalized-linear inverse problems.

4.

Combining FIRE and DDIM, we propose the DDfire diffusion posterior sampler.

5.

We demonstrate the excellent accuracy of DDfire on several imaging inverse problems: box inpainting, Gaussian and motion blur, super-resolution, and phase retrieval.

Background

Given training data drawn from distribution
p_{0}
, diffusion models corrupt the data with ever-increasing amounts of noise and then learn to reverse that process in a way that can generate new samples from
p_{0}
.
In the main text, we assume the variance-exploding (VE) diffusion formulation
(
Song et al., 2021b
)
, whereas
Appendix
A
provides details on the variance-preserving (VP) formulation, including
DDPM
(
Ho et al., 2020
)

and
DDIM
(
Song et al., 2021a
)
.

The VE diffusion forward process can be written as a stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\sqrt{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\sigma^{2}(t)]/\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{w}

over
t
from
0
to
T
, where

\sigma^{2}(t)
is a variance schedule and

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{w}
is the standard Wiener process (SWP)

(
Song et al., 2021b
)
.
The corresponding reverse process runs the SDE

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\sigma^{2}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\sigma^{2}(t)]/\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}}
backwards over
t
from
T
to
0
, where

p_{t}(\cdot)
is the marginal distribution of
\bm{x}
at
t

and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}}
is the SWP run backwards.
The “score function”
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x})
can be approximated using a deep neural network (DNN)
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)
trained via denoising score matching
(
Hyvärinen, 2005
)
.

In practice, time is discretized to
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, yielding the SMLD from
Song & Ermon (2019)
, whose forward process,

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}+\sqrt{\sigma_{t+1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}\bm{w}_{t}
, with i.i.d
\{\bm{w}_{t}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}=0
,
implies that

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma_{t}\bm{\epsilon}_{t},\quad\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})

(3)

for all
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
.
The SMLD reverse process then uses i.i.d
\{\bm{n}_{t}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
in

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t+1}+(\sigma_{t+1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t+1}(\bm{x}_{t+1})+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}(\sigma_{t+1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2})}{\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}}\bm{n}_{t+1}.

(4)

To exploit side information about
\bm{x}_{0}
, such as the measurements
\bm{y}
in an inverse problem, one can simply replace
p_{t}(\cdot)
with
p_{t}(\cdot|\bm{y})
in the above equations
(
Song et al., 2021b
)
.
However, most works aim to avoid training a
\bm{y}
-dependent approximation of the conditional score function
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
.
Rather, they take an “unsupervised” approach, where
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)\approx\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
is learned during training but
\bm{y}
is presented only at inference
(
Daras et al., 2024
)
.
In this case, approximating
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
is the key technical challenge.

There are two major approaches to approximate
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
.
The first uses the Bayes rule to write

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
and then replaces
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
with the score approximation
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)
.
But the remaining term,
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
, is intractable because

p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})=\int p(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{0})p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}

with unknown
p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t})
, and so several approximations have been proposed.
For example, DPS
(
Chung et al., 2023a
)
uses

p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t})\approx\delta(\bm{x}_{0}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t})
,
where
\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t}
is the approximation of
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t}\}
computed from
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)
using Tweedie’s formula:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t}=\bm{x}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t).

(5)

Similarly,
\Pi
GDM
(
Song et al., 2023
)
uses

p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t})\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0};\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t},\zeta_{t}\bm{I})
with some
\zeta_{t}
.
However, a drawback to both approaches is that they require backpropagation through
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)
, which increases the cost of generating a single sample.
In Fig.
4
, we show that DDfire offers a
1.5\times
speedup over DPS at an equal number of NFEs.

The second major approach to approximating
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
uses (
1
) with
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
approximated by a quantity that we’ll refer to as
\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t,\bm{y}}
.
For example, with AWGN-corrupted linear measurements

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\bm{Ax}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},~~\bm{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),

(6)

DDNM
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
approximates
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
by first computing
\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t}
from (
5
) and then performing the hard data-consistency step

\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t,\bm{y}}=\bm{A}^{+}\bm{y}+(\bm{I}-\bm{A}^{+}\bm{A})\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t}
,
where
(\cdot)^{+}
is the pseudo-inverse.
DDS
(
Chung et al., 2024a
)
and DiffPIR
(
Zhu et al., 2023
)
instead use the soft data-consistency step

\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t,\bm{y}}=\arg\min_{\bm{x}}\|\bm{y}-\bm{Ax}\|^{2}+\gamma_{t}\|\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{0|t}\|^{2}
with some
\gamma_{t}>0
.
DDRM
(
Kawar et al., 2022a
)
is a related technique that requires a singular value decomposition (SVD), which is prohibitive in many applications.

There are other ways to design posterior samplers.
For example,
Laumont et al. (2022)
and
Jalal et al. (2021)
use Langevin dynamics.
RED-diff
(
Mardani et al., 2024
)
and SNORE
(
Renaud et al., 2024
)
inject white noise into the RED algorithm
(
Romano et al., 2017
)
, whose regularizer’s gradient equals the score function
(
Reehorst & Schniter, 2019
)
.

Bouman & Buzzard (2023)
;
Coeurdoux et al. (2024)
;
Wu et al. (2024)
;
Xu & Chi (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2025)
use Markov-chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) in the diffusion reverse process.

3
Approach

We aim to accurately approximate the conditional denoiser
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
at each step of the diffusion reverse process.
We treat the standard linear model (SLM) (
6
) first and the generalized linear model (GLM) later.

3.1
Fast iterative renoising (FIRE) for the SLM

In this section, we describe the FIRE algorithm, which approximates
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{r}_{\mathsf{init}},\bm{y}\}
assuming
\bm{y}
from (
6
) and
\bm{r}_{\mathsf{init}}=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{init}}\bm{\epsilon}
with
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and some
\sigma_{\mathsf{init}}>0
.
FIRE performs half-quadratic splitting (HQS) PnP with a scheduled denoising variance
\sigma^{2}
, similar to DPIR from
Zhang et al. (2021)
, but injects colored noise
\bm{c}
to ensure that the error in the denoiser input
\bm{r}
remains white.
It is beneficial for the denoiser to see white input error during inference, because it is trained to remove white input error.
The basic FIRE algorithm iterates the following steps
N\geq 1
times, after initializing
\bm{r}\leftarrow\bm{r}_{\mathsf{init}}
and
\sigma\leftarrow\sigma_{\mathsf{init}}
:

S1)

Denoise
\bm{r}
assuming AWGN of variance
\sigma^{2}
, giving
\bar{\bm{x}}
.

S2)

MMSE estimate
\bm{x}_{0}
given
\bm{y}
from (
6
) and the prior
\bm{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\bm{x}},\nu\bm{I})
with some
\nu>0
, giving
\hat{\bm{x}}
.

S3)

Update the denoising variance
\sigma^{2}
via
\sigma^{2}\leftarrow\sigma^{2}/\rho
with some
\rho>1
,

S4)

Update
\bm{r}\leftarrow\hat{\bm{x}}+\bm{c}
using colored Gaussian noise
\bm{c}
created to ensure
\cov\{\bm{r}-\bm{x}_{0}\}=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}
.

S1)
-
S3)
are essentially DPIR with regularization strength controlled by
\nu
and a geometric denoising schedule with rate controlled by
\rho
, while
S4)
injects colored noise.
In contrast, other renoising PnP approaches like SNORE
(
Renaud et al., 2024
)
inject white noise.
Section
4.1
examines the effect of removing
\bm{c}
or replacing it with white noise.
Next we provide details and enhancements of the basic FIRE algorithm.

In the sequel, we use “
\bm{d}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\sigma)
” to denote a neural-net approximation of the conditional-mean denoiser
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}\}
of
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}
with
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
.
Given a score function approximation
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)\approx\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x})
as discussed in Sec.
2
, the denoiser can be constructed via (
5
) as

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},\sigma)=\bm{x}+\sigma^{2}\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},t)\text{~with $t$ such that $\sigma_{t}=\sigma$}.

(7)

When FIRE estimates
\bm{x}_{0}
from the measurements
\bm{y}
and the denoiser output
\bar{\bm{x}}
, it employs a Gaussian approximation of the form
\bm{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\bm{x}},\nu\bm{I})
, similar to DDS,
DiffPIR,
and prox-based PnP algorithms.
But it differs in that
\nu
is explicitly estimated.
The Gaussian approximation
\bm{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\bm{x}},\nu\bm{I})
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{0}=\bar{\bm{x}}+\sqrt{\nu}\bm{e},~~\bm{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}).

(8)

Suppose
\bm{x}_{0}=\bar{\bm{x}}+\sqrt{\nu_{0}}\bm{e}
with
\bm{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
, where
\nu_{0}
denotes the true error variance.
Then (
6
) and (
8
) imply

\displaystyle\E\{\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bar{\bm{x}}\|^{2}\}=\E\{\|\bm{Ax}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}-\bm{A}\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{\nu_{0}}\bm{A}\bm{e}\|^{2}\}=\E\{\|\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}+\sqrt{\nu_{0}}\bm{A}\bm{e}\|^{2}\}=m\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+\nu_{0}\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2},

(9)

assuming independence between
\bm{e}
and
\bm{w}
.
Consequently, an unbiased estimate of
\nu_{0}
can be constructed as

\displaystyle\big(\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bar{\bm{x}}\|^{2}-m\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}\big)/\|\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}\triangleq\nu.

(10)

Figure
12
shows that, in practice, the estimate (
10
) accurately tracks the true error variance
\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bar{\bm{x}}\|^{2}/d
.

Under (
6
) and (
8
), the MMSE estimate of
\bm{x}_{0}
from
\bm{y}
and
\bar{\bm{x}}
can be written as
(
Poor, 1994
)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\arg\min_{\bm{x}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}\|\bm{y}-\bm{Ax}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\nu}\|\bm{x}-\bar{\bm{x}}\|^{2}\bigg\}=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bar{\bm{x}}\bigg).

(11)

Equation (
11
) can be computed using conjugate gradients (CG) or, if practical, the SVD
\bm{A}=\bm{USV}^{\mathsf{T}}
via

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}=\bm{V}\bigg(\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\bm{x}}\bigg).

(12)

In any case, from (
8
) and (
11
), the error in
\hat{\bm{x}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}\!+\!\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}[\bm{A}\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}]+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}[\bm{x}_{0}\!-\!\sqrt{\nu}\bm{e}]\bigg)\!-\!\bm{x}_{0}=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}\!+\!\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}\!-\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\sqrt{\nu}}\bm{e}\bigg),

(13)

and so the covariance of the error in
\hat{\bm{x}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\cov\{\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}_{0}\}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{4}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{1}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\triangleq\bm{C}.

(14)

From (
14
) we see that the error in
\hat{\bm{x}}
can be strongly colored.
For example, in the case of inpainting, where
\bm{A}
is formed from rows of the identity matrix, the error variance in the masked pixels equals
\nu
, while the error variance in the unmasked pixels equals
(1/\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu)^{-1}\leq\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}
.
These two values may differ by many orders of magnitude.
Since most denoisers are trained to remove white noise with a specified variance of
\sigma^{2}
, direct denoising of
\hat{\bm{x}}
performs poorly, as we show in Sec.
4.1
.

To circumvent the issues that arise from colored denoiser-input error, we propose to add “complementary” colored Gaussian noise
\bm{c}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
to
\hat{\bm{x}}
so that the resulting
\bm{r}=\hat{\bm{x}}+\bm{c}
has an error covariance of
\sigma^{2}\bm{I}
, i.e., white error.
This requires that

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}-\bm{C}=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}-\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}\bm{V}\bm{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{SV}^{\mathsf{T}}+\frac{1}{\nu}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}=\bm{V}\Diag(\bm{\lambda})\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\text{~for~}\lambda_{i}=\sigma^{2}-\frac{1}{s_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu}

(15)

for

Note that a key assumption of Theorem

Because this may not hold in practice, we propose to replace

3.3
Putting FIRE into diffusion

Sections
3.1
and
3.2
detailed the FIRE algorithms for the SLM (
6
) and the GLM (
22
), respectively.
In both cases, the FIRE algorithm approximates
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{r},\bm{y}\}
given the measurements
\bm{y}
and the side-information
\bm{r}=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
.
Thus, recalling the discussion in Sec.
2
, FIRE can be used in the SMLD reverse process as an approximation of
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
by setting
\bm{r}=\bm{x}_{t}
and
\sigma=\sigma_{t}
.

Instead of using SMLD for the diffusion reverse process, however, we use DDIM from
Song et al. (2021a)
, which can be considered as a generalization of SMLD.
In the sequel, we distinguish the DDIM quantities by writing them with subscript
k
.
As detailed in App.
C
, DDIM is based on the model

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma_{k}\bm{\epsilon}_{k},\quad\bm{\epsilon}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),

(25)

for
k=1,\dots,K
, where
\{\sigma_{k}^{2}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a specified sequence of variances.
The DDIM reverse process iterates

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=h_{k}\bm{x}_{k}+g_{k}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\varsigma_{k}\bm{n}_{k}

(26)

\displaystyle\varsigma_{k}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}(\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k-1}^{2})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}},~~h_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}-\varsigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}},~~g_{k}=1-h_{k}

(27)

over
k=K,\dots,2,1
, starting from
\bm{x}_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{K}^{2}\bm{I})
, using i.i.d
\{\bm{n}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and some
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\geq 0
.
When
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}=1
and
K=T
, DDIM reduces to SMLD.
But when
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}=0
, the DDIM reverse process (
26
) is deterministic and can be considered as a discretization of the probability-flow ODE
Song et al. (2021a)
, which can outperform SMLD when the number of discretization steps
K
is small
(
Chen et al., 2023
)
.

For a specified number
K
of DDIM steps (which we treat as a tuning parameter), we set the DDIM variances
\{\sigma_{k}^{2}\}_{k=1}^{K}
as the geometric sequence

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}=\sigma_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}\Big(\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{k-1}{K-1}},~~k=1,\dots,K

(28)

for some
\sigma_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}
that are typically chosen to match the minimum and maximum variances used to train the denoiser
\bm{d}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)
or score approximation
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
So for example, if
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot)
was trained over the DDPM steps
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
for
T=1000
, then we would set
\sigma_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}=(1-\overline{\alpha}_{1})/\overline{\alpha}_{1}
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}=(1-\overline{\alpha}_{1000})/\overline{\alpha}_{1000}
with
\overline{\alpha}_{t}
; see (
37
) for additional details.

Next we discuss how we set the FIRE iteration schedule
\{N_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and variance-decrease-factor
\rho>1
.
In doing so, we have two main goals:

G1)

Ensure that, at every DDIM step
k
, the denoiser’s output-error variance is at most
\nu_{\mathsf{thresh}}
at the final FIRE iteration, where
\nu_{\mathsf{thresh}}
is some value to be determined.

G2)

Meet a fixed budget of
N_{\mathsf{tot}}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}
total NFEs.

Note that, because the denoiser’s output-error variance increases monotonically with its input-error variance, we can rephrase
G1)
as

G1enumi)

Ensure that, at every DDIM step
k
, the denoiser’s input-error variance is at most
\sigma_{\mathsf{thresh}}^{2}
at the final FIRE iteration, where
\sigma_{\mathsf{thresh}}^{2}
is some value to be determined.

Although
\sigma_{\mathsf{thresh}}^{2}
could be tuned directly, it’s not the most convenient option because a good search range can be difficult to construct.
Instead, we tune the fraction
\delta\in[0,1)
of DDIM steps
k
that use a single FIRE iteration (i.e., that use
N_{k}=1
) and we set
\sigma_{\mathsf{thresh}}^{2}
at the DDIM variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
of the first reverse-process step
k
that uses a single FIRE iteration, i.e.,
1+\lfloor(K-1)\delta\rfloor\triangleq k_{\mathsf{thresh}}
.
(Note that
k_{\mathsf{thresh}}=1
when
\delta=0
and
k_{\mathsf{thresh}}=K-1
for
\delta\approx 1
.)
All subsequent
1
1

1

Recall that the reverse process counts backwards, i.e.,
k=K,K\!-\!1,\dots,2,1
.
DDIM steps
k<k_{\mathsf{thresh}}
will then automatically satisfy
G1enumi)
because
\sigma_{k}^{2}
decreases with
k
.

To ensure that the earlier DDIM steps
k>k_{\mathsf{thresh}}
also satisfy
G1enumi)
, we need that
\sigma^{2}_{k}/\rho^{N_{k}-1}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{thresh}}
, since
\sigma^{2}_{k}
is the denoiser input-error variance at the first FIRE iteration and
\sigma^{2}_{k}/\rho^{N_{k}-1}
is the denoiser input-error variance at the last FIRE iteration.
For a fixed
\rho>1
, we can rewrite this inequality as

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ln\sigma^{2}_{k}-\ln\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{thresh}}}{\ln\rho}+1\triangleq\underline{N}_{k}.

(29)

Because
N_{k}
is a positive integer, it suffices to choose

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle=\lceil\max\{1,\underline{N}_{k}\}\rceil~\forall k.

(30)

\rho

We find this value using bisection search.
For a given

k_{\mathsf{thresh}}\cdot 1+(K-k_{\mathsf{thresh}})\cdot 2

The definition of

then implies that

N_{\mathsf{tot}}\geq K(2-\delta)+\delta-1
and thus

K\leq(N_{\mathsf{tot}}+1-\delta)/(2-\delta)\triangleq K_{\mathsf{min}}

3.4
Relation to other methods

To solve inverse problems, a number of algorithms have been proposed that iterate denoising (possibly score-based), data-consistency (hard or soft), and renoising.
Such approaches are referred to as either plug-and-play, Langevin, or diffusion methods.
(Recall the discussion in Sections
1
-
2
.)
While existing approaches use white renoising, the proposed DDfire uses colored renoising that whitens the denoiser input error.

When estimating
\bm{x}_{0}
from the measurements
\bm{y}
of (
6
), methods such as DDS, DiffPIR, DAPS, and SNORE use a Gaussian prior approximation of the form
\bm{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\bm{x}},\nu\bm{I})
.
But while they control
\nu
with tuning parameters,
DDfire explicitly estimates
\nu
via (
10
).
See App.
G
for a detailed comparison of DDfire to DDS, DiffPIR, and SNORE.

Although we are unaware of prior work combining EP with diffusion, there is a paper by
Meng:AAAI:24
that applies EP to the annealed Langevin dynamics approach from
Song & Ermon (2020)
, although for the specific task of dequantization.
Like with DPS and
\Pi
GDM, Bayes rule is used to write
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})
and the first term is approximated by
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t},t)
(recall Sec.
2
).
The second term is approximated with multiple EP iterations, each of which solves a linear system using an SVD.
DDfire differs in using colored renoising, an SVD-free option, and DDIM diffusion, which allows far fewer NFEs.

5
Conclusion

To solve linear inverse problems, we proposed the Fast Iterative Renoising (FIRE) algorithm, which can be interpreted as the HQS plug-and-play algorithm with a colored renoising step that aims to whiten the denoiser input error.
We then extended the linear FIRE algorithm to the generalized-linear case using expectation propagation (EP).
Since these FIRE algorithms approximate the measurement-conditional denoiser
\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}\}
, or equivalently the measurement-conditional score
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})
, they can be readily combined with DDIM for diffusion posterior sampling, giving the “DDfire” algorithm.
Experiments on box inpainting, Gaussian and motion deblurring, and 4
\times
super-resolution with FFHQ and ImageNet images show DDfire outperforming DDRM,
\Pi
GDM, DDS, DiffPIR, DPS, RED-diff, and DAPS in PSNR, LPIPS, and FID metrics in nearly all cases.
Experiments on noisy FFHQ and ImageNet phase retrieval (both OSF and CDP versions) show DDfire outperforming HIO, prDeep, DOLPH, DPS, RED-diff, and DAPS in all cases.
Finally, DDfire offers fast inference, with better LPIPS-versus-runtime curves than the competitors.

Appendix A
VP formulation

In the main text, we describe DDfire for the VE SDE formulation from
Song et al. (2021b)
and the corresponding SMLD discretization from
Song & Ermon (2019)
.
Here, we describe it for the VP SDE from
Song et al. (2021b)
and the corresponding DDPM discretization from
Ho et al. (2020)
.

From
Song et al. (2021b)
, the general SDE forward process can be written as

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{w}

(31)

for some choices of
\bm{f}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
, where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{w}
is the standard Wiener process (i.e., Brownian motion).
The reverse process can then be described by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\big(\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ln p_{t}(\bm{x})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}},

(32)

where
p_{t}(\cdot)
is the distribution of
\bm{x}
at time
t
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}}
is the reverse Wiener process.
In the VE-SDE,
\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{0}
and
g(t)=\sqrt{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\sigma^{2}(t)]/\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}
for some variance schedule
\sigma^{2}(t)
, but in the VP-SDE,
\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)\bm{x}
and
g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)}
for some variance schedule
\beta(t)
.
When discretized to
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, the VP forward process becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t-1}

(33)

with i.i.d.
\{\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
, so that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t}

(34)

with
\alpha_{t}\triangleq 1-\beta_{t}
,

\overline{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
, and

\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
.
Throughout, we write the VP quantities with tildes to distinguish them from the VE quantities.
The DDPM reverse process then takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}+\beta_{t}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\ln p(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})\big)+\Sigma_{t}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{t}\quad\text{with}\quad\Sigma_{t}^{2}\triangleq\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}

(35)

and is typically initialized at
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
.
By rewriting (
34
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}}\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t}

(36)

and comparing it to (
3
), we recognize the VP/VE relationships

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}=\sigma_{t}^{2}~\Leftrightarrow~\overline{\alpha}_{t}=\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(37)

Furthermore, assuming that
\overline{\alpha}_{T}\ll 1
, the VP initialization
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
is well approximated by the VE initialization
\bm{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{T}^{2}\bm{I})
.

Appendix B
DDIM details for VP

The DDIM reverse process from
Song et al. (2021a)
provides an alternative to the DDPM reverse process that offers a flexible level of stochasticity.
When describing VP DDIM, we will write the quantities as
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k},\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k},\overline{\alpha}_{k}
to distinguish them from the corresponding VP DDPM quantities
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{n}}_{t},\overline{\alpha}_{t}
, and we will write the total number of steps as
K
.
Like (
34
), DDIM is built around the model

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k},\quad\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}).

(38)

Adapting the first two equations from
Song et al. (2021a, App.D.3)
to our notation, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\bigg(\frac{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}-\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}\bigg)+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}\,\E\{\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}

(39)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}

(40)

with
\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and tunable
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\geq 0
.
When
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}=1
and
K=T
, DDIM reduces to DDPM.
But when
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}=0
, the reverse process is deterministic.
In fact, it can be considered a discretization of the probability flow ODE
(
Song et al., 2021a
)
, which often works much better than the SDE when the number of discretization steps
K
is small.
We now write (
39
) in a simpler form.
Applying
\E\{\cdot|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}
to both sides of (
38
) gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\E\{\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}-\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}}{\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}},

(41)

and plugging (
41
) into (
39
) gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}-\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}}{\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}\bigg)

(42)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}+\widetilde{h}_{k}\big(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}-\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}\big)

(43)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{h}_{k}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}+\widetilde{g}_{k}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}

(44)

for

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{g}_{k}\triangleq\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}-\widetilde{h}_{k}\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}.

(45)

Thus the VP DDIM reverse process can be described by (
40
), (
44
), and (
45
) with
\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})~\forall k
and initialization
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
.

Appendix C
DDIM details for VE

We now provide the details for the VE version of DDIM.
Starting with the VP DDIM reverse process (
44
), we can divide both sides by
\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k-1}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{h}_{k}\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}\frac{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}+\frac{\widetilde{g}_{k}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\frac{\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}\tilde{\bm{n}}_{k}

(46)

and leveraging the VP-to-VE relationship (
37
) to write

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=h_{k}\bm{x}_{k}+g_{k}\E\{\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}\}+\varsigma_{k}\bm{n}_{k}\quad\text{with}\quad h_{k}=\frac{\widetilde{h}_{k}\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}},\quad g_{k}=\frac{\widetilde{g}_{k}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}},\quad\varsigma_{k}=\frac{\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}

(47)

with
\bm{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})~\forall k
and initialization
\bm{x}_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{K}^{2}\bm{I})
.
Plugging
\widetilde{g}_{k}
from (
45
) into (
47
), we find

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}-\widetilde{h}_{k}\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}=1-h_{k}.

(48)

Then plugging (
40
) into (
47
), we find

\displaystyle\varsigma_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}}{\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\right)}

(49)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}-\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\right)}=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\left([1+\sigma_{k}^{2}]-[1+\sigma_{k-1}^{2}]\right)}

(50)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}(\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k-1}^{2})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}.

(51)

Finally, noting from (
37
), (
47
), and (
51
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\varsigma_{k}^{2}\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}=\frac{\varsigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}=\frac{\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}^{2}}{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}(\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k-1}^{2})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}=\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right),

(52)

we plug
\widetilde{h}_{k}
from (
45
) into (
47
) to find

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}}

(53)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{k}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k}}\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}{\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\bigg(1-\frac{\widetilde{\varsigma}_{k}^{2}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}}\bigg)}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\bigg(1-\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}^{2}\bigg(1-\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg)}

(54)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\bigg(1-\frac{\varsigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}\bigg)}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\varsigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k-1}^{2}-\varsigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}.

(55)

The VE DDIM reverse process is summarized in (
26
)-(
27
).

Appendix D
Speeding up CG

In this section, we describe a small modification to FIRE that can help to speed up the CG step.
When CG is used to solve (
11
), its convergence speed is determined by the condition number of
\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+(\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\nu)\bm{I}

(
Luenberger & Ye, 2016
)
.
Thus CG can converge slowly when
\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\nu
is small, which can happen in early DDfire iterations.
To speed up CG, we propose to solve (
11
) using
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}
in place of
\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
, for some
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}>\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
.
Since the condition number of
\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+(\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\nu)\bm{I}
is at most
\nu s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+1
, we can guarantee a conditional number of at most
10\,001
by setting

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}=\nu s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}\max\{10^{-4},\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}/(\nu s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2})\}.

(56)

Although using
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}>\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
in (
11
) will degrade the MSE of
\hat{\bm{x}}
, the degradation is partially offset by the fact that less noise will be added when renoising
\bm{r}
.
In any case, the modified (
11
) can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu)^{-1}(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bar{\bm{x}}/\nu)

(57)

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu)^{-1}(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}[\bm{Ax}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}]/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+[\bm{x}_{0}-\sqrt{\nu}\bm{e}]/\nu)

(58)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu)^{-1}(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}-\bm{e}/\sqrt{\nu}),

(59)

in which case
\hat{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{C})
with covariance

\displaystyle\bm{C}

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu)^{-1}\big(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}/\widehat{\sigma}^{4}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu\big)(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}/\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu)^{-1}

(60)

\displaystyle=\big(\bm{VS}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{SV}^{\mathsf{T}}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+\bm{I}/\nu\big)^{-1}\big(\bm{VS}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{SV}^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{y}}/\widehat{\sigma}^{4}_{\mathsf{y}}+\bm{I}/\nu\big)\big(\bm{VS}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{SV}^{\mathsf{T}}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+\bm{I}/\nu\big)^{-1}

(61)

\displaystyle=\bm{V}\Diag(\bm{\gamma})\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\text{~for~}\gamma_{i}=\frac{s_{i}^{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{4}+1/\nu}{[s_{i}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu]^{2}}.

(62)

The desired renoising variance then becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}-\bm{C}=\bm{V}\Diag(\bm{\lambda})\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\text{~for~}\lambda_{i}=\sigma^{2}-\frac{s_{i}^{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{4}+1/\nu}{[s_{i}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu]^{2}}

(63)

and we can generate the colored noise
\bm{c}
via (
16
) if the SVD is practical.
If not, we approximate
\bm{\Sigma}
by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{2}-\nu)\bm{I}+\xi\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}\text{~~with~~}\xi=\frac{1}{s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}}\left(\nu-\frac{s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{4}+1/\nu}{[s_{\mathsf{max}}^{2}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu]^{2}}\right)

(64)

and generate the colored noise
\bm{c}
via (
19
).
It is straightforward to show that
\xi\geq 0
whenever
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}\geq\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
, in which case
\sigma^{2}\geq\nu
guarantees that
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
is a valid covariance matrix.
Figure
6
shows the close agreement between the ideal and approximate
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
-renoised error spectra both when
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}=\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
and when
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{y}}>\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}
.

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
1

To prove Theorem
1
, we begin by writing the key FIRE steps with explicit iteration index
n\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{x}}[n]

\displaystyle=\bm{d}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{r}[n],\sigma[n])

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}[n]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bar{\bm{x}}[n]\bigg)

(66)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}[n\!+\!1]

\displaystyle=\max\{\sigma^{2}[n]/\rho,\nu[n]\}

(67)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}[n]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}[n\!+\!1]-(s_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}+1/\nu[n])^{-1},~~i=1,\dots,d

(68)

\displaystyle\bm{n}[n]

\displaystyle=\bm{V}\Diag(\bm{\lambda}[n])^{1/2}\bm{\varepsilon}[n],~~\bm{\varepsilon}[n]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})

(69)

\displaystyle\bm{r}[n\!+\!1]

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{x}}[n]+\bm{n}[n]

(70)

Our proof uses induction.
By the assumptions of the theorem, we know that there exists an iteration
n
(in particular
n=1
) for which
\bm{r}[n]=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma[n]\bm{\epsilon}[n]
with
\bm{\epsilon}[n]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and finite
\sigma[n]
.
Then due to the denoiser assumption, we know that
\bar{\bm{x}}[n]=\bm{x}_{0}-\sqrt{\nu[n]}\bm{e}[n]
with
\bm{e}[n]=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and known
\nu[n]<\sigma^{2}[n]
.
We assume that this value of
\nu[n]
is used in lines (
66
)-(
68
).
Using these results and (
6
), we can rewrite (
66
) as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}[n]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\bm{Ax}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w}\big)+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\big(\bm{x}_{0}-\sqrt{\nu[n]}\bm{e}[n]\big)\bigg)

(71)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}-\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\sqrt{\nu[n]}}\bm{e}[n]\bigg)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{C}[n])

(72)

for

\displaystyle\bm{C}[n]

\displaystyle\triangleq\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{4}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)\bigg(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{1}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}

(73)

by leveraging the independent-Gaussian assumption on
\bm{e}[n]
.
From this and (
68
)-(
69
), we can then deduce

\displaystyle\bm{n}[n]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}[n])\text{~with~}\bm{\Sigma}[n]\triangleq\bm{V}\Diag(\bm{\lambda}[n])\bm{V}^{\mathsf{T}}=\sigma^{2}[n\!+\!1]\bm{I}-\bm{C}[n]

(74)

so that, from (
70
),

\displaystyle\bm{r}[n\!+\!1]

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{C}[n]+\bm{\Sigma}[n])=\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0},\sigma^{2}[n\!+\!1]\bm{I})

(75)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\bm{r}[n\!+\!1]

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma[n\!+\!1]\bm{\epsilon}[n\!+\!1],~~\bm{\epsilon}[n\!+\!1]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}).

(76)

Thus, by induction, if
\bm{r}[n]=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma[n]\bm{\epsilon}[n]
with
\bm{\epsilon}[n]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
holds at
n=1
, then it holds at all
n>1
.

Recall that the theorem also assumed that
\nu[n]<\sigma^{2}[n]
for all
n
.
Thus, there exists a
\rho>1
for which
\sigma^{2}[n]/\rho>\nu[n]
for all
n
, for which we can rewrite (
67
) as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}[n\!+\!1]=\sigma^{2}[n]/\rho~\forall n.

(77)

Consequently, for any iteration
n\geq 1
we can write

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}[n]=\sigma^{2}[1]/\rho^{n-1}=\sigma_{\mathsf{init}}^{2}/\rho^{n-1}.

(78)

Finally, because the error covariance on
\hat{\bm{x}}[n]
obeys

\displaystyle\bm{C}[n]=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}^{2}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\frac{1}{\nu[n]}\bm{I}\bigg)^{-1}<~\nu[n]\bm{I}~<~\sigma^{2}[n]\bm{I}=\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{init}}^{2}}{\rho^{n-1}}\bm{I}

(79)

we see that the error variance in
\hat{\bm{x}}[n]
decreases exponentially with
n
and thus
\hat{\bm{x}}[n]
converges to the true
\bm{x}_{0}
.

In this section, we compare SLM-FIRE/DDfire to other renoising PnP schemes for the SLM that involve a proximal data-fidelity step or, equivalently, MMSE estimation of
\bm{x}_{0}
under the Gaussian prior assumption (
8
):
DDS, DiffPIR, and SNORE, which are detailed in Alg.
4
, Alg.
5
, and Alg.
6
, respectively.

Comparing SLM-FIRE/DDfire (see Alg.
1
and Alg.
2
) to DDS, we see that both approaches use CG for approximate MMSE estimation under a Gaussian prior approximation and both use a DDIM diffusion update.
But DDS uses the hyperparameters
\gamma_{\mathsf{dds}}
and
M_{\mathsf{cg}}
(the number CG iterations, which is usually small, such as four) to adjust the noise variance
\nu
in the Gaussian prior approximation (
8
), while SLM-FIRE/DDfire estimates
\nu
at each iteration.
Also, SLM-FIRE/DDfire injects colored Gaussian noise to whiten the denoiser input error while DDS injects no noise outside of DDIM.

Comparing SLM–IRE/DDfire to DiffPIR, we see that both perform MMSE estimation under a Gaussian prior approximation and both use a DDIM-like diffusion update.
But DiffPIR uses the hyperparameter
\lambda_{\mathsf{diffpir}}
to adjust the noise variance
\nu
in the Gaussian prior approximation (
8
), while SLM-FIRE/DDfire estimates
\nu
at each iteration.
Also, SLM-FIRE/DDfire injects colored Gaussian noise to whiten the denoiser input error while DiffPIR injects no noise outside of its DDIM-like step.

Comparing SLM-FIRE to SNORE, we see that both perform MMSE estimation under a Gaussian prior approximation and both use renoising.
But SNORE uses white renoising while SLM-FIRE uses colored renoising to whiten the denoiser input error.
Also, SNORE uses the hyperparameter
\delta_{\mathsf{snore}}
to adjust the noise variance
\nu
in the Gaussian prior approximation (
8
), while SLM-FIRE estimates it at each iteration.
Furthermore, since SNORE is based on the RED algorithm
(
Romano et al., 2017
)
, its denoiser output is scaled and shifted.
Finally, SNORE has many more tuning parameters than SLM-FIRE.

Appendix H
Implementation details

H.1
Inverse problems

For the linear inverse problems, the measurements were generated as

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\bm{Ax}_{0}+\sigma_{\mathsf{y}}\bm{w},~~\bm{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})

(80)

with appropriate
\bm{A}
.
For box inpainting, Gaussian deblurring, and super-resolution we used the
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
implementations from
Kawar et al. (2022b)
.
For motion deblurring, we implemented our own
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
with reflect padding.
All methods used these operators implementations except DiffPIR, which used the authors’ implementations.
Motion-blur kernels were generated using
Borodenko (2020)
.

For phase retrieval, the measurements were generated using the method from
Metzler et al. (2018)
:

\displaystyle y_{j}^{2}=|z_{0,j}|^{2}+w_{j},~~w_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha_{\mathsf{shot}}^{2}|z_{0,j}|^{2}),~~j=1,\dots,m,

(81)

where
\alpha_{\mathsf{shot}}
controls the noise level and
\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{Ax}_{0}
, with the values of
\bm{x}_{0}
scaled to lie in the range
[0,255]
.
This is an approximation of the Poisson shot-noise corruption model in that the intensity
y_{j}^{2}/\alpha_{\mathsf{shot}}^{2}
is approximately
\mathrm{Poisson}((|z_{0,j}|/\alpha_{\mathsf{shot}})^{2})
distributed for sufficiently small values of
\alpha_{\mathsf{shot}}
.
We implemented the oversampled-Fourier
\bm{A}
by zero-padding the image by 2
\times
in each direction and then passing the result through a unitary FFT.
For CDP phase retrieval, we set
\bm{A}=[\bm{A}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\dots,\bm{A}_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
for
\bm{A}_{l}=L^{-1/2}\bm{F}\Diag(\bm{c}_{l})
, where
\bm{F}
is a
d\times d
FFT and
\bm{c}_{l}
contain i.i.d. random entries uniformly distributed on the unit circle in the complex plane, and where
L=4
.
In both cases,
\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}=\bm{I}
.

H.2
Evaluation protocol

For the linear inverse problems, we run each method once for each measurement
\bm{y}
in the 1000-sample test set and compute average PSNR, average LPIPS, and FID from the resulting recoveries.

For OSF phase retrieval, following
Chung et al. (2023a)
, we run each algorithm four times and keep the reconstruction
\hat{\bm{x}}
that minimizes the measurement residual
\|\bm{y}-|\bm{A}\hat{\bm{x}}|\|
.
Performance metrics are then evaluated after resolving the inherent spatial shift and conjugate flip ambiguities associated with phase retrieval (see, e.g.,
Bendory et al. (2015)
).
Note global phase ambiguity is not an issue due to the non-negativity of our images.
For the CDP experiments, we run each algorithm only once and don’t perform ambiguity resolution, because it is unnecessary.

DDRM.

We ran DDRM for 20 NFEs using the authors’ implementation from
Kawar et al. (2022b)
with minor changes to work with our codebase.

DiffPIR.

We ran DiffPIR for 20 NFEs using the authors’ implementation from
Zhu et al. (2024)
without modification.
Hyperparameters were set according to the reported values in
Zhu et al. (2023)
.

\Pi
GDM.

We ran
\Pi
GDM for 100 NFEs.
Since the authors do not provide a
\Pi
GDM implementation for noisy inverse problems in
NVlabs (2023)
, we coded
\Pi
GDM ourselves in Python/PyTorch.
With problems for which an SVD is available, we computed
(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}+\zeta_{k}\bm{I})^{-1}
using the efficient SVD implementation of
\bm{A}
from the DDRM codebase
Kawar et al. (2022b)
, and otherwise we used CG.

DDS.

We ran DDS for 100 NFEs. We leveraged the authors’ implementation in
Chung et al. (2024b)
to reimplement DDS in our codebase.
We tuned the DDS regularization parameter
\gamma_{\mathsf{dds}}
via grid search and used
\eta_{\mathsf{ddim}}=0.8
and
50
CG iterations.

DPS.

For the linear inverse problems, we ran DPS for 1000 NFEs using the authors’ implementation from
Chung et al. (2023b)
without modification, using the suggested tuning from
Chung et al. (2023a, Sec. D.1)
.

For phase retrieval, we also used 1000 NFEs, but made minor adjustments to the DPS authors’ implementation to accommodate the likelihood
p_{\mathsf{y|z}}(y|z)=\mathcal{N}(y;|z|,\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{y}})
, which was used by all methods for fairness. We used grid-search tuning to minimize LPIPS on a 100-image validation set.

RED-diff.

We ran RED-diff for 1000 NFEs using the authors’ implementation from
NVlabs (2023)
, with minor changes to work with our codebase.
We tuned the RED-diff learning rate,
\lambda
, and data fidelity weight
v_{t}
to minimize LPIPS with a 100-image validation set.
For phase retrieval, we made similar code modifications to handle our likelihood as with DPS.

DAPS.

We ran DAPS for 1000 NFEs using the authors’ implementation from
Zhang et al. (2024)
, with minor changes to work in our codebase.
The tuning parameters were set as in
Zhang et al. (2025)
.
For phase retrieval, we made similar code modifications to handle our likelihood as with DPS.

DOLPH.

We ran DOLPH for 1000 NFEs.
Since the DOLPH authors did not release an implementation, we implemented it ourselves in Python/PyTorch and used grid-search to find the step-size that minimized LPIPS on a 100-image validation set.
For phase retrieval, we made similar code modifications to handle our likelihood as with DPS.

HIO.

We translated the MATLAB implementation of HIO from
Metzler (2018)
to Python and set the step-size parameter to
0.9
.
We then followed the runtime procedure described in
Metzler et al. (2018)
:
For the OSF experiments, HIO is first run 50 times, for 50 iterations each, from a random initialization.
The estimate
\hat{\bm{x}}
with the lowest measurement residual
\|\bm{y}-|\bm{A}\hat{\bm{x}}|\|
is then used to reinitialize HIO, after which it is run for 1000 more iterations.
Finally, the second and third color channels in the result are shifted and flipped as needed to best match the first color channel.
For the CDP experiments, HIO is run once for 200 iterations from a random initialization.

prDeep.

We used the Python implementation from
Hekstra et al. (2018)
.
As recommended in
Metzler et al. (2018)
, we initialized prDeep with the HIO estimate for OSF experiments and with an all-ones initialization for CDP experiments
(The HIO estimate was also used by DDfire for ImageNet OSF).
We tuned
\lambda
on a grid to minimize LPIPS on a 100-image validation set.

H.5
DDfire with guidance

Algorithm
7
details an extension of DDfire that takes guidance from an externally provided estimate
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}
.
The approach can be explained as follows.
Recall from (
38
) and App.
C
that the iteration-
k
VE DDIM estimate
\bm{x}_{k}
is assumed to obey the model

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{k}^{2}\bm{I}).

(82)

If we set
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}=\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}+\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}\bm{v}_{k}
with
\bm{v}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and sufficiently large
\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}
, then it is reasonable to model

\displaystyle p(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}};\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}\bm{I}).

(83)

Furthermore, if
\bm{v}_{k}
is generated independently of
\bm{x}_{k}
at each iteration
k
, then

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}_{k},\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{k}^{2}\bm{I})\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}};\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}\bm{I})

(84)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0};\bm{x}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}\bm{I})\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0};\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}},\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}\bm{I})

(85)

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{0};\bm{r}_{k},\nu_{k}\bm{I})\text{~~for~}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bm{r}_{k}=\frac{\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}}\bm{x}_{k}+\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}\\
\displaystyle\nu_{k}=\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}}\Big)^{-1}\end{cases}

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\bm{r}_{k};\bm{x}_{0},\nu_{k}\bm{I}),

(87)

which means that i)
\bm{r}_{k}
is a sufficient statistic for the estimation of
\bm{x}_{0}
from
\bm{x}_{k}
and
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}

(
Poor, 1994
)
and ii) that
\bm{r}_{k}
can be modeled as an AWGN-corrupted version of
\bm{x}_{0}
with AWGN variance
\nu_{k}
.
Thus, whereas standard DDfire passes
(\bm{x}_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k})
to FIRE in iteration
k
, the guided version of DDfire instead passes
(\bm{r}_{k},\nu_{k})
to FIRE at iteration
k
.
Note that this necessitates that
\sigma^{2}_{k}
be replaced by
\nu_{k}
when computing
\underline{N}_{k}
in (
29
).
Thus concludes the explanation of Alg.
7
.
As for the choice of
\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{guide}}
, it should be large enough relative to the error variance in
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}
for (
83
) to hold.
Thus we suggest choosing
\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{guide}}
to be at least
10\times
the error variance in
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}
, which can be estimated using (
10
).
But as
\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{guide}}
grows larger, less guidance is provided.
For example, if
\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{guide}}
is large relative to
\sigma_{K}^{2}
, then essentially no guidance will be provided (since
\bm{r}_{k}\approx\bm{x}_{k}
and
\nu_{k}\approx\sigma_{k}^{2}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
when
\sigma^{2}_{\mathsf{guide}}\gg\sigma_{K}^{2}
).
For ImageNet OSF phase retrieval, we set
\sigma_{\mathsf{guide}}^{2}
at
50\times
the error variance of
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{guide}}=\hat{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{HIO}}
.
