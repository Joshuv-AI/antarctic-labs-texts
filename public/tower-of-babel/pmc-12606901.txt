Analysis and comparison of chickenpox outbreaks in two schools at different educational stages: a mixed retrospective observational study
Authors: Hongxin Lyu, Minhua Chen, Haozheng Zhou, Zhen Zhang, Yu Zeng, Huawei Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaricella (commonly known as chickenpox) poses a major public health challenge in China, frequently triggering public health emergencies within schools. Distinct age demographics and vaccination rates drive divergent epidemic patterns in primary versus high schools, necessitating comparative analysis of outbreak characteristics and interventions to optimize targeted prevention strategies.MethodsA mixed retrospective observational study was conducted to systematically assess varicella outbreaks across two Shenzhen schools, quantifying key epidemiological parameters including outbreak duration and serial intervals. We employed a multidimensional analytical framework to characterize intergenerational transmission patterns and compare epidemic progression between distinct educational stages. Intervention effectiveness was rigorously evaluated through stratified analyses of gender-specific attack rates and vaccine-conferred immunity.ResultsThe proportion of cases presenting with a rash as the initial symptom in primary school students (60.41%) was significantly higher compared to high school students (34.78%) (χ2 = 9.28, P = 0.002). Fever was found to correlate with a longer recovery period for high school students (P = 0.01), while breakthrough varicella (BV) cases in primary school had a shorter recovery time at School A (10.15 ± 2.83 days) compared to School B (P = 0.003). The duration of illness was longer for primary school cases than for high school cases (15.0 days vs. 9.5 days) in the fourth generation stage. The varicella vaccine offered enhanced protection for primary school students at all stages of outbreak. We observed that the absence of emergency vaccination implementation and class suspension was associated with OR of 3.336 (95%CI:1.272–8.747) and 2.122 (95%CI:1.130–3.985), respectively. Intervention measures exhibited varying effects on disease incidence based on gender in both schools. Moreover, emergency vaccination in School A worked across all vaccination history groups, while School B’s closure/disinfection only reduced cases in two-dose history recipients (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe characteristics of individuals affected by varicella outbreaks varied across different stages of education. Analyzing intergenerational epidemic data can enhance our understanding of the pattern of epidemic transmission. Vaccine history in older children exhibited a relatively limited impact on morbidity rates. Background

Varicella is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by infection with the varicella-zoster virus (VZV), primarily affecting children and adolescents. It is transmitted through inhalation of droplets containing respiratory secretions from individuals with chickenpox or shingles rash, leading to widespread papules and blisters on the body [1]. Additionally, it can result in severe complications such as secondary skin and soft tissue infections, pneumonia, and central nervous system disorders [2].

Varicella is a significant infectious disease that has given rise to public health emergency events (PHEE) in educational institutions, including schools and kindergartens, in China. It has emerged as a considerably grave public health concern [3, 4]. The occurrence of varicella PHEE primarily concentrates in schools, accounting for over 99% of the total varicella PHEE cases, with primary schools being predominantly affected. Nevertheless, in recent years, there has been an upward trend in reported varicella cases among junior high schools, high schools, and universities [5].

The varicella vaccine (VarV) is the most effective measure for preventing and controlling chickenpox, exhibiting significant efficacy in reducing the incidence and mortality rate of the disease. In healthy children, vaccination with VarV has demonstrated an efficacy ranging from 84% to 86% in completely preventing chickenpox [6, 7]. However, following a single dose of VarV vaccination, there may be a rapid decline in serum antibody positivity rates over time [8], leading to breakthrough cases during outbreaks [9]. The majority of chickenpox cases predominantly affect individuals aged below 6 years [10]. Relevant subsequent investigations [11] have revealed that individuals tend to develop an enhanced resistance against vesicles as they age; conversely, younger students demonstrate a lower level of infection resistance and inadequate protection against varicella-zoster virus invasion [12]. As the grade level advances, there is an inverse relationship between the incidence rate of chickenpox and age due to the development of immunity through active or passive immunization [3]. Consequently, changes in epidemic cases occurrence are expected.

Primary and secondary schools are main venues for chickenpox outbreaks, thereby constituting critical domains for epidemic outbreak prevention and control. The immature development of immune systems among elementary school students, coupled with their active participation in group activities during extracurricular engagements, complicates the implementation of prompt isolation protocols when sporadic varicella cases emerge [13]. This epidemiological characteristic presents significant challenges in containing disease transmission within educational settings. Moreover, their limited comprehension of interview questions hampers the investigation of epidemiological clues through analytical methods. Conversely, the onset epidemic outbreaks in high schools pose a challenge to the cooperation with emergency immunization due to some parents’ inability to express consent for vaccination. Additionally, high schools exhibit distinct epidemic characteristics compared to primary schools due to variations in vaccine coverage and higher transmission rates resulting from heavy academic workloads and prolonged periods spent studying within enclosed classrooms. The findings in Beijing, China [14] demonstrated a significant increase in the secondary infection rate when students with rashes were not promptly isolated within their enclosed classrooms, thus confirming the propensity for virus transmission in such settings.

To elucidate the epidemiological similarities and divergences in chickenpox outbreaks across distinct educational stages, while establishing an evidence-based framework for context-specific containment strategies, this investigation employed an epidemiologically matched case-control design to systematically compare transmission dynamics, outbreak progression patterns, and intervention efficacy between varicella outbreaks occurring in elementary versus secondary school environments. Through multidimensional analysis of outbreak trajectories, vaccination coverage impact, and containment protocol effectiveness, the study delineates critical differentials in epidemic propagation mechanisms across developmental stages, providing an evidence base for stratified public health responses in educational institutions.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This retrospective study encompassed all students involved in two varicella outbreaks that occurred in two different schools from September 2023 to July 2024. Based on the epidemiological characteristics of chickenpox transmission and the activities during the outbreak, cases and controls were selected from the affected grades in each school during the outbreak period. Previous studies have indicated that individuals with a history of chickenpox infection have a less than 1% chance of reacquiring chickenpox. Hence, relevant controls without any documented history of chickenpox infection were selected for this study. Non-clinically diagnosed cases of chickenpox and those without an epidemiological association with the outbreak event were excluded.

Cases selection

School A is a private primary school with 6 grades, while School B is a private international high school with 3 grades. Epidemiological investigation revealed that the outbreak at School A primarily affected grades 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, involving 48 cases across 9 classes. Conversely, the outbreak at School B was concentrated in grade 11, involving 23 cases across 7 classes (See supplementary material). Due to the high potential for chickenpox transmission through contact, we consider the affected class as a single entity and select all the non-infected individuals with no prior history of chickenpox as controls in order to minimize disparities in exposure distribution. Therefore, we only considered close contacts within the outbreak-affected classes and included all individuals from these classes. A total of 69 individuals from School A and 38 individuals from School B were excluded, ultimately selecting 343 individuals from School A and 132 individuals from School B as controls. Notably, no instances of reinfection were observed during the outbreaks in either institution. The intervention protocol was analyzed using a retrospective cohort study design, with complete inclusion of all individuals in the outbreak-affected classes.

Data collection

The study collected essential participant information, including age, gender, grade level, time of onset and recovery, and initial symptoms. The epidemic duration of each generation was calculated as the days between the onset of the last and the first case. The appearance of the subsequent generation must exceed the duration of the current generation, with the exception that the determination of the second generation was based on an average incubation period of 14 days [15]. Moreover, the intergenerational interval refers to the period between the onset of the previous generation’s last case and the subsequent generation’s first case. The incubation period of a case refers to the duration from the first confirmed contact with a diagnosed case to the symptom’s appearance.

Pre-epidemic vaccination details of the participants were obtained from the Immunization Program Information Management System of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, which included dosage and date of last dose. After conducting a comprehensive epidemiological investigation, School A witnessed the occurrence of 6 successive generations of cases, while School B experienced 5 generations. We collected data on the duration and intergenerational interval in each generation and recorded the incubation of cases involved. Breakthrough varicella (BV) was defined as an infection occurring more than 42 days after receiving the attenuated varicella vaccine, with a documented history of vaccination.

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Longhua District Center for Disease Control and Prevention (20240801). Informed consent was obtained from the parents of students by the respective schools. The datasets generated and analyzed during this retrospective study are not publicly available due to ethical restrictions and participant privacy concerns. All data were anonymized and handled in compliance with the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Longhua District Center for Disease Control and Prevention institutional review board guidelines.

Analysis of outbreak characteristics

We assessed differences in the two educational institutions according to previous vaccination status and sex, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the epidemic’s magnitude. Through correlation analysis, we examined the duration of epidemics and the temporal intervals between these generations. By integrating spatial investigation with a comparative examination of disease progression among different generations from both institutions, we aimed to formulate plausible transmission hypotheses. Furthermore, this study investigated the impact of pre-outbreak differential vaccination dosing on individual susceptibility during distinct outbreak phases. Drawing on policy and control practices, the present study examined the impacts of diverse interventions implemented in two educational institutions. However, to mitigate inter-classroom influence, only comparisons were made with classes that have experienced disease when analyzing the impact of vaccine history on disease occurrence during each respective stage.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation represent continuous variables that follow a normal distribution, while the median and interquartile range (IQR) represent the remaining variables. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were used to express correlations. Stratified analysis was conducted using the Mantel-Haenszel test. Univariate data were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test, and continuous data were compared using the student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. For case control study the association measure of the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence intervals (CI) were assessed. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0). Vaccine effectiveness for the cohort analysis was assessed according to the formula (1 − Attack Rate in Vaccinated Group / Attack Rate in Unvaccinated Group). Statistically significant differences were considered when bilateral P values < 0.05.

Results

Investigation and basic information

The epidemiological outbreak investigation data gathered from historical patient visit data showed that School A had its first confirmed cases on September 30, 2023, while School B reported theirs on May 6, 2024. Both outbreaks originated from index cases. Based on divergent institutional consent policies, School A enacted a 4-day emergency prophylactic vaccination protocol while School B implemented a 3-day containment and disinfection regimen (Fig. 1). The teaching activities of School A were organized by grade levels, with grades 3 and below considered lower grades and housed separately from the senior classes. School B adopted a hierarchical pedagogical approach, with different grade levels on separate floors and students using staircases for vertical movement. The risky activities presented in School A and School B encompass morning exercises and elective courses. Considering the circumstances, School A implemented emergency vaccination measures, whereas School B opted for suspension and disinfection.

Data information and analysis

The age range of cases in School A ranged from 6 to 12 years, with a median age of 10 years, while all cases in School B were 17 years old. No statistical difference in the incubation period of the cases was observed between School A (14.96 ± 1.99 days) and School B (14.77 ± 2.74 days)(P = 0.81). The initial symptoms in all cases were either rash or fever. The proportion of cases with a rash as the initial symptom was significantly higher in School A (60.41%, n = 29/48) compared to School B (34.78%, n = 8/23) (χ2 = 9.28, P = 0.002). The average recovery period for cases with fever versus rash as initial symptoms in School A was 11.63 ± 3.15 days vs. 12.17 ± 4.04 days, while in School B was 12.47 ± 3.39 days vs. 8.75 ± 3.93 days. Notably, we observed a prolonged convalescent period correlated with fever in patients from school B (P = 0.01), whereas no such phenomenon was noted from school A (P = 0.625). Additionally, BV cases in School A exhibited a shorter recovery period (10.15 ± 2.83 days) compared to cases without a history of vaccination (13.25 ± 3.73 days) (P = 0.003). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in School B (P = 0.791) (supplementary material).

The distribution of case-control subjects between the two schools did not exhibit any statistically significant differences (χ2 = 0.644, P = 0.442). The demographic information of cases and controls for the two outbreaks is presented in Table 1. Except for a statistically significant disparity in vaccine doses administered at School A between cases and controls, no noticeable variations could be analyzed.

Comparative analysis between generations

The epidemiological investigation revealed 6 generations of cases in School A and 5 generations in School B (Fig. 1). The median duration of generational outbreaks was 5.00 (IQR 3.50-9.00) days in School A, while it extended to 9.50 (IQR 7.50-10.75) days in School B. Additionally, intergenerational intervals in School A showed a gradual decline with the epidemic’s progression, whereas School B exhibited regular fluctuations. The duration and interval of generations in School A had a clear linear trend (χ2 = 7.436, P = 0.006) and correlation (r=-0.48, P < 0.001), which was not observed in School B. In the fourth generation of the epidemic, cases in school A had a longer observed recovery duration than those in school B (Table 2).

Influence of vaccination and suspension on epidemic transmission

A phased case-control analysis of the two outbreaks revealed that cases in School A without vaccine history (58.33%) had a higher susceptibility to varicella infection compared to School B (21.74%). After stratification, unvaccinated individuals presented an odds ratio (OR) of 4.466 (95%CI:2.423–8.231) meaning they had more than 4 fold higher risk of chickenpox infection than vaccinated individuals in primary school. The impact of primary school students’ vaccination history became evident in the second (P = 0.001), third (P = 0.002), and fourth (P = 0.005) stages of the epidemic, indicating an increased risk for those who had not received varicella vaccination as the epidemic progressed. However, this phenomenon was not observed among high school students (Table 3).

Stratified measures by sex and vaccination history showed statistically significant differences in morbidity (Table 5). Furthermore, the implementation of distinct measures by the two institutions led to disparate outcomes when stratified. The interventions in School A significantly reduced the incidence rate among females from 12.31% to 3.95%. In contrast, the interventions in School B were specifically effective for males (P = 0.009). Moreover, the effectiveness of the intervention may also be influenced by the vaccination dose history. After stratification by vaccine dose, we observed an OR of 3.336 (95%CI: 1.272–8.747) of unimplemented measures in School A and 2.122 (95%CI:1.130–3.985) in School B, indicating an increased risk. Incorporating the population who received two doses of vaccine at B school into the analysis revealed a significant increase in risk among unvaccinated individuals (P = 0.033).

Discussion

Chickenpox outbreaks are prevalent among school-aged children and are characterized by high transmissibility and rapid spread. The resulting disease burden can significantly impact student health, educational provision in schools, and normal family life [16]. Therefore, the prevention and control of varicella epidemics have become a crucial task in school health initiatives. Both the outbreaks, in the current study, were initiated by the index patient, which is often identified as a risk factor for an outbreak [17]. During the epidemic, younger children were more likely to become active carriers of the infection [18], confirming the observation that School A experienced an initial outbreak among lower grades. Close contact served as the primary mode of transmission for chickenpox and appears to be associated with classrooms within schools. Infection risks decreased when different educational activities occurred on separate floors [9, 19], as our investigation’s findings supported. Epidemiological surveillance revealed both institutions exhibited engagement in risk-intensive activities including structured recreational periods and collaborative learning sessions, whereas private educational establishments demonstrated deficiencies in implementing preventive protocols to attenuate transmission risks inherent in such high-exposure scenarios.

In this study, the incubation period of cases in schools at different educational stages was approximately 14 days, consistent with previous studies [15]. Interestingly, a higher proportion of patients in primary school presented rash as an initial symptom compared to high school. In Germany, the prodromal symptoms of young children, such as mild headaches and moderate fever, are often less pronounced in comparison to adults. Moreover, there exists an extended time interval between infection and detection [20]. These circumstances may lead to cases overlooking their illness and not being promptly isolated during the early stage of the epidemic, thereby increasing the risk of disease spread. Cases from School B were approaching adulthood, and adults tend to experience more pronounced prodromal symptoms after acquiring varicella [21], which may prolong their recovery time. Conversely, children in school A exhibited cutaneous eruptions as an indication of illness stemming from mild prodromal symptoms. Breakthrough cases have become common among Chinese school-age children and accounted for over 40% of this epidemic analysis (supplementary materials), which differs from the situation during early childhood [22]. Consistent with Qin W et al.‘s research findings [23], BV cases had shorter courses, particularly among primary school children.

Due to the delayed reporting of the epidemic, timely intervention by the CDC was hindered, resulting in both schools experiencing more than 4 generations of cases. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of each stage of the epidemic. The correlation between the duration and intervals of transmission in School A suggested the potential involvement of different groups in spreading, which can be represented as distinct compartments within an infectious disease dynamic model [16, 24]. Conversely, given that School B conducted classes exclusively in interconnected classrooms on the same floor, transmission can be perceived as occurring within a distinct spatial environment. As the epidemic progressed, School A exhibited an increasing trend in case recovery time. Compared to School B where cases had relatively average recovery times, it indicated that School A encountered more complex viral superimposed transmissions and began impacting patients’ courses before intervention measures took effect. Both outbreaks initially displayed similar generational intervals of close to 14 days, consistent with previous studies [15]. However, as proliferation continued, there was a tendency for interval times to shorten. The fluctuation observed in generational interval times at School B signified a more intertwined pattern of transmission aligning with our compartmental hypothesis.

Vaccination with attenuated live vaccines represented the most efficacious approach for preventing varicella infection [25]. Our findings indicated that younger children in lower grades exhibited a higher likelihood of displaying vaccine effectiveness during outbreaks characterized by high infection frequency. Previous research in Chongqing, China has demonstrated a progressive increase in the seropositivity rate for anti-varicella zoster virus with advancing age [26], which could be attributed to wild virus infections or emergency vaccinations administered during epidemics [2]. Nevertheless, antibody levels gradually diminished over time and exerted minimal influence on controlling the incidence rate among older children [27], potentially explaining why School B did not manifest vaccine protection disparities. The absence of statistical significance regarding differences observed among higher grades at School A during the fifth phase outbreak also lent support to the above vaccine hypothesis.

To effectively mitigate the risk of chickenpox among high-risk individuals, such as students, it is imperative to enhance the vaccination rate. Our implemented vaccination strategies yielded a commendable coverage rate [28], thereby aiding in curtailing outbreaks within lower grade levels. The immunization failures observed in classes such as 501 and 506 were likely attributed to the delayed identification of early infected cases, as it took more than 15 days for antibodies to develop following vaccine administration [16]. Previous research had indicated the growing challenges associated with emergency vaccinations for near-adult higher grades on campus [29]; hence, we resorted to employing meticulous epidemiological analysis as an alternative approach.

Investigation findings revealed that there may exist a robust transmission process within elective courses conducted in Class 2 at School B, exerting a significant impact on the entire epidemic cycle. As illustrated in Table 3, a significant decrease in the susceptible population was observed during the fourth stage of transmission, exhibiting patterns akin to those observed within confined environments. Enclosed spaces were susceptible to outbreaks [30]. Subsequent inquiry unveiled that all educational activities solely relied on air conditioning ventilation within grade 11 classrooms, inadvertently facilitating transmission.

No significant differences were observed in the analysis of gender ratios among cases and controls in two outbreaks. However, contrasting outcomes were obtained from the intervention measures. One contributing factor was that males exhibited higher levels of participation in activities, poorer health habits, and increased susceptibility to infection [25]. Further investigation revealed that the outbreak in School B was predominantly driven by males, thus naturally favoring control measures tailored toward them. Nevertheless, it is plausible that females may have a higher baseline serum positivity rate [31], as supported by a previous study indicating a greater incidence rate among males aged 4–14 [32]. Consequently, the observed disparities in the effectiveness of emergency vaccinations at school A can be attributed to females exhibiting a lower frequency of risky behaviors and possessing a higher baseline immunity (Table 6).

A serological study conducted among individuals aged 12 to 29 in Afghanistan revealed a positive correlation between age and immunity, with a 4% increase in the probability of immunity for each additional year [33]. The disparity in immune response between unvaccinated high school students and those who received only one vaccine dose before turning six diminishes over time. To effectively enhance the VZV IgG serum positivity rate, it is necessary to implement a two-dose vaccination regimen [34]. Consequently, unlike School A, considering individuals who have received two doses becomes essential before observing the noticeable effects of control measures on vaccinated populations at School B.

We carried out a comprehensive analysis of the outbreaks in two schools and found that cross-grade transmission was more likely to occur in primary schools, which appeared to be influenced by the activity characteristics of younger children. Younger children have a greater need for health education compared to adolescents [35]. The duration and interval of the outbreaks could partially reflect the transmission patterns. Higher-grade children might engage in more frequent activities within localized areas compared to lower-grade children, resulting in irregular intergenerational transmission and increased risks. High vaccination coverage [36] and detailed epidemiological analysis have been demonstrated as effective measures. Furthermore, both school outbreaks indicated gender-specific roles in disease transmission, necessitating targeted interventions and education efforts. Moreover, single-dose vaccination had limited supportive effects on adolescents unless they completed the full two-dose vaccine regimen.

This study may have certain limitations. There was a potential for recall bias in the statements provided by primary school patients regarding their symptoms, which might lead to an overestimation of the proportion of initial symptoms. We refrained from evaluating vaccine effectiveness based on the timing of the last vaccination, considering potential confounding effects arising from seroepidemiology when comparing different vaccination groups over time [37], unless there was a similarity in vaccination cycles among populations in both schools. Additionally, the observed impact of School A measures within the vaccinated population might also be influenced by variations in background vaccination history. Moreover, it is crucial to note that the conclusions drawn in this article are solely based on retrospective observations, and establishing causal relationships necessitates the implementation of real-time interventions for verification.

Conclusion

Our research indicated that the characteristics of individuals affected by chickenpox outbreaks vary across different educational stages. The impact of vaccination history was comparatively less pronounced for highschool’s children in contrast to primaryschool’s children. Revealing precise transmission indicators became imperative in scenarios where the implementation of emergency vaccination measures is unfeasible. This research enhanced our understanding of school-based varicella outbreaks while offering robust support for developing more precise and efficacious preventive strategies.
