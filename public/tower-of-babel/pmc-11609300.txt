Transcutaneous intravascular laser irradiation of blood affects plasma metabolites of women
Authors: Enzo Martins Benevento, Fabíola Socorro Silva Lisboa, Luisa de Oliveira Kaneko, Vanessa Bertolucci, Álex Aparecido Rosini Silva, Danilo Cardoso de Oliveira, André Cabral Sardim, Ivan Gustavo Masselli dos Reis, Andréia M. Porcari, Leonardo Henrique Dalcheco Messias
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The effectiveness of indirect Intravascular laser irradiation of blood (ILIB) is not fully understood. In this study, we provided a novel experiment that employs metabolomics to investigate the effects of ILIB in women. Twenty-eight volunteers underwent indirect ILIB and had their plasma collected before and after this procedure. The ILIB was applied at the radial artery for 30 min, using low-power photobiomodulation (660 nm), and a power output of 0.1 W. Plasma samples were extracted and analyzed using liquid chromatography—high-resolution mass spectrometry in an untargeted approach. Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis revealed 151 molecules with the Variable In Projection score of ≥ 1. From these, 26 were identified. After checking for molecules related to dietary intake, fasting, medication, or part of the human exposome, 15 were affected by ILIB. The abundances of Estradiol 17b-glucuronide 3-sulfate, CAR 14:3, PI 22:6/PGJ2, and CAR 12:1 significantly increased by ILIB, while AcylGlcADG 62:9, Tyrosyl-Glutamine, and CDP-DG 22:3/PGF1 had the contrary effect. ILIB was shown to modulate molecules from different chemical classes, although its impact on plasma metaboloma was minimal. Further research is warranted to fully elucidate the implications of these findings across various metabolic pathways, thus advancing the science surrounding ILIB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80169-9.

Introduction

Since Endre Mester proposed photobiomodulation in the clinical setting1, studies have greatly advanced in understanding the local and systemic effects generated by this technique2–6. The systemic photobiomodulation, also known as intravascular laser irradiation of blood (ILIB), was developed to treat cardiovascular diseases due to its rheological effects. Narrative reviews have discussed the potential ILIB effects to treat lung7 and chronic systemic8 diseases. This tool has also been shown to effectively reduce blood sugar levels in type 2 diabetic patients9, in addition to decreasing arginase expression and activating the nitric oxide synthase/nitric oxide pathway in the same population10. Recently, ILIB improved the energy metabolism of infertile women exhibiting indications of ovarian aging by increasing the expression of citrate synthase, succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit A, and fumarate hydratase11.

Considering the direct application of ILIB, an intravenous catheter is introduced so that a laser can continuously irradiate blood throughout the body12. However, this application may not be accessible in extra-hospital environments due to its invasiveness. In this scenario, the transcutaneous approach, or indirect application, facilitates the administration of ILIB as it is less invasive. In this approach, well-vascularized sites, such as the sublingual and intranasal regions, and primarily under the radial artery in the wrist, are used13.

There is a scarcity of reports demonstrating the efficacy of ILIB. Utilizing polychromatic visible and infrared polarized light in the sacral area proved effective in rapidly reducing elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine plasma levels and increasing concentrations of anti-inflammatory factors; comparable outcomes were observed in in vitro experiments conducted with blood samples from volunteers14. In women without a history of systemic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or heart disease, ILIB served as an adjunctive method for regulating blood pressure (both systolic and diastolic), glucose levels, triglycerides, and cholesterol (including total, low-density, and high-density lipoprotein)15. Adult rats subjected to red diode light irradiation in the caudal vein exhibited decreased lung inflammation through modulation of mast cell degranulation and interleukin-10 levels16.

While promising, the indirect application of ILIB remains in the embryonic stage. In this regard, laboratory techniques with a broad detectability spectrum may be useful in demonstrating the effects of this application. Metabolomics, one of the youngest omics sciences, allows for the identification and analysis of a vast number of metabolites present in biological matrices17–23. Plasma metabolomics revealed new molecular signatures in diseases like Alzheimer’s24,25, pulmonary tuberculosis26, and Coronavirus27, but can be also used in other pathologies diagnostics28. Additionally, metabolomics revealed that invasive ILIB reduced glucose, glucose 6-phosphate, dehydroascorbic acid, R-3-hydroxybutyric acid, L-histidine, and L-alanine, while increasing L-arginine in type 2 diabetic patients29. Conversely, this technique has yet to be applied to investigate the effects of indirect ILIB.

Therefore, a novel experiment that employs metabolomics to investigate the effects of ILIB is presented. From a non-driven hypothesis perspective30, we aim to identify the greatest number of molecules that contribute to understanding the potential systemic alterations induced by the indirect ILIB.

Materials and methods

This is an observational report with pre vs. post-design described according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology31 (STROBE) checklist (Supplementary File 1). The University of São Francisco operates a physiotherapy clinic accessible to the municipality’s population. The clinic experiences high demand from women for the various treatments offered, including specialties such as Cardiology, Pulmonology, Dermatology, Sports Medicine, Neurology, Oncology, Orthopedics, and Women’s Health. The choice to focus on women was made to increase the sample size. Women without neurological deficits (motor or sensory) or a history/diagnosis of cancer were contacted both in person and digitally. The exclusion criteria were: (a) uncontrolled diabetes mellitus; (b) untreated systemic arterial hypertension; and (c) unwillingness to participate in the study. The lack of control of diseases such as diabetes and hypertension consisted of the absence of medical monitoring and medication use for these conditions. If the volunteers reported these conditions but did not have medical follow-up, they would not be considered. Before women signed an informed consent form to participate, all inquiries and uncertainties regarding ILIB and outcomes were thoroughly resolved. This study was conducted following the Helsinki criteria and approved by the Ethics Committee of São Francisco University (Approval Number: 63939422.2.0000.5514).

Experimental design

The volunteers underwent two visits to the multidisciplinary research laboratory at the University of São Francisco (Bragança Paulista, BR). During the first visit, researchers explained the procedure and assessed the eligibility of the participants. In addition to specific medical history questions related to the study’s exclusion criteria, variables such as body mass, height, and consequently body mass index (BMI) were analyzed for participant categorization. An experienced nurse also collected 5 mL of venous blood using tubes containing Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for hematological analyses (Pentra 80, California, USA). During the second visit, the ILIB application took place with the volunteers who met the eligibility criteria and agreed to participate in the study (Fig. 1).

ILIB

After arriving fasting for at least 12 h, the participants consumed the same breakfast (342 kcal; carbohydrates-73.84 g; protein = 4.42 g; lipids = 4.58 g). This approach eliminates the confounding factor associated with food intake, which could affect plasma metabolites and prevent conclusions about the ILIB intervention. Immediately after ingestion, blood was collected for analysis of metabolites before ILIB intervention.

The volunteers were seated in comfortable chairs and positioned their wrists on a suitable structure for the intervention application. Low-power photobiomodulation was applied in the form of ILIB technique at a visible red (660 nm) wavelength with a power output of 0.1 W, using a beam output area on the laser pen’s BICO of 1.5 mm2 (irradiance of power of 6.67 W/cm2), with a semiconductor laser emitter GaA1As and InGaAIP, powered by a Li-Ion 7.6 V/650 mA battery (MM Optics, Model Duo, São Carlos, BR), registered with the National Health Surveillance Agency (ANVISA) under number 80051420022. The ILIB lasted 30 min and was applied in the radial artery (located via palpation), in the distal epiphysis of the radius bone. The total energy applied during ILIB was 180 J, considering the power of 0.1 W multiplied by the duration (1800 s) and Energy density of 12,000 J/cm2 (Table 1). Neither the volunteer nor the equipment were repositioned during the intervention. Immediately after the ILIB, blood was again collected for analysis of metabolites.

Blood sample collection and plasma acquisition

Before blood collection, participants were advised to abstain from alcohol consumption or the ingestion of any unfamiliar food or beverage for three days. After collection, samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, plasma was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent metabolomic analysis.

Metabolites extraction

Before extraction, a 20 µL aliquot from each sample was reserved for the creation of a pooled sample, which served as a quality control (QC). QC samples were interspersed every 10 samples to monitor any deviations in extraction and system stability. Sample extraction and analysis were conducted randomly to minimize any instrumental and biological biases. Metabolites from plasma (180 µL) were extracted using cold methanol (380 µL). After vortexing (30 s), the tubes underwent centrifugation (12,000 RPM, 10 min at 4 °C). The resulting lower organic layer (420 µL) was collected and dried under N2 flow. Dried samples were reconstituted in 150 µL of a solution comprising acetonitrile (ACN): H2O (1:1, % v/v).

Mass spectrometry, detection, and identification of untargeted metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics analyses were adapted from established methodologies32. An ACQUITY UPLC system coupled with a XEVO-G2XS Quadrupole Time-of-Flight (QToF) mass spectrometer (Waters, Manchester, UK) equipped with an Electrospray Ionization (ESI) source operating in positive (ESI+) ionization mode was utilized. Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC® CSH C18 column (2.1 mm x 100 mm x 1.7 μm, Waters), with mobile phases composed of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B), flowing at a rate of 0.4 mL min−1. The column gradient transitioned from 10 to 40% B over 0.5 min, then to 90% B over 4.5 min, and maintained for 2 min before reverting to 10% B. The system was equilibrated for 2 min, resulting in a total run time of 13 min with injection volumes set at 1 µL.

The mass spectrometer was operated in MSE mode within a m/z range of 50–1200 Da, with an acquisition time of 0.5 s per scan. MSE analysis utilized a collision energy of 6 V for low energy and a ramp of 20–50 V for high collision energy. Leucine enkephalin was used as the lock mass for mass accuracy, and a 0.5 mM sodium formate solution provided mass calibration. Additional parameters included a source temperature of 135 °C, desolvation temperature of 550 °C, desolvation gas flow of 900 L h−1, capillary voltage of 3.2 kV, and cone voltage of 40 V.

Raw data processing employed Progenesis™ QI v2.4 software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for adduct selection, peak alignment, deconvolution, and compound annotation based on MSE experiments. The software considered adducts such as [M + H]+, [M + K]+, [M + Na]+, and [M + H-H2O]+. Each sample yielded an intensity table of ions, labeled by retention time and nominal masses, known as ‘features’, based on their intensities retrieved from the extracted ion chromatogram areas.

Data processing with Progenesis™ QI v2.4 software involved metabolite identification through fragmentation score, mass accuracy, and isotope similarity, ensuring mass errors ≤ 5 ppm for the precursor ions and ≤ 10 ppm for the fragments. External spectra libraries including LipidMaps, Human Metabolome Database, and MoNA—MassBank of North America were consulted. For enhanced fragment matches and compatibility with external libraries, the in-house-developed open-source tool “SDF2PQI” was utilized33.

Statistical analyses

Data is presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated for SD. The MetaboAnalyst 6.0 environment was adopted for the metabolomics analysis34. Molecules were filtered based on technically repeatable QC samples using Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) greater than 20%. The dataset was median-normalized, log-transformed, and scaled by pareto. Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to classify and discriminate molecules before and after the ILIB. Cross-validation of the model was verified using the Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation method. Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) was assessed considering molecules with a VIP score of ≥ 135,36. After confirming the normal distribution of these data, a paired t-test along with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to compare these molecules. The ILIB technique remains relatively uncharted territory in human studies, especially regarding its impact on metabolomics. Owing to this, coupled with the complexities of univariable and multivariable analyses commonly employed in omics research, determining a minimum sample size proved unfeasible. Nonetheless, efforts were directed towards assessing the statistical power of molecules exhibiting variations post-ILIB compared to pre-treatment levels. Statistical power was calculated by accounting for the effect size between time points, sample size, and alpha and beta at 0.05 and 0.95, respectively. Models such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest were employed to classify the differential molecules before and after ILIB. Specifically, for the Support Vector Machine, the Biomarker Analysis tool from MetaboAnalyst 6.0 was utilized. In addition to the linear algorithm, two latent variables were considered. For the Random Forest model, seven predictors were used with 1000 trees. The confusion matrices generated by both models were evaluated for sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive, and negative predictive values using the platform provided by the National Telehealth Program Brazil Networks37.

Results

Twenty-eight women met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and agreed to participate in the study (Fig. 2). Of the 31 individuals who consented to participate in the study, two women had a history of cancer, and one failed to attend the intervention session. The sample characterization is shown in Table 2. Regarding associated diseases, 13 women are hypertensive, and 2 are diabetic. In terms of education, 17% did not complete elementary school, while 21% finished this stage. The majority of the sample (42%) consisted of women with complete high school education, while 17% held higher education degrees. Regarding marital status, 46% are married, while 54% are single, widowed, or divorced. As for employment, 32% are retired, while 68% are still actively working.

The metabolomic analysis detected 4547 features. The analytical precision and reproducibility of the assay can be accessed by the PCA scores plot displayed in Supplementary File 2, which shows the grouping pattern of the QC samples. Following the RSD filter, 697 molecules proceeded to the multivariable analysis. PLS-DA discriminated samples from before and after the ILIB procedure (Fig. 3A), and the predictive ability of this model was confirmed by the cross-validation, represented by the Q2 coefficient (Component 1—Accuracy = 0.983; R2 = 0.944; Q2 = 0.648; Component 2—Accuracy = 0.983; R2 = 0.988; Q2 = 0.687) (Fig. 3B). The loadings plot reveals that ILIB induces both increases and decreases in the abundance of different features (Fig. 3C). This initial PLS-DA was adopted for the preliminary verification of molecules with a VIP score ≥ 1. Among the detected molecules, 151 exhibited a VIP ≥ 1, and 26 were identified with low mass error (range = -4.74–4.98). Six of these molecules are likely remnants from dietary intake (Pyroglutamylglycine, Amino adipic acid dimethyl ester, Lactose-lysine, DL-2-Aminooctanoic acid, Eicosenoic acid, and N2-(2-Carboxymethyl-2-hydroxysuccinoyl)arginine), three are metabolites derived from medications (2-Diphenylmethylpiperidine, N-Palmitoyl Glutamic acid, Sulfoxone), one (Arginyl-glycyl-aspartic acid) is part of the human exposome, and one related to fasting (L-Octanoylcarnitine).

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate the impact of indirect ILIB on plasma metabolites in women. Following the procedure, three lipids along with one steroidal glycoside exhibited an increase in abundance, whereas two other lipids, as well as one peptide, demonstrated the contrary effect.

Due to technological advancements related to the metabolic effects of photobiomodulation, current studies attribute its cellular-level effects to the wavelength used in the intervention. The fundamental principle is that photons emitted by lasers are absorbed by chromophores, specialized receptors with electron absorption bands, triggering a series of biological responses38. For instance, green (495–570 nm) and blue (400–500 nm) light wavelengths have been reported to affect components such as the electron transport chain, opsins, flavins, flavoproteins, porphyrins, and nitric oxide-containing nitrite reductases39. Regarding non-invasive ILIB, a recent systematic review in animal models retrieved a study that demonstrated this technique (red light − 650 nm) had no impact on erythrocytes, leukocytes, thrombocytes, hemoglobin, or serum lipid levels, but positively microcirculation while decreased hepatocyte steatosis and inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver and spleen40. Specifically related to the wavelength used in this study (i.e., 660 nm), cytochrome oxidase is recognized as a chromophore, which may explain why the visible red wavelength can potentially modulate mitochondrial responses38. However, when applying indirect ILIB, it becomes challenging to trace its effects, whether on extracellular molecules or cellular modulations.

It should be noted that the sample considered consists of women who are likely postmenopausal, although a clinical assessment was not conducted as it was outside the scope of this study. Age and menopause represent potential factors contributing to estrogen deficiency41,42. ILIB increased Estradiol 17b-glucuronide 3-sulfate, a metabolite from estrogen metabolism. The synthesis of estrogens entails intricate biomolecular pathways43, and its regulation depends on intracellular signaling pathways, hormones regulation, transcription, and local factors44. In this context, the literature supports that the reduction of ovarian hormones can be a potent source of oxidative stress45. As the ovary is the primary site of estrogen production44, it is plausible to speculate that indirect ILIB may have contributed to a reduction in oxidative stress. While studies support the notion that oxidative stress can be attenuated by photobiomodulation46–48, it is premature and largely speculative to assert that indirect ILIB generated systemic responses like that oxidative stress reduction ultimately led to modulations in uterine estrogen metabolism.

Another molecule that shares the same difficulty for discussion was the PI 22:6/PGJ2. This metabolite is an oxidized phosphatidylinositol, distributed within cellular substructures such as the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus membrane, and cell membrane, present in all cells and tissues. It exerts physiological effects on various conditions including several cancers, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, seizures, Parkinson’s, and Alzheimer’s diseases. In terms of biological processes, it plays roles in apoptosis, cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation. These effects align with suggestions in the literature regarding the adoption of photobiomodulation38. Therefore, the physiological mechanism behind the increase in PI 22:6/PGJ2 following indirect ILIB could be linked to cellular modulations in function. Given the relationship between mitochondrial activity and cellular differentiation49, a potential key player in this process could be the modulation of cytochrome oxidase. However, it is not possible to determine which tissues were specifically affected by this intervention or which exact cellular responses were altered. Hence, there are numerous potential mechanisms by which ILIB increased this metabolite in plasma.

CAR 14:3 and CAR 12:1 are acylcarnitines. Acylcarnitines play a crucial role in facilitating the transport of fatty acids into the mitochondria for energy production via β-oxidation, thereby enabling the transportation of long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane50,51. On the other hand, plasma acylcarnitines were not correlated with their tissue counterparts in mouse strains (BALB/cJ and C57BL/6N)52. Thus, it is hypothesized that ILIB-mediated enhancement of acylcarnitine metabolism necessitates further comparison between plasma and tissue compartments. Conversely, in patients with chronic spinal cord injury, direct ILIB administration (via intravenous catheter) was observed to elevate mitochondrial DNA copy number, leukocyte adenosine triphosphate synthesis, and total antioxidant capacity, while concurrently reducing malondialdehyde levels53. The authors assert that ILIB may be effective in mitigating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. However, the observed increases in CAR 14:3 and CAR 12:1 in our experiment do not definitively indicate that transcutaneous ILIB improves mitochondrial function in women. Nevertheless, these findings present additional avenues for investigating the effects of ILIB on β-oxidation, particularly when comparing various tissues.

ILIB also decreased metabolites in plasma. Tyrosyl-Glutamine is a dipeptide produced by an incomplete breakdown product of protein catabolism. Aleidi et al.54 demonstrated that Tyrosyl-Glutamine along with other glutamine-containing dipeptides were up-regulated in type 2 diabetes, and then returned to obese comparable levels after metformin administration. Our sample consisted of two diabetic patients, and only one reported the use of metformin. Therefore, it is unlikely that the observed decrease of Tyrosyl-Glutamine is related to this factor. The discussion regarding the ILIB effects on plasmatic amino acids is limited. In a case report, Li et al.55 found that ILIB efficiently reduced serum homocysteine levels and promoted cerebral circulation in a 62-year-old man with subacute stroke. Direct comparisons between studies are certainly ambitious. However, it is arguable that enhancements in microcirculation may be induced both by directly and indirectly applied ILIB. This factor may have contributed to the reduction of Tyrosyl-Glutamine levels.

The last two lipids significantly reduced after ILIB were AcylGlcADG 62:9 and CDP-DG 22:3/PGF1, which are associated with the biosynthesis of phospholipids. Phospholipids are widely distributed in nature and serve as essential constituents of cell membranes, playing integral roles in cellular metabolism and signaling pathways56–58. On the other hand, we could not find studies associating the ILIB with AcylGlcADG 62:9 and CDP-DG 22:3/PGF1 or even phospholipid metabolism.

Another crucial point that must be highlighted concerns the primary inferences drawn from the PLSDA analysis in this study. Although the molecules highlighted by this model exhibited good accuracy via classification models (Fig. 6), only 4.2% of the variance is explained by the first component of the PLSDA. This indicates that 96% of the variance in our data cannot be attributed to the indirect ILIB intervention. Combined with the identification of 15 molecules, these findings strongly suggest that the indirect ILIB has a minimal impact on the plasma metabolome. Professionals working with this tool in practical settings should consider this fact. Moreover, the literature cited earlier supporting potential physiological mechanisms linked to the modulation of these molecules does not consistently employ the same wavelength or energy levels used in our study. As mentioned elsewhere, direct ILIB has been receiving increased attention in specialized literature, whereas indirect ILIB, despite being widely commercialized, lacks strong scientific evidence. This limitation hinders a more in-depth discussion of the findings presented in this report.

Furthermore, the literature frequently discusses metabolic variation due to aging59–63 or sex64–68. This study focused on women likely to be postmenopausal, which limits the generalization of our findings to women and men of different ages. Therefore, future studies involving different age groups and including male participants are needed to better understand potential sex-specific or age-related differences when applying ILIB in conjunction with metabolomics.

However, our study boasts several strengths. Metabolomics analysis in biofluid studies necessitates quality assurance69,70. Researchers meticulously standardized every aspect of data collection, including the same breakfast intake for all participants. All blood samples underwent identical processing procedures, including time standardization for plasma acquisition and storage. In terms of bioinformatics, both multivariable and univariable analyses were employed to focus solely on molecules genuinely affected by ILIB. Although the PLSDA suggests that the impact of indirect ILIB on the plasma metabolome is minimal, the model can still be considered trustworthy, given that the delta between R² and Q² was 0.371,72. Additionally, we addressed the sample size estimation issue by calculating the statistical power of the paired t-test. Lastly, this study is pioneering in demonstrating the impact of indirect ILIB on plasma metabolites.

Conclusion

This study marks the first exploration into the impact of indirect ILIB on plasma metabolites in women. While three lipids and one steroidal glycoside were upregulated by ILIB, another two lipids and one peptide were reduced following this procedure. Further research is warranted to fully elucidate the implications of these findings across various metabolic pathways, thus advancing the science surrounding ILIB.
