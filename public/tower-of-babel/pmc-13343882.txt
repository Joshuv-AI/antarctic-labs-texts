Navigating false-positive outcomes after stool-based colorectal cancer screening tests: a scoping review
Authors: Amal Cheema, Katherine Levy, Audrey H. Calderwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFor patients undergoing colorectal cancer screening with stool-based tests followed by a negative colonoscopy, management can be challenging due to potential concern for upper aerodigestive cancer or extra-colonic cancer. We sought to synthesize contemporary clinical approaches and cancer outcomes following a positive stool test and a negative colonoscopy and to identify areas in need of further research.MethodsBecause of the heterogeneity of published research and available evidence, we conducted a scoping review of English-language, peer-reviewed, and full-text articles through September 2025.Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts followed by full text assessment, and data extraction. Data were synthesized thematically to explore the outcomes and work-up strategies associated with false-positive results in FIT and mt-sDNA tests within the context of extra-colonic cancer screening.ResultsOf 3442 studies identified, 49 underwent full text review of which 8 met inclusion criteria. Most patients were female and ≥50 years; however, reporting of demographic information was variable. The brand of stool-test, definition of stool-test positivity, definition of negative colonoscopy, and follow-up time were variably reported and defined. In terms of outcome, ~0.5% of patients had CRC on second colonoscopy. Incidence of non-colorectal aerodigestive or upper GI cancer ranged from 0-4.3%.ConclusionThere is a critical need for a standardized reporting framework among studies evaluating outcomes after positive stool-test and negative colonoscopy to better synthesize across studies and ultimately, improve clinical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04900-8.

Introduction

While colonoscopy remains the preferred modality for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening in the US due to its high sensitivity and specificity, screening programs in many countries are increasingly relying on non-invasive stool tests. Fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) detects human hemoglobin in stool, while multitarget stool DNA (mt-sDNA) detects both human hemoglobin in stool as well as DNA and hemoglobin biomarkers associated with colonic neoplasia. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends FIT every year, mt-sDNA every 1 to 3 years, or a colonoscopy every 10 years for screening purposes [1].

A positive stool-based screening test requires a follow-up colonoscopy to evaluate the source of positivity. Among patients with a positive FIT or mt-sDNA test, 95% and 87% of patients, respectively, will have a finding to explain the positive stool result; however, 5% (1 out of 20) and 13% (1 out of 10), respectively, will have a normal (“negative”) colonoscopy, a so called “false-positive result.” [2–4]. Hemorrhoids, anal fissures, inflammatory bowel disease, male sex, age above 65 years, body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 kg/m3, current smoking, aspirin use, non-neoplastic findings at screening, hyperglycemia, hypertension, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use are associated with false positive results [5–9].

False positive CRC stool-based screening results can be challenging for clinicians to manage due to concern for potentially missing cancer elsewhere in the body. Some hypothesize that fecal hemoglobin detected in FIT and mt-sDNA could potentially originate from aerodigestive cancers, such as those in the proximal gastrointestinal (GI) tract (e.g., esophagus, stomach), as well as oral, nasal, and throat cavity lesions [10]. In a large, multiethnic US population, Zhou et al. found that FIT-positive patients had a higher adjusted risk of proximal GI cancer within 3 years of FIT compared to FIT-negative patients [11]. A review of 21 studies investigating upper GI tract lesions in participants with fecal occult blood test (FOBT)-positive results found that same-day gastroscopy may yield 25% more malignancies than colonoscopy alone [12]. Subsequent testing for false positives may include invasive procedures, such as endoscopy, capsule endoscopy, repeat colonoscopies, repeat FIT tests, or CT scans, resulting in additional time and costs for patients, as well as potentially unnecessary anxiety.

The aim of this review was to synthesize contemporary clinical approaches and cancer outcomes following a positive stool test and a negative colonoscopy, and to identify areas in need of further research. Ultimately, understanding the yield of these screenings, particularly the extra-colonic cancer findings, would help guide management after a positive stool test and negative colonoscopy, thereby potentially improving patient outcomes.

Methods

We planned to conduct a systematic review; however, given the heterogeneity of the published research and the early stages of available evidence, we pivoted to a scoping review with the goals of identifying the available evidence and methods in the field, clarifying key definitions related to false-positive outcomes, and identifying knowledge gaps [13]. A comprehensive literature search of English-language, peer-reviewed, and full-text articles was initially conducted in PubMed in October 2023, repeated in October 2024, and then again in September 2025. The search strategy aimed to identify relevant literature on colorectal and aerodigestive cancer detection methods, with a focus on colonoscopy and stool-based tests. It combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords related to cancer detection methods and anatomical locations (Appendix 1). The search terms encompassed various screening methods (e.g., colonoscopy, endoscopy, sigmoidoscopy) and cancer-related terms (e.g., cancer, neoplasm, malignancy), as well as specific anatomical terms (e.g., intestine, gastric, esophagus). Additionally, it included terms related to stool-based tests such as fecal immunochemical tests (FIT), stool DNA tests, and occult blood testing. The PubMed search was compared to other databases, such as Embase, to confirm that PubMed’s coverage was comprehensive. Two additional curated searches were performed in Embase and Web of Science in March 2026 on papers that addressed the risk of aerodigestive or extra-colonic cancers in people who have had a positive FIT or mt-sDNA test but a negative colonoscopy.

Two independent reviewers (AC, KL) screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved articles to identify potentially relevant studies using Covidence. Full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if they focused on the cancer outcomes of or testing algorithms, such as repeat stool-based testing [14], and included patients with false-positive results. Studies were excluded if they were prevalence studies, case studies, commentaries, guidelines, or test validation studies. Studies were excluded if the population was not low risk or asymptomatic, or if screening used the fecal occult blood test rather than FIT or mt-sDNA. Studies were also excluded if they focused only on post-colonoscopy CRC [15] rather than investigating the relationship between stool-based testing and the detection of aerodigestive cancers. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer (AHC). Data extraction was performed by two reviewers (AC, KL) using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (e.g., author, year of publication, study design), participant characteristics, details of the screening or surveillance tests (FIT, mt-sDNA), false-positive rates, implications of false-positive results, and work-up strategies. Supplementary information was obtained as available.

Given the heterogeneity of the studies and the incomplete availability of data, a meta-analysis was not conducted. A narrative synthesis approach was employed to summarize the findings of included studies. Data were synthesized thematically to explore the outcomes and work-up strategies associated with false-positive results in FIT and mt-sDNA tests within the context of extra-colonic cancer screening. This scoping review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for Scoping Reviews to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting of methods and findings [16].

Human ethics and consent to participate

Not applicable. As the review did not involve primary data or analysis of individual data from human subjects, no ethics review nor consent to participant was required.

Results

A total of 3,442 studies were identified, of which 3,393 studies were excluded at the title and abstract review stage based on prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Among the 49 studies sought for retrieval, 41 were subsequently excluded after full-text assessment. The reasons for exclusion were as follows: editorial articles (n = 4), studies not written in English (n = 1), and studies with inappropriate outcomes, incorrect study design, or an inappropriate population (n = 36). Ultimately, eight studies were deemed eligible for inclusion and form the basis of our analysis and synthesis.

Participant inclusion criteria

Participants included in the studies varied and included screening populations and individuals at low to average risk of CRC. Studies often excluded participants based on age (i.e., outside of the screening ages), recent colonoscopy, previous cancer diagnosis, incomplete colonoscopies, or a history of inflammatory bowel disease, gastrointestinal bleeding, or colonic neoplasia or polyps. While most studies were conducted in screening populations, Cotter et al. included one of three study sites in which participants were under surveillance or had a history of neoplasm [18]. Definitions for positive FIT (e.g., cut-off of ≥ 20 µg hemoglobin per gram of feces [19]) or positive mt-sDNA (e.g., as per manufacturer guidelines) were provided in a few of the studies, though brands were not routinely reported, and when reported, varied across study sites. Most participants received a single FIT with one or two sample collection tubes for enrollment in the study.

Definitions

As shown in Table 2, definitions varied across the studies with clinically important heterogeneity. The definition of a negative colonoscopy varied across the papers or was implied to be without the finding of colorectal neoplasia or advanced adenomas. Berger et al. described a negative colonoscopy as one without colorectal neoplasia but allow for non-advanced adenoma or hyperplastic polyps [20]. On the other hand, De Klaver et al. described a positive colonoscopy as “adenoma ≥ 10 mm and/or with ≥ 25% villous component and/or with high-grade dysplasia” [4]. Some papers did not clearly define negative colonoscopies. Likewise, there is a lack of clarity of whether a FIT or DNA test yielded a positive result. For positive test definitions, the papers often implied deferring to company testing cut-offs, such as a positive mt-sDNA defined by the validated logistic regression algorithm cutoff, or to national screening thresholds, but this was not often clearly reported. FIT positivity thresholds varied across the studies. Thus, false-positive outcome definitions varied both implicitly and explicitly. While a false-positive FIT result was generally described as a positive stool test followed by a negative diagnostic colonoscopy, Berger et al. proposed language such as “discordant” results [20]. De Klaver et al., on the other hand, use the terminology “FIT-positives without [advanced neoplasm] AN” [4].

Clinical modifiers

A couple of the studies adjusted for risk factors or clinical history, such as sex, anal disorders, and proton pump inhibitor use [19]; however, most studies focused on a screening population without prior history of CRC.

Follow-up times

Follow-up time was variably reported and defined. Definitions included the interval between study enrollment and either a positive stool test or a negative colonoscopy, and the interval between cancer diagnosis and the end of follow-up. The time interval between a positive stool test and a colonoscopy was generally not reported. Follow-up time from study enrollment to a cancer diagnosis ranged from < 1 year to > 5 years; median follow-up times were 1 year [21], 2–3 years [19], 3 years post-colonoscopy [22], within 3 years after FIT [23], 4 years [18], and 5.3 years [20]. De Klaver et al. included cancer diagnoses within 3 years after FIT, assuming cancers would have been detected at the time of the stool-based test [4]. In the included studies, patients sometimes underwent various subsequent tests during surveillance after a false-positive result. Such tests included repeat stool test with colonoscopy and endoscopy [21]; follow-up colonoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy, or EGD [18]; or repeat colonoscopy, endoscopy, or capsule endoscopy [19]. Other studies have identified cancer through a review of endoscopic, radiographic, and histopathologic reports [20] or linkage via the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O) [4] or a national cancer registry [23].

Outcomes

Cancer was an outcome reported in these studies; however, some studies looked only at upper GI cancers (i.e., stomach, small bowel), cancers (i.e., oral, pharyngeal, laryngeal, esophageal, lung), CRC, or a combination of these. Upper GI cancers sometimes include esophageal types [22]. While overall cancer appeared rare in follow-up, 0.5% of participants were found to have CRC on second colonoscopy, and the occurrence of non-colorectal aerodigestive or upper GI cancer ranged from 0% to 1.95% [20] and 4.3% [18]. Of 1,748 patients with positive FIT, Pakneshan et al. found only three patients who had a negative index colonoscopy and were diagnosed with esophageal squamous cell (n = 2) and poorly differentiated gastric cancer (n = 1) [22]. De Klaver et al. found that independent of colonoscopy findings, FIT-positive patients were at a significantly higher risk of being diagnosed with aerodigestive cancers, particularly EGD-detectable cancers [4].

Discussion

In this scoping review regarding outcomes after a positive stool-based CRC screening test and a subsequent negative colonoscopy, the primary finding among the eight relevant papers was the variability in all aspects of the study design, including definitions of false-positive results and negative colonoscopy [24] and reported outcomes. The limited available evidence with variability in reporting reinforces the importance of exercising caution in interpreting findings overall and highlights the need for more consistently reported data to allow for interpretation and synthesis of results.

Despite the heterogeneity in reporting among these studies, the occurrence of extracolonic cancer was consistently rare across all studies. While subsequent testing identified isolated cases of cancer or non-neoplastic changes that potentially explained the positive stool test, the rate of cancer in the false-positive population appeared very low, often < 1 to 2%. These findings on FIT and mt-sDNA mirror a systematic review of false-positive FOBT tests, which found that 1–7% of patients across nine studies were found to have upper GI cancer and insufficient evidence of routine EGD in false-positive patients [25]. However, in acknowledging the mechanistic differences between mt-sDNA and FIT including addition of detection of methylation markers which increase with age, the mt-sDNA population may incur a higher baseline false-positive test rate than the FIT population [26, 27]. The mt-sDNA positive studies identified a wider variety of cancers, such as pancreas and lung, whereas FIT-positive studies focused on tumors that bleed into the gastrointestinal lumen. Nonetheless, given the differences in sample size, duration, definitions, and populations across these studies, we cannot yet determine whether mt-sDNA or FIT provides greater information on extracolonic cancer risk. Determining if incident CRC in a screening population are new CRCs or CRCs missed in the initial screening remains a challenge and elapsed time since baseline colonoscopy and the quality of colonoscopy (e.g. endoscopist adenoma detection rate, adequacy of bowel preparation, completeness of exam) play large roles [14, 15, 24, 28–30]. A cancer occurrence after a follow-up time of five to ten years may not be related to an initial stool test. While not a direct focus of this paper, patients with a positive FIT but a negative colonoscopy appear to remain at elevated risk for interval CRC compared to those with a negative FIT [3, 28]. Among patients with a FIT-positive test, Rivero-Sánchez et al. found a low incidence (0.4%) of post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer within 3 years of the index colonoscopy, citing the importance of colonoscopy quality [3]. Endoscopists with lower detection rates of adenomas and sessile serrated lesions have been shown to have a significantly higher frequency of negative colonoscopies after positive stool tests [31]. A benchmark target of ≥ 40% for ADR in patients with mt-sDNA + or FIT+ tests and 20% for sessile serrated lesions in mt-sDNA+ patients has been suggested. Overall, emphasis should be placed on high-quality colonoscopy (i.e. optimal detection with adequate bowel preparation complete to the cecum with adequate withdrawal time and optimal resection of pre-neoplastic polyps) for both baseline screening and follow-up of positive stool-based test and are critical in determining whether a “negative” colonoscopy is truly false-positive or simply a result of a missed lesion [32].

Further investigation is needed to inform screening intervals and the surveillance of extracolonic findings following false-positive results. Follow-up time varied across all the studies from 0 to around 5 years. De Klaver et al. used a three-year observation period, assuming cancers diagnosed within the window could have been detected at the time of FIT testing [4]. With a 3-year cumulative incidence of EGD-detectable cancers below 1%, the study group did not recommend EGD for asymptomatic FIT-positive patients. Similarly, Cotter et al. and Berger et al., who had follow-up periods closer to 5 years, concluded that aggressive follow-up is unjustified for patients with false-positive results and high-quality baseline colonoscopies, based on current observational data [18, 20]. However, larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to confirm these findings as the heterogeneity in follow-up duration makes cross-study comparisons difficult. Some cancers like pancreatic and lung cancer have long latency periods in which a 4 to 5 year follow-up may be insufficient; likewise, a 2 to 3 year follow-up period may not definitively inform the clinician regarding cancer risk if false-positive results suggest occult cancer.

A key takeaway of this review is the critical need for standardized research methodologies to improve generalizability. To facilitate the translation of research into clinical practice, we recommend the standardized reporting framework outlined in Fig. 2. Essential elements include precise definitions of test outcomes (including brands and cut-offs) and comprehensive demographic data to identify disparities [33–35]. Studies should describe pertinent risk factors that may affect false-positive results, such as genetic profile, social history, or clinical history, and adjust accordingly. Furthermore, reporting specific time intervals—from stool test to colonoscopy and subsequent cancer diagnosis—is critical for refining surveillance approaches [15, 36]. Finally, as stool-based testing expands, biomedical ethics research regarding patient comprehension of false-positive results will be vital for ensuring adherence and informed decision-making [37, 38].

While we await more robust data on this topic, practical guidance on specific clinical scenarios in which one might consider additional testing can be offered. For example, if a patient with a positive FIT followed by a negative high-quality colonoscopy also has iron deficiency anemia, an EGD should be considered or if they also have unintentional weight loss or other focal symptoms, a CT scan should be considered. If a patient with a positive FIT has a colonoscopy that is limited by inadequate bowel preparation or fast withdrawal (inspection) time, a repeat high-quality colonoscopy should be considered prior to other testing.

A key strength of our study is the focus on modern stool immunochemical tests, as prior studies included or focused solely on the older gFOBT tests. While the literature documents possible risk factors or confounders that may lead to false-positive results, there remains a paucity of data on the clinical management and outcomes following a false-positive result, particularly in terms of predicting cancer risk. Our review reveals gaps in the literature in our understanding of the work-up and the importance of false-positive results in the context of immunochemical stool test results. Given the variability of study design, participant characteristics, definitions, and reporting, we were, however, limited in our ability to perform a meta-analysis or complete a systematic review of the data. Summary statistics, follow-up time, and participant characteristics varied. In addition, because scoping reviews do not aim to produce a critically appraised synthesized result, an assessment of methodological limitations or risk of bias was not performed. Nonetheless, retrospective design and selection biases affect many of these papers. Stool test platforms are also heterogeneous. Even the largest studies may be inadequately powered to detect rare cancer outcomes. The true cancer incidence may be underestimated due to a lack of standardized surveillance protocols for aerodigestive cancers. We also did not include papers written in languages other than English, potentially missing data in countries where FIT screening and upper GI cancers may be more prevalent.

In summary, a negative colonoscopy following a positive FIT or mt-sDNA test may not be clinically equivalent to a negative colonoscopy in primary screening. However, the current observational research in limited populations demonstrates a very low absolute risk of proximal GI cancer which does not support routine work-up, such as EGD or other testing for extracolonic malignancies in asymptomatic patients as the potential harms likely outweigh the benefits. Instead, the data emphasize the importance of a high-quality baseline colonoscopy [34]. While our current understanding maintains that the cancer rate in this false-positive population appears low, further research is warranted to identify best practices for screening of extracolonic cancers [1, 39] and to investigate the relationship between false-positive results and aerodigestive cancer while adjusting for potential confounding variables. Future work should also compare how a false-positive mt-sDNA may differ between a false-positive FIT outcome in the same population.
