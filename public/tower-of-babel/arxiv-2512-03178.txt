TDCOSMO. XXIV. Measurement of the Hubble constant from the doubly lensed quasar HE 1104 − - 1805
Eric Paic ,, Frédéric Courbin, Christopher D. Fassnacht, Aymeric Galan, Martin Millon, Dominique Sluse, Devon M. Williams, Simon Birrer, Elizabeth J. Buckley-Geer, Michele Cappellari, Frédéric Dux, Xiang-Yu Huang, Shawn Knabel, Cameron Lemon, Anowar J. Shajib, Sherry H. Suyu, Tommaso Treu, Kenneth C. Wong, Lise Christensen, Veronica Motta, Alessandro Sonnenfeld

Abstract
Time-delay cosmography leverages strongly lensed quasars to measure the Universe’s current expansion rate, H 0 H_{\rm 0} , independently from other methods. The latest TDCOSMO milestone measurement primarily used quadruply lensed quasars for their mass profile constraints. However, doubly lensed quasars, being more abundant and offering precise time delays, could expand the sample by a factor of 5, significantly advancing toward a 1% precision measurement of H 0 H_{\rm 0} . We present the first TDCOSMO analysis of a doubly imaged source, HE 1104 − - 1805, including the measurement of the four necessary ingredients. First, by combining 17 years of data from the SMARTS, Euler and WFI telescopes, we measure a time delay of 176.3 − 10.3 + 11.4 {}^{+11.4}_{-10.3} days. Second, using MUSE data, we extract stellar velocity dispersion measurements in three radial bins with 5% to 13% precision. Third, employing F160W HST imaging for lens modelling and marginalizing over various modelling choices, we measure the Fermat potential difference between the images. Fourth, using wide-field imaging, we measure the convergence added by objects not included in the lens modelling. By combining these four ingredients, we measure the time delay distance and the angular diameter distance to the deflector favoring a power-law mass model over a baryonic and dark matter composite model. The measurement was performed blindly to prevent experimenter bias and resulted in a Hubble constant of H 0 = 64.2 − 5.0 + 5.8 H_{\rm 0}=64.2^{+5.8}_{-5.0} × \times λ int \lambda_{\rm int} km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 \mathrm{km}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} , where λ int \lambda_{\rm int} is the internal mass sheet degeneracy parameter. This is in agreement with the TDCOSMO-2025 milestone and its precision for λ int = 1 \lambda_{\rm int}=1 is comparable to that obtained with the best-observed quadruply lensed quasars (4-6%). This work is a stepping stone towards a precise measurement of H 0 H_{\rm 0} using a large sample of doubly lensed quasars, supplementing the current sample. The next TDCOSMO milestone paper will include this system in its hierarchical analysis, constraining λ int \lambda_{\rm int} and H 0 H_{\rm 0} jointly with multiple lenses.

In recent years, the

Time-delay cosmography formalism

The determination of
H_{\rm 0}
using the time delay observable in strong lens systems was first suggested by
77
and further developed by
82
. In this section, we will review the main points of this formalism, highlighting how the redshifts of the source (
z_{\rm s}
) and the lens (
z_{\rm d}
), the time delay between each image (
\Delta t_{\rm AB}
), the resolved kinematics, the LOS, the mass model of the lens and the position of the source are utilized to measure
H_{\rm 0}
.
2
2

2

We refer the interested reader to the extensive reviews of
8
and
105
for more details.

2.1
Lensing formalism

The time delay
\Delta t_{\rm AB}
between two images
A
and
B
of a given strong lens system is given by:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\rm AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{\Delta t}}{c}\left(\tau\left(\mathbf{\theta_{\rm A}},\mathbf{\beta}\right)-\tau\left(\mathbf{\theta_{\rm B}},\mathbf{\beta}\right)\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\text{with }D_{\Delta t}

\displaystyle\equiv(1+z_{\rm d})\frac{D_{\rm d}D_{\rm s}}{D_{\rm ds}},

(2)

\displaystyle\text{and }\tau\left(\mathbf{\theta},\mathbf{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{\theta}-\mathbf{\beta}\right)^{2}-\phi\left(\mathbf{\theta}\right),

(3)

where
\mathbf{\theta}
is the apparent angular position of an image and
\mathbf{\beta}
the position of the source,
D_{\Delta t}
is the so-called ”time-delay distance” defined as a function of the lens redshift
z_{\rm d}
and the angular distances
D_{\rm d}
,
D_{\rm s}
and
D_{\rm ds}
, respectively, between the observer and the lens, the observer and the source and the lens and the source. Finally, the Fermat potential
\tau(\mathbf{\theta},\mathbf{\beta})
depends on the lens potential
\phi(\mathbf{\theta})
, which can be determined by modelling the mass distribution of the lens.

We can generalize Eq.
3
in the case where multiple lenses lie at different redshifts, creating
P
different lens planes. The time delay then becomes
(
82
, e.g., )
:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\rm AB}=\sum^{\rm P}_{i=1}\frac{D_{\Delta t,i,i+1}}{c}\left[\frac{(\mathbf{\theta}_{\rm A,\textit{i}}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\rm A,\textit{i+1}})^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mathbf{(\theta}_{\rm B,\textit{i}}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\rm B,\textit{i+1}})^{2}}{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\zeta_{i,i+1}\left(\phi_{i}(\mathbf{\theta}_{\rm A,\textit{i}})+\phi_{i}(\mathbf{\theta}_{\rm B,\textit{i}})\right)\vphantom{\frac{D_{\Delta t,i,i+1}}{c}}\right],

(4)

D_{\Delta t,i,i+1}\equiv{(1+z_{i})}\frac{D_{i}D_{i+1}}{D_{ii+1}},\ \ \ \ \

(5)

\zeta_{i,i+1}\equiv\frac{D_{i,i+1}D_{\rm s}}{D_{i+1}D_{\rm is}},

(6)

By defining
D_{\Delta t,{\rm eff}}\equiv D_{\Delta t,l,s}
as the time-delay distance between the main lens plane and the source plane, the time delay can be rewritten:

\Delta t_{\rm AB}=\frac{D_{\Delta t}^{\rm eff}}{c}\Delta\phi_{\rm{AB}}^{\rm{eff}}.

(7)

With the effective Fermat potential:

\Delta\phi^{\rm eff}(\theta)=\sum^{\rm P}_{i=1}\frac{1+z_{i}}{1+z_{\rm d}}\frac{D_{i}D_{i+1}D_{\rm ds}}{D_{\rm d}D_{\rm s}D_{i,i+1}}\left[\frac{(\theta_{i}-\theta_{i+1})^{2}}{2}-\zeta_{i,i+1}\psi_{i}(\theta_{i})\right].

(8)

As pointed out by
38
, the observables of a strong lensing system such as the image position and the flux ratios are invariant with respect to the mass-sheet transform (MST). This transformation of the convergence
\kappa(\theta)
and the source plane coordinates
\mathbf{\beta}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\lambda}\left(\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\kappa\left(\theta\right)+(1-\lambda)\ ,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\beta_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbf{\beta}.

(10)

This degeneracy between the mass of the lens and the size of the source is called the mass-sheet degeneracy (MSD) and implies a degeneracy between
H_{\rm 0}
and the time delay.

Although a pure mass sheet extending up to infinity in the lens plane is unphysical, the MSD is the manifestation of two physical effects: 1) the lensing effect of all the galaxies along the line
of sight (LOS) other than the main lens. We refer to this effect as the external MSD and it can be treated as equivalent to a mass sheet with a surface mass density
\kappa_{\rm ext}
in units of the critical density for lensing; 2) the internal mass sheet degeneracy, which results from small mismatch between the true mass profile of the lens and its model.

The total internal and external MSD parameters defined in Eq.
9
can therefore be decomposed into two terms:

\lambda=(1-\kappa_{\rm ext})\lambda_{\rm int},

(11)

where
\lambda_{\rm int}
is the internal MSD parameter.

The external part of the MSD can be statistically derived if
\kappa_{\rm ext}
is estimated from the mass of the galaxies along the LOS. This can be done either by counting the galaxies in a large aperture and comparing the density of the LOS to numerical simulations
(see e.g.
113
, for detail)
or with weak lensing
(
103
;
102
)
. The time-delay distance
D_{\Delta t}
can then be corrected
D_{\Delta t}\ =D_{\Delta t}\mathrm{{}^{\prime}}/\left(1-\kappa_{\rm ext}\right)
, where
D_{\Delta t}\mathrm{{}^{\prime}}
is the time-delay distance from mass modeling without accounting for
\kappa_{\rm ext}
. The detailed computation of
\kappa_{\rm ext}
is presented in Section
5.4
.

Following previous TDCOSMO single-lens analyses
(
118
;
79
;
87
;
117
)
, we assume no internal MST (i.e.
\lambda_{\rm int}
=1). The next collaboration milestone that will hierarchically model all TDCOSMO lenses will release this parameter.

2.2
Kinematic modelling

The kinematic observable is the luminosity-weighted line-of-sight projected stellar velocity dispersion, denoted as
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
. It is measured by targeting stellar absorption lines and quantifying their width with high-resolution spectra.

As in TDCOSMO25, we use the spherically-aligned Jeans Anisotropic Modelling (JAM) method. This method builds on the orbital distribution
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})
of position and velocity of the stars in 3D motion in the galactic potential
\Phi
. This potential is described by the steady-state collisionless Boltzmann equation
(
7
, hereafter, BT, eq. 4-13b))
:

\sum^{3}_{i=1}\left(v_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_{i}}\right)=0.

(12)

We then obtain a single spherical Jeans equation (BT eq. 4-54):

\frac{d\left[\rho_{*}(r)\sigma^{2}_{r}(r)\right]}{dr}+\frac{2\beta_{\rm ani}(r)\rho_{*}(r)\sigma_{r}^{2}(r)}{r}=-\rho_{*}\frac{d\Phi(r)}{dr},

(13)

with
\rho_{*}(r)
the stellar density distribution and
\beta_{\rm ani}
the orbital anisotropy, defined as a function of the ratio of tangential over radial velocity dispersion components,
\sigma_{\theta}
and
\sigma_{r}
:

\beta_{\rm ani}\equiv 1-\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}.

(14)

Given
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
, the values of
\sigma_{r}
and
\sigma_{\theta}
are degenerate, leaving
\beta_{\rm ani}
nearly unconstrained. This introduces a degeneracy between
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
and the 3D mass profile known as the mass-anisotropy degeneracy (MAD)
(
6
;
63
)
.
As demonstrated in Appendix
A.2
, the lens galaxy of HE 1104
-
1805 is a slow rotator. We hence follow
100
and use the measurements of
23
on slow rotating galaxies to apply the uniform prior
0.87\leq\frac{\sigma_{\theta}^{2}}{\sigma_{r}^{2}}\leq 1.12
.

A solution of Eq.
13
is given by:

\sigma^{2}_{r}(r)=\frac{G}{\rho_{*}(r)}\int^{\infty}_{r}\frac{M(s)\rho_{*}(s)}{s^{2}}\mathcal{J}_{\beta}(r,s)ds,

(15)

with
M(r)
the mass enclosed within the radius
r
and

\mathcal{J}_{\beta}(r,s)=\exp\left[\int^{s}_{r}2\beta_{\rm ani}(r^{\prime})\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right].

(16)

We then get the modelled velocity dispersion along the LOS,
\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm model}
with (BT, eq. 4-60):

\left(\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm model}\right)^{2}=\frac{2}{\Sigma_{*}(R)}\int_{R}^{\infty}\left[1-\beta_{\rm ani}(r)\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]\frac{\rho_{*}(r)\sigma_{r}^{2}(r)}{\sqrt{r^{2}-R^{2}}}rdr,

(17)

with
R
the projected radius and
\Sigma_{*}
the enclosed surface stellar density, which can be constrained from the luminosity profile of the lens
I(R)
assuming, for instance, a constant mass-to-light ratio

\Upsilon

\Sigma_{*}(R)=\Upsilon I(R)
.

To compare this with the observed velocity dispersion along the LOS,
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
, we weigh
\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm model}
with the Point Spread Function (PSF,
\mathcal{P}
) convolved light profile of the lens:

\left(\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm sph}\right)^{2}=\frac{\int_{\rm A}I(R)\left(\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm model}\right)^{2}\ast\mathcal{P}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A}{\int_{A}I(R)\ast\mathcal{P}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}A}.

(18)

Following the methodology of TDCOSMO25, we do not account for the inclination angle of the lens galaxy during the JAM modelling. To prevent bias raised by
51
, we adopt the
axisymmetric correction factor

\sigma_{\rm axi}^{\rm ap}/\sigma_{\rm sph}^{\rm ap}
proposed by these authors. This factor was computed using the shape distribution from
57
and assuming random inclination. We then bin this factor in the same fashion as the IFU data (see Section
4.2
for more details) to obtain a correction factor for each bin, respectively:
0.992\pm 0.004
,
0.977\pm 0.001
and
0.993\pm 0.004
.
The final
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
to be compared with data is then given by:

\sigma^{\rm LOS}=\sigma^{\rm LOS}_{\rm sph}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{\rm axi}^{\rm ap}}{\sigma_{\rm sph}^{\rm ap}}

(19)

The prediction of the observed LOS velocity dispersion from any model, irrespective of the approach, can be decomposed into a cosmology-dependent ratio of angular diameter distances and a cosmology-independent part following
11
;
10
:

\displaystyle\left(\sigma^{\rm LOS}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa_{\rm ext})\frac{D_{\rm s}}{D_{\rm ds}}\frac{c^{2}}{J(\xi_{\rm lens},\xi_{\rm light},\beta_{\rm ani})},

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\kappa_{\rm ext})}{1+z_{\rm d}}\frac{D_{\rm\Delta t}}{D_{\rm d}}\frac{c^{2}}{J(\xi_{\rm lens},\xi_{\rm light},\beta_{\rm ani})},

(21)

where the dimensionless quantity
J
relies on the deflector model parameters (
\xi_{\rm lens}
and
\xi_{\rm light}
) and
\beta_{\rm ani}
. Moreover,
J
considers observational conditions and luminosity-weighting within the dispersion measurement aperture, as previously demonstrated in studies such as
6
and
104
.

As the angular diameter distances are sensitive to
\Omega_{\rm m}
only to the second order, they are primarily sensitive to
H_{\rm 0}
. Large samples of lenses and combination with probes independent from Time-delay Cosmography are required to constrain other parameters
(
59
;
90
, e.g.)
.

3.1
Optical photometric monitoring

As one of the first discovered strongly lensed quasars, multiple attempts at measuring the time delay of HE 1104
-
1805 with different observation campaigns have been published in the literature. First,
116
published 18 spectroscopic observations with the 3.6m ESO telescope spanning over 5 years and estimated that the time delay between images A and B,
\Delta t_{\rm AB}
, should be between 109 and 292 days. Reanalysis of these data by
45
and
74
showed that this sampling is insufficient for a precise delay. With a better sampling of the R-band photometry between 1997 and 2006 with the OGLE and SMARTS programs
3
3

3

http://www.astro.yale.edu/smarts/

76
estimated
\Delta t_{\rm AB}=152\pm 3
days using a Legendre-polynomial fit technique. Later,

\Delta t_{\rm AB}=162.2\pm 6.1

However, the method employed depends on physical assumptions on the quasar accretion disk profile and population of microlenses that could bias the measurement.

In this work, we use 3 new unpublished datasets and apply the established

The first dataset is provided by the SMARTS telescope, which monitored it from 2003 to 2016 with a
\sim

(

3.2
Curve shifting procedure

The time delay was computed using the method described extensively by
65
relying on free knot spline fitting implemented in the PyCS python package
4
4

4

We used version 3.0 from
https://cosmograil.gitlab.io/PyCS3/

(
67
)
. Free-knot splines are piece-wise polynomials where the mean number of days between two consecutive knots is controlled by the parameter
\eta
which filters out variation with shorter time scales. A single free-knot spline is used to model the intrinsic variability of the quasar, which is identical in both light curves, meanwhile, an additional spline is fitted on each light curve to model the extrinsic variability caused mainly by microlensing. The number of degrees of freedom is controlled by the hyper-parameter
\eta
for the intrinsic spline. For the extrinsic splines
n_{\rm ml}
sets the number of knots throughout the whole curve. We fit simultaneously the intrinsic, image A extrinsic, and image B extrinsic components and the time delay between the light curves. A visual first guess of the time delay is used as a starting point of the fit
5
5

5

This visual measurement is done on the publicly available D3CS tool:
https://obswww.unige.ch/~millon/d3cs/COSMOGRAIL_public/
. The bottom panel of Fig.
2
shows an example of such fit, giving us a point estimate of the time delay.

We repeat the fit 800 times with starting points randomized uniformly in a range of 10 days around the first guess value, we take the median value as the time delay measurement for a given (
\eta
,
n_{\rm ml}
).

To assess the uncertainty of this measurement in a data-driven way, a set of mock light curves is generated with built-in time delays uniformly drawn in an interval of
\pm 10
days around the median value obtained previously. We use the generative noise model described by
65
to ensure that the simulated light curves have the same noise properties as the real data. The distribution of the difference between the measurement and the truth for each of the mocks gives an estimation of the systematic uncertainty (mean of the distribution) and the statistical uncertainty (standard deviation) of the time delay estimation. The final uncertainty of the time delay estimation is the sum in quadrature of these two sources of uncertainties.

The choice of values for
\eta
and
n_{\rm ml}
is guided by the fact that quasars vary significantly faster than microlensing. Moreover, whether a given feature is mathematically attributed to the intrinsic or extrinsic splines depends on the flexibility balance between the splines. We therefore reproduce the same measurement for different values of these two hyper-parameters. Following
65
, we take
\eta\in[25,35,45,55]
days and
n_{\rm ml}\in[9,11,13,15]
days when fitting the SMARTS, ECAM and the merged datasets. In contrast, the microlensing in the much shorter WFI dataset is fitted with a spline having either 1 or 2 knots (
n_{\rm ml}\in[1,2]
) since the microlensing variability is expected to alter the light curve on time scales longer than a year.

The left panel of Fig.
3
shows the time-delay measurements obtained for each hyper-parameter configuration (
\eta
,
n_{\rm ml}
) considered.
The measurements obtained are consistent with each other with variable precision. We note that for a given
n_{\rm ml}
, fits with
\eta=55
are slightly lower, which shows that the intrinsic fit is marginally too rigid. We are therefore conservative by keeping this value and should not use higher values for
\eta
. We gradually marginalise the most precise measurement with the others in order of increasing precision until the tension with the rest of the sample is lower than the threshold
\tau
. Following the methodology of
65
, we use
\tau=0.5
(i.e. no individual measurement is further than 0.5
\sigma
away from the final measurement) to have a conservative uncertainty estimation without being biased by outlier measurements.

Since the three variability components are degenerate and not physically informed, the resulting splines cannot be interpreted as the true source and microlensing variations. However, a visual inspection shows that the magnitude scales of the extrinsic variations are below
0.3
mag over several years, consistent with the expected microlensing behavior
(
71
;
83
;
72
, e.g.)
. We, however, note that the number of features in the extrinsic splines is higher than in most systems, which hints toward a more complex microlensing behavior, as observed by
81
in previously published lightcurves, making the extrinsic fit sensitive to the hyperparameters changes.

The same experiment is carried out separately for each dataset and the right panel of Fig.
3
shows the time delay measured obtained in each case, the combined value, and the measurement on the merged ECAM+SMARTS+WFI dataset. The marginalization over the three datasets would be relevant if each dataset would be subject to different sources of systematic errors in the photometric measurement. However, the photometry was computed with the same method for each dataset, and no discrepancies were observed between time delays measured with ECAM and WFI data on other lens systems
(
65
;
64
, e.g.)
. As shown by the lower panel of Fig
2
, the extrinsic variations are complex during the 2017 season. The particular case of WFI is challenging because it covers only 253 days: given the long time delay, the light curves overlap on less than 100 days. Even though distinct features are visible and well-sampled in the WFI data, the complexity of the microlensing noticed previously cannot be adequately modelled with a single season.
The time delay with the WFI dataset is, thus, not robust enough for the marginalized time delay value to be trusted. Therefore, we prefer to use the ECAM+SMARTS+WFI dataset, which allows us to use the entire period of the light curve to constrain the microlensing behaviour as well as to take advantage of the high cadence and high signal-to-noise (SNR) display of the WFI dataset.

We do not take into account a possible microlensing time delay
(
101
)
because it impacts the time delay only by a day in the worst cases
(
16
)
, which is negligible given the length of the time delay. Therefore, we will use the time-delay
\Delta t_{\rm AB}=176.3^{+11.4}_{-10.3}
days, i.e. a 5.8
\%
precision for our cosmography analysis.

5.2
Perturbers and groups explicitly included in the lens model

As shown in Fig.
1
we identify 9 perturbers within 5
′′
of the lens in the HST imaging. Our initial step hence involves identifying the perturbers that we need to explicitly include to the lens model.

The “flexion shift” was introduced by
62
to determine which line-of-sight galaxies should be explicitly included in the lens modelling and which ones should be implicitly accounted for through the computation of
\kappa_{\rm ext}
.
The specific influence of line-of-sight objects depends on whether the dominant-lens approximation holds, indicating that the critical density of these objects is much smaller than that of the primary deflector
(
41
)
.
The difference in lens image position induced by a given nearby galaxy is given by:

\Delta_{3}x=f\left(\beta\right)\frac{\left(\theta_{\rm E,lens}\cdot\theta_{\rm E,pert}\right)^{2}}{\theta^{3}},

(23)

with
\theta_{\rm E,lens}
and
\theta_{\rm E,pert}
as the Einstein radii of the lens and perturber and
\theta
as the angular separation of the lens and perturber. The function
f\left(\beta\right)
equals 1 if the perturber is between the lens and the observer while
f\left(\beta\right)=\left(1-\beta\right)^{2}

if the perturber is behind the main lens.
\beta

is the pre-factor of the lens deflection in the multiplane lens equation:
\beta=\left(D_{\rm dp}D_{\rm s}\right)/\left(D_{\rm p}D_{\rm ds}\right)
.
62
found that perturbers with
\Delta_{3}x\leq 10^{-4}
are accurately accounted for with a global external convergence term whereas perturbers with a higher flexion shift should be individually modelled with a multi-plane treatment.

We use the
M_{*}-\sigma^{\rm LOS}
relation obtained
121
on galaxies up to redshift 0.7 to estimate the mass-to-light ratio, M/L, of the P5 perturber using its velocity dispersion
\sigma^{\rm LOS}

P5
measured with the MUSE data (see Appendix
B.2
for more details). The flux determined with a circular aperture around P5 in the F160W band converted into luminosity using a fiducial cosmology with
H_{\rm 0}=70

\mathrm{km}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
and
\Omega_{\rm m}=0.3
. By applying the same M/L ratio to the other perturbers, we estimate their mass and
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
. Given the uncertainty of the
\sigma^{\rm LOS}

P5
measurement, the uncertainty on the fiducial cosmological parameters and the fact that some perturbers are assumed to be further the range studied by
121
can be neglected.
By approximating the perturbers’ mass distributions with an SIS we compute their Einstein radius,
\theta_{\rm E,SIS}
with :

\theta_{\rm E,SIS}=4\pi\left(\frac{\sigma^{\rm LOS}}{c}\right)^{2}\frac{D_{\rm ps}}{D_{\rm s}}

(24)

with
D_{\rm ps}
and
D_{\rm s}
the angular diameter between the source and perturber and between the observer and the source, respectively.

We assume that the perturbers without a redshift measurement lie between the closest perturber P5 and the source. We therefore repeat the computation with redshifts uniformly drawn between
z_{5}
and
z_{\rm s}
and take the mean and the 16th and 84th percentile (

We can then estimate the flexion shift caused by each perturber using Eq.
23

5.4
External convergence (
\kappa_{\rm ext}
) measurement

Similarly to the previous time-delay cosmography studies
(
78
;
12
;
79
, e.g.)
, we use the number count technique to determine
\kappa_{\rm ext}
. This quantity determines the contribution of objects not included in the lens modelling.
Number counts involve measuring the galaxy number density near the lens as a summary statistic and comparing it to reference fields. This comparison helps determine whether the LOS is over- or under-dense compared to the average background
(
40
;
47
;
113
, e.g.)
.

It can be summarised in 4 main steps:

•

Lines of sight (typically several arcminutes wide squares) are drawn from a large comparison field and compared to the LOS of the lens. Modern survey datasets covering hundreds to thousands of square degrees ensure a sufficiently large comparison field to avoid sampling bias. The photometric catalogues of the fields are cleared from objects more distant than the lensed source and fainter than the used magnitude cut on the
i
¡ 24
(
40
, e.g., )
. This cut is designed to consider only objects with reliable photometry.

•

The weight value of a given LOS
W_{i}
is then computed as the ratio of the weighted number counts for galaxies in the lens field to the same statistic in the reference field.

W_{i}=\frac{\Sigma_{j}w_{j,{\rm lens}}}{\Sigma_{j}w_{j,i}}

(25)

where
j
indexes the galaxies in a given LOS and
w
is the weight of a galaxy. Different types of weights can be considered
(
47
;
78
;
113
, e.g., )
such as the galaxy’s distance to the center (
1/r_{j}
), its potential (
m_{j}/r_{j}
), its redshift (
z_{j}(z_{\rm s}-z_{j})
) or simply be the same for each galaxy (
w_{j}=1
). The weight value obtained, therefore act as a tracer of the external convergence of the lens LOS.

•

Similar weight values are computed in simulated fields with known
\kappa_{\rm ext}

(
49
)
. Previous works
(
78
;
113
, e.g.)
, have shown that using the Millennium Simulation yields a reliable estimate of
\kappa_{\rm ext}
because it provides catalogues of galaxies for several values of the external convergence at sufficient points to represent the Universe accurately. Since the weight value relies on ratios, many dependencies on the simulation’s underlying cosmological parameters are expected to cancel out.

•

The posterior probability of
\kappa_{\rm ext}
can hence be computed as follows:

p(\kappa_{\rm ext}|d)=\int p_{\rm sim}(\kappa_{\rm ext}|W_{i})p(W_{i}|d)\Pi_{i}dW_{i}.

(26)

Here,
p(W|d)
represents the probability distribution of the weighting scheme
W
given the data, and
p_{\rm sim}(\kappa_{\rm ext}|
W
)
denotes the probability distribution of
\kappa_{\rm ext}
in the simulated dataset, conditioned on a specific value of the
weight
W
.
47
showed that the combination of multiple evaluations relying on different weights (while adequately taking into account covariances between
estimates) significantly improves the precision and robustness of the measurement.

As detailed previously, the treatment of perturbers within 5
′′
from the lens is part of the explicit lens model. We, therefore, mask this region from the number counting and remove the galaxies forming the perturber groups presented in Table
2
, as in
113
we limit the field to 120
′′
around the lens. The weights used are the number counts
w_{1}
and the inverse distance
w_{1/r}
of objects. Their computation is displayed in Fig.
7
and Fig.
8
displays the final measurement
\kappa_{\rm ext}\ =-0.019_{-0.020}^{+0.058}
.
Our procedure in fact determines
\kappa_{\rm s}
, the convergence up to the source which we use as a proxy for
\kappa_{\rm ext}
. The external convergence is technically computed using the observer-deflector and deflector-source convergences
\kappa_{\rm d}
and
\kappa_{\rm ds}
through:
\kappa_{\rm ext}=\left(1-\kappa_{\rm d}\right)/\left(1-\kappa_{\rm s}\right)\cdot\left(1-\kappa_{\rm ds}\right)
. However,
99
showed that ignoring this correction only affects the
H_{\rm 0}
measurement when the LOS up to the deflector is significantly over- or under-dense, which is not the case here. At the population level, the expected bias on
H_{\rm 0}
due to this approximation is about 0.1%, which is negligible compared to the present 4.6% precision of TDCOSMO25.

Therefore, this measurement is used for the rest of the cosmographic analysis, without further correction, in compliance with the perturber inclusion strategy.

6.2.1
Main deflector mass and light model:

Following the methodology of the most recent TDCOSMO lens models
(
87
;
88
, e.g., )
, we model the lens galaxy with two families of models: power law and a composite alternatively.

In the first case, the mass is parametrized as a power-law elliptical mass distribution (PEMD) for which the convergence at the position
\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right)
in the frame aligned with the major and minor axis of the deflector is given by:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm PL}\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right)=\frac{3-\gamma_{\rm pl}}{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{\rm E}}{\sqrt{q_{m}\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}/q_{m}}}\right)^{\gamma_{\rm pl}-1},

(27)

where
\theta_{\rm E}
is the Einstein radius,
\gamma_{\rm PL}
the slope of the profile and
q_{m}
its axis ratio. This convergence map is then rotated by the position angle
\phi_{m}
to belong to the on-sky coordinate frame.

The lens light is modelled by a superposition of two Sérsic profile which gives the light intensity at the position
\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right)
following:

\displaystyle I_{\rm{Sersic}}\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right)=I_{\rm eff}\exp\left\{-b_{n}\left[\left(\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}/q^{2}}}{\theta_{\rm eff}}\right)^{1/n}-1\right]\right\}

(28)

with
\theta_{\rm eff}
the effective radius (i.e. the product average of semi-major and semi-minor axis), also defined as the half-light radius thanks to the normalizing factor
b_{n}
,
I_{\rm eff}
the intensity at
\theta_{\rm eff}
.
q
is its axis ratio and
n
the Sérsic index.

The position of the two Sersic profile are tied together throughout the fit and the position of the mass component’s center is fixed on the light component.

The chameleon profile can be parametrized as the difference between two non-singular
isothermal ellipsoid (NIE) profiles
(
98
, e.g., )
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm Chm}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{\kappa_{0,{\rm Chm}}}{(1+q)}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}/q^{2}+4w_{\rm c}^{2}/(1+q)^{2}}}-\right.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}/q^{2}+4w_{t}^{2}/(1+q)^{2}}}\right]

(29)

where
q
is the axis ratio and
w_{\rm c}
and
w_{\rm t}
are sizes of the cores of the two NIE with
w_{\rm t}
¿
w_{\rm c}
.

For the composite model, the baryonic mass
is described by a double chameleon profile (i.e. two superposed chameleon profiles presented in Eq.
29
).

The dark matter halo is modelled with an elliptical Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile, whose volumic density is given by:

\rho(r)=\frac{\rho_{0}}{\left(\frac{r}{R_{\rm s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{r}{R_{\rm s}}\right)^{2}}

(30)

where
\rho_{0}
is a normalization and
R_{\rm s}
is the scale radius.

Following the measurement of
44
on galaxies that have a similar
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
as the one measured in this system, we expect the NFW’s scale radius to be within
R_{\rm s}=58\pm 8
kpc. To prevent any prior bias, we take this value as a starting point within a wide uniform prior
R_{\rm s}\sim\mathcal{U}(25,100)
kpc.

We use a double chameleon profile to model the light, and the fitted parameters are fixed to the parameters of the baryonic mass model.

To avoid the lens light modelling from being biased by background light while still predicting accurate flux at the position of the arc, we mask pixels further than 4.3
′′
from the lens. We adjust the mask locally to mask the light of all the perturbers and mask the lens’s central pixels because the light is not fitted perfectly in this region. Masking the few pixels close to the center of the lens prevents the model from predicting source flux at this position, which can possibly bias the inferred mass profile.

6.2.2
Source light model

The visible source light is smooth and we model it with a single elliptical Sérsic profile in both families of models. Contrary to previous TDCOSMO studies, no further structure is detected in the lensed arc. Thus, it is not required to add shapelets to describe extra complexity.

6.2.3
Perturbers model

To include the perturbers in the mass model, we add an SIS component to the mass model for each perturber. Even though we do not fit their light in the final model, an preliminary Sérsic fit gives us their position. Since P5 and P6 have different redshifts than the lens, we use the multi-lens-plane formalism introduced in Eqs.
4
and
8
. For the other perturbers considered, we assume that they lie in the same plane as the lens. We consider the perturbers P2, P5, and P6 as they have the highest flexion shifts, and P9 because of its proximity to image B.
Furthermore, the perturber groups listed in Table
2
are parametrized with NFW profiles. We estimate their scale radii using their virial mass (based on measured
R_{\rm 200}
and
v_{\rm disp}
) and estimation of the NFW concentration parameter,
R_{\rm 200}/R_{\rm S}
from
32
. The groups 4, 5 and 6 are alternatively included in the model.

6.2.5
Posterior sampling and combination of different models:

After obtaining a satisfactory PSF, shown in Fig.
18
, we use
Lenstronomy
’s built-in MCMC routine relying on EMCEE
(
42
)
to sample the light and mass parameters (
\xi_{\rm light}
and
\xi_{\rm mass}
) posterior by maximizing the likelihood expressed as:

\mathcal{P}(\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass}|{\rm d})\propto\mathcal{L}(d|\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})p(\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass}),

(31)

where
\mathcal{L}(d|\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})
is the likelihood of the parameters given the data and
p(\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})
is the prior on the parameters. During this step, the data used to constrain the posterior are the unmasked pixels of the HST observation
\mathcal{D}_{\rm Img}
and the time delay,
\Delta t_{\rm AB}
. The likelihood maximized by the sampling is therefore computed with:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(d|\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})=\mathcal{L}({\rm Img}|\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}(\Delta t_{\rm AB}|\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass})

(32)

which is, up to an additive constant,

\displaystyle=\log\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}^{N_{\rm pix}}\frac{(\mathcal{D}_{\rm Img,i}-M_{\rm Img,\textit{i}})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right]+\log\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\Delta t_{\rm AB}-M_{\Delta t_{\rm AB}})^{2}}{\sigma_{\Delta t_{\rm AB}}}\right],

(33)

where
M_{\rm Img}
and
M_{\Delta t_{\rm AB}}
are the model predicted image and time delay of the system ;
\sigma_{i}
is the pixels’s noise and
\sigma_{\Delta t_{\rm AB}}
the uncertainty on the time delay.
We use 10 times as many walkers as there are parameters in the model, and we use the last 1 000 out of 20 000 iterations to construct the posterior of a given modelling setup. The best reconstructions of the HST imaging with power-law and composite models shown in Fig.
9
demonstrate that all relevant features in the light are correctly predicted.
The convergence profile obtained by each model family in every perturber-inclusion scenario are displayed in Fig.
10
. We notice that, with both model families, the convergence of the perturbers P5, P6 and G1 varies substantially between the
different scenarios. This can be attributed to the fact that these components are aligned in the same direction relative to the main lens, resulting in a degenerate effect on the system. Conversely, the convergence of the pertubers P2, P9 and groups G2, G3 are more stable as they are more azimuthally spread.
We also note that in every scenario, the composite model is cuspier than the power-law one.

The BIC values computed with the maximum likelihood of each model are presented in Table
3
. The variance of the BIC is
\sigma_{\Delta BIC}
= 73 within the power-law models and
\sigma_{\Delta BIC}
= 48 within the composite models.

Similarly to
10
and
88
, we determine the weight of each posterior distribution following:

\mathcal{W}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\Delta BIC}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x)\rm{exp}\left(-\frac{(BIC-x)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\Delta BIC}}\right)dx,

(34)

where
f(x)
is the evidence ratio function given by:

\displaystyle f\left(x\right)\equiv\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $x\leq$ BIC${}_{\rm min}$,}\\
\rm{exp}(\rm{BIC}_{\rm min}-$x$)&\textrm{if $x$ > BIC${}_{\rm min}$,}\\
\end{cases}

(35)

with BIC
min
the BIC of the reference model with the minimum BIC value.

To create a single posterior out of
N
multiple models, we sum all the posteriors using their normalized weight:

\mathcal{W}=\frac{\sum_{n}\mathcal{W}_{n}p(\xi_{\rm light},\xi_{\rm mass}|d)}{\sum_{n}\max(\mathcal{W}_{n})}.

(36)

This procedure is done separately for the composite and power law models, and we compare the results in Fig.
11
. For both models, the Einstein radius the slope and the time-delay distance are robust to the different perturber configurations whereas the external shear shows multimodality because the G6 group center is azimuthally significantly different from groups G4 and G5 (see Fig.
10
). A blind comparison of the two modelling families allows us to see that a 2.1
-\sigma
disparity between the two families on
\gamma
induces a 1.3
-\sigma
difference the Fermat potential and 1
-\sigma
for the time-delay distance between the two families.
The external shear we infer for HE 1104
-
1805 is higher than in other TDCOSMO lenses. As shown by
36
, model-predicted
\gamma_{\rm ext}
in power-law + shear configurations is often overestimated even in perturber-free systems because it is degenerate with potential azimuthal structures, which have negligible impact on
H_{\rm 0}
at the population level
(
108
)
.
In Section
5.2
, we consider only 9 perturbers, out of which 5 are explicitly modelled, but many more are visible in the right panel of Fig.
1
. These perturbers are expected to induce external shear on the main lens and induce a large
\gamma_{\rm ext}
. More marginally, the positions of the center of mass of the groups of pertubers, G1-G6, are poorly constrained and might also inflate the value of
\gamma_{\rm ext}
.

8.1
Time-delay

The measurement of the time delay is marginally higher than previous estimates
(e.g.
76
;
70
, measured
\Delta t_{\rm AB}
= 162
\pm
6.1 days)
. It relies on the longest and best-sampled lightcurve of that system with the two most recent (ECAM and WFI) datasets having at least twice the cadence as the previous SMARTS dataset. It was estimated with a data-driven algorithm whose accuracy was assessed on multiple simulated and observed light curves, which is crucial, as we saw that the extrinsic variability is particularly challenging to model in this system. We therefore have the most robust estimate for this object.

8.2
Resolved kinematics

The main limitation of the resolved velocity dispersion measurement comes from the proximity and brightness of image A to the lens galaxy. Nevertheless, by subtracting the quasar images, we were able to extract spectra from 3 concentric bins with SNR ¿ 14
\AA^{-1}
. Using state-of-the-art methodology to constrain the effect of stellar template fitting hyperparameters (polynomial degrees and stellar template libraries), we provide the velocity dispersion measurement in each bin. We ensured the robustness of these measurements against different spectral and spatial masks and prove that the lens is a slow rotator (see Appendix
A.2
). The latter justifies the use of spherically aligned JAM for the kinematic modelling.

8.3
Image and kinematic modelling

The image reconstruction was performed with the two main parametrization families: power-law and composite. Three configurations of nearby perturbers were accounted for individually, with the addition of SIS and NFW components and the resulting measurements were marginalized upon these configurations.

The main difference between the two models resides in the slope of the radial mass profile
\gamma
. Such difference was already observed in modelling the quadruply lensed quasar WGD 2038
-
4008
(
88
)
, where the system’s compactness only allowed to probe the inner regions of the mass profile.
In the present case, the resolved velocity dispersion heavily favors the power-law model over the composite for this system. This may be due to the fact that HE 1104
-
1805’s configuration offers too few constraints for a composite model thus leading to potential biases or simply that the power law model is a better description over the range probed.
The discrepency between the external convergence,
\kappa_{\rm ext}
, and the external shear,
\gamma_{\rm ext}
, inferred independently might be counter-intuitive in light of simulations’ properties
(
99
, e.g.)
. However, in this work
\gamma_{\rm ext}
is primarly encompassing the effect of perturbers within 5
′′
of the main deflector whereas
\kappa_{\rm ext}
is inferred from galaxies up to 120
′′
away, excluding the ones within 5
′′
. As discussed in Section
6.2.5
, the large value of
\gamma_{\rm ext}
may may arise from genuine external shear or simply be inflated by inherent lens-modeling degeneracies. We cannot disentangle the contribution of each effect with the available data, nor rule out unmodeled convergence. However, since the brightest perturbers we consider are already low-mass (see upper part of Table
2
), any additional convergence from fainter perturbers should be small.

8.4
Implications for Time delay cosmography

This work shows that the high precision of the time-delay measurement and the use of resolved kinematics allows us to reach a precision of 8.5% on
H_{\rm 0}
with a double lens quasar which is comparable to previous TDCOSMO single lens measurements ranging from 4% to 28%
(
66
;
117
)
.
The main limitation of this work is the fact that we fix the internal mass-sheet degeneracy parameter,
\lambda_{\rm int}
= 1. This parameter will be constrained during the next joint hierarchical modelling of the TDCOSMO lens sample. The
H_{\rm 0}
value and precision of this system are therefore bound to change as:
H_{\rm 0}

\rightarrow

H_{\rm 0}

\lambda_{\rm int}
, (
\delta
H_{\rm 0}
/
H_{\rm 0}
)
{}^{2}\rightarrow(\delta
H_{\rm 0}
/
H_{\rm 0}

)^{2}+\delta\lambda_{\rm int}^{2}
.

Overall, this work is a stepping stone towards building a sample of double-lens quasar-based
H_{\rm 0}
measurement which is vital to investigate potential biases of the TDCOSMO method and to accelerate progress toward a 1% measurement of
H_{\rm 0}
.

Is the lens rotating ?

To investigate bias in the
\sigma^{\rm LOS}
measurement due to possible rotation of the lens galaxy, we design three spatial masks cutting half of the lens light area as shown in the left panel of
15
. The three masks are chosen to ensure an equivalent SNR for the three configurations while minimising the spatial overlap. With the same methodology as described in Section
4.2

v^{\rm LOS}_{1,2,3}

The bottom panel of Fig.
15
shows that there is no sign of ordered rotation. These observations are, hence, consistent with the assumption that the lens galaxy is a slow rotator.
