Szegő mapping and Hermite–Padé polynomials for multiple orthogonality on the unit circle
Rostyslav Kozhan 1, Marcus Vaktnäs 1

Abstract
. We investigate generalized Laurent multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle satisfying simultaneous orthogonality conditions with respect to r r probability measures or linear functionals on the unit circle. We show that these polynomials can be characterized as solutions of a general two-point Hermite–Padé approximation problem. We derive Szegő-type recurrence relations, establish compatibility conditions for the associated recurrence coefficients, and obtain Christoffel–Darboux formulas as well as Heine-type determinantal representations. Furthermore, by extending the Szegő mapping and the Geronimus relations, we relate these Laurent multiple orthogonal polynomials to multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line, thereby making explicit the connection between multiple orthogonality on the unit circle and on the real line.



1.
Introduction

While multiple orthogonality on the real line has been extensively developed over the past decades, its analogue on the unit circle, introduced by Mínguez and Van Assche
[
MOPUC1
]
, still remains a relatively new area of study. Nevertheless, recent work
[
MOPUC2
,
KVMOPUC
,
KVMLOPUC1
,
KNik
,
HueMan
]
indicates that multiple orthogonality on the unit circle exhibits a rich and intricate structure that warrants a unified and systematic treatment.

In this paper, we introduce a generalized notion of Laurent multiple orthogonal polynomials, which provides a natural framework for the generalized Hermite–Padé problem and for connecting with the Szegő mapping and Geronimus relations, linking the theory to multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line. Before presenting our main results, we summarize some foundational aspects of the theory that will be needed throughout the paper. In particular, we summarize key facts about orthogonal polynomials on the real line (Section
1.1

), their connections to Padé and Hermite–Padé approximation, and the Szegő mapping (Section
1.3
). A concise overview of our main results is provided in Section
1.4
.

1.1.
Orthogonality on the real line and Hermite–Padé approximation

We start by reviewing the basic theory of orthogonal and multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line, together with the associated Padé and Hermite–Padé approximation problems.
Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with all moments

c_{j}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{j}d\mu(x),\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\},

finite. The Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of
\mu
is the function, analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, given by

(1)

m(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\mu(x)}{x-z}=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}z^{-j-1},\qquad z\to\infty.

The monic (i.e., with leading term 1) orthogonal polynomials of
\mu
are then defined by requiring
\deg{P_{n}}=n
and

(2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{n}(x)x^{k}d\mu(x)=0,\qquad k=0,\dots,n-1.

They satisfy the three-term recurrence relation

(3)

xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+b_{n}P_{n}(x)+a_{n}P_{n-1}(x),

where
a_{n}>0
,
n\geq 1
, (
a_{0}:=0
) and
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are called the Jacobi coefficients.
See
[
SzegoBook
,
SimonL2
]
for more details.

One of the classical settings in which orthogonal polynomials naturally appear is Padé approximation, see, e.g.,
[
Baker
]
for a comprehensive treatment.
Given a function
m(z)
as in (
1
), the Padé problem seeks polynomials
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
of degrees at most
n
and
n-1
, respectively, such that

(4)

P_{n}(z)m(z)+Q_{n}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-1}),\qquad z\to\infty.

It can be shown that all the solutions
P_{n}
of this problem are exactly the orthogonal polynomials defined by (
2
).

The Hermite–Padé approximation problem concerns simultaneous approximation of Cauchy–Stieltjes transforms, generalizing (
4
). Let
m_{1}(z),\ldots,m_{r}(z)
be the Cauchy–Stieltjes transforms of
r
measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
on
\mathbb{R}
. For a multi-index
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, we look for polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}
and
Q_{\bm{n},j}
of degree
\leq|\bm{n}|:=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}
and
\leq|\bm{n}|-1
satisfying

(5)

P_{\bm{n}}(z)m_{j}(z)+Q_{\bm{n},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}-1}),\qquad z\to\infty,\quad j=1,\ldots,r.

One of the fundamental results in this theory is that a polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
solves (
5
) if and only if it satisfies simultaneous orthogonality conditions

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\bm{n}}(x)x^{k}d\mu_{j}(x)=0,\qquad k=0,\ldots,n_{j}-1,\quad j=1,\ldots,r,

with respect to the system of measures

\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})
. Such polynomials are called (type II) multiple orthogonal polynomials of
\bm{\mu}
.
This is a very well developed area of research, see, e.g.,
[
Aptekarev
,
Ismail
,
Applications
,
Nikishin
]
and references therein.

1.2.
Orthogonality on the unit circle and two-point Hermite–Padé approximation

Let us now introduce the parallel theory on the complex unit circle
[
OPUC1
,
OPUC2
,
GeronimusBook
,
SzegoBook
]
. We start with a probability measure supported on
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, and denote its moments

c_{j}=\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}w^{-j}d\mu(w),\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

The Carathéodory function of
\mu
is defined to be the analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}
function
F(z)
given by

(7)

F(z)=\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}\frac{w+z}{w-z}d\mu(w)=\begin{cases}c_{0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k},&z\to 0,\\
-c_{0}-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{-k}z^{-k},&z\to\infty.\end{cases}

The monic orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{n}(z))_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
\mu
are defined by requiring
\deg{\Phi_{n}}=n
and

(8)

\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}\Phi_{n}(w)w^{-k}d\mu(w)=0,\qquad k=0,\dots,n-1.

They satisfy the Szegő recurrence relations given by

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(z)

\displaystyle=z\Phi_{n}(z)+{\alpha}_{n+1}\Phi^{*}_{n}(z),

(10)

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}_{n+1}(z)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)+\bar{\alpha}_{n+1}z\Phi_{n}(z),

where the Szegő dual polynomials
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\bar{z})}
can be defined via
\Phi_{n}^{*}(0)=1
and

(11)

\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}\Phi^{*}_{n}(w)w^{-k}d\mu(w)=0,\qquad k=1,\dots,n.

The recurrence coefficients
\alpha_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, belong to open unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<1\right\}
, and are known as the Verblunsky coefficients of
\mu
(also referred to in the literature as Schur, Szegő, or Geronimus coefficients).
1
1

1

We warn the reader that here we are choosing
\alpha_{n}=\Phi_{n}(0)
instead of the nowadays more commonly used
\alpha_{n}=\overline{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}
(see the historical discussion in
[
OPUC1
, p.10]
).

The associated Padé approximation problem takes the following
two-point
form, see, e.g.
[
Baker
,
Bultheel
,
JNT
,
PeherstorferSteinbauer
]
and references therein.
Given a function
F(z)
as in (
7
), we seek polynomials
\Phi_{n}
and
\Psi_{n}
of degrees at most
n
, such that

(12)

\displaystyle\Phi_{{n}}(z)F(z)+\Psi_{{n}}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0,

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{{n}}(z)F(z)+\Psi_{{n}}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-1}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow\infty.

All polynomial solutions
\Phi_{n}
of (
12
)–(
13
) are constant multiples of the orthogonal polynomials defined by (
8
).

In
[
MOPUC1
]
, Mínguez and Van Assche considered the Hermite–Padé approximation problem of finding
polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n}}(z),\Psi_{\bm{n},1}(z),\ldots,\Psi_{\bm{n},r}(z)
of degree
\leq|\bm{n}|
that satisfy

(14)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n}}(z)F_{j}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n_{j}}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0,

(15)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n}}(z)F_{j}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-1}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow\infty,

for
r
Carathéodory functions (
7
)
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}
associated with measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
on
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}
, and showed that
\Phi_{\bm{n}}(z)
solves (
14
)–(
15
) if and only if it
fulfills the simultaneous orthogonality relations

(16)

\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}\Phi_{\bm{n}}(w)w^{-k}d\mu_{j}(w)=0,\qquad k=0,\ldots,n_{j}-1,\quad j=1,\ldots,r.

The polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n}}(z)
are therefore called (type II) multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. See
[
MOPUC1
,
MOPUC2
,
KVMOPUC
]
for further results on the properties of
\Phi_{\bm{n}}
.

1.3.
Szegő mapping and Geronimus relations

Finally, we recall the Szegő mapping and the Geronimus relations, which provide a fundamental connection between the theories of orthogonal polynomials on the real line and on the unit circle. Given any probability measure
\gamma
supported on the real interval
[-2,2]
, one can define a probability measure
\mu=\operatorname{Sz}(\gamma)
to be the probability measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}
, that is invariant under the reflection
e^{i\theta}\mapsto e^{-i\theta}
and satisfies

(17)

\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}g(2\cos\theta)d\mu(e^{i\theta})=\int_{-2}^{2}g(x)d\gamma(x),

for all measurable functions
g
on
[-2,2]
. Conversely, any probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}
that is invariant under
e^{i\theta}\mapsto e^{-i\theta}
arises as
\mu=\operatorname{Sz}(\gamma)
for a unique probability measure
\gamma
on
[-2,2]
determined by (
17
). Szegő showed
[
Szego
]
that the orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}
on the real line with respect to
\gamma
and the orthogonal polynomials
\Phi_{n}
on the unit circle with respect to
\mu
are connected by the identities

(18)

P_{n}(z+z^{-1})=\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{2n}}\big(z^{-n}\Phi_{2n}(z)+z^{n}\Phi_{2n}(1/z)\big)

Later, Geronimus
[
GeronimusRelations
,
GeronimusBook
]
derived explicit formulas relating the recurrence coefficients of
\gamma
and of
\mu
:

(19)

\displaystyle a^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha_{2n-2})(1-\alpha_{2n-1}^{2})(1-\alpha_{2n}),\qquad

\displaystyle n\geq 1,

(20)

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{2n})\alpha_{2n-1}-(1+\alpha_{2n})\alpha_{2n+1},\qquad

\displaystyle n\geq 0,

with the convention
\alpha_{-1}=0
.

1.4.
Summary of main results

In this paper we introduce the Laurent multiple orthogonal polynomials defined by

(21)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)w^{-k}d\mu_{j}(w)=0,\qquad k=-m_{j},\dots,n_{j}-1,\qquad j=1,\dots,r,

(22)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\in\operatorname{span}\left\{z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|},z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|+1},\dots,{z^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}}\right\}.

Here
\bm{n}
and
\bm{m}
are two arbitrary multi-indices in
\mathbb{N}^{r}
. In particular, if
\bm{m}=\bm{0}
, then
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{0}}
are the
multiple orthogonal polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n}}
of Mínguez–Van Assche
[
MOPUC1
]
.

The motivation for studying this class of polynomials is two-fold. First, we show (see Sections
3
–
4
) that a Laurent polynomial
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)
satisfying (
22
) fulfills the orthogonality conditions (
21
) if and only if it solves the two-point Hermite–Padé approximation problem

(23)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F_{j}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n_{j}}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0,

(24)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F_{j}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-m_{j}-1}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow\infty,

for suitable Laurent polynomials
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}
. Here
F_{j}
are the Carathéodory functions associated with the measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
.
In the scalar case
r=1
, one readily verifies that
\Phi_{n;m}(z)=z^{-m}\Phi_{n+m}(z)
.
If
r\geq 2
, such a trivial relationship with
\Phi_{\bm{n}}
in (
16
), of course, no longer holds.

Our second motivation for introducing the Laurent framework (
21
) is that it naturally accommodates the Szegő mapping and the Geronimus relations. This allows us to connect our results with the theory of multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line, a connection that is not available in the multiple polynomial setting (
16
) considered in
[
MOPUC1
,
MOPUC2
,
KVMOPUC
]
. These connections are established in Theorems
10.2
and
10.3
in Section
10
.

To achieve this goal, we first develop the underlying framework, introducing the relevant notions and establishing several structural results that will be used throughout the paper. In Section
5
, we show that the polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
satisfy a family of nearest-neighbour recurrence relations, which can be viewed as a natural extension of the classical Szegő recurrences for orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. In Section
6
we derive a system of nearest-neighbour compatibility relations satisfied by the recurrence coefficients and the orthogonal polynomials. Section
7
is devoted to a collection of identities that follow from these relations; although somewhat technical in nature, they provide the main tools required for the proof of the Geronimus relations. In Section
8
we establish analogues of the Heine determinantal formulas for the Laurent polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
, as well as for all associated recurrence coefficients. Section
9
contains the Christoffel–Darboux formula.
The results of Sections
5
,
6
, and
9
follow the same pattern as for the multiple orthogonal polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n}}
, with the main distinction that
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
involves two independently varying multi-indices.
The corresponding proofs follow closely arguments developed in our
[
KVMOPUC
]
, with appropriate modifications to accommodate the present more general framework.

We also note that in this work we adopt the broader setting, where orthogonality with respect to measures is replaced by orthogonality with respect to moment linear functionals. This generalization does not introduce any additional technical complications, and all statements and proofs extend naturally to it. Readers who prefer the classical setting may simply interpret each functional as integration with respect to a measure.

Laurent orthogonal polynomials have also been considered in several very recent works
[
KVMLOPUC1
,
KNik
,
HueMan
]
.
The polynomials in
[
HueMan
]
correspond to the mixed multiple orthogonality setting. While their work studies recurrence relations, Christoffel–Darboux formula, and spectral transformations, only for very specific multi-indices do their polynomials coincide with ours, because their focus is restricted to step-line indices. Furthermore, because they do not introduce the II
∗
and I
∗
families, their formulas take a substantially different form.
The polynomials
\varphi_{2\bm{n}}
in
[
KVMLOPUC1
,
KNik
]
coincide with the Laurent polynomials
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{n}}
studied here, and normality (existence and uniqueness) for
\varphi_{\bm{n}}
was established for broad classes of measures, including Angelesco, AT, and Nikishin systems on the unit circle. Notice that this establishes existence and uniqueness of
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{n}}
and of the Hermite–Padé approximants for (
23
)–(
24
) in the case
\bm{n}=\bm{m}
. The extent to which these results generalize to arbitrary indices
(\bm{n};\bm{m})
is currently an interesting open problem. Another natural question concerns the location of zeros, which is closely related to the issue of normality, as indicated by the ideas in
[
KVInterlacing
]
. We leave these questions for future work.

2.
Orthogonality Relations and Normality

Let
r
sequences
(c_{k,j})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
j=1,\ldots,r
, of arbitrary complex numbers be given; we refer to them as moments. We define the corresponding moment functionals
L_{j}
as the linear maps on the space of Laurent polynomials with complex coefficients, uniquely determined by

(25)

L_{j}[w^{-k}]=c_{k,j},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad j=1,\dots,r.

Of special importance is the case when a functional
L_{j}
is induced by a probability measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}

(26)

L_{j}[w^{-k}]=\int_{\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}}w^{-k}d\mu_{j}(w),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In what follows let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers,
\mathbb{N}
be the set of non-negative integers. Given two vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, we write
\bm{u}\geq\bm{v}
if
u_{j}\geq v_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,r
.
Finally, we denote
|\bm{v}|=v_{1}+\ldots+v_{r}
. Note that we do
not
take the absolute value of
v_{j}
, even though
v_{j}
may be negative; this is done on purpose. In particular,
\bm{u}\geq\bm{v}
implies
|\bm{u}|\geq|\bm{v}|
.

Given two
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
indices
\bm{n}
,
\bm{m}
, let us denote

(27)

\mathfrak{C}_{2r}=\left\{(\bm{n};\bm{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\times\mathbb{Z}^{r}:n_{j}+m_{j}\geq 0\mbox{ for all }j\right\}

for the remainder of the text.

For
(\bm{n};\bm{m})\in\mathfrak{C}_{2r}
, define the
(|\bm{n}|+|\bm{m}|)\times(|\bm{n}|+|\bm{m}|)
matrix

(28)

T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|-m_{1},1}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|-m_{1}+1,1}\\
c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|-m_{1}+1,1}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|-m_{1}+2,1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|+n_{1}-1,1}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|+n_{1},1}\\[1.99997pt]
\hline\cr[0.5pt/2pt]\\[-11.99998pt]
&\vdots\\[1.99997pt]
\hline\cr[0.5pt/2pt]\\[-10.00002pt]
c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|-m_{r},r}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|-m_{r}+1,r}\\
c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|-m_{r}+1,r}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|-m_{r}+2,r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{\left|\bm{m}\right|+n_{r}-1,r}&\cdots&c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|+n_{r},r}\end{array}\right).

If
n_{j}=-m_{j}
for some
j
, then the corresponding
j
-th block in the above matrix is empty. Furthermore, if
\bm{n}=-\bm{m}
, then we formally take
T_{\bm{n};-\bm{n}}=1
.

We say that
(\bm{n};\bm{m})\in\mathfrak{C}_{2r}
is normal if
\det T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\neq 0
. It should not be surprising that this notion is related to the uniqueness of appropriately normalized Laurent orthogonal polynomials, as described next.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\bm{L}=(L_{1},\dots,L_{r})
be a system of linear functionals and
(\bm{n};\bm{m})\in\mathfrak{C}_{2r}
, with
\bm{n}\neq-\bm{m}
.

(i)

\det T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\neq 0
if and only if there exists a unique Laurent polynomial
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
of the form

(29)

\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)=z^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}+\ldots+\alpha_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|},

that satisfies

(30)

L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)w^{-k}]=0,\quad-m_{j}\leq k\leq n_{j}-1,\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

(ii)

\det T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\neq 0
if and only if there exists a unique Laurent polynomial
\Phi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
of the form

(31)

\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}^{*}(z)=\beta_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}z^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}+\ldots+z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|}

that satisfies

(32)

L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}^{*}(w)w^{-k}]=0,\quad-m_{j}+1\leq k\leq n_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

(iii)

\det T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\neq 0
if and only if there is a unique vector
\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}=(\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},1},\dots,\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},r})
of Laurent polynomials of the form

(33)

\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)\in\operatorname{span}\big\{z^{k}\big\}_{k=-n_{j}}^{m_{j}-1},

that satisfies

(34a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}[\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(w)w^{-k}]=

\displaystyle 0,

-\left|\bm{n}\right|+1\leq k\leq\left|\bm{m}\right|-1
,

(34b)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}[\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(w)w^{-k}]=

\displaystyle 1,

k=-\left|\bm{n}\right|
.

(iv)

\det T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\neq 0
if and only if there is a unique vector
\bm{\Xi}^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}=(\Xi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},1},\dots,\Xi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},r})
of Laurent polynomials of the form

(35)

\Xi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)\in\operatorname{span}\big\{z^{k}\big\}_{k=-n_{j}+1}^{m_{j}},

that satisfies

(36a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}[\Xi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(w)w^{-k}]=

\displaystyle 0,

-\left|\bm{n}\right|+1\leq k\leq\left|\bm{m}\right|-1
,

(36b)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}[\Xi^{*}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(w)w^{-k}]=

\displaystyle 1,

k=\left|\bm{m}\right|
.

Proof.

Treating the coefficients of
\Phi_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}
in (
29
) as unknowns, write the system of equations (
30
). Its
(\left|\bm{n}\right|+\left|\bm{m}\right|)\times(\left|\bm{n}\right|+\left|\bm{m}\right|)
coefficient matrix is
T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
which proves (i).

Similar arguments work in other cases, leading to the same coefficient matrix
T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
in (ii) and to the transpose of
T_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
in (iii) and (iv).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Observe that in (iii) and (iv) we are using
\bm{n}
-indices with the negative sign and
\bm{m}
-indices with the positive sign, as opposed to the cases (i) and (ii). This is to ensure duality, i.e., that uniqueness of
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
is equivalent to the uniqueness of
\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
, as the proposition above shows.

Remark 2.3
.

In the case of orthogonality with respect to one probability measure (so that
r=1
,
\bm{n}=n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\bm{m}=m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n+m\geq 0
), it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\Phi_{n;m}(z)

\displaystyle=z^{-m}\Phi_{n+m}(z),\qquad

\displaystyle\Phi_{n;m}^{*}(z)

\displaystyle=z^{-m}\Phi^{*}_{n+m}(z),

\displaystyle{\Xi}_{n;m}(z)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\kappa_{n+m-1}}z^{-n}\Phi^{*}_{n+m-1}(z),\qquad

\displaystyle{\Xi}^{*}_{n;m}(z)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\kappa_{n+m-1}}z^{-n+1}\Phi_{n+m-1}(z),

where
\kappa_{j}=L[\Phi_{j}(w)w^{-j}]=L[|\Phi_{j}(w)|^{2}]
.

The Laurent polynomials described in (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv) will be called type II, type II
∗
, I, and I
∗
, respectively. For the case of indices of the form
(\bm{n};-\bm{n})
(for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
), which are all normal, it is natural to take

Let us introduce the following notation. Given a function

.

(44)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(\bm{L}^{\sharp})=\bar{\beta}_{\bm{m};\bm{n}}(\bm{L}),

3.
A General Two-Point Hermite–Padé Problem of Type II

In this section we discuss the Hermite–Padé approximation problem associated with the Laurent polynomials defined in the previous section.

To each linear functional
L_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, given in (
25
), we assign the formal power series

(49)

F_{j}^{(0)}(z)=c_{0,j}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k,j}z^{k},\quad F_{j}^{(\infty)}(z)=-c_{0,j}-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{-k,j}z^{-k}.

Since

(50)

\frac{w+z}{w-z}=\begin{cases}1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{w^{k}},\qquad&\mbox{if }|z|<|w|,\\
-1-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{w^{k}}{z^{k}},\qquad&\mbox{if }|z|>|w|,\\
\end{cases}

the pair
(F_{j}^{(0)},F_{j}^{(\infty)})
can formally be viewed as the generalized Carathéodory function

(51)

F_{j}(z)=L_{j}[(w+z)(w-z)^{-1}].

In the case when
L_{j}
is associated with a probability measure
\mu_{j}
as in (
26
), then (
49
) are the power series expansions of the Carathéodory function
F_{j}(z)
(
7
) of
\mu_{j}
at
0
and at
\infty
.

Let two multi-indices
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,
\bm{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be given. Consider the generalized two-point Hermite–Padé problem of finding the Laurent polynomial

(52)

\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}\in\operatorname{span}\big\{z^{k}\big\}_{k=-\left|\bm{m}\right|}^{\left|\bm{n}\right|},

that simultaneously satisfies

(53)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F_{j}^{(0)}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{n_{j}}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0,

(54)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F_{j}^{(\infty)}(z)+\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-m_{j}-1}),\qquad

\displaystyle z\rightarrow\infty,

for some Laurent polynomials
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},1},\ldots,\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},r}\in\operatorname{span}\big\{z^{k}\big\}_{k=-\left|\bm{m}\right|}^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}
.

Note that here we take
\bm{n}\geq\bm{0}
and
\bm{m}\geq\bm{0}
, which is more restrictive than in the previous section. This is related to the fact that
F^{(0)}
and
F^{(\infty)}
start from the
z^{0}
-term, and therefore the problem (
52
), (
53
), (
54
) can become ill-posed if
n_{j}<0
or
m_{j}<0
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Any Laurent polynomial
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
that solves (
52
), (
53
), (
54
)
automatically satisfies the orthogonality conditions (
30
),
and
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}
is given by

(55)

\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=L_{j}\Big[\frac{w+z}{w-z}\big(\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)-\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)\big)\Big]+L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)].

Conversely, any Laurent polynomial
\Phi_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}\in\operatorname{span}\left\{z^{k}\right\}_{k=-\left|\bm{m}\right|}^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}
that satisfies (
30
) solves the Hermite–Padé problem (
52
), (
53
), (
54
) together with the Laurent polynomial (
55
).

Remark 3.2
.

Combining this theorem with the results in
[
KVMLOPUC1
,
KNik
]
, we can see that up to a normalization constant, the solution to the Hermite–Padé problem (
52
), (
53
), (
54
) with
\bm{m}=\bm{n}
is unique if
\bm{L}
is an Angelesco, AT, or Nikishin system (with

) on the unit circle.

Proof.

Let us write
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
and
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}
in the form

(56)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)=\kappa_{-\left|\bm{m}\right|}z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|}+\dots+\kappa_{\left|\bm{n}\right|}z^{\left|\bm{n}\right|},

(57)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)=\lambda_{-\left|\bm{m}\right|,j}z^{-\left|\bm{m}\right|}+\dots+\lambda_{\left|\bm{n}\right|,j}z^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}.

where there are finitely many non-zero

and

\displaystyle b_{k,j}=-\kappa_{k}c_{0,j}-2\kappa_{k+1}c_{-1,j}-\dots-2\kappa_{\left|\bm{n}\right|}c_{-\left|\bm{n}\right|+k,j}.

For (

)–(

) to hold we must have

(60)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,j}=a_{k,j},\qquad

\displaystyle k=-\left|\bm{m}\right|,\dots,n_{j}-1,

(61)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,j}=b_{k,j},\qquad

\displaystyle k=-m_{j},\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|.

This implies

(62)

a_{k,j}=b_{k,j},\qquad k=-m_{j},\dots,n_{j}-1,

and by (
25
) and (
58
)–(
59
), this turns into (
30
).

From (
60
)–(
61
) we also see that
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}
is uniquely determined by
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
. To compute
\Psi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}

\displaystyle R^{(0)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle=L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)]+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k}L_{j}[w^{-k}\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)],

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle R^{(\infty)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle=-L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)]-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{-k}L_{j}[w^{k}\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)],

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to\infty.

By (
30
)
we get

(66)

\displaystyle R^{(0)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(z^{n_{j}}),

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to 0,

(67)

\displaystyle R^{(\infty)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle=-L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(w)]+\mathcal{O}(z^{-m_{j}-1}),

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\to\infty.

Therefore we can apply the

functional to obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle={R}^{(0)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)-\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F^{(0)}_{j}(z)+L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}(w)],

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)

\displaystyle={R}^{(\infty)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m},j}(z)-\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)F^{(\infty)}_{j}(z)+L_{j}[\Phi_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}(w)].

By (

)-(

satisfies equalities (

)–(

Conversely, given
\Phi_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}
and the corresponding

\bm{\Xi}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}=(\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},1},\dots,\Xi_{\bm{n};\bm{m},r})

\displaystyle R^{(0)}_{\bm{n};\bm{m}}(z)

10.
Szegő Mapping

Let us recall the main notions from the theory of orthogonal and multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line with respect to moment functionals acting on the space of complex polynomials
(with nonnegative powers, in contrast to the Laurent setting (
25
)). To this end, let
M
be defined by

(148)

M[x^{k}]=m_{k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},

where
m_{k}
are arbitrary complex numbers.

Often
M
is induced by a probability measure
\gamma
on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with all finite moments

(149)

M[x^{k}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}d\gamma(x),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Orthogonal polynomials with respect to
M
are defined by
\deg P_{n}=n
and

M[P_{n}(x)x^{k}]=0,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1,

Recall
[
Chihara
]
that a moment functional
M
is called quasi-definite if there is a unique monic
P_{n}
and
\deg{P_{n}}=n
, for each positive integer
n
. This is equivalent to all Hankel matrices
(m_{j+\ell})_{j,\ell=0}^{n-1}
being invertible. This is automatic if
M
is associated with a positive measure on
\mathbb{R}
with finite moments and infinite support, as in (
149
).

Similarly, a moment functional
L
on the space of Laurent polynomials (
25
) is called quasi-definite if a monic polynomial
\Phi_{n}
with
\deg\Phi_{n}=n
and

L[\Phi_{n}(z)z^{-k}]=0,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1,

exists and is unique for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is equivalent to all Toeplitz matrices
(c_{j-\ell})_{j,\ell=0}^{n-1}
being invertible and is automatic if
L
is associated with a positive measure with infinite support on
\mathbb{\partial\mathbb{D}}
as in (
26
).

Given a system
\bm{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{r})
of moment functionals (
148
), the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}
with respect to the multi-index
\bm{n}
and the system
\bm{M}
are non-zero polynomials of at most degree
\left|\bm{n}\right|
, satisfying the orthogonality relations

(150)

M_{j}[P_{\bm{n}}(x)x^{k}]=0,\qquad k=0,\dots,n_{j}-1,\qquad j=1,\dots,r.

Such polynomials always exists, and we say that the index
\bm{n}
is normal if there exists a unique
P_{\bm{n}}
with
x^{\left|\bm{n}\right|}
-coefficient equal to
1
.

The type I polynomials are non-zero vectors
\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}=(A_{\bm{n},1},\dots,A_{\bm{n},r})
, except for
\bm{A}_{\bm{0}}=\bm{0}
, where
A_{\bm{n},j}
are polynomials of degree at most
n_{j}-1
for each
j=1,\dots,r
, and

(151)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}M_{j}[A_{\bm{n},j}(x)x^{k}]=0,\qquad k=0,\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|-2.

\bm{n}\neq\bm{0}
is normal if and only if there exists a unique
\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}
with

(152)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}M_{j}[A_{\bm{n},j}(x)x^{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}]=1.

When
\bm{n}
is normal we always work with the above normalizations for the type I and type II polynomials.

Assuming all the indices are normal (we then say that the system is perfect), there exist
[
NNRR
,
Ismail
]
coefficients
a_{\bm{n},j},b_{\bm{n},j}
, called the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients, so that

(153)

xP_{\bm{n}}(x)=P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{k}}(x)+b_{\bm{n},k}P_{\bm{n}}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},j}P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x),

as well as

(154)

x\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}(x)=\bm{A}_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k}}(x)+b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},k}\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},j}\bm{A}_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}(x).

Recall the definition of the Szegő mapping in Section
1.3
for the case of measures. It is easy to extend this construction to linear functionals. Indeed, the equalities

(155)

L[(w+w^{-1})^{k}]=M[x^{k}],\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}

set up one-to-one correspondence between all linear functionals
M
on the space of polynomials (i.e., (
148
)), and all linear functionals
L
on the space of Laurent polynomials (i.e., (
25
)) with the symmetry

(156)

L[w^{k}]=L[w^{-k}],\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

We denote this correspondence by
L=\operatorname{Sz}(M)
and its inverse by
M=\operatorname{Sz}^{-1}(L)
