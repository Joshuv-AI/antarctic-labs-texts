Can vitamin D supplementation affect cardiometabolic factors in children and adolescence with overweight and obesity? A grade-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Amir Hossein Faghfouri, Mahsa Mahmoudinezhad, Pedram Pam, Sanaz Barazandeh, Fatemeh Faramarzi, Yousef Mohammadpour, Vali Musazadeh, Shahsanam Gheibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin D have been proposed as a supplement to improve cardiometabolic risk factors in obese/overweight children and adolescents. However, findings evidence remains inconsistent. This meta-analysis aimed to assess the effects of vitamin D supplementation on cardiometabolic risk factors in obese/overweight children and adolescents.MethodsA systematic review of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar up to April 2025 was searched. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of vitamin D supplementation on cardiometabolic factors including anthropometric indices, glycemic state and lipid profile. Data were pooled using a random-effects model to calculate weighted mean differences (WMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsNine studies with 440 participants were included. The meta-analysis revealed that vitamin D supplementation no significantly improved BMI, BMI-Z, FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, TG, TC, and LDL-C. However, it was accompanied with a significant reduction in HDL-C level. Subgroup-analysis showed that vitamin D2 showed a greater reduction in HOMA-IR compared to vitamin D3, though the effect was statistically significant (WMD = -0.51, 95% CI: -1.00 to -0.03; p = 0.038).ConclusionsThe current meta-analysis revealed vitamin D supplementation has no favorable effect on cardiometabolic risk factors. More high-quality and large-scale trials are needed to provide more robust evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05795-2.

Background

Overweight and obesity as a global health challenge, is defined by excessive body fat [1]. However, overweight and obesity are characterized by charts of gender-specific body mass index (BMI)-for-age percentile in children and adolescents [2]. BMI percentile for age between 85 and 95 th has been defined as overweight and greater than 95 th percentile is considered as obesity in children and adolescents [3]. Epidemiological studies indicate that pediatric obesity has risen significantly over the past decades. Research shows that of overweight and obesity has doubled between 1990 and 2021 in children and adolescents. Also, obesity alone has tripled in children and adolescents aged 5–14 [4]. Several approaches have been implicated in the progression of obesity, including biological, socioeconomic, and environmental influences [5]. Childhood obesity is a strong predictor of adulthood obesity, which is closely linked to pathogenesis of cardiometabolic complications [6]. Clinical manifestations of cardiometabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia and insulin resistance typically appear in adulthood, often originates during the childhood and track into adulthood [7, 8]. Accordingly, childhood obesity and its metabolic components have become a growing global concern. This critical insight underscores the importance of early identification and timely intervention to optimize preventive strategies and mitigate long-term risks. It has been illustrated that vitamin D deficiency is linked to elevated risk of metabolic disorders [9].

Vitamin D previously known as a lipophilic steroid pro-hormone primarily involved in the calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, has been found to have broader roles beyond skeletal features recently [10, 11]. Emerging research highlights an inverse correlation between vitamin D deficiency and metabolic signatures such as elevated fasting insulin, increased HOMA-IR, dyslipidemia, and increased BMI in children and adolescents [12]. It has been shown that vitamin D deficiency is associated with obesity, glucose intolerance, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia among children and adolescents [13].

In this context, several meta-analyses have been conducted. Corsello et al. reported an elevated serum 25(OH)D levels based on vitamin D supplementation of ≤ 2000 IU per day in obese and overweight children over a duration of 12 to 48 weeks [14]. On the other hand, some studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of vitamin D on cardiometabolic factors [8], while others have not supported its positive impact in this regard [15]. Hauger et al. reported increased level of low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) following vitamin D administration in individuals aged 4–19 years [8]. In contrast, a meta-analysis revealed that vitamin D treatment was not associated with significant changes in term of BMI, LDL-C, and total cholesterol, triglyceride (TG) levels in overweight/obese children and adolescents [16]. However, it was accompanied with an improvement in insulin resistance in children and adolescents [16]. Although several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been published on vitamin D supplementation in pediatric populations, important gaps remain. First, many of the previous reviews included children and adolescents with heterogeneous health statuses, including non-obese individuals [16]. Second, while some earlier studies share a similar title, their objectives are different. Third, previous reviews have typically taken a single-dimensional approach, assessing only one category of outcomes, whereas our study takes a multi-dimensional perspective, simultaneously evaluating glycemic control, lipid profile, and anthropometric indices. Fourth, our review updates the existing evidence base, incorporating more recent trials and providing a current synthesis of findings. Most importantly, previous meta-analyses have not stratified results based on the type of vitamin D supplementation (D2 vs. D3), while our study conducted subgroup analyses to distinguish their differential effects. To address these limitations, the present study specifically focused on evaluating the efficacy of vitamin D supplementation in overweight and obese children and adolescents. Additionally, the presence of conflicting findings in existing studies motivated us to conduct this updated meta-analysis exploring the link between vitamin D and metabolic components such as anthropometrics measurement, glycemic state and lipid profile in overweight/obese children and adolescents, which could enable its use as an affordable strategy to alleviate the burden of metabolic diseases in society.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in compliance with the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [17]. The study protocol is officially registered on PROSPERO under the registration code CRD42025 654,166.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search on PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Google Scholar up to April 2025. The search was restricted to randomized controlled trials. The search strategy details are available in Supplementary Table 1. In order to ensure the comprehensive inclusion of relevant studies, we also manually searched the reference lists of eligible studies, as well as relevant reviews and meta-analyses. The literature search was conducted by two independent investigators.

Eligibility criteria

Following the completion of the searches, two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts of the identified articles to exclude those that were irrelevant. The full texts of the remaining articles were then retrieved, and all relevant studies were identified accordingly. This meta-analysis included studies that met the following criteria:

P (Participants): Children and adolescents aged 2 to 18 years, specifically those with obesity or overweight, without any chronic disease or specific health issues, I (Intervention/Exposure): Vitamin D supplementation (in any form or dose), C (Comparator): No intervention or placebo group, O (Outcomes): At least one of the following outcomes: BMI, BMI z-score, fasting blood sugar (FBS), insulin, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), or high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C).

We included only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in this meta-analysis. Studies were excluded if they: (a) were non-randomized, (b) lacked sufficient data on the outcomes of interest, (c) focused solely on adults aged 18 and older, or (d) were single-dose studies.

Data extraction

Two researchers, independently extracted the data, with any disagreements resolved under the guidance of the chief investigator. When data were unavailable, we reached out to the corresponding author via email to request the required information. The extracted details from each included study comprised the first author's name, year of publication, study location, study duration, participant demographics (including gender, mean age, and mean BMI or BMI z-score), study design, health status of the study population, sample size for each group, vitamin D supplementation dosage, and measurements of BMI, BMI z-score, FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C both before and after the intervention.

Data synthesis

The primary outcome was evaluated by determining the mean differences in BMI, BMI z-score, FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C between participants in the vitamin D supplementation group and the placebo group. Effect sizes for all variables were reported as weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Evidence pooling was conducted using the inverse variance method, and the overall effect size was estimated through a random-effects model based on the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. The standard error of the mean (SEM) was converted to standard deviation (SD) using the formula: SD = SEM × √n, where n represents the number of participants in each group [18]. Heterogeneity between groups was assessed using Cochrane's Q test (with significance defined as P < 0.1) and the I2 statistic, where an I2 value exceeding 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity [19]. Sensitivity analyses were also performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies or groups of studies on the overall findings. For outcomes with fewer than ten observations, Begg's test was conducted to evaluate publication bias. However, since there were 8 studies for FBS, both the funnel plot and Egger’s test were applied. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 14.0; StatCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Quality assessment and meta-evidence

Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB2) was used to assess the quality of the included studies [20]. system consists of five criteria to evaluate the risk of bias, which are as follows: Randomization Process, Deviation from Intended Interventions, Missing Outcome Data, Measurement of the Outcome, and Selection of the Reported Result. After evaluating these domains for each study, an overall judgment of"low risk","some concerns", or "high risk" is assigned to indicate the level of bias in the study. The assessment of the overall risk of bias was determined based on specific criteria: studies were categorized as having a low risk of bias when all domains were deemed to have a low risk; categorized as having some concerns when at least one domain raised some concerns but none were considered high risk; and categorized as high risk of bias if at least one domain was assessed as high risk or if multiple domains raised some concerns. The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach, which considers evidence from RCTs to be highly reliable but allows for adjustments based on specific predefined criteria [21].

Results

Studies selection

From an initial pool of 1984 articles identified during the primary search, 476 ineligible and duplicate studies were excluded. Screening of titles and abstracts led to the removal of an additional 1483 studies based on predefined inclusion criteria: 1097 studies had unrelated titles or abstracts, 378 were animal studies, and 8 were review articles. This left 25 articles for full-text screening, during which 16 were excluded for lacking the required information. Ultimately, 9 studies (representing 10 effect sizes) met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. Five trials were conducted in the USA [22–26], one in Poland [27], one in Belgium [28], one in France [29], and one in Sri Lanka [30]. These trials involved a total of 440 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 20 to 109. The duration of interventions varied from 6 to 26 weeks. Most interventions included vitamin D3 supplementation, while some utilized vitamin D2. The doses ranged widely from 1,200 IU/day to 6,000 IU/day, 2,500 IU/week to 50,000 IU/week, and intermittent doses of 150,000 IU administered at specific intervals. Some studies also incorporated additional components such as structured diets, lifestyle programs, physical activity, or weight-loss interventions. Effect of vitamin D on BMI and BMI-Z

Vitamin D supplementation showed no significant effect on BMI (WMD = −0.31, 95% CI: −0.92 to 0.29; p = 0.31, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.438; based on four RCTs involving 206 participants) (Fig. 2A). Also, no significant result was observed for BMI-Z (WMD = −0.01, 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.10; p = 0.89, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.502; based on two RCTs involving 106 participants) (Fig. 2B).Fig. 2Mean difference and 95% CIs presented in forest plot of the studies on the effects of vitamin D on BMI (A), and BMI-Z score (B)

Effect of vitamin D on glycemic parameters

FBS

Five studies (six effect sizes) involving a total of 256 participants investigated the impact of D on FBS. The combined effect size indicated that D did not have a significant influence on FBS (WMD = −1.25 mg/dl, 95% CI: −3.15 to 0.65; p = 0.19, I2 = 21.6%, p = 0.271) (Fig. 3A). Vitamin D2 had a greater effect on reducing FBS compared to vitamin D3, although this effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −1.94 mg/dl, 95% CI: −4.38 to 0.50; p = 0.119, I2 = 13.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 1).Fig. 3Mean difference and 95% CIs presented in forest plot of the studies on the effects of vitamin D on FBS (A), insulin (B), HOMA-IR (C) and QUICKI (D)

Mean difference and 95% CIs presented in forest plot of the studies on the effects of vitamin D on FBS (A), insulin (B), HOMA-IR (C) and QUICKI (D)

Insulin

A total of 256 participants across five studies (six effect sizes) were analyzed to assess the effect of vitamin D on insulin. The pooled effect size revealed no significant impact of D on insulin levels (WMD = −0.19 µU/mL, 95% CI: −2.26 to 1.89; p = 0.85, I2 = 41.9%, p = 0.126) (Fig. 3B). Vitamin D2 had a greater effect on reducing Insulin compared to vitamin D3, although this effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −0.35 µU/mL, 95% CI: −2.59 to 1.90; p = 0.763, I2 = 0.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

HOMA-IR

Four studies, encompassing five effect sizes and a total of 227 participants, examined the effect of vitamin D on HOMA-IR. The pooled analysis revealed that D did not significantly impact HOMA-IR (WMD = −0.26, 95% CI: −1.10 to 0.57; p = 0.53, I2 = 65.3%, p = 0.021) (Fig. 3C). Vitamin D2 showed a greater reduction in HOMA-IR compared to Vitamin D3, though the effect was statistically significant (WMD = −0.51, 95% CI: −1.00 to −0.03; p = 0.038, I2 = 0.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

QUICKI

Two studies, comprising a total of 79 participants, showed that D had no significant effect on QUICKI (WMD = 0.01, 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.03; p = 0.47, I2 = 86.6%, p = 0.006) (Fig. 3D).

Effect of vitamin D on lipid profiles

TC

A total of four studies (five effect sizes) involving 221 participants investigated the impact of D on TC. The pooled effect size indicated that D had no significant influence on TC (WMD = −2.27 mg/dl, 95% CI: −8.29 to 3.75; p = 0.46, I2 = 8.9%, p = 0.356) (Fig. 4A). Vitamin D3 demonstrated a greater reduction in TC levels compared to Vitamin D2, although this effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −2.10 mg/dl, 95% CI: −11.97 to 7.77; p = 0.629, I2 = 29.2%) (Supplementary Fig. 4).Fig. 4Mean difference and 95% CIs presented in forest plot of the studies on the effects of vitamin D on TC (A), TG (B), HDL-C (C), and LDL-C (D)

Mean difference and 95% CIs presented in forest plot of the studies on the effects of vitamin D on TC (A), TG (B), HDL-C (C), and LDL-C (D)

TG

Five studies (six effect sizes) involving a total of 252 participants assessed the impact of D on TG. The pooled effect size indicated that D had no significant influence on TG (WMD = 0.33 mg/dl, 95% CI: −7.17 to 7.82; p = 0.93, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.946) (Fig. 4B). Vitamin D2 demonstrated a greater reduction in TG levels compared to Vitamin D3, although this effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −0.52 mg/dl, 95% CI: −10.54 to 9.50; p = 0.808, I2 = 0.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

HDL-C

Five studies involving 252 participants investigated the impact of D on HDL-C levels. The overall analysis indicated that D significantly reduced HDL-C (WMD: −2.16 mg/dl; 95% CI: −3.88 to −0.44; p = 0.01), with low heterogeneity observed (I2 = 10.4%, p = 0.349) (Fig. 4C). Vitamin D3 demonstrated a greater reduction in HDL-C levels compared to Vitamin D2, although this effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −3.42 mg/dl, 95% CI: −6.89 to 0.05; p = 0.054, I2 = 60.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

LDL-C

Vitamin D supplementation showed no significant effect on LDL-C (WMD = −1.91 mg/dl, 95% CI: −7.59 to 3.77; p = 0.51, I2 = 20.9%, p = 0.281; based on four RCTs with five effect sizes, 221participants) (Fig. 4D). Vitamin D2 had a greater effect on lowering LDL-C levels compared to Vitamin D3, but the effect was not statistically significant (WMD = −6.36 mg/dl, 95% CI: −14.03 to 1.32; p = 0.105, I2 = 0.0%) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

For HOMA-IR and HDL-C, the sensitivity analysis revealed significant changes. For HOMA-IR, after removing the study by Deruyter et al. [28], the result changed from non-significant to significant (WMD = −0.58, 95% CI: −1.04 to −0.12). For HDL, the result changed from significant to non-significant after removing the study by Deruyter et al. [28], (WMD = −1.25, 95% CI: −3.08 to 0.58), and after removing the study by Nader et al. [23], the result changed to (WMD = −2.20, 95% CI: −4.50 to 0.10). For all other factors, the sensitivity analysis did not show any changes in the results.

Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s test, which showed no significant evidence of publication bias for BMI, FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C.

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the effect of vitamin D in metabolic signatures among children and adolescents. Our results indicated that vitamin D administration was not significantly associated with improvements in anthropometric measurements (BMI), glycemic markers (FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI), or lipid profile parameters (TC, TG, and LDL-C). Vitamin D has been proposed to lower parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels, which may, in turn, reduce intracellular calcium flux in adipocytes and subsequently suppress lipogenesis [31]. Whereas, our study findings indicate that vitamin D supplementation does not significantly reduce BMI levels in overweight/obese children and adolescents. This outcome could be attributed to the hypothesis that vitamin D is sequestered within the adipose tissue of obese children and adolescents, limiting its bioavailability in circulation [32]. Consequently, it seems that the safe dosage of vitamin D supplementation administered may not have been sufficient to achieve its beneficial effects in obese children in the current study. This claim was supported by Nassar et al. that vitamin D is more efficient in non-obese than in obese children [33]. Despite the significant increase in serum vitamin D levels achieved through supplementation, only a small percentage of children were classified within the vitamin D sufficiency group [33]. Similarly, vitamin D supplementation was successful in 50% of obese children compared to 89% in non-obese children [34], and in 48% of overweight children compared to 62% of those with normal weight [35]. Therefore, Italian Pediatric Society guidelines suggests higher doses of vitamin D for obese children and adolescents for age [36]. The Endocrine Society recommends a daily intake of 400–1,000 IU for children < 1 years old who are at risk of vitamin D deficiency, which is 2–3 times higher than the standard recommended dose for their age group [36].

Moreover, active metabolite of vitamin D could exert its favorable effects on the glycemic status in adult population. A meta-analysis by Mirhosseini et al. reported a significant improvement in HOMA-IR levels following vitamin D supplementation in adults [37]. Similarly, the meta-analysis by Tabatabaeizadeh et al. reported a positive effect of vitamin D supplementation on FBS [38].While, glycemic markers including FBS and insulin levels couldn’t show significant changes following vitamin D supplementation in our study. Also, HOMA-IR and QUICKI as surrogate markers of insulin resistance and sensitivity, were not associated with vitamin D administration, respectively. This discrepancy may be explained by population differences, The study by Tabatabaeizadeh et al. included adults and individuals with higher baseline metabolic disturbances. In addition, our study applied GRADE methodology to assess the certainty of evidence, which may have led to more cautious interpretation of findings. Similarly, Hauger et al. meta-analysis, indicated overall no effect of vitamin D (either D2 or D3) supplementation on glycemic state [8]. Their meta-regression analyses demonstrated that changes in vitamin D concentration are inversely associated with HOMA-IR in overweight/obese children and adolescents. This suggest that, vitamin D may exert its beneficial effects on insulin resistance when serum 25(OH)D levels exceed 70 nmol/L [8]. However, our subgroup analyses revealed that vitamin D2 was accompanied with a significant reduction in HOMA-IR level. Although, vitamin D3 is regarded as potent form of vitamin D with more beneficial effects, our findings reported that vitamin D2 was associated with a significant reduction in HOMA-IR level, which suggest that some mechanisms may underlie this effect.

Accordingly, it is expected that vitamin D2 may achieve therapeutic effects. Although Vitamin D2 and D3 differ structurally, both are hydroxylated in the liver to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the main circulating form used to assess vitamin D status [39]. However, Vitamin D3 has greater bioavailability and a longer half-life in circulation, largely due to its higher affinity for vitamin D binding protein [40]. As a result, most studies have focused on and attributed favorable metabolic effects primarily to Vitamin D3. Nevertheless, the findings of this study demonstrate that Vitamin D2 can also exert beneficial effects, particularly in improving insulin resistance. Furthermore, it is worth noting that both vitamin D2 and D3 are converted to their active form which can bind to vitamin D receptors (VDRs) [39]. Likewise, VDR activation contribute to transcription of genes involved in glucose transport (GLUT4) and insulin signaling pathways [41]. However, the differential effect observed in our study may originate from differences in absorption rate, tissue distribution of D2 and D3 in studied population, or metabolic pathways depending on individual’s health status. Also, different baseline vitamin D status may affect the responsiveness to supplementation too. Accordingly, from a clinical perspective, this finding supports the consideration of Vitamin D2 as a potential adjunctive therapy in pediatric populations that are at risk of insulin resistance. However, there is insufficient evidence to support a difference between vitamin D₂ and D₃ to improve glycemic status.

Moreover, vitamin D failed to show significant differences on total cholesterol, LDL-C and TG levels. In contrast, vitamin D treatment reduced HDL-C level significantly. Whereas, a meta-analysis revealed that higher doses of vitamin D are associated with elevated level of HDL-C in overweight/obese children and adolescents [16]. It seems that, these contradictory results may be attributed to variations in the baseline vitamin D, dietary habits, dietary intakes, physical activity, and the participant’s overall health status, all of which could influence individual responses to treatment. In addition, this inconsistency may be influenced by perinatal factors, i.e., gestational age (GA) and weight for GA. Previous studies have illustrated a positive association between small for GA and increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases such as insulin resistance [42, 43] and dyslipidemia [43] in childhood and adolescence. Furthermore, subgroup analysis revealed that significant reduction of HDL-C is attributed to vitamin D2 form. This intriguing finding warrants further investigation to elucidate the probable mechanisms. This differential effect may be explained by their distinct metabolic properties. As discussed above, vitamin D2 have lower affinity to vitamin D binding protein which may contribute to different biological effects [40]. Some studies indicate that vitamin D2 bind to VDRs to modulate the lipid metabolism [39]. Moreover, vitamin D2 may affect cholesterol reverse transport which could explain the HDL-C reduction [44].

Totally, our results indicated that vitamin D administration was not significantly associated with improvements in anthropometric measurements (BMI), glycemic markers (FBS, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI), or lipid profile parameters (TC, TG, and LDL-C). These results can help guide healthcare professionals in making decisions regarding vitamin D supplementation in clinical practice. They can provide preventive approaches aimed at metabolic health. In addition, future studies should be designed to evaluate the metabolic effects of vitamin D2 and D3, long-term effects, and explore the optimal dosage in pediatric population. In addition, further investigation should be conducted to evaluate the effects of genetics differences which may be accompanied with different responses to intervention.

This study possesses several strengths that enhance the value. First, it addresses the global concern of childhood obesity, which often persists into adulthood and is associated with a range of serious health complications. Specific targeted population (children and adolescents) ensures that the findings are relevant to a vulnerable and high-risk group, which enhance the external validity of the study. Second, this study aimed to provide multipolar approach in relation to vitamin D effects by evaluating cardiometabolic factors including anthropometric indices, glycemic indices, and lipid profile. This comprehensive evaluation strengthens the reliability of the findings on various aspects of metabolic health. This study has several limitations too that is better to be acknowledged. The main limitation is small number of included studies which limits our ability to consider the effect of seasonal variations and obesity severity on vitamin D levels. Additionally, the small sample size may compromise the reliability of the findings. Moreover, only articles published in English were included which could point to publication bias.

Conclusion

The updated systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the effect of vitamin D in metabolic signatures and revealed no significant ameliorating properties among pediatric and adolescent population. Thus, vitamin D as a cost-effective agent requires further evaluation at micro-level and larger sample size to better elucidate its potential benefits.
