Cross-Modal Urban Sensing: Evaluating Sound–Vision Alignment Across Street-Level and Aerial Imagery
Pengyu Chen, Xiao Huang, Teng Fei, Sicheng Wang

Abstract
Environmental soundscapes convey substantial ecological and social information regarding urban environments; however, their potential remains largely untapped in large-scale geographic analysis. In this study, we investigate the extent to which urban sounds correspond with visual scenes by comparing various visual representation strategies in capturing acoustic semantics. We employ a multimodal approach that integrates geo-referenced sound recordings with both street-level and remote sensing imagery across three major global cities: London, New York, and Tokyo. Utilizing the AST model for audio, along with CLIP and RemoteCLIP for imagery, as well as CLIPSeg and Seg-Earth OV for semantic segmentation, we extract embeddings and class-level features to evaluate cross-modal similarity. The results indicate that street view embeddings demonstrate stronger alignment with environmental sounds compared to segmentation outputs, whereas remote sensing segmentation is more effective in interpreting ecological categories through a Biophony–Geophony–Anthrophony (BGA) framework. These findings imply that embedding-based models offer superior semantic alignment, while segmentation-based methods provide interpretable links between visual structure and acoustic ecology. This work advances the burgeoning field of multimodal urban sensing by offering novel perspectives for incorporating sound into geospatial analysis.

Introduction

The urban environment, a complex tapestry of social, ecological, and physical systems, increasingly benefits from multimodal data integration for comprehensive analysis
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Heidler et al. 2023
)
. Within this data-rich landscape, sound—the intrinsic acoustic signature of a place—remains a conspicuously underutilized modality in mainstream geographic and urban research
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2025
;
Arzberger et al. 2025
)
. While visual datasets, notably aerial imagery and street-level photography, have become foundational to contemporary spatial analysis
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Biljecki and Ito 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, the rich environmental, social, and ecological information encapsulated within urban soundscapes is often neglected. These auditory dimensions offer critical insights into diverse urban phenomena, including public health dynamics
(
Aletta et al. 2025a
;
Kong and Han 2024
)
, ecological processes
(
Arzberger et al. 2025
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
, and even patterns of social equity
(
Kim et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
; yet, their systematic integration into urban sensing frameworks is still nascent.

Recent advancements in multimodal machine learning, however, are beginning to illuminate pathways to bridge this disciplinary gap
(
Guzhov et al. 2022
;
Girdhar et al. 2023
;
Heidler et al. 2023
)
. A particularly compelling avenue of inquiry lies in understanding the alignment between sound and image—that is, the degree to which auditory scenes correspond with the visual characteristics of their environments
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Quanquan Rui 2024
)
. Elucidating this relationship holds significant promise for advancing urban monitoring capabilities, enriching ecological modeling, and fostering more holistic and inclusive representations of urban existence.
This study, therefore, addresses two fundamental questions:

1.

To what extent do environmental soundscapes align with visual representations of the urban fabric, encompassing both proximate street-level perspectives and synoptic remote sensing imagery

2.

How do different methodologies for visual representation influence the fidelity of this cross-modal correspondence?

We investigate this by systematically comparing two distinct families of visual representation strategies: sophisticated embedding-based models (exemplified by CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
and RemoteCLIP
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
), renowned for capturing latent semantic features, and explicit semantic segmentation models (such as CLIPSeg
(
Lüddecke and Ecker 2022
)
and SegEarth-OV
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
), which provide class-based spatial delineations. By pairing these visual feature sets with embeddings derived from a state-of-the-art audio processing model (AST)
(
Gong et al. 2021
)
, we aim to quantify cross-modal similarities through both direct embedding comparisons and ecologically-informed segmentation analyses.
The principal contributions of this research are twofold:

1.

We propose and implement a comprehensive analytical framework for the cross-modal investigation of urban sound and image data, uniquely integrating both street-level and aerial visual perspectives.

2.

We conduct a systematic comparative evaluation of the efficacy of CLIP-based embeddings versus semantic segmentation techniques in discerning and quantifying the alignment between auditory and visual urban data.

In general, this work seeks to contribute substantively to the emergent field of multimodal urban sensing, offering foundational insights for the future incorporation of acoustic information into large-scale geospatial analyses and a deeper understanding of the complex interplay between the sights and sounds of our cities.

2
Related Work

2.1
Urban Environmental Soundscape Analysis

The field of urban soundscape research has evolved toward a comprehensive understanding of acoustic environments as dynamic socio-ecological systems
(
Aletta et al. 2025a
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
. This shift reflects a growing recognition that soundscapes are not merely acoustic by-products of urban life but serve as meaningful indicators of environmental quality
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, biodiversity
(
Arzberger et al. 2025
)
, public health, and psychological well-being
(
Aletta et al. 2025b
;
Kong and Han 2024
)
.
The scholarly literature identifies three principal strands. The first strand concentrates on soundscape perception and human well-being, employing controlled experiments or immersive media to evaluate how sound sources such as traffic, birdsong, or human voices affect comfort, sociability, and perceived appropriateness
(
Jingwen Cao 2024
)
. The findings indicate that when human-centered sounds are prioritized, they augment perceived sociability, whereas natural sounds contribute to tranquility. The second strand underscores automated sensing and ecological inference, frequently utilizing AI-based sensing systems such as the Smart Soundscape Sensing (SSS) framework
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
. These systems strive to transcend the limitations of manual acoustic surveys through the integration of real-time sensor networks, multi-threaded data acquisition, and algorithmic classification. Importantly, soundscapes are employed as proxies for ecological dynamics, particularly for the monitoring of urban avian biodiversity
(
Arzberger et al. 2025
)
. The third area of inquiry applies contextual and text-driven analytics. For instance, textual data such as visit purpose, emotional tagging, and time of day have been employed to cluster and map urban soundscapes
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
. A notable trend is the shift towards employing participatory methods (e.g., causal loop diagrams) to model the complex interactions among soundscapes, environmental justice, and urban morphology
(
Aletta et al. 2025b
)
.

2.2
Visual Representations of Urban Environments

The visual dimension has long been a cornerstone of urban research, offering tangible representations of the city’s fabric. The recent confluence of breakthroughs in artificial intelligence and the unprecedented availability of large-scale visual datasets—from street-level panoramas to aerial imagery—has catalyzed a profound transformation in how we analyze and understand urban spaces
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Attributes like perceived safety, the abundance of greenery, and overall walkability are increasingly quantified using advanced computer vision techniques such as semantic segmentation and graph-based spatial modeling
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Ito et al. 2024
)
. A robust ecosystem of open-source, pre-trained models—including DETR
(
Carion et al. 2020
)
, DINOv2
(
Oquab et al. 2024
)
, CLIPSeg
(
Lüddecke and Ecker 2022
)
, and SAM
(
Kirillov et al. 2023
)
—furnishes powerful tools for object detection, semantic parsing, and feature extraction from these images. These methods enable the quantification of scene-level characteristics, like vegetation ratios or the complexity of built forms, which in turn inform our understanding of human behavior, mobility patterns, and place experience. Complementing this is aerial imagery, which offers broader spatial coverage and valuable temporal continuity, making it indispensable for large-scale urban analysis. Semantic segmentation applied to these top-down views supports crucial tasks such as urban land cover classification
(
Li et al. 2024c
)
and has been extensively deployed to monitor urban expansion,map vegetation distribution, and assess environmental risks
(
Boulila et al. 2021
;
Pešek et al. 2024
;
Ray et al. 2024
)
. The remote sensing community has benefited immensely from curated datasets like LoveDA
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, FLAIR
(
Garioud et al. 2022
)
, and DOTA
(
Ding et al. 2021
)
, which have significantly improved class balance and spatial resolution for land cover segmentation. Concurrently, a suite of sophisticated models, including MoCaE
(
Oksuz et al. 2024
)
, LSKNet
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
, SegEarth-OV
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
, and SegEarth-R1
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, continues to push the boundaries of accuracy and scalability in detection and segmentation applications.

Critically, researchers have begun to leverage these visual modalities to infer urban acoustic patterns. For instance, Zhao et al.
(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
successfully employed street view images to estimate fifteen distinct soundscape indicators using machine learning. Similarly, visual features from urban scenes have been used to model dominant sound types across cities such as Fuzhou
(
Quanquan Rui 2024
)
. These studies undeniably highlight the potential of vision-based models to augment or even substitute traditional acoustic sensing in certain contexts.

However, a fundamental limitation pervades much of this vision-centric acoustic prediction: sound is predominantly treated as a target variable to be predicted from visual inputs, rather than as a co-informative modality offering its own rich, independent stream of information. This approach, while achieving predictive accuracy on certain metrics, inherently fails in several key aspects. Firstly, it misses the nuance and dynamism of sound; visual cues can correlate with general sound types (e.g., a park with "nature sounds"), but they cannot capture the specific acoustic events, their temporal patterns, or their qualitative character (e.g., the specific bird species, the intensity of traffic, the sudden interruption of a siren). Sound, in its essence, is a time-varying signal, and reducing it to a static label predicted from an image discards this vital temporal dimension. Secondly, it overlooks the synergistic potential of cross-modal information. The relationship between sight and sound is not merely correlational but deeply interactive; sounds can disambiguate visually ambiguous scenes, and visual context can alter the interpretation of sounds. By treating sound solely as a dependent variable, we forfeit the opportunity to model these complex interdependencies where each modality informs and refines the understanding derived from the other. Lastly, it risks creating an impoverished and potentially biased model of the urban environment. If our models learn that "green pixels" predict "quiet," they may fail in noisy parks or over-generalize in areas where visual cues are misleading. This can lead to an incomplete, and at times incorrect, representation of the true sensory experience of a place, particularly its acoustic ecology which is often decoupled from simple visual correlates. The auditory world contains information that vision alone simply cannot access.

2.3
Towards a multisensory urbanism: The convergence of audio and vision

Recent advancements in multimodal machine learning are paving a compelling new research trajectory: the integrated understanding and modeling of the joint latent space where visual and auditory environmental data converge. Three prominent trends are shaping this frontier. One major trend involves joint embedding models, which extend the successes of vision-language models (e.g., CLIP). Researchers are now developing architectures that align audio with visual features within a shared semantic space. Models like Audioclip
(
Guzhov et al. 2022
)
and ImageBind
(
Girdhar et al. 2023
)
demonstrate that sound can be effectively mapped alongside images and text, enabling powerful zero-shot classification, cross-modal retrieval, and even rudimentary reasoning. In these frameworks, sound is elevated from mere metadata to a primary, information-rich signal. Another significant direction is seen in supervised and self-supervised learning on co-located data. The availability of datasets pairing co-located sound and image data has spurred innovation in remote sensing and environmental monitoring. The SoundingEarth dataset
(
Heidler et al. 2023
)
], for example, leverages naturally co-occurring aerial imagery and ambient audio recordings to pretrain image backbones in a label-free, self-supervised manner. The resultant models show substantial performance gains on downstream scene classification tasks compared to traditional ImageNet pretraining, highlighting the value of audio as a supervisory signal for visual learning. Furthermore, task-specific fusion architectures are being developed to tailor integration. Recognizing that naive feature concatenation is often suboptimal, researchers are designing sophisticated fusion methodologies to address modality misalignment and exploit cross-modal synergies for specific tasks. The TFAVCNet model
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
, for instance, employs a two-stage fusion mechanism to effectively integrate ground-level environmental audio with aerial (remote sensing) imagery for enhanced scene classification. Similarly, cross-task transfer learning approaches have demonstrated that priors learned from sound can significantly improve the performance of aerial image classification tasks
(
Hu et al. 2020
)
.Beyond predicting sound from vision, experimental studies have even explored the inverse: using sound to infer visual properties. Binaural audio recordings, for example, have been employed to predict semantic labels, spatial depth information, and even object masks, often using established visual networks as a target for knowledge distillation
(
Vasudevan et al. 2020
)
. These "hearing to see" approaches underscore the potent, often untapped, information embedded within ambient sound.

Despite these promising strides, significant challenges and opportunities remain. Many existing multimodal models prioritize classification or recognition tasks over deeper explanation and interpretation. The crucial aspects of interpretability, ecological grounding, and nuanced spatial reasoning across modalities are comparatively less explored. This leaves a compelling intellectual space for research that seeks not only to link sound and vision but to weave them into a coherent understanding of place, grounded in ecological principles and capable of elucidating the complex interplay of sensory inputs that define urban environments.

3
Dataset

Our study integrates geo-referenced sound and imagery data from three major global cities in Europe, North America, and Asia: London, New York City, and Tokyo. These cities were selected for their urban diversity, rich environmental and social contexts, and broad range of land cover types, providing a robust testbed for analyzing cross-modal alignment between environmental soundscapes and visual representations. In total, 175 sites were sampled—100 in London, 40 in New York City, and 35 in Tokyo.

3.1
Sound Data

Environmental audio recordings were obtained from
Radio Aporee

(
Radio Aporee 2025
)
, an open platform that hosts crowd-sourced soundscapes annotated with precise geographic coordinates. The dataset consists of short ambient clips reflecting a wide range of urban contexts, including parks, traffic corridors, waterfronts, and residential areas.

To ensure consistency and ecological relevance, we applied several filtering criteria. Recordings dominated by human speech were excluded, as they tend to obscure broader environmental sound patterns. Indoor recordings were removed to maintain alignment with outdoor visual scenes. In addition, clips featuring transient or event-specific sounds—such as alarms, crowd outbursts, or isolated vocalizations—were excluded to focus on stable acoustic environments.

Through this filtering process, we curated a dataset of audio samples that represent the ambient and persistent character of urban soundscapes, suitable for comparison with corresponding visual features.

3.2
Visual Data

Two types of imagery were paired with the sound recordings: street-level images, obtained via Google Street View
(
Google 2025b
)
, and aerial images, sourced from Google Earth
(
Google 2025a
)
. Street-level imagery provides ground-level perspectives of the environment, capturing details such as built structures, vegetation, and urban texture. Aerial imagery complements this by offering broader spatial context and patterns in land use.

To align visual data with the environmental nature of the sound clips, we excluded indoor views and scenes captured under unusual or transient conditions such as snow, rain, or nighttime lighting. These filters ensured that visual representations were stable, interpretable, and relevant to the ambient sound environment.

3.3
Data Alignment and Preprocessing

Each audio clip is associated with a unique geographic coordinate, which serves as the anchor for retrieving both street-level and aerial imagery. All data points are spatially aligned and rigorously screened to minimize contextual inconsistencies. To ensure compatibility with the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) model, each audio recording is segmented into 10-second intervals, conforming to the model’s expected input length as specified in its original architecture
(
Gong et al. 2021
)
. This carefully curated multimodal dataset provides a consistent and reliable foundation for subsequent embedding- and segmentation-based analyses of urban environments.

4.1
Sound Feature Extraction

To represent the acoustic characteristics of urban environments, we employ the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST), a pretrained transformer-based model designed for sound classification tasks. This study uses AST as the sound feature extractor; its output embeddings are treated as high-level representations of the input soundscape.

Each raw audio clip, represented in waveform format
x(t)\in\mathbf{R}^{T}
, is first transformed into a log-Mel spectrogram. This transformation involves applying the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), followed by a Mel-scale filterbank and logarithmic compression:

S(f,\tau)=\log\left(\text{Mel}\left(\left|\text{STFT}(x(t))\right|^{2}\right)\right)

(1)

where
f
denotes the Mel-frequency bin and
\tau
represents the temporal frame index. The resulting spectrogram
S\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times T^{\prime}}
encodes time-frequency energy distribution and is treated as a 2D image-like input.

The spectrogram is divided into non-overlapping patches
\{P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{N}\}
, each of which is flattened and linearly projected into an embedding space:

\mathbf{e}_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{e}\cdot\text{vec}(P_{i})+\mathbf{b}_{e}

(2)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the embedding of patch
P_{i}
, and
\mathbf{W}_{e}
and
\mathbf{b}_{e}
are learnable parameters.

These token embeddings are then passed through a Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder, which models temporal and spectral dependencies using self-attention. The final output is a 768-dimensional feature vector serves as a compact summary of the soundscape, encoding both spectral content (e.g., pitch, timbre) and temporal patterns (e.g., rhythm, modulation). We use this representation in downstream similarity computation, treating it as the sound modality’s latent feature vector.

4.2
Visual Feature Extraction

To extract semantic representations from visual data, we employ CLIP-based models due to their ability to map visual and textual modalities into a shared semantic space. This property enables direct comparison between images and audio embeddings, making CLIP well-suited for cross-modal alignment tasks.

CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining) takes an image
I
and a corresponding text caption
T
as input, and encodes them into feature vectors using separate encoders:

\mathbf{z}_{\text{img}}=f_{\theta}(I),\quad\mathbf{z}_{\text{text}}=g_{\phi}(T)

(3)

where
f_{\theta}
is a vision transformer (ViT) and
g_{\phi}
is a text transformer. The model is trained to maximize the cosine similarity of matching pairs
(\mathbf{z}_{\text{img}},\mathbf{z}_{\text{text}})
while minimizing similarity to non-matching ones. After training, the image encoder
f_{\theta}
is used in our work to extract visual features.

For street view imagery, we use OpenAI’s pretrained CLIP model (ViT-L/14) to generate a 768-dimensional feature vector
\mathbf{z}_{\text{CLIP}}^{\text{street}}
. For aerial imagery, we adopt RemoteCLIP, which is fine-tuned on aerial images datasets to better capture overhead spatial structures. It produces a corresponding vector
\mathbf{z}_{\text{CLIP}}^{\text{aerial}}\in\mathbb{R}^{768}
.

These embeddings are later compared to AST-based sound embeddings using cosine similarity. The shared embedding space allows us to assess how well visual and acoustic modalities align semantically across urban scenes.

4.3
Semantic Segmentation

To complement the embedding-based analysis, we also apply semantic segmentation to obtain interpretable, class-level representations of visual scenes. Segmentation provides explicit spatial and categorical information about objects and land cover types, enabling us to evaluate multimodal alignment in a more structured, human-interpretable form.

Figure 4:
Semantic segmentation examples from street view (CLIPSeg) and aerial imagery (Seg-Earth OV).

For street-level imagery, we use CLIPSeg, a prompt-based segmentation model derived from CLIP. Given an input image and a textual prompt (e.g., "building", "tree"), CLIPSeg predicts a dense binary mask highlighting the region associated with the prompt. It leverages the CLIP image encoder to preserve visual semantics and generalize to unseen categories without task-specific training.

For aerial imagery, we adopt Seg-Earth OV, a training-free open-vocabulary segmentation model also based on CLIP. Designed specifically for remote sensing, it enhances CLIP’s patch-level features using an upsampling module (SimFeatUp) and applies a debiasing operation to remove global [CLS] token influence. This results in fine-grained and interpretable land cover maps across urban scenes.

Both segmentation outputs are post-processed to compute the proportion of pixels assigned to each class, forming a scene-level class distribution vector.

4.4
Similarity Computation

To evaluate the alignment between soundscapes and visual scenes, we compute cross-modal similarity using two complementary approaches: embedding-based similarity and segmentation-based similarity.

In the embedding-based approach, we calculate cosine similarity between AST audio embeddings
\mathbf{z}_{\text{AST}}
and visual embeddings derived from CLIP or RemoteCLIP:

\text{sim}_{\text{embed}}=\cos\left(\mathbf{z}_{\text{AST}},\mathbf{z}_{\text{CLIP}}\right)

(4)

This measures the semantic closeness of sound and image representations in a shared latent space, capturing abstract features such as texture, context, and content.

In the segmentation-based approach, we compare scene-level class distribution vectors
\mathbf{p}_{\text{seg}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
, where
C
is the number of semantic classes. These vectors represent the proportion of each visual class within the segmented image. Similarity is computed using cosine distance between paired sound-image samples:

\text{sim}_{\text{seg}}=\cos\left(\mathbf{p}_{\text{seg}}^{\text{a}},\mathbf{p}_{\text{seg}}^{\text{v}}\right)

(5)

where
\mathbf{p}^{\text{a}}
corresponds to the audio-mapped ecological distribution (via BGA) and
\mathbf{p}^{\text{v}}
to the visual segmentation-derived distribution.

Together, these two similarity measures allow us to evaluate both abstract cross-modal alignment (through embeddings) and interpretable ecological correspondence (through segmentation).

4.5
Ecological Class Mapping: BGA

To interpret segmented scenes in ecological terms, we adopt the Biophony–Geophony–Anthrophony (BGA) classification framework
(
Pijanowski et al. 2011-03
)
. This taxonomy enables us to relate visual land cover types to broad categories of environmental sound sources, enhancing the ecological interpretability of our cross-modal analysis
(
Rey-Baquero et al. 2021
)
.

1.

Biophony
refers to sounds generated by non-human biological organisms, such as birds, insects, and other animals.

2.

Geophony
encompasses natural non-biological sounds, including wind, rain, flowing water, and other geophysical processes.

3.

Anthrophony
includes all human-related sound sources, such as traffic, construction, industrial machinery, and speech.

Visual segmentation outputs are mapped to these acoustic categories based on a predefined correspondence between land cover classes (e.g., vegetation, water, road, building) and their most likely sound-producing agents. For instance, green space classes are linked to Biophony, while impervious surfaces such as roads and buildings are linked to Anthrophony. This mapping allows us to estimate the likely ecological composition of soundscapes based solely on visual scene structure, offering a novel lens for analyzing sound–image alignment in urban environments.

5
Results

In this section, we present our findings from cross-modal similarity analysis across both embedding-based and segmentation-based representations. Results are grouped by feature type and evaluated for statistical significance and ecological interpretability.

5.1
Embedding-Based Similarity

We computed cosine similarities between AST audio embeddings and visual embeddings extracted using CLIP (for street view) and RemoteCLIP (for aerial imagery), both using the ViT-L/14 backbone. All correlation values were statistically significant (
p<0.001
), indicating consistent associations between visual and acoustic modalities across the dataset (Figure
5
).

Figure 5:
Correlation between audio and visual embeddings. Street view embeddings exhibit stronger correlation with sound features. Combining both street and aerial views yields the highest performance, supporting the complementarity of these perspectives.

Among the two visual data, street view imagery demonstrated a stronger correlation with sound features (
r=0.19
) than aerial imagery (
r=0.14
). When the embeddings from both views were combined, the overall correlation with sound similarity further increased to
r=0.21
, suggesting that multimodal visual perspectives encode complementary cues relevant to urban soundscapes. The correlation between aerial and street view embeddings themselves was moderate (
r=0.27
), implying partial redundancy but also sufficient independence to justify fusion.

Across cities, we observe consistent trends: both aerial and street-level imagery provide meaningful visual information about the urban sound environment, and combining the two improves alignment further. Correlations are visualized for each city in Figure
6
.

Figure 6:
City-level correlation between sound similarity and visual similarity across modalities. Street view and combined embeddings generally outperform aerial imagery alone, particularly in dense urban areas like New York City.

Both London and Tokyo exhibit a consistent pattern in which street view embeddings correlate more strongly with sound features than aerial embeddings. In London, the correlation increases from
r=0.14
(aerial) to
r=0.20
(street), while in Tokyo, the rise is from
r=0.23
to
r=0.26
. In both cities, the combined visual modality yields the highest correlation—
r=0.22
for London and
r=0.29
for Tokyo—supporting the notion that top-down and ground-level perspectives capture complementary visual cues relevant to urban soundscape patterns. These results suggest that in mixed-use or moderately dense urban environments, both visual modalities contribute meaningfully to acoustic inference.

New York City, however, presents an instructive deviation. Here, aerial visual similarity shows almost no correlation with sound similarity (
r=0.04
), whereas street view embeddings maintain a modest correlation (
r=0.14
). This likely reflects the extreme verticality and dense built environment of NYC, where top-down aerial imagery captures mostly roof surfaces and road layouts but misses fine-grained features (e.g., roadside vegetation, human activity zones) that are more visible at street level. As a result, the combined view correlation is lower in NYC (
r=0.12
) than in the other cities.

These patterns indicate that while aerial imagery provides useful macro-structural information, its value for sound inference is context-dependent. Street-level views offer finer-grained ecological and architectural detail that becomes crucial in dense urban cores.

In addition to the aggregate correlation analysis, we provide representative visual examples to further elucidate the alignment or divergence between visual and acoustic modalities (Figures
7
and
8
). Upon examining aerial imagery, it becomes evident that high visual similarity does not necessarily equate to high sound similarity. This incongruity may manifest due to key auditory sources—such as biophonic activity (e.g., birdsong), anthropogenic noise (e.g., vehicles, conversations), or machinery—not being visible from overhead perspectives. Conversely, scenes exhibiting low visual similarity might still present analogous soundscapes if small yet acoustically significant features—such as isolated vegetative patches or water bodies—are present. These observations highlight the limitation of considering aerial imagery as a comprehensive proxy for sound, thereby justifying our subsequent application of image segmentation to isolate such features for more granulated analysis.

Figure 7:
Visual examples from aerial imagery illustrating cases of visual–acoustic alignment and divergence. High visual similarity does not always yield similar soundscapes due to the limited visibility of biophonic and anthropogenic sound sources. Conversely, acoustically similar scenes may contain visually subtle yet ecologically dominant elements like vegetation or water.

Regarding street view imagery, divergences also occur but originate from alternative causes. Despite the visual field being more detailed and proximate, similar-appearing urban environments can generate disparate acoustic profiles due to localized activities, variable traffic densities, or transient events. Thus, high visual similarity coupled with low sound similarity reflects contextual sensitivity, whereas low visual similarity associated with high sound similarity often arises when semantically distinct scenes contain shared key acoustic elements (e.g., the presence of trees or traffic). These instances underscore the intricacy inherent in visual–acoustic relationships and advocate the significance of multimodal representation within the domain of urban sensing.

6
Discussion

6.1
Asymmetry in modality performance: Street view VS. aerial imagery

A prominent finding, as detailed in Section 5.1, is the consistent difference in how street-view and aerial imagery performed across the two analytical methods. Embeddings from street-view images showed a stronger and more direct alignment with auditory features when compared directly, with a correlation of
r=0.19
with sound features, which was higher than aerial imagery’s correlation of
r=0.14
. Combined, these visual modalities yielded an even higher correlation of
r=0.21
, suggesting their complementary nature. In contrast, the semantic segmentation of aerial imagery, as discussed in Sections 5.2 and 5.3, proved more effective and offered more interpretable ecological categorizations when applying the Biophony-Geophony-Anthrophony (BGA) framework. For instance, aerial segmentation similarity showed a statistically significant positive correlation with sound similarity (
r=0.14
), while street view segmentation had a much weaker relationship (
r=0.05
). This divergence implies that each type of visual data captures unique, yet complementary, aspects of the urban soundscape.
Street-level imagery, by its very nature, provides an up-close perspective of the urban setting. This proximity makes it more adept at capturing fine-grained contextual details, such as the immediate presence of vehicles, pedestrian activity, or nearby vegetation, that are more directly and temporally linked to the sounds recorded in that specific location. Advanced embedding models, such as CLIP, appear proficient at encoding these subtle, nuanced details, effectively preserving them within the embedding space for comparison across different modalities. This is reflected in the city-specific results where street view embeddings generally outperformed aerial embeddings in correlating with sound, such as in London (
r=0.20
for street vs.
r=0.14
for aerial) and Tokyo (
r=0.26
for street vs.
r=0.23
for aerial).
Conversely, aerial imagery offers a broader, bird’s-eye view, emphasizing larger land cover types and their spatial arrangements. While this perspective might be less sensitive to fleeting, localized sounds, it aligns more closely with persistent ecological categories and general soundscape characteristics that can be inferred from landscape-level features. This inherent characteristic explains its superior performance in the BGA-based ecological mapping (Section 5.3), where overarching environmental classifications are of primary importance. For example, in aerial imagery, all three BGA categories (Anthrophony, Biophony, and Geophony) demonstrated statistically significant positive correlations with acoustic similarity, with Anthrophony showing the strongest performance (r=0.145).

6.2
The underperformance of street-view segmentation: A closer look

A noteworthy outcome, highlighted in Section 5.2, was the comparatively weak and often insignificant correlation observed between sound features and the similarity scores derived from the segmentation of street-view imagery. Across all samples, street view segmentation yielded a correlation of only
r=0.05
with sound similarity, and city-specific analyses showed this trend consistently, with correlations near zero or even slightly negative (e.g.,
r=-0.03
in New York,
r=0.05
in London, and
r=0.08
in Tokyo, all statistically weak or non-significant). This phenomenon can be traced to several contributing factors. Firstly, the inherent characteristics of the segmentation models used, like CLIPSeg, need to be considered. While these models are capable of identifying a diverse range of object categories, their output may sometimes lack the pixel-level precision or the consistent geometric alignment with complex urban scenes that is necessary for a nuanced interpretation of acoustic environments. The process of translating a visual object mask directly into an acoustic counterpart is not always straightforward or reliable.
Secondly, urban soundscapes are inherently dynamic and are frequently shaped by temporary, ephemeral events such as a passing emergency vehicle, transient construction noise, or conversations. A static visual segmentation, regardless of its accuracy, fails to capture this crucial temporal dimension that defines many sound events. This is a key challenge when trying to link static visual data to dynamic auditory experiences.
Furthermore, a critical disparity often exists between what is visually salient in an image and what has a significant auditory impact. For instance, a relatively small visual element, such as a distant but loud motorcycle or an inconspicuous air conditioning unit, might dominate the acoustic environment of a location. Yet, this sound source could occupy a minimal portion of the segmented image, or it might even be entirely hidden from view. This fundamental disconnect between visual prominence and acoustic significance likely underlies the limited effectiveness of street-level image segmentation in predicting soundscape characteristics, as evidenced by the weak BGA category correlations for street view, where Biophony and Anthrophony showed negligible or negative correlations (e.g.,
r=-0.023
for Biophony).

6.3
Practical Implications and Potential Applications

Overall, this study advances the emerging field of multimodal urban sensing and contributes to urban science by revealing the multifaceted relationships between urban visual structures and acoustic environments. First, our exploration demonstrates the potential of using existing visual data to infer or monitor soundscape characteristics in urban areas lacking acoustic sensors. Furthermore, this study presents a methodological framework for incorporating sound as an information source into large-scale geospatial analytics. Finally, it highlights the importance of integrating soundscapes into urban planning, design, and landscape architecture, as sound is closely linked to visual elements and may exert a compounded influence on people’s perception, mood, and well-being within multisensory environments.

6.4
Limitations and future directions

While this research presents compelling findings, it is crucial to acknowledge several inherent limitations that provide avenues for future investigation. Firstly, the deep learning models employed in this study (AST, CLIP, RemoteCLIP, CLIPSeg, and Seg-Earth OV) were utilized in their pre-trained state, without being specifically fine-tuned on the urban sound-image dataset compiled for this research. Although this approach promotes generalizability, it might also mean that some level of task-specific optimization was not achieved, potentially limiting the upper bounds of the cross-modal alignment that could be observed. The correlations, while significant, were moderate (e.g., the highest combined visual embedding to sound correlation was
r=0.21
), suggesting room for improvement.

Secondly, the core assumption underpinning our methodology for sound-image alignment is the idea of spatio-environmental synchronicity—that the sound and the image were captured at the exact same place and time, representing the same environmental conditions. However, ambient sound, particularly within intricate urban topographies, can often originate from sources that are beyond the immediate field of view of the paired image. Sound can also be significantly influenced by complex acoustic propagation paths, such as reflections off buildings or occlusions by structures. This potential spatial mismatch, which is especially pronounced in dense urban settings like New York City, where aerial visual similarity showed almost no correlation with sound similarity (
r=0.04
) in the embedding-based analysis, inevitably introduces an element of uncertainty into the analysis. The paper notes that in NYC, aerial imagery captures mostly roof surfaces and misses fine-grained features more visible at street level.

Finally, the study is constrained by the nature and quality of the publicly sourced data used, namely Radio Aporee for sound recordings and Google Earth/Street View for imagery. Despite the implementation of rigorous data cleaning and filtering protocols, such as excluding recordings dominated by human speech or indoor recordings, there remains a possibility that latent biases or residual noise within these datasets could have subtly influenced the observed correlations. To address this concern and to bolster the reliability of the conclusions drawn, robustness checks were performed by analyzing data across three geographically disparate and structurally diverse cities: London, New York, and Tokyo. The consistent trends that were observed across these distinct urban environments—such as street view generally outperforming aerial imagery in embedding-based comparisons, and aerial segmentation being more informative for BGA categories—lend credence to the generalizability of the core observations regarding modality performance and methodological suitability. For instance, Tokyo consistently showed strong alignment in aerial BGA categories, particularly Biophony (
r=0.23
) and Anthrophony (
r=0.25
). Future research could aim to mitigate these limitations by curating bespoke datasets with more controlled data collection protocols, exploring fine-tuning strategies for the models, and developing more sophisticated methods to account for spatial and temporal misalignments.

7
Conclusion

This study explored the alignment between environmental soundscapes and visual representations of urban environments using a multimodal framework that integrates street-level and Aerial imagery with geo-referenced sound recordings. By comparing feature-based and segmentation-based approaches across modalities, we assessed the extent to which different visual representations encode meaningful acoustic information.

Our results show that embedding-based representations from CLIP and RemoteCLIP align more strongly with sound embeddings than semantic segmentation outputs. Street view imagery performs best in capturing fine-grained auditory semantics, while remote sensing segmentation—despite lower overall correlation—offers more interpretable insights through ecological class mapping. Specifically, the Biophony–Geophony–Anthrophony (BGA) classification reveals a structured relationship between land cover and likely sound types, especially in aerial views.

These findings demonstrate that visual features can serve as useful proxies for environmental sound, with potential applications in soundscape-aware urban planning, acoustic ecology, and large-scale multimodal sensing. The ability to infer ecological sound composition from visual data opens new pathways for passive, scalable acoustic monitoring in cities.

Future work may focus on improving alignment through fine-tuning of visual and acoustic models, incorporating temporal context, or developing unified cross-modal training objectives. Expanding the framework to include richer contextual data (e.g., time of day, weather, human activity) may also enhance robustness and ecological interpretability.
