Teaching and Evaluating LLMs to Reason About Polymer Design Related Tasks
Dikshya Mohanty, Mohammad Saqib Hasan, Syed Mostofa Monsur, Size Zheng

Abstract
Research in AI4Science has shown promise in many science applications, including polymer design. However, current LLMs prove ineffective on this problem space because: (i) most models lack polymer-specific knowledge (ii) existing aligned models lack coverage of knowledge and capabilities relevant to polymer design. Addressing this, we introduce PolyBench , a large scale training and test benchmark dataset of more than 125K polymer design related tasks, leveraging a knowledge base of 13 13 + data points obtained from experimental and synthetic sources to ensure broad coverage of polymers and their properties. For effective alignment using PolyBench , we introduce a knowledge-augmented reasoning distillation method that augments this dataset with structured CoT. Furthermore, tasks in PolyBench are organized from simple to complex analytical reasoning problems, enabling generalization tests and diagnostic probes across the problem space. Experiments show that small language models (SLMs), of 7B to 14B parameters, trained on PolyBench data outperform similar sized models, and even closed source frontier LLMs on PolyBench test dataset while demonstrating gains on other polymer benchmarks as well.

To design and evaluate LLM-based solutions for polymer design, we need large scale broad-coverage datasets. However, constructing such a resource is difficult because (i) the dataset must be grounded in verified scientific outcomes with precise syntactic structural representations, (ii) multi-dimensional design-centric data is often sparse and finding complete records across different dimensions is difficult, and (iii) there are no ready sources that provide reasoning traces that connect the outcome data points to the tasks themselves. To address these difficulties, we design a systematic, subject matter expert (SME) informed, and comprehensive process with four key contributions:

Design Related Tasks Grounded in Scientific Data

Using existing polymer data sources of experimental and other fundamental knowledge, along with chemistry tools such as RDKit
24
, we curate polymer profiles and derive
PolyBench
, a natural language benchmark of
125k+
polymer design related tasks, with inputs from Polymer Scientists.

Task Design and Organization

To reflect the multi-objective nature of polymer design, we need a wide-coverage of tasks, reasoning needed and the kinds of information needs that are likely useful for polymer design related tasks. Keeping this as our goal, we organize
PolyBench

concepts
, with tasks span across basic understanding to complex design—structural understanding, conceptual knowledge, property prediction, comparison, ranking, advanced reasoning covering complex analytical problems, as well as design and synthesis tasks.

Knowledge-Augmented Reasoning and Distillation

To teach models effective step-by-step reasoning strategies grounded in complete structural and property data, which mirror how SMEs deal with such tasks, we devise a data grounded distillation strategy by prompting frontier models with the correct data and reasoning strategy outlines informed by SMEs. We further curate the reasoning traces thus distilled through automated fact checking for additional verification.

Benchmarking and Understanding gaps in model reasoning

Our evaluations show frontier models and even domain aligned chemistry LLMs perform poorly on design and synthesis tasks. Training small sized LMs (7B-14B) using our knowledge-augmented reasoning distillation procedure leads to substantial improvements, especially in design and synthesis tasks. Last, using the diagnostic support in our benchmark we conduct an error analysis that helps understand whether model failures stem from a lack of foundational chemical knowledge (a skill gap) or the inability to combine them in a compositional multi-step reasoning (a compositionality gap).

In summary, this paper introduces a systematic process for creating polymer design related tasks grounded in scientific data for evaluation, along with distilled reasoning traces for training. Benchmarking with the resulting comprehensive resource shows its utility for training models and the potential for deep impact in the polymer domain, as well as for furthering reasoning abilities of LLMs in the broader
AI4Science
context.

3.1
Challenges

There are four main challenges in developing LLM-based solutions:

1) Polymer Representation
Reasoning over polymer structures need rich syntactic representation, like
SMILES
, which can encodes repeating and hierarchical structures in polymers. This reasoning must be correct as a slight change results in a different polymer.
While LLMs can reason complex syntax (e.g. programming languages) , we find that even chemistry-aligned models, exposed to
SMILES
, struggle
2
2

2

While there are other architectures such as graph-structured models (e.g. GNNs
46
) that are better suited for
SMILES
strings, we’d like to emphasize that our goal is to improve LLMs ability to
natively
reason over these representations in conjunction with the tasks as a more scalable solution.
.

2) Diverse Foundational Skills
Polymer design requires multiple skills and foundational knowledge - structural interpretation, property reasoning, comparison/ranking under constraints, and synthesis planning - rather than a single isolated capability.
Teaching these skills to LLMs requires translating them to scalable, evaluable task categories, which is challenging as the corresponding data remains siloed across multiple sources.

3) Task Complexity
Polymer design is complex and multi-objective: design related needs require reasoning across multiple properties, constraints, and goals (e.g., property trade-offs plus synthesis feasibility). This also demands
cross-scale
reasoning - connecting local substructures (monomers / functional groups) and global factors (chain length, topology, architecture) to design task needs.

4) Compositional Reasoning & Generalization

To effectively navigate this complexity, models require compositional multi-step capabilities – for example generating candidate polymer modifications might involve understanding the given polymer structure, propose edits, make comparative property analysis and produce synthesis reasoning. Training and evaluation must therefore span these interconnected task types and test generalization to new polymers, new properties, and their conjunctions. Having explicit
reasoning traces
is critical to traceback and link the final decision to the underlying decisions required to reach it.

These challenges show that LLMs for polymer design require: (i) comprehensive multi-task training data spanning structural understanding, property inference, and synthesis planning, and (ii) explicit chain-of-thought reasoning mirroring how scientists decompose and solve complex problems. To this end, we introduce
PolyBench
, a comprehensive benchmark suite for polymer reasoning, (Section
4
) for alignment and evaluation.

4
PolyBench

We build
PolyBench
, a large-scale, broad-coverage dataset to teach and test LLMs for polymer design. We have three desiderata for
PolyBench
:
First
, since polymer design is a workflow rather than a single task - we want the testbed to cover a range of polymer tasks with varied complexities - from foundational skills (structural interpretation, conceptual knowledge, property reasoning) to downstream design-oriented queries (e.g., comparison/ranking and synthesis feasibility).
Second
, because polymer design is a highly specialized domain where "correct" answers must adhere to specific syntax & design constraints, we require the generation of explicit reasoning traces to make the basis for answers inspectable and enable failure diagnosis, this would give us the ability to trace back through the decision chain and identify types of failure.

Third
, because polymers span across diverse classes/families (e.g., polythene, polyesters
38
;
16
), our evaluation needs to reflect generalization to these new unseen polymers scenarios. Manually curating such data is expensive in terms of time and expertise.

How do we create such high-quality broad-coverage data? Our main idea is to first combine open-source and experimentally verified polymer data on fundamental descriptions, properties and synthesis (
subsection B.2
) with large coverage. Then, using instruction following LLMs, we obtain reasoning trajectories grounded in real-world data (
subsection 4.3
). For coverage across different generalization capabilities, we organize
PolyBench
into tasks with test splits yielding valuable diagnostics (
subsection 4.1
).

4.1
Polymer Tasks Design and Organization

We derive our task categories from subject-matter expert (SME) input and common day-to-day workflows in polymer research, where scientists iteratively interpret structures, apply domain concepts, screen candidates by properties, compare and filter under constraints, and plan feasible synthesis routes.
Using this workflow, we identify and organize six
reasoning
task categories of
PolyBench
- spanning across foundational skills to end-to-end design - that together capture the core capabilities a model needs to support polymer design-related tasks. These are: (i)
Structural Understanding
(polymer representations, syntax, and structure parsing), (ii)
Conceptual Knowledge
(core polymer science principles), (iii)
Property Prediction
(structure
\rightarrow
property estimation), (iv)
Property Comparison & Ranking
(multi-criteria screening under constraints), (v)
Advanced Property Reasoning
(mechanistic and trade-off reasoning), and (vi)
Synthesis & Design
(monomer/reaction selection and constrained design). We provide additional details and examples in Appendix
B.4
.

4.2
Task Generation

We generate tasks from established polymer property databases to ensure broad coverage of polymers while maintaining scientific rigor. For each polymer, we construct a standardized
polymer profile
by aggregating its structural representation (e.g., IUPAC/SMILES

3

To support our task categories - we create tasks using two complementary processes: (i)
programmatic
tasks derived directly from our knowledge base. These cover structural queries such as counting and functional-group identification, representation conversions, property targets, and structured synthesis decisions such as reaction/reagent/product selection, as well as constraint-based screening, all rendered into natural-language questions with constrained answer formats. For example, if a polymer profile includes a
SMILES
string and a recorded property value, we can turn it into a QA item by asking for that property and using the profile value as the gold answer; and (ii)
Distilled
task generation, where we prompt

with the polymer profile(s)
and
a category-conditioned instruction that constrains both content and format to produce polymer-specific QA pairs grounded in the same underlying data. For example, given a monomer

string, we prompt
Claude-3.5-Sonnet
to generate a grounded structural-reasoning question. These two mechanisms complement each other in coverage: programmatic generation produces questions with fully specified gold targets for categories with objective answers, while distilled task generation is used to instantiate the open-ended QA formats in categories where the prompt must be in natural language, while conditioned on same polymer profile.

Each category uses one to multiple task formats—open-ended questions, ranking, multiple choice, numerical predictions, and SMILES generation. Within each category, tasks vary in complexity from single-step reasoning problems to multi-step problems requiring reasoning over different information a single query. 4.3
Knowledge-Augmented Distillation

Our task instances provide instructions and answers, but training models for polymer design also requires reasoning traces that reflect how polymer chemists justify decisions for unfamiliar polymers. Directly distilling CoT from frontier LLMs is unreliable, since these models often lack polymer-specific knowledge. Moreover, teacher-student distillation can inherit teacher limitations; we mitigate this by grounding the teacher on exhaustive polymer profiles and gold targets, so it primarily explains provided evidence rather than generating facts from memory, which reduces hallucination risk in the distilled traces. Our
knowledge-augmented
distillation pipeline consists of: (1)
Knowledge injection
- we augment each prompt with the relevant polymer profile (source-dataset fields and RDKit-derived structural attributes) so reasoning is grounded in complete structural and property evidence; (2)
Reasoning structure
- we provide high-level guidance to decompose the task and produce a structured justification before the final answer (Appendix
C.2
); and (3)
Automated verification
- we run fact-checking with
Claude-3.5-Sonnet
- this verification achieves
\sim 80\%
accuracy in identifying correct reasoning chains; manual inspection suggests most flagged errors are mild (e.g., oversimplified explanations, imprecise terminology, or a missing intermediate step) rather than severe hallucinations. Importantly, these issues are confined to the generated CoT traces - the final answers are mostly ground-truth labels for our questions. We therefore retain all traces and attach verification labels as scalable quality indicators, which is computationally feasible at our dataset scale (
100k
+ instances) and is further supported by expert human analysis (Section
7
), showing that knowledge-augmented distillation improves trace quality over direct distillation. We use
GPT-4o
and
Claude-3.5-Sonnet
as teacher models to generate high quality reasoning traces for
PolyBench
using our knowledge-augmented distillation pipeline. Additional details for this pipeline are in
B.5
- including prompts, automatic and manual verification of the reasoning traces.

8
Quantifying the Compositionality Gap

Standard metrics fail to explain
why
models fail on polymer design tasks. Hence, using our diagnostic set from
4.1
, we run three core experiments using the
Phi-4-14B+CoT
: (i)
Exp 1 (Skill Gap)
tests whether the model can answer atomic sub-questions in isolation; (ii)
Exp 2 (Skill Selection & Accuracy)
measures whether the model’s
CoT
covers the required sub-questions (recall) and whether the included steps are correct (precision); and (iii)
Exp 3 (Compositionality Gap)
evaluates end-task performance when the model is given additional context (sub-questions (SubQs) and, sub questions and gold answers (SubQs + gold)). Additional details in
G.4
.

The model often knows the necessary pieces (high SubQ precision in Exp 1), but it struggles to select and carry forward the right constraints/steps in its own reasoning (low recall in Exp 2), and even when given SubQ/SubQA as context (Exp 3), composing everything into a consistently constraint-satisfying solution remains brittle — this is evidence of a compositionality gap rather than pure knowledge missing.

9
Conclusion

Polymer synthesis requires deep understanding of polymer structures and properties, something that general purpose and chemistry domain LLMs lack.
Addressing this limitation, we develop
PolyBench
- a comprehensive benchmark for polymer understanding spanning six important task types from structure parsing to synthesis, with CoT traces grounded in hard experimental data. Training and testing a suite of 7B-14B models on
PolyBench
highlights the effectiveness of
PolyBench
as a training dataset, outperforming baselines and frontier LLMs. We believe this work advances polymer informatics by giving a useful benchmark to the community and provides a blueprint for building science domain LLMs.

Limitations

We propose
PolyBench
, a benchmark for training AI models for polymer domain. Using
PolyBench
, we finetune models and test them against baselines on our own benchmark as well other established benchmarks. Despite our comprehensive evaluation pipeline, we are aware of the limitations of our work.
Firstly, our dataset is augmented with LLM generated CoT trajectories. Even though these trajectories are generated with a lot of target polymer context, model hallucinations might seep in, resulting in noisy lower quality CoT. In some cases, the CoT might be too complex due to the additional context to make it a useful signal for downstream alignment of smaller LLMs. While the CoTs have been verified using self-verification along with manual verification of a small subset, there is still room for error. Hence, training on such trajectories can seep in the teacher LLM’s misinterpretations and hallucinations into the aligned model. We note that reasoning traces may reflect teacher-model conventions and may over-emphasize certain explanation styles; we therefore include verification labels and discuss risks of imprinting/bias in the Limitations section. Second, our
PolyBench
currently builds upon the open-source datasets about polymers and their properties. The current set of tasks and queries are actually extensions, building upon the tasks proposed by other researchers along with new tasks resulting from augmentations using
RDKit
. While our tasks might be larger than previously proposed works which usually focus on single tasks or properties, our set of tasks is still limited by what is at hand. Data such as lab synthesis planning and action.
PolyBench
does not also teach models how to use coding and/or polymer based coding frameworks in order to solve problem. Given the agentic framework that is popular nowadays, lack of tasks for teaching tool usage in polymer domain is also a significant limitation in terms of tasks. Third,
PolyBench
contains information only in the text modality. Multiple works have shown that incorporating other modalities can significantly improve performance in chemistry domain tasks. For instance, including images of the molecule or graphical information along with textual information can significantly boost performance in a number of downstream tasks. Also, provision of other modalities enables researchers to align multi-modal models for polymer domain. Finally, our evaluation using
PolyBench
was only done for single-step inference. Neither
PolyBench
nor our evaluation pipeline was designed for agentic approach. Multiple recent studies have shown that LLMs offer significant performance gains when acting as agents, being able to do multi-step reasoning and utilize relevant tools. Given how comprehensive
PolyBench
is in terms of polymer design tasks, being able to further enhance its design by incorporating agentic approach through relevant tools and training trajectories, could have enhanced the benchmark.

Experimental Property Databases.

The foundation of our dataset comprises experimentally validated polymer-property relationships from four key sources. PolymersML
7
(18,667 homopolymer instances) provides the most comprehensive property coverage spanning physical properties (density, specific volume), thermal properties (glass transition temperature, crystallization temperature), and mechanical properties (tensile strength, compression modulus). Bicerano Polymers
2

17
(3,655 instances) focuses on quantum-mechanical and electronic properties such as bandgap, electron affinity, ionization energy, and dielectric constant. PolymerDB
19
(224 instances) provides fundamental molecular descriptors including molecular weight, molar volume, solubility parameters, and glass transition temperatures with associated property ranges.

Structural and Topological Data.

To enable comprehensive structural understanding, we incorporate ToPoRg
21
(1,342 polymer instances) with explicit topological annotations (cyclic, linear) and graph-based representations (nodes, edges). ChEMBL
13
(2,851 monomer instances) provides monomer SMILES with base representations and CXSMILES extensions marking attachment sites
8
. These sources provide the structural foundation necessary for syntax understanding and molecular parsing tasks.

Synthesis and Reaction Data.

PN2S
30
contributes 11,165 instances documenting monomer-to-polymer transformations for both copolymers and homopolymers, specifying reactants, products, and reaction types. We supplement this with the OMG (Organic Materials Generator)
22
databases, which comprise 77,281 characterized monomers and 12,886,131 synthetically generated polymers. From this large synthetic collection, we sample 10,000 polymers along with their corresponding monomers to balance dataset composition and computational feasibility. OMG Monomers categorizes monomers by functional groups (e.g., di-amine, di-isocyanate, di-ol, lactam, cyclic ether) and provides synthetic accessibility scores. OMG Polymers documents systematic polymerization reactions linking reactants to products. Together, these sources bridge the gap between individual monomers and final polymer structures through both documented experimental pathways and systematic synthetic exploration.

A.2
Data Integration and Standardization

Combining multiple sources raises several issues in terms variations along representation, units of measurement, and structural aspects.

Standardization.

We address these through a twostep approach. For molecular standardization, we use RDKit
24
to automatically canonicalize all SMILES strings, ensuring consistent representations by removing redundant stereochemistry, neutralizing charged species, and standardizing tautomeric forms. For property standardization, we consulted with polymer scientists to establish standard units across all datasets (e.g., temperatures in Kelvin, density in g/cm³, solubility parameter in MPa1̂/2). We also identify and remove duplicate entries by comparing canonical SMILES strings and consolidate property values from multiple sources when the same polymer appears in different datasets.

Computational Structural Augmentation.

Working with polymer scientists, we identified a comprehensive set of molecular descriptors relevant for polymer understanding. All polymer and monomer structures are processed through RDKit
24
to automatically extract these features, including basic molecular properties (molecular weight, heavy atom count), structural characteristics (rotatable bonds, ring counts, complexity indices), hydrogen bonding features (donors, acceptors), and functional group identifiers (alcohols, amines, carbonyls, halogens, etc.). These features provide consistent structural representations across all data sources and enable models to learn from interpretable chemical descriptors.

B.3
Task Details

Structural Understanding:
This category tests the model’s ability to comprehend polymer molecular architecture through four task types: (1)
Syntax Understanding
evaluates whether models can interpret polymer notation systems, (2)
Structural Understanding
assesses comprehension of polymer topology and composition, (3)
Structural Understanding with Counting
requires quantitative analysis of structural components, and (4)
Syntax-to-Structure Translation
tests the ability to convert between different polymer representation formats. These tasks use question-answering format.

Conceptual Knowledge:
Tasks evaluating fundamental polymer science knowledge, including
Conceptual Understanding
of polymer principles and
Structural Understanding with Reasoning
, which requires explaining the relationship between structure and function. These tasks are evaluated in question-answering format.

Property Prediction:
Direct numerical prediction tasks where models must predict quantitative polymer properties (e.g., glass transition temperature, tensile strength, thermal conductivity) from molecular structure. Performance is measured using numerical regression metrics.

Property Comparison and Ranking:
Multiple-choice tasks requiring comparative reasoning about polymer properties, including: (1)
Property Ranking
where models order polymers by property values, (2)
Property Comparison
between polymer pairs, and (3)
Design Reasoning with Structural Constraints
spanning multiple sub-tasks: filter-then-optimize selection, logical composition of requirements, constrained ranking under multiple criteria, and triple intersection of property constraints.

Advanced Property Reasoning:
Complex reasoning tasks in question-answering format: (1)
Property Decomposition with Mechanistic Understanding
requires models to explain the mechanistic origins of polymer properties, (2)
Property Inference with Structural Decomposition
involves inferring properties by analyzing structural components, and (3)
Multi-Property Reasoning with Tradeoffs
assesses understanding of competing property requirements in polymer design.

Synthesis and Design:
Practical polymer synthesis tasks including: (1)
Product Prediction
(predicting polymer structure from monomers and reactions), (2)
Monomer Identification
(identifying required monomers for target polymers), (3)
Second Monomer Identification
(completing monomer pairs for copolymerization), and (4)
Reaction Identification
(determining appropriate polymerization reactions). The first three tasks require generating valid
SMILES
representations, while reaction identification uses question-answering format. We also include open-ended
Design Reasoning with Structural Constraints
tasks.

B.4
Task Design

We create and organize the tasks in
PolyBench
into six
reasoning task
that build on each other, from basic understanding to complex design: (i)
Structural Understanding
foundational skills for working with polymer structures—translating between different representations of polymers, identifying structural features, counting atoms and functional groups, and understanding SMILES syntax. (ii)
Conceptual Knowledge
tests whether models understand core polymer science principles and can apply this knowledge to reason about molecular structures and their properties. (iii)
Property Prediction
tests predicting numerical property values directly from molecular structures, learning the relationships between structure and properties. (iv)
Property Comparison and Ranking
involves comparing, ranking, and filtering across multiple criteria, logical composition of constraints, and finding optimal candidates that satisfy several property requirements simultaneously. (v)
Advanced Property Reasoning
tackles more complex analytical problems: breaking down properties to understand the underlying mechanisms, decomposing structures to predict properties, and reasoning about trade-offs when multiple properties matter. (vi)
Synthesis and Design
covers the full design process from choosing the right monomers and predicting reaction products to identifying suitable reactions and planning synthesis routes under various constraints.

B.5
Knowledge-Augmented Distillation

The tasks collected above contain task instruction and the answer but to teach models to reason about these appropriately, we also need structured reasoning traces that reflect how polymer chemists think about unknown polymers. To this end, we seek to distill stepwise Chain-of-Thought (CoT) from frontier models. As argued earlier, even frontier LLMs do not have adequate foundational polymer knowledge and reasoning. Therefore, direct distillation by prompting frontier LLMs is ineffective.

We address this by providing specific relevant knowledge for each question along with the answer, and targeted high-level instructions for how to structure the reasoning. Our distillation pipeline has the following three steps:

1) Knowledge Injection
For each question, we augment the prompt with comprehensive profiles for the specific polymer(s) of interest including all information from the source datasets and RDKit-generated structural features—experimental properties, structural descriptors, molecular fingerprints, and domain annotations. This nudges the LLMs to generate reasoning grounded in complete structural and property data rather than superficial pattern matching from SMILES alone.

2) Reasoning Structure
To ensure that LLMs follow structured reasoning, we ask them to decompose each task into subtasks, prioritize structural understanding, and articulate solutions through structured reasoning chains before providing final answers. This mirrors how polymer chemists approach unfamiliar materials—consulting all available data before reasoning to conclusions. Example prompts can be seen in Appendix
C.2

3) Automated Verification
As an additional quality control layer, we implement automated fact-checking of generated CoT traces
29
. We sample QA pairs and use Claude
3
to evaluate the factual accuracy and chemical validity of each reasoning step. This process achieves approximately
80
% accuracy in identifying correct reasoning chains. On further review, we observe that most of the errors stem from minor issues such as oversimplification (omitting secondary contributing factors to properties), imprecise terminology (using common chemical names/formulae instead of precise SMILES representation), or incomplete explanations (correct reasoning but missing one step in the chain). These errors are confined to the generated CoT traces; the final answers are the ground truths for our questions. We retain all traces along with their verification labels rather than filtering incorrect ones. Given our dataset scale (100K+ instances), this provides quality indicators for each trace while maintaining computational feasibility. Our
manual quality analysis
by experts (see Section
7
) show this knowledge-augmented distillation greatly improves quality over direct distillation.

D.2
PolyBench
Evaluation Task Details

We evaluate our PolyLM models on
PolyBench
test split, which contains diverse tasks designed to assess polymer understanding and design capabilities. The benchmark covers the following task categories:

Structural Understanding:
Tasks assessing comprehension of polymer molecular architecture, including syntax understanding, structural interpretation, component counting, and notation translation.

Conceptual Knowledge:
Tasks evaluating fundamental polymer science principles and structure-function relationships.

Property Prediction:
Direct numerical prediction of quantitative polymer properties (e.g., glass transition temperature, tensile strength) from molecular structure.

Property Comparison and Ranking:
Multiple-choice tasks requiring comparative reasoning, including property ranking, pairwise comparison, and design reasoning under structural constraints (filter-then-optimize, logical composition, constrained ranking, triple intersection).

Advanced Property Reasoning:
Reasoning tasks requiring property decomposition with mechanistic understanding, property inference from structural analysis, and multi-property tradeoff reasoning.

Synthesis and Design:
Practical synthesis tasks including product prediction, monomer identification, second monomer identification (
SMILES
generation), reaction identification, and design reasoning with structural constraints.

PolyBench
Test set is intentionally out-of-distribution with respect to
PolyBench
train set, containing unseen polymers, properties and novel instructions to simulate real-world polymer discovery.

D.4
Evaluation Metrics (Detailed)

We employ task-specific quantitative and qualitative metrics to comprehensively assess model performance across diverse reasoning and generation tasks. Below we describe each metric in detail.

ROUGE-L (RgL)

ROUGE-L
31
measures the longest common subsequence (LCS) between the predicted and reference text, capturing both fluency and content overlap. It is particularly suited for open-ended or abstractive responses, where exact lexical match may not fully reflect semantic correctness. Higher values indicate closer alignment to human-written answers.

LLM-as-a-Judge (Jdg)

Following
4
, we employ a large language model (
Phi-4-14B
) to score model outputs on a
Likert scale (1–7)
under a blind evaluation setup. This metric complements automatic scores by reflecting human-aligned preferences for coherence, factuality, and reasoning quality. Scores are averaged across annotators and instances to yield the final preference rating.

Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r)

The Pearson correlation coefficient quantifies linear agreement between predicted and ground-truth numeric values, defined as:

r=\frac{\sum_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}\sum_{i}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}}},

where
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are predicted and true values. A value close to 1 indicates strong positive correlation and thus better predictive consistency.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

MAE measures the average absolute deviation between predicted and reference values:

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|.

Lower MAE indicates more accurate numerical predictions, complementing correlation-based metrics by reflecting absolute deviation magnitude.

Exact Match (EM)

For counting and discrete-output tasks, EM measures the percentage of predictions that exactly match the gold label. It is a strict metric emphasizing precise numerical or categorical correctness.

Similarity (Sim.)

For
SMILES
generation tasks,
Similarity
is computed as the Tanimoto coefficient between molecular fingerprints of the predicted and reference structures, using the
RDKit
library
24
. This metric evaluates whether generated molecules preserve the intended chemical and structural characteristics of the target compound, reflecting the model’s ability to reproduce meaningful molecular features.

Validity (Val.)

Validity
measures the proportion of syntactically and chemically valid
SMILES
strings among generated samples, determined via
RDKit
parsing and sanitization routines
24
. It ensures that model outputs correspond to chemically plausible molecules adhering to
SMILES
grammar and chemical valency rules. High validity indicates robust chemical reasoning and structural correctness.

Accuracy (MCQ)

For multiple-choice question (MCQ) tasks, accuracy reflects the proportion of correctly selected options relative to the total number of questions, directly measuring discriminative reasoning performance.

Kendall’s Tau Correlation (Pair)

For pairwise ranking and comparison tasks, Kendall’s Tau measures ordinal agreement between predicted and reference rankings. It is computed as:

\tau=\frac{C-D}{C+D},

where
C
and
D
denote the number of concordant and discordant pairs, respectively. Values closer to 1 indicate strong agreement in ranking order.

Together, these metrics provide a balanced evaluation of factual accuracy, structural fidelity, and human-aligned reasoning quality across both symbolic and natural-language outputs.

•

GPT-4o

In contrast, pairwise ranking benefits from direct comparative pattern matching, where forced justifications corrupt learned property-structure correlations. This suggests CoT is beneficial for explicit constraint satisfaction but detrimental for comparative judgments.

G.4
Additional Diagnotics

We probe whether errors in polymer design arise from missing atomic skills or from failures to select and compose otherwise-correct subskills into a constraint-satisfying solution
43
. We use a sample of 300 question from
PolyBench
’s validation set, and prompt
GPT-OSS-20B
o decompose each question into 3-15 hierarchical sub-questions (sub-Qs) along with a set of required skills to answer each sub-question, which serve as our diagnostic probes to isolate the root cause of the issue that leads to low scores. In total, we have a total of 2.6K subquestions. We generate "gold" answers for these sub-questions using
GPT-4o
to be used in our experiments as context.

Our investigation is structured around three core experiments:

•

Exp 1: The Skill Gap (Atomic Knowledge):
We evaluate whether the model can answer the sub-Qs in isolation. This tests the baseline "atomic" knowledge (e.g., monomer molecular weights, functional group identification) required for the task.

•

Exp 2: Skill Selection & Evaluation:
We evaluate the model’s self-generated Chain-of-Thought (CoT). We measure
Recall
(did the model’s CoT mention the necessary sub-Qs?) and
Precision
(were the mentioned sub-steps actually correct?).

•

Exp 3: The Compositional Gap:
We provide the model with "Gold" answers to the sub-Qs as thinking guidance before it generates the final polymer design. If performance doesn’t improve significantly, it confirms the failure was not a lack of knowledge, but a failure in integrating all the information into a coherent, structured narrative.
