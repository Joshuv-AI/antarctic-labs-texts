Activating lattice oxygen in metal oxyhydroxides as durable electrodes for industrial-scale seawater splitting
Authors: Weiju Hao, Liugang Wu, Yiming Wang, Pengyu Zhao, Shuo Weng, Jiacheng Zhang, Jinchen Fan, Li Zhai, Yanhui Guo, Hexing Li, Guisheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalytic seawater splitting powered by renewable electricity is a promising route for large-scale green hydrogen production, yet remains challenging due to electrode corrosion and competing chlorine evolution at high current densities. Developing robust and efficient anodic catalysts is therefore essential. Here, we show an in-situ growth strategy to transform iron foam into boron-doped cobalt-iron oxyhydroxide (B-FeCoOOH) nanosheets. Boron incorporation and a disordered lattice generate abundant oxygen vacancies, lowering kinetic barriers for water oxidation. The catalyst requires only 325 mV to deliver 1.0 A cm−2 and exhibits less than 2% performance decay after 600 h in alkaline seawater. Mechanistic studies indicate that oxygen vacancies activate the lattice oxygen oxidation mechanism, bypassing the scaling limitations of the conventional adsorbate evolution mechanism. The disordered structure enhances structural flexibility to accommodate dynamic reconstruction, mitigating active site dissolution. This strategy is extendable to other transition metal-based oxyhydroxides, enabling the fabrication of large-area, self-supporting electrodes. This work establishes a general strategy for designing durable catalysts for practical seawater electrolysis.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Hydrogen energy

Introduction

Using intermittent renewable power sources (e.g., solar and wind energy) to drive saline water/seawater electrolysis is one of the most promising and sustainable methods for producing clean hydrogen, thereby helping address the energy crisis and achieve carbon neutrality1,2. Compared to the relatively mature technology for hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in seawater at the anode is suffering from electrode corrosion, sluggish kinetics, and the undesirable chlorine evolution reaction (CER), which hinder large-scale, real-world application of green hydrogen production from seawater1,3. Herein, the design and synthesis of low-cost, highly efficient, robust anode catalysts are key to addressing the aforementioned challenges4. Currently, noble-metal-based catalysts, e.g., IrO2 and RuO2, exhibit state-of-the-art OER performance; however, their high prices and limited reserves on earth become a bottleneck restricting their practical industrial application5,6. Additionally, the noble-metal-based catalysts also exhibit high performance in CER. The high concentration of chloride ions in seawater (~0.5 M) would significantly affect the Faraday efficiency of OER, and be transferred into highly corrosive hypochlorite species, leading to severe electrode corrosion and compromising catalytic durability7:

Transition metal oxyhydroxides (MOOH), constituted by earth-abundant transition metals (M = Fe, Co, or Ni), are substitutes for noble-metal-based catalysts in seawater electrolysis, which exhibit high OER performance in alkaline conditions8,9. Previous work has shown that the OER process of MOOH mainly follows two reaction mechanisms: the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) and the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM)10,11. In particular, the LOM pathway, which involves the activation of lattice oxygen atoms as redox-active centers that directly participate in O₂ formation12, overcomes the inherent kinetic and thermodynamic limitations of AEM, thereby exhibiting high OER performance11,13,14. However, the activation of lattice oxygen atoms in the LOM pathway often leads to the formation and refilling of oxygen vacancies, causing structural instability and degradation of catalytic performance15,16. Despite several systems exhibiting notable overall water-splitting performance at 500 mA∙cm−2, the MOOH catalysts still suffer from limited intrinsic activity and a short stability of >100 hours17. Considering the competing CER under alkaline conditions (E⁰ = 0.89 V vs. RHE) is approximately 480 mV higher than that of OER (E⁰ = 0.40 V vs. RHE)18,19, it remains an urgent need to develop OER catalysts achieving industrial-level current densities (>1.0 A cm−2) at low overpotentials (<480 mV), thus ensuring high Faraday efficiency of OER and minimizing the occurrence of CER in alkaline seawater environments20,21.

In this work, we optimize the band structure of FeOOH by introducing Co3+ ions and stabilizing oxygen vacancies (Ovac). Borohydride-etched iron foam (IF) serves as both a structural template and a defect inducer, leading to the formation of B-FeOOH with abundant structural imperfections. Co-doping further modulates the precursor structure, facilitates intramolecular electron redistribution, and promotes the generation of high-valent Fe species accompanied by compressive lattice strain22. During electrooxidation, partial Fe leaching induces the evolution of amorphous nanosheet-like hydroxylated metal oxides, which integrate into the reconstructed FeCoOOH surface layer, thereby minimizing the loss of active species and lattice oxygen. The leaching of Fe induced the formation of Ovac, further driving electronic redistribution. Experimental and theoretical analyses revealed that anodic activation triggered redox processes at both metal and lattice oxygen sites, involving the formation and replenishment of Ovac. The experiments confirmed the existence of the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM), with a reduced reaction energy barrier facilitated by a shift in the rate-determining step (RDS). In alkaline simulated seawater (1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl), B-FeCoOOH demonstrated high stability, achieving overpotentials of only 186 mV and 240 mV at current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2 respectively. Moreover, the catalyst exhibited stable operation for 1440 h at 0.5 A cm−2, 1080 h at 0.7 A cm−2, and 600 h at an industrial-level current density of 1.0 A cm−2 respectively. Notably, the system achieves an industrial-level current density of 1.6 A cm−2 at a low overpotential (<480 mV), ensuring strong Cl⁻ corrosion resistance in alkaline seawater environments. The coexistence of Fe leaching and stabilized Ovac balanced system charge, maintaining Ovac stability and preventing further leaching of Fe³⁺ during OER cycles. Leveraging this strategy, corrosion-resistant electrodes incorporating Co, Cu, and Mo were successfully fabricated, enabling large-scale and cost-effective designs. This approach not only provides a generalizable experimental strategy for constructing hydroxylated metal oxides but also offers valuable insights into the mechanisms for enhancing OER performance by precisely modulating active site formation through the adjustment of Ovac and surface reconstruction.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of anode catalysts

As the schematic illustration shows in Fig. 1a, a facile in situ growth method was developed to transform the surface of Fe foam, a low-cost, earth-abundant and highly conductive substrate (Fig. S1), into boron-doped Fe-Co-based hydroxyl oxide (B-FeCoOOH) nanosheets, forming the Fe foam@B-FeCoOOH electrode. Three-dimensional Fe foam was etched by acid and then immersed in a 0.2 M NaBH4 solution, after which the surface of Fe foam (IF) was converted into boron-doped iron hydroxyl oxide (B-FeOOH) nanosheets (Fig. S2). Co atoms were then introduced into the lattice by immersing B-FeOOH nanosheets in a 0.2 M Co(NO3)2 solution, producing partly lattice-disordered B-FeCoOOH nanosheets.

This in situ growth method enabled the facile and rapid preparation of large-area electrodes (i.e., 10.0 × 10.0 cm2 in Fig. 1b), facilitating the mass production of electrodes capable of high-current seawater electrolysis. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed a porous, nanosheet-assembled surface on the Fe foam after the in situ growth process (Figs. 1c and S3, S4), which was further confirmed by transmission electron microscope (TEM) images (Figs. 1d and S5a–e). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image revealed the ultrathin nature of B-FeCoOOH nanosheets (Fig. 1e). To accurately measure the thickness of these nanosheets, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed. The nanosheets exhibited a tapered morphology, with thicknesses ranging from ~6 nm at the center to ~2 nm at the edges (not accounting for nanosheet stacking; Fig. S6). This ultrathin structure enhanced the surface-to-mass ratio and promoted effective exposure of active sites, ensuring intimate contact between the catalyst and electrolyte.

Notably, both crystalline and amorphous regions were observed in high-resolution TEM images of B-FeCoOOH nanosheets (Fig. S7a–d). In the aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) image of B-FeCoOOH nanosheets (Fig. 1f), well-defined crystalline lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.158 nm can be found (red-marked region, Fig. 1g), which can be attributable to the (002) planes of FeCoOOH. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns from the red-marked region also displayed clear diffraction spots corresponding to the (002) planes of FeCoOOH (Figs. 1h and S8)23. Concurrently, lattice-disordered parts, i.e., amorphous parts, can be found in the B-FeCoOOH nanosheets (blue-marked region, Fig. 1i), along with the characteristic diffuse rings of amorphous structures (Fig. 1j). The presence of these disordered regions and atomic defects led to the formation of small crystalline domains and crystalline-amorphous phase boundaries. These multidimensional defects may provide additional exposed sites or serve as active centers that promote catalytic performance. The HAADF-STEM image and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results (Fig. 1k) showed that the signals of Fe, Co, B, and O were homogeneously distributed throughout nanosheets, further confirming the successful synthesis of B-FeCoOOH nanosheets.

The precise crystal structure and coordination environment of the B-FeCoOOH catalyst were systematically investigated using Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and synchrotron radiation X-ray absorption spectroscopy. In the XRD patterns presented in Fig. 2a, only diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Fe (PDF#06-0696) were observed, indicating that the B-FeOOH and B-FeCoOOH nanosheets on the Fe foam surface are weakly crystalline19,21. As shown in the Raman spectra in Fig. 2b, the main peaks of B-FeCoOOH at ~250 and ~380 cm−1 were attributed to the characteristic vibrational modes of FeOOH, while the broad band in the 660–700 cm−1 region was assigned to lattice-disordered FeOOH (d-FeOOH)24. The band in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region is indexed to lattice oxygen-involved vibrations arising from atomic defects and surface disordered sites25, which may originate from oxygen vacancies and structural defects at amorphous regions or crystalline–amorphous phase boundaries.

EPR measurements of B-FeCoOOH exhibited a pronounced signal at g = 2.003, significantly stronger than those of iron foam and B-FeOOH (Fig. 2c), demonstrating a high concentration of unpaired electrons and abundant oxygen vacancies. XPS analysis further probed the surface chemical states. The Fe 2p spectrum of B-FeCoOOH (Fig. 2d) shifted to higher binding energy relative to B-FeOOH, reflecting electron transfer from Fe to the more electronegative Co via the Fe–O–Co motif, generating catalytically active Fe species.26,27. Notably, the O 1 s XPS spectrum of B-FeCoOOH (Fig. 2e) exhibited three characteristic peaks located at 530.16, 531.11, and 532.03 eV, corresponding to lattice M-O, M-OH, and oxygen vacancy (Ovac) characteristic signals, respectively28–30. In the O 1 s spectra, the metal-lattice oxygen (M-O) signals of B-FeCoOOH showed a significant positive shift compared with B-FeOOH, suggesting that the favored lattice oxygen (M-O(2-δ)−) oxidation involved in the electrochemical activation process. The surface-adsorbed water peak (~534 eV) disappeared, while the oxygen vacancy signal became prominent, demonstrating that Co incorporation promoted abundant oxygen vacancy formation. The B-Fe and B-O bonds of B 1 s orbitals in B-FeOOH could be observed in Fig. 2f. This finding indicates that in the first step of the B-FeCoOOH synthesis process, the acid etching process and the reaction between NaBH4 and the Fe substrate led to the formation of B-M bonds. In the second step, partial oxidation and precipitation of B primarily created defects rather than forming borides, demonstrating that the introduction of Co accelerated B leaching to generate a defective surface31,32.

The local coordination environment and chemical state of Fe atomic sites in B-FeCoOOH were further investigated by X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses. As shown in Fig. 2g, the Fe K-edge XANES absorption edge of B-FeCoOOH was located between that of metallic Fe foil and FeOOH standards, while being closer to the latter, indicating that the Fe species were in a relatively high oxidation state. Fourier transform analysis showed two prominent peaks at 1.4 and 2.7 Å, corresponding to the first-shell Fe–O and the second-shell Fe–O–Co scattering paths3, respectively (Fig. 2h). Quantitative analysis of the Fe K-edge EXAFS spectra further revealed that the average oxidation state of Fe in the B-FeCoOOH system was +2.6 (Fig. 2i), confirming its distinct electronic configuration33. The primary interaction of Fe3+ with O2− occurred through donation. Upon coupling with Co cation, the π repulsion of Fe-O bonds became stronger due to the electronic influence of Co-O, which induced partial electron transfer from Co to Fe. The interaction between Fe2+ and Fe3+ with O2− states leads to a balance between electronic repulsion and Fe-O bond stabilization, resulting in an overall average oxidation state of +2.6. Further wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 2j–l) corroborated these results, showing that the first coordination shell of Fe atoms was primarily composed of O ligands with additional Co coordination14. The overlap of Fe and Co d orbitals facilitated electron delocalization, enhanced electronic conductivity, and improved reaction kinetics. This orbital overlap also strengthened the bonding interactions, endowing the catalyst with structural robustness while maintaining its distinctive atomic configuration.

Moreover, the wetting behavior of pure water droplets on the B-FeCoOOH electrode surface was analyzed with intervals of 0, 60, and 120 ms, using the IF substrate as a reference. The water droplets completely wetted the B-FeCoOOH electrode within 60 ms, while the wetting kinetics on the IF surface were slower (Fig. S9), indicating that B-FeCoOOH is more favorable for the adsorption of water molecules or OH⁻, which is beneficial for the OER process.

Electrocatalytic OER performance

To evaluate the industrial applicability of B-FeCoOOH, we constructed an anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzer with B-FeCoOOH as the anode and Raney Ni as the cathode (Fig. 3a–c). In response to growing demands for advanced medical facilities and environmentally friendly health solutions, we designed a stable and cost-effective electrode system integrated into a compact AEM electrolyzer for simultaneous hydrogen and oxygen generation34. Under system testing, the current density reached 400 mA cm−2 at 1.7 V, with hydrogen and oxygen production rates of 120 mL min−1 and 60 mL min−1, respectively (Vedio 1).

The reaction of Fe foam with varying concentrations of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) and Co cations at different reaction times was optimized by measuring linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) plots (Figs. S10, S11). After optimization, the overpotentials of B-FeCoOOH at current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2 in 1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl (pH = 14.0) at 298 K without iR compensation were 268 and 343 mV, respectively, lower than those of B-FeOOH (343 and 472 mV) (Fig. S12a, b). Additionally, the overpotentials of B-FeCoOOH at current densities of 100, 500, and 1000 mA cm−2 after 90% iR compensation were 287, 306, and 325 mV (Fig. 3d). The B-FeCoOOH electrode achieved industrial-scale OER current density of 1.6 A cm−2 at 480 mV overpotential19. In alkaline natural seawater, B-FeCoOOH showed competitive performance relative to two recently reported catalysts and a commercial Ru-Ir-Ti electrode35. To avoid interference from oxidation peaks in the overpotential assessment, the performance was also evaluated at a fixed current density of 100 mA cm−2 where B-FeCoOOH remained competitive with most reported electrocatalysts (Table S1).

To explore the kinetics of its OER reaction, the Tafel slope values of the studied catalysts were further calculated in a same current density range to reveal the intrinsic catalytic kinetics of the compounds (Fig. 3e). The Tafel slope value (68.79 mV dec−1) of B-FeCoOOH was the smallest over a relatively large range of current densities (10 ~ 100 mA cm−2), indicating the fastest transfer coefficient and the highest catalytic kinetics. The Nyquist plots based on electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) are shown in Fig. S13a, where B-FeCoOOH exhibited the smallest charge-transfer resistance (Rct) showing the fastest charge-transfer efficiency compared to other electrocatalysts. To further elucidate the reaction kinetics during the OER process, EIS measurements were also conducted at different applied overpotentials (Fig. S13b, c). The results reveal that the Rct values of B-FeCoOOH progressively decrease with increasing overpotential, suggesting accelerated charge-transfer processes under OER operating conditions. Notably, B-FeCoOOH consistently displays lower Rct values than B-FeOOH at identical potentials, further confirming the enhanced catalytic kinetics induced by Co incorporation and boron modulation.

Considering that the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was positively correlated with the double-layer capacitance (Cdl), the Cdl values of the electrocatalysts were determined by the non-Faraday zone CV curves (Fig. S14a, b). The Cdl values of B-FeCoOOH were measured to be 13.9 mF cm−2 (Fig. S14c), substantially higher than that of B-FeOOH (6.71 mF cm−2). Accordingly, the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of B-FeCoOOH reached 347.5 cm² (Fig. S14d), approximately twice that of B-FeOOH (167.8 cm²), indicating its markedly larger intrinsic catalytic surface area. Intrinsic catalytic activity was an important parameter in evaluating electrode performance36. The turnover frequency (TOF), which indicated the number of molecules reacted per active site per second, was a key indicator of catalyst efficiency. It was found that the TOF value of B-FeCoOOH was 0.141 s−1 at a potential of 1.530 V (vs. RHE) (Fig. S15a), which was about twice as high as that of B-FeOOH (0.076 s−1), indicating that the instantaneous efficiency of the OER-catalyzed was high. This enhanced catalytic performance was attributed to the presence of abundant multidimensional defects and oxygen vacancies, which markedly enhanced its catalytic activity. Stability represented a critical metric for the industrial implementation of electrocatalytic electrodes. The B-FeCoOOH electrode exhibited high durability, demonstrating a minimal potential shift of 18 mV after 5000 to 10,000 CV cycles at 400 mA cm−2 (without iR compensation), while maintaining nearly unchanged potentials at lower current densities (Fig. S15b). Cobalt doping further enhanced both the OER selectivity and structural robustness of the material. Notably, under conditions relevant to commercial alkaline electrolysis (current density >0.5 A cm−2), B-FeCoOOH achieved overpotentials of 317 and 474 mV (without iR compensation) at 0.5 and 1.0 A cm−2, respectively, even in a highly aggressive 5.0 M NaCl electrolyte at 353 K (Fig. S15c).

Achieving high OER selectivity in simulated seawater due to competition from chlorine precipitation reaction (CER) was crucial, and OER selectivity was evaluated based on a rotating ring disk electrode (RRDE) (Fig. S16). The detail experimental as SI), in which O2, Cl2 generated on the disk catalyst electrode were quantified by electrochemical reduction on the ring electrode. Notably, at 1.67 V, the CER was almost negligible (CER), and the OER selectivity reached 100% in simulated seawater (1.0 M NaCl + 0.5 M NaCl) (Fig. S17a). The LSV curves clearly showed that the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) current density at the disk electrode increased linearly with rotation speed (400–2500 rpm), whereas the ring electrode current remained essentially constant (Fig. S17b). Consistent with this high OER selectivity, TOF-SIMS analysis revealed a lower surface Cl− concentration on B-FeCoOOH compared to B-FeOOH (Fig. S18). Specifically, the surface Cl−/OH− ratio was 0.939:1.0 for B-FeOOH and 0.615:1.0 for B-FeCoOOH, indicating that the latter exhibits stronger Cl− repulsion and improved anti-chloride performance in simulated seawater.

To further investigate the electrolysis process in real seawater, gas products were generated at the cathode and anode in a sealed H-type electrolytic cell with 1.0 M KOH + seawater electrolyte, and were collected to evaluate the Faradaic efficiency. The measured H2:O2 volume ratio was ~2:1, giving Faradaic efficiencies of 98.3% for the HER and 97.7% for the OER (Fig. 3f). Additionally, a 0.5 M KI reagent solution was further employed to detect the chlorine collected in the solution after the anodic reaction by iodometric titration. This result confirmed the absence of chlorine reduction at the ring electrode and further verified the high selectivity of the catalytic electrode. The designed B-FeCoOOH catalyst, with its demonstrated high activity and selectivity, showed significant potential for seawater electrolysis applications.

Due to the high catalytic activity and durability of the B-FeCoOOH catalysts under both laboratory and industrial conditions. A library of substrate materials, including iron foam, nickel foam, and copper foam, was selected to demonstrate the versatility of this strategy (Fig. S19). B-FeCoOOH and B-FeNiOOH were fabricated on IF, B-NiFeOOH, B-NiMoOOH and B-NiCoOOH on NF, B-CuFeOOH and B-CuCoOOH on copper foam. As shown in Fig. S20 and Table S2, the SEM and ICP test results confirmed the successful design and fabrication of the corresponding electrodes. Further TEM characterization and elemental mapping verified the homogeneous distribution of all constituent elements (Fig. S21).

Electrochemical evaluation by LSV (Fig. 3g) revealed enhanced OER performance for all electrodes, and to avoid interference from oxidation peaks, overpotentials at a fixed current density of 100 mA cm−2 were compared (Fig. 3h). By designing an efficient catalytic electrode to realize industrial-scale catalysis, this strategy provided a certain foundation for the mild and rapid preparation of hydroxyl transition metal oxides through the natural growth method of ion exchange. The high catalytic activity and long-term stability of B-FeCoOOH are summarized in the radar plot shown in Fig. 3i. Considering the scarcity of freshwater resources, the development of electrocatalysts capable of stable operation in real seawater is essential for the sustainable, large-scale production of hydrogen via water electrolysis.

To evaluate the long-term durability of B-FeCoOOH and its practical applications, the constant current density time tests were conducted in 1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl37. The results indicate that B-FeCoOOH operated stably for 1440 h at 0.5 and for 1080 h at 0.7 A cm−2 without significant potential fluctuations (Fig. 3j), respectively, which fully illustrates B-FeCoOOH exhibits high stability of OER38. The series of results indicates that the catalytic electrodes of the in-situ growth cation exchange method exhibit high catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability. This performance meets medical oxygen requirements in specialized settings, with notable advantages in portability and cost efficiency compared to existing oxygen generation systems. The device shows potential for on-site hydrogen production and, with further development, could be integrated with intermittent renewable energy sources to support large-scale hydrogen and oxygen generation.

Analysis of OER reaction pathway

The catalytic mechanism and origin of the OER activity of B-FeCoOOH were investigated through in situ spectroscopy and post-reaction structural analyses. In situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor the dynamic structural evolution of the catalyst during electrolysis. As shown in Fig. 4a, B-FeOOH exhibited Raman peaks at 383 and 680–720 cm−1 corresponding to FeOOH and Fe2O3-related vibrational features. In contrast, B-FeCoOOH displayed characteristic peaks at ~250, 383, and 535 cm−1 under anodic polarization39 (Fig. 4b). A broad band at 670–689 cm−1 was attributed to disordered or amorphous FeOOH species18. As the applied potential increased from 1.33 to 1.63 V vs. RHE, the peaks at ~238 and 380 cm−1 gradually intensified, suggesting progressive surface oxidation and structural evolution of the catalyst under anodic potentials.

Simultaneously, cobalt-related structural evolution was observed. At 1.33 V (vs. RHE), a broad Raman band centered at ~570 cm−1 emerged, corresponding to the A1g vibration of CoOOH, suggesting potential-induced transformation of Co species. With further increase in potential to 1.43 V (vs. RHE), the spectral features of defective FeOOH progressively overlapped with those of CoOOH and increased in intensity, indicating the formation of Fe/Co oxyhydroxide intermediates during OER. These results suggested that dynamic surface reconstruction occurred under anodic conditions, leading to the generation of catalytically active oxyhydroxide species. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy further revealed electronic structure modulation in B-FeCoOOH (Fig. S22). The O 1 s spectra showed a higher proportion of M-O(2-δ) species together with a slightly lower Fe 2p binding energy compared with the reference sample (Fig. S23a), indicating the formation of oxygen vacancies through the 2Fe³⁺ → 2Fe²⁺ + Ovac mechanism. These defects could enhance the adsorption and activation of reaction intermediates during OER.

Meanwhile, compositional evolution of boron occurred during electrolysis. The B 1 s signal became undetectable in the XPS spectra after the OER test (Fig. S23b), suggesting progressive leaching of boron species under anodic conditions. Cross-sectional SEM elemental mapping confirmed uniform distributions of Fe, Co, and O across the catalyst layer, whereas no detectable boron signal was observed (Fig. S24 and Table S3). TOF-SIMS analysis further revealed that the surface boron primarily existed as BO− and BO2− fragments (Fig. S25), confirming its oxidized state prior to leaching. The removal of boron likely generated nanoscale voids and transport channels, resulting in a more porous structure that facilitated electrolyte penetration and gas diffusion. Post-reaction HR-TEM further revealed the formation of a thin amorphous surface layer while the underlying crystalline framework remained intact, and SAED patterns displayed diffuse rings characteristic of amorphous structures (Fig. S26). These results indicated that boron leaching triggered surface reconstruction, forming an amorphous oxyhydroxide layer enriched with high-valence Fe/Co species and oxygen vacancies, which was likely responsible for the enhanced OER activity of B-FeCoOOH.

Furthermore, post-OER XPS was conducted to probe the surface electronic structure. The Fe 2p and Co 2p spectra exhibit a noticeable shift toward higher binding energies after OER (Fig. S27), indicating the formation of highly oxidized Fe/Co species during the catalytic process. Meanwhile, the intensity of the B 1 s peak at ~194 eV significantly decreases after electrolysis, which is consistent with the partial leaching of boron and the accompanying surface reconstruction process. To further identify the chemical nature of this reconstructed surface, TOF-SIMS measurements were conducted (Fig. S28). The results revealed the prominent signals of FeOOH−, FeO−, CoOOH−, and CoO− species, confirming the formation of a mixed Fe–Co oxyhydroxide phase after OER. Combined with the attenuation of boron-related signals, these findings provide direct evidence of a dynamic compositional reconstruction toward a Co-containing Fe-based (oxy)hydroxide surface, which serves as the actual electrocatalytic active phase.

To suggest that lattice oxygen participates in the OER process. As shown in Fig. S29 all catalysts exhibited a certain degree of pH dependence in OER activity, with the current density of B-FeCoOOH decreasing more rapidly as the pH value decreased. B-FeCoOOH displayed stronger pH dependence in OER activity compared to B-FeOOH, indicating that the OER kinetics were more likely to be limited by the chemical deprotonation step in the decoupled proton-electron transfer process of LOM. This was attributed to the decrease in charge-transfer energy (Δ), which facilitated electron transfer and weakened the *OH affinity. The proton reaction order (ρRHE = ∂logj/∂pH) of FeCoOOH (0.823) was higher than that of B-FeOOH (0.469) at 1.8 V (Fig. S29a), suggesting that the non-coordinated proton-electron transfer process in low concentrations of hydroxide ions could trigger OER40,41. Furthermore, the negative oxygen intermediates O22− and O2− were detected using tetramethylammonium cation (TMA⁺) as a chemical probe (Fig. S29b, c). B-FeCoOOH exhibited a significant decrease in OER activity in 1.0 M TMAOH, due to the inhibition of LOM by the strong binding of TMA⁺ cations42. In contrast, B-FeOOH showed only a slight decrease in activity (Fig. S29d), indicating that B-FeCoOOH had higher lattice oxygen activity and a more favorable LOM pathway.

To directly probe lattice oxygen participation, in situ Raman isotope-labeling experiments were performed (Fig. S30a, b). After 18O labeling under anodic polarization, the characteristic Fe-O vibrational band (~668 cm−1) gradually shifted toward higher wavenumbers during subsequent electrolysis in 16O electrolyte, consistent with mass-dependent vibrational behavior43. This shift evidences dynamic oxygen exchange between the catalyst lattice and the electrolyte, indicating the involvement of lattice oxygen in the OER process.

In addition, the changes in the electrode surface pH during the OER process were further confirmed by in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy44, as shown in Fig. S31. The absorption peak of hydroxide ions typically appeared in the region of 240–300 nm45. The intensity of this absorption peak was proportional to the concentration of hydroxide ions, making it suitable for quantitative analysis of their concentration. In the spectral range of 240–250 nm, an absorption peak corresponding to OH⁻ was observed46, and as the anodic potential increased gradually from 1.23 V (vs. RHE) to 1.63 V (vs. RHE), the OH⁻ absorption weakened (Fig. S31b). The higher the voltage during the OER process, the greater the pH change on the electrode surface, which indicated that the lattice oxygen was involved in the OER process, proving that the performance of the catalytic material had a strong pH correlation, which agreed with the analytical results of the LOM mechanism described above.

To probe the electrochemical kinetics and the evolution of oxygen-containing intermediates at the Fe active site, in situ Bode plots were collected at different potentials (Fig. S32). Peaks in the low- and high-frequency regions are assigned to interfacial charge-transfer reactions and electronic conduction within the inner catalyst layer, respectively. B-FeCoOOH exhibits a distinct transition peak at 1.48 V, earlier than that of B-FeOOH at 1.53 V, indicating that B and Co incorporation facilitate the oxidation of Fe(OH)2 to FeOOH. The electrode interface transitions from diffusion-limited to Faradaic-dominated behavior, with co-catalytic contributions from both Fe and Co sites (Fig. S32b). Nyquist analysis further shows that B-FeCoOOH exhibits smaller charge-transfer resistance (Rct) than B-FeOOH at all measured potentials, reflecting faster interfacial electron transfer and enhanced OER kinetics. Together, these results indicate that the restructured B-FeCoOOH possesses electronic properties and surface characteristics favorable for efficient oxygen evolution.

Specifically, excess OH was strongly adsorbed on the Fe site to form FesiteOOH, which reacted with the adjacent Co site, further promoting the formation of active CositeOOH species. The formation of FesiteOOH led to a change in the electronic structure around the Co/Fe bimetallic site, making the adjacent Co atoms unstable due to their higher free energy. Therefore, Co atoms tended to trap the ligand from the neighboring FesiteOOH at low potentials under alkaline OER conditions, thus realizing the transformation from FesiteOOH to CositeOOH to ensure their stability. The co-catalytic effect between Fe and Co not only released the excess adsorbed OH on the Fe sites, but also accelerated the formation of CositeOOH reactive species, which improved the OER activity through synergistic effects. The OER activity was enhanced through this synergistic effect. The corrosion resistance of the highly active and stable B-FeCoOOH material was further explored through dynamic potentiodynamic polarization curves (PDP). Corrosion potential and corrosion current are important parameters influencing the actual corrosion behavior of the electrode. The PDP curves (Fig. S33) showed that in a 1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl/seawater environment, the corrosion potential of B-FeCoOOH (0.385 V vs. RHE) was higher than that of B-FeOOH (0.148 V vs. RHE). These results indicated that the rationally designed B-FeCoOOH electrode had higher corrosion resistance than B-FeOOH.

Typically, electrocatalysts with LOM could easily exceed the thermodynamic limitations of AEM and exhibited higher oxygen precipitation activity. However, the involvement of active dot matrix oxygen also led to the creation of oxygen vacancies on the surface of the electrocatalyst, resulting in the easy dissolution of unsaturated metal sites in the electrolyte and reduced stability. LOM achieved high activity of the electrocatalyst at the expense of stability. This was not conducive to the stable operation of the electrocatalyst, but the introduction of Co exposed more active sites in B-FeCoOOH and generated a large number of hydroxyl metal oxides to act as nano-barriers during the reconstruction process, preventing the structure from collapsing to improve the reason for stability in the OER, which was consistent with many reports47.

Experimental results revealed that the creation of oxygen vacancies via NaBH4 treatment and Co doping alters the electronic density of Fe, resulting in the formation of abundant hydroxide oxides. The OER pathway in Co-doped FeOOH proceeded via LOM (Fig. 4c), substantially enhancing OER performance while achieving high stability and corrosion resistance, laying a theoretical foundation for the realization of potential real industrial applications48. Transition metal hydroxyl oxides (MOOH, M = Fe, Co, Ni) undergo irreversible structural reconstruction under electrooxidation conditions, serving as active OER catalysts. Catalysts derived from high-valent metal centers and enriched with oxygen vacancies (Ovac) can trigger the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM), thereby enhancing OER activity. This provided a significant approach for the precise design and optimization of OER pathways through electrocatalysis. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the impact of Co doping on the band structure of FeOOH. The calculation models included FeOOH and FeCoOOH. Co-doping induced structural changes in FeOOH, altering the electronic structure around Fe and forming transition metal hydroxide oxides enriched with oxygen vacancies (Figs. 4d and S34 and 35).

Charge density difference analysis indicated pronounced electron accumulation at oxygen sites bridging Fe and Co, indicative of charge redistribution and electronic modulation (Figs. 4e and S36). After the formation of oxygen defects in B-FeCoOOH, both Fe and Co sites showed electron accumulation, while defect sites experienced significant electron depletion. This behavior could favor the absorption of negatively charged OH⁻ from the alkaline electrolyte at the defect sites, thereby promoting OER41,49. The density of states (DOS) profiles of FeOOH and B-FeCoOOH near the Fermi level exhibited continuous distribution (Fig. S37a, b). Calculating the density of states of the O 2p orbitals reveals the effective activation of lattice oxygen. Compared with FeOOH (−3.11 eV), the O 2p band center of B-FeCoOOH (−2.73 eV) shifted upward toward the Fermi level, favoring electron removal from oxygen sites and suggesting the effective activation of lattice oxygen via cobalt modulation13,50. A further analysis of the Mott-Hubbard splitting in the d orbitals revealed that the overlap between the O 2p orbitals and the metal d orbitals in transition metal hydroxides generated M-O bonding (oxygen-characterized) and M-O* antibonding (metal-characterized) bands. The M-O* antibonding band underwent splitting into an unoccupied upper Hubbard band (UHB) and an electron-filled lower Hubbard band (LHB) due to strong d-d Coulomb interactions (U). The energy difference between the Fe 3 d band center and the O 2p band center, associated with the oxygen-characterized Fe-O bonding band, is defined as the charge-transfer energy (Δ), determined by the positions of the band centers51. The energy gap between UHB and LHB reflected the U parameter, whose increase LHB to penetrate the M-O bonding band. This penetration facilitated the removal of electrons from the M-O bonding band, thereby enabling lattice oxygen oxidation. As shown in Fig. S37c, the U value of FeCoOOH (3.82 eV) was higher than that of FeOOH (3.64 eV), confirming its enhanced ability to activate lattice oxygen52. Co-doping may introduce additional electrons into the antibonding orbitals, potentially weakening the Fe-O bond and promoting lattice oxygen involvement. This involvement could contribute to the formation of oxygen vacancies (Ovac) through the LOM pathway. The presence of Ovac further shifted the O 2p energy level upward and decreased the Δ value, facilitating efficient intramolecular electron transfer53. This process generated oxygen holes and activated lattice oxygen, thereby potentially facilitating the LOM pathway.

The adsorption energies of oxygen on Fe and Co active sites in FeCoOOH were calculated. The Fe site exhibited a higher oxygen adsorption energy (−1.3291 eV−1) compared to the Co site (−1.9986 eV−1), indicating stronger oxygen desorption ability at the Fe site54, which was identified as the active site for the reaction (Figs. S37d and S38). The Gibbs adsorption free energy diagrams for FeOOH and FeCoOOH were computed under both AEM and LOM pathways. As shown in Fig. 4f, g, the adsorption free energy was analyzed for Fe and Co atoms as adsorption sites within the AEM pathway. In the optimized FeOOH model, the energy barrier for the potential-determining step (PDS) in the AEM pathway was 0.916 eV. For FeCoOOH, the energy barrier for the rate-determining step (RDS) on the Fe site in the AEM pathway (1.181 eV) was lower than that on the Co site (1.546 eV), affirming Fe as the active site. Within the LOM pathway, the Gibbs free energy diagram for the Fe site (Fig. 4h) reveals RDS energy barriers of 1.21 eV for FeOOH and 0.72 eV for FeCoOOH. For FeOOH, the PDS energy barrier in the AEM pathway (0.916 eV) was lower than the RDS barrier in the LOM pathway (1.21 eV), indicating that the OER process in undoped FeOOH follows the AEM pathway. Conversely, in FeCoOOH, the RDS barrier in the LOM pathway (0.72 eV) is significantly lower than that in the AEM pathway (1.181 eV), suggesting that the OER process followed the LOM pathway55. These results indicate that undoped FeOOH appears to follow the AEM pathway, whereas the introduction of Co into B-FeCoOOH may modulate and enhance OER activity through the LOM pathway.

The presence of Ovac sites significantly altered the adsorption energy of OH* on FeOOH, facilitating the involvement of lattice oxygen in the OER process. The calculations suggest that Co doping could facilitate a transition of the OER pathway in FeOOH from AEM to LOM, thereby contributing to the enhanced OER performance.

Expanding catalytic applications

This work will further explored the use of seawater resources for water splitting to produce hydrogen, taking full advantage of solar, wind, and tidal energy to reduce hydrogen production costs and minimize energy loss during transportation, ultimately facilitating the realization of green hydrogen (Fig. 5a), which could be applied in industrial production and daily life of households to reduce the loss of non-renewable energy sources, such as coal, and reduce carbon dioxide emissions and achieve carbon neutrality. Given the high electrocatalytic performance of B-FeCoOOH in simulated seawater, we further evaluated its OER activity in natural seawater. The seawater was collected from Sanjia Harbor in the Pudong New Area of Shanghai, China (31.13 N, 121.46 E), and alkalized with 1.0 M KOH+Seawater to achieve a pH of 13.6.

As shown in Fig. 5b, leveraging the high anodic catalytic activity of B-FeCoOOH, we constructed an anion exchange membrane (AEM, Fumasep FAA-3) electrolyzer using B-FeCoOOH (3.5 cm × 3.5 cm) as a bifunctional electrode (Vedio 1). By connecting a solar panel array in series with this AEM electrolyzer, a portable integrated water electrolysis hydrogen and oxygen generator model was assembled. The solar panels convert intermittent solar energy into electricity and store it in a rechargeable battery pack, which powers the AEM electrolyzer to produce H2 and O2. At a current density of 100 mA cm−2, the integrated oxygen generator assembled with the B-FeCoOOH-based electrolyzer can achieve a hydrogen production rate of 3.0 mL min⁻¹ and an oxygen production rate of 1.5 mL min⁻¹ (Fig. 5d). Additionally, this rate is competitive with that of commercial small-scale hydrogen-oxygen devices based on precious metal Pt and Ir catalysts. Its key advantage lies in the cost-effectiveness of efficient non-precious metal electrode materials and the use of photovoltaic power to produce green hydrogen from seawater, holding potential commercial development value. To further expand the applications, stability electrolysis tests were conducted at 100 mA cm−2 for 60 min in 0.1 M KOH + seawater and 1.0 M KOH + seawater, respectively. As shown in Fig. S39, a significant absorption peak appeared at 352 nm in the 0.1 M KOH + seawater electrolyte. This observation indicated the presence of the byproduct ClO⁻, suggesting that under mildly alkaline conditions, the higher overpotential at 100 mA cm−2 promoted the competitive CER. In contrast, no I₃⁻ was detected in the 1.0 M KOH + seawater electrolyte, indicating an OER efficiency approaching 100% in strongly alkaline environments56.

As depicted in Fig.5e, the optimized catalyst exhibited stable operation at a high current density of 1.0 A cm−2 for over 600 hours57. Furthermore, the colorimetric method of N, N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DPD) was used to go through the reaction solution to check whether there was any chlorine generation, and it was found that the solution never appeared red. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was further used to detect the leaching of Fe from the solution, which caused the solution to appear increasingly yellow (Table S4). This observation confirmed that no CER occurred. Comparative tests on other Fe-Ni-based substrates (Table S5) demonstrated that B-FeCoOOH exhibited enhanced stability58. In summary, the catalyst exhibited high OER selectivity and catalytic activity, achieving sustained seawater electrolysis at industrial current densities with overpotentials below 480 mV. Notably, the electrode delivered an industrial-level current density of 1600 mA cm−2 at an overpotential of 480 mV, demonstrating its effectiveness capability for seawater oxidation under practically relevant conditions.

Discussion

We synthesized defect-rich B-FeCoOOH via a rapid ion-exchange method, achieving high activity and stability for water and seawater oxidation. Co substitution modulated the electronic structure, elevating the O 2p level, generating abundant oxygen vacancies, and activating lattice oxygen via the LOM pathway, thereby lowering the rate-determining step barrier. Partial Fe leaching triggered surface reconstruction, forming hydroxide metal oxide nano-barriers that preserved active sites, enabling overpotentials of 306 and 325 mV at 0.5 and 1.0 A cm−2, with stable operation exceeding 600 h under industrial current densities. B-FeCoOOH maintained high performance in simulated and real seawater, as well as in industrial electrolytes (5 M KOH, 353 K), and could be fabricated on diverse substrates (Fe, Ni, Cu) at large-scale, demonstrating the generality and robustness of the approach. This work establishes a design strategy for defect-engineered, lattice oxygen-activated OER catalysts, combining electronic modulation, surface reconstruction, and nanoscale defects to deliver highly active, durable, and scalable electrodes for practical hydrogen production from alkaline water and seawater.

Methods

Materials

The chemicals used in this study include cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₂ ∙ 6H₂O, AR, 99.99%), iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO₃)₃ ∙ 9H₂O, AR, 99.99%), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂ ∙ 6H₂O, AR, 99.99%), sodium chloride (NaCl, AR, 99.9%), sodium borohydride (NaBH₄, AR, 98.0%), anhydrous ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH, AR, 99.7%), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36%), all purchased from Titan Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Perfluoroelastomer (5 wt% perfluoropolymer) was purchased from Alfa Aesar (China) Chemical Ltd. All chemicals were used without further purification. Deionized water was purified using a pure water purifier (18 MΩ cm²). Iron foam (IF), nickel foam (NF), copper foam (CF), and nickel-molybdenum foam (NMF) were purchased from Guangjiayuan New Materials Co., Ltd., Kunshan, and Qingbei New Energy (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China. Platinum sheet electrodes were purchased from Gaoshrui Lian Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China.

Synthesis of B-FeOOH

Commercial Fe foam (thickness: 2 mm, pore size: 60 PPI) was cut into pieces of 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm. Prior to catalyst growth, the Fe foam substrates were cleaned to remove surface oxides and contaminants. Specifically, the Fe foam was immersed in 0.3 M HCl solution and sonicated (40 kHz, ultrasonic bath) for 3 min. The substrates were then rinsed 2–3 times with deionized water, followed by sequential rinsing with absolute ethanol (2–3 times) to remove residual impurities. After cleaning, the substrates were dried under a nitrogen flow and immediately used for subsequent synthesis.

Subsequently, the pretreated Fe foam was immersed in a 0.3 M NaBH4 aqueous solution at room temperature (~25 °C) for 30 min. During the immersion process, hydrogen gas bubbles produced from the hydrolysis of NaBH₄ facilitated the oxidation process, leading to the formation of loosely arranged B-FeOOH nanosheets on the surface. After the reaction, the obtained B-FeOOH samples were rinsed several times with deionized water and dried in air.

Construction of B-FeCoOOH@IF electrodes

To synthesize the optimized defect-rich iron-cobalt oxyhydroxide (B-FeCoOOH) catalyst, the B-FeOOH sample obtained from the NaBH4 treatment (see “Synthesis of B-FeOOH”) was immediately immersed in 50 mL of 0.2 M cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, ≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich) aqueous solution. After reacting at 25 ± 2 °C for 60 min, the resulting solid was rinsed three times with deionized water and dried in air at 30 °C for 2 h. To determine the optimal synthesis conditions, additional B-FeCoOOH samples were prepared by varying the concentration of Co(NO₃)₂ (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 M) and the immersion time (5, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min). Additionally, other B-MXMYOOH (MX, MY = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) catalysts were synthesized using the same procedure with the corresponding metal nitrate salts.

Materials characterizations

The microstructure of the series of catalytic electrodes was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Phenom ProX). The crystal structure was investigated with an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE) and further verified through high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). The thickness of the B-FeCoOOH catalyst was measured using a Bruker Multimode 8 atomic force microscope (AFM). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha) was employed to analyze the surface elemental valence states of the catalysts. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM 100 F) was utilized to study the microstructure, while element mapping was obtained using EDS-equipped SEM and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED)-assisted TEM. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20) was conducted to analyze chemical bond changes before and after the OER process. In-situ UV-Vis spectroscopy was applied to examine the formation of intermediate species during reactions, shedding light on the electrochemical process and reaction mechanisms. Raman spectroscopy, both in situ and ex situ, was performed using a Horiba iHR320 spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm continuous wave laser to confirm the presence of hydroxide oxides and their transformation during reactions. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Bruker A300 spectrometer) was used to characterize oxygen vacancies. The elemental composition of the prepared catalytic electrodes was determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES). The hydrophilic properties of the materials were assessed using a contact angle goniometer (JC2000C1). Finally, the electrochemical performance was evaluated with an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E, Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd.).

The setup of theory calculation

In order to investigate the OER properties of FeCoOOH in alkaline environments, our spin-polarised density functional theory (DFT) calculations59,60 were performed, using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), employing plane-wave basis sets combined with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method61,62. The exchange-correlation potential was treated using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)63 parametrization. To account for van der Waals interactions, the Grimme DFT-D3 correction was applied64. We constructed a model with FeOOH (002) as the adsorption surface, and for FeCoOOH, we substituted some of the Fe sites on the FeOOH (002) surface with Fe42Co6O94H48, which closely aligned with the experimental characterization. A vacuum region of ~18 Å was included to prevent interaction between periodic images. The energy cutoff was set to 450 eV, and Brillouin-zone sampling was performed using a Monkhorst-Pack grid with a 1 × 1 × 1 k-point mesh65. The structures were fully relaxed until the maximum force on each atom was less than 0.02 eV/Å, and the energy convergence criterion was set to 10−5 eV. “The optimized atomic coordinates of all computational models are provided as Supplementary Data 1.”

To detect potential chlorine-containing byproducts from the competitive CER, the anolyte was analyzed after prolonged electrolysis. The formation of Cl2 was tested by mixing a 5 mL aliquot of the electrolyte with 0.5 M KI solution; the absence of a color change indicated no I3⁻ was formed, ruling out Cl2 production. Additionally, UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy was used to screen for ClO− in the post-electrolysis electrolyte, monitoring the characteristic absorption band at 352 nm.

Stability testing

To evaluate the long-term stability of the catalytic electrodes under high-current operation, continuous chronopotentiometric tests were carried out in alkaline simulated seawater (1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl, pH = 14.0) and alkaline real seawater (1.0 M KOH + seawater, pH = 13.6; 0.1 M KOH + seawater,pH = 12.7) using a Henghui DC power supply (PLD-3010) at current densities of 500, 700, and 1000 mA cm−2, respectively. All stability tests were performed in a two-electrode configuration without E-iR compensation. The reported potentials are the applied cell voltages as recorded.
