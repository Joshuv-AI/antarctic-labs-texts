Estetrol‐based combined oral contraceptives: A systematic review of clinical outcomes
Authors: Mathilde Råberg, Ingeborg Rose Vad, Kara Hildur Axelsdóttir, Ellen Christine Leth Løkkegaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCombined oral contraceptive pills (COCs) are a common contraceptive method among women of reproductive age. Ethinylestradiol (EE) is a frequently used estrogen component in COCs. However, EE may increase the risk of cardiovascular complications by affecting coagulation, fibrinolysis, and blood pressure. Lowering the dose of EE to minimize the risk has been linked to disadvantageous bleeding patterns. The natural estrogen estetrol (E4) has been introduced as an alternative component to EE. E4 has been suggested to have beneficial effects on targeted tissue with a limited impact on the liver and coagulation, compared to EE‐containing COCs, and thus, offers a potential for improving the safety of future COCs. This systematic review examines the safety and efficacy of COCs containing E4 compared to traditional COCs.Material and MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Embase and PubMed databases. Studies investigating the effects of COCs containing E4 in a study population of healthy women of reproductive age were included. All clinical outcomes related to contraceptive efficacy, bleeding patterns, mood and sexual health, endocrine and liver metabolism parameters, and markers for cardiovascular health were evaluated. Literature reviews, meta‐analyses, and in vivo and animal studies were excluded. Retrieval and assessment of articles were performed in Covidence software following PRISMA guidelines and Cochrane risk of bias assessment tools.ResultsFourteen studies were included in the review. Results indicate that E4‐containing COCs offer a high contraceptive reliability, predictable menstrual cycles, and favorable bleeding patterns. E4‐containing COCs effectively suppressed ovulation while allowing for rapid return of ovulation after discontinuation. Furthermore, E4‐containing COCs present milder effects on coagulation factors and a more neutral impact on thrombin generation, suggesting a reduced risk for venous thromboembolisms.ConclusionsE4‐containing COCs could potentially be a safer and more effective alternative to contraceptive use. The findings should be interpreted with caution, as study limitations such as design and confounder bias, small study populations, and short follow‐up times characterize the studies included in this review. Further, industry sponsorship bias cannot be ruled out. Further independent research is needed to confirm long‐term safety and applicability of E4‐containing COCs across diverse populations.

Key message

Estetrol appears to be an effective and potentially safer contraceptive option than ethinylestradiol‐containing COCs, with favorable effects on ovarian suppression, bleeding pattern, hormonal profile, and thrombotic markers, though long‐term safety remains to be confirmed by independent studies.

INTRODUCTION

Each contraceptive method has its own strengths and weaknesses, with suitability depending on individual needs. Factors such as effectiveness, side effects, and ease of use influence the choice of method. 1 In 2019, 842 million women in the reproductive age (15–49 years) used a modern contraceptive method, and the oral contraceptive pill was among the most common, with over 20% of women using it in 27 countries. 2

Combined oral contraceptives (COCs) contain both a progestin and an estrogen component. Progestin inhibits ovulation, while the estrogen enhances contraceptive efficacy, maintains a regular bleeding pattern, and prevents symptoms of estrogen deficiency. Among the estrogens used in COCs, ethinylestradiol (EE) is the most widely employed due to its high oral bioavailability, long half‐life, and strong potency. However, EE stimulates hepatic protein synthesis and affects the vascular endothelium, slightly increasing the risk of cardiovascular complications. 3

Reducing the EE dose can help limit these effects, but often results in a less favorable bleeding pattern. 4 To further enhance safety and tolerability, newer COC formulations use lower hormone doses, more selective progestins, and alternative estrogens. Yet, EE‐based COCs remain associated with increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared with non‐use and adverse effects such as headache, mood changes, and irregular bleeding. 5

Estetrol (E4), a naturally occurring estrogen produced by the fetal liver during pregnancy and synthetically derived from plant sources, has recently been introduced as a new estrogen component in COCs. 6 , 7 E4 exhibits a unique mechanism of action: It selectively activates nuclear estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) but antagonizes membrane ER. This tissue‐selectivity activity may allow E4 to exert estrogenic effects where beneficial (e.g., uterus, brain, bone) while limiting hepatic stimulation and its associated coagulation risks. 6 , 8 Due to its tissue‐selective activity and limited hepatic impact, E4 represents a promising candidate for improving the safety profile of combined contraceptives.

This systematic review aims to evaluate the current clinical evidence on E4‐containing COCs, focusing on contraceptive effectiveness, bleeding patterns, hepatic and endocrine effects, mood and sexual function, and cardiovascular risk profile.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guideline 9 and registered with PROSPERO CRD420251133470.

Search strategy, sources, and eligibility

Relevant literature was identified using PubMed and Embase databases up to April 30, 2025, using the keywords “estetrol,” “drospirenone,” and their synonyms. The complete search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) The study population consisted of healthy women of reproductive age (15–49 years). (2) Participants were consistently exposed to a COC containing E4. (3) The study design included original clinical research. (4) Studies were published in English or Scandinavian languages. Studies were excluded if they were animal or in vitro studies, reviews or meta‐analyses, or pharmacokinetic‐only reports. All clinical outcomes were included if the study met the abovementioned eligibility criteria.

Selection process

Study selection was performed using Covidence software, which automatically removed duplicates. Title and abstract screening were then conducted independently by two reviewers (MR and IRV). Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Full texts of the remaining articles were assessed by the same reviewers, with consensus used to resolve any discrepancies.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by the reviewers (MR and IRV) using a template of study characteristics, including author, year of publication, study design, number of participants, duration, and types of exposure to COCs, main outcomes, and findings.

Risk of bias assessment

Evaluation of the quality of the articles was performed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (Rob2) for randomized clinical trials. 10 The assessment tool covers the following domains of potential bias: Sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of personnel and participants, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Non‐randomized trials were evaluated using the risk of bias in non‐randomized studies of interventions (Robins‐I V2), including standard ROBINS‐I V2 domains. 11 Additionally, the GRADE approach was used to evaluate the risk of industry sponsorship bias, as Cochrane tools do not include approaches for evaluating funding bias. 12 Risk of bias was assessed independently by the two reviewers (MR and IRV), followed by consensus agreement.

Evidence synthesis

Evidence synthesis was conducted separately for each clinical outcome, and relevant findings from all studies reporting effects on a given outcome were narratively summarized. Pooling of data and meta‐analysis were not considered relevant.

Specification of relevant exposures and outcomes

Relevant exposure was defined as use of a COC containing E4 for at least one treatment cycle according to the study protocol. Outcomes were included if they reflected clinically meaningful endpoints related to contraceptive effectiveness, ovarian suppression, bleeding patterns, mood, sexual function, endocrine or metabolic markers, hemostatic parameters, and cardiovascular risk factors or androgen‐related outcomes. Ovulation suppression was considered relevant when assessed by validated measures such as progesterone levels or Hoogland scores. Contraceptive effectiveness required reported pregnancy rates or Pearl index (PI; pregnancies per 100 woman‐years of exposure) values under consistent use. Bleeding outcomes were included if recorded across multiple treatment cycles. Hormonal, metabolic, and cardiovascular parameters were included when supported by clinical data rather than pharmacokinetic measures. Patient‐reported outcomes were included for mood and sexual function if validated tools were used.

RESULTS

A total of 193 articles were identified: 53 from PubMed and 140 from Embase. Forty‐three duplicate articles were removed with Covidence software, and an additional 8 duplicates were manually removed during screening. Title and abstract screening excluded 91 articles, leaving 51 articles to be assessed for eligibility. Of these, 37 were excluded: 12 articles did not meet the study design criteria, 8 were not available in English or Scandinavian languages, and 16 were only available as abstracts. The remaining 14 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram showing the study selection process.

Study characteristics

The eligible studies, published between 2015 and 2025, originated from the Netherlands, Finland, Italy, the United States, Canada, Europe, and Russia. The studies included six randomized controlled trials (RCTs), four non‐randomized studies, and four single‐arm studies, with sample sizes ranging from 48 to 1864 participants and study durations of three to thirteen cycles. All studies were evaluated as having some concerns or a high risk of bias. Among the RCT studies, risk of bias was mainly due to the open‐label design and therefore lack of blinding for both participants and researchers, and absence of appropriate analysis of deviations from intervention. Lack of confounder control was the main reason for the high risk of bias evaluation in the non‐randomized and single‐arm studies. Several studies were based on overlapping populations while reporting different clinical outcomes, which limits the number of unique participants represented across the evidence base. Contraceptive efficacy

Two phase‐III studies investigated the contraceptive efficacy of 15 mg E4/drospirenone (DRSP) over 13 menstrual cycles, respectively. Contraceptive efficacy was measured using PI to estimate both overall and method‐failure pregnancy rates (pregnancies occurring despite correct pill use). Participants were instructed to use the study contraceptive and avoid using an additional contraceptive method during the study period. At‐risk cycles were defined as those in which participants reported using the study drug and having intercourse at least once during the cycle, without using any additional contraceptive methods. 13 , 14

Creinin et al. reported an estimated overall PI of 2.65 pregnancies/100 woman‐years (95% CI: 1.73–3.88). When adjusting for user‐failure, that is, poor pill compliance, the method failure PI was 1.43 (95% CI 0.78–2.39). 14

Gemzell‐Danielsson et al. found an overall PI of 0.47 pregnancies/100 woman‐years (95% CI: 0.15–1.11), and a method‐failure PI of 0.29 (95% CI: 0.06–0.83). 13 The authors concluded that the study achieved the required precision of the estimate according to guidelines on steroid contraceptives, since the distance between the PI and the upper limit of its 95% CI was ≤1 for both the overall and the method‐failure PI. 13 , 15 This criterion was fulfilled for the method‐failure PI, but not for the overall PI, in the study by Creinin et al. 14 Hence, 15 mg E4/DRSP showed a high contraceptive efficacy in the study by Gemzell‐Danielsson et al.; the higher PI observed in the study by Creinin et al. indicates some uncertainty about the estimated efficacy. 13 , 14

Ovarian suppression

Three studies assessed ovulation suppression and suppression of gonadotropin secretion over three to six treatment cycles. In a dose‐finding study by Duijkers et al., all tested E4 doses (5–20 mg) combined with progestin suppressed ovulation, with the 20 mg dose showing the strongest effect, comparable to a standard EE/DRSP regimen. Progesterone levels remained <5 nmol/L throughout all cycles, and no ovulations were recorded, aside from one likely caused by incomplete luteal regression with elevated progesterone at baseline. Endometrial thickness was similarly reduced across treatment arms, and ovulation resumed within 17–21 days post‐treatment with no dose‐dependent differences observed. 16

In an RCT comparing 15 mg E4/DRSP with 20 mcg EE/DRSP over three cycles, 65.8% of women in the E4 group and 83.8% in the EE group showed no ovarian activity by cycle 3 (Hoogland score 1). Active follicle‐like structures were more frequent in the E4 group (21.1% vs. 5.4%), though mean follicular diameter remained <10 mm in both groups, whereas ovulation occurred in three cycles among two EE‐users, while no ovulations were observed with E4. Endometrial suppression was similar in both groups. Post‐treatment ovulation returned on average after 15.5 days (E4) and 18.1 days (EE). 17

Overall, studies consistently show that E4‐containing COCs suppress follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), although the inhibition is less pronounced than with EE‐containing comparators. In a dose‐finding study, FSH and LH remained higher with 5 and 10 mg E4 than with EE/DRSP, while suppression at 20 mg E4 was comparable. 16 Another study reported 15 mg E4/DRSP fully inhibited ovulation despite milder gonadotropin suppression (mean FSH 4.3 mU/mL and LH 4.8 mU/mL vs. 1.7 and 1.8 mU/mL with EE/DRSP). 17 Although no statistical comparisons were provided, these results suggest that moderate gonadotropin suppression may be sufficient to achieve ovulation inhibition with E4‐based COCs. Similarly, Klipping et al. reported milder gonadotropin suppression with E4/DRSP, with minimal effects on LH, whereas EE‐containing COCs induced marked suppression of both FSH and LH. 18 These findings suggest that E4‐containing COCs effectively suppress ovulation, though with lower suppression compared to EE‐containing COCs. This may reflect a milder endocrine effect, potentially reducing side effects, but could also affect contraceptive effectiveness under typical use, where user‐failures might occur.

All included studies aimed to investigate the contraceptive efficacy and/or endocrine and metabolic effects of E4‐containing COCs. However, when investigating the efficacy of steroid contraceptives, a study duration of at least six months is desired. 15 Consequently, the long‐term efficacy of E4 in achieving ovarian suppression remains undetermined.

Bleeding patterns

Three studies assessed bleeding patterns and cycle control with E4‐based COCs. In a phase II trial, 15 mg E4/DRSP showed the lowest rates of unscheduled bleeding (16.9%) and absence of withdrawal bleeding (3.5%) after six cycles, compared with 47.8% and 27.1% in the Estradiol valerate (E2V)/Dienogest (DNG) group. 19 Daily rates of unscheduled bleeding/spotting and early withdrawal were consistently lower in the 15 mg E4/DRSP group than in the other E4 regimens. 19 Cycle control over 13 cycles was evaluated in two phase III studies, both demonstrating predictable bleeding patterns with scheduled withdrawal bleeding lasting 4–5 days. 13 , 14 Most unscheduled bleeding events were limited to spotting (55.2% in the 2021 study 14 ; 71.8% in the 2022 study 13 ). Absence of scheduled bleeding peaked at 18% in cycle 3 and declined to 13.3% by cycle 12. 14 Complete amenorrhea remained below 10% in both populations. 13 , 14 In addition, the bleeding patterns observed with E4/DRSP appear comparable to those reported for established EE‐containing COCs, which are typically characterized by predictable withdrawal bleeding and low rates of unscheduled bleeding. 20 Overall, the findings indicate that E4‐containing COCs may offer consistent and acceptable cycle control.

Hemostatic profile and VTE risk

Five studies examined the effects of E4‐containing COCs on hemostasis and VTE risk. Kluft et al. found that E4/DRSP caused minimal, insignificant changes in anticoagulation markers (antithrombin, protein S, and activated protein C resistance (APCr)) and lower procoagulant activity than EE/DRSP over three cycles. Free tissue factor pathway inhibitor antigen significantly decreased in both groups, but to a lesser extent with E4/DRSP, while D‐dimer and prothrombin fragments F1 + 2 increased significantly with EE/DRSP and decreased with E4/DRSP. 21

Similarly, Morimont et al. reported that thrombin‐generation parameters (endogenous thrombin potential (ETP), peak thrombin, and mean velocity rate index (mVRI)) remained within the reference range for E4/DRSP but exceeded it for EE/DRSP after six cycles. 22 Douxfils et al. found that E4/DRSP had a significantly lower impact on APC resistance and plasminogen than EE/DRSP and EE/levonorgestrel (LNG), while changes in fibrinogen, prothrombin, factor VIII, vWF, and PAI‐1 were smaller or nonsignificant across groups. 23 VTE incidence was evaluated in two phase III trials. Creinin et al. reported no cases among E4/DRSP users, while Gemzell‐Danielsson et al. reported one case out of 1553 participants, indicating a low VTE risk of E4‐containing COCs. 13 , 14 Collectively, these findings indicate that E4/DRSP exerts a milder effect on hemostatic pathways than EE‐based COCs, consistent with a potentially lower thrombotic risk.

Androgen parameters

Four studies evaluated androgen‐related outcomes. In a six‐cycle RCT, Klipping et al. observed that E4/DRSP and EE/LNG reduced free testosterone by 50%, compared with a 71% reduction in the EE/DRSP group, although the difference between E4/DRSP and EE/DRSP was not statistically significant. Similarly, moderate reductions in androstenedione and dihydrotestosterone were seen with E4/DRSP compared to greater decreases with EE‐based COCs, but these differences were not significant. Sex hormone‐binding globulin (SHBG) increased by 55% with E4/DRSP, 251% with EE/DRSP, and 74% with EE/LNG. 18 In a non‐randomized observational study, total testosterone (~1.5 nmol/L) was similar across seven COC regimens, but EE‐based users showed a lower free androgen index (FAI) (~1.5% vs. ~3.1%) and greater SHBG increases (306% vs. 44.5% with E4/DRSP; p < 0.001). 24 Mawet et al. also observed small SHBG increases with E4/DRSP and decreases with E4/LNG, which the authors attributed to the androgenic activity of LNG. 25 Similarly, Douxfils et al. reported smaller SHBG increases with E4/DRSP compared with EE/DRSP, supporting the interpretation that E4 induces less hepatic stimulation of SHBG. 23 Across studies, total testosterone remained stable with both E4‐ and EE‐containing COCs, suggesting that differences in androgen parameters are mainly driven by changes in SHBG and thereby free androgen availability. E4/DRSP was consistently associated with smaller SHBG increases and higher FAI than EE‐containing COCs, suggesting a milder impact on androgen status.

Menstrual and premenstrual symptoms

Three studies evaluated menstrual and premenstrual symptoms. In a phase III trial using the Menstrual Distress Questionnaire (MDQ), E4/DRSP users showed significant reductions in menstrual pain, water retention, and negative affect, particularly among contraceptive starters; over 40% reported improvement in cramps, breast tenderness, fatigue, and mood swings, while concentration scores remained unchanged. Among women switching from a different hormonal contraceptive method to E4/DRSP (“switchers”), no significant differences in symptom scores of pain, negative affect, and impaired concentration were found compared to their baseline values while on previous COCs. However, premenstrual and menstrual symptoms of water retention increased significantly among switchers compared to their baseline values while on previous contraception, indicating a worsening of this symptom. 26 Another phase III trial showed similar results: menstrual pain decreased by 3.3 points, water retention by 1.5–2.0 points, and premenstrual negative affect by 1.2 points among starters, all significant, whereas no relevant changes occurred among switchers. 27

To explore perceived tolerability, a phase II study assessing user satisfaction found similar trends. Premenstrual symptom improvement was most frequent with E2V/DNG, followed by 15 mg E4/DRSP, while worsening was least common with E4/DRSP (11.7%) compared to E2V/DNG (12.2%) and higher for E4/LNG (27.1%) or 20 mg E4/DRSP (19.2%). 28 Taken together, these findings suggest that 15 mg E4/DRSP improves premenstrual and menstrual symptoms in new users and maintains symptom control in switchers, with outcomes and user satisfaction comparable to E2V/DNG.

Mood and sexual function

Two studies assessed mood, well‐being, and sexual function during E4/DRSP use. In a phase II trial, improvements in mood and general well‐being were reported by 19.9–27.6% of E4/DRSP users, depending on dosage, comparable to E2V/DNG (25%) and higher than E4/LNG (13.7%). Satisfaction was highest with 15 mg E4/DRSP (73.1%), with continuation willingness similar to that of the E2V/DNG group. 28

Caruso et al. found that, after ≥6 months, E4/DRSP users had significantly higher sexual desire and FSFI scores (mean 31.5 vs. <22.6 with EE‐based COCs). SHBG levels were lower and FAI levels higher in E4 users, and both were significantly correlated with sexual function scores. Women using EE‐based COCs showed FSFI scores below the established dysfunction threshold (<26.55 points), indicating sexual dysfunction, and higher levels of sexual distress. Overall, E4/DRSP was associated with higher treatment satisfaction, better psychological well‐being, and more favorable sexual function scores compared to EE‐containing COCs, with effects comparable to E2V/DNG.

Hepatic and metabolic effects

Hepatic and metabolic effects of E4‐containing COCs were evaluated in three studies. Klipping et al. found that triglyceride levels increased by 24% with E4/DRSP compared to a 28% increase with EE/LNG and 65.5% increase with EE/DRSP. E4/DRSP had minimal impact on high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), total cholesterol, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and no significant effect on carbohydrate metabolism. 18 In the study by Kluft et al., angiotensinogen increased by 25–31% in the E4/DRSP groups, whereas the increase was 156% with EE/DRSP. 21 Mawet et al. reported limited effects of E4‐containing COCs on liver proteins and lipid metabolism, bone turnover markers, or growth factors (e.g., IGF‐1). 25 Overall, E4/DRSP showed modest effects on hepatic proteins, lipids, and carbohydrate metabolism, which were consistently smaller than those observed with EE‐containing COCs. indicating a more neutral hepatic and metabolic profile.

DISCUSSION

This review identified 14 studies assessing clinical outcomes of COCs containing E4 across key outcome domains. The included studies varied considerably in design, ranging from RCTs to non‐RCTs and single‐arm studies. Many studies were open‐label, with small sample sizes and short follow‐up durations, contributing to heterogeneity in the evidence base.

Overall, the available evidence indicates that E4/DRSP provides effective ovulation suppression and high contraceptive reliability. Gemzell‐Danielsson et al. reported a PI of 0.47, while Creinin et al. study found a PI of 2.65, 14 though this estimate did not meet the required precision and might indicate some uncertainty. 15 In comparison, a pooled analysis of an established EE/DRSP pill reported an overall PI of 0.80 (95% CI 0.80–1.30) and a method‐failure PI of 0.41 (95% CI 0.41–0.85). 20 Thus, the findings by Gemzell‐Danielsson et al. suggest efficacy comparable to established EE‐containing COCs. Differences in PI estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as regulatory guidance emphasizes their sensitivity to population characteristics and adherence patterns rather than biological contraceptive efficacy alone. 15 The North American study included a more heterogeneous population, including higher proportions of participants with BMI ≥30 kg/m2, a factor associated with higher oral contraceptive failure under typical use, while the lower method‐failure PI suggests that user‐related factors, such as imperfect pill use, contributed to the higher overall estimate. 14 , 28 Despite some uncertainty regarding typical‐use efficacy estimates, the available evidence supports effective ovulation suppression and contraceptive effectiveness under correct use. In addition, E4/DRSP was associated with favorable bleeding profiles. Bleeding patterns were predictable, with low rates of unscheduled bleeding and high user satisfaction reported across studies. 13 , 14 , 19 Further, the effect of the combination of E4/DRSP on mood as well as premenstrual complaints was favorable to E4/LNG combinations, and especially the 15 mg E4/DRSP combination was comparable to COCs containing E2V. 28 Menstrual and premenstrual symptoms improved after E4/DRSP initiation, though symptoms among women who previously had been using COCs did not differ, except for the symptom of water retention, which worsened. 26 , 27 A better sexual function score was reported among E4/DRSP users compared to women on EE‐containing COCs, where scores met the threshold for sexual dysfunction. 24

The impact on androgen parameters also appeared more favorable in E4/DRSP groups compared to EE‐containing COCs. Studies reported smaller increases in SHBG, less suppression of free testosterone, and more moderate reductions in gonadotropins such as FSH and LH levels. 16 , 18 , 28 This may contribute to a more tolerable hormonal side‐effect profile. Yet, total testosterone levels were similar across COCs, and no significant differences were found.

In terms of cardiovascular safety, E4‐containing COCs were consistently associated with milder changes in hemostatic markers and reduced thrombin generation compared to EE‐based formulations, 21 , 23 , 29 suggesting a potentially lower risk of VTE. Metabolic outcomes also appeared more favorable with E4/DRSP compared to EE‐containing COCs. Smaller increases in triglycerides, SHBG, and angiotensinogen were reported, while carbohydrate metabolism remained unaffected. 18 , 21 , 23 , 25 Mawet et al. further reported no relevant changes in markers of bone turnover or growth factors. 25

This review includes 14 studies that collectively address a wide range of clinical outcomes, including ovarian suppression, bleeding patterns, hormonal effects, cardiovascular parameters, and user‐reported experiences such as mood and sexual function. The broad inclusion of outcomes allowed for a comprehensive overview of E4/DRSP's profile compared to established COCs. However, this breadth also introduced considerable heterogeneity in both study design and reported outcomes, which may reduce the depth of analysis within individual domains and complicate comparisons across studies.

Furthermore, all included studies were assessed to have some concerns or a high risk of bias, primarily due to open‐label designs, lack of confounder control, and small sample sizes. The vast majority of studies were industry‐sponsored, raising the possibility of funding bias as well. Additionally, four studies included overlapping populations, which may lead to overestimation of the strength or consistency of the evidence. Follow‐up periods were relatively short (up to 13 cycles), limiting the knowledge on long‐term effects and safety.

The available evidence suggests that E4/DRSP is a promising COC option, with efficacy and user satisfaction comparable to EE‐containing COCs, and possibly a more favorable profile in terms of coagulation, metabolism, and hormone‐related side effects. The consistently lower SHBG increases, less suppression of free testosterone, and more moderate gonadotropin inhibition may contribute to a more favorable side‐effect profile. This could explain the improved mood, well‐being, and sexual function reported among users. 22 , 26 Reduced thrombin generation and stable hemostatic markers suggest a potentially lower VTE risk, although this remains to be confirmed in larger, independent studies with longer follow‐up. Additionally, benefits in premenstrual symptoms, bleeding patterns, weight stability, and sexual function may support higher acceptability and continuation rates. However, findings on sexual function were derived from a single observational study and should therefore be interpreted with caution. 22

While these findings are encouraging, they must be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations and risk of bias. Notably, 13 of the 14 were industry‐funded, raising concerns about potential sponsor influence. The limited diversity of study populations and short follow‐up durations further highlight the need for independent, long‐term phase IV trials to establish the safety and applicability of E4‐containing COCs across broader and more diverse populations.

CONCLUSION

E4‐containing COCs, particularly combined with DRSP, appear to provide an effective and potentially safer alternative to traditional EE‐based COCs. Reported benefits include reliable contraceptive efficacy, predictable bleeding patterns, more favorable hormone and coagulation profiles, and high user satisfaction. However, current evidence is limited by short study durations, high risk of bias, and lack of independent research. Further long‐term, independently conducted studies in diverse populations are needed to confirm the safety, acceptability, and broader clinical applicability of E4‐containing COCs.
