New benzoxanthenone and benzanthrone derivatives from Tricholaena teneriffae L. roots: structure elucidation, α-amylase inhibitory activity and molecular docking studies
Authors: Henda Keskes, Ines Dhouib, Emna Khdhiri, Houcine Ammar, Anthony Abou Dib, Jean-Hugues Renault, Noureddine Allouche, Bassem Khemakhem, Hichem Ben Salah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ethyl acetate extract of Tricholaena teneriffae L. roots exhibited significant α-amylase inhibitory activity. Phytochemical analysis of this extract led to the isolation of seven natural products: five 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one derivatives (1–5) and two 7H-benzo[de]anthracen-7-one derivatives (6–7). Among them, compounds 1, 3, 4, 5, and 7 are reported here for the first time as new natural derivatives. Structural elucidation was performed via 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopy, allowing for a precise determination of the substitution patterns of both the benzo[a]xanthen-12-one and benzo[de]anthracen-7-one frameworks. Among them, compounds 6 (IC50 = 0.308 ± 0.015 μM) and 4 (IC50 = 0.480 ± 0.024 μM) showed the most potent inhibitory effects. Molecular docking studies revealed that the presence of both hydroxyl and methoxy groups, as in compound 6, enhances inhibition through strong hydrogen bonding and π-type interactions, while methoxy substitution on ring C, as in compound 4, increases binding affinity via interactions with Glu233 and Asp197. The photophysical properties of seven isolated dyes were examined in dichloromethane by UV–visible and fluorescence spectroscopy. Hydroxyl and methoxy substituents enhanced electron donation, leading to red-shifted absorption and emission compared to the unsubstituted analogue 2. The strongest red shift was observed for the dimethoxylated dye 3, while the hydroxylated dye 6 exhibited higher intensities than its methoxy analogue 7. These results highlight the importance of specific position and nature of substituents in modulating α-amylase inhibition and photophysical behavior in root-derived metabolites.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01833-x.

Introduction

The Poaceae, commonly known as the grass family, is the fifth largest family of flowering plants, comprising approximately 50 tribes, 660 genera, and about 10,000 species worldwide [1]. This family holds remarkable economic and medicinal importance owing to its wide distribution and high species diversity [2]. Numerous plants of Poaceae family have been traditionally used in folk medicine to manage various ailments, including hypertension, diabetes, inflammation, parasitic infections, ulcers, and oxidative stress [3–6].

The Poaceae family encompasses plants with diverse pharmacological properties, largely attributed to their rich content of bioactive natural products. Many species have been investigated for their antioxidant, antibacterial, and enzyme-inhibitory activities, highlighting their potential as sources of novel therapeutic agents [7].

Among the bioactive constituents of this family, xanthones (9H-xanthen-9-ones, also known as dibenzo-γ-pyrones) represent an important class of oxygenated heterocycles with well-established roles in medicinal chemistry [8]. Their biological activities are closely related to their tricyclic scaffold but vary depending on the type and position of substituents [9]. Xanthones and their derivatives (Fig. 1) have attracted significant attention due to their unique pharmacological and photophysical properties. The rigid polycyclic aromatic core of xanthones promotes diverse biological interactions, while their conjugated structures confer intrinsic fluorescence, enabling applications as molecular probes and therapeutic agents [10].Fig. 1Structure of xanthone

Tricholaena teneriffae L. (Poaceae) is a wild grass species widely distributed in arid and semi-arid regions, known for its remarkable tolerance to environmental stresses such as salinity, drought, and nitrogen deficiency. Despite its ecological importance, its phytochemical composition remains largely unexplored. To date, only one study has investigated the chemical constituents of the aerial parts of T. teneriffae, revealing its potential as a source of phenolic acids, with quinic acid and 4,5-di-O-caffeoylquinic acid as major components in alcoholic extracts. The methanolic fraction exhibited the highest antioxidant and antibacterial activities [11]. While the chemical constituents of the aerial parts of T. teneriffae have been recently reported by Yosra et al. [12] the secondary metabolite profile of its root system remains entirely undocumented. Crucially, the occurrence of naturally occurring fluorescent pigments within this species, along with their photophysical behavior, has yet to be explored. In the present study, seven fluorescent scaffolds were isolated from the root ethyl acetate extract: five 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one derivatives (1–5) and two 7H-benzo[de]anthracen-7-one derivatives (6–7). Their structures were elucidated using spectroscopic methods. The characteristic fluorescence of these molecules was used as a selection criterion, suggesting potential bioactivity. Furthermore, structure–activity relationships were explored with particular focus on the influence of methoxy and hydroxy groups, which are known to modulate pharmacological activity. The α-amylase inhibitory potential of these compounds was assessed, supported by molecular docking analyses to clarify their atomic-level interactions with the enzyme.

Overall, this study aims to isolate and characterize fluorescent xanthone-related compounds from T. teneriffae roots and to elucidate how structural variations, particularly methoxylation patterns, affect their biological efficacy. These findings provide new insights into the pharmacological potential of xanthone derivatives from the Poaceae family.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The roots of T. teneriffae were gathered in April 2023 from Bou-Hedma National Park in Sidi Bouzid, southern Tunisia (33°30′ N and 9°38′ E). The formal identification of the plant material was performed by Dr Imed Mezghani (Faculty of Sciences of Sfax, Tunisia). A voucher specimen (LCSN-TT-2023) has been deposited in the Herbarium of the Organic Chemistry Laboratory (Natural Products Unit), Faculty of Sciences of Sfax, University of Sfax, Tunisia. The aerial parts showed a leaf-to-stem ratio of about 1.5. This area has an arid Mediterranean climate marked by low and irregular rainfall, hot dry summers, and mild winters. Average annual precipitation is roughly 180 mm, while potential evapotranspiration exceeds 1000 mm. The mean yearly temperature is around 17 °C, with winter lows near 4 °C and summer highs above 36 °C. After collection, both aerial and root materials were dried in the shade for about 20 days, ground into powder, and stored at room temperature in dry, dark conditions until further use.

Extraction and isolation

Dried powdered roots of T. teneriffae (100 g) were extracted by maceration with ethyl acetate (EtOAc; 3 × 600 mL) at room temperature for 72 h. The combined extracts were filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C, yielding 3.0 g of crude EtOAc extract. The extract was stored at 4 °C in the dark until further use.

The EtOAc extract (3.0 g), which exhibited the highest α-amylase inhibitory activity, was subjected to silica gel column chromatography using a gradient of hexane–EtOAc (100:0 → 0:100), followed by elution with methanol (MeOH). Based on TLC profiling, 18 fractions (Frs. 1–18) were collected.

Fractions 4, 5, 6 and 7 (combined, 200 mg) were purified by preparative TLC using hexane–dichloromethane (40:60) to yield five compounds (1–5). Fractions 9 and 10 (combined, 600 mg) were further chromatographed over silica gel using a hexane–dichloromethane gradient (100:0 → 0:100), followed by MeOH washing, affording two additional compounds (6–7).

Structure elucidation

The chemical structures of the five new natural derivatives (1, 3, 4, 5, and 7) and two known compounds (2 and 6) were characterized using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) and infrared (IR) spectroscopy. Their structures were further elucidated using 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C NMR, HSQC, H–H COSY, and HMBC), providing detailed information on the molecular formula, functional groups, and proton and carbon environments of the compounds.

α-Amylase inhibitory activity assay

The α-amylase inhibitory activity was evaluated following the method of Wang et al. [13]. In brief, the reaction mixture contained 50 μL of sample solution or reference drug (Acarbose) at different concentrations, 500 μL of 1% (w/v) starch solution, 400 μL of 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.6), and 50 μL of α-amylase solution (5 U/mL). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 3 mL of 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent was added, and the mixture was heated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min, then cooled and diluted with 20 mL of distilled water. The absorbance was measured at 550 nm, and the residual α-amylase activity (R, %) was calculated using the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \left( \% \right)R = \left( {\frac{Asamples}{{Acontrol}}} \right) \times 100 \% $$\end{document}where Acontrol is the absorbance of the control and Asamples is the absorbance of the test sample at different concentrations.

Molecular docking

The α-amylase structure (PDB ID: 1OSE, 2.30 Å) was prepared for molecular docking by removing non-essential molecules and adding polar hydrogens. The binding site of the native acarbose ligand was used to define a grid box with dimensions 80 × 56 × 46 Å (centered at X = 32.773, Y = 37.361, Z = 1.737). Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina [14] to calculate binding energies. The resulting binding poses were visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer to identify specific interactions between the ligands and the enzyme.

General experimental procedures

1D and 2D NMR analysis were acquired at room temperature on a Bruker Ascend 400 MHz (1H) and 100 MHz (13C) spectrometer using deuterated solvents (CDCl3, DMSO-d6). Chemical shifts are given in ppm: parts per million. IR spectra were determined on a PerkinElmer spectrum 100 FT-IR spectrometer in a diamond disk. Silica gel (70–230 mesh, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for column chromatography (CC). High resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) was recorded on a Waters XevoG2-XS Qtof spectrometer (coupled with HPLC Acquity H-Class) for ESI. Absorbance was acquired in spectrophotometer Thermo Fisher Scientific Spectronic™ 200E. Organic solvents were distilled before use.

Spectroscopic measurements

Helios Omega ThermoScientific spectrophotometer was used to record steady-state absorption spectra and Perkin Elmer LS55 spectrofluorometer to measure steady-state emission spectra. We carried out spectroscopic measurements with a standard rectangular quartz cell (10 × 10 mm, 3.5 mL). The emission spectrum was recorded using the excitation wavelength of the absorption maximum (emission/excitation slit = 1 nm).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8). All experiments were carried out in triplicate and included at least three biological replicates. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

One-dimensional NMR data provided initial insights, while two-dimensional NMR experiments (COSY, HSQC, HMBC) were crucial for elucidating proton–carbon correlations and establishing full molecular connectivity. Characterization of 11-hydroxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one (compound 1)

Compound 1 was obtained as a yellow powder (40.7 mg, 20.35% yield). High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) showed a pseudo-molecular ion peak [M + H]+ at m/z 263.0729, calculated for 263.0708 (Fig. S1), consistent with the molecular formula C17H11O3. The IR spectrum showed characteristic absorption bands at 3228 cm−1 (hydroxyl group), 1640 cm−1 (conjugated carbonyl), 1611 cm−1 (aromatic C=C), and 1298 cm−1 (C–O stretching) (Fig. S2).

1H NMR analysis (400 MHz, CDCl3) (Fig. S3a) revealed a singlet at δH 13.29 ppm, attributed to a hydroxyl proton involved in intramolecular hydrogen bonding with the adjacent carbonyl (C-12, δC 184.19 in 13C NMR) [15]. Combined 13C NMR and HSQC analyses (Fig. S3b, c) reveal nine aromatic methine carbons and seven non-protonated aromatic carbons, including an oxygenated carbon at δC 161.89 (C-11), which was deshielded due to the electron-withdrawing effect of the hydroxyl group [16]. Notably, a characteristic down-field singlet was observed at δH 9.97 ppm, assigned to the H-1 proton. This significant deshielding is attributed to the bay-region effect, where H-1 experiences strong magnetic anisotropy and steric compression due to its spatial proximity to the C-12 carbonyl group. This assignment is in excellent agreement with the literature value reported for the related 9,11-dihydroxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one (δH 9.91 ppm) [17].

1H–1H COSY analysis (Fig. S3d) revealed distinct correlations between H-9 (δH 7.62 ppm) and the adjacent protons H-8 (δH 7.02 ppm) and H-10 (δH 6.88 ppm), indicating a tri-substituted aromatic ring (Fig. 3). HMBC correlations (Fig. S3e) from H-10 (δH 6.88) to C-11 (δC 161.89), C-11a (δC 110.39), and C-8 (δC 106.5) confirmed the fusion of rings A and B via positions 7a and 11a. Ortho coupling between H-6 (δH 7.56) and H-5 (δH 8.18) with J = 9.2 Hz supported their assignment on a 1,2-disubstituted aromatic ring. Additional HMBC correlations between H-5 and the oxygenated aromatic carbon at C-6a (δC 157.91), as well as between H-6 and C-12a (δC 113.39), unambiguously establish the fusion of ring C with the γ-lactone ring B at positions 6a and 12a. Finally, the protons attributed to ring D exhibited mutual couplings through HMBC interactions (Fig. 3) from H-5 (δH 8.18, ring C) and H-4 (δH 7.94, ring D) to the quaternary carbons C-4b (δC 130.15) and C-4a (δC 130.71), respectively. This confirms the fusion of rings C and D at these positions.Fig. 3The main HMBC and H–H COSY correlations observed in compound 1

The main HMBC and H–H COSY correlations observed in compound 1

These HMBC and COSY correlations unequivocally confirm the tetracyclic framework of compound 1. It is important to note that while its core is similar to the synthetic 9,11-dihydroxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one [17] (Table S1), our natural compound is clearly distinguished by the presence of a single hydroxyl group at C-11. This is confirmed by the tri-substituted pattern of ring A (H8-H9-H10) and the absence of an oxygenated quaternary carbon at C-9. Consequently, compound 1 is reported here as a new natural derivative of the 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one series, characterized for the first time by comprehensive 2D NMR analysis.

Characterization of 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one (compound 2)

Compound 2 was obtained as a yellow powder (21.7 mg, 10.85% yield). HRESIMS showed a pseudo-molecular ion peak [M + H]+ at m/z 247.0755 (calculated for 247.0759) (Fig. S4), consistent with the molecular formula C17H11O2. The IR spectrum exhibited characteristic bands at 1665 cm−1 (conjugated carbonyl C=O), 1625 cm−1 (aromatic C=C stretching), and 1235 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching of the xanthone ring), confirming the structural features of the compound.

Comparison of the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 1 (Fig. S5a) and 2 revealed the absence of the hydroxyl signal in the latter, replaced by a supplementary aromatic proton (H-11) at 8.48 ppm. In compound 1, the OH group forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the carbonyl, causing a significant deshielding of C-12 (δ ~ 184–185 ppm). In contrast, the carbonyl signal in compound 2 appears at δ ~ 178 ppm (Table 1), confirming the absence of chelation. This approximately 7 ppm upfield shift is a diagnostic marker for the loss of the peri-hydroxyl group [15].

While compound 2 has been previously reported as a synthetic product [18], and was recently identified in the aerial parts of T. teneriffae by Yosra et al. [12] (Table S1) this study reports its isolation from the root system for the first time. In our investigation, compound 2 serves as the unsubstituted reference scaffold, allowing for a precise evaluation of how hydroxy and methoxy groups modulate the photophysical and inhibitory properties in derivatives 1 and 3–5. The structure was fully confirmed by 2D NMR, showing the complete tetracyclic aromatic system without further substitution.

Characterization of 5,6-dimethoxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one (compound 3)

Compound 3 was obtained as a yellow powder (12.7 mg, 6.35% yield). HRESIMS provided a pseudo-molecular ion peak [M + H]+ at m/z 307.0977 (calculated for 307.0971) (Fig. S6), corresponding to the molecular formula C19H15O4. The IR spectrum (Fig. S7) confirmed the presence of the methoxy groups with a characteristic band at 2850 cm−1.

The combined 1H NMR and HSQC data of compound 3 (Fig. S8a, c), indicated the presence of two methoxy groups, resonating at δH 4.05/δC 61.56 and δH 4.06/δC 56.53. The specific positions of these groups were unequivocally determined by HMBC correlations (Fig. 4). A key correlation was observed between the methoxy protons at HMBC correlations (Fig. 4) were observed between the methoxy proton signal at δH 4.05 and the quaternary carbon at δC 148.86 (C-5) (Fig. S8e). Similarly, the protons at δH 4.06 correlated with the carbon at δC 143.21 (C-6). These assignments were further supported by HMBC cross-peaks from H-4 (δH 8.56) to C-6 and from H-3 (δH 7.55) to C-4a (δC 126.0), confirming the 5,6-dimethoxy substitution pattern on ring C. While an 11-methoxy isomer has been previously reported as a synthetic analogue [19], our study is the first to report this 5,6-dimethoxy substitution in nature. Consequently, compound 3 is identified as a new natural product, named 5,6-dimethoxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one. Characterization of 5-methoxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one (compound 4)

Compound 4 was obtained as a yellow powder (28.3 mg, 14.15% yield). HRESIMS exhibited a pseudo-molecular ion peak [M + H]+ at m/z 277.0869 (Fig. S9), calculated for 277.0865, consistent with the molecular formula C18H13O3. The IR spectrum (Fig. S7) exhibited characteristic bands at 1646 cm−1 (conjugated C=O), 1610 cm−1, 1515 (aromatic C=C), and 1277 cm−1 (C–O stretching).

The 1H NMR and HSQC data of compound 4 (Fig. S10a, c) revealed the presence of a methoxy group resonating at δH 4.13/δC 56.17 and an isolated aromatic proton δH 6.81/δC 95.60. In the HMBC spectrum (Fig. 5), the methoxy protons at δH 4.13 exhibited a 3J correlation with the carbon at C-5 (δC 161.44) (Fig. S10e), indicating substitution at this position. Additionally, the proton H-6 (δH 6.81) showed long-range correlations with two olefinic carbons at δC 109.2 (C-12a) and at δC 123.60 (C-4a) (Fig. 5). Altogether, the data strongly support the localization of both the methoxy group and the isolated aromatic proton on the C-ring, establishing the structure of compound 4 as a novel natural derivative, named 5-methoxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one.Fig. 5The main HMBC correlations observed in compound 4

Characterization of 4-methoxybenzo[a]xanthen-12-one (compound 5)

Compound 5 was obtained as a yellow powder (5 mg, 2.50% yield). HRESIMS showed a pseudo-molecular ion peak [M + H]+ at m/z 277.0869 (calculated for 277.0865) (Fig. S9), corresponding to the molecular formula C18H13O3. The IR spectrum of compound 5 was similar to that of compounds 3 and 4 (Fig. S7), reflecting the presence of the same 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one core and methoxy substituent.

The 1H NMR and HSQC data for compound 5 (Fig. S11a, c) indicate the presence of a methoxy group resonating at δH 4.07/δC 55.70. In the 1H–1H COSY spectrum (Fig. S11d), proton H-2 (δH 7.72 ppm) showed cross-peaks with H-1 (δH 9.70 ppm) and H-3 (δH 7.02 ppm), consistent with a tri-substituted aromatic ring D. Key HMBC correlations (Fig. 6) from H-2 (δH 7.72) to the quaternary carbons C-4 (δC 155.70) and C-4b (δC 132.50) (Fig. S11e), further support the assignment of the methoxy group at position C-4 of ring D. Based on this comprehensive spectroscopic evidence (1H, 13C, COSY, HSQC, and HMBC), compound 5 was identified as 4-methoxy-12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one. Characterization of 6-hydroxy-3-methoxy-7H-benzo[de]anthracen-7-one (compound 6)

After establishing the core, the positions of the substituents were determined through further HMBC analysis. Characterization of 6-methoxy-7H-benzo[de]anthracen-7-one (compound 7)

Compound 7 was obtained as a yellow crystalline solid (23 mg, 11.5% yield). Its IR spectrum was similar to that of compound 6, except for the absence of the broad –OH band around 3178 cm−1, while the methoxy C–O stretch at 2849 cm−1 (Fig. S14), the carbonyl stretches, and the aromatic bands were retained.

The 13C NMR (100 MHz, DMSO-d₆) spectrum exhibited seventeen distinct carbon resonances, despite the molecular formula C18H12O2, as determined by HRESIMS, which showed a pseudo-molecular ion peak at m/z 261.05623 [M + H]+ (calculated for C18H13O2, 261.0546) (Fig. S15). Although the formula contains eighteen carbons, the 13C NMR spectrum (Fig. S16b) displayed seventeen resonances, indicating a signal overlap at δC 118.28 and 129.26 due to magnetic equivalence within the aromatic system.

Comparison of the 1H and 13C NMR spectra of compounds 6 and 7 (Table 2; Table S2) revealed the absence of the characteristic singlet corresponding to the chelated hydroxyl proton near δ 10 ppm in compound 7.

Notably, a significant shielding of the C-6 carbon was observed in compound 7 (δC 131.49 ppm) compared to the hydroxylated analog 6 (δC 156.20 ppm). This upfield shift is attributed to the replacement of the hydroxyl group by a methoxy group, which suppresses the strong intramolecular hydrogen bond with the neighboring carbonyl oxygen. Furthermore, the electron-donating mesomeric effect (+M) of the methoxy group increases the electron density at the ipso (C-6), ortho, and para positions, leading to the observed shielding of the C-6 nucleus.

The 1H–1H COSY correlations (Fig. S16d) showed that proton H-2 (δH 7.61 ppm) was coupled with H-1 (δH 8.31 ppm) and H-3 (δH 7.73 ppm), indicating a tri-substituted aromatic ring (Fig. 8). Molecular docking and structure–activity relationship (SAR)

The experimental effects of the bioactive compounds can be interpreted based on their structural features through molecular docking and structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies. Herein, an in silico investigation was conducted to characterize the interaction profile of the tested compounds with the active site of α-amylase. The crystal structure of porcine pancreatic α-amylase (PPA) (PDB ID: 1OSE) represents one of the crucial enzymes involved in carbohydrate hydrolysis. The catalytic reaction is mediated by three essential residues: Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300. This active site is organized into several subsites, designated from −1 to + n, where the negative and positive numbers correspond to the binding positions of the substrate residues relative to the cleavage [24].

In our study, seven isolated metabolites shared a common xanthone skeleton but differed in their functional groups, leading to variations in their inhibitory activity against the α-amylase enzyme. Therefore, it was essential to investigate the binding interactions between these analogues and the enzyme’s active sites, with a focus on assessing the effect of their substituents. The binding affinities of these metabolites within the active sites of the target enzyme were analyzed. The metabolites compounds 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 exhibited comparable affinities toward α-amylase, with binding energy values of −9.5, −9.5, −8.4, −8.2, −9.2, −8.7 and −9.1 kcal/mol, respectively. Among the tested metabolites, two of them (compounds 6 and 7) feature a fused ring D with rings A and B. Indeed, compound 7 is mono-substituted, featuring a single methoxy group at ring A. In contrast, compound 6 is di-substituted and contains hydroxy group at ring A and methoxy group at ring D. The complex compound 7-alpha amylase was stabilized by hydrophobic interactions with Trp 59 and Val 163 and hydrogen interaction with Gln 63 (Fig. 11B). In the compound 6-alpha amylase complex (Fig. 11A), the hydroxyl group at ring A of compound 6 forms a hydrogen interaction with Asp197 a catalytic residue. This compound forms a π–anion electrostatic interaction with the carboxylate group of Asp300. A van der Waals interaction was observed between this ligand and Gln63. The compound–α-amylase complex was also stabilized by several hydrophobic interactions, including π–σ, π–π stacked, π–π T-shaped, and π–alkyl interactions, involving residues Trp59, Tyr62, Leu162, Val163, Leu165, and Ala198.Fig. 11Visualization of enzyme–ligand interactions (2D): binding modes of compounds 6 (A) and 7 (B) with α-amylase (PDB code: 1OSE)

The interaction profiles of the remaining metabolites (compounds 1–5), which share a fused ring D with ring C, were also analyzed (Fig. 12).Fig. 12Visualization of enzyme–ligand interactions (2D): binding modes of compound 2 (A), compound 1 (B), compound 5 (C), compound 4 (D) and compound 3 (E) with α-amylase (PDB code: 1OSE)

Compound 2 (Fig. 12A), the unsubstituted metabolite, formed a hydrogen bond through its oxygenated aromatic carbon with His305. This ligand also exhibited hydrophobic interactions with Trp59 and Val163. Compound 1 (Fig. 12B) and compound 5 (Fig. 12C), bearing hydroxyl (–OH) and methoxy (–OCH3) substituents on rings A and D respectively, showed also moderate alpha-amylase inhibitory activity. Both maintained the same interaction profile as unsubstituted metabolite compound 2, particularly the hydrogen bond with His305 mediated through the carbonyl group on ring B.

In the other hand, the interaction profiles of compounds 4 (Fig. 12D) and 3 (Fig. 12E) were analyzed to understand the impact of their substituents on inhibitory activity. Compound 4, which carries a single methoxy group on ring C, exhibited stronger inhibition than compound 3, which contained two substituents on the same ring. Docking analysis revealed that this enhanced activity was primarily due to the formation of a strong hydrogen bond between the methoxy substituent and the catalytic residue Glu233, suggesting that substitution on ring C favors an optimal spatial orientation for enzyme inhibition. In addition, compound 4 forms van der Waals interaction with the catalytic residue Asp 197. The residue Tyr 151 formed hydrogen bond with this metabolite. Moreover, hydrophobic interactions were illustrated between compound 4 and Leu 162, Ala 198, His 201 and Ile 235. In contrast, compound 3 which contained two substituents on ring C, showed a markedly reduced activity in the in vitro study and failed to interact with the catalytic residues of α-amylase. This loss of activity could be attributed to steric hindrance introduced by the additional substituent, disrupting the compound’s ability to fit efficiently into the active site. In fact, the compound 3-alpha amylase complex was stabilized with hydrophobic interaction with Trp 59, Tyr 62, Leu 162 and Val 163. Hydrogen interaction with His 305 was also found in this complex.

Overall, these findings highlight the critical influence of substitution position and type on both binding affinity and inhibitory potency, offering a promising structural feature for further optimization of α-amylase inhibitors.

Conclusion

This work establishes Tricholaena teneriffae L. roots as a source of 12H-benzo[a]xanthen-12-one and 7H-benzo[de]anthracen-7-one derivatives with notable α-amylase inhibitory activity. Structure–activity analysis, supported by molecular docking, showed that hydroxyl and methoxy substituents play a key role in strengthening interactions with catalytic residues and improving inhibition. In addition, photophysical evaluation highlighted the impact of these substituents on the electronic properties of the dyes, particularly their absorption and emission profiles. Overall, the findings provide new insights into the structural features governing both the biological and optical behavior of this compound class and offer a useful basis for future development of antidiabetic and photofunctional molecules.
