Self-reported questionnaires to assess indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients: a scoping review
Authors: Dhanusha Punyadasa, Nicola J Adderley, Gavin Rudge, Prasad Nagakumar, Shamil Haroon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe indoor home environment plays a crucial role in determining the outcome of respiratory diseases, including asthma. Researchers, clinicians, and patients would benefit from self-reported questionnaires to assess indoor home environmental exposures that may impact on respiratory health.ObjectiveTo review self-reported instruments for assessing indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients and to characterise their content, development, and psychometric properties.DesignA scoping review was conducted with content assessment.MethodsA literature search was conducted in Embase and PubMed using the key words housing quality, questionnaire and asthma and their index terms, covering articles published in English between January 2000 to July 2023. Articles in which questionnaires or single item questions were used to assess indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients in middle- and high-income countries were included. We excluded articles in which the questionnaire required an interviewer or onsite observations and those conducted in low-income countries.ResultsWe screened 1584 articles to identify 44 studies containing self-reported questionnaires measuring indoor home environmental exposures. 36 studies (82%) were cross sectional, 35 (80%) had a sample size of greater than 1000 participants, and 29 (66%) were conducted in children. Most studies (86%, n = 38) had binary (yes/no) or multiple-choice responses. 25 studies (57%) included a recall period of 12 months. 32 studies (73%) had a response rate of greater than 50%. Dampness, biological exposures (e.g. mould), and second-hand tobacco smoke were the most assessed indoor home environmental exposures. Childhood asthma (54%, n = 24) and asthma symptoms (36%, n = 16) were the most examined asthma related outcomes. The exposure most associated with adverse asthma outcomes was exposure to damp (79%, n = 35). 13 studies (29%) had developed a self-reported instrument by adapting questions from previous studies and almost all instruments (n = 42 studies, 95%) had not been validated.ConclusionsThe scoping review did not identify a comprehensive, validated self-reported questionnaire for assessing indoor home environmental exposures in patients with asthma. There is need to develop and validate a robust but pragmatic self-reported instrument, incorporating the findings from this review.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20418-8.

Background

Asthma is one of the most common chronic respiratory conditions affecting all age groups.

Although there has been a decrease in mortality from asthma due to therapeutic advancements [1], it still imposes a significant burden on healthcare systems and society [2]. The prevalence of asthma ranges from 10 to 12% in the developed world [3]. It is one of the leading causes for acute unscheduled healthcare utilisation [4]. Asthma also ranks among the highest causes of disability adjusted life years (DALY) for children [5]. Better control of the disease would likely reduce the social and economic burden associated with the condition.

Environmental factors have a significant impact on asthma disease activity [6]. There is increasing recognition that the indoor home environment plays a vital role in asthma development, persistence and disease severity. A cross-sectional study conducted among 24,784 participants from Taiwan explored the relative contribution of indoor environmental exposures to adulthood asthma. They found a significant association (OR 1.8, 95%CI 1.08–3.23) between home exposure and new-onset asthma in adults [7]. Indoor home environmental exposures that may contribute to poor asthma outcomes include dampness, biological exposures from pests, pets and mould, house dust mites, indoor respiratory irritants from combustion and volatile compounds, second-hand tobacco smoke, indoor air quality, temperature, overcrowding, and adequacy of ventilation [8–10]. Recent evidence has highlighted the importance of the indoor home environment of asthma patients for achieving better disease control [11, 12]. However indoor home environments and sources of domestic air pollutants vary considerably across geographical areas due to differences in socioeconomic, cultural and lifestyle factors. Thus, there is a need to objectively evaluate indoor home environments to inform recommendations for remedial measures [13].

Healthcare providers’ assessments of indoor home environmental exposures of their asthma patients are particularly important for managing the disease but are seldom performed. Such assessments are also important for informing public health measures and health policies to improve asthma outcomes. Evaluating indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients during clinical assessments requires access to practical and validated instruments. Self-reported questionnaires are an efficient approach for assessing these exposures because they can be administered in clinical practice with minimum cost, human resources, and disruptions to workflow. Several relevant self-reported questionnaires and single item questions have been developed for epidemiological studies of asthma but have not been comprehensively reviewed. We therefore conducted this review to help clinicians and researchers identify which may be of use in clinical practice and research, and to determine whether there is a need to develop and validate new self-reported questionnaires for assessing indoor home environmental exposures for asthma research and care.

Methods

We conducted a scoping literature review to identify, evaluate and map self-reported measures of indoor home environmental exposures in patients with asthma or asthma symptoms. Reporting of this scoping review was guided by the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews. Our search focused on self-reported questionnaires or single item questions that assessed indoor home environmental exposures. We use the term “instrument” to refer to self-reported questionnaires or questions selected for the review.

Study selection criteria

Studies were included if their outcome of interest comprised asthma or asthma symptoms and assessed indoor home environmental exposures using self-reported questionnaires. Studies that either exclusively measured indoor home environmental exposures or featured them as a main questionnaire item were considered eligible. Only studies published in peer-reviewed journals in English between January 2000 to July 2023 were included.

Studies were excluded if their indoor home environmental exposure assessment instruments required an interviewer or on-site observations by external assessors, or the study settings were not from high or middle-income countries. Instruments presented only in abstracts and conference proceedings were also excluded.

Search strategy

The initial search strategy was applied to Embase to confirm the relevance of the results and refine the search strategy. Reference lists from several relevant studies were also searched manually to expand the search. Subject headings were adapted for each database. Searches were carried out by a single investigator on 11th August 2023 searching for studies published between 1st January 2000 and 30th July 2023. We searched Embase and PubMed using a variety of Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and free text keywords to capture the concepts of subject headings more precisely. The detailed electronic search strategy for each database is presented in the supplementary Table 1. Our review did not include a search of grey literature.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened, and the full text of potentially eligible studies was then assessed according to the eligibility criteria by a single investigator. Any uncertainties in study selection were resolved through discussion with all authors.

Once duplicates had been removed, 1584 articles were screened using their titles and abstracts. We first screened for articles that described capturing data on indoor home environmental exposures among people with asthma or asthma symptoms. Once those studies had been identified, we checked whether their data collection approach used self-reported questionnaires.

Out of the 162 articles retained for full text evaluation, 118 articles were excluded due to the following reasons: studies did not assess indoor home environmental exposures (n = 15), indoor home environmental exposures were not self-reported (n = 79), studies did not specify the intended method of administering indoor home environmental exposure assessment instruments (n = 11), study outcomes did not include asthma or asthma symptoms (n = 4), or the study setting was not based in a middle or high income country (n = 9). 44 articles describing self-reported indoor home environmental exposure assessment instruments were finally included (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out for each study using a standardised data extraction form. The data extracted from each study included study author, publication year, study design, country, study population, sample size, indoor home environmental exposures evaluated, asthma related outcomes examined, and main findings of each study in relation to indoor home environmental exposure and asthma outcomes. Data were also extracted for all identified self-reported instruments on the method used to evaluate indoor home environmental exposures, the method of obtaining self-reported responses, recall period used, response rate, and questionnaire development and validation methods.

Content assessment

A quantitative content assessment was performed based on the principal domains of indoor home environmental exposures that have evidence-based associations with asthma [14]. These included measures to improve indoor air quality (e.g. use of air conditioner/air purifier/air humidifier/dehumidifier), environmental (second-hand) tobacco smoke, indoor respiratory irritants from volatile organic compounds, indoor respiratory irritants from end products of indoor combustion, biological exposures, indoor ventilation, presence of indoor heating, indoor temperature, dampness, indoor cleaning frequency, housing type and quality, and presence of overcrowding. We assessed and summarised whether each of the self-reported instruments covered these domains. The reported associations between self-reported indoor home environmental exposures and asthma outcomes were also recorded.

Results

The scoping review identified 44 articles reporting self-reported instruments measuring indoor home environmental exposures in relation to asthma outcomes (Supplementary Table 2).

Dampness (n = 41 studies, 93%), biological exposures (n = 32, 73%), and environmental tobacco smoke (n = 31, 70%) were the most assessed indoor home environmental exposures, while indoor temperature was the least assessed (n = 1, 2%; Supplementary Table 3).

Asthma related outcomes

Asthma related outcomes examined by studies included presence of childhood asthma, adult asthma, asthma symptoms, asthma phenotypes, exacerbations of asthma, asthma severity, and asthma control. The presence of childhood asthma (54%, n = 24) was the most common asthma related outcome examined, while exacerbations of asthma (2%, n = 1) and asthma control (2%, n = 1) were the least examined outcomes (Table 1).

Indoor home environmental exposures associated with asthma outcomes

Out of the 12 indoor home environmental exposures evaluated among the included studies, 9 exposures (dampness, biological exposures, environmental tobacco smoke, indoor combustion byproducts, indoor volatile compounds, house type and quality, indoor air quality appliances, ventilation, and presence of indoor heating) were found to be significantly associated with asthma related outcomes (Table 2). Indoor home environmental exposures frequently associated with adverse asthma outcomes were dampness (n = 35 studies, 79%), biological exposures (n = 13, 29%) and environmental tobacco smoke (n = 11, 25%). Dampness and environmental tobacco smoke were found to have the largest associations with adverse asthma outcomes. By contrast, the presence of indoor heating was found to be associated with improved asthma outcomes. Studies that examined biological exposures and indoor air quality improvement methods had mixed findings (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2).

Characteristics of self-reported instruments

Table 3 summarises the characteristics of the self-reported questionnaires and question items included in the review. The most common method for capturing self-reported data on indoor home environmental exposures were the use of binary response (yes/no) questions and multiple-choice questions (MCQs) (n = 16, 36%). 15 studies (34%) had used only binary response questions and six (14%) had used only MCQs. Seven studies (16%) had used a combination of scales/scoring systems along with yes/no questions and MCQs. Commonly used methods for obtain self-reported responses were by posting questionnaires to participants, by use of tablet computers at research setting, and by sending questionnaires home via school children to be completed by their parents (Supplementary Table 4). The most frequently reported recall period was the past 12 months (57%, n = 25). However, six studies (14%) had used recall periods in excess of a year. 32 studies (73%) reported response rates greater than 50% while eight studies (18%) did not report the response rate.

Development and validation of self-reported instruments

13 studies (29%) had developed a self-reported instrument by adapting questions from previous studies (Table 3). 8 studies (18%) had used questions derived from the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) questionnaire [15, 16]. The European Community Respiratory Health Survey questionnaire and Global Asthma Network questionnaire were the other sources of questions used to develop self-reported instruments. However, 21 studies (48%) did not report the method used to develop their self-reported instruments.

Out of the 44 studies, 42 (95%) did not report the method of validation of their self-reported instrument. A cross-sectional study conducted by Ahlroth Pind C et al. (2017) reported that their self-reported questions on assessing patient-reported signs of dampness had been previously validated, with significant agreement between observed and self-reported signs of building dampness: water damage (Cohen’s Kappa 0.4: p = 0.001), visible mould (Cohen’s Kappa 0.36; p = 0.004), and at least one sign of building dampness (Cohen’s Kappa 0.3; P = 0.007). Considering the observed signs of dampness as gold standard, they have reported 75% sensitivity and 81% specificity for self-reported questions on signs of water damage. For self-reported questions on visible mould growth, sensitivity and specificity were 50% and 91% respectively [17]. A self-reported instrument used by Northrigge J et al. (2010) was pilot tested among native speakers prior to the study to ensure clarity and accuracy (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Main findings

A number of self-reported instruments have been developed in epidemiological studies across a range of countries to assess indoor home environmental exposures in children and adults with asthma. These capture data on a variety of factors such as indoor exposure to damp, biological exposures, and environmental tobacco smoke, which are associated with adverse asthma outcomes. These instruments typically included a combination of multiple choice and binary response questions, some of which were adapted from large epidemiological studies, with recall periods mostly within the previous 12 months. Many studies reported relatively high response rates exceeding 50%.

However, our review also found considerable variation in these self-reported instruments, with little uniformity among the included questions. Most instruments also lacked adequate coverage of key indoor home environmental exposures relevant to respiratory health. Critically, most studies did not describe the methods for instrument development or validation, highlighting a need for transparently reported and validated instruments to be developed.

Relationship to other studies

According to our review, the most frequently assessed self-reported indoor home environmental exposure was exposure to damp and 79% of the included studies reported a significant association with adverse asthma outcomes. This is in line with strong evidence on the association between objectively measured indoor dampness and adverse respiratory outcomes [18, 19]. However, there was no uniformity in the method of evaluating self-reported dampness. Some studies had used scoring systems to evaluate self-reported dampness [20, 21] while others had assessed this using binary response questions [22, 23].

There is also evidence of a causal relationship between biological exposures like mould and adverse asthma outcomes [9]. In our review, biological exposures were evaluated by 71% of the studies and 29% reported that it was associated with adverse asthma outcomes. However, two studies reported exposure to pets had a protective effect on asthma outcomes, which aligns with previous research suggesting higher levels of indoor pet or pest allergens in early life are associated with a lower risk of asthma [24]. This suggests that biological exposures need to be carefully categorised into those that may be protective (e.g. exposure to pets in early life) and those that may be harmful (e.g. exposure to mould) to asthma outcomes.

The association between household environmental tobacco smoke and adverse asthma outcomes is well established [25]. In our review, environmental tobacco smoke was assessed in 69% of studies but only 24% reported an association with adverse asthma outcomes. This suggests that the self-reported measures of indoor second-hand smoke identified in our review may not adequately capture this exposure and other validated self-reported measures are needed.

Previous literature has shown indoor ventilation to be a significant factor in determining asthma outcomes and good ventilation has been identified as an important measure for improving the indoor environment for people with asthma [9]. Poor ventilation increases the exposure to indoor respiratory irritants by increasing their concentration and the length of exposure time within the household. Previous studies also reported that poor domestic ventilation is associated with increased risk of asthma exacerbations in children [26]. However domestic ventilation was not included in 80% of the self-reported instruments included in our review.

Only 34% of the included studies had assessed the use of indoor air quality appliances such as air purifiers in relation to asthma outcomes and found mixed results in their association. There is growing evidence that poor indoor air quality is a major contributor to respiratory morbidity and indoor air quality appliances have the potential to improve these health outcomes [27]. However, further studies are required to elucidate the impact of these indoor air quality improvement measures on asthma outcomes.

Indoor volatile compounds, such as chemicals from cleaning products, are emerging as a new class of indoor home environmental risk factor for adverse respiratory effects, particularly in high income countries [28, 29]. In our review, 34% of the included studies had evaluated these indoor home environmental exposures in relation to asthma outcomes and 14% reported a significant association between indoor volatile compounds and asthma outcomes.

Even though adverse health effects due to combustion of solid fuel for household cooking and heating are commonly reported in studies of rural households in developing countries [29], 59% of studies in our review, which were based in middle- and high-income countries, also evaluated the impact of indoor combustion byproducts on asthma outcomes. However, only 14% of studies in our review demonstrated a significant association between indoor combustion byproducts and asthma outcomes.

Indoor temperature had been evaluated by only one study in the present review, even though it is likely to be associated with asthma-related morbidity [27, 30]. Results from previous studies have shown that extreme hot and cold temperatures both increase the incidence of asthma attacks, albeit through different mechanisms [31]. Therefore, assessment of indoor temperature by self-reported measures may still be important in evaluating indoor home environmental exposures relevant to asthma outcomes, but further research is needed to investigate how best to capture these data.

Adverse indoor home environmental exposures and poverty are interrelated, and poverty is often a confounding factor in most health and environmental studies [9]. However, only a few studies included in our review had captured data on poverty and its impact on indoor home environmental conditions [32] and asthma outcomes [33, 34]. Future research should ensure that measures of poverty are captured and potentially incorporated into measures of indoor home environmental exposures to ensure this important risk factor is adequately accounted for.

A lack of information on the reliability and validity of most self-reported instruments included in this review suggests a need for detailed psychometric testing of self-reported instruments. Engman et al. (2007) validated a self-reported instrument assessing indoor home environmental exposures to dampness, ventilation and household type and quality, by comparing self-reported responses with external inspections [35]. Cohen’s kappa was used to measure the agreement between inspectors’ observations and self-reports. The self-reported instrument was found to be reliable in evaluating technical parameters like the type of house and ventilation system, particularly for single family houses. It was less reliable at evaluating indications of dampness and mould. However, the time lapse of 18–24 months between administration of the self-reported instrument and the inspections could have influenced the findings. Suggested ways of improving the validity of such instruments were the inclusion of simple drawings, pictures or examples to visually demonstrate the exposures being assessed [35].

Strengths and limitations

We included a large number of studies that used self-reported instruments to assess indoor home environmental exposures in people with asthma, published over the last two decades, and provided insights on their development, content and association with asthma outcomes.

However, our review has a number of limitations. We only included studies published in English and did not explore studies published in other languages. We also only included studies from high- and middle-income countries and 53% were from China, Sweden, the USA, and Australia. Our findings are therefore not generalisable to low-income countries. Asthma is a heterogenous disease, and the impact of the indoor home environment is influenced by age, geography, social, cultural and economic factors. It is therefore challenging to implement self-reported measures universally, highlighting the need to have locally adapted instruments that are contextually appropriate for the populations under investigation.

We acknowledge that our content assessment was quantitative, and further work is needed to provide a more detailed qualitative assessment of existing instruments.

82% of the studies evaluating indoor home environmental exposures were cross sectional studies. Thus, the reported associations between self-reported indoor home environmental exposures and asthma outcomes need to be interpreted with caution and should be validated against longitudinal studies.

We observed a lack of consensus in definitions of indoor home environmental exposures assessed by self-reported instruments which made comparisons between instruments challenging. Furthermore, many of the instruments included in our review used multiple choice and binary response questions to evaluate indoor home environmental exposures which may have led to measurement or misclassification bias. A few self-reported instruments had attempted to minimise this by incorporating scales or scoring systems to assess exposures like dampness [20, 21], ventilation and indoor cleaning [36]. Appropriately developed scales and weighted scoring systems may improve the measurement properties of such instruments and should be considered in further studies.

Implications for practice, policy and research

The findings of our review have implications for future research evaluating the prevalence and relationship between indoor home environmental exposures and asthma outcomes. There is a need for consensus on the definitions of indoor home environmental exposures affecting asthma outcomes and the methods for capturing these data for clinical practice and research using self-reported instruments. This could enable researchers and healthcare providers to develop a more robust understanding of the burden of adverse indoor home environmental exposures [13], and their relationship to poor asthma outcomes.

Our review highlights the need for a standardised and validated self-reported instrument that adequately covers the breadth of relevant indoor home environmental exposures that affect asthma outcomes. Greater clarity and consistency are also required in the method of administration, scoring and interpretation of the findings of such instruments. Use of a validated self-reported questionnaire in routine clinical practice to assess indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients could guide targeted management and social interventions [37]. This could in turn enable policymakers to invest in evidence-based interventions for the benefit of asthma patients.

62% of self-reported instruments in our review had used a recall period of 12 months or less in assessing indoor home environmental exposures. However, 16% of the included instruments had a recall period of more than 12 months which increases the risk of recall bias. Future development of self-reported instruments for assessing indoor home environmental exposures should carefully consider the impact of recall bias when determining recall periods. 73% of the instruments had a response rate of greater than 50%, indicating the potential feasibility of implementing similar instruments successfully in routine asthma care.

Two thirds of the selected studies were conducted among children and childhood asthma was the outcome of interest for 54% of the included studies. Children, due to their size, are at closer proximity to the ground than adults and spend considerable time in the indoor home environment, resulting in a higher risk of exposure to indoor pollutants [38, 39]. Further research is needed to understand whether separate self-reported instruments are required for children and adults to accurately assess relevant indoor home environmental exposures.

Conclusion

There is considerable variation in existing self-reported instruments for measuring indoor home environmental exposures in asthma patients and a need for high quality validated measures that incorporate the findings of this review.
