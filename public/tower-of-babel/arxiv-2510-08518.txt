Randomized truncation of quantum states
Aram W. Harrow, Angus Lowe, Freek Witteveen

Abstract
A fundamental task in quantum information is to approximate a pure quantum state in terms of sparse states or, for a bipartite system, states of bounded Schmidt rank. The optimal deterministic approximation in each case is straightforward, and maximizes the fidelity: keep the largest entries or singular values. On the other hand, random mixtures of sparse states can achieve quadratically improved trace distances, and yield nontrivial bounds on other distance measures like the robustness. In this work, we give efficient algorithms for finding mixtures of sparse states that optimally approximate a given pure state in either trace distance or robustness. These algorithms also yield descriptions of efficiently samplable ensembles of sparse, or less-entangled, states that correspond to these optimal mixed approximations. This can be used for the truncation step of algorithms for matrix product states, improving their accuracy while using no extra memory, and we demonstrate this improvement numerically. Our proofs use basic facts about convex optimization and zero-sum games, as well as rigorous guarantees for computing maximum-entropy distributions.



1
Introduction

In this paper we study approximations of pure quantum states by randomly drawn states which are sparse in the standard basis, or have low Schmidt rank. Given a
d
-dimensional vector
v
and a sparsity parameter
k\leq d
, keeping just the
k
largest entries yields the maximum inner product with
v
among all possible
k
-sparse vectors; that is, vectors having at most
k
nonzero entries. Similarly, it is well known that the optimal low-rank approximation to a matrix, as measured by any unitarily invariant norm, is obtained by truncating (keeping just a few of) its singular values. Another instance of this problem arises when simulating quantum many-body systems using tensor networks, where multipartite states are represented using states that have low Schmidt rank with respect to several partitions of the subsystems.

In the setting of quantum information, the unit vector
v
corresponds to a pure quantum state
\rho=vv^{\dagger}
. An approximation to
\rho
can once again be obtained by keeping the top
k
entries of
v
, normalizing, and taking the quantum state corresponding to this new unit vector. In other words, one may deterministically truncate to a
k
-sparse pure state. This solution, however obvious, is not always optimal among those quantum states whose operational meaning is the preparation of a
k
-sparse pure state, potentially at random. We refer to this latter, more general class of approximation methods as
randomized truncation
.

When error is measured in a way that corresponds to a single fixed test, such as fidelity or expected energy, then randomness does not help. However, when error is measured by examining
the maximum of many linear tests – as in the case of trace distance error – then randomness can offer a significant improvement. In particular, mixtures of
k
-sparse states can achieve better trace distances compared to what is possible using pure approximations, by as much as a quadratic factor. If one considers instead the robustness (or equivalently max relative entropy) as a distance measure, there is an even stronger justification for considering randomness, since then only mixed states can achieve any nontrivial approximation.

1.1
Simple example

To make the benefits of mixed approximations concrete, we will describe a simple but instructive example.
Let
v=(\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},\sqrt{\varepsilon},\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{\top}

and suppose we want to approximate this by a 2-sparse state.
The best approximation in fidelity is given by either

w_{1}\propto(\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},\sqrt{\varepsilon},0)^{\top}
or
w_{2}\propto(\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},0,\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{\top}
,
both giving a fidelity
F(v,w_{i})=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
, and hence a trace distance of
T(v,w_{i})=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
If we look at the density matrix

\displaystyle vv^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}1-2\varepsilon&\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}\\
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&\varepsilon&\varepsilon\\
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&\varepsilon&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

(1)

then we see that when truncating to one of the
w_{i}
, the dominant error in trace distance comes from the off-diagonal terms on the order of
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
We can mitigate this by taking a uniform mixture of states
\tilde{w}_{1}\sim(\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},2\sqrt{\varepsilon},0)^{\top}
and
\tilde{w}_{2}\sim(\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},0,2\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{\top}
.
This way, we see that the mixed state

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{w}_{1}\tilde{w}_{1}^{\top}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{w}_{2}\tilde{w}_{2}^{\top}=\frac{1}{{1+2\varepsilon}}\begin{pmatrix}1-2\varepsilon&\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}\\
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&2\varepsilon&0\\
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}&0&2\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

(2)

only differs from
v
in trace distance to order
\varepsilon
instead of
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
Intuitively, the scarce resource here is the superposition between the 1st entry and the 2nd and 3rd entries, and we can boost this by increasing the amplitude of the 2nd and 3rd entries. This introduces inaccuracies of order
\varepsilon
along the diagonals while fixing the error of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. The tradeoff between these two sources of error is also visible in the larger example illustrated in
Figure

1
.

The approximation in
Eq.

2
turns out to achieve the optimal robustness of
2\varepsilon
, meaning
vv^{\top}\preceq(1+2\varepsilon)\sigma
. In
Section

6
we will see more generally how to construct this sort of robustness-optimal ensemble.

1.2
Overview of results

Efficient algorithms.

This paper systematically studies the question of the optimal approximation of pure states by mixtures of
k
-sparse states. This is both a computational and a mathematical problem.
Our main result is a collection of efficient algorithms that can find these randomized approximations.

the optimal value of

;

2.

-sparse pure states from this distribution in time

These algorithms are given in

, building on sampling algorithms described in

. Specifically, the trace distance version of this statement follows from Theorems

, and

In

.

When randomized truncation is useful.

In
1.1
, it is important that our target states are pure states. The more general question of approximating mixed states with mixtures of
k
-sparse states, or even determining whether a given mixed state can be decomposed into
k
-sparse states, is
\NP

Section

3
.
We also discuss a range of representative examples in
Section

to give a sense of when the quadratic improvement in trace distance is or is not possible. This turns out to depend on the decay rate of the sorted entries of the target vector, and specifically on the relation between the

Numerics and application to MPS.

An important motivation for our work is the application to randomized methods for simulating quantum systems, which we explore numerically in
Section

8
. In particular, matrix product states are a widely used technique for modelling quantum states where our randomized truncation schemes can be used as a drop-in replacement for the usual deterministic approach to reducing bond dimension.
Code for our paper is available at
https://github.com/angusjlowe/rtrunc
. It includes a package for computing optimal approximations for pure states as well as code to reproduce the plots in this paper.

1.3
Related work

Randomized approximations to classical and quantum objects have appeared in several different contexts.

Randomized compiling.

A related question to the one we study in this work is how well unitary operators can be approximated by mixtures of unitaries. Here, the power of randomness is somewhat greater for channels than for states, which we discuss further in
Section

1.4
. Prior work by Campbell
[
Cam17
]
and Hastings
[
Has16
]
first showed (nonconstructively) that mixing unitaries can quadratically reduce diamond-norm error under general conditions. This led to randomized product formulas for Hamiltonian simulation
[
COS19
,
Cam19
,
OWC20
,
HW23
]
, rotations
[
Low21
,
KMB24
]
, and Quantum Signal Processing
[
MR25
]
. The general question of approximating the set of all unitaries by convex combinations of an allowed set of unitaries was studied by Akibue, Kato and Tani
[
AKT24b
]
, who found that mixing quadratically improves the worst-case error, i.e., given an
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-net of unitaries, taking convex combinations yields an
O(\varepsilon)
-net in diamond norm.

Probabilistic state synthesis.

Randomized approximations to states have also been considered previously in
[
AKT22
,
AKT24a
]
. Given a set of allowed pure states in the random mixture, these works show a quadratic improvement in accuracy for the problem of
worst-case approximation
(i.e. the hardest possible target state
v
) as well as when certain symmetry conditions are met. Their setting does not substantially overlap with ours since we consider approximations for arbitrary pure states, which in general do not obey their symmetry conditions. Indeed, in

Section

7
, we give examples where the quadratic accuracy
improvement of
[
AKT24a
]
does not apply. Refs.
[
AKT22
,
AKT24a
]
also
provide algorithms based on semidefinite programming for finding the optimal randomized approximation, whose runtime scales polynomially in the number of allowed states in the mixture. In our setting, this number is at least exponential in the sparsity parameter
k
(see
Section

3
for more
discussion). Although the prior work on randomized compiling and probabilistic state synthesis do not resolve our problem, their findings of widespread quadratic advantage serve as inspiration for pursuing efficient algorithms applicable to our setting.

Resource theory of coherence.

The idea of a “resource theory” is to define a set of “free” states and/or operations, and then to use this to define distances from these objects which quantify some resource of interest
[
CG19
]
. The original example of this was to make separable states and LOCC operations free and to seek to quantify the amount of entanglement in a non-separable state
[
BBPS96
]
. One example of a natural resource measure is the robustness (related to the max-relative entropy) of a given state
\rho
with respect to the free states
[
VT99
,
LBT19
]
. The resource theory that directly relates to our work is that of “
k
-incoherence” (also known as “
k
-coherence”), which treats mixtures of
k
-sparse states as free, and quantifies the distance of a given state from this set; see
[
SAP17
]
for a review of this topic.
The
k=1
case was studied in
[
CGJ+16
]
, using both the trace distance and robustness as resource measures. The
k>1
case was analyzed in detail in
[
Reg17
,
JLP+18
]
. Among other results, these works show that for pure states, different notions of robustness are equal to each other and can be expressed in terms of a norm called the
k
-support norm (see
2.9
). Our result on the robustness in
Section

6
can be seen as a constructive version of the upper bound of the robustness given in Theorem 1 of
[
JLP+18
]
.

The
k
-support norm.

Both the optimal fidelity and the optimal robustness can be understood in terms of two norms which are dual to one another: the top-
k
norm, and the
k
-support norm. These are defined in
[
AFS12
]
and reviewed in our paper in
Section

2.1
. In
[
AFS12
]
these norms were used to develop convex relaxations for the task of finding sparse solutions to regression problems. They also gave an expression for the proximal operator for the
k
-support norm
[
AFS12
, Section 4]
which is similar to our trace distance optimization in
Section

5
.
Gauge norms are also studied in optimization settings
[
CRPW12
]
where the goal of finding a simple
model can be formalized through constraints on sparsity or rank, or more generally, being a linear combination of a small number of atoms.

Tensor network Monte Carlo.

An important application of truncation, both deterministic and randomized, is to reduce the bond dimension in matrix product states and tensor networks. An immediate application of our work is to approximate a tensor by a lower-rank tensor, as we will discuss in more detail in
Section

8
. Ferris
[
Fer15
]
has proposed a tensor truncation scheme with some similarities to ours, and this idea has also been studied more recently in
[
Tod24
]
. We defer the detailed comparison of our work with
[
Fer15
]
to
Section

8
. As a brief summary of the comparison, our methods provably minimize robustness or trace distance for bipartite states, while Ferris’ method samples indices to keep using a heuristic which is not based on the solution to an optimization problem.

Randomized SVD.

When truncating bond dimensions in a tensor network, one typically performs many singular value decompositions (SVDs). Since the
O(d^{3})
(or
O(d^{\omega})
with
\omega\approx 2.37
in theory) runtime to perform an SVD can be prohibitive, there has been a long line of work (reviewed in
[
KT24
]
) on randomized algorithms for this task. In general, these are faster and less accurate than performing a deterministic SVD and truncating. The intention behind our algorithms, by contrast, is to be slower and more accurate.

1.4
Proof techniques and intuition

Our work shows that in order to approximate a pure state, given a library of simpler pure states, the best accuracy comes from introducing randomness. This may at first seem counterintuitive, but there are a few reasons why we might expect this to be the case.

One explanation comes from zero-sum games. The optimal trace distance approximation can be viewed as such a game where, given a target pure state
v
, we seek the value of

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{0\preceq M\preceq\mathds{1}}\tr(M(vv^\dag-\sigma)).

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is the set of mixtures of
k
-sparse states. This has the form of a zero-sum game, with the
\sigma
player trying to minimize the distinguishability and the
M
player trying to maximize it. A common feature of equilibria is that the optimal strategies are mixed. In game terms, the
\sigma
player often does better with a mixed strategy because they may then perform simultaneously well against many possible responses by the
M
player. This picture also tells us that a fixed
M
obviates the need for a mixed strategy by the
\sigma
player. This happens when the goal is to minimize an observable such as the energy of a system, or when maximizing the fidelity with a fixed state. In these cases, optimal pure strategies exist, while optimal mixed strategies may or may not also exist.

This principle also explains why randomness helps with approximating unitaries. The diamond norm involves a maximization over both the final distinguishing measurement
and
the choice of input state. In this case, randomness can be even more helpful in reducing diamond norm error. For example, the unitary
\exp(i\frac{\pi}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i)
needs
\Omega(n)
gates to approximate deterministically to accuracy
\varepsilon
but only
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
gates if we allow randomness
[
Cam19
,
CHKT21
]
. If the input state is fixed then we are back in the setting of approximating states. But if we fix
both
the input state and the final observable, then again deterministic approximation can achieve the optimal error.

Finally, our results illustrate the value of importance sampling of sets, the main idea for which we now sketch. Importance sampling often refers to the task of sampling an element
i
from a set, say
[d]
, and using this to construct an estimator of some quantity of interest. For example, suppose
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
are positive reals with
\sum_{i}v_{i}=V
and the goal is to estimate
s=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}x_{i}
. A natural unbiased estimator – familiar from Monte Carlo algorithms – is obtained by sampling
i
with probability
v_{i}/V
and outputting
\hat{s}_{i}\coloneq Vx_{i}
. This procedure can alternatively be viewed as constructing a randomized
1
-sparse approximation to a vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
. Namely, if
(e_{j}:j=1,\dots,d)
are the standard basis vectors and
i
is drawn randomly with probability
v_{i}/V
then the vector-valued random variable
Ve_{i}
is an unbiased estimator of
v
.

Now, if we wish to generalize to a
k
-sparse approximation then we need to sample a random subset of the indices
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subset[d]
. As before, we can choose to impose that this
k
-sparse estimator be unbiased for the target vector
v
. This means that if
i\in S
with probability
q_{i}\in(0,1]
then the random vector ought to be of the form

\frac{v_{i_{1}}}{q_{i_{1}}k}e_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{v_{i_{k}}}{q_{i_{k}}k}e_{i_{k}}.

(4)

Since
|S|=k
and the
q_{i}
represent marginal probabilities, we must have
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}=k
. The task at hand – say, minimizing the variance of some function of this random vector – then informs the desired values of the marginal probabilities
q_{1},\dots,q_{d}
.

For the randomized approximations in robustness and trace distance, we do not explicitly enforce that the
k
-sparse states be unbiased in the sense described above. Nevertheless, the right intuition is conveyed by importance sampling of sets. In particular, the construction of the optimal ensemble of
k
-sparse pure states in robustness, described in
Section

6
, is of the form in
Eq.

4
with
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
set to
1
for some
m\leq k
and
q_{i}\propto v_{i}
for each
i=m+1,\dots,d
. Furthermore, a similar construction holds for the optimal trace distance ensemble, described in
Section

5.2
, upon deterministically truncating some of the indices in a way that depends on the form of the target state
v
.

Carrying out the sampling, or computing the density matrix corresponding to this ensemble, is a nontrivial algorithmic challenge which we address
Section

4
. Namely, we require some method of efficiently computing and representing a distribution over
k
-element subsets of
[d]
subject to marginal constraints. Fortunately, such algorithms are known to be feasible from the literature on maximum entropy sampling. We draw on this past work and improve upon it as well.

1.5
Open questions

We give a comprehensive analysis of the computational problem of finding optimal randomized approximations of pure quantum states by
k
-sparse states or states with Schmidt rank
k
. However, there are various interesting questions for future work.

Application to tensor networks.

We propose tensor network truncation as a natural application of randomized truncation. How useful this is in practice, and for
which
applications precisely, will require detailed numerical study, which we leave to follow-up work.

Computing the sampling weights.

We use a black-box result based on the ellipsoid method to argue that the sampling weights can be efficiently computed. While this has polynomial scaling, it is not very practical. Numerically, using a Newton method and a diagonal approximation of the Hessian works well. It would be interesting to give rigorous performance bounds for this method. It may also be that the heuristic version of Newton’s method can be further improved.

Applications beyond quantum states.

We focus on the case where the vector we try to approximate is a quantum state. A natural follow-up direction is to investigate applications beyond this interpretation. This directly leads to the question of generalization of other norms than the 1-norm, such as the operator norm or general
p
-norms. Moreover, it may not be natural to impose a normalization condition on the approximating vectors; this also changes the optimization problem.

Relation to Hamiltonian simulation.

As described in

Section

1.3
, there are several randomized Hamiltonian
simulation methods that can be viewed as solving a related problem.
Namely, given a Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i=1}^{L}v_{i}h_{i}
, can we
approximate
e^{-iHt}
with a random mixture of

\exp(-i\sum_i w_i h_i)
for a collection of
k
-sparse vectors
w
?
The qDRIFT algorithm can be viewed as doing this for 1-sparse
w
.
Applied to a starting state
\ket{\psi}
, and assuming

h_{i}^{2}\propto I
, this means approximating
e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi}
with a
mixture of superpositions of
\ket{\psi}
and
h_{i}\ket{\psi}
. Applying
our robustness-optimal scheme with
k=2
, and neglecting higher-order
terms, turns out to yield precisely qDRIFT. Improving this to higher
values of
k
is a compelling open problem. One of the key
difficulties is that qDRIFT addresses only first-order and
second-order error while modern Hamiltonian simulation methods
generally control higher-order errors as well. An exploration of methods for partially randomized Hamiltonian simulation is initiated in recent work
[
GWS+25
]
.

2.1
Measuring the distance to coherent or entangled states

We briefly recall standard quantum distance measures; see e.g.
[
NC12
, Ch. 5]
.
The
fidelity
between states
\rho,\sigma
is

F(\rho,\sigma)={\lVert\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\rVert}_{1}.

If
\sigma=vv^{\dagger}
is pure, then
F(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{\langle v,\rho v\rangle}
, and if
\rho=ww^{\dagger}
is also pure we have
F(vv^{\dagger},ww^{\dagger})=|\langle v,w\rangle|
.
The
trace distance
is

T(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\rho-\sigma}_{1}.

When convenient, we abbreviate
F(v,w):=F(vv^{\dagger},ww^{\dagger})
and similarly for
T
.
We also use
F(v,\rho)
and
T(v,\rho)
for comparisons between a mixed state
\rho
and a pure state given as a unit vector
v
. For two pure states
v,w
these are related by
F(v,w)^{2}+T(v,w)^{2}=1
. More generally, the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities relate
F
and
T
by

1-F\leq T\leq\sqrt{1-F^{2}}.

(7)

If one of the two states is pure, the lower bound can be improved, and we have

1-F^{2}\leq T\leq\sqrt{1-F^{2}}.

(8)

We are interested in the maximum fidelity (or minimum trace distance) achievable by approximating a state with one that has limited coherence or entanglement. The lower bound in
Eq.

8
implies that, for pure states, we cannot hope to attain more than a quadratic advantage. Here and throughout, we let
d,a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
a,b\leq d
.

Definition 2.3

(Fidelity with
\mathcal{I}_{k}
or
\mathcal{S}_{k}
)
.

For
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, let

F_{k}(\rho):=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}F(\rho,\sigma).

For
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b})
, let

F_{k}^{(E)}(\rho):=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}F(\rho,\sigma).

As in the case of

, if

is a unit vector corresponding to a pure state we abbreviate
F_{k}(vv^{\dagger})
by

and similarly for

. These measures are straightforward to compute for pure states.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
is pure then for any positive integer

it holds that

F_{k}(v)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|v_{i}^{\downarrow}|^{2}.

Similarly, if
v\in\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}
is a bipartite state with a Schmidt decomposition of the form in

6
, we have the following.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
v
is of the form given in

6
then for any positive integer

it holds that

F_{k}^{(E)}(v)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

By definition,
F_{k}^{(E)}(v)^{2}=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\langle v,\sigma v\rangle
. The function

is linear and the set

is convex so it suffices to consider a maximization over the extreme points of

F_{k}^{(E)}(v)^{2}=\max_{\varphi}|\langle\varphi,v\rangle|^{2}
where the maximization is over pure states on

∎

These facts agree with the intuition that if we want to maximize the overlap with a

3
Computational hardness for mixed states

How hard is it to compute approximations by sparse states or states with small Schmidt numbers?
For an arbitrary state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
the problem is computationally intractable.
In fact, even weak membership of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is
\NP
-hard
3
3

3

Weak membership of a convex set
S
is the task of deciding whether
\rho\in S
or
\rho
is
\varepsilon
-close to
S
in Euclidean distance. Weak linear optimization is the task of approximately solving
\max_{\sigma\in S}\tr[M\sigma]
up to additive error
\varepsilon
. These terms are defined in detail in
[
GLS12
]
.
.
As a consequence, computing
F_{k}(\rho)
,
F_{k}^{(E)}(\rho)
,
T_{k}(\rho)
, and
T_{k}^{(E)}(\rho)
is
\NP
-hard in general.

For entanglement, weak membership of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is precisely the separability problem, which is known to be
\NP
-hard
[
Gur03
,
Gha08
]
.
In contrast, checking 1-sparsity is trivial:
\rho\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
if and only if
\rho
is diagonal in the standard basis.
However, for larger
k
, the problem again becomes
\NP
-hard.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
and
k
, the problem of weak membership of
\rho
in
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is
\NP
-hard.

Proof.

Weak membership, weak separation, and weak linear optimization are polynomial-time equivalent for convex sets
[
GLS12
]
.
Thus it suffices to consider weak linear optimization over
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, i.e.

\displaystyle m_{*}\;=\;\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tr[M\sigma].

(21)

The maximum is achieved at an extreme point of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, namely a pure
k
-sparse state.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle m_{*}\;=\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[d]\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\;\norm{M_S}_{\infty},

(22)

where
M_{S}
is the
k\times k
principal submatrix of
M
indexed by
S
.

We consider the case where
M=A_{G}
is the adjacency matrix of a graph
G
on
d
vertices.
Then each
M_{S}
is the adjacency matrix
A_{H}
of the induced subgraph
H=G[S]
.
For any
k
-vertex graph
H
, we have
\norm{A_H}_{\infty}\leq k-1
, with equality if and only if
H
is the complete graph
K_{k}
.
Moreover Proposition 1.3.1 of
[
CRS10
]
implies that
if
H
is not complete then
\norm{A_H}_{\infty}
is bounded away from
k-1
by an inverse polynomial in
k
.
Thus the
k
-Clique problem reduces to weak linear optimization over
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, proving
\NP
-hardness.
∎

We emphasize that this hardness requires
k
to be a variable. If
k
is fixed then
\mathcal{I}_{k}
can be expressed as a semidefinite program of size
d^{O(k)}
, since any
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
can be expressed as a mixture of states in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{S})
for
S
ranging over all subsets of
[d]
of size
k
. Here
\mathbb{C}^{S}
means the
|S|
-dimensional subspace of states with support contained in
S
. The hardness results shows that this SDP is essentially optimal if finding
k
-cliques in
d
-vertex graphs takes time
d^{\Omega(k)}
. This latter conjecture is implied by the Exponential Time Hypothesis and other plausible complexity-theory conjectures
[
ABW15
]
.

For
pure
states, membership in
\mathcal{I}_{k}
and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is easy to decide: in the first case by support size, in the second by Schmidt rank.
We have already seen that for pure
v
, the fidelities
F_{k}(v)
and
F_{k}^{(E)}(v)
are easy to compute.
In
Section

5
we will show that the trace distances
T_{k}(v)
and
T_{k}^{(E)}(v)
can also be computed efficiently, although these algorithms are considerably more complicated than those for the fidelity.

Our algorithms for
T_{k}(v)
and
R_{k}(v)
thus rely crucially on
v
being a pure state and involve details of the properties of trace distance and robustness. For a general distance measure on quantum states
D(\rho,\sigma)
, one can define
D(v,\mathcal{I}_{k})\coloneq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}D(vv^{\dagger},\sigma)
. We do not have a general reduction from
D(v,\mathcal{I}_{k})
to the pairwise distances
D(\rho,\sigma)
, nor do we know examples where the pairwise distances are easy but calculating
D(v,\mathcal{I}_{k})
is hard.

4
Max-entropy sampling of subsets with given marginals

4.1
Importance sampling of sets

We make use of efficient methods to compute and sample from distributions over
\ell
distinct integers drawn from the set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, with
n\geq\ell
. Equivalently, we are interested in random
n
-bit strings of Hamming weight
\ell
, representing inclusion in the draw.
We refer to this set as
H_{\ell}\coloneq\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|x|=\ell\}

Distributions over
H_{\ell}
arise, for example, in physics when studying systems of
\ell
fermions restricted to occupy
n
distinct modes. They also implement the importance sampling of sets that we described in
Section

1.4
.
For randomized truncation they are relevant because our methods, described in
Sections

5
and
6
, involve sampling random fixed-size subsets of indices from the vector to be truncated.

In each case, the input is the alphabet size
n
, the size of the set
\ell
, and the marginal probabilities
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
that each element is included in the set. It will be convenient to assume that each
q_{j}\in(0,1)
, and this is without loss of generality, since if
q_{j}
is 0 or 1 then we can deterministically leave out or include that element and do not need to involve it in the sampling. Normalization requires that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}=\ell
.
Given these inputs our goal is to find a distribution
p:H_{\ell}\to[0,1]
which can be represented efficiently and satisfies four desired criteria:

1.

marginal probability constraints are satisfied, i.e.,
q_{j}=\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}p(x)x_{j}
for every
j\in[n]
;

2.

two-element marginals

Q_{i,j}\coloneq\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}p(x)x_{i}x_{j}

can be computed in time
\poly(n)
;

3.

the distribution can be sampled from in time
\poly(n)
; and

4.

negative correlation (of indicator random variables):
Q_{i,j}\leq q_{i}q_{j}
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

4.2
Max-entropy distributions

A convenient, although not the only, solution to the above constraints is the maximum-entropy distribution. Namely, it suffices to use the solution to the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{H_{\ell}}}{\textnormal{max.}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(p)\coloneq\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}p(x)\log(1/p(x))

(23)

\displaystyle\textnormal{s.t.}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}p(x)x_{j}=q_{j}

\displaystyle\forall j\in[n],

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}p(x)=1.

This is precisely the problem studied in
[
CDL94
]
, a generalization of which is the subject of more recent work
[
SV14
,
SV19
]
.
Since there are exponentially many variables and only
n+1
constraints, most work on the subject focuses on the dual optimization problem. First we observe that the optimal solution to
Eq.

23
takes the form

\displaystyle p^{\star}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\mu_{j}^{\star}}}{Z}\ \forall x\in H_{\ell},\quad Z\coloneq\sum_{x\in H_{\ell}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\mu_{j}^{\star}}

(24)

for some parameters
(\mu_{j}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
:
j\in[n])
.
Physicists will recognize this as a Gibbs distribution with fermionic energy levels given by
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, temperature set to one and the total number of particles constrained to be
\ell
. Within the field of survey sampling, it is also called sampling with unequal probabilities and without replacement, or conditional Poisson sampling
[
CDL94
,
Til06
]
. For a self-contained proof of a slightly more general fact than the above, see
[
SV14
, Lemma A.1]
.

The optimal distribution therefore has a succinct description in terms of just
n

\mu_{1}^{\star},\dots,\mu_{n}^{\star}
, which correspond to the optimal dual variables for the dual problem:

\displaystyle\textnormal{min.}

\displaystyle g_{q}(\mu)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}q_{i}+\log(\sum_{x\in H_\ell}\ee^{-\sum_{j=1}^nx_j\mu_j})

(25)

\displaystyle\textnormal{s.t .}

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\forall j\in[n]

where
q\coloneq(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})^{\top}
.

\displaystyle q(\mu)_{i}={Q}(\mu)_{ii}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mu_{i}}Z_{\mu}(\ell-1,\{i\}^{c})}{Z_{\mu}(\ell,[n])}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n].

(28)

Here the superscript
c
means complement with respect to
[n]
, so
\{i,j\}^{c}
refers to
[n]\backslash\{i,j\}
.

It remains only to compute
Z_{\mu}
. This is achieved via the recursive formula

Z_{\mu}(\ell,S)=Z_{\mu}(\ell,S\backslash\{i\})+e^{-\mu_{i}}Z_{\mu}(\ell-1,S\backslash\{i\}),

(29)

whenever
i\in S
. We can use this to evaluate
Z_{\mu}=Z_{\mu}(\ell,[n])
by taking
i=1,\ldots,n
. For more general
S
we can likewise iterate through the elements of
S
. It is important to use a consistent order so that only
O(\ell|S|)
different
Z_{\mu}(\cdot,\cdot)
need to be computed. The recursion stops with the boundary conditions

Z_{\mu}(0,S)=1
for any
S\subseteq[n]
and
Z_{\mu}(\ell,S)=0
whenever
\ell>|S|
.

Similar ideas can be used to sample from
p_{\mu}
. We sequentially sample
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
. To sample
x_{i}
conditioned on our choices for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}
we need to calculate

\Pr[x_{i}|x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}]=\frac{e^{-\mu_{i}}Z_{\mu}(\ell-1-x_{1}-\cdots-x_{i-1},\{i+1,\ldots,n\})}{Z_{\mu}(\ell-x_{1}-\cdots-x_{i-1},\{i,\ldots,n\})}.

(30)

These arguments only show poly-time algorithms. See
Appendix

B
for algorithms achieving the stated runtime.
∎

Finally, we record the fact that marginals under the max-entropy distributions discussed above are negatively correlated, in the following sense.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
we have
0<{Q}(\mu)_{ij}<q(\mu)_{i}q(\mu)_{j}
.

Proof.

The expressions in
Eqs.

27
and
28
are sums of positive terms, so
Q_{ij}
and
q_{i}
are all positive. For
Q_{ij}<q_{i}q_{j}
, this is Property 2 from
[
CDL94
]
, and is proven there by applying
Eq.

29
to
Eqs.

27
and
28
.
∎

Finally we turn to the inverse problem of finding
\mu
such that
q(\mu)=q^{\star}
given some input
q^{\star}
.
Naively
Eq.

23
is a convex program with
|H_{\ell}|=\binom{n}{\ell}
variables, while
Eq.

25
has only
n
variables but the objective function requires summing over
\binom{n}{\ell}
terms. Fortunately, this exponentially large sum can be summed over efficiently, due to
4.1
, and this will be the basis for efficient algorithms.

We also need to show that an accurate solution of
Eq.

25
yields a good approximation of
p^{\star}
.
A straightforward calculation reveals that the dual objective function in
Eq.

25
can be rewritten as

g_{q}(\mu)=\mathrm{H}(p^{\star})+\mathrm{D}_{\textnormal{KL}}(p^{\star}\;\|\;p_{\mu}).

(31)

This implies that approximate dual solutions lead to approximate primal solutions. Specifically, by Pinsker’s Inequality, an
\varepsilon
-accurate solution
\mu
to the dual problem gives a solution
p_{\mu}
to the primal problem which is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
in total variation distance from the optimum
p^{\star}
. Hence, it suffices to approximately solve the dual problem, which can be accomplished through the ellipsoid method, as shown by Straszak and Vishnoi
[
SV19
]
. This leads to the following, which we state without proof.

Lemma 4.3

(Implied by
[
SV19
, Theorem 15]
)
.

Let
q_{j}\in(0,1)
for each
j\in[n]
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. There is an algorithm running in time
\poly(n,\log(1/\eps))
which computes a vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\norm{\mu}_{\infty}\leq\poly(n,\log(1/\eps))
and

\mathrm{d}_{\textnormal{TV}}(p_{\mu},p^{\star})\leq\varepsilon

where
p^{\star}
is the optimal solution to the optimization problem in
Eq.

23
.

Instead of using the ellipsoid method, it may be possible to derive an explicit polynomial bound on the time required to solve for
\mu^{\star}
using Newton’s method and a self-concordant barrier function (see, e.g.,
[
NN94
,
BV04
]
) to remain within the ball of radius
R
, which is actually provided as an explicit polynomial in
[
SV19
, Theorem 7]
. We leave this to future work.

This concludes the argument that a max-entropy construction satisfies the desired criteria listed at the beginning of this section, and that a polynomial-size representation of this distribution, in the form of the parameters
\mu
, can be computed in polynomial time.

4.4
Fast algorithms in practice

If the marginal probabilities
q_{j}
are bounded away from zero and one by at least some small constant, then the iterative method in
[
CDL94
, Theorem 2]
computes these parameters in
O(\ell^{2}n)
steps. Alternatively, one can use the fact that the gradient of the dual objective function is

\nabla_{\mu}g_{q}(\mu)=q-q(\mu).

Since
g_{q}
is convex, the roots of
\mu\mapsto q-q(\mu)
are global minima. We can therefore try to solve for the optimal
\mu
using the Newton’s method, as proposed in
[
Che00
]
, with the iterates

\displaystyle\mu^{(t+1)}=\mu^{(t)}+K(\mu^{(t)})^{-1}(q-q(\mu^{(t)})).

(32)

Here,
[K(\mu)]_{ij}\coloneq{Q}(\mu)_{i,j}-q(\mu)_{i}q(\mu)_{j}
is a covariance matrix under the distribution
p_{\mu}
, which can be computed efficiently by
4.1
.

In practice, both these methods appear to converge quickly to the optimal solution, and we use the second of these in our numerics. In fact, since the covariance matrix
K(\mu)
typically has small off-diagonal elements, we take them to be zero as a heuristic to speed up the computation. As a reminder, the diagonal elements are
q(\mu)_{i}(1-q(\mu)_{i})
. The divergence in step size as
q(\mu)_{i}
approaches 0 or 1 corresponds to the fact that these probabilities appear only in the limit of
\mu_{i}\rightarrow\pm\infty
.
We refer the interested reader to Refs.
[
Til06
]
for additional details on these algorithms and their history.

5
Approximations in trace distance

In this section we give efficient algorithms to compute the optimal randomized truncation to a pure state with respect to trace distance. More specifically, given a target unit vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, our first algorithm, given in
Section

, computes the value

\displaystyle T_{k}(v):=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\frac{1}{2}{\lVert vv^{\dagger}-\sigma\rVert}_{1}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{0\preceq M\preceq\mathds{1}}\tr(M(vv^\dag-\sigma)).

(33)

It also returns a description of a measurement
M^{\star}=mm^{\dagger}
with
m\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
(\sigma^{\star},M^{\star})
is a saddle point, for some
\sigma^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
. A second algorithm, given in
Section

5.2
, then returns a description of the optimal solution
\sigma^{\star}
, either explicitly in the standard basis or in the form of an ensemble of
k
-sparse pure states
\{(p_{i},v_{i})\}_{i}
where the distribution

can be efficiently sampled.

5.1
Computing the trace distance and optimal measurement

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
k\leq d
and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector. There is an algorithm (
Algorithm

1
) running in time
O(dk+d\log d)
that computes
T_{k}(v)
as well as a measurement operator
M^{\star}
such that
(\sigma^{\star},M^{\star})
is a saddle point of the minimax in
Eq.

33
, for some
\sigma^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
.

Our proof will proceed by first showing that the optimal
M
can be taken to be of the form
mm^{\dagger}
, i.e. a rank-1 measurement. Then we derive the structure of
m
, proving that it consists of two pieces that are proportional to the entries of
v
with a flat region in the middle (when sorted). At this point, there is only a single parameter and it can be found by solving a cubic equation. Then in
Section

5.2
we will use this to find an optimal approximating state.

The fact that
M
can be taken without loss of generality to be rank 1 is due to the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, density matrix
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and unit vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the maximum in the optimization problem

\max_{0\preceq M\preceq\mathds{1}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

is attained by a rank-1 matrix.

Proof.

By
A.9
, the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
vv^{\dagger}-\sigma
lies in the interval
[0,1]
, while the second largest eigenvalue lies in
[-1,0]
. If
\sigma=vv^{\dagger}
then the optimal value of the maximization on the right-hand side of
Eq.

37
is zero, and
M=mm^{\dagger}
is an optimal solution for any unit vector
{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Otherwise,
vv^{\dagger}-\sigma
has just a single positive eigenvalue, and an optimal solution is given by the rank-1 projector onto this eigenspace.
∎

The proof of the next lemma uses
5.2
as well as some of the facts in
Section

A.1
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
k\leq d
and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector.
Then
T_{k}(v)
is equal to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{max.}&\quad\langle m,v^{\downarrow}\rangle^{2}-\norm{{m}}_{(k)}^{2}\\
\textnormal{s.t.}&\quad{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
&\quad\norm{{m}}_{2}=1.\end{aligned}

(34)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of Hermitian matrices
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
such that
0\preceq M\preceq\mathds{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
the subset of such matrices which are rank-1. We have

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

(35)

\displaystyle=\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

(36)

where the first line follows from
5.2
and in the second line we used Sion’s minimax theorem
A.1
. By
A.3
there exists a saddle point
(\sigma^{\star},M^{\star})\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\times\mathcal{M}
such that the right-hand side of
Eq.

36
is equal to

\displaystyle\tr(M^\star(v v^\dagger-\sigma^\star))

\displaystyle=\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma^\star)).

(37)

Appealing once again to
5.2
, the maximum in
Eq.

37
is attained by a rank-1 matrix. Hence, the saddle point above is without loss of generality in the set
\mathcal{I}_{k}\times\mathcal{M}_{1}
and the right-hand side of
Eq.

36
is equal to

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tr(M^\star(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

\displaystyle\leq\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma)).

(38)

Eqs.

35
,
36
and
38
establish an inequality in the opposite direction of the minimax inequality, which always holds. Hence, the inequality in
Eq.

38
holds with equality, and we have

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

\displaystyle=\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\tr(M(v v^\dagger-\sigma))

(39)

\displaystyle=\max_{{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\ \norm{{m}}_{2}=1}\lvert\langle m,v\rangle\rvert^{2}-\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\ \langle m,\sigma m\rangle

(40)

\displaystyle=\max_{{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\ \norm{{m}}_{2}=1}\lvert\langle m,v\rangle\rvert^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|m_{i}^{\downarrow}|^{2}.

(41)

where the third line follows from
2.4
. Finally, by
Eq.

20
we can replace
v
with
v^{\downarrow}
in the optimization in
Eq.

41
without changing the optimal value. After making this modification we are free to restrict the feasible set to
\{{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\norm{{m}}_{2}=1\}
without loss of generality.
∎

The optimization in
Eq.

34
bears some resemblance to the computation of the proximal operator for the
k
-support norm, as studied in
[
AFS12
]
, and can be solved in a similar way. To begin executing this strategy, we require the following fact.

Lemma 5.4
.

A point
{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an optimal solution for the optimization problem in
Eq.

34
if and only if
\norm{{m}}_{2}=1
and there exists a
\lambda\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{{u}}_{(k,*)}^{2}+\norm{{m}}_{(k)}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\langle u,m\rangle\quad\textnormal{where}\quad{u}\coloneq\langle v^{\downarrow},m\rangle v^{\downarrow}-\lambda{m}.

(42)

Proof.

We make use of basic facts from convex analysis, given in
Section

A.2
. The optimization problem in
Eq.

34
is equivalent to minimizing
f({m})
over all
{m}\in\Omega
, where
f({m})\coloneq-\frac{1}{2}\langle m,v^{\downarrow}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\norm{{m}}_{(k)}^{2}
and
\Omega\coloneq\{{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\norm{{m}}_{2}\leq 1\}
.
Relaxing the norm constraint here is valid by monotonicity of the objective function.
By Fermat’s Rule the point
{m}
is an optimal solution if and only if
0\in\partial f({m})+\mathcal{N}_{\Omega}({m})
, where
\partial f(m)
is the subdifferential of
f
at
m
and
\mathcal{N}_{\Omega}(m)
is the normal cone at
m
. When
{m}
is a unit vector, the normal cone
\mathcal{N}_{\Omega}({m})
is just the ray generated by the vector
{m}
. Also, the subdifferential can be computed as

\partial f({m})=-\langle m,v^{\downarrow}\rangle v^{\downarrow}+\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}\norm{\cdot}_{(k)}^{2}\big)({m}).

We therefore have that the optimality condition for a given unit vector
{m}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle v^{\downarrow},m\rangle v^{\downarrow}-\lambda{m}\in\partial\big(\frac{1}{2}\norm{\cdot}_{(k)}^{2}\big)({m})

(43)

for some
\lambda\geq 0
. The equivalence between this condition and
Eq.

is a direct consequence of
A.8
.
∎

For any

\displaystyle\theta^{{v}}_{r,\ell}(\lambda)

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

to the optimization problem in

{m}=\frac{{\widetilde{m}}}{\norm{{\widetilde{m}}}_{2}}

\displaystyle{\widetilde{m}_{i}}=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{i}^{\downarrow}}{1+\lambda},&i=1,\dots,k-r-1\\
\theta_{r,\ell}^{{v}}(\lambda),&i=k-r,\dots,\ell-1\\

(45)

for some

,
\ell\in\{k+1,\dots,d+1\}

such that

\displaystyle\theta^{{v}}_{r,\ell}(\lambda)\in\frac{1}{1+\lambda}[v^{\downarrow}_{k-r},v^{\downarrow}_{k-r-1})\cap\frac{1}{\lambda}(v^{\downarrow}_{\ell},v^{\downarrow}_{\ell-1}],

(46)

and

2.

\langle v^{\downarrow},\widetilde{m}\rangle=1
.

Moreover, for such a unit vector

, it holds that

\lambda=T_{k}(v).

Proof.

To simplify notation we assume
{v}
has real, nonnegative entries and is sorted in nonincreasing order. We may also assume

is nonnegative and sorted in nonincreasing order without loss of generality since the objective function is nondecreasing under the replacement
{m}\to m^{\downarrow}
. By
5.4
, it holds that
{m}
is optimal for the problem in

34
if and only if
\norm{{m}}_{2}=1
and there is a

such that
\norm{{u}}_{(k,*)}^{2}+\norm{{m}}_{(k)}^{2}=2\langle u,m\rangle
where
{u}=\langle v,m\rangle v-\lambda{m}
. Note we are neglecting the case where

since this implies the optimal solution is

, which contradicts

and

having no entries equal to zero
4
4

4

This is by complementary slackness: since

is a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint

, if

then there is an

.
. By

, the unit vector

is optimal for the problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{max.}&\quad\langle u,m\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\norm{{m}}_{(k)}^{2}\\

(47)

5.2
Computing and sampling the optimal mixed approximation

In this section, we describe the density matrix
\sigma^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
which attains the minimum value
T_{k}(v)
in the optimization problem in
Eq.

33
, i.e., the optimal randomized truncation of
v
. We then show that this density matrix corresponds to an ensemble of
k
-sparse pure states which can be efficiently sampled, upon obtaining
T_{k}(v)
and
m
from
Algorithm

1
. The ensemble consists of pure states which are proportional to the vector obtained by keeping the top
k-r-1
entries of
v
deterministically, truncating indices greater than
\ell-1
, and drawing
r+1
nonzero indices at random from the remaining
\ell-k+r
indices. An example of the marginal inclusion probabilities
q_{j}
for
j=k-r,\dots,\ell-1
is given in
Figure

3(b)
and, as we will see in the proof of
5.7
, it holds that

\displaystyle q_{j}=\frac{v_{j}}{\theta}-\lambda\quad\textnormal{for every}\quad j\in\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}

(62)

where
\lambda=T_{k}(v)
and
\theta=\theta_{r,\ell}^{v}(\lambda)
. The algorithm can also straightforwardly be applied to approximate an entangled quantum state by sampling states having Schmidt-rank at most
k
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
satisfy
k\leq d
, let
\eta\in(0,1)
, and let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector. There is an algorithm, requiring at most
\textnormal{poly}(d,\log(1/\eta))
steps, which computes the entries in the standard basis of a density matrix
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
such that

T(v,\sigma)\leq T_{k}(v)+\eta.

Proof.

We use the shorthand
T_{k}=T_{k}(v)
and assume that
{v}
is real, nonnegative, and sorted in nonincreasing order. The relevant phases and ordering can easily be handled upon solving the problem with this simplifying assumption. We make use of the minimax equality

\displaystyle T_{k}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\norm{x}_{2}=1}\langle x,v\rangle^{2}-\langle x,\sigma x\rangle=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\norm{x}_{2}=1}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\ \langle x,v\rangle^{2}-\langle x,\sigma x\rangle.

(63)

which was established in the proof of
5.3
. Let
(\sigma^{\star},m)
be a saddle point for the this minimax problem. We must have

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\ \langle m,\sigma m\rangle=\langle m,\sigma^{\star}m\rangle

(64)

which implies that
\sigma^{\star}
is a mixture of pure states
w
attaining the maximum in

\displaystyle\max\{|\langle m,w\rangle|^{2}:\norm{w}_{2}=1,\ \norm{w}_{0}\leq k\}.

(65)

By
5.6
,
m\propto\widetilde{m}
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{m}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{i}}{1+\lambda},&i=1,\dots,k-r-1\\
\theta,&i=k-r,\dots,\ell-1,\\
\frac{v_{i}}{\lambda},&i=\ell,\dots,d\end{cases}

(66)

for some
r\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
,
\ell\in\{k+1,\dots,d+1\}
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
v_{k-r-1}/(1+\lambda)>\theta>v_{\ell}/\lambda
. Also,
\lambda=T_{k}
, and all these quantities can be efficiently computed. Eqs. (
65
) and (
66
) then imply that each state
w
in the mixture is of the form

\displaystyle w(S)\coloneq\frac{1}{\norm{\widetilde{m}}_{(k)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-r-1}\widetilde{m}_{i}e_{i}+\theta\sum_{j\in S}e_{j}\right)

(67)

for some
S\subseteq\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}
satisfying
|S|=r+1
. Denote by
\Omega
the collection of all such
S
. Then we can without loss of generality write
\sigma^{\star}=\bbE_{S}w(S)w(S)^{\dagger}
for some random
S\in\Omega
. Next, observe that

\displaystyle T_{k}

\displaystyle=\max_{x:\norm{x}_{2}=1}x^{\dagger}\left(vv^{\dagger}-\sigma^{\star}\right)x

(68)

and
m
is an optimal solution for this problem, which leads to the eigenvector equation

(vv^{\dagger}-\sigma^{\star})m=T_{k}m.

Consequently,

\displaystyle T_{k}\widetilde{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{k}m}{\langle m,v\rangle}

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle m,v\rangle}\left[\langle v,m\rangle v-\bbE_{S}\ \langle w(S),m\rangle w(S)\right]

(70)

\displaystyle=v-\bbE_{S}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k-r-1}\widetilde{m}_{i}e_{i}+\theta\sum_{j\in S}e_{j}\right]

(71)

\displaystyle=v-\sum_{i=1}^{k-r-1}\widetilde{m}_{i}e_{i}-\theta\sum_{j=k-r}^{\ell-1}q_{j}e_{j}

(72)

where
q_{j}\coloneq\Pr_{S}\left[j\in S\right]
is the marginal probability that the index
j
is included in the random subset
S
, for each
j\in\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}
. Hence, for each
j\in\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}
it holds that
T_{k}\theta=v_{j}-\theta q_{j}
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle q_{j}=\frac{v_{j}}{\theta}-T_{k}.

(73)

One may verify that
\sum_{j=k-r}^{\ell-1}q_{j}=r+1
by making use of the fact that
\theta=\theta^{v}_{r,\ell}(\lambda)
, as in
5.5
, which implies these are valid single-element marginal probabilities, following the discussion in
Section

4
.

We have successfully derived the fact that
\sigma^{\star}
is a mixture of pure states
w(S)
where
S
is a random subset of
\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}
of fixed size
r+1
, whose marginal inclusion probabilities satisfy
Eq.

73
. Since each quantity on the right-hand side of that equation can be efficiently computed (see
Algorithm

1
) we can also compute these marginal probabilities efficiently.

Now, suppose that taking
S
to have the max-entropy distribution
p^{\star}
(as in
Section

4
) subject to the marginal constraints given by the
q_{j}
, results in a density matrix
\sigma^{\star}=\bbE_{S\sim p^{\star}}w(S)w(S)^{\top}
which is optimal. Then by
4.3
, using
\textnormal{poly}(d,\log(1/\eta))
operations we can find a set of weights
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, specifying a distribution
p_{\mu}
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\textnormal{TV}}(p^{\star},p_{\mu})\leq\eta
. Setting
\sigma\coloneq\bbE_{S\sim p_{\mu}}w(S)w(S)^{\top}
, this implies
T(\sigma,\sigma^{\star})\leq\eta
and, by Triangle Inequality,
T(v,\sigma)\leq T_{k}+\eta
. Moreover, setting
y\coloneq m_{1:k-r-1}
we have

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{1}{\norm{m}_{(k)}^{2}}\left[yy^{\top}+\theta\sum_{i=k-r}^{\ell-1}q(\mu)_{i}(ye_{i}^{\top}+e_{i}y^{\top})+\theta^{2}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=k-r}^{\ell-1}Q(\mu)_{jj^{\prime}}e_{j}e_{j^{\prime}}^{\top}\right]

(74)

where
q(\mu)_{i}\coloneq\Pr_{S\sim p_{\mu}}[i\in S]
and
Q(\mu)_{ij}\coloneq\Pr_{S\sim p_{\mu}}\left[i\in S\land j\in S\right]
for every
i,j\in\{k-r,\dots,\ell-1\}
are the single- and two-element marginal inclusion probabilities. By
4.1
, each term in the square brackets can be computed efficiently, and the claim follows.

It remains to show that taking
S
to have the max-entropy distribution
p^{\star}
subject to these marginal constraints results in an optimal density matrix
\sigma^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
. But this follows from the observation that for any two distributions
p_{1},p_{2}:\Omega\to[0,1]
with the same single-element marginal probabilities, we necessarily have

\displaystyle\left(\bbE_{S_{1}\sim p_{1}}w(S_{1})w(S_{1})^{\top}-\bbE_{S_{2}\sim p_{2}}w(S_{2})w(S_{2})^{\top}\right)m

\displaystyle=\norm{m}_{(k)}\left(\bbE_{S_{1}\sim p_{1}}w(S_{1})-\bbE_{S_{2}\sim p_{2}}w(S_{2})\right)

(75)

\displaystyle=0,

(76)

and this holds in particular for
p_{1}=p^{\star}
and
p_{2}
any choice of distribution over the subsets
S\in\Omega
which results in an optimal density matrix.
∎

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the
\eta
-accurate approximation to the optimal density matrix obtained by the algorithm in

. Moreover, subsequent samples can be obtained in

Take the ensemble of pure states to be the random pure state
w(S)
defined in
Eq.

, where

is a random subset of indices having a distribution which is

5.3
Coherence versus entanglement for the trace distance

In this section we show that for approximating pure states by density matrices with low Schmidt number, the results on the
k
-sparse approximations carry over directly.
Recall that given any state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b})
, the optimal approximation by a state with Schmidt rank at most
k
is the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle T_{k}^{(E)}(\rho):=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}T(\rho,\sigma).

(77)

We prove the following.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}
have a Schmidt decomposition

\displaystyle{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\;{x_{i}}\otimes{y_{i}}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})^{\top}.

(78)

It holds that
T_{k}^{(E)}(v)=T_{k}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, choose the Schmidt basis to be the standard basis, so

\displaystyle{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\;e_{i}\otimes e_{i},

(79)

and define the
k
-sparse states with respect to the basis of states
e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
.
First of all it is clear that
T_{k}^{(E)}(v)\leq T_{k}(v)
since, with this definition of sparsity,
\mathcal{I}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

In order to conclude that we have equality, we need to show that we can restrict the optimization over
\mathcal{S}_{k}
which defines
T_{k}^{(E)}(v)
to
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. To this end, observe that the argument of
5.3
applies with
\mathcal{I}_{k}
replaced by
\mathcal{S}_{k}
without alteration, which shows that

\displaystyle T_{k}^{(E)}(v)

\displaystyle=\max_{M:\norm{M}_{\infty}\leq 1}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\tr\left(M(vv^{\dagger}-\sigma)\right)

(80)

\displaystyle=\max_{m:\norm{m}_{2}\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\langle m, v\rangle}^{2}-\norm{m}_{(E,k)}^{2}.

(81)

where
\norm{m}_{(E,k)}^{2}\coloneq\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\tr(m m^\dagger\sigma)
equals the sum of the squares of the
k
largest Schmidt coefficients of
{m}
, by
2.5
.
Once we show that the optimal
{m}
is of the form
{m}=\sum_{i}m_{i}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
, we can conclude that
T_{k}^{(E)}({v})=T_{k}({v})
, since then it is clear we can optimize over
\mathcal{I}_{k}
instead of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
Consider an arbitrary
{m}=\sum_{ij}m_{ij}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
.
We denote by
e_{ij}
the matrix
e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}
.
Let
P=\sum_{i}e_{ii}\otimes e_{ii}
and let
{\tilde{m}}=\sum_{i}m_{ii}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}=P{m}
.
Let
\Phi:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b})
be the quantum channel with the action
\Phi:X\mapsto\bbE UXU^{\dagger}
where
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b})
is a random unitary operator of the form

\displaystyle U=\sum_{i,j}z_{i}z_{j}^{\star}e_{ii}\otimes e_{jj}

(82)

with uniformly random phases
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
|z_{i}|=1
. This implies that
\Phi
acts on matrix elements through

\displaystyle e_{ij}\otimes e_{kl}\mapsto\begin{cases}e_{ij}\otimes e_{kl}&\text{if }i=j\text{ and }k=l\text{ or }i=k\text{ and }j=l\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(83)

Furthermore, the channel is self-adjoint
\Phi=\Phi^{\dagger}
and preserves Schmidt number.
Acting on the rank-1 projection operator
mm^{\dagger}
gives

\displaystyle\Phi(mm^{\dagger})=\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\dagger}+\sum_{i\neq j}\absolutevalue{m_{ij}}^{2}e_{ii}\otimes e_{jj}\eqcolon\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\dagger}+B

(84)

for some operator
B
which is positive semidefinite.
In particular, for any
{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{a}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{b}
with Schmidt rank at most
k
, we have

\displaystyle|\langle\tilde{m},w\rangle|^{2}=\tr\left(mm^{\dagger}\Phi(ww^{\dagger})\right)-w^{\dagger}Bw\leq\norm{m}_{(E,k)}^{2}

(85)

where the inequality follows from the fact that
\Phi(ww^{\dagger})\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
and the second term is at most zero. Maximizing over
{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
we arrive at
\norm{\tilde m}_{(E,k)}\leq\norm{m}_{(E,k)}
. Since
\absolutevalue{\langle m, v \rangle}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\langle\tilde m, v \rangle}^{2}
we conclude that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\langle m, v \rangle}^{2}-\norm{m}_{(E,k)}^{2}\leq\absolutevalue{\langle\tilde m, v \rangle}^{2}-\norm{\tilde m}_{(E,k)}^{2}

(86)

and hence we can maximize over
{m}
of the form
{m}=\sum_{i}m_{i}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
.
∎

5.4
Variance estimates for ensembles

A randomized truncation yields a
k
-sparse state
{w(S)}
with some probability. Given an observable
M
(along with a method to compute its expectation with respect to a target state) one may use the random variable
w(S)^{\dagger}Mw(S)
as an estimator for the original expectation value of interest, incurring some bias and variance in the process. (For a deterministic truncation, there is no variance.) The trace distance value bounds the bias, while the variance can be related to the quality of an approximation using the individual states
{w(S)}
comprising the ensemble. In this section, we record bounds on the variance in such a process, which may inform the feasibility of using these methods in numerical simulations, e.g., using MPS. (See
Section

8
.) In fact, these bounds hold for generic ensembles of pure states.

Recall the trace distance is given by

\displaystyle T(v,\sigma)=\max_{\norm{M}_{\infty}\leq 1}\tr\left(M(vv^{\dagger}-\sigma)\right)=\max_{\norm{M}_{\infty}\leq 1}\E\tr\left(M(vv^{\dagger}-w(S)w(S)^{\dagger})\right).

(87)

We have the following.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
S
be a random variable, let
v
be an arbitrary pure state, and let
w(S)
be a random pure state such that
\E w(S)w(S)^{\dagger}=\sigma
.

1.

The expected value of the trace distance between
w(S)
and
v
is bounded by

\E T(v,w(S))\leq\sqrt{T(v,\sigma)}.

(88)

2.

Let
M
be any observable with
\norm{M}_{\infty}\leq 1
. It holds that

\Var[w(S)^{\dagger}Mw(S)]\leq T(v,\sigma)\left(1+\sqrt{T(v,\sigma)}\right)^{2}.

(89)

Proof.

We can bound the second moment of the random variable
T(v,w(S))
through

\displaystyle\E T(v,w(S))^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\E|\langle v,w(S)\rangle|^{2}=1-F(v,\sigma)^{2}\leq T(v,\sigma)

(90)

where the second equality uses
\sigma=\E w(S)w(S)^{\dagger}
and the inequality follows from
Eq.

8
. Then the first bound in the lemma is a consequence of the relationship between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
norms for random variables. For the second bound, note that

\displaystyle\left|\tr\left(M(vv^{\dagger}-w(S)w(S)^{\dagger})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq T(v,w(S))

(91)

which implies

\displaystyle\E\tr\left(M(vv^{\dagger}-w(S)w(S)^{\dagger})\right)^{2}\leq\E T(v,w(S))^{2}\leq T(v,\sigma).

(92)

Let
\norm{X}_{L_{2}}
denote the
L_{2}
norm of a random variable
X
. By Triangle Inequality and
Eq.

92
,

\displaystyle\norm{w(S)^\dag M w(S) - \Tr(\sigma M)}_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Tr(M(\sigma- vv^\dag))\right|+\sqrt{T(v,\sigma)}

(93)

The claim follows upon noting that the first term on the right-hand side above is at most
T(v,\sigma)
, and
\E w(S)^{\dagger}Mw(S)=\Tr(\sigma M)
.
∎

These bounds are fairly constraining: suppose that
F_{k}(v)=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
and we are in a case where randomized truncation is close to the optimal value allowed by the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities, i.e.,
T(v,\sigma)=O(\varepsilon)
. Then the above argument shows that
on average
the trace distance
T(v,w(S))
must be
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
. But we also know that the optimal pure state approximation has trace distance
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, so the states
{w(S)}
must be close to the optimal deterministic approximations with high probability. Additionally, in this “good” regime the standard deviation of the estimator is at most
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, which is on the same order as the error from the best deterministic truncation even after a single sample. Obtaining multiple samples using the randomized truncation then decreases the variance, from this already-competitive starting point.

Approximations in robustness

Recall that a simple expression for the robustness with
\mathcal{I}_{k}
of a pure state is derived in
[
JLP+18
]
. (See
2.12
.) Namely, for any unit vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we have

R_{k}(v)={\lVert v\rVert}_{(k,*)}^{2}-1.

By the characterization of the
k
-support norm from
2.10
, this value is efficiently computable
[
AFS12
]
, in time
O(d\log d)
.
In this section, we show that the optimal density matrix in robustness is also efficiently computable and samplable, via the same max-entropy construction used in
Section

5.2
.

6.1
Computing and sampling the mixed approximation in robustness

The following result can be shown using a simpler version of the construction leading to
5.7
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
satisfy
2\leq k\leq d
, let
\eta\in(0,1)
, and let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector. There is an algorithm, requiring at most
\textnormal{poly}(k,d,\log(1/\eta))
steps, which computes the entries in the standard basis of a density matrix
\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
such that

T(\tau,\tau^{\star})\leq\eta

where
\tau^{\star}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tau^{\star}=\frac{vv^{\dagger}+R_{k}(v)\sigma}{1+R_{k}(v)}\quad\textnormal{for some}\quad\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{2}.

(94)

Proof.

We use the shorthand
R_{k}=R_{k}(v)
and, as in the proof of
5.7
, assume that
v
is real, nonnegative, and sorted in nonincreasing order.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of all subsets
S\subseteq\{k-r,\dots,d\}
satisfying
|S|=r+1
.
For any
S\in\mathcal{S}
, define the state

\displaystyle u(S)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{k-r-1}v_{i}e_{i}+\frac{s_{k-r}^{v}}{r+1}\sum_{j\in S}e_{j}

(95)

These states are not generally unit vectors; in fact,
\langle u(S),u(S)\rangle=1+R_{k}
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
. We use them to construct the (not necessarily normalized) mixture

\widetilde{\tau}=\bbE_{S}u(S)u(S)^{\dagger}

(96)

for a yet-to-be-specified distribution over
\mathcal{S}
. Then
\tr(\widetilde{\tau})=1+R_{k}
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}

\displaystyle=v_{1:k-r}v_{1:k-r}^{\top}+\frac{s^{v}_{k-r}}{r+1}\sum_{i=k-r}^{d}q_{i}(v_{1:k-r}e_{i}^{\top}+e_{i}v_{1:k-r}^{\top})+\left(\frac{s^{v}_{j-r}}{r+1}\right)^{2}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=k-r}^{d}Q_{j,j^{\prime}}e_{j}e_{j^{\prime}}^{\top}

(97)

where
q_{i}=\Pr_{S}[i\in S]
and
Q_{ij}\coloneq\Pr_{S}\left[i\in S\land j\in S\right]
for every
i,j\in\{k-r,\dots,d\}
, are the single- and two-element marginal inclusion probabilities.
Now, suppose that the distribution over
S\in\mathcal{S}
is the max-entropy distribution whose single-element marginals are given by
q_{i}=v_{i}(r+1)/s_{k-r}^{v}
. Evidently,
\sum_{i=k-r}^{d}q_{i}=r+1
, so this is a valid set of marginal probabilities. Then

\displaystyle\Delta\coloneq\widetilde{\tau}-vv^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s_{k-r}^{v}}{r+1}\right)^{2}\sum_{i,j=k-r}^{d}\left(Q_{ij}-q_{i}q_{j}\right)e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}.

(98)

Since, for every
i=k-r,\dots,d
we have
Q_{ii}=q_{i}
, the matrix
\Delta
has diagonal elements

\Delta_{ii}=v_{i}\quantity(\frac{s_{k-r}^v}{r+1} - v_i),

(99)

which are positive by the condition in
Eq.

16
. On the other hand, by
4.2
,
Q_{ij}<q_{i}q_{j}
for every
i,j\in\{k-r,\dots,d\}
with
i\neq j
. Finally, we can compute the
i^{\text{th}}
row sum of
\Delta
as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k-r}^{d}\Delta_{i,j}\propto\sum_{j=k-r}^{d}(Q_{ij}-q_{i}q_{j})

\displaystyle=\bbE_{S}\mathbf{1}_{i\in S}\left(\sum_{j=k-r}^{d}\mathbf{1}_{j\in S}\right)-q_{i}(r+1)=0.

(100)

In summary,
\Delta
has (i) nonnegative diagonals, (ii) nonpositive off diagonals, and (iii) rows summing to 0. Such matrices are known as diagonally dominant and are proportional to an element of
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, by Lemma 1 in
[
JLP+18
]
. Thus, we have established:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}=vv^{\dagger}+R_{k}\sigma\qquad\textnormal{for some}\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{2}.

(101)

Setting
\tau^{\star}=\widetilde{\tau}/(1+R_{k})
gives the desired optimal density matrix. Of course, we cannot compute exactly the one- and two-element marginals corresponding to the max-entropy distribution. But we can do so for an approximation that is
\eta
close in total variation distance in time
\textnormal{poly}(d,\log(1/\eta))
. This leads to an approximation
\tau
to
\tau^{\star}
which is
\eta
-close in trace distance, using Lemmas
4.1
and
4.3
, in exactly the same way as in the proof of
5.7
.
∎

The construction above also tells us how to efficiently sample from the ensemble corresponding to the robustness-optimal density matrix, as in the case for the trace distance.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the

-accurate approximation to the optimal density matrix obtained by the algorithm in

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of
5.8
upon taking the random pure state to be proportional to

, defined in

, rather than

.

7
Examples

We have derived the
optimal

k
-sparse trace distance approximations to pure states.
If
v
is a pure state, and
v_{k}
is the optimal pure
k
-sparse approximation, then we know that
F(v,v_{k})^{2}+T(v,v_{k})^{2}=1
.
In other words, if
F_{k}(v)=F(v,v_{k})=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
, we have
T(v,v_{k})=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
On the other hand, when we allow approximation by mixed states, the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities as in

only constrain the optimal trace distance as

T_{k}(v)=T(v,\rho)\geq 1-F(v,\rho)^{2}\geq 1-F_{k}(v)^{2}=\varepsilon

In the introduction, we already saw an example where indeed

.
However, there are also cases where

.

This raises the question: is such a quadratic improvement in approximation by allowing randomized truncation a very special case, or is it common?
We will now give a broad set of examples, which shows that a quadratic improvement happens in many cases.
The examples are discussed from the perspective of
k
-sparsity, but also all have bipartite entanglement equivalents.
In these examples, we will denote by

the dimension of the Hilbert space, and we let
\varepsilon
denote truncation error in fidelity, so

. If we fix an allowed error
\varepsilon

that achieves this error (so we can write

or

).

The easiest way to upper bound

is by either giving an explicit approximation

, or by using the robustness, which is computed by the
k
-support norm, using
2.10
.
Lower bounding

can either be done by the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities, or by explicitly choosing a vector

which lower bounds the optimization problem in

Uniform state.

A state for which there is no advantage.

8
Application to matrix product states

We now describe a natural application of our framework to tensor networks. We focus on matrix product states (MPS), but the principle applies to other tensor network states as well.
Tensor networks are a natural area of application. In many algorithms, the bond dimension increases, for instance, when applying a two-qubit gate in a quantum circuit. Truncating the bond dimension then limits the computational and memory cost of the simulation and is a crucial subroutine of many tensor network algorithms
[
Bañ23
]
. In the usual approach, the first step is to bring the MPS into canonical form, which yields the Schmidt coefficients, or
entanglement spectrum
, along each bond. To attain the optimal fidelity after a single truncation, one may deterministically keep the largest Schmidt coefficients. At this point, one option is to truncate the bonds one at a time, bringing the MPS into canonical form again after each truncation. The quality of this approximation depends on the entanglement spectrum of the different subsystems.

The deterministic-truncation approach is designed to optimize the fidelity with the target state.
In applications where one cares about expectation values of observables, this may not be the most natural figure of merit.
Using randomized truncation, we may be able to obtain better approximations of expectation values, with relatively low bond dimensions. This comes at a cost: we now have a randomized approximation, which has lower error, but has some variance and we may need to take multiple samples and repeat the calculation to get a good estimate.

We will now describe the setting and application more precisely.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
with computational basis
\{e_{i_{1}\dots i_{n}}=e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}}:i_{j}\in[d]\}
.
A
matrix product state
(MPS) of bond dimensions
(r_{0},\dots,r_{n})
with
r_{0}=r_{n}=1
is defined by

v\;=\;\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in[d]}\Big(A^{[1]}_{i_{1}}A^{[2]}_{i_{2}}\cdots A^{[n]}_{i_{n}}\Big)\,e_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}},

(133)

where for each site
j
,
A^{[j]}_{i_{j}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{j-1}\times r_{j}}
.

Across the bipartition
[1{:}m]
versus
[m{+}1{:}n]
, we can write a Schmidt decomposition

v\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{r_{m}}s^{(m)}_{j}\,e^{(m)}_{j}\otimes f^{(m)}_{j},\qquad s^{(m)}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s^{(m)}_{r_{m}}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{r_{m}}\big(s^{(m)}_{j}\big)^{2}=1,

(134)

with orthonormal Schmidt vectors
\{e^{(m)}_{j}\}
and
\{f^{(m)}_{j}\}
. In canonical form,
the
s^{(m)}_{j}
are the singular values of the center matrix
\Lambda_{m}
on bond
m
.

We first review the usual deterministic bond dimension truncation. We fix a target bond dimension
D
(this takes the role of the parameter
k
).
The deterministic truncation at bond
m

keeps the
D
largest Schmidt coefficients
\{s^{(m)}_{j}\}_{j\leq D}
and discards the rest,
i.e. projects onto
\Span\{e^{(m)}_{j}\otimes f^{(m)}_{j}:j\leq D\}
.
Let
w^{(m)}
denote the normalized truncated state at that single cut.
Then the fidelity satisfies

F(v,w^{(m)})\;=\;\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{(m)}}\qquad\varepsilon^{(m)}\coloneqq\sum_{j>D}\!\big(s^{(m)}_{j}\big)^{2}.

(135)

Since we perform the truncation with respect to a specified cut, this is exactly the same as the problem of approximating a pure bipartite state by a state with Schmidt rank at most
D
.

Figure 5
:
Estimating
\langle Z_{5}\rangle
(the Pauli-Z operator on the 5
{}^{\text{th}}
qubit) with respect to four randomly generated states on 9 qubits. We first express each state as an MPS in canonical form. We then replace the Schmidt coefficient tensors along each of the 8 edges with a diagonal tensor whose entries obey a power law scaling with parameter

, as in

estimate is the sample mean of 100 samples obtained by applying the optimal (bipartite) randomized truncation in trace distance to each bond. For smaller values of
\gamma
, there is a clear improvement using randomized truncation.

If we truncate
every
bond to dimension at most
D
simultaneously to obtain an MPS

w
, then the truncations are independent and the overall fidelity satisfies
[
VC06
]

F(v,w)\geq\sqrt{1-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\varepsilon^{(m)}}.

(136)

The special case of truncating a single bond corresponds to approximation by a state with Schmidt rank at most
D
.
The general case, of approximating a many-body state by an MPS with bond dimension at most
D
on all bonds is more complicated.
The above strategy is not necessarily optimal (even for the fidelity). Indeed, in contrast to the bipartite case, it is known that the best product state approximation (corresponding to
D=1
) to a multipartite state is
\NP
-hard to compute, already for
n=3
parties
[
HL13
]
, so one does not expect efficient algorithms for finding globally optimal MPS approximations.

In light of the above discussion, and our results on optimal low Schmidt rank approximations, we propose a randomized truncation scheme.
At each bond truncation step, instead of deterministically keeping the top
D
Schmidt coefficients, we compute the optimal trace distance (or robustness) approximation by bond dimension
D
, and sample efficiently using the algorithms from
Section

5.2
or
Section

6
.

The expected effect of this randomized truncation is a lower trace-distance error to the target state, which is associated to a lower worst-case error in the expectation value of observables. What this means for the observables we care about in practice is a complicated question which we will mostly leave to future work. What does “in practice” means? And which observables do we care about?

As a preliminary investigation,
we show in
Figure

5
an example of estimating the expectation value of a local observable with respect to a random MPS on 11 qubits, using both deterministic and randomized truncation, and observe a consistent improvement using randomized truncation.
When we use randomized truncation, we may need to take multiple samples (i.e. repeat the calculation multiple times) to reduce the variance of the estimate.
We have proven in
5.10
that the variance of randomized truncation is not too large, and numerically we observe that the variance is well behaved in the example in
Fig.

5
. In particular the standard deviation of randomized estimator is on the order of the bias (deterministic error) of the previous deterministic method.

This randomized truncation scheme can in principle be used in any MPS algorithm that requires bond dimension truncation; whether it is beneficial in practice will depend on the application at hand and how well trace distance or robustness correlate with the relevant metrics.
Crucially, the computational cost of the randomized truncation is comparable to that of the standard deterministic truncation. It requires computing the Schmidt decomposition at each bond (which is also needed in any case for deterministic truncation), and then applying the algorithms from
Section

5.2
or
Section

6
. These algorithms require computing the weights (which can be done efficiently, and in practice has cost
O(r_{m}D)
per iteration when using the Newton method with the diagonal approximation), and then the sampling step takes
O(D)
, which is subleading compared to the SVD step.
We should now compare the cost of taking multiple samples using randomized truncation, with the cost of a larger bond dimension using deterministic truncation to reach the same precision. We leave a detailed analysis of this tradeoff to future work.

The use of a randomized tensor network truncation algorithm has previously been proposed by Ferris
[
Fer15
]
, which we discuss for comparison.
There are two key differences. Before getting into the differences, we can understand both our robustness-optimal scheme and Ferris’ as examples of using importance sampling to produced unbiased
k
-sparse estimators of the target vector
v
. The general framework in both cases is to choose a distribution
p
over
S\in\binom{[d]}{k}
and to replace
v
with a random vector

w(S)=\sum_{i\in S}\frac{v_{i}}{q_{i}k}e_{i},

(137)

with
S\sim p
and
q_{i}\coloneq p[i\in S]
(as in
Eq.

4
). The
v_{i}/q_{i}
coefficients arise from the importance-sampling framework and yield the unbiased condition
\bbE[w(S)]=v
.
(We are glossing over the normalization condition which we use but Ferris doesn’t. Thus our states actually satisfy
\bbE[w(S)]=(1+R)^{-1/2}v
.)

One somewhat superficial difference between our approach and Ferris’s is that we use normalized states and Ferris does not. This is a matter of convention. Since his states are unbiased, their expectation is normalized, and so each state in the ensemble is close to normalized. Iterating his method enough times will lead to an exponentially growing variance
[
Tod24
]
, while iterating approximations using normalized states will instead incur growing bias.

A larger difference is that our paper chooses the
q_{i}
in a way that minimizes robustness. In
Section

4
we describe how to find
\mu
from
q
. Ferris, by contrast, simply sets
e^{-\mu_{i}}\coloneq v_{i}^{2}
. This is a reasonable heuristic, since larger values of
v_{i}
yield larger values of
q_{i}
. But it is not justified beyond this.
How Ferris’ approach compares with our partially randomized approach is an interesting question for future work.

Appendix A
Background material

For the convenience of the reader, we collect a few standard definitions and facts from convex analysis, nonlinear optimization and matrix analysis used in the proofs in the main text.

A.1
Minimax and zero-sum games

A minimax inequality of the form

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)\leq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)

(138)

holds for arbitrary choices of sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
and a function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. Here and throughout when we take maxima and minima of functions, it is implied that these values are attained. Such an inequality admits a game-theoretic interpretation as follows. Consider a zero-sum, two-player game where the payoff function for the
x
-player is
-f(x,y)
and that for the
y
-player is
+f(x,y)
. Then the right-hand side of
Eq.

138
represents the worst-case payoff for the
y
-player when they “go second” in the game, i.e., the strategy for the
y
-player may depend on
x
. Through this lens, the minimax inequality amounts to the statement that going second in the game is a privilege. We encounter a specific example of such a two-player game in
Section

5
.

A minimax theorem claims that the minimax inequality holds with equality under certain conditions. One example which suffices for our purposes is provided below. We refer the interested reader to
[
Sim95
]
for a history of other minimax theorems.

Theorem A.1

(Implied by Thm. 3 in
[
Fan52
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be compact, convex subsets of a Hilbert space and
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function such that:

1.

f(\cdot,y)
is continuous and convex on
\mathcal{X}
for every fixed
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and

2.

f(x,\cdot)
is continuous and concave on
\mathcal{Y}
for every fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Then

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y).

(139)

A related concept is the existence of a saddle point, also referred as an equilbrium point in the context of two-player games.

Definition A.2

(Saddle point)
.

Given arbitrary sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
and a function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, a point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a saddle point if

\displaystyle f(x^{\star},y)\leq f(x^{\star},y^{\star})\leq f(x,y^{\star})

(140)

for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

The following lemma states that the existence of a saddle point is equivalent to a minimax equality where both the minimum and the maximum are attained.

Lemma A.3

(Well-known)
.

A saddle point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
for the function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
exists if and only if

\displaystyle f(x^{\star},y^{\star})

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y).

(141)

Proof.

Suppose
Eq.

139
holds. Then we may choose

\displaystyle x^{\star}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y),\qquad y^{\star}\in\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)

in which case

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y^{\star})\leq f(x^{\star},y^{\star})\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x^{\star},y)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)

and, by the minimax equality, the two inequalities hold with equality. Hence, the relations in
Eq.

140
are satisfied.

On the other hand, suppose there is a saddle point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Then

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x^{\star},y)\leq f(x^{\star},y^{\star})\leq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y^{\star})\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)

(142)

where the second and third inequalities use the relations in
Eq.

140
. But this is an inequality in the opposite direction of the minimax inequality, which always holds.
∎

A.2
Convex analysis

We now list some standard facts from convex analysis. See
[
BL06
]
for an introduction to these concepts.

Definition A.4

(Subgradient and subdifferential)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. A vector
g\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
subgradient
of
f
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

\displaystyle f(y)-f(x)\;\geq\;\langle g,\,y-x\rangle\qquad\text{for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(143)

The set of all such subgradients is the
subdifferential
of
f
at
x
, denoted
\partial f(x)
.

Definition A.5

(Normal cone)
.

For
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x\in\Omega
, the
normal cone
of
\Omega
at
x
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\Omega}(x)=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \langle g,\,y-x\rangle\leq 0\ \text{for all }y\in\Omega\}.

(144)

Lemma A.6

(Fermat’s rule)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
have nonempty relative interior. A point
x\in\Omega
minimizes
f
over
\Omega
if and only if

\displaystyle 0\in\partial f(x)+\mathcal{N}_{\Omega}(x).

(145)

This first-order optimality condition is valid even when the objective function is not differentiable. If
f
is differentiable and the feasible set is specified using equality constraints only then this gives back the usual method of Lagrange multipliers.

Given a convex set
\mathcal{X}
in a finite-dimensional real inner-product space and
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
Fenchel conjugate

f^{*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle f^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\bigl\{\langle x,y\rangle-f(x)\bigr\}.

(146)

Lemma A.7
.

Let
\norm{\cdot}
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{x}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle f^{*}(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{y}_{*}^{2},

(147)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{*}
is the dual norm, defined as
\norm{y}_{*}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Bqty{\langle x,y\rangle: \norm{x}\leq 1}
.

Corollary A.8
.

Let
\norm{\cdot}
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with dual
\norm{\cdot}_{*}
. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

y\in\partial(\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\cdot}^{2})(x)
, and
x
optimizes
\max_{x^{\prime}}\{\langle x^{\prime},y\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{x'}^{2}\}
.

2.

x\in\partial(\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\cdot}_{*}^{2})(y)
, and
y
optimizes
\max_{y^{\prime}}\{\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{y'}_{*}^{2}\}
.

3.

\norm{x}^{2}+\norm{y}_{*}^{2}=2\langle x,y\rangle
.

A.3
Spectral stability

The following is a well-known consequence of Weyl’s Inequality concerning the spectral stability of Hermitian matrices. We let
\lambda_{1}(A),\lambda_{2}(A),\dots
denote the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix
A
sorted in nonincreasing order.

Fact A.9

(Spectral stability)
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
1\leq k\leq d
and
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be Hermitian matrices. It holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(A+B)-\lambda_{k}(A)\in[\lambda_{d}(B),\lambda_{1}(B)].

Appendix B
Faster algorithms for max-entropy sampling

In this section we prove
4.1
. We use notation defined there. Here the algorithms for
Z
and
p_{\mu}
are new, while those for
q
and
Q
are lightly adapted from
[
Til06
]
, which in turn is summarizing earlier work.

Computing the partition function
Z
.

Thm 3 of
[
CDL94
]
states that for
1\leq\ell\leq|S|
we have

Z(\ell,S)=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{j+1}p_{j}(S)Z(\ell-j,S),

(148)

where
p_{j}(S)
is the power-sum symmetric polynomial

p_{j}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}e^{-j\mu_{i}}.

(149)

Thus, given
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
, we can compute
Z(1,S),\ldots,Z(\ell,S)
in time
O(\ell^{2})
.

A naive computation of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
would take time
O(n\ell)
. However, they can be computed in time
O(n\poly\log(n))
using algorithm attributed to Strassen in Corollary 2.25 of
[
BLCS96
]
. The rough idea is that all the power sums can be encoded in terms of a power series
g(t)=\sum_{j\geq 0}p_{j}t^{j}
, which can be calculated using

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-x_{j}t}=n-t\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}

(150)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_{i}t).

(151)

We can compute
f(t)
(thinking of it as a univariate polynomial) using a multiplication tree. This entails
n/2
multiplications of degree-1 polynomials,
n/4
multiplications of degree-2 polynomials, etc., for a total time of
O(n\log^{2}n)
, assuming that multiplying degree-
n
polynomials takes time
O(n\log n)
. For the rest of the calculation we only need to track the coefficients of
1,t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{\ell}
; formally, we can do our computations modulo
t^{\ell+1}
.

Putting this together we obtain a runtime of
O(n\log^{2}n+\ell^{2})
.

Computing
q
and
Q
.

Write
q_{j}^{(\ell)}
to make the
\ell
-dependence of
q_{j}
explicit. Then
Result 22(i) from
[
Til06
]
states that

q_{j}^{(\ell)}=e^{-\mu_{j}}(1-q_{j}^{(\ell-1)})\frac{Z(\ell-1,[n])}{Z(\ell,[n])}.

(152)

Note that
q_{j}(0)=0
. Then given knowledge of
Z(1,[n]),\ldots,Z(\ell,[n])
from the previous step we can compute each
q_{j}
in time
O(\ell)
. Computing all of
q
takes time
O(n\ell)
.
(Result 22(iii) from
[
Til06
]
is another option, yielding the same runtime.)

For
Q_{ij}
, when
\mu_{i}\neq\mu_{j}
there is a formula that can be evaluated in constant time. In this case, Result 23(ii) from
[
Til06
]
states

Q_{ij}=\frac{q_{i}e^{-\mu_{j}}-q_{j}e^{-\mu_{i}}}{e^{-\mu_{j}}-e^{-\mu_{i}}}.

(153)

The remaining entries of
Q
can be computed using the fact that
Q_{ii}=q_{i}
and
\sum_{j}Q_{ij}=q_{i}n
. Concretely,
when
\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}
, define
C\coloneq\absolutevalue{\{ j : j\neq i, \mu_j = \mu_i\}}
, and then

Q_{ij}=\frac{1}{C}q_{i}(n-1)-\frac{1}{C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\\
\mu_{k}\neq\mu_{i}\end{subarray}}Q_{ik}\quad\mbox{when}\quad i\neq j,\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}.

(154)

This can also be made
O(1)
time per entry, if we assume that the
\mu
vector is sorted. Thus
Q
can be computed in time
O(n^{2})
.

Sampling from
p_{\mu}
.

Exact sampling is possible in time
O(n\ell)
using Procedure 1 of
[
CDL94
]
, which is also described in Section 5.6.11 of
[
Til06
]
. As the procedure is somewhat involved, we will not reproduce it here. Instead we will describe a method for sampling
p_{\mu}
in expected time
O(n)
that we have not seen previously in the literature.

First, given
\mu
, define the product distribution
P_{\mu}
where
x_{i}
is distributed according to
\text{Ber}(e^{-\mu_{i}})
, i.e. the Bernoulli random variable with
\Pr[x_{i}=1]=e^{-\mu_{i}}
and
\Pr[x_{i}=0]=1-e^{-\mu_{i}}
. Then
p_{\mu}
is equivalent to conditioning
P_{\mu}
on the event
x\in H_{\ell}
. Next, we will shift
\mu
so that
\sum_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}}=\bbE_{P_{\mu}}[|x|]=\ell
. This does not change
p_{\mu}
.

The sampling algorithm is as follows.

•

Sample
x
from
P_{\mu}
.

•

Repeat the following
n
times.

–

If
x\in H_{\ell}
then output
x
and stop.

–

Choose
i\in[n]
uniformly randomly and replace
x_{i}
with
\text{Ber}(e^{-\mu_{i}})
.

We claim that this procedure halts with probability
\Omega(1)
, and conditional on halting, outputs a sample from
p_{\mu}
. To prove the latter claim, let
x{(0)},x{(1)},x{(2)},\ldots
denote the values of
x
produced by the algorithm. By construction,
x{(0)}
is distributed according to
P_{\mu}
. And since the update procedure is Glauber dynamics, the subsequent
x{(t)}
are as well. Finally, the algorithm halts when
x{(t)}\in H_{\ell}
, thus producing a sample from
P_{\mu}
conditioned on
H_{\ell}
. This yields a sample from
p_{\mu}
.

It remains only to analyze the success probability.
Define
s(x)
to be
+1
if
|x|>\ell
,
0
if
|x|=\ell
, and
-1
if
|x|<\ell
. A sufficient, but not necessary, condition for the algorithm to succeed is for
s(x(0))s(x(n))
to equal 0 or -1. If the product is 0, then either
x(0)
or
x(n)
is in
H_{\ell}
. If the product is -1 then the Hamming weight went from
>\ell
to
<\ell
(or vice versa) and so at some intermediate point we had
x(t)\in H_{\ell}
. It is possible for the algorithm to succeed even if
s(x(0))s(x(n))=1
, but we will not analyze this case. Our proof strategy will be use the mixing properties of the walk to show that the signs of
s(x(0))
and
s(x(n))
have bounded correlations, and thus have a decent chance of either having opposite signs or of being zero.

Let
\pi\coloneq P_{\mu}(H_{\ell})
. It turns out that
\pi\gtrsim\ell^{-1/2}
, but we will not use this fact. Darroch
[
Dar64
]
proved that
\ell
is a median for the Hamming weight of
x
. This means that

\absolutevalue{\bbE_{x\sim P_\mu}[s]}_{x\sim P_{\mu}}[s]=\absolutevalue{P_\mu[|x|>\ell]-P_\mu[|x|<\ell]}\leq\pi.

(155)

Let
T
denote the transition matrix of the update step in which we resample a random coordinate of
x
. This is reversible, with spectral gap
1/n
and stationary distribution
P_{\mu}
. For
f,g:\{0,1\}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
, define
\langle f,g\rangle\coloneq\bbE_{x\sim P_{\mu}}[f(x)^{*}g(x)]
. Reversibility means that
T
is Hermitian with respect to this inner product and the gap means that

\langle f,(I-T)f\rangle\geq\frac{1}{n}\Var(f)=\frac{1}{n}\quantity(\langle f,f\rangle- \langle f,1\rangle^2)\ .

(156)

Let
f=s-\bbE[s]
so that
\langle f,1\rangle=0
. Rearranging the gap equation we obtain

\displaystyle\langle f,T^{n}f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\quantity(1-\frac 1n)^{n}\langle f,f\rangle

(157)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-1}(\langle s,s\rangle-\pi^{2})

(158)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-1}(1-\pi^{2})\ .

(159)

On the other hand,
\langle f,T^{n}f\rangle=\langle s,T^{n}s\rangle-\bbE[s]^{2}
.
Thus

\langle s,T^{n}s\rangle\leq e^{-1}(1-\pi^{2})+\bbE[s]^{2}\leq e^{-1}+(1-e^{-1})\pi^{2}\ .

(160)

The LHS is equal to
\bbE[s(x(0))s(x(n))]
and this is
\geq 2\Pr[s(x(0))s(x(n))=1]-1
. Rearranging we find that

\Pr[s(x(0))s(x(n))=1]\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-1})(1-\pi^{2})\ .

(161)

Now suppose that
\pi\leq 1/2
. Then the RHS is
\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-1})(1-1/4)<0.87
and there is a constant probability of success. On the other hand with probability
\pi
the first sample
x(0)
is already in
H_{\ell}
, so if
\pi>1/2
then we also obtain a constant probability of success. Either way, each iteration of the sampling algorithm succeeds with probability
\Omega(1)
, so the expected number of repetitions is
O(1)
.
