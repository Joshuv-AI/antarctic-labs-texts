Functional Laws of Large Numbers for Marked Hawkes Processes and Compound Marked Hawkes Processes
Tomasz R. Bielecki

Abstract
We give functional laws of large numbers for a class of marked Hawkes processes and marked compound Hawkes processes with a general mark space. Our results provide some complement to those presented in e.g. [ BDHM13 ] and [ HX21 ] . As an example we provide an application to analysis of time limit of an insurance ruin process.



Keywords
: marked Hawkes process, marked compound Hawkes process, functional law of large numbers, insurance ruin process

: Primary 60G55, 60G57,   Secondary 97M30

Introduction

Functional limit theorems in the theory of stochastic processes are the results that describe the limiting behavior of families of processes as time goes to infinity. They are important as they generalize single-point limit theorems to describe the behavior of entire paths of processes. Here, we study a special type of functional limit theorems. Specifically, we study functional laws of large numbers for a family of marked Hawkes processes and some families of marked Hawkes processes.

The object of interest here is a marked Hawkes process
N=((T_{n},X_{n}))_{n\geq 1}
with a Borel mark space
(\mathcal{X},\mathbfcal{X})
.
We associate with the process
N
an integer-valued random measure on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\mathbfcal{X})
, also denoted by
N
.

The main objective of this paper is to study the limit of
\frac{N((0,vT],A)}{T}
when
T\rightarrow\infty
for
v\geq 0
and
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
. In this regard, the main result of the paper is Theorem
4.1
.

This result relates to Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
, which deals with the case of a multivariate Hawkes process without simultaneous excitations. Such a process can be seen as a marked Hawkes process with discrete mark space
\mathcal{X}
, where marks identify the univariate components of the process. So, our generalization amounts to considering a general Borel mark space. The techniques of our key technical lemmas are in the spirit of the techniques used in
[
BDHM13
]
. But, because our mark space is not discrete, our proofs are much more intricate.
Theorem
4.1
establishes convergence in the mean square sense, whereas Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
provides uniform convergence in the mean square sense. Please see Remark
4.2
that sheds light on this difference.

Theorem
4.1
also relates to Theorem 3.6 in
[
HX21
]
. Our set-up corresponds to the set-up in
[
HX21
]
with, using their notation,

being a null process and
\mu(\cdot)\equiv m.
This, in particular, means that Condition 3.2 in
[
HX21
]
, assumed in that paper to prove Theorem 3.6, is not satisfied in our set-up, and thus the results of Theorem 3.6 in
[
HX21
]
are not applicable here. In particular, it needs to be noted that the techniques used for the proof of Theorem 3.6 in
[
HX21
]
are different in nature from the techniques the techniques employed in the present paper to prove Theorem
4.1
. To give more insight into the relationship between the present work and
[
HX21
]
and
[
BDHM13
]
we note that the results of Theorem 3.6 in
[
HX21
]
are not applicable in the set-up of
[
BDHM13
]
. In particular, again, the techniques used for the proof of Theorem 3.6 in
[
HX21
]
are different in nature from the techniques the techniques employed in
[
BDHM13
]
to prove Theorem 1 therein. In this regards we note that in the second paragraph of Section 1.1 in

2
Marked Hawkes Process and Compound Marked Hawkes Process

In this section we introduce the objects that we study in this paper.

2.1
Marked Hawkes Process

Here we recall the concept of a marked Hawkes process. The version presented below is a special case of the generalized marked Hawkes process introduced in
[
BJN22
]
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
(\mathcal{X},\mathbfcal{X})
be a Borel space.
We take
\partial
to be a point external to
\mathcal{X}
, and we let
\mathcal{X}^{\partial}:=\mathcal{X}\cup\partial
. On
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
we consider a marked point process (MPP)
N
with mark space
\mathcal{X}
, that is, a sequence of random elements

N=((T_{n},X_{n}))_{n\geq 1},

(2.1)

where for each
n
:

(1)

T_{n}
is a random variable with values in
(0,\infty]
,

(2)

X_{n}
is a random variable with values in
\mathcal{X}^{\partial}
,

(3)

T_{n}\leq T_{n+1}
, and if
T_{n}<+\infty
then
T_{n}<T_{n+1}
,

(4)

X_{n}=\partial
iff
T_{n}=\infty
.

The explosion time of
N
, say
T_{\infty}
, is defined as

T_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}T_{n}.

Following the typical techniques used in the theory of marked point processes (MPPs) we associate with the process
N
an integer-valued random measure on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\mathbfcal{X})
, also denoted by
N
and defined as

N(dt,dx):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\delta_{(T_{n},X_{n})}(dt,dx)\mathbbm{1}_{\{T_{n}<\infty\}},

(2.2)

so that

N((0,t],A)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{T_{n}\leq t\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{n}\in A\}},

where
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
. We postulate that the corresponding Hawkes kernel is given as

\kappa(t,A)=\left(m+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{X}}f(t-s,x)N(ds,dx)\right)\mathbb{Q}(A),

(2.3)

for
t\geq 0
and
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is a probability measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathbfcal{X})
,
m
is a non-negative constant, and
f(\cdot,\cdot)
is a non-negative, bounded measurable function such that

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|f(\cdot,x)\|^{2}_{L^{1}}\mathbb{Q}(dx)<\infty.

(2.4)

Proposition 2.7 in
[
BJN22
]
establishes that under this set-up the sequence
X_{i},\ i=1,2,\ldots
is an i.i.d. sequence with the law of
X_{1}
equal to
\mathbb{Q}
. Moreover, the random variables
X_{n+1},\ n=1,2,\ldots
are independent from
T_{i},\ i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Proposition 2.5 in
[
BJN22
]
gives sufficient conditions on
\kappa
under which
T_{\infty}=\infty
almost surely. In the set-up of the present paper these conditions read that

\rhd

there exist real-valued, continuous and non-negative functions
\beta
and
\gamma
such that for
t\geq 0
we have
f(t-s,x)\leq\beta(t)\gamma(s)
,

\rhd

\mathbb{E}(\kappa(\cdot,\mathcal{X}))
is continuous.

Both these conditions are satisfied for a large class of kernels
\kappa
, such as the exponential ones for example.

Thus, the random measure
\nu
defined as

\nu(dt,dx)=\kappa(t,dx)dt

(2.5)

is the compensator of
N
with respect to the natural filtration of
N
.

We use the notation
N(t,A)
for
N((0,t],A)
, and
N(t)
for
N((0,t],\mathcal{X})
. In particular,
N(0,A)=N(0)=0.

2.2
Compound Marked Hawkes Process

Let
N_{t}
,
t\geq 0
be the classical Hawkes process. The classical compound Hawkes process is given as

\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}Z_{n},\quad t\geq 0,

for a sequence
Z_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
of real valued random variables (see e.g.
[
ST10
]
).

This concept generalizes in a natural way to compound marked Hawkes processes.
We will consider two examples of compound marked Hawkes processes that are motivated by applications in insurance, in particular in cyber-insurance (see e.g.
[
ZS21
]
) .

2.2.1
Compound Marked Hawkes Process – Example 1

Fix
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
and let
Z^{A}_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
be a sequence of real valued random variables. We define process
C^{A}
as

C^{A}_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N(t,A)}\,Z^{A}_{n}=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{A}(s)N(ds,A),\quad t\geq 0,

(2.6)

where
\zeta^{A}(s)=Z^{A}_{n}
on
(T_{n-1},T_{n}]
.

In the special case with
A=\mathcal{X}
we simplify the notation to

C:=C^{\mathcal{X}},\ Z:=Z^{\mathcal{X}},\ \zeta:=\zeta^{\mathcal{X}}.

Thus,

C_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}\,Z_{n}=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta(s)N(ds),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.7)

2.2.2
Compound Marked Hawkes Process – Example 2

Let
\varphi(x)
be a real-valued, bounded Borel function. We define process
D^{\varphi}
as

D^{\varphi}_{t}=\int_{(0,t]}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)N(ds,dx)=\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}\varphi(X_{n}),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.8)

This is a special case of (
2.7
) where
Z_{n}=\varphi(X_{n})
.

In the insurance context this would correspond to the amount of claim
\varphi(X_{n})
depending on the type of the claim
X_{n}
.

2.3
Thinning representation

We denote the intensity of
N
by
\lambda(\cdot)
,
so that

\lambda(t)=\kappa(t,\mathcal{X}).

Let
\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz)
be a time homogeneous Poisson random measure on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\mathbfcal{X}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
, defined on an appropriate extension of our probability space, with intensity
ds\mathbb{Q}(dx)dz
. Then, we have for a bounded measurable function
h

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(s,x)N(ds,dx)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}h(s,x)\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz)

see, e.g., Lemma 1 in
[
Mas98
]
. In particular, for
h(s,x)=I_{\{(0,t]\}}(s)I_{\{A\}}(x)
,
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
,

N(t,A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}I_{\{A\}}(x)N(t,dx)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}I_{\{A\}}(x)\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz).

(2.9)

Consequently,

\kappa(t,A)=\mathbb{Q}(A)\left(m+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz)\right).

(2.10)

Thus we obtain the representation of kernel (
2.3
) in terms of a time homogeneous Poisson random measure
\mathcal{N}
. Hence, in
particular,

\lambda(t)=m+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz).

(2.11)

3
Auxiliary results

While proving auxiliary results we will use some ideas from
[
HX21
]
.

Towards this end we first define

F(t)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(t,x)\mathbb{Q}(dx),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.1)

We make the standing assumption that
F
is stable:

\|F\|_{L^{1}}<1.

(3.2)

Taking expectation on both sides in (
2.11
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))

\displaystyle=m+\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)dz\mathbb{Q}(dx)ds\right)

\displaystyle=m+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(s))f(t-s,x)\mathbb{Q}(dx)ds

\displaystyle=m+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(s))F(t-s)ds.

So, we obtain the convolution Volterra equation of the second type

\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))=m+F\ast\mathbb{E}(\lambda)(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.3)

where
\ast
denotes the convolution operator.

Let
r
be the resolvent of
-F
defined as the unique solution to the Volterra convolution equation (
3.4
) below (which exists due the assumption (
3.2
); see Theorem 3.1 and Definition 3.2 in Chapter 2 of
[
GLS90
]
)

r(t)=F\ast r(t)-F(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.4)

Define
R(t)=-r(t),\ t\geq 0.
Then, the unique solution to (
3.3
) is given as (see Theorem 3.5 in Chapter 2 of
[
GLS90
]
on page 44)

\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))=m\left(1+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)ds\right),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.5)

Now, define

F_{1}(t)=F(t),\quad F_{n+1}(t)=F_{n}\ast F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}F_{n}(t-s)F(s)ds,\ n=1,2,3,\ldots.

Then

||F_{n}||_{L^{1}}=||F||^{n}_{L^{1}}.

(3.6)

Indeed, for
n=2
we have

\displaystyle||F_{2}||_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{t}F_{1}(t-s)F(s)ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{\infty}I_{s\leq t}F(t-s)F(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}I_{s\leq t}F(t-s)dt\right)F(s)ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{s}^{\infty}F(t-s)dt\right)F(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(u)du\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s)ds=||F||^{2}_{L^{1}}.

For
n>2
the proof proceeds accordingly.

Under assumption (
3.2
) the series of
F_{n},\ n=1,2,3,\dots
converges in
L^{1}
and we obtain that

R=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n}.

(3.7)

Since
F_{n}\geq 0
, then in view of (
3.6
) we have
\|R\|_{L^{1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|F_{n}\|_{L^{1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|F\|^{n}_{L^{1}}
. Thus

\|R\|_{L^{1}}=\frac{\|F\|_{L^{1}}}{1-\|F\|_{L^{1}}}<\infty.

(3.8)

Note that (
3.5
) and (
3.8
) imply that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))<\infty.

(3.9)

The first lemma will be useful for us, and it generalizes Lemma 2 in
[
BDHM13
]
to our set-up.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(N(t,A))=\mathbb{Q}(A)tm+\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)\mathbb{E}(N(s,A))ds.

(3.10)

Proof.

Let

\Lambda(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(u-))du=\int_{0}^{r}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(u))du.

So, by (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), and using Fubini’s theorem and definition of
F
(see (
3.1
)), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(N(t,A))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}I_{\{A\}}(x)N(ds,dx)\bigg)=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}I_{\{A\}}(x)\kappa(s,dx)ds\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{\mathcal{X}}I_{\{A\}}(x)\bigg(m+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,y)\mathcal{N}(dr,dy,dz)\bigg)\mathbb{Q}(dx)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}ds\bigg(m+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,y)\mathcal{N}(dr,dy,dz)\bigg)\mathbb{Q}(A)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}(A)mt+\int_{0}^{t}ds\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(r))f(s-r,y)dr\mathbb{Q}(dy)\bigg)\mathbb{Q}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}(A)mt+\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}F(s-r)d\Lambda(r).

Now, again by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}F(s-r)d\Lambda(r)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}F(s-r)ds\right)d\Lambda(r)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{t-r}F(s)ds\right)d\Lambda(r).

(3.11)

Let

\widehat{F}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}F(s)ds.

Then, by integration by parts,

\displaystyle 0=\Lambda(t)\widehat{F}(0)-\Lambda(0)\widehat{F}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{F}(t-r)d\Lambda(r)-\int_{0}^{t}F(t-r)\Lambda(r)dr.

(3.12)

Hence, from (
3.11
) and the fact that
\kappa(t,A)=\mathbb{Q}(A)\lambda(t)
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}F(s-r)d\Lambda(r)=\int_{0}^{t}F(t-r)\mathbb{Q}(A)\Lambda(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}F(t-r)\left(\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{r}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(u))du\right)dr=\int_{0}^{t}F(t-r)\mathbb{E}(N(r,A))dr.

The proof is complete. ∎

Lemma 3.2

\mathbb{E}(N(t,A))=\mathbb{Q}(A)\left(tm+m\int_{0}^{t}sR(t-s)ds\right).

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq L\}}\Big(\int_{0}^{L-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)\Big|^{2}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq L\}}\Big(\int_{0}^{L-s}F(w)dw\Big)^{2}dN(s,A)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}(A)\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq L\}}\Big(\int_{0}^{L-s}F(w)dw\Big)^{2}\lambda(s)ds\Big)\leq\mathbb{Q}(A)\|F\|_{L^{1}}^{2}\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)ds\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{Q}(A)\|F\|_{L^{1}}^{2}m(1+\|R\|_{L^{1}})t,

where in the second equation we use (
3.19
), and in the last inequality we use
(
3.5
). Hence, taking
L=t
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)\Big|^{2}\Big)\leq\mathbb{Q}(A)\|F\|_{L^{1}}^{2}m(1+\|R\|_{L^{1}})t

and thus

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)\Big|^{2}\Big)\leq\mathbb{Q}(A)\|F\|_{L^{1}}^{2}m(1+\|R\|_{L^{1}})T,

which proves (
3.22
). The proof of (
3.23
) is done in an analogous way. ∎

Given the above we can prove the following result:

Lemma 3.6
.

Assuming (
3.2
) we have

T^{-1}|N(T,A)-\mathbb{E}(N(T,A))|\longrightarrow 0

(3.24)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

Let
n(t,A):=N(t,A)-\mathbb{E}(N(t,A))
.
From (
3.10
) and (
3.20
) we have

\displaystyle n(t

\displaystyle,A)=M(t,A)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{Q}(A)\lambda(s)ds-\mathbb{E}(N(t,A))

\displaystyle=M(t,A)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{Q}(A)\lambda(s)ds-\mathbb{Q}(A)tm-\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)\mathbb{E}(N(s,A))ds

\displaystyle=M(t,A)+\mathbb{Q}(A)\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}(\lambda(s)-m)ds}_{=:I_{1}(t)}-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)N(s,A)ds}_{=:I_{2}(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)n(s,A)ds.

Using integration by parts trick as in (
3.12
) and definition of
M
(see (
3.20
)) we have

\displaystyle I_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)N(s,A)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dN(s,A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)+\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)\lambda(s)ds.

By definition of

and using Fubini’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}(\lambda(s)-m)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(s-u,x)N(du,dx)\Big)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\int_{u}^{t}f(s-u,x)ds\Big)N(du,dx).

Now substituting
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
into formula for

, and using (

), yields

\displaystyle n(t,A)

\displaystyle=M(t,A)+\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-u}f(s,x)ds\Big)\widetilde{N}(du,dx)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)+\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)n(s,A)ds.

So we have

where

\displaystyle J(t,A)

\displaystyle:=M(t,A)+\mathbb{Q}(A)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-u}f(s,x)ds\Big)\widetilde{N}(du,dx)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A).

(3.25)

Thus by the basic renewal theorem (see, e.g., Lemma 3 in
[

) we have

\displaystyle n(t,A)=J(t,A)+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)J(s,A)ds.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|n(t,A)|\leq(1+\|R\|_{L^{1}})\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|J(s,A)|.

\displaystyle|J(t,A)|

\displaystyle\leq|M(t,A)|+\mathbb{Q}(A)\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-u}f(s,x)ds\Big)\widetilde{N}(du,dx)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t-s}F(w)dw\Big)dM(s,A)\Big|.

(3.28)

Our goal is to prove

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}}E|n(T,A)|^{2}\rightarrow 0

when

Using the Doob’s inequality and (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|M(t,A)|^{2}\Big)\leq 4\mathbb{E}|M(T,A)|^{2}=4\mathbb{E}[M(\cdot,A)]_{T}=4\mathbb{E}N(T,A)

(3.30)

where the equalities

are consequence of Proposition I.45.c) in
[
JS03
]

since process

is of finite variation and

.

Now, using successively (

), (
3
), (
3
), (
3.22
)
and (

∎

4
Functional LLN for
N
and for Compound Marked Hawkes Process

4.1
Functional LLN for Process
N

In this section we assume that (
3.2
) and
(
3.14
) hold, and we use the convention that
\frac{0}{0}=0
, so that, for example, the expression
T^{-1}N(Tv,A)
is defined for
T=0
.

The following theorem corresponds to Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
. The theorem represents a particular instance of functional limit theorems for families of stochastic process. Here, we deal with a family of marked Hawkes processes parameterized by a non-negative real variable
v
. The essence of the theorem is that with the
T^{-1}
averaging the family converges to a deterministic function of
v
. The parameter
v
provides rescaling of the physical time, with
0\leq v<1
modelling stretching the time (time dilation), for example hours to days, and
v>1
modeling accelerating the time, for example hours to milliseconds.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let us fix
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
. The family of processes
\{(T^{-1}N(Tv,A))_{T\geq 0},\ v\geq 0\}
(parameterized by
{v\geq 0}
)
converges to the function
\nu(v)=vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1),\ v\geq 0,
point-wise in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
as
T\rightarrow\infty
. That is, for each
v\geq 0
,

T^{-1}N(Tv,A)-\nu(v)\longrightarrow 0

as
T\rightarrow\infty
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

The result is obvious for
v=0
since
T^{-1}N(0,A)=0=\nu(0)
for all
T>0
. For
v>0
it is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.6
.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle T^{-1}N(Tv,A)-\nu(v)=\ T^{-1}N(Tv,A)-vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)=

\displaystyle T^{-1}(N(Tv,A)-\mathbb{E}(N(Tv,A)))+\left(T^{-1}\mathbb{E}(N(Tv,A))-vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\right).

So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T^{-1}N(Tv,A)-vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left(T^{-1}N(Tv,A)-\mathbb{E}(N(Tv,A))\right)^{2}+2\left(T^{-1}\mathbb{E}(N(Tv,A))-vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\right)^{2}.

The first term on the right hand side of the above inequality converges to zero as
T\rightarrow\infty
by Lemma
3.6
, and the second term on the right hand side converges to zero as
T\rightarrow\infty
by Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Here we shed some light on the difference between Theorem
4.1
and Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
.
The marked Hawkes process with a finite set of marks considered in
[
BDHM13
]
is cast there in the format of an unmarked multivariate Hawkes process. An unmarked Hawkes process can be seen as a marked Hawkes process with
\mathcal{X}
being a singleton, say
\mathcal{X}={\{0\}}
. If in our set-up we take
\mathcal{X}={\{0\}}
, then

M(\cdot,{\{0\}})=\widetilde{N}(\cdot,{\{0\}}).

Consequently, using (
3
) we obtain

J(\cdot,{\{0\}})=M(\cdot,{\{0\}}),

and in this case, invoking (
3.27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|n(t,{\{0\}})|\leq(1+\|R\|_{L^{1}})\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|M(t,{\{0\}})|.

(4.1)

Hence and from (
3
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|n(t,{\{0\}})|^{2}\Big)\leq CT

(4.2)

for a finite constant
C>0
. This implies that

T^{-1}|N(Tv,{\{0\}})-\mathbb{E}(N(Tv,{\{0\}}))|\longrightarrow 0

(4.3)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
uniformly in
v
on any bounded interval, which in turn, jointly with Lemma
3.3
, implies that

T^{-1}N(Tv,{\{0\}})-vm(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\longrightarrow 0

as
T\rightarrow\infty
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
uniformly in
v
on any bounded interval, which agrees with what Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
states. However, since the mark space
(\mathcal{X},\mathbfcal{X})
considered in this paper is a general one, the results of Theorem 1 in
[
BDHM13
]
do not apply here, and thus Theorem 4.1 can not be cast into the framework considered in
[
BDHM13
]
.

4.2
Functional LLN for Process
C^{A}

Recall that for
A\in\mathbfcal{X}
and a sequence
Z^{A}_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
, of real valued random variables the process
C^{A}
is defined as

C^{A}_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N(t,A)}\,Z^{A}_{n}=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{A}(s)N(ds,A),\quad t\geq 0,

where
\zeta^{A}(s)=Z^{A}_{n}
on
(T_{n-1},T_{n}]
.
Assume that
Z^{A}_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
is an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with finite first moment and independent from
N
.
We then have the following

Theorem 4.3
.

The family of processes
\{(T^{-1}C^{A}_{Tv})_{T\geq 0},\ v\geq 0\}

(parameterized by
{v\geq 0}
) converges to the function
\upsilon(v)=vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\mathbb{E}(Z^{A}_{1}),\ v\geq 0,
point-wise in probability as
T\rightarrow\infty
. That is, for each
v\geq 0
, we have

\frac{C^{A}_{Tv}}{T}\longrightarrow\upsilon(v)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in probability.

Proof.

The result is obvious for
v=0
(c.f. the proof of Theorem 4.1). Thus, we take
v>0
in the rest of the proof.
We have

\frac{C^{A}_{Tv}}{T}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N(Tv,A)}\,Z^{A}_{n}}{N(Tv,A)}\frac{N(Tv,A)}{T}.

It follows from Theorem
4.1
that

N(Tv,A)\longrightarrow\infty

in probability, when
T\rightarrow\infty
.
Consequently, combining this with the strong law of large numbers for the sequence
Z^{A}_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
, and using Theorem 2.2 in
[
Gut09
]
we conclude that

\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N(Tv,A)}\,Z^{A}_{n}}{N(Tv,A)}\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}(Z^{A}_{1})

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in probability.
It also follows from Theorem
4.1
that

\frac{N(Tv,A)}{T}\longrightarrow vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)

in probability as
T\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, using the above and applying the continuous mapping theorem we conclude that

\frac{C^{A}_{Tv}}{T}\longrightarrow vm\mathbb{Q}(A)(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\mathbb{E}(Z^{A}_{1})

in probability, as
T\rightarrow\infty
.∎

Example 4.4

(Application in insurance)
.

As it has been observed, clustering and self-exciting arrivals of claims is an inherent feature of claims arrival processes that is faced by insurance companies. See e.g.
[
DZ12
]
or
[
Mag15
]
. Thus, modeling claims arrivals in terms of a marked Hawkes process is fully justified and supported by empirical evidence.

Consider an insurance company. Let
N
be a marked Hawkes process that models the claims arrivals. We take discrete mark space, say
\mathcal{X}={\{x_{k},\,k=1,2,\ldots,K\}}
, where
x_{k}
s represent type/severity of arriving claims.

Now, let
Z^{(k)}_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
, be i.i.d. random variables, independent of
N
, representing the claim sizes triggered by the claim of type/severity
x_{k}
. A classical problem in insurance is the problem of long-time behavior of the surplus process.

The surplus process, say
\mathcal{R}
, is given here as the following extension of the classical Cramer-Lundberg model and termed a compound marked Hawkes process:

\mathcal{R}_{t}=r+ct-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N((0,t],\{x_{k}\})}\,Z^{(k)}_{n},

(4.4)

where
r
is the initial capital and
c
is the insurance premium rate. This model generalizes the compound Hawkes surplus model originally introduced in
[
ST10
]
.
Now, let us set
\mu^{k}=\mathbb{E}(Z^{(k)}_{1})
for
k=1,2,\ldots,K.
Then, Theorem
4.3
gives us that for
v\geq 0

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{Tv}}{T}\longrightarrow v\left(c-m(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu^{k}\mathbb{Q}(\{x_{k}\})\right)

(4.5)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in probability.

Remark 4.5
.

Consider a special case of the above example where the claim sizes do not depend on the mark. Thus,
Z^{(k)}_{n}=Z_{n}=Z^{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
,
n=1,2,\ldots,
where
Z_{n},\ n=1,2,\ldots
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. So, here we have

\mathcal{R}_{t}=r+ct-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{N((0,t],\{x_{k}\})}\,Z_{n}=r+ct-\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}\,Z_{n}=r+ct-C_{t}.

(4.6)

Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})=\mathbb{E}(Z^{\mathcal{X}})
. Accordingly, using Proposition
4.3
and taking
v=1
we obtain the following special case of the LLN for the ruin process

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T}}{T}\longrightarrow c-\mu m(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)

(4.7)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, in probability.

We will now show that this agrees with the classical LLN for a ruin process driven by a compound marked Hawkes process, as presented in
[
ST10
]
,
[
Swi18
]
or
[
SNJB25
]
, for example.
Towards this end we set (assuming integrability)

H(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t,x)dt,\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

Thus, using Fubini’s theorem,

{\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}H(x)\mathbb{Q}(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t,x)dt\right]\mathbb{Q}(dx)}

{=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(t,x)\mathbb{Q}(dx)\right]dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(t)dt=||F||_{L^{1}}.}

The classical stability condition for marked Hawkes processes is that
\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})<1
which, in view of the above, is the same as (
3.2
). Finally, also using (
3.8
) and (
4.7
), we get that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T}}{T}\longrightarrow\

\displaystyle c-\mu m(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1)=c-\frac{\mu m}{1-||F||_{L^{1}}}=c-\frac{\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m}{1-\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})}

(4.8)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, which is a version of the classical LLN for a ruin process driven by a compound marked Hawkes process.

An important condition for insurance risk management is the so called net-profit condition (c.f.
[
RSST99
]
). In our set-up the net-profit condition takes the form

c>\frac{\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m}{1-\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})}.

Recall that if the net profit condition is violated then ruin is inevitable. Taking
f(\cdot,\cdot)\equiv 0
and
m>0
yields the classical Crammer-Lundberg model. In this case
\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})=0
and we recover
the classical net-profit condition

c>\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m.

Since
\frac{\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m}{1-\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})}>\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m
we see that the net-profit condition is more restrictive in the presence of the self-excitation effect in arrival of claims. This means that if the insurer recognizes the change of the structure of claims arrivals (from Poisson model to Hawkes model) then in order to prevent inevitability of ruin they should increase the premium rate
c
above
\frac{\mathbb{E}(Z_{1})m}{1-\mathbb{E}H(X_{1})}
if needed. ∎

4.3
Functional LLN for Process
D^{\varphi}

Recall that for a real-valued, bounded Borel function
\varphi(x)
the process
D^{\varphi}
is defined as

D^{\varphi}_{t}=\int_{(0,t]}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)N(ds,dx)=\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}\varphi(X_{n}),\quad t\geq 0.

We begin by observing that from (
3.19
) and Lemma
3.2
it follows that

\mathbb{E}(D^{\varphi}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}\varphi(X_{n})\right)=\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{(0,t)}\lambda(s)ds\right)=\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}m\left(t+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)sds\right),

where
\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\mathbb{Q}(dx).

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that the following integrability condition is satisfied

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(f(u,x)+\int_{0}^{u}R(u-r)f(r,x)dr\right)du\right]^{2}\mathbb{Q}(dx)<\infty.

(4.9)

Then, the family of processes
\{(T^{-1}D^{\varphi}_{vT})_{T\geq 0},\ v\geq 0\}
(parameterized by
v\geq 0
)
converges to the function
\psi(v)=\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}vm(\|R\|_{L^{1}}+1),\ v\geq 0,
point-wise in probability as
T\rightarrow\infty
. That is,

\frac{D^{\varphi}_{vT}}{T}\longrightarrow\psi(v)

(4.10)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
in probability, for any
v\geq 0.

Proof.

To begin we write
D^{\varphi}
using
\widetilde{N}
(see (
3.21
))

D^{\varphi}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\widetilde{N}(ds,dx)+\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)ds.

Using the Doob’s maximal inequality and (
3.9
) we get for any
V\in(0,\infty)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{v\in[0,V]}\left(T^{-1}\int_{0}^{vT}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\widetilde{N}(ds,dx)\right)^{2}\right)\leq\frac{C}{T}\int_{0}^{V}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(Ts))ds\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi^{2}(x)\mathbb{Q}(dx)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{T},

where
C
and
C^{\prime}
are constants not depending on
T
. Thus,
\sup_{v\in[0,V]}T^{-1}\int_{0}^{vT}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(x)\widetilde{N}(ds,dx)
converges to zero in probability as
T\rightarrow\infty.

Thus, to prove (
4.10
) it is enough to prove

\frac{\varphi_{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{Tv}\lambda(s)ds}{T}\longrightarrow\psi(v)

(4.11)

in probability as
T\rightarrow\infty
(for any
v\geq 0
).
We start with showing that

T^{-1}\left|\int_{0}^{Tv}\lambda(s)ds-\int_{0}^{Tv}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(s))ds\right|\longrightarrow 0

(4.12)

in probability for any
v\geq 0.
Towards this end we proceed as follows. Denote
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz)=\mathcal{N}(ds,dx,dz)-ds\mathbb{Q}(dx)dz
, which is a (local) martingale measure.
Using the representation (
2.11
) of
\lambda
we obtain

\lambda(t)=F\ast\lambda(t)+m+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz).

(4.13)

and thus

R\ast\lambda(t)=R\ast(F\ast\lambda)(t)+R\ast m(t)+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(dr,dx,dz)\right)ds.

From (
3.4
) we have that
R
satisfies
R=F+R\ast F
and thus
(F+R\ast F)\ast\lambda(t)=R\ast\lambda(t)
, so the above gives

F\ast\lambda(t)=R\ast m(t)+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(dr,dx,dz)\right)ds.

Thus, from this and from (
4.13
) we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda(t)

\displaystyle=m+R\ast m(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(dr,dx,dz)\right)ds

\displaystyle=m+R\ast m(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}f(t-s,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}\int_{0}^{t-s}R(t-s-r)f(r,x)dr\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz)

\displaystyle=m+R\ast m(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}\left(f(t-s,x)+\int_{0}^{t-s}R(t-s-r)f(r,x)dr\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz),

(4.14)

where the second equality follows by applying Fubini’s theorem to

\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(r-)}f(s-r,x)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(dr,dx,dz)\right)ds.

Now, from (
4.3
) and (
3.5
) it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Tv}\lambda(t)dt-\int_{0}^{Tv}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{Tv}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(s-)}\left(f(t-s,x)+\int_{0}^{t-s}R(t-s-r)f(r,x)dr\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(ds,dx,dz)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{v}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{0}^{\lambda(Ts-)}\left[\int_{0}^{T(v-s)}\left(f(u,x)+\int_{0}^{u}R(u-r)f(r,x)dr\right)du\right]\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(Tds,dx,dz).

(4.15)

Given the above we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{Tv}\lambda(t)dt-\int_{0}^{Tv}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))dt\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{v}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(Ts))ds\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(f(u,x)+\int_{0}^{u}R(u-r)f(r,x)dr\right)du\right]^{2}\mathbb{Q}(dx).

(4.16)

Thus, using (
3.9
) and (
4.9
) we conclude that

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{Tv}\lambda(t)dt-\int_{0}^{Tv}\mathbb{E}(\lambda(t))dt\right)^{2}=0,

which gives (
4.12
). Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)ds\right)=m\left(t+\int_{0}^{t}sR(t-s)ds\right)

(4.17)

(see Lemma
3.2
) and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Tv}sR(Tv-s)ds=-\int_{0}^{Tv}(Tv-s)R(Tv-s)ds+\int_{0}^{Tv}TvR(Tv-s)ds,

using (
3.17
) (which is a consequence of assumption (
3.14
))
we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Tv}sR(Tv-s)ds\longrightarrow\psi(v)

(4.18)

as
T\to\infty
.
Finally, from (
4.12
), (
4.17
), (
4.18
) we obtain (
4.11
) and we conclude the proof.
