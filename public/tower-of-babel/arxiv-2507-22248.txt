EFFECTIVE RADIUS OF A DISCRETE MOVING POLYMER
Jiaming Chen

Abstract
. We present a discrete space-time stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) model to describe the dynamics of a weakly self-avoiding polymer with intrinsic length J J . By introducing a penalty factor tailored to the discrete setting, we establish that the polymer’s root mean squared radius scales linearly with J J . This scaling behavior differs from the law derived by Mueller and Neuman [ MN22 ] in the continuous framework, highlighting both the distinct nature of the discrete model and its analytical tractability for studying polymer behavior in lattice-like environments.

Primary, 60H15; Secondary, 82D60.

1.
Introduction

Over the past few decades, random polymers have attracted significant attention across disciplines, including chemistry, physics, and mathematics, due to their ubiquity in the physical world. These complex structures play a central role in areas such as molecular theory, statistical mechanics, and probability theory. For a comprehensive overview from the physical sciences’ perspective, we refer to Doi and Edwards
[
DEE88
]
, while rigorous mathematical treatments can be found in
[
Hol09
]
and
[
BDCGS12
]
. A detailed survey of key results and open problems in the one-dimensional setting is provided by König and van der Hofstad
[
vdHK01
]
.

A polymer is a molecule composed of many “monomers” (groups of atoms) linked together through chemical bonds. From a mathematical perspective, modeling polymers is challenging due to the complex interactions that influence their spatial configurations. The most basic model treats the polymer as a simple random walk, where the time parameter represents the progression along the chain and each new segment is added in a random direction with identical step distributions. However, this model fails to capture an essential physical constraint: two monomers cannot occupy the same position in space, and in fact, tend to repel one another when in proximity. This phenomenon, known as the
excluded volume effect
, motivates a fundamental modification by introducing a penalty for self-intersections. Extensive research has been conducted on this topic; see
[
MS13
]
for a detailed account.

The
Rouse model

[
RJ53
]
serves as a foundational framework for studying the dynamics of polymer solutions without self-avoidance, modeling the polymer as a chain of beads connected by harmonic springs. Let
(R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{N})
be the position (vectors) of the beads, then for internal beads
(n=2,3,...,N-1)
,

\zeta\frac{dR_{n}}{dt}=\kappa(R_{n+1}-2R_{n}+R_{n-1})+f_{n}.

For the end beads
(n=1
and
n=N)
,

\zeta\frac{dR_{1}}{dt}=\kappa(R_{2}-R_{1})+f_{1},~\zeta\frac{dR_{N}}{dt}=\kappa(R_{N-1}-R_{N})+f_{N},

where
\zeta,\kappa
are constants and
f_{n}
is a Gaussian random force indexed on
n
.

Doi and Edwards
[
DEE88
]
obtain the following stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) as a continuum limit of the Rouse model. This equation, also known as the
Edwards-Wilkinson model
, takes the form:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=\partial^{2}_{x}u+\dot{W}(t,x),\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x),\end{split}

where
(\dot{W}(t,x))_{t\geq 0,x\in[0,J]}
is a two-parameter white noise and
u_{0}
is a continuous and bounded initial function. The authors argue that, over long time scales, key universal properties of the polymer, such as scaling behavior, should be consistent between the discrete and continuous models.

To the best of the author’s knowledge, Mueller and Neuman
[
MN22
]
were the first to rigorously analyze the scaling behavior of a dynamically evolving and weakly self-avoiding polymer that incorporates time as a variable. In reality, even when the underlying model is continuous, we typically observe it only at discrete points in space or time. While discretizing continuous models is standard in both theoretical and practical settings, such discretizations require careful treatment. Verifying that the discrete model preserves the expected scaling behavior thus requires new techniques and careful analysis. The goal of this paper is to study a discrete-time and discrete-space version of the polymer model in one dimension under suitable assumptions.

We now consider the following discretized model:

(1.1)

\begin{split}u(t+1,n)-u(t,n)&=\kappa\left(u(t,n+1)-2u(t,n)+u(t,n-1)\right)+\xi(t,n),\\
u(0,n)&=u_{0}(n),\end{split}

which describes the temporal evolution of the function
u(t,n)
under a discrete Laplacian operator perturbed by a standard Gaussian random variable
\xi(t,n)
. To ensure convergence and stability of the numerical scheme, we set the diffusion coefficient to
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
. Without loss of generality, the analysis is restricted to grid points within the domain
[0,T]\times[0,J-1]
, corresponding to the index sets
\mathcal{T}:=\{0,1,...,T\}
and
\mathcal{J}:=\{0,1,...,J-1\}
for
T,J\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Here
u_{0}
is a bounded and continuous initial profile. In the absence of external interactions, we assume the noise variables are independent and characterized by the following moments:

(1.2)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\xi(t,n)]&=0,\\
\mathbb{E}[\xi(t,n)\xi(s,m)]&=\delta_{ts}\delta_{nm},\end{split}

where
\delta
denotes the Kronecker delta. Since the endpoints of the polymer are not fixed, we impose discrete Neumann boundary conditions at the hypothetical beads
u(\cdot,-1)
and
u(\cdot,J)
:

(1.3)

u(\cdot,-1)=u(\cdot,0),~u(\cdot,J)=u(\cdot,J-1).

The Green’s function corresponding to equation (
1.1
) under the Neumann boundary in (
1.3
) is given by:

(1.4)

G_{t}(n,k)=\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}a_{m}\left(\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)\right)^{t}\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(n+0.5)}{J}\right)\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(k+0.5)}{J}\right),

where
a_{m}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{1}{J}}&m=0;\\
\sqrt{\frac{2}{J}}&m=1,...,J-1.\end{cases}

So the solution
u(t,n)
can be computed by

(1.5)

u(t,n)=\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}G_{t}(n,m)u_{0}(m)+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}G_{t-s}(n,m)\xi(s,m).

We now incorporate self-avoidance into our discrete polymer model. A classical approach in statistical mechanics is to modify the reference probability measure by assigning a penalty proportional to the number of self-intersections. Specifically, we weight each path by the exponential of the negative product of a parameter and the pairwise self-intersection count. These weights are often referred to as
Boltzmann weights
in statistical mechanics.

In our setting, since the solution
u(t,n)\in\mathbb{R}
while the index
n
belongs to a discrete set, we approximate the self-intersection by introducing a finite spatial resolution, as first proposed by
[
Lin25
]
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, for each time
t
, we define the approximate self-intersection count as

(1.6)

\begin{split}N_{\varepsilon}(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}1_{|u(t,i)-u(t,j)|\leq\varepsilon}.\end{split}

This expression quantifies the number of pairs of monomers (indexed by
i
and
j
) that are within
\varepsilon
distance of each other at time
t
, capturing the local crowding of the polymer. One might consider defining the self-interaction over the entire time horizon via the total sum

\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{J-1}1_{|u(t,i)-u(t,j)|\leq\varepsilon}.

However, following the rationale in
[
MN22
]
, there is no physical reason why different parts of the polymer cannot occupy the same spatial location at different times. Therefore, it is more appropriate to work with the time-slice-based definition in (
1.6
).

Let
P_{T,J}
denote the uniform probability measure on the space of polymer configurations
(u(t,n))_{t\in\mathcal{T},n\in\mathcal{J}}
. We define a penalizing factor:

(1.7)

\mathcal{E}_{T,J,\beta,\varepsilon}=\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{\varepsilon}(t)\right),

and define the corresponding self-repelling polymer measure
Q_{T,J,\beta}
via

(1.8)

Q_{T,J,\beta}(A)=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{P_{T,J}}[1_{A}\mathcal{E}_{T,J,\beta}]}{Z_{T,J,\beta}},\text{ for all measurable set $A$},

where the partition function
Z_{T,J,\beta}
is given by

(1.9)

Z_{T,J,\beta}=\mathbb{E}^{P_{T,J}}[\mathcal{E}_{T,J,\beta}].

The parameter
\beta>0
is a parameter governing the strength of self-repellence and typically represents the inverse temperature of the system. It is immediate that
J\leq N_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq J^{2}
for all
t
, as each monomer interacts with itself and at most
J-1
others.

The probability measure
Q_{T,J,\beta}
is known as the
J-
polymer measure with strength of self repellence
\beta
. Equation (
1.7
) defines what is known as the
Domb-Joyce model
for soft polymers (see
[
MS13
]
Section 10.1). It models the polymer in equilibrium with itself and its environment.
Each self-intersection at a fixed time incurs an energy cost of
\beta
, which translates into a penalty of
e^{-\beta}
for that configuration. This induces an effective repulsion that causes the polymer to spread out over space.

For notational convenience, we suppress subscripts except for the time horizon
T
, and write:

P_{T}=P_{T,J},~Q_{T}=Q_{T,J,\beta},~\mathcal{E}_{T}=\mathcal{E}_{T,J,\beta},~Z_{T}=Z_{T,J,\beta}.

Throughout this paper, we use
C
and
C^{\prime}
to denote a generic positive constant, whose value may change from line to line. Dependence of constants on specific parameters is explicitly indicated by listing the parameters in parentheses.

In Section 2, we state our main theorem regarding the spatial extent of the polymer under the self-repelling measure
Q_{T}
. Section 3 provides a lower bound for the partition function
Z_{T}
. Sections 4 and 5 establish tail bounds for the effective radius, addressing the lower and upper deviations, respectively.

2.
Main Result

A fundamental quantity in characterizing the spatial configuration of a polymer is its macroscopic spatial extent. In this work, we focus on the
radius of gyration
, a widely accepted measure defined as the mean squared distance of the polymer’s segments from its center of mass. This quantity captures the average dispersion of the polymer in space and serves as a robust indicator of its effective size.

We define the center of mass of the polymer configuration at time
t
by

(2.1)

\bar{u}(t)=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}u(t,n),

and introduce the root mean square radius of gyration over time horizon
T
as

(2.2)

R(T,J)=\left[\frac{1}{TJ}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}(u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t))^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

This definition generalizes a classical concept discussed in Equation
(2.6)
on page 9 of
[
DEE88
]
, where the polymer’s size is approximated by the end-to-end distance
|R_{N}-R_{1}|
for a polymer
(R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{N})
. However, as emphasized by the authors on page 22 of the same reference, this end-to-end distance is not always an appropriate measure, especially for branched polymers where it may be ill-defined. Even though our focus is on linear polymers, we adopt the radius of gyration
R(T,J)
as it more comprehensively captures the spatial structure through fluctuations around the center of mass.

In the context of the one-dimensional Domb-Joyce model, Bolthausen
[
Bol90
]
, using large deviation techniques, showed that the end-to-end distance grows linearly with polymer length
J
almost surely, that is, it is approximately
C(\beta)J
for small values of the self-repellence parameter
\beta
.

More recently, Mueller and Neuman
[
MN22
]
established that the effective radius of a moving, weakly self-avoiding polymer scales as
J^{5/3}
in the continuous setting. In contrast, we provide a result for a discrete self-avoiding polymer model which indicates that the radius of gyration grows linearly with
J
, consistent with classical scaling laws for equilibrium polymers.

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists constants
K_{1}(\varepsilon),K_{2}>0
, independent of
\beta
and
J
, such that

\lim_{T\to\infty}Q_{T}[K_{1}J\leq R(T,J)\leq K_{2}J]=1.

Remark 2.1
.

Notably, most known results for weakly self-avoiding polymers do not yield an end-to-end distance that explicitly depends on the self-repellence parameter
\beta
. Our result is consistent with this observation, as the root mean square radius
R(T,J)
is shown to be asymptotically independent of
\beta
, for all
\beta\in(0,\infty)
.

Remark 2.2
.

Our result aligns with classical equilibrium results (i.e., models without a time component), which also exhibit linear scaling of the effective radius with respect to
J
. This suggests that the inclusion of temporal dynamics is not the primary factor driving the
J^{5/3}
scaling found in
[
MN22
]
. Rather, it is plausible that the passage from discrete to continuous-time dynamics, along with structural differences in the underlying model, accounts for the discrepancy. A detailed investigation into the continuum limit of the discretized polymer dynamics may provide further insight into this divergence.

3.
Lower Bound on the Partition Function

In this section, we analyze the behavior of the partition function
Z_{T}
and derive a rigorous lower bound. To this end, we construct a penalized path by introducing a drift term, following the methodology outlined in Section 3 of
[
Bol90
]
, which we will define precisely below. This approach is standard: under the tilted measure induced by the drift, Jensen’s inequality is applied to estimate the lower tail probability of the path possessing the prescribed drift. We observe that adding a constant drift suffices to establish our main result in the discrete setting, even though this effectively shifts the solution to equation (
1.5
) horizontally.

The Gaussian random variables
\xi(t,j)
in (
1.2
) are assumed to be independent indexed by discrete time
t
and spatial coordinate
j
. Denote by
\Lambda(a)
the log-moment generating function of
\xi
, defined as

(3.1)

\Lambda(a):=\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(ay)dP(y)\right)=\frac{a^{2}}{2},

where the expectation is taken with respect to the standard Gaussian distribution
P(y):=P_{T}(\xi\leq y)
. We introduce a tilted probability measure
\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}
defined by its Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
P_{T}
:

(3.2)

\frac{d\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}}{dP_{T}}=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\prod_{j=0}^{J-1}\exp(a\xi(t,j))/\exp(\Lambda(a)).

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}
and
\widehat{\text{Var}}
denote expectation and variance under
\mathbb{E}^{\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}}
, respectively. By construction,

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\xi(t,j)]=a,\quad\widehat{\text{Var}}[\xi(t,j)]=1.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to equation (
1.7
) combined with the definition of
Z_{T}
in (
1.9
) and (
3.2
), we obtain

(3.3)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{T}\log Z_{T}&=\frac{1}{T}\log\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{\varepsilon}(t)-\log\frac{d\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}}{dP_{T}}\right)\right]\\
&\geq-\frac{\beta}{T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{\varepsilon}(t)\right]-\frac{1}{T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\frac{d\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}}{dP_{T}}\right].\end{split}

The second term on the right-hand side can be computed explicitly by (
3.1
), linearity of expectation and independence:

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\left[a\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\xi(t,j)]-\Lambda(a)\right]=-J(a\Lambda^{\prime}(a)-\Lambda(a))=-\frac{a^{2}J}{2},\end{split}

Thus, the primary difficulty reduces to estimating the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.3
).

By the definition of
N_{\varepsilon}(t)
in (
1.6
), the expectation under
\widehat{P}^{(a)}_{T}
can be written as

-\frac{\beta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}(|u(t,i)-u(t,j)|\leq\varepsilon).

Inspired by the approach in
[
MN22
]
, we introduce the discrete pinned string defined by

(3.4)

\begin{split}u_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n)&:=\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}G_{t-s}(n,m)\xi(s,m)\\
&\quad\quad+\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s}(n,m)-G_{t_{0}-s}(n_{0},m)\right)\xi(s,m)\\
&=:A+B\end{split}

which takes an infinite past
(s\to-\infty)
and anchoring the string at a reference point
n_{0}
at time
t_{0}
. The first term
A
will be vanished when
t=t_{0}
. Our immediate goal is to establish that the pinned string is well-defined and that the spatial increments form a stationary process in time. Since
A
is a finite sum, it suffices to verify the convergence of
B
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
T>0
,

\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq T}\max_{n,n_{0}\in\mathcal{J}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[B^{2}\right]<\infty.

Proof.

Recall that the kernel
G_{t}(n,m)
admits the
L^{2}-
eigenfunction expansion in (
1.4
)

G_{t}(n,k)=\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k),

where
\rho_{m}=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right),\phi_{m}(n)=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(n+0.5)}{J}\right)
. Because
\{\xi(s,m)\}
are independent standard Gaussians under
\widehat{P}^{(a)}_{T}
, the second moment of
B
is

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[B^{2}\right]&=\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s}(n,m)-G_{t_{0}-s}(n_{0},m)\right)^{2}\\
&=\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\left[a_{j}\rho_{j}^{t-s}\phi_{j}(m)\phi_{j}(n)-a_{j}\rho_{j}^{t_{0}-s}\phi_{j}(m)\phi_{j}(n_{0})\right]\\
&\quad\quad\cdot\left[a_{k}\rho_{k}^{t-s}\phi_{k}(m)\phi_{k}(n)-a_{k}\rho_{k}^{t_{0}-s}\phi_{k}(m)\phi_{k}(n_{0})\right]\\
&=\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}a_{j}a_{k}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\phi_{j}(m)\phi_{k}(m)\right)\left[\rho_{j}^{t-s}\phi_{j}(n)-\rho_{j}^{t_{0}-s}\phi_{j}(n_{0})\right]\\
&\quad\quad\cdot\left[\rho_{k}^{t-s}\phi_{j}(n)-\rho_{k}^{t_{0}-s}\phi_{j}(n_{0})\right]\\
&=\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\left[\rho_{j}^{t-s}\phi_{j}(n)-\rho_{j}^{t_{0}-s}\phi_{j}(n_{0})\right]^{2}\\
&<\infty,\end{split}

as applying orthogonality to the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}(m)\}
and
|\rho_{j}|<1
for
j>1
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

(Shift invariance of the pinned string). For any fixed
t_{0}\in\mathcal{T}
and
n_{0}\in\mathcal{J}
, under the measure
\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}
, the increment process

U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n_{1},n_{2}):=u_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n_{1})-u_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n_{2}),\quad t\in\mathcal{T},n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathcal{J},

is stationary in time; i.e., for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
and
t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}
,

\left(U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t_{1},n_{1},n_{2})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}\left(U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t_{2},n_{1},n_{2})\right).

Proof.

Define the time-shift operator
\theta_{h}
on noise by
(\theta_{h}\xi)(t,n):=\xi(t+h,n)
. Recall the definition of
u_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n)
in (
3.4
) and express
U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n_{1},n_{2})
in terms of the noise increments,

\begin{split}U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n_{1},n_{2})&=\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s}(n_{1},m)-G_{t-s}(n_{2},m)\right)\xi(s,m)\\
&\quad\quad+\sum_{s<t_{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s}(n_{1},m)-G_{t-s}(n_{2},m)\right)\xi(s,m)\\
&=\sum_{s<t}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s}(n_{1},m)-G_{t-s}(n_{2},m)\right)\xi(s,m).\end{split}

Applying the time shift,

\begin{split}U_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t+h,n_{1},n_{2})&=\sum_{s<t+h}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t+h-s}(n_{1},m)-G_{t+h-s}(n_{2},m)\right)\xi(s,m)\\
&=\sum_{s^{\prime}<t}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s^{\prime}}(n_{1},m)-G_{t-s^{\prime}}(n_{2},m)\right)\xi(s^{\prime}+h,m)\\
&=\sum_{s^{\prime}<t}\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}\left(G_{t-s^{\prime}}(n_{1},m)-G_{t-s^{\prime}}(n_{2},m)\right)(\theta_{h}\xi)(s^{\prime},m).\end{split}

Since the noise
\{\xi(t,n)\}
is a stationary Gaussian field, and
\theta_{h}
preserves its law, the distribution of the increment process is invariant under time shifts. This completes the proof.
∎

From now on, denote
u(t,n):=u_{t_{0},u_{0}}(t,n)
for simplicity. The stationarity of
u
implies

\begin{split}-\frac{\beta}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}(|u(t,i)-u(t,j)|\leq\varepsilon)=-\beta\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}(|u(0,i)-u(0,j)|\leq\varepsilon).\end{split}

For
i=j
, the probability trivially satisfies
\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}(u(0,i)=u(0,j))=1
. For
i\neq j
, the increment
u(0,i)-u(0,j)
is Gaussian under
\widehat{P}^{(a)}_{T}
, but its mean and variance require explicit computation. By linearity of expectation, the Gaussian nature of
\xi
and Fubini’s theorem (since the series converges absolutely in
L^{2}
), we interchange expectation and summation:

(3.5)

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}[G_{s}(i,n)-G_{s}(j,n)]\xi(s,n)\right]\\
&=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}[G_{s}(i,n)-G_{s}(j,n)]\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\xi(s,n)]\\
&=a\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)^{s}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(n+0.5)}{J}\right)\right)\\
&\quad\quad\cdot\left[\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(i+0.5)}{J}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(j+0.5)}{J}\right)\right]\\
&=0.\end{split}

Note that the summation over
n
of
\phi_{m}(n)
vanishes due to orthogonality and the eigenbasis structure, the entire expression evaluates to zero. Equivalently, since the initial profile in expectation is constant, the solution of the discrete heat equation preserves this constant expectation at all times, yielding zero mean increment.

Lemma 3.3
.

(Variance estimate for spatial increments). For any distinct
i,j\in\mathcal{J}
, there exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that

CJ|i-j|\leq\widehat{\text{Var}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]\leq C^{\prime}J|i-j|.

Proof.

Recall that the kernel
G_{t}(n,m)
admits the
L^{2}-
eigenfunction expansion in (
1.4
)

G_{t}(n,k)=\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k),

where
\rho_{m}=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right),\phi_{m}(n)=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(n+0.5)}{J}\right)
and the definition of
u_{t_{0},n_{0}}(t,n)
in (
3.4
). Without loss of generality, assume
i>j
. The variance can be written as

(3.6)

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{Var}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]&=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}[G_{s}(i,n)-G_{s}(j,n)]^{2}\widehat{\text{Var}}[\xi(s,n)]\\
&=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{s}\phi_{m}(n)\left(\phi_{m}(i)-\phi_{m}(j)\right)\right]^{2}\\
&=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}^{2}\rho_{m}^{2s}\left(\phi_{m}(i)-\phi_{m}(j)\right)^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\phi^{2}_{m}(n)\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)}{1-\cos^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)}\left[\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(i+0.5)}{J}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(j+0.5)}{J}\right)\right]^{2}.\end{split}

We now derive the lower bound. Define

M:=\max\left\{m\in\mathcal{J}:m<\frac{2J}{i-j}\right\}.

Then for each
m=1,\dots,M
, we have

0<\frac{m\pi(i-j)}{2J}<\pi,

and since
(M+1)(i-j)\geq 2J
, it follows that

M(i-j)\geq 2J-(i-j)\geq J+1.

Using the inequality
\sin x\geq Cx
for
(0,\pi)
, we obtain

\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi(i-j)}{2J}\right)\geq C\frac{m^{2}(i-j)^{2}}{J^{2}}.

Thus, we estimate the variance in (
3.6
) from below as

(3.7)

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{Var}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]&\geq CJ^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi(i-j)}{2J}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi(i+j+1)}{2J}\right)}{m^{2}}\\
&\geq C(i-j)^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi(i+j+1)}{2J}\right)\\
&=C(i-j)^{2}\left[2M+1-\frac{\sin\left(\frac{(2M+1)(i+j+1)\pi}{2J}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{(i+j+1)\pi}{2J}\right)}\right]\\
&\geq C(i-j)^{2}\left[2M-\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{(i+j+1)\pi}{2J}\right)}\right].\end{split}

Without loss of generality, we may assume
i+j<J-1
, so that by symmetry of the sine function near
\frac{\pi}{2}
,

\sin\left(\frac{(i+j+1)\pi}{2J}\right)\geq\sin\left(\frac{(i-j)\pi}{2J}\right)\geq\frac{C(i-j)}{J}.

Therefore,

(3.8)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{(i+j+1)\pi}{2J}\right)}\leq\frac{CJ}{i-j}\leq C^{\prime}M.\end{split}

Combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we obtain

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{Var}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]&\geq C(i-j)^{2}M\geq CJ(i-j).\end{split}

On the other hand, the mean value theorem yields

\left[\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(i+0.5)}{J}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(j+0.5)}{J}\right)\right]^{2}\leq 4\wedge\left(\frac{m\pi(i-j)}{J}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, from (
3.6
),

(3.9)

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{Var}}[u(0,i)-u(0,j)]&\leq\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{4\wedge\frac{m^{2}\pi^{2}}{J^{2}}(i-j)^{2}}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)}\\
&\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}}\wedge(i-j)^{2}\\
&\leq C\left((i-j)^{2}+\int_{1}^{\frac{J}{i-j}}(i-j)^{2}dz+J^{2}\int_{\frac{J}{i-j}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^{2}}dz\right)\\
&\leq CJ(i-j),\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

This lemma implies that the standard deviation of the polymer’s end-to-end radius grows linearly with the polymer length
J
.

For distinct indices
i\neq j
, from (
3.5
) and Lemma
3.3
, we have the following upper bound:

\widehat{P}^{(a)}_{T}(|u(0,i)-u(0,j)|\leq\varepsilon)\leq 2\Phi\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{CJ|i-j|}}\right)-1,

where
\Phi
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian distribution. Let
d=|i-j|
, for each integer
d=1,2,\dots,J-1
, the number of index pairs
(i,j)
satisfying
|i-j|=d
is
2(J-d)
. Consequently, summing over all pairs
(i,j)
yields

(3.10)

\begin{split}\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\widehat{P}_{T}^{(a)}(|u(0,i)-u(0,j)|\leq\varepsilon)&\leq J+\sum_{d=1}^{J-1}2(J-d)\left[2\Phi\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{CJd}}\right)-1\right]\\
&\leq J+\sum_{d=1}^{J-1}2(J-d)\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{CJd}}\\
&=J+\frac{C\varepsilon}{\sqrt{J}}\sum_{d=1}^{J-1}\frac{J-d}{\sqrt{d}}\\
&\leq J+\frac{C\varepsilon}{\sqrt{J}}\left(J^{3/2}+o\left(J^{3/2}\right)\right)\\
&=CJ+o(\varepsilon J)\\
&\leq CJ,\end{split}

where the second inequality

2\Phi(x)-1\leq\frac{2x}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

for
x\geq 0
follows from the fact that

\begin{split}\Phi(x)-\frac{1}{2}&=\int_{0}^{x}\phi(y)dy\leq\int_{0}^{x}\phi(0)dy=\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\end{split}

with
\phi
denoting the standard Gaussian density. Furthermore, for the third inequality, note that the function
f(x)=\frac{J-x}{\sqrt{x}}
is monotone decreasing on
[1,J]
, we apply integral comparison to obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{d=1}^{J-1}\frac{J-d}{\sqrt{d}}&\leq\int_{1}^{J}\frac{J-x}{\sqrt{x}}dx=J\int_{1}^{J}x^{-1/2}dx-\int_{1}^{J}x^{1/2}dx=CJ^{3/2}+o\left(J^{3/2}\right).\end{split}

Combining these estimates in (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) yields the lower bound in (
3.3
) as

(3.11)

\frac{1}{T}\log Z_{T}\geq-C\beta J-\frac{a^{2}J}{2}.

4.
Small distance tail estimate

In this section, we establish an upper bound on the lower tail of
R(T,J)
, showing that this function rarely takes values that are too small when
T
is large. Specifically, we prove the following result:

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a constant
K_{1}(\varepsilon)>0
, independent of
\beta
and
J
, such that

\lim_{T\to\infty}Q_{T}\left[R(T,J)<K_{1}J\right]=0.

Fix
K>0
, and define the event
A_{K,T}:=\{R(T,J)\leq K\}
. By the definition of
R(T,J)
in (
2.2
), on the event
A_{K,T}
, we must have

(4.1)

\left|\left\{t\in\mathcal{T}:\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}(u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t))^{2}\leq 2K^{2}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{T}{2},

where
|\cdot|
denotes the cardinality of a set and
\bar{u}
defined in (
2.1
) is the spatial average of
u(t,\cdot)
at time
t
. Observe that if the variance satisfies

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}(u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t))^{2}\leq 2K^{2},

then, by a simple counting argument, it follows that

(4.2)

\left|\left\{n\in\mathcal{J}:u(t,n)\in[\bar{u}(t)-2K,\bar{u}(t)+2K]\right\}\right|\geq\frac{J}{2}.

Let
d_{t}^{\pm}=\bar{u}(t)\pm 2K
, and note that
d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=4K
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, then there exist integers
z^{+}
,
z^{-}\in\mathbb{Z}
and a parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

d_{t}^{+}=z^{+}\varepsilon-\alpha\varepsilon,\quad d_{t}^{-}\in(z^{-}\varepsilon-\alpha\varepsilon,z^{-}\varepsilon+(1-\alpha)\varepsilon].

To quantify the local density of the solution
u(t,\cdot)
at time
t
, we define the number of spatial sites that fall within a given interval of length
\varepsilon
as:

(4.3)

l_{\varepsilon,t}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{J}1_{u(t,i)\in(z\varepsilon-\alpha\varepsilon,z\varepsilon+(1-\alpha)\varepsilon]}.

Under the inclusion conditions stated in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), it follows that on the event
A_{K,T}
,

(4.4)

\left|\left\{t\in\mathcal{T}:\sum_{z=z^{-}}^{z^{+}-1}l_{\varepsilon,t}(z)\geq\frac{J}{2}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{T}{2}.

We note that even if two values
u(t,i)
and
u(t,j)
satisfy
|u(t,i)-u(t,i)|<\varepsilon
, they may fall into different intervals as defined in (
4.3
). Nevertheless, the following inequality holds:

(4.5)

N_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}l^{2}_{\varepsilon,t}(z),

where
N_{\varepsilon}(t)
is the approximate self-intersection count introduced earlier in (
1.6
).

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the sum over
z
, and combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), we obtain, on the event
A_{K,T}
,

(4.6)

\begin{split}\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{\varepsilon}(t)&\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}l^{2}_{\varepsilon,t}(z)\\
&\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{z=z^{-}}^{z^{+}-1}l^{2}_{\varepsilon,t}(z)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{z^{+}-z^{-}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{z=z^{-}}^{z^{+}-1}l_{\varepsilon,t}(z)\right)^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4K+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{T}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon TJ^{2}}{32K+8\varepsilon}.\end{split}

We now estimate the logarithmic asymptotic probability under the measure
Q_{T}
. Applying the inequality (
3.11
) and (
4.6
) to a penalizing factor
\mathcal{E}_{T}
defined in (
1.8
) and setting
K=K_{1}J
, we have

\begin{split}\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log Q_{T}\left[R(T,J)<K_{1}J\right]&\leq\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log\mathbb{E}^{P_{T}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{T}1_{R(T,J)<K_{1}J}\right]\\
&\quad-\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log Z_{T}\\
&\leq-\frac{\beta\varepsilon J^{2}}{32K_{1}J+8\varepsilon}+C\beta J+\frac{a^{2}J}{2}.\end{split}

Since
a>0
is arbitrary, we may optimize over
a
and choose
0<K_{1}<C(\varepsilon)
sufficiently small so that the coefficient is strictly negative. This implies that the exponential decay rate is negative, and thus

\lim_{T\to\infty}Q_{T}\left[R(T,J)<K_{1}J\right]=0,

which completes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

5.
Large distance tail estimate

In this section, we establish an asymptotic upper bound for the tail probability of the quantity
R(T,J)
defined in (
2.2
). The result is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists a constant
K_{2}>0
, independent of
\beta
and
J
, such that

\lim_{T\to\infty}Q_{T}\left[R(T,J)>K_{2}J\right]=0.

To prove this result, we start with analyzing the structure of
\bar{u}(t)
in (
2.1
) by

\begin{split}\bar{u}(t)&=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}u(t,n)\\
&=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}G_{t}(n,k)u_{0}(k)+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}G_{t-s}(n,k)\xi(s,k)\\
&=:\bar{S}_{1}+\bar{S}_{2}.\end{split}

Recall that the kernel
G_{t}(n,m)
admits the
L^{2}-
eigenfunction expansion in (
1.4
)

G_{t}(n,k)=\sum_{m=0}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k),

where
\rho_{m}=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right),\phi_{m}(n)=\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(n+0.5)}{J}\right).
Using trigonometric identities and orthogonality properties, we find that for
m\geq 1
,

\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\phi_{m}(n)=0.

Thus, only the
m=0
term contributes to the spatial average
\bar{u}(t)
, yielding

\bar{S}_{1}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}a_{0}u_{0}(k)=a_{0}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}u_{0}(k),

and

\bar{S}_{2}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}a_{0}\xi(s,k)=a_{0}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\xi(s,k).

Substituting into the full solution from (
1.5
), we isolate the deviation from the mean:

u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k)u_{0}(k)+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t-s}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k)\xi(s,k).

As
|\rho_{m}|<1
for
m\geq 1
, the deterministic term (involving
u_{0}
) decays to zero uniformly in
n
as
t\to\infty
. Therefore, the long-time behavior of
R(T,J)
is governed entirely by the stochastic term. To simplify, we assume
u_{0}\equiv 0
, so that

\begin{split}u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t)&=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}a_{m}\rho_{m}^{t-s}\phi_{m}(n)\phi_{m}(k)\xi(s,k)\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}X_{t}^{(m)}\phi_{m}(n),\end{split}

where

X_{t}^{(m)}:=a_{m}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\rho_{m}^{t-s}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\phi_{m}(k)\xi(s,k)

satisfies the recursion

X_{t+1}^{(m)}=\rho_{m}X_{t}^{(m)}+\tilde{\xi}_{t}^{(m)},\quad X_{0}^{(m)}=0,

with
\tilde{\xi}_{t}^{(m)}:=a_{m}\rho_{m}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{J-1}\phi_{m}(k)\xi(t,k)
an i.i.d Guassian random variable of variance
\sigma_{m}^{2}=\rho^{2}_{m}
. Thus, for any
1\leq m\leq J-1
,
X_{t}^{(m)}
is an AR(1) process, and using the orthonormality of
\{\phi_{m}(n)\}
, we obtain

(5.1)

R(T,J)^{2}=\frac{1}{TJ}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}(u(t,n)-\bar{u}(t))^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(X_{t}^{(m)}\right)^{2}.

We now apply the large deviation principle established in
[
BS93
]
for a quadratic functional of the autoregressive process
S_{T}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(X_{t}^{(m)}\right)^{2}
with an explicit rate function.

Lemma 5.1

(
[
BS93
]
)
.

If
-1<\rho_{m}<1
, then
\left\{S^{(m)}_{T}\right\}
satisfies a large deviation principle with good rate function

I(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2x}{1+\sqrt{4\rho_{m}^{2}x^{2}+1}}\right)+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{m}^{2}}\left[(\rho_{m}^{2}+1)x-\sqrt{4\rho_{m}^{2}x^{2}+1}\right]&\text{for }x>0,\\
\infty&\text{for }x\leq 0.\end{cases}

Proof.

For a fixed
m
, we denote
X_{t}:=X^{(m)}_{t}
and
S_{t}:=S^{(m)}_{t}
for notational convenience. The integration of the Gaussian density yields the following identity for any
\lambda<\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{m}}:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda X^{2}_{t+1}\right)|X_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\lambda\sigma_{m}^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda\rho_{m}^{2}X_{t}^{2}}{1-2\lambda\sigma_{m}^{2}}\right).

Using this expression, we apply iterated conditional expectations and exploit the assumption
X_{0}=0
. This allows us to compute the moment-generating function of
S_{T}
as

\mathbb{E}[\exp(yS_{T})]=\prod_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-2\sigma_{m}^{2}\lambda_{k})^{-1/2},

where the squence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is defined recursively by

\lambda_{k+1}=\frac{\rho_{m}^{2}\lambda_{k}}{1-2\lambda\sigma_{m}^{2}}+y,

with initial condition
\lambda_{0}=y<\frac{1}{2\sigma_{m}^{2}}
. The remainder of the proof proceeds as in
[
BS93
]
, thereby establishing the desired result.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

From a direct computation, the rate function
I(x)
vanishes at
x=\frac{1}{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}
, which implies that large deviations occur only when
x>\frac{1}{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}
.

Thanks to Lemma
5.1
and Remark
5.1
, for any
K>\frac{1}{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}
, we have

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\log P\left(S^{(m)}_{T}>K\right)\leq-I(K),

and hence, there exists a
T(m)
such that, for all
T>T(m)
,

(5.2)

P\left(S^{(m)}_{T}>K\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{TI(K)}{2}\right).

We now aggregate over
m
to estimate the tail probability of
R(T,J)
. Define the normalizing constant

(5.3)

c_{0}(J)=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\frac{1}{{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}}\right)^{-1}=\left[\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\csc^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{J}\right)\right]^{-1}=\left(\frac{J^{2}-1}{3}\right)^{-1}.

Now fix
K>0
such that
K^{2}c_{0}(J)>1
, or equivalently,

K>\sqrt{\frac{J^{2}-1}{3}}.

Then, for
T>\max\limits_{m=1,\dots,J}T(m)
, (
5.1
), (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) imply

\begin{split}\limsup_{T\to\infty}P[R(T,J)>K]&=\limsup_{T\to\infty}P\left[R(T,J)^{2}>K^{2}\right]\\
&=\limsup_{T\to\infty}P\left(\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}S_{T}^{(m)}>K^{2}\right)\\
&\leq\limsup_{T\to\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}P\left(S_{T}^{(m)}>\frac{c_{0}(J)K^{2}}{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}\right)\\
&\leq\limsup_{T\to\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\exp\left(-\frac{TI\left(\frac{c_{0}(J)K^{2}}{1-\rho_{m}^{2}}\right)}{2}\right)=0.\end{split}

Each term in the sum decays exponentially in
T
, and the total number of terms is
J-1
, so the full sum also vanishes as
T\to\infty
. Thus, setting
K=K_{2}J
, we conclude that

\lim_{T\to\infty}Q_{T}\left[R(T,J)>K_{2}J\right]=0,

as desired. This completes the proof of Proposition
5.1
as well as Theorem
2.1
.
