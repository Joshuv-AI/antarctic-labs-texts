Virtual reality–based music attention training for acquired brain injury: A randomized crossover study
Authors: Eunju Jeong, Yeajin Ham, Su Jeong Lee, Joon‐Ho Shin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis single‐blind randomized crossover study aimed to explore the effectiveness of virtual reality–based music attention training (VR‐MAT) on cognitive function and examine its potential as a cognitive assessment tool in people with acquired brain injury (ABI). Overall, 24 participants with cognitive impairment secondary to a first‐ever ABI underwent VR‐MAT and conventional cognitive training (CCT) 3 months after onset. This was performed in two 4‐week phases, over 8 weeks. During VR‐MAT, participants engaged in attention training through a four‐level virtual drumming program designed to enhance various attentional aspects. In contrast, during CCT, participants underwent structured conventional training, including card sorting and computerized training. Neuropsychological evaluations were performed preintervention, during the fourth and eighth weeks, and post‐intervention using tests to evaluate attention and executive function, along with global neuropsychological assessments. In the VR‐MAT group, significant differences were observed between pre‐ and post‐intervention in the trail making test‐black and white version B (p = 0.009) and version B–A (p = 0.018) and clinical dementia rating‐sum of boxes (p = 0.035). In the CCT group, significant differences were observed in spatial working memory (p = 0.005) and the mini‐mental state examination scores (p = 0.003). VR‐MAT is an effective cognitive intervention that is particularly beneficial for improving attention in people with ABI.

INTRODUCTION

Cognitive function is fundamental for the successful execution of daily tasks and effective participation in rehabilitation programs. For instance, attention is crucial for activities such as driving or following complex instructions, whereas executive functions, including planning and problem solving, are essential for activities such as cooking and managing finances. Cognitive impairment is common in individuals with acquired brain injury (ABI) and significantly hinders their ability to engage in rehabilitation, perform daily activities, and participate socially. This ultimately affects their quality of life. 1 , 2 Attention deficits and executive dysfunction are especially prevalent among individuals with ABI and manifest in various forms These forms include diminished alertness, heightened distractibility, and compromised supervisory attention control. Given their substantial role in cognitive dysfunction, addressing attention deficits is critical for ABI rehabilitation. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Previous studies have shown that patients with ABI find auditory attention particularly challenging; 7 , 8 however, consistent and targeted auditory attention training is crucial for promoting cognitive improvements post‐ABI. 9

A body of literature on music, neuroscience, and rehabilitation has revealed positive outcomes in attention and executive functioning in patients with post‐ABI cognitive impairment, including those diagnosed with traumatic brain injury and stroke. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These beneficial effects of music, particularly on attentional and executive functions, are attributed to the dynamic and evolving nature of music, which promotes arousal. Moreover, music facilitates neurophysiological responses that engage brain regions critical to cognitive processes. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 For example, listening to music improves attentional functions necessary for processing auditory information, even when listeners are not actively engaging with the music. 21 Studies indicate that different modes of listening can stimulate brain regions associated with different attention types, and that music can be used to develop and assess auditory attention skills. 13 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Recent advances have applied active music engagement in cognitive rehabilitation, with promising results for improving attention in individuals with ABI. 12 , 29 , 30 , 31 Additionally, music attention control training (MACT) has demonstrated benefits for enhancing attention in diverse groups. These include youths with neurodevelopmental issues and psychiatric patients, with marked improvements in selective and divided attention after structured training sessions. 6 , 32 , 33 , 34 Comparisons with standard treatments suggest the potential of MACT as an effective intervention for attentional enhancement in various populations. Recently, Jones investigated the use of instrumental music to address cognitive impairments following ABI, demonstrating its potential efficacy. 6 Additionally, Siponkoski et al. employed a crossover randomized controlled trial to explore the clinical efficacy and neural basis of music therapy in improving cognitive function in patients with traumatic brain injury. Their results highlighted improvements in executive functions and prefrontal neuroplasticity.

However, the efficacy of music therapy in improving cognitive function has not yet been established. A recent systematic review revealed limited evidence supporting the effectiveness of music therapy on memory and attention, although it showed benefits for gait or upper‐limb function. 35 Similarly, in patients with dementia, music therapy did not show evidence of improving cognition; however, it positively impacted emotions, such as anxiety. 36 These mixed results may be influenced by the multifaceted nature of cognitive functions, such as attention, memory, and executive functioning. Additionally, the reliability of music therapy for ABI has been questioned owing to variations in its therapeutic applications, highlighting the need for standardized therapeutic protocols. 15 , 35 Although Jones et al. offered preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of MACT in improving attention in adults with ABI, they also emphasized the need for systematic studies to bridge the literature gap. Shin and Jeong claimed that the significance of music in behavioral changes during therapy was underemphasized, with a focus primarily on assessing functional outcomes rather than evaluating musical engagement. Understanding the interplay between music and clinical changes is crucial to validate the effectiveness of music therapy and optimize music‐based interventions. Therefore, they developed a virtual reality–based music attention training (VR‐MAT), which incorporates replicable therapeutic protocols and music‐based assessment tools for cognitive rehabilitation after ABI.

We aimed to compare the effects of VR‐MAT on cognitive functions, especially attentional and executive functions, with those of dose‐matched conventional cognitive training (CCT) in patients with ABI. We assessed various cognitive function outcomes to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the effects of VR‐MAT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This single‐blind, randomized crossover study compared the efficacy of VR‐MAT with that of CCT in individuals with ABI. Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two treatment sequences: VR‐MAT followed by CCT (VC) or CCT followed by VR‐MAT (CV) (Figure 1). Randomization was performed using a computer‐generated sequence and concealed using sealed opaque envelopes drawn from a container. Group allocation was performed by sequentially opening sealed envelopes. The interventions were conducted for 30 min, five times a week, and over 8 weeks (divided into two 4‐week phases). Participants continued their standard rehabilitation program consisting of 1 h of physical therapy and 30 min of occupational therapy with no additional cognitive interventions.

CONSORT diagram of the present study.

Participants

Participants who experienced cognitive impairment after ABI and were admitted to a rehabilitation hospital between September 2015 and April 2018 were recruited. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first‐ever ABI with mild to moderate cognitive impairment (clinical dementia rating [CDR] scores of 0.5–1) 37 ; (2) an ABI diagnosis made at least 3 months prior and that was supported by medical records or brain imaging; (3) adequate upper‐limb strength (≥3 on the Medical Research Council scale); and (4) age >19 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pre‐existing cognitive deficits prior to an ABI; (2) severe aphasia or cognitive communication disorder; (3) any neurological or psychiatric disorders other than ABI; (4) severe depression (Geriatric Depression Scale‐Short Form score of ≥8); and (5) uncontrolled medical illnesses such as severe cardiac or respiratory disease. This study was approved by the institutional review board of our center (NRC‐2015‐04‐032) and registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (KCT0003783). All participants provided written informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We enrolled 24 participants into the VC and CV groups (n = 12 in each group). The sample size was determined based on the novelty of the VR‐MAT and conformed to the standard sample size recommendations for pilot studies. 38 , 39

Interventions

Virtual reality–based music attention training

VR‐MAT involves visually guided bilateral drumming within a multisensory, virtual environment. 40 VR‐MAT consists of four levels of training that were designed to hierarchically rehabilitate various attention types (Figure 2). VR‐MAT Level 1 is a one‐handed drumming task and VR‐MAT Level 2 is a two‐handed drumming task. In VR‐MAT Levels 1 and 2, participants are asked to play an electronic drum using drumsticks, following a four‐beat rhythmic pattern presented visually on a computer screen. The visual cues are balls falling from the top of a display onto the virtual drum surface. The ball color, number, and time interval are varied to guide the target drum and rhythm pattern. VR‐MAT Level 3 is a two‐hand drumming task that employs the rhythmic patterns of VR‐MAT Level 2 in reverse. Finally, VR‐MAT Level 4 randomly alternates between VR‐MAT Levels 2 and 3, leading to a shift in mindsets through the cognitive music interaction tasks. VR‐MAT Levels 1 and 2 use visual perception, temporary retention, movement planning, and motor execution, whereas the back‐and‐forth experience in VR‐MAT Levels 3 and 4 activates higher order cognitive processes, such as mental flexibility and executive function.

Examples of virtual reality–based music attention therapy (VR‐MAT). (A) VR‐MAT Level 1, one‐handed drumming. (B) VR‐MAT Level 2: two‐handed drumming. (C) VR‐MAT Level 3: two‐handed drumming reversed. (D) VR‐MAT Level 4: two‐handed drumming randomized.

The participants were provided with a visual presentation of a virtual drum on a computer screen with moving visual cues (i.e., red and blue balls that fell from the top of the screen to the surface of the virtual drum) to guide the patterns and timing of their drumming. As participants played the drum in response to visual cues, they received both visual and auditory feedback indicating whether their drumming was correct or incorrect. The auditory feedback provided was a kick or snare drum sound depending on the side of the drum that the participants played. In contrast, the visual feedback varied depending on response accuracy: (1) If the drum stroke occurred within ±100 ms, a flashlight was displayed (matched response); (2) If the drum stroke preceded the exact target point within −200 to −100 ms, the ball exploded (mismatch response); and (3) If the ball was missed within the specified time range, no visual cue was presented (unmatched response).

In the VR‐MAT setup, two electronic drums were connected to a computer running Unity software (Unity Technologies). The setup was operated using an Android personal computer with visual and auditory displays (Figure 3). The original version of the VR‐MAT was developed to include the application of a head‐mounted display, to provide an immersive experience. 40 In the present study, however, it was delivered in the form of computer simulations on a display monitor, to prevent issues of cybersickness and disorientation. 41 , 42 To mitigate these risks, some studies have recommended non‐immersive VR, such as using two‐dimensional computer screens instead of head‐mounted displays. 43 , 44 All behavioral responses during the VR‐MAT were recorded in the system. Experienced occupational therapists were seated with the participants completing the VR‐MAT interventions. Each session (task) comprised 20–21 trials of the four subtasks for an approximate completion time of 30 min.

Conventional cognitive training

CCT was designed to reflect attention process training and hierarchically structured to address focused, sustained, selective, alternating, and divided attention. The specifications of the CCT were as follows. (1) Focused attention training: aims to enhance the ability to concentrate on a single relevant stimulus while ignoring distractions; it involves simple reaction time tasks such as pressing a button in response to a specific stimulus or identifying and responding to a target object or letter among a set of distractors. (2) Sustained attention training: focuses on maintaining consistent attention and concentration on a task for an extended period; it includes tasks such as mentally tracing a path through a maze or listening to an auditory passage without losing focus. (3) Selective attention training: develops the ability to focus on relevant information while ignoring irrelevant stimuli; it includes tasks such as finding specific target words among distractor words or identifying specific sounds amid background noise. (4) Alternating attention training: develops the ability to efficiently shift focus between tasks or stimuli; it involves tasks such as switching between identifying shapes and colors in a series. (5) Divided attention training: focuses on the ability to process and respond to multiple stimuli simultaneously; it involves dual‐task activities such as performing calculations while engaging in physical activity.

CCT was implemented step by step to ensure that basic attentional skills were solidified before progressing to more demanding tasks. CCT comprised activities such as card sorting, dictation, buzzer use, paper‐and‐pencil tasks, and computerized cognitive exercises, all of which have been validated in patients with ABI. 45 , 46 The training was tailored to each participant's cognitive function, with difficulty level adjustments. The CCT was administered by experienced occupational therapists who provided immediate feedback following the completion of each task and summarized the participants’ performance at the end of each session.

Outcome measurement

Outcome assessments were performed at baseline, 4 weeks (midpoint of crossover), and 8 weeks (post‐intervention) by a blinded neuropsychologist, as previously described. Outcome assessments were conducted over 2 days, with each session lasting approximately 30–40 min to minimize fatigue. One day was dedicated to clinical cognitive assessments, and the following day was reserved for computerized neuropsychological tests. Planned breaks were included to mitigate potential fatigue or difficulty during assessments. However, none of the participants reported any difficulties. The VR‐MAT behavioral test was performed four times: pre (day 1), Mid‐1 (day 8), Mid‐2 (day 15), and post (day 22).

Primary outcome measure

Trail making test

The trail making test (TMT) 47 , 48 consisted of two separate parts. In Part A, participants were required to sequentially connect 25 circled numbers distributed in a standardized random layout (e.g., 1‐2‐3). In Part B, participants alternately connected circled numbers from 1 to 13 and circled letters from A to L (e.g., 1‐A‐2‐B‐3‐C). The TMT‐B&W included white numbers within black circles and black numbers within white circles on a test sheet and was divided into Parts A and B. In the TMT‐B&W Part A (TMT‐B&W‐A), the participants drew a line to sequentially connect the 25 circled numbers without lifting their pens or pencils from the test sheet. In the TMT‐B&W Part B (TMT‐B&W‐B), consecutive numbers alternated between white and black backgrounds (e.g., 1‐W‐2‐B‐3‐W‐4‐B). Part A was a test of visual search and motor speed skills, whereas Part B assessed higher level cognitive abilities, such as mental flexibility. The TMTs measured the time required to complete Parts A and B. We collected the TMT‐A, TMT‐B, TMT‐B&W‐A, and TMT‐B&W‐B data. Differences between TMT‐B and TMT‐A (TMT‐B–A) and between TMT‐B&W‐A and TMT‐B&W‐B (TMT‐B&W‐B–A) were calculated to represent isolated measures of executive functioning, discounting psychomotor speed, and visual searching. 40 We set the TMT and TMT‐B&W as the primary outcome measurements.

Secondary outcome measures

Computerized neuropsychological tests

For the executive functioning assessment, we administered computerized neuropsychological tests, namely the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB; Cambridge Cognition) Eclipse version 5.0.0. 49 This included the attention switching task (AST), the stop signal task (SST), and the spatial working memory (SWM) task. These tests assess functions associated with the frontal lobe, including attention, cognitive flexibility, response inhibition (impulse control), and retention and manipulation of visuospatial information related to strategy and working memory.

Attention switching test. The AST evaluated the ability to process conflicting information. Visual stimuli (such as arrows on the screen) were either congruent or incongruent depending on whether the direction and placement of the arrows were identical. Two consecutive trials were non‐switched when identical to the preceding trial or switched when different. The participants were asked to respond to the direction of the arrows while ignoring their placement. Behavioral indicators included the latency of correct responses to congruent (AST‐congruent latency) or incongruent stimuli (AST‐incongruent latency), correct ratio (rate of correct response), congruency cost (additional time taken for incongruent vs. congruent stimuli), and switching cost (additional time taken for switched vs. non‐switched trials). Better performance was indicated by a low latency and high percentage of correct responses.

Stop signal test. The SST assessed the ability to inhibit irrelevant responses. The visual stimuli comprised different arrow directions, whereas the auditory stimuli included tones with different pitches. Participants were instructed to react to an arrow stimulus by choosing one of two options based on the arrow's direction unless an auditory signal indicated that they should withhold their response. SST performance was gauged using the stop signal reaction time (SSRT) and the proportion of successful stops. A low SSRT and high success rate reflected better inhibitory control.

Spatial working memory test. The SWM test measured the ability to retain and manipulate visuospatial information, which was indicative of SWM and executive functioning. The participants were asked to search for tokens within a set of boxes on the screen and use them to fill a column on the same screen. We measured between errors—revisiting a box where a token had already been found—and strategy—the efficiency of the search pattern. Fewer errors and a more systematic search pattern suggested superior performance.

Clinical cognitive measures

For a comprehensive cognitive assessment, we administered the CDR (global CDR [0–3], CDR‐sum of boxes [CDR‐SB; 0–18]), 37 global deterioration scale (GDS [1–8]), 50 and the mini‐mental state examination (MMSE; 0–30). The CDR was determined through semi‐structured interviews. Global cognitive performance was assessed in six domains (memory, orientation, judgment and problem solving, community affairs, home and hobbies, and personal care) on a five‐point scale ranging 0–3. An algorithm integrated the six domain scores into one overall category of severity: the global CDR score (range: 0–3; higher scores indicate greater cognitive impairment). The CDR‐SB score (range: 0–18, with higher scores indicating a more severe stage of dementia) was determined by averaging the ratings for each of the six categories, thereby providing an expanded and more quantitative version of the scale. Although originally developed for Alzheimer's disease, the CDR has been validated and recommended for use in ABIs, including vascular cognitive impairment and brain injury. 51 , 52 , 53 The GDS comprises a brief clinical test to rate dementia severity. It evaluates a wide range of cognitive functions, including memory, orientation, attention, and executive functioning, which are relevant to general cognitive ability beyond dementia alone. 54 The scale is designed to detect subtle cognitive changes, making it useful for assessing cognitive function across a spectrum, from normal aging to severe dementia. Several studies have successfully employed the GDS in non‐dementia populations, demonstrating its applicability in measuring cognitive function among individuals with ABI. 55 , 56 , 57 The GDS requires a clinician to rate dementia severity on a scale of 1 (asymptomatic) to 7 (late dementia). The measure was developed with heavy emphasis on memory function and the ability to complete activities of daily living (such as dressing, traveling, and eating). The MMSE (range: 0–30), the most widely used screening test for cognitive deficits, comprises 30 items. 58

VR‐MAT behavioral data

Behavioral data on performance during the VR‐MAT were collected. The performance was classified as matched if the drum strokes fell within ±100 ms; otherwise, the performance was classified as unmatched. VR‐MAT accuracy (VR‐MAT‐acc) was calculated by dividing the number of matched strokes by the total number of balls. Variables were indicated as “variable name‐level N” to describe each VR‐MAT level. For example, “VR‐MAT‐acc‐Level 1” indicated Level 1 accuracy data.

Statistical analysis

For neuropsychological outcome measures, a crossover analysis was used to evaluate the differences between the two study sequences (VC vs. CV), arising from the specific nature of the crossover design. 59 , 60 To test for carryover effects, unpaired t‐tests were performed for changes observed during each intervention period. If the carryover effects were not significant, the changes in VR‐MAT and CCT in the VC and CV were aggregated. Consequently, the results were aggregated into two groups for the VR‐MAT and CCT interventions to enable direct comparison between them. If the carryover effects were statistically significant, the second‐period data were excluded and only the first‐period data were used. The carryover effect was observed only with SST direction errors, in the stop‐and‐go trials; we compared them using only first‐period data. The remaining variables from the VC and CV groups were compared between VR‐MAT and CCT. Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests (or paired t‐tests) were used to evaluate changes in outcome measures before and after the intervention, to ensure the homogeneity of the distribution, and the equality of variances was verified. Intergroup differences in VR‐MAT and CCT were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (or two‐tailed t‐test).

For the VR‐MAT behavioral data, normality was confirmed through visual inspection of a Q–Q plot. We then performed two‐way repeated‐measures analyses of variance (RMANOVA) for VR‐MAT‐acc to track changes in VR‐MAT by level (Levels 1–4) and time (pre, Mid‐1, Mid‐2) and post hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction. Data were analyzed using R software version 4.2.2 (http://www.r‐project.org). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Of the 24 participants enrolled in the study, one in the CV group dropped out during the CCT phase (before the 4‐week intervention period). Therefore, data from 23 participants were analyzed. The average ages of the VC and CV groups were 50.4 ± 15.7 and 43.0 ± 13.4 years, respectively. No significant intergroup differences were observed in age, sex, education, or time as ABI onset (Table S1). No significant intergroup differences were observed in pretreatment MMSE scores or other cognitive assessments (Table S2). None of the participants had a musical background or were engaged in music‐related work prior to having an ABI. During the study, one participant in the CV group withdrew midway because of a transfer to another rehabilitation hospital.

DISCUSSION

This randomized crossover study highlights the positive impact of VR‐MAT on cognitive function, particularly in terms of attention switching and executive function, among individuals with ABI. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to implement music‐based cognitive training using VR. This is the first empirical study to compare the effects of music‐based cognitive training with those of CCT in patients with ABI. Most previous studies have compared the effects of music therapy on cognition with those of conventional physical training. 14 , 61 , 62 Our findings demonstrate that VR‐MAT leads to improvements in TMT‐B, TMT‐B&W, and CDR‐SB scores, reflecting enhancements in complex cognitive functions and the general cognitive state. In contrast, CCT facilitated improvements in SWM between errors and MMSE scores, indicating its effectiveness in addressing executive function, particularly SWM.

The TMT‐A and TMT‐B share neuropsychological functions, including visual search and tracking, sequencing, and sustained attention. The TMT‐B requires additional complex cognitive processes and is more closely associated with cognitive flexibility. Similarly, the difference between the TMT‐A and TMT‐B reflected executive functions without the general processing speed measured using the TMT‐A. Therefore, our results indicate that VR‐MAT improves more complex functions, such as executive function, rather than simple psychomotor speed. Similarly, significant improvements with music‐based interventions have been reported in TMT‐B rather than in other outcome measures, including TMT‐A, memory, and frontal assessment battery scores. 13 , 14 , 15 , 63 , 64 This supports the fact that cognitive training through instrumental music playing benefits executive functioning and enhances arousal levels. More importantly, the processing and playing of rhythmic patterns provided in a VR‐MAT session appeared to enhance the cognitive coherence and performance readiness needed for the TMT rather than for the SWM test. 65 Collectively, our findings support the specific effect of VR‐MAT on more complex types of cognitive function, such as executive function and cognitive flexibility, rather than simple attention. This was similarly observed in the behavioral changes due to music, as indicated by Levels 3 and 4 of the VR‐MAT‐acc (Figure 5). This suggests that patterns and tasks presented at different levels of complexity in the VR‐MAT are appropriate for enhancing the cognitive abilities relevant to such clinical measurements.

The CDR‐SB and MMSE scores improved in both the CCT and VR‐MAT groups. CDR‐SB measures cognitive and functional abilities across multiple domains. Thus, the improvement in the VR‐MAT scores indicated both global cognition and daily functioning. VR‐MAT integrates attention with visual cueing, short‐term memory of visual patterns, motor performance, and auditory and tactile feedback, leading to widespread brain activation. 66 , 67 Moreover, CDR‐SB is heavily dependent on participants’ reports, which can be significantly influenced by the subjective experience and emotional state of the participants. Owing to its musical nature, VR‐MAT involves activities that are often enjoyable and engaging, potentially enhancing mood and emotional well‐being. These mood‐related benefits, such as a participant's interest in and sense of fun, might have influenced results other than the MMSE score. Although we did not measure emotional aspects, numerous studies have demonstrated that active music playing is enjoyable. It promotes neuroplastic changes in the sensorimotor, auditory, and memory systems, and modulates mood. 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72

Bilateral drumming induces body movement, which has a profound effect on the motor and emotional systems. These VR‐MAT characteristics may be associated with a 100% retention rate during VR‐MAT intervention, highlighting its feasibility in patients with ABI. Recent advances in computer‐assisted cognitive rehabilitation have improved accessibility for people with neurological impairments, enabling its use in various settings, including home and nonhospital environments. 73 However, adverse effects, such as mental fatigue, hinder the use of computer‐assisted cognitive rehabilitation. 73 , 74 In our study, occupational therapists observed that two participants experienced fatigue during CCT, whereas none of the participants reported fatigue during VR‐MAT. These findings suggest that the music components of VR‐MAT may significantly enhance engagement and may be experienced as less fatiguing than CCT. This may be attributed to the gamified features of VR‐MAT, which potentially boost positive emotions and motivation to participate.

The musical tasks involving virtual drum playing were meticulously designed by one of the authors, a board‐certified music therapist, to align with the model of attention hierarchy used in cognitive rehabilitation. Thus, training specific types of attention or all types of attention sequentially and hierarchically is possible. Second, VR‐MAT is simple to administer in a consistent and reliable manner, allowing more individuals with ABI to benefit from music‐based interventions. In particular, prior musical training was not required for the intervention to be effective as none of the participants had a prior musical background. Third, given that diverse technologies are becoming increasingly integrated into music therapy settings, 75 VR‐MAT may represent a new or alternative interface that allows healthcare providers, including music therapists, to mitigate the rehabilitative affordances of music‐based interventions. VR‐MAT collects and evaluates the accuracy of musical behavior; therefore, healthcare providers can make data‐based decisions when selecting the type of rhythm and the task level in VR‐MAT. They can also determine the number of repetitions for the training. Furthermore, as the number of VR‐MAT cases increases and a sufficient data pool is created, self‐administered patient training will become possible.

Study limitations

This study had a few limitations. First, VR‐MAT does not utilize the innate social nature of music therapy, in which participants interact with other participants or therapists. Music therapy often involves interactive activities in which the therapist and patient engage together, helping patients practice skills such as turn‐taking, listening to others, and responding musically, all of which are relevant to attention. Additionally, group‐based music therapy inherently fosters a shared experience, making therapy more enjoyable and increasing patients’ desire to participate, leading to greater engagement in therapy. Our VR‐MAT was conducted between the participants and a computer rather than between the participants and a therapist. It was also designed as a solitary activity and was not conducted as group therapy. Therefore, the efficacy of VR‐MAT may be lower than that of traditional music therapy owing to the absence of direct human interaction and a shared social experience. Second, the trial had a limited sample size and employed a crossover design without incorporating a washout period. Third, the Geriatric Depression Scale used as a screening test was not originally developed for our study population. Although originally targeted at older populations, it has been validated in various populations, including nongeriatric populations. 76 , 77 Fourth, we did not include musical backgrounds in the inclusion and exclusion criteria. These factors may have affected participation or the effects of the intervention; however, none of the participants in this study had a background in music education or professional music performance.

CONCLUSION

VR‐MAT improved executive function, global cognitive performance, and cognitive performance in patients with ABI. These findings support the efficacy of VR‐MAT as a successful intervention and assessment tool for the cognitive rehabilitation of patients with ABI. Therefore, this novel VR‐MAT approach may be an innovative cognitive rehabilitation technique.
