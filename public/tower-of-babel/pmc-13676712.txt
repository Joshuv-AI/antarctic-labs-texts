Biases in high throughput sequencing methods affect microbial molecular ecology networks and keystone taxa identification in the Cinnamomum migao rhizosphere and phyllosphere

Published: 2026

Introduction

In nature, there exists a close relationship between epiphytic microorganisms and their host plants. Healthy microbial-host interactions play a crucial role in promoting plant growth and development, enhancing productivity, and protecting plants from pathogenic microorganisms (Bowman and Arnold, 2021). In long-term co-evolution history, plants and microorganisms have formed a closely interconnected symbiotic entity, with rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbial communities being the most representative, symbolizing belowground and aboveground plant microecosystems, respectively (Bashir et al., 2022; Compant et al., 2025; Sohrabi et al., 2023). The symbiotic interaction network between these microbial communities and their hosts persists throughout the plant's life cycle, harboring diverse functional microorganisms—including pathogens, symbionts, and decomposers—that form stable microbial ecological networks (MENs), profoundly influencing both microbial community dynamics and host productivity (Zeng et al., 2024). At the ecosystem level, these interactions affect host plant health and nutrient cycling processes while the formation of microbial MENs is driven by multiple factors including host plant exudates, pH, rainfall, and radiation (Li et al., 2023). Although differences exist between phyllosphere and rhizosphere microbial communities, both significantly impact plant health and environmental stress adaptation. Despite early academic recognition of the importance of rhizosphere-phyllosphere microbial MENs, the ecological processes involving these complex microbial communities remain largely enigmatic—often referred to as "dark matter" in scientific understanding (Bao et al., 2020).

In recent years, technologies such as amplicon sequencing, absolute amplicon sequencing, 2bRAD-M, and metagenomics have provided powerful tools for exploring the composition and interactions of phyllosphere and rhizosphere microbial communities (Boughner and Singh, 2016; Weiss et al., 2016). With the introduction of network theory into the microbial community interactions, algorithms including Sparse Correlations for Compositional data, Molecular Ecological Network Analysis, Local Similarity Analysi, and Co-occurrence Network inference have been proposed, offering theoretical frameworks to infer microbial interactions from sequencing datasets using mathematical principles (Matchado et al., 2021; Weiss et al., 2016). Notably, current microbial network construction predominantly relies on amplicon relative quantification datasets, overlooking differences in total microbial biomass and transcriptional levels across samples. Increasing evidence suggests that using “absolute abundance” for microbial network construction can provide more ecologically meaningful insights than relative quantification, correcting biases derived from relative abundance data (Maghini et al., 2023). For instance, metagenomics-based MENs exhibit significantly higher complexity than amplicon-based networks, primarily because metagenomics achieves comprehensive annotation covering fungal, bacterial, and archaeal, better reflecting species roles within microbial communities. In contrast, amplicon sequencing (ITS, 16S, 18S) typically annotates only one domain (fungi, bacteria, or archaea) and captures only relative species abundance. Although multi-domain network algorithms have been progressively developed, they still primarily reflect inter-domain taxa correlations rather than the complete picture. Merging multi-domain taxa abundances from amplicon sequencing for network construction may introduce biases in understanding species interactions (Feng et al., 2019; Quince et al., 2017). The rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbial communities comprising fungi, bacteria, archaea, and even algae engaged in mutualistic, predatory, and parasitic relationships, constructing MENs encompassing all these taxa is crucial for understanding the microbial community dynamics (Rainey and Quistad, 2020). To overcome amplicon sequencing’s limitations in multi-domain quantification, researchers have proposed using exogenous internal standards to measure the absolute abundances of bacteria, archaea, and fungi in rhizosphere ecosystems, this method enables more accurate microbial interaction analysis and network construction (Faust, 2021). However, substantial research is still needed to elucidate how different sequencing depths and corresponding algorithms affect the structural composition, interspecies interactions, and network architecture. Revealing authentic microbial molecular ecology network in microenvironments such as the phyllosphere and rhizosphere and their impacts on hosts is essential for clarifying microbial evolution and host co-evolution. However, research on this hotspot remains insufficient (Yu et al., 2022).

Cinnamomum migao H. W. Li (Lauraceae) is a Chinese endemic species exclusively distributed in the dry-hot valleys along the Yunnan-Guizhou-Guangxi province border region, its fruits have been used by ethnic minorities in Southwest China to treat cardiovascular diseases (Li et al., 2007). Due to extreme rarity and habitat destruction, C. migao has been classified as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species (de Kok, 2024). Our previous studies revealed close associations between the fruit of C. migao quality and keystone taxa in the rhizospheric microbial interaction network, with rhizosphere demonstrating strong microbial recruitment and selection capabilities (Chen et al., 2023a; Sun et al., 2024). However, a comprehensive understanding of fruit’s epiphytic microbial networks remain limited. The assembly of epiphytic microbiota depends on sophisticated communication and signaling networks between host plants and microbes. Elucidating the authentic interaction networks and driving mechanisms between rhizospheric and phyllospheric microbial and host plants holds significant scientific importance, though sequencing method critically impacts understanding of these processes (Tkacz et al., 2018). Therefore, this study integrates multiple methods: relative quantitative amplicon sequencing, absolute quantitative amplicon sequencing, 2bRAD-M, and metagenomic sequencing to construct microbial datasets for rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities. Non-targeted metabolomics was employed to analyze volatile metabolites, investigating species diversity, network parameters, keystone taxa, and the relationships between volatile metabolites and microbial recruitment in different compartments under various sequencing methods (Schäfer et al., 2023).

Our results showed that plant rhizosphere and phyllosphere form distinct niches, harboring significantly divergent microbial communities with differential diversity. The discrepancies in absolute amplicon sequencing versus amplicon sequencing of microbial amplicons between phyllosphere and rhizosphere significantly influenced the properties of MENs constructed from these communities and the identification of keystone taxa, further highlighting the importance of implementing absolute quantification in microbial studies. Furthermore, methodological factors related to sequencing resolution—including microbial community boundary delineation and annotation depth - collectively impacted the network characteristics and keystone taxa identification in both phyllosphere and rhizosphere microbial systems. Notably, volatile metabolites from C. migao rhizosphere and phyllosphere were found to show potential correlations with microbial community systems, although causal relationships cannot be established from this study.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Sampling was carried out in a five-year-old C. migao plantation in Pingtang County, Guizhou Province, China. A 20 × 20 m plot was established, and ten healthy plants with uniform growth were randomly selected from diagonal and central positions to form composite biological replicates-comprising 10 biological replicates each for the rhizosphere and phyllospher. Rhizosphere soil was collected using sterilized shovels to excavate soil profiles to a depth of 40 cm around each plant, ensuring the root system remained intact. Fine roots (diameter < 1.5 mm) were traced along lateral roots, and soil beyond 2 mm from the root surface was carefully removed. The tightly adhered rhizosphere soil was then transferred into sterile tubes, processed by vortexing and centrifugation in buffer. For leaf sampling, foliage was collected from the mid-canopy in the four cardinal directions per plant and pooled into a composite sample. Phyllosphere microbes were recovered by elution with potassium phosphate buffer and collected on 0.22 μm filters. Processed rhizosphere and phyllosphere sample stored at −80 °C for microbial sequencing, additional untreated leaf and root samples were stored at 4 °C for metabolomic profiling.

High throughput sequencing

DNA extraction The DNA extraction of all samples from the rhizosphere and phyllosphere follows the following procedure: Total DNA was extracted from all samples using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States), the DNA was then diluted with sterile water to a consistent concentration of 10 ng/µL for downstream analyses.

Amplicon library construction and sequencing To characterize bacterial and fungal communities, the hypervariable V3 - V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene and the ITS1 region were amplified using barcode-labeled primer sets 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) / 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′) and ITS1 (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) / ITS2 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′), respectively. PCR was carried out in 15 µL reactions consisting of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix, 2 µM of each primer, and 10 ng of template DNA (measured by Qubit 4.0 fluorometer). The thermal cycling conditions comprised initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min; 30 cycles (determined by preliminary cycle optimization tests to ensure amplification within the exponential phase without plateau saturation) of denaturation (98 °C, 10 s), annealing (50 °C, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 30 s); followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min (Liu et al., 2016).

Absolute amplification library construction and sequencing To enable absolute quantification of microbial communities, multiple spike-ins with identical conserved regions to the internally transcribed spacer and variable regions replaced by random sequence with ∼40% GC content were artificially synthesized. These standards contained conserved regions matching natural 16S rRNA sequences and artificial variable regions with no significant similarity to known public database entries. Each spike-in was added at four concentrations (10³ to 10⁶ copies per reaction). Amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA V3-V4 region was performed using barcoded primers 341F(5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) / 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′), while the fungal ITS1 region was amplified with primers ITS1 (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) / ITS2 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR reactions were conducted in 15 µL volumes containing 10 ng template DNA (quantified via Qubit 4.0, identical to amplicon sequencing to maintain comparability), 4 µL of 5 × FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL FastPfu DNA polymerase, and ddH₂O. The thermal profile included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 45 s) (Jiang et al., 2019).

2bRAD-M library construction and sequencing Library preparation for 2bRAD-M sequencing was conducted following the method of (Wang et al., 2012a) with minor adjustments. Genomic DNA (The reaction system had a total volume of 15 μL, DNA 1 pg-200 ng) was digested using 4 U of BcgI at 37 °C for 3 h. Adaptors were then ligated to the digested fragments in a 20 µL reaction system containing 0.2 µM of each adaptor and 800 U of T4 DNA ligase, incubated at 4 °C for 12 h. The ligation products were amplified by PCR, and amplicons were separated on an 8% polyacrylamide gel. DNA fragments approximately 100 bp in size were excised and eluted in nuclease-free water at 4 °C for 6–12 h. A secondary PCR was performed to incorporate sample-specific barcodes using primers with platform-compatible sequences. Each 20 µL PCR reaction contained 6 µL of the eluted product, 0.2 µM of each primer, 0.3 mM dNTPs, 1 × Phusion HF buffer, and 0.4 U of Phusion high-fidelity DNA polymerase. Final libraries were purified with the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) and sequenced on an Illumina Nova PE150 platform. All laboratory procedures were carried out by Qingdao OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Sun et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2012b).

Metagenome library construction and sequencing Paired-end libraries were constructed from genomic DNA fragmented to approximately 350 bp using a Covaris M220 system. Library preparation was performed with the NEXTFLEX™ Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, USA), and sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform (Illumina Inc., USA).

Bioinformation analysis and data processing

Amplicon sequencing After demultiplexing, raw sequences were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.19.6) and merged with FLASH (v1.2.11). Denoising was performed using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME 2 under standard parameters to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with single-nucleotide resolution. To minimize the effect of sequencing depth on downstream analyses, samples were rarefied to 20,000 sequences per sample, resulting in an average Good’s coverage of 97.90%. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was conducted using the naive Bayes classifier implemented in QIIME 2. Exogenous spike-in sequences were removed prior to subsequent analyses (Chen et al., 2018; Magoč and Salzberg, 2011).

Absolute Amplification sequencing Processed sequences were quality-filtered with fastp (v0.19.6) and merged using FLASH (v1.2.11). Denoising was performed in QIIME 2 (v2020.2) with the DADA2 plugin under default parameters to infer amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) based on sample-specific error profiles. Spike-in-derived ASVs were excluded prior to quantification. Absolute abundances of ASVs were estimated by constructing standard curves from the known concentrations of spike-in sequences and their respective read counts. Taxonomy was assigned using the naive Bayes classifier in QIIME 2 (Chen et al., 2018; Magoč and Salzberg, 2011).

2bRAD-M sequencing A total of 404,199 microbial genomes (bacteria, archaea, and fungi) were retrieved from the GTDB and Ensemble databases. For each genome, in silico restriction fragments were generated for all 16 type 2B restriction enzymes using custom Perl scripts, establishing a comprehensive 2bRAD reference database. The simulated tags were cataloged by their GCF identifiers along with full taxonomic lineages. Unique 2bRAD tags (occurring only once per genome) were subsequently screened across all genomes. Species-specific markers were defined as tags that uniquely mapped to a single species without cross-species matches, forming a dedicated 2bRAD marker database. Taxonomic information can be queried via the GTDB website (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/), which supports lookup using GCF/GCA accession numbers, NCBI taxon names, or GTDB taxonomy (Parks et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2022a).

Metagenome sequencing Metagenome sequencing. Quality-filtered reads were assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.1.2), with contigs shorter than 300 bp discarded. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from the assembled contigs via Prodigal (v2.6.3), and ORFs under 100 bp in length were removed. A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed with CD-HIT (v4.7) using 90% identity and 90% coverage as clustering thresholds. Gene abundance per sample was quantified with SOAPaligner (v2.21), requiring a minimum alignment identity of 95%. Prior to assembly and gene prediction, host-derived and non-microbial sequences were removed from the metagenomic datasets using a taxonomic classification-based filtering strategy. Specifically, quality-filtered reads were classified taxonomically using Diamond (v2.1.3) against the standard NR20230830 database (Blastp, E-value ≤ 1e-05). Reads classified as Streptophyta (Viridiplantae, including plant nuclear, mitochondrial, and chloroplast sequences) were discarded as host-derived contamination (Table S1). Reads classified as Arthropoda were retained only when they could be confidently assigned to microbial-associated taxa; otherwise, they were excluded as potential environmental contaminants. Taxonomic assignments with a Kraken2 confidence score below 0.5 were also discarded to minimize false-positive classifications. Reads passing this filtering step were used for subsequent assembly and gene prediction (Hyatt et al., 2010; Li et al., 2008).

Metabolomics Data Processing and annotation Following GC–MS analysis, raw data were processed using MassHunter Quantitative Analysis (v10.0.7) to generate a three-diMENional matrix in CSV format containing sample names, metabolite identifiers, and peak intensities. The resulting matrix was curated by removing internal standards and known artifacts (e.g., noise, column bleed, and derivatization reagent peaks). After peak alignment and deduplication, metabolites were identified by comparison against the Fiehn and NIST reference libraries (Ding et al., 2022).

Multivariate statistics and analysis

Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed using Spearman’s correlation among taxa (ASVs) detected shared in eight samples. Correlation thresholds and statistical significance (P-values) were determined using the “dplyr” package in R.(Feng et al., 2022). Correlations with P < 0.05 and r > 0.8 were retained for network construction using the “ggClusterNet” package in R. (Wen et al., 2022). Networks were visualized in Gephi 0.9.2. Modules were detected using a greedy modularity optimization algorithm. The topological role of each node was determined based on within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi), calculated with the “ggClusterNet” package in R. Nodes were classified into four categories: module hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.62), network hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi > 0.62), connectors (Zi ≤ 2.5, Pi > 0.62), and peripherals (Zi ≤ 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.62), taxa falling into the first three categories are referred to as “putative keystone taxa” or “topologically important taxa,” rather than biologically validated keystone species, to reflect the computational nature of their identification (Huang et al., 2022). α diversity, including the Shannon diversity and richness indices, was calculated to assess within - sample microbial diversity. β diversity, based on Bray - Curtis distances, was used to evaluate differences in community structure among samples. Overlapping features across samples were visualized using UpSet plots generated with the “UpSetR” package. Associations between keystone taxa and metabolic profiles were examined using Mantel tests (“LinkET” package). A neutral community model was applied to elucidate microbial assembly processes and assess the role of metabolites in shaping rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities. To identify volatile metabolites most strongly associated with microbial community composition, we applied Random Forest (RF) regression using the randomForest package (v4.7) in R. Due to the limited sample size (n = 10 per habitat),used a two-step feature filtering strategy to mitigate overfitting and multicollinearity: metabolites with coefficient of variation (CV) 〈 5% across samples were removed. Among the remaining metabolites, Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were calculated. For any pair with |ρ| 〉 0.8, only the metabolite with higher mean abundance was retained using the findCorrelation function from the “Caret” package. The RF models were trained with 500 trees to ensure convergence of variable importance estimates. Feature importance was measured by the percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE) and node purity (IncNodePurity). To assess whether the model performance exceeded random expectations, 999 permutations of the response variable (the β-diversity vector of microbial communities) were performed, and the LOOCV R² was recalculated for each permuted dataset. Due to the unavailability of an independent test set, the RF analysis was interpreted as an exploratory ranking tool for metabolite importance rather than a definitive predictive classifier. Model performance metrics-the LOOCV R² (Rhizosphere: 0.74, Phyllosphere: 0.63) and Root Mean Square Error (Rhizosphere: 0.19, Phyllosphere: 0.27). (Chen et al., 2023b).

Results

Differences in microbial communities between rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao by 4 sequencing methods

In amplicon sequencing and absolute amplicon sequencing, rhizosphere bacteria were co-annotated to 43 phyla (dominant taxa: Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, Actinobacteriota), while phyllosphere bacteria were annotated to 27 phyla (dominant taxa: Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota). Rhizosphere fungi were annotated to 16 phyla (dominant taxa: Ascomycota, Basidiomycota), and phyllosphere fungi to 7 phyla (dominant taxa: Ascomycota, Basidiomycota). In 2bRAD-M sequencing, rhizosphere were annotated to 60 phyla (dominant taxa: Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, Actinomycetota, Verrucomicrobiota, Dormibacterota, Gemmatimonadota, Chloroflexota), and phyllosphere samples to 39 phyla (dominant taxa: Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Ascomycota). Metagenomic sequencing revealed 252 phyla in the rhizosphere (dominant taxa: Acidobacteriota, Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota, Chloroflexota, Planctomycetota) and 235 phyla in the phyllosphere (dominant taxa: Ascomycota, Pseudomonadota, Streptophyta, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Basidiomycota, Arthropoda) (Fig. 1a). The Upset Venn diagram revealed that four sequencing methods identified 124 shared genera in the rhizosphere(Fig. 1b), and 125 shared genera in the phyllosphere, indicating that expanding the scope of microbial community sequencing significantly increases identified taxa but reduces shared taxa (Fig. 1c). α-diversity showed that rhizosphere microbial diversity was higher than phyllosphere at 4 sequencing methods (Fig. S1). PCoA revealed significant differences in microbial community composition between rhizosphere and phyllosphere, with clear clustering into two distinct groups. Differences in microbial MENs and keystone taxa identification in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao in 4 sequencing methods

MENs were constructed using microbial communities (at the ASV level) from the rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao annotated by 4 sequencing methods. Absolute amplicon sequencing not only affected the estimation of microbial abundance but also influenced the number of nodes and edges in the microbial network, consequently altering network parameters including connectance and degree centralization. Similar patterns were observed in bacterial networks, fungal networks, and multi-domain bacteria-fungi-environment networks, fully confirming the influence of quantification methods on the construction of microbial MENs. Furthermore, 2bRAD-M sequencing and metagenomic sequencing annotated more microbial taxa, leading to a gradual increase in the number of nodes and edges within the networks. Among these, metagenomic sequencing, capable of annotating protists, prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and algae, constructed the largest MENs in both the rhizosphere and phyllosphere. The differences were also observed in node attributes like centralization, closeness and centralization degree between networks built with different sequencing methods. Upon incorporating metabolite components and constructing metabolite (environment)-microbial MENs (including amplicon-bacteria-environment), it was found that many microbial taxa in the original networks exhibited close relationships with metabolites. Due to the greater diversity of microbial taxa in the rhizosphere, the bipartite networks there were more complex than those in the phyllosphere. Additionally, networks constructed in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere using different sequencing methods showed substantial differences in attribute composition, such as centralization closeness and centralization degree. Based on Zi - Pi, key nodes within the constructed MENs were identified to determine crucial taxa. The results indicated that as the annotation scope expanded, the number of keystone taxa gradually increased in both rhizosphere and phyllosphere MENs. However, no keystone taxa were identified in the phyllosphere under absolute quantification. Due to the greater microbial diversity inhabiting the rhizosphere, more keystone taxa were detected there. Regarding microbial-environment MENs, the number of identified keystone taxa decreased. In the phyllosphere, keystone taxa were only detected through metagenomic sequencing—the annotation method covering the broadest taxonomic range. Acidobacteriota emerged as keystone taxa common to the rhizosphere, while Ascomycota and Basidiomycota were consistently annotated across sequencing methods in the phyllosphere. With the introduction of metabolomic data (environmental factors), numerous taxa previously unrecognized as key nodes were newly identified as crucial. Meanwhile, some originally identified keystone taxa maintained their importance within the microbial-metabolite MENs. It is worth noting that all keystone taxa identified here are based on network topological properties (Zi-Pi) and should be interpreted as candidate keystone taxa. The R² values of the phyllosphere and rhizosphere NCM indicated that both amplicon sequencing and absolute amplicon sequencing data revealed microbial communities (bacteria/fungi) to be predominantly structured by deterministic processes. However, as the sequencing-annotated communities domain increase, in 2bRAD-M (bacteria, fungi, archaea) and metagenomic sequencing (bacteria, fungi, archaea, algae, etc.)—the rapid increase in microbial taxa revealed a progressive rise in stochastic influences on community assembly. Additionally, NCM analysis indicated that enhanced sequencing depth and expanded species diversity annotations were associated with varying degrees of improvement in microbial community evenness across both rhizosphere and phyllosphere habitats.

Discussion

Different sequencing methods may affect the understanding of microbial communities in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao, although significant differences between rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbial communities across all sequencing methods

Exploring the interrelationships between different species within microbial communities is crucial for understanding their community structure and function. Although significant progress has been made in understanding the mechanisms of microbial community assembly and their ecological roles, many unresolved questions remain. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionized our understanding of microbial ecology, enabling deeper insights into how microbes influence plant ecology and evolution (Pajares-Murgo et al., 2024; Singer et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021). In this study, we utilized 4 sequencing methods to profile the rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbiomes of C. migao. Meanwhile, we investigated their influences on MENs construction, community assembly, and the recruitment processes involving microbes and VOCs. The results indicated that 2bRAD-M, which employs unique molecular tags, effectively avoids high host contamination and can annotate fungi, bacteria, and archaea. Metagenomic sequencing can annotate multi-domain microbial taxa, including bacteria, fungi, archaea, viruses. Compared to amplicon sequencing, metagenomic sequencing annotated 5 to 10 times more microbial taxa in both the C. migao rhizosphere and phyllosphere (Sun et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2022). While metagenomic sequencing allows for relatively accurate species-level annotation, it suffers from host gene contamination issues. A major challenge with second-generation amplicon quantification is its fundamental inability to accurately annotate species at the genus and species levels. Although 2bRAD-M enables species-level annotation, it results in a large proportion of unclassified taxa, posing significant challenges for in-depth understanding of species interactions and ecological functions. The recent advent of third-generation full-length sequencing technologies has substantially improved the resolution of species identification in amplicon sequencing (Ghezzi et al., 2024; Tedersoo et al., 2021).

The four sequencing methods revealed differences in the annotated microbial taxa between the rhizosphere and phyllosphere at both the phylum and genus/species levels. These difference further manifests in the community structure and diversity of C. migao rhizosphere and phyllosphere, the rhizosphere microbial community exhibits higher diversity and a more complex MENs structure, and roots niche a greater number of annotated taxa (Chen et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024). This is likely because the phyllosphere is an open system exposed to the air, resulting in a more variable microbial community with fewer inherently colonizable taxa. In contrast, the root system represents a relatively closed environment compared to the phyllosphere. The abundant microbial taxa present in the soil reservoir provide a broader pool for community assembly and recruitment, thereby fostering more complex and dynamic communities in the rhizosphere (Bai et al., 2015). Simultaneously, significant differences exist in the composition and content of VOCs metabolites between the roots and leaves of C. migao. Both the rhizosphere and phyllosphere release up to 40% of photosynthetically fixed carbon in the form of VOCs, thereby influencing their chemical ecology. It is possible that these differential chemical profiles are associated with the recruitment and assembly of microbial communities in the rhizosphere versus the phyllosphere, though further experimental validation is needed to establish causal relationships (Bisht et al., 2025; Kulkarni et al., 2024).

Differences and causes of microbial MENs in C. migao rhizosphere under different sequencing methods

With the expansion of sequencing-annotated microbial databases, MENs constructed via 2bRAD-M and metagenomic sequencing exhibit significantly higher complexity than those built using relative or absolute abundance data from amplicon sequencing. This is attributed to the comprehensive annotation “resolution ratio” of 2bRAD-M and metagenomics, which enables the identification of microbial taxa including fungi, bacteria, and archaea, thereby more holistically reflecting interspecies interactions within communities. In contrast, amplicon sequencing relies on the amplification of specific gene fragments and can only annotate single-domain microbes (Aminu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Merging multi-domain species abundance data from amplicon sequencing to construct MENs remains challenging, potentially leading to biased interpretations of species relationships in rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities. Our MENs based on amplicon ITS (for fungi) and 16S rDNA (for bacteria) confirmed this limitation (Logares et al., 2014). Nevertheless, multi-domain MENs assembled from amplicon sequencing data revealed keystone taxa undetectable in single-domain (bacterial or fungal) MEN, further underscoring the necessity of multi-domain integration.

The rhizosphere and phyllosphere host diverse microbial communities (including fungi, bacteria, archaea, and algae) that engage in mutualistic, predatory, and parasitic relationships. Constructing MENs that treat these taxa as an integrated biome is therefore essential, as single-domain networks fail to accurately capture true interspecies interactions(Singh et al., 2004). Additionally, absolute quantification of microbial species (bacteria or fungi) influences network parameters and diversity differently than relative quantification, thereby altering the identification of keystone taxa in MENs (Props et al., 2017; Tkacz et al., 2018). This pattern was consistently observed in bacterial, fungal, and multi-domain MENs of C. migao rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities. Interdependency in relative quantification leads to inaccurate heritability estimates, while data sparsity and missing values complicate statistical analyses - particularly for high-abundance dominant taxa. Additionally, microbial co-abundance patterns introduce biases in heritability assessments. Our results further demonstrate that species abundance variations profoundly impact the interpretation of interspecies interactions during MENs construction, consequently affecting network parameters and keystone node identification.(Yang et al., 2018). On another front, challenges persist in measuring MEN, partly due to community complexity. For instance, a community of 5000 species entails over 10 million pairwise relationships. Metagenomic annotations of C. migao rhizospheres and phyllospheres revealed >10,000 microbial species, posing imMENe challenges for deciphering pairwise or multi-species relationships. Compounding this, diverse interaction types (e.g., linear, exponential, periodic) among microbes remain difficult to comprehensively detect (Lopatkin and Collins, 2020).

The discrepancies in results arising from inherent technical differences among sequencing approaches also warrant careful consideration. Amplicon sequencing targets specific marker genes, offering cost-effective deep coverage; however, its taxonomic resolution is inherently limited, and primer biases remain unavoidable (Sze and Schloss, 2019). 2bRAD-M, although enabling species-level resolution through reduced-representation genome sequencing, relies on a pre-constructed reference database and frequently generates a substantial proportion of unassigned reads (Sun et al., 2022b). Metagenomic sequencing provides the broadest taxonomic coverage, yet it is susceptible to host-derived DNA contamination; this issue is further exacerbated when reference genomes are unavailable for the removal of host-contaminated datasets (Quince et al., 2017). Collectively, these methodological limitations contribute to a spurious increase in the numbers of nodes and edges observed in the 2bRAD-M and metagenomic networks, thereby distorting the inferred co-occurrence patterns and the centrality-based identification of putative keystone taxa (Deng et al., 2012). Consequently, any network-generated hypothesis should be validated by independent experimental approaches, and conclusions regarding keystone taxa should be drawn with greater caution until perturbation-based (e.g., removal) assays are conducted (Jiang et al., 2025). For the amplicon-sequencing component, we employed 30 cycles for conventional relative-quantification amplicon sequencing and 27 cycles for absolute-quantification amplicon sequencing. This distinction was deliberate: the lower cycle number in the absolute-quantification protocol is essential for preserving a linear relationship between endogenous templates and synthetic spike-in standards, as excessive cycling would saturate low-abundance templates and thus undermine the proportionality necessary for accurate copy-number determination (Ahn et al., 2012). However, this trade-off may under-amplify rare taxa in the absolute-quantification libraries, potentially compromising the detection of low-abundance ASVs that could serve as connectors or module hubs within networks (Gonzalez et al., 2012). Although the inherent differences among sequencing methods may confound cross-method comparisons, our primary objective was not to directly compare absolute abundances derived from different approaches, but rather to evaluate whether the quantification strategy (relative versus absolute) systematically influences network topology and keystone identification—a comparison that remains valid despite these technical distinctions (Lv et al., 2019). Nonetheless, the impact of amplification parameters on the detection of rare taxa and the robustness of co-occurrence inferences warrant cautious interpretation and should be systematically assessed in future method-oriented studies.

Influence of root and leaf VOCs metabolite on microbial recruitment and network interactions under different sequencing methods

The species composition and metabolite function of keystone taxa (keystone metabolites) identified in the single-domain MENs, multi-domain MENs, and microbe-environment MENs generated from rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao using different sequencing methods revealed that only a subset of keystone taxa were shared. Notably, some taxa were exclusively identified within the microbe-metabolite MENs. This phenomenon arises from factors including abundance quantification methods (amplicon sequencing vs. absolute amplicon sequencing) and the scope of species interactions. These factors can alter the linear relationships between species within the community, compounded by the inability of single-domain MENs to fully capture species interactions (Matchado et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2025). A major challenge remains the potential bias in sample representativeness, while sample homogeneity was carefully considered during selection for the different sequencing methods. In this study, soil microbial diversity was measured using high-throughput sequencing although the amount of soil used for DNA extraction has minimal impact on microbial abundance determination, it significantly alters the resolution of microbial species richness, community composition, and co-occurrence patterns. Conversely, substantially impacts the construction of microbial MENs and the identification of keystone taxa within communities, a finding clearly demonstrated in our study (Li et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022).

Furthermore, keystone metabolites identified from microbe-environment (metabolite) MENs sunder different sequencing methods overlapped considerably with microbes that significantly contribute to microbial recruitment, as predicted by random forest models. Specific VOCs, such as eucalyptol and 4,7-dimethylundecane, were identified as metabolites potentially associated with microbial community assembly and diversity, although these associations require further experimental confirmation (Naranjo-Ortiz and Gabaldón, 2019). Further analysis revealed that single-domain MEN, multi-domain MEN, and microbe-environment (metabolite) MENs generated for the rhizosphere and phyllosphere of C. migao by different sequencing methods identified few MENs keystone taxa at the ASV level (Ng et al., 2025). However, the identified keystone taxa, predominantly belonging to the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota phyla and fulfilling multiple ecological roles (including decomposers, symbionts, and pathogens), exhibited high functional similarity. Significant overlap was observed between taxa predicted by random forest models and the identified keystone taxa, further confirming the importance of these keystone taxa in maintaining community stability and species diversity (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Critically, it is important to note that while keystone taxa identified from single-domain MEN, multi-domain MEN, and microbe-environment (metabolite) MENs generated under varying sequencing resolution, boundary, and quantification methods showed only partial overlap taxonomically, they largely represented key functional guilds with high functional similarity (Banerjee et al., 2018). The neutral theory model demonstrates that stochastic processes intensify with greater annotation depth, indirectly revealing a key limitation of relying exclusively on mathematical linearity and nodal connectivity for keystone taxa identification. Further investigation into these taxa’s ecological functions remains crucial to identify the authentic drivers of community stability and diversity maintenance (Rawstern et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

Conclusion

In this study, we employed 4 sequencing methods for annotating rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbial communities of C. migao and conducted multivariate analysis after measuring root and leaf VOC metabolites. Our approach revealed that different sequencing methods impact the understanding of MENs construction and species interactions within these microbial communities. Key findings suggested that distinct plant organs provided unique ecological niches, potentially contributing to highly divergent microbial community compositions between the phyllosphere and rhizosphere, which may have influenced network structures. Concurrently, annotation scope and quantification methods significantly influenced the construction of microbial MENs in both niches, which was associated with the identification of keystone taxa. Moreover, volatile metabolites in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere were potentially associated with microbial community assembly and recruitment, although further validation is needed. These findings underscored the significance of sequencing methodologies (annotation scope, quantification methods) for delineating rhizosphere-phyllosphere microbial MEN, defining community boundaries, and identifying keystone taxa. These insighted highlight the need for more stringent high-throughput sequencing approaches to study interspecies relationships within microbial communities, as well as the necessity of further in-depth verification of the ecological functions of keystone taxa to ensure reliability.
