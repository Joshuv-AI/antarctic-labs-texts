Association between low‐carbohydrate diet scores and macrovascular events or all‐cause mortality in adults with type 1 diabetes in Japan
Authors: Junko Sato, Tomoya Mita, Takuya Negami, Shohei Fujikawa, Haruki Nagashima, Eri Takagi, Shuhei Aoyama, Emi Otani, Yuya Nishida, Fuki Ikeda, Katsumi Aso, Hirotaka Watada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionThe effects of lifestyle factors on macrovascular events or all‐cause mortality remain unclear in type 1 diabetes. We examined the associations of diet, physical activity, and sleep with these outcomes among adults with type 1 diabetes living in Japan.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter prospective cohort included 352 adults with type 1 diabetes from the Juntendo–Aso Type 1 Diabetes cohort. Lifestyle factors were assessed by questionnaire. Dietary intake was evaluated using a brief‐type self‐administered diet history questionnaire, from which Total, Plant, and Animal low‐carbohydrate diet scores were calculated. The primary composite outcome was the first occurrence of ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, or all‐cause mortality. Associations were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsMean age was 51.4 ± 15.1 years, mean HbA1c was 7.9 ± 1.1%, and carbohydrate accounted for 49.3% of energy intake. During follow‐up, 35 participants experienced the primary composite outcome. In multivariable Cox models, the Total LCDS (HR, 0.90; 95% CI: 0.82–0.98; P = 0.012), Plant LCDS (HR, 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.94; P = 0.003), and Animal LCDS (HR, 0.93; 95% CI: 0.87–0.996; P = 0.037) were inversely associated with the outcome.ConclusionsAmong adults with type 1 diabetes living in Japan, low‐carbohydrate dietary patterns, particularly the Plant low‐carbohydrate diet score, were inversely associated with macrovascular events or all‐cause mortality.

INTRODUCTION

Macrovascular events are a leading cause of mortality in type 1 diabetes, and their prevention remains a major challenge 1 . Glycemic management has improved with continuous glucose monitoring, insulin pump therapy, and automated insulin delivery systems. Nevertheless, macrovascular risk is not fully explained by hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) 2 , 3 , prompting interest in modifiable factors beyond glycemic control, including diet. Because patients with T1D require lifelong insulin replacement, dietary intake directly affects glycemic variability, insulin requirements, and hypoglycemia risk. Carbohydrate counting permits insulin adjustment to carbohydrate intake and improves postprandial control, glycemic variability, and quality of life. However, guidance centered on carbohydrate counting may not emphasize the balance of carbohydrate, fat, and protein, partly because evidence on macronutrient balance and long‐term prognosis in T1D remains limited 4 , 5 .

We previously reported that higher low‐carbohydrate diet scores (LCDSs), calculated from a brief‐type self‐administered diet history questionnaire (BDHQ), were associated with fewer macrovascular events or deaths in type 2 diabetes 6 . We therefore examined lifestyle factors, particularly dietary factors, associated with the primary composite outcome among adults with type 1 diabetes patients living in Japan.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

This multicenter, prospective, observational study used data from the Juntendo–Aso Type 1 Diabetes cohort study 7 , 8 . As previously reported, participants were recruited from outpatient clinics at two institutions. Details of the study design and inclusion and exclusion criteria have been described previously 7 .

A total of 352 outpatients were enrolled between December 2019 and September 2021. Participants were followed for 260 ± 8 weeks to assess the occurrence of study outcomes.

During the study period, all participants received standard diabetes care from their attending physicians, with no restrictions on medication use. At each visit, the study investigators assessed and adjudicated clinical outcomes.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of Juntendo University Hospital and Aso Clinic (Juntendo University Ethics Committee: H19‐0169 and H19‐0231; Aso Clinic Ethics Committee: A2020001 and A2019002). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Follow‐up was prespecified as 260 ± 8 weeks. Participants with the primary composite outcome were followed until its first occurrence. Those remaining event‐free through the prespecified period were censored on the last date within that period when vital and event status were confirmed. Participants lost because of hospital transfer, discontinued visits, or other reasons were censored on the last date they were confirmed alive and free of the outcome.

Outcomes were adjudicated independently of dietary data, which were merged with the outcome dataset only after adjudication; thus, the adjudicator was blinded to dietary information. Outcomes were ascertained from electronic medical records, referral letters, other relevant records, and questionnaires completed by attending physicians. Disagreements were resolved by reviewing all available information and, if necessary, contacting the attending physician. The principal investigator made the final adjudication.

Questionnaire survey

Dietary intake during the month preceding study enrollment was assessed using a validated BDHQ. The BDHQ evaluates the intake frequency of 58 food and beverage items and estimates daily total energy and nutrient intakes using a dedicated dietary assessment algorithm 9 , 10 . The study had no prespecified exclusion criterion based on total energy intake.

Three LCDSs were calculated to assess macronutrient composition and source 11 . Protein and fat intakes were divided into 11 ascending categories and carbohydrate intake into 11 descending categories. Carbohydrate categories received 10 to 0 points from lowest to highest intake; protein and fat categories received 0 to 10 points. The Total LCDS summed the carbohydrate, total protein, and total fat scores. Plant and Animal LCDSs substituted plant‐ or animal‐derived protein and fat, respectively. Each score ranged from 0 to 30.

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index 12 . Morningness–eveningness preference was evaluated using the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire 13 . Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory‐II, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms 14 . Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire 15 .

Baseline dietary intake, sleep quality, chronotype, depressive symptoms, and physical activity were evaluated.

Data collection

Blood and urine samples were collected. HbA1c, lipid profiles, and indices of renal and hepatic function were measured using standard laboratory methods. Urinary albumin excretion was measured in spot urine samples using a latex agglutination assay. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using a previously reported equation 16 .

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges), and categorical variables as numbers (percentages). Baseline characteristics were compared according to the presence or absence of the primary composite outcome. Student's t‐test or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for continuous variables, and the χ2‐test or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variables.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations between lifestyle factors and the primary composite outcome.

The primary composite outcome was prespecified as a clinical outcome and prospectively ascertained. Evaluation of the associations between dietary factors and clinical outcomes, including analyses using the Total, Plant, and Animal LCDSs, was prespecified as secondary analyses before the initiation of the statistical analyses. However, none of the LCDSs were designated as the primary exposure in this study.

Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2, the primary model, was adjusted for age, sex, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, history of macrovascular disease, systolic blood pressure, and total energy intake. Covariates were selected primarily for clinical relevance as potential confounders. Baseline comparisons (Table 1) provided supportive information but were not the sole basis for selection.

Sequential analyses added diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, history of macrovascular disease, systolic blood pressure, and total energy intake to the age‐ and sex‐adjusted model and estimated each LCDS association after every addition.

Exploratory analyses examined the robustness of the Plant LCDS association and its relationship with total energy intake. HRs were calculated per 1‐point, 1‐standard deviation (SD), and 5‐point increases without prior transformation. The Plant LCDS was also categorized into tertiles, with the lowest as reference. Trends across tertiles were evaluated by linear regression for continuous variables and logistic regression for binary variables. Its correlation with total energy intake was assessed using Spearman's coefficient, and multicollinearity after simultaneous adjustment was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs).

To examine the stability of the Plant LCDS estimate, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was performed to calculate the HR and bias‐corrected 95% CI.

In a further exploratory analysis, the associations of the proportions of energy derived from protein and fat with the primary composite outcome were evaluated using the same covariates as in the primary analysis, including total energy intake.

Plant LCDS sensitivity analyses used parsimonious and more extensively adjusted models. Further analyses excluded participants with baseline macrovascular disease or, in a 1‐year landmark analysis, an outcome within the first year.

To describe the LCDS scoring criteria, the actual intake ranges corresponding to scores of 0–10 were reported for each nutrient. Associations among the three LCDSs and between each LCDS and total energy intake were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition version 18.2.1.

RESULTS

Among 352 participants, mean age was 51.4 ± 15.1 years, 146 (41.5%) were male, mean body mass index was 22.9 ± 3.3 kg/m2, diabetes duration was 14.8 ± 10.0 years, and HbA1c was 7.9 ± 1.1%. Mean energy intake was 1,705 kcal/day; carbohydrate, protein, and fat provided 49.3%, 16.0%, and 29.3%, respectively.

Median follow‐up was 1,799 days (IQR, 1,748–1,834), totaling 1,487.7 person‐years. Sixty‐four participants were lost to follow‐up: 57 transferred and 7 discontinued regular visits. The risk sets at 1, 3, and 5 years comprised 324, 290, and 259 participants.

During follow‐up, 35 participants (9.9%) experienced the primary composite outcome: 8 had ischemic heart disease (1 later developed peripheral artery disease), 7 had cerebrovascular events, 7 had peripheral artery disease, and 13 died. Deaths were due to cancer in 2 (gastric and pancreatic), sepsis in 2, aspiration pneumonia in 3, and unknown causes in 6.

Participants with the primary composite outcome were older, had higher systolic blood pressure and prevalences of diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and history of macrovascular disease, and had lower eGFR (Table 1). Sex, HbA1c, and total energy intake did not differ significantly.

Distribution of LCDSs and dietary variables

Median energy intake was 1,650.9 kcal/day (IQR, 1,315.8–2,026.1; range, 431.2–4,022.6). Table S1 presents intake ranges (g/day) corresponding to scores of 0–10 for carbohydrate and total, plant, and animal protein and fat. Lower carbohydrate intake received higher scores; 48.08–117.67 g/day received 10 points.

Median (IQR; range) scores were 15 (11–19; 4–30) for the Total LCDS, 15 (12–18; 6–25) for the Plant LCDS, and 15 (11–20; 1–30) for the Animal LCDS. Required dietary data were available for all 352 participants; no imputation was performed.

The Total LCDS correlated with the Plant (ρ = 0.710) and Animal (ρ = 0.893) LCDSs, and Plant correlated with Animal (ρ = 0.385; all P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2). Correlations with total energy intake were ρ = 0.516, 0.543, and 0.303 for the Total, Plant, and Animal LCDSs, respectively (all P < 0.001).

Associations between LCDSs and the primary composite outcome

Associations of the Total, Plant, and Animal LCDSs with the primary composite outcome were examined using Cox proportional hazards models. In the univariable and age‐ and sex‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, none of the three LCDSs was significantly associated with the primary composite outcome (Tables S3 and S4).

The primary analysis adjusted for age, sex, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, history of macrovascular disease, systolic blood pressure, and total energy intake. HRs per 1‐point increase were 0.90 (95% CI: 0.82–0.98; P = 0.012) for Total, 0.85 (0.76–0.94; P = 0.003) for Plant, and 0.93 (0.87–0.996; P = 0.037) for Animal LCDS; all were inversely associated with the outcome (Table 2).

Sequential adjustment analyses

Sequential adjustment analyses examined changes in the LCDS estimates (Table S5). None was significant in Models 1–5, which sequentially added retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, history of macrovascular disease, and systolic blood pressure to age and sex. After total energy intake was added in Model 6, all three HRs decreased and became significantly inverse.

For the Plant LCDS in particular, the estimate changed relatively markedly after total energy intake was added, and the Plant LCDS had the lowest HR among the three LCDSs in Model 6. We therefore conducted additional exploratory analyses of the Plant LCDS to examine the stability of its association and its relationship with total energy intake.

Additional exploratory analyses of the plant LCDS

The HR for the primary composite outcome per 1‐SD increase (3.97 points) in the Plant LCDS was 0.52 (95% CI: 0.33–0.79; P = 0.003). The HR per 5‐point increase was 0.44 (95% CI: 0.25–0.74; P = 0.003).

The Plant LCDS was also categorized into tertiles: T1 (≤13 points; n = 132), T2 (14–17 points; n = 115), and T3 (≥18 points; n = 105) (Table S6).

Across increasing Plant LCDS tertiles, total energy and intakes of carbohydrate (174.3 to 234.3 g/day), protein (51.9–82.3 g/day), fat (40.1–73.2 g/day), plant protein, plant fat, and salt increased (P < 0.05). Carbohydrate energy proportion decreased, whereas protein and fat proportions increased (P < 0.05). Higher tertiles also showed shorter diabetes duration, higher HDL cholesterol, and lower Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire scores.

The Plant LCDS correlated moderately with total energy intake (Spearman's ρ = 0.543). In the fully adjusted model, VIFs were 1.41 for the Plant LCDS and 1.50 for energy intake and ranged from 1.12 to 1.50 for all variables. Thus, simultaneous inclusion did not produce substantial multicollinearity.

Bootstrap analysis with 1,000 iterations was also performed to assess the stability of the results. The HR for the Plant LCDS was 0.84 (bias‐corrected 95% CI: 0.76–0.94), similar to the estimate from the primary analysis (HR, 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.94) (Figure S1).

Exploratory analyses of nutrient composition

In exploratory analyses using the primary model, protein energy proportion (HR, 0.87; 95% CI: 0.78–0.98; P = 0.021) and fat energy proportion (HR, 0.94; 95% CI: 0.89–0.99; P = 0.020) were inversely associated with the primary composite outcome.

Sensitivity analyses

The inverse Plant LCDS association persisted in parsimonious and extended models (Table S7). Adjustment for age, sex, site, and energy intake (352 participants; 35 events) gave an HR of 0.87 per point (95% CI: 0.78–0.96; P = 0.009). Additional adjustment for diabetes duration, smoking, physical activity, diabetic nephropathy, and history of macrovascular disease (342 participants; 33 events) gave an HR of 0.83 (0.73–0.93; P = 0.002) (Table S7).

After excluding baseline macrovascular disease (307 participants; 17 events), the Plant LCDS HR was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.75–0.99; P = 0.038). A 1‐year landmark analysis excluding early events (324 participants; 23 events) gave an HR of 0.87 (0.77–0.996; P = 0.044) (Figure S1).

DISCUSSION

In adults with type 1 diabetes living in Japan, the LCDSs were inversely associated with the primary composite outcome. Low‐carbohydrate diets reduce carbohydrate energy while increasing protein and fat and may improve weight, glycemic control, triglycerides, and insulin requirements 17 , 18 . Their long‐term safety and associations with macrovascular events or mortality remain uncertain and may depend on the nutrients replacing carbohydrate.

The LCDS quantifies low‐carbohydrate patterns from carbohydrate, protein, and fat intake 11 ; Plant and Animal LCDSs distinguish protein and fat sources. Among US adults with incident type 2 diabetes, patterns emphasizing plant protein and fat were associated with lower all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality 11 . In Japanese type 2 diabetes, Total and Animal LCDSs were inversely associated with the primary composite outcome 6 . Their relevance to type 1 diabetes remains uncertain because excessive carbohydrate restriction may affect hypoglycemia and ketosis. In this study, all three LCDSs were inversely associated with the outcome. Compared with our type 2 diabetes cohort 6 , the type 1 diabetes cohort had similar energy intake, lower carbohydrate proportion, and higher fat proportion. Carbohydrate provides approximately 55–60% of energy in the general Japanese population 19 , vs 49.3% here. Thus, this cohort already had a relatively low‐carbohydrate pattern, possibly reflecting type 1 diabetes dietary management, although comparisons with type 2 diabetes require caution.

Importantly, the LCDSs evaluated in this study did not reflect extreme carbohydrate restriction but rather differences in the quality and sources of protein and fat among adults with type 1 diabetes who already consumed a relatively low‐carbohydrate diet. In addition, the Total, Plant, and Animal LCDSs were all significantly inversely associated with the primary composite outcome only after further adjustment for total energy intake. This suggests that, when examining the associations between low‐carbohydrate dietary patterns and long‐term outcomes, total energy intake may need to be considered in addition to the nutrient composition captured by the LCDSs. Thus, our findings suggest that nutrient composition, in the context of total energy intake, may influence the association with the primary composite outcome.

The Plant and Animal LCDSs reflect macronutrient patterns, not individual foods or food groups. They should not be interpreted as comprehensive measures of a healthy plant‐based diet or an animal‐centered diet. Nevertheless, the inverse Plant LCDS association may partly reflect fiber, unsaturated fatty acids, polyphenols, and antioxidants in plant foods, which may reduce inflammation and oxidative stress and improve endothelial function. Plant‐based patterns may also affect gut microbiota; increased short‐chain fatty acid‐producing bacteria and reduced chronic inflammation may influence atherosclerosis 20 , 21 . In contrast, the Animal LCDS was also significantly inversely associated with the primary composite outcome in the fully adjusted model. Animal‐derived protein and fat are obtained from a variety of food sources, including fish and seafood, dairy products, and meat, which may differ in their nutritional composition and health effects. Therefore, the inverse association observed for the Animal LCDS may have been influenced by differences in the food sources providing animal‐derived protein and fat.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this observational study cannot establish causality. Second, questionnaire‐based dietary assessment is susceptible to measurement error and recall bias. Third, residual confounding remains possible. Fourth, the small number of events limited power and precision, particularly in sensitivity analyses, although their estimates were generally consistent with the primary analysis. Fifth, because the composite included macrovascular events and all‐cause mortality, we cannot determine whether associations were driven by nonfatal events, death, or both. Separate fully adjusted analyses and a competing‐risk analysis were not performed because the limited events could produce overfitting and unstable estimates. Sixth, despite consistent analyses excluding baseline macrovascular disease and early events, reverse causation remains possible. Seventh, evaluating three LCDSs raises multiplicity concerns. No prospective multiplicity plan covered all exploratory analyses; however, all three LCDS associations in the primary model remained after false discovery rate correction (Table S8). Generalizability is also uncertain because Japanese adults with type 1 diabetes may differ from Western populations in body mass index and macronutrient intake. In conclusion, the LCDSs, particularly the Plant LCDS, were inversely associated with macrovascular events or all‐cause mortality. The quality and sources of protein and fat replacing carbohydrate may be relevant to this association. Larger prospective and interventional studies are needed.
