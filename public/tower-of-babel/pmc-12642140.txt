Successful multidisciplinary treatment of cardiac tamponade from type A aortic dissection rupture in a pregnant woman with pre-eclampsia: a case report
Authors: Chunbo Shi, Jinliang Chen, Xianhu Fu, Aner Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic dissection is a rare but potentially fatal condition. In pregnant women, although aortic dissection incidence is low, it presents with sudden onset, rapid progression, frequent diagnostic errors, substantial surgical risks, and high mortality for both mother and fetus.Case summaryA 38-year-old Chinese woman, gravida 5 para 2, with chronic hypertension and pre-eclampsia, experienced sudden back pain and dyspnea at 37 weeks of gestation. Within 90 min, she was diagnosed with aortic dissection and cardiac tamponade. A multidisciplinary team conducted a cesarean section, ligated the ascending branches of the bilateral uterine arteries, and performed intrauterine balloon tamponade. Concurrently, they executed coronary artery repair, along with the ascending aorta and partial aortic arch replacement. Both maternal and fetal outcomes were favorable, and the patient’s fertility was preserved.ConclusionType A aortic dissection during pregnancy is a critical condition. Early diagnosis and tailored plans through multidisciplinary cooperation can achieve good maternal and neonatal outcomes. Uterine artery ligation and Bakri balloon compression during cesarean sections effectively preserve fertility.Keywords: Aortic dissection, Pre-eclampsia, Pregnancy, Case report

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) occurs when the inner layer of the aortic wall tears, often due to congenital defects, pregnancy, or hypertension. This tear allows blood to infiltrate the vessel wall, forming true and false lumens within the aorta. AD during pregnancy is rare, with an incidence of 14.5 per million. In 1965, DeBakey introduced a classification system for AD on the basis of the location of the primary intimal tear and the extent of dissection. Type I involves a tear in the ascending aorta or aortic arch, affecting the ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending thoracic aorta, and abdominal aorta. Type II features a tear in the ascending aorta, with dissection confined to the ascending aorta. Type III exhibits a tear distal to the left subclavian artery, affecting the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. In 1970, Daily proposed the Stanford classification, focusing on the extent of dissection. AD involving the ascending aorta is classified as Stanford type A, corresponding to DeBakey types I and II. AD affecting only the descending thoracic aorta and its distal parts is classified as Stanford type B, aligning with DeBakey type III. Type A aortic dissection (TAAD) represents 50–89% of cases and has a pre-hospital mortality rate of 53% [1]. Type B aortic dissection (TBAD) accounts for 11–21% of cases and has a slightly lower mortality rate. The fetal loss rate is 50%, posing significant risks to both mother and fetus [2]. The mortality risk escalates by 1–3% per hour, requiring prompt treatment upon diagnosis.

Risk factors for pregnancy complicated by AD include advanced maternal age, hypertension, congenital heart disease, and connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome. Hypertension stands out as a critical and modifiable risk factor for AD [3]. Recent findings suggest that, beyond gestational hypertension, the number of pregnancies and deliveries may also increase the risk of aortic dissection [4]. This paper presents a case of a gravida 5 para 2 (G5P2) pregnant woman with chronic hypertension and pre-eclampsia who experienced a sudden aortic dissection rupture with cardiac tamponade at 37 weeks gestation. Following emergency multidisciplinary intervention, both mother and baby had favorable outcomes.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old Chinese woman, gravida 5 para 2, with a pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) of 26 kg/m2 presented to the emergency department with sudden back pain and dyspnea lasting 1 hour. She had a history of two cesarean sections and hypertension identified at 15 weeks of gestation. The patient was prescribed aspirin to mitigate the risk of pre-eclampsia. Labetalol hydrochloride was administered to manage her blood pressure, maintaining it at approximately 140/90 mmHg. At 24 weeks of gestation, she was diagnosed with gestational diabetes and commenced insulin therapy. At 36 weeks of gestation, the patient discontinued labetalol hydrochloride without medical authorization, presuming her blood pressure had stabilized. An hour prior to admission, she experienced acute back pain, dyspnea, limb rigidity, urinary incontinence, and a transient loss of consciousness lasting 2 minutes. The patient’s vital signs upon admission were as follows: temperature, 36.8 °C; blood pressure, 78/41 mmHg; and heart rate, 110 beats per minute (bpm). The apical impulse was diminished and cardiac border enlarged, and a systolic blowing murmur was detected in the precordial area. The fetal heart rate ranged from 60 to 100 bpm, with no uterine contractions. The patient’s laboratory test results are presented in Table 1. Echocardiography showed dilation of the ascending aorta with a 40 mm inner diameter, a floating intimal flap, and pericardial hemorrhage (Fig. 1A).

A consultation with a cardiothoracic surgery expert cardiologist was requested, and the patient underwent aortic computed tomography angiography (CTA), which revealed TAAD and pericardial effusion (Fig. 1B). A multidisciplinary team, including obstetricians, cardiothoracic surgeons, critical care physicians, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists, convened and decided to perform aortic repair during a cesarean section. Under general anesthesia, obstetricians performed a lower uterine segment cesarean section, delivering a live neonate weighing 2570 g. The Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 minutes were 1, 6, and 8, respectively. To prevent postpartum hemorrhage, bilateral uterine artery ligation and intrauterine balloon tamponade were utilized, resulting in an intraoperative blood loss of 500 mL. During the cesarean section, the patient’s blood pressure dropped to 60/40 mmHg. The thoracic surgeons promptly performed a thoracotomy, removing 500 mL of pericardial blood, which stabilized the blood pressure at 120/72 mmHg. After the cesarean section, thoracic surgeons continued with the operation, discovering intraoperatively a dilated and discolored aortic root with a 5 cm rupture located 1 cm above the right coronary artery ostium. Coronary artery repair, ascending aortic replacement, and partial aortic arch replacement (PAA) were performed. Postoperatively, the pregnant patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU), and the newborn was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). The newborn was discharged after 14 days, and the patient was discharged 18 days after the operation. Echocardiography indicated a smooth artificial vessel wall without abnormal echoes 17 days post-operation (Fig. 2A), and chest CT confirmed that the artificial blood vessel remained in place (Fig. 2B). Pathological examination identified dissecting thrombi formation in focal aortic regions. The patient underwent monthly follow-ups in the thoracic surgery clinic, demonstrating a favorable outcome.

Discussion

Aortic dissection arises from a tear in the aortic intima, allowing blood to infiltrate and separate the intima from the media, stratifying the vessel wall. During pregnancy, increased blood volume and heightened cardiac output from the left ventricle elevate the impact force on the aortic wall. Estrogen inhibits collagen and elastic fiber deposition, while progesterone enhances non-collagen protein deposition in the aortic wall, both contributing to the formation and rupture of dissections. Additionally, relaxin further compromises vessel wall integrity by inducing the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-3 mRN [5]. Compression of the aorta by an enlarged uterus progressively elevates aortic pressure, increasing the risk of aortic dissection. Chronic hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome, are associated with an elevated risk of aortic dissection during pregnancy. Hypertension resulting from pre-eclampsia is the leading cause of perinatal aortic dissection [6, 7]. Pregnant individuals with chronic hypertension who have irregular medication adherence, lack prior treatment, or have poorly controlled blood pressure are at an increased risk of aortic dissection [8]. Gao Weina et al. [3] analyzed the clinical characteristics of nine pregnant women with pre-eclampsia complicated by aortic dissection. Their analysis showed that seven of the women had a background of chronic hypertension, and all nine exhibited severe blood pressure levels during pregnancy.

Aortic dissection aneurysm is a life-threatening condition characterized by rapid progression and a narrow diagnostic window. The presentation is often complicated during pregnancy. Patients often experience abrupt, severe, tearing pain in the anterior chest, scapular region, back, upper abdomen, and other areas [9]. Involvement of the ascending aorta can lead to aortic valve insufficiency and dilation, causing blood regurgitation. The compression of nerve receptors due to the regurgitation triggers reflexive pain. Compression or obstruction of aortic dissection may affect aortic branches, often causing acute ischemic symptoms in the associated regions or organs. Secondary rupture of the adventitia may result in cardiac tamponade or massive hemorrhage, potentially leading to death.

The primary approach to diagnosing and treating TAAD prioritizes maternal survival while safeguarding the fetus [10]. For gestational ages under 28 weeks, aortic surgery is advised first, followed by decisions on pregnancy termination based on specific circumstances. At gestational ages of 28 weeks or more, performing aortic surgery during a cesarean section is advisable [7]. The surgical management of type A aortic dissection is contingent upon the extent of aortic involvement. The standard surgical approaches include aortic root reconstruction and aortic arch replacement. In 2010, Bavaria [11] introduced a hybrid surgical approach combining aortic arch vessel reconstruction with thoracic endovascular aortic repair for treating type A aortic dissection involving the descending aorta. This method involves percutaneously implanting a covered stent at the affected site of the descending aorta after completing the aortic arch reconstruction to alleviate organ ischemia.The risk of perinatal hemorrhage increases owing to systemic heparinization during cardiopulmonary bypass. During surgery, it is imperative to closely monitor coagulation function. Upon detecting active bleeding and coagulation dysfunction, prompt and proactive administration of component blood transfusion or coagulation factor preparations is essential. Some studies advocate for hysterectomy immediately during cesarean section to reduce the risk of postpartum hemorrhage and AD induced by a subsequent pregnancy [7, 12]. However, this approach may not be suitable for young women desiring future fertility. Multiple studies have demonstrated that hemostasis techniques, such as Bakri balloon tamponade, uterine packing with strips, B-Lynch suture, and uterine artery ligation, effectively prevent and treat postpartum hemorrhage [13].

Conclusion

AD is a serious complication that often occurs during late pregnancy and the early postpartum period. Patients with high-risk factors for AD require close monitoring of blood pressure and aortic condition throughout gestation [14]. For individuals with high risk of pre-eclampsia, the use of oral calcium supplements and low-dose aspirin has been demonstrated to reduce this risk [15]. In patients with chronic hypertension and pre-eclampsia, maintaining blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg during pregnancy is crucial to mitigate the risk of AD. Once an AD ruptures, it can lead to immediate mortality for both the mother and fetus. Consequently, prompt intervention is critical regardless of the dissection’s classification. Multidisciplinary collaboration is essential to optimize outcomes and survival rates. During caesarean section, the combination of bilateral uterine artery ligation with Bakri balloon compression can effectively achieve uterine cavity hemostasis while preserving fertility potential.
