The effect of solution-focused group counselling on rumination and life satisfaction
Authors: Erdi Kulbaş, Abdulkadir Muhammed Memduh Fakirullahoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to investigate effect of solution-focused brief group counseling—emphasizing individuals’ strengths and future-oriented goals—on reducing rumination and enhancing life satisfaction in mothers of children with special needs. A quasi-experimental design with pre-test, post-test, and 6-month follow-up measurements was employed, including experimental and control groups. Firstly, the Self-Rumination Scale was applied to 110 mothers of children with moderate to severe intellectual disabilities, aged between 30 and 45. Twenty-eight mothers scoring above 27.58 were identified, and 18 eligible participants without major psychiatric disorders who could regularly attend sessions were randomly assigned to the experimental (n = 9) or control group (n = 9). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.The intervention was implemented in six sessions, drawing on the brief and focused structure emphasized by De Shazer and Berg in solution-focused brief therapy. The sessions utilized a comprehensive approach using key techniques, including the miracle question to highlight participants’ strengths and goals, scaling questions to monitor progress, and identifying exceptions to identify functional behavioral patterns. No intervention was applied to the control group. Two-Factor Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Repeated Measures was used to examine group × measurement interaction effects for self-rumination and life satisfaction scores across time. While no immediate significant reduction in self-rumination was observed at post-test, a significant decrease emerged at the 6-month follow-up, showing a significant group × time interaction (F(2, 32) = 5.65, p < .05, η² = 0.261). For life satisfaction, the experimental group demonstrated a significant increase compared to the control group, which was sustained at follow-up (F(2, 32) = 6.924; p < .05, η² = 0.302). Both SRS and LSS scores exhibited large effect sizes according to Cohen’s conventions, and the 95% confidence intervals indicated clear and pronounced group differences. The sphericity assumption required for repeated measures ANOVA was examined using Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied for the LSS to adjust degrees of freedom where necessary. These findings indicate that solution-focused brief group counseling may have delayed but lasting benefits for reducing rumination, while its effectiveness for enhancing life satisfaction is evident at the post-test and follow-up assessments.Keywords: Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT), Group counseling, Delayed intervention effects, Mothers of children with special needs, Life satisfaction, Rumination, Cultural influences in Turkey, Caregiver mental health

Introduction

The family, the smallest unit of the social system, is the first place where children experience the process of growth and development [1]. Having a healthy child is the expectation of every parent. However, when a child is born with some special needs, parents realize that their expectation and the reality are not the same [2]. Approximately 10% of children aged 0–17 years are included individuals with at least one disability, as indicated by UNICEF data [3]. Furthermore, data from the Turkish Statistical Institute [4] reveal that 6.9% of the population aged 3 years and over in Turkey are individuals with at least one disability. While the rate of intellectual disability globally was determined to be 1.2% between 2015 and 2018 [5], in Turkey, intellectual disability was reported at a rate of 1.5% among children in the 2–14 age group, ranking first among types of disability [6]. Studies conducted in Turkey suggest that parents of children with special needs experience higher levels of psychological distress due to social stigmatization, lack of support, and economic burden [7]. This process is accompanied by a multitude of vital and emotional experiences. In this sense, the mother, who is the closest figure to the child, has to face many responsibilities and difficulties [8].

Every mother wants her children to grow up healthy and happy and to be able to live life to the fullest without any restrictions. For a mother, receiving a diagnosis of disability for her child is a difficult emotional experience [9]. During this process, parents may experience feelings of disappointment, deep sadness and shame. In addition, many parents are drawn into negative thoughts such as guilt and fear for the future when they learn about their children’s deficiencies [8]. These thoughts sometimes help plan the future; however, they can also manifest as repetitive and exhausting ruminations [10]. The person can direct all his/her attention to these feelings and thoughts. Thoughts that continuously revolve around the same topic are explained by the concept of rumination [11].

Rumination

Nolen-Hoeksema’s response styles theory conceptualizes rumination as a passive and repetitive focus on the causes and consequences of one’s depressive state. Rather than facilitating emotional processing or problem-solving, this cognitive style is regarded as a maladaptive strategy that maintains, and may even exacerbate, negative mood [12]. As a psychological concept, rumination is defined as passively thinking about causal factors after a negative experience and thinking about the event itself, the negative emotions resulting from it, and possible negative consequences [13]. Within this unified framework, rumination is understood as a multidimensional construct that can vary in both affective content and cognitive control. On the other hand [14], noted that studies on rumination have focused either on negative affect [15] or positive emotional states [16] and defined rumination as repetitive thoughts about positive and negative affect that function in terms of psychological adjustment [17]. These affective distinctions can be viewed as moderators within Nolen-Hoeksema’s response styles theory, clarifying when rumination becomes maladaptive or adaptive [18].

Meta-analytic evidence supports the role of rumination as a risk factor that sustains negative affect and psychopathology [18]. Moreover, two types of ruminative responses have been identified in the literature: intrusive rumination, characterized by involuntary thoughts about a distressing event, and deliberate rumination, which involves a conscious effort to repeatedly think about the event. Integrating these subtypes into the response styles framework provides a coherent model that connects the automatic and reflective dimensions of ruminative thought [17]. Rumination directs an individual’s attention to negative events and thus exacerbates or prolongs negative emotional reactions during interpersonal communication [19]. Rumination, as a repetitive action, is often linked to the constant repetition of negative aspects [20].

Parents of children with special needs also experience many negative thoughts due to daily life activities and the uncertainty they feel about their children’s future. Parents constantly think about their children’s future in the context of their daily activities (eating, toileting, etc.) This type of ruminative thinking significantly impacts their mental and physical health [21].

The ruminative thinking style has been linked to the concept of life satisfaction in the literature and has been identified as a negative predictor of life satisfaction [14]. Indeed, Diener’s theory of subjective well-being emphasizes two central components in individuals’ evaluations of their lives: cognitive appraisals, such as life satisfaction, and emotional experiences, defined by the balance of positive and negative affect. Within this framework, subjective well-being is understood as a state in which positive emotions and life satisfaction outweigh negative emotions [15]. Taken together, these theories predict that ruminative thinking styles may reduce subjective well-being. Rumination, which constantly directs attention to negative thoughts, is thought to positively impact both life satisfaction and subjective well-being while reducing positive emotions.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction is defined as a cognitive dimension of subjective well-being, reflecting individuals’ contentment with their lives as they compare their current living standards to their own expectations [22]. According to [23], life satisfaction is a cognitive judgment that is formed over time about the expectations and opportunities encountered in life. The conceptualization of life satisfaction within the subjective well-being framework permits the systematic assessment of its predictors, such as the negative influence of rumination and the positive influence of social support.

Having a child who requires lifelong care creates immense pressure on families, coupled with financial difficulties, physical illnesses, and various developmental challenges. This increases parents’ concerns about their children’s future and decreases their life satisfaction [24]. Individuals who experience rumination tend to focus more on the negative aspects of events because they cannot find sufficient and effective solutions to their problems, leading to a negative impact on their life satisfaction [25, 26]. At the same time, ruminative thinking style has been associated with impaired life satisfaction [14] and appears to be a negative predictor of life satisfaction [27].

In families having children with special needs, uncertainties in the processes of care, education and treatment, along with inadequate living conditions, reduce parents’ life satisfaction [28]. According to [29], life satisfaction is negatively affected because parents of children diagnosed with disabilities are exposed to stressful life transitions. However, the strength or direction of this relationship varies depending on mediating variables such as social support, perceived caregiver burden, and economic status, as well as moderating factors like cultural variables. In the context of Turkey, social support and perceived caregiver burden stand out as key mediators affecting parents’ life satisfaction [30]. Turkey’s collectivistic structure may influence coping mechanisms for stress; Hanımoğlu’s [31] study indicates that women benefit more from emotional support, while men benefit more from problem-focused strategies. In light of these cultural differences, it is crucial to use therapeutic approaches that emphasize strengths and resources.

Solution-Focused brief therapy

The key to helping individuals cope with their mental problems is counselling and psychotherapy. Psychotherapeutic interventions aimed at increasing life satisfaction and reducing depressive symptoms are also effective in reducing psychological distress, promoting disengagement from maladaptive ruminative thoughts [32], and promoting reductions in repetitive negative thought patterns [33].

Solution-focused psychological counselling, which has continued to be effective in the field of psychotherapy in recent years, is preferred because it is short-term, less costly, and includes solution-oriented interventions. Representatives of this approach include Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, Bill O’Hanlon, Michelle Weiner-Davis and Scott Miller. This approach adopts an optimistic stance toward the client and change. It places the emphasis on individuals’ achievements, strengths, existing resources, beliefs about change, exceptions and effective solutions. In this approach, the number of sessions does not exceed 10 and usually varies between 4 and 6. The basic techniques of the approach include the pre-session change technique, the formulation of the first session, the miracle question, rating questions, exceptional cases, coping questions and the crystal ball technique [34–37].

Solution-focused brief counseling can reduce mental repetition and fixation on negative thoughts by encouraging individuals to focus on solutions and future goals rather than the current problem. In this model, the client’s strengths, previous exceptional moments, and resources are revealed; This helps individuals break thought cycles and shifts their thinking from problem-focused inquiry to a solution- and goal-oriented framework [38]. This shift can increase life satisfaction by increasing an individual’s sense of perceptual control and their capacity to direct meaningful aspects of life [39]. These theoretical expectations are supported by recent meta-analyses examining the effects of solution-focused brief counseling on variables such as mental health, general psychosocial functioning, and well-being. For instance, one meta-analysis [40] found a significant effect of solution-focused brief counseling on general psychosocial problems; this effect size was larger in groups without a diagnosis of any mental disorder, yet was reported to be moderate in clinical groups.

In a meta-analysis evaluating experimental studies, the overall effect of Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) on psychosocial problems was reported to be large (g = 1.17) [41]. However, it may show limitations when addressing more complex difficulties such as chronic stress or trauma. When comparing SFBT with problem-focused therapies like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), it has been reported that negative content was significantly higher in CBT sessions, while positive content was significantly higher in SFBT sessions [42]. When assessed in terms of cultural context, the preference for SFBT among Turkish mothers can reinforce the optimism and resilience narratives valued in caregiving processes, strengthening mothers’ coping skills with the challenges they face, and thereby potentially increasing the intervention’s effectiveness [43].

When the literature is reviewed, it is seen that solution-focused brief counselling has produced effective results in samples of high school and university students.It has been found that solution-focused thinking among students creates positive changes in anxiety, stress, depression, problem-solving failure [44], behavioural problems [45] and subjective well-being [46].In addition, the effectiveness of solution-focused brief counselling has been tested on adult individuals [47], but studies on mothers having children with special needs have been found to be limited in terms of both the applied therapeutic method and self ruminations and life satisfaction variables.

In Turkey, mothers of children with special needs are influenced by cultural factors such as societal stigma, family roles, and access to healthcare services. These factors can directly affect maternal rumination and life satisfaction. In particular, the high caregiving burden and social isolation experienced by mothers may lead to psychological problems such as depression and anxiety, as well as lower life satisfaction [48].

Additionally, the process by which mothers come to accept their children’s special needs varies depending on societal norms and the level of family support. During this process, the stress and uncertainty experienced by mothers may increase rumination and negatively impact life satisfaction.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the emotional challenges faced by mothers of children with special needs within the cultural context of Turkey and to explore how these challenges influence rumination and life satisfaction.

Previous research suggests that solution-focused brief group counselling (SFBGC) techniques, such as goal setting and identifying exceptions, help individuals shift attention from repetitive negative thoughts to solution-oriented thinking [34, 49]. By focusing on exceptions to problems and small steps toward solutions, SFBGC is expected to interrupt ruminative cycles. Therefore, hypothesis 1 is:

There will be a significant decrease in the rumination levels of individuals who participate in a solution-focused brief group counselling program (experimental group) compared to those who do not participate (control group), and this decrease will continue to exist in the follow-up measurements 6 months after program completion, moderated by perceived social support levels prevalent in Turkish families.

SFBGC techniques, including the use of the miracle question and highlighting individual strengths, are designed to enhance positive cognitions and goal attainment, which are strongly associated with higher life satisfaction [50]. By facilitating recognition of personal achievements and possible solutions, SFBGC is expected to increase life satisfaction. Therefore, hypothesis 2 is:

The individuals participating in the Solution-Focused Brief Group Counseling program (experimental group) will show a significant increase in life satisfaction compared to those who do not participate (control group), and this increase will be mediated by the levels of perceived social support commonly prevalent in Turkish families and will be maintained at the 6-month follow-up.

Materials and methods

Research model

The current study was conducted using a quasi-experimental 2*3 factorial design with experimental and control groups, based on a pretest, posttest, and follow-up test, to examine the effects of solution-focused brief group counseling on rumination and life satisfaction. A 6-month follow-up test was included in the study to examine the long-term effects of the intervention and to observe changes in habitual cognitive processes such as rumination over time [51, 52].

To further control for maturation effects, both experimental and control groups were assessed simultaneously across identical intervals. No active intervention was applied to the control group during the study period, ensuring that any observed changes can be more confidently attributed to the intervention rather than the passage of time. An a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 for a two-group, three-measurement repeated-measures ANOVA (f = 0.25, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80, correlation = 0.7) indicated that a total sample size of 18 participants is sufficient to detect medium-sized interaction effects.

In this design, the independent variable of the study is the 6-session solution-focused brief group counselling program, and the dependent variable is the pre-test, post-test and follow-up test data obtained from the Self Rumination Scale (SRS) and Life Satisfaction Scale (LSS). Study group

This study was conducted among mothers of children with special needs attending high school during the 2023–2024 academic year. During the pre-screening process, Self-Rumination Scale was administered to 110 mothers, and 28 mothers who scored above 27.58 were identified. Although no standardized clinical cutoff has been established in the Turkish validation study, the mean score of the current sample (M = 27.58) was adopted as a study-specific threshold to identify participants exhibiting relatively higher levels of self-rumination. Eighteen mothers with no history of serious psychiatric disorders, who reported being able to attend sessions regularly, were between the ages of 30 and 45, and had children with moderate to severe intellectual disabilities were included in the study. Based on the established criteria, the 18 mothers were randomly assigned to the experimental (n = 9) and control (n = 9) groups. A summary is provided in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Measures

Self-Rumination scale

The Turkish validity and reliability studies of the Self-Rumination Scale, developed by [53] was conducted by [54]. Possible scores from the scale range from 12 to 60. Increasing scores indicate an increased level of rumination. In the construct validity analysis of the scale, the one-dimensional structure consisting of 10 questions was confirmed (χ2 = 53.97, sd = 31, p =.006, RMSEA = 0.051, CFI = 0.99, GFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.99, NFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.039).The factor loadings of the scale range from 0.44 to 0.78, and the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was found to be 0.86. In the reliability analyses conducted on the data of the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was found to be 0.85.

In addition to internal consistency, the Turkish adaptation reported test–retest reliability over a 2-week period (r =.81), suggesting temporal stability of scores [55]. Furthermore, studies involving caregivers and adult populations have shown that the Self-Rumination Scale is sensitive to intervention-related changes, with standardized mean differences ranging from 0.45 to 0.65 in cognitive-behavioral and psychoeducation programs [56, 57]. Beyond its temporal stability and responsiveness to change, the scale’s construct validity has also been robustly supported through associations with related psychological constructs. Convergent validity was supported through significant positive correlations with depressive symptoms (r =.52–0.61) and anxiety (r =.46–0.57), and negative correlations with psychological well-being (r = −.48) [55, 58, 59]. These findings confirm the construct validity and responsiveness of the scale in both general and caregiver-specific samples.

Life satisfaction scale

The validity and reliability studies of the “Life Satisfaction Scale” developed by [59] were by [64]. Possible scores on the scale range from 5 to 25. Increasing scores indicate increased life satisfaction. The Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.88. Goodness of fit values ​​were calculated as χ2/sd = 1.17, NFI = 0.99; NNFI = 1.00, CFI = 1.00, SRMR = 0.019. The results of the factor analysis revealed that the Life Satisfaction Scale had a single-factor structure, as in the original scale, and consisted of 5 items, as in the original scale. In the reliability analyses conducted on the data collected in the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was found to be 0.80.

Beyond internal consistency and factorial validity, previous studies demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability (r =.87) over a 3-week interval in Turkish adult samples [60]. Moreover, findings from psychosocial intervention research involving caregiver populations provide further support for the instrument’s responsiveness to change, with standardized mean differences ranging from 0.40 to 0.55 following structured support and well-being programs [65, 66].

When examining the convergent validity of the scale, evidence indicates negative correlations between life satisfaction scores and depressive symptomatology (r = −.53) and perceived stress (r = −.49), as well as positive correlations with psychological well-being (r =.61) and optimism (r =.56) [59, 60, 67].

In order to structure the sessions, experimental studies and programs in national and international literature [61–63] were examined. As a result, the sessions were created by integrating the principles and techniques of solution-focused group counselling with practices aimed at reducing self rumination and increasing life satisfaction. The intervention consisted of six structured sessions based on the principles of Solution-Focused Brief Group Counseling (SFBGC). As summarized analytically in Table 2, each session was designed to operationalize the specific therapeutic mechanisms hypothesized to influence study outcomes, reducing maladaptive relapses and increasing life satisfaction.

Early sessions emphasized goal clarification and future-oriented reframing through core SFBGC strategies such as the Miracle Question, Predicting the Future, and Rating Questions. These methods were intended to attenuate deliberate rumination by promoting solution-focused cognitive shifts and adaptive goal engagement. Intermediate sessions incorporated Strengths Identification, Offering Praise, and Exception-Finding techniques to reinforce self-efficacy, amplify positive affect, and consolidate adaptive coping pathways through experiential learning. The concluding session centered on reflective integration and the generalization of acquired cognitive-behavioral skills to daily life contexts, thereby supporting sustained behavioral and emotional regulation.

The therapist conducting the group intervention in this study holds a doctorate in Counseling and Guidance. He teaches the “Solution-Focused Brief Therapy” course at the university for both undergraduate and graduate levels. He has also completed a total of 40 h of training in solution-focused therapy and positive psychotherapy. This therapist’s experience, both academic and practical, contributed to the scientific and ethical conduct of the group sessions.

Data analysis

To determine the sample size at the beginning of the study, an a priori power analysis using G*Power for a two-group, three-measurement repeated-measures ANOVA (f = 0.25, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80, correlation = 0.7) indicated that a total sample size of 18 participants would be sufficient to detect medium-sized interaction effects [69]. According to Cohen [69], d = 0.8 represents a large effect, and such parameters are methodologically appropriate in studies where strong manipulations are anticipated. However, the limitations of the current study should be noted, as this sample size may be insufficient to reliably detect smaller effect sizes [70, 71].

Prior to hypothesis testing, assumptions for parametric analyses were evaluated. According to [72], in order to use parametric tests, the data must have a normal distribution and the variance distributions must be homogeneous. Levene’s tests confirmed homogeneity for pre-test scores on both the Life Satisfaction Scale (LSS; F = 0.102, p >.05) and the Self-Rumination Scale (SRS; F = 3.933, p >.05). Given that each group comprised fewer than 30 participants, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Results indicated that distributions did not significantly deviate from normality across pre-test, post-test, and follow-up assessments (LS: pre-test p =.740, post-test p =.177, follow-up test p =.230, SR: pre-test p =.297, post-test p =.190, follow-up test p =.663), supporting the suitability of parametric procedures.

The sphericity assumption required for repeated measures ANOVA was examined using Mauchly’s test. The results indicated that the SRS met the sphericity assumption (W(2) = 0.711, p >.05; ε = 0.711), whereas the LSS did not (W(2) = 0.564, p <.05; ε = 0.564). Accordingly, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied to adjust the degrees of freedom and ensure conservative inference. The decision to retain parametric analyses was further supported by the robustness of ANOVA to moderate normality violations and by central limit theorem considerations for repeated-measures designs, even under relatively small samples (n ≥ 15 per cell). Additionally, as a sensitivity analysis, the main effects and interactions were re-examined using non-parametric Friedman tests and 1,000-sample bootstrapped confidence intervals to confirm the stability of the observed effects. These procedures demonstrated convergent results, reinforcing the robustness of the findings.

Following the confirmation of these assumptions, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed to evaluate the effects of group (experimental vs. control) and measurement time (pre-test, post-test, follow-up) on the dependent variables. Significant interactions were further examined using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0, with an alpha level of 0.05 for statistical significance. Additionally, the same assumption-checking procedures were applied to the socio-demographic comparisons to ensure the validity of group equivalence analyses. The distributions of categorical variables were examined using chi-square tests, while the homogeneity and normality assumptions were verified for continuous covariates prior to multivariate analyses. In this context, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was performed to control for potential baseline imbalances across demographic factors such as education level.

Effect sizes (η² and d) were calculated and interpreted in accordance with Cohen’s benchmarks [69] to enhance the transparency and interpretability of the results.

Table 3 shows the distribution of socio-demographic characteristics of the parents in the experimental and control groups. In the experimental group, 88.9% of the parents were married and 11.1% were single, while in the control group, all parents were married. No significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of the number of children; in the experimental group, 55.6% of the children came from families with one child and 44.4% from families with two children. In the control group, 44.4% had one child, 33.3% had two children, and 22.2% had three or more children.

When examining the parents’ educational levels, 11.1% in the experimental group had completed primary school, 55.6% had completed middle school, and 33.3% had completed high school. In the control group, 44.4% had completed primary school, 22.2% had completed middle school, 22.2% had completed high school, and 11.1% had completed university. No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of economic status or the child’s gender.

As a result of chi-square analyses, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of marital status (χ²=1.059; p =.303), number of children (χ²=2.254; p =.324), and educational status (χ²=4.286; p =.232) (p >.05).

In addition to univariate comparisons, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to verify baseline equivalence across socio-demographic covariates and pre-test measures. Results indicated that educational status (Wilks’ Λ = 0.977, F(2,14) = 0.162, p =.852) and group membership (Wilks’ Λ = 0.902, F(2,14) = 0.757, p =.487) did not have a statistically significant effect on the baseline outcome variables. Moreover, adjusted means were examined and no imbalance exceeded 10%; therefore, no ANCOVA adjustment was necessary. These findings confirm that the experimental and control groups were comparable at baseline, minimizing potential confounding effects and reinforcing the internal validity of the study. Generally speaking, the experimental and control groups show a similar distribution in terms of socio-demographic characteristics.

The results of an independent samples t-test are also presented to determine whether there was a pre-test difference between the experimental and control groups. The results of the independent samples t-test conducted on the Self-Rumination Scale pre-test scores revealed that there was no significant difference between the experimental and control groups (SRS: t(16) = 1.165; p >.05). Similarly, no significant difference was found between the pre-test scores of the Life Satisfaction Scale of the experimental and control groups (LSS: t(16) = −0.29; p >.05). Therefore, it can be said that the experimental and control groups were similar in terms of sociodemographic information and pre-test data before the program implementation.

Discussion

In the current study, the effects of solution-focused brief group counselling on rumination and life satisfaction were examined. In the current study, there occurred no significant decrease in the self ruminations of the individuals who participated in the solution-focused brief group counselling program (experimental group) compared to those who did not participate in this program (control group). However, a significant decrease was observed in the follow-up measurements made 6 months after the completion of the program. However, the joint effect of measurement*group was found to be significant. The effectiveness of studies conducted in mixed designs or the implemented experimental program depends on the Measurement*Group joint effect. In this respect, it is seen that there occurred a significant difference in the participants’ self ruminations. In other words, the effect of the intervention on rumination was not found to be significant at post-intervention, but became significant at follow-up measurement due to the significant group-by-time interaction (F(2, 32) = 5.65, p <.05, η²=0.261).

It is important to highlight that the intervention did not yield an immediate reduction in rumination at the post-test assessment. The significant decrease emerged only at the six-month follow-up, suggesting a delayed treatment effect. This temporal pattern implies that, although participants may have acquired solution-focused skills during the sessions, the effective integration and consistent application of these strategies required time to exert a measurable influence on their habitual cognitive style. Such a trajectory aligns with theoretical perspectives emphasizing that cognitive processes, such as rumination, undergo gradual modification as individuals internalize and repeatedly employ newly acquired coping mechanisms. This delayed effect can be accounted for by the Dual-Process Theory [74]. Rumination is predominantly associated with automatic and habitual cognitive processes (System 1), whereas the solution-oriented skills acquired through Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) operate at the level of conscious and controlled processing (System 2). Consequently, the integration of these newly acquired cognitive strategies into individuals’ daily routines may require time, resulting in a gradual decrease in automatic ruminative cycles that becomes statistically observable only at the follow-up assessment.

When the relevant literature was reviewed, no studies were found examining the effect of solution-focused brief group counselling on rumination in adults. In studies conducted on different samples [63], found that solution-focused brief counselling reduces the rumination levels of high school students. Again, in the study conducted by [10] on university students, it was found that the solution-focused approach was effective on rumination and the same effect continued in the follow-up measurements. In this respect, the relevant results support the findings of the current study. However, SFBT shows limitations in addressing more complex difficulties, such as trauma, compared to problem-focused approaches like CBT [41, 42].

The cultural context may constitute an additional explanatory factor, given that family dynamics and caregiving practices vary substantially across societies. These contextual differences suggest that, although the present findings are consistent with prior evidence supporting the efficacy of solution-focused approaches, they also underscore the intervention’s relevance and adaptability within a distinct and underrepresented population. However, due to the limited number of similar studies, findings on concepts related to rumination were also examined in national and international literature. Accordingly, a positive relationship was found between the rumination levels of the parents of children with special needs and their stress reactions (hopelessness, helplessness), anxiety and depression levels [23, 75]. In this sense, when the literature on related concepts is examined, it can be said that the findings of the current study are parallel to the research findings showing that solution-focused brief counselling is effective on anxiety and depression [76, 77].

According to the theory of response styles [12], directing individuals’ attention and focus elsewhere will produce effective results in reducing rumination. Focusing on the positive or useful points will be the first key to the solution for individuals who have a problem-based life. Solution-focused brief counselling is a present and future-based intervention method with its own unique techniques and focus on the useful parts rather than the problem [78]. By directing the experimental group’s focus toward solutions and useful parts, rather than dwelling on perceived problems, the intervention made in the current study may have been successful in reducing rumination.

It is very important to be able to create concrete and realistic goals in solution-focused brief counselling. These goals should be simple, clear and solution-oriented [64]. In the first two sessions, the “Rating Questions” and “Miracle Question” techniques were used to help individuals create new goals in their lives and check whether they achieved these goals. The leader supported the members while determining the goals and checks were made through feedback at the beginning of each session. In this way, members were able to focus on the positive aspects of their lives and experience positive changes. In addition, the “My Strengths” activity conducted in the fourth session allowed group members to focus on their positive attributes, past achievements, and the aspects of themselves that others admire. This facilitated the uncovering of their internal resources and abilities. It is believed that the “Rating Questions”, “Miracle Question” and “My Strengths” techniques contributed significantly to the success of the intervention process.

Culturally, the findings suggest that SFBT aligns well with Turkish familism and collectivist values emphasizing mutual support and resilience. Although developed in Western individualistic contexts, SFBT’s optimistic and resource-focused orientation resonates with Turkish mothers’ relational coping styles and community-centered strengths.Local evidence [31] similarly highlights optimism and social support as culturally salient protective factors. However, this cultural alignment is interpretive, as collectivism or familism were not directly measured.

Another variable examined in the present study is life satisfaction. The findings revealed that the life satisfaction of individuals who participated in the solution-focused short-term group counseling program significantly increased compared to the control group, and this improvement was maintained at follow-up. This result aligns with previous studies reporting that solution-focused brief interventions enhance adaptive outcomes among parents of children with special needs, such as greater parental self-efficacy [79] and higher resilience levels [80]. Beyond the context of families with special-needs children, empirical evidence across diverse populations demonstrates that solution-focused approaches can reduce anxiety and depression while improving quality of life and life satisfaction [81–83]. Collectively, these studies indicate that such interventions yield small-to-moderate effect sizes on indicators of well-being, suggesting consistent short-term efficacy across heterogeneous samples.

The improvement in life satisfaction observed in the study can be understood through Diener’s model of subjective well-being, which defines life satisfaction as an individual’s cognitive evaluation of the overall quality of their life circumstances [14, 84]. It is plausible that increases in parental self-efficacy and perceived coping competence serve as mediators linking solution-focused counselling to improved life satisfaction. The group-based delivery format may have further amplified these effects by fostering shared understanding, mutual support, and collective efficacy—mechanisms particularly salient in collectivistic sociocultural contexts such as Turkey.

Parents of children with special needs have to cope with difficult living conditions and go through stressful times due to their child’s condition. Parents’ life satisfaction decreases with the addition of daily life problems along with negative feelings such as blaming themselves and having pessimistic feelings about the future [29]. In this sense, group counselling provides individuals with the opportunity to feel that they are not alone through the sharing technique. Another contribution of solution-focused brief counselling is that it emphasizes “Exceptional Moments” by reminding members that there is a life other than the problems in their lives. It is believed that the “Offering Praise”, “Cheerleader Effects” and “Exceptional Moments” activities, conducted from the third session until the final session to encourage members to develop positive attitudes towards themselves and their lives, also contributed to increased life satisfaction. Overall, the findings underscore the potential of solution-focused group interventions as an empirically grounded, culturally sensitive, and time-efficient approach that enhances life satisfaction and concurrently mitigates self-rumination among parents of children with special needs.

Limitations and suggestions

Despite the proven effectiveness of the program in the current study, it also has some limitations. Rumination and life satisfaction were measured only through self-report measures in this study. In this context, the lack of qualitative data can be considered a limitation. While this approach facilitated confidentiality and ease of administration, future studies could incorporate mixed-methods designs combining self-report, behavioural, and qualitative data to enhance validity. Although rumination is divided into two dimensions in the literature: intentional and intrusive, it was examined together in this study. This holistic approach provided a general picture of cognitive processing; however, future research could differentiate between these dimensions to better understand which aspects of rumination are most affected by the intervention. The intervention was not expected to affect both dimensions. The lack of an active control group to control for placebo effects and the fact that the intervention was carried out by the same therapist limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, potential therapist allegiance effects should be acknowledged as a limitation, as the same therapist also participated in the research process. Follow-up measurements were conducted 6 months after the posttest. Future research could extend these follow-up periods to examine long-term sustainability. Furthermore, the sample size of this study was limited to 18 mothers of children with moderate to severe intellectual disabilities. Larger sample sizes could be considered in the future. This study is limited to a program based on a solution-focused approach to reducing rumination and increasing life satisfaction in mothers with special needs. This allows for the development of studies based on different counselling approaches. The effectiveness of these programs and the current program can then be comparatively examined.
