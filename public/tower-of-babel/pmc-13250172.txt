Enhancing research capacity among local actors providing surgical care in low-resource conflict settings
Authors: Amila Ratnayake, Hannah B Wild
Published: 2026

Casualty data from the war in Sudan

The war in Sudan that began in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces now ranks among the world’s largest humanitarian and public health crises. According to recent WHO data, the conflict has left 34 million people in need of humanitarian aid and 21 million lacking basic health services. Drone strikes and the use of explosive weapons in populated areas have been devastating, and repeated attacks have crippled the healthcare system, already weakened by disease and hunger.1 2

This issue of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery Open publishes a report on abdominal injuries during the war in Sudan led by local authors.3 Faculty based at Wad-Madni Teaching Hospital, a conflict-affected region 180 km south of Khartoum, conducted retrospective data collection on casualties with conflict-related abdominal wounds over a period of 6 months in 2023. Injuries predominantly affected civilians (87.5%). The mean delay from injury to admission was 27 hours (range 1–148). Combat injuries were more often gunshot wounds, involved multiple organs, and were linked to longer delays to treatment (47 vs 19 hours, p=0.003) and longer hospital stays. These findings provide insight into the affected population, injury patterns, and highlight research priorities at a national level in the study setting.

Local research capacity in low-resource conflict settings

The Sudanese author team should be commended for recognizing the importance of data collection and successfully conducting this research under significant pressure in austere circumstances. The wartime experience of high-resource militaries is extensively analyzed due to registries such as the Department of Defense Trauma Registry. In contrast, most knowledge acquired during casualty care in low-resource conflict settings is not preserved due to difficulty maintaining registries, lack of dedicated personnel and information systems for analysis, and the need to prioritize care for large casualty loads in resource-constrained environments. Local author teams from neglected conflict settings encounter a range of other obstacles to publishing their data.4

Casualty data from low-resource conflict settings not only provide insight into injury patterns and performance improvement opportunities within local health systems, but are also valuable to humanitarian and military actors providing care in similar environments or preparing for conflict dynamics such as large-scale combat operations. The Explosive Weapons Trauma Care Collective (EXTRACCT), a working group established to reduce preventable death from conflict-related injury in low-resource settings, leads several initiatives to increase access to casualty data from low-resource conflict settings. These efforts include an open-access toolkit for researchers in low-resource conflict settings on casualty data collection which was developed by a local team in the Sahel, as well as EXTRACCT’s Casualty Data Initiative, a volunteer network of qualified proofreaders and coauthors available to support research by local author teams.5,7 Recognition of the limitations of what data can feasibly be collected in austere and volatile conflict settings on the part of journals and reviewers is required while simultaneously upholding academic standards. Publications such as this series from Sudan in top-tier journals like Trauma Surgery and Acute Care Open are important steps to address the evidence gap on casualty care in low-resource conflict settings.
