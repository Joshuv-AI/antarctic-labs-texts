Mesoscopic Fluctuations in Statistical Systems


Abstract
The fluctuations are termed mesoscopic, when their typical size is essentially larger then the average distance between the nearest neighbors, while being much smaller than the overall system size. Since the features of mesoscopic fluctuations are essentially different from those of the surrounding matter, they can be interpreted as fluctuations of one phase occurring inside another host phase. In condensed matter, these fluctuations are of nanosize. They can occur in many-body systems of different nature, for instance, they are typical for condensed matter, can appear in systems of trapped atoms, and also arise in biological and social systems. A survey of the experimental evidence for the occurrence of mesoscopic fluctuations in different materials and systems is given. The main attention is paid to a general theoretical approach for describing them. Applications of the approach are also discussed.

1
Introduction

The appearance of mesoscopic fluctuations is widely known to occur in a number
of many-body systems, such as condensed-matter systems, systems of trapped atoms,
as well as in biological and social systems. Usually, the features of mesoscopic
fluctuations essentially differ from those of the host phase, which allows for their
interpretation as phase fluctuations. They can be called by different names, such
as heterophase fluctuations, nanoscale phase separation, nanoscale phase fluctuations,
and like that. These guises have the main common features corresponding to the
appearance of local fluctuations of one phase inside another host material. These
fluctuations usually are randomly distributed in space and in time, which makes their
description highly complicated.

Let us specify why the fluctuations are called mesoscopic. Generally, there are for
this two reasons. First, the sizes of the phase embryos are intermediate between the
average distance from the nearest neighbors and the overall system size. Say, the
average distance between the particles in a condensed-matter system, or between neurons
in the brain, or between agents in a society is
a
, while the overall size of the
considered system is
l_{exp}
. Then the typical size
l_{mes}
of a mesoscopic phase
embryo is in the range

a\;\ll\;l_{mes}\;\ll\;l_{exp}\;.

(1)

The other reason for calling these fluctuations mesoscopic is the duration of their
lifetime
t_{mes}
that usually is between the interaction time
t_{int}
and the
experimental observation time
t_{exp}
,

t_{int}\;\ll\;t_{mes}\;\ll\;t_{exp}\;.

(2)

The fluctuation lifetime has to be larger than the interaction time in order that
the embryo would really represent another phase, but not just a locally nonequilibrium
microscopic fluctuation of the same phase.

The mesoscopic phase fluctuations are usually randomly distributed in the space of
the sample. The probability of their appearance is not prescribed but has to be
self-consistently defined by the considered system properties. Strictly speaking,
on microscopic scale, a system with mesoscopic fluctuations is not equilibrium but
can be only quasi-equilibrium. However, on macroscopic scale, corresponding to a
sample averaged over space and time, the renormalized system looks as equilibrium,
which allows for the development of a quasi-equilibrium approach and the construction
of effective statistical models. Of course, heterophase fluctuations can also appear
in strongly nonequilibrium systems, as will be considered below.

The ideas on the occurrence of heterophase fluctuations in condensed matter have long
history, first being applied to water-ice phase transition. It has been conjectured

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
that below the melting temperature water contains
ice-like clusters, while above the melting point there arise local liquid-like
fluctuations in ice. Frenkel
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
emphasized that
heterophase fluctuations can appear in different types of condensed matter and their
account is necessary for the accurate description of condensed-matter systems. There
have been a number of experimental works confirming the existence of these fluctuations
in condensed matter, with taking them into account by means of phenomenological models.

The main characteristics of such mesoscopic fluctuations depend on the matter where they
appear
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. For example, in condensed matter the typical
length and time scales are as follows. The interaction radius is
r_{int}\sim 10^{-8}
cm,
the radius of a mesoscopic fluctuation is of nanosize, being
l_{mes}\sim 10^{-7}
cm,
the interaction time is
t_{int}\sim 10^{-14}
s, the local equilibration time is

t_{loc}\sim 10^{-13}
s, and the mesoscopic fluctuation time is
t_{mes}\sim 10^{-12}
s
or longer.

In trapped atomic clouds, the parameters can be widely varied. For instance, in a
nonequilibrium droplet state
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, the typical
interaction time can be
t_{int}\sim 10^{-8}
s and the local equilibration time,

t_{loc}\sim 10^{-3}
s, while the coherence length, defining the droplet size,

l_{coh}\sim 10^{-5}
cm, and the fluctuating droplet lifetime,
t_{mes}\sim 10^{-2}
s.

In nuclear matter, the strong-interaction time is
t_{int}\sim 10^{-24}
s and the local
equilibration time is
t_{loc}\sim 10^{-23}
s. For realistic parameters, the deconfinement
transition is a crossover, where there can coexist droplets of hadron phase intermixed
with quark-gluon phase
[
13
,
14
]
.

The specific features of the present review are as follows.

(i) We give a survey of recent literature on the subject, emphasizing the points that,
to our mind, require additional attention.

(ii) We present a general statistical approach allowing for the description of different
systems with mesoscopic phase fluctuations. This approach is applicable to classical as well
as quantum systems.

(iii) Using the developed general approach, we consider not only condensed matter but
also discuss other systems with mesoscopic fluctuations, such as cold trapped atoms and
biological or social systems.

3
Spatial and temporal scales

In statistical systems, it is possible to distinguish several important spatial and temporal
scales defining the physics of the underlying processes

[
8
,
9
,
144
]
.

The basic spatial scales are the interaction length, or interaction radius,
r_{int}
,
and the mean interparticle distance,
a
. These quantities define the mean particle density

\rho
and the characteristic velocity
v
by the relations

\rho a^{3}\;\approx\;1\;,\qquad v\;\sim\;\frac{\hbar}{mr_{int}}\;,

where
m
is particle mass. They also define the mean free path

\lambda\;\sim\;\frac{1}{\rho r_{int}^{2}}\;\sim\;\frac{a^{3}}{r_{int}^{2}}\;.

(4)

The size of a mesoscopic fluctuation
l_{mes}
, according to (1), is essentially larger than
the mean interparticle distance.

The above spatial scales prescribe the characteristic temporal scales. Thus the interaction
time is

t_{int}\;\sim\;\frac{r_{int}}{v}\;\sim\;\frac{1}{\hbar}\;mr_{int}^{2}\;.

(5)

The local equilibration time is

t_{loc}\;\sim\;\frac{\lambda}{v}\;\sim\;\frac{ma^{3}}{\hbar r_{int}}\;,

(6)

so that its relation with the interaction time becomes

t_{loc}\;\sim\;\frac{a^{3}}{r_{int}^{3}}\;t_{int}\;.

(7)

The typical lifetime of a mesoscopic fluctuation reads as

t_{mes}\;\sim\;\frac{l_{mes}}{v}\;\sim\;\frac{1}{\hbar}\;mr_{int}l_{mes}\;,

(8)

from where

t_{mes}\;\sim\;\frac{l_{mes}}{r_{int}}\;t_{int}\;\sim\;\frac{r_{int}^{2}l_{mes}}{a^{3}}\;t_{loc}\;.

(9)

For condensed matter,
r_{int}\sim a
, hence
\lambda\sim a
, and the mean interparticle
distance
a\sim 10^{-8}
cm, while
l_{mes}\gtrsim 10a
. Then the interaction time is

t_{int}\;\sim\;\frac{ma^{2}}{\hbar}\;\sim\;10^{-14}-10^{-13}\;{\rm s}\;,

(10)

the local equilibration time becomes of the order of the interaction time,

t_{loc}\sim t_{int}
, and for the mesoscopic fluctuation time, we have

t_{mes}\;\sim\;\frac{l_{mes}}{a}\;t_{loc}\;\gtrsim\;10^{-13}-10^{-12}\;{\rm s}\;.

(11)

In the similar way, it is straightforward to estimate the characteristic quantities for
other systems. For example, for nuclear matter
[
13
,
14
]
, the local
equilibration and interaction times are of the order of
t_{loc}\sim 10^{-24}-10^{-23}
s,
so that the mesoscopic fluctuation time is
t_{mes}\gtrsim 10^{-23}-10^{-22}
s.

Depending on the considered time scale, statistical systems require different types of
description. For a closed system, there are the following relaxation stages. At the

dynamic stage

0\;<\;t\;<\;t_{int}\qquad(dynamic\;stage)\;,

(12)

the system can be strongly nonequilibrium and high-order correlation functions (strictly
speaking all) are required for its correct description. At the
kinetic stage

t_{int}\;<\;t\;<\;t_{loc}\qquad(kinetic\;stage)\;,

(13)

the Bogolubov functional hypothesis
[
145
]
is valid, when all higher-order
correlation functions become functionals of a single-particle distribution function. At
the
heterophase stage

t_{loc}\;<\;t\;<\;t_{mes}\qquad(heterophase\;stage)\;,

(14)

it is necessary to take into account the existence of mesoscopic phase fluctuations. At
the
hydrodynamic stage

t_{mes}\;<\;t\;<\;t_{eq}\qquad(hydrodynamic\;stage)\;,

(15)

the single-particle distribution function can be expressed in terms of time only through
some of averages, like the average density or energy. After the equilibration time
t_{eq}
,
the system, as a whole, is in equilibrium,

t_{eq}\;<\;t\;<\;\infty\qquad(equilibrium\;stage)\;.

(16)

For condensed matter, where
t_{loc}\sim t_{int}
, the kinetic stage is absent.

Even if the system, as a whole, is equilibrium on large scales, its description depends
on the intervals of time
\Delta t
which are considered. On the interval of time

0\;<\;\Delta t\;<\;t_{loc}\qquad(nonequilibrium)\;,

(17)

the systems is strongly nonequilibrium permanently accomplishing microscopic quantum
and thermal fluctuations. On the mesoscopic time interval

t_{loc}\;<\;\Delta t\;<\;t_{mes}\qquad(quasi-equilibrium)\;,

(18)

a quasi-equilibrium picture is appropriate considering the arising mesoscopic phase
fluctuations. For large times, when

\Delta t\;\gg\;t_{mes}\qquad(equilibrium)\;,

(19)

the overall system can be treated as equilibrium, although taking into account the
underlying microscopic and mesoscopic fluctuations.

[

,
147
,
148
]
. In this approach, it is necessary to define the
spaces of states corresponding to different phases, to describe the location of different
phases by manifold indicator functions
[
149
,
150
]
, as suggested in Refs.

[
151
,
152
,
153
,
154
]
, and to average over phase
configurations. Here, we follow the general approach developed in Refs.

[
155
,
156
,
157
,
158
]
. Because of the importance of
understanding the principal ideas the theory is based on, the main points of the approach
are explained below. Throughout the paper, the Planck and Boltzmann constants are set to one.

4.1
Quantum statistical ensemble

Mesoscopic phase fluctuations exist in both quantum as well as classical systems. The method
of treating them is general for both types of systems. Let us start with the most general
situation of quantum systems, while returning to classical systems later on.

A quantum system requires, first, to define the space of accessible microscopic states of
that system. The microscopic states can be represented by linear combinations over basis
states
\varphi_{n}
, with
n
being a quantum index. Respectively, one says that the system
is characterized by a Hilbert space

{\cal H}\;=\;\overline{{\cal L}}\{\;\varphi_{n}\;\}

(20)

that is a closed linear envelope over the given basis. The latter is assumed to be
orthonormalized. The system state is defined by a statistical operator
\hat{\rho}
that is
a semi-positive trace-one operator on
\mathcal{H}
. The pair
\{\mathcal{H},\hat{\rho}\}

is a
quantum statistical ensemble
.

Local observables are represented by operators
\hat{A}
on
\mathcal{H}
forming the
algebra of local observables
\mathcal{A}
. The latter is a von Neumann algebra, hence the
subalgebra of bounded operators on a Hilbert space, which is self-adjoint, closed in the
weak operator topology, and containing the identity operator. Observable quantities are the
averages

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle\;\equiv\;{\rm Tr}_{\cal H}\hat{\rho}\;\hat{A}\;=\;\sum_{n}(\varphi_{n},\;\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\varphi_{n})\;,

(21)

where the trace operation is over
\mathcal{H}
. The collection of the averages for all
observable quantities is a
statistical state

\langle\;{\cal A}\;\rangle\;=\;\{\;\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle\;\}\;.

(22)

One usually considers macroscopic statistical systems in the thermodynamic limit, where
the number of particles
N
and the system volume
V
tend to infinity, while the average
particle density being bounded,

N\;\rightarrow\;\infty\;,\qquad V\;\rightarrow\;\infty\;,\qquad\frac{N}{V}\;\rightarrow\;const\;.

(23)

In what follows, when writing
N\rightarrow\infty
, we imply the thermodynamic limit (
23
).

If the considered system can exhibit several thermodynamic phases, enumerated by an index

f=1,2,\ldots
, there occurs the decomposition of observables related to the decomposition
of the Hilbert space into the direct sum

{\cal H}\;\mapsto\;\bigoplus_{f}\;{\cal H}_{f}\qquad(N\rightarrow\infty)

(24)

of spaces corresponding to different phases. Mathematical details of this decomposition
can be found in Refs.

[
8
,
9
,
159
,
160
,
161
,
162
,
163
]
.

The structure of the space of states (
24
) means that the system can exist in one of
the phases, but not in several at once.

4.2
Methods of phase selection

In order to study a particular phase
f
, it is necessary to consider not the whole space

\mathcal{H}
but only the related subspace
\mathcal{H}_{f}
. The straightforward way is
to calculate the averages by restricting the trace operation to the required subspace,
which is called the method of restricted traces
[
164
,
165
]
. The
microscopic states of different phases can be distinguished by different symmetry
properties. Then the selection of a particular phase is named symmetry breaking. Between
several admissible phases that one is actually realized, which is the most thermodynamically
stable, enjoying, say, the least free energy. This is called spontaneous symmetry breaking.
Different phases are distinguished by different order parameters or, more generally, by
different order indices
[
166
,
167
,
168
,
169
,
170
]
.

A convenient way of symmetry breaking, named the
method of quasiaverages
, was
formulated by Bogolubov
[
171
,
172
,
173
,
174
]
.
Suppose the system is characterized by a Hamiltonian
H
that is invariant with respect to
transformations forming a group. The total Hilbert space of the system is also invariant
with respect to the same group. In order to break the symmetry, it is possible to introduce
the Hamiltonian

H_{f\varepsilon}\;=\;H+\varepsilon\Gamma_{f}

(25)

by adding a term
\Gamma_{f}
breaking the symmetry to a subgroup related to the symmetry
of the required phase, with
\varepsilon
being a real-valued parameter. The observable
quantity
A_{f}
, for a system in phase
f
, corresponding to an operator
\hat{A}
, is
given by the quasiaverage

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{1}{N}\;A_{f}\;\equiv\;\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\;\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{1}{N}\;\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{f\varepsilon}\;,

(26)

where the average on the right-hand side is calculated with Hamiltonian (
25
). Note
that the limits on the right-hand side are not commutative.

A convenient modification of this method is provided by the method of thermodynamic
quasiaverages
[
154
,
175
]
, where, instead of Hamiltonian (
25
),
the Hamiltonian

H_{f}\;=\;H+\frac{1}{N^{\gamma}}\;\Gamma_{f}\qquad(0<\gamma<1)

(27)

is introduced. The thermodynamic quasi-average is given, instead of two limits in
(
26
), by the single thermodynamic limit,

A_{f}\;\equiv\;\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{f}\;,

(28)

where the average is calculated with Hamiltonian (
27
).

There exist several other methods of selecting phases, which can be found in Refs.

[
8
,
164
,
176
,
177
]
.

4.3
Weighted Hilbert spaces

The main idea of all methods for phase selection can be explained by introducing weighted
Hilbert spaces
[
8
,
9
,
178
]
. Then one defines a probability
distribution
p(\varphi)
over the basis
\{\varphi_{n}\}
, so that

\sum_{f}p_{f}(\varphi_{n})\;=\;1\;,\qquad 0\;\leq\;p_{f}(\varphi_{n})\;\leq\;1\;.

The
weighted Hilbert space
is the Hilbert space with the weighted basis,

{\cal H}_{f}\;\equiv\;\{\;{\cal H},\;p_{f}(\varphi)\;\}\;.

(29)

The observable quantities related to a phase
f
are given by the averages

A_{f}\;\equiv\;{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\hat{\rho}\;\hat{A}\;\equiv\;\sum_{n}p_{f}(\varphi_{n})\;(\varphi_{n}\;,\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\varphi_{n})\;.

(30)

The probability distribution
p_{f}(\varphi)
selects the microscopic states typical of
the required phase
f
so that to yield the corresponding phase characteristics. If the
thermodynamic phases are distinguished by the type of symmetry, it is possible to define
the order operator
\hat{\eta}
, whose weighted average gives the order parameter

\eta_{f}\;\equiv\;\langle\;\hat{\eta}\;\rangle_{f}\;=\;{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\hat{\rho}\;\hat{\eta}

(31)

for each phase
f
. More generally, even when no specific symmetry is ascribed to the
phases, the latter can be distinguished by the order indices of correlation matrices

\omega(\hat{C}_{nf})\;\equiv\;\frac{\log||\;\hat{C}_{nf}\;||}{\log|\;{\rm Tr}\hat{C}_{nf}\;|}\;,

(32)

where
\hat{C}_{nf}
is an
n
-th order correlation matrix for the
f
-th phase

[
166
,
167
,
168
,
169
,
170
,
179
]
.

4.4
Spatial phase configuration

As is clear from the above, the standard approach allows for the consideration of only
pure phases. In order to take into account mesoscopic heterophase fluctuations, it is
necessary to generalize the approach by including the account of the mesoscopic
fluctuations.

Assume that we are making a snapshot of the system at a fixed moment of time. The system,
consisting of several phases intermixed in the space, can be characterized by means of
the manifold indicator functions
[
149
,
150
]
showing the location of
the phases,

\displaystyle\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1\;,&~{\bf r}\;\in\;V_{f}\\
0\;,&~{\bf r}\;\not\in\;V_{f}\end{array}\right.\;.

For the simplicity of notation, we denote the spatial part, occupied by a phase
f
, and
the volume of this part by the same letter
V_{f}
. The collection of the manifold indicator
functions

\xi\;\equiv\;\{\;\xi_{f}({\bf r}):~{\bf r}\in V\;,~f=1,2,\ldots\;\}

(36)

realizes the spatial orthogonal covering due to the orthogonality property

\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;\xi_{g}({\bf r})\;=\;\delta_{fg}\;\xi_{f}({\bf r})

(37)

and the normalization conditions

\sum_{f}\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;=\;1\;,\qquad\int_{V}\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;=\;V_{f}\;.

(38)

The collection
\xi
defines the spatial phase configuration.

The phases can occupy different spatial locations and can be of different shapes. This
variety can be described by introducing for each phase volume the orthogonal subcoverings
with the help of the representation

\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n_{f}}\xi_{fi}({\bf r}-{\bf a}_{fi})\;,

(39)

separating the phase volume into small cells, where

\displaystyle\xi_{fi}({\bf r})\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1\;,&~{\bf r}\;\in\;V_{fi}\\
0\;,&~{\bf r}\;\not\in\;V_{fi}\end{array}\right.

are submanifold indicator functions with the properties of orthogonality

\xi_{fi}({\bf r})\;\xi_{fj}({\bf r})\;=\;\delta_{ij}\;\xi_{fi}({\bf r})

(43)

and normalization

\int_{V}\xi_{fi}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;=\;V_{fi}\;,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n_{f}}V_{fi}\;=\;V_{f}\;.

(44)

The vectors
{\bf a}_{fi}
characterize the spatial locations of the cells.

By taking a sufficiently large number
n_{f}
of small cells, with the volumes
V_{fi}
,
it is possible to represent any spatial configuration of phases, so that the additive
Gibbs conditions
[
180
,
181
]
for the number of particles and volumes be
valid:

\sum_{f}N_{f}\;=\;N\;,\qquad\sum_{f}V_{f}\;=\;V\;.

(45)

The notion of a separating surface was introduced by Gibbs
[
180
]
who emphasized
that the separating surface is a mathematical abstraction and its exact position can be
chosen arbitrarily. Its location can be fixed in a unique manner by imposing some additional
conditions. The separating surface can be located in such a way that the surface density of
the matter be zero, which implies the additivity of local observables, like in condition
(
45
). Being an imaginary mathematical abstraction, the Gibbs separating surface should
not be confused with an interface layer having a finite density of matter. A thorough
description of the Gibbs separating surface is given in Ref.
[
8
]
.

4.5
Operators of local observables

Since the system contains local parts corresponding to different phases, the microscopic
states of the whole system with coexisting phases pertain to the Hilbert space

{\cal F}({\cal H})\;=\;\bigotimes_{f}{\cal H}_{f}\;,

(46)

in which the factor spaces are the weighted Hilbert spaces characterizing the related
phases. The triple, consisting of the total space
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})
, base spaces

\mathcal{H}_{f}
, and the projection map from the total space to a base space is termed
fibre bundle. The tensor product (
46
) is called the
fibred space over a
Hilbert space

[
182
,
183
,
184
]
. Note that a similar
representation exists for Fock spaces
[
185
]
.

The operators of local observables, acting on the fibred space
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})
,
depend on the phase configuration given by
\xi
and can be represented by the direct sum

\hat{A}(\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}\hat{A}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;.

(47)

In particular, the operator of the number of particles reads as

\hat{N}(\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}\hat{N}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;.

(48)

And the energy Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}(\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}\hat{H}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;.

(49)

The algebra of operators of local observables, acting on
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})
,
is denoted as
\mathcal{A}(\xi)
.

4.6
Heterophase statistical operator

The statistical operator of a heterophase system
\hat{\rho}(\xi)
can be defined as
a minimizer of an information functional, under imposed constraints. First of all, the
statistical operator has to be normalized,

{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;{\cal D}\xi\;=\;1\;,

(50)

where the trace is over the fibred space (
46
) and the integral over
\xi
implies
a functional integration over the set (
36
) of the manifold indicator functions. This
integration symbolizes the averaging over phase configurations. The averaging is required
due to the mesoscopic nature of the phase fluctuations, whose lifetime is much shorter
than the observation time and whose locations are randomly distributed over the sample.

The second constraint is the definition of the average energy

{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\hat{H}(\xi)\;{\cal D}\xi\;=\;E\;.

(51)

The given total number of particles in the system implies the condition

{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\hat{N}(\xi)\;{\cal D}\xi\;=\;N\;.

(52)

Similarly, one can impose other constraints uniquely defining a representative ensemble

[
8
,
9
,
186
,
187
,
188
]
.

The general information functional has the Kullback-Leibler
[
189
,
190
]

form

I[\;\hat{\rho}\;]\;=\;{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\ln\;\frac{\hat{\rho}(\xi)}{\hat{\rho}_{0}(\xi)}\;{\cal D}\xi+\alpha\left[\;{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;{\cal D}\xi-1\;\right]\;+

+\;\beta\left[\;{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\hat{H}(\xi){\cal D}\xi-E\;\right]+\gamma\left[\;{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\hat{N}(\xi){\cal D}\xi-N\;\right]\;,

(53)

in which
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are Lagrange multipliers and
\hat{\rho}_{0}(\xi)

is a prior statistical operator imposing restrictions on the distribution of phase
configurations, if these are known. In the case that no prior information on the random
distribution of configurations is available, the prior statistical operator is assumed
to be constant.

Minimizing the information functional yields the
heterophase statistical operator

\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;=\;\frac{\hat{\rho}_{0}(\xi)\exp\{-\beta H(\xi)\}}{{\rm Tr}\hat{\rho}_{0}(\xi)\exp\{-\beta H(\xi)\}}\;,

(54)

with the chemical potential
\mu=-\gamma T
,
\beta=1/T
, temperature
T
, and the
grand Hamiltonian

H(\xi)\;\equiv\;\hat{H}(\xi)-\mu\;\hat{N}(\xi)\;.

(55)

When there is no information on the trial distribution of the phases, the statistical
operator becomes

\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z}\;\exp\{-\beta H(\xi)\;\}\;,

(56)

with the partition function

Z\;=\;{\rm Tr}\int\exp\{\;-\beta H(\xi)\;\}\;{\cal D}\xi\;.

(57)

In the second-quantization representation, the energy Hamiltonian of the phase
f
, taking
into account the identity

\int_{V_{f}}d{\bf r}\;=\;\int_{V}\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;,

reads as

\hat{H}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;=\;\int\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left[\;-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+U({\bf r})\;\right]\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\int\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;\xi_{f}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}

(58)

and the number-of-particle operator of the phase
f
is

\hat{N}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;=\;\int\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;.

(59)

Here
\psi_{f}({\bf r})
is the representation of a field operator on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{f}
;
U({\bf r})
is an external potential, and
\Phi({\bf r}
is an
interaction potential. Here and in what follows, where the integration over the space
is not shown, it is implied that the integration is over the whole system volume
V
.
The internal degrees of freedom, such as spin, isospin, or like that, can be taken into
account by representing the field operators as columns whose rows are labeled by the
internal degrees of freedom.

4.7
Averaging over phase configurations

Technically, the averaging over phase configurations is accomplished by defining the
integration over the manifold indicator functions. For this purpose, we introduce the
variable

x_{f}\;=\;\frac{1}{V}\int\xi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;=\;\frac{V_{f}}{V}

(60)

that varies in the interval
[0,1]
, satisfying the normalization condition

\sum_{f}x_{f}\;=\;1\;,\qquad 0\;\leq\;x_{f}\;\leq 1\;.

(61)

As is explained in Sec. 4.4, the overall system volume can be partitioned into small
cells whose locations are described by the submanifold indicator functions (
4.4
).
The functional integration consists of two steps. First, by changing the cell locations
it is possible to create various phase configurations, provided the normalization
(
61
) is valid and the cells are asymptotically small, such that

n_{f}\;\rightarrow\;\infty\;,\qquad V_{fi}\;\rightarrow\;0\;,\qquad x_{f}\;=\;const\;.

(62)

At the second step, the phase concentrations are to be varied, which means the variation
of
x_{f}
in the range
[0,1]
.

Thus the differential measure of the functional integration is

{\cal D}\xi\;=\;\delta\left(\sum_{f}x_{f}-1\right)\;\prod_{f}dx_{f}\;\prod_{f}{\cal D}\xi_{f}\;,

(63)

with

{\cal D}\xi_{f}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n_{f}}\frac{d{\bf a}_{fi}}{V}\qquad(n_{f}\;\rightarrow\;\infty)\;.

(64)

The functional integration over the manifold indicator functions yields the following
results
[
8
,
155
,
156
,
157
,
158
]
.

Lemma
. Let us consider the class
\mathcal{C}_{f}
of the functionals having the structure

C_{f}(\xi_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}\int C_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}_{j}\;.

(65)

The averaging over phase configurations, under fixed concentrations
x_{f}
, gives

\int C_{f}(\xi_{f})\;{\cal D}\xi_{f}\;=\;C_{f}(x_{f})\;,

(66)

where

C_{f}(x_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}x_{f}^{n}\;\int C_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;d{\bf r}_{1}d{\bf r}_{2}\ldots d{\bf r}_{n}\;.

(67)

Theorem 1
. Let the representation of the Hamiltonian on the space
\mathcal{H}_{f}

pertain to the class
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, having the form

H_{f}(\xi_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}H_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}_{j}\;.

(68)

Then it follows that

\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(\xi_{f})\}\;{\cal D}\xi_{f}\;=\;\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(x_{f})\}\;,

(69)

where

H_{f}(x_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}x_{f}^{n}\;\int H_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;d{\bf r}_{1}d{\bf r}_{2}\ldots d{\bf r}_{n}\;.

(70)

Theorem 2
. Let the thermodynamic potential be

\Omega\;=\;-T\ln Z\;=\;-T\ln{\rm Tr}\int\exp\{\;-\beta H(\xi)\;\}\;{\cal D}\xi\;,

(71)

with the Hamiltonian

H(\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}H_{f}(\xi_{f})

(72)

acting on the fibred space (
46
), with
H_{f}(\xi_{f})
being of the class
\mathcal{C}_{f}
,
as defined in (
68
). Then the averaging over phase configurations yields

\Omega\;=\;{\rm abs}\;\min_{\{w_{f}\}}\Omega(\{w_{f}\})\;,

(73)

under the normalization condition

\sum_{f}w_{f}\;=\;1\;,\qquad 0\;\leq\;w_{f}\;\leq\;1\;.

(74)

Here

\Omega(\{w_{f}\})\;=\;-T\ln{\rm Tr}\;\exp\{-\beta H_{eff}\}\;,

(75)

with the effective Hamiltonian

H_{eff}\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}H_{f}(w_{f})\;,

(76)

where

H_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}w_{f}^{n}\;\int H_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;d{\bf r}_{1}d{\bf r}_{2}\ldots d{\bf r}_{n}\;.

(77)

In other words,

\Omega\;=\;\sum_{f}\Omega_{f}(w_{f})\;,\qquad\Omega_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;-T\ln{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\;\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(w_{f})\}\;.

(78)

The quantity
w_{f}=V_{f}/V
, minimizing the thermodynamic potential, plays the role of

phase probability
.

Theorem 3
. The observable quantity for an operator (
47
), with the terms of
the class
\mathcal{C}_{f}
,

\hat{A}_{f}(\xi_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}\int A_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}_{j}\;,

(79)

given by the average

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle\;=\;{\rm Tr}\int\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;\hat{A}(\xi)\;{\cal D}\xi\;,

(80)

with the statistical operator

\hat{\rho}(\xi)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z}\;\exp\{-\beta H(\xi)\}\;,

(81)

after averaging over phase configurations, reduces to

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle\;=\;\sum_{f}\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}(w_{f})\;\rangle\;,

(82)

where

\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}(w_{f})\;\rangle\;\equiv\;{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\hat{\rho}_{f}(w_{f})\;\hat{A}_{f}(w_{f})\;,

(83)

with the statistical operator

\hat{\rho}_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{f}}\;\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(w_{f})\}\;,

(84)

the partition function

Z_{f}\;=\;{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(w_{f})\}\;,

(85)

and the renormalized terms

\hat{A}_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;\sum_{n}w_{f}^{n}\;\int A_{f}({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})\;d{\bf r}_{1}d{\bf r}_{2}\ldots d{\bf r}_{n}\;.

(86)

For example, the renormalized expression for Hamiltonian (
58
) is

H_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;w_{f}\int\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left[\;-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+U({\bf r})\;\right]\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\int\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(87)

while the renormalized number-of-particle operator (
59
) becomes

\hat{N}_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;w_{f}\int\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;.

(88)

It is easy to notice that if mesoscopic fluctuations are absent, and just a single phase
can exist, we return to the standard formulation for describing statistical systems.

4.8
Surface thermodynamic potential

As has been emphasized above, the Gibbs separating surface is an imaginary dividing
boundary between the phases, and no surface operators or other microscopic quantities
exist at the microscopic level. But surface characteristics appear at the macroscopic
level, when studying thermodynamic properties. Thus the surface thermodynamic potential
is defined
[
180
,
191
]
as the difference

\Omega_{sur}\;\equiv\;\Omega-\Omega_{G}

(89)

between the actual thermodynamic potential of a heterophase system in the volume
V
and
the thermodynamic potential of the Gibbs mixture, which is the sum of the thermodynamic
potentials of pure phases occupying the related volumes
V_{f}
,

\Omega_{G}\;=\;\sum_{f}w_{f}\;\Omega_{f}(1)\;.

(90)

Hence the surface thermodynamic potential is

\Omega_{sur}\;=\;\Omega-\sum_{f}w_{f}\;\Omega_{f}(1)\;.

(91)

By definition (
73
), we have

\Omega\;=\;{\rm abs}\;\min_{\{w_{f}\}}\Omega(\{w_{f}\})\;\leq\;\min_{f}\Omega_{f}(1)\;.

(92)

Moreover,

\min_{f}\Omega_{f}(1)\;\leq\;\sum_{f}w_{f}\;\Omega_{f}(1)\;,

(93)

because of which

\Omega\;\leq\;\sum_{f}w_{f}\;\Omega_{f}(1)\;.

(94)

Then from (
91
) it follows that the heterophase system is stable when

\Omega_{sur}\;\leq\;0\;.

(95)

In the similar way, it is straightforward to define the surface quantities corresponding
to the averages for the operators of observables, as the difference between the actual
average for a heterophase system and the sum of the averages for the Gibbs mixture
components,

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{sur}\;\equiv\;\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle-\sum_{f}w_{f}\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}(1)\;\rangle\;,

(96)

which gives

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{sur}\;=\;\sum_{f}\left[\;\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}(w_{f})\;\rangle-w_{f}\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}(1)\;\rangle\;\right]\;.

(97)

The set of surface observables (
97
) constitutes the surface state. Notice that the
surface number of particles is zero,
\langle\hat{N}(\xi)\rangle_{sur}=0
, in agreement
with the definition of the Gibbs separating surface. However the surface energy

\langle\hat{H}(\xi)\rangle_{sur}
is not zero, since the renormalized Hamiltonian is
not linear in the phase probabilities
w_{f}
.

4.9
Thermodynamics of heterophase systems

All thermodynamic characteristics can be calculated as soon as we find a thermodynamic
potential. For concreteness, we have considered above the grand thermodynamic potential.
Other thermodynamic potentials can be obtained either by employing Legendre transformations

[
192
]
or by directly calculating the required potentials following the same
scheme as above. For instance, for the free energy of a heterophase system, we find

F\;=\;\sum_{f}F_{f}(w_{f})\;,

(98)

with the terms

F_{f}(w_{f})\;=\;-T\ln{\rm Tr}_{{\cal H}_{f}}\exp\{-\beta\hat{H}_{f}(w_{f})\}\;.

(99)

The surface free energy reads as

F_{sur}\;=\;F-\sum_{f}w_{f}\;F_{f}(1)\;.

(100)

The principal difference from the description of pure phases is the existence of the
additional thermodynamic quantities called
geometric phase probabilities

w_{f}\;=\;\frac{V_{f}}{V}\qquad(f=1,2,\ldots)

(101)

that are defined as minimizers of a thermodynamic potential. The phase probabilities
appear due to the occurrence of mesoscopic phase fluctuations. It may happen that these
fluctuations exist not for all thermodynamic parameters but arise under the variation
of one or several parameters. For instance, it may happen that below some temperature

T_{nuc}
, the system consists of a pure phase, say phase
f
, so that

w_{f}\;=\;1\qquad(T<T_{nuc})\;,

(102)

while above this temperature the system becomes heterophase, when

w_{f}\;<\;1\qquad(T>T_{nuc})\;.

(103)

The corresponding temperature is called the
nucleation temperature
.

Except the phase probabilities, it is possible to define the phase fractions

n_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{f}}{N}\qquad(f=1,2,\ldots)\;,

(104)

which are related with the phase probabilities through the phase density

\rho_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{f}}{V_{f}}\;=\;\frac{n_{f}}{w_{f}}\;\rho

(105)

and the average particle density

\rho\;\equiv\;\frac{N}{V}\;=\;\sum_{f}w_{f}\;\rho_{f}\;.

(106)

If the phases do not differ by their densities, then the phase probabilities and phase
fractions coincide with each other,

w_{f}\;=\;n_{f}\qquad(\rho_{f}=\rho)\;.

(107)

5
Classical heterophase systems

In the previous section, the general approach for taking account of mesoscopic heterophase
fluctuations in quantum systems is presented. Of course, these fluctuations are not a
privilege of only quantum systems, but do exist in classical systems as well. The overall
approach can be straightforwardly reformulated for classical systems, as is sketched below.

5.1
Classical statistical ensemble

A classical statistical system of many particles
N
is characterized by the position and
momentum variables

q\;=\;\{{\bf q}_{1},{\bf q}_{2},\ldots,{\bf q}_{N}\}\;,\qquad p\;=\;\{{\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\ldots,{\bf p}_{N}\}\;.

(108)

In total, for a
d
-dimensional space, there are
2Nd
variables, whose collection

\{q,p\}
forms the
space of microstates
. This space is made measurable introducing
the differential measure

d\mu(q,p)\;=\;\frac{dq\;dp}{N!(2\pi\hbar)^{3N}}\;.

(109)

Then the measurable space of microstates constitutes the
phase space

{\cal M}\;=\;\{\;q,p,\mu(q,p)\;\}.

(110)

Defining a probability distribution
\rho(q,p)
makes the pair
\{\mathcal{M},\rho(q,p)\}

a
classical statistical ensemble
.

The observables are given by the functionals
A(q,p)
whose averages

\langle\;A\;\rangle\;=\;\int A(q,p)\;\rho(q,p)\;d\mu(q,p)

(111)

over the phase space yield observable quantities. The collection of the latter defines
a
classical statistical state
.

Generally, the total phase space can characterize different thermodynamic phases, being the
direct sum of subspaces corresponding to particular pure accessible phases,

{\cal M}\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}{\cal M}_{f}\;.

(112)

Depending on the given thermodynamic parameters, that pure phase is actually realized that
is the most thermodynamically stable.

5.2
Selection of thermodynamic phases

In order to select a required thermodynamic phase, it is necessary to restrict the phase
space, thus defining restricted averages
[
8
]
, similarly to the use of
restricted traces for quantum systems. The general way is to employ weighted phase spaces

[
193
]
, which is analogous to employing weighted Hilbert spaces in quantum
theory. For this purpose, one has to define a weighting function
\nu_{f}(q,p)
,
such that

\sum_{f}\nu_{f}(q,p)\;=\;1\;,\qquad 0\;\leq\;\nu_{f}(q,p)\;\leq\;1\;,

(113)

whose role is to select the part of the phase space typical of the needed thermodynamic
phase. The phase space complimented by the weighting function forms the
weighted
phase space

{\cal M}_{f}\;=\;\{\;{\cal M},\;\nu_{f}(q,p)\;\}\;.

(114)

Introducing the weighted differential measure

d\mu_{f}(q,p)\;=\;\nu_{f}(q,p)\;d\mu(q,p)

(115)

allows one to characterize the chosen phase by the averages related to the observable
quantities

A_{f}\;=\;\int A(q,p)\;\rho(q,p)\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)\;.

(116)

5.3
Classical heterophase probability distribution

The following consideration of classical systems with mesoscopic phase fluctuations is
completely similar to the quantum case. A heterophase system is characterized by the
fibred phase space

{\cal F}({\cal M})\;=\;\bigotimes_{f}{\cal M}_{f}\;.

(117)

Introducing the manifold indicator functions, showing the localization of different phases,
one obtains the local observables
A_{f}(q,p,\xi_{f})
. The observable quantities are given by
the averages of the functionals

A(q,p,\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}A_{f}(q,p,\xi_{f})

(118)

over the weighted phase spaces and the averaging over phase configurations,

\langle\;A\;\rangle\;=\;\int A(q,p,\xi)\;\rho(q,p,\xi)\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi\;,

(119)

where

d\mu(q,p)\;=\;\bigotimes_{f}d\mu_{f}(q,p)\;.

(120)

The system Hamiltonian has the form

H(q,p,\xi)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}H_{f}(q,p,\xi_{f})\;,

(121)

with the terms

H_{f}(q,p,\xi_{f})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{i})\;\left[\;\frac{{\bf p}^{2}}{2m}+U({\bf r}_{i})\;\right]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{i})\;\xi_{f}({\bf r}_{j})\;\Phi({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})\;.

(122)

The probability distribution has to satisfy the normalization condition

\int\rho(q,p,\xi)\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi\;=\;1

(123)

and the definition of the average energy

\int\rho(q,p,\xi)\;H(q,p,\xi)\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi\;=\;E\;.

(124)

The information functional reads as

I[\;\rho\;]\;=\;\int\rho(q,p,\xi)\;\ln\;\frac{\rho(q,p,\xi)}{\rho_{0}(q,p,\xi)}\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi\;+\;

+\;\alpha\;\left[\;\int\rho(q,p,\xi)\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi-1\;\right]+\beta\;\left[\;\int\rho(q,p,\xi)\;H(q,p,\xi)\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi-E\;\right]\;.

(125)

If there is no prior information on the distribution of heterophase fluctuations, the
trial distribution
\rho_{0}(q,p,\xi)
is a constant. Then the minimization of the
information functional results in the probability distribution

\rho(q,p,\xi)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z}\;\exp\{-\beta H(q,p,\xi)\}\;,

(126)

with the partition function

Z\;=\;\int\exp\{-\beta H(q,p,\xi)\}\;d\mu(q,p)\;{\cal D}\xi\;.

(127)

5.4
Results of averaging over phase configurations

The averaging over phase configurations, realized as the functional integration over
the manifold indicator functions, is accomplished in the same way as is described in
the previous sections above. As a result, we come to the following picture.

The effective Hamiltonian takes the form

H_{eff}(q,p)\;=\;\bigoplus_{f}H_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;,

(128)

with the terms

H_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\;\frac{{\bf p}_{i}^{2}}{2m}+U({\bf r}_{i})\;\right]+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}\Phi({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})\;.

(129)

The probability distribution is

\rho(q,p)\;=\;\bigotimes_{f}\rho_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;,

(130)

with the factors

\rho_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{f}}\;\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\}

(131)

and the partition functions

Z_{f}\;=\;\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\}\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)\;.

(132)

If
\nu_{f}(q,p)=\nu_{f}(q)
does not depend on momenta, then, after the integration over
momenta, one has

Z_{f}\;=\;\left(\frac{mT}{2\pi w_{f}}\right)^{3N/2}\frac{1}{N!}\int\exp\left\{\;-\;\frac{w_{f}^{2}}{2T}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}\Phi({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})\;-\;\frac{w_{f}}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}U({\bf r}_{i})\;\right\}\;\nu_{f}(q)\;dq\;.

(133)

The observable quantities are defined by the averages

\langle\;A\;\rangle\;=\;\sum_{f}\langle\;A_{f}\;\rangle\;,

(134)

where

\langle\;A_{f}\;\rangle\;=\;\int\rho_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;A_{f}(q,p,w_{f})\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)\;.

(135)

5.5
Quasiaverages and weighted spaces

The choice of a weighted phase space, required for describing particular
thermodynamic phases, can be realized in different ways
[
8
,
9
]

allowing for the definition of restricted phase spaces for classical systems

[
5
]
or restricted traces for quantum systems
[
164
]
.
A convenient method is the method of quasiaverages that was mentioned by
Kirkwood
[
194
]
and developed by Bogolubov

[
172
,
173
,
174
]
. Below we show that the
method of quasiaverages is a particular case of the method of weighted spaces.

The average (
135
) can be written in the form

\langle\;\hat{A}_{f}\;\rangle\;=\;\frac{\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(q,p)\}A_{f}(q,p)\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)}{\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f}(q,p)\}\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)}\;.

(136)

In the method of quasiaverages, one introduces a Hamiltonian

H_{f\varepsilon}(q,p)\;\equiv\;H_{f}(q,p)+\varepsilon\;\Gamma_{f}(q,p)

(137)

by adding a term breaking the symmetry of the Hamiltonian
H_{f}
, and reducing it to
a subgroup of symmetry characterizing the phase
f
. The quasiaverages are defined
by the relation

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{1}{N}\;\langle\;A_{f}\;\rangle\;=\;\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\;\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\;\frac{\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f\varepsilon}(q,p)\}A_{f}(q,p)\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)}{N\int\exp\{-\beta H_{f\varepsilon}(q,p)\}\;d\mu_{f}(q,p)}\;,

(138)

where the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
is taken after the thermodynamic limit. Comparing
(
136
) and (
138
), we see that they coincide provided that in (
136
) one
defines the weight

\nu_{f}(q,p)\;=\;\exp\{-\beta\varepsilon\Gamma_{f}(q,p)\}\;,

(139)

keeping in mind that
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
after the thermodynamic limit.

6
Models of heterophase systems

The theory of systems with mesoscopic phase fluctuations is illustrated below by several
examples. The derivation of effective Hamiltonians, obtained by averaging over heterophase
configurations, has been explained above.

6.1
Magnetic materials with paramagnetic fluctuations

Let us consider a ferromagnetic material on a lattice with a fixed spatial structure,
where there can arise paramagnetic fluctuations. The effective Hamiltonian has the
form

H\;=\;H_{1}\bigoplus H_{2}\;,

(140)

in which

H_{f}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\;UN-w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}\;{\bf S}_{if}\cdot{\bf S}_{jf}\;.

(141)

Here
U
is the average strength of direct particle interactions, while
J_{ij}>0

is an exchange interaction potential. Note that the term containing the direct
interactions
U
cannot be omitted, since it depends of the phase probabilities

w_{f}
. This term is principal for the existence of paramagnetic fluctuations.

The order parameter is

s_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{NS}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\;\langle\;S_{jf}^{z}\;\rangle

(142)

under the condition

s_{1}\;>\;0\;,\qquad s_{2}\;=\;0\;,

(143)

classifying the first phase as ferromagnetic, with the second phase corresponding
to paramagnetic fluctuations. The lattice is assumed to be fixed and being sufficiently
deep, so that all spins are well localized in their lattice sites.

The model of such a ferromagnet with mesoscopic phase fluctuations, and its
properties, have been studied, in mean-field approximation, in Refs.

[
50
,
195
,
196
,
197
,
198
,
199
]
.
Depending on the parameter

u\;\equiv\;\frac{U}{J}\qquad\left(J\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}J_{ij}\right)\;,

(144)

there can occur the following cases.

For
u\leq 0
, paramagnetic fluctuations cannot appear. The sample is purely ferromagnetic
below the critical temperature
T_{c}=0.5
, where by the phase transition of second order
the sample turns into paramagnetic phase.

For
0<u<0.5
, the system is purely ferromagnetic below the temperature
T_{0}
, where
it transforms into paramagnetic phase by the phase transition of first order. The transition
temperature is in the interval
0.09<T_{0}<0.18
.

For
0.5\leq u<1.5
, in the region below
T_{0}
, the system is in the mixed phase of a
ferromagnet with paramagnetic fluctuations. The mixed phase transforms into paramagnetic
phase by the first-order phase transition at temperature
T_{0}
in the range

0.125<T_{0}<0.182
.

For
u=1.5
, the heterophase system is ferromagnetic with paramagnetic fluctuations
below the tricritical point
T_{c}^{*}=0.182
. The tricritical point lays on the boundary
between first and second order phase transitions
[
200
]
.

For
u>1.5
, the sample is heterophase, being a ferromagnet with paramagnetic fluctuations
below the critical temperature
T_{c}=0.125
, where it becomes nonmagnetic through the
second-order phase transition.

In the random-phase approximation, the transition between the ferromagnet with paramagnetic
fluctuations and a paramagnet, for realistic parameters, occurs through first-order phase
transition
[
201
]
.

Several other models of magnets with mesoscopic fluctuations have been considered, such as
antiferromagnets with paramagnetic fluctuations
[
202
]
, heterophase Hubbard model

[
203
]
, heterophase Vonsovsky-Zeener model
[
50
]
, heterophase
ferromagnets with phonon excitations
[
204
]
, heterophase generalization of the
Nagle
[
205
,
206
]
model with short- and long-range interactions

[
207
]
, heterophase spin-glass model with paramagnetic fluctuations

[
207
]
, and a ferromagnet with magnetic reorientations and paramagnetic
fluctuations
[
208
,
209
,
210
]
. Two-dimensional heterophase
Ising-type model, describing a ferromagnet with paramagnetic fluctuations turns out to be
metastable
[
211
]
. These models were considered at zero magnetic field.
Switching on a nonzero external magnetic field leads to the suppression of mesoscopic
paramagnetic fluctuations in magnets
[
212
]
.

6.2
Ferroelectrics with paraelectric fluctuations

There are numerous examples of ferroelectrics exhibiting paraelectric fluctuations

[
66
,
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
213
,
214
,
215
,
216
]
. We consider ferroelectrics with the phase transitions of the
order-disorder type
[
217
]
. Atoms or molecules, composing a ferroelectric, are
located on a lattice where each site is formed by a double well. Electric dipoles appear
due to atomic displacement inside double wells. Taking into account paraelectric fluctuations
leads
[
218
]
to the effective Hamiltonian
H=H_{1}\bigoplus H_{2}
with the
partial Hamiltonians

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}E_{0}-w_{f}\sum_{j}\left(\Omega_{j}S_{jf}^{x}+D_{j}S_{jf}^{z}\right)\;+

+\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\;U_{ij}+B_{ij}S_{if}^{x}S_{jf}^{x}-J_{ij}S_{if}^{z}S_{jf}^{z}\right)\;.

(145)

Here, the first term, linear in
w_{f}
, can be omitted, since in the total Hamiltonian it
gives a constant
w_{1}E_{0}+w_{2}E_{0}=E_{0}
. The second term describes the tunneling between
the wells of a double-well potential at the site
j
, with the tunneling frequency
\Omega_{j}
.
The third term is caused by strain energy due to external forces or to the action of
electric field. The quantity
U_{ij}
is a matrix element of direct particle interactions,
while
B_{ij}
and
J_{ij}
are the matrix elements of exchange interactions, transverse
and longitudinal. Details on the parameters of the model are given in Refs.

[
219
,
220
]
. Similar Hamiltonians describe atoms in optical lattices

[
221
,
222
]
and some macromolecular systems
[
223
]
.

The order parameters characterize the mean atomic imbalance

s_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j}\;\langle\;S_{jf}^{z}\;\rangle\;.

(146)

The index
f=1
corresponds to the ferroelectric (ordered) phase, while the index
f=2
,
to the paraelectric (disordered) phase, so that

s_{1}\;>\;s_{2}\;,

(147)

and when the external strain is absent, then
s_{1}
remains finite, but the order parameter
of the disordered phase tends to zero,

s_{2}\;\rightarrow\;0\qquad(D_{j}\rightarrow 0)\;.

(148)

For equal lattice sites, one can set

\Omega_{j}\;=\;\Omega\;,\qquad D_{j}=D_{0}\;.

(149)

The value of
B_{ij}
is usually small and can be omitted. The behavior of the system
depends on the parameters

u\;\equiv\;\frac{U}{J}\;,\qquad\omega\;\equiv\;\frac{\Omega}{J}\;,\qquad h\;\equiv\;\frac{D_{0}}{J}\;.

(150)

As is seen, the Hamiltonian (
145
) is close by its structure to the Hamiltonian
(
141
). The overall behavior of the order parameters
s_{f}
is analogous to that of
the order parameters of ferromagnets with mesoscopic paramagnetic fluctuations.

6.3
Systems with mesoscopic density fluctuations

Microscopic density fluctuations are characterized by phonons. But there also may happen
mesoscopic density fluctuations so that the system represents an inhomogeneous two-density
fluctuating mixture. Systems with mesoscopic density fluctuations can model solids with
regions of disorder or, more likely, liquid or gaseous systems with inhomogeneous density.
As an example, let us consider a lattice-gas model

[
5
,
224
,
225
,
226
,
227
,
228
]
modified so that to
take into account mesoscopic density fluctuations

[
229
,
230
,
231
,
232
]
.

The renormalized effective Hamiltonian has the form
H_{eff}=H_{1}\bigoplus H_{2}
, where
the partial Hamiltonians are

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\int\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left[\;\hat{H}_{L}({\bf r})-\mu\;\right]\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\int\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{(}{\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(151)

here
\hat{H}_{L}({\bf r})
is a Hamiltonian containing external fields, for instance a
Hamiltonian of an optical lattice, and
\Phi({\bf r})
is an interaction potential. The
phases are distinguished by their density,

\rho_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{f}}{V_{f}}\;=\;\frac{1}{V}\int\langle\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;d{\bf r}\;,

(152)

with the number of atoms in phase
f
being

N_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\int\langle\;\psi_{f}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;d{\bf r}\;.

(153)

One phase is denser than the other,

\rho_{1}\;>\;\rho_{2}\;.

(154)

The lattice sites can be either occupied or free.

The field operators can be expanded over localized orbitals,

\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;=\;\sum_{nj}e_{jf}\;c_{nj}\;\varphi_{nj}({\bf r})\;,

(155)

where the variable
e_{jf}=0,1
, depending on whether the site is free or occupied,
and the index
j=1,2,\ldots,N_{L}
enumerates the lattice sites. Each lattice site can
host not more than one atom, which is expressed as the unipolarity condition

\sum_{n}c_{nj}^{\dagger}\;c_{nj}\;=\;1\qquad c_{mj}^{\dagger}\;c_{nj}^{\dagger}\;=\;0\;.

(156)

The atoms filling the lattice are assumed to be well localized, such that the terms
associated with the hopping of atoms from one lattice site to another are much smaller
than the interaction terms of localized atoms. Then Hamiltonian (
151
), in the
single-band approximation, reduces to the lattice model

H_{f}\;=\;-w_{f}\;\mu\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}e_{jf}+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{L}}\Phi_{ij}\;e_{if}\;e_{jf}\;.

(157)

The following calculations can either employ the variables
e_{fj}=0,1
or it can be
convenient to pass to the pseudospin variables
S_{fj}^{z}=\pm 1/2
through the canonical
transformation

e_{jf}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}+S_{jf}^{z}\;,\qquad S_{jf}^{z}\;=\;e_{jf}-\;\frac{1}{2}\;.

(158)

In the latter case, we come to the pseudospin Hamiltonian

H_{f}\;=\;\frac{1}{8}\;N_{L}\;\left(w_{f}^{2}\Phi-4w_{f}\;\mu\right)\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;\left(w_{f}^{2}\Phi-2w_{f}\;\mu\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}S_{jf}^{z}+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{L}}\Phi_{ij}\;S_{if}^{z}\;S_{jf}^{z}\;,

(159)

in which

\Phi\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{L}}\Phi_{ij}\;.

(160)

The densities of the phases can be represented as

\rho_{f}\;=\;\frac{N_{f}}{V_{f}}\;=\;\frac{N_{L}}{2V}\;(1+s_{f})\;,

(161)

where the mean pseudospins are

s_{f}\;\equiv\;2\;\langle\;S_{jf}^{z}\;\rangle\;.

(162)

Due to condition (
154
), we have the inequality

s_{1}\;>\;s_{2}\qquad(\rho_{1}>\rho_{2})\;.

(163)

Similarly to other spin models with an external field, in the mean-field approximation
the two-density mixed state with mesoscopic fluctuations can exist in a finite temperature
range between the lower and upper nucleation temperatures. The other condition is the low
filling factor
\nu
, such that

0\;<\;\nu\;\equiv\;\frac{N}{N_{L}}\;<\;\frac{1}{2}\;.

(164)

Hence not more than half of the lattice sites can be occupied. This is why such a
two-density fluctuating state corresponds rather to a gaseous substance. The lower
nucleation temperature becomes zero for
\nu<0.3
. The upper nucleation temperature

T_{nuc}^{*}
, where
w_{1}(T_{nuc}^{*})=0
, reads as

T_{nuc}^{*}\;=\;\frac{\nu\Phi}{(1-2\nu)\ln(1/\nu-1)}\;.

(165)

It is useful to note that, as for other spin models, the mean-field approximation is not
the best tool for describing spin-ordering effects in the disordered phase, such as
paramagnetic phase. For more correctly characterizing the disordered phase, it is necessary
to resort to more refined approximations taking into account short-range order, which,
however, makes the consideration much more complicated.

6.4
Mesoscopic structural fluctuations

In the vicinity of a phase transition between two crystallographic structures with close
properties there appear local structural fluctuations
[
233
,
234
]
.
Let us consider the mesoscopic fluctuations of one crystallographic structure inside
another structure. Following the general approach expounded above, the effective
renormalized Hamiltonian reads as
H=H_{1}\bigoplus H_{2}
, with
H_{f}
for
f=1
and

f=2
corresponding to two different structures
[
235
,
236
]
. This type
of fluctuations occurs in a variety of phase transition regions. Therefore we illustrate
the related method of description of such a typical situation with more detail.

Starting with the standard form of the partial Hamiltonians (
151
), where

H_{L}({\bf r})\;=\;-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}\;,

(166)

we expand the field operators over the well-localized Wannier functions
[
237
]
,

\psi_{f}({\bf r})\;=\;\sum_{j}c_{jf}\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{jf})\;,

(167)

keeping in mind the lowest band. The excited states will be considered through phonon
excitations over the ground state
[
238
,
239
,
240
]
. Since the
phase probabilities
w_{f}
enter the Hamiltonian in a nontrivial way, we explain the
derivation of the phonon Hamiltonian in sufficient details.

In what follows, in the majority of expressions, not to overload notations, we omit the
phase index
f
, keeping in mind that all quantities in the Hamiltonian
H_{f}
depend
on the type of the crystallographic structure, hence on the index
f
. Then Hamiltonian
(
151
) takes the form

H_{f}\;=\;-w_{f}\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}\;c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}+w_{f}\sum_{j}\left(\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2m}-\mu\right)\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{j}U_{jj}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{ij}\;c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{i}\;,

(168)

in which

J_{ij}\;=\;-\int w^{*}({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i})\;\left(-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}\right)\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}\qquad(i\neq j)\;,

U_{ij}\;=\;\int|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;|\;w({\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\;=\;\int w^{*}({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;(-\nabla^{2})\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;\;d{\bf r}\;.

(169)

There are two kinds of crystalline matter, being either a coherent periodic system,
consisting of not localized particles
[
241
,
242
]
or a system
of localized particles oscillating around their lattice sites. We consider a crystal
formed by localized particles, whose oscillations are described as self-consistent
phonons
[
38
,
243
]
.

We employ the no-double-occupancy constraint and no-hopping condition, according to
which

c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;=\;1\;,\qquad c_{j}\;c_{j}\;=\;0\;,\qquad c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;=\;\delta_{ij}\;.

(170)

The term containing the chemical potential can be omitted, since in the total Hamiltonian

H
it gives a constant term
w_{1}\mu+w_{2}\mu=\mu
. Then Hamiltonian (
168
)
simplifies to

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{j}\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{ij}\;.

(171)

The phase probabilities are defined as the minimizers of the thermodynamic potential,
while respecting the normalization condition
w_{1}+w_{2}=1
. This, with the notation

w_{1}\;\equiv\;w\;,\qquad w_{2}\;=\;1-w\;,

(172)

gives the equation

\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial w}\;=\;\left\langle\;\frac{\partial H}{\partial w}\;\right\rangle\;=\;\left\langle\;\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial w}\;\right\rangle+\left\langle\;\frac{\partial H_{2}}{\partial w}\;\right\rangle\;=\;0\;.

(173)

From here, it follows

w\;=\;\frac{2\Phi_{2}+K_{2}-K_{1}}{2(\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2})}\;,

(174)

where, restoring the dependence on the phase index
f
, we define the kinetic and
potential energies

K_{f}\;\equiv\;\left\langle\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\frac{{\bf p}_{j}^{2}}{2m}\;\right\rangle\;,\qquad\Phi_{f}\;\equiv\;\left\langle\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{ij}\;\right\rangle\;.

(175)

Phonon excitations are introduced in the standard way

[
244
,
245
,
246
]
, by treating the spatial coordinate
as an operator

{\bf r}_{j}\;=\;{\bf a}_{j}+{\bf u}_{j}

(176)

characterizing the deviation
{\bf u_{j}}
from the lattice site
{\bf a_{j}}
, so that

{\bf a}_{j}\;=\;\langle\;{\bf r}_{j}\;\rangle\;,\qquad\langle\;{\bf u}_{j}\;\rangle\;=\;0\;.

(177)

Often, it is convenient to use the relative variables

{\bf r}_{ij}\;\equiv\;{\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j}\;,\qquad{\bf a}_{ij}\;\equiv\;{\bf a}_{i}-{\bf a}_{j}\;,\qquad{\bf u}_{ij}\;\equiv\;{\bf u}_{i}-{\bf u}_{j}\;.

(178)

Recall that the effective particle interaction
U_{ij}
, defined in (
169
), depends
on the type of the crystallographic lattice through the Wannier functions associated with
the considered crystalline phase, hence depending on the phase index
f
,

U_{ij}\;=\;U_{f}({\bf r}_{ij})\;.

(179)

The potential energy is the sum

B_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{f}({\bf r}_{ij})\;.

(180)

The latter can be expanded in powers of the deviations from the lattice sites
u_{i}^{\alpha}

giving

B_{f}\;\simeq\;U_{f}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;,

(181)

where the notation is used:

U_{f}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{f}({\bf a}_{ij})\;,\qquad B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;\equiv\;\frac{\partial^{2}B_{f}}{\partial a_{i}^{\alpha}\partial a_{j}^{\beta}}\;.

(182)

Then Hamiltonian (
171
) becomes

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{j}\frac{{\bf p}_{j}^{2}}{2m}+w_{f}^{2}\;U_{f}N+\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{ij}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;.

(183)

Introducing the phonon operators
b_{ks}
by the transformations

{\bf u}_{j}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{ks}\frac{{\bf e}_{ks}}{\sqrt{m\omega_{ks}}}\;\left(b_{ks}+b_{-ks}^{\dagger}\right)\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,

{\bf p}_{j}\;=\;-\;\frac{i}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{ks}\sqrt{m\omega_{ks}}\;{\bf e}_{ks}\;\left(b_{ks}-b_{-ks}^{\dagger}\right)\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,

(184)

and defining the phonon frequency
\omega_{ks}
from the eigenproblem

w_{f}\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\;\sum_{\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;e_{ks}^{\beta}\;=\;\omega_{ks}^{2}\;e_{ks}^{\alpha}\;,

(185)

where
{\bf e}_{ks}
is a polarization vector, we come to the phonon Hamiltonian

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{ks}\;\left(b_{ks}^{\dagger}\;b_{ks}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+w_{f}^{2}\;U_{f}\;N\;.

(186)

To explicitly stress the dependence on the phase probability
w_{f}
, it is useful to define
the frequency

\varepsilon_{ks}^{2}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;e_{ks}^{\alpha}\;e_{ks}^{\beta}\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;,

(187)

such that

\omega_{ks}\;=\;\sqrt{w_{f}}\;\varepsilon_{ks}\;.

(188)

It is straightforward to find the mean kinetic energy

K_{f}\;=\;\frac{1}{4N}\sum_{ks}\omega_{ks}\;\coth\left(\frac{w_{f}\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)

(189)

and the interaction energy

\Phi_{f}\;=\;\langle\;B_{f}\;\rangle\;=\;U_{f}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ij}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;,

(190)

which are related by the equality

w_{f}\;\Phi_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\;U_{f}+K_{f}\;.

(191)

Then the kinetic energy can be written as

K_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ij}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;.

(192)

While for the phonon correlation function, we have

\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;=\;\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2N}\sum_{ks}\frac{e_{ks}^{\alpha}e_{ks}^{\beta}}{m\omega_{ks}}\;\coth\left(\frac{w_{f}\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)\;.

(193)

The phase probability (
174
) takes the form

w\;=\;\frac{2U_{2}+3(K_{2}-K_{1})}{2(U_{1}+U_{2})}\;.

(194)

The probability
w=w_{1}
shows the fraction of the system occupied by the crystallographic
phase
f=1
and, respectively, the fraction
w_{2}=1-w
, of the phase
f=2
. Note that
the same expression (
194
) can be obtained from the minimization of the free energy

F=F_{1}+F_{2}
with respect to the phase probability
w
, where

F_{f}\;=\;w_{f}^{2}\;U_{f}+\frac{T}{N}\sum_{ks}\ln\left[\;2\sinh\left(\frac{w_{f}\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)\;\right]\;.

(195)

6.5
Fluctuations in frustrated materials

Structural fluctuations, considered in the previous section, can occur around the point
of structural phase transition. At sufficiently low temperature below the transition
point the more stable structure prevails. However, there exist materials that do not
form a well defined crystalline structure even at very low temperatures because of the
frustration effect caused by competing interactions
[
247
]
. The spatial
structure of such frustrated materials is formed of a combination of two or more
coexisting structures and random regions. Frustrated materials are different from
glass that is defined as “a nonequilibrium, non-crystalline state of matter that appears
solid on a short time scale but continuously relaxes towards the liquid state”

[
248
]
. A frustrated material can possess a not completely ordered structure,
not relaxing to anything, even at its ground state
[
247
]
. The frustrated
material also differs from the liquid state, where the particles are uniformly randomly
distributed in space, although can contain quasi-crystalline cluster fluctuations

[
15
,
16
,
17
,
249
]
. There exists a wide variety of
frustrated materials, such as complex polymers
[
250
]
.

With the evolution of nano-fabrication, it is now possible to create frustrated materials
for realizing virtually any geometrical structures. This has recently opened a path
toward the deliberate design of novel materials with exotic states often not found in
natural substances
[
251
]
. In the present section, we consider a simple model
of a frustrated matter that is formed of coexisting crystalline and random structures,
where the coexistence is caused by competing interactions favoring different spatial
orders.

To describe the crystalline phase arising in a frustrated matter, we can use the theory
of the previous section, where
w_{1}\equiv w
is the probability of the crystalline
structure, while
w_{2}=1-w
now will be the probability of a random structure. In order
to stress the essence of the approach, we simplify the consideration by resorting to the
Debye approximation.

A very important quantity characterizing crystalline state is the phonon correlation
function (
191
) and the related mean-square deviation

r_{0}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\;\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}\;u_{i}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;,

(196)

for which we have

r_{0}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ks}\frac{1}{m\omega_{ks}}\;\coth\left(\frac{w\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)\;.

(197)

Recall that
\omega_{ks}=\sqrt{w}\varepsilon_{ks}
. The latter can be written as

\varepsilon_{ks}^{2}\;\equiv\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}D_{k}^{\alpha\beta}\;e_{ks}^{\alpha}\;e_{ks}^{\beta}\;,

(198)

with the dynamical matrix

D_{k}^{\alpha\beta}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}B_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;.

(199)

In the Debye approximation, one accepts the isotropic phonon spectrum defined as the
average

\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s}\varepsilon_{ks}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{\alpha}D_{k}^{\alpha\alpha}\;\equiv\;D_{k}\;,

(200)

and the dynamical matrix is taken in the long-wave limit
D_{k}\simeq Dk^{2}
, so that
from (
198
) one obtains the spectrum

\varepsilon_{k}\;=\;c_{0}\;k\qquad(0\leq k\leq k_{D})\;,

(201)

where

c_{0}\;=\;\sqrt{D}\;,\qquad k_{D}\;=\;(6\pi^{2}\rho_{1})^{1/3}

(202)

and
\rho_{1}=N_{1}/V_{1}
is the average density.

Then the Debye approximation for the mean-square deviation reads as

r_{0}^{2}\;=\;\frac{9w\rho_{1}}{2m\Theta_{D}\rho}\int_{0}^{1}x\;\coth\left(\frac{\Theta_{D}}{2T}\;x\right)\;dx\;,

(203)

in which the effective Debye temperature

\Theta_{D}\;\equiv\;w^{3/2}T_{D}

(204)

is renormalized by the presence of the random structure, as compared to the Debye
temperature

T_{D}\;\equiv\;c_{0}k_{D}

(205)

of the pure crystal without such structural fluctuations.

In stable crystals, the mean-square deviation is certainly smaller than the half of the
distance between the nearest neighbors,
r_{0}^{2}<a/2
, which is called the Lindemann
criterion of stability
[
252
]
. Sometimes, one connects this criterion
with the melting of crystal. However this is exactly a stability criterion that is not
directly connected with melting. The instability of crystals occurs at temperatures
essentially higher the melting temperature, as confirmed by numerous calculations

[
34
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
253
,
254
,
255
,
256
,
257
,
258
]
. Also, the Lindemann instability can exist in optical
lattices formed by laser beams, when the melting as such does not happen

[
259
,
260
]
. A detailed review of the literature on melting is
presented in Ref.
[
261
]
.

The free energy of the crystalline state, coexisting with the competing random structure,
reads as

F_{1}\;=\;w^{2}U_{1}+9T\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}\;\ln\left[\;2\sinh\left(\frac{w^{3/2}T_{D}}{2T}\;x\right)\;\right]\;dx\;.

(206)

For the random-structure phase, the grand Hamiltonian

H_{2}\;=\;w_{2}\int\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\left(-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}-\mu\right)\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{2}^{2}\int\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}

(207)

has to be complimented with an appropriate approximation. Typical particle interactions
usually contain nonintegrable hard cores precluding from the use of the Hartree or
Hartree-Fock approximations due to the nonintegrability of interaction potentials. This
problem can be cured by resorting to the Kirkwood approximation
[
194
]
that
is a correlated Hartree approximation

\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;=\;g({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\left\{\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\rangle+\right.

+\left.\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})-\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\rangle\;\right\}\;,

(208)

where
g({\bf r})
is a pair correlation function. Notice that averaging the relation
(
208
) yields the definition of the pair correlation function

g({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;=\;\frac{\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle}{\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\rangle}\;.

(209)

It has been shown
[
108
,
262
]
that, starting with the Kirkwood
approximation, it is possible to develop a self-consistent perturbation theory for Green
functions of any order. In this theory, the interaction potential always enters as the
regularized potential

\overline{\Phi}({\bf r})\;\equiv\;g({\bf r})\;\Phi({\bf r})

(210)

that is always integrable. A convenient way for defining the regularizing correlation
function
g(r)
is given in
[
263
]
.

The average density of the random phase

\rho_{2}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{2}}{V_{2}}\;=\;\frac{1}{V}\int\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle d{\bf r}

(211)

defines the chemical potential
\mu
. Keeping in mind its randomness, the density of
the random phase can be taken as uniform,

\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;=\;\rho_{2}\;.

(212)

In the Kirkwood approximation, the grand Hamiltonian (
207
) becomes

H_{2}\;=\;w_{2}\int\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left(-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+w_{2}\rho_{2}\Phi_{0}-\mu\right)\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}+E_{0}\;,

(213)

where

\Phi_{0}\;\equiv\;\int\overline{\Phi}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}

(214)

and

E_{0}\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\;w_{2}^{2}\;\frac{\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\;\Phi_{0}\;N\;.

(215)

Passing to the Fourier expansion for the field operators, we have

H_{2}\;=\;w_{2}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\;a_{k}^{\dagger}\;a_{k}+E_{0}\;,

(216)

with the spectrum

\omega_{k}\;=\;\frac{k^{2}}{2m}+w_{2}\rho_{2}\Phi_{0}-\mu\;.

(217)

The grand potential per particle

\Omega_{2}\;=\;-\frac{T}{N}\;\ln{\rm Tr}\;e^{-\beta H_{2}}

reduces to

\Omega_{2}\;=\;\pm T\sum_{k}\ln\left(1\mp e^{-\beta w_{2}\omega_{k}}\right)+E_{0}\;.

(218)

Assuming that
\exp{\beta\mu_{eff}}\ll 1
yields

\Omega_{2}\;=\;-\;\frac{T}{\rho}\;\left(\frac{mT}{2\pi w_{2}}\right)^{3/2}\;e^{\beta\mu_{eff}}+E_{0}\;,

(219)

where

\mu_{eff}\;\equiv\;w_{2}\;(\mu-w_{2}\rho_{2}\Phi_{0})\;.

(220)

The density of the random phase (
211
) takes the form

\rho_{2}\;=\;\left(\frac{mT}{2\pi w_{2}}\right)^{3/2}\;e^{\beta\mu_{eff}}\;,

(221)

which leads to

w_{2}\;\mu\;=\;w_{2}^{2}\;\rho_{2}\;\Phi_{0}+\frac{3}{2}\;T\;\ln\left(4\pi\;w_{2}\;\frac{E_{k}}{T}\right)\;,

(222)

with the notation for the characteristic kinetic energy

E_{k}\;\equiv\;\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{2m}\;\cong\;\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2ma^{2}}\;.

(223)

Thus the grand potential per particle for the random phase is

\Omega_{2}\;=\;-T\;\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho}+\frac{E_{0}}{N}\;.

(224)

To simplify the equations, we can keep in mind that the random and crystalline phases
differ in spatial structure, but do not differ much in the average densities, so that

\rho_{1}\approx\rho_{2}\approx\rho
. For the free energy of the random phase containing
clusters of crystalline fluctuations,
F_{2}=\Omega_{2}+w_{2}\mu
, we obtain

F_{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{2}^{2}\;\rho\;\Phi_{0}+\frac{3}{2}\;T\;\ln\left(4\pi\;w_{2}\;\frac{E_{k}}{T}\right)-T\;.

(225)

The total free energy of the system is
F=F_{1}+F_{2}
, with the phase probabilities

w=w_{1}
and
w_{2}=1-w
defined as the minimizers of the free energy
F
.

For the convenience of numerical calculations, let us introduce the following
dimensionless parameters: the relative cohesive energy

u\;\equiv\;\frac{U_{1}}{T_{D}}\;,

(226)

the ratio of the characteristic potential energy to the Debye temperature

g\;\equiv\;\frac{\rho\Phi_{0}}{2T_{D}}\;,

(227)

and the ratio of the characteristic kinetic energy to the Debye temperature

e_{K}\;\equiv\;\frac{E_{K}}{T_{D}}\;.

(228)

The dimensionless free energy of the system can be written in the form

f\;=\;f_{1}+f_{2}\;,

(229)

in which

f\;\equiv\;\frac{F}{T_{D}}\;,\qquad f_{1}\;\equiv\;\frac{F_{1}}{T_{D}}\;,\qquad f_{2}\;\equiv\;\frac{F_{2}}{T_{D}}\;.

(230)

Measuring temperature in units of the Debye temperature, we have

f_{1}\;=\;w^{2}\;u+9T\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}\;\ln\left[\;2\sinh\left(\frac{w^{3/2}}{2T}\;x\right)\;\right]\;dx\;,

f_{2}\;=\;(1-w)^{2}\;g+\frac{3}{2}\;T\;\ln\left[\;4\pi(1-w)\;\frac{e_{k}}{T}\;\right]-T\;.

(231)

The probability of the crystalline phase
w
is defined as a minimizer of the free energy:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}\;=\;0\;,\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial w^{2}}\;>\;0\;.

(232)

The free energy
f
of the coexisting frustrated system, combining the crystalline phase
and the random uniform phase, has to be compared with the free energies of the pure
crystalline solid,

f_{sol}\;=\;u+9T\int_{0}^{1}x^{2}\;\ln\left[\;2\sinh\left(\frac{x}{2T}\right)\;\right]\;dx\;,

(233)

and of pure random structure,

f_{ran}\;=\;g+\frac{3}{2}\;T\;\ln\left(4\pi\;\frac{e_{k}}{T}\right)-T\;.

(234)

The general behavior of concrete frustrated materials, modelled by the competing
crystalline and random structures, can be done by fixing the parameters
u
,
g
,
and
e_{K}
. These parameters for many ordinary solids can easily be found in the books
on solid-state physics
[
243
,
264
,
265
,
266
]
. However,
frustrated materials are less studied and their parameters are less known. In addition,
employing the advance of nano-fabrication, it is nowadays possible to form a variety
of novel frustrated materials with different parameters often not found in natural
substances
[
251
]
. The typical behavior of the free energies for the
considered frustrated system at low temperature below the Debye temperature
(T\ll 1)

is shown in Fig. 1. We keep in mind that the cohesive parameter
u
has to be negative,
characterizing the potential well for a particle, with the absolute value comparable
with the Debye temperature, while the relative kinetic parameter
e_{K}
is positive
and small, being of order
10^{-3}
. The frustration parameter
g
is usually positive

[
247
]
.

6.6
Superfluid fluctuations in solids

If in a solid there can appear regions of disorder, such as pores and dislocations, the
question arises whether these regions can house the embryos of liquid-like phase, and
if so, whether these liquid-like formations could exhibit the property of superfluidity
and the related Bose condensation. The possibility of arising superfluid properties in
solids with dislocations, e.g. in solid
4
He, has been recently widely discussed, as
can be inferred from the reviews
[
267
,
268
,
269
,
270
,
271
,
272
]
. However, no convincing experimental evidence for the existence
of superfluidity in the dislocations of solid
4
He has been presented. Although some
Monte Carlo calculations do show the occurrence of superfluidity in solid
4
He with
dislocations (see the above reviews), however it is not possible to rule out that the
signs of superfluidity are caused by finite-size and finite-time effects. The recent
rather accurate numerical calculations employing path-integral ground state method

[
273
]
provide compelling evidence for the absence of intrinsic superfluidity
in dislocation cores of hcp
4
He.

In order to find out whether liquid-like mesoscopic fluctuations in a solid with disorder
regions could exhibit superfluid properties, it is necessary to study an explicit model
of such a solid. A statistical model of a solid with mesoscopic liquid-like fluctuations
allowing for the appearance of superfluidity has been considered in Ref.
[
274
]
.

Let the field operators
\psi_{1}({\bf r})
correspond to solid phase and
\psi_{2}({\bf r})
,
to liquid-like phase. The density of the solid phase is periodic over the lattice vectors,

\langle\;\psi_{1}^{\dagger}({\bf r}+{\bf a})\;\psi_{1}({\bf r}+{\bf a})\;\rangle\;=\;\langle\;\psi_{1}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{1}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;,

(235)

while the liquid phase is uniform,

\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;=\;\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}(0)\;\psi_{2}(0)\;\rangle\;.

(236)

The liquid phase allows for the global gauge symmetry breaking,

\langle\;\psi_{2}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\;\neq\;0\qquad\left(\rho_{0}\equiv\frac{N_{0}}{V_{2}}\right)\;,

(237)

which is necessary for the occurrence of Bose condensate, with density
\rho_{0}
. Note that
the global gauge symmetry breaking is a necessary and sufficient condition for Bose-Einstein
condensation
[
112
,
115
,
275
,
276
]
. The latter is a sufficient
condition for superfluidity. The symmetry breaking is realized by the Bogolubov

[
172
,
173
,
174
]
shift

\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;=\;\eta({\bf r})+\psi_{un}({\bf r})\;,

(238)

in which

\eta({\bf r})\;=\;\langle\;\psi_{2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{\rho_{0}}

(239)

is the order parameter or condensate wave function and
\psi_{un}({\bf r})
is the field
operator of uncondensed particles. The fractions of condensed and uncondensed particles
are denoted as

n_{0}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{0}}{N_{2}}\;,\qquad n_{un}\;\equiv\;\frac{N_{un}}{N_{2}}\;,

(240)

where
N_{0}
is the number of condensed particles,
N_{un}
is the number of uncondensed
particles, and
N_{2}
is the number of particles in the liquid phase.

The gauge symmetry breaking leads to the nonzero anomalous density

\sigma({\bf r})\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{\rho}\;\langle\;\psi_{un}({\bf r})\;\psi_{un}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;=\;\sigma

(241)

that principally cannot be neglected as soon as the symmetry is broken. The anomalous
average, together with condensed particles, define the sound velocity
s
by the equation

s^{2}\;=\;4\pi\gamma\;(n_{0}+\sigma)\;.

(242)

The crystalline phase with liquid-like fluctuations is described as is explained in the
previous section. Keeping in mind the zero-temperature case, we get the energy

E_{1}\;=\;w_{1}^{2}\;U_{1}+\frac{9}{8}\;w_{1}^{3/2}\;T_{D}\qquad(T=0)\;,

(243)

with the cohesive or configurational energy

U_{1}\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{1}({\bf a}_{ij})\;.

The superfluid Bose-condensed phase is described by the self-consistent approach advanced
in Refs.
[
277
,
278
,
279
,
280
,
281
,
282
]

(see also
[
112
,
115
,
116
,
120
,
122
,
186
,
187
]
). For the energy at zero temperature, we have

\frac{E_{2}}{E_{K}}\;=\;\frac{16s^{5}}{15\pi^{2}}\;w_{2}^{7/2}+4\pi\gamma w_{2}^{2}\;\left(1+n_{un}^{2}-2n_{un}\;\sigma-\sigma^{2}\right)\;.

(244)

The phase probabilities are defined as the minimizers of the total system energy in the
presence of mesoscopic fluctuations,

E\;=\;E_{1}(w_{1})+E_{2}(w_{2})\;.

(245)

This energy has to be compared with the energy
E_{sol}(1)
of the pure solid phase and the
energy
E_{liq}(1)
of the pure liquid phase. At zero temperature, the most stable state of
the system is defined by the lowest energy. It is shown
[
274
]
that, in general,
there could exist crystals with superfluid fluctuations, arising inside dislocations,
provided materials with appropriate parameters would exist.

It is interesting to consider the particular case of hcp
4
He, whose characteristic
parameters have been widely studied

[
283
,
284
,
285
,
286
,
287
,
288
]
. Our
investigation
[
274
]
demonstrates that, in the frame of the considered model,
hcp
4
He cannot exhibit superfluid properties, in agreement with numerical studies

[
273
]
.

\psi_{sf}({\bf r})

. The order parameter for the
superconducting phase is the non-zero anomalous average

\langle\;\psi_{s1}({\bf r})\;\psi_{-s1}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;\not\equiv\;0\;,

(246)

while for the non-superconducting phase the anomalous average is zero,

\langle\;\psi_{s2}({\bf r})\;\psi_{-s2}({\bf r})\;\rangle\;\equiv\;0\;.

(247)

The field operators can be expanded over a complete set of functions,

\psi_{sf}({\bf r})\;=\;\sum_{k}c_{sf}({\bf k})\;\varphi_{k}({\bf r})

(248)

that are the plane waves for a uniform system,
{\bf k}
being momentum, or Bloch functions
for a crystalline lattice,
{\bf k}
being quasi-momentum.

In the momentum representation, the order parameters are the anomalous averages

\sigma_{f}\;\equiv\;\langle\;c_{-sf}(-{\bf k})\;c_{sf}({\bf k})\;\rangle\;,

(249)

satisfying the conditions

\sigma_{1}({\bf k})\;\not\equiv\;0\;,\qquad\sigma_{2}({\bf k})\;\equiv\;0\;.

(250)

It is possible to work in a restricted space where spin and momentum are explicitly conserved,
so that

c_{sf}^{\dagger}({\bf k})\;c_{zf}({\bf p})\;=\;\delta_{sz}\;\delta_{kp}\;c_{sf}^{\dagger}({\bf k})\;c_{sf}({\bf k})\;,

c_{sf}({\bf k})\;c_{zf}({\bf p})\;=\;\delta_{-sz}\;\delta_{-kp}\;c_{-sf}(-{\bf k})\;c_{sf}({\bf k})\;.

(251)

The system Hamiltonian is
H_{eff}=H_{1}\bigoplus H_{2}
, where

H_{f}\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{s}\int\psi_{sf}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left[\;\hat{K}_{f}({\bf r})-\mu\;\right]\;\psi_{sf}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;w_{f}^{2}\sum_{sz}\int\psi_{sf}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{zf}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;V_{f}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{zf}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{sf}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;.

(252)

Here
\hat{K}_{f}({\bf r})
is a kinetic term and
V_{f}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})
is an effective
potential of particle interactions.

Resorting to the Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov approximation, it is straightforward to diagonalize
the Hamiltonians
H_{f}
by means of the Bogolubov canonical transformation

c_{sf}({\bf k})\;=\;u_{f}({\bf k})\;a_{sf}({\bf k})+v_{f}({\bf k})\;a_{-sf}^{\dagger}({\bf k})\;,

(253)

in which the coefficient functions
u_{f}({\bf k})
and
v_{f}({\bf k})
are defined by the
diagonalization condition.

One more form of the order parameters is the gap

\Delta_{f}({\bf k})\;=\;w_{f}\sum_{p}J_{f}({\bf k},{\bf p})\;\sigma_{f}({\bf p})\;,

(254)

where
J_{f}({\bf k},{\bf p})
is a matrix element of the effective interaction potential.
Since the gap is connected with the anomalous average, for the superconducting and normal
phases we have

\Delta_{1}({\bf k})\;\not\equiv\;0\;,\qquad\Delta_{2}({\bf k})\;\equiv\;0\;.

(255)

Detailed explanations of calculations are given in Refs.

[
84
,
86
,
87
,
88
]
. For the isotropic gap, depending only
on the modulus of the momentum, at the Fermi surface, the gap
\Delta\equiv\Delta_{1}(k_{F})
,
where
k_{F}\approx(3\pi^{2}\rho_{e})^{1/3}
, and
\rho_{e}
is the charged particles density,
the gap equation is

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda}{\sqrt{(\Delta/\sqrt{w_{1}}\;\omega_{0})^{2}+x^{2}}}\;\tanh\left[\;\frac{w_{1}^{3/2}\;\sqrt{(\Delta/\sqrt{w_{1}}\;\omega_{0})^{2}+x^{2}}}{2T}\;\omega_{0}\;\right]\;dx\;=\;1\;,

(256)

where
\omega_{0}
is the characteristic phonon frequency and

\Lambda\;\equiv\;w_{1}\;N(0)\;\left[\;\frac{|\;\alpha\;|^{2}}{w_{1}\omega_{0}^{2}}-J_{C}(k_{F})\;\right]

(257)

is the effective coupling parameter. Here
N(0)
is the density of states at the Fermi
surface. The first term of the coupling parameter is due to electron-phonon coupling with
the parameter
\alpha
. The second term

J_{C}(k_{F})\;=\;\frac{\pi e_{0}^{2}}{k_{F}^{2}}\;\ln\;\left(1+4\;\frac{k_{F}^{2}}{\varkappa^{2}}\right)

(258)

is caused by Coulomb interactions, with
e_{0}
being the carrier charge and the screening
radius
\varkappa^{-1}
defined by

\varkappa^{2}\;=\;\frac{4}{a_{B}}\;\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\;\rho_{e}\right)^{1/3}\;.

Introducing the notation for the dimensionless electron-phonon coupling parameter

\lambda\;\equiv\;N(0)\;\frac{|\;\alpha\;|^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}

(259)

and for the Coulomb coupling

\mu^{*}\;\equiv\;N(0)\;J_{C}(k_{F})

(260)

allows us to write the effective coupling (
257
) as

\Lambda\;=\;\lambda-w_{1}\;\mu^{*}\;.

(261)

The equation for the critical temperature follows from (
256
) by setting zero gap, which
gives

(\lambda-w_{1}\;\mu^{*})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\;\tanh\left(\frac{w_{1}^{3/2}\omega_{0}}{2T_{c}}\;x\right)\;dx\;=\;1\;.

(262)

As is evident, the superconductivity can exist provided that
\lambda>w_{1}\mu^{*}
. Hence
superconductivity can occur in bad conductors for which
\lambda<\mu^{*}
, so that
superconductivity could not arise in a pure sample, but the appearance of mesoscopic phase
fluctuations reduces the influence of the Coulomb interaction and superconductivity becomes
possible.

The consideration can be extended to the case of anisotropic superconductors whose properties
depend on the direction of momentum
[
88
]
. In that case, the effective interaction
is expended over a basis characterizing the system anisotropy

J_{1}({\bf k},{\bf p})\;=\;\sum_{ij}J_{ij}\;\chi_{i}({\bf k})\;\chi_{j}^{*}({\bf p})\;.

(263)

The gap is also expanded over this basis,

\Delta({\bf k})\;=\;\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}\;\chi_{i}({\bf k})\;,

(264)

which reduces the gap equation to the system of uniform algebraic equations of the type

\Delta_{i}\;=\;\sum_{j}A_{ij}\;\Delta_{j}\;.

(265)

The system of uniform algebraic equations enjoys nontrivial solutions when

{\rm det}(\hat{1}-\hat{A})\;=\;0\;,

(266)

where
\hat{A}
is the matrix of elements
A_{ij}
.

The analysis shows that superconductors with mesoscopic fluctuations enjoy the following
properties distinguishing these superconductors from those without such fluctuations:

1.

Mesoscopic fluctuations in a superconductor can arise only when, in addition to effective
attracting electron-phonon interactions there exist repulsive Coulomb interactions.

2.

Mesoscopic fluctuations enable the appearance of superconductivity in a sample even if
it were impossible in the matter without such fluctuations, for instance in bad conductors.

3.

The critical temperature for a mixture of gap waves with different symmetry is higher
than the critical temperature related to any pure gap wave from this mixture, so that the
mixture of
s
-wave and
d
-wave superconductivity enjoys a higher temperature than any of
these waves separately.

4.

In bad conductors, the critical temperature as a function of the superconducting fraction
has the typical bell shape
[
88
]
.

7
Debye-Waller and Mössbauer factors

The appearance of mesoscopic fluctuations in the vicinity of phase transition points can
be noticed, e.g. by measuring sound velocity. In the presence of mesoscopic fluctuations
of two coexisting phases (see Secs. 6.4 and 6.5), sound velocity has the form

s\;=\;w_{1}^{3/2}\;c_{1}+w_{2}^{3/2}\;c_{2}\;,

(267)

where
c_{f}
is the sound velocity in a pure phase
f
. At the phase transition point, the
sound velocity experiences a noticeable decrease because of scattering on mesoscopic
fluctuations. This decrease can be characterized by the relative sagging

\delta_{s}\;=\;\frac{s-c_{f}}{c_{f}}\;.

(268)

If the sound velocity in pure phases does not vary much, so that
c_{1}\approx c_{2}
, then,
assuming that
w\approx 1/2
, we find
\delta_{s}\approx-0.29
.

As far as the existence of mesoscopic fluctuations influences the mean-square deviation
of particles, the appearance of these fluctuations can be revealed by measuring the
Mössbauer or Debye-Waller factors. Since mesoscopic fluctuations are the most
pronounced in the vicinity of phase transitions, where they lead to the mean-square
deviation increase, the Mössbauer and Debye-Waller factors should exhibit noticeable
sagging in the vicinity of transition points

[
73
,
204
,
235
,
236
,
289
,
290
,
291
]
.

The total Debye-Waller or Lamb-Mössbauer factor for a mixture of two phases is the sum

f_{M}\;=\;w_{1}\;f_{M1}+w_{2}\;f_{M2}\;.

(269)

The Debye-Waller and Lamb-Mössbauer factors for a phase
f
enjoy the similar forms

f_{Mf}\;=\;\exp\left(-\;\frac{1}{3}\;k_{0}^{2}\;r_{f}^{2}\right)\;.

(270)

Here
k_{0}
is the momentum of a gamma-quantum in the case of the Mössbauer effect and
the momentum of an X-ray quantum or a neutron momentum in the case of the Debye-Waller
factor. The quantity
r_{f}^{2}/3
is the mean-square deviation in the direction of the
X-ray quantum or gamma quantum, or neutron.

The mean-square deviation for a phase
f
in the Debye approximation is

r_{f}^{2}\;=\;\frac{9w_{f}\rho_{f}}{2m\Theta_{f}\rho}\int_{0}^{1}x\;\coth\left(\frac{\Theta_{f}}{2T}\right)\;x\;dx\;,

(271)

with the effective Debye temperature

\Theta_{f}\;\equiv\;w_{f}^{3/2}\;T_{Df}\;,

(272)

where
T_{Df}
is the Debye temperature of the pure phase
f
. At low and high temperatures,
we have

r_{f}^{2}\;\simeq\;\frac{9\rho_{f}}{4mT_{Df}\sqrt{w_{f}}}\qquad(T\ll T_{Df})\;,

r_{f}^{2}\;\simeq\;\frac{9\rho_{f}T}{m\rho T_{Df}^{2}w_{f}^{2}}\qquad(T\gg T_{Df})\;.

(273)

The value of the factor
f_{M}
essentially depends on phase probabilities, both explicitly
and through
f_{Mf}=f_{Mf}(w_{f})
. The existence of mesoscopic fluctuations around the
points of structural transitions can be characterized by the relative variation

\delta f_{M}\;\equiv\;\frac{f_{M}-f_{Mf}(1)}{f_{Mf}(1)}\;.

(274)

To better stress the role of mesoscopic fluctuations, let us consider the case, when,
in the absence of mesoscopic fluctuations, the factors
f_{Mf}(1)
do not change much,
so that
f_{M1}(1)\approx f_{M2}(1)
. And assume that, at the point of a phase
transition,
w\approx 1/2
. Then the presence of mesoscopic fluctuations results in
the sagging at the transition point of the factor
f_{M}
described by the variation in
the interval

f_{Mf}^{3}-1\;<\;\delta f_{M}\;<\;f_{Mf}^{0.414}-1\;.

(275)

For instance, if
f_{Mf}=0.9
, then
-0.27<\delta f_{M}<-0.043
. In many cases, the
transition temperature is higher then the Debye temperature,
T_{c}\gg T_{Df}
. In that
case the sagging is estimated as
\delta f_{M}\approx-0.27
. This kind of saggings of
Lamb-Mössbauer factors have been observed at several structural phase transitions

[
72
,
292
]
.

8
Conclusion

The problem of describing mesoscopic phase fluctuations in many-body systems is addressed.
A general approach is developed allowing for taking account of these fluctuations. Several
models of condensed-matter systems with mesoscopic fluctuations are considered.

The basic idea of describing mesoscopic fluctuations is as follows. Suppose a statistical
system is given, with microscopic states forming a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Generally,
this space contains various microscopic states corresponding to different thermodynamic
phases allowed for that system. The consideration of each phase requires an appropriate
mathematical description satisfying the constraints characterizing the considered phase.
This implies that not all microscopic states of the total Hilbert space are equally
important for describing the particular chosen phase, since there are typical states
characterizing different particular phases. In the simplest case, the definition of
observable quantities for a particular phase can be done by employing the method of
restricted trace selecting only those microscopic states that are typical of the studied
phase. More generally, one can introduce the weighted Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{f}
,
where the admissible microscopic states are weighted, with the higher weights ascribed
to the considered phase
f
. In practice, the weighting can be realized by imposing
appropriate constraints on the observable quantities. The direct sum of the weighted
Hilbert spaces,
\bigoplus_{f}\mathcal{H}_{f}
, characterizes all admissible phases of the
studied statistical system. All calculations for a particular phase
f
are always
accomplished in the related space
\mathcal{H}_{f}
. This is a kind of Hilbert space
localization
[
293
]
. Thus the space
\bigoplus_{f}\mathcal{H}_{f}
shows that
the system can be in one of the phases, either phase
f=1
or
f=2
, etc, but no
coexistence of phases is possible. The description of several coexisting phases requires
the use of the fibered Hilbert space
\bigotimes_{f}\mathcal{H}_{f}
. The definition of
pure thermodynamic phases often needs to consider symmetries corresponding to the phases
and the concept of symmetry breaking, which requires to resort to the thermodynamic limit.
Nevertheless, sufficiently large finite systems allow for the definition of asymptotic
symmetries
[
294
]
, which makes it straightforward to deal with finite systems.
The latter is important as far as mesoscopic fluctuations in finite systems can be even
more pronounced, for example being due to the boundary effects.

The theory of heterophase systems exhibiting mesoscopic phase fluctuations is illustrated
by several models including magnetic materials with paramagnetic fluctuations,
ferroelectrics with paraelectric fluctuations, systems with mesoscopic density
fluctuations, crystalline structures with structural fluctuations, frustrated materials
with coexisting crystalline and random structures, solids with superfluid fluctuations
inside the regions of disorder, and superconductors with mesoscopic normal-phase
fluctuations. The influence of mesoscopic fluctuations on the Debye-Waller and Mössbauer
factors is demonstrated.
