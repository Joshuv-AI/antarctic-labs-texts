Spontaneous regression of a broad-based protruded cervical disc: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Sadık Tokar, Mustafa Arslan, Mehmet Edip Akyol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Cervical disc herniations rarely spontaneously regress.Case Description: A 28-year-old female presented with myeloradiculopathy attributed to a C6-C7 cervical disc. She opted for conservative management and fully recovered neurologically 3 months later. The follow-up MR study at 3 months showed marked reduction of the disc herniation, whereas 6 months demonstrated near-complete regression.Conclusion: A 28-year-old female who originally presented with a C6-C7 disc herniation and myeloradiculopathy. Six months later, she experienced full spontaneous neurological recovery without surgery and the magnetic resonance-documented near-complete C6-C7 disc regression.

INTRODUCTION

Over 90% of patients experiencing acute cervical radiculopathy due to a cervical disc herniation (CDH) can achieve recovery without requiring surgery.[10] Here, a 28-year-old female presented with myeloradiculopathy attributed to a C6–C7 CDH; 6 months later, her neurological findings resolved, and the magnetic resonance (MR) scan documented near-completed disc regression.

CASE REPORT

A 28-year-old female patient presented with myeloradiculopathy (i.e., motor 4/5, hyperactive lower extremity reflexes, and C7 sensory loss to pin appreciation) attributed to an MR-documented C6-C7 disc herniation. Initially, she did not improve following the administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications. The MR showed ventral cord/root compression at the C6-C7 level with associated signal changes [Figures 1a and b]. The patient declined an anterior cervical discectomy and fusion; rather, she was treated conservatively with analgesics and muscle relaxants. Three months later, she noted only mild residual cervical discomfort, and her motor/sensory deficits had resolved; the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) also demonstrated a significant reduction in the size of the C6–7 disc [Figures 2a and b]. Six months later, she only had occasional neck pain without any accompanying symptoms/signs, and the MRI now revealed near-complete regression of the C6-C7 disc herniation [Figures 3a and b].

DISCUSSION

Spontaneous regression in CDH was initially documented by Krieger and Maniker in 1992,[4] and subsequently by Rahimizadeh et al.[6] (26 cases), Turk and Yaldiz[8] (14 cases), Gürkanlar et al.[2] (6 cases), Vinas et al.[9] (4 cases), and Sharma et al. (2021)[7], who published a comprehensive review including 75 previously reported cases along with their own case report [Table 1]. Spontaneous regression likely occurs due to dehydration and subsequent shrinkage, retraction, and an inflammatory response (i.e., due to macrophage infiltration and neovascularization) resulting in enzymatic degradation/phagocytosis of the extruded or sequestrated fragments.[1,3,5,7] Regression is more commonly observed in extruded or sequestrated CDHs; this case involved regression of a broad-based protrusion. The literature demonstrates a near equal ratio of males to females undergoing spontaneous cervical disc regression, most commonly involving the C5– C6 (51%), by the C6–C7 (36%), C4–C5 (10%), and C3– C4 (3%) levels.[7] Therefore, in selected patients with mild, non-progressive deficits who exhibit early clinical improvement, non-surgical management, as in this case, may be appropriate.

CONCLUSION

Spontaneous regression of CDH rarely occurs. Only a selected group of patients who demonstrate spontaneous neurological improvement in their myeloradicular syndromes with corroborative MR evidence of continued/progressive disc regression/resolution may become candidates for conservative management.
