LocalKMeans: Convergence of Lloyd’s Algorithm with Distributed Local Iterations
Harsh Vardhan, Heng Zhu, Avishek Ghosh, Arya Mazumdar

Abstract
In this paper, we analyze the classical K K -means alternating-minimization algorithm, also known as Lloyd’s algorithm (Lloyd, 1956), for a mixture of Gaussians in a data-distributed setting that incorporates local iteration steps. Assuming unlabeled data distributed across multiple machines, we propose an algorithm, LocalKMeans, that performs Lloyd’s algorithm in parallel in the machines by running its iterations on local data, synchronizing only every L L of such local steps. We characterize the cost of these local iterations against the non-distributed setting, and show that the price paid for the local steps is a higher required signal-to-noise ratio. While local iterations were theoretically studied in the past for gradient-based learning methods, the analysis of unsupervised learning methods is more involved owing to the presence of latent variables, e.g. cluster identities, than that of an iterative gradient-based algorithm. To obtain our results, we adapt a virtual iterate method to work with a non-convex, non-smooth objective function, in conjunction with a tight statistical analysis of Lloyd steps.

Introduction

The scale of data processing in modern applications has increased substantially over the last few decades. In response to this, distributed computing has become an integral component of machine learning and leveraging the power of local computing nodes has become an important direction of research. It is well known that in such a system, a major source of latency is the communication time between the compute nodes and the central server. Moreover, in framework such as Federated Learning
Konecnỳ et al. (2016)
, this communication cost is directly linked with the bandwidth usage of the participating compute nodes and hence resource constrained.

One natural way to reduce communication cost is via
local steps
, where the compute nodes (or
machines
) run several iterates of the local training algorithm before communicating to the central server. Indeed, when the training algorithm is stochastic gradient descent (SGD), the resulting algorithm, namely localSGD is immensely popular in distributed learning
Stich (2019)
;
Bao et al. (2024)
;
Gorbunov et al. (2021)
;
Haddadpour et al. (2019)
;
Woodworth et al. (2020)
.

The concept of local steps is not only pertinent to a particular learning algorithm (like SGD) or paradigm
(like supervised learning), and can be extended to any data-driven iterative algorithm where data is distributed. In this paper, we use local steps for unsupervised methods, and quantify the benefit of local steps. To the best of our knowledge, such an approach has not been studied theoretically.

Classically, for an unsupervised problem like clustering or mixture models, canonical algorithms like Alternating Minimization (AM) (see
Lloyd (1982)
;
Vattani (2009)
;
Yi et al. (2014)
;
Yi et al. (2016)
or its soft variant Expectation Maximization (EM) (see
Balakrishnan et al. (2017)
;
Daskalakis et al. (2017)
are employed. In particular, for the
K
-means clustering (where the task is to partition a given dataset into
K
clusters such that a squared-error objective is minimized), the popular AM algorithm (or Lloyd’s algorithm) is widely used. The algorithm alternatively estimates the
K
cluster centers and assigns data points based on its distance from the cluster centers. It has been shown in
Lu and Zhou (2016)
that Lloyd’s algorithm indeed converges when the dataset is a mixture of high-dimensional Gaussians, provided some technical conditions on separation and initialization are met.

In this work, we analyze the Lloyd’s algorithm in the distributed learning framework with local steps. Our algorithm, namely LocalKMeans, performs
L
local updates of Lloyd’s on compute nodes before communicating to the central server and aggregates the estimates. As we can see, LocalKMeans reduces the communication cost by a factor of
L
. We quantify the cost of the local steps and show theoretically (as well as experimentally) that we do not get a significant penalty in terms of both the clustering error and the number of iterations, while gaining significantly in communication cost.

Baselines:
We consider two standard baselines for our algorithm –

Proof Techniques:
To establish convergence of global misclustering
A^{(t)}
at every local step
t
for
L>1
, we apply the virtual iterate method used for analysis of local steps in supervised learning
Karimireddy et al. (2020)
;

;
Koloskova et al. (2020)
to the analysis of Lloyd’s algorithm for mixture of Gaussians
Lu and Zhou (2016)

This extension is not straight-forward, as virtual iterate in supervised learning requires each local (on each machine) objective function to be smooth, and at least convex, if we want convergence in the objective function. For KMeans, the local misclustering is a sum of indicator functions which is neither smooth nor convex, and requires good local initialization to show progress in a single Lloyd’s step. Additionally, Lloyd’s algorithm has no tunable parameters like step-size in gradient descent for supervised learning to control the error of local steps. Addressing all these issues requires a novel modification of the virtual iterate method. We carefully analyze iterations where aggregation occurs and does not occur, and obtain tight bounds on deviation between local and global quantities (models
\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}
and misclustering
A_{i}-A

Experiments:
We conducted extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets and compare with baselines of centralized Lloyd’s and Lloyd’s without aggregation. The misclustering error of LocalKMeans is much better than Lloyd’s without aggregation and closer to centralized Lloyd’s. \lvert\lvert z_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i,j}-\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}\geq\lvert\lvert-z_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i,j}-\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}\implies\langle\theta^{\star}+\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j},\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle\leq 0
, where
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j}=z_{i,j}\mathbf{w}_{i,j}
. Note that the datapoint
\mathbf{x}_{i,j}
uses the local model
\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
for local steps. However, when
L
divides
t
,
\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
is replaced by the aggregated global model
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
, and we obtain the same progress as single-step of centralized Lloyd’s as long as
A^{(t)}\leq A^{(0)}
. Note that this is automatically satisfied at
t=0
, which implies
A^{(1)}\leq\frac{1}{4}
for large
mn
and
r
. When
L
doesn’t divide
t
, we need to account for the difference
\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
, where
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}
is the virtual iterate. Since the misclustering
A^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbb{I}\{\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j}+\theta^{\star},\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle\leq 0\}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function, we can obtain the following bound (Eq (
12
)).

\displaystyle A^{(t+1)}\leq\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{I}\{\beta_{1}\lvert\lvert\theta^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq-\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j},\theta^{\star}\rangle\}+\frac{A^{(t)}}{4}+\Phi_{1}+\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{I}\{\beta_{2}\lvert\lvert\theta^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq-\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j},\theta^{\star}\rangle\}+\Phi_{2}+\frac{\Delta^{(t)}}{30}

The first
3
terms are similar to those for centralized Lloyd’s, with a different
\beta_{1}
and the last
3
terms are the price paid for local steps. We need
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
, for the first and fourth term to be
<\frac{1}{2}
. These are only possible if
A^{(t)}<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}-\delta_{2}
and
A_{i}^{(t)}-A^{(t)}\leq\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\log r}}{r},\forall i\in[m]
for a constant
\delta_{2}>0
. This is possible if
A^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{4}
, but note that it is not satisfied for
t=0
. Therefore, using this analysis for all steps would require a worse global initialization. The fifth term, is an upper bound on
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert\lvert\bar{\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}_{i}\rvert\rvert^{2}
, where
\bar{\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{i,j}
. The expectation of this term is
\leq\frac{d}{nr^{2}}
, which provides
\Phi_{2}
dependent on
n
and a dependence of
\exp(-n)
in the probability of error. The last term
\Delta^{(t)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}
is the deviation term commonly used in the analysis of virtual iterate
Karimireddy et al. (2020)
. Existing analyses
Karimireddy et al. (2020)
of

unroll it to the last aggregation step,
\tau=\lfloor t/L\rfloor\cdot L
, and use
\Delta^{(\tau)}=0
. However, this forces
\Delta^{(t)}
to grow exponentially with

. Existing analysis

set step size in gradient descent proportional to

to handle this. As we do not have any tunable parameters in our algorithm, we use the fact that

, and a weaker bound on
\Delta^{(t)}
in terms of
A^{(t)}
and
A^{(t-1)}
by unrolling it for only
1

A_{i}^{(t)}-A^{(t)}\leq\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\log r}}{2r}
, which uses an inductive argument by analyzing each local step on each machine, requiring large
n
.

(Special case of centralized Lloyd’s)

5
Conclusion

We have proposed a distributed variant of Lloyd’s algorithm, which incorporates local steps for communication efficiency. We use a novel modification of the virtual iterate method to show that theoretically, it requires the same initialization as centralized Lloyd’s, but, it’s final error is slightly worse.

In practice, its performance is similar to centralized Lloyd’s.
Important directions for future work include improving the final error for LocalKMeans, and extending it to clients that are heterogeneous. Note that
Lu and Zhou (2016)
provides the tightest analysis of Lloyd’s algorithm
Lu and Zhou (2016)
, however, a weaker analysis may be more amenable to local steps.

D.6.1
Base Case

First, we prove the base case for Lemma
D.8
at iteration

Bound on
G_{i}^{(1)}

To compute

, note that we can write a version of Lemma
D.10
at
t=0
for a single machine. Note that directly applying Lemma
D.10
for each machine

would not suffice, as we would require a union bound over all machines which would result in high probability terms of

\log m=\mathcal{O}(n)

obtaining high probability terms of the form
\exp(-n)
.

We use the same decomposition of
\nu_{kh,i}^{(t)}
used in the proof of Lemma
D.10
which is presented in
Lu and Zhou (2016)
. If
\Lambda^{(0)}\leq\frac{1-\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}
, then we have,

\displaystyle\nu_{kh,i}^{(1)}\leq\underset{I_{1}^{\prime}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j\in S_{k,i}^{\star}}\mathbb{I}\{\frac{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{4}\lvert\lvert\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rangle\}}}+\underset{I_{2}^{\prime}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j\in S_{k,i}^{\star}}\frac{16}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}\Gamma^{4}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Gamma_{h,i}^{(0)}-\Gamma_{k,i}^{(0)}\rangle^{2}}}

Note that the terms
I_{1}^{\prime}
and
I_{2}^{\prime}
resemble the corresponding terms
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
in proof of Lemma
D.10
.
For the term
I_{2}
, we use the same bound as Lemma
D.11
, but take a union bound over all machines. Therefore, with probability

, we have,

\displaystyle I_{2}^{\prime}\leq\frac{384(\nu_{k,i}^{\star}+d)}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}\Gamma^{2}}(\Lambda^{(0)})^{2}

Note that we could do this as
\Gamma_{k,i}^{(0)}=\Gamma_{k}^{(0)},\forall i\in[m],k\in[K]
as the iteration
t=0
is divisible by
L
, so the same model is used for all machines.

For the term
I_{1}^{\prime}
, as it is a sum of
\nu_{k,i}^{\star}

\Pr[\frac{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{4}\lvert\lvert\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rangle]
, by Hoeffding’s inequality, we have, with probability
1-m\exp(-\frac{\delta_{3}}{K^{2}}\nu_{k,i}^{\star})
,
\forall i\in[m]
, for some constant
\delta_{3}>0
.

\displaystyle I_{1}^{\prime}\leq\nu_{k,i}^{\star}\exp(-r_{K}^{2})+\frac{\delta_{3}}{K}\nu_{k,i}^{\star}

Further,
\Pr[\frac{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{4}\lvert\lvert\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rangle]\leq\exp(-r_{K}^{2})

\nu_{k,i}^{\star}\geq\beta m\nu_{k}^{\star}\geq\alpha\beta mn
. Since
\log m=\mathcal{O}(n)
,
\beta m=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\delta_{1}\leq 0.1
, the high probability bound is
\exp(-n)
.

Summing over
h\in[K],h\neq k
, and dividing by
\nu_{k,i}^{\star}
we obtain the false positive rate.

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{h\in[K],h\neq k}\nu_{kh,i}^{(1)}}{\nu_{k,i}^{\star}}\leq K\exp(-r_{K}^{2})+\delta_{3}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{384\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}r_{K}}\Lambda^{(0)}\right)^{2}

Now, choosing

Further, this implies

\displaystyle\max_{i\neq i^{\prime}\in[m],k\in[K]}\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert\leq\frac{\delta_{1}\Gamma}{2},\quad\forall t\geq 1

\displaystyle\lvert\nu_{k,i}^{(1)}-\nu_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(1)}\rvert\leq\lvert\nu_{kk,i^{\prime}}^{(1)}-\nu_{kk,i}^{(1)}\rvert+\sum_{h\in[K],h\neq k}\lvert\nu_{hk,i^{\prime}}^{(1)}-\nu_{hk,i}^{(1)}\rvert

\displaystyle\nu_{hk,i^{\prime}}^{(1)}-\nu_{hk,i}^{(1)}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{z}_{i^{\prime},j}^{(1)}=k,z_{i^{\prime},j}=h\}-\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{z}_{i,j}^{(1)}=k,z_{i,j}=h\}

Plugging this bound into the expression for Eq (

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-1}\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(1)}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(1)}\rvert\rvert\leq\frac{12KC}{\alpha\beta m}\lambda+\frac{2}{r_{K}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{12\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{\beta m}}

. Now, we prove that these inequalities are true for iteration
t+1
.
Note that the bounds on
G_{i}^{(t)}
and
\Lambda_{i}^{(t)}
are same as those for the
2
-cluster case, which allows us to show,

\displaystyle G_{i}^{(t+1)}\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t+1)}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}\rvert\rvert\leq\frac{\delta_{1}\Gamma}{2}

Bound on
G_{i}^{(t)}

To compute a bound on
G_{i}^{(t)}
, we compute
\nu_{kh,i}^{(t+1)}
by a decomposition for Lemma
D.11
. As long as
\Lambda^{(t)}\leq\frac{1-\epsilon^{\prime}-\delta_{2}}{2}
and
\Lambda_{i}^{(t)}\leq\Lambda^{(t)}+\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}
, we have,

\displaystyle\nu_{kh,i}^{(t+1)}\leq

\displaystyle\underset{I_{1}^{\prime\prime}}{\underbrace{\sum_{\in S_{k,i}^{\star}}I\{\frac{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{4}\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq 2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\theta_{h}-\theta_{k}\rangle\}}}+\underset{I_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j\in S_{k,i}^{\star}}\frac{16}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}\Gamma^{4}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Gamma_{h}^{(t)}-\Gamma_{k}^{(t)}\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underset{I_{3}^{\prime\prime}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j\in S_{k,i}^{\star}}\frac{4}{\delta_{2}^{4}\Gamma^{4}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Delta_{h,i}^{(t)}-\Delta_{k,i}^{(t)}\rangle^{2}}}

Note that
I_{1}^{\prime\prime},I_{2}^{\prime\prime}
and
I_{3}^{\prime\prime}
correspond to the terms
I_{1},I_{2}
and
I_{3}
respectively in the proof of Lemma
D.11
. We slightly modify the bounds on these inequalities to ensure that we obtain a high probability term of
\exp(-n)
.

Consider the term
I_{1}^{\prime\prime}
. By using Hoeffding’s inequality, for all machines and all pairs of clusters, with probability
1-\frac{mK(K-1)}{2}\exp(-2C^{2}n)
, we have for some constant
C>0

\displaystyle I_{1}^{\prime\prime}\leq\nu_{k,i}^{\star}\exp(-(\epsilon^{\prime}r_{K})^{2})+2Cn

For the term
I_{2}^{\prime\prime}
, we use the corresponding bound from proof of Lemma
D.11
, but on
n
points instead. Further, we take a union bound over all machines and pairs of clusters. Therefore, with probability
1-\frac{K(K-1)m}{2}\exp(-n)
, we have,

\displaystyle I_{2}^{\prime\prime}\leq\frac{384(\nu_{k,i}^{\star}+d)\sigma^{2}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}\Gamma^{2}}(\Lambda^{(t)})^{2}\leq\frac{768\alpha n\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}r_{K}^{2}}(\Lambda^{(t)})^{2}\leq\frac{16\alpha n\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}r_{K}^{2}}

We finally use the fact that
\Lambda^{(t)}\leq 0.2,\forall t\geq 1
.

For the term
I_{3}^{\prime\prime}
, we again use the induction hypothesis (Eq (
26
)).

\displaystyle I_{3}^{\prime\prime}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{192n\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}\max_{k\in[K],i\in[m]}\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{192n\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}\max_{k\in[K],i\in[m]}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[m],i^{\prime}\neq i}\frac{\nu_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}}{\nu_{k}^{(t)}}\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{48n\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}\delta_{1}^{2}}{\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}\leq\frac{n\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{2\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}

We we Cauchy-Schwartz inequality followed by plugging in the value of
\delta_{1}\leq 0.1
.

The true negative rate is the following,

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in[K],h\neq k}\frac{\nu_{kh,i}^{(t+1)}}{\nu_{k,i}^{\star}}\leq\frac{K(I_{1}^{\prime\prime}+I_{2}^{\prime\prime}+I_{3}^{\prime\prime})}{\nu_{k,i}^{\star}}

Since
\nu_{k,i}^{\star}\geq\alpha\beta mn
, we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in[K],h\neq k}\frac{\nu_{kh,i}^{(t+1)}}{\nu_{k,i}^{\star}}\leq\frac{K}{\beta m}\left(\exp(-(\epsilon^{\prime}r_{K})^{2})+2C+\frac{16\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}r_{K}^{2}}+\frac{\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{2\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}\right)

Note that for large
r_{K}
, constant
\beta m
and
K
, small
\frac{d}{n}
, we can choose constants
C,\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\delta_{2}
, since
\Lambda^{(t)}\leq 0.2
such that the RHS is
\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Further, for the false positive rate, we change the denominator from
\nu_{k,i}^{\star}
to
\nu_{k,i}^{(t)}
. By the above bound on the true negative rate,
\nu_{k,i}^{(t)}\geq\nu_{kk,i}^{(t)}\frac{1}{2}\nu_{k,i}^{\star}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha\beta mn
. Note that this only changes the numerator of the true negative rate by a factor of
2
. Again, the constants
C,\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\delta_{2}
can be chosen such that the false positive rate is also
\leq\frac{1}{2}
. This implies
G_{i}^{(t+1)}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Bound on
\Lambda_{i}^{(t)}

From the analysis in the base case, we only need to bound the following term
\forall h,k\in[K],i\neq i^{\prime}\in[m]
.

\displaystyle\lvert\nu_{hk,i}^{(t+1)}-\nu_{hk,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}\rvert

First, consider the case when
h\neq k
. Using the decomposition of indicator functions,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{\hat{z}_{i,j}^{(t+1)}=h,z_{i,j}=k\}\leq\mathbb{I}\{\hat{z}_{i,j}^{(t+1)}=h,z_{i,j}=k\}+\Xi_{i,i^{\prime},h,k,j},\quad\text{ where, }

\displaystyle\Xi_{i,i^{\prime},h,k,j}\leq\mathbb{I}\{\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq 2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rangle\}

We consider the LHS and RHS of the components inside the indicator function individually.
Consider the LHS

\displaystyle\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}\rvert\rvert^{2}\geq((\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}+\delta_{2}^{2})\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}\rvert\rvert^{2}

Note that to show this is true as long as
(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}+(\delta_{2})^{2}\leq\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{2}
by the induction step.

For the term on RHS, we decompose it as

\displaystyle 2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i}^{(t)}\rangle-2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t)}-\hat{\theta}_{h,i}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j}-\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}\rangle+2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j}-\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\Gamma_{k}^{(t)}-\Gamma_{h}^{(t)}\rangle+2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Delta_{k,i}^{(t)}-\Delta_{h,i}^{(t)}\rangle-2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\Delta_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}-\Delta_{h,i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rangle

Now, we split
\delta_{2}^{2}
into two parts one for each of the terms of
\Delta
and
(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}
into two parts one for the terms of
\Gamma
and the other for the terms of
\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}
. This gives a decomposition similar to the
2
-cluster case if we sum over all
j\in[n]
.

\displaystyle\lvert\nu_{hk,i}^{(t+1)}-\nu_{hk,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}\rvert\leq I_{4}+I_{5}+I_{6}+I_{7}

For the term
I_{4}
which resembles
I_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, we use Hoeffding’s to bound it for some constant
C
. Note that the noise variance is doubled now as the noise is
\mathbf{w}_{i,j}-\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j}
.

\displaystyle I_{4}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}\lvert\lvert\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq 2\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j}-\mathbf{w}_{i^{\prime},j},\theta_{k}-\theta_{h}\rangle\}\leq n\exp(-r_{K}^{2})+8Cn

For the term
I_{5}
, we can use the bound of
I_{2}^{\prime\prime}
with double the noise variance.

\displaystyle I_{5}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{4}{\delta_{2}^{4}\Gamma^{4}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Delta_{h,i}^{(t)}-\Delta_{k,i}^{(t)}\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{32\alpha n\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{(\epsilon^{\prime})^{4}r_{K}^{2}}

For the terms
I_{6}
and
I_{7}
which differ in only
i
and
i^{\prime}
, we use the bound of
I_{3}^{\prime\prime}
.

\displaystyle I_{6}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{4}{\delta_{2}^{4}\Gamma^{4}}\langle\mathbf{w}_{i,j},\Delta_{h,i}^{(t)}-\Delta_{k,i}^{(t)}\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{n\alpha\max\{\frac{d}{n},1\}}{2\delta_{2}^{4}r_{K}^{2}}

This gives us a bound on
\lvert\nu_{hk,i}^{(t+1)}-\nu_{hk,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}\rvert
. Note that for the bound of
\delta_{1}
, we need to divide by
\nu_{k,i}^{(t+1)}
and
\nu_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}
each of which is
\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha\beta mn
and multiply by
\frac{3K\lambda}{2}
. Each of the terms
I_{4}-I_{7}
can be made arbitrarily small for large
r_{K}
and
n
. If
\beta m,K,\lambda
are constants, we can choose
C
such that the bound on
\lvert\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k,i}^{(t+1)}-\hat{\theta}_{k,i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}\rvert\rvert\leq\frac{\delta_{1}\Gamma}{2}
.
This completes the proof.
