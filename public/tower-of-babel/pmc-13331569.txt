Feasibility and Safety of Distal Transradial Access in Patients With Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest Undergoing Emergency PCI: A Single‐Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Shiori Kawakami, Norimasa Taniguchi, Takeshi Yamada, Tetsuya Hata, Shunsuke Nakajima, Fumihiko Sano, Hideya Nakano, Mitusko Yokouchi, Aiko Ko, Akihiko Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDistal transradial access (dTRA) has gained attention for its lower risk of vascular complications compared to conventional transradial access (TRA). However, its feasibility and safety in patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) undergoing emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remain uncertain.AimTo evaluate the procedural success, safety, and clinical outcomes of dTRA in OHCA patients undergoing emergency PCI.MethodsThis retrospective single‐center study included 48 OHCA patients who underwent emergency PCI for presumed coronary etiology between January 2022 and December 2025. dTRA was the default access strategy unless contraindicated. Among them, dTRA was attempted in 44 patients. Patients in whom dTRA was successfully achieved were included in the main analysis. Clinical, procedural, and outcome data were comprehensively reviewed, including access‐site complications and neurological status.ResultsdTRA was successfully achieved in 43 of 44 attempted cases (97.7%). The median door‐to‐balloon time was 67 min. Mechanical circulatory support was used in 38 patients (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: 17; intra‐aortic balloon pumping: 29). Access‐site complications included one case of limb ischemia requiring amputation and three minor hematomas (EASY classification grade 3−5) with no major bleeding (BARC 3−5). The 30‐day mortality was 51.2% (22/43), and among the 21 survivors, 14 (66.7%) achieved a favorable neurological outcome (CPC 1−2).ConclusionsIn this OHCA cohort requiring emergency PCI, dTRA was feasible and safe in the majority of patients, including those receiving mechanical support. However, critical ischemic complications may still occur under unstable systemic conditions, warranting careful patient selection and vigilant post‐procedural monitoring.Keywords: distal radial artery, out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest

Introduction

Out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains one of the most critical and time‐sensitive emergencies in contemporary cardiology, associated with high mortality and neurological morbidity despite advances in resuscitation science and post‐resuscitation care [1, 2]. When the underlying cause is presumed to be acute coronary syndrome, emergent coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) are often required. In these scenarios, the selection of vascular access plays a pivotal role—not merely as a procedural step, but as a factor that may influence clinical outcomes.

Among available access routes, the transradial approach (TRA) may become the first‐line choice for patients with presumed myocardial infarction even in the setting of OHCA, since it is recommended in PCI for ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [3]. Notably, compared to transfemoral access, TRA is associated with lower access‐site bleeding, fewer vascular complications, shorter hospital stays, and improved overall outcomes. These benefits have been demonstrated in multiple randomized controlled trials and are reflected in current European and American guidelines recommending TRA as the preferred approach for primary PCI in STEMI patients [4, 5]. Therefore, in clinical practice, this strategy is often extrapolated to patients with OHCA undergoing emergency PCI, especially when coronary artery disease is strongly suspected. However, it is important to note that most of these landmark trials excluded patients with OHCA, thereby limiting the generalizability of their findings to this high‐risk population. As such, while TRA is widely accepted for hemodynamically stable STEMI patients, its safety and efficacy in OHCA cases remain less well established—particularly in the setting of ongoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation or circulatory collapse, where transfemoral access has historically been favored.

Amid ongoing uncertainty regarding the applicability of conventional transradial access (TRA) in patients with OHCA, a novel vascular access technique—distal TRA (dTRA)—has been introduced [6]. This approach preserves the proximal segment of the radial artery, potentially reducing the risk of radial artery occlusion (RAO), while offering additional benefits such as reduced bleeding, improved patient comfort, and earlier mobilization [6]. These features are particularly advantageous in the acute STEMI setting, where bleeding control and vascular integrity are critically important.

Despite its theoretical advantages, dTRA is technically more demanding, with a higher incidence of puncture failure, even in experienced hands. This challenge raises concerns regarding potential delays in reperfusion—an especially critical factor in time‐sensitive conditions such as OHCA due to acute coronary syndromes. While the safety and feasibility of dTRA have been increasingly reported in routine STEMI care, its utility in OHCA patients undergoing emergency PCI remains underexplored, as is also the case with conventional radial access in this high‐risk population.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective single‐center study to evaluate the procedural feasibility, safety profile, and short‐term clinical outcomes of dTRA in patients with Utstein‐defined OHCA undergoing emergency PCI for presumed coronary causes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, single‐center study included consecutive patients who experienced out‐of‐hospital cardiopulmonary arrest and were admitted to our institution between January 2022 and December 2025. Among 799 patients who met the Utstein criteria for OHCA, 48 underwent emergency PCI for presumed acute myocardial infarction. The Utstein criteria refer to a standardized international template for defining and reporting OHCA, including witnessed or unwitnessed cardiac arrest of presumed cardiac origin, with attempted resuscitation by emergency medical services (EMS). This definition ensures consistency and comparability across studies.

PCI Procedure

Distal radial artery access (dTRA) was the default strategy and was attempted in all eligible cases, unless contraindicated (e.g., patients with existing RAO or on chronic renal failure). In cases where distal radial access was deemed unsuitable or failed, alternative access routes—including conventional radial, femoral, or brachial approaches—were selected at the discretion of the operator based on the patient's clinical condition. Events of crossover from the distal radial to other access sites were recorded. Culprit lesions were identified based on angiographic appearance, clinical context, and treated with balloon angioplasty and/or stent implantation at the operator's discretion. Use of mechanical circulatory support (e.g., intra‐aortic balloon pumping [IABP] or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO]) was determined based on the patient's hemodynamic status.

Ultrasound‐Guided Technique for dTRA

In all cases, the distal radial puncture was performed under real‐time ultrasound guidance to ensure precise cannulation, particularly in the context of OHCA, where hypotension, peripheral vasoconstriction, and diminished pulse volume are common. Patients were positioned with the wrist slightly extended and the hand in a neutral or thumb‐up position to expose the anatomical snuffbox. After local infiltration with lidocaine or saline, the dTRA was punctured using a 20‐gauge Surflo I.V. catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). A LOGIQ e ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) equipped with a high‐frequency L10‐22‐RS linear probe (22 MHz) was used throughout the procedure.

Successful access was confirmed by arterial blood flashback, followed by advancement of a 0.018‐in. guidewire and insertion of a 6 Fr Glidesheath Slender (Terumo Corporation, Japan). Post‐procedural ultrasound was selectively performed to assess for access‐site complications such as hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, or RAO. The distal radial artery diameter and puncture time were not routinely recorded because ultrasound was primarily used to facilitate safe vascular access in the emergency setting rather than for quantitative vessel assessment.

Access‐Site Hemostasis Protocol

After the procedure was completed, the sheath was retrieved partially with a STEPTY (NICHIBAN Co, Japan), developed for stopping the flow of blood using pressure immobilization with 6 mm thick pad at the central portion, then applied at the site. Then the operators pressed the pad lightly with their thumb and took out the catheter, wrapped an elastic bandage around the hand for 3–5 h in PCI. The compression site was checked after 2–5 h of hemostasis by nurses. If the routine check‐up revealed no bleeding or hematoma, compression was released. However, if bleeding at the puncture site or swelling with a minor hematoma was detected, an additional 30 min to 1 h compression was maintained.

Neurological Outcome Assessment

Neurological outcomes at 30 days were assessed using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale. The CPC scale classifies neurological status into five categories: CPC 1 (good cerebral performance), CPC 2 (moderate disability), CPC 3 (severe disability), CPC 4 (coma or vegetative state), and CPC 5 (brain death). A favorable neurological outcome was defined as CPC 1 or 2, indicating independence in daily living. Neurological status was determined through chart review and clinical examination findings during follow‐up in the cardiology or intensive care unit, and when available, corroborated with data from rehabilitation or neurology services.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, procedural data, and outcomes. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as means with standard deviations, whereas non‐normally distributed variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. No formal hypothesis testing was conducted due to the observational nature and limited sample size of the study. All analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R designed for medical statistics.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of our hospital. Given the retrospective and observational design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. In accordance with institutional policy, a public notice was posted in the hospital informing patients that anonymized data from medical records may be used for research purposes. Patients were given the opportunity to opt out. All patient data were fully anonymized before analysis to ensure confidentiality and privacy protection.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between January 2022 and December 2025, a total of 799 patients with OHCA were admitted to our institution. Among them, 48 patients (6.0%) underwent emergency PCI due to presumed acute coronary syndrome. The mean age of the cohort was 68.8 ± 12.5 years, and 14 patients (29.2%) were female. dTRA was initially attempted in 44 patients. Of these, 43 successfully underwent PCI via dTRA, while one case required crossover to brachial access due to puncture failure. The remaining patients underwent PCI via conventional radial access in 1 patient (2.1%) and femoral access in 3 patients (6.3%) (Table 1).

Procedural Characteristics

All dTRA procedures were performed under ultrasound guidance (n = 43, 100%). Right radial puncture was achieved in 41 patients (95.4%). Heparin was administered at a dose ranging from 7000 to 10,000 units based on the operator's discretion, with a median dose of 10,000 [8000, 12,000] units. Regarding catheter selection, a 6 French guiding system was used in all patients, with either a conventional sheath (n = 25, 58.1%) or a sheathless approach (n = 18, 41.9%) (Table 3).

Clinical Outcomes

Of the 48 patients, one (2.3%) experienced forearm RAO with subsequent digital ischemia. Three patients developed forearm hematomas classified as Grade 1 or 2 according to the EASY classification. Importantly, no major bleeding events (BARC type 3–5) were observed. Thirty‐day mortality was 51.2% (22/43), reflecting the severity of underlying cardiac and systemic conditions in this cohort. Among the 43 patients, 14 (32.6%) achieved a favorable neurological outcome, defined as a CPC score of 1 or 2. Access‐site complications were rare; however, one patient developed critical limb ischemia requiring amputation, despite successful distal radial access and adequate hemostasis.

These findings suggest that distal radial access is both feasible and safe in most OHCA patients undergoing emergency PCI. Nonetheless, access‐related complications, although infrequent, can occur, particularly in the setting of systemic inflammation and hemodynamic instability (Table 3).

Discussion

In this single‐center retrospective study, we evaluated the feasibility and safety of dTRA in patients with OHCA undergoing emergent PCI. Our findings demonstrate a high procedural success rate (97.7%) with a low crossover rate and a favorable safety profile, even in a hemodynamically compromised population. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically assess the use of dTRA in the OHCA setting—a population largely excluded from prior dTRA investigation.

The distal radial approach, first introduced by Kiemeneij in 2017, has gained attention as a potentially less invasive alternative to conventional TRA [6]. Early reports indicated advantages such as lower RAO rates, shorter hemostasis duration, and improved patient comfort. In Kiemeneij's initial series, which included 6 STEMI and 17 NSTEMI patients, dTRA was feasible and safe, with no major bleeding or forearm RAO reported. Since then, several observational studies and randomized trials have confirmed these benefits, particularly in elective PCI settings [7, 8, 9]. Furthermore, a recent RCT reported a RAO rate of only 0.3% in patients treated with dTRA, along with reduced bleeding events and hemostasis times [10]. While dTRA has shown promise in elective and STEMI settings, its role in severely compromised patients—particularly those with OHCA—remains largely unexplored.

This knowledge gap mirrors the broader pattern observed with conventional TRA, where most randomized trials have excluded OHCA populations, despite growing real‐world interest in applying radial access in these high‐risk patients. Notably, a recent observational study reported that the radial approach was associated with significantly better outcomes with respect to its primary endpoint (all cause of mortality or bleeding of any type or stent thrombosis) than the femoral approach in OHCA patients [11]. These findings strongly support the safety and efficacy of radial access even in severely compromised patients, including those with OHCA, and may indicate that dTRA could serve as a viable alternative access route for PCI even in this population.

Nonetheless, dTRA presents further technical challenges—particularly in STEMI or OHCA cases—due to the smaller vessel caliber, absence of pulsation in shock states, and limited access time. Kiemeneij's initial study observed a puncture failure rate of 11.4% [6]. Further analysis suggested that a minimum of 200 cases may be needed to overcome the learning curve and achieve a consistent success rate above 94% [12]. Operator expertise, therefore, plays a critical role in procedural success and complication avoidance [13]. Ultrasound‐guided puncture is also considered to play a crucial role as a valuable adjunct to increase first‐pass success and minimize complications in complex patients, including those with cardiac arrest [14].

In our current cohort, puncture success was achieved in 97.7% of patients—an outcome that is highly favorable compared to prior STEMI dTRA studies. Importantly, ultrasound was employed in all cases, which may have contributed to this high success rate. No major hemorrhagic complications occurred, and one case of finger ischemia was observed. The latter event appeared to be associated with profound hemodynamic deterioration and systemic inflammation, rather than access‐related factors alone. Although rare, such ischemic complications highlight the need for vigilance, particularly in patients with shock‐induced vasoconstriction or multiple vasopressor use. This is consistent with prior observations that systemic pathophysiology—rather than puncture technique—may underlie such adverse events in extreme cases [15].

Overall, our findings support the procedural feasibility and early safety of dTRA in OHCA patients. However, the approach should be reserved for experienced operators and centers with established ultrasound‐guided access protocols. Additional large‐scale prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify patient selection, access technique, and risk mitigation strategies in this unique high‐risk population.

Conclusion

In this single‐center retrospective analysis of OHCA patients undergoing primary PCI, the dTRA was shown to be technically feasible and safe in most cases, with low crossover rates and acceptable access‐site complication rates. However, patient‐specific factors such as hemodynamic instability and systemic inflammation may still predispose to ischemic events at the puncture site. These findings underscore the importance of individualized vascular access strategies and close post‐procedural monitoring. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate the safety and long‐term efficacy of dTRA in this critically ill population.
