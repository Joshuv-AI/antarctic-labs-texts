Frieze patterns and combinatorics of curve singularities
Eleonore Faber, Bernd Schober

Abstract
. We study the connection between Conway–Coxeter frieze patterns and the data of the minimal resolution of a complex curve singularity: using Popescu-Pampu’s notion of the lotus of a singularity, we describe a bijection between the dual resolution graphs of Newton non-degenerate plane curve singularities and Conway–Coxeter friezes. We use representation theoretic reduction methods to interpret some of the entries of the frieze coming from the partial resolutions of the corresponding curve singularity. Finally, we translate the notion of mutation, coming from cluster combinatorics, to resolutions of plane complex curves.

On the other hand,

There are several other singularity invariants encoding data of the resolution, i.e., the Enriques diagram and the Eggers–Wall tree. García Barroso, González Pérez and Popescu-Pampu introduced in
[
23
]
the notion of a lotus of a curve singularity, which is a certain simplicial complex, with the purpose of being able to read off all of these invariants. That notion evolved from a previous notion of lotus introduced by Popescu-Pampu in
[
41
]
, in order to have a common geometric interpretation of Enriques diagrams and dual graphs associated to constellations of infinitely near points of a smooth point of a surface. A lotus can also be defined abstractly and in the present paper we will be interested in

C

3.15

In particular, the dual resolution graph is part of the boundary of the lotus of the curve singularity and the negative of the weights of the vertices are given as the number of triangles in the lotus incident to the given vertex.

This is the surprising first connection to friezes, since the quiddity sequence of a Conway–Coxeter frieze is determined in the same way! Now one is inclined to ask if there are more connections between friezes and resolutions of curve singularities: the present paper first makes the correspondence between resolutions and friezes precise and then investigates whether one can see other cluster theoretic phenomena in resolutions, such as mutation, and in particular whether the other entries of the frieze have an interpretation in terms of singularity invariants.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section

3.18
). Our main Thm.

Definition 2.7

A

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&b_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
c_{1}&y_{2}&b_{2}&0&\cdots&\cdots\\
0&c_{2}&y_{3}&b_{3}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
0&\cdots&0&c_{n-2}&y_{n-1}&b_{n-1}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&c_{n-1}&y_{n}\end{pmatrix}\ .

for all

Example 2.8

Lemma 2.13
.

For
\frac{n}{q}=\llbracket b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}\rrbracket
with
\gcd(n,q)=1
and
q<n
the following holds:

\frac{n}{q}=\frac{P_{r}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})}{P_{r-1}(b_{2},\ldots,b_{r})}\ .

The lemma follows from an induction on
r
using that
\llbracket b_{1},\ldots,b_{r},b_{r+1}\rrbracket=b_{1}-\frac{1}{\llbracket b_{2},\ldots,b_{r+1}\rrbracket}

and that the recursion
P_{n}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=y_{n}P_{n-1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})-P_{n-2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-2})

[
37
, Number 547]
holds.

Using the mentioned recursion, one can deduce that

P_{n}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and
P_{n-1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})
have no common divisor, for
n\geq 1
, via an induction on
n
.
This together with (
2.2.3
) and
Lemma
2.13
implies:

.

,

, and

3.
Resolution of singularities and lotuses

Here we recall basic notions about complex plane curve singularities and their resolutions of singularities. In particular, we will be interested in the
lotus
of a plane curve singularity. We follow
[
23
]
, where more details and references about this material can be found.

Let
S
be a smooth complex analytic surface, and consider the germ
(S,s)
at the point
s\in S
. A
curve
C
in a complex surface

S
is an effective Cartier divisor of
S
, i.e., a complex subspace of
S
that is locally at
s
defined by the vanishing of a non-zero holomorphic function. Choosing a holomorphic coordinate system
(x,y)
at
s
, we have
\mathcal{O}_{S,s}\cong\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
the ring of convergent power series with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}=(x,y)
. If
C
passes through
s\in S
, the germ
(C,s)
is thus locally defined by
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
and
\mathcal{O}_{C,s}\cong\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}/(f)
.
Since
(S,s)
is locally isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, we call
C
a
plane curve singularity
. Locally at
s
the curve
C
is given as
V(f)=\{f(x,y)=0\}
. Recall that in this setting the singular locus of
(C,s)
is defined by the vanishing locus of the Jacobian ideal
J_{C}:=(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\frac{\partial f}{\partial y})
, see e.g.
[
15
]
. In the following we will only consider reduced curve singularities, that is,
C
is a reduced complex analytic space, so that locally at
s
the convergent power series
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
is reduced.

Note that one can also consider
f
as a formal power series (as done in
[
23
]
), i.e., define a plane curve singularity in
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{S,s}\cong\mathbb{C}\llbracket x,y\rrbracket
.

Let
(C,s)
and
(D,s)
be two curve singularities in
(S,s)
defined by functions
f,g\in\mathcal{O}_{S,s}
respectively. The
intersection number
of
C
and
D
at
s
is denoted by
C\cdot D
and defined by

C\cdot D:=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{O}_{S,s}/(f,g))\ .

More generally, the intersection number of two divisors
C
and
D
on any smooth complex surface may be calculated whenever at least one of them has compact support.
Moreover, the
self-intersection number

C\cdot C
or
C^{2}
at
s
can also be defined, see
[
32
, Thm. 2.3]
.
Note that the self-intersection number may be negative.

3.1.
Blowups and resolution graphs

In the following we will define the resolution graph of a plane curve singularity (sometimes called weighted dual graph). This material is well-known and covered in the literature, see e.g.,
[
23
, Section 2.4]
,
[
17
, Chapter 5]
,
[
53
, Section 3.6]
. We will only introduce the notions needed without any proofs.

First recall the
blowup of a point
s
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
: choose coordinates
(x,y)
at
s
(so that
s
can be thought of as the origin) and denote by
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
the projective line with coordinates
(X:Y)
(lines through
s
). Define
\Bl_{s}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
as the set of points in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying the equation

(3.1.1)

xY-yX=0\ .

The projection of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
onto
\mathbb{C}^{2}
yields the
blowup map

\pi:\Bl_{s}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Any point
s^{\prime}=(\alpha,\beta)\neq s=(0,0)
determines a unique
(X:Y)=(\alpha:\beta)
and thus
\pi^{-1}(s^{\prime})
is a point, whereas
\pi^{-1}(s)=:E
is a curve isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
, called the
exceptional divisor
of the blowup. The same construction can be carried out for a point
s
on a smooth complex surface
S
(introducing local coordinates
(x,y)
in a neighbourhood of
s
). One can check, see e.g.
[
23
, Section 2.4]
,
[
53
, Section 3.2]
, that this yields a well-defined blowup morphism that is independent of the chosen coordinates, denoted by
\pi:\widetilde{S}\xrightarrow{}S
, where
\widetilde{S}
is the blown-up surface.
Since blowups are isomorphisms away from the corresponding centers,
it makes sense to choose as the center of a blowup a finite set of points
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{\tau}\}
.

For a complex surface
S
and a finite set of points
Z:=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{\tau}\}\subseteq S
a
sequence of blowups with centers lying above
Z
is defined as a proper, birational morphism
\pi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
factoring as

\pi=\varphi_{\ell}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1},

for some
{\ell}>0
, where

(1)

\varphi_{1}\colon S^{(1)}:=\Bl_{Z}(S)\to S
is the blowup with center the given points
Z
,

(2)

for
i>0
,

\varphi_{i+1}\colon S^{(i+1)}\to S^{(i)}
is the blowup of the complex surface
S^{(i)}
with center a finite set of points
Z^{(i)}\subseteq E^{(i)}
,
where
E^{(i)}:=\pi_{i}^{-1}(Z)\subset S^{(i)}
is called the
exceptional locus
of
\pi_{i}:=\varphi_{i}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{1}
,
and

(3)

\widetilde{S}:=S^{({\ell})}
.

Notice that the restriction
\pi_{i}|_{S^{(i)}\backslash E^{(i)}}\colon S^{(i)}\backslash E^{(i)}\to S\backslash Z
is an analytic isomorphism, and
S^{(i)}
is smooth for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
.

Since we are working with germs, we always assume that
Z=\{s\}
is a single point. If
C\subseteq S
is a curve passing through
s
, then its preimage
\pi_{i}^{-1}(C)
is called the
total transform
of
C
in
S^{(i)}
and the closure of
\pi_{i}^{-1}(C)\backslash E^{(i)}
in
S^{(i)}
is called its
strict transform
.

Let
C
be a curve in a smooth complex surface
S
. Then an
embedded resolution of singularities
of
C
is a sequence of blowups

\pi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
as defined above, such that

(1)

\widetilde{S}
is smooth,

(2)

the total transform of
C
is a normal crossing divisor, and

(3)

the strict transform of
C
is smooth.

One can show that there always exists an embedded resolution of singularities by a sequence of blowups of points, see e.g.
[
17
, Thm. 5.4.2]
,
[
53
, Thm. 3.4.4]
. Moreover, this resolution can be chosen
minimal
, that is, any other embedded resolution of
C
factors through it. For a constructive proof see
[
53
, p. 48f]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
C
be a curve in a smooth complex surface
S
and let
\pi\colon\widetilde{S}\xrightarrow{}S
be an embedded resolution of singularities of
C
. Its
(weighted dual) resolution graph
is a simple finite connected graph whose vertices are labeled by the irreducible components of the exceptional locus of
\pi
and two vertices are connected by an edge if their associated curves intersect in
\widetilde{S}
.
The weight of each vertex corresponding to a component
E_{j}
of the exceptional locus is the self-intersection number
E_{j}^{2}
on
\widetilde{S}
.

Sometimes the components corresponding to the strict transform of
C
in
\widetilde{S}
are taken into account as additional vertices, in which case they are drawn with an arrowhead without a weight.

When
\pi:\widetilde{S}\xrightarrow{}S
is the minimal embedded resolution of singularities of
C
, then we will write
\Gamma(C)
for its weighted dual resolution graph.

Example 3.2
.

The cusp in
S=\mathbb{C}^{2}
is defined by
f=y^{2}-x^{3}
.
One can show that a minimal embedded resolution is obtained with three blowups:
first we blow up the origin.
For explicit computations, one usually uses that the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
is covered by two affine charts.
Using the notation of (
3.1.1
), there is
the
X
-chart, where the projective coordinate
X
is invertible, as well as the
Y
-chart, where
Y
is invertible.
In the
X
-chart, the equation (
3.1.1
) can be rewritten as
y=x\frac{Y}{X}
.
By setting
y^{\prime}:=\frac{Y}{X}
, we see that the
X
-chart is the affine chart with coordinates
(x,y^{\prime})
and we have to apply the substitution
y=xy^{\prime}
in order to determine the total transform of
f
.
(The situation in the
Y
-chart is completely analogous.)
Hence, in the
X
-chart of
S^{(1)}
,
the total transform is
f_{1}:=x^{2}(y^{\prime 2}-x)
and the exceptional divisor is
E_{1}=V(x)
.
In the
Y
-chart the strict transform is smooth and does not meet
E_{1}
.
In order to lighten the notation, we apply the usual abuse of notation and write
y
instead of
y^{\prime}
for the new coordinate in the
X
-chart, for example.

The center for the second blowup is the origin of the
X
-chart,
which yields the total transform
f_{2}:=x^{2}y^{3}(y-x)
in the
Y
-chart of the second blowup . Here the exceptional divisors are locally given by
E_{1}=V(x)
,
E_{2}=V(y)
and the strict transform is smooth. However, the total transform is not a normal crossing divisor, hence we need to blowup the origin of the given chart.
Observe that no further blowups are required in the
Y
-chart of the second blowup.

In the
Y
-chart of the third blowup the total transform is
f_{3,Y}=x^{2}y^{6}(1-x)
with exceptional divisors
E_{1}=V(x)
and
E_{3}=V(y)
, whereas in the respective
X
-chart we obtain the total transform
f_{3,X}:=x^{6}y^{3}(y-1)
with exceptional divisors seen
E_{2}=V(y)
and
E_{3}=V(x)
.
The total transform has normal crossings and thus we have obtained an embedded resolution of singularities.

Using the formula
[
23
, Prop. 2.37]
for computing the self-intersection numbers of the
E_{i}
, we obtain the dual resolution graph
\Gamma(V(y^{2}-x^{3}))
, see Fig.

3.2.
Fans, toric geometry and lotus of a Newton fan

A lotus is a simplicial complex that encodes the dual resolution graph as well as other graphs related to the resolution, such as the Eggers–Wall tree. Lotuses were introduced by Popescu-Pampu in
[
41
, Section 5]
, see
[
23
]
for an extensive introduction.

In the following we consider reduced curves
C
on a smooth complex surface
S
. In this paper we are mainly interested in simple lotuses coming from Newton fans.

For the basics of toric geometry see
[
13
,
22
]
. Let
N
be a lattice of rank
2
with chosen basis
e_{1},e_{2}
. As usual, we denote by
N_{\mathbb{R}}=N\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
the real vector space generated by
N
, that is,
N_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We denote the cone generated by
e_{1},e_{2}
by
\sigma_{0}=\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
. The
slope
of an element
w=ce_{1}+de_{2}\in N_{\mathbb{R}}\backslash\{0\}
is
\frac{d}{c}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
one denotes by
p(\lambda)
the unique primitive element of
N
contained in
\sigma_{0}
with slope
\lambda
.

A
fan

\Sigma
of the lattice
N
is a finite set of strictly convex (rational) cones
\sigma
that is closed under the operation of taking faces and such that if
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are contained in
\Sigma
, then
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
is a face of each
\sigma_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
N=\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle
be a rank
2
lattice with associated cone
\sigma_{0}\cong\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\rho}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
be a finite set.
We define the
fan of
\mathcal{E}
,
\Sigma(\mathcal{E})=\Sigma(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\rho})
,
to be the fan subdividing
\sigma_{0}
by the rays
p(\lambda_{i})
, where
i\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\frac{3}{2}\}
, then
p(\frac{3}{2})=2e_{1}+3e_{2}
and the fan
\Sigma(\frac{3}{2})
consists of the two two-dimensional cones
\sigma_{1}=\langle e_{1},2e_{1}+3e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and
\sigma_{2}=\langle 2e_{1}+3e_{2},e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
, the three one-dimensional cones
\sigma_{11}=\langle e_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
,
\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{21}=\langle 2e_{1}+3e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
,
\sigma_{22}=\langle e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and the cone
\{0\}
.

\Sigma(\frac{3}{2})

We will often choose suitable coordinates at a given point of a smooth surface coming from blowups. For this we say that a
cross
on a smooth surface germ
(S,s)
is a pair
(L,L^{\prime})
of transversal smooth branches on
(S,s)
. A local coordinate system
(x,y)
on the surface germ
defines the cross

(L,L^{\prime})
if
L=V(x)
and
L^{\prime}=V(y)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,s}\cong\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
be a nonzero element, written
f=\sum a_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
.
The
support of
f
is
\mathrm{supp}(f)=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}:a_{ij}\neq 0\}
.
The
Newton polyhedron

\mathcal{N}(f)
is the following convex subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
:

\mathcal{N}(f):=\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{supp}(f)+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}\ .

Here
+
denotes the Minkowski sum,
A+B:=\{a+b\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
for
A,B
subsets of an additive group.
The
boundary
of
\mathcal{N}(f)
is denoted by
\partial\mathcal{N}(f)
. Note that if we choose coordinates relative to a cross
(L,L^{\prime})
, then we can define the weight lattices
N:=N_{L,L^{\prime}}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
M:=M_{L,L^{\prime}}=N^{\vee}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and the cones
\sigma_{0}=\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\simeq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
and
\sigma_{0}^{\vee}\simeq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
. Then
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq\sigma_{0}^{\vee}\cap M\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
and

\mathcal{N}(f)=\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{supp}(f)+(\sigma_{0}^{\vee}\cap M))\subseteq\sigma_{0}^{\vee}\ .

The
Newton fan

\Sigma(f)
of
f
is the fan in
N
obtained by subdividing the cone
\sigma_{0}
using the rays orthogonal to the compact edges of the Newton polyhedron
\mathcal{N}(f)\subseteq\sigma_{0}^{\vee}
of
f
. More generally, a
Newton fan

\Sigma
in
N
is any fan subdividing the regular cone
\sigma_{0}
.

Example 3.6
.

The Newton polyhedron
\mathcal{N}(x^{3}-y^{2})
has only one compact edge
\overline{(3,0),(0,2)}
.
The orthogonal ray is generated by the primitive vector
w=2e_{1}+3e_{2}
. Thus the Newton fan is
\Sigma(f)=\Sigma(\frac{3}{2})

of Example
3.5
.

Suppose that
(L,L^{\prime})
defines a cross on
(S,s)
and
C
is a curve singularity in
(S,s)
. One can show
[
23
, Prop. 4.13]
that the Newton polyhedron
\mathcal{N}(f)
and the Newton fan
\Sigma(f)
do not depend on the choice of the defining functions
x,y,f
of the curve germs
L,L^{\prime},C
.

Now we have gathered all the necessary notation to define
\Lambda(\Sigma)
, the lotus of a Newton fan
\Sigma
(see Def.
3.9
).

Definition 3.9
.

Let
N
be a lattice of rank
2
with basis
(e_{1},e_{2})
.

(1)

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
its
lotus

\Lambda(\lambda)
is the union of petals of the universal lotus
\Lambda(e_{1},e_{2})
which intersect the ray
of slope
\lambda
. For
\lambda=0
or
\lambda=\infty
, we set
\Lambda(\lambda):=[e_{1},e_{2}]
.

(2)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
The
lotus
of
\mathcal{E}
, denoted by
\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
, is the union
\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{E}}\Lambda(\lambda)
. If
\Sigma=\Sigma(\mathcal{E})
is a Newton fan, its lotus is defined as
\Lambda(\Sigma):=\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
. Sometimes, a lotus of a Newton fan is called a
Newton lotus
.

In the present article, we will only consider Newton lotuses, for more general lotuses with several petals see
[
23
]
.

From the lotus one can read off the resolution graph of a plane curve singularity (see Section
3.3
) as its lateral boundary. For this we need some more notation:

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\Lambda
be a Newton lotus. If
\Lambda=[e_{1},e_{2}]
, then set
\partial_{+}\Lambda:=[e_{1},e_{2}]
, and if
\Lambda\neq[e_{1},e_{2}]
, then let
\partial_{+}\Lambda
be the compact and connected polygonal line that is the complement of the open segment
(e_{1},e_{2})
in the boundary of the lotus
\Lambda
. The polygonal line
\partial_{+}\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda
is called the
lateral boundary
of the lotus
\Lambda
.

If
\lambda\in[0,\infty]
, then one can associate to it a unique point on
\partial_{+}\Lambda
with slope
\lambda
, denoted by
p_{\Lambda}(\lambda)
. For a Newton lotus
\Lambda=\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
is some finite set, for any
\lambda\in\mathcal{E}
the point
p_{\Lambda(\mathcal{E})}(\lambda)
is called the
marked point of
\lambda
in
\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
. The lotus
\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
together with its marked points
p_{\Lambda(\mathcal{E})}(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{E}
is called a
marked lotus
.

We call a vertex different from
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
a
pinching point
of
\Lambda
if it belongs to only one petal of it. Moreover, if
\Lambda\neq[e_{1},e_{2}]
, then the lattice point connected to
e_{1}
(resp.
e_{2}
) in the lateral boundary
\partial_{+}\Lambda
is called the
first interior point
(resp.
last interior point
) of the lateral boundary. The vertices
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
of
\Lambda
are called
basic
vertices, and lattice points contained in the lateral boundary of
\Lambda
are called
lateral vertices
. Note that the lateral vertices of
\Lambda
are given as the vertices
\Lambda\cap N
, which are not basic.

Marked points make it possible to distinguish lotuses:

Example 3.11
.

Consider the Newton lotuses
\Lambda_{i}=\Lambda(\mathcal{E}_{i})
with
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{\frac{3}{2}\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{\frac{3}{2},\frac{2}{1},\frac{1}{1}\}
. As unmarked lotuses, we have
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
, but
\Lambda_{1}
has only one marked point (the pinching point
(2,3)

3.3.
Lotus of a toric resolution of a Newton non-degenerate curve and the dual resolution graph

In this section we briefly recall toric resolutions of curves by subdivision of fans and how to obtain the lotus and the dual resolution graph.

Recall that a cone
\sigma
in a lattice
N
is
regular
if it can be generated by a subset of a basis of
N
. Consequently, a fan
\Sigma
is
regular
if all its cones are regular. Here note that we set
\langle\varnothing\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}:=\{0\}
, so that
\{0\}
is a regular cone.

For
2
-dimensional lattices
N
, there is a minimal regular subdivision for any fan
\Sigma
in
N
, that is, any other regular subdivision refines it, see
[
38
, Prop. 1.19]
. Thus for a
2
-dimensional fan
\Sigma
in
N
, define the
regularization
\Sigma^{{\rm reg}}

as the minimal regular subdivision of
\Sigma
.

The regularization
\sigma^{\rm reg}
of a
2
-dimensional strictly convex cone
\sigma
in a lattice
N
of rank
2
is obtained by looking at the compact faces of the boundary of the convex hull of
(\sigma\cap N)\backslash\{0\}
(see
[
38
, Prop. 1.19]
). Then use the rays given by the primitive integral vectors on this boundary to subdivide the cone.
For a fan
\Sigma
, its regularization is given as the union of the regularizations of its cones.

To any cone

(affine) toric variety

(resp. the

One can further define toric morphisms and modifications, we refer to
[
23
, Section 3.3]
,
and one can show that a subdivision of the cone
\sigma_{0}
given by a fan
\Sigma
yields a equivariant birational morphism
\psi^{\Sigma}_{\sigma_{0}}\colon X_{\Sigma}\xrightarrow{}X_{\sigma_{0}}
.
The preimage of
0\in X_{\sigma_{0}}
is the exceptional divisor of
\psi^{\Sigma}_{\sigma_{0}}
.
Furthermore, one obtains the minimal resolution of a toric surface via (cf.
[
23
, Prop. 3.28]
): For a non-regular cone
\sigma
in the rank
2
lattice
N
, the toric modification
\psi^{\sigma^{\rm reg}}_{\sigma}\colon X_{\sigma^{\rm reg}}\xrightarrow{}X_{\sigma}
is the minimal resolution of the affine toric surface
X_{\sigma}
. Consequently, for any fan
\Sigma
in
N
, the toric modification
\psi^{\Sigma^{\rm reg}}_{\Sigma}\colon X_{\Sigma^{\rm reg}}\xrightarrow{}X_{\Sigma}
is the minimal resolution of singularities of
X_{\Sigma}
.

Coming back to curves, one can obtain a resolution of a Newton non-degenerate curve
C
(cf. Def.
3.15
) on a complex surface
S
by a toric modification.
However, to be precise, one has to work with
toroidal varieties and modifications in the toroidal category
(see
[
23
, Section 3.4]
). The objects in this category are defined as follows: a
toroidal variety
is a pair
(S,\partial S)
, where
S
is a normal complex variety and
\partial S
is a reduced divisor on
S
, such that the germ of
(S,\partial S)
at any point
s\in S
is locally analytically isomorphic to
(X_{\sigma},\partial X_{\sigma})
, the germ of an affine toric variety
X_{\sigma}
and its boundary
\partial X_{\sigma}
. A morphism
\psi\colon(S_{2},\partial S_{2})\xrightarrow{}(S_{1},\partial S_{1})
between toroidal varieties is a complex analytic morphism
\psi:S_{2}\xrightarrow{}S_{1}
such that
\psi^{-1}(\partial S_{1})\subseteq\partial S_{2}
. Such a morphism is a toroidal modification if the underlying morphism
\psi
is a modification as defined in
[
23
, Def. 2.31]
.

Let
(S,s)
be the germ of a smooth complex surface. We choose a cross on
(L,L^{\prime})
on
(S,s)
(giving us the coordinates
(x,y)
) and define the lattices
N_{L,L^{\prime}}
,
M_{L,L^{\prime}}
and cone
\sigma_{0}
as before. Any subdivision
\Sigma
of
\sigma_{0}
yields an analytic modification
\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}\colon S_{\Sigma}\xrightarrow{}S
of
S
. If we set
\partial S:=L+L^{\prime}
and
\partial S_{\Sigma}:=\psi^{-1}(L+L^{\prime})
, then
\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}\colon(S_{\Sigma},\partial S_{\Sigma})\xrightarrow{}(S,L+L^{\prime})
is a toroidal modification, called the
modification of
S
associated with
\Sigma
relative to the cross
(L,L^{\prime})
.

For a curve
C
on
(S,s)
,
the
Newton modification of
S
defined by
C
relative to the cross
(L,L^{\prime})
is defined as
\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{C}:(S_{\Sigma_{L,L^{\prime}}(C)},\partial S_{\Sigma_{L,L^{\prime}}(C)})\xrightarrow{}(S,L+L^{\prime})
, the modification of
S
associated with
\Sigma_{L,L^{\prime}}(C)
relative to the cross
(L,L^{\prime})
, where
\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{C}:=\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{\Sigma_{L,L^{\prime}}(C)}
,
for
\Sigma_{L,L^{\prime}}(C):=\Sigma(f)
the Newton fan of
C
relative to the cross
(L,L^{\prime})
and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,s}
is a local equation for
C
at
s
.
One also denotes the strict transform of
C
under
\psi_{L,L^{\prime}}^{C}
by
C_{L,L^{\prime}}
.

A toroidal modification
\pi\colon(S_{\Sigma},\partial S_{\Sigma})\xrightarrow{}(S,L+L^{\prime})

is called a
toroidal pseudo-resolution
if

(1)

the boundary
\partial S_{\Sigma}
of
S_{\Sigma}
contains the reduction of the total transform
\pi^{*}(C)
of
C
;

(2)

the strict transform of
C
under
\pi
does not contain singular points of
S_{\Sigma}
.

If moreover
S_{\Sigma}
is smooth, then
\pi
is called a
toroidal embedded resolution
.

In
[
23
, Algorithm 4.22]
an algorithm for a toroidal pseudo-resolution is given. It is also explained how to get an embedded toroidal resolution from a toroidal pseudo-resolution. We mostly care about the special case of Newton non-degenerate curves, so we will first introduce this notion and then say more about the algorithm.

4.
Constructing dual resolutions graphs and lotuses from CC-friezes

We have seen in the previous sections that every lotus
\Lambda(C)
for a Newton non-degenerate curve
C\subseteq S
corresponds to a triangulated
(m+2)
-gon, where
m
is the number of irreducible exceptional curves in the minimal resolution of
C
. Hence one can associate a frieze to it. In this section we show that for any CC-frieze
\mathcal{F}
there exists a Newton non-degenerate curve
C
such that its dual resolution graph (that is, the lateral boundary

\partial_{+}\Lambda(C)
of the corresponding lotus) is given by the quiddity sequence of
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a CC-frieze of width
w
and suppose the first
w+1
terms

a_{1},\ldots,a_{w+1}
in the quiddity sequence of
\mathcal{F}
are known. Then the remaining two elements
a_{w+2},a_{w+3}
in the quiddity sequence are uniquely determined.

Proof.

Write the frieze with entries
m_{i,j}
, where the indexing follows the pattern as in (
2.2.1
): the indices
i,j
are in
\mathbb{Z}
, with
i\leq j\leq w+i+3

and the boundary conditions are
m_{i,i}=0
,
m_{i,i+1}=1
,
m_{i,w+i+2}=1
,
m_{i,w+i+3}=0
. The quiddity sequence is
m_{i-1,i+1}=a_{i}
for

i\in\{1,\ldots,w+3\}
.
Assuming that the
m_{i-1,i+1}
for

i\in\{1,\ldots,w+1\}

are known, one can calculate all the entries in the triangle below using the diamond rule, that is, for

i\in\{0,\ldots,w\}
,
all
m_{i,j}
for
i+2\leq j\leq w+2
. Thus we can calculate all entries in the diagonals
m_{0,j}
and
m_{1,j}
, in particular,
the two entries
m_{0,w+1}
and
m_{1,w+2}
.
By
[
14
, (6.3), p. 306]

we have

(4.0.1)

m_{i,j}=m_{j,i+w+3}=m_{i+w+3,j+w+3}\ .

Applying the first equality of (
4.0.1
) for
i=k-w-2
and
j=k-1
,
we obtain

m_{k-w-2,k-1}=m_{k-1,k+1}\ .

For
k=w+2
(resp.
k=w+3
), this provides

m_{0,w+1}=m_{w+1,w+3}
(resp.
m_{1,w+2}=m_{w+2,w+4}
).
Since
a_{k}=m_{k-1,k+1}
for all
k
, the assertion of the lemma follows.
∎

To sum up the correspondence between triangulated polygons and cluster categories of type

: from a polygon
P
with
m

\mathcal{T}
one obtains a quiver

, which is mutation equivalent to a Dynkin quiver of type
A_{m-3}
. From the quiver one then constructs the cluster category
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
. The indecomposable objects in
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
are in bijection with the diagonals in

form a

in

.

For the corresponding frieze
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T})

\mathcal{C}_{Q}
defines the same frieze (so
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{F}(Q)
). We have already discussed that the entries “1” of
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T})
are precisely the ones corresponding to the diagonals in
\mathcal{T}
. One can get this frieze
\mathcal{F}(Q)
also by looking at the AR-quiver of
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
and evaluating the cluster character to
1
at the indecomposable direct summands of the cluster tilting object. For details we refer to
[
4
]
.

5.2.
Reduction of cluster categories and friezes

Reduction of a cluster category was first studied by Iyama and Yoshino
[
27
]
. It was used for reduction of friezes in
[
5
]
and more generally for reduction of Frobenius extriangulated categories in
[
18
]
. Here we only briefly describe the operation on a frieze and the associated polygon, for proofs of the statements see loc. cit.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{Q}
be the cluster category of a Dynkin quiver
Q
. For any rigid indecomposable object
M\in\mathcal{C}
, that is, any
M
such that
\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(M,M)=0
, define

M^{\perp}:=\{X\in\mathcal{C}:\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(M,X[1])=0\}=\{X\in\mathcal{C}:\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X,M[1])=0\}\ ,

and the factor category

\mathcal{C}(M):=M^{\perp}/\langle\add M\rangle\ .

We call
\mathcal{C}(M)
the
reduction (of
\mathcal{C}
) with respect to
M
.
By
[
27
, Thm. 4.2 and 4.7]
the category
\mathcal{C}(M)
is also triangulated and
2
-Calabi–Yau and there is a cluster character. Now going back to the corresponding frieze
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{C}
, in
[
5
, Prop. 5.3]
it was shown that if one reduces along a rigid module
M
for which the corresponding frieze entry is equal to
1
, then one obtains a so-called mesh frieze for
\mathcal{C}(M)
, the
reduction of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
M
.
This is explained in detail in
[
18
, Section 8]
(in the context of Frobenius extriangulated categories, which also applies here).

Concretely, for a frieze
\mathcal{F}
coming from a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of an
m
-gon
P
, this means that cutting
P
along a diagonal
[i,j]
of the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
(corresponding to the indecomposable module
M_{ij}
in the AR-quiver of
\mathcal{C}
), one obtains two smaller polygons
P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
and hence two smaller friezes
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
:

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a frieze coming from a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of an
m
-gon
P
and assume that
[i,j]\in\mathcal{T}
for some
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
with
i<j
and
|i-j|>1
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
the corresponding cluster category, and by
M_{ij}
the module corresponding to
[i,j]
.
Then one obtains by reduction with respect to
M_{ij}
the factor category
\mathcal{C}(M_{ij})
and the reduction of the frieze with respect to
M_{ij}
. This mesh frieze yields two friezes
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
from
\mathcal{F}
, as pictured in Fig.
22
.

Proof.

The categorical proof of this fact is in
[
18
, Thm. 8.7 and Example 8.8]
. Note that loc. cit. is in terms of Grassmannian cluster categories, which are a Frobenius version of the categories
\mathcal{C}
we consider here. The Plücker frieze of
[
18
, Example 8.8]
is our frieze
\mathcal{F}
with entries
p_{ij}
as in (
5.1.1
). See below, Fig.
22
, for a schematic picture of the reduction at the entry
p_{ij}
and the two smaller friezes
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
. In particular, note that the entry
p_{ij}
becomes part of the boundary of both of these friezes (the boundary rows consist of entries
1
only). Since the diagonal
[i,j]\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
p_{ij}=1
and indeed all the entries in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
satisfy the frieze relations.

Picture with the friezes
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
.

∎

In our context, on the singularity side, we have fixed a cross
(L,L^{\prime})
(i.e., the boundary diagonal
[1,m]
of a lotus
\Lambda(f)
). Considering
\Lambda(f)
as a triangulated polygon
P
, this boundary diagonal will be contained in either
P^{\prime}
or
P^{\prime\prime}
.
We then define
P({[i,j]})
the
reduction of
P
with respect to
[i,j]
as

P({[i,j]}):=\begin{cases}P^{\prime}&\text{ if }[1,m]\in P^{\prime}\ ,\\
P^{\prime\prime}&\text{ if }[1,m]\in P^{\prime\prime}\ .\end{cases}

Then we say that the frieze from
P({[i,j]})
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}({[i,j]})
, is the
reduction of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
[i,j]
. We can explicitly describe the quiddity of
\mathcal{F}({[i,j]})
: in Lemma
5.5
we start with the classical case of cutting off an ear that was already considered by Conway and Coxeter (and is part of their induction argument for proving Thm.
2.11
, cf. Remark
5.6
) and then deal with the general case in Prop.
5.7
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a frieze coming from a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of an
m
-gon
P
with quiddity sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Assume that the diagonal
[i-1,i+1]\in\mathcal{T}
, where
i-1,i+1
are the representatives modulo
m
in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Then the reduction
\mathcal{F}({[i-1,i+1]})
is

(1)

the trivial frieze if
i=1
or
i=m
(note: in those cases, the considered diagonals are
[2,m]
,
[1,m-1]
, respectively),

(2)

the frieze with quiddity

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-2},p_{i-2,i+1},p_{i-1,i+2},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{m})

if
i\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
.

Proof.

Note that reducing along a diagonal
[i-1,i+1]
means that we divide the polygon
P
into an
(m-1)
-gon
P^{\prime}
and a triangle
P^{\prime\prime}
. In the frieze, this means that we choose the entry
p_{i-1,i+1}
in the first row. Then we can reduce categorically:
p_{i-1,i+1}
corresponds to the module
M_{i-1,i+1}
and the AR-quiver of
\mathcal{C}(M_{i-1,i+1})
is given by the AR-quiver of
\mathcal{C}
with the indecomposables in the two regions
\mathscr{R}_{\rightarrow}(\tau^{-1}M_{i-1,i+1})=\mathscr{R}_{\rightarrow}(M_{i+1,i+2})
and
\mathscr{R}_{\leftarrow}(\tau M_{i-1,i+1})=\mathscr{R}_{\leftarrow}(M_{i-2,i})
deleted. This means that we delete the two diagonals
(p_{i,i+2},\ldots,p_{i-2,i})
and
(p_{i-2,i},\ldots,p_{i,i+2})
in the frieze. Note that in fact the two diagonals contain the same elements. For a picture, see (
5.2.1
) (with
m=9
and

and the two diagonals and

marked).

(5.2.1)

Clearly,

or

if and only if

is contained in the trivial frieze

corresponding to the triangle

. Now assume that
i\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
. The quiddity sequence of
\mathcal{F}
is given as

\begin{array}[]{c}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-2},a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_{m-1},a_{m})=\\[5.0pt]
=(p_{2,m},p_{13},\ldots,p_{i-3,i-1},p_{i-2,i},p_{i-1,i+1},p_{i,i+2},p_{i+1,i+3},\ldots,p_{m-2,m},p_{1,m-1})\ .\end{array}

Then the new quiddity can be read off similarly as in (

) as

(5.2.2)

(p_{m,2},p_{13},\ldots,p_{i-3,i-1},p_{i-2,i+1},p_{i-1,i+2},p_{i+1,i+3},\ldots,p_{m-1,1})\ .

∎

The quiddity for the reduced frieze (

5.3.
Partial resolutions and reduction of friezes

In this section the reduction of a frieze
\mathcal{F}(\Lambda(f))
as in the last Section
5.2
will be related to partial lotuses of the corresponding curve singularity
C=V(f)
.

Definition 5.10
.

(1)

Let
\pi=\varphi_{\ell}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{1}\colon\widetilde{S}\xrightarrow{}S
be a sequence of blowups corresponding to a subdivision of the fan
\sigma_{0}
by rays
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}
, i.e., we choose a cross
(L,L^{\prime})
in
S
and then each blowup is the blowup in the origin of one of the charts from the previous blowups.
The
lotus associated to
\pi
is defined as
\Lambda(\pi):=\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{E}
is the set of the slopes
\lambda_{i}
of the
w_{i}
. We call the frieze
\mathcal{F}(\pi):=\mathcal{F}(\Lambda(\pi))
the
frieze of
\pi
.

(2)

Let
\pi\colon\widetilde{S}\xrightarrow{}S
be the minimal resolution of the Newton non-degenerate curve
(C,s)\subseteq(S,s)
given by
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
, and let
\Lambda(f)
be the associated lotus. Note that
\pi=\varphi_{\ell}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{1}
can be written as a sequence of blowups in points and thus
\Lambda(f)=\Lambda(\pi)
. Then we call a sequence of blowups
\pi^{\prime}=\varphi^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi^{\prime}_{1}\colon\widetilde{S^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{}S
a
partial resolution of
C
if
\Lambda(\pi^{\prime})\subset\Lambda(f)
is a sublotus.

Here partial resolution means in particular that the strict transform
C^{\prime}
of
C
under
\pi^{\prime}
is partially resolved in the sense that the Puiseux characteristics
(m;\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{g})
of
C^{\prime}
is strictly smaller than that of
C
, for notation and precise statement see
[
53
, Thm. 3.5.5]
.

Note that a sublotus of
\Lambda(f)
must always contain the base petal, that is, at least the first blowup must be
\varphi_{1}
.

The main result of this section is to connect reduction of friezes to partial resolutions:

Theorem 5.11
.

Consider the curve
C=V(f)
, where
f
is assumed to be Newton non-degenerate, and its minimal resolution
\pi
.
Then the frieze
\mathcal{F}(\pi^{\prime})
of any partial resolution
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
is obtained as a reduction of the frieze of
\pi
. In particular, if the dual resolution graph
\Gamma(f)
is of type
A_{m-2}
with self-intersection numbers
\{-a_{i}\}_{i=2}^{m-1}
, then the dual graph of the exceptional curves appearing in
\pi^{\prime}
is of type
A_{k}
for some
k\leq m-2
and the self-intersection numbers
\{-b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
correspond to negatives of entries in the frieze of
\pi
.

Proof.

Since
C=V(f)
is Newton non-degenerate, its minimal resolution
\pi
consists of Steps 1 to
3^{reg}
of the resolution algorithm of
[
23
, Algorithm 1.4.22 and Prop. 1.4.29]
and hence the dual resolution graph corresponds to the boundary of the Newton lotus
\Lambda(f)
, see Thm.
3.17
. The same theorem shows that
\Lambda(f)=\Lambda(\pi)
corresponds to a triangulated
m
-gon
P
. The quiddity sequence of the frieze of
\pi
is then
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where the
a_{i}
’s for
i\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
are precisely the negatives of the self-intersection numbers appearing in
\Gamma(f)
and
a_{1}
and
a_{m}
are uniquely determined by
P
, see Lemma
4.1
.

By definition of a partial resolution
\pi^{\prime}
(Def.
5.10
(2)), the lotus of
\pi^{\prime}
corresponds to one of the two smaller polygons
P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
obtained by cutting
P
along one of the diagonals in its triangulation. Without loss of generality, assume that the lotus of
\pi^{\prime}
corresponds to
P^{\prime}
. Using Prop.
5.7
one can compute the quiddity sequence of the frieze of
\pi^{\prime}
explicitly: if
[i,j]
is an diagonal in the triangulation of
P
(corresponding to the chart of a blowup), then the elements in the quiddity are entries in the frieze of
\pi
given by one of the two formulas in the proposition.
∎

This result allows us to see that the entries
p_{ij}
of the frieze that appear in the quiddity sequences of the reduction of a frieze also correspond to (negatives) of the self-intersection numbers of exceptional curves. In particular, the frieze of
\pi
contains all the information about any partial resolution
\pi^{\prime}
.

7.
Further questions

We end by discussing further directions that could be considered as continuation.

Up to now, we always considered Newton non-degenerate curves and their corresponding lotuses.
There are plane curves that are not Newton non-degenerate and it is possible to define a lotus associated to them by gluing disjoint unions of lotuses of the form as considered in the present article, e.g., see

[
23
, Def. 5.26]
.
Hence, it is reasonable to ask

Question 7.1
.

How do the results of the present article generalize for (plane) curves that are not Newton non-degenerate? In particular, can we associate “higher” friezes to them?

First considerations suggest that re-embeddings resulting from which the curve becomes Newton non-degenerate in some higher-dimensional ambient space provide a suitable perspective.
In this case,
a natural candidate to substitute triangulations of polygons by triangles would be their analog using
d
-simplices instead of triangles,
where
d
is the dimension of the eventual ambient space.
One motivation for the re-embeddings is Teissier’s perspective on the problem of local uniformization via overweight deformations, see
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
.
See also
[
25
,
16
]
, where the resolution of curves with one toric morphism is discussed,
and
[
52
]
, where it is shown that whenever a desingularization exists then it can be realized as a single toric morphism.

Let us explain the reasons for this approach on an example.

Example 7.2
.

Consider the plane curve
C\subset\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
defined by the vanishing locus of

f=(y^{2}-x^{3})^{5}-x^{14}y\in\mathbb{C}[x,y]\ .

The Newton polyhedron has a single compact edge with vertices
(15,0)
and
(0,10)
.
Since the restriction of
f
to this edge is
(x^{2}-y^{3})^{5}
,
which is not smooth on the torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})_{x,y}^{2}
as it is not reduced,
the curve is not Newton non-degenerate.
We re-embed by introducing a new variable
z
fulfilling the relation

z=y^{2}-x^{3}
, i.e.,
we apply the isomorphism

\mathbb{C}[x,y]\cong\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/\langle z-y^{2}+x^{3}\rangle
and determine the image of
C
considered as curve in
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
.
By substitution, the latter can be described by the relations

\begin{array}[]{lcl}y^{2}-x^{3}&=&z\ ,\\[3.0pt]
z^{5}-x^{14}y&=&0\ .\end{array}

By choosing the weights
W(x)=10,W(y)=15,W(z)=31
,
it can be seen that this is an overweight deformation of the toric variety
\mathcal{X}:=V(y^{2}-x^{3},z^{5}-x^{14}y)

(for the definition of an overweight deformation, we refer to
[
50
, Def. 3.1]
).
In particular, there is a close connection between the resolution of
\mathcal{X}
and that of
C
.

Since
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
lives in a three-dimensional ambient space,
the underlying lattice
N
has rank
3
and
the toric resolution of
\mathcal{X}
is a subdivision of the cone
\sigma_{0}:=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
.
Hence, it is reasonable to work with tetrahedra instead of triangles for the associated lotus.
Furthermore, in the re-embedded situation,
the blowup of the closed point has three charts and not two,
which also supports the approach via tetrahedra (cf. Remark
3.13
).

In Def.
7.3
, we propose a definition for the variant of the universal lotus in the setting of Newton non-degenerate curve singularities living in higher-dimensional ambient spaces.
Notice that this is a special case reflecting that the desingularization is obtained by blowing up closed points.
In
[
41
, Section 9]
, Popescu-Pampu introduced the definition of an arbitrary dimensional universal lotus.
The latter is far more general, but also more technical than Def.
7.3
.

Definition 7.3
.

Let
N
be a lattice of rank
d
with a chosen basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
.
We define the
base petal
as the convex and compact
d
-simplex
\delta(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})\subseteq N_{\mathbb{R}}

with vertices
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d},e_{1}+\cdots+e_{d}
.
The points
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
are called the
basic vertices
of the petal.

We construct more petals as follows:
Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
be the vertices of any facet of the petal
\delta(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
for which
e_{1}+\cdots+e_{d}
is one of the vertices.
Since
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
is a basis for
N
, the petal
\delta(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
is the one constructed from this basis.
In other words, it is the
d
-simplex with vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d},v_{1}+\cdots+v_{d}
.

By continued iteration of this process, we obtain an infinite simplicial complex in
\sigma_{0}:=\langle e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\cong\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
,
which we call the
universal barycentric lotus
\Lambda(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
of
N
relative to the basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
.

Since this leads us to higher-dimensional lotuses, we could also pass from curves to singularities of dimension
\geq 2
.
The notion of Newton non-degenerate singularities is not limited to curves.
More generally, quasi-ordinary singularities are a natural class to be taken into account.
Given
X\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of dimension
d
it is called
quasi-ordinary
if there exists a projection
X\to\mathbb{A}^{d}_{\mathbb{C}}
such that its discriminant locus is a simple normal crossing divisor.
In
[
36
]
, the irreducible hypersurface case is considered.
More precisely, there is a construction of re-embeddings using weighted polyhedra determined so that a connection as overweight deformation of an irreducible toric variety is deduced.

Notice that, in view of Remark
3.13
, there are more possible centers than just closed points in higher dimension.
This needs to be encountered, when investigating the higher dimensional case,
cf.
[
41
, Section 9]
.

.

How do the results of the present article generalize to higher dimensions?
