Failed Mucosal Allostasis Distinguishes Murine IgG‐ From IgE‐Mediated Recurrent Severe Anaphylaxis
Authors: Abdurrahman Simsek, Muhammed Ali Kizmaz, Tugce Bozkurt, Sude Toprakkaz, Mehmet Emre Topcu, Musa Ozgur Ozyigit, Ahmet Akkoc, Dane Ediger, Ferah Budak Sener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnaphylaxis is classically framed around IgE‐FcεRI, yet a subset of severe reactions engages an IgG–FcγR‐mediated arm whose consequences for mucosal barrier integrity and epithelial repair remain undefined under recurrence. We propose that mucosal surfaces sustain function during severe anaphylaxis through coordinated operation of immunological composition, structural integrity and reparative capacity—which we term mucosal allostasis—and asked whether the two pathways diverge along this framework.MethodsBALB/c mice were resolved into IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated arms using a hybrid active/passive (ASA‐PSA) sensitisation model and subjected to acute or recurrent severe anaphylaxis, with pharmacological reversal enabling matched rechallenge. Lung, small intestine, colon and serum were profiled across cytokine, mediator, junctional, repair and bacterial 16S rDNA panels; ILC subsets and junctional integrity were resolved by flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry.ResultsDespite comparable systemic clinical severity, the two pathways diverged sharply at the mucosal level. IgE‐mediated recurrence preserved junctional expression and mounted a coordinated AREG/EGF/periostin/TFF3/MUC2 repair programme, whereas IgG‐mediated recurrence dismantled both: Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1 and E‐cadherin declined in tissue and rose reciprocally in serum, repair mediators failed to engage where junctional injury was greatest, and bacterial DNA translocation rose 48‐fold, alongside a type 2/regulatory to inflammatory shift and an ILC2 to ILC1/NCR+ ILC3 reorganisation.ConclusionRecurrent IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated severe anaphylaxis diverge into two mechanistically opposite allostatic phenotypes. IgG‐mediated recurrence produces an allostatic collapse in which structural failure proceeds uncoupled from reparative engagement, identifying barrier–repair coupling as a candidate axis for intervention in refractory anaphylaxis.Keywords: anaphylaxis, epithelial barrier, IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis, mucosal allostasis, tight junction proteins

Introduction

Mucosal surfaces form the primary immunological interface between the organism and its environment, sustaining function under continuous inflammatory and antigenic stress not by resisting injury but by absorbing it, repairing it and returning to a workable steady state. This capacity depends on tight coupling between how immune signals are set at the surface, how the epithelial architecture holds under load, and how repair engages before the next wave of injury arrives [1, 2]; we term this coordinated operation mucosal allostasis. Failure of any one of these processes locally is often tolerated; failure of coupling between them is not, and once mucosal function decouples from repair, local damage escalates into a systemic amplifier that shapes the trajectory of allergic disease.

The epithelial barrier hypothesis proposes that environmental stressors, inflammatory stimuli, and allergic responses disrupt epithelial integrity and thereby contribute to the development of many chronic conditions, including allergic diseases [3, 4]. Within this framework, the mucosal barrier itself operates as an active pathophysiological node that amplifies systemic inflammation. Once integrity is lost, allergens, inflammatory mediators and luminal microbial products cross tissue boundaries and enter the systemic circulation [5]. In severe and recurrent anaphylactic episodes, local mucosal injury may thereby escalate into a self‐reinforcing cycle that magnifies the systemic inflammatory response.

Anaphylaxis is classically attributed to IgE‐FcεRI crosslinking on mast cells and basophils, triggering the release of histamine, prostaglandin D2 and cysteinyl leukotrienes, which together drive vasodilation, vascular leakage, bronchoconstriction, hypothermia, cutaneous scratching and piloerection, and increased intestinal peristalsis with diarrhoea. An alternative IgG‐mediated pathway, operating through Fcγ receptors on macrophages, neutrophils and basophils, has also been described, particularly under high antigen burden [6]; in murine models this pathway is mediated predominantly by platelet‐activating factor (PAF) and drives profound vascular collapse. Clinically, IgG‐mediated mechanisms have been implicated in a subset of refractory reactions responding poorly to conventional therapy, where human IgG immune complexes activate neutrophils through FcγRIIA and FcγRIIIB while canonical IgE biomarkers remain absent [7, 8, 9].

Severe anaphylaxis compromises barrier integrity through hypoperfusion, vascular leakage and cytokine‐driven inflammation. Activation of myosin light chain kinase and accumulation of phosphorylated myosin light chain (pMLC) intensifies actomyosin contraction, mechanically uncouples intercellular junctions, and drives heterogeneous loss of tight and adherens junction components including Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1 and E‐cadherin [10]. Systemic release of these junctional proteins reflects epithelial shedding under injury, while translocation of luminal microbial products into the circulation and distant organs extends local mucosal failure into a systemic amplifier of inflammation [11]. Mucosal tissues counter such injury through allostatic repair programmes operating across distinct functional layers: trefoil factors and mucins reseal the epithelial surface, growth factors including amphiregulin (AREG) and EGF sustain epithelial proliferation, and matrix‐associated mediators such as periostin organise tissue remodelling [12, 13, 14, 15]. Mast cell proteases mark systemic and local anaphylactic activation, whereas histamine, PAF, PGD2, and cysteinyl leukotrienes set the severity of the acute vascular response [16]. Innate lymphoid cells provide the cellular axis coupling immune programme to tissue outcome, with the ILC1/ILC2 balance and NCR+ ILC3‐driven epithelial protection each contributing to the direction between damage and repair [17].

Whether IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis differentially compromise mucosal barrier integrity and repair, and whether the ILC1/ILC2 balance separates their tissue‐level consequences under recurrent injury, has not been resolved. Defining these dynamics requires an experimental design that moves beyond classical single‐shock sensitisation/challenge protocols. We therefore exploited a hybrid active/passive sensitisation model (ASA‐PSA) in BALB/c mice to resolve IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated pathways across both acute and recurrent severe anaphylaxis, integrating measurements of junctional integrity, epithelial repair and tissue remodelling, anaphylactic effector activation, microbial translocation and tissue‐specific ILC composition across pulmonary and intestinal compartments. We hypothesised that IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated severe anaphylaxis engage fundamentally distinct trajectories of mucosal injury, repair and systemic barrier dysfunction.

Methods

Animals and Ethics

All experiments were approved by the Bursa Uludağ University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments (app. no. 2025‐02‐07). Male and female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks; Bursa Uludağ University Laboratory Animal Unit) were housed under standard conditions with ad libitum access to chow and water and randomly assigned after 7‐day acclimatisation to control, acute IgE, acute IgG, recurrent IgE (R.IgE) and recurrent IgG (R.IgG) groups, sex‐balanced. A core body temperature of 28°C was predefined as the humane endpoint; terminal procedures were performed under deep sevoflurane anaesthesia and death was confirmed by cervical dislocation.

Experimental Design and Pooling Strategy

Sixty‐eight BALB/c mice were included: 12 mice per anaphylaxis group (acute IgE, acute IgG, R.IgE, R.IgG) and 10 mice per acute and recurrent sham‐control group. Clinical outcomes (score, temperature, diarrhoea, endpoint attainment, survival) were recorded at the individual‐animal level. Molecular and microbial readouts were analysed at the biological‐pool level (n = 4 pools per group), assembled exclusively within group, tissue, time point and clinical severity. Nine animals that did not reach the humane endpoint (acute IgE, n = 2; acute IgG, n = 3; R.IgE, n = 2; R.IgG, n = 2) were combined into a single survivor pool per group and evaluated exploratorily.

Sensitisation and Induction of Anaphylaxis

Mice were actively sensitised by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection 14–18 days before challenge with ovalbumin (OVA; 150 μg), pertussis toxin (0.2 μg) and alum (1.5 mg) (all Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). In IgG‐mediated groups, endogenous IgE‐dependent activity was suppressed with the anti‐IgE monoclonal antibody R1E4 (10 μg i.p. on Day 10 after sensitisation and again 72, 48 and 24 h before challenge; Kerafast, USA), and mice additionally received anti‐OVA IgG1 (clone L71; 30 μg intravenously [i.v.] 24 h before challenge; Chondrex, USA), producing a hybrid active/passive sensitisation. Anaphylaxis was induced by 650 μg OVA i.v.; control animals received saline. Mice were monitored for 90 min or until the humane endpoint (Figures S1–S3; Data S1: SM1–SM3).

Induction of Recurrent Anaphylaxis

Recurrent‐group mice underwent an acute challenge, and once a clear decline in body temperature had been established the ongoing reaction was pharmacologically reversed by combined administration of the PAF receptor antagonist CV‐6209 (100 μg i.v.; MedChem, USA) and the H1 antagonist cyproheptadine (200 μg i.p.; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Following recovery, mice were maintained for 14–18 days before an identical second OVA challenge under the same monitoring protocol. Acute‐group mice underwent neither reversal nor rechallenge [Figure S4, Data S1:SM4].

Assessment and Scoring of Anaphylaxis Severity

Body temperature was monitored by thermal imaging (Uni‐T, China) and corrected against rectal thermometry from 50 paired measurements (mean inter‐method offset 0.213°C). Antigen‐specific humoral responses were confirmed by qualitative ELISA (BT Lab, China) for serum anti‐OVA IgE and anti‐OVA IgG. Anaphylaxis severity was quantified using a predefined weighted composite scoring system (S) integrating 15 binary‐coded clinical features across four functional domains (behavioural, respiratory/systemic, critical neurological, intestinal; maximum raw score 106), normalised as Score100 = 100 × S/106 and classified as normal (0), mild (1–25), moderate (26–50), severe (51–75) or very severe/critical (76–100). Animals showing unresponsiveness to tactile stimulation or loss of the righting reflex were classified as severe irrespective of composite score (Data S1: SM5).

Processing of Tissue and Serum Samples

Following euthanasia, lung, small intestine (SI), colon and serum were collected and partitioned prospectively according to downstream assay requirements. Flow cytometry fractions were processed within 30 min by collagenase/DNase enzymatic dissociation without fixation. Immunohistochemistry fractions were fixed overnight in 2% paraformaldehyde and paraffin‐embedded. Tissue ELISA fractions were stored at −80°C and disrupted in a common lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (sodium orthovanadate, sodium fluoride); a single homogenate per specimen was used across ELISA panels where assay compatibility permitted. Bacterial DNA fractions were frozen immediately without exposure to lysis buffers. Tissue PGD2 was assessed as its stable metabolite 9α,11β‐PGF2α after homogenisation in the presence of indomethacin. Serum was separated by centrifugation, aliquoted and stored at −80°C with no more than one freeze–thaw cycle; PGD2‐MOX aliquots were derivatised with methoxylamine hydrochloride on the day of analysis (Data S1: SM6).

ELISA and Bead‐Based Immunoassay Analyses

Biomarkers were quantified with commercially available ELISA and multiplex bead‐based immunoassay kits validated for the corresponding sample matrix, following the manufacturers' protocols. Tissue and serum biomarkers were organised into four functional panels: (i) junctional and contractility‐associated proteins—ZO‐1, Occludin, Claudin‐1, E‐cadherin and pMLC (BT Lab, China); (ii) epithelial repair and tissue‐remodelling mediators—AREG, periostin, CHI3L1, EGF, TFF3 and MUC2 (BT Lab, China); (iii) mast cell proteases mMCP‐1 and mMCP‐7 (BT Lab, China; Reed Biotech, China); (iv) cytokines IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐10, IL‐13 and TNF‐α, quantified simultaneously by the LEGENDplex bead‐based multiplex assay (BioLegend, USA) with flow‐cytometric readout on a Navios EX cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Early‐phase serum (3–5 min post‐challenge) was assayed for histamine, PAF, LTC4 and PGD2‐MOX (BT Lab, China; Cayman, USA). Late‐phase samples (~60 min, at the humane endpoint) covered lung, SI, colon and serum, with histamine and PAF reassessed, LTC₄/D₄/E₄ (Reed Biotech, China) replacing LTC₄, and 9α,11β‐PGF2α (Cayman, USA) quantified as the stable PGD2 metabolite (Data S1: SM7).

Bacterial DNA Isolation and Quantification

Total DNA was isolated from lung, SI, colon and serum using a silica‐membrane extraction kit (Nepenthe Biotechnology, Türkiye) per the manufacturer's instructions; only samples meeting A260/A280 ≥ 1.6 and A260/A230 ≥ 1.8 were included. Bacterial DNA was quantified using the Femto Bacterial DNA Quantification Kit (Zymo Research, USA), which employs universal 16S rDNA primers, on a Rotor‐Gene Q platform (Qiagen, Germany), with C t values converted against the kit standard curve and specificity confirmed by melting‐curve analysis. Extractions were performed in a pre‐decontaminated workspace with UV‐sterilised consumables; reagent‐only extraction blanks and no‐template controls were processed with every batch [Figure.S5, Data S1: SM8].

Isolation and Flow Cytometric Characterisation of ILC Populations

Mononuclear cells were isolated from freshly collected lung, SI and colon by mechanical dissociation, EDTA‐based epithelial release for intestinal tissues, and recovery from a discontinuous 40%/80% Percoll gradient (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). ILCs were enriched by negative selection (Mouse ILC Enrichment Kit; STEMCELL Technologies, Canada), and viability was assessed with an amine‐reactive fixable dye (Invitrogen, USA). Following surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilised for intranuclear detection of RORγt and EOMES and acquired on a Navios EX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Doublets and lineage‐positive cells (CD3, CD5, CD11b, Ly6G/Ly6C, F4/80, CD19) were excluded; Lin−CD45+EOMES− events defined the total ILC compartment. Subsets were resolved by NKp46 and RORγt expression as ILC1 (NKp46+RORγt−), NCR+ ILC3 (NKp46+RORγt+) and NCR− ILC3 (NKp46−RORγt+); within NKp46−RORγt− events, ILC2s were identified as KLRG1+NKp46− (Data S1: SM9). The full gating strategy is shown in Figure S6.

Immunohistochemical Staining and H‐Score Quantification

Lung and intestinal specimens (< 3 mm) were fixed overnight in 2% paraformaldehyde, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned at 5–8 μm. Following deparaffinisation, endogenous peroxidase quenching (3% H2O2) and citrate‐buffer heat‐induced antigen retrieval, sections were blocked with bovine serum albumin and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ZO‐1, Occludin and Claudin‐1 (Affinity Biosciences, USA), then visualised with a poly‐HRP secondary detection system (Elabscience, China). For each marker, an H‐score (0–300) was calculated as the sum of the products of staining intensity (0–3) and the percentage of cells in each category, evaluated across five randomly selected high‐power fields per section at ×400 magnification by two blinded observers; inter‐observer agreement was assessed by Spearman's rank correlation and discrepancies resolved by consensus (Data S1: SM10).

Statistical Analysis

Clinical outcomes were analysed at the individual‐animal level and biomarker, mediator and bacterial DNA measurements at the biological‐pool level (n = 4 pools per group). Analytes were z‐standardised within each tissue compartment and organised into six composite modules: (i) type 2/regulatory cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, IL‐10); (ii) inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6); (iii) mast cell/lipid mediators (mMCP‐1, mMCP‐7, histamine, PAF, 9α,11β‐PGF2α, total LTC₄/D₄/E₄); (iv) structural barrier/contractility (Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1, E‐cadherin, pMLC, CHI3L1); (v) repair/mucosal restitution (AREG, EGF, periostin, TFF3, MUC2); and (vi) an immunohistochemical junctional score (mean H‐scores for Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1). Junctional and structural barrier proteins were directionally inverted before aggregation. Group comparisons used Welch's ANOVA with Games–Howell post hoc or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's post hoc according to distributional characteristics (Shapiro–Wilk), adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR < 0.05). Prespecified pairwise comparisons along the isotype axis (acute IgE vs. acute IgG; R.IgE vs. R.IgG) and the recurrence axis (acute IgE vs. R.IgE; acute IgG vs. R.IgG) used Welch's t‐test; fold changes were log2‐transformed. Correlations were assessed by Pearson's coefficient, or Spearman's rank where appropriate, at the individual‐pool level for directly matched analytes and at the tissue × condition group‐mean level otherwise. Principal component analysis was performed on a z‐standardised matrix of mediator, barrier, bacterial DNA and ILC measurements. Heat‐map z‐scores were constrained to ±2 SD; serum barrier‐protein z‐scores were sign‐inverted for heat‐map representation only. Survivor data were evaluated descriptively. All analyses were performed in R (v4.6.1) (Data S1: SM11).

Results

IgE‐ and IgG‐Mediated Anaphylaxis Produce Comparable Systemic Clinical Severity

Both IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated antigen challenge elicited a rapidly developing systemic anaphylactic response marked by profound hypothermia (Figure 1a,b). Composite clinical severity was comparable between the two pathways under both acute and recurrent conditions (Figure 1c). Gastrointestinal involvement, however, revealed a clear pathway‐dependent divergence: diarrhoea occurred in 31% and 63% of animals in the acute IgE and acute IgG groups and in 25% and 81% of R.IgE and R.IgG groups (Figure 1d), superimposing a distinct clinical signature on an otherwise similar systemic phenotype. Endpoint animals sustained progressive hypothermia, whereas survivors showed thermal recovery after the initial decline (Figure 1b,e); serial thermal imaging corroborated these divergent trajectories (Figure 1f). No robust sex‐dependent differences emerged within the available sample size [Figures S7 and S8]. Subsequent tissue, molecular and cellular analyses were confined to endpoint animals to anchor mechanistic comparisons within a clinically homogeneous severe‐anaphylaxis phenotype; survivor pools were reserved for exploratory evaluation [Figures S9–S11].

IgE and IgG Anaphylaxis Polarise Opposing Cytokine Programmes That Sharpen With Recurrence

Despite comparable clinical severity, the two pathways engaged markedly divergent cytokine landscapes across lung, SI, colon and serum, with the divergence sharpening under recurrence (Figure 2A). IgE‐mediated anaphylaxis carried a predominantly type 2/regulatory signature: IL‐4, IL‐5 and IL‐13 rose across all four compartments in acute IgE and climbed higher in R.IgE, and a pronounced IL‐10 elevation was confined to R.IgE. IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis was dominated by TNF‐α and IL‐6, both exceeding acute IgE levels in acute IgG and rising further across all compartments in R.IgG, while the type 2/regulatory profile remained suppressed. Composite module scores confirmed the reciprocal pattern, with the type 2/regulatory and inflammatory modules segregating oppositely between the two pathways and further separating under recurrence (Figure 2B,C).

Mast Cell Proteases and Lipid Mediators Define Pathway‐Specific Effector Signatures

Early systemic lipid mediators separated along pathway lines: serum LTC₄ and PGD2‐MOX rose preferentially in the IgE groups, PAF concentrated in the IgG groups and both patterns were preserved after recurrence; histamine climbed in both without pathway distinction (Figure 3B). In the terminal phase, PAF exceeded IgE levels across all four compartments and peaked in R.IgG intestinal tissue and serum (Figure 3A), while total LTC₄/D₄/E₄ and 9α,11β‐PGF2α were elevated in acute IgE, sustained and further strengthened in R.IgE, and remained suppressed in IgG groups. mMCP‐1 rose across all compartments in IgE groups and remained low in IgG groups; mMCP‐7 rose in both acutely, was reinforced by recurrence, and peaked in R.IgE. Histamine intensified with recurrence in both pathways, peaking in R.IgE without decisive pathway partition. Heat‐map z‐score distributions clustered IgE samples along mMCP‐1, PGD2 and cysteinyl leukotriene axes and IgG samples along the PAF axis (Figure 3C,D).

Recurrent IgG‐Mediated Anaphylaxis Precipitates a Structural Barrier Collapse

In the acute phase, IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis carried more pronounced junctional protein loss and higher pMLC than IgE across pulmonary and intestinal tissues (Figure 4A). Recurrent challenge widened the divergence: R.IgE preserved junctional protein expression across most compartments, whereas R.IgG showed a broad decline in Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1 and E‐cadherin, most pronounced in intestinal tissues, alongside concurrent elevation of pMLC and CHI3L1. The tissue‐level decline in R.IgG was mirrored by a reciprocal rise in serum concentrations of the same junctional proteins, a pattern present but attenuated in acute IgG and largely absent from IgE groups. Composite barrier module scores separated the two pathways sharply under recurrence, with R.IgE preserved and R.IgG shifted into a pronounced negative signature (Figure 4B). Tissue barrier integrity and serum barrier‐associated protein levels were strongly inversely correlated across the dataset, with the relationship substantially stronger in the IgG than the IgE arm (Figure 4C). R.IgG samples clustered in the region of low tissue integrity and high serum burden, and diarrhoea‐positive animals populated the same region.

Recurrent IgE and IgG Anaphylaxis Diverge Into Preserved Versus Collapsed Mucosal Allostasis

In the acute phase, AREG was elevated in the IgE group across lung, SI and colon, whereas EGF, periostin, TFF3 and MUC2 showed heterogeneous compartment‐dependent distributions (Figure 5A). Recurrent challenge sharpened the divergence. In R.IgE, AREG, EGF, periostin, TFF3 and MUC2 rose concurrently across tissue compartments, with TFF3 and MUC2 rising most sharply in the SI. In R.IgG, AREG, EGF and periostin remained low at the tissue level, while TFF3 and MUC2 declined in tissue and rose in serum, directionally concordant with the barrier protein inversion in the same group. Composite repair module scores diverged only marginally in the acute phase but sharply under recurrence, with R.IgE elevated and R.IgG suppressed (Figure 5B). Structural barrier integrity and the repair module were strongly positively correlated across tissue samples (Figure 5C): R.IgE samples clustered in the region combining preserved barrier integrity and high repair, whereas R.IgG samples clustered in the region combining low barrier integrity and weak repair.

Discussion

Mucosal surfaces sustain function under continuous injury by coordinating three dimensions: an immunological composition that calibrates effector and resolution signals, a structural integrity that preserves the epithelial barrier, and a reparative capacity that closes each wave of injury before the next arrives. We term the coordinated operation of these three dimensions mucosal allostasis, extending to the barrier surface the framework by which tissues defend homeostasis through active adjustment rather than passive maintenance [18]. Here we show that severe anaphylaxis reorganises mucosal allostasis along pathway‐specific trajectories, and that recurrence resolves them into two mechanistically opposite phenotypes: IgE‐mediated anaphylaxis sustains coordinated operation of the three dimensions across recurrent challenge, whereas IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis dismantles it, escalating injury without compensatory repair. We define this outcome as allostatic collapse. The axis on which the two pathways separate is the coupling between injury and repair, not the magnitude of injury alone.

The mechanistic foundation of an alternative IgG‐mediated route to anaphylaxis was established by Finkelman and colleagues, who showed that FcγR engagement on macrophages, neutrophils, and basophils drives PAF release and vascular derangement without the classical mast cell pathway [19, 20], later mapped in murine studies of its cellular effectors, mediators and antibody subclass hierarchies [21, 22]. Human evidence remains indirect: refractory reactions lacking canonical IgE biomarkers respond poorly to epinephrine [23], reduced serum PAF‐acetylhydrolase activity tracks with fatal outcome [24], and human neutrophils release PAF ex vivo through FcγRIIA and FcγRIIIB engagement by immune complexes [25]. The clinical scenarios in which this arm plausibly operates, food allergy, drug reactions and idiopathic anaphylaxis, share a feature that most murine models do not reproduce: the reaction is not singular, and pathway‐specific differences consolidate or fade only under repetition, which is the ground on which the present work operates.

Prior murine models have engaged the sensitised state at a single point of provocation. Some intervene before the reaction, inducing allergen‐neutralising antibody responses through virus‐like particle platforms [26], redirecting established Th2 responses through epicutaneous or probiotic‐based immunotherapy [27, 28], or reshaping tolerance with dietary butyrate [29]. Others interrogate which effector cells carry it, an approach that has proven methodologically fraught: antibody‐independent depletion shows that apparent neutrophil contributions can arise from FcγR‐mediated priming by the depleting antibodies rather than from the cells removed [30]. What unites these approaches is a single‐event frame—the reaction is treated as one provocation to be prevented, resolved or attributed. Yet the coupling between injury and repair is not a distinction that any single provocation can make, because the two programmes remain convergent within the transient window of an acute reaction. Their separation requires that both be driven to their functional extremes, and only repeated challenge does so. Fixing sensitisation and challenge while varying only recurrence is therefore not one design among several, but the condition under which the coupling axis becomes observable—and along this axis, rather than in the magnitude of the initial insult, the IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated arms diverge.

Among the three dimensions, structural integrity is the one most directly exposed to the forces of an anaphylactic reaction, and its failure most immediately compromises both the compositional and reparative dimensions. The epithelial barrier has been repositioned from a passive casualty of chronic allergic inflammation to one of its active determinants [31, 32, 33], and our data extend this shift to anaphylaxis itself. The IgE‐mediated arm preserved junctional protein expression across most compartments, whereas the IgG‐mediated arm drove a broad decline in Claudin‐1, Occludin, ZO‐1 and E‐cadherin, most pronounced in the SI, echoing tight junction loss in murine food allergy under repeated challenge [34]. Concurrent elevation of the contractile marker pMLC and the epithelial stress signal CHI3L1 defines the phenotype as an orchestrated dismantling of the barrier programme rather than the disturbance of any single junctional component. Elevated pMLC reflects perijunctional actomyosin contraction that retracts tight junction proteins before any measurable fall in total protein [35], and whose inhibition attenuates both epithelial leak and IL‐4/IL‐5/IL‐13 release in airway inflammation [36]; CHI3L1 acts as an active driver of junctional disruption whose genetic deletion in murine food allergy preserves epithelial ultrastructure and attenuates severity [37]. Histological quantification resolved a barrier that failed through different junctional components in different compartments, indicating a pathway‐specific trajectory dismantled from within, through mobilisation of the same machinery that maintains it under coupled conditions.

The reshaping registered beyond the tissue. In the R.IgG‐mediated arm, the tissue‐level fall in junctional proteins was mirrored by a reciprocal rise in serum, an inversion strongest under recurrence, weaker under acute IgG‐mediated challenge, and largely absent from the IgE‐mediated groups, tightening into a robust inverse relationship between tissue integrity and circulating barrier burden across the dataset. This directional coupling is most parsimoniously read as active shedding of junctional fragments from injured epithelium, consistent with metalloproteinase‐driven ectodomain cleavage [38, 39]. The shed compartment is not silent: soluble E‐cadherin engages macrophage VEGF/VEGFR2 signalling to amplify IL‐6 and IL‐1β release [40], so fragments shed from a failing barrier can feed the inflammatory tone that drove their release, and this phenotype anchors to the clinical picture, since animals with the lowest tissue integrity and highest circulating burden developed diarrhoea. The systemic footprint extended to microbial content: serum bacterial DNA rose in both recurrent groups but was selectively amplified along the IgG‐mediated arm, with the largest increases in intestinal tissues and the circulation, aligning with clinical evidence of increased circulating bacterial 16S rDNA during anaphylactic episodes [41]. Colonic bacterial DNA increased only after recurrent challenge, indicating that a single provocation was insufficient to breach the colonic barrier. Junctional loss, circulating barrier proteins and bacterial translocation converge as three interlocking axes of a single integrated damage phenotype (Figure S12). Yet this convergence is not necessarily terminal: commensal supplementation restores Occludin, Claudin‐1 and ZO‐1 with concurrent IL‐10 induction in allergic mice [42], reframing collapse as a modifiable state rather than a fixed endpoint, and locating the R.IgG lesion not in an epithelium unable to repair but in one that fails to engage repair. A distinct microbial coordinate operates upstream of the barrier, where microbes can metabolise ingested allergen and dampen the systemic exposure that reaches it [43]; our intravenous challenge deliberately bypasses this coordinate to isolate the effector reaction, and in doing so leaves open whether the severity of the R.IgG collapse we observe is shaped in part by the absence of this upstream damping.

Structural failure did not occur in isolation. It unfolded alongside the second dimension of mucosal allostasis, reparative capacity, which normally engages in coupled operation with injury so that each wave of damage is closed before the next arrives. Under acute challenge, the pathways diverged only marginally: AREG was elevated in the IgE‐mediated arm across pulmonary and intestinal compartments, but EGF, periostin, TFF3 and MUC2 followed heterogeneous distributions. Recurrence resolved this ambiguity. The IgE‐mediated arm mounted a concurrent restitution programme spanning AREG‐ and EGF‐driven proliferation [44, 45], IL‐4/IL‐13‐induced periostin organising matrix deposition [46], and TFF3‐mediated resealing through EGFR‐driven migration over paracellular injury [47], with TFF3 and MUC2 rising most sharply in the SI. The IgG‐mediated arm followed the opposite trajectory: AREG, EGF and periostin remained low, while TFF3 and MUC2 fell most sharply in the SI, precisely where junctional loss and pMLC elevation had been most pronounced. The reparative programme did not engage where the structural insult was greatest. A tissue‐to‐serum inversion mirrored that of the barrier proteins, with TFF3 and MUC2 falling in tissue while rising in serum, most consistent with disrupted compartmentalisation rather than productive local restitution. The strong positive correlation between barrier integrity and the reparative module confirms these two dimensions as arms of a single coupled programme; its uncoupling is the defining mechanistic feature of allostatic collapse (Figures S13 and S14).

Underlying these structural and reparative asymmetries lay a compositional signature that unfolded across cellular source, cytokine environment and effector mediator output, and it diverged sharply between the two arms. The IgE‐mediated response engaged a type 2/regulatory cytokine programme that was already established acutely and largely maintained under recurrence, with the coordinated rise of IL‐10 alongside IL‐4, IL‐5 and IL‐13 positioning IL‐10 not as a counter‐regulator but as an integral component of a resolution‐permissive programme [48, 49, 50]; the IgG‐mediated response instead escalated a TNF‐α/IL‐6‐dominant inflammatory programme progressively across tissue compartments, so that recurrence acted as a selective consolidator rather than a symmetrical amplifier. The mediator profile extended this asymmetry: PAF dominated the IgG‐mediated response and exceeded IgE‐mediated levels under recurrence [51], whereas the IgE‐mediated arm was defined by early‐phase LTC₄ and PGD2 engagement rising further with repetition [52] and by mMCP‐1 as a pathway‐restricted protease signature, with mMCP‐7 rising across both arms in a recurrence‐sensitive manner consistent with the reported dissociation between mucosal and connective tissue mast cell‐associated proteases. Histamine remained more pronounced along the IgE‐mediated arm yet retained a substantial IgG‐mediated component, supporting evidence that murine IgG‐mediated passive anaphylaxis carries a mast cell‐ and histamine‐dependent contribution [53, 54]. At the cellular level, the R.IgE‐mediated arm adopted a type 2‐oriented ILC profile with marked intestinal ILC2 expansion, whereas the recurrent IgG‐mediated arm shifted across all three tissues towards rising ILC1 and NCR+ ILC3 proportions and a contracted ILC2 compartment; ILC2 frequency tracked the type 2/regulatory cytokine and mast cell/lipid mediator modules, while the combined ILC1/NCR+ ILC3 signature carried the strongest correlation with the inflammatory module, identifying the ILC compartment as the convergent cellular motor of the compositional asymmetries observed at the cytokine and mediator levels. The intestinal ILC2 expansion of the IgE‐mediated arm is consistent with the ILC2‐derived amphiregulin axis that supports mucosal restitution, whereas the NCR+ ILC3 expansion of the IgG‐mediated arm, ordinarily associated with IL‐22‐driven epithelial protection, here coincided with structural collapse and defective repair, in line with the context‐dependent loss of ILC3 protective function under sustained inflammatory signalling [55, 56, 57]. The intestinal weighting of this response is unlikely to be incidental. Gut‐resident ILC2s, expanded by tuft‐cell IL‐25, disseminate type 2 immunity from the intestine to the lung [58], defining an axis along which mucosal type 2 tone is set at its intestinal origin. Read against this circuit, our R.IgE arm retains ILC2 output at that origin, whereas the R.IgG arm contracts it across all three compartments, a pattern more consistent with loss at the source of the axis than with redistribution along it.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The hybrid ASA‐PSA design resolved the IgE‐mediated and IgG‐mediated arms under matched acute and recurrent challenge but remains an OVA‐based model that does not capture the antigenic diversity, comorbid barrier states or immunological heterogeneity of human anaphylaxis. The IgG‐mediated arms received R1E4 conditioning and passive anti‐OVA IgG1 transfer in addition to active sensitisation, because active sensitisation alone did not yield a stable, reproducible severe IgG‐mediated reaction. This asymmetry, intrinsic to the design, cannot cleanly separate the conditioning from FcγR engagement as a contributor to the divergence, and remains a limitation the present data cannot fully resolve. One observation constrains it: the transferred IgG1 dose lies within the hypothermic envelope in which graded passive transfer reproduced the active‐sensitisation slope (Figure S2a), so the manipulation does not itself inflate severity relative to the IgE‐mediated arm. This bounds severity but not pathway attribution. Definitive attribution to FcγR engagement will require receptor‐level validation: FcγR‐blocking or FcγR‐deficient challenge, subclass‐resolved transfer, and, most decisively, an FcγR‐humanised model. The integrated associations across cytokine modules, junctional loss, repair mediators, circulating barrier proteins, ILC composition and bacterial DNA define pathway‐specific phenotypes but do not establish causal direction, and the mechanistic links between axes remain hypotheses generated by the observed covariance structure. Phenotypes were characterised at protein and tissue‐composition level; the underlying transcriptional and epigenomic programmes were not assessed and represent a natural next step. Circulating junctional proteins were quantified by ELISA, which does not resolve intact molecules from proteolytic fragments; direct biochemical identification of the circulating species by Western blot or mass spectrometry remains to be established. Bacterial translocation was likewise inferred from tissue and serum DNA burden rather than viable culture. The study was designed as a severe anaphylaxis model, and the small number of surviving animals in the R.IgG‐mediated arm precluded formal comparison and is reported in the Supporting Information; sex‐based effects, which did not reach statistical significance at the current group sizes, are presented graphically in the same section. Finally, terminal tissue measurements offer high‐dimensional resolution across organs but only limited temporal resolution of how inflammatory activation, junctional disruption, failed repair and microbial translocation unfold.

Taken together, these findings recast recurrent severe anaphylaxis as a pathway‐specific reorganisation of mucosal allostasis, in which IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated responses diverge across its immunological, structural and reparative dimensions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to resolve recurrent IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated systemic anaphylaxis in parallel while integrating cytokine architecture, effector mediator engagement, junctional integrity, epithelial repair, circulating barrier‐associated proteins, microbial translocation and innate lymphoid composition within a single mucosal framework. Within this framework, R.IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis emerges as an allostatic collapse phenotype in which these dimensions decouple, leaving structural failure without compensatory repair, while the R.IgE‐mediated response preserves the coupling between injury and restitution. Future studies should determine whether this organisation is recapitulated in human recurrent and refractory anaphylaxis, and whether restoring barrier–repair coupling can alter disease trajectory.

Figure S1: Experimental design of acute and recurrent IgE‐ and IgG‐mediated anaphylaxis. All mice were actively sensitised with ovalbumin (OVA) on Day 0.
