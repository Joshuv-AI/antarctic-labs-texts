Bridging the gap: advanced chromatin biotechnologies for circadian epigenomic profiling in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Sehrish Khan, Raed Said, Christopher Eskiw, François Thuot, Benoit Guay, Petros Papagerakis, Silvana Papagerakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy remain the main therapies for treating head and neck cancers, however new modalities have emerged with an emphasis to understand the complexity of the tumor biology. One emerging layer of complexity is the circadian clock, disrupted in many cancers, contributes to pathogenesis and progression due to its crosstalk with cancer pathways. While transcriptional alterations of core clock genes have been described in Head and Neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), the higher-order epigenetic and chromatin-based mechanisms underlying circadian dysregulation remain poorly defined. In this review, we highlight the potential of several chromatin-based biotechnologies in characterizing circadian clock disruption and identifying epigenetic biomarkers in HNSCC. Powerful assays including Chromatin conformation capture, Chromatin immunoprecipitation, DNA methylation profiling, ATAC-seq and single cell technologies can dissect circadian disruption at multiple regulatory layers. These approaches enable genome-wide mapping of chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, and 3D chromatin organization. Integrating these approaches across HPV-positive (HPV+) and HPV-negative (HPV−) HNSCC provides a framework to determine whether circadian disruption reflects transcriptional deregulation alone or involved structural genome remodeling that actively drives tumor progression. Linking circadian disruption to epigenomic reprogramming in HNSCCs can serve to identify mechanisms of therapeutic resistance and identify vulnerabilities. Advancing chromatin-informed circadian biomarker discovery in HNSCC has the potential to shift precision oncology from static genomic profiling toward dynamic, architecture-based epigenetic regulation.Graphical abstractIntegrated multi-omics framework for circadian epigenomic profiling in HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC. Longitudinal circadian sampling combined with chromatin accessibility profiling (ATAC-seq), histone modification mapping (ChIP-seq), DNA methylation analysis, three-dimensional genome organization (Hi-C), single-cell technologies, spatial genomics, and AI-driven analytics provides a comprehensive framework for identifying circadian biomarkers, therapeutic vulnerabilities, and chronotherapy-based precision medicine strategies.

Circadian rhythms crosstalk with key cellular processes

Circadian rhythms are intrinsic biological processes that align the physiological cellular functions with the day and night cycle [1]. Displaying periods of approximately 24 h, these rhythms synchronize functions such as the sleep–wake cycle, hormone secretion, body temperature regulation as well as cellular key functions including cell division, DNA repair and metabolism [2]. At the molecular level, circadian rhythmicity is governed by a self-regulatory transcriptional-translational feedback loop (TTL) involving key clock genes, including Clock (Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Kaput), Bmal1 (Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like 1), Period (Per1, Per2, Per3), Cryptochrome (Cry1, Cry2), Rev-erbα (NR1D1), and Rorα (RAR-Related Orphan Receptor Alpha). Overview of head and neck cancers (HPV+ and HPV−) etiology, clinical features, and current biomarker limitations

HNSCC arises from mucosal epithelial surfaces of the pharynx, larynx, nose, paranasal sinuses and oral cavity [5]. It is a multifactorial disease caused by exposure to tobacco smoke, long-term alcoholism, betel nut chewing, viruses, genetic polymorphisms and maybe poor oral hygiene [6].

Clinically, patients may present with pain, swelling, bleeding, decreased mobility of the tongue, dysphonia, dysphagia, cranial nerve dysfunction and lymph nodes involvement [7]. HNSCC is a significant health concern worldwide, with approximately 900,000 new cases and 450,000 deaths annually [8, 9]. Incidence and mortality rate vary geographically due to differences in ethnicity, environmental exposure, diet and socioeconomic status [10].

Since 2018, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) has staged oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma separately based on HPV status, reflecting fundamental biological and prognostic differences between HPV positive (HPV+) and HPV negative (HPV−) cancers [11]. The ICON-S international reported a cohort of 2600 patients from which 1906 had HPV + and 696 patients had HPV− oropharyngeal cancer and demonstrating distinct survival outcomes under AJCC 7th edition [11, 12]. In 2022, in the United States, 27% of patients were diagnosed as stages (I-II), 51% were advanced stages (stages III, IVB), and 15% presented with distant metastases (stage IV) [8, 9]. Survival rate varies by stage at diagnosis, with advanced stages having survival rates as low as 34%.

Historically, HNSCC predominantly affected older men (between ages of 55–74) with a history of tobacco/alcohol use and lower socioeconomic status. However, shifting socioeconomic trends have led to a 14% decline in incidence rates of HNSCC in the United States, largely due to a decrease in tobacco use [13]. Emerging factors such as viral infections have broadened the population at risk, with a 15.5% increasing occurrence among younger individuals, three times more prevalent in males but also an increase in females, non-smokers and non-drinkers [8, 9].

Clinically, HPV+ oropharyngeal cancers responds better to treatment (radiotherapy or surgery) and have a higher survival rate [14]. In contrast, HPV- oropharyngeal cancers typically have poor prognosis and greater therapeutic resistance. These differences highlighting the significance of understanding distinct mechanisms of both HPV+ and HPV− squamous cell carcinomas driven HNSCC [14, 15]

Biomarker utility in HNSCC is strongly influenced by the divergent biology and epigenetics of HPV-negative and HPV-positive as summarized in Fig. 2 [15]. While several markers have been identified, their clinical performance remains inconsistent, highlighting ongoing challenges in standardization and the need for more precise and functionally informative biomarkers [16]. The most common biomarker, p16 overexpression (detected by immunohistochemistry), serves as a surrogate for HPV-driven oncogenesis but lacks specificity, as it can be upregulated in HPV− tumors [17]. Detection of HPV DNA confirms viral presence but does not establish transcriptionally active infection, potentially leading to misclassification due to passenger HPV [18]. While E6/E7 mRNA expression is considered the gold standard for confirming active viral oncogenic activity, it is technically demanding and not routinely implemented in clinical practice [19]. Emerging approaches such as circulating HPV DNA (ctDNA) offer promise for non-invasive disease monitoring but remain insufficiently standardized [20]. Overall, current biomarkers in HPV + HNSCC, including p16, HPV DNA, E6/E7 mRNA, and ctDNA, are limited by issues of specificity, inability to consistently confirm functional viral activity and limited predictive power for treatment response, underscoring the need for more precise and functionally informative biomarkers. Circadian disruption as a clinical biomarker in HNSCC

Given the central role of circadian rhythms in regulating DNA repair, cell-cycle progression, apoptosis, and metabolism, it is not surprising that circadian dysregulation has been increasingly implicated in cancer development [31]. Therefore, emerging evidence suggests that circadian rhythm disruption has important diagnostic and prognostic value in cancer.

Multiple studies have demonstrated downregulation of core clock genes in HNSCC [32, 33]. As summarized in Table 1, in 40 patients, Hsu et al. reported significant decreases in PER, CRY and BMAL1 expression [32]. Among these genes, CRY2 showed the most significant decrease with a ninefold downregulation. These alterations correlated with tumor severity, size, age, and survival. Wang et al. developed a circadian rhythm-related gene prognostic index (CRRGPI) composed of nine downstream circadian-associated genes involved in immune regulation, metabolism, and tumour progression in HNSCC [34, 35]. The CRRGPI successfully stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, where high scores likely reflecting greater circadian dysregulation were associated with poorer survival, increased tumour stemness, metabolic reprogramming, p53 mutations, and an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment, while low scores correlated with stronger anti-tumour immune responses and improved prognosis. The authors suggested that circadian-associated biomarkers may have clinical value for predicting prognosis. Another study demonstrated that increased PER1 and CLOCK expression in peripheral blood leukocytes post-surgery was associated with longer patient survival, whereas persistent downregulation correlated with poorer prognosis [36, 37]. Beyond its diagnostic and prognostic value, circadian disruption may also have therapeutic implications. The concept of chronotherapy is based on the principle that treatment efficacy and toxicity can vary according to the time of day due to circadian regulation of drug metabolism, DNA repair, cell-cycle progression, and immune function [38]. In several cancer types, administration of chemotherapy at specific circadian phases has been associated with improved therapeutic responses for example oxaliplatin synergy with PER2 expression rhythm improved treatment outcomes in oral squamous cell carcinoma [39]. Furthermore, pharmacological modulation using small molecules that target core clock components, including REV-ERB agonists and CRY stabilizers, have demonstrated anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in preclinical cancer models. These findings suggest that circadian rhythms may serve not only as biomarkers of disease progression but also as therapeutic targets. However, despite growing evidence linking circadian disruption to cancer progression, the underlying mechanisms driving circadian dysregulation and how it mechanistically contributes to aggressive tumour phenotypes, immune suppression, and tumour progression remain largely unclear. Importantly, these findings can be expanded into evaluating that circadian disruption may not only alter downstream gene expression but may also be associated with broader changes in chromatin accessibility and genome organization, where more aggressive tumours may exhibit distinct chromatin states and regulatory architectures compared to less aggressive tumours, supporting chromatin organization as a potential mechanistic biomarker in HNSCC.Table 1Circadian gene dysregulation in cancersStudy (author, year)[32][38][40]Cancer tissueHNSCCBreast, lung, HNSCC, colorectalHematologic, HepaticGeneBMAL1, PER, CRYPER, CRYBMAL1, CLOCKGene expression changeDownregulatedDownregulatedUpregulatedClinical outcomeLower survivalLower survivalIncreased tumor aggressivenessProliferation

Layers of epigenetic rewiring of the circadian clock in HNSCC

Beyond transcriptional feedback loops, the circadian clock is tightly controlled by epigenetic regulation. The mechanisms involve reversible modifications that alter gene expression without changing the underlying DNA sequence (Fig. 3). They shape circadian timing by influencing chromatin accessibility, transcriptional amplitude, protein abundance, nuclear trafficking, and proteasomal turnover of clock components [41]. In cancer, these same epigenetic systems are frequently reprogrammed. Altered DNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNA activity, and higher-order chromatin structure can destabilize rhythmic control over pathways central to tumor biology, including proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism, and DNA repair [41, 42]. This positions the circadian epigenome as a mechanistic bridge linking clock disruption to tumorigenesis.Fig. 3Epigenetic mechanisms tightly control gene expression and chromatin architecture. DNA methylation typically occurs at CpG islands and, when present in gene promoter regions, leads to transcriptional repression by preventing transcription factor binding and recruiting chromatin-condensing proteins [6]. Histone modifications including methylation (Me), acetylation (Ac), phosphorylation (P), ubiquitination (Ub), and sumoylation (SUMO) modulate chromatin structure and accessibility. Histone acetylation is generally associated with an open chromatin state (euchromatin) favorable for transcription, whereas methylation can either repress or activate gene expression depending on the specific residue modified [41]. RNA interference (RNAi) involves small non-coding RNAs such as siRNAs or miRNAs, that bind to complementary mRNA transcripts, leading to their degradation or translational repression, thus blocking gene expression post-transcriptionally [6]. At a higher level of genome organization, chromatin looping, mediated by architectural proteins such as Cohesin, brings distant regulatory DNA elements, including enhancers, into proximity with target promoters. These three-dimensional interactions enable precise spatial and temporal control of gene expression [43]

Four major types of epigenetic mechanisms are known: DNA methylation, histone modification, ribonucleic acid interference (RNAi) and chromatin organization [6]. DNA methylation consists of the addition or removal of a methyl group at the 5th carbon of cytosine residues within CpG dinucleotides (regions of cytosine linked to a guanine), catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMTA3A, DNMT3B) [44]. This modification is essential for normal development by regulating DNA–protein interactions, chromatin structure, and gene expression dysregulation of which is a hallmark of cancer. Promoter CpG island hypermethylation frequently represses tumor suppressor genes, whereas global hypomethylation can promote chromosomal instability [6]. In head and neck cancers, aberrant hypermethylation commonly affects genes involved in cell-cycle (CDKN2A/p16), apoptosis (p14), cell–cell adhesion (E-cadherin), and DNA repair (MGMT: Methylguanine-DNA Methyltransferase) [6]. PER proteins have been linked to rhythmic regulation of global DNA methylation, and disruption of PER function can blunt these oscillations [45]. These findings suggest that disruption of circadian regulation may contribute to aberrant DNA methylation landscapes in HNSCC, linking temporal control of gene expression to epigenetic instability. Histone modifications regulate chromatin structure and accessibility by altering histone tails, thereby modulating how tightly DNA is packed around nucleosomes (~ 147 DNA base pairs wrapped around histone octamers composed of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4) [6]. These modifications control the transition between transcriptionally heterochromatin (inactive) and euchromatin (active) states shown to follow a circadian rhythm [46]. Several examples illustrate direct links to the core clock machinery; the histone acetyltransferase (HAT) p300 acetylates Histone 3 lysine 14 (H3K14) and H3K9, at promoters of clock-controlled genes to promote chromatin opening and transcription. In contrast, SIRT1 (Sirtuin-1) deacetylates BMAL1, to suppress transcriptional output [47, 48]. PER proteins further modulate chromatin states by recruiting histone-modifying complexes. For example, histone deacetylase (HDAC2) forms a complex with PER2 protein at the PER1 promoter to deacetylate H3K9, locally enforcing a repressive chromatin state [49]. External cues can rapidly influence chromatin dynamics; light pulses stimulate rapid acetylation of histones at the PER2 promoter in the mouse SCN [50]. Together, these studies support a model in which acetylation [47], SUMOylating [51], phosphorylation [52], methylation [53] are integral to circadian regulation. In cancer, disruption of histone modifications may contribute to aberrant chromatin states, suggesting that circadian dysregulation reflects broader epigenetic remodeling processes rather than isolated gene expression changes. A third layer of epigenetic regulations involves small non-coding RNAs, including small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), which modulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by targeting specific mRNAs for degradation for translational repression [54]. In oropharyngeal cell models, altered miRNA expression have been linked to epigenetic silencing, often driven by DNA hypermethylation, suggesting coordinated disruption across epigenetic layers in HNSCC [6]. Several miRNAs have been directly implicated in circadian regulation. For example, miR-34a modulates PER2/BMAL1 expression via SIRT1, miR-107 represses CLOCK, and miR-9 modulates BMAL1. Others, such as miR-21 and the miR-106b-25 cluster, act through signalling pathways like NF-κB, STAT3, AKT, and TGF-β that intersect with circadian regulation. These observations suggest that miRNA dysregulation represents a key interface between epigenetic remodeling and circadian disruption, contributing to tumor progression in HNSCC [55]. At a higher level, three-dimensional (3D) genome organization plays a critical role in coordinating circadian gene expression [56]. Chromosomes occupy discrete regions within the nuclear volume, known as chromosome territories. These territories are not static; and dynamically reposition in response to cellular cues. Within a chromosome territory, topologically associated domains (TADs) represent insulated interaction neighborhoods in which genomic elements interact more frequently than with regions outside the domain [57, 58]. TADs serve as fundamental units of genome organization and typically contain groups of genes regulated by shared transcription factors and regulatory elements, enabling coordinated control of gene expression [56, 59]. TAD boundaries are maintained by architectural proteins such as CTCF (CCCTC-binding factor) and cohesin, which insulate enhancer activity and restrict regulatory interactions to appropriate targets [60]. This 3D genomic framework is particularly relevant for circadian biology, as thousands of genes exhibit rhythmic expression, necessitating precise temporal and spatial coordination of regulatory interactions. Circadian control is therefore increasingly understood to involve rhythmic chromatin remodeling, including time-dependent histone modifications and dynamic enhancer-promoter looping that enable phase-specific transcriptional activation or repression [3]. For example, the clock gene CRY1 forms a dynamic enhancer-promoter loop only at night located within its first intron [61]. Similarly, in a study by Furlan-Magaril et al., Hi-C studies in mice further support circadian cycling in chromosomal contacts [62]. Although, the precise mechanism of loop formation is not understood, the physical distances between the core clock genes serve as a predictors of their transcriptional output [3]. Together, these studies highlight the dynamic nature of genome folding in regulating circadian gene expression. Importantly, when chromatin topology is disrupted, as frequently occurs in cancer, these 3D changes can generate stable, disease-specific expression programs that may serve as biomarkers [63]. For example, HPV integration has been shown to reshape chromatin architecture by introducing CTCF binding sites, thereby disrupting TAD structures and activating oncogenic programs [64].

Although, the precise mechanism of loop formation is not understood, the physical distances between the core clock genes serve as a predictors of their transcriptional output [3]. Together, these studies highlight the dynamic nature of genome folding in regulating circadian gene expression. Importantly, when chromatin topology is disrupted, as frequently occurs in cancer, these 3D changes can generate stable, disease-specific expression programs that may serve as biomarkers [63]. For example, HPV integration has been shown to reshape chromatin architecture by introducing CTCF binding sites, thereby disrupting TAD structures and activating oncogenic programs [64].

Chromatin-based advanced biotechnologies to evaluate circadian biomarkers in HNSCC

Chromatin-based advanced biotechnologies have transformed cancer research by providing insights into mechanisms of genome organization, chromatin accessibility and epigenetic regulation alteration in diseases [65]. Building on the mechanistic framework described above, these approaches provide powerful tools to dissect how circadian dysregulation is embedded within chromatin architecture and to identify epigenetic biomarkers in HNSCC.

Chromatin conformation capture (3C, 4C, Hi-C): mapping 3D genome architecture

Chromatin conformation capture (3C) enables the study of three-dimensional (3D) genome organization, by detecting physical interactions between distal genomic elements (enhancers, promoters and genes). These interactions are particularly relevant in cancer, where disruption of chromatin loops and TADs can lead to oncogenic transcriptional deregulation [66]. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, 3C is based on the principal that genomic regions in close spatial proximity can be “captured” through formaldehyde crosslinking [67]. Crosslinked chromatin is digested using restriction enzymes (e.g. HinDIII, DpnII) generating “sticky” ends. The overhangs or blunt ends are then filled with nucleotides and labeled with biotin. Next, the free ends of digested DNA are ligated together essentially creating chimeric fragments from two different loci that reflect spatial proximity. These ligation products can be detected by PCR (polymerase chain reaction) and sequencing to identify interacting regions. Several derivative versions exist to expand the scale and resolution of 3C-based approaches (Circular Chromosome Conformation Capture (4C) and High-Throughput Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) to [67]. 4C builds on 3C by enabling unbiased, genome-wide scanning for all regions that interact with a single locus of interest (“one-vs-all”) making it powerful for identifying novel enhancer-promoter or regulatory associations beyond a predetermined set of contacts. In 4C, after standard crosslinking and restriction enzyme digestion, a second restriction enzyme is used and circular ligation is performed followed by inverse PCR and sequencing to map interaction partners. In contrast, High-throughput Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) captures genome-wide chromatin interactions (“all-vs-all”). After crosslinking, digestion, biotin labeling and proximity ligation, pair-end sequencing produces interaction maps that reveal global genome folding features topologically associated domains (TADs), chromatin loops, and A/B compartments (Active/Inactive compartments). Hi-C maps have shown that disruptions in TAD boundaries or abnormal looping patterns contribute to oncogene activation, and underlie structural genome instability in cancer [68, 69]. Collectively, these studies establish that 3D chromatin organization is not merely a structural feature of the genome but functions as an active regulatory layer governing circadian dynamics, oncogenic rewiring, and tumor subtype specification. However, despite extensive evidence of circadian disruption, genomic instability, and transcriptional subtype heterogeneity in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), the three-dimensional genome architecture underlying these processes remains uncharacterized. This represents a critical gap, particularly given that HNSCC provides a uniquely informative context: HPV-positive tumors feature viral integration events and chromatin remodeling, whereas HPV-negative tumors are mainly driven by Tp53 mutations, replication stress, and high structural variation burden. Although, both tumour types exhibit altered circadian gene expression, yet no study to date has systematically mapped compartment dynamics, TAD organization, enhancer–promoter loop networks, or structural variant-driven chromatin rewiring at circadian loci in HNSCC. Addressing this gap is essential to determine whether circadian disruption in HNSCC reflects transcriptional deregulation alone or a manifestation of higher-order chromatin architectural remodeling that actively drives tumor progression.

ChIP and ChIP-seq: defining histone landscapes and transcription factor binding at circadian loci

While 3C-based approaches capture how the genome is folded, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays reveal which proteins occupy specific genomic regions, including transcription factors and histone marks [72]. This distinction is critical for circadian biology, where time-of-day-dependent transcription is coordinated through rhythmic changes in chromatin state. Accordingly, genome-wide ChIP-seq profiling can identify active and repressed regulatory elements, oncogenic enhancers, and altered chromatin states that may serve as tumor-specific epigenetic biomarkers. As shown in Fig. 4b ChIP, the cells are crosslinked (commonly with formaldehyde) to preserve protein-DNA interactions. After cell lysis and chromatin fragmentation, ChIP-grade antibodies are added which bind to the protein of interest. In turn, protein A/G coated beads are added that bind to the antibody-protein-DNA complexes, allowing for their physical separation from the rest of the chromatin. The crosslinks are then reversed, and the remaining DNA fragments originally associated with the protein are purified and sequenced using high-throughput methods (ChIP-seq), revealing the genome-wide binding maps [72]. Chromatin Immunoprecipitation addresses several cancer-relevant questions: (1) which transcription factors bind promoters/enhancers of genes driving tumor growth (2) whether overexpression aligns with active histone marks (3) whether regulatory circuitry is rewired by comparing tumor vs normal chromatin occupancy patterns [73]. Across HNSCC, including tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), BMAL1 consistently emerges as a tumor-suppressive circadian regulator, whose loss promotes proliferation and chemoresistance [74, 75]. In both TSCC and NPC, BMAL1 is epigenetically dysregulated either through promoter hypermethylation or chromatin-mediated repression. Reactivation of BMAL1 suppresses oncogenic targets (e.g., TERT, CDK5) while increasing sensitivity to paclitaxel or cisplatin [74, 75]. However, despite these mechanistic insights, no study has systematically compared BMAL1 chromatin occupancy, histone landscapes (e.g., H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K27me3), or enhancer rewiring between HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC. It also remains unclear whether HPV oncogenes (E6/E7) reshape circadian chromatin architecture or alter BMAL1 binding at enhancers to regulate metabolic, immune or survival programs. Comprehensive ChIP-seq analyses in HPV + versus HPV− models could define subtype-specific circadian epigenetic programs, uncover functional interaction between circadian transcription factors cooperate and viral oncogenic signalling pathways.

DNA methylation profiling: measuring stable epigenetic silencing at circadian loci

DNA Methylation Profiling captures methylation patterns that can lock genes into long-term repression or activation states [79]. This approach relies on sodium bisulfite conversion, which converts unmethylated cytosines (C) to uracil (U), read as thymine (T) following PCR amplification, while methylated cytosines remain unchanged (Fig. 4c). Subsequent amplification and sequencing allows quantitative assessment of DNA methylation of promoters and enhancers, facilitating the identification of disease-associated epigenetic alterations [79]. Several platforms fall into the DNA methylation profiling, including array-based approaches (e.g., Illumina 450 K and EPIC arrays), reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS), targeted bisulfite sequencing (TBS-seq), whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), each offering different levels of genomic coverage and resolution [34, 35]. Although promoter methylation of circadian genes such as CRY1, PER1 and PER3 has been described in cancers including chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), systematic DNA methylation profiling of the core circadian network has not been performed in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Existing studies are restricted to individual genes and limited CpG array platforms, leaving enhancer regions and broader clock components unexplored. Given the well-established epigenetic dysregulation and molecular heterogeneity of HNSCC, comprehensive methylation profiling of circadian loci across tumor subtypes, including HPV-positive and HPV-negative disease, represents a significant gap in the field.

ATAC-seq: identifying chromatin accessibility in circadian dysregulation

Another emerging biotechnology that complements these approaches is Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with Sequencing (ATAC-Seq). This technique provides genome-wide mapping of chromatin accessibility. Accessible chromatin regions provide information on open regulatory regions and provide insights into transcriptional regulatory landscapes that may be dynamically modulated across the circadian cycle or disrupted in cancer. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, ATAC-Seq employs the enzyme Tn5 transposase to cleave open chromatin and tag accessible regions of chromatin with sequencing adapters. These tagged DNA fragments are then amplified, sequencing and aligned to a reference genome [83]. Despite increasing evidence that circadian regulators reshape chromatin accessibility in hormone-driven and metabolic cancers, their role in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains poorly defined. While dysregulation of core clock genes such as BMAL1, PERs, CRYs, and REV-ERBα has been reported in HNSCC, there is limited mechanistic understanding of how these factors influence chromatin accessibility and enhancer activity in this disease. Most existing studies in HNSCC rely on gene expression analyses without integrating ATAC-seq or functional chromatin profiling to determine whether circadian disruption directly reprograms regulatory landscapes. Furthermore, whether circadian-mediated chromatin remodeling contributes to HPV status specific differences, therapy resistance, or metastatic potential in HNSCC remains an open question. Addressing these questions will be critical to define how circadian dysregulation is embedded within chromatin accessibility landscapes and contributes to tumor heterogeneity in HNSCC.

Single-cell based technologies for HNSCC diagnosis

Tumors are heterogeneous, adding a significant layer of complexity to cancer progression [88]. Within a single tumor, cells can show distinct genetic, epigenetic, local invasion and metastatic potential [89]. Tumor heterogeneity arises from chromosomal instability, microRNAs, tumor microenvironment, cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) and is one of the primary reasons why cancer treatments fail [90]. So far, the mutational landscape of head and neck cancers has been heavily based on bulk sequencing technologies which fails to capture the individual cells within a tumor. Recently, there has been a shift from bulk sequencing technologies to single-cell sequencing. Bulk methods average signals across a population of cells, masking the distinct profiles of rare or unique cell subpopulations [91]. On the other hand, single cell sequencing technologies have emerged as powerful tools to address these limitations. Single-cell approaches are a broad term for a variety of technologies (sc-genomics, sc-epigenomics, sc-transcriptomics) that not only evaluate individual cells at the genomic and epigenomic level but also analyze immune infiltration, tumor heterogeneity, tumor microenvironment and more recently cell specific circadian clock dynamics [92, 93]. Although single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed profound differences in immune-cell composition, intercellular communication networks, and B-cell-associated prognostic signatures between HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC, the extent to which circadian clock dysregulation influences these cellular interactions and immune states remains largely unknown [92, 94]. In the studies summarized above, circadian rhythm disruption (CRD) scores were derived from snapshot measurements of circadian gene expression rather than direct assessment of temporal oscillations across the circadian cycle. Consequently, these studies infer circadian dysregulation from transcriptional profiles but do not directly determine whether individual cells exhibit altered 24-h rhythmicity. Furthermore, integrating additional molecular layers, including protein abundance, post-translational modifications, chromatin accessibility, epigenetic regulation, and spatial transcriptomics, will be necessary to establish causal relationships between circadian disruption and tumor progression, as circadian regulation occurs at multiple levels beyond mRNA expression. Therefore, advanced single-cell approaches, including scATAC-seq, single-cell multiome sequencing, and chromatin conformation technologies, offer promising opportunities to characterize cell-specific circadian states, identify regulatory elements controlling clock gene expression, and elucidate epigenetic mechanisms underlying HPV-associated tumor progression.

Emerging technologies: spatial epigenomics and artificial intelligence approaches

Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas exhibit substantial spatial heterogeneity, with distinct tumor regions displaying differences in immune infiltration, hypoxia, stromal interactions, and therapeutic response [99]. While recent single-cell studies have improved our understanding of cellular heterogeneity, they generally require tissue dissociation and therefore lose information regarding the spatial organization of cells within the tumor microenvironment [100]. As circadian regulation is influenced by both intrinsic molecular mechanisms and extrinsic environmental cues, preserving spatial context may be critical for understanding how circadian dysregulation contributes to tumor progression. Emerging technologies such as Xenium and Visium HD offer spatial transcriptomics, preserve tissue architecture while providing high-resolution molecular profiling [101]. For example, Xenium-based spatial transcriptomic analysis of patient-derived HNSCC organotypic cultures identified distinct transcriptional states between invasive and non-invasive tumor regions and revealed mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance [102]. Although spatial transcriptomic approaches have not yet been extensively applied to investigate circadian dysregulation in HPV + and HPV- HNSCC, they can be used to determine whether clock gene expression and circadian networks vary across spatially distinct tumor niches, including invasive fronts, hypoxic regions and immune-rich environments.

The increasing complexity of transcriptomic, epigenomic, spatial, and chromatin accessibility datasets has also driven the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning approaches in cancer research. In HNSCC, AI-based frameworks have been used to improve prognostic stratification and identify clinically relevant biomarkers [103]. Multi-omic integration approaches have successfully characterized cancer-associated fibroblast populations and uncovered novel therapeutic targets [104]. Although these approaches have not yet been widely utilized to investigate circadian dysregulation, they provide a promising framework for merging transcriptomic, epigenomic, spatial, and chromatin accessibility datasets.

Current challenges of advanced chromatin-based biotechnologies

Despite significant advances in single-cell and chromatin-based technologies, several challenges continue to limit their application in circadian cancer research. A major obstacle is that most available datasets represent a single snapshot in time, whereas circadian rhythms are dynamic processes that require longitudinal sampling across multiple time points to accurately characterize 24-h oscillatory behavior. Consequently, many studies infer circadian disruption from clock gene expression signatures rather than directly measuring rhythmic gene expression, chromatin accessibility, or transcription factor activity over time [36, 37, 105].

Technical limitations also remain a significant challenge for single cell technologies. Cell isolation procedures can affect cell viability and alter gene expression profiles [93]. Furthermore, the low quantity of DNA and RNA in individual cells requires amplification steps that can introduce technical biases and distort gene expression measurements [106]. Single-cell datasets are also highly complex and require sophisticated bioinformatic analyses, which can be affected by technical noise, limited cell numbers, and incomplete representation of the original cell population [106]. For chromatin conformation technologies such as Hi-C and ChIP, experimental workflows involving chromatin digestion, ligation, and amplification, can introduce technical while differences in restriction enzymes, library preparation strategies and analytical pipelines hinder comparisons across studies [107].

DNA methylation profiling faces additional challenges, including variability in bisulfite conversion efficiency, high sequencing costs, and limited validation of many candidate biomarkers in large and diverse patient cohorts [108]. Similarly, ATAC-seq is highly sensitive to sample preparation, nuclei isolation, and library construction conditions, requiring extensive protocol optimization [109]. In addition, limitations in genome annotations, challenges in nucleosome positioning, and the absence of standardized bioinformatic pipelines can complicate data interpretation [110].

Importantly, the application of advanced chromatin-based technologies to HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC remains limited. Most existing studies have focused on characterizing tumor heterogeneity, immune cell composition, and intercellular communication while giving little attention to circadian regulation. Addressing these challenges will require the development of longitudinal multiomic studies, improved computational methods, and integrated experimental frameworks capable of simultaneously capturing temporal, spatial, and epigenetic dimensions of circadian biology.

Conclusion

Chromatin-based technologies have significantly advanced our understanding of how genome organization, epigenetic regulation, and transcriptional control shape cancer biology. Approaches such as Hi-C, ChIP-seq, DNA methylation profiling, ATAC-seq, and emerging single-cell methodologies have revealed that circadian regulation extends far beyond rhythmic gene expression and is intricately linked to chromatin architecture, enhancer activity, epigenetic remodeling, and three-dimensional genome organization. Evidence from multiple cancer types suggests that circadian clock components can function as either tumor suppressors or oncogenic drivers in a context-dependent manner, often through interactions with chromatin regulatory networks.

Despite these advances, the relationship between circadian disruption and chromatin organization remains largely unexplored in HNSCC, particularly in the context of HPV + and HPV- subtypes.

Given the profound molecular, epigenetic, and clinical differences between these subtypes, understanding how HPV oncogenes reshape circadian regulatory networks and chromatin landscapes represents a critical unmet need. Addressing this gap will be critical to determine whether circadian disruption reflects transcriptional changes or higher-order chromatin reorganization driving tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Integrative chromatin based advanced biotechnologies hold strong potential to identify subtype-specific circadian biomarkers, and to shift precision oncology toward dynamic, epigenome-informed strategies for cancer diagnosis and treatment. Together, these advances position chromatin-informed circadian biology as a promising framework for uncovering novel biomarkers and therapeutic vulnerabilities in HNSCC.
