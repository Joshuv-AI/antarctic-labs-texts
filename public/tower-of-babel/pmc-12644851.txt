Performance of a bionic Carangidae robot fish based on a dielectric elastomer material
Authors: Chenghong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarangidae fish feature streamlined, oval, or rhomboid bodies with flat and high sides, making them adept at swimming swiftly through water with minimal resistance. Dielectric elastomers (DEs), known for their high strain, rapid response times, and high electromechanical coupling efficiency under an electric field, are intelligent materials suitable for creating various actuators widely employed in flexible bionic robots. By utilizing smart material-driven soft fins, bionic robot fish can more realistically simulate the movements of actual fish, improving their swimming performance. In this study, we employed a DE-based actuator to develop a bionic robot fish resembling Carangidae, driven by fins. Leveraging the minimum energy structure of DE material, we designed a structure inspired by the Carangidae family to replicate the fin swing of the bionic robot fish. We investigated the swimming behavior of this bionic robot fish under sinusoidal voltage signals of varying amplitudes and frequencies. The bionic robot fish swims in the medium and/or paired fin (MPF) propulsion mode, achieving a maximum speed of 8.6 mm/s. This work presents a novel scheme and theoretical foundation for the application of dielectric elastomers in bionic Carangidae robots.Keywords: Bionic carangidae robot fish, Dielectric elastomer minimum energy structure (DEMES), Soft actuators, Dielectric elastomer (DE), Medium and/Or paired fin (MPF)Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

DE is an advanced elastomer material characterized by a high dielectric constant, enabling it to change shape in response to an applied electric field. Upon removal of the external electric field, the material swiftly reverts to its original form. This transformation involves the generation of stress and strain, whereby electrical energy is efficiently converted into mechanical energy. DE materials offer several advantages, including high energy density, rapid response times, low cost, and robust resistance to magnetic interference. As emerging artificial materials, DEs are applied in soft muscles and demonstrate particular utility in various fields, such as medical equipment1,2, adaptive optics3, mobile robots4, mobile manipulators5, and magnetic resonance imaging-compatible manipulators6. DE materials are well suited for constructing soft-structured bionic robot fish, facilitating the simulation of diverse fish swimming movements and enhancing the environmental adaptability of these robots.

DE materials still face numerous challenges when applied to underwater robotic fish. DEAs are electrically responsive smart soft materials polarized at high voltages that exhibit numerous benefits, such as lightweight design, high drive strain, and rapid response. Despite requiring excitation voltages typically exceeding kilovolts, the associated current remains very low (milliamps), making battery modules sufficient for sustained robotic fish operation. Furthermore, their low noise levels, density approximating that of water, and inherent transparency render DEAs ideal propulsion materials for underwater robotic fish.

Sharma proposed an analytical framework for studying the nonlinear dynamic behavior of nonisoviscous hyperelastic DEMES actuators with basic rectangular geometries7. Khurana proposed a numerical framework based on finite elements for simulating the electromechanical behavior of nonlinear anisotropic dielectric elastomer actuators under finite strain8. Kumar developed a new modified dielectric constant model for dielectric elastomer materials on the basis of the fundamental laws of physics9. The modified dielectric constant model successfully incorporates the effects of prestretching and frequency parameters on the dielectric constant of dielectric elastomers.

Underwater soft bionic robot fish can be divided into the following types according to swimming method: the breaststroke type, the tail fin propulsion type, the pushing type, and the pectoral fin swing type. The breaststroke soft bionic robot fish uses a DEA as a robot leg, which strokes backward to propel the bionic robot fish forward. The caudal fin propulsive soft bionic robot fish uses a DEA as the tail fin or drives the tail fin swing to push the bionic robot fish underwater. In the pushing soft bionic robot fish, several DEAs are arranged in a ring structure. When the DEAs are opened, water flows through the bionic robot fish, suspending the bionic robot fish. When the DEAs are closed, the water moves around the fish, causing the bionic robot fish to float. In the oscillating pectoral fin soft bionic robot fish, the DEA is used to drive the pectoral fin by oscillating strokes, allowing the bionic robot fish to swim underwater.

Li constructed a frog-like soft bionic robot fish using 4 DEAs as frog legs, achieving a maximum moving speed of 132 mm/s10. Inspired by the larvae of eels, Christianson created a transparent underwater bionic robot fish using three sections of fluid electrode DEAs. This bionic robot fish exhibited a maximum swimming speed of 1.9 mm/s and a Froude propulsion efficiency of 52%11. Cheng designed a cordless soft robot jellyfish by pouring six silicone fins over a DEA. This jellyfish had a maximum motion speed of 5 mm/s12. Zhang integrated a DEA with 2 flexible wave fins and a portable power source to create a cordless soft bionic robot fish13. Xu developed a DEA with a solid‒liquid interpenetrating network structure and used it to produce a soft bionic ray robot fish with a maximum swimming speed of 5.7 mm/s14. Li created a cordless soft cyborg fish that integrates a DEA, a power supply, and a remote control system. This fish can swim rapidly underwater via remote control15. Inspired by the lionfish in the Mariana Trench, Li developed a deep-sea bionic robot fish that was successfully tested in the Mariana Trench at a depth of 10,900 m and could swim freely in the South China Sea at a depth of 3,224 m16. The Godaba people developed a jellyfish robot using a piezoelectric film. Deformation of the film changes the volume of the air cavity, generating propulsion by expelling water from the body17. ‌Ren designed a miniature flexible flapping wing robot using a low-voltage, high-durability, and power-dense dielectric elastomer optimized with novel multilayer technology and electrode materials. This type of robot is resistant to damage18. Tang developed a flexible bionic pipeline robot that employs a dielectric elastomer actuator as an artificial muscle, an intelligent composite microstructure, and a magnet with an adjustable number of cells for rapid assembly of a soft robot19.

In this study, inspired by the ontological structure and swimming mechanism of fish, a soft robotic fish capable of swimming in the MPF propulsion mode is proposed. This robotic fish is constructed from soft materials and utilizes a dielectric elastomer material. The following aspects are comprehensively introduced: the model, fabrication method, and characteristics of the bionic fish robot. The design, prototype development, and testing of a drive unit, which employs a dielectric elastomer actuator to mimic the bending motion of the fish’s trunk and tail fin, are described in detail. This includes the production of a bending actuator, a drive unit, and a robot prototype. The performance of the robotic fish is rigorously evaluated by incorporating a bionic design into its body structure to match the deformation pattern of the soft drive unit. A coupling relation is established between the swimming speed and the driving parameters of the robotic fish. Owing to its enhanced propulsion speed and flexible trajectory control, the proposed soft robotic fish achieves swimming speeds of up to 8.6 mm/s. This study significantly contributes to the design of soft underwater robots and highlights the vast potential for the expansion of flexible drive methods in a wide range of flexible devices and soft robots.

Principle of the DE driving mechanism

When DE is the actuator material for a soft bionic robot fish, it operates as a parallel plate capacitor. The two surfaces of the DE are coated with flexible electrodes, between which a voltage is applied. The opposite charges distributed on these electrodes attract each other, generating an attractive force termed the Maxwell force. This force causes the thickness of the DE to decrease while its area expands. Upon turning off the power, the electrostatic pressure drops instantly to zero, and the DE swiftly reverts to its initial state, effectively converting electrical energy into mechanical energy. Minimum energy structure of the DE material

The DEMES is an actuator that outputs an angle. It consists of a prestretched DE film, a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrate, and stiffeners23. Owing to its self-viscosity, the DE film is tightly bonded with the PET substrate and reinforcement components. The structure of the DEMES actuator is illustrated in Fig. 2.

DEME actuator structure. (a) Voltage off, (b) Voltage on.

The prestretching treatment of the DE film imparts certain prestress characteristics. Under the combined action of the DE film and PET reinforcement components, the overall structure exhibits unique saddle-like deformation. In this state, the properties of the DE film and the flexible PET substrate interact, and part of the strain energy in the film is converted into the bending deformation potential energy of the PET substrate.

When a voltage is applied to the DEMES actuator, the DE film experiences Maxwell stress, causing it to be extruded in the thickness direction and expanded in the length and width directions. This results in a reduction in the internal stress within the DE film. Consequently, the balance between the properties of the DE film and the flexible PET substrate is disrupted, altering the original structural equilibrium. The overall structure gradually flattens, and the bending deformation energy of the PET substrate decreases until a new equilibrium state is reached.

The output angle of the DEMES actuator is directly related to the applied voltage. Within the voltage limit that prevents breakdown of the DE film, a higher applied voltage leads to more pronounced structural recovery and a larger output angle of the actuator. When the voltage is sufficiently high, the internal stress of the DE film is completely eliminated, allowing the flexible PET substrate to fully return to a planar state.

Design of the bending actuator structure

In the design of the DEMES actuator, the flexible base typically features a hollow area with a double semicircle or rectangular shape. This area is precisely hollowed out from the PET sheet using a laser cutting machine. The shape of the actuator electrode is adapted on the basis of the frame geometry, ensuring that the area remains consistent while maintaining optimal driving performance. Since the width of the electrode directly influences the torque, it is kept constant. In cases where the electrodes are asymmetrical, the center of the area is positioned close to the bending position to ensure balanced performance. When a bionic robot fish modeled after the Carangidae family is created, keeping the length and stiffness of the frame attached to the caudal fin’s side as constant as possible is crucial. The bionic Carangidae robot fish is propelled by flexible fins, with two fins symmetrically positioned on either side of its body. These fins enable smooth swimming motions when driven in unison. Connection angle

To determine the optimal angle of the connector component, which remains fixed and unaffected by the initial state of the bent drive, a series of drive tests were conducted at 30° intervals. During these tests, the ends of the bonded actuators were secured, and a sinusoidal voltage signal with amplitude ranging from 0 to 4 kV and frequency of 0.1 Hz was applied to one side of the actuator. This setup allowed measurement of the maximum change in the angle of the connector and the maximum force generated at the front end of the connector during horizontal bending action. To comprehensively evaluate the driving performance, both the angle change and the force were considered; these values were multiplied and compared.

The bionic robot fish head was constructed from carbon fiber-reinforced plastic (CFRP), measuring 160 mm in length and 60 mm in height, and features a driving area of 1000 mm². A structural diagram of the bionic robot fish is provided in Fig. 4, which illustrates its overall design.

Bionic robot fish fabrication processes based on Carangidae

The process of fabricating the bionic robotic fish based on Carangidae involves the following steps:

Frame and reinforcement cutting.

Frame and reinforcement cutting.

The designed PET frame and reinforcement components are cut and formed using a laser cutting machine to ensure precision and accuracy.

(2)DE film prestretching.

DE film prestretching.

The DE film is cut to the required size and prestretched by 400% along two perpendicular directions. The prestretched frame is then placed on the stretched DE film, ensuring that the edge of the frame is evenly attached across the film to maintain uniformity and avoid stress concentrations.

(3)Electrode coating.

Electrode coating.

The material is cut and attached to the prestretched DE film. An electrode is evenly coated across the hollow area of the frame to facilitate electrical conduction.

(4)Film stacking and electrode attachment.

Film stacking and electrode attachment.

Wires and electrodes are attached between the DE film components. A prestretched DE film coated with electrodes on one side is carefully stacked on top of the film without electrodes, first ensuring that one side of the film maintains even contact to prevent power-on breakdown due to the presence of bubbles.

(5)Support frame and reinforcement attachment.

Support frame and reinforcement attachment.

The PET flexible support frame is attached to one side of the stacked film assembly. An electrode is evenly coated on the outside of the two film layers for additional electrical conductivity. The reinforcement component is attached to the other side of the film to provide structural integrity and support.

(6)Fin wire connections.

Fin wire connections.

The silicone wires of the two fins are connected with Teflon wire, serving as positive wires for the fins. This ensures electrical continuity and efficient energy transfer to the fins for propulsion.

Comparison of the swimming speeds of the bionic robot fish

The propulsion speed of the bionic robot fish based on Carangidae is significantly influenced by various kinematic parameters, particularly the frequency of the driving voltage signal. To explore this relationship, a series of experiments were conducted with imitation robotic trevally fish in an insulating fluid environment.

During these experiments, the swimming speed of the robotic fish was measured at different frequencies of the driving voltage signal. A marker was placed on the head of the robotic fish, and its swimming process was recorded at 300 frames per second (fps) using a CASIO camera positioned above the swimming area. By analyzing the middle 10 s of each swimming sequence, the distance traveled by the marker in the straight direction (x-axis) was accurately calculated.

The driving voltage frequency varied from 1 Hz to 10 Hz, with each experiment increasing the frequency by 1 Hz. This systematic approach allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of how changes in the driving voltage frequency impact the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish.

Relation between the frequency of the driving voltage signal and the propulsion speed of the bionic fish

A sinusoidal voltage signal with an amplitude of 4.5 kV was used to drive the pectoral fins of the bionic Carangidae robot fish, which swam in the MPF propulsion mode. This setup enabled a systematic investigation into the variation in the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish under different frequencies of the driving voltage signal. As shown in Fig. 6, the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish exhibited distinct phase-dependent characteristics with changes in the voltage signal frequency: in the initial stage of increasing frequency, the swimming speed increased as the frequency increased, reaching a peak when the frequency reached 3 Hz. Specifically, when the connection angle of the components was set to 60°, the maximum swimming speed of the robot fish reached 8.6 mm/s; when the connection angle was adjusted to 90°, the maximum swimming speed decreased slightly to 7.6 mm/s. However, as the frequency of the voltage signal continued to increase beyond 3 Hz, the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish began to decline gradually, reaching the lowest point at a frequency of 5 Hz—at this point, the swimming speed was approximately 50% lower than that at 2 Hz.

Swimming speed of the bionic robot fish at different frequencies. (a) Connection angle of 60 degrees, (b) Connection angle of 90 degrees.

This phenomenon fully reflects the complexity of the interaction between the driving signal and the propulsion mechanism of the bionic robot fish. The optimal frequency (3 Hz) corresponding to the maximum swimming speed identified in the experiment indicates that an “optimal matching point” exists: At this frequency, the oscillation of the pectoral fins can be most effectively converted into forward propulsive force. When the frequency is lower than this optimal value, the oscillation speed of the pectoral fins is relatively slow, making it difficult to generate sufficient propulsive force; when the frequency exceeds this value, the excessively high oscillation frequency instead introduces hydrodynamic inefficiencies or additional resistance, ultimately leading to a decrease in the overall swimming speed.

Specifically, when the frequency increased from 1 Hz to 3 Hz, the response frequency of the dielectric elastomer (DE) actuator essentially matched the voltage signal, and the oscillation amplitude of the pectoral fins increased with increasing frequency. According to hydrodynamic principles, the propulsive force generated by the bionic robot fish at this stage was greater than the water flow resistance, resulting in an increase in the swimming speed (reaching 8.6 mm/s at a 60° connection angle and 7.6 mm/s at a 90° connection angle). The difference in the swimming speed between the two connection angles stems from the influence of the connection angle on the oscillation trajectory of the pectoral fins: at a 60° connection angle, the backward oscillation trajectory of the pectoral fins is closer to a “streamlined” shape, with less water flow separation (lower drag coefficient), and the propulsion efficiency is 12% greater than that at a 90° connection angle.

When the frequency exceeded 3 Hz, the viscoelastic hysteresis effect of the dielectric elastomer began to manifest: the molecular chain movement of the DE material could not keep up with the changes in the high-frequency voltage signal, leading to a reduction in the oscillation amplitude of the pectoral fins and a phase lag of the oscillation relative to the voltage signal. Moreover, high-frequency oscillation also generated a “superposition effect” in the water flow—before the vortex formed by the previous oscillation dissipated, the next oscillation had already started, and the mutual interference between the vortices further weakened the propulsion effect, ultimately resulting in a decrease in the swimming speed.

Influence of the driving voltage on the swimming speed of the bionic fish

When the frequency is fixed at 3 Hz (the optimal frequency), the voltage increases from 4 kV to 4.5 kV, the electric field strength increases, the stress of the Maxwell transform increases, and the in-plane strain of DE increases by 7%. The swing amplitude of the fin is directly proportional to the DE strain, and the propulsion force increases with the square of the swing amplitude. Therefore, the swimming speed increased from 7.1 mm/s to 8.6 mm/s, which matched the increase in DE strain.

The core driving force for the bionic robot fish’s fins was a sinusoidal voltage signal—chosen for its ability to simulate the periodic, smooth fin movements of real Carangidae fish, which rely on rhythmic appendage motion to generate stable propulsion. In this experimental setup, the frequency of the sinusoidal voltage was strictly fixed at 3 Hz, a value previously identified as the “optimal frequency” for the bionic robot fish. The key variable adjusted was the amplitude of the sinusoidal voltage, which was incrementally increased from 4 kV to 4.5 kV. This incremental adjustment allowed for precise tracking of how small changes in electrical input translated to changes in the robot’s mechanical output—specifically, its cruising speed in an insulating fluid environment. The experimental data, including the voltage amplitude and corresponding swimming speed, are shown in Fig. 7, which provides a clear, quantitative representation of the relationship between these two parameters.

Driving voltage and swimming speed curves.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish is distinctly positively correlated with the amplitude of the driving voltage signal: as the voltage amplitude increases, the swimming speed increases consistently. This trend is not arbitrary but stems from the fundamental electromechanical coupling mechanism of the dielectric elastomer (DE) actuator—the “muscle” behind the bionic robot fish’s fins. When the voltage amplitude increases, the electric field strength within the DE material increases proportionally. According to Maxwell’s stress theory, this increase in electric field strength directly amplifies the Maxwell stress acting on the DE film: the electrostatic attraction between the flexible electrodes coated on the DE’s two surfaces becomes stronger, forcing the DE film to compress in its thickness direction and expand more significantly in its in-plane (length and width) directions. This expansion manifests as an increase in the DE’s in-plane strain; measurements during the experiment revealed that when the voltage rose from 4 kV to 4.5 kV (at a fixed 3 Hz frequency), the in-plane strain of the DE material increased by 7%.

Specifically, when the voltage was 4 kV, the bionic robot fish’s swimming speed was 7.1 mm/s; as the voltage gradually increased to 4.5 kV, the swimming speed increased in tandem, ultimately peaking at 8.6 mm/s. This speed increase—from 7.1 mm/s to 8.6 mm/s—closely aligns with the 7% increase in DE strain, validating the direct causal link between the voltage amplitude, DE mechanical deformation, and the robot’s swimming performance. These experimental results confirm that within the tested voltage range (4 kV to 4.5 kV), the DE actuator operates in a stable, efficient regime where electrical energy is effectively converted into mechanical work.

Influence of the connection angle on the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish

To ensure the optimal performance of the bionic Carangidae robot fish, precise control of the initial angle of the fins is crucial. This is achieved by adjusting the connection angle during the fabrication of the DEA. It is imperative to maintain a constant submerged area of the fins in water to ensure a stable driving force. In this experiment, connection angles of 60 degrees and 90 degrees were examined, and the swimming speeds of the bionic robot fish corresponding to these different connection angles are presented in Fig. 8.

Comparison of the swimming speeds of the bionic robot fish with different connection angles.

The experimental results indicated that the angle of the actuator connector significantly affects the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish. Through comparative tests on the two connection angles (60 degrees and 90 degrees), we observed intriguing dynamic patterns: when the frequency of the driving voltage signal was set to 3 Hz, the bionic robot fish with a 60-degree connection angle achieved the maximum swimming speed, which was notably 12% greater than that of the bionic robot fish with a 90-degree connection angle. These findings fully highlight the importance of fine-tuning the connection angle to maximize swimming efficiency.

When the connection angle is 60 degrees, the initial position of the fin forms a 60-degree angle with the bionic robot fish body. During the backward swing, the fin needs to rotate only 14 degrees to reach the maximum propulsion angle, resulting in a short start-up delay. When the connection angle is 90 degrees, the initial position of the fin is perpendicular to the bionic robot fish body. To reach the maximum propulsion angle during the backward swing, the fin needs to rotate 30 degrees, leading to a longer start-up delay. In the high-frequency driving scenario of 3 Hz, this delay increases the phase difference between the fin swing and the voltage signal, reducing the propulsive force transmission efficiency by 12%. Therefore, the bionic robot fish with a 60-degree connection angle exhibits a more advantageous swimming speed.

Further analysis revealed that the optimal swimming speed of the bionic robot fish varies with the frequency of the driving voltage signal. For instance, at frequencies such as 1 Hz, 2 Hz, 4 Hz, and 5 Hz, the swimming speed of the bionic robot fish with a 90-degree connection angle was slightly greater than that with a 60-degree connection angle; however, at a frequency of 3 Hz, the advantage of the 60-degree connection angle was fully demonstrated.

In low-frequency environments, the actuator has sufficient time to overcome the start-up delay, and the fin swing amplitude corresponding to the 90-degree connection angle is larger, enabling the generation of a slightly higher propulsive force. Under low-frequency driving conditions, the water flow velocity is relatively low, and the turbulent resistance acting on the fins with a 90-degree connection angle is smaller. At this time, the difference in resistance is smaller than the difference in propulsive force, ultimately resulting in a slightly higher swimming speed of the bionic robot fish with a 90-degree connection angle.

Swimming movements of the bionic robot fish

Evaluating the swimming performance of a bionic robot fish requires a detailed analysis of its swimming movements. The number of swimming movements is a pivotal parameter in this assessment, as it directly reflects the efficiency and fluidity of the robot’s propulsion mechanism. In this study, the swimming movements of the bionic Carangidae robot fish were meticulously examined under various frequencies of the driving voltage signal.

As illustrated in Fig. 9, the frequency of the driving voltage signal significantly affects the number of swimming movements performed by the bionic robot fish. When the connection angle of the actuator was set to 60 degrees, the bionic robot fish exhibited a distinct pattern in its swimming movements. At a frequency of 1 Hz, the bionic robot fish executed 0.02 swimming movements, indicating a relatively slow yet deliberate swimming pace. As the frequency increased, the number of swimming movements gradually decreased, which suggests that the bionic robot fish was unable to sustain a high frequency of movements under higher driving frequencies.

Number of swim movements of the bionic robot fish under driving voltage signals with different frequencies.

In contrast, when the connection angle was adjusted to 90 degrees, the bionic robot fish displayed different behaviors. At 1 Hz, it achieved a greater number of swimming movements (0.034), demonstrating a more dynamic swimming style compared to the configuration with a 60-degree connection angle. However, as the frequency increased to 2 Hz, the number of swimming movements sharply decreased, indicating that the bionic robot fish was less efficient in maintaining a high number of movements at this frequency when it was equipped with a 90-degree connection angle.

These findings underscore the intricate relationship between the connection angle of the actuator and the swimming performance of the bionic robot fish. Under different connection angles and frequencies, the bionic robot fish achieved varying numbers of swimming movements, highlighting the necessity of carefully calibrating these parameters to optimize the robot’s swimming efficiency. The performance of the bionic robot fish is significantly affected by the connection angle, and specific combinations of angles and frequencies facilitate more fluid and efficient swimming movements.

At 1 Hz, the number of movements with a 60-degree connection angle was 0.02, whereas that with a 90-degree connection angle was 0.034. The reason for the greater number of movements at 90 degrees is that at low frequencies, the swing amplitude is large, resulting in a large displacement in the first cycle. However, the movement frequency is low, leading to poor continuity of propulsion. At 3 Hz, the number of movements with a 60-degree connection angle is essentially synchronized with the frequency, and the continuity of propulsion is optimal, thus achieving the peak speed.

At high frequencies (5 Hz), the number of movements at both 60 degrees and 90 degrees decreases because of the “incomplete movement” caused by actuator lag. Specifically, the forward swing starts before the fin completes the backward swing, and the water flow resistance during the forward swing offsets part of the propulsion generated by the backward swing, resulting in a sharp decrease in propulsion efficiency. The trend in Fig. 9 indicates that the number of movements needs to be matched with the response capability of the actuator and hydrodynamics.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed a bionic robot fish utilizing a dielectric elastomer actuator to replicate the bending motion of a fish’s body and tail fin, thereby enhancing the performance of the robot. The robot operates in the MPF propulsion mode, which mimics the natural swimming patterns of fish.

To achieve this goal, we first addressed the challenge of incorporating both planar stretching/contraction and bending motions into the dielectric elastomer actuator. This was accomplished by fabricating a bending actuator using the DEMES. The DEMES design allows the actuator to exhibit the required deformation patterns, enabling it to mimic the dynamic movements of a fish’s fins.

Next, we focus on the curved movements of a fish’s body and tail fin. To this end, we developed a robotic fish capable of mimicking the swimming pattern of Carangidae fish. The core of this robot is a drive unit that bends left and right using a bending actuator. This drive unit was constructed by directly gluing one end of the two bending actuators together and connecting the other end with a connector. This design ensures that the drive unit can effectively replicate the undulating motion of a fish’s body and tail fin.

With the drive unit in place, we connected a tail fin made of CFRP to it. This tail fin provides the necessary propulsion force when the drive unit is activated. After all the components were assembled, we produced a prototype of the robotic fish and conducted swimming experiments to evaluate its performance.

The experimental results revealed that the robotic fish demonstrated excellent propulsion characteristics. Preliminary calculations suggest a thrust range of 12–15 mN at 3 Hz/4.5 kV. At an optimal frequency of 3 Hz and a connector angle of 60°, the robotic fish achieved a maximum swimming speed of 8.6 mm/s. This speed is significantly less than that of real fish, at approximately 1/15 of their natural swimming speed. These findings indicate that further optimization of the design of the robot is necessary to improve its swimming performance.

Nevertheless, the bionic robotic fish demonstrated excellent propulsion characteristics. Future work should focus on studying the regulatory behavior mechanisms of fish and optimizing the structure of the robot to reduce fluid resistance. With advancements in material science, sensing technology, and control algorithms, the swimming performance of underwater bionic robots is expected to improve in the future.
