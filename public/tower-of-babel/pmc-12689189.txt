Targeting the intrinsic bone nerve supply for joint denervation in osteoarthritis: A Copernican revolution, or just another fad relegated to the graveyard of scientific history?
Authors: Nafisseh S Warner, Steven P Cohen
Published: 2025

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has been used to treat chronic pain for nearly 100 years, evolving to become one of the most commonly utilized procedures for osteoarthritis [1,2]. Yet, in every area of the body, the results of RFA are decidedly mixed, resulting in payor denials and reduced access to care [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Improving patient selection through phenotyping or by increasing selection threshold (e.g., requiring a higher percentage of pain relief from diagnostic blocks or requiring multiple blocks) may in some cases increase the RFA success rate. However, this comes at the unavoidable expense of withholding therapy from patients likely to benefit (e.g., those obtaining 50–74% relief from blocks when higher thresholds are required by payors). It also excludes those who, despite a lower likelihood of benefit, may experience an extraordinary upside (e.g., individuals suffering from depression, sleep disturbances, work absenteeism, weight gain due to inactivity, and seeking alternatives to opioids, surgery and unemployment, whose quality of life trajectory is dramatically reversed) [3,6,7]. The most advantageous way to improve RFA outcomes is via technical optimization (i.e., using larger electrodes inserted in optimal orientations, at more nerve sites, with longer heating times); however, these modifications are likely to benefit only a small proportion of patients, and may adversely affect revenue, leading to reduced community uptake. Collectively, these circumstances strongly indicate the need for novel ablative treatment strategies.

Riding into this setting 2 years ago was a proposal by a well-known neuroscientist to completely revise the way clinicians view ablation: Marshall Devor endorsed conducting studies to determine whether targeting the intrinsic nerve supply to the knee joint might result in better outcomes than the ‘flawed’ approach now of trying to lesion the extensive, and anatomically variable, extrinsic nerve supply [8]. Devor suggested that injecting polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, a.k.a. bone cement) into epiphyseal bone end(s) or the synovial capsule, to capture the intrinsic fibers responsible for knee osteoarthritis (KOA) pain, would be more effective than RFA of the extrinsic genicular nerves, which have yielded conflicting results in clinical trials.5 The ablation of nerves by ‘cementing’ them during vertebral augmentation-as was recommended, albeit for knee osteoarthritis by Devor in his original article-has been proposed as one mechanism for pain relief after compression fractures, and is supported by clinical trials demonstrating efficacy for basivertebral nerve ablation [[8], [9], [10]]. Whether this assertion is true or not remains to be determined, but in science- and life itself-execution is more important than idea generation, which are a dime a dozen.

To explore innervation of the knee joint in greater detail, Tran et al. performed a cadaveric study of a convenience sample of 19 femur specimens [11]. By photographing the distal aspects of each femur and enhancing images through an open-source imaging processing software, they aimed to quantify the distribution of epiphyseal nutrient foramina, thereby informing the optimization of denervation techniques that reliably capture nerves providing intrinsic innervation of subchondral bone. Specifically, they divided the distal femur, in both lateral and medial views, into 4 quadrants of equal widths for nutrient foramina quantification, with quadrant 1 being most anterior and quadrant 4 being most posterior. Anteriorly, the femur was divided into medial and lateral halves. In medial and lateral views, approximately 75–80% of foramina were in quadrants 2 and 3, or the middle portion of the bone, with modestly greater foramina density in the more anterior quadrant 2 (i.e., approximately 40–45%) than quadrant 3 (i.e., approximately 35%). Overall, the distribution of nutrient foramina in medial and lateral views resembled a bell-shaped curve, with approximately 10% of foramina located in the anterior and posterior tails. Anteriorly, most (71%) nutrient foramina were found medially, and although not quantified in greater detail, sample images suggest that most were likely distributed near the midline.

The authors should be applauded for their work describing the anatomic distribution of epiphyseal nutrient foramina. They provide potential targets for intrinsic genicular nerve blocks targeting afferents supplying subchondral bone, RFA, and neurolysis by means of alcohol, cement, or some other means. Identifying these neural targets can obviate some of the original concerns raised in the editorial by Cohen et al. regarding the pitfalls involved in directly instilling cement in the distal femur (i.e., intraosseous injections), such as the potential for avascular necrosis and concerns regarding the difficulty of joint replacement of bone filled with cement; this latter point is important because no treatment, including arthroplasty, provides lifelong analgesia [12]. Yet, the sheer number and site variability of the nutrient foramina identified in dissections raises another point besides questions regarding biological plausibility that besets traditional genicular nerve RFA – is it possible, or economically feasible in the U.S., to effectively target most of the intrinsic nerve supply, with studies comparing genicular nerve RFA paradigms consistently demonstrating superiority when more than 3 nerves are treated? [13] Despite this, the 3-nerve paradigm remains by far the most common technique in clinical practice, in part because payors in the United States do not reimburse for more than 3 nerves, but also because of ignorance of the literature.

Further, there are important methodological limitations to this study, which raise additional questions regarding optimal treatment targets. First, the authors are unable to provide details about those from whom the femur samples originated, including most notably age, sex, history of knee pain or injury, and osteoporosis. Even in the absence of a direct impact of these factors on the anatomic location of nutrient foramina, these biological variables could influence cortical morphology and, in turn, the detection and quantification of nutrient foramina. Second, although they provide the overall distribution of nutrient foramina across all specimens, it is unclear how much variability there may be between individuals – do most femurs follow the distribution as summarized, such that targeting nerves in quadrants 2 or 3 (on lateral and medial views) makes sense for most patients, or is there considerable variability such that some individuals have distinct distributions (e.g., anterior or posterior-predominant phenotypes)? If so, how do we identify those patients in daily practice? Without basic demographic and clinical data, these questions are hard to answer. Third, the authors do not quantify differences in the overall distribution of nutrient foramina on the medial versus lateral aspects of the femur. Viewed anteriorly, they describe a medial predominance of foramina, which is supported by studies showing that medial joint pain often exceeds that of lateral pain [14,15]. Yet, in patients with valgus deformities, lateral joint degeneration often precedes that of the medial compartment, and these patients often present with a predominance of lateral joint pain [16]. Again, having clinical data to correlate with anatomical findings would be helpful. Finally, the knee joint connects the distal femur to the proximal tibia, such that knee pain may arise from both distal femoral and proximal tibial subchondral structures. By only evaluating foramina distribution on one side of the joint line, the analysis remains incomplete. Taken together, these limitations question the direct applicability of their findings to any given patient in clinical practice.

Up until the 16th and 17th centuries when Copernicus proposed a heliocentric model of the solar system, most scientists believed in the geocentric Ptolemaic model, a view which dominated for over 1500 years, wherein the earth was situated at the center of the universe. And until the early 20th century, the Newtonian model of motion was thought to predominate for all objects, seen and unseen, before scientists such as Max Planck, Werner Heisenberg, Niels Bohr and Albert Einstein gave birth to probabilistic quantum theory to explain motion in subatomic particles. These now-debunked or modified theories were espoused by premier scientists for ‘eons’ before a novel theory, at first discounted by the general scientific community (as they always are), caused a ground-breaking paradigm shift. Whether this shift towards targeting the intrinsic joint supply rather than the extrinsic supply becomes a revolutionary transformative breakthrough in how we think of joint denervation, or yet another of the thousands of discredited fads is not known at this point, but we'd be foolish to summarily dismiss it.

In summary, Tran et al. have provided a thought-provoking anatomic analysis of distal femur innervation. By describing the most common locations of nutrient foramina, they postulate enhanced anatomic targets for knee interventions such as RFA and neurolysis. Future research must bridge the gap between cadaveric findings and clinically viable, patient-specific interventions for chronic knee pain, and determine where in history this theory resides: at the pinnacle of science, or the enormous graveyard of refuted ideas.
