Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo: A Hybrid Approach to Data Assimilation in 4DVAR
Abhiram Sripat

Abstract
The combination of quantum algorithms with classical methods presents a promising frontier in computational efficiency, especially in fields like numerical weather prediction. This paper explores the synergy between the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC) in addressing the computational challenges posed by data assimilation, particularly within the Four-Dimensional Variational Data Assimilation (4DVAR) framework. In addition, we integrate insights from particle filter methods, which have recently been reformulated to work within variational approaches, as proposed by Bocquet et al. (2024). This hybrid quantum-classical framework not only reduces the computational overhead but also enhances accuracy in high-dimensional and nonlinear systems like climate modeling, weather forecasting, and space weather prediction. We present a detailed mathematical framework, including the variational formulation, algorithmic implementation, parameter optimization strategies, and potential hardware considerations for realizing this hybrid approach.



I
Introduction

Four-Dimensional Variational Data Assimilation (4DVAR) is widely employed in numerical weather prediction (NWP), where it optimizes model states over time by minimizing the discrepancy between observed and predicted data. However, the large scale and nonlinearity of these models make traditional optimization methods and sampling techniques computationally expensive, especially as higher resolutions and finer temporal scales are sought for better predictive accuracy.

Recently, quantum algorithms have shown promise in improving the efficiency of both optimization and sampling. The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) can be used to solve combinatorial optimization problems using quantum circuits, while Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC) improves the efficiency of sampling from complex posterior distributions by leveraging quantum superposition and amplitude amplification.

Meanwhile, the latest developments in particle filter methods, particularly in their integration with variational data assimilation frameworks (Bocquet et al., 2024), present an opportunity to address the limitations of traditional data assimilation. Particle filters allow for non-linear, non-Gaussian state estimation, providing a more flexible framework for solving high-dimensional problems.

In this paper, we propose a hybrid approach that integrates QAOA and QMCMC with particle filters, improving both the optimization and sampling stages in the 4DVAR framework. This combination offers computational advantages while maintaining accuracy in data assimilation, particularly for high-dimensional systems such as climate models.

II
Mathematical Framework

II-A

Four-Dimensional Variational Data Assimilation (4DVAR)

The 4DVAR problem aims to minimize a cost function that balances the fit between the model’s background state and observational data over a time window. Mathematically, the cost function is:

\begin{split}J(x_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{T}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k}))^{T}R_{k}^{-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k})),\end{split}

(1)

where:
-
x_{0}
is the initial state of the model.
-
x_{b}
is the background state or prior estimate.
-
B
represents the background error covariance matrix.
-
y_{k}
represents the observations at time step
k
.
-
H_{k}
is the observation operator that maps the model state to the observational space.
-
R_{k}
represents the observational error covariance matrix.

The state evolves according to the model dynamics:

x_{k+1}=M_{k}(x_{k}),

(2)

where
M_{k}
is the model operator at each time step.

The goal is to optimize the initial state
x_{0}
such that the cost function
J(x_{0})
is minimized, providing a good fit between the model and observational data across the time window.

II-B

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

QAOA is a quantum algorithm designed to approximate the solution to combinatorial optimization problems. In this case, the 4DVAR cost function can be encoded as a quantum Hamiltonian, and QAOA can be used to find a near-optimal solution.

Let
H_{C}
represent the cost Hamiltonian that encodes the 4DVAR cost function, and
H_{M}
be a mixing Hamiltonian. QAOA operates by applying alternating sequences of these operators to an initial quantum state:

|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle=U_{M}(\beta_{p})U_{C}(\gamma_{p})\cdots U_{M}(\beta_{1})U_{C}(\gamma_{1})|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(3)

where
U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}
and
U_{M}(\beta)=e^{-i\beta H_{M}}
, with
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
representing variational parameters.

The goal is to optimize the parameters
\gamma
and
\beta
to minimize the expectation value:

\langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|H_{C}|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle,

(4)

providing a good approximation to the global minimum of the cost function.

II-C

Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC)

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a widely used method for sampling from complex probability distributions in Bayesian inference. However, classical MCMC methods, such as the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, can suffer from slow convergence and inefficiency, particularly in high-dimensional spaces and in the presence of highly correlated variables. Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC) leverages the power of quantum computing to accelerate the sampling process by improving the acceptance rates of proposed samples and exploring the solution space more efficiently. This section explores how QMCMC can be applied to the 4DVAR framework to improve the accuracy and speed of data assimilation.

II-C
1
Classical MCMC Overview

Classical MCMC methods operate by constructing a Markov chain where the states correspond to samples from the target distribution. The chain is designed to have the target distribution as its stationary distribution. In the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, new samples
x^{\prime}
are proposed based on a proposal distribution
q(x^{\prime}|x)
, where
x
is the current state. The new sample is accepted with the acceptance probability:

\alpha(x,x^{\prime})=\min\left(1,\frac{p(x^{\prime})q(x|x^{\prime})}{p(x)q(x^{\prime}|x)}\right),

(5)

where
p(x)
is the target distribution. If the proposed sample is accepted, the chain moves to
x^{\prime}
, otherwise, it stays at
x
.

This method is widely used, but it often suffers from slow mixing times (i.e., the time it takes for the Markov chain to explore the entire state space) and a high rejection rate when the dimensionality of the problem increases. The quantum version of this method, QMCMC, addresses these limitations by utilizing quantum algorithms to improve the sampling efficiency.

II-C
2
Quantum Speedup in QMCMC

Quantum-enhanced MCMC exploits the power of
quantum amplitude amplification
to speed up the convergence of the sampling process. One of the core components of quantum advantage in QMCMC is the use of
Grover’s algorithm
for amplitude amplification, which enhances the probability of selecting high-likelihood samples, effectively accelerating the exploration of the state space.

In the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, a large number of samples may be rejected, leading to inefficiencies. In contrast, QMCMC can amplify the probability of proposed samples that are likely to be accepted, reducing the number of rejected samples. This quantum advantage translates to a quadratic speedup in the acceptance rate over classical MCMC methods.

The core idea of quantum amplitude amplification is as follows. Suppose we start with a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
that is a superposition of all possible configurations
|x\rangle
:

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x)}|x\rangle,

(6)

where
p(x)
is the probability associated with each state
|x\rangle
. The goal is to amplify the amplitude of states corresponding to high-likelihood samples. To achieve this, we define a Grover operator
G
as:

G=2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I,

(7)

where
|\psi\rangle
is the uniform superposition over all states and
I
is the identity operator. Repeated applications of
G
amplify the amplitude of the desired states, making them more likely to be selected upon measurement.

By applying the Grover operator to states that correspond to accepted proposals in the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, QMCMC significantly reduces the rejection rate and accelerates the sampling process.

II-C
3
QMCMC in the 4DVAR Framework

In the context of 4DVAR, the target distribution is the posterior distribution of the model state
x_{0}
given the observations and prior knowledge. This posterior distribution is often complex and high-dimensional, making classical MCMC methods inefficient. QMCMC can be applied to accelerate the process of sampling from this posterior distribution, improving the overall efficiency of the data assimilation process.

1.

Initial Proposal via QAOA
: After QAOA finds a near-optimal initial state
x_{0}^{*}
, we use QMCMC to refine this state by sampling from the posterior distribution. The initial sample distribution can be taken as the output of the QAOA algorithm.

2.

Quantum Proposal Distribution
: Instead of proposing new samples using classical methods, we use a quantum proposal distribution. This is achieved by constructing a quantum circuit that encodes the proposal distribution in a quantum state. The quantum proposal distribution is designed to explore the state space more efficiently by using quantum superposition and entanglement.

3.

Quantum Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm
: In QMCMC, the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule is modified using quantum amplitude amplification. Specifically, we apply Grover’s algorithm to amplify the probability of proposals that are likely to be accepted. This reduces the number of rejected proposals and accelerates the convergence of the Markov chain.

The quantum version of the acceptance probability becomes:

\alpha_{q}(x,x^{\prime})=\min\left(1,\frac{p(x^{\prime})q_{q}(x|x^{\prime})}{p(x)q_{q}(x^{\prime}|x)}\right),

(8)

where
q_{q}(x^{\prime}|x)
is the quantum proposal distribution, which is more efficient than the classical version
q(x^{\prime}|x)
.

4.

Quantum Resampling
: After several steps of QMCMC, quantum resampling can be employed to ensure that the particles (samples) remain diverse. Quantum resampling can be implemented using quantum circuits that perform weighted sampling, amplifying the weights of the particles with higher likelihoods.

II-C
4
Theoretical Speedup and Convergence Analysis

The quantum advantage in QMCMC is derived from its ability to use amplitude amplification to achieve a quadratic speedup over classical MCMC. Specifically, Grover’s algorithm provides a quadratic improvement in the acceptance rate, reducing the number of iterations required to sample from the posterior distribution.

Let
\epsilon
be the fraction of samples that are accepted in the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. In QMCMC, the expected number of iterations required to generate an accepted sample is reduced to
O(1/\sqrt{\epsilon})
due to amplitude amplification, compared to
O(1/\epsilon)
in the classical case. This provides a significant computational advantage, particularly in high-dimensional systems where
\epsilon
is small.

Convergence analysis of QMCMC shows that the Markov chain converges to the correct posterior distribution, provided that the detailed balance condition is satisfied. The quantum version of the detailed balance condition is given by:

p(x)q_{q}(x^{\prime}|x)=p(x^{\prime})q_{q}(x|x^{\prime}),

(9)

which ensures that the chain remains reversible and converges to the desired posterior distribution.

II-C
5
Practical Implementation on NISQ Devices

While QMCMC promises significant theoretical advantages, its practical implementation on Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices poses challenges. NISQ devices are limited by noise and decoherence, which can reduce the effectiveness of quantum amplitude amplification. However, several techniques can be employed to mitigate these issues:

•

Error Mitigation
: Techniques such as zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error cancellation can be used to reduce the impact of noise on the QMCMC algorithm.

•

Shallow Circuits
: To minimize the effect of decoherence, we can design shallow quantum circuits that implement the Grover operator with a minimal number of gates.

•

Hybrid Classical-Quantum Approaches
: In practice, QMCMC can be implemented as part of a hybrid classical-quantum approach, where the quantum component is used to handle the most computationally intensive parts of the algorithm (e.g., proposal generation and acceptance) while the classical component manages simpler tasks like resampling.

II-D

Particle Filters in Data Assimilation: A Quantum-Enhanced Perspective

II-D
1
Classical Particle Filters: Foundations and Challenges

Particle filters (PF), also known as Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods, represent a class of algorithms used to estimate the posterior probability distribution of a system’s state. Unlike traditional variational methods, which often rely on Gaussian approximations and linear models, particle filters offer a flexible and nonparametric framework capable of handling non-Gaussian distributions and nonlinear dynamics, which are often encountered in geophysical systems and data assimilation applications such as weather forecasting and climate modeling.

In particle filters, the posterior distribution of the state is approximated using a set of discrete samples, or ”particles,” each associated with a weight that reflects its likelihood given the observations. This process is iterative, with particles being propagated forward according to the model dynamics and then re-weighted based on the likelihood of new observations.

Mathematically, at each time step
k
, the particle set
\{x_{k}^{(i)},w_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
represents an approximation of the posterior distribution
p(x_{k}|y_{1:k})
, where
x_{k}^{(i)}
are the particles, and
w_{k}^{(i)}
are their associated weights. The algorithm proceeds in three stages:

1.

Prediction (Propagation)
:

x_{k+1}^{(i)}=M_{k}(x_{k}^{(i)})+\eta_{k}^{(i)},

where
M_{k}
is the model operator, and
\eta_{k}^{(i)}
represents stochastic noise.

2.

Update (Weighting)
:

w_{k+1}^{(i)}=w_{k}^{(i)}\cdot p(y_{k+1}|x_{k+1}^{(i)}),

where
p(y_{k+1}|x_{k+1}^{(i)})
is the likelihood of observing
y_{k+1}
given the state
x_{k+1}^{(i)}
.

3.

Resampling
: To avoid particle degeneracy (i.e., when a few particles dominate the weight distribution), resampling is performed, where particles are replicated or discarded based on their weights.

Challenges
: Classical particle filters suffer from several limitations, particularly in high-dimensional systems, where the number of particles required to represent the posterior distribution scales exponentially with the dimension of the state space. This phenomenon, known as the
curse of dimensionality
, severely limits the applicability of particle filters in complex systems such as global weather models, where the state space can have millions of degrees of freedom.

Additionally, classical resampling techniques introduce noise and lead to loss of diversity in the particle set, making it difficult to accurately represent multimodal distributions, a frequent occurrence in nonlinear geophysical systems.

II-D
2
Quantum-Enhanced Particle Filters

To overcome the computational challenges associated with high-dimensional state spaces, we propose integrating quantum algorithms into the particle filter framework. This new class of
Quantum-Enhanced Particle Filters (QEPF)
leverages the inherent parallelism of quantum systems to mitigate the curse of dimensionality, accelerate particle resampling, and improve the convergence properties of the filter.

Quantum State Encoding of Particles

In QEPF, each particle
x_{k}^{(i)}
is encoded as a quantum state. Instead of storing particles in classical memory, the state space of the particles is represented as a quantum superposition:

|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{w_{k}^{(i)}}|x_{k}^{(i)}\rangle,

where
|x_{k}^{(i)}\rangle
is the quantum state corresponding to the
i
-th particle, and
w_{k}^{(i)}
represents the weight of the particle. This superposition allows the quantum system to process multiple particles simultaneously, thereby reducing the computational complexity associated with high-dimensional spaces.

Quantum Parallelism
: One of the key advantages of encoding particles in quantum states is the ability to perform operations on all particles simultaneously. For example, during the prediction step, the evolution of all particles under the model dynamics
M_{k}
can be performed in parallel via unitary quantum evolution, drastically reducing computational time.

Quantum-Enhanced Resampling via Grover’s Algorithm

Resampling, a key step in classical particle filters, can be enhanced using quantum algorithms. Grover’s search algorithm can be applied to resample particles based on their weights, with a quantum speedup over classical methods. Specifically, we use quantum amplitude amplification to increase the probability of selecting particles with higher weights, effectively focusing computational resources on the most likely regions of the state space.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of particles to be resampled, and let the operator
G
be the Grover operator:

G=2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I,

where
|\psi\rangle
is the quantum state representing the weighted particles. By applying
G
iteratively, we amplify the probability of selecting high-weight particles while suppressing the selection of low-weight particles, achieving a quantum speedup in the resampling process.

Quantum Variational Resampling

Traditional resampling methods often introduce bias or lead to a loss of particle diversity, especially in complex, multimodal distributions. To address this, we propose a
Quantum Variational Resampling (QVR)
technique that dynamically adjusts the resampling process based on the underlying distribution of the particles.

In QVR, the resampling is formulated as a variational optimization problem:

\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i}D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\theta}(x)||p(x)),

where
D_{\text{KL}}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the quantum state
p_{\theta}(x)
represented by the variational ansatz and the true posterior distribution
p(x)
. The parameters
\theta
of the quantum state are optimized to minimize this divergence, ensuring that the resampled particles maintain diversity and accurately represent the posterior distribution.

The variational circuit used for resampling can be trained using gradient-based methods, where the gradient of the cost function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is estimated via the parameter-shift rule:

\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}+\frac{\pi}{2})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}-\frac{\pi}{2})}{2}.

This quantum variational approach allows for adaptive resampling, dynamically adjusting to the structure of the posterior distribution at each time step.

Quantum Variational Particle Filters (QVPF)

The full integration of quantum resources into particle filtering leads to the development of
Quantum Variational Particle Filters (QVPF)
, which combine QAOA-based particle initialization, QMCMC-based weight computation, and QVR for adaptive resampling.

The steps of QVPF are as follows:

1.

Quantum Initialization (QAOA)
: QAOA is used to generate an initial set of particles, optimizing for a set of diverse and high-probability particles that efficiently represent the initial posterior distribution.

2.

Quantum Propagation
: The particles are evolved forward in time using quantum circuits that parallelize the application of the model operator
M_{k}
across all particles.

3.

Quantum Resampling (QVR)
: Variational resampling ensures that the particle set remains diverse and representative of the posterior distribution, even in the presence of nonlinear dynamics.

4.

Quantum Weighting (QMCMC)
: Quantum-enhanced MCMC is used to efficiently compute particle weights and sample from the posterior, leveraging quantum amplitude amplification for faster convergence.

II-D
3
Convergence and Scalability: Formal Proof of Quantum Speedup in QVPF

Step 1: Classical Particle Filter Complexity

In classical particle filters, the computational complexity is dominated by the resampling and propagation steps. The overall complexity per time step for a classical particle filter is:

O(N\cdot d)+O(N)+O(N)\approx O(N\cdot d),

where
N
is the number of particles and
d
is the dimensionality of the state space.

Step 2: Quantum Speedup in Resampling

Quantum amplitude amplification provides a quadratic speedup for resampling. In classical resampling, the complexity is
O(N)
, while quantum amplitude amplification reduces this to:

O(\sqrt{N}).

Step 3: Quantum Parallelism in Propagation

Quantum propagation allows us to evolve all particles simultaneously using unitary operations, reducing the complexity from
O(N\cdot d)
to
O(d)
, as the model dynamics are applied to all particles in parallel.

Step 4: Overall Time Complexity of QVPF

The overall time complexity per time step of QVPF is:

O(d)+O(\sqrt{N})+O(N).

For large
N
, resampling becomes the dominant term, and the quantum speedup in this step leads to substantial computational savings.

Proof of Convergence for QVPF:
Let
p(x_{k}|y_{1:k})
be the true posterior distribution at time step
k
. In classical particle filters, the convergence rate to the true posterior scales as
O(1/\sqrt{N})
. In QVPF, quantum amplitude amplification reduces the variance in the resampling step, leading to a quadratic improvement in the convergence rate. The total error scales as:

O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

demonstrating a quantum-enhanced convergence rate for QVPF.

III
Hybrid QAOA-QMCMC Approach Enhanced with Particle Filters

We propose a hybrid approach that integrates QAOA and QMCMC with particle filters to address the computational challenges of high-dimensional, non-linear data assimilation.

III-A

QAOA for Particle Initialization

The initialization of particles is a critical step in particle filter-based approaches for data assimilation. Traditionally, particles are drawn from a proposal distribution, often based on prior knowledge of the system or model dynamics. This can be inefficient, particularly in high-dimensional systems, where poorly chosen particles may result in low weights, contributing little to the state estimate.

To overcome these limitations, we leverage the
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
for enhanced particle initialization. QAOA is capable of finding approximate solutions to combinatorial optimization problems, which in our case involves selecting a particle set that approximates the true posterior distribution efficiently.

III-A
1
Mathematical Formulation

In particle filters, the goal is to approximate the true posterior distribution
p(x_{0}|y)
, where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the initial state of the system, and
y
represents the observations. Let the 4DVAR cost function
J(x_{0})
, as defined earlier, be the objective function for particle initialization:

\begin{split}J(x_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{T}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k}))^{T}R_{k}^{-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k})),\end{split}

(10)

where
x_{b}
is the background state and
B
and
R_{k}
are covariance matrices for background and observational errors, respectively.

The goal is to use QAOA to generate an initial particle set
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}
that minimizes
J(x_{0})
. QAOA works by alternating between applying a
problem Hamiltonian

H_{C}
, which encodes
J(x_{0})
, and a
mixing Hamiltonian

H_{M}
, which encourages transitions between particle states.

III-A
2
Encoding the Problem Hamiltonian

We encode the cost function
J(x_{0})
into a Hamiltonian
H_{C}
, which operates on a set of qubits representing the state
x_{0}
. For simplicity, assume that each element of
x_{0}
is discretized and encoded into binary form using
m
qubits per state variable, giving a total of
n\times m
qubits.

The cost Hamiltonian is defined as:

H_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J(x_{0}^{(i)})|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle\langle x_{0}^{(i)}|,

where
|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle
represents the quantum state corresponding to particle
x_{0}^{(i)}
, and
J(x_{0}^{(i)})
is the cost function evaluated at that particle.

III-A
3
Mixing Hamiltonian

The mixing Hamiltonian
H_{M}
promotes transitions between different particle states. For our application, we choose a simple form of the mixing Hamiltonian as:

H_{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{n\times m}X_{j},

where
X_{j}
is the Pauli-X operator acting on qubit
j
, flipping the qubit and inducing transitions between basis states, thereby ensuring exploration of the search space.

III-A
4
QAOA Algorithm

The quantum state representing the particle ensemble is initialized as a uniform superposition of all possible particle states:

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x_{0}}|x_{0}\rangle,

where
N
is the total number of particles. The QAOA procedure applies alternating unitaries derived from the problem and mixing Hamiltonians:

|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle=U_{M}(\beta_{p})U_{C}(\gamma_{p})\cdots U_{M}(\beta_{1})U_{C}(\gamma_{1})|\psi_{0}\rangle,

where
U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}
and
U_{M}(\beta)=e^{-i\beta H_{M}}
. The parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
are optimized to minimize the expected value
\langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|H_{C}|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle
, which corresponds to finding the particle set that best approximates the true posterior distribution.

III-A
5
Measurement and Particle Selection

Once the state
|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle
is evolved, it is measured, collapsing the superposition into one of the particle states
|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle
. Repeated measurements yield a set of particle states
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}
, which are initialized in a manner optimized for the data assimilation process. This provides a high-quality particle set for the subsequent resampling and refinement steps in the particle filter process.

III-A
6
Quantum Advantage in Particle Initialization

•

Reduction in Particle Degeneration:
Classical particle filters often suffer from particle degeneration, where many particles contribute little to the final state estimate due to low likelihoods. QAOA addresses this by initializing particles closer to the true posterior, reducing the need for frequent resampling.

•

Efficient Exploration of State Space:
The alternating application of
H_{C}
and
H_{M}
allows for a more efficient exploration of the high-dimensional state space compared to classical random sampling methods.

•

Scalability:
As the state space dimension
n
grows, the quantum parallelism inherent in QAOA allows for efficient handling of the exponentially growing number of possible states.

By incorporating QAOA into the particle initialization phase, we create a more robust and efficient method for selecting particles in high-dimensional data assimilation problems. The resulting particles are not only more representative of the posterior distribution but also enhance the overall efficiency of the particle filter process, reducing computational overhead and improving accuracy in applications like climate modeling and weather forecasting.

III-B

QMCMC for Particle Weighting and Resampling

In traditional particle filters, after the particle set is initialized, each particle is assigned a weight based on its likelihood of matching the observed data. However, as the dimensionality of the system increases, the computational cost of calculating these weights and performing resampling grows exponentially, often leading to particle degeneracy, where most particles contribute little to the final estimate.

Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC)
offers an efficient way to compute these particle weights and perform resampling by leveraging quantum superposition and amplitude amplification. This quantum approach can accelerate the sampling process, thereby improving convergence and reducing the computational burden in high-dimensional systems.

III-B
1
Classical MCMC and its Limitations

In classical
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
, the goal is to sample from the posterior distribution
p(x_{0}|y)
using a Markov chain, where each step proposes a new particle based on a transition probability, and the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm determines whether to accept or reject the proposed particle.

Given a current particle
x_{0}^{(i)}
, the algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

Propose a new particle
x_{0}^{\text{new}}
based on a proposal distribution
q(x_{0}^{\text{new}}|x_{0}^{(i)})
.

2.

Calculate the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance ratio:

\alpha=\min\left(1,\frac{p(x_{0}^{\text{new}}|y)q(x_{0}^{(i)}|x_{0}^{\text{new}})}{p(x_{0}^{(i)}|y)q(x_{0}^{\text{new}}|x_{0}^{(i)})}\right).

3.

Accept
x_{0}^{\text{new}}
with probability
\alpha
, or keep
x_{0}^{(i)}
otherwise.

Classical MCMC is effective in low-dimensional systems, but it suffers from slow convergence and high rejection rates in high-dimensional, complex systems such as those encountered in data assimilation.

III-B
2
Quantum-enhanced MCMC

QMCMC improves upon classical MCMC by using
quantum amplitude amplification
to accelerate the sampling process, making it more efficient in high-dimensional systems. The key advantage of QMCMC is its ability to sample from the posterior distribution in fewer steps by leveraging quantum superposition, which allows the quantum system to explore multiple potential states (particles) simultaneously.

The key to QMCMC is the use of the
Grover operator
:

G=2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I,

where
|\psi\rangle
represents the uniform superposition of all possible states, and
I
is the identity operator. The Grover operator is used to amplify the probabilities of selecting ”good” states—those that have a higher likelihood of matching the observations.

III-B
3
QMCMC Algorithm for Weighting and Resampling

We propose the following QMCMC procedure to efficiently calculate particle weights and perform resampling in a particle filter:

State Initialization:

Given an initial set of particles
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}
generated by QAOA, we initialize the quantum state as a uniform superposition over all particle states:

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle,

where
N
is the number of particles. This superposition allows the quantum system to explore all possible particles simultaneously.

Quantum Proposal and Metropolis-Hastings Acceptance:

Instead of proposing a new particle based on a classical transition distribution, we use a quantum circuit to propose new particles in parallel. Let the unitary operator
U_{p}
represent the quantum proposal operator that transforms the current state
|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle
to a new proposed state
|x_{0}^{\text{new}}\rangle
:

U_{p}|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle=|x_{0}^{\text{new}}\rangle.

The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance criterion is implemented as a quantum amplitude amplification process, where the probability of accepting a new particle is amplified based on the acceptance ratio
\alpha
. This is done by defining a reflection operator
R_{\alpha}
that flips the sign of states with high acceptance probabilities:

R_{\alpha}=I-2|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle\langle x_{0}^{(i)}|\text{ if }\alpha<1.

The Grover operator is then applied to amplify the amplitude of the new state
|x_{0}^{\text{new}}\rangle
if it has a high likelihood.

Particle Weighting and Resampling:

After applying the Grover operator
G
, the quantum state is measured, collapsing the superposition into a classical state
|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle
. The particle weight
w^{(i)}
is calculated based on the posterior likelihood:

w^{(i)}=p(y|x_{0}^{(i)}).

Quantum amplitude amplification ensures that particles with higher weights are more likely to be selected during resampling.

To perform resampling, we prepare the quantum state as a weighted superposition of the particle states:

|\psi_{w}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{w^{(i)}}|x_{0}^{(i)}\rangle.

The resampling process involves measuring the state
|\psi_{w}\rangle
, with particles that have higher weights being more likely to be selected for the next iteration. This quantum-enhanced resampling reduces the problem of particle degeneracy, as low-weight particles are automatically suppressed by the amplitude amplification process.

Quantum Advantage in Weighting and Resampling

The key advantages of QMCMC in particle weighting and resampling are:

•

Faster Sampling:
By using quantum superposition and amplitude amplification, QMCMC samples from the posterior distribution in fewer steps than classical MCMC, reducing the computational cost of particle weighting and resampling.

•

Improved Convergence:
The quantum parallelism in QMCMC ensures that high-likelihood particles are selected more frequently, improving the convergence of the particle filter and reducing the number of particles required.

•

Reduction in Particle Degeneracy:
Low-weight particles are naturally suppressed in QMCMC, reducing the impact of particle degeneracy and improving the overall accuracy of the particle filter.

III-C

Variational Particle Filters

Particle filters have long been used in data assimilation to estimate the posterior distribution of a system’s state by propagating a collection of particles through the model dynamics. Each particle is assigned a weight based on the likelihood of the observations, and the resampling step ensures that the particles accurately represent the posterior distribution. However, particle filters face significant challenges in high-dimensional systems due to the ”curse of dimensionality,” which requires exponentially more particles as the system’s state space grows.

To address these issues,
Bocquet et al. (2024)
introduced a
variational formulation of particle filters
, combining the flexibility of particle filters with the robustness of variational data assimilation. The goal is to derive an optimization problem where the particles are treated as variational parameters, minimizing the discrepancy between the model and observational data. This method has the potential to handle non-linear, non-Gaussian systems more effectively than traditional approaches like 4DVAR alone.

However, despite these advantages, the variational particle filter still suffers from computational complexity, particularly in high-dimensional spaces. By integrating
quantum algorithms
, specifically the
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
and
Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC)
, we propose a
Quantum Variational Particle Filter (QVPF)
that mitigates these computational challenges.

III-C
1
Variational Formulation in Particle Filters

In a traditional particle filter, the system’s state
x_{t}
at time
t
is represented by a set of
N
particles
\{x_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and corresponding weights
\{w_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. These particles evolve according to the model dynamics and are updated at each time step based on new observations. In this setup, the goal is to approximate the posterior distribution
p(x_{t}|y_{0:t})
of the state given observations
y_{0:t}
.

In the
variational particle filter
, the posterior distribution is reformulated as an optimization problem. The goal is to minimize a variational cost function that balances model accuracy and the fit to the observational data. The cost function
\mathcal{J}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(x_{0},\{w_{k}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{T}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\langle w_{k},(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k}))^{T}R_{k}^{-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k}))\right\rangle,

(11)

where:

•

x_{0}
is the initial state estimate.

•

x_{b}
is the background state (the prior estimate).

•

B
is the background error covariance matrix.

•

y_{k}
are the observations at time
k
.

•

H_{k}(x_{k})
is the observation operator, mapping model states to observational space.

•

R_{k}
is the observational error covariance matrix.

•

w_{k}
are the particle weights at time step
k
.

This formulation allows for the joint optimization of the particle proposals and the weights, ensuring that the particles better represent the system’s true state, especially in non-linear and non-Gaussian systems. The cost function
\mathcal{J}
is minimized with respect to both the initial state
x_{0}
and the particle weights
\{w_{k}\}
, which improves the quality of the particle ensemble over time.

III-C
2
Optimizing Particle Proposals with QAOA

One of the key challenges in variational particle filters is the
optimization of the proposal distribution
. The proposal distribution determines how new particles are drawn from the prior or from an approximation of the posterior. In high-dimensional systems, finding a good proposal distribution is computationally expensive, as classical methods often fail to efficiently explore the state space.

Here, we introduce
QAOA
to optimize the proposal distribution in a quantum-enhanced manner. In the QAOA framework, the proposal distribution is treated as a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
, and the optimization process involves applying alternating layers of problem-specific unitary operators. Specifically, the cost function from the variational particle filter is encoded as a Hamiltonian
H_{C}
, and the QAOA procedure minimizes this Hamiltonian:

|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{p}e^{-i\beta_{l}H_{M}}e^{-i\gamma_{l}H_{C}}\right)|\psi_{0}\rangle.

Where:

•

H_{C}
encodes the variational cost function
\mathcal{J}
, including the discrepancy between the model and the observations.

•

H_{M}
is a mixing Hamiltonian that introduces transitions between different particle proposals by flipping qubits.

•

\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
are variational parameters optimized by a classical optimizer.

By applying QAOA, we can find an optimized quantum state that represents the most likely particle proposals. The quantum superposition enables the exploration of a large number of potential proposals simultaneously, making the optimization of the proposal distribution far more efficient than classical methods. The variational parameters
\gamma
and
\beta
are tuned to minimize the overall cost function, ensuring that the particle proposals are well-suited to the dynamics of the system and the available observations.

III-C
3
Quantum-Enhanced Weight Updates with QMCMC

Once the particle proposals have been generated using QAOA, the next step in the variational particle filter is to compute the particle weights based on the likelihood of the observations. In classical particle filters, this involves evaluating the likelihood function for each particle, which becomes computationally prohibitive in high-dimensional systems. Moreover, frequent resampling is required to prevent particle degeneracy, where a small number of particles dominate the ensemble.

To address these challenges, we introduce
Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC)
to efficiently compute the particle weights and resample the particles. In QMCMC, we use quantum amplitude amplification to accelerate the acceptance of particles that better fit the observational data, effectively reducing the computational burden.

In classical MCMC, a new particle is proposed, and its acceptance is determined by the
Metropolis criterion
:

\alpha=\min\left(1,\frac{p(y_{k}|x_{k}^{(i)})}{p(y_{k}|x_{k}^{(j)})}\right),

where
p(y_{k}|x_{k}^{(i)})
is the likelihood of the observation given the particle state
x_{k}^{(i)}
.

In QMCMC, the
Grover operator

G
is used to amplify the probability of accepting ”good” particles (i.e., particles that satisfy the Metropolis criterion):

G=2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I.

This operator increases the likelihood of selecting particles that are closer to the true posterior distribution, thereby speeding up the sampling process. By applying Grover’s algorithm iteratively, we can reduce the number of rejected particles and ensure faster convergence toward the true posterior.

III-C
4
Quantum Resampling

Resampling is a critical step in particle filters that helps to eliminate low-weight particles and focus computational resources on particles that better represent the posterior distribution. In classical particle filters, resampling is computationally expensive, particularly in high-dimensional systems where the likelihood of particle degeneracy is high.

Using QMCMC for the resampling process allows us to maintain particle diversity and prevent degeneration. Quantum amplitude amplification ensures that particles with higher weights are selected more frequently, reducing the number of particles required to accurately represent the posterior distribution.

Quantum resampling works by applying a quantum walk over the particles, with higher-probability particles being more likely to be sampled and replicated. This quantum-enhanced resampling process ensures that the particle filter maintains a diverse and accurate representation of the system’s state, even in high-dimensional spaces.

III-C
5
Summary of the Benefits of Quantum Variational Particle Filters

The combination of variational principles, particle filters, and quantum algorithms in the
Quantum Variational Particle Filter (QVPF)
offers several key advantages:

•

Reduction in Particle Requirements
: The quantum-enhanced optimization and resampling processes reduce the number of particles required to accurately represent the posterior distribution, addressing the curse of dimensionality faced by classical particle filters.

•

Efficient Proposal Distribution
: Using QAOA to optimize the proposal distribution ensures that particles are generated in a way that is more consistent with the system’s dynamics and observations, improving accuracy and efficiency.

•

Faster Weight Updates and Resampling
: QMCMC speeds up the process of computing particle weights and performing resampling, allowing the particle filter to scale to larger, more complex systems without an exponential increase in computational resources.

•

Improved Accuracy in Nonlinear and Non-Gaussian Systems
: The variational framework allows for better handling of nonlinearities and non-Gaussian errors, which are common in real-world data assimilation problems.

By integrating quantum algorithms into the variational particle filter framework, we create a powerful tool for data assimilation in high-dimensional, complex systems such as climate models, atmospheric simulations, and space weather prediction.

V
Parameter Optimization and Convergence Analysis

Efficient optimization of variational parameters and ensuring convergence in both QAOA and QMCMC are crucial to the performance of the Quantum Variational Particle Filter (QVPF). This section provides a rigorous mathematical formulation of these problems, followed by a detailed analysis of how the curse of dimensionality can be mitigated using quantum parallelism.

V-A

Optimizing QAOA Parameters

V-A
1
Variational Cost Function in QAOA

In QAOA, the goal is to minimize the
variational cost function
that is derived from the data assimilation problem. For the 4DVAR cost function:

\begin{split}J(x_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{T}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k}))^{T}R_{k}^{-1}(y_{k}-H_{k}(x_{k})),\end{split}

(12)

we encode it as a Hamiltonian
H_{C}
in QAOA. The total state of the quantum system at any depth
p
of the QAOA circuit is given by:

|\psi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle=U_{M}(\beta_{p})U_{C}(\gamma_{p})\cdots U_{M}(\beta_{1})U_{C}(\gamma_{1})|\psi_{0}\rangle,

where:

•

U_{C}(\gamma_{j})=e^{-i\gamma_{j}H_{C}}
applies the problem Hamiltonian
H_{C}
,

•

U_{M}(\beta_{j})=e^{-i\beta_{j}H_{M}}
applies the mixing Hamiltonian
H_{M}=\sum_{i}X_{i}
(Pauli-X operator).

The variational cost function
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is then the expected value of the Hamiltonian
H_{C}
in the state
|\psi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle
:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\langle\psi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|H_{C}|\psi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle.

The optimization task is to find the variational parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
that minimize
\mathcal{L}
:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}).

V-A
2
Gradient Descent with Parameter-Shift Rule

To minimize
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, gradient-based methods such as
gradient descent
can be employed. The gradient of the cost function with respect to each parameter
\gamma_{i}
or
\beta_{i}
is required. Since direct computation of the gradient in quantum systems is challenging, we use the
parameter-shift rule
.

For any parameter
\theta_{i}\in\{\gamma_{i},\beta_{i}\}
, the gradient can be approximated as:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}+\frac{\pi}{2})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}-\frac{\pi}{2})}{2}.

This formula allows us to compute the gradient by evaluating the cost function at shifted values of the parameter, which can be done via quantum measurement.

V-A
3
Natural Gradient Descent

While standard gradient descent methods are effective, the
natural gradient
method offers an improvement by considering the geometry of the parameter space. The natural gradient uses the
Fisher information matrix

F
to adjust the step sizes, thus speeding up convergence in highly non-convex landscapes:

\Delta\theta_{i}=-\eta F^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}),

where
\eta
is the learning rate, and
F
is the Fisher information matrix, which is approximated by quantum measurements.

Natural gradient descent ensures faster convergence to optimal parameters by adapting to the curvature of the cost function landscape.

V-A
4
Reinforcement Learning and Machine Learning Approaches

In complex quantum systems where the landscape of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is too rugged for traditional methods, reinforcement learning (RL) can be used to optimize the parameters. The RL agent interacts with the quantum system, adjusts the parameters based on rewards derived from the cost function, and learns an optimal strategy for parameter selection. Additionally, surrogate models based on machine learning can predict the cost function landscape, reducing the number of quantum evaluations needed.

V-B

Convergence of QMCMC

In Quantum-enhanced Markov Chain Monte Carlo (QMCMC), convergence is crucial to ensure that the particle filter accurately represents the posterior distribution. QMCMC improves on classical MCMC by providing a quadratic speedup using quantum amplitude amplification.

V-B
1
Quantum Amplitude Amplification

In classical MCMC, the convergence time to reach the stationary distribution is
O(\epsilon^{-2})
, where
\epsilon
is the desired error. In QMCMC,
quantum amplitude amplification
reduces this to
O(\epsilon^{-1})
, providing a quadratic speedup. The Grover operator
G
amplifies the probability of selecting “good” states (i.e., states satisfying the Metropolis criterion):

G=2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I.

By iterating the Grover operator
G
over the particle states, we amplify the probability of selecting high-likelihood particles while reducing the likelihood of low-weight particles. This accelerates the convergence of the sampling process, allowing QMCMC to sample efficiently from the posterior distribution.

V-B
2
Detailed Balance and Ergodicity

For QMCMC to converge to the correct stationary distribution, it must satisfy the
detailed balance
condition:

P(x^{(i)}\to x^{(j)})P(x^{(j)}\to x^{(i)})=P(x^{(j)}\to x^{(i)})P(x^{(i)}\to x^{(j)}),

where
P(x^{(i)}\to x^{(j)})
is the transition probability between states
x^{(i)}
and
x^{(j)}
. Detailed balance ensures that the transition probabilities are symmetric, which is critical for the Markov chain to converge to the correct posterior.

Moreover, the Markov chain must be
ergodic
, meaning that every possible state can be reached from any other state, ensuring full exploration of the state space. Quantum walks are designed to be ergodic by construction, ensuring that the system does not become trapped in local minima.

V-B
3
Convergence Diagnostics

Several metrics are used to monitor convergence in QMCMC:

•

Effective sample size (ESS)
: This measures the number of independent samples that have been drawn from the posterior. A larger ESS indicates faster convergence.

•

Autocorrelation time
: This is the time required for the samples to become decorrelated from their initial state. Shorter autocorrelation times indicate faster convergence.

By using these diagnostics, we can ensure that the QMCMC algorithm has reached the stationary distribution and is providing accurate samples for the particle filter.

V-C

Mitigating the Curse of Dimensionality

The
curse of dimensionality
refers to the exponential growth in the number of particles required as the dimensionality of the state space increases. In high-dimensional systems, classical particle filters become computationally prohibitive due to the need for exponentially many particles to accurately represent the posterior distribution.

V-C
1
Quantum Parallelism and Particle Proposals

One of the key advantages of using QAOA in the variational particle filter is that it leverages
quantum parallelism
to optimize particle proposals. Instead of generating each particle proposal sequentially, QAOA prepares a superposition of all possible particle proposals and evolves the quantum state in parallel. This allows QAOA to explore multiple potential proposals simultaneously, reducing the number of required particles.

Let
|\psi_{0}\rangle
represent the initial superposition of all particle states. The quantum circuit evolves this superposition according to the QAOA evolution, and after applying the problem and mixing Hamiltonians, the final state is:

|\psi_{f}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}|x_{i}\rangle,

where
\alpha_{i}
represents the amplitude of the
i
-th particle state
|x_{i}\rangle
. Quantum parallelism allows us to compute the likelihoods and proposals for all
N
particles in superposition, providing an exponential reduction in the number of particle evaluations.

V-C
2
Quantum-Enhanced Resampling

Resampling is critical for maintaining particle diversity in high-dimensional systems. In classical particle filters, resampling involves duplicating high-weight particles and eliminating low-weight particles, which is computationally expensive in large state spaces.

QMCMC accelerates the resampling process using quantum amplitude amplification. By applying the Grover operator
G
to the particle weights, we can selectively amplify particles with higher likelihoods, ensuring that they are more likely to be resampled. This
quantum-enhanced resampling
process reduces the number of particles needed to represent the posterior distribution and mitigates the curse of dimensionality.

Implementing the hybrid

VI-A

Quantum Hardware Requirements

The quantum algorithms central to this hybrid approach (QAOA and QMCMC) place specific demands on quantum hardware, particularly in terms of the number of qubits, gate fidelities, and circuit depth. Given that both algorithms involve iterative quantum circuits and classical optimization, the hardware must support sufficient quantum resources to handle complex calculations over high-dimensional spaces.

VI-A
1
Number of Qubits

The number of qubits required is closely related to the dimensionality of the state vector
x_{0}
in the 4DVAR problem. Each element of
x_{0}
can be encoded using multiple qubits, depending on the desired precision. In practice, the number of qubits
n
needed scales with the problem size and the number of particles in the particle filter.

For instance, if we represent each component of the state vector
x_{0}
with
k

d

n_{\text{qubits}}=d\cdot k

In large geophysical systems, such as weather models with hundreds of variables (e.g., temperature, humidity, wind vectors) at various grid points, this number could grow rapidly. While current quantum devices offer tens or low hundreds of qubits, this number must increase significantly to accommodate real-world data assimilation tasks. For small test cases, current devices may suffice, but scaling to higher dimensions will require hardware advances.

VI-A
2
Gate Fidelity and Error Rates

Quantum gate fidelity is critical to the success of both QAOA and QMCMC. The success of these algorithms depends on maintaining coherence across multiple iterations, as errors can quickly propagate through the system. Given that QAOA involves repeated application of unitary transformations governed by
H_{C}
(cost Hamiltonian) and
H_{M}
(mixing Hamiltonian), the quantum device must maintain high fidelity during these operations to avoid introducing noise that distorts the optimization.

Typical error rates for near-term devices (NISQ devices) range from
10^{-3}
to
10^{-4}
per gate. The depth
D
of the circuit is another factor to consider since longer circuits amplify the effects of noise. The total error probability after a circuit of depth
D
and with
g
gates per layer is approximately
1-(1-p_{\text{error}})^{D}
, where
p_{\text{error}}
is the per-gate error rate. In practice, circuits must be kept shallow to mitigate these effects, necessitating efficient circuit design for the QAOA and QMCMC steps.

VI-A
3
Connectivity and Circuit Depth

QAOA relies on entangling operations between qubits representing different elements of the state vector. The quantum hardware must support
high qubit connectivity
to allow for interactions between distant qubits in the state space. Devices with limited connectivity may require additional SWAP gates, which increase the circuit depth and introduce more noise.

The depth of the QAOA circuit depends on the number of layers
p
, with each layer involving two operators:
H_{C}
and
H_{M}
. As the number of layers
p
increases, the circuit becomes deeper, making it more susceptible to noise. For small instances, current NISQ devices can handle a few layers of QAOA, but solving larger instances will require improvements in quantum coherence and gate fidelity.

Similarly, QMCMC involves quantum walks and amplitude amplification, which also require entangling gates and multi-qubit operations. The circuit depth for QMCMC must be optimized to maintain coherence over the course of the algorithm.

VI-B

Near-Term Quantum Devices (NISQ Era)

Current quantum devices are in the
Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ)
era, characterized by the ability to perform limited quantum computations while still grappling with noise, decoherence, and relatively small numbers of qubits. NISQ devices can be used to demonstrate proof-of-concept implementations of the hybrid QAOA-QMCMC approach but will face limitations when scaling to realistic data assimilation tasks.

VI-B
1
Proof-of-Concept Experiments

In the near term, small-scale
proof-of-concept experiments
can be conducted using NISQ devices to demonstrate the feasibility of the hybrid approach. These experiments could focus on simplified, low-dimensional models of climate or atmospheric systems, where only a few variables are considered. For instance, a weather model with just a few grid points or a small number of variables (e.g., temperature and pressure) could be used as a test case.

The goal of these experiments would be to:

•

Validate the effectiveness of QAOA in optimizing the initial particle proposals.

•

Demonstrate the quantum speedup offered by QMCMC in updating particle weights and performing resampling.

•

Identify the limitations of current quantum devices, such as error rates and qubit connectivity, that must be addressed for larger-scale implementations.

VI-B
2
Quantum-Classical Hybrid Architecture

Due to the constraints of NISQ devices, it is practical to adopt a
hybrid quantum-classical architecture
, where the quantum device handles the most computationally intensive parts (e.g., QAOA for optimization and QMCMC for sampling), while classical processors handle the less demanding tasks, such as model propagation and observational data assimilation.

In this architecture:

•

The classical processor handles the propagation of the particles through the model dynamics, as this step remains computationally feasible on classical hardware.

•

The quantum processor is employed for the QAOA optimization of particle proposals and QMCMC resampling, leveraging quantum parallelism for computational efficiency.

•

The classical and quantum components communicate iteratively, where the quantum hardware provides optimized states that the classical processor uses to update the model.

This hybrid approach maximizes the potential of available quantum resources while maintaining the accuracy and robustness of the classical methods.

VI-C

Scalability

Scalability is a significant concern when moving from small, proof-of-concept implementations to larger, real-world applications like high-resolution weather forecasting or climate modeling. The ability to scale the hybrid approach depends on the availability of quantum devices with more qubits, lower error rates, and better connectivity.

VI-C
1
Qubit Scaling

For real-world applications, the number of qubits required will increase dramatically as the state vector grows in dimensionality. In a practical setting, weather models may have hundreds of variables at each grid point, with thousands of grid points over a geographical region. Each of these variables must be represented by multiple qubits to achieve the necessary precision. The required number of qubits could range from hundreds to thousands, depending on the granularity of the model and the size of the region being modeled.

To make the QVPF approach viable for large-scale data assimilation, significant advances in quantum hardware are required, particularly in terms of qubit number, error correction, and circuit depth.

VI-C
2
Circuit Depth and Quantum Error Correction

As quantum hardware matures, the introduction of
quantum error correction (QEC)
will be essential to ensure scalability. QEC can reduce the impact of noise and decoherence, enabling deeper circuits and more complex computations. However, QEC comes with the overhead of additional qubits, as each logical qubit may require hundreds of physical qubits for error correction. Balancing the need for qubit scaling and error correction will be a key challenge in making the hybrid approach scalable.

VI-C
3
Parallelization of Quantum Circuits

Parallelization of quantum circuits could help mitigate some of the scalability challenges. For example, different parts of the QAOA optimization could be performed in parallel on multiple quantum processors or on a device with multi-core quantum processing units (QPUs). Similarly, the QMCMC process can be parallelized, where different quantum cores handle different portions of the resampling step.

Simulations can provide insights into:

VII
Potential Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

The hybrid QAOA-QMCMC approach enhanced with particle filters presents an exciting opportunity for advancing data assimilation techniques, particularly in high-dimensional and non-linear systems. However, the complexity of the integration between quantum algorithms and classical methods brings about several challenges. Recent advancements in both quantum computing and classical methodologies offer promising pathways to overcoming these obstacles. Below, we explore the primary challenges associated with this hybrid approach and how ongoing developments in quantum technology can provide solutions.

One of the most significant hurdles facing both classical and quantum-enhanced particle filters is the high dimensionality of the state spaces involved. As the dimensionality of a system increases, the number of particles required to represent the posterior distribution accurately grows exponentially. This problem becomes particularly severe in applications such as high-resolution climate models and complex systems like weather forecasting and space weather prediction, where state spaces can easily span thousands of variables. Managing these state spaces efficiently while maintaining accuracy is a primary concern.

Quantum algorithms such as QAOA and QMCMC are well-positioned to address this challenge by leveraging quantum parallelism. Quantum computers can process exponentially large state spaces in parallel, exploring a much broader range of particle proposals than classical methods. QAOA optimizes particle proposals across multiple dimensions simultaneously, drastically reducing the number of particles required while maintaining high-quality representation of the state space. QMCMC further accelerates the sampling and resampling processes, ensuring that even in large, high-dimensional state spaces, the particles are efficiently updated and resampled. Moreover, by integrating variational methods with quantum algorithms, we can leverage the structural properties of the underlying physical models. This allows computational resources to be focused on the most relevant regions of the state space, guided by domain-specific knowledge about the behavior of systems such as atmospheric or oceanic processes.

Recent progress in quantum hardware, with increasing qubit counts and improved connectivity, holds the potential to mitigate the dimensionality challenge even further. As hardware developments continue, quantum systems will be better equipped to handle the complexities of larger state spaces, enabling us to tackle even more challenging problems in the future.

Another key issue facing quantum-enhanced data assimilation is quantum noise and decoherence, particularly in Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices. These devices are susceptible to noise, which introduces errors in quantum computations. Such errors could impact the accuracy of QAOA and QMCMC, potentially leading to suboptimal particle proposals or inefficient sampling. Noise and decoherence become especially problematic as the depth of the quantum circuits increases, making it harder to maintain quantum coherence throughout the computation.

Despite these limitations, significant strides have been made in developing error mitigation techniques. For instance, zero-noise extrapolation involves running quantum algorithms at different noise levels and extrapolating the result to a zero-noise limit, allowing for the correction of errors introduced by decoherence. Similarly, probabilistic error cancellation employs classical post-processing to undo the effects of noise on quantum computations, while error-resilient versions of QAOA are designed with shallower circuit depths, making them less prone to decoherence while still delivering good approximations for particle proposals. Advances in quantum error correction and fault-tolerant quantum computing will further diminish these issues over time, allowing quantum systems to perform more reliably. In the meantime, hybrid approaches that combine classical pre-processing with quantum optimization and sampling provide an effective workaround to current hardware limitations.

The interaction between classical and quantum systems presents another area of concern. The hybrid QAOA-QMCMC approach relies on this interaction, with a classical optimizer responsible for tuning the variational parameters and managing the data flow between quantum circuits. Communication overhead between classical and quantum processors can slow down the overall optimization process, particularly in real-time applications where time constraints are critical, such as in weather forecasting.

Minimizing communication overhead and optimizing the classical optimizer are crucial to overcoming this challenge. Batch processing, for example, reduces the frequency of communication between the quantum and classical components, minimizing delays. Additionally, employing efficient classical optimizers—such as gradient-based methods or reinforcement learning techniques—can decrease the number of quantum iterations required to find the optimal parameters, speeding up the hybrid workflow. Parallelization of quantum circuits, where hardware allows, can further reduce computation time by enabling larger amounts of data to be processed simultaneously. Improvements in quantum control systems and quantum hardware interfaces will streamline communication between classical and quantum processors, ensuring greater efficiency in real-time applications.

The integration of quantum algorithms with classical techniques introduces a certain level of complexity, as combining variational methods, particle filters, and quantum algorithms demands careful design and calibration. The complexity inherent in this hybrid system may lead to difficulties in debugging, tuning, and scaling the algorithms for larger applications.

A modular design approach is key to managing this complexity. By developing each component of the system independently—such as the QAOA-based particle proposal generation or QMCMC-based resampling—testing and optimization can be done incrementally before fully integrating them into the broader framework. This incremental approach ensures that each part of the system functions as expected before moving on to more complex integrations. Furthermore, machine learning algorithms can be employed to automate the tuning of parameters, both in the quantum algorithms and in the classical parts of the particle filter, reducing the burden of manual calibration. This dynamic adaptation helps the system adjust to different data assimilation problems as they arise. The modular nature of the system also makes it scalable, as individual components can be optimized and scaled independently, allowing the overall framework to handle larger datasets and more complex models without a significant increase in system complexity.

As the integration of quantum and classical techniques becomes more standardized, supported by the maturation of software libraries and development environments such as IBM Qiskit, Google Cirq, and Xanadu PennyLane, the complexity of incorporating quantum algorithms into existing workflows will continue to diminish. These tools will make it easier for researchers and practitioners to adopt and implement hybrid quantum-classical approaches like QAOA-QMCMC in real-world applications, facilitating smoother transitions from theoretical development to practical deployment.
