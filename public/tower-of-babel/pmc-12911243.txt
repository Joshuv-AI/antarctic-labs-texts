Comparative efficacy of myofascial release versus stretching combined with high-powered pulsed therapeutic ultrasound in amateur overhead athletes with active trapezius trigger point pain: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Nizamuddin Khan, Tushita Ghai, Richa Kashyap, Priyadarshani Bhat, Fuzail Ahmad, Amir Iqbal, Talal Alghadir, Faisal Alghadir, Shahnawaz Anwer, Ahmad H Alghadir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActive myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) in the upper trapezius are a leading source of neck pain and functional limitation in athletes. Although myofascial release (MFR) and stretching are frequently used, their comparative effectiveness when combined with high-power pulsed therapeutic ultrasound (HPPT-US) remains unclear.ObjectiveTo compare the efficacy of MFR + HPPT-US versus stretching + HPPT-US on pain intensity, cervical range of motion (Cx-ROM), muscle length (ML), and neck disability in amateur overhead athletes with unilateral upper trapezius active MTrPs. Trial design: A two-arm parallel group, randomized clinical trial design.MethodsThirty-two athletes meeting Travell and Simons’ criteria for active MTrPs were randomized to receive either MFR + HPPT-US (Group A) or stretching + HPPT-US (Group B), administered thrice weekly for two weeks. Primary outcome measure—Pain intensity and secondary outcomes—Cx-ROM, ML, and Neck Disability Index (NDI) were recorded using a visual analog scale (VAS), universal goniometer, Vernier caliper, and neck disability index (NDI) questionnaire, at baseline, week 1, week 2, and one-week follow-up. 26 out of 32 data of enrolled participants were analyzed using a two-way mixed repeated-measures ANOVA (Group × Time) with Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests; statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (η²ₚ) for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise differences.ResultsSignificant main effects of Group (η²ₚ = 0.07–0.26) and Time (η²ₚ = 0.83–0.92) were observed for all outcomes, with a significant Group × Time interaction for ML (η²ₚ = 0.34). Both interventions produced significant improvements across time points; however, Group A demonstrated greater gains following treatment. At follow-up, between-group differences favored Group A for VAS (MD = − 0.77; 95% CI − 1.31 to − 0.39; d = 1.12), Cx-ROM (MD = 2.69°; 95% CI 0.80 to 4.58; d = 1.07), ML (MD = 0.80 cm; 95% CI 0.27 to 1.33; d = 1.14), and NDI (MD = − 4.70; 95% CI − 8.04 to − 1.36; d = 1.09). Improvements were consistent across post-baseline assessments, although the magnitude of change varied by outcome.ConclusionThe combination of MFR and HPPT-US was more effective than stretching with HPPT-US in reducing pain, increasing ML and Cx-ROM, and improving functional outcomes in amateur overhead athletes with active upper trapezius MTrPs. This combined approach may offers superior clinical benefits for managing MTrP-related neck pain and promoting faster recovery in athletic rehabilitation settings.Trial registrationThe study protocol was retrospectively registered to the “ClinicalTrials.gov” under an assigned Identifier: NCT07002593 on 25/05/2025 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07002593).Keywords: Myofascial pain syndromes, Ultrasonic therapy, Athletes, Trigger points, Range of motion

Introduction

Neck pain associated with active myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) in the upper trapezius is a prevalent and debilitating condition among athletes, contributing to functional limitations, reduced range of motion (ROM), and impaired performance [1–3]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that the UT is among the most frequently affected muscles in myofascial pain syndromes. In chronic neck pain populations, active UT MTrPs are present in over 80% of cases [4], and even in asymptomatic individuals, latent MTrPs occur in approximately 20–23% [5]. In shoulder pain cohorts, UT MTrPs are reported in nearly 58% of patients, contributing to altered scapular kinematics and decreased functional capacity [6]. These findings underscore the UT’s clinical relevance in maintaining shoulder and cervical function.

MTrPs are hyperirritable nodules within taut skeletal muscle bands that elicit localized tenderness, referred pain, and neuromuscular dysfunction [7]. Their pathophysiology involves both peripheral sensitization—through inflammatory mediator release and nociceptor activation—and central sensitization, which alters pain modulation in the central nervous system [8]. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to chronic disability, diminished athletic performance, and increased socioeconomic burdens through medical costs and lost productivity [9–11].

Biomechanically, the UT plays a crucial role in overhead athletes by stabilizing and upwardly rotating the scapula to achieve optimal glenohumeral alignment during arm elevation. It acts synergistically with the lower trapezius and serratus anterior to maintain scapulothoracic rhythm and prevent shoulder impingement [1, 12, 13]. However, excessive or imbalanced UT activation can alter scapular kinematics, increase stress on the rotator cuff and cervical spine, and predispose athletes to overuse injuries. Efficient UT function is essential for kinetic chain coordination and effective force transmission during repetitive overhead movements in sports such as volleyball, tennis, and swimming [6, 12, 13].

In athletes, UT MTrPs frequently arise due to repetitive loading, overuse, and biomechanical stress, particularly in sports requiring overhead activity or static cervical postures (e.g., volleyball, swimming, cycling, wrestling) [14, 15]. Conventional treatments include stretching, therapeutic exercise, and myofascial release (MFR), often supplemented with modalities such as therapeutic high-power-pain-threshold ultrasound (HPPT-US), which can enhance tissue healing and reduce inflammation [16, 17]. Stretching techniques, including static and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation aim to improve muscle elasticity and ROM [18], whereas MFR delivered manually or via instrument-assisted soft tissue mobilization (IASTM), targets fascial restrictions and muscle tension [19]. when combined with HPPT ultrasound, these interventions may potentiate outcomes by increasing local blood flow and disrupting trigger point adhesions [20, 21]. Nevertheless, comparative evidence between MFR + HPPT ultrasound versus stretching + HPPT ultrasound remains limited, particularly in athletic populations [22, 23].

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the efficacy of MFR combined with HPPT-US versus stretching combined with HPPT-US on pain, ROM, muscle strength, and functional limitations in athletes with active UT MTrPs. Prior research suggests that integrating manual therapies with HPPT-US may produce greater results by addressing fascial restrictions [24] and tissue microcirculation [16]. We hypothesized that MFR + HPPT ultrasound will lead to greater improvements in pain and mobility compared to stretching + HPPT ultrasound, offering evidence-based insights for optimizing rehabilitation and performance in sports medicine.

Materials and methods

Study design

This randomized controlled trial employed a two-arm, parallel-group repeated-measures design to compare the efficacy of MFR plus HPPT-US and stretching plus HPPT-US in athletes with active upper trapezius MTrPs’. Outcomes assessments were conducted at baseline on Day 1 pre-intervention, and week 1 and 2 immediately post-intervention, and follow up assessment after one-week completion of study’s intervention (after 3-week from baseline). This design minimized bias and allowed examination of temporal changes and between-group differences [25].

Study participants

Fifty-four adult amateur overhead athletes with chronic neck pain were screened, of whom 32 met eligibility criteria and were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: (1) male amateur athletes aged 20–40 years participating in overhead sports (volleyball, tennis, swimming, cricket, baseball, or badminton); (2) unilateral active upper trapezius MTrPs diagnosed per Travell and Simons’ diagnostic criteria (presence of palpable taut band, spot tenderness, referred pain, and local twitch response) [7]; (3) symptom duration of 2–3 months; and (4) provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included (1) individuals with fibromyalgia [26], (2) bilateral upper trapezius’s MTrPs, (3) received prior trigger-point therapy or injections within one-year (4) history of acute trauma/systemic disease, or neurological deficits, and (5) showed non-cooperation in the study.

Amateur overhead athletes were defined as non-professionals regularly engaged in sports requiring repeated arm elevation at recreational, school, or collegiate levels without access to specialized conditioning or medical supervision, making them more prone to shoulder and neck overuse injuries from repetitive throwing, striking, or serving movements [27–29].

Settings and recruitment

The participants were referred by orthopedic consultants and recruited via hospital-based outreach (banners, leaflets, and posters). The trial was conducted in the outpatient rehabilitation department at King Khalid University Hospital (Riyadh, Saudi Arabia) between August 2022 and April 2023. All sessions were performed in standardized clinical rooms equipped with ultrasound therapy units and assessment tools.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned (n = 16 per group) using a computer-generated block randomization to ensure allocation concealment and minimizing selection bias [30]. Group allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes opened only after baseline assessment by the department head to prevent selection bias. To minimize measurement bias, outcome assessor was blinded to group assignments.

Three physical therapists delivered the interventions but were not involved in assessing outcomes, ensuring separation of roles. One administed HPPT-US for all participants to maintain consistency, and two others separately delivered either MFR or stretching. Therapists and participants were informed only that both interventions were expected to be beneficial to minimize expectancy bias.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was pain intensity measured by the 10-cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain) (VAS; 0–10 cm) [31–33]; 2). The secondary outcomes were cervical ROM, neck disability, and muscle length. Cervical ROM (Cx-ROM) assessed using a universal goniometer for flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and rotation [34–37]; 3). Neck Disability Index (NDI-Ar), the Arabic-validated version (Cronbach’s α = 0.89; ICC = 0.96), assessing pain-related disability across 10 items [9, 38, 39]; and 4). Upper trapezius muscle length (ML) measured using a Vernier caliper as the linear distance between the mastoid process and acromioclavicular joint, a method shown to have excellent reliability (ICC = 0.89) [24, 40, 41].

A single blinded assessor conducted all measures at baseline, post-intervention (weeks 1 and 2), and at the week 3 follow-up to maintain consistency.

Pain intensity

The assessment of pain intensity was conducted using a standardized 10-cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), following established protocols to ensure reliability and validity [31]. Participants were seated comfortably in a quiet environment and instructed to mark a point along an unmarked horizontal line that best represented their current upper trapezius pain intensity, with the left anchor (0 cm) indicating “no pain” and the right anchor (10 cm) representing “worst imaginable pain.” The scale was presented horizontally on a firm backing, and participants were given adequate time to self-administer the assessment without clinician influence to prevent bias. The distance from the left anchor to the participant’s mark was measured precisely in millimeters using a ruler, with scores recorded to the nearest whole number. To maintain consistency, the same VAS tool was used for all assessments under consistent lighting conditions, and participants were asked to refrain from taking analgesic medications for at least four hours prior to evaluation.

This method demonstrates excellent psychometric properties, with high validity (r = 0.71–0.78 compared to other pain scales) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.94), while a change of 15–20 mm is considered clinically meaningful in musculoskeletal pain conditions [32, 33].

Cervical range of motion (Cx-ROM)

The assessment of Cx-ROM was conducted using a standardized full-circle goniometer (National 360 Goniometer, National Tools Ltd., New Delhi, India) protocol to ensure reliable and valid measurements of flexion, extension, lateral flexion (right and left), and rotation (right and left) [34, 36]. For all measurements, participants were seated upright with back support and shoulders relaxed to minimize compensatory movements [34–37]. Cervical range of motion assessment for both flexion and extension began from a standardized neutral neck position, with the vertex facing upward and aligned vertically to the ground. In this position, the goniometer’s fulcrum was placed at the external auditory meatus, the stationary arm was held perpendicular to the ground, and the movable arm was aligned along the base of the nose [37]. From this starting position, cervical flexion was measured as participants performed maximal chin-to-chest movement, with a normal range of 50–60° [37]. Cervical extension was then measured from the same neutral position by aligning the movable arm with the midline of the forehead as participants tilted their head maximally backward, with a normal range of 55–75° [37].

Cervical lateral flexion was assessed for both right and left sides, beginning from a standardized neutral neck position with the vertex facing upward and aligned vertically to the ground [37]. For each assessment, the goniometer’s fulcrum was placed at the C7 spinous process (selected over T1 for improved reliability), the stationary arm aligned parallel to the acromion line, and the movable arm positioned along the midline of the head. Participants performed maximal lateral bending of the neck to the right and left, without trunk rotation or shoulder elevation [37]. This method demonstrated particularly strong intra-rater reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.91 to 0.94, and an expected normal range of 35–45° per side.

The cervical rotation measurements right and left, respectively, were obtained with the fulcrum positioned at the cranial vertex, the stationary arm aligned parallel to the shoulders, and the movable arm following the tip of the nose during maximal head rotation. This method demonstrated good intra-rater reliability (ICC = 0.88–0.92) and a normal range of 70–90° per side [37].

All measurements followed strict protocols: three repeated trials per motion with 30-second rest intervals to prevent fatigue, averaged for final values, with the same examiner performing all assessments to maintain consistency. Active range of motion (participant-initiated movement) was measured unless otherwise indicated. This comprehensive protocol, supported by current research evidence, ensures accurate and reproducible assessment of cervical mobility in both clinical and research settings [34–37]. The use of anatomical landmarks with demonstrated reliability and standardized positioning techniques enhances the validity of the obtained measurements while minimizing inter-rater variability.

Neck disability

The functional impact of upper trapezius active trigger point pain was assessed using the Arabic version of the Neck Disability Index (NDI-Ar), a 10-item tool designed to evaluate how neck pain affects daily living [9, 38]. This version has been culturally adapted and validated for use in Gulf countries, including Saudi Arabia. The total score ranges from 0 to 50 and is categorized as follows: 0–4 (no disability), 5–14 (mild), 15–24 (moderate), 25–34 (severe), and ≥ 35 (complete disability). The NDI-Ar demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 and excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93–0.97). Its responsiveness was supported by a strong correlation with global change ratings (r = 0.81, p < 0.001), and no floor or ceiling effects were reported [39].

Upper trapezius muscle length

Upper trapezius muscle length was measured using Vernier caliper [40]. Participants maintained the same standardized seated position. The examiner palpated and marked two key anatomical landmarks: the mastoid process (posterior to the ear) and the acromioclavicular joint (distal end of the clavicle). Using precision Vernier calipers (BMB Vernier Caliper, Bombay Tools Centre Pvt Ltd., Mumbai, Maharashtra, India), the straight-line distance between these two points was measured while the participant maintained a neutral head position i.e., with cervical spine in neutral alignment and shoulders relaxed. Recent studies demonstrate this method has excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.89) when performed by trained clinicians [40]. To ensure accuracy, we implemented three key modifications to traditional protocols: (1) measurements were taken during both relaxed state and at end-range cervical lateral flexion to assess tissue extensibility, (2) skin markings were made with surgical pens to ensure consistent landmark identification, and (3) a standardized tension of 20 g was applied to the caliper jaws to prevent tissue compression artifacts. These procedures are supported by recent myofascial research showing improved reliability (SEM = 1.2 mm) compared to traditional methods [24, 41]. Three consecutive measurements were averaged, with the participant standing briefly between measurements to eliminate tissue creep effects.

Interventions

Both groups underwent interventions three times per week for two weeks. Group A (MFR + HPPT-US) received a 10-minute manual myofascial release (MFR) technique targeting unilateral active upper trapezius trigger points (MTrPs), followed by a 5-minute session of HPPT-US (1.5 W/cm², 1 MHz, pulsed mode) [16, 43]. Group B (Stretching + HPPT-US) participants performed three 30-second static stretches for the upper trapezius (neck side flexion with contralateral rotation) [14], followed by the same HPPT-US protocol.

All interventions were delivered under clinical supervision to ensure protocol adherence and participant safety.

Safety monitoring

No adverse events or pain exacerbations were observed or reported across sessions—intervention or outcomes assessments. All participants tolerated the interventions and outcomes assessment well.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Sub-Committee at King Saud University (Approval No. RRC-2022-07, 17 May 2022) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). The trial was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07002593, May 25 2025) [44]. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Sample size Estimation

A priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, & Buchner, 2009) for an independent samples t-test (two-tailed). The primary outcome was pain intensity measured by the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Preliminary data from a pilot study involving two groups (Group A: 4.03 ± 0.77; Group B: 4.89 ± 0.71) yielded a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.16). Using this effect size, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (1 – β) of 0.80, the estimated minimum required sample size was 24 participants (12 per group). To compensate for a potential attrition rate of up to 25%, the final sample size was adjusted to 32 participants (16 per group).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS, version 26 (IBM SPSS v.26 for window, Armonk, NY, USA). Data were first screened for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A per-protocol analysis was conducted, including only participants who completed all intervention sessions and follow-up assessments, to ensure reliable estimation of treatment effects. A two-way mixed repeated-measures ANOVA (Group × Time) was applied to assess both within-subject effects (changes across four assessment points: baseline, week 1, week 2, and follow-up) and between-subject effects (differences between the two interventions). This model appropriately accounts for correlated repeated measurements within participants and allows evaluation of interaction effects reflecting differential changes over time between groups. When significant main or interaction effects were detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests were used to control for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes for ANOVA were reported as partial eta-squared (η²ₚ), interpreted as small = 0.01, medium = 0.06, and large ≥ 0.14 [45–47], whereas pairwise comparisons were expressed using Cohen’s d (small = 0.2, medium = 0.5, large ≥ 0.8) [45, 48].

Results

Participant flow

After screening 54 potential participants, 32 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in the study. Twenty-two individuals were excluded for the following reasons: 14 failed to meet inclusion criteria, five were unavailable for the complete study duration, and three declined participation without specifying a reason.

During the intervention phase, Group A experienced one withdrawal and two dropouts at follow-up, while Group B had two withdrawals and one dropout. These attrition events resulted in a final analytic sample of 26 participants (13 per group), ensuring balanced group sizes for valid statistical comparison (Fig. 1).

Data characteristics and baseline comparison

Demographic and clinical data were summarized using descriptive statistics and presented in tables and figures. The normality of all baseline variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to selecting appropriate inferential analyses. Baseline characteristics are displayed in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were found between groups for age, pain intensity (VAS), cervical range of motion (Cx-ROM), muscle length (ML), or Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores (p > 0.05), indicating that both groups were comparable and homogeneous at study entry.

Post-hoc comparisons

Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise analyses confirmed significant within-group improvements from baseline to all post-treatment time points (p < 0.001) for VAS, Cx-ROM, ML, and NDI in both groups. The magnitude of change was large across outcomes, with paired-sample effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 0.8) confirming strong treatment responses. Additional improvements between week 1 and week 2, and between week 2 and follow-up, also reached statistical significance for most outcomes, confirming continued progress throughout the intervention phase.

Between-group comparisons demonstrated no differences at baseline (p > 0.05). However, at post-baseline assessments, Group A (MFR + HPPT-US) exhibited significantly greater improvements than Group B (Stretching + HPPT-US) in all outcomes (adjusted p ≤ 0.01). The largest and most consistent between-group differences were observed for muscle length (ML), supporting the significant interaction detected in the mixed ANOVA. Group A also maintained lower VAS and NDI scores and higher Cx-ROM at follow-up, reflecting superior pain relief, functional recovery, and soft-tissue extensibility. Discussion

Table 4 The present randomized clinical trial aimed to compare the efficacy of myofascial release (MFR) combined with high-powered pulsed therapeutic ultrasound (HPPT-US) versus stretching combined with HPPT-US in treating amateur athletes with active myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) in the upper trapezius muscle. The study evaluated outcomes in terms of pain intensity (VAS), cervical range of motion (ROM), muscle length (ML), and functional disability (NDI) across four time points using a two-way mixed repeated-measures ANOVA (Group × Time), followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons.

The results demonstrated significant main effects of Group and Time for all outcomes (p < 0.001), confirming overall improvement across sessions and greater mean gains in the MFR + HPPT-US group. A significant Group × Time interaction was found only for muscle length (ML) (p < 0.001), indicating that changes over time differed between interventions, with Group A achieving more substantial improvement. Although the interaction effects for pain, ROM, and NDI were non-significant, Group A consistently demonstrated lower mean pain scores and disability and higher ROM and ML values at all post-baseline points. These findings suggest that the combined approach provides both statistically and clinically meaningful benefits, particularly in enhancing muscle extensibility and functional recovery.

These findings align with previous studies supporting the effectiveness of MFR and ultrasound therapy in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. For instance, Ajimsha et al. [43] and Castro-Sanchez et al. [48, 49] reported that MFR significantly reduces pain and disability in patients with neck and shoulder dysfunction by improving fascial mobility and decreasing muscle tone. Similarly, Gurcay et al. [23] and Bisset et al. [50] found that therapeutic ultrasound, especially in pulsed modes or high-intensity modes, improves soft tissue repair and pain modulation through increased local microcirculation and cell metabolism. Moreover, Kostopoulos and Rizopoulos [51] emphasized the addressing fascial restrictions is crucial for restoring neuromuscular coordination and preventing recurrence, supporting the observed improvements in ML and NDI in the current study.

In contrast, some studies have reported limited or short-term benefits when MFR or US therapy is used in isolation. For example, Fernández-de-Las-Peñas et al. [8] noted that stretching alone yield modest improvements in pain and mobility, likely because it fails to address the underlying myofascial dysfunction. Likewise, Robertson et al. [20] questioned the therapeutic efficacy of ultrasound therapy, especially when applied with low intensity or short durations. The present results therefore suggest that combining MFR with HPPT-US may yield additive or synergistic effects by simultaneously addressing mechanical and neurophysiological aspects of myofascial dysfunction.

Mechanistically, MFR is a manual technique likely enhance fascial mobility, reduce myofascial adhesions, and activate mechanoreceptors that modulate pain perception and muscle tone [43, 51]. Concurrently, HPPT-US is a biophysical modality (deep tissue heating/mechanical effects) may induce mechanical and thermal effects that increase local blood flow, reduces inflammatory mediators, and promotes cellular repair mechanisms—processes that collectively accelerate tissue repair and pain relief [17, 50]. The significant interaction effect for muscle length observed in this trial could thus be attributed to the additive impact of fascial release with deep-tissue biophysical stimulation, resulting in more efficient restoration of muscle extensibility and function.

Clinical implications

The current findings highlight the clinical value of integrating MFR with HPPT-US for amateur athletes with upper trapezius MTrPs, particularly in sports requiring repetitive overhead activity. This approach appears more effective than stretching-based protocols in achieving early pain reduction, improved cervical kinematics, and enhanced soft-tissue flexibility. Clinically, this combination can facilitate a faster return to training and may serve as a preferred first-line strategy in athletic rehabilitation programs targeting myofascial dysfunction.

Limitations and future recommendations

This study has several limitations. The sample was relatively small, limited to male amateur athletes, restricting the generalizability of findings to female professional or non-athlete populations. The follow-up period was short (one-week post-intervention), preventing conclusions about the long-term sustainability of treatment effects. Additionally, psychological and workload-related factors (e.g., stress, training intensity, and recovery habits) were not evaluated but could influence treatment responsiveness. A per-protocol analysis was adopted to evaluate true intervention efficacy among participants who completed all sessions; however, future trials with larger samples may consider intention-to-treat approaches to enhance generalizability and minimize attrition bias [51, 52, 53]. Bilateral myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) were not assessed in this study to reduce confounding variability and ensure consistent treatment focus on a single affected side.

Future research should include more diverse cohorts, longer follow-up durations, and direct comparisons of MFR + HPPT-US with other evidence-based modalities such as dry needling, laser therapy, or neuromuscular re-education. Additionally, investigating physiological biomarkers—such as local perfusion, inflammatory mediators, or electromyographic activation patterns—may clarify the mechanisms contributing to myofascial recovery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that both interventions (MFR + HPPT-US and Stretching + HPPT-US) were effective in improving pain, cervical mobility, muscle length, and functional ability in amateur athletes with active upper trapezius MTrPs. However, participants receiving MFR + HPPT-US showed consistently greater overall gains in pain reduction, cervical ROM, and neck disability, with a statistically significant group × time interaction for muscle length, indicating superior improvements in tissue extensibility over the intervention period. These findings suggest that combining myofascial release with high-power pulsed ultrasound can enhance rehabilitation outcomes, particularly for restoring muscle flexibility and function. Further large-scale and long-term trials are recommended to confirm these effects and determine their generalizability across athletic populations.
