Cardioprotective Mechanisms of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitors
Authors: Lei Qi, Ernest M Wright, Xinjiang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKey words: cardioprotective effect, cardiovacular disease, heart failure, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have demonstrated cardioprotective effects in reducing major adverse cardiovascular events in several large cardiovascular outcome trials.1 Clinical application of SGLT2 inhibitors presents a paradigm shift in the management of patients with heart failure (HF) across the spectrum of ejection fraction, independent of glycemic control. However, despite SGLT2 inhibitors offering well-documented clinical benefits and carrying a class I recommendation in HF management guidelines, the molecular mechanisms underlying their cardioprotective effects remain unclear.

The identification of an active cotransport mechanism of sodium and glucose in the small intestine in the 1960s, followed by the cloning of SGLT1 in 1987 and SGLT2 in 1992, paved the way for the development of selective SGLT2 inhibitors by pharmaceutical companies in the 1990s.2 Phlorizin, the prototype SGLT inhibitor first isolated from apple tree root bark nearly two centuries ago, requires parenteral administration due to its poor oral availability.1 In 1999, an oral phlorizin derivative T-1095 demonstrated promising potential for treating hyperglycemia in diabetic rat models by nonselectively inhibiting SGLT activities. Subsequently, between 2013 and 2023, more selective SGLT2 inhibitors including canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and bexagliflozin were approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus. In addition, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and a dual SGLT1 and SGLT2 inhibitor sotagliflozin have been approved for HF management to reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization.3 The IC50 values for selective SGLT2 inhibitors range from 1 to 3 nM for human SGLT2, compared to 700 to 8,300 nM for human SGLT1, whereas sotagliflozin exhibits low IC50 values for both SGLT1 (2 nM) and SGLT2 (40 nM).2 Structural models suggest that the glucose moiety in SGLT2 inhibitors binds to the glucose-binding site on the external face of SGLT2 transporters with the aglycone tail extending into the external vestibule in the Na+-bound, outward-facing conformation of the transporter (Figure 1).2

The SGLT2 gene is predominantly expressed in the renal proximal tubule, with negligible expression in other human tissues including heart. Furthermore, a positron emission tomography coupled with an SGLT2 inhibitor, 4-(18F)fluoro-dapagliflozin (F-Dapa), has been developed to map the distribution of functional SGLT2 protein in mice. F-Dapa selectively accumulates in the renal cortexes of wild-type and SGLT1 knockout mice but is undetectable in SGLT2-knockout mice. In contrast, only nonspecific binding is observed in the heart and other organs.2

How do SGLT2 inhibitors achieve cardioprotective effects in patients with HF despite the absence of appreciable expression and functional activity of SGLT2 in the heart? In a mouse model of HF induced by transaortic constriction and apical myocardial infarction, SGLT2 knockout alone does not provide significant cardiac benefits. However, empagliflozin treatment improves left ventricular systolic function and reduces cardiac remodeling in both wild-type and SGLT2 knockout mice, further suggesting beneficial effects of SGLT2 inhibitors are likely independent of direct inhibition of SGLT2 activity. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain potential cardioprotective phenomena of SGLT2 inhibitors with off-target direct cardiac effects and/or non-cardiac systemic effects, such as inhibition of cardiac sodium-hydrogen exchanger 1 (NHE1) and NaV1.5 channel, myocardial/mitochondrial energetics, attenuation of cardiac fibrosis and inflammation, weight loss, and regulation of epicardial adipose tissue and aortic endothelial function (Figure 1).1,2

Increased intracellular sodium concentration in cardiomyocytes contributes to the pathogenesis of HF through impaired calcium homeostasis and excitation-contraction coupling, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic remodeling, and augmented electrical instability and arrhythmia risk. Empagliflozin has been shown to rapidly lower intracellular sodium and calcium concentration in isolated cardiomyocytes, likely through direct inhibition of NHE1.4 The inhibitory effect may be mediated by the binding of the glucose moiety of SGLT2 inhibitors to the sodium-binding site of NHE1. Furthermore, cariporide, an NHE1- specific inhibitor, mimics the beneficial effects of empagliflozin in a mouse model of HF.4 Empagliflozin likely confers its effects against HF through inhibiting NHE1 activity and enhancing nitric oxide signaling.4 Empagliflozin has also been found to decrease sodium entry by inhibiting the late sodium channel current in a mouse model of HF.5 Homology modeling suggests that the glucose moiety of SGLT2 inhibitors binds to the same fDIII-DIV pocket of the human NaV1.5 channel as local anesthetics such as lidocaine.5 By lowering intracellular sodium concentration through inhibition of NHE1 and NaV1.5, SGLT2 inhibitors can contribute to restore calcium homeostasis, improve excitation-contraction coupling, and suppress arrhythmias in HF patients.

Myocardial energetics is disrupted in HF, resulting in impaired mitochondrial energy production due to an imbalance between fatty acid oxidation, ketone oxidation, and glycolysis. Dapagliflozin treatment enhances myocardial ketone utilization by increasing circulating ketone concentration and myocardial ketone oxidation while reducing pyruvate oxidation in a rat model of HF. Cardiac inflammation and fibrosis are involved in the pathological cardiac remodeling in HF. SGLT2 inhibition suppresses the activation of the cardiac nuclear-binding domain-like receptor 3 inflammasome and alleviates transforming growth factor-β1-induced fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. These effects contribute to the prevention and reversal of adverse cardiac remodeling in HF.

Besides possible off-target direct cardiac effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, extracardiac mechanisms have also been put forward including regulation of the cardiorenal axis; osmotic diuresis and natriuresis, autonomic balance by reducing sympathetic tone, and increasing parasympathetic activity, weight loss, reduction in epicardial adipose tissue, improved vascular function, enhanced erythropoietin production, and reduced gut microbiome formation of uremic toxins.

In summary, cell culture and animal model experiments as well as clinical evidence suggest SGLT2 inhibitors have pleiotropic protective effects on cardiovascular pathophysiology. Similarly, although statins primarily function by inhibiting the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway, they exert additional beneficial effects independently of cholesterol biosynthesis reduction. Further characterization through large-scale omics studies such as spatial sequencing, plasma proteomics, metabolomics, and functional genomics at the whole-body level may provide further new insights into the pleiotropic cardioprotective mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors and help identify novel therapeutic targets for HF management.
