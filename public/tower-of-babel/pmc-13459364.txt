Smartphone addiction and its associated factors among public university students in Malaysia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nor Asiah Muhamad, Nur Hasnah Ma’amor, Tengku Puteri Nadiah Tengku Baharudin Shah, Nurul Hidayah Jamalluddin, Fatin Norhasny Leman, ‘Izzah ‘Athirah Rosli, Nik Athirah Farhana Nik Azhan, Mohd Fadzli Mohamad Isa, Norliza Chemi, Muhammad Arif Muhamad Rasat, Norni Abdullah, Mona Lisa Md Rasip, Norli Abdul Jabbar, Nurashikin Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmartphone use has become deeply embedded in daily life, particularly among university students, who may adopt it as a coping mechanism for psychological distress. Excessive or maladaptive use may lead to smartphone addiction, a growing public health concern linked to adverse mental health outcomes. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of smartphone addiction and examine its association with depression, anxiety, and loneliness among public university students in Malaysia.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted between July 2022 and January 2024 among 7,278 students from 20 Malaysian public universities. Data were collected using validated instruments, including the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJGLS-6). Descriptive statistics were used to estimate prevalence, and multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify associated factors of smartphone addiction.ResultsThe prevalence of smartphone addiction was 30.1%. Depression (42.6%), anxiety (41.9%), and loneliness (83.8%) were also highly prevalent. After adjustment, factors associated with smartphone addiction included female gender (AOR 1.148; 95% CI 1.021–1.291), Bumiputera Sabah or Sarawak ethnicity (AOR 1.235; 95% CI 1.031–1.479), higher academic year, and daily smartphone use exceeding 10 h (AOR 5.277; 95% CI 3.514–7.925). Depression (AOR 1.968; 95% CI 1.745–2.218), anxiety (AOR 2.060; 95% CI 1.789–2.372), and loneliness (AOR 1.438; 95% CI 1.207–1.714) were independently associated with smartphone addiction.ConclusionSmartphone addiction is common among Malaysian university students and is strongly associated with demographic factors, psychological distress, and excessive smartphone use. Interventions addressing mental well-being and promoting healthier digital behaviors are essential to reduce addiction risk in this population.Keywords: Smartphone addiction, Depression, Anxiety, Loneliness, University students, Malaysia

Introduction

The rapid evolution of information technology has positioned smartphones as an essential component of modern life, facilitating communication, information access, and social connectivity. Globally, smartphone adoption has increased substantially, rising by 38.38% since 2021 and reaching an estimated 4.69 billion users in 2025, with projections indicating further growth to 5.83 billion users by 2028 [1]. Malaysia reflects these global trends, with approximately 29 million smartphone users recorded in 2021, a figure expected to increase alongside the nation’s projected population growth of 1.74 million by 2025 [2]. Worldwide, younger individuals are consistently identified as the predominant users of smartphone technology [3, 4], and Malaysia is no exception. National data indicate that individuals aged 20 to 34 demonstrated the highest ownership rate at 93.6% in 2021 [5], a finding further supported by the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) 2021 survey, which reported usage rates exceeding 80% among those under 35 years of age [6]. Together, these trends highlight the pervasive and increasing reliance on smartphones among younger populations.

As smartphone usage continues to surge, concerns regarding smartphone addiction have gained prominence. Smartphone addiction which also referred to as problematic or compulsive smartphone use encompasses excessive and uncontrolled patterns of use that interfere with daily functioning, interpersonal relationships, and psychological well-being [7, 8]. Recent statistics from 2024 report that individuals now spend an average of 4.49 h per day on their smartphones [9], while Peng et al. documented daily usage exceeding 5 h among Chinese university students [10]. Increasing screen time has been closely associated with the risk of smartphone addiction, a trend consistently observed across global populations [11, 12]. These patterns underscore the importance of examining smartphone use behaviours and their potential psychosocial consequences, particularly among younger adults who demonstrate the highest levels of engagement.

Growing evidence suggests that smartphone addiction is closely intertwined with various aspects of psychological distress, particularly depression, anxiety, and loneliness. Excessive smartphone use has been associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms, potentially due to disrupted sleep patterns, reduced physical activity, and increased engagement in passive or emotionally triggering online content [13, 14]. Furthermore, individuals with anxiety may rely on smartphones as a coping mechanism to alleviate discomfort, maintain constant social connection, or avoid stressful situations; however, this reliance can reinforce maladaptive behaviours and exacerbate anxiety over time [15]. Loneliness has also been identified as a significant correlate of smartphone addiction, with lonely individuals more likely to use smartphones as a means of seeking virtual social interaction or emotional comfort [16]. While such coping strategies may provide temporary relief, prolonged dependence on digital engagement can contribute to heightened isolation, reduced face-to-face interaction, and deeper feelings of loneliness [17].

Studies among university and college students consistently demonstrate strong associations between smartphone addiction and these mental health conditions. Depression and anxiety have been reported as significant predictors of problematic smartphone use, while loneliness often plays a mediating role, linking emotional distress to compulsive smartphone engagement [18–20]. Given that university students represent a population particularly vulnerable to psychological distress due to academic pressures, transitional life stages, and social expectations, understanding these relationships is critical. Exploring how depression, anxiety, and loneliness contribute to smartphone addiction may provide valuable insights for designing targeted mental health interventions and digital well-being initiatives.

Justification for this study

University students represent one of the most vulnerable groups for developing smartphone addiction due to their high levels of digital engagement, academic pressures, and transitional life circumstances. Many studies have focused on general populations or adolescents, with comparatively fewer addressing young adults in higher education settings, particularly within Southeast Asian contexts where cultural norms, academic environments, and social expectations may differ [17, 19, 20]. In Malaysia, the prevalence of smartphone dependency among young adults is rising alongside increasing digital integration in academic and social life. However, local evidence examining how psychological distress contributes to smartphone addiction remains limited and fragmented. Most existing studies treat depression, anxiety, and loneliness as independent predictors, with less emphasis on understanding how these factors interact or potentially mediate one another.

By identifying the most influential psychological predictors, universities and health professionals can implement early screening strategies, preventive mental health programs, and digital well-being initiatives designed to reduce addiction risk and promote healthier smartphone habits. Hence, this study was conducted to determine the association between depression, anxiety, loneliness with smartphone addiction among public university students in Malaysia and the levels of depression, anxiety, and loneliness within the study population.

Methodology

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional survey was conducted from July 2022 to January 2024 using a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design to ensure the representativeness of public university students in Malaysia. The sampling frame consisted of all twenty public universities, which were first stratified by institution. In the first stage, three faculties per university were randomly selected, resulting in a total of 60 faculties. In the second stage, students aged 18 to 30 who were enrolled in the selected faculties were invited to participate. This approach ensured proportional representation across diverse academic disciplines and institutional settings.

The study targeted undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in the following twenty designated public universities: Universiti Islam Antarabangsa Malaysia (UIAM), Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), Universiti Malaya (UM), Universiti Malaysia Kelantan (UMK), Universiti Malaysia Pahang (UMP), Universiti Malaysia Perlis (UniMAP), Universiti Malaysia Sabah (UMS), Universiti Malaysia Sarawak (UNIMAS), Universiti Malaysia Terengganu (UMT), Universiti Pendidikan Sultan Idris (UPSI), Universiti Pertahanan Nasional Malaysia (UPNM), Universiti Putra Malaysia (UPM), Universiti Sains Islam Malaysia (USIM), Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM), Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin (UniSZA), Universiti Teknikal Malaysia Melaka (UTeM), Universiti Teknologi Malaysia (UTM), Universiti Teknologi MARA (UiTM), Universiti Tun Hussein Onn Malaysia (UTHM), and Universiti Utara Malaysia (UUM).

Within each selected faculty, all eligible students who were present during the scheduled data collection sessions were invited to participate. Participation was voluntary, and no individual-level random selection was performed. Therefore, while probability sampling was applied at the faculty (cluster) level, the final stage of recruitment was based on voluntary participation among those available at the time of data collection.

According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia, the total number of students enrolled in 20 public higher education institutions in 2020 was 584,576 [21]. Based on previous studies, the prevalence of smartphone addiction among university students has been reported to range from approximately 20% to 50% across different populations [17, 18, 20]. For the purpose of sample size estimation, a conservative estimate of 30% was used to balance precision and feasibility. Using a single proportion formula with a 95% confidence level and a precision of 0.02, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 2,017 respondents. After adjusting for a design effect of 1.5 and a 20% non-response rate, the final minimum required sample size was 3,781 participants. The achieved sample size of 7,278 exceeded this requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power and precision.

Data collection procedure

Prior to data collection, formal authorization was obtained from each participating university in accordance with institutional governance procedures. Approvals were secured through the university administration, typically via the Registrar’s Office or Research Management Centre, depending on institutional requirements. This approval included permission to conduct the study within the selected faculties and to report findings in aggregate form without institution-level comparison.

Following institutional approval, the research team coordinated with the Department of Student Affairs (HEPA) at each university to organize recruitment, identify suitable data collection venues, and align schedules with academic calendars to minimize disruption to teaching activities.

Data collection was conducted in phases following the designated schedule provided by each institution. Sessions were arranged in lecture halls, auditoriums, tutorial rooms, or multipurpose spaces with sufficient seating capacity. Before each session commenced, students were assembled and provided with a standardized briefing delivered by trained investigators. The briefing covered the study’s objectives, expected duration, inclusion criteria, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures and procedures for completing the questionnaire. Students were encouraged to ask questions to ensure full understanding before deciding whether to participate.

Participation was entirely voluntary and no academic incentives were offered. Electronic informed consent was obtained in two stages to ensure ethical compliance and clarity. First, verbal informed consent was collected during the briefing, during which students confirmed their willingness to proceed. This was followed by a compulsory online consent form embedded at the beginning of the digital questionnaire. Only students who affirmed consent in the online form were able to access and complete the survey items.

The self-administered questionnaire was disseminated using a QR code linked to a secure Google Forms platform. Students scanned the code using their smartphones, and the survey generally required 10–15 min to complete. To ensure completeness and reduce the incidence of missing data, all questionnaire items were set as mandatory. Additionally, institutional email verification was required before submission. This measure ensured that each participant used an official university email address and prevented multiple submissions from the same individual.

To further safeguard data integrity, attendance during each session was recorded and cross-referenced against the university’s student registration list to prevent duplicate participation. The investigators monitored the room throughout the session to provide technical assistance, ensure privacy, and maintain standardized administration procedures. Upon successful completion of the survey, students received a small token of appreciation, typically in the form of stationery items, as a gesture of thanks for their time and cooperation.

Questionnaire and measures

The questionnaire comprised five sections: a demographic section followed by four validated psychometric scales assessing depression, anxiety, loneliness, and smartphone addiction.

Section A: demographics

This section captured participants’ institutional and academic information (university and faculty, current year of study), sociodemographic data (age, race), and smartphone-related behaviours (average daily smartphone use and primary reasons for using smartphones). To preserve confidentiality, direct personal identifiers such as names and student identification numbers were not collected.

Section B: depression

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). The Malay version of the PHQ-9 has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.70). The instrument contains 9 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a total score from 0 to 27. Consistent with the PHQ-9 scoring guidance, the presence of a depressive disorder was considered when participants endorsed at least four items within the designated shaded response range [22, 23].

Section C: anxiety

Anxiety was assessed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7). The Malay translation validated by Sherina et al. (2012) reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.74. The GAD-7 comprises 7 items rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), producing a total score between 0 and 21. Drawing on psychometric evidence for adolescents and young adults, a cut-off score of ≥ 8 was applied in this study to indicate probable anxiety disorder [24–26].

Section D: loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the 6-item De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJGLS-6) [27]. The Malay version has been validated with a Cronbach’s α of 0.71. Items on the DJGLS-6 include both negatively and positively worded statements and use a 3-point response format (“yes”, “more or less”, “no”), producing total scores from 0 to 6. According to the original scoring algorithm, scores of 2–4 represent moderate loneliness and 5–6 indicate severe loneliness [28]. In this study, a threshold of ≥ 2 was used to define loneliness, thereby capturing individuals experiencing at least moderate levels of loneliness. This approach is consistent with its use in previous epidemiological studies [29].

Section E: smartphone addiction

Problematic smartphone use was evaluated using the Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Version (SAS-SV) [30]. The Malay adaptation of the SAS-SV has shown excellent internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) [31]. The short form contains 10 items rated on a 6-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), with higher total scores reflect greater levels of smartphone addiction. For prevalence estimation, the total score was dichotomized using a cut-off of ≥ 36 to classify as “addicted” and < 36 as “not addicted,”. Although the original SAS-SV proposed lower gender-specific cut-offs (31 for males and 33 for females) [30], a higher threshold of ≥ 36 was adopted based on the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC)- derived optimal threshold reported in the Malay SAS-SV validation study among Malaysian adolescents, which identified this value as a better indicator of more severe problematic smartphone use [32].This stricter threshold was used to improve specificity and to reduce potential overestimation of prevalence.

For analytic purposes, scores from each validated instrument were transformed into dichotomous variables based on established cut-offs in the literature. This categorical coding facilitated the computation of prevalence estimates and supported logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with smartphone addiction.

Data management and statistical analysis

All datasets underwent a rigorous data management process prior to analysis to ensure accuracy, reliability, and completeness. Raw data exported from Google Forms were initially screened for duplicate entries using institutional email identifiers and timestamp checks. Following deduplication, the datasets were cleaned and cross-checked against the original questionnaire structure to confirm proper variable coding, response formatting, and alignment with predefined measurement scales. Logical consistency checks were performed to identify out-of-range values, contradictory responses, or patterns suggestive of inattentive responding. Any discrepancies identified were reviewed and corrected according to established data-cleaning protocols.

Missing data were not present in the final dataset, as all questionnaire items were configured as mandatory within the online survey platform and participants were required to complete all items prior to submission. Therefore, no imputation methods were applied.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of participants, including age, sex, year of study, field of study, and university affiliation. Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to describe the distribution and prevalence of depression, anxiety, loneliness, and smartphone addiction within the sample. These descriptive indicators provided an overview of psychological well-being and digital behaviour among university students across the 20 institutions.

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine bivariate associations between continuous scores of smartphone addiction scores and psychological variables (depression, anxiety, and loneliness). Correlation coefficients (r) and corresponding p-values were reported to reflect the strength and direction of these associations. Prior to analysis, assumptions for Pearson’s correlation were assessed, including approximate normality of continuous variables, linearity of relationships, an absence of significant outliers, and were deemed to be adequately satisfied.

The use of both correlation and regression analyses served complementary purposes. Correlation analysis was applied to explore the strength and direction of relationships between continuous variables, while logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with smartphone addiction as a dichotomous outcome, based on established cut-off scores. This combined approach enabled both detailed evaluation of continuous relationships and estimation of adjusted associations relevant for prevalence and risk interpretation.

Prior to multivariate analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All variables demonstrated VIF values below five, indicating that no significant multicollinearity was present and that all predictors could be reliably included in the regression analysis. Following this, multiple logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with smartphone addiction, classified dichotomously as addicted versus non-addicted based on established cut-off scores.

Variables showing statistically significant associations in univariate analyses were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model. Model building employed forward selection, backward elimination, and enter methods to ensure robustness and consistency of the final model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to quantify the magnitude and direction of the associations between psychological factors and smartphone addiction. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05. Model diagnostics, including goodness-of-fit tests and residual analyses, were performed to evaluate the adequacy and stability of the final regression model.

Ethical approval

This study was fully funded by the Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR IR-22-00965-YZW). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical approved by the Malaysia Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health (Ref: 22-00965-YZW, August 21st, 2023). Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the deans of each participating public university in Malaysia. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to completion the questionnaire.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this study. Findings will be shared via academic publications, conference presentations, and summary reports.

Factors associated with smartphone addiction

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with smartphone addiction among university students. As shown in Table 3, univariate analysis identified several sociodemographic and psychological characteristics associated with higher odds of smartphone addiction. Female students had higher odds compared to males (OR 1.440, 95% CI 1.293–1.605). Students aged 21–25 years also showed higher odds (OR 1.349, 95% CI 1.206–1.510). Academic year demonstrated a similar pattern, with third-year students (OR 1.528, 95% CI 1.344–1.738) and fourth- or fifth-year students (OR 1.410, 95% CI 1.178–1.687) showing higher odds compared with first year students. Longer daily smartphone use was also associated with higher odds of smartphone addiction, particularly among those using smartphones for more than three hours per day.

Psychological factors showed strong associations with smartphone addiction. Students reporting depressive symptoms (OR 3.098, 95% CI 2.792–3.437), anxiety (OR 3.353, 95% CI 2.967–3.789), or loneliness (OR 2.346, 95% CI 1.997–2.756) had higher odds of smartphone addiction. Ethnic differences were also observed, with Chinese (OR 0.772, 95% CI 0.437–0.793) and Indian students (OR 0.547, 95% CI 0.411–0.728) showing lower odds compared with Malay students.

In the multivariate model (Table 3), several variables remained statistically significant after controlling for confounding variables. Female gender (AOR 1.148, 95% CI 1.021–1.291) and Bumiputera Sabah and Sarawak ethnic group (AOR 1.235, 95% CI 1.031–1.479) were associated with higher odds of smartphone addiction. Academic year remained associated, with third-year students (AOR 1.409, 95% CI 1.228–1.618) and fourth- or fifth-year students (AOR 1.318, 95% CI 1.088–1.598) showing higher odds relative to first-year students. Duration of smartphone use demonstrated a graded association, where students using smartphones for 3–6 h per day had higher odds (AOR 1.627, 95% CI 1.088–2.432), and those using smartphones for more than 10 h per day showed the highest odds (AOR 5.277, 95% CI 3.514–7.925). Indian students had lower odds of smartphone addiction (AOR 0.730, 95% CI 0.537–0.992).

Psychological distress remained associated with smartphone addiction in the adjusted model. Students reporting depression (AOR 1.968, 95% CI 1.745–2.218) and anxiety (AOR 2.060, 95% CI 1.789–2.372) showed higher odds of smartphone addiction. Loneliness was also associated with higher odds (AOR 1.438, 95% CI 1.207–1.714, p < 0.001).

Overall, the findings suggest that gender, ethnicity, academic standing, daily smartphone usage, and psychological distress particularly depression, anxiety, and loneliness are factors associated with higher odds of smartphone addiction in this population.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence and associated factors of smartphone addiction among university students in Malaysia and explored the relationship between psychological distress (depression, anxiety, and loneliness) and problematic smartphone use. The findings showed that 31.1% of students met the criteria for smartphone addiction, indicating a relatively lower prevalence compared with many international reports. Higher prevalence has been reported in Iraq (78.3%) [32], India (73%) [33], Saudi Arabia (71.9%) [34], Bangladesh (61.4%) [35], and Iran (97.8%) [36], although considerable variation exists within countries. In contrast, lower or comparable rates have been reported in China (29.8%) [37], Serbia (22.7%) [12], Palestine (37.9%) [38], and India (26.8%) [39]. Local comparisons suggest that the prevalence among Malaysian university students is lower than previously reported [18, 40], including Malaysian adolescents (37.1%) [41]. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in cultural norms, digital behaviour patterns, lifestyle factors, as well as variations in measurement tools, scoring systems, and cut-off criteria used across studies. The lack of standardization across measurement tools highlights the importance of establishing more consistent and culturally adaptable diagnostic thresholds for smartphone addiction.

Duration of smartphone use was significantly associated with smartphone addiction, consistent with evidence identifying screen time as an important behavioural correlate [12, 18, 42]. Gender differences were also observed, with higher proportions of smartphone addiction among female students, in line with several international studies [20, 36, 43]. However, inconsistent findings have also been reported, with some studies reporting higher addiction risk among males [32, 42] or no significant gender differences [40, 41]. These inconsistent findings may reflect gendered variations in smartphone use patterns, motivations for digital engagement, types of online activities, and coping preferences. Evidence suggests that females often engage more intensively with social communication features, driven by internalizing tendencies and social pressures, whereas males may exhibit externalizing behaviours and gravitate toward entertainment-based digital content [44, 45].

Ethnic differences were also observed, with higher proportions of smartphone addiction among students from Bumiputera Sabah and Sarawak. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [40, 41, 46, 47], although contextual factors such as geographical relocation, cultural adjustment, and separation from familial networks are not always examined in detail. Given that the participating universities were primarily located in Peninsular Malaysia, students from East Malaysia may rely more heavily on smartphones for maintaining social ties, managing homesickness, and facilitating adaptation to new environments. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously. They do not indicate a direct effect of ethnicity, but may reflect underlying socioeconomic, geographic, or institutional factors, including differences in access to resources, social support, and adaptation contexts. Additionally, unmeasured confounding variables may have contributed to these associations, therefore, causal interpretations should be avoided. Similar adjustment-related dynamics may influence younger students transitioning into university life, as supported by studies showing heightened addiction risk among those aged 18 to 20 [36, 48]. While age was not a significant predictor in the present study, academic year was. Students in their third year and beyond exhibited higher addiction risk, consistent with findings from Saudi Arabia [48], though contrasting with studies suggesting that first-year students are more vulnerable [49]. These inconsistencies may relate to differing academic pressures, workload intensity, and coping strategies that vary across programs, faculties, and institutions.

Psychological distress was also associated with smartphone addiction. Students reporting depression, anxiety, or loneliness showed higher proportions of smartphone addiction, consistent with findings from both local and international studies on depression [20, 39–41, 43, 47], anxiety [39, 47], and loneliness [46, 50–53]. Notably, loneliness was highly prevalent in this study. This high prevalence should be interpreted with caution, as the use of a ≥ 2 threshold on the DJGLS-6 captures both moderate and severe loneliness and, while consistent with prior epidemiological studies, may overestimate the burden of clinically significant loneliness in this population. Cultural factors may also influence how loneliness is perceived and reported among Malaysian university students. Despite its high prevalence, loneliness showed the weakest correlation with smartphone addiction, suggesting that although widely experienced, its association with problematic smartphone use may be less direct or may operate through more complex or indirect pathways compared to depression and anxiety. Interestingly, loneliness was also common among students who were not classified as having smartphone addiction, indicating that it may represent a broader underlying experience not limited to problematic smartphone use. Given that social media browsing was the most frequently reported reason for smartphone use in this study, it is plausible that students engage with these platforms to maintain social connection or cope with feelings of loneliness. However, as this study did not directly assess usage motivations or coping behaviours, this interpretation should be considered exploratory.

These associations may suggest that smartphones may be used as a coping mechanism for emotional distress, for example through seeking social reassurance or distraction. However, this study did not directly assess coping behaviours or emotional regulation. Therefore, such mechanisms should be considered as plausible hypotheses rather than definitive explanations. Alternatively, higher smartphone use may co-occur with poorer psychological well-being, such as reduced sleep quality, lower physical activity, and increased exposure to distressing online content. Depression, anxiety, and loneliness are conceptually related constructs that may overlap as indicators of psychological distress. Although multicollinearity was not observed statistically, this conceptual overlap should be considered when interpreting their independent associations.

When considered together, the three psychological variables demonstrated differing strengths of association with smartphone addiction, with anxiety showing the strongest relationship, followed by depression, and then loneliness. This pattern may reflect differences in how individuals engage with smartphones in response to emotional states. For instance, individuals with anxiety may be more inclined toward frequent checking behaviours, reassurance-seeking, and constant connectivity, which can reinforce habitual smartphone use [14, 15]. In contrast, depressive symptoms may be associated with more passive forms of engagement, such as prolonged scrolling or disengagement from offline activities [13, 14]. Although loneliness was highly prevalent, its weaker association suggests that it may function as a more distal or indirect factor, potentially influencing smartphone use through other psychological pathways [13]. These distinctions highlight the importance of examining specific forms of psychological distress individually, rather than treating them as a single construct, when investigating problematic smartphone use.

Importantly, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes any conclusions about directionality. The observed relationships may be bidirectional, where psychological distress is associated with increased smartphone use, while excessive use may in turn be associated with worsening emotional well-being. Additionally, unmeasured factors such as personality traits, coping styles and social support may influence these associations.

Overall, the findings suggest that smartphone addiction among university students is associated with a combination of sociodemographic and psychological factors. Excessive smartphone use may serve as an adaptive but ultimately maladaptive response to emotional distress, while demographic characteristics such as gender, ethnicity, and academic year shape susceptibility. These results highlight the need for universities to consider digital well-being as an integral component of student support systems.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference, and precludes establishing temporal relationships between psychological distress and smartphone addiction. As such, the observed associations may be bidirectional, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify directionality.

Second, reliance on self-administered questionnaires introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability bias, although the use of validated tools helps mitigate these concerns.

Third, the sample was predominantly Malay (approximately 80%), reflecting the demographic composition of the participating public universities but limiting generalizability to more diverse populations or private institutions. Future research should include broader institutional and demographic representation.

Fourth, although validated scales were used, the choice of cut-off points and their cultural applicability may influence prevalence estimates. In particular, the use of a ≥ 2 threshold for the DJGLS-6 captures both moderate and severe loneliness and, while consistent with prior epidemiological studies, may overestimate the prevalence of clinically significant loneliness in this population. Cultural differences in the perception and reporting of loneliness may further affect these estimates. Additionally, loneliness demonstrated a relatively weak correlation with smartphone addiction despite its high prevalence, suggesting that its relationship with problematic smartphone use may be more complex or indirect.

Finally, although a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design was applied at the institutional and faculty levels, participation at the individual level was voluntary. Consequently, the study may be subject to self-selection bias, which could affect the representativeness of the sample and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Recommendations

Several recommendations arise from these findings. Future research should employ longitudinal or cohort-based designs to establish causal pathways and account for temporal variations in psychological distress and smartphone use. Mixed-methods approaches combining surveys with qualitative interviews may deepen understanding of students’ motivations, experiences, and coping mechanisms. Inclusion of more diverse university populations would improve generalizability and enhance the accuracy of prevalence estimates. Objective digital-use metrics, such as screen-time logs or tracking applications, should be integrated to reduce reliance on self-reported data.

From an intervention perspective, universities should incorporate digital well-being and mental health programs into their student support systems. These may include accessible counselling services, mental health screening, peer support networks, and resilience-building or stress-management workshops. Programs promoting healthy smartphone habits such as mindfulness-based interventions, digital literacy training, and time-management strategies could help students develop healthier coping mechanisms. Tailored interventions targeting high-risk groups, particularly those with depressive or anxiety symptoms, extended smartphone use, or loneliness, may be especially beneficial.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that excessive smartphone use frequently functions as a coping mechanism for psychological distress particularly symptoms of depression, anxiety, and loneliness and that demographic factors such as gender, ethnicity, and year of study further modify an individual’s risk of developing problematic use. To prevent escalation, universities should adopt a comprehensive approach that addresses both the underlying mental-health needs and the behavioural drivers of smartphone dependence. Practical measures include accessible counselling and psychological services, routine screening and early identification of students at risk, structured peer-support networks, and resilience-building workshops. Complementary interventions such as digital-wellbeing education, time-management and healthy screen-use training, mindfulness-based stress reduction programs, and facilitated opportunities for face-to-face social engagement can help students develop alternative coping strategies and reduce reliance on their devices. Integrating these elements into student welfare systems, orientation programs, and academic support services offers a coordinated pathway to lower smartphone addiction while simultaneously improving overall student well-being.
