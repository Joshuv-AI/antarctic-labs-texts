Measuring the Complexity of Countable Presburger Models
Jason Block

Abstract
. We take two approaches to classifying the complexity of Presburger models: Scott analysis and degree spectra. In particular, we investigate the possible Scott sentence complexities and possible degree spectra of models of Presburger arithmetic. Many of our results will be achieved by showing how given a linear order ℒ \mathcal{L} , we can construct a Presburger group P ℒ P_{\mathcal{L}} that maintains much of the structure of ℒ \mathcal{L} .



1.
Introduction

In his 1965 article
[
31
]
, Dana Scott showed that every countable structure can be identified up to isomorphism by an infinitary sentence. That is for every countable structure
\mathcal{A}
, there exists an
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
sentence
\phi
such that given any countable
\mathcal{B}
,

\mathcal{B}\models\phi\iff B\cong\mathcal{A}.

We call such a
\phi
a
Scott sentence
of
\mathcal{A}
. We define the
Scott rank
of
\mathcal{A}
to be the least ordinal
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{A}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence. A slightly finer-grained notion then Scott rank is
Scott sentence complexity
, which we define as the least
\Gamma
among
\{\Sigma_{\alpha},\Pi_{\alpha},d\text{-}\Sigma_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}
such that
\mathcal{A}
has an infinitary
\Gamma
Scott sentence.

While Scott rank and Scott sentence complexity measure the difficulty of describing a countable structure, the difficulty of computing a copy of such a structure is given by its
degree spectrum
. Given a countable structure
\mathcal{A}
, we define the degree spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
to be the set of Turing degrees of copies of
\mathcal{A}
. In this article we examine the possible Scott ranks, Scott sentence complexities and degree spectra of models of
Presburger arithmetic
, which is just the first order theory of
(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
. We refer to models of this theory as
Presburger groups
.

Questions regarding degree spectra and Scott analysis have long been asked about
Peano arithmetic
, the much studied sub-theory of
\text{Th}(\mathbb{N},+,\cdot,<,0,1)
, which includes multiplication. As shown by Tennenbaum
[
32
]
in 1959, no nonstandard model of Peano arithmetic has a computable copy. In contrast, there are many nonstandard models of Presburger arithmetic with computable copies (e.g.
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Z}
). Thus, although degree spectra can distinguish the standard model of Peano arithmetic, it fails to do so for Presburger arithmetic.

In
[
28
]
, Montalbán and Rossegger examined the
Scott spectrum
of Peano arithmetic. The Scott spectrum of a theory
T
is the set of all Scott ranks of countable models of
T
. Montalbán and Rossegger found this Scott spectrum to be
\{\alpha:\alpha=1\text{ or }\omega\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}\}
. In contrast, we will see by Corollary
3.2
that Presburger arithmetic has complete Scott spectrum. That is, its Scott spectrum contains all countable positive ordinals. Further Scott analysis was done for Peano arithmetic by Gonzalez, Łełyk, Rossegger and Szlufik in
[
11
]
where they found the possible Scott sentence complexities for these models. Similarly, we investigate the possible Scott sentence complexities of Presburger groups in Section
3
.

Dariusz Kalociński coined the term
simplest model property
to describe a theory that has a unique model of least Scott rank. It was shown in
[
28
]
that only the standard model of Peano arithmetic has Scott rank
1
, and so Peano arithmetic has the simplest model property. We will get the same result for Presburger arithmetic by Corollary
3.22
. Thus, unlike degree spectra, Scott analysis succeeds in identifying the standard models of both Peano and Presburger arithmetic. It should be noted that in a forthcoming paper, Kalociński independently proved that Presburger arithmetic has the simplest model property using different methods.

Given a countable linear order
\mathcal{L}
, we describe how to construct a countable Presburger group
P_{\mathcal{L}}
that retains much of the structure of
\mathcal{L}
. In
[
12
]
, Gonzalez and Rossegger investigate the possible Scott sentence complexities for linear orders. Further work on the topic was done by Gonzalez, Harrison-Trainer and Ho in
[
10
]
. In particular, the authors of
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
were able to find all the possible Scott complexities of linear orders. We are able to carry over many of their results to Presburger groups of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
. In particular, we will see in Theorem
3.1
that there exist Presburger groups with the following Scott sentence complexities:

•

\Pi_{\alpha}
for all ordinals
\alpha>1
;

•

d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha}
for all successor ordinals
\alpha>1
;

•

\Sigma_{\alpha}
for all successor ordinals
\alpha>4
.

Furthermore, we show in Theorem
4.7
that there is no Presburger group with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{3}
. We leave open for now whether there are Presburger groups with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{4}
. Note, other complexities not mentioned above are not possible for infinite structures in general.

To prove that no Presburger group has Scott complexity
\Sigma_{3}
, we must look beyond just Presburger groups of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
. Furthermore, we must look beyond the class of what we call
plain
Presburger groups, which we do in Section
4
. The class of non-plain Presburger groups includes the recursively saturated models, which we show to all have Scott rank
2
(Proposition
4.1
).

In Section
5
we find some notable degree spectra of Presburger groups. Many (though not all) of these spectra will come from groups of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
. We will see by Theorem
5.8
that
\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}
. Thus, we may make use of past results about the degree spectra of linear orders to find degree spectra for Presburger groups. In addition to the spectra of groups of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
, we also show that the degree spectrum of a Presburger group can be the set
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}\}
, the set
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
or the set
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
for any
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
(see Theorem
5.17
).

Last, we pose some open questions in Section
6
.

1.1.
Scott Analysis

Given a language
L
, we denote the set of (finitary) first order
L
-formulas as
L_{\omega,\omega}
. We may expand to the set of infinitary first order
L
-formulas, denoted
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
, by allowing the use of the symbols
\VV
and
\WW
, which denote countably infinite disjunctions and conjunctions respectively. A formula is
\Sigma^{in}_{0}
if and only if it is
\Pi^{in}_{0}
if and only it is a quantifier free
L_{\omega,\omega}
formula. Given a countable ordinal
\alpha
, we say that a formula is
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
if it can be expressed in the form

\VV_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(\exists\vec{y})[\psi_{i}(\vec{x},\vec{y})]

where each
\psi_{i}
is
\Pi^{in}_{\beta_{i}}
with
\beta_{i}<\alpha
. A formula is
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
if its negation is
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
, which is to say it can be expressed in the form

\WW_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(\forall\vec{y})[\neg\psi_{i}(\vec{x},\vec{y})].

We say that a formula is
d
-
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
if it can be expressed as the conjunction of a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
and a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
formula (and is thus the "difference" of
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
formulas).

The (parameterless) Scott rank of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})
, is the least
\alpha>0
that satisfies the equivalent conditions of the following theorem of Montalbán:

Theorem 1.1

(From
[
25
]
)
.

Given a countable structure
\mathcal{A}
and a countable ordinal
\alpha>0
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

Every automorphism orbit in
\mathcal{A}
is
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
-definable; and

(2)

\mathcal{A}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence; and

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is uniformly
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}
-categorical.∎

It should be noted that a Scott rank will always be a countable ordinal. A structure
\mathcal{A}
is uniformly
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha}
-categorical if there is a Turing operator
\Psi
along with an oracle
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for all structures
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{A}
,

\Psi^{(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{C})\oplus S)^{(\beta)}}

is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{C}
, where
\beta=\alpha
if infinite and
\beta=\alpha-1
if finite. That is,
1+\beta=\alpha
. Here
\mathcal{D}(B)
denotes the atomic diagram of
\mathcal{B}
, which may be viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{N}
(see e.g.
[
26
]
).

We define the
parameterized Scott rank
of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{A})
, to be the least
\alpha
such that
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})=\alpha
for some tuple
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{<\omega}
. The following theorem gives a similar result to Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.2

(From
[
25
]
)
.

Given a countable structure
\mathcal{A}
and a countable ordinal
\alpha>0
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\text{pSR}(\mathcal{A})\leq\alpha
; and

(2)

\mathcal{A}
has a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha+2}
Scott sentence; and

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is
\Delta^{0}_{\alpha}
-categorical on a cone. ∎

We denote the Scott sentence complexity of
\mathcal{A}
as
\text{SC}(\mathcal{A})
. Recall that this is the least
\Gamma
among
\{\Sigma_{\alpha},\Pi_{\alpha},d\text{-}\Sigma_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}
such that
\mathcal{A}
has an infinitary
\Gamma
Scott sentence. For a comprehensive overview of Scott sentence complexity, see
[
14
]
. It was shown by A. Miller
[
22
]
that if a structure
\mathcal{A}
has a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence and a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence with
\alpha\geq 2
, then
\mathcal{A}
also has a
d
-
\Sigma_{\beta}^{in}
Scott sentence for some
\beta<\alpha
. This gives that there will indeed be a "least" such
\Gamma
so long as we define
\Sigma_{\alpha},\Pi_{\alpha}<d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha}
.

1.2.
Degree Spectra

The degree spectrum of a structure
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{A}):=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\exists\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}\text{ with }\deg(\mathcal{B})=\boldsymbol{d}\}.

In
[
18
]
, Knight showed that the degree spectrum of a structure is either upwards closed or equal to a singleton. Furthermore, the degree spectrum of a structure
\mathcal{A}
will be a singleton if and only if there exists a finite subset of
\mathcal{A}
such that
any permutation of
\mathcal{A}
that fixes this subset is an automorphism. Such structures are called
automorphically trivial
(not to be confused with having a trivial automorphism group). None of the structures discussed in this paper are automorphically trivial, and so all degree spectra discussed will be upwards closed.

Note that if
P
is a Presburger group with a computable copy (such as
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Z}
) then
\text{DgSp}(P)
is just the set of all Turing degrees. If there is a degree
\boldsymbol{c}
such that
\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{A})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{c}\leq\boldsymbol{d}\}
, then we say that (the isomorphism class of)
\mathcal{A}
is of degree
\boldsymbol{c}
. We will see by Proposition
2.7
and Corollary
5.5
that there exists a Presburger group of each degree. However, the more interesting case is when a structure does not have any degree, and we will construct many such Presburger groups in section
5
.

2.
Presburger Arithmetic

Definition 2.1
.

Presburger arithmetic is the first order theory of the structure
(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
.

Mojżesv Presburger, for whom the theory is named, showed in his 1927 article
[
29
]
that
\text{Th}(\mathbb{Z},+,0,1)
is decidable and axiomatized by the the axioms for torsion-free abelian groups along with the following axiom for each natural number
n\geq 1
:

(\forall x)(\exists y)[ny=x\vee ny+1=x\vee\cdots\vee ny+(n-1)=x].

Note,
ny
is shorthand for
\overbrace{y+\cdots+y}^{\text{$n$-many}}
.
At the suggestion of Tarski, Presburger proved this result using quantifier elimination
[
13
]
. In a supplement to
[
29
]
, Presburger remarked that
\text{Th}(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
can be shown to be decidable using a similar quantifier elimination argument.

The fact that Presburger arithmetic is complete, decidable and computably axiomatizable distinguishes it from other similar theories. The theory
\text{Th}(\mathbb{N},+,\cdot,<,0,1)
, which includes multiplication and is often called
True arithmetic
, is complete but not decidable nor computably axiomatizable. Peano arithmetic, a sub-theory of true arithmetic, is computably axiomatized but is not complete by Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem
[
7
]
. Additionally, the set of consequences of the axioms of Peano arithmetic is not decidable, as exemplified by the resolution of Hilbert’s tenth problem (see e.g.
[
5
]
).

Some authors use the name Presburger arithmetic to refer to the theory of the naturals under addition instead of the integers. There is not much difference between choosing one over the other, as a model of
\text{Th}(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
can be turned into a model of
\text{Th}(\mathbb{N},+,<,0,1)
by simply removing the negative elements and vice versa. We choose
\text{Th}(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
for this paper as the models we get are groups and thus more pleasant to work with. However, all of our main results still hold for models of
\text{Th}(\mathbb{N},+,<,0,1)
.

Definition 2.2
.

A model of Presburger arithmetic is called a Presburger group.

Remark 2.3

(See e.g.
[
20
]
)
.

A structure
\mathcal{A}
is a Presburger group if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is a discretely ordered abelian group (with the constant
1
defined as the successor of
0
) that models the following sentence for all integers
n\geq 2
:

(\forall x)(\exists y)[ny=x\vee ny+1=x\vee\cdots\vee ny+(n-1)=x].

As a result of these axioms, we get that the residue modulo
n
of an element of a Presburger group is definable for every
n\geq 1
. This allows us to define the
residue sequence
of an element of a Presburger group.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
A
be a Presburger group. We define the residue sequence of
a\in A
as the sequence
\rho(a)=(a\mod 1,a\mod 2,a\mod 3,...)
.

This function
\rho
gives a homomorphism from a Presburger group to the following profinite group:

Definition 2.5
.

The (additive) group of profinite integers
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
is the inverse limit of the inverse system
\langle\mathbb{Z}/i\mathbb{Z},f^{j}_{i}\rangle_{(I,\prec)}
where
I
is the positive integers, the partial order
\prec
is defined such that
i\prec j
if
i
divides
j
, and
f^{j}_{i}
is defined so that
f^{j}_{i}(x)\equiv x\mod i
. That is,
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
is the collection of integer sequences
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
such that
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}/i\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{i}\equiv a_{j}\mod i
whenever
i
divides
j
.

Note, it follows from the Chinese remainder theorem that
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\prod_{p\in P}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
where
P
is the set of all primes and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the
p
-adic integers.

We have that every element of a Presburger group has residue sequence in
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
. Conversely, given an element
\hat{r}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
we can augment any given Presburger group to include a new element with residue sequence
\hat{r}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given
\hat{r}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\hat{r}=(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
and a Presburger group
A
, we define the Presburger group

A[\hat{r}]:=\left\{a+\frac{X-r_{n}}{n}z/\sim:a\in A,z\in\mathbb{Z},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\right\}

where
\sim
is an equivalence relation defined by

a_{1}+\frac{X-r_{n}}{n}z_{1}\sim a_{2}+\frac{X-r_{m}}{m}z_{2}

if
mz_{1}=nz_{2}
and
nma_{1}-mr_{n}z_{1}=nma_{2}-nr_{m}z_{2}
. Addition on
A[\hat{r}]
is defined by

\bigg(a_{1}+\frac{X-r_{n}}{n}z_{1}\bigg)+\bigg(a_{2}+\frac{X-r_{m}}{m}z_{2}\bigg)=

\bigg(a_{1}+a_{2}+\frac{r_{nm}-r_{n}}{n}z_{1}+\frac{r_{nm}-r_{m}}{m}z_{2}\bigg)+\frac{X-r_{nm}}{nm}(mz_{1}+nz_{2}).

We say

a+\frac{X-r_{n}}{n}z>0

if either
z>0
or if
z=0
and
a>0
.

Note that the element
X:=0+\frac{X-0}{1}1
of
A[\hat{r}]
has residue sequence
\hat{r}
. Additionally, note that
X>a
for all
a\in A
.

Using this technique to augment the standard Presburger group
\mathbb{Z}
, we get the following result:

Proposition 2.7
.

Given any Turing degree
\boldsymbol{c}
, there exists a Presburger group whose degree spectrum is
\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{c}\leq\boldsymbol{d}\}
.

Proof.

It is clear that given a residue sequence
\hat{r}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
, we can compute a copy of
\mathbb{Z}[\hat{r}]
and that given a copy of

\mathbb{Z}[\hat{r}]
, we can compute
\hat{r}
. Hence, we need only show that any set of natural numbers can be encoded into a residue sequence.

Fix some
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the set of primes in increasing order. We define the element
\hat{r}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
as follows:

r_{1}=0

r_{p_{k}^{m}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\in S\\
0&\text{if }k\notin S\par\end{cases}

for all
m\geq 1
. For non-prime powers
n>1
,
r_{n}
is the unique integer in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv r_{(p_{1}^{m_{1}})}\mod{p_{1}^{m_{1}}}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\vdots

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv r_{p_{i}^{m_{i}}}\mod{p_{i}^{m_{i}}}

where
p_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots p_{i}^{m_{i}}=n
. Note, the existence and uniqueness of
r_{n}
is guaranteed by the Chinese remainder theorem. This
\hat{r}
is Turing equivalent to
S
.
∎

2.1.
Divisible Ordered Abelian Groups

Definition 2.8
.

A divisible ordered abelian group is a totally ordered abelian group
V
such that for all
x\in V
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have
qx\in V
.

By
qx
we mean the unique element of
y\in V
that satisfies
ax=by
where
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q=\frac{a}{b}
. Note that every divisible ordered abelian group forms a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
. The first order theory of divisible ordered abelian groups is a complete theory and has quantifier elimination (see e.g.
[
21
]
).

Definition 2.9
.

Let
G
be an ordered group (not necessarily divisible). Given
x\in G
, define
|x|=\max(x,-x)
. We say that
x,y\in G
are Archimedean equivalent (denoted
x\sim y
) if there exists a positive integer
N
such that
N|x|>|y|
and
N|y|>|x|
. If
N|x|<|y|
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then we say
x\ll y
.

It is clear that
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
G
. Given
x\in G
, we let
[x]
denote the equivalence class under
\sim
containing
x
. Note, the only element of the equivalence class
[0]
is
0
. Thus, we typically ignore this equivalence class.

Definition 2.10
.

The Archimedean rank of an ordered group
G
(denoted

\text{AR}(G)
) is the set
\{[x]:0\neq x\in G\}
ordered by
[x]<[y]
if and only if
x\ll y
.

Given any linear order
\mathcal{L}
, we can construct a divisible ordered abelian group with Archimedean rank
\mathcal{L}
:

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a linear order and let
V_{l}
be a divisible ordered abelian group for each
l\in\mathcal{L}
. Recall that the direct product
\prod_{l\in\mathcal{L}}V_{l}
is the set of functions
f:\mathcal{L}\to\bigcup_{l\in\mathcal{L}}V_{l}
such that
f(l)\in V_{l}
for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
. We define the direct sum

\bigoplus_{l\in\mathcal{L}}V_{l}:=\left\{f\in\prod_{l\in\mathcal{L}}V_{l}:\text{supp}(f)\text{ is finite}\right\}

where
\text{supp}(f)=\{l\in\mathcal{L}:f(l)\neq 0_{V_{l}}\}
. Defining addition as coordinate-wise, and defining
f>0
if
f(\max(\text{supp}(f)))>0
, we get that this direct sum is a divisible ordered abelian group.

Definition 2.12
.

Given a linear order
\mathcal{L}
, we define

V_{\mathcal{L}}:=\bigoplus_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{Q}.

Note that the
\text{AR}(V_{\mathcal{L}})=\mathcal{L}
.

Observation 2.13
.

Let
P
be a Presburger group, and let
V
be a divisible ordered abelian group. The structure
(V\times P,+,<,0,1)
with
0:=(0_{V},0_{P}),1:=(0_{V},1_{P})
, addition as coordinate-wise addition and ordering as the lexicographical ordering is a Presburger group. Note, an element
(v,p)\in V\times P
has the same residue sequence as
p
.

Definition 2.14
.

Given a linear order
\mathcal{L}
, we define the Presburger group

P_{\mathcal{L}}:=V_{\mathcal{L}}\times\mathbb{Z}.

Definition 2.15
.

A Presburger group
P
is called plain if every element of
P
has the same residue sequence as an integer. That is, given
x\in P
, there exists some
z\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\rho(x)=\rho(z)
.

Proposition 2.16
.

If
P
is a plain Presburger group, then there exists a divisible ordered abelian group
V
such that
P\cong V\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Consider
x\in P
. Since
P
is plain, there exists a unique integer
z_{x}
that has the same residue sequence as
x
. Define
x^{\prime}=x-z_{x}
. Note that
x^{\prime}
has a residue sequence of all zeros. Define
V=\{x^{\prime}:x\in P\}
. It is not hard to see that
V
is a subgroup of
P
. Thus, addition and ordering are defined for
V
. Since every element of
V
has a residue of all zeros, for all
v\in V
and positive
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is some
u\in V
with
nu=v
. This gives that
\frac{v}{n}\in V
for all
n
and so we get that
qv\in V
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q},v\in V
. Hence,
V
is a divisible ordered abelian group.

Note that every element of
P
is of the form
v+z
with
v\in V
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
|v|>0
(where
|v|
is defined as
\max(v,-v)
), then
|v|
is larger than all integers. Hence,
v+z>0
if and only if
v>0
or
v=0
and
z>0
. This gives that
P\cong V\times\mathbb{Z}
as witnessed by the map
v+z\mapsto(v,z)
.
∎

3.
Scott Complexities of Presburger Groups

We now examine the possible Scott sentence complexities of Presburger groups. Our findings can be summarized by the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

There exist Presburger groups with the following Scott sentence complexities:

•

\Pi_{\alpha}
for all countable ordinals
\alpha>1
;

•

d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha}
for all countable successor ordinals
\alpha>1
;

•

\Sigma_{\alpha}
for all successor ordinals
\alpha>4
.

Furthermore, there is no Presburger group with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{3}
.

This theorem follows directly from Propositions
3.24
,
3.25
,
3.26
,
3.28
,
3.29
,
3.30
,
3.34
and
3.35
as well as Theorem
4.7
below. All of these Scott sentence complexities are achievable for linear orders, as shown in
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
. The authors of
[
12
]
also gave a linear order with complexity
\Sigma_{4}
and showed that
\Sigma_{3}
is not achievable for linear orders. We leave open for now whether there exist Presburger groups with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{4}
(Question
6.1
). Other complexities not mentioned above are not possible for infinite structures in general.

As a consequence of Theorem
3.1
, we get that Presburger arithmetic has complete Scott spectrum. That is:

Corollary 3.2
.

Given a countable ordinal
\alpha>0
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott rank
\alpha
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact there exists a Presburger group with Scott sentence complexity
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
for all
\alpha
and from Theorem
1.1
.
∎

This distinguishes Presburger arithmetic from Peano arithmetic, as it was shown in
[
28
]
that no model of Peano arithmetic can have a finite Scott rank other than
1
. It was further shown in
[
11
]
that
\Pi_{\omega}
,
\Sigma_{\omega+1}
and
\Sigma_{\omega+2}
are not achievable Scott sentence complexities for models of Peano arithmetic and that
\Pi_{\omega+2}
is not achievable for true arithmetic, which further distinguishes Presburger arithmetic from these theories.

We also get an analogous result to Corollary
3.2
for parameterized Scott rank.

Corollary 3.3
.

Given a countable ordinal
\alpha>0
, there exists a Presburger group with parameterized Scott rank
\alpha
.

Proof.

If
\text{SC}(\mathcal{A})=\Pi_{\alpha+1}
then the least
\beta
such that
\mathcal{A}
has a
\Sigma_{\beta}^{in}
Scott sentence is
\beta=\alpha+2
. Thus, the result follows from Theorems
3.1
and
1.2
.
∎

Almost all of the Presburger groups we use to achieve these complexities are of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
where
\mathcal{L}
is an infinite linear order (see Definition
2.14
). As shown by the following lemmas, the Scott rank of such a
P_{\mathcal{L}}
can only differ from
\mathcal{L}
by at most
1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a countably infinite linear order. We have
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
, we need only show that
P_{\mathcal{L}}
is uniformly
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})}
categorical. Take any
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\cong P_{\mathcal{L}}
and let
1+\alpha=\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
. We can build a linear order
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\cong\mathcal{L}
as follows. First define
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{A}
so that
f(n)=a_{m}
where
m
is the least such that
\rho(a_{m})=\rho(0)
(where
\rho(a_{m})
is the residue sequence of
a_{m}
),
a_{m}>0_{\mathcal{A}}
, and
a_{m}
is not Archimedean equivalent to
1_{\mathcal{A}}
or any
f(l)
with
l<n
. Define
<_{f}
such that
n<_{f}m
if
f(n)<f(m)
and define
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}=(\mathbb{N},<_{f})
. Define
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\cong\mathcal{L}
analogously. Note, both
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
are computable from
(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}))^{\prime}
. By definition of Scott rank, there is some set
S
such that we can (uniformly) compute an isomorphism
h:\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\to\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
from
((\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus S)^{\prime})^{(\alpha)}=(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus S)^{(1+\alpha)}
. By mapping
0_{\mathcal{A}}\mapsto 0_{B}
,
1_{\mathcal{A}}\mapsto 1_{\mathcal{B}}
and
f(n)\mapsto g(h(n))
we can generate an isomorphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
. This isomorphism was (uniformly) computable from
(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus S)^{(1+\alpha)}
, and thus
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+1+\alpha=1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a countable infinite linear order. We have
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})\leq\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

We show that
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})}
-categorical. Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{L}
and let
1+\alpha=\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
. Using
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
, we can uniformly compute structures
\hat{\mathcal{A}},\hat{\mathcal{B}}\cong P_{\mathcal{L}}
along with injections
f:\mathcal{A}\to\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
g:\mathcal{B}\to\hat{\mathcal{B}}
such that
f(x)\ll f(y)
if and only if
x<y
(and likewise for
g
). By definition of Scott rank, there is some
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that we can uniformly compute an isomorphism
h:\hat{\mathcal{A}}\to\hat{\mathcal{B}}
from
(\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{B}})\oplus S)^{(\alpha)}
. Thus, we get that
g^{-1}\circ h\circ f
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
and this isomorphism was (uniformly) computable from
(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus S)^{\alpha}
. Hence,
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})\leq 1+\alpha=\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
.
∎

We also get an analogous result for parameterized Scott rank.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\mathcal{L}
is a countable infinite linear order then
\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})
and
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})\leq\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

The proofs of the previous two lemmas can be adapted to show that
P_{\mathcal{L}}
is
\Delta^{0}_{1+\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})}
-categorical on a cone and that
\mathcal{L}
is
\Delta^{0}_{\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})}
-categorical on a cone.
∎

Recall that if
\alpha
is an infinite ordinal then
1+\alpha=\alpha
. This yields the following corollary:

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})\geq\omega
, then
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
. Similarly, if
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})\geq\omega
, then
\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})
. ∎

We will also see by Theorem
3.33
that if
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Gamma_{\alpha}
, then
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq\Gamma_{1+\alpha}
(for
\Gamma=\Pi,\Sigma
or
d
-
\Sigma
). We conjecture that
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\Gamma_{1+\alpha}
for all infinite linear orders
\mathcal{L}
, though this remains open for now (Question
6.2
). However, we are able to show that
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\Gamma_{1+\alpha}
for enough linear orders
\mathcal{L}
to achieve all of the Scott sentence complexities claimed in Theorem
3.1
.

3.1.
Back-and-Forth Relations

In order to calculate Scott sentence complexities, we make use of
back-and-forth relations
. For a comprehensive reference on these relations, see e.g.
[
4
]
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be structures in the same language, let
\alpha
be a countable ordinal and let
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
be tuples of the same length in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively. We say
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
if every finitary
\Sigma_{1}
formula true of
\vec{b}
in
\mathcal{B}
is also true of
\vec{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
. Given
\alpha>1
, we say
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
if for all
1\leq\beta<\alpha
and
\vec{d}\in\mathcal{B}^{<\omega}
, there exists some
\vec{c}\in\mathcal{A}^{<\omega}
such that
(\mathcal{B},\vec{b},\vec{d})\leq_{\beta}(\mathcal{A},\vec{a},\vec{c})
.

We may also write
\mathcal{A}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}
by viewing
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
as the empty tuple. These back-and-forth relations are especially useful as a result of the following theorem of Karp:

Theorem 3.9

(From
[
17
]
)
.

Given an ordinal
\alpha\geq 1
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
; and

(2)

Every
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
formula true of
\vec{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
is true of
\vec{b}
in
\mathcal{B}
; and

(3)

Every
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
formula true of
\vec{b}
in
\mathcal{B}
is true of
\vec{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
. ∎

This yields us the following lemmas about the relation "
\leq_{1}
" on linear orders and Presburger groups:

Lemma 3.10

(See e.g.
[
4
]
Chapter 15)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be countable linear orders with
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
and
\vec{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
. We have
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
if and only if for all
\vec{d}\in\mathcal{B}^{<\omega}
, there is some
\vec{c}\in\mathcal{A}^{<\omega}
such that the ordering on
\vec{b},\vec{d}
in
\mathcal{B}
is the same as the ordering of
\vec{a},\vec{c}
in
\mathcal{A}
. ∎

Lemma 3.11

(See e.g.
[
4
]
Chapter 15)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be countable linear orders. Take
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
and
\vec{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
with
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
and
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{k}
. Define
\mathcal{A}_{0},...,\mathcal{A}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
as the open intervals between the elements of
\vec{a}
. That is,
\mathcal{A}_{0}=(-\infty,a_{1})
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}=(a_{1},a_{2})
,
\dots
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}=(a_{k},\infty)
. Define
\mathcal{B}_{0},...,\mathcal{B}_{k}
analogously. We will have
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
if and only if for all
i\leq k
,
|\mathcal{A}_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{B}_{i}|
. ∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
P
and
R
be Presburger groups. If
\vec{p}\in P^{<\omega}
and
\vec{r}\in R^{<\omega}
satisfy the same
\Pi_{0}
formulas, then
(R,\vec{r})\leq_{1}(P,\vec{p})
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a finitary
\Sigma_{1}
formula such that
P\models\psi(\vec{p})
. By quantifier elimination, there is a quantifier free formula
\psi^{\prime}
such that
\psi^{\prime}(\vec{x})\ \equiv\psi(\vec{x})
. Thus,
P\models\psi^{\prime}(\vec{p})
and so
R\models\psi^{\prime}(\vec{r})
since
\vec{p}
and
\vec{r}
satisfy the same
\Pi_{0}
formulas. Hence,
R\models\psi(\vec{r})
and so
(R,\vec{r})\leq_{1}(P,\vec{p})
by Karp’s theorem (
3.9
). ∎

Definition 3.13
.

Given a Presburger group
P
,
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
p\in P
with
\rho(p)=\rho(0)
, we define
qp
to be the unique element
p^{\prime}\in P
that satisfies
ap=bp^{\prime}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\frac{a}{b}=q
.

The fact that
\rho(p)=\rho(0)
guarantees the existence of
qp
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
. Note, every element of
P_{\mathcal{L}}
can be expressed in the form
(f,z)
where
z\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{Q}
with
\text{supp}(f)
finite (see Definitions
2.12
and
2.14
).

Definition 3.14
.

Given a countable linear order
\mathcal{L}
, define the embedding
\pi:\mathcal{L}\to P_{\mathcal{L}}
such that
\pi(l)=(f_{l},0)
where
f_{l}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{Q}
is defined so that
f_{l}(l)=1
and
f_{l}(m)=0
for all
m\neq l
. Take any
p\in P
. There is a unique tuple
(l_{1},...,l_{k})\in\mathcal{L}^{<\omega}
with each
l_{i}<l_{i+1}
, a unique tuple
(q_{1},...,q_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
with each
q_{i}\neq 0
and a unique
z\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

p=q_{1}\pi(l_{1})+\cdots+q_{k}\pi(l_{k})+z.

Given a formula
\phi(x)
in the language of Presburger arithmetic, define
\phi_{p}^{\#}(x_{1},...,x_{k})
to be the same as
\phi(x)
except with all mentions of
x
replaced with
q_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+q_{k}x_{k}+z
.

Definition 3.15
.

Define
\tau:P_{\mathcal{L}}\to\mathcal{L}^{<\omega}
such that
\tau(p)=(l_{1},...,l_{k})
as in the above definition. Define
t_{p}:\mathcal{L}^{|\tau(p)|}\to P_{\mathcal{L}}
to be the map defined by

t_{p}(x_{1},...,x_{k})=q_{1}\pi(x_{1})+\cdots+q_{k}\pi(x_{k})+z.

Note that
\phi^{\#}_{p}
has the same complexity as
\phi
. Additionally, note that
t_{p}(\tau(p))=p
and that
\phi^{\#}_{\pi(l)}=\phi
for any
l\in\mathcal{L}
. These definitions can be extended in the obvious way to handle tuples
\vec{p}
.

Lemma 3.16
.

P_{\mathcal{L}}\models\phi(\vec{p})
if and only if
P_{\mathcal{L}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\vec{p}}(\pi(\tau(\vec{p}))
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the above definitions.
∎

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a countable linear order with
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{L}^{<\omega}
. Take
p\in P_{\mathcal{L}}
such that:

•

0\ll p
; and

•

\rho(p)=\rho(0)
; and

•

p
is not a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of
\pi(\vec{a})
.

There exists an automorphism
G:P_{\mathcal{L}}\to P_{\mathcal{L}}
such that
G
fixes
\pi(\vec{a})
and
G(p)=\pi(l)
for some
l\in\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Since
\rho(p)=\rho(0)
, we have that
p=(v,0)
for some
v\in V_{\mathcal{L}}
. Let
f_{a}
be as in Definition
3.14
for each
a
in
\vec{a}
. It is enough to show that there is an automorphism
H:V_{\mathcal{L}}\to V_{\mathcal{L}}
that fixes each
f_{a}
and maps
v\mapsto f_{l}
for some
l\in\mathcal{L}
. The existence of such an
H
is clear since
V_{\mathcal{L}}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with basis
\{f_{l}:l\in\mathcal{L}\}
, and
v
is linearly independent from
f_{a_{1}},...,f_{a_{k}}
. Taking
m\in\mathcal{L}
such that
f_{m}
is archimedean equivalent to
v
, the map
H
that sends
v
to
f_{m}
and fixes all other
f_{l}
yields the automorphism we desire.
∎

Proposition 3.18
.

Take any countable linear orders
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Define the embeddings
\pi_{1}:\mathcal{A}\to P_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\pi_{2}:\mathcal{B}\to P_{\mathcal{B}}
as in Definition
3.14
. Given any ordinal
\alpha\geq 1
and tuples
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
and
\vec{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
,

(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})\iff(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi_{1}(\vec{a}))\leq_{1+\alpha}(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi_{2}(\vec{b})).

Proof.

We prove this by induction, beginning with the base case
\alpha=1
. Define
\tau_{1}:P_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathcal{A}^{<\omega}
and
\tau_{2}:P_{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathcal{B}^{<\omega}
as in Definition
3.15
. Suppose that
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
. We have by Lemma
3.10
that given any
\vec{p}\in P_{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
, there is some
\vec{c}\in\vec{\mathcal{A}}^{m}
such that the ordering on
\vec{b},\tau_{2}(p_{1}),...,\tau_{2}(p_{n})
in
\mathcal{B}
is the same as the ordering on
\vec{a},c_{1},...,c_{m}
in
\mathcal{A}
(note that
m=|\tau_{2}(p_{1})|+\cdots+|\tau_{2}(p_{n})|
). It follows that
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
satisfies the same
\Pi_{0}
formulas as
\left(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\tau_{2}(\vec{p})\right)
. Take any
\Pi_{0}
formula
\phi
. We will have
P_{\mathcal{B}}\models\phi(\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p})
if and only if

P_{\mathcal{B}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p}}\left(\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\tau_{2}(\vec{p})\right).

Since
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
satisfies the same
\Pi_{0}
formulas as
\left(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\tau_{2}(\vec{p}))\right)
, this holds if and only if

P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p}}\left(\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c})\right).

As noted after Definition
3.15
, we will have that
\psi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{a})}=\psi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{b})}=\psi
for any formula
\psi
. Define
\vec{r}\in P_{\mathcal{A}}^{n}
by

\vec{r}=\left(t_{p_{1}}(c_{1},...,c_{|\tau_{2}(p_{1})|}),\dots,t_{p_{n}}(c_{m-|\tau_{2}(p_{2})|+1},...,c_{m})\right).

We get that
\psi^{\#}_{\vec{p}}=\psi^{\#}_{\vec{r}}
for any
\psi
and
\tau_{1}(\vec{r})=\vec{c}
. Thus, we have

P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r}}\left(\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c})\right)

which holds if and only if
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi(\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{r})
since
\tau_{1}(\vec{r})=\vec{c}
. This will hold for all
\Pi_{0}
formulas, and we can find such an
\vec{r}
given any
\vec{p}
. Hence, we get that
(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p})\leq_{1}(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r})
for some
\vec{r}
given any
\vec{p}
and so
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}))\leq_{2}(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}))
.

For the other direction, suppose that
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}))\leq_{2}(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}))
. Define
\mathcal{A}_{0},...,\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0},...,\mathcal{B}_{k}
as in Lemma
3.11
. Define the formula

\varphi_{n}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\exists y_{1},...,y_{n}\WW_{i\in\mathbb{N}}[ix_{1}<y_{1}~\&~iy_{1}<y_{2}~\&~\cdots~\&iy_{n}<x_{2}]

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Two positive elements
p
and
r
of a Presburger group will satisfy
\varphi_{n}(r,p)
if and only if there are at least
n
archimedean equivalence classes between those of
r
and
p
. Since each
\varphi_{n}
is
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
, if
P_{\mathcal{B}}\models\varphi_{n}(b_{i},b_{i+1})
then
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\varphi_{n}(a_{i},a_{i+i})
by Karp’s theorem, and so
|\mathcal{A}_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{B}_{i}|
for each
i
, and so
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
by Lemma
3.11
.

Now for the inductive step, suppose the result holds for all
1\leq\beta<\alpha
. Suppose further that
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
. Take any
\vec{p}\in P_{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
and
\Pi^{in}_{\beta}
formula
\phi
such that
1\leq\beta<1+\alpha
. If
P_{\mathcal{B}}\models\phi(\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p})
, then
P_{\mathcal{B}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p}}\left(\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\tau_{2}(\vec{p}))\right)
. Since
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
, there is some
\vec{c}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(\mathcal{B},\vec{b},\tau_{2}(\vec{p}))\leq_{\beta}(\mathcal{A},\vec{a},\vec{c})
. By inductive hypothesis, this gives that
(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\tau_{2}(\vec{p})))\leq_{1+\beta}(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
and so
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p}}(\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
. Defining
\vec{r}
as in the first paragraph of this proof we get that
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r}}(\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
and so
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi^{\#}_{\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r}}(\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\tau_{1}(\vec{r}))
and so
P_{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi(\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r})
. Since this works for any
\Pi_{\beta}^{in}
formula
\phi
with
1\leq\beta<1+\alpha
, we have that
(P_{B},\pi(\vec{b}),\vec{p})\leq_{\beta}(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r})
for all such
\beta
. Since we can find such an
\vec{r}
given any
\vec{p}
, we get that
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}))\leq_{1+\alpha}(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}))
.

For the other direction, suppose that
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}))\leq_{1+\alpha}(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}))
. Given any
\vec{d}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\beta
with
1\leq\beta<\alpha
, there exists some
\vec{r}\in P_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\vec{d}))\leq_{1+\beta}(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r})
. Without loss of generality, suppose each element of
\vec{d}
is distinct form those in
\vec{b}
. Since every
\Pi^{in}_{2}
formula modeled by
(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\vec{d}))
is also modeled by
(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\vec{r})
, we get that each
r
in
\vec{r}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.17
. Thus, there is an automorphism of
P_{\mathcal{A}}
that fixes
\pi(\vec{a})
and maps
\vec{r}
to
\pi(\vec{c})
for some
\vec{c}\in\mathcal{A}^{<\omega}
. Hence, we have
(P_{\mathcal{B}},\pi(\vec{b}),\pi(\vec{d}))\leq_{1+\beta}(P_{\mathcal{A}},\pi(\vec{a}),\pi(\vec{c}))
and so
(\mathcal{B},\vec{b},\vec{d})\leq_{\beta}(\mathcal{A},\vec{a},\vec{c})
by inductive hypothesis. Hence,
(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B},\vec{b})
. ∎

Corollary 3.19
.

Given countable linear orders
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
and an ordinal
\alpha\geq 1
,

\mathcal{A}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\iff P_{\mathcal{A}}\leq_{1+\alpha}P_{\mathcal{B}}.

∎

3.2.
Finding the Complexities

As shown in Theorem 5.1 of
[
1
]
, no infinite structure can have a
\Sigma_{2}^{in}
Scott sentence. Thus the least possible Scott sentence complexity for a Presburger group is
\Pi_{2}
, which is achieved by the standard model:

Proposition 3.20
.

The standard Presburger group
\mathbb{Z}
has Scott sentence complexity
\Pi_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{Z}
does not have a
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence, it is enough to show that
\mathbb{Z}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence. Such a sentence is given by

\forall x\left[\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x=n1~\vee~x+n1=0\right]

where
n1
is shorthand for
\overbrace{1+\cdots+1}^{n\text{-many}}
. ∎

This gives that
\text{SR}(\mathbb{Z})=1
. We will see below that no other Presburger group has this Scott rank:

Lemma 3.21
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a Presburger group, then
\mathcal{A}\leq_{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Take any tuple
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is a Presburger group, there is a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
inside of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\vec{a}
be the elements within the copy of
\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathcal{A}
that correspond to
\vec{b}
. We have that both
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
are definable, as all elements of
\mathbb{Z}
are definable without quantifiers. Thus, any finitary
\Sigma_{1}
formula
\phi(\vec{x})
true about
\vec{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
is equivalent to a finitary
\Sigma_{1}
sentence
\phi^{\prime}
true in
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\text{Th}(\mathcal{A})=\text{Th}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have that
\mathbb{Z}\models\phi^{\prime}
and thus
\mathbb{Z}\models\phi(\vec{b})
as well. Hence,
(\mathbb{Z},\vec{b})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{A},\vec{a})
and so
\mathcal{A}\leq_{2}\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Corollary 3.22
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a nonstandard Presburger group, then
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})>1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nonstandard Presburger group. Since
\mathcal{A}\leq_{2}\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathcal{A}
cannot have a
\Pi^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence and so
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})>1
.
∎

It should be noted that Corollary
3.22
was independently proven by Dariusz Kalociński using different methods in forthcoming work. We can use this to help find a Presburger group with Scott sentence complexity
d
-
\Sigma_{2}
. Let
P_{1}
denote the Presburger group
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Z}
. The Scott sentence complexity of
P_{1}
will be evident from the fact that
\text{SR}(P_{1})=2
but
\text{pSR}(P_{1})=1
.

Proposition 3.23
.

\text{SC}(P_{1})=d
-
\Sigma_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
P_{1}\ncong\mathbb{Z}
, we get that it has no
\Pi^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence. By Theorem
1.1
, we can show that
\text{SR}(P_{1})=2
by demonstrating that the automorphism orbits in
P_{1}
are
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
-definable. Note that residue sequences must be preserved under automorphism. Thus if
(q_{1},z_{1})
is automorphic to
(q_{2},z_{2})
, then
z_{1}=z_{2}
. However, any automorphism of
(\mathbb{Q},+,<)
can be extended to an automorphism of
P_{1}
. The automorphisms of
(\mathbb{Q},+,<)
are exactly those generated by multiplying everything by a positive rational. Hence, the automorphisms of
P_{1}
are exactly those defined by
(q,z)\mapsto(rq,z)
where
r
is some positive rational number. This gives that there are three automorphism orbits for every
z\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\{(q,z):q>0\},\,\{(q,z):q<0\}\,\text{and }\{(0,z)\}.

Each of these orbits can be defined by the following formulas respectively:

\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x\equiv z\mod n\,\,\&\,\,\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overbrace{1+\cdots+1}^{\text{$n$-many}}<x,

\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x\equiv z\mod n\,\,\&\,\,\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overbrace{1+\cdots+1}^{\text{$n$-many}}+x<0

and

\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x\equiv z\mod n\,\,\&\,\,\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overbrace{1+\cdots+1}^{\text{$n$-many}}>|x|.

Each of these formulas is
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
, so we get that
\text{SR}(P_{1})=2
and so
P_{1}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence.

As for the parameterized Scott rank, note that by adding a constant
c
to the language to represent the element
(1,0)\in P_{1}
, we get that an isomorphism between any two copies of
P_{1}
can be computed (given the atomic diagrams of the copies) simply by sending
c
to
c
and
1
to
1
. Thus,
\text{SR}(P_{1},c)=1
and so
\text{pSR}(P_{1})=1
. This gives that
P_{1}
has a
\Sigma^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence but no
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence, as no infinite structure has a
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence. Since
P_{1}
has both a
\Sigma^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence and a
\Pi^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence, it must have a
d
-
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
Scott sentence as well.
∎

It should be noted that if we define
P_{n}=\mathbb{Q}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}
for any positive integer
n
, a similar proof as above yields that
\text{SC}(P_{n})=d
-
\Sigma_{2}
.

Let us now show the existence of the remaining finite Scott sentence complexities claimed in Theorem
3.1
. All of the finite cases are covered by the next three propositions.

Proposition 3.24
.

Given a natural number
n\geq 2
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Pi_{n}
.

Proof.

For the
n=2
case, recall that the standard Presburger group
\mathbb{Z}
has Scott complexity
\Pi_{2}
. As noted in the proof of Corollary 3.10 of
[
12
]
, the linear order
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cdot\mathbb{Q}
has Scott complexity
\Pi_{2n+2}
but
\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}\leq_{2n+2}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cdot\mathbb{Q}
. It follows from Lemma
3.4
that
P_{\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cdot\mathbb{Q}}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{2n+3}
Scott sentence and from Corollary
3.19
that
P_{\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}}\leq_{2n+3}P_{\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cdot\mathbb{Q}}
. Thus,
P_{\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cdot\mathbb{Q}}
has no
\Sigma^{in}_{2n+3}
sentence and so it has Scott complexity
\Pi_{2n+3}
.

Similarly, the linear order
\omega^{n}
has Scott complexity
\Pi_{2n+1}
but
\omega^{n}+\omega^{n}\leq_{2n+1}\omega^{n}
(see e.g.
[
4
]
Lemma 15.10). Thus, the same reasoning as above gives that
\text{SC}(P_{\omega^{n}})=\Pi_{2n+2}
.
∎

Proposition 3.25
.

Given a natural number
n\geq 5
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
12
]
, for every natural number
m\geq 4
there is an infinite linear order
\mathcal{L}
such that
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Sigma_{m}
as well as an
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\ncong\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L}\leq_{m}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. It follows from Lemma
3.6
that
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has a
\Sigma_{m+1}
Scott sentence and from Corollary
3.19
that
P_{\mathcal{L}}\leq_{m+1}P_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
. Thus,
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has no
\Pi_{m+1}
Scott sentence and so it has Scott complexity
\Sigma_{m+1}
.
∎

Proposition 3.26
.

Given a natural number
n\geq 2
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
d
-
\Sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

We saw in Proposition
3.23
that
\text{SC}(P_{1})=d
-
\Sigma_{2}
.
Given
n\geq 2
, it was shown in the proof of Corollary 3.10 of
[
12
]
that there exists an infinite linear order
\mathcal{L}
such that
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=d
-
\Sigma_{n}
and linear orders
\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}\ncong\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\leq_{n}\mathcal{L}\leq_{n}\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, as in the above two proofs, we get that
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{n+2}
Scott sentence and a
\Sigma^{in}_{n+2}
Scott sentence, but no
\Pi^{in}_{n+1}
nor
\Sigma^{in}_{n+1}
Scott sentence. Hence,
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=d
-
\Sigma_{n+1}
.
∎

We now turn our attention to achieving the infinite Scott sentence complexities. We begin with the complexities whose indices are at least
2
greater than a limit, for which we can make use of the following lemma:

Lemma 3.27

(See e.g.
[
27
]
Table 1)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a countable structure and let
\alpha\geq 1
be a countable ordinal.

•

\text{SC}(\mathcal{A})=\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
if and only if
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{A})=\alpha
and
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})=\alpha+2
.

•

\text{SC}(\mathcal{A})=\Pi_{\alpha+2}
if and only if
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{A})=\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})=\alpha+1
.

•

\text{SC}(\mathcal{A})=d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
if and only if
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{A})=\alpha+1
and
\text{SR}(\mathcal{A})=\alpha+2
. ∎

Proposition 3.28
.

Given a countable ordinal
\alpha\geq\omega
, there exist Presburger groups with Scott sentence complexities
\Sigma_{\alpha+2},\Pi_{\alpha+2}
and
d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
.

Proof.

Fix any countable
\alpha\geq\omega
. It was shown in
[
12
]
that there exist countable linear orders
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
and
\mathcal{L}_{3}
with Scott sentence complexities
\Sigma_{\alpha+2},\Pi_{\alpha+2}
and
d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
respectively. By Lemma
3.27
, we have that
\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L}_{1})=\alpha
and
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L}_{1})=\alpha+2
and so
\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}_{1}})=\alpha
and
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}_{1}})=\alpha+2
by Corollary
3.7
. Hence, by Lemma
3.27
, we have that
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}_{1}})=\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
. An analogous argument yields that
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}_{2}})=\Pi_{\alpha+2}
and
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}_{3}})=d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha+2}
.
∎

As shown in
[
1
]
, no countable structure can have Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{\lambda}
nor
d
-
\Sigma_{\lambda}
for a limit ordinal
\lambda
. We now show the existence of Presburger groups with complexities
d
-
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
,
\Pi_{\lambda+1}
,
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}
for every countable limit ordinal
\lambda
.

Proposition 3.29
.

Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
d
-
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
.

Proof.

We show that
P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}
has the desired Scott complexity. Note,
\text{SR}(\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda})=\lambda+1
and
\text{pSR}(\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda})=\lambda
(see e.g.
[
27
]
), and so
\text{SR}(P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}})=\lambda+1
and
\text{pSR}(P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}})=\lambda
by Corollary
3.7
. Thus,
P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}
has both a
\Sigma_{\lambda+2}^{in}
and a
\Pi_{\lambda+2}^{in}
Scott sentence. However, we have

\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}\leq_{\lambda+1}\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}\leq_{\lambda+1}\omega^{\lambda}

(see e.g.
[
4
]
) and so

P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}\leq_{\lambda+1}P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}\leq_{\lambda+1}P_{\omega^{\lambda}}

by Corollary
3.19
(note
1+\lambda+1=\lambda+1
). Hence,
P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}
has no
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}^{in}
nor
\Pi_{\lambda+1}^{in}
Scott sentence and so
\text{SC}(P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}})=d
-
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
.
∎

Proposition 3.30
.

Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Pi_{\lambda+1}
.

Proof.

We show that
P_{\omega^{\lambda}}
has the desired Scott complexity. Since
\text{SR}(\omega^{\lambda})=\lambda
,
\text{SR}(P_{\omega^{\lambda}})=\lambda
by Corollary
3.7
and so
P_{\omega^{\lambda}}
has a
\Pi_{\lambda+1}^{in}
Scott sentence. However since
P_{\omega^{\lambda}+\omega^{\lambda}}\leq_{\lambda+1}P_{\omega^{\lambda}}
, we have that
P_{\omega^{\lambda}}
has no
\Sigma^{in}_{\lambda+1}
Scott sentence and so
\text{SC}(P_{\omega^{\lambda}})=\Pi_{\lambda+1}
.
∎

The remaining cases,
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}
, are the most difficult. To achieve them, we find a method for converting a
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}^{in}
or
\Pi^{in}_{\lambda}
Scott sentence for
\mathcal{L}
into a Scott sentence for
P_{\mathcal{L}}
of the same complexity.

Lemma 3.31
.

Given
\alpha\geq 1
, a linear order
\mathcal{L}
and a
\Pi_{\alpha}^{in}
sentence
\phi
in the language of linear orders, there exists a
\Pi_{1+\alpha}^{in}
sentence
\phi^{*}
in the language of Presburger arithmetic such that
\mathcal{L}\models\phi
if and only if
P_{\mathcal{L}}\models\phi^{*}
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction while describing how to construct the formula
\phi^{*}(\vec{x})
from
\phi(\vec{x})
. Starting with the atomic case, define

x<^{*}y:=\WW_{n\in\mathbb{N}}nx<y

where "
nx
" is short hand for the sum of
n
many
x
, and define

x=^{*}y:=\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}nx>y~\&~ny>x.

Note that given positive elements
x,y
in a Presburger group,
x<^{*}y
if and only if
x
is in a lesser archimedean equivalence class than
y
and
x=^{*}y
if and only if
x
and
y
are archimedean equivalent. Given a
\Pi_{0}
formula
\phi(\vec{x})
, we just define
\phi^{*}(\vec{x})
to be the corresponding boolean combination of atomics using
<^{*}
and
=^{*}
instead of
<
and
=
. Thus, such a
\phi^{*}(\vec{x})
will be
\Delta^{in}_{2}
(i.e. both
\Pi^{in}_{2}
and
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
).

Now suppose that
\phi(\vec{x})
is
\Pi^{in}_{1}
. We have that

\phi(\vec{x})=\WW_{i}(\forall y_{1},...,y_{k})\psi_{i}(\vec{x},\vec{y})

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
with each
\psi_{i}
a
\Pi_{0}
formula. Define

\phi^{*}(\vec{x}):=\WW_{i}(\forall y_{1},...,y_{k})\left[\left(\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}y_{1}<n~\vee~\cdots~\vee~y_{k}<n\right)~\vee~\psi_{i}^{*}(\vec{x},\vec{y})\right].

Since each
\psi_{i}^{*}
is
\Delta^{in}_{2}
, the formula
\phi^{*}(\vec{x})
is
\Pi_{2}^{in}
. The purpose of the condition "
\left(\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}y_{1}<n~\vee~\cdots~\vee~y_{k}<n\right)~\vee~
" is to assure that we are only requiring tuples
\vec{y}\in P_{\mathcal{L}}^{<\omega}
of elements that are positive and not equal to an integer to satisfy each
\psi^{*}
. That is, since
P_{\mathcal{L}}=V_{\mathcal{L}}\times\mathbb{Z}
, we are only requiring
\psi_{i}^{*}
to hold for elements
y
of the form
(v,z)
with
v>0
. It is clear that if
\phi
is a
\Pi^{in}_{1}
sentence, then
P_{\mathcal{L}}\models\phi^{*}
if and only if
\mathcal{L}\models\phi
, as we get
\mathcal{L}
by modding out
V_{\mathcal{L}}
by archimedean equivalence and
\phi^{*}
is essentially a statement about the non-zero archimedean equivalence classes of
V_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Now suppose that the result holds for formulas of complexity up to
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
and that
\phi(\vec{x})
is
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha+1}
. Given a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
formula
\varphi(\vec{x})
, we can define
\varphi^{*}(\vec{x})=\neg(\neg\varphi)^{*}(\vec{x})
. We have that

\phi(\vec{x})=\WW_{i}(\forall y_{1},...,y_{k})\psi_{i}(\vec{x},\vec{y})

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
with each
\psi_{i}
a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
formula. Defining

\phi^{*}(\vec{x}):=\WW_{i}(\forall y_{1},...,y_{k})\left[\left(\VV_{n\in\mathbb{N}}y_{1}<n~\vee~\cdots~\vee~y_{k}<n\right)~\vee~\psi_{i}^{*}(\vec{x},\vec{y})\right]

we have the
\Pi^{in}_{1+\alpha+1}
formula that we desire, as each
\psi_{i}^{*}
is
\Sigma^{in}_{1+\alpha}
.
∎

Proposition 3.32
.

Take any
\alpha\geq\omega
. If
\mathcal{L}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence, then
P_{\mathcal{L}}
also has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence. If
\mathcal{L}
has a a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence, then
P_{\mathcal{L}}
also has a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence.

Proof.

We prove the
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
case. The proof of the
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha}
case is analogous. Suppose
\mathcal{L}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence. Define
Pr
to be the (infinite) conjunction of all the Presburger axioms. Note,
Pr
is
\Pi^{in}_{3}
. Recall that
\rho(x)
denotes the residue sequence of the element
x
of a Presburger group. Define

Plain:=(\forall x)\VV_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho(x)=\rho(z).

Given a Presburger group
P
, we have
P\models Plain
if and only if
P
is a plain Presburger group. Note,
Plain
is also
\Pi^{in}_{3}
.

Recall that we write
x\sim y
when
x
and
y
are archimedean equivalent and write
|x|
to denote the max of
\{x,-x\}
. Define

\Psi:=(\forall x,y)\left[\rho(x)\neq\rho(0)~\vee~\rho(y)\neq\rho(0)~\vee~\neg(x\sim y)~\vee~\VV_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}n|x|=m|y|\right].

Given a plain Presburger group
P
, we have that
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
for some divisible ordered abelian group
V
by Proposition
2.16
. If
P\models\Psi
then given two elements of the form
x=(v,0)
and
y=(u,0)
, if
x\sim y
then
y
is a rational multiple of
x
. This holds if and only if
V
has the form
V_{\mathcal{K}}
for some linear order
\mathcal{K}
, and so
P\models\Psi
if and only if
P\cong P_{\mathcal{K}}
for some
\mathcal{K}
. Note,
\Psi
is
\Pi^{in}_{2}
.

Last, let
\phi
be a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence of
\mathcal{L}
. Let
\phi^{*}
be as in the previous lemma. Since
1+\alpha=\alpha
, we have that
\phi^{*}
is
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
. Define

\Phi:=Pr~\wedge~Plain~\wedge~\Psi~\wedge~\phi^{*}.

If
P\models\Phi
, then since
\Phi
contains
Pr
,
Plain
and
\Psi
,
P
must have the form
P_{\mathcal{K}}
for some linear order
\mathcal{K}
. Since
P\models\phi^{*}
, we have by the previous lemma that
\mathcal{K}\cong\mathcal{L}
. Hence,
\Phi
is a
\Pi_{\alpha}^{in}
Scott sentence for
P_{\mathcal{L}}
.
∎

Theorem 3.33
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a countably infinite linear order. If
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Gamma_{\alpha}
, then
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq\Gamma_{1+\alpha}
(for
\Gamma=\Pi,\Sigma
or
d
-
\Sigma
).

Proof.

If
\alpha
is infinite, then the
\Sigma_{\alpha}
and
\Pi_{\alpha}
cases follow from Proposition
3.32
. If
\alpha
is finite, then the
\Sigma_{\alpha}
case follows from the fact that
\text{pSR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+\text{pSR}(\mathcal{L})
(Lemma
3.6
). The
\Pi_{\alpha}
case follows from the fact that
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
(Lemma
3.4
).

For the
d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha}
cases, recall that a structure has a
d
-
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{in}
Scott sentence if and only if it has both a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha+1}
and a
\Sigma^{in}_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence.
∎

Proposition 3.34
.

Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
.

Proof.

In
[
10
]
, it is shown that there is a linear order
\mathcal{L}
with Scott complexity
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
. By Theorem
3.33
, the Presburger group
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has a
\Sigma^{in}_{\lambda+1}
Scott sentence. Since
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
,
\mathcal{L}
has no
\Pi^{in}_{\lambda+1}
Scott sentence. Thus,
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})=\lambda+1
and so
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\lambda+1
by Lemma
3.4
. Hence,
P_{\mathcal{L}}
also has no
\Pi^{in}_{\lambda+1}
Scott sentence and so
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
.
∎

Proposition 3.35
.

Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
, there exists a Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Pi_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
12
]
, there exists a linear order
\mathcal{L}
with Scott complexity
\Pi_{\lambda}
. By Theorem
3.33
, the Presburger group
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has a
\Pi_{\lambda}^{in}
Scott sentence. If
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
is not
\Pi_{\lambda}
, then since a Scott complexity cannot be
\Sigma_{\lambda}
or
d
-
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, there would have to be some
\alpha<\lambda
such that
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has a
\Pi^{in}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence. This would imply that
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq\alpha
which implies that
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})\leq\alpha
which contradicts that
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Pi_{\lambda}
. Hence,
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\Pi_{\lambda}
.
∎

4.
Non-Plain Presburger Groups

With the exception of those constructed in Proposition
2.7
, all Presburger groups discussed so far have been plain. That is, they only contained elements whose residue sequence was that of an integer. Since an element of a Presburger group can have any element of the continuum size group
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
as its residue sequence, we have barely scratched the surface of the class of countable Presburger groups.

Of particular interest are the recursively saturated Presburger groups. Given a Presburger group
P
let
P_{+}
denote the set of nonnegative elements of
P
. Note that
P_{+}
is a model of
\text{Th}(\mathbb{N},+,<,0,1)
. It was shown by Lipshitz and Nadel in
[
19
]
that a countable nonstandard
P_{+}
can be expanded to a model of Peano arithmetic if and only if it is recursively saturated. Given any computable
\hat{r}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
, the type
T_{\hat{r}}(x)=\{(\exists y)[ny+r_{n}=x]:n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\}
is computable and finitely realizable in every Presburger group. Thus if
P
is a recursively saturated Presburger group and
\hat{r}
is computable, then
P
contains an element with residue sequence
\hat{r}
. As shown by the following proposition, it can be readily seen that recursively saturated Presburger groups all have Scott rank 2.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
P
is a countable recursively saturated Presburger group then
\text{SR}(P)=2
.

Proof.

Since
P
is nonstandard,
\text{SR}(P)\geq 2
by Corollary
3.22
. If
P
is recursively saturated, then
P
is homogeneous (see e.g.
[
20
]
). That is given
\vec{p},\vec{q}\in P^{<\omega}
of the same length,
\text{tp}(\vec{p})=\text{tp}(\vec{q})
if and only if
\vec{p}
is automorphic to
\vec{q}
. Additionally, since Presburger arithmetic has elimination of quantifiers down to
\Delta^{0}_{1}
formulas (again see e.g.
[
20
]
), we get that
\vec{p}
is automorphic to
\vec{q}
if and only if they satisfy the same
\Delta^{0}_{1}
formulas. Let
\Psi
be the set of all
\Pi^{0}_{1}
formulas satisfied by
\vec{p}
. We get that the automorphism orbit of
\vec{p}
is

\{\vec{x}:P\models\WW_{\psi\in\Psi}\psi(\vec{x})\}.

Thus, all automorphism orbits in
P
are
\Sigma^{in}_{2}
-definable and so
\text{SR}(P)\leq 2
by Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We now turn our attention to showing that there is no Presburger group with Scott complexity
\Sigma_{3}
.
To do so, we must consider all possible Presburger groups, not just the plain ones. In
[
8
]
, Goncharov, Lempp and Solomon consider computable ordered abelian groups in general. In particular, they show that a computable ordered abelian group is computably categorical if and only if it has a finite
basis
(defined below).

Definition 4.2
.

Let
G
be an ordered abelian group. Given
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G
, we say that
\{g_{1},...,g_{n}\}
is linearly independent if given
k_{1},...,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

k_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}g_{n}=0

if and only if each
k_{i}=0
. A maximal linearly independent subset of
G
is called a basis of
G
. The cardinality of a basis of
G
is called the dimension of
G
.

Note, in
[
8
]
they use the term
rank
instead of dimension. However to avoid confusion with Scott rank, we will call it dimension.
In
[
8
]
, the authors show how given a computable
G
of infinite dimension, one can build a computable
H\cong G
such that there is no computable isomorphism from
G
to
H
. The same constructions can be used to show how given any Turing degree
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable
G
, one can build a
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable
H
such that there is no
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable isomorphism from
G
to
H
. This yields the following:

Theorem 4.3

(Essentially Theorem 1.8 in
[
8
]
)
.

If
G
is a countable ordered abelian group, then
G
is computably categorical on a cone if and only if
G
has a finite basis. ∎

By Theorem
1.2
, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 4.4
.

An ordered abelian group has a
\Sigma^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence if and only if it has a finite basis. ∎

Note that the standard Presburger group
\mathbb{Z}
has dimension
1
, and all other Presburger groups have dimension at least
2
. In fact, the Presburger groups with dimension
2
are exactly those of the form
\mathbb{Z}[\hat{r}]
(as in Definition
2.6
).

It should be noted that unlike with vector spaces, it is not necessary for every element of an ordered abelian group
G
to be a linear combination of elements of a basis of
G
. For example,
\{2\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{Z}
. However, we do get a similar property:

Lemma 4.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
G
be an ordered abelian group with basis
\{b_{1},...,b_{n}\}
. Given any
g\in G
with
g\neq 0_{g}
, there exists a unique integer sequence
(m,k_{1},...,k_{n})
such that:

•

m>0
; and

•

\text{GCD}(m,k_{1},...,k_{n})=1
; and

•

mg=k_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}b_{n}
.

We call such an equation a
(reduced) dependence equation
for
g
over
\{b_{1},...,b_{n}\}
.

Definition 4.6
.

Given
(g_{1},...,g_{n})\in G^{n}
, we define
\text{cut}(g_{1},...,g_{n})
to be the set of
k_{1},...,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

k_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}g_{n}>0.

Theorem 4.7
.

There is no Presburger group with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{3}
.

Proof.

We need only show that any Presburger group with a
\Sigma^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence also has a
\Pi^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence. Fix such a
P
. We have by Corollary
4.4
that
P
has a finite basis. Fix elements
p_{1},...,p_{n}
which, along with
1
, form a basis of
P
. Note, this gives that
P
has dimension
n+1
. Let the formula
\Phi(x_{1},...,x_{n})
be the conjunction of the following for each
i\leq n
:

•

\rho(x_{i})=\rho(p_{i})
; and

•

\text{cut}(1,x_{1},...,x_{n})=\text{cut}(1,p_{1},...,p_{n})
; and

•

The set
\{1,x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
is linearly independent.

Recall that
\rho(x)
denotes the residue sequence of an element
x
of a Presburger group. Note that
\Phi
is
\Pi^{in}_{1}
. Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be an oracle that codes each
\rho(p_{i})
as well as
\text{cut}(1,p_{1},...,p_{n})
. Given a copy
\mathcal{B}\cong P
and a tuple
\vec{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{n}
, it is decidable from
(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus T)^{\prime}
whether or not
\mathcal{B}\models\Phi(\vec{b})
.

Take
\mathcal{A}\cong P
with
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{A}\models\Phi(a_{1},...,a_{n})
. Define
f:P\to\mathcal{A}
such that
f(0_{P})=0_{\mathcal{A}}
,
f(1_{P})=1_{\mathcal{A}}
and
f(p_{i})=a_{i}
for all
i
. Given any other
p\in P
, take the (unique) integers
m,k_{0},k_{1},...,k_{n}
such that
mp=k_{0}+k_{1}p_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}p_{n}
with
m>0
and GCD
(m,k_{0},...,k_{n})=1
as in Lemma
4.5
. Define
f(p)
to be the element
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
ma=k_{0}+k_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}a_{n}
. The existence of
a
follows from the fact that
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(p_{i})
for each
i\leq n
. The uniqueness of
a
follows from the fact that these groups are torsion free.

Thus we have that
f
is well defined and injective. To see that
f
is surjective, suppose there was some
b\in\mathcal{A}
that was not in the image of
f
. We would have that
b
does not satisfy any dependence equation over
1,a_{1},...,a_{n}
. This would imply that the set
\{1,a_{1},...,a_{n},b\}
is linearly independent which would contradict that
P
(and thus
\mathcal{A}
) has dimension
n+1
. Hence, there is no such
b
and we have that
f
is a bijection. It is clear that addition is preserved under
f
. Furthermore if
q\in P
with
q>0
, then
f(q)>0
as well since
\text{cut}(1,p_{1},...,_{n})=\text{cut}(1,a_{1},...,a_{n})
. Hence, order is also preserved and so
f
is an isomorphism.

Given two copies
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\cong P
, we can compute an isomorphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
by simply finding tuples
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
and
\vec{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{n}
with
\mathcal{A}\models\Phi(\vec{a})
and
\mathcal{B}\models\Phi(\vec{b})
and then following the same steps we used to build
f
above. Note, such an isomorphism is (uniformly) computable from
(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\oplus\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})\oplus T)^{\prime}
which gives that
P
is uniformly
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{0}_{2}
-categorical. Hence,
P
has a
\Pi^{in}_{3}
Scott sentence by Theorem
1.1
. ∎

5.
Some Degree Spectra of Presburger Groups

5.1.
Computing Divisible Ordered Abelian Groups

We can make use of the fact that
V\times\mathbb{Z}
is a Presburger group when
V
is a divisible ordered abelian group to build Presburger groups with some notable degree spectra. In particular, given any
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
we will be able to construct groups with degree spectra of the form

\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}

for all computable non-limit ordinals
\alpha\neq 2
and

\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}

for all computable non-limit ordinals
\alpha\neq 0,2
(Theorem
5.17
). For the limit case, we will get a similar result (Corollary
5.12
). Furthermore, given any countable linear order
\mathcal{L}
, we will see by Theorem
5.8
that the Presburger group
P_{\mathcal{L}}
has degree spectrum

\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}.

As shown by the following proposition, being able to compute
V
allows you to compute
V\times\mathbb{Z}
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider the Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
with
V
a divisible ordered abelian group. We have
\text{DgSp}(V)\subseteq\text{DgSp}(P)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}\cong V
and
\deg(\mathcal{A})=\boldsymbol{d}
. It is clear that there is a
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable copy of the Presburger group
\mathcal{A}\times\mathbb{Z}\cong P
. Hence,
\text{DgSp}(V)\subseteq\text{DgSp}(P)
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

If
P
and
V
are as in the above proposition and
V
is nontrivial, then
\{\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}:\boldsymbol{d}\in\text{DgSp}(P)\}\subseteq\text{DgSp}(V)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}\cong P
with
\deg(\mathcal{B})=\boldsymbol{d}
. Consider the substructure
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{B}
generated by the element
1_{\mathcal{B}}
. We have that
\mathcal{C}\cong(\mathbb{Z},+,<,0,1)
and that
\mathcal{C}
is c.e. in
\mathcal{B}
. Hence,
\mathcal{C}
is
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
-computable and so the structure
\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{C}
, which is isomorphic to
V
, is
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
-computable.
∎

This may lead one to wonder if
\text{DgSp}(V)
is the same as
\text{DgSp}(P)
with
P
and
V
as above. This question remains open for now (Question
6.4
).

By theorem of Holder
[
16
]
, each Archimedean ordered group is isomorphic to a subgroup of the additive ordered group on
\mathbb{R}
. Given such an ordered group, we define its
divisible closure
as follows:

Definition 5.3
.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We define the divisible closure
C(R)
to be the smallest divisible ordered abelian group such that
R\subseteq C(R)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.

For example,
C(\{1,\sqrt{2}\})=\{a+b\sqrt{2}:a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\}
.
We previously saw by Proposition
2.7
that every upper cone of Turing degrees is the degree spectrum of some Presburger group, though the groups constructed were not plain Presburger groups. The following proposition uses an alternative construction that will yield plain Presburger groups with these spectra:

Proposition 5.4
.

Given any Turing degree
\boldsymbol{d}
, there exists a divisible ordered abelian group whose degree spectrum is the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
.

Proof.

Take
r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the set
\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:q<r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\}
is of degree
\boldsymbol{d}
. Consider the divisible ordered abelian group
V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\cong C(\{1,r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\})
. Note that given an
\omega
-presentation
\mathcal{A}
of a divisible ordered abelian group and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:qa_{n}<a_{m}\}
is computable in
\deg(\mathcal{A})
. Hence, given any copy
\mathcal{B}\cong V_{\boldsymbol{d}}
, the atomic diagram
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
computes
\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:q<r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\}
. Hence,
\deg(\mathcal{B})
is at least
\boldsymbol{d}
and so we have that the degree spectrum of
V_{\boldsymbol{d}}
is a subset of the the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
.

It is clear that
\boldsymbol{d}
can compute a copy of
V_{\boldsymbol{d}}
. Thus, because the degree spectrum of a non-automorphically trivial structure is upwards closed, we have that the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
is a subset of the degree spectrum of
V_{\boldsymbol{d}}
.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Given any Turing degree
\boldsymbol{d}
, there exists a (plain) Presburger group whose degree spectrum is the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
.

Proof.

Consider
V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\times\mathbb{Z}
. By Proposition
5.1
, we have that the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
is a subset of
\text{DgSp}(V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\times\mathbb{Z})
. Now suppose we have
\mathcal{A}\cong V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\times\mathbb{Z}
with
\deg(\mathcal{A})=\boldsymbol{c}.
Note that there exists an isomorphism
f:V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathcal{A}
and
a_{n},a_{m}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
f(1,0)=a_{n}
and
f(r_{\boldsymbol{d}},0)=a_{m}
. We have that
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
computes the set
\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:qa_{n}<a_{m}\}=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:q<r_{\boldsymbol{d}}\}
. Hence, we have that
\boldsymbol{c}\geq\boldsymbol{d}
and so all elements of
\text{DgSp}(V_{\boldsymbol{d}}\times\mathbb{Z})
are in the cone above
\boldsymbol{d}
.
∎

We can also use divisible ordered abelian groups to get Presburger groups with more interesting degree spectra. The following lemma will be useful in doing so:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a copy of a divisible ordered abelian group. If the atomic diagram
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is c.e. in some degree
\boldsymbol{d}
, then
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable.

Proof.

We show how using a
\boldsymbol{d}
-oracle, one can determine whether or not
\mathcal{A}
satisfies a given atomic sentence. Viewing
\mathcal{A}
as purely relational, all elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
are of one of the following forms:

\displaystyle a=0

\displaystyle a<b

\displaystyle a+b=c

for some
a,b,c\in\mathcal{A}
.

Use the oracle to start enumerating the elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
.
Given
a\in\mathcal{A}
, to determine if
a=0
is in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, simply wait for a sentence of the form
a^{\prime}=0
to be enumerated. If
a^{\prime}=a
, then
a=0
is in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, otherwise it is not. To see if
a+b=c
is in
\mathcal{D}(A)
, wait until something of the form
a+b=c^{\prime}
to be enumerated. Check if
c=c^{\prime}
. Since
<
is a total order, to determine if
a<b
is in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, simply wait for either
a<b
or
b<a
to be enumerated.
∎

5.2.
Degree Spectrum of
P_{\mathcal{L}}

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a linear order. Recall that we define the divisible ordered abelian group
V_{\mathcal{L}}
as
\bigoplus_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{Q}
and the Presburger group
P_{\mathcal{L}}
as
V_{\mathcal{L}}\times\mathbb{Z}
(see Definitions
2.12
and
2.14
). We now show that
\text{DgSp}(V_{\mathcal{L}})=\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Given any countable linear order
\mathcal{L}
,

\text{DgSp}(V_{\mathcal{L}})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a copy of
V_{\mathcal{L}}
of degree
\boldsymbol{d}
. The Archimedean equivalence relation
\sim
(as in Definition
2.9
) is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}
, and so
(\mathcal{C}-\{0_{\mathcal{C}}\})/\!\sim
, which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
as a linear order, is
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
-computable. Hence,
\text{DgSp}(V_{\mathcal{L}})\subseteq\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}
.

For the other direction, suppose that
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})
. It follows that there is some
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable set
T
such that
T^{\prime}=\{e:\Phi^{T}_{e}(e)\downarrow\}
computes a copy
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, there exists some Turing functional
\Psi
such that
\Psi^{T^{\prime}}(i,j)=1
if
a_{i}<_{\mathcal{A}}a_{j}
and
\Psi^{T^{\prime}}(i,j)=0
if
a_{j}<_{\mathcal{A}}a_{i}
. We use our oracle
T
and functional
\Psi
to compute the atomic diagram of a copy
\mathcal{B}
of
V_{\mathcal{L}}
. We do so by continually "guessing" at initial segments
\sigma
of
T^{\prime}
. We use the notation
\preceq
and
\prec
to denote initial segments and proper initial segments respectively. Note, if we define
\sigma_{n,m}\in 2^{n}
such that
\sigma_{n,m}(e)=1
if and only if
\Phi^{T}_{e,m}(e)\downarrow
, we have that
\sigma_{n,m}
will be an initial segment of
T^{\prime}
for sufficiently large
m
.

At each stage
s
of the construction, we will have a set
H_{s}
of elements in
\mathcal{B}
of the form
h_{\sigma,i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma
that still "look like" they may be initial segments of
T^{\prime}
. That is,
\sigma
such that
\sigma(e)=0
if and only if
\Phi^{T}_{e,s}(e)\uparrow
. We treat all elements of
H_{s}
as if they are in their own Archimedean equivalence class. If at a later stage
t
we have that
\Phi^{T}_{e,t}(e)\downarrow
for an
e
such that
\sigma(e)=0
, then we conclude that
\sigma
is not an initial segment of
T^{\prime}
and we make it so each element of
H_{t}
of the form
h_{\sigma,i}
will actually be Archimedean equivalent to some other element of
H_{t}
and we exclude
h_{\sigma,i}
from
H_{t+1}
. Defining
H=\lim_{s}H_{s}
, we will have that all elements of
H
are of the form
h_{\sigma,i}
for
\sigma\prec T^{\prime}
. Given
h_{\sigma,i},h_{\rho,j}\in H
, we will have
h_{\sigma,i}\ll h_{\rho,j}
if and only if
a_{i}<_{\mathcal{A}}a_{j}
. Additionally, each nonzero Archimedean equivalence class of
\mathcal{B}
will contain exactly one element of
H
, and every element of
\mathcal{B}
will be a finite
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of elements in
H
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{Q}\cong V_{\mathcal{L}}
.

For simplicity, we will view
\mathcal{B}
as purely relational. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{B}
is divisible, the element
qx
with
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
x\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is well defined and can be found computably in the atomic diagram
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
.
Thus, we may add multiplication by each
q\in\mathbb{Q}
to our language. We get that the sentences in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
will be those of the following forms:

•

b_{i}=0

•

b_{i}<b_{j}

•

qb_{i}=b_{j}

•

b_{i}+b_{j}=b_{k}

where
b_{i},b_{j},b_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We now give instructions for how, using our
\boldsymbol{d}
-oracle
T
, one can enumerate
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
. Note, it will thus follow from Lemma
5.6
that
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
is
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable.

Construction:

We let
I_{s}
denote the set of possible initial segments of
T^{\prime}
through stage
s
. That is,
I_{s}=\{\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}:|\sigma|\leq s\text{ and }\Phi^{T}_{e,s}(e)\downarrow\text{ if and only if }\sigma(e)=1\}
. We make use of a function
f
that will tell us for how large an
n\in\mathbb{N}
the function
\Psi^{\sigma}
can tell the order of all elements
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
with
i<n
when only running for
s
steps. Specifically given
\sigma\in 2^{<\omega}
, we define
f(\sigma,s)
to be the largest natural number
n\leq s
such that
\Psi^{\sigma}(i,j)
halts within
s
steps for all
i,j<n
. Note, as
\sigma
approaches
T^{\prime}
and
s
approaches infinity,
f(\sigma,s)
approaches infinity.

For each
s>0
, define the set
X_{s}\subseteq I_{t}\times\mathbb{N}
to be all elements of the form
(\sigma,m)
that satisfy the following:

(i)

m\leq f(\sigma,s)
; and

(ii)

m>f(\rho,s)
for all
\rho\prec\sigma
.

Define
H_{s}=\{h_{\sigma,m}:(\sigma,m)\in X_{s}\}
. We will define an ordering on
H_{s}
so that if
\rho\preceq\sigma
, then
h_{\rho,m}<h_{\sigma,n}
if and only if
\Psi^{\sigma}(m,n)=1
. The purpose of condition (ii) is so
H=\lim H_{s}
will contain exactly one element of the form
h_{\sigma,m}
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
and so
H
will be isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
as a linear order.

Let
\tau
be the shortest initial segment of
T^{\prime}
such that
\Psi^{\tau}(0,1)\downarrow
. Let
r
be the number of stages it takes for
\Psi^{\tau}(0,1)
to halt. Define
t=\max(|\tau|,r)
. For reasons that will become clear later, we begin our construction at stage
t
instead of stage
0
.

Stage
t
:

Add the sentence
b_{0}=0
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
. For each
h\in H_{t}
, pick a fresh element
b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
and define
b_{i}=h
. Add the sentence
h>0
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
for all
h\in H_{t}
. Given
h_{\sigma,m},h_{\rho,n}\in H_{t}
, add the sentence
h_{\rho,m}<h_{\sigma,n}
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B})
if either:

•

\rho\preceq\sigma
and
\Psi^{\sigma}(m,n)=1
; or

•

\sigma\preceq\rho

•

\sigma

Stage

•

\rho\preceq\sigma

; or

•

\sigma\preceq\rho

\Psi^{\rho}(m,n)=1

•

\sigma

(ii)

(iii)

For every finite tuple
(h_{1},...,h_{n})\in H^{<\omega}

h_{i}

(iv)

If we have

Define

. Note that

. Furthermore, if

with

h_{\rho,m}<_{\mathcal{B}}h_{\sigma,n}
if and only if
\Psi^{\tau}(m,n)=1

if and only if

. Hence,
(H,<_{\mathcal{B}})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
. Additionally, our instructions give that

, and every element of
\mathcal{B}
has the form

∎

Theorem 5.8
.

Given any countable linear order
\mathcal{L}
,

\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\text{DgSp}(V_{\mathcal{L}})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L})\}.

Proof.

Since
P_{\mathcal{L}}=V_{\mathcal{L}}\times\mathbb{Z}
, we have by Proposition
5.1
that
\text{DgSp}(V_{\mathcal{L}})\subseteq\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
. Now suppose that
\boldsymbol{d}\in\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{L}})
and that
\mathcal{A}
is a
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable copy of
P_{\mathcal{L}}
. Note that
\text{AR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\mathcal{L}^{*}
where
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\mathcal{L}\cup\{X\}
where
X
is a left-endpoint not in
\mathcal{L}
. Since Archimedean equivalence in
\mathcal{A}
is c.e. in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, we have that
\mathcal{L}^{*}
has a
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
-computable copy, and so it is clear that
\mathcal{L}
must have a
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
-computable copy as well.
∎

This gives that computing a copy of
P_{\mathcal{L}}
is often easier than computing
\mathcal{L}
. For example in
[
23
]
, R. Miller constructed a linear order
\mathcal{K}
that has no computable copy but does have copies of every noncomputable
\Delta^{0}_{2}
-degree. Since
\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{K})
contains
\boldsymbol{0}^{\prime}
, we get that
\text{DgSp}(P_{\mathcal{K}})
is simply the collection of all Turing degrees.

Theorem
5.8
allows us to leverage existing results about the degree spectra of linear orders to build degree spectra of Presburger groups. For example Ash, Downey, Jockusch and Knight showed in

[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
that given a computable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
and a degree
\boldsymbol{d}\geq\boldsymbol{0}^{(\alpha)}
, there is a linear ordering having
\alpha^{th}
jump degree
\boldsymbol{d}
sharply. In particular:

Proposition 5.9

(Lemma 2.1 in
[
6
]
)
.

Given any computable ordinal
\beta\geq 2
that is not a limit ordinal and any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, there exists a linear order
\mathcal{L}(\beta,S)
such that
\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L}(\beta,S))=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\beta)}\}.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Given any computable ordinal
\alpha\geq 3
that is not a limit ordinal and any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, there exists:

(1)

A divisible ordered abelian group
V
and a Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}
; and

(2)

A divisible ordered abelian group
V
and a Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}
.

Proof.

Take
\beta
such that
1+\beta=\alpha
. For (1), take
V=V_{\mathcal{L}(\beta,S)}
. We have by Proposition
5.8
that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\in\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L}(\beta,S))\}=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }(\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime})^{(\beta)}\}=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}
. For (2), take
V=V_{\mathcal{L}(\beta,S\oplus\overline{S})}
and note that
S\oplus\overline{S}
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}
if and only if
S
is computable in
\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}
.
∎

We can get a similar result for the case where
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
, we say that an increasing sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
fundamental sequence
for
\lambda
if each
\alpha_{n}<\lambda
and
\bigcup_{n}\alpha_{n}=\lambda
.

Proposition 5.11

(Lemma 2.4 in
[
6
]
)
.

Given any limit ordinal
\lambda
and any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, there exists a fundamental sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
\lambda
and a linear order
\mathcal{L}(\lambda,S)
such that

\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{L}(\lambda,S))=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S_{n}\text{ is computable in $\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha_{n})}$ uniformly in $n$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\},

where
S_{n}=\{m:(n,m)\in S\}
. ∎

Corollary 5.12
.

Given any limit ordinal
\lambda
and any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, there exists a fundamental sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
\lambda
, a divisible ordered abelian group
V
, and a Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S_{n}\text{ is computable in $\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha_{n})}$ uniformly in $n$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\}
, where
S_{n}=\{m:(n,m)\in S\}
. ∎

5.3.
One-Jump Spectra

Absent from Corollary
5.10
are the cases where
\alpha
is
0,1
or
2
. This is largely due to the absence of the
\alpha=0,1
cases from Proposition
5.9
. It was shown by Richter in
[
30
]
and by Knight in
[
18
]
that the sets
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
are the degree spectra of linear orders if and only if
S
is computable. Thus, we must look beyond structures of the form
V_{\mathcal{L}}
and
P_{\mathcal{L}}
to fill in these gaps in Corollary
5.10
.

We already saw by Propositions
2.7
and
5.4
and Corollary
5.5
that for any
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, the set
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}\}
is the degree spectrum of a divisible ordered abelian group as well as a Presburger group. We now turn our attention to the case involving a single jump of
\boldsymbol{d}
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The set of Turing degrees
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
is the degree spectrum of a divisible ordered abelian group.

Proof.

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an enumeration of the primes. Recall that given
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we define
C(R)
to be the divisible closure of
R
(Definition
5.3
). Define

V_{S}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}U_{n}

where

U_{n}=\begin{cases}C(\{1,\sqrt{p_{n}}\})&\text{if }n\in S\\
\mathbb{Q}&\text{if }n\notin S.\end{cases}

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a copy of
V_{S}
of degree
\boldsymbol{d}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the formula

\phi_{n}:=(\exists x,y)\Big[\WW_{\sqrt{p_{n}}>q\in\mathbb{Q}}qx<y\,\,\&\,\,\WW_{\sqrt{p_{n}}<q\in\mathbb{Q}}qx>y\Big].

We have that
\mathcal{A}\models\phi_{n}
if and only if
n\in S
. Note that each
\phi_{n}
is
\Sigma^{c}_{2}
. That is, each
\phi_{n}
is a
computable infinitary

\Sigma^{in}_{2}
sentence (for background on computable infinitary logic, see e.g.
[
4
]
). Hence,
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
can enumerate the set of
n
such that
\mathcal{A}\models\phi_{n}
and can therefore enumerate
S
.

For the other direction, suppose that
S
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
. It follows that there exists a
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable set
T
such that
\text{Fin}^{T}=\{e:\text{dom}(\Phi^{T}_{e})\text{ is finite}\}=S
. We will now build a
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable copy
\mathcal{B}\cong V_{S}
. We do so by building
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable copies
\mathcal{B}_{n}\cong U_{n}
for all
n
and define
\mathcal{B}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Since each
\mathcal{B}_{n}
will be
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable,
\mathcal{B}
will be
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable as well.

Since each
U_{n}
is Archimedean, each can be viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{R}
by Holder’s theorem
[
16
]
. We begin the construction of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
by adding in an element
X
that we treat like
1\in U_{n}
. At each stage
s+1
of the construction, we have an element
h_{s}
that we have been treating like
\sqrt{p_{n}}
. If the domain of
\Phi_{n}^{T}
gains no new elements at stage
s+1
, then we let
h_{s+1}=h_{s}
and keep treating this element like
\sqrt{p_{n}}
. If the domain of
\Phi_{n}^{T}
does gain a new element at stage
s+1
, then we stop treating
h_{s}
like it is
\sqrt{p_{n}}
by finding a “suitable”
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and adding the sentence
qX=h_{s}
. By a suitable
q
, we just mean one such that adding the sentence
qX=h_{s}
will not contradict any of the sentences we have already added. Such a
q
can be found by taking some rational that is sufficiently close to
1/\sqrt{p_{n}}
. We then find a fresh element to call
h_{s+1}
which we begin to treat like
\sqrt{p_{n}}
.

Note, if the domain of
\Phi_{n}^{T}
is finite, then there will be a sufficiently large
k
such that
h_{k}=h_{m}
for all
m\geq k
. This element will turn out to satisfy “
\sqrt{p_{n}}X=h_{k}
.” In the case where
\Phi_{n}^{T}
is infinite, every
h_{s}
will turn out to be a rational multiple of
X
, and thus no element will be “
\sqrt{p_{n}}X
.”

For simplicity, we will view each
\mathcal{B}_{n}
as purely relational. Furthermore, since each
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is divisible, the element
qx
with
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
x\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is well defined and can be found computably in the atomic diagram
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
.
Thus, we may add multiplication by each
q\in\mathbb{Q}
to our language. We get that the sentences in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
will be those of the following forms:

•

b_{i}=0

•

b_{i}<b_{j}

•

qb_{i}=b_{j}

•

b_{i}+b_{j}=b_{k}

where
b_{i},b_{j},b_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We now give instructions for how, using a
\boldsymbol{d}
oracle, one can enumerate
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
. Note, it will thus follow from Lemma
5.6
that
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
is
\boldsymbol{d}
-computable.

Construction:

Fix an oracle
T\in\boldsymbol{d}
. Let
W_{n,m}^{T}
denote the domain of
\Phi_{n}^{T}
after running for
m
steps. Let
\{u_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a computable decreasing, and
\{l_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a computable increasing, sequence of rationals such that
\lim_{i}u_{i}=\lim_{i}l_{i}=\sqrt{p_{n}}
. Throughout the construction, we will have an increasing sequence
\{k_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
that will keep the interval
[l_{k_{i}},u_{k_{i}}]
sufficiently tight.

Stage
0
:

Add the sentences
b_{0}=0_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
and
b_{0}<b_{1}
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
. We will henceforth refer to
b_{1}
as
X
. Let
k_{0}=0
and let
b_{2}=h_{0}
.

Stage
s+1
:

(i)

Check if
|W^{T}_{n,s+1}|>|W^{T}_{n,s}|
.

•

If no, let
k_{s+1}=k_{s}+1
and let
h_{s+1}=h_{s}
.

•

If yes, find the smallest natural number
k_{s+1}>k_{s}
such that given any

with the sentences
qX=b_{i}
and

already in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
for some

q\neq q^{\prime}u_{k_{s+1}}
. Add
u_{k_{s+1}}X=h_{s}
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
. Additionally, for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that there exists
b_{j}
with
qh_{s}=b_{j}
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
, add the sentence
u_{k_{s+1}}qX=b_{j}
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
. Find a fresh element of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to call
h_{s+1}
.

(ii)

Let
\{q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let

and

. Find the least
m,p\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

•

-\frac{r}{t}
is not in the interval
[l_{{k_{s+1}}},u_{k_{s+1}}]
for any

(this will help us decide which elements are positive later on); and

•

The sentences
q_{m}X=b_{i}
and
q_{p}h_{s+1}=b_{j}
are not yet in

for any

.

b_{c}
and
b_{d}

q_{m}X=b_{c}
and

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
.

(iii)

Suppose we have the sentences

and
b_{j}=q^{\prime}h_{s+1}

b_{c}

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{B}_{n})
.

(iv)

Suppose

has appeared in a sentence already in

. We have that

with

. Suppose we have such elements

,

, and

with

and

and

to

.

Note, by our careful selection of

and

during part (ii) of each stage, we are guaranteed that

•

•

If
W_{n}^{T}
is finite, then define

. If

is infinite, then we say that

does not exist. All elements

(or just
qX

qX=b_{i^{\prime}}
. Call this stage
p
. Note, it is not possible for
qX=b_{j^{\prime}}
to have entered at part (ii) as there was already an element equal to
qX
. This means that
p<s
and it must have entered at part (i) due to
b_{j^{\prime}}=q^{\prime\prime}h_{k_{p}}
and
q^{\prime\prime}u_{k_{p+1}}=q
. However, we choose
k_{p+1}
at part (i) of stage
p+1
to avoid this happening. Hence, this is not possible and we get that if
b_{i},b_{j}=qX+q^{\prime}h
, then
b_{i}=b_{j}
.

Furthermore, it is clear from our instructions that there will be elements equal to
qX
and
qh
(or just
qX
if
h
does not exist) for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
. Because of part (iii) of the construction, there will also be elements equal to
qX+q^{\prime}h
if
h
exists for all
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Thus, we get that the maps
qX+q^{\prime}h\mapsto q+q^{\prime}\sqrt{p_{n}}
and
qX\mapsto q
are bijections from
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to
C(1,\sqrt{p_{n}})
and
\mathbb{Q}
in the cases where
h
does and does not exist respectively. Furthermore, it is clear from our construction that these maps are isomorphisms of divisible ordered abelian groups. Hence, we get that
\mathcal{B}_{n}\cong C(1,\sqrt{p_{n}})
if
W^{T}_{n}
is finite and
\mathcal{B}_{n}\cong\mathbb{Q}
otherwise.
∎

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The set of Turing degrees
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
is the degree spectrum of a Presburger group.

Proof.

Consider the structure
P=V_{S}\times\mathbb{Z}
where
V_{S}
is as in the previous proof. By Proposition
5.1
, we have that
\text{DgSp}(V_{S})=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\text{DgSp}(P)
.

For the other direction, suppose that
\mathcal{A}\cong P
. We will have that
n\in S
if and only if

\mathcal{A}\models(\exists x,y)\left[\WW_{\sqrt{p_{n}}>\frac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}}ax<by\,\,\&\WW_{\sqrt{p_{n}}<\frac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}}ax>by\right].

In particular, if
x
and
y
are witnesses to this sentence in
V_{S}
then
(x,0)
and
(y,0)
are witnesses in
P
. This sentence is
\Sigma^{c}_{2}
. Hence, we get that
S
is c.e. in
\deg(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The set of Turing degrees
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is computable in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
is the degree spectrum of a divisible ordered abelian group.

Proof.

Consider
V_{S\oplus\overline{S}}
(as defined in the previous proof). We have that the degree spectrum of
V_{S\oplus\overline{S}}
is
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\oplus\overline{S}
is c.e. in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is computable in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}
}.
∎

Corollary 5.16
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The set of Turing degrees
\{\boldsymbol{d}:S
is computable in
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}\}
is the degree spectrum of a Presburger group. ∎

Combining these with our previous results, we get the following:

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
\alpha
be a computable ordinal that is not a limit ordinal, and let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

If
\alpha\neq 0,2
, then there exists a divisible ordered abelian group
V
and a Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is c.e.\ in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}
.

(2)

If
\alpha\neq 2
, then there exists a divisible ordered abelian group
V
and a Presburger group
P=V\times\mathbb{Z}
such that
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)=\{\boldsymbol{d}:S\text{ is computable in }\boldsymbol{d}^{(\alpha)}\}
.

Proof.

For (1), the case of
\alpha=1
follows from Proposition
5.13
and Corollary
5.14
. The remaining cases are given in Corollary
5.10
. For (2), the case of
\alpha=0
follows from Proposition
5.4
and Corollary
5.5
, and the case of
\alpha=1
follows from Corollaries
5.15
and
5.16
. The remaining cases are given in Corollary
5.10
.
∎

Recall that we saw a similar result above in Corollary
5.12
for the case where
\alpha
is a limit ordinal.

6.
Further Questions

6.1.
Scott Analysis

Question 6.1
.

Are there any Presburger groups with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{4}
?

Since there are no linear orders with Scott sentence complexity
\Sigma_{3}
, our methods used in Section
3
cannot yield us such a Presburger group of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
. However, there are many other forms that Presburger groups can take.

Question 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a countable linear order.

(i)

Must we have
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
?

(ii)

If
|\mathcal{L}|>1
and
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Gamma_{\alpha}
(with
\Gamma=\Pi,\Sigma
or
d
-
\Sigma
), must we have
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\Gamma_{1+\alpha}
?

Note that if
\mathcal{L}
is the linear order containing only a single point, then
\text{SC}(\mathcal{L})=\Pi_{1}
but
\text{SC}(P_{\mathcal{L}})=\text{SC}(P_{1})=d
-
\Sigma_{2}
by Proposition
3.23
. All linear orders considered in this paper satisfy both (i) and (ii), and we have that (i) will hold so long as
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
is infinite (see Corollary
3.7
). However, must this hold for all linear orders?

Question
6.2
is closely related to the following open question posed by Gonzalez and Harrison-Trainor:

Question 6.3

(Question 1.10 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a countable structure with the property that whenever
\mathcal{B}
is a countable structure and

\mathcal{A}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}
then
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}
. Does
\mathcal{A}
have a
\Pi_{\alpha}
Scott sentence?

If the answer to Question
6.3
is yes, then given
\mathcal{L}
with
\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})=n
there would have to be some linear order
\mathcal{K}\ncong\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathcal{L}\leq_{n}\mathcal{K}
. Thus,
P_{\mathcal{L}}\leq_{1+n}P_{\mathcal{K}}
by Corollary
3.19
and so
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\geq 1+n
. This, along with the fact that
\text{SR}(P_{\mathcal{L}})\leq 1+\text{SR}(\mathcal{L})
(Lemma
3.6
), would yield a positive answer to Question
6.2
part (i).

6.2.
Degree Spectra

Question 6.4
.

Let
V
be a nontrivial divisible ordered abelian group, and let
P
be the Presburger group
V\times\mathbb{Z}
. Must we have
\text{DgSp}(V)=\text{DgSp}(P)
?

All of the Presburger groups of the form
V\times\mathbb{Z}
that we examined in this paper have this property. Additionally, we saw by Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
that
\text{DgSp}(V)\subseteq\text{DgSp}(P)
and that
\{\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}:\boldsymbol{d}\in\text{DgSp}(P)\}\subseteq\text{DgSp}(V)
. However, it
is not obvious how to strengthen Proposition
5.2
. Although
V
is always co-c.e. in
P
, it is not clear how to computably enumerate
V
given the atomic diagram of
P
.

Question 6.5
.

Are Presburger groups universal for degree spectra?

We call a class of structures
\mathfrak{C}

universal for degree spectra
if for every countable non-automorphically trivial structure
\mathcal{B}
, there exists some
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{C}
such that
\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{A})\cong\text{DgSp}(\mathcal{B})
. It was shown by Hirschfeldt, Khoussainov, Shore and Slinko in
[
15
]
that the following classes of structures are universal for degree spectra: symmetric irreflexive graphs, partial orderings, rings, integral domains, commutative semi-groups, and 2-step nilpotent groups. It was further shown by R. Miller, Poonen, Schoutens and Shlapentokh in
[
24
]
that the class of fields is universal for degree spectra as well. It should be noted that linear orders are not universal for degree spectra, so we must look beyond Presburger groups of the form
P_{\mathcal{L}}
to answer Question
6.5
.
