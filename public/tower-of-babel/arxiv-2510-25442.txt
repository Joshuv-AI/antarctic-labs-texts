Minimizing point configurations for tensor product energies on the torus
Dmitriy Bilyk, Nicolas Nagel, Ian Ruohoniemi

Abstract
We study point configurations on the torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} that minimize interaction energies with tensor product structure which arise naturally in the context of discrepancy theory and quasi-Monte Carlo integration. Permutation sets on 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} and Latin hypercube sets in higher dimensions (i.e. sets whose projections onto coordinate axes are equispaced points) are natural candidates to be energy minimizers. We show that such point configurations that have only one distance in the vector sense minimize the energy for a wide range of potentials, in other words, such sets satisfy a tensor product version of universal optimality. This applies, in particular, to three- and five-point Fibonacci lattices. We also characterize all lattices with this property and exhibit some non-lattice sets of this type. In addition, we obtain several further structural results about global and local minimizers of tensor product energies.



1
Introduction

1.1
Energies with product structure

Denote by
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
the unit torus, where we will identify elements in
\mathbb{T}
with those in the half-open interval
[0,1)
. In the present paper we explore the properties of minimizers of interaction energies on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with kernels that have tensor product structure. To be more precise, consider the kernel
F:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
of the form

F(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})=f(t_{1})\dots f(t_{d}),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. Throughout the paper we will assume that
f
, viewed as a non-negative
1
-periodic function on
\mathbb{R}
, is
even and continuous
on
(0,1)
. In most situations
f
will actually be assumed to be continuous on the whole real line, but some results also apply when
f
is singular at
0
in the sense of
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}f(t)=+\infty
. We will refer to both
F
and
f
as
potentials
throughout the paper.

For a (multi)set of
N
points
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
with components
\mathbf{x}^{k}=(x^{k}_{1},\dots,x^{k}_{d})
, we define the
tensor product energy
as

E_{F}(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}F({\bf{x}}^{k}-{\bf{x}}^{\ell})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f(x_{i}^{k}-x_{i}^{\ell}).

(1.2)

This quantity can be intuitively viewed as the total potential energy of
N
particles in positions
{\bf x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,{\bf x}^{N-1}
which interact according to the potential given by
F
. One is then naturally interested in minimizing the energy (
1.2
) over all
N
-point configurations and finding minimizing configurations of points, i.e. equilibrium distributions. We shall call such configurations
energy minimizers
. While extensive literature exists on energy minimization in various domains in the case where the kernel
F({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})
depends on the Euclidean (or another) distance between
{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
(see for example the book
[
1
]
), kernels with tensor product structures have not received the same attention
[
23
,
29
]
.

The condition that
f
is even, or equivalently
f(t)=f(1-t)
, means that
f(x-y)
depends only on the
geodesic distance

\displaystyle\delta(x,y)=\min\{|x-y|,1-|x-y|\}

(1.3)

between
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{T}
. For most potentials of interest,
f
is decreasing on
(0,1/2]
, as this corresponds to the interaction being repulsive in each coordinate.

We remark that the energy (
1.2
) of a configuration is invariant under symmetries of the torus (translations, reflections, and permutations of coordinates) and all uniqueness statements, e.g. in Definition
1.1
or Theorem
2.8
, are to be understood modulo these symmetries. In particular, since the energy (
1.2
) is translation invariant, we may assume without loss of generality that
{\bf x}^{0}=(0,\dots,0)
.

Energies of the type (
1.2
) with tensor product kernels (
1.1
) naturally arise in several fields such as discrepancy theory and quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration. Warnock’s formula
[
22
,
23
,
33
]
states that minimizing the periodic
L^{2}
-discrepancy of
N
points in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is equivalent to minimizing the energy (
1.2
) with the kernel (
1.1
), where
f(t)=\frac{1}{2}-t+t^{2}
on
[0,1)
. It is also known
[
23
,
29
]
that minimizing the worst-case integration error of the quasi-Monte Carlo rule over a certain Sobolev space corresponds to the energy minimization problem with the potential

\displaystyle f_{p}(t)=1+\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{1}{6}-t+t^{2}\right),

(1.4)

where
p>0
is a fixed parameter (notice that
f_{6}=3f
, where
f
is the aforementioned potential from Warnock’s formula). Similar statements hold for other reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (see the Appendix §
A
and
[
2
,
3
]
or
[
30
, §9]
). The relation to discrepancy theory provides possible candidates for energy minimizers, namely low-discrepancy sets.

1.2
Dimension
d=2
: Permutation sets and Fibonacci lattices

Some of the most well-known low-discrepancy point sets in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
include the digit-reversing
van der Corput sets
when
N=2^{n}
(see e.g.
[
22
]
for a definition) and the
Fibonacci lattices
which will keep appearing in this paper: if
N=F_{j}
is a Fibonacci number, the
N
-point Fibonacci lattice is defined by

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}\coloneqq\left\{\left(\frac{k}{F_{j}},\left\{\frac{kF_{j-1}}{F_{j}}\right\}\right):k=0,1,\dots,F_{j}-1\right\},

(1.5)

where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. This set can be thought of as a rational approximation to the famous Kronecker set
\big\{(k/N,\{k\varphi\}):k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\}
, where
\varphi
is the golden section. Fibonacci lattices have been studied extensively in the context of discrepancy theory and numerical integration and perform strongly in these settings
[
4
,
5
]
.

Both of the aforementioned examples, the van der Corput set and the Fibonacci lattice, have important structural properties. First, these are
N
-point configurations that are subsets of the
(N\times N)
-grid

\left\{\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right):\,k,\ell=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\}

in
[0,1)^{2}\simeq\mathbb{T}^{2}
. We shall call such sets
grid sets
. More importantly these examples are of the form

X_{\sigma}=\{{\bf x}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}=\left\{\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\sigma(k)}{N}\right):k=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\},

(1.6)

where
\sigma
is a permutation of the set
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
(if we assume
{\bf x}^{0}=(0,0)
, that is we fix
\sigma(0)=0
, we can view
\sigma
just as easily as a permutation of
\{1,\dots,N-1\}
).

Definition 1.1
.

We shall call configurations
X\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
which (up to translations) have the form (
1.6
)
permutation sets
.

A special place among the permutation sets is taken by
integration lattices
(which we shall refer to simply as
lattices
), i.e. sets of the form

\left\{\left(\frac{k}{N},\left\{\frac{hk}{N}\right\}\right):k=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\},

(1.7)

where
h
and
N
are relatively prime integers. The condition that
h
and
N
are relatively prime guarantees that (
1.7
) is indeed a permutation set. Notice that such sets are restrictions to
[0,1)^{2}
of lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
generated by the vectors
(1/N,h/N)
and
(0,1)
, which are
1
-periodic in both coordinate directions. Fibonacci lattices (
1.5
) are particular examples of such sets.

We note that permutation sets are those sets whose projections onto both
x
- and
y
-axes are
N
equispaced points, i.e. they are grid sets with exactly one point in each row and each column. This interpretation suggests that, since many energies in dimension
d=1
are minimized by equispaced points (see §
2.1
), it is natural to expect that permutation sets minimize tensor product energies on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for large classes of potentials. We then have the following question:
which conditions on the potential
f
guarantee that minimizers of the tensor product energy (
1.2
) are permutation sets?
This question admits a variety of versions and interpretations: e.g., when does this hold for all
N
, for specific values of
N
, or for all
N
large enough? When are energies minimized by lattices? Does it hold if sets are restricted to the
(N\times N)
-grid?

Based on the particularly important role played by the Fibonacci lattices in low discrepancy constructions and on ample numerical evidence
[
23
]
, we conjecture that whenever
N
is a Fibonacci number,
the Fibonacci lattice minimizes the energy for a broad class of potentials
f
. This conjectured phenomenon (the same configuration minimizing a large variety of energies) is a natural analogue of the notion of
universal optimality
that has been studied on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}

[
10
]
and in the plane
[
11
]
, and is known to be very rare.

In the end, we remark that the condition that
f
is logarithmically convex is very natural as it is necessary and sufficient for the tensor product
F
to be convex as a function of
d
variables
[
31
]
.

1.3
Higher dimensions
d\geq 3

In dimensions
d\geq 3
it becomes significantly harder to construct good point sets. To the knowledge of the authors, no study of global optimizers of
L_{2}
-discrepancy or quasi-Monte Carlo worst case errors in dimensions
d\geq 3
has been carried out so far and only asymptotic results are known
[
9
,
19
]
. A straightforward extension of the concept of permutation sets in higher dimensions to sets on the
N^{d}
-grid
\{k/N:k=0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{d}
leads to Latin (hyper)cubes.

Definition 1.2
.

A
Latin hypercube set
is defined by permutations
\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}
of the set
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
via

X_{\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}}=\left\{\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\sigma_{2}(k)}{N},\dots,\frac{\sigma_{d}(k)}{N}\right):k=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\}.

In other words, it is a set whose projections on all coordinate axes are equispaced points. Thus a
permutation set
is a Latin hypercube set with
d=2
.
Analogously to
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, lattices in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
of the form

\left\{\left(\frac{k}{N},\left\{\frac{h_{2}k}{N}\right\},\dots,\left\{\frac{h_{d}k}{N}\right\}\right):k=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\},

which are generated by a vector

\left(\frac{1}{N},\frac{h_{2}}{N},\dots,\frac{h_{d}}{N}\right)

with
\gcd(h_{2},N)=\dots=\gcd(h_{d},N)=1
, are common examples of Latin hypercube sets.

Just as in the case of permutation set in dimension
d=2
, the same questions naturally arise in dimension
d\geq 3
:
For which potentials
f
do Latin hypercubes minimize the tensor product energy? Can one identify specific lattices or other Latin hypercube sets which minimize large classes of energies?

1.4
Main results

Even though complete answers to the aforementioned questions appear to be out of reach for now, we provide a plethora of interesting results
which open up a new direction in energy minimization. In particular, we prove the following:

•

As a first indication that permutation and Latin hypercube sets might naturally be minimizers of tensor product energies in all dimensions
d\geq 2
, we show that points in (local) minimizers cannot coincide in any coordinate provided that the potential
f
generates sufficient repulsion at small scales (Theorem
3.1
).

•

In Theorem
2.3
, which can be considered the main result of the paper, we prove that in all dimensions
d\geq 2

Latin hypercube sets with a single distance vector
(in other words, sets of maximal separation in which each interaction contributes the same amount to the energy) minimize the tensor product energy with potential
f
whenever the energy with potential
g=\log f
on
\mathbb{T}
is minimized by equispaced points. Sufficient conditions for this property include the following:

(a)
f
is logarithmically convex and decreasing on
(0,1/2]
, or

(b)
\log f(t)
is absolutely monotone with respect to
s=\cos(2\pi t)
.

(See §
2.1
for more details)

•

We exhibit many sets in dimensions with a single distance vector in dimensions
d\geq 2
. In fact, in Theorem
2.8
we characterize all the lattices with a single distance vector. We also give many examples of non-lattice sets with this property. This provides a large collection of point configurations which satisfy this tensor product version of universal optimality.

•

In particular, the
3
- and
5
-point Fibonacci lattices are easily seen satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
. Thus the
5
-point Fibonacci lattice
\Phi_{5}
minimizes the energy for potentials
f
as above, see Corollary
2.5
, i.e.
\Phi_{5}
is universally optimal with respect to these classes of potentials (and a similar result holds for the
3
-point Fibonacci lattice, Corollary
2.7
).

•

Moreover, the
5
-point Fibonacci lattice
\Phi_{5}
minimizes the tensor product energy (
1.2
) among all
5
-point permutation sets if
f
is only assumed to be decreasing on
(0,1/2]
, Lemma
2.6
.

•

If
f
is decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
, lattices have the smallest energy among all sets of the same
permutation type
(see Definition
4.1
), i.e. energy minimization under these restriction yields permutation sets (Theorem
4.2
). As a corollary, under the same conditions every lattice is a local energy minimizer (Corollary
4.4
).

•

We also address energy of
4
- and
6
-point sets on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. For
N=4
, we find global minimizers of the energy when
f
is differentiable on
(0,1)
, and decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
(Proposition
5.2
), which extends a numerical result from
[
23
]
to a wide range of potentials.

Moreover, we observe that a
4
-point permutation set almost never minimizes a tensor product energy and is never the unique minimizer (Remark
5.3
.

•

For
N=6
a similar construction yields a minimizer among all
6
-point configurations restricted to a natural choice of permutation type (Proposition
5.4
).

In the Appendix we provide a sampler of relevant potentials and discuss their properties.

2
Optimality of Latin hypercube sets with a single distance vector

In this section we will present the aforementioned collection of point configurations which minimize broad classes of tensor product energies (i.e. possess a certain version of universal optimality). To understand the relevant properties of the underlying potentials, we first discuss energy on the circle.

2.1
Equispaced points as energy minimizers on the circle

While the one-dimensional case
\mathbb{T}
does not exhibit tensor product structure, it is an important starting point for our investigations. In this setting, if all points repel each other equally, it is natural to expect the energy

E_{f}(x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{N-1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}f({{x}}^{k}-{{x}}^{\ell})

to be minimized by
equispaced points

\{{{x}}^{0},{{x}}^{1},\dots,{{x}}^{N-1}\}=\left\{\frac{0}{N},\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{N-1}{N}\right\}\subset\mathbb{T}.

In the context of numerical integration, the optimality of equispaced points was proven in a broad sense in
[
34
]
. However, in the general energy setting, the precise characterizations of the potentials
f
for which this happens is still elusive. Some known sufficient conditions on
f
are formulated below. Recall that we always assume
f
(as a
1
-periodic function on
\mathbb{R}
) to be even, i.e.
f(t)=f(1-t)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

E_{f}(x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{N-1})\geq E_{f}\left(\frac{0}{N},\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{N-1}{N}\right)

(2.1)

for all
x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{N-1}\in[0,1)
provided that
f
satisfies any one of the following conditions:

(i)

f
is convex and decreasing on
(0,1/2]
;

(ii)

f(t)=h(\cos(2\pi t))
where
h:[-1,1)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is absolutely monotone (up to an additive constant), i.e.
h
is a
C^{\infty}
-function satisfying
h^{(k)}(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in(-1,1)
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iii)

f\in C(\mathbb{T})
and
f(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}a_{m}\cos(2\pi mt)
pointwise with
a_{m}\geq 0
for all
m>0
, and
a_{m}=0
for all
m>0
with
N|m
.

Condition (i) is classical and goes back to
[
17
]
, see also
[
6
]
. Condition (ii) is a special case
d=1
of the celebrated theorem of Cohn and Kumar
[
10
]
on universal optimality on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. It is not hard to see that these conditions overlap, but do not imply each other. Observe that these two conditions apply to all values of
N\in\mathbb{N}
simultaneously. In fact, for a given value of
N
, the positivity of
h^{(k)}
in condition (ii) only needs to be checked up to order
k\leq N-1
. Condition (iii) is a simple calculus exercise (positivity of the coefficients even implies that the Fourier series converges absolutely and uniformly).
Fejér kernels

f_{\nu}(t)=\frac{1}{\nu}\left(\frac{\sin(\nu\pi t)}{\sin(\pi t)}\right)^{2}=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{\nu-1}\left(1-\frac{m}{\nu}\right)\cos(2\pi mt)

are examples of potentials for which (iii) holds, but neither (i) nor (ii) applies for a fixed
N\geq\nu\geq 3
.

None of the conditions (i)–(iii) are necessary. Positive definiteness of
f
(equivalent to positivity of the coefficients in the cosine Fourier series) is a simple necessary condition for (
2.1
) to hold for all
N
large enough, but it is not sufficient.

If (
2.1
) holds, i.e.
N
equispaced points in
\mathbb{T}
minimize the energy
E_{f}
, the potential
f
will be called
N
-equispacing
. As an example, the aforementioned potential (
1.4
) arising in quasi-Monte Carlo integration is
N
-equispacing for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
p>0
as it fulfills both (i) and (ii) (as follows from
[
27
, equation (13)]
) above.

2.2
Sets with a single distance vector

Since tensor product energies depend on distances in individual coordinates independently, rather than on actual distances between points, it is natural to consider a vector-valued analogue of distances. For points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
define the
distance vector
between them as

\operatorname{dv}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(\delta(x_{\tau(1)},y_{\tau(1)}),\dots,\delta(x_{\tau(d)},y_{\tau(d)})),

where
\delta
denotes the geodesic distance on
\mathbb{T}
as in (
1.3
) and
\tau
is a permutation of
\{1,\dots,d\}
such that

\delta(x_{\tau(1)},y_{\tau(1)})\leq\dots\leq\delta(x_{\tau(d)},y_{\tau(d)}),

i.e. we take the geodesic distance in every component and sort them increasingly. For
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
let

\operatorname{dv}(X)=\{\operatorname{dv}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{x}^{\ell}):k\neq\ell\}

be the set of all distance vectors among points in
X
. We shall be interested in Latin hypercube sets
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
satisfying

\#\operatorname{dv}(X)=1,

i.e. having only
a single distance vector
. We remark that such sets are natural tensor product analogues of one-distance sets which have been well studied in other geometric contexts, e.g. on spheres (simplices with center of mass in
\mathbf{0}
) and projective spaces (equiangular tight frames), see
[
12
]
. However, this tensor product setting does not seem to have previously appeared in the literature.

Since for the tensor product energy
E_{F}
the interaction between two points only depends on the distance vector between them, we see that for a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#X=N
with a single distance vector
\{(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})\}=\operatorname{dv}(X)
we have

\displaystyle E_{F}(X)=(N^{2}-N)\prod_{i=1}^{d}f(\rho_{i}).

(2.2)

The following lemma counts the frequency of different entries in the common distance vector in such a set. The main point of this computation is to show that the frequency distribution coincides with the distribution of distances in
d
copies of
N
equispaced points in
\mathbb{T}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#X=N
be a Latin hypercube set with a single distance vector, whose common distance vector is given by
\{(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})\}=\operatorname{dv}(X)
. Then for every
r=1,\dots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor
it holds

\#\left\{i=1,\dots,d:\rho_{i}=\frac{r}{N}\right\}=\begin{cases}\frac{2d}{N-1},&r<\frac{N}{2},\\
\frac{d}{N-1},&r=\frac{N}{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We will show, via double counting, that

\displaystyle(N^{2}-N)\cdot\#\left\{i=1,\dots,d:\rho_{i}=\frac{r}{N}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\cdot\#\left\{(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2}:\delta\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right)=\frac{r}{N}\right\},

from which the claim follows since

\#\left\{(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2}:\delta\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right)=\frac{r}{N}\right\}=\begin{cases}2N,&r<\frac{N}{2},\\
N,&r=\frac{N}{2}.\end{cases}

Since
X
is a Latin hypercube, there are permutations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d}
such that

X=\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}=\left\{\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}(k)}{N},\dots,\frac{\sigma_{d}(k)}{N}\right):k=0,1,\dots,N-1\right\}

(with
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{id}
the identity on
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
). Consider the array
A=[a_{(k,\ell),i}]
with rows indexed by
(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2},k\neq\ell
and columns indexed by
i=1,\dots,d
, with entries

a_{(k,l),i}=\delta\left(\frac{\sigma_{i}(k)}{N},\frac{\sigma_{i}(\ell)}{N}\right).

We will count for how many entries it holds
a_{(k,\ell),i}=\frac{r}{N}
. The row corresponding to
(k,\ell)
consists, by definition, of the entries of
\operatorname{dv}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{x}^{\ell})
in some order. By the single distance property, this row contains exactly

\#\left\{i=1,\dots,d:\rho_{i}=\frac{r}{N}\right\}

such entries, so that there are

(N^{2}-N)\cdot\#\left\{i=1,\dots,d:\rho_{i}=\frac{r}{N}\right\}

in total of them in
A
. On the other hand, since
\sigma_{i}
is a permutation, in the
i
-th column we have

\displaystyle\#\left\{(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2}:\delta\left(\frac{\sigma_{i}(k)}{N},\frac{\sigma_{i}(\ell)}{N}\right)=\frac{r}{N}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\left\{(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2}:\delta\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right)=\frac{r}{N}\right\},

so in all of
A
there are

d\cdot\#\left\{(k,\ell)\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{2}:\delta\left(\frac{k}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right)=\frac{r}{N}\right\}

such entries. This finishes the proof.
∎

For an
N
-point Latin hypercube set with a single distance vector to exist in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
one thus needs to fulfill the divisibility condition

\displaystyle\frac{N-1}{\gcd(2,N-1)}\bigg|d,

(2.3)

in particular for a given dimension
d
they can only exist up to
N\leq 2d+1
points.

2.3
Latin hypercube sets with a single distance vector as energy minimizers

The following is the main energy minimization result of this paper. It states that as long as
\log f
is
N
-equispacing, then an
N
-point Latin hypercube set with a single distance vector
X\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
minimizes the corresponding tensor product energy
E_{F}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#X=N
be a Latin hypercube set with a single distance vector. Let
f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a potential, such that the potential
g=\log f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
N
-equispacing. Then

E_{F}(Y)\geq E_{F}(X)

for all
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#Y=N
.

According to Proposition
2.1
, the result of Theorem
2.3
can be applied if
\log f
is decreasing and convex (i.e.
f
is logarithmically convex) as a function of geodesic distance or if
\log f(t)
is absolutely monotone when rewritten as a function of
s=\cos(2\pi t)
, see e.g.
[
20
]
for more information on logarithmic absolute monotonicity.

Thus, as mentioned earlier, this result can be viewed as an analogue of the classical universal optimality results on spheres and projective spaces
[
10
,
12
]
in the sense that one configuration minimizes the energy for a large class of potentials. In fact, in
[
10
]
universal optimality was shown using the linear programming method for
sharp configurations
, i.e. sets which are spherical designs of high order and have few pairwise distances. The sets with a single distance vector have an extreme version of the latter property in the tensor product sense.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
Y=\{\mathbf{y}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#Y=N
. Since the exponential function is convex, we can apply Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle E_{F}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f(y_{i}^{k}-y_{i}^{\ell})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}g(y_{i}^{k}-y_{i}^{\ell})\right)

\displaystyle\geq(N^{2}-N)\exp\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}-N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}g(y_{i}^{k}-y_{i}^{\ell})\right).

By assumption, for all
i=1,\dots,d
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}g(y_{i}^{k}-y_{i}^{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}g\left(\frac{k}{N}-\frac{\ell}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2N\sum_{r=1}^{(N-1)/2}g\left(\frac{r}{N}\right),&N\text{ odd},\\
2N\sum_{r=1}^{(N-2)/2}g\left(\frac{r}{N}\right)+Ng\left(\frac{N}{2}\right),&N\text{ even}\end{cases}

and by Lemma
2.2
thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}-N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}g(y_{i}^{k}-y_{i}^{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{N-1}\begin{cases}2\sum_{k=1}^{(N-1)/2}g\left(\frac{k}{N}\right),&N\text{ odd},\\
2\sum_{k=1}^{(N-2)/2}g\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)+g\left(\frac{N}{2}\right),&N\text{ even}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\#\left\{i=1,\dots,d:\rho_{i}=\frac{r}{N}\right\}\cdot g\left(\frac{r}{N}\right),

where
\{(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{d})\}=\operatorname{dv}(X)
is the unique distance vector in
X
. In total we get

E_{F}(Y)\geq(N^{2}-N)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}g(\rho_{i})\right)=E_{F}(X)

by (
2.2
), finishing the proof.
∎

2.5
Classification of lattices with a single distance vector

In this section we will classify all lattices with a single distance vector. For small values of
N
this can be checked straightforwardly. Note that for each
N=2,3,4
there is, up to torus symmetry, only one lattice in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
N
points: the corresponding generating vector is

\left(\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}\right).

The lattices with
N=2,3
points are easily seen to have a single distance vector, while the case of
N=4
defines precisely two distance vectors.

Theorem 2.8
.

A lattice
\Lambda\subseteq[0,1)^{d},\#\Lambda=N\geq 3
has a single distance vector if and only if
N
is prime and the generating vector of
\Lambda
is of the form (up to torus symmetry)

\bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\underbrace{\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{2}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{\frac{n}{N},\dots,\frac{n}{N}}_{m\text{ times}}\bigg)

with
N=2n+1
and
d=mn
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
be a lattice with a single distance vector such that
\#\Lambda=N\geq 3
. By applying torus symmetries we may assume the generating vector to have the form

\mathbf{h}=\left(\frac{h_{1}}{N},\dots,\frac{h_{d}}{N}\right)

with
1=h_{1}\leq\dots\leq h_{d}\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
. In particular, the distance vector among points in
\Lambda
must be of the form

\operatorname{dv}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{h})=\left(\frac{h_{1}}{N},\dots,\frac{h_{d}}{N}\right),

by Lemma
2.2
thus

\left(\frac{h_{1}}{N},\dots,\frac{h_{d}}{N}\right)=\bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\underbrace{\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{2}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{\frac{n}{N},\dots,\frac{n}{N}}_{m\text{ times}}\bigg)

with
n=\frac{N-1}{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
m=\frac{2d}{N-1}
. Furthermore,
N
must be relatively prime to
1,\dots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, so that it must already be prime itself.

It remains to show that any lattice with generating vector

\bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\underbrace{\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{2}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{\frac{n}{N},\dots,\frac{n}{N}}_{m\text{ times}}\bigg)

with
d=mn
and
N=2n+1
prime is a lattice with a single distance vector. Indeed, for an arbitrary
k\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
consider the distance vector from
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)
to

\mathbf{x}^{k}=\left(\left\{\frac{h_{1}k}{N}\right\},\dots,\left\{\frac{h_{d}k}{N}\right\}\right)

with
h_{1}=\dots=h_{m}=1,h_{m+1}=\dots=h_{2m}=2,\dots,h_{(n-1)m+1}=\dots=h_{nm}=n
. We will show that this distance vector does not depend on
k
. If there were
h,\tilde{h}\in\{1,\dots,n\},h\neq\tilde{h}
with

\left\{\frac{hk}{N}\right\}=\left\{\frac{\tilde{h}k}{N}\right\},

then we would either get

hk\equiv\tilde{h}k\mod N\quad\Rightarrow\quad h=\tilde{h}

or

hk\equiv N-\tilde{h}k\mod N\quad\Rightarrow\quad(h+\tilde{h})k\equiv 0\mod N\quad\Rightarrow\quad h+\tilde{h}=N,

both leading to contradictions since
h\neq\tilde{h}
and
h+\tilde{h}\leq 2n<N
. Thus

\operatorname{dv}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}^{k})=\bigg(\underbrace{\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\underbrace{\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{2}{N}}_{m\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{\frac{n}{N},\dots,\frac{n}{N}}_{m\text{ times}}\bigg)

and by the lattice structure this is also the distance vector between any other two point from the lattice.
∎

We see that if
2d+1
is prime, the bound on
N
mentioned after (
2.3
) can be attained by such a lattice construction.

3
Energy minimizers have distinct coordinates

In the present section we demonstrate that, under very mild assumptions on the function
f
, both global and local energy minimizers must necessarily have distinct coordinates. For example, in the two-dimensional case
\mathbb{T}^{2}
this implies no two points in the minimizing configuration can lie on the same horizontal or vertical line. This brings us one step closer to understanding when permutation sets or Latin hypercube sets minimizer tensor product energies. The result is formalized in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be continuous,
1
-periodic, even and satisfy the following assumptions:

(i)

f(t)
is differentiable on
(0,1)
,

(ii)

there are constants
C>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
f(t)\leq f(0)-Ct
for all
0\leq t<\varepsilon
.

Consider an
N
-point configuration
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that
x^{0}_{1}=\dots=x^{j}_{1}
for some
j\geq 1
and
x^{k}_{1}\neq x^{0}_{1}
for all
k>j
(if such
k
exist). Define a perturbation of
X
by

X_{r}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}^{0}(r),\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\},

where
\mathbf{x}^{0}(r)=(x_{1}^{0}+r,x_{2}^{0},\dots,x_{d}^{0})
for
r\in[-1/2,1/2)
. Then the function

r\mapsto E_{F}(X_{r})

does not have a local minimium at
r=0
.

As a direct consequence we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
and let
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
be a (local) minimizer of the corresponding energy
E_{F}
. Then

x_{i}^{k}\neq x_{i}^{\ell}

for all
i=1,\dots,d
and
k,\ell=0,1,\dots,N-1,k\neq\ell
.

That is, points in minimizing configurations must necessarily have distinct coordinates.

Remark
.

Assumption (ii) in Theorem
3.1
(which means that
f
has a sharp kink at
t=0
) can be interpreted as saying that the repulsion induced by the interaction
F
is quite strong at small distances in each coordinate.
We observe that if
f
is singular at zero, i.e.
f(0)=+\infty
, the statement of Corollary
3.2
would have been obvious. It is a known phenomenon
[
8
]
that when the local repulsion is weak, then minimizers of energies tend to cluster. Thus, this result can be viewed as complimentary: minimizers do not cluster when the local repulsion is strong.

Observe that assumption (ii) is satisfied by various natural kernels, in particular the aforementioned
f(t)=1/2-t+t^{2}
for
t\in[0,1]
which corresponds to the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy, or more generally the QMC potential (
1.4
) for
0<p<24
(where it stays positive).

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we begin with a simple observation: if a function has a downwards-opening kink, then it cannot have a local minimum there.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function with
G(r)\leq G(0)-C|r|
in
-\varepsilon<r<\varepsilon
for some
C>0
. Let
H:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be differentiable at
r=0
. Then
r\mapsto G(r)+H(r)
does not have a local minimum at
r=0
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Without loss of generality let
x^{0}_{1}=\dots=x^{j}_{1}=0
be the common coordinate. Consider the perturbation
\mathbf{x}^{0}(r)=(r,x_{2}^{0},\dots,x_{d}^{0})
while keeping all other coordinates fixed. We separate the terms of
E_{F}(X_{r})
that depend on
r
and treat the rest as a constant
K_{1}>0

\displaystyle E_{F}(X_{r})=

\displaystyle f(r)\cdot 2\sum_{k=1}^{j}\prod_{i=2}^{d}f(x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{k})+2\sum_{k=j+1}^{N-1}f(r-x_{1}^{k})\prod_{i=2}^{d}f(x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{k})+K_{1}.

Let

G(r)=f(r)\cdot 2\sum_{k=1}^{j}\prod_{i=2}^{d}f(x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{k})

and

H(r)=2\sum_{k=j+1}^{N-1}f(r-x_{1}^{k})\prod_{i=2}^{d}f(x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{k})+K_{1}.

By (i) and since
x_{1}^{k}\neq 0
for
k>j
we have that
H(r)
is differentiable at
r=0
. Also note that

K_{2}=2\sum_{k=1}^{j}\prod_{i=2}^{d}f(x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{k})>0

is a positive constant since
f
only takes on positive values and
j\geq 1
so that the sum is nonempty. Hence (ii) implies that

G(r)\leq K_{2}(f(0)-C|r|)=G(0)-CK_{2}|r|

in a neighborhood around
r=0
. Thus
G
and
H
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
, hence
E_{F}(X_{r})=G(r)+H(r)
does not have a local minimum in
r=0
.
∎

4
Permutation type and winding number

In this section we show that lattices minimize tensor product energies with logarithmically convex potential
f
among large classes of sets which share structural similarities with the lattice. In particular, lattices are local minimizers of such energies. To this end, we prove a lower bound on the energy in terms of the permutation type of the configuration.

Given a point set in
\mathbb{T}^{d}\simeq[0,1)^{d}
, we can always assume that the
x_{1}
-coordinates are ordered, i.e.
x_{1}^{k}\leq x_{1}^{\ell}
for
k<\ell
. However the same need not hold for the other coordinates. Instead when
d\geq 2
we can order the points by one of the other coordinates and obtain a permutation that relates this ordering to our original ordering. We obtain the following definition:

Definition 4.1
.

For a given point set
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}\simeq[0,1)^{d}
, suppose
S=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of permutations of
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
satisfying

x_{i}^{\sigma^{-1}_{i}(0)}\leq x_{i}^{\sigma^{-1}_{i}(1)}\leq\dots\leq x_{i}^{\sigma^{-1}_{i}(N-1)}

for all
i
. Then we say
S
is a
permutation type
of
X
.

For example, we note that the Latin hypercube set
X_{\sigma_{2},...,\sigma_{d}}
given in Definition
1.2
has permutation type
(\text{id},\sigma_{2},...,\sigma_{d})
. We will generally assume that the first coordinates are ordered, i.e.
\sigma_{1}
is the identity permutation, and by translating so that
\mathbf{x}^{0}=(0,0,...,0)
we will also assume that
\sigma_{i}(0)=0
for all
i
. The permutation type is uniquely defined if and only if all points have distinct coordinates in the sense of §
3
. For any permutation
\sigma
we will define the
k
-th winding number
associated with
\sigma
by

\omega_{\sigma}(k):=\#\{j:\sigma((j+k)\textup{ mod }N)<\sigma(j)\}.

For notational simplicity, input values outside of the domain of
\sigma
will be implicitly assumed to be taken modulo
N
. Conceptually, this quantity counts how many times the permutation winds around the torus, moving along it with step size
k
. With this definition we are now ready to state the lower bound for the energy.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume the configuration
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
has unique permutation type
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d})
. Then the following inequality holds for all potentials
f
decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
:

E_{F}(\mathbf{x}^{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1})\geq N\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f\left(\frac{\omega_{\sigma_{i}}(k)}{N}\right).

If
f
is strictly decreasing, equality is obtained if and only if the point set
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1}\}
is a lattice.

Proof.

By regrouping the terms, the energy can be written in the form

E_{F}(\mathbf{x}^{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}F(\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k+\ell}),

(4.1)

if we identify
\mathbf{x}^{k+\ell}=\mathbf{x}^{k+\ell-N}
in the case
k+\ell>N-1
. For
x,y\in[0,1)\simeq\mathbb{T}
we define

\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x,y)=\begin{cases}y-x,&y\geq x,\\
1+y-x,&y<x,\end{cases}

which can be thought of as the
counterclockwise
geodesic distance. Note that this need not always equal the geodesic distance
\delta
defined in (
1.3
), but the periodicity and evenness of
f
implies that the value of
f
is independent of the choice between these two distances as its argument. Thus we rewrite

F\left(\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k+\ell}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}f\left(x_{i}^{k}-x_{i}^{k+\ell}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}f\left(\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k+\ell})\right).

Since
f
is logarithmically convex and decreasing on
(0,1/2]
, it is log-convex on
(0,1)
, and hence
the function
F
is convex as a function of
d
variables on
(0,1)^{d}
. Therefore, we can apply Jensen’s inequality to the inner sum in the right-hand side of (
4.1
) and we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{F}(\mathbf{x}^{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}F\left(\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{1}^{k},x_{1}^{k+\ell}),\dots,\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{d}^{k},x_{d}^{k+\ell})\right)\\
&\geq N\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}F\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{1}^{k},x_{1}^{k+\ell}),\dots,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{d}^{k},x_{d}^{k+\ell})\right)\\
&=N\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k+\ell})\right).\end{split}

(4.2)

Observe that
\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k+\ell})=1+x_{i}^{k+\ell}-x_{i}^{k}
if and only if

x_{i}^{k+\ell}<x_{i}^{k}
, i.e.
x_{i}^{\sigma^{-1}_{i}(\sigma_{i}(k+\ell))}<x_{i}^{\sigma^{-1}_{i}(\sigma_{i}(k))}
, which is equivalent to
\sigma_{i}(k+\ell)<\sigma_{i}(k)
. This occurs for
\omega_{\sigma_{i}}(\ell)
values of
k
(note that unique permutation type means that we can ignore the case of equality between coordinates). Otherwise it equals
x_{i}^{k+\ell}-x_{i}^{k}
. Telescoping the sum, we conclude that

\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k+\ell})={\omega_{\sigma_{i}}(k)},

and thus, as desired,

E_{F}(\mathbf{x}^{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N-1})\geq N\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f\left(\frac{\omega_{\sigma_{i}}(k)}{N}\right).

Finally, we note that there are two cases for equality in Jensen’s inequality in (
4.2
): either
f
is linear on the relevant domain (which cannot be the case if
f
is strictly decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
) or the argument of
F
is independent of
k
, i.e.

\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k}-x_{i}^{k+\ell})=\delta_{\circlearrowleft}(x_{i}^{k^{\prime}}-x_{i}^{k^{\prime}+\ell})\text{ for all }i,\ell,k,k^{\prime},

which by their periodic structure is fulfilled by lattices. Conversely, if this condition is satisfied we note that since a unique permutation type means none of the coordinates coincide, the
\ell=1
case implies that our configuration is a lattice. This proves the desired equivalence.∎

We immediately obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 4.3
.

Assume that the potential
f
is decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
be an
N
-point integration lattice, and let
Y\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
be an
N
-point configuration with the same permutation type as
X
. Then

E_{F}(Y)\geq E_{F}(X).

In other words, lattices minimize the energy among all sets with the same permutation type. Observing that a small perturbation of a lattice does not change the permutation type, we arrive directly at local optimality.

Corollary 4.4
.

If a point set
X
is a lattice and
f
is decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
, then
X
is a local minimum of
E_{F}
.

The last two corollaries exhibit an example of the behavior discussed in the introduction: certain permutation sets (namely, lattices) minimize the energy among large classes of more general (non-permutation) sets.

Finally we note that the case
N=3
can also be addressed by these methods because this case has only two permutation types in each coordinate, and all permutation sets in this case are lattices (up to the symmetries of the torus). Thus a special case of Corollary
2.7
when
f
is logarithmically convex, can be obtained as a consequence of Corollary
4.3
.

5
The cases
N=4
and
N=6
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}

In the setting of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the cases
N=4
and
N=6
are unique: the only possible lattices are the identity lattice that places all points on
y=x
, and its reflection that places all points on
y=1-x
. However, unlike in the case
N=3
(which like
N=5
is covered by Corollary
2.7
) there are other permutation sets that are not one of these two cases.

We first observe that the permutation set whose permutation type is the identity cannot strictly minimize the energy, even among permutation sets.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a potential.
Then, for all permutations
\sigma
of
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
,
we have

E_{F}(X_{\operatorname{id}})\geq E_{F}(X_{\sigma}),

where
\operatorname{id}
denotes the identity permutation and
X_{\sigma}
is a permutation set with permutation type
\sigma

(see (
1.6
)).

Proof.

The energy of
X_{\sigma}
takes the form

E_{F}(X_{\sigma})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=0\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{N-1}f\left(\frac{k-\ell}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{\sigma(k)-\sigma(\ell)}{N}\right).

As multisets we have

\displaystyle\left\{f\left(\frac{k-\ell}{N}\right):k,\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\},k\neq\ell\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{f\left(\frac{\sigma(k)-\sigma(\ell)}{N}\right):k,\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\},k\neq\ell\right\},

by the rearrangement inequality
[
21
]
the energy
E_{F}
is thus maximized if

f\left(\frac{k-\ell}{N}\right)=f\left(\frac{\sigma(k)-\sigma(\ell)}{N}\right)

for all
k,\ell
, which is the case for the identity permutation.
∎

Thus, for
N=4
and
6
, candidates for energy minimizers must be something other than a lattice. We shall consider these cases in detail.

5.1
The
N=4
case

In the case
N=4
, up to symmetries of the torus, there is only one other permutation set besides the identity permutation, namely

\displaystyle X^{(4)}\coloneqq\{(0,0),(1/4,1/2),(1/2,1/4),(3/4,3/4)\},

(5.1)

which by Proposition
5.1
minimizes the energy among all
4
-point permutation sets. If
f
is differentiable on
(0,1)
, one can perform gradient descent to approach a local minimizer. Following off of an observation in
[
23
]
we claim that with appropriate convexity conditions this will actually yield a global minimizer, and we present a simple condition how to determine it.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
f
be differentiable on
(0,1)
, as well as decreasing and logarithmically convex on
(0,1/2]
. Then there exists an
a\in(0,1/2)
such that the
4
-point configuration

X_{a}^{(4)}\coloneqq\{(0,0),(a,1/2),(1/2,a),(1/2+a,1/2+a)\}

is a minimizer of
E_{F}
. This value of
a
satisfies

f^{\prime}(a)f(1/2)=f^{\prime}(1/2-a)f(1/2-a).

(5.2)

If
f
is strictly logarithmically convex, this value of
a
is unique.

We would like to point out that in
[
23
]
such a set was obtained as the energy minimizer of
E_{F}
with
f(t)=\frac{1}{2}-t+t^{2}
(Fig.
2
) using a proof assisted with numerics. Proposition
5.2
provides a simple way to find such minimizers for a wide range of potentials and admits a transparent proof, which we now present.

Proof.

We note that
f
being logarithmically convex results in the tensor product potential
F
being convex on
(0,1)\times(0,1)
, see
[
31
]
. This means that as a function of
\mathbf{x}^{i}
and
\mathbf{x}^{j}
, the expression
F(\mathbf{x}^{i}-\mathbf{x}^{j})
is convex when the order of the
x
- and
y
-coordinates of the two points is fixed. Since the sum of convex functions is convex, we conclude that
E_{F}
is convex as a function of the coordinates of our three unfixed points (we assume
\mathbf{x}^{0}=(0,0)
) when we restrict it to point configurations of a specific permutation type. Viewing the coordinates of our three unfixed points as an element of
[0,1)^{6}
, we observe that a convex combination of two element in this space that are of the same permutation type will result in another element of the same permutation type. Therefore the set of all configurations of the same permutation type is a convex set and thus any local minimizer of
E_{F}
in this set is a minimizer among all point sets of the same permutation type.

Figure 2:
The best
4
-point permutation set and the energy minimizer
X_{a}^{(4)}
for the potential
f(t)=\frac{1}{2}-t+t^{2}
. Here,
a=0.22669\dots
is the solution to the cubic equation
2a^{3}+a-\frac{1}{4}=0
.

From Corollary
4.3
we know that the identity lattice minimizes the energy among sets of the same permutation type, however by Proposition
5.1
it will not be a (unique) minimizer among all
4
-point configurations in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Considering torus symmetries, we need to check only one other permutation type, namely the one of
X_{a}^{(4)}
. Thus, we only need to show that, for
a
satisfying (
5.2
),
X_{a}^{(4)}
is stationary with respect to
E_{F}
in order to demonstrate that it is an energy minimizer. For this, we consider the gradient of the energy functional
E_{F}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{x}^{3})
in terms of the
6
variables
\mathbf{x}^{1}=(x_{1}^{1},x_{2}^{1})
,
\mathbf{x}^{2}=(x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2})
,
\mathbf{x}^{3}=(x_{1}^{3},x_{2}^{3})
. Taking the partial derivative with respect to any of these
6
coordinates, we see that it is
0
if and only if (
5.2
) holds. We thus conclude that
X_{a}^{(4)}
is a minimizer. We know such an
a
exists because

E_{F}(X_{a}^{(4)})=4f(a)f(1/2)+2f(1/2-a)^{2}

is a convex function in
a
and thus must have a minimum (the cases of
a=0
and
a=1/2
are not minimizers by Corollary
3.2
). If
f
is strictly logarithmically convex then
E_{F}(X_{a}^{(4)})
is a strictly convex function of
a
and has a unique minimizer, so (
5.2
) is satisfied by only one value of
a
.
∎

With this result we reduce the complexity of the problem down to a single variable, making it much more approachable for potentials satisfying the given condition. If we consider the potential given in (
1.4
), for example, we find that the energy minimizer is
X_{a}^{(4)}
with
a=a(p)
satisfying the cubic

\frac{p^{2}}{2}a^{3}+2p\left(1-\frac{p}{24}\right)a-\frac{p}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{24}\right)=0.

It can be noted that for
0<p\leq 6
, where (
1.4
) is logarithmically convex, we have
|a(p)-\frac{1}{4}|<0.024
. This phenomenon, that global optimizers do not stray too far from permutation sets, was first observed in
[
23
]
. Figure

shows the global minimizer for the concrete potential of the periodic

-discrepancy, equivalently
p=6
above.

Remark 5.3
.

We finish this subsection by noting that the permutation set (
5.1
), i.e.
X_{1/4}^{(4)}
, will only satisfy the condition given in (

) if

, in which case it will also yield the same energy as the identity permutation. Thus, for potentials that satisfy the conditions of Proposition

, there cannot be a unique energy minimizer that is a permutation set.

5.2
The

In the

.

Assume

is differentiable on
(0,1)
, as well as decreasing and logarithmically convex on

. Then there exists

\displaystyle X_{a}^{(6)}\coloneqq\{

is a minimizer of

among all
6

f^{\prime}(a)f(1/2-a)+f^{\prime}(2a)f(2a)=f^{\prime}(1/2-a)f(a)+f^{\prime}(1/2-2a)f(1/2).

(5.3)

If

Proof.

Appendix A
A collection of potentials

In this section we collect some potentials which are relevant in the context of tensor product energy. Natural examples arise from discrepancy theory, quasi-Monte Carlo integration and harmonic analysis. FWe also give other examples which might shed light on optimal point configurations.

Quasi-Monte Carlo integration.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and let
a_{-1},a_{0},a_{1},\dots,

a_{s}\geq 0
be parameters with
a_{s}>0
and
a_{-1}+a_{0}>0
. Let
H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T})
be the Sobolev space of periodic functions
f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f^{(k)}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T})
for all weak derivatives of order
k=0,1,\dots,s
, equipped with the norm (which is a norm by the assumptions on the parameters
a_{i}
)

\|f\|_{H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T})}^{2}\coloneqq a_{-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x)\,\text{d}x\right)^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{s}a_{k}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{(k)}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}x.

This turns
H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T})
into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel given by

K_{(}x,y)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}m(x-y))}{a_{-1}\delta_{m,0}+\sum_{k=0}^{s}a_{k}(2\pi m)^{2k}}.

For
d
dimensions we simply take a
d
-fold tensor product
H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T}^{d})=H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T})\otimes\dots\otimes H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T})
, whose kernel can be given by

K_{^{d}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}K_{(}x_{i},y_{i}).

Spaces like these are sometimes also called
Korobov spaces

[
13
,
15
,
18
,
26
]
, although note that some authors use this term for different spaces
[
16
,
24
,
25
]
. For
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#X=N
the worst case error of the quasi-Monte Carlo rule can be determined to be
[
30
]

\sup_{\|f\|_{H^{s}_{(}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}\mathbf{x}\right|^{2}=-\frac{1}{(a_{-1}+a_{0})^{d}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}K_{^{d}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Minimizing the worst case error is thus equivalent to minimizing

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X\\
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}K_{^{d}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

which, since the kernel is of the form
K_{^{d}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}f_{(}x_{i}-y_{i})
, can be interpreted as a tensor product energy. A particular example would be, for
p>0
a parameter,
a_{-1}=1
,
a_{s}=p^{-1}
and
a_{0}=a_{1}=\dots=a_{s-1}=0
, for which the kernel is of the form

K_{(}x,y)=1+(-1)^{s-1}\frac{p}{(2s)!}B_{2s}(x-y),

where
B_{2s}
is a Bernoulli polynomial of degree
2s
, given by

\displaystyle B_{2s}(t)=-(2s)!\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}mt)}{(2\pi\mathrm{i}m)^{2s}}.

(A.1)

The case of
s=1
and
p\in\{1,6\}
was considered in
[
23
]
. More generally, these potentials were studied for
s=1
in
[
29
]
. Similar but slightly different potentials were considered in
[
7
]
.

(Smooth) Discrepancy.

Discrepancy quantifies how uniform a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
is in a geometric way. We describe the definition here and show its relation to the quasi-Monte Carlo method. Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and for
x\in\mathbb{R}
define the B-splines

B_{0,1}^{1}(x)\coloneqq\chi_{[0,1)}(x)

and recursively

B_{0,1}^{s}(x)\coloneqq\int_{x-1}^{x}B_{0,1}^{s-1}(u)\,\text{d}u=\int_{0}^{1}B_{0,1}^{s-1}(x-u)\,\text{d}u.

Note that
\operatorname{supp}B_{0,1}^{s}=[0,s]
. With
(x)_{+}\coloneqq\max\{0,x\}
we even have explicitly

B_{0,1}^{s}(x)=\frac{1}{(s-1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{s}(-1)^{k}{s\choose k}(x-k)_{+}^{s-1}.

For
0\leq\alpha<1
and
0<\delta\leq 1
set

B_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\coloneqq B_{0,1}^{s}\left(\frac{x-\alpha}{\delta}\right)=\delta^{-1}\int_{0}^{\delta}B_{\alpha,\delta}^{s-1}(x-u)\,\text{d}u

with
\operatorname{supp}B_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}=[\alpha,\alpha+s\delta]
. For
x\in[0,1)\simeq\mathbb{T}
introduce the periodization

\tilde{B}_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}B_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(k+x)=\delta^{-1}\int_{0}^{\delta}\tilde{B}_{\alpha,\delta}^{s-1}(x-u)\,\text{d}u,

where
x-u\in\mathbb{T}
with wrap-around on the torus. Finally, let

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\coloneqq\delta^{s-1}\tilde{B}_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s-1}(x-u)\,\text{d}u.

(A.2)

It is easy to verify that

•

\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{1}(x)=\chi_{[\alpha,\{\alpha+\delta\})}(x)
in the sense of periodic intervals
[
22
]
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part,

•

\varphi_{\alpha,1}^{s}(x)\equiv 1
and

•

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\,\text{d}x=\delta^{s}
.

Let us collect some facts about these functions
\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}
.

Lemma A.1
.

We have the Fourier representation (with convergence in
L_{2}
)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)=\delta^{s}+\sum_{m\neq 0}\left(\frac{1-\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}m}\right)^{s}\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}m(x-\alpha)).

(A.3)

Furthermore, for all
x,y\in[0,1)
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(y)\,\text{d}\delta\,\text{d}\alpha=\frac{1}{2s+1}+\frac{(-1)^{s-1}}{(s!)^{2}}B_{2s}(|x-y|).

(A.4)

Proof.

We prove (
A.3
) via induction on
s
. By periodicity we may assume
\alpha=0
. For
s=1
we have
\varphi_{0,\delta}^{1}(x)=\chi_{[0,\delta)}(x)
with Fourier coefficients

\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{0,\delta}^{1}(x)\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}mx)\,\text{d}x=\begin{cases}\delta,&m=0,\\
\frac{1-\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}m},&m\neq 0.\end{cases}

Assuming the formula holds for
s-1
, we have (using periodicity)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{0,\delta}^{s}(x)\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}mx)\,\text{d}x=\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{0,\delta}^{s-1}(x-u)\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}mx)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}mu)\,\text{d}u\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{0,\delta}^{s-1}(x)\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}mx)\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\delta^{s},&m=0,\\
\left(\frac{1-\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}m}\right)^{s},&m\neq 0.\end{cases}

This shows (
A.3
). We use this Fourier representation to show the second formula (
A.4
). Indeed, we can then write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(y)\,\text{d}\delta\,\text{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2s+1}+\sum_{m\neq 0}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{1-\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}m}\right|^{2s}\,\text{d}\delta\right)\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}m(x-y)).

Since for
t\in\mathbb{R}
it holds

|1-\exp(-\mathrm{i}t)|^{2}=-\exp(-\mathrm{i}t)(1-\exp(\mathrm{i}t))^{2},

for
t=2\pi m\delta
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|1-\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta)|^{2s}\,\text{d}\delta

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}\int_{0}^{1}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}sm\delta)(1-\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}m\delta))^{2s}\,\text{d}\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}(-1)^{s+k}{2s\choose k}\int_{0}^{1}\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}(k-s)m\delta)\,\text{d}\delta

\displaystyle={2s\choose s}.

This finally leads to the desired result (using (
A.1
))

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(x)\varphi_{\alpha,\delta}^{s}(y)\,\text{d}\delta\,\text{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2s+1}+{2s\choose s}\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}m(x-y))}{(2\pi m)^{2s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2s+1}+\frac{(-1)^{s-1}}{(s!)^{2}}B_{2s}(|x-y|).

∎

The
s
-smooth periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy
of a point set
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#X=N
will be defined as

L_{2,s}^{\text{per}}(X)^{2}\coloneqq\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\int_{(0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{\alpha_{i},\delta_{i}}^{s}(x_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{\alpha_{i},\delta_{i}}^{s}(x_{i})\,\text{d}\mathbf{x}\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\underline{\delta}\,\text{d}\underline{\alpha},

where
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
and
\underline{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{d})
. From Lemma
A.1
we conclude the following
Warnock-type formula
for periodic discrepancy (the case for
s=1
already appearing in
[

,

]
).

Theorem A.2
.

It holds

\displaystyle L_{2,s}^{\text{per}}(X)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(2s+1)^{d}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{2s+1}+\frac{(-1)^{s-1}}{(s!)^{2}}B_{2s}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)\right).

While the general equivalence of QMC and (smooth) discrepancy is well-known

Tensorized Poisson kernel.

The
Poisson kernel
with parameter
r\in(0,1)
is given by

P_{r}(t)=\sum_{m\neq 0}r^{|m|}\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}mt)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos(2\pi t)+r^{2}}

for
t\in\mathbb{T}
. Tensorization leads to the kernel

K^{d}_{P_{r}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d}P_{r}(x_{i}-y_{i}),

For fixed
r
this can be seen as a kernel over a Sobolev space of infinite dominating mixed smoothness (with certain exponential decay of the Fourier coefficients).
