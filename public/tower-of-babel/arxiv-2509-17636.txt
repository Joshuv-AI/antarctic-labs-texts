Whitening Spherical Gaussian Mixtures in the Large-Dimensional Regime
M. R. M. Boudjemaa, A. Kalle, X. Mai, J. H. de M. Goulart, C. Févotte

Abstract
Whitening is a classical technique in unsupervised learning that can facilitate estimation tasks by standardizing data. An important application is the estimation of latent variable models via the decomposition of tensors built from high-order moments. In particular, whitening orthogonalizes the means of a spherical Gaussian mixture model (GMM), thereby making the corresponding moment tensor orthogonally decomposable, hence easier to decompose. However, in the large-dimensional regime (LDR) where data are high-dimensional and scarce, the standard whitening matrix built from the sample covariance becomes ineffective because the latter is spectrally distorted. Consequently, whitened means of a spherical GMM are no longer orthogonal. Using random matrix theory, we derive exact limits for their dot products, which are generally nonzero in the LDR. As our main contribution, we then construct a corrected whitening matrix that restores asymptotic orthogonality, allowing for performance gains in spherical GMM estimation.

Introduction

Whitening is a classical technique in unsupervised learning and latent variable model estimation
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
.
Concretely, it exploits the latent geometry of the data by constructing a linear transformation that eliminates pairwise correlations and normalizes variances.
This elementary yet powerful idea leads to a significant simplification of certain estimation tasks.
For instance, whitening reduces independent component analysis (ICA) to the estimation of an orthogonal rotation of the whitened signals
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
.
Recent estimators of latent variable models based on decomposing tensors built from high-order moments also rely on whitening, as an intermediate step that makes tensor decomposition solvable by spectral methods
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
.

This strategy applies, in particular, to the estimation of spherical Gaussian mixture models (GMM)
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
. For concreteness, consider a spherical Gaussian mixture model (GMM) with
K
components, weights
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
(positive, summing to one), means
\{\bm{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and a common variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, so that an observation
{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
is distributed as

{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\sim\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{k},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P}).

(1)

As usual, one seeks to estimate the parameters
\sigma^{2}
and
\{(\omega_{k},\bm{\mu}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
from
N
i.i.d. samples
{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
. The uncentered covariance matrix reads

\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,\bm{\mu}_{k}\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P}=\mathbf{M}_{2}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P},

(2)

where
\mathbf{M}_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\bm{\mu}_{k}\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\top}
encodes the “inter-cluster” covariance.
Since
\mathbf{M}_{2}
carries information on the mixture structure,
it makes sense to whiten the data with respect to this covariance, which amounts to constructing from
\mathbf{\Sigma}
a
K\times P

whitening matrix

\mathbf{W}
such that
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
.
By construction, such a linear map performs two things at once: (i) dimensionality reduction, since it maps data onto the
K
-dimensional “signal” subspace; (ii) orthogonalization of the whitened means
{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{k}
(see Section
3
for more details).

Now, Hsu and Kakade showed in
[
5
]
that one can exploit third-order moments of
{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
to build an order-3 tensor
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
having a structure analogous to
\mathbf{M}_{2}
, that is,
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\otimes 3},
where
\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\otimes 3}:=\bm{\mu}_{k}\otimes\bm{\mu}_{k}\otimes\bm{\mu}_{k}.

This fact alone is relevant for estimation, since this decomposition of
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
into rank-one tensors is notoriously unique under mild constraints, unlike that of
\mathbf{M}_{2}
.
Yet, computing the above decomposition is generally challenging, and this is where whitening helps: after whitening the data, one obtains a corresponding (transformed) tensor which is
orthogonally decomposable
, namely

{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,(\mathbf{W}\,\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\otimes 3}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{-1/2}\,{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\otimes 3},

(3)

with
\{{\mathbf{v}}_{k}:=\omega_{k}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
orthonormal (as proven in
Section

3
).
Hence, the estimation problem reduces to an orthogonal tensor decomposition on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, and thus is solvable by stable spectral algorithms; see
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
. This whitening-plus-tensor-decomposition procedure can serve as a stand-alone estimator or as an initialization to a maximum likelihood estimation algorithm such as EM.

However, in practice the whitening matrix is built from the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}:=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top}
, which is not always an accurate estimate of
\mathbf{\Sigma}

[
8
]
.
This happens in particular in many modern applications where data are high-dimensional and (relatively) scarce, a situation which is formalized by the so-called
large-dimensional regime
(LDR), where both
P
(data dimension) and
N
(sample size) tend to infinity, with fixed
P/N=c\in(0,\infty)
. In this (common) regime, random matrix theory (RMT) models
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
as a low-rank “signal” perturbed by isotropic noise (a spiked model) and predicts that it is
spectrally distorted
: its eigenvalues follow a Marchenko-Pastur law and its leading eigenvectors are rotated away from the true ones
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
.
As a consequence, the whitening matrix
\mathbf{W}
built from
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

fails to orthogonalize the GMM component means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
, and the whitened tensor moment
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
loses its orthogonal structure, which in turn degrades tensor-based GMM estimators.

Our goal in this paper is twofold: (i) precisely quantifying the impact of these distortions on
the whitening step employed in these estimators, when operating in the LDR; (ii) proposing a corrected whitening based on RMT results for mitigating this impact. Specifically, we show that
standard whitening no longer orthogonalizes the GMM clusters’ means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
in the LDR, by giving exact closed-form limits of the dot products between whitened means. We then construct an RMT-corrected whitening operator that reweighs empirical eigenpairs to
restore orthogonality of the transformed means. Finally, as an illustration, we plug the corrected whitening into the
LearnGMM
algorithm of
[
5
]
, one of the simplest of tensor-based spherical GMM estimators.
Our numerical results clearly show the degradation of its performance in the LDR, and the improvement brought by our corrected whitening.

Model and assumptions

We will henceforth consider the spherical GMM specified in (

Assumptions.

We work under the following regime:

(A1)

Large-dimensional regime (LDR).
The sample number
N
and the dimension
P
grow large at the same rate with fixed
P/N=c\in(0,\infty)
.

To avoid trivial cases where estimation becomes either too easy or impossible in this regime, we also impose the following assumptions:

(A2)

Non-degeneracy of signal subspace.
We assume that
\rank(\mathbf{M}_{2})=K
for
\mathbf{M}_{2}
the low rank term in (
2
).

(A3)

Non-vanishing, non-degenerate components.
The variance
\sigma^{2}
, and the prior probabilities
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are all nonzero and constant with respect to
P
and
N
, for fixed
K
.

(A4)

Constant signal strength.
The norms of the GMM means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
and their alignments
\langle\bm{\mu}_{i},\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle
are assumed to be constant with respect to
N
and
P
.

Phase transition of sample covariance in the LDR.

Consider the following spectral decompositions for the covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}
given in (
2
) and its empirical version
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\top}
:

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\lambda_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf T},\quad\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf T},

(4)

where
\{(\lambda_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{P},\{(\hat{\lambda}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{P}
are respectively the eigenpairs of
\mathbf{\Sigma},\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{P},\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\hat{\lambda}_{P}
. Using (
2
), we get

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\sigma^{2}\ell_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf T}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P},\text{ with }\ell_{k}={(\lambda_{k}-\sigma^{2})}/\sigma^{2}.

(5)

Under (A4), well-known results from RMT indicate a phase transition occurs at
\sqrt{c}
for
\ell_{k}
: spikes with
\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}
generate outlier eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
and nonzero asymptotic eigenvector alignments between
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
; spikes with
\ell_{k}\leq\sqrt{c}
are absorbed by the Marchenko–Pastur distribution bulk and the alignments
\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\rangle
vanish
[
14
]
,
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1

(Spiked–covariance limits in the LDR; adapted from
[
14
]
,
[
10
]
)
.

Consider the Gaussian mixture model (
1
), and the uncentered covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}
defined in (
2
) with its eigenpairs
\{(\lambda_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{P}
and those
\{(\hat{\lambda}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{P}
of its empirical version
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
as specified in (
4
), and
\ell_{k}
as introduced in (
5
).
Then, under (A1)–(A4),

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{k}\;\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\;\tilde{\lambda}_{k}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}\}}\beta_{k}\lambda_{k}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{k}\leq\sqrt{c}\}}\,(1+\sqrt{c})^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{b}}\;\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}\}}\,\psi_{k}{\mathbf{a}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{b}},\quad\forall{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},

(7)

where
\beta_{k}=1+\frac{c}{\ell_{k}}
and

\psi_{k}=1-\frac{\beta_{k}-1}{\beta_{k}}\frac{1+\ell_{k}}{\ell_{k}}
.

Remark 1
.

Directly applying (
7
) to
{\mathbf{a}}={\mathbf{b}}={\mathbf{u}}_{k}
gives the asymptotic (squared) alignment between
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
:

{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}=(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k})^{2}\;\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\;\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}\}}\,\psi_{k}=:\zeta_{k}.

Remark 2
.

When
c>0
and
\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}
, one still has
\psi_{k}<1
, so empirical eigenvectors do not perfectly align with their population counterparts. In addition, sample eigenvalues are distorted:
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}=\beta_{k}\lambda_{k}
for recoverable spikes and
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}
when
\ell_{k}\leq\sqrt{c}
. Both these effects are precisely why whitening becomes ineffective in the LDR.

3
Whitening in large dimension

Population whitening matrix.

According to (
4
), the low-rank term
\mathbf{M}_{2}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
introduced in (
2
) admits the spectral decomposition
\mathbf{M}_{2}=\mathbf{U}_{K}\,\mathbf{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{U}_{K}^{\top}
where
{\mathbf{U}}_{K}:=[{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times K}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}=\diag(\lambda_{1}-\sigma^{2},\dots,\lambda_{K}-\sigma^{2})
.
Assumption (A1) implies that matrix
\mathbf{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is non-singular.
The population whitening matrix with respect to the inter-cluster covariance
\mathbf{M}_{2}
is properly defined as

\mathbf{W}\;:=\;\mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1/2}\,{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times P}
.
Indeed, with this definition,

\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{M}_{2}\,\mathbf{W}^{\top}\;=\;\mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1/2}\,{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}({\mathbf{U}}_{K}\,\mathbf{\Gamma}\,{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}){\mathbf{U}}_{K}\,\mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1/2}\;=\;\mathbf{I}_{K}.

Since
\mathbf{M}_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,\bm{\mu}_{k}\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\top}
, then
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\,(\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{k})(\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{K}.

Using the rescaled and whitened means
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\omega_{k}^{1/2}\,\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
introduced in (
3
), the previous identity rewrites as
\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}\;=\;\mathbf{I}_{K},
which means that the family
\{{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is orthonormal.
It follows that
\|\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{k}\|^{2}=\omega_{k}^{-1}
and
\langle\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
.

Whitening matrix estimation.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{2}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P}
(for some chosen variance estimator
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
).
Upon computing the spectral decomposition of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{2}
and keeping its
K
dominant eigenpairs,
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{2}\approx\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}\,\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}
, one can estimate the whitening matrix as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{-1/2}\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times P}
,
where the subscript
K
indicates that only the
K
leading eigenpairs are retained.
In the classical regime (
P
constant and
N\to\infty
),
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{W}
and the orthogonality between whitened means is (asymptotically) verified.
Yet, this property breaks down in the LDR, as seen next.

3.1
Residual alignment and phase transition

We now examine the loss of orthogonality between whitened means that arises in the LDR, as a consequence of Theorem
1
. Given the above estimate of the whitening matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
, the main quantity of interest is the empirical residual alignment between component means defined below:

\rho_{i,j}\ :=\ \frac{\big|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle\big|}{\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{i}\|\ \|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\|},

(8)

for
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
is built from a reduced spectral decomposition of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{2}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{P}
, it depends on the chosen variance estimator
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
.
In the classical regime, simply taking
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\hat{\lambda}_{p}
with any
p>K
yields a consistent estimator of
\sigma^{2}
. Yet, this no longer holds in the LDR where
\hat{\lambda}_{p}
with
p>K
are distributed according to the Marchenko-Pastur law on the support
[\sigma^{2}(1-\sqrt{c})^{2},\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}]

[
9
]
.
Nevertheless, using Assumptions (A1) and (A4) one can show that the estimator
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{NP}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\|^{2}
is consistent, even when the data are not centered.
Hence, in the following we consider that
\sigma^{2}
is consistently estimated, focusing only on the effect of the spectral distortion described by Theorem
1
.

Lemma 1

(Limit of residual alignment)
.

Assume (A1)–(A4) and consider the same notation as in Theorem
1
. Set
d_{k}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\ell_{k}\geq\sqrt{c}}\,\psi_{k}/(\tilde{\lambda}_{k}-\sigma^{2})=\mathbbm{1}_{\ell_{k}\geq\sqrt{c}}\,\psi_{k}/(\beta_{k}\lambda_{k}-\sigma^{2})
,

\mathbf{D}:=\diag(d_{1},\ldots,d_{K})
and
\mathbf{T}:=\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\,{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}
.
Then, we have

\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\ \langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle,

(9)

and, whenever both
\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i}\rangle>0
and
\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle>0
,

\rho_{i,j}\ \overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\ \rho^{\infty}_{i,j}:=\frac{\big|\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle\big|}{\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i}\rangle}\ \sqrt{\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle}}\,.

(10)

Proof.

Notice first that

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle=\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k})(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{j})}{\hat{\lambda}_{k}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}}.

The asymptotic limit of
(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k})(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{j})
is given by letting
{\mathbf{a}}=\bm{\mu}_{i},{\mathbf{b}}=\bm{\mu}_{j}
in (
7
). Retrieving also the limiting value of
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
from (
6
) and recalling that
\hat{\sigma}^{2}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\sigma^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\ell_{k}\geq\sqrt{c}}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k})({\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{j})}{\tilde{\lambda}_{k}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k})({\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{j})=\langle\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\mathbf{T}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}\rangle,

thus recovering (
9
).

3.2
Corrected whitening

Lemma
1
shows that the empirical whitening map
\hat{\mathbf{W}}
fails to orthogonalize the means in the LDR because both eigenvalues and eigenvector alignments are distorted according to (
6
)–(
7
), implying that the coordinates of the whitened means are biased by some scalar factors. However, these factors can be predicted from (
6
)–(
7
). Hence, one can perform the following “scalar” correction: replace each empirical eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
by a consistent estimator of
\lambda_{k}
and compensate for eigenvector alignment
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
shrinkage by dividing it by a consistence estimator of
\psi_{k}
. Similarly corrected estimators based on RMT results have been derived in other contexts (see, e. g.,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
).

Concretely, we proceed as follows. We may express
\lambda_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
as functions of
\ell_{k}
in (
6
), and then express
\ell_{k}
as a function of
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}
(when
\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}
), which is tantamount to choosing the positive root of
\ell_{k}^{2}+(1+c-\tilde{\lambda}_{k}/\sigma^{2})\ell_{k}+c=0
. By replacing
\sigma
by
\hat{\sigma}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}
by
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
, we obtain the expression of the corrected spike:

\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\,\rm c}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{k}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}-(1+c)\;+\;\sqrt{\Big(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{k}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}-(1+c)\Big)^{2}-4c}\right).

Then the corrected estimator of
\lambda_{k}
is

\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{\,\rm c}\;=\;\hat{\sigma}^{2}\,(1+\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\,\rm c})
.
Next, define the finite-
(P,N)
corrected versions of the overlap factors:

\hat{\beta}_{k}^{\,\rm c}\;=\;1+\frac{c}{\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\,\rm c}},\qquad\hat{\psi}_{k}^{\,\rm c}\;=\;1-\frac{\hat{\beta}_{k}^{\,\rm c}-1}{\hat{\beta}_{k}^{\,\rm c}}\,\frac{1+\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\,\rm c}}{\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\,\rm c}}.

Using these quantities, we can correct both eigenvalue inflation and eigenvector shrinkage via the diagonal scaling

\mathbf{\Phi}\;:=\;\diag\!\Bigg(\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\ell}_{1}^{\,\rm c}>\sqrt{c}\}}}{\sqrt{(\hat{\lambda}_{1}^{\,\rm c}-\hat{\sigma}^{2})\,\hat{\psi}_{1}^{\,\rm c}}},\;\ldots,\;\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\ell}_{K}^{\,\rm c}>\sqrt{c}\}}}{\sqrt{(\hat{\lambda}_{K}^{\,\rm c}-\hat{\sigma}^{2})\,\hat{\psi}_{K}^{\,\rm c}}}\Bigg),

by simply defining the
corrected whitening
matrix as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}:=\mathbf{\Phi}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{K\times P}
. These definitions directly lead to a proof of the following theorem, which is our main result.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic dot products under corrected whitening)
.

Assume (A1)–(A4) and consider the same notation as in Theorem
1
.
Let

\mathbf{H}:=\diag(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{1}>\sqrt{c}\}},\ldots,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{K}>\sqrt{c}\}})
.
Then, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

\big\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}\bm{\mu}_{j}\big\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\ \bm{\mu}_{i}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{U}}_{K}\,{\mathbf{H}}\,\mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1}{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\,\bm{\mu}_{j}.

In particular, if
\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}
for all
k=1,\ldots,K
, then

\big\langle\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}\bm{\mu}_{j}\big\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\ \big\langle\mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{i},\ \mathbf{W}\bm{\mu}_{j}\big\rangle=\omega_{i}^{-1}\,\delta_{ij}.

4
Application to spherical GMM

We now illustrate the impact of our results, and in particular of our corrected whitening matrix, on the performance of a tensor-based algorithm for spherical GMM estimation.
For concreteness, we will take the
LearnGMM
algorithm of
[
5
]
, which is arguably one of the simplest algorithms of this kind.
In
LearnGMM
, after the estimated whitening matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
is obtained from
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, a new set
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\}_{n=N+1}^{2N}
of
N
independent samples is required to estimate the whitened tensor moment
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W})
defined in (

). Letting
\bm{\xi}_{n}=\widehat{{\mathbf{W}}}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
for

\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}})
of
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W})
in

\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{n=N+1}^{2N}\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\otimes 3}\\
-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(\bar{\bm{\xi}}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes\bar{\bm{\xi}}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes\bar{\bm{\xi}}\Bigr),

(11)

where
\bar{\bm{\xi}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}\bm{\xi}_{n}

\mathbb{R}^{K}

One can then apply a spectral decomposition algorithm to

4.1
Corrected whitened third moment

In the LDR, the estimator
\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}},\widehat{\mathbf{W}})

11

{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W})

Nonetheless, we claim in the following that this property can be recovered by simply replacing the whitening matrix by our corrected version
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}
.
Specifically, define the corrected estimator
\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\,\rm c}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c})
by modifying the formula (
11
) as follows:

\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\,\rm c}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{n=N+1}^{2N}(\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\,\rm c})^{\otimes 3}\\
-\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{\rm c}\hat{\psi}_{k}^{\rm c}}\Bigl(\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\,\rm c}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\,\rm c}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\otimes\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\,\rm c}\Bigr),

(12)

where
\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\,\rm c}=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
and
\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\,\rm c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}\bm{\xi}_{n}^{\,\rm c}
.

Theorem 3

(Consistency of the corrected whitened third moment)
.

Assume (A1)–(A4) and consider the same notation as in Theorem
1
. If
\ell_{k}>\sqrt{c}
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, then

\widehat{{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\,\rm c}_{3}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c},\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\ {\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}),

where
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W})
is as defined by (
3
).

Remark 4
.

While consistency of the low-dimensional object
{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{3}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W})
is ensured by using
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\,\rm c}
, the component means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
are
not
consistently estimated because the signal subspace
\text{span}\{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{u}}_{K}\}

1

Still, it can be shown that our correction allows recovering their projection onto the estimated subspace
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{k}
, which is the best that can be achieved when whitening is used.

5
Conclusion

By exploiting results pertaining to spiked covariance matrix models coming from random matrix theory, we precisely characterized the loss of orthogonality of the whitened GMM means that takes place in the large-dimensional regime.
This result allowed us to propose a simple scalar correction that restores their asymptotic orthogonality, leading to a significant performance improvement, especially under low signal-to-noise ratio, as illustrated by our numerical experiments.
