Retrospective study comparing first- and new-generation proximal humerus locking plates in 152 proximal humerus fracture cases
Authors: Xhulio Prifti, Julien Chapleau, Emilie Sandman, Jonah Hébert-Davies, G-Yves Laflamme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to compare proximal humerus fracture outcomes in terms of stability and quality of reduction when using the first-generation plate and a new-generation plate.MethodsThis retrospective consecutive cohort study included patients with proximal humerus fractures operated between 2007 and 2023 in a level 1 adult trauma center. The inclusion criteria were fracture of the proximal third of the humerus with pre-operative, post-operative, and follow-up radiographs, operated with either one of the plates studied. Arthroplasty, nailing, and conservative management were excluded. Demographic data were collected from the trauma database, and radiological measurements were performed by an independent evaluator. The neck-shaft angle, calcar displacement, greater tubercle height, humeral head offset, and plate position were measured.ResultsOne hundred fifty-two patients met the inclusion criteria. Sixty-two percent were women (n = 95), with a mean of 59.5 years and an average follow-up time of 24 months. There were 123 first-generation plates and 29 new-generation plates. There was no difference in age, smoking, follow-up time, or initial reduction quality between groups. At the last follow-up, there was a significant difference in plate height. This is influenced by the varus collapse (plate will appear “higher”). The new-generation plate was lower (distance between the plate and the greater tubercle) than the first-generation plate, with 17.6 ± 5.2 mm vs. 13.7 ± 5.6 mm at the early post-operative x-ray, and 15.2 ± 4.3 mm vs. 11.4 ± 4.7 mm at the last radiograph (P < .001 in both cases). There was more secondary displacement of the neck-shaft angle in the first-generation group (9.1° vs. 4.5° [P = .018]). There were more men in the group with the new plate design, but no significant difference was found between sexes in the measured variables, including the measured displacement parameters (P > .192).ConclusionsThese medium-term outcomes showed an acceptable plate position with the new-generation plate and less secondary humeral head displacement.Keywords: Proximal humerus fracture, Humeral locking plate, Outcomes, Humeral fractures, Shoulder fracture, Bone plates

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) are the third most common fracture in the geriatric population after proximal femur and distal radius fractures. They represent 4-10% of all fractures with an annual incidence of 31-250 per 100,000 adults.5 They are mostly caused by low-energy trauma in the elderly and high-energy trauma in younger patients.

Most PHFs are treated conservatively, considering that nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures can heal properly and have good functional outcomes.13 Only 10-20% of them require surgical treatment,13 with several possible techniques to restore shoulder function, namely plate and screws, nails, and shoulder arthroplasty. Over the last 2 decades, the advent of the locking plate system has revolutionized the treatment of these fractures.4 Studies first started reporting on the use of first-generation locking plates for PHFs in 2003.22 The PHILOS plate by Synthes was available around 2001 in most of Europe and North America. There are numerous studies reporting good functional outcomes, as well as important technical pearls to prevent complications related to implant design like screw cutout and loss of reduction.1, 2, 3,8,14,16,18,19,21 Different designs were suggested over time to lower complication rates, such as increasing the number of calcar screws, different sizing options to account for different patient morphologies, blunt tip screws for the humeral head, and plate extensions to stabilize the posterior greater tubercle. Eventually, a new-generation plate, the Ortholoc plate, was created to include a locking system with more proximal blunt-tipped screws and a posterior wing to support the greater tubercle.

Nevertheless, there is still a lack of consensus about the best treatment algorithm for the surgical management of PHFs,11,14 and more specifically which type of plate to choose. Therefore, this study aimed to compare 2 specific types of plates to determine whether the newer generation plate was better than the older plate. The primary outcome was the radiographic stability of the reduction at the last follow-up, to compare between the first-generation and new-generation plates. The secondary outcome was the radiographic complication rate reported with each system. The main hypothesis is that the newer generation plate will show less varus collapse and less cutout.

Materials and methods

This retrospective case-control study included PHF patients treated surgically between 2007 and 2023 in a level 1 adult trauma center. The inclusion criteria were adult patients with fractures in the proximal third of the humerus, with full pre-operative, post-operative, and follow-up radiographs, operated on with a first-generation plate, the LCP Synthes plate (DePuy Synthes, West Chester, PA) or a new-generation plate, the Ortholoc SPS plate (Stryker, Portage, MI, USA). Exclusion criteria were patients who underwent conservative treatment, shoulder arthroplasty, or nailing and those treated for pathological fractures or without a complete x-ray imaging sequence.

The data were extracted from the institutional database, and radiographic measurements were made by 2 surgeons (X.P., J.C.) who did not perform any of the surgeries (neck-shaft angle, calcar displacement, greater tubercle height, humeral head offset, and plate position). The data collected were age at fracture, sex, side affected, Neer fracture classification, neck angulation, and complications (Figs. 1 and 2).

Radiographic measurement method

The neck-shaft angle was defined as the angle between a line through the center of the humeral head perpendicular to the anatomic neck plane and a line through the proximal humeral shaft axis.10 The calcar displacement was the distance in mm from the medial humeral neck and the corresponding part on the humeral diaphysis.

The greater tubercle height was the distance measured in mm of the tangential line from the highest point of the greater tubercle and the tangential line from the highest point of the humeral head.

The humeral head offset was the distance in mm between 2 parallel lines, one passing through the humeral shaft axis and the second one through the center of the humeral head.

Plate position was the distance in mm between the tangential lines passing through the highest point of the plate and the tangential line passing through the highest point of the humeral head in the anteroposterior x-ray view.

Malunion and malreduction were defined as a persistent displacement of 5 mm or more of the greater tubercle fragment, or a neck-shaft angle less than 120° or more than 140°.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics, including mean, range, and standard deviation, were used to define the cohort for continuous data. Categorical data were expressed in counts and percentages. Normal distribution of continuous data was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The median might be given in the case of asymmetric distribution. To compare both groups, the Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables and chi-square for dichotomous variables. Statistics were performed using Easymed stats and a P lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Data from 152 patients were analyzed. There were more women (W: 62% vs. M: 38%) with a mean age at the time of surgery of 59.5 ± 15.8 years. There were 110 low-energy trauma cases (falls from standing height), and 38 (26%) were involved in high-energy trauma. These data were missing from hospital records for 4 patients. Overall, 24 patients (16%) needed reoperation; 14 underwent surgery to remove fixation material, 3 for new reduction and osteosynthesis, 6 needed hemiarthroplasty, and only 1 needed reverse shoulder arthroplasty.

The new-generation plate was used in 29 patients, while the remaining 123 cases received the first-generation plate (81%). The mean follow-up for the entire cohort was 23.98 ± 24.6 months, and 9.6 ± 11.2 months for the new-generation plate and 27.4 ± 25.6 for the first-generation plate. When comparing the new- and first-generation groups, there was no significant difference in the overall complication rate; there was no difference in implant-related complications or other types of complications. Plate position was significantly different between groups. The new-generation plate was lower (distance between the plate and the greater tubercle) than the first-generation plate, with 17.6 ± 5.2 mm vs. 13.7 ± 5.6 mm at the early post-operative x-ray, and 15.2 ± 4.3 mm vs. 11.4 ± 4.7 mm at the last radiograph (P < .001 in both cases).

There was no significant difference in the cervical-diaphyseal angle at the first follow-up radiograph, with 132° ± 7.8° for the new-generation and 130° ± 15.5° for the first-generation (P = .416). However, neck-shaft angle was greater for the new-generation plate than the other plate at 1 year (133° ± 14.5° vs. 127° ± 7.8° [P = .009]).

Humeral head offset was also similar for both groups at the early post-operative evaluation (5.9 ± 4.3 for the first-generation and 5.6 ± 3.6 for the new-generation, P = .481), although it was lower for the new-generation plate at the last follow-up radiograph (4.6 ± 2.4 for the new-generation and 6.4 ± 4.3 for the first-generation, P = .017) (Table IV).

Discussion

With the aging population, the prevalence of PHFs is increasing worldwide, but the optimal treatment choice is still under debate, especially because post-operative complications are common, up to 40% in a recent study by Marsalli et al.20 Furthermore, studies report more complications in the aging population.7 Plate fixation is associated with complications related to plates and screws, secondary fracture displacement, and avascular necrosis.15 The first widely used plate was the PHILOS© plate by Synthes9,17 with adequate outcomes, although over time complications remained a concern. Plate and screw design are among the various factors that can have an impact on plate fixation, as reported in a systematic review by Zdero et al.23 These considerations have played a role in the new designs proposed by manufacturers.6,12 Among the proposed updates, the Ortholoc© plate increased the number of proximal screws, changed screw location in the humeral head, provided blunt-tipped screws, and 3 guides adapted to different patient bone sizes. Nevertheless, proximal humerus fracture management is challenging, with several factors coming into play, namely the surgical learning curve and plate position. Indeed, plate height can be affected by the contours of the plate and which area is the best fit on the proximal humerus. Moreover, with varus collapse, the plate may appear higher compared to the epiphysis.

The present study found that the 2 groups had similar outcomes in terms of complications. The other distinctive feature was that the new design allowed plates to be implanted lower, and which may translate in less subacromial impingement in abduction.

There are several limitations to this study, inherent to its retrospective design. First is the number of patients in each group, with many more in the older generation plate group. With time, more data will be available and provide a more complete portrait of the benefits and drawbacks of the new design. Second, there were significant differences in follow-up time and fracture severity, which could have biased the interpretation of the results. Indeed, older females are typically more represented in PHF studies. Nevertheless, a secondary analysis comparing males and females revealed no difference in fixation stability or complications. It should be noted that women in this study had a significantly smaller humeral head, and surgeons should consider this factor when choosing screw length. In terms of fracture severity, although the new plate group had more Neer 3 and 4 fractures, the neck-shaft angle was more stable.

Conclusion

These medium-term outcomes showed a better plate position with the new-generation plate, with less varus and less secondary humeral head displacement, and a small but significant change in neck-shaft angle with first-generation plates. An independent factor that still needs to be assessed is the clinical impact of these radiological differences.
