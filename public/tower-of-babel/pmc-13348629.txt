Effects of ensiling temperature on fermentation quality, enzyme activities, and metabolomic profiles in Pediococcus acidilactici L10-inoculated native grass silage
Authors: La Zhu, Yuting Yan, Jian Bao, Muqier Zhao, Zhijun Wang, Pengbo Sun, Xingquan Yan, Mingjian Liu, Rina Su, Gentu Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cold regions, the efficient preservation of native grass is often constrained by low temperatures during the ensiling process. This study investigated the fermentation quality, enzyme activity patterns, and metabolomic profiles of native grass silage inoculated with Pediococcus acidilactici L10 under varying temperature conditions. All silage samples were inoculated with Pediococcus acidilactici L10 and stored at three temperatures (25℃, 15℃, or 5℃), with three biological replicates per treatment. Integrated analyses were conducted over a 60-day period, including measurements of fermentation parameters and enzyme activities on days 3, 7, 15, 30, and 60, as well as untargeted metabolomic analysis on day 60. Ensiling temperature significantly influenced the fermentation characteristics of the L10-inoculated silage system. Compared with the 25℃ treatment, the 15℃ and 5℃ treatments exhibited lower final pH values, greater lactic acid accumulation during the mid-to-late fermentation stages, lower ammonia-nitrogen content, and higher residual water-soluble carbohydrate content. Enzyme activity analysis indicated that the activities of hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, pyruvate kinase, and lactate dehydrogenase activities varied with ensiling temperature and were associated with patterns of lactic acid production and carbohydrate utilization. Metabolomic analysis conducted on day 60 revealed temperature-associated differences in metabolites related to soluble carbohydrates, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism. In particular, the 15℃ treatment exhibited a more favorable overall profile among the tested temperatures within the L10-inoculated silage system, accompanied by higher relative abundances of D-galactose, stachyose, L-proline, and caprylic acid. Overall, these results indicate that ensiling temperature plays a critical role in shaping fermentation dynamics and metabolic profiles in L10-inoculated native grass silage, and provide a foundation for future studies evaluating inoculant performance under low-temperature conditions.Keywords: Native grass silage, Pediococcus acidilactici, Low-temperature ensiling, Metabolomics, Glycolytic enzymes, Fermentation quality

Introduction

Globally, the sustainable development of livestock production is increasingly constrained by resource limitations and environmental concerns [1]. The effective preservation and utilization of roughage, especially native pasture, are critical for reducing feed costs, ensuring a year-round balanced feed supply, and stabilizing livestock production systems [2]. First, native grasses typically harbor a low abundance of epiphytic lactic acid bacteria on their surfaces and possess complex, heterogeneous microbial communities with limited capacity to initiate fermentation. Consequently, it is difficult to rapidly establish a microbial consortium dominated by beneficial LAB during the early stage of ensiling [3]. Second, compared with dedicated silage crops such as corn, native grasses generally contain lower levels of fermentable substrates, particularly water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC), which further restrict LAB proliferation and acid production. As a result, acidification proceeds slowly, delaying the decline in silage pH [4, 5]. Together, these inherent characteristics increase the risk of inadequate fermentation in native grass silage, which may promote protein degradation and the proliferation of undesirable microorganisms. Silage fermentation is a key strategy for preserving the nutritional value of fresh forage, and its success depends largely on the rapid establishment of LAB-dominated microbial communities [5]. However, in cold regions or seasons, low environmental temperatures can markedly inhibit the activity of many commercial LAB inoculants [6, 7]. Such inhibition can delay fermentation initiation, impair fermentation completeness, increase dry matter losses, reduce nutritional quality, and, in severe cases, result in silage failure due to undesirable microbial activity [8]. Therefore, the development and application of silage additives suitable for low-temperature conditions, particularly LAB strains capable of supporting fermentation under such environments, are of practical importance for improving roughage utilization in high-latitude or high-altitude regions and supporting stable regional livestock production.

In recent years, microbial regulation of low-temperature silage fermentation has become a research focus in feed science. However, the physiological mechanisms by which LAB respond to cold-induced metabolic constraints during low-temperature ensiling remain poorly understood. In LAB metabolism, the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway is a central route for carbohydrate catabolism and lactic acid production. Within this pathway, hexokinase (HK), phosphofructokinase (PFK), and pyruvate kinase (PK) are key irreversible regulatory enzymes involved in glycolytic flux, whereas lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactic acid [9, 10]. Under low-temperature ensiling conditions, the activity patterns of these enzymes may be associated with differences in acidification dynamics and carbohydrate utilization [11]. To date, most studies have focused on endpoint silage quality, whereas information on the dynamic relationships among enzyme activity patterns, fermentation characteristics, and metabolite profiles under different ensiling temperatures remains limited.

Against this background, the present study aimed to compare the temperature-dependent fermentation characteristics, enzyme activity patterns, and metabolomic profiles of native pasture silage inoculated with Pediococcus acidilactici L10. The experiment included three ensiling temperatures (25℃, 15℃, and 5℃), and samples were collected after 3, 7, 15, 30, and 60 days of fermentation. Nutritional and fermentation quality indicators were measured, together with the dynamic activities of four glycolysis-related enzymes—HK, PFK, PK, and LDH—were dynamically monitored. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was further conducted on silage samples collected on day 60 to characterize endpoint metabolite differences in metabolite profiles among temperature treatments. The novelty of this work lies in the integration of time-course enzyme activity monitoring with untargeted metabolomics to describe temperature-associated biochemical and metabolic responses within the L10-inoculated native grass silage system. Because all experimental treatments were inoculated with L10, this study specifically investigated the effects of ensiling temperature on fermentation quality, enzyme activities, and metabolite profiles within the L10-inoculated silage system, rather than the independent efficacy of L10.

Materials and methods

Lactic acid bacteria strain

A LAB strain, Pediococcus acidilactici L10, was isolated from silage samples collected in Inner Mongolia. The strain was identified by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and the sequence was deposited in the NCBI GenBank database (Accession No. PZ213244). In addition, the strain has been deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) with the accession number CGMCC 34,259. For characterization purposes described in this section, the strain was cultured at 37℃ as detailed below.

The strain Pediococcus acidilactici L10 was inoculated into sterile MRS broth for growth and acid-production assays at 37℃. Acid‑production assay: A bacterial suspension of the test strain was prepared and inoculated into sterile MRS broth at an inoculum volume fraction of 3%. The culture was incubated at 37℃ for 24 h, and the pH was measured every 2 h. Growth assay: A bacterial suspension of the test strain was prepared and inoculated into sterile MRS broth at an inoculum volume fraction of 3%. The culture was incubated at 37℃ for 24 h; the optical density (OD) was measured every 2 h to record growth status. A growth curve was plotted based on the recorded OD values. Growth was classified as follows: OD 1.0-1.5 indicated normal growth; OD 0.5-1.0 indicated weak growth; OD below 0.5 indicated no growth.

Silage preparation

The plant materials utilized in this silage study were sourced from fresh native pasture in the Hulunbuir meadow steppe. Collection was conducted on July 21, 2024, based on the criterion of harvesting at the optimal growth stage of the dominant or constructive species to ensure high-quality forage. The vegetation was dominated by the following species: Chinese Leymus (Leymus chinensis Trin Tzvel.), Baikal Needlegrass (Stipa baicalensis Roshev.), Mongolian Leek (Allium mongolicum Regel.), and Artemisia scoparia (Artemisia scoparia Waldst. & Kit.)(Identified by: Bin Zhang, Inner Mongolia Agricultural University). Voucher specimens of the four studied species were deposited in the Herbarium of the National Botany Experimental Teaching Demonstration Center (Inner Mongolia Agricultural University, Hohhot, China). The deposition numbers are as follows: Leymus chinensis (Trin.) Tzvelev (No. HLBE20230021), Stipa baicalensis Roshev. (No. HLBE20230034), Allium mongolicum Regel (No. HLBE20230025), and Artemisia scoparia Waldst. & Kit. (No. HLBE20230043). The Pediococcus acidilactici L10 strain was uniformly sprayed onto the forage of each treatment at an application rate of 10⁵ CFU/g fresh material (FM). After thorough mixing of FM with the additive (Pediococcus acidilactici L10), approximately 300 g of fresh forage was evenly blended and packed into vacuum-sealed polyethylene bags (30 cm × 23 cm). Each treatment was performed in triplicate, resulting in three independent biological replicates per sampling time point. All subsequent biochemical, enzymatic, and metabolomic analyses were conducted separately on these three biological replicates to ensure statistical rigor. Samples were stored at 5 ℃, 15 ℃, and 25 ℃ for 3, 7, 15, 30, and 60 days.

Chemical composition and fermentation characteristics analyses

The determination methods for each nutritional component were as follows: Dry matter (DM) content (%, fresh weight basis) was measured according to the Chinese National Standard GB/T 6435 [12]; crude protein (CP) content (%, dry matter basis) was determined using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer [13]; fiber components were analyzed with an ANKOM A2000i fiber analyzer to determine acid detergent fiber (ADF) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) contents (%, dry matter basis) [14]; water‑soluble carbohydrates (WSC) (%, dry matter basis) were quantified by the anthrone‑sulfuric acid colorimetric method [15]; and ether extract (EE) content (%, dry matter basis) was determined by ether extraction using an ANKOM XT15i fully automated fat analyzer. A 10 g silage sample was mixed with 90 mL distilled water and homogenized by constant shaking for 2 min, followed by filtration to obtain the silage extract. The pH was measured using a pH meter. Lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), and butyric acid (BA) contents (%, dry matter basis) were determined using an Agilent 1100 series high‑performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system. Ammonia nitrogen (NH₃‑N) content (%, total nitrogen) was analyzed by the phenol‑hypochlorite colorimetric method [16].

Determination of key enzymes in sugar metabolism

The activities of hexokinase (HK), phosphofructokinase (PFK), pyruvate kinase (PK), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were determined using commercial kits (Jiangsu Addison Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay principles were based on the change in absorbance at 340 nm resulting from the oxidation/reduction of NADH/NAD+, coupled with specific substrate conversions. Enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to catalyze 1 µmol of substrate per minute and was expressed as Units per gram of fresh weight (U/g FW).

Metabolite analysis

A 50 mg silage sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube containing grinding beads (6 mm diameter) and 400 µL of an extract mixture of methanol and water (4:1 volume ratio) containing 0.02 mg/mL of L-2-chloro chicory alanine as an internal standard was added. The samples were ground in a frozen tissue grinder at − 10 ℃ for 6 min at a frequency of 50 Hz and extracted by sonication at 5 ℃ for 30 min at 40 kHz. After extraction, the samples were refrigerated at − 20 ℃ for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to an injection vial containing an inserted tube to be analyzed. One QC sample was prepared for every six samples, and 20 µL of the supernatant was mixed as the QC sample. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-Fourier transform mass spectrometry (UPLC-FTMS) detection was performed using a Thermo Fisher UHPLC -Q Exactive HF-X analytical system with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, USA), mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was 3 µL, and the column temperature was set at 40℃. The samples were ionized with an electrospray source, and the mass spectrometry data were acquired in positive and negative ion modes, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the measurements of native grass using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Prior to ANOVA, the normality of the data and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Multiple-range tests using Duncan’s were used to assess differences between treatments. For metabolomic data, p-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) to account for multiple comparisons. Metabolome data analyses were performed using the Majorbio I-Sanger Cloud Platform.

The effect of temperature on silage fermentation quality

The chemical and fermentation characteristics of Pediococcus acidilactici L10-inoculated silage stored at different temperatures are presented in Tables 2 and 3. The interaction between ensiling temperature and ensiling duration had a highly significant effect (P < 0.01) on the pH, LA, AA, DM, and WSC contents of native grass forage silage. A significant effect (P < 0.05) was observed on CP content, whereas no significant effect (P > 0.05) was detected on PA, BA, NH₃‑N, ADF, or NDF contents. During the first 7 days, L10-inoculated silage stored at 25℃ reached a lower pH than that stored at 15℃ and 5℃. However, from day 15 to day 60, the pH values in the 5℃ and 15℃ treatments continued to decrease, ultimately reaching values comparable to or lower than those observed at 25℃. The trend in LA content was opposite to that of pH: more LA accumulated in the early ensiling period at 25 ℃, whereas greater accumulation occurred in the mid‑to‑late stages at 15 ℃ and 5 ℃. AA content accumulated substantially by day 30 at all three temperatures. PA was detected on days 15 and 30 at 15 ℃. Neither temperature nor ensiling duration had a significant effect on BA content. NH₃‑N levels were higher at 25 ℃.

A declining trend was noted in the contents of DM, NDF, and ADF during the ensiling process. Under 25 ℃ conditions, a pronounced decrease in CP content was observed during the ensiling process. In contrast, at 5 ℃ and 15 ℃, the decline in CP was not marked, and the post‑ensiling content remained relatively high. Similarly, water‑soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content showed a comparable pattern: after fermentation at 5 ℃ and 15 ℃, a greater residual amount of WSC was retained. These results indicate that, within the L10-inoculated silage system, ensiling temperature strongly influenced fermentation quality and nutrient preservation.

Effect of temperature on the activities of key enzymes involved in sugar metabolism during ensiling

The effects of temperature and ensiling duration on the activities of key enzymes involved in sugar metabolism were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Overall, enzyme activities remained relatively high during the initial and active fermentation stages, particularly from days 3 to 15. No significant difference in HK activity was observed between the 25℃ and 5℃ treatments during ensiling, whereas a declining trend was observed at 15℃. Nevertheless, HK activity remained at detectable and relatively high levels under both 15℃ and 5℃ conditions. LDH activity varied with ensiling temperature and duration. On day 3, LDH activity was lower at 5℃, whereas different patterns were observed on days 7, 15, and 60. Across all temperature treatments, LDH activity declined significantly by day 30 (P < 0.05), which coincided with the transition from active acidification to a more stable fermentation phase. PFK and PK activities also varied significantly among temperature treatments. On day 30, PFK activity was relatively high at both 25℃ and 5℃, and by day 60, PFK activity tended to increase as temperature decreased. PK activity was significantly higher at 15℃ than at the other temperatures on days 3 and 7, while the activity at 25℃ exceeded that at 5℃ during the early fermentation stage. After 30 days of fermentation, pyruvate kinase activity at 15℃ remained higher than that in the other treatments. However, since all experimental treatments were inoculated with L10 under different temperature conditions, these enzyme activity patterns should be interpreted as temperature-dependent responses rather than direct evidence that L10 specifically enhances glycolytic enzyme activity.

To identify significant metabolic shifts, differential metabolites were selected based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores > 1.0, and adjusted p-values < 0.05. Given the importance of carbohydrates and amino acids in determining silage quality, we conducted a more detailed examination of these metabolite categories. Compared to 25 ℃, low temperature increased the levels of carbohydrates, including Keto-3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid, D-Galactose, and Neuraminic acid. In contrast, L‑Phenylalanine, argininosuccinate, N‑Choloylglycine, pyroglutamic acid, and L‑Aspartic acid were downregulated in the protein fraction (Table 4).

Critical pathway analysis

In the metabolomic analysis, we found that the metabolites were primarily concentrated in amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and lipid metabolism. The expression of key metabolites including citric acid, aconitic acid, stachyose, pectin, N‑choloylglycine, histidinal, 1‑pyrroline‑5‑carboxylic acid, caprylic acid, 4‑hydroxyandrostenedione glucuronide, norepinephrine, and tetrahydrocortisol showed significant up or down regulation.

Discussion

This study compared the fermentation quality, nutritional composition, enzyme activity patterns, and metabolomic profiles of native grass silage inoculated with Pediococcus acidilactici L10 under different ensiling temperatures. The results indicate that ensiling temperature was associated with changes in fermentation characteristics, nutrient preservation, glycolysis-related enzyme activities, and endpoint metabolite profiles within the L10-inoculated silage system.

In temperate and cold regions, silage preparation can be challenging because ambient temperatures during autumn harvest often decrease to approximately 15℃ [17]. While some studies have reported that low temperatures can slow LAB metabolism, delay pH decline, prolong fermentation, and increased risk of spoilage [18], others have demonstrated successful silage preservation at low temperatures using temperature-adapted inoculants [19, 20]. These contrasting findings highlight that the success of low-temperature ensiling depends largely on inoculant selection and the temperature adaptability of LAB strains. The success of silage fermentation is closely related to environmental conditions during the fermentation period, highlighting the importance of temperature management for maintaining silage quality [21, 22]. High-quality silage fermentation is generally associated with better preservation of nutritional components [23]. During ensiling, protein degradation is typically initiated by native plant proteolytic enzymes, which hydrolyze proteins into peptides and free amino acids [24, 25]. These products are subsequently degraded into amides, amines, and ammonia through microbial activity [26]. As shown in Table 2, after 60 days of ensiling, the NH3-N content in the 5℃ treatment was lower than that in the 25℃ treatment, whereas the 15℃ treatment showed a similar but slightly lower value. The lower or numerically reduced NH3-N content observed under the 5℃ and 15℃ treatments may be associated with slower proteolysis and gradual LA accumulationunder these temperature conditions. After 60 days of ensiling, the 5℃ and 15℃ treatments exhibited lower final pH values of 4.30 and 4.32, respectively, and higher lactic acid contents of 13.81 and 11.76, respectively, compared with the 25℃ treatment. Zhu et al. [27] reported that LAB markedly affect bacterial diversity and fermentation metrics in oat silage, enhancing fermentation through lowered pH and NH3-N content. In the present study, the lower pH values and reduced NH3-N accumulation, particularly under the 5℃ treatment, suggest a relatively favorable fermentation profile within the L10-inoculated silage system. Nutritional composition is also an important indicator for evaluating silage quality [28].

Previous studies have reported temperature-dependent changes in dry matter loss during ensiling, although the direction and magnitude of these changes may vary among forage types and fermentation systems. As the primary fermentable substrate for LAB during ensiling, water‑soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content is a key factor influencing silage quality [29]. During ensiling, WSC is converted into organic acids, predominantly lactic acid, thereby lowering pH and contributing to silage preservation [30]. Native grass typically contains relatively low initial WSC concentrations. After 60 days of ensiling, WSC content remained relatively low in the 25℃ treatment. In contrast, higher residual WSC concentrations were retained in the 15℃ and 5℃ treatments. The observed temperature-dependent trend in WSC suggests that ensiling temperature may modulate both microbial utilization of WSC and the release of soluble carbohydrates from plant tissues during fermentation [31]. Because all treatments were inoculated with P. acidilacticiL10 and no uninoculated control was included, this pattern cannot be attributed solely to inoculation; rather, it may reflect the combined effects of ensiling temperature, microbial activity, and substrate utilization. Lower pH values can inhibit the growth and metabolism of undesirable microorganisms such as Clostridium [32]. Concurrently, cell-wall components may be degraded through microbial, enzymatic, and acid hydrolysis during silage fermentation, resulting in changes in NDF content. In this study, a decreasing trend in NDF was observed as ensiling progressed. Reich et al. [33] reported that inoculation with Pediococcus acidilactici reduced the ADF content in corn silage, indicating that LAB inoculation may influence fiber fractions; however, the ADF response in the present study was temperature-dependent.

The preservation of WSC and accumulation of LA under different ensiling temperatures were further examined in relation to the measured enzyme activity profiles. HK, as an entry enzyme of glycolysis [34], showed relatively higher initial activity at 15℃ and 5℃, which was consistent with active carbohydrate utilization during the early stage of fermentation. PFK, a key regulatory enzyme involved in glycolysis [35], exhibited temperature-dependent activity patterns; relatively sustained activity at 15℃ coincided with greater LA production and WSC retention, whereas the activity pattern observed at 25℃ was accompanied by more rapid WSC depletion. Pyruvate kinase catalyzes the final step of glycolysis and contributes to pyruvate and ATP formation [36], whereas lactate dehydrogenase is involved in lactate formation during homolactic fermentation [37]. The activity profiles of these enzymes were broadly consistent with the fermentation characteristics under different ensiling temperatures. However, these enzyme activity patterns and fermentation outcomes represent correlative associations, and the present data do not provide direct evidence that changes in HK, PFK, PK, or LDH activity caused the observed differences in carbon partitioning or lactic acid accumulation. Therefore, our results suggest an association among low-temperature conditions, enzyme activity dynamics, and fermentation performance, rather than demonstrating a direct mechanistic link. Among the tested temperatures, the relatively stable enzyme activity profile observed at 15℃ were associated with favorable fermentation characteristics.

This study employed liquid chromatography‑mass spectrometry (LC‑MS) to analyze metabolites in native pasture grass silage (60‑day fermentation) under low‑temperature conditions, aiming to further elucidate the impact of low temperature on these metabolites. Silage samples collected on day 60 were selected for metabolomic analysis because fermentation was expected to have reached a relatively stable stage by that time. In the present study, the highest relative abundance of D-galactose was observed in the 15℃ treatment. Although low temperature may slow the overall degradation of plant cell walls [38], certain acid-tolerant or temperature-adapted microorganisms may still contribute to pectin degradation and the gradual release of galactose [39]. LAB generally preferentially utilize glucose and fructose, whereas galactose metabolism may be less efficient under certain fermentation conditions [40]. Consequently, the consumption rate of released galactose may be lower than its release rate, leading to accumulation. This interpretation is consistent with our observation of higher residual WSC at 15 ℃, because galactose may be retained as part of the soluble carbohydrate pool. The relative abundance of N-choloylglycine decreased under lower-temperature treatments, with the lowest value observed at 15℃. Because N-choloylglycine is annotated to bile acid-related metabolism and may reflect microbial-associated metabolic activity [41], its lower relative abundance under low-temperature conditions may indicate altered microbial metabolism in the silage system. However, the present study did not directly analyze microbial community composition or specific spoilage microorganisms such as Clostridium or E. coli. Therefore, the relationship between N-Choloylglycine variation and undesirable microbial activity should be regarded as tentative and requiring further validation [42]. Proline, a key compatible solute, can accumulate under stress conditions such as low temperature, osmotic challenge, and oxidative stress, thereby helping maintain cellular integrity and homeostasis [43]. Accordingly, the low‑temperature groups in this study exhibited substantially higher L‑proline concentrations compared to the 25 ℃ treatment.

Metabolomic analysis conducted on day 60 further characterized temperature-associated differences in the metabolite profiles of native grass silage. The results indicated differences in metabolites related to carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism pathways (Fig. 6). However, because metabolomic profiling was performed only at the final fermentation time point, these data should be interpreted as endpoint associations rather than evidence of dynamic pathway regulation or causal mechanisms. The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle is a central hub of energy metabolism [44]. Citrate exhibited a higher relative abundance in the 5℃ treatment and a lower relative abundance in the 25℃ treatment. This pattern may suggest altered organic acid metabolism under low-temperature conditions, although measurements of TCA-cycle enzyme expression or metabolic flux would be required to confirm this interpretation. Citrate may also be involved in intracellular pH regulation or biosynthetic processes [45]. The higher relative abundance of stachyose and UDP-D-galactose observed at 15℃ was consistent with the higher residual WSC content, suggesting that moderate low-temperature ensiling may be associated with better retention of WSC. Similarly, the higher relative abundances of pectin- and stachyose-related metabolites at 15℃, together with the observed changes in fiber fractions, may indicate an association between structural carbohydrate modification and the accumulation or retention of soluble degradation products [46]. Nevertheless, this interpretation remains tentative because the present study did not directly quantify cell-wall degradation intermediates or microbial enzymes involved in fiber hydrolysis. Under the 15℃ condition, CP was relatively well preserved, and several nitrogen-containing metabolites showed distinct accumulation patterns. For instance, 1-pyrroline-5-carboxylic acid, a precursor involved in proline metabolism and stress tolerance [47, 48], showed a higher relative abundance at 15℃. Putatively annotated norepinephrine, which has been reported to participate in plant stress signaling and antioxidant responses [49], was also enriched at 15℃, potentially reflecting stress-associated responses of plant tissues during low-temperature ensiling. Similarly, gamma-glutamyl-L-putrescine, a conjugated form of putrescine, showed higher relative abundance at 15 ℃. Polyamines and their conjugates are known to be involved in cold acclimation and cellular protection in plants [50]. These observations suggest that the 15 ℃ ensiling condition was associated with the retention or higher relative abundance of stress-related nitrogenous metabolites. However, whether these metabolites directly contributed to nitrogen preservation requires further functional validation. Caprylic acid has been reported to possess antifungal and antibacterial properties [51]. In this study, its higher relative abundance at 15℃ may be associated with the relatively favorable fermentation profile observed at this temperature. However, because antimicrobial activity, spoilage microorganism abundance, and microbial community composition were not directly measured, the role of caprylic acid in inhibiting spoilage microorganisms cannot be confirmed from the present data. Therefore, the observed increase in caprylic acid should be interpreted as a potential indicator of microbial or lipid metabolic changes rather than as evidence of a bacteriostatic mechanism. Future studies combining targeted metabolite quantification, microbial community analysis, and antimicrobial validation assays are needed to clarify whether caprylic acid or related lipid metabolites contribute to silage preservation under low-temperature conditions.

In summary, the present results indicate that, within L10-inoculated native grass silage, ensiling temperature was associated with differences in fermentation quality, nutrient preservation, glycolysis-related enzyme activities, and endpoint metabolite profiles. The 15℃ treatment showed a relatively favorable overall fermentation and nutritional profile among the tested temperatures, but this should not be interpreted as an optimal threshold for L10 efficacy because no non-inoculated control was included. The current evidence supports a temperature-dependent comparison within an L10-inoculated silage system, rather than a direct demonstration of inoculant efficacy or mechanism. Future studies should include uninoculated controls, microbial community analysis, and functional validation experiments to determine the specific contribution of P. acidilactici L10 to silage fermentation under low-temperature conditions.

Conclusion

This study compared the fermentation quality, enzyme activity patterns, and metabolomic profiles of native grass silage inoculated with Pediococcus acidilactici L10 under three ensiling temperatures (25℃, 15℃, and 5℃). Within the L10-inoculated silage system, the 15℃ and 5℃ treatments exhibited lower final pH values, reduced NH3-N accumulation, and greater WSC preservation than the 25℃ treatment. Enzyme activity analysis indicated that the activities of HK, PFK, PK, and LDH varied with ensiling temperature and were associated with patterns of lactic acid production and carbohydrate utilization. Untargeted metabolomic analysis further revealed temperature-associated differences in the relative abundances of compatible solutes, carbohydrate-related metabolites, nitrogen-containing metabolites, and lipid metabolites, including L-proline, 1-pyrroline-5-carboxylic acid, D-galactose, stachyose, and caprylic acid. Among the tested temperatures, the 15℃ treatment showed a relatively favorable overall fermentation and nutritional profile within the L10-inoculated treatments. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding how temperature influences fermentation dynamics in L10-inoculated silage and offer guidance for future studies evaluating the performance of inoculants under low-temperature conditions.
