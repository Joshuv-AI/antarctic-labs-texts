Early prediction of alopecia areata using machine learning modeling of neuro stress immune signatures from multi datasets
Authors: Anxin Chen, Lin Shang, Yingjiao Ju, Fenglin Zhuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlopecia areata (AA) is an easy-recurring disease that presents huge challenges globally. An efficient clinical tool to predict AA onset would be valuable for timely intervention. We extracted six AA-related datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). GO, KEGG, GSEA, GSVA and CIBERSORT algorithm were performed to elucidate the characteristics of AA. Feature genes were identified using LASSO regression and Random Forest algorithms. Five machine learning algorithms (Logistic Regression, K-nearest neighbors, Elastic Net, XGBoost and LightGBM) were employed to construct predictive models, with internal and external validation conducted to determine the optimal model. Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to interpret the best-performing model and shiny framework was applied to establish an online predictive website. Five datasets (GSE45512, GSE68801, GSE80342, GSE58573, GSE74761) were integrated as train set and GSE148346 was defined as test set. Tissue regeneration and immune dysregulation were the key factors in AA pathogenesis. Three feature genes (KRT83, PPP1R1C, PIRT) were selected for model construction, with innate immune response, neural inflammatory and stress being a potential regulator for AA. The XGBoost model outperformed other algorithms, SHAP provided explanations for predictions and an online predictive website was established. Our study provides a potential “neuro-stress-immune” interplay insight into the pathogenesis of AA and establishes a clinically applicable predictive model for AA onset.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33927-2.Keywords: Machine learning, Autoimmune diseases, Alopecia areata, Predictive learning models, DiagnosisSubject terms: Biological techniques, Genetics, Molecular biology, Biomarkers

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is a common hair loss disease in department of dermatology worldwide. The clinical features of AA contain non-scaring, recurrent, sudden and unpredictable hair loss1. AA affects approximately 2% of the global population in all ages, regions and genders2 and leads to higher risk of anxiety and depression3. The most common subtype of AA is patchy alopecia, which can recover within one year or progress to more severe subtypes, such as alopecia totalis (AT) or alopecia universalis (AU)4,5. To date, the classic theory of the etiology of AA is the immune privilege (IP) loss of the lower epithelium part of hair follicle (HF) (the bulge region, the niche for HF stem cells (HFSCs) and downwards to the hair bulb), thus result in autoimmune cascade within hair follicles and then hair loss occurs6–8. However, the trigger of AA has not been thoroughly understood yet, current mainstream theories include genetic factors, mental stress, allergy reaction, infections, gut microbiota and so on1,4,5, thus making it hard to predict and prevent.

The development of bioinformatics technologies in recent years has led to greater appreciation of their significant potential in biomedical applications. The processing of gene expression data through different analysis methods has helped us have a deep insight into the pathogenesis and therapy mechanism of AA. GEO database hosts numerous high-quality AA related-datasets with detailed sample information, representing untapped resources for advancing research. To date, many studies have made use of these datasets to determine marker genes and construct models through machine learning methods to predict the morbidity, recurrence or progress probability of AA9–14. Yet no study has performed a comprehensive review of all AA datasets, integrated them effectively, or conducted model performance evaluations through visible figures and reasonable explanation.

In this article, we took a holistic look at datasets containing AA samples in GEO database, extracted six of them after evaluation and integrated five of them as train set and another one as test set to construct AA predictive models. We summarized the characteristics of AA through enrichment analysis and immune-filtrated analysis. Two machine learning methods (LASSO Regression and Random Forest) were employed to determine feature genes of AA. WGCNA and three drugs significantly linked with feature genes were identified, uncovering their potential neuro-stress biological functions. Five machine learning methods (Logistic Regression, K-nearest neighbors, Elastic Net, Extreme Gradient Boosting and Light Gradient Boosting Machine) were employed to build the models. We evaluated performance of models multilaterally and successfully interpreted the best model using SHAP algorithm, with an online predictive website developed. Finally, validation of down-regulated expression of PPP1R1C was performed through immunohistochemistry.

Result

Accumulation of train and test set

In this study, we scanned all datasets containing AA samples in GEO database. Based on certain criteria such as specimens, profiling levels, technologies and platform IDs, these datasets can be classified into several clusters (Table 1). Scalp biopsy samples in specimen, bulk-RNA in profiling level, microarray in technology, GPL570 in platform ID were the dominant elements with largest sample size in these clusters respectively. Six datasets (GSE45512, GSE68801, GSE80342, GSE58573, GSE148346, GSE74761) share the common features that conformed to these dominant elements. Hence, these six datasets were extracted to continue our analysis to maximize sample size for our models (Table 2), Con samples and AA samples were selected from non-treated patients (Supplementary Table 1).

After converting each dataset into gene expression matrix, GSE148346 had a gene count of 14,979 while other datasets had a gene count of 22,880. Hence, we combined 5 datasets (GSE45512, GSE68801, GSE80342, GSE58573, GSE74761) as train set and GSE148346 was defined as test set. Finally, the train set had 82 AA samples and 76 Con samples with a gene count of 22,880, the test set had 43 AA samples and 17 Con samples with a gene count of 14,979. All genes in the test set were present in the train set. Before batch effect correction, there is significant heterogeneity among these datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1o). Following batch correction within the train set, few batch effect were observed among samples in the train set (Supplementary Fig. 1p). However, distinct batch effects persisted between the train set and the test set (Supplementary Fig. 1p), which laid a foundation for the subsequent validation of the model’s generalizability.

Regeneration and immune status were key factors in AA

A total of 177 DEGs with 32 up-regulated and 145 down-regulated were screened in train set (Fig. 1a,b), whereas a total of 172 DEGs with 6 up-regulated and 166 down-regulated were screened in test set (Fig. 1c,d). There were 115 down-regulated (Fig. 1e) and 1 up-regulated (Fig. 1f) DEGs shared by both train set and test set. Typically, AA is associated with keratinocyte dysfunction, diminished regenerative capacity and aberrant autoimmune activation5,15. A lot of genes encoding KRTs and KRTAPs were down-regulated in both train set and test set. In train set, KEGG analysis outcomes (Fig. 2a,b) signified pathways closely correlated to immune features (viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction). Immune-related molecular functions, such as cytokine activity and chemokine receptor binding. For GO analysis outcomes (Fig. 2c,d), regeneration-related process like hair cycle, skin and epidermis development, keratinocyte differentiation, keratinization was significant. GO analysis of DEGs in test set revealed almost the same result (Fig. 2e,f).

To further explore the immune feature in AA, we next conducted immune filtrated analysis. Each type of immune cell had different proportions (Fig. 3e). More immune cells were engaged in correlation with each other in AA samples (n = 20, Fig. 3f) compared to Con samples (n = 16, Fig. 3g). Proportions of CD8 + T cells, γδT cells and M1 macrophages were significantly up-regulated in AA samples. Meanwhile, proportions of resting memory CD4 + T cells, activated NK cells, activated dendritic cells, resting mast cells and neutrophils were significantly down-regulated in AA samples (Fig. 3h).

KRT83, PPP1R1C and PIRT were feature genes in AA

Two distinct machine learning algorithms (LASSO regression and Random Forest) were utilized to select feature genes. LASSO regression identified 13 candidate genes (Fig. 4a,b) while RF identified 18 candidate genes (Fig. 4c,d). After intersecting these two results, we finally got 3 feature genes for AA: KRT83, PPP1R1C and PIRT (Fig. 4e). ROC curves elucidated great potential of feature genes in predicting AA (Fig. 4f,h). They were all significantly down-regulated in AA in train set (Fig. 1a, 4i) and test set (Fig. 1c). Also, the 100-time bootstrap resampling validation performed on LASSO and RF models demonstrated the reliable and stable potential of these three feature genes as diagnostic biomarkers (Fig. 4j,k).

Then we explore the link between feature genes and immune cells, feature genes were all positively correlated with resting memory CD4 + T cells, neutrophils, activated dendritic cells and negatively correlated with M1 macrophages and γδT cells (Fig. 4l–q). The majority of them belonged to innate immune system, indicating that innate immune signatures may be more evident compared to adaptive immunity in AA. Among them, γδT cells and M1 macrophages were up-regulated, hinting their essential roles in AA5,6,19–22. For γδT cells, their excessive activities could result in potentiation of IFN-γ signaling pathway, followed by proceeding autoimmune cascade reactions6. For M1 macrophages, their reduction might promote cyclic activation of HFSCs, and inhibition of M1 macrophages-derived wnt proteins could facilitate anagen transition for hair follicles23. Moreover, γδT cells are also able to enhance the activities of M1 macrophages through an IFN-γ dependent manner, or even directly regulate activation of M1 macrophages in topical skin24,25, leading to autoimmune and inflammatory status in skin.

To further explore the biological relevance between feature genes and AA beyond statistical correlation. We performed WGCNA on the train set (Fig. 5a–e). After calculating the correlation between the eigengenes of each module and the AA phenotype (Fig. 5e), module MEbrown turned out to be the modules most strongly correlated with AA (r = −0.71, P = 1.2*10–22). Furthermore, three feature genes were all in this module. We performed GO analysis on all genes in module MEborwn. Top 20 GO terms are closely associated with neural activation and signal transmission (e.g., regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, modulation of chemical synaptic transmission), ion channel activity (e.g., metal ion transmembrane transporter activity, regulation of membrane potential), as well as regeneration-related process of skin (Fig. 5f).

Hypothesis of “Neuro-stress-immune” interplay in AA

KRT83 encodes keratin-83 and forms the intermediate filament network in the hair shaft and maintains the integrity of hair structure26. On the other hand, PPP1R1C mediates a variety of cellular process, especially neuropsychiatric diseases27 while PIRT was the key regulator of TRPV1, mediating heat and pain perception and thus influencing cutaneous neural process28. Although there was no direct research unveiling their link with AA, we assumed that they might have impacts on the upstream pathway of immune response in AA——neural stress, as neuropsychiatric disorder is an important predisposing factor for alopecia areata19. In addition, γδT cells and M1 macrophages are all closely connected with stress reaction20,29–31.

To confirm our hypothesis, we gathered neural stress-related gene sets to perform GSVA in train set. Significant enrichment of neural inflammatory and degeneration-related gene sets was shown in AA samples, whereas neural regeneration-associated gene sets were markedly enriched in Con samples (Fig. 6a). GSVA in test set revealed roughly the same result (Fig. 6b). Neuro-inflammatory response (NIR) and neuro-degeneration gene sets were enriched in both train and test set. As inflammation was a crucial characteristic in AA, NIR gene set was used for further analysis. The majority of NIR genes (total 84 genes) showed correlations with the feature genes in train set (n = 74, Fig. 6c) and test set (n = 59, Fig. 6d). Genes positively or negatively correlated with the feature genes displayed obvious overlap and similar correlation patterns in the train and test set. In addition, for nearly every gene, its correlation with the three feature genes was concordance (either positive or negative) (Fig. 6c,d). Among these genes, JAK2, TNF and so on have been extensively reported in the literature to exhibit a strong association with AA5. Moreover, most immune cells showed correlations with NIR genes, particularly M1 macrophages and γδT cells (Fig. 6e). The association was even more pronounced in the test set (Fig. 6f). This might be a suggestive sign of crosstalk between immune cells and neural stress.

Given the novel functions of feature genes, a gene-drug interaction network was established (Fig. 7a). Three hub drugs significantly associated with all feature genes were filtered (Fig. 7b): glucose, adenosine triphosphate, ethacrynic acid (Fig. 7c). Although none of these three drugs had been reported to directly affect AA, glucose and adenosine triphosphate were essential parts of mitochondrial electron transport chain, ethacrynic acid was known as an inducer of ROS32, a key medicator of oxidative stress. Conclusions above suggested potential association between feature genes, NIR genes and oxidative stress, probably unveil the “neuro-stress-immune” interplay in AA.

XGBoost model exhibited the best predictive performance

The calculation of VIF indicated multicollinearity concerns (VIF = 5.1870) between KRT83 and PPP1R1C, PIRT and PPP1R1C(Table 3), which might have a slight adverse impact on the linear model33. In contrast, the performance of ensemble learning models such as XGBoost and LightGBM is less severely affected by variable multicollinearity. After comprehensive consideration, five machine learning-based supervised learning model (LR, LightGBM, KNN, XGBoost and ENet) were utilized to construct AA models.

We calculated five models’ classification performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score and G-mean), ROC curves (including AUC values) and DCA curves together to see which model possessed the best efficacy. For classification performance metrics (Fig. 8a,b), XGBoost model had the highest accuracy, sensitivity, precision, F1 score and G-mean in internal validation with narrow confidence intervals, followed closely by LightGBM, and there was a certain gap between these two with other models. However, there was no difference between all models (KNN excepted) in external validation, which might attribute to insufficient sample size of test set. For ROC curves (Fig. 8c,d), all models exhibited AUC values exceeding 0.8, with the exception of the external validation of the KNN model. XGBoost had the highest AUC value no matter in internal validation or external validation. For DCA curves (Fig. 8e,f), as the threshold probability increased, the net benefit of all curves gradually decreased in a fluctuating manner. All models provided a higher net gain than “Treat All” or “Treat None” within the probability threshold range of 0–81.25%. When the threshold exceeded approximately 81.25%, most of the curves (internal validation of lightGBM and XGBoost excluded) dropped to intersect with the Treat All and Treat None curves, indicating that the model’s net benefit became almost indistinguishable from that of “Treat All” and “Treat None” at this point, and might no longer hold a significant advantage at higher thresholds. Overall, the prob curve of the XGBoost model was higher than those of the other models, indicating that this model could achieve better net benefit when making decisions, LightGBM, ENet and LR followed, while KNN performed the worst. All in all, we finally ascertained that the XGBoost model exhibited the most superior performance.

It was worth noting that the AUC values of XGBoost in both internal validation and external validation were very close to 1, which raised our concerns about insufficient sample size and overfitting. To rule out the case of overfitting, we conducted an internal five-fold cross-validation on the train set of the XGBoost model (mean AUC = 0.8819; SD = 0.0647) (Fig. 8g), which denoted that the model had good discrimination ability and maintained consistent performance across different data. To rule out the case of insufficient sample size, we generated learning curves of all models. Most models (XGBoost, lightGBM, Enet, and Logistic Regression) demonstrate excellent and stable performance with very low LogLoss once the training sample size exceeds 60 (KNN excepted) (Fig. 8h). Power analysis confirmed that the sample size was large enough to demonstrate the result (Supplementary Table 2).

For feature genes description, PPP1R1C contributed most significantly to the model, followed by PIRT, with KRT83 having the least contribution (Fig. 9a). Higher expression level of feature genes led to lower SHAP values, coordinating with the fact that feature genes were down-regulated in AA (Fig. 9a,b). PPP1R1C had the strongest interaction effect with the other two feature genes (Fig. 9c), which was consistent with the calculation results of VIF (Table 3). When they acted independently, high expression levels had a negative impact on the SHAP values. When they interacted with other feature gene, the distribution of SHAP values were broader across different expression levels (Fig. 9c). To further validate the correlation between feature genes and immune cells, we included immune cells together with feature genes to form the SHAP interaction plot (Fig. 9h). Among the top 10 interaction combinations (Supplementary Table 3), half were the combination of featured genes and immune cells. This indicated that the predictive power of the model relies significantly on the interplay between gene expression levels of feature genes and microenvironment containing immune cells. Additionally, PPP1R1C was featured in the top four strongest interaction pairs, partnering with both other genes (KRT83, PIRT) and innate immune cells (neutrophils and dendritic cells activated). This strongly suggested that PPP1R1C’s role in classifying samples is not standalone but is critically dependent on its interactions with other features.

To adapt the clinical application, we built an online predictive website for XGBoost model (https://yuai.shinyapps.io/XGboost_for_alopecia_areata/).

Discussion

With a worldwide prevalence of 2%, alopecia areata ranks as the second most common form of non-scarring alopecia36. Due to intricate mechanism of AA, it’s hard to predict the “trigger point” and take intervention in time for AA patients clinically. However, early onset of AA (such as patchy AA) can easily develop into severe subtypes of AA (such as AT or AU), so it’s necessary to find a way to predict onset of AA and take early intervention. In this article, we successfully discovered neural stress and immune features of AA. Next, five machine learning-based predictive models were constructed based on three feature genes. At last, we selected XGBoost model by objective assessment and proved its interpretability utilizing SHAP algorithm.

Biopsy is a widely used dermatologic operation in clinical practice, frequently employed for pathologic staining—the gold standard for diagnosing many cutaneous disorders. It remains the most reliable and efficient method for evaluating lesional and peri-lesional pathology characteristics. In department of dermatology, it is easier to obtain biopsy samples compared with other department in hospital since skin is highly regenerative and exposed to the outside of the body. After obtaining biopsy samples, other than pathologic staining, gene expression profiling is another way to make good use of the samples. Only expression level of three genes is needed for our model. This greatly reduces the cost, despite real-world study is needed to validate the efficacy. Nevertheless, our model’s key advantage lies in its ability to detect molecular changes prior to clinical symptoms, enabling early prediction—a capability beyond the reach of trichoscopy. Thus, for patients whose scalp biopsies may have already been obtained for other reasons, our model can be leveraged to provide a more precise prediction, thereby enabling earlier preventive measures. However, for patients who have already developed symptoms, established clinical manifestations such as the SALT score, exclamation mark hairs, and yellow dots observed via trichoscopy remain more diagnostically significant, cost-effective and convenient than our model.

One of the most prominent features of KRT83 is that its mutation can significantly affect hair quality and lead to monilethrix37. To our knowledge, there are some solid evidences demonstrating correlations between KRT83 with AA. Some bioinformatic analysis have corroborated KRT83 as a key gene in AA9,38. Also, studies have found that autoreactive T cells have specific reaction towards keratinocytes in AA39,40. The whole keratin family proteins should be prioritized in AA mechanistic studies9,10. PPP1R1C has talent in the neural reconstruction during inflammation41. As reported by Nobel Prize in 2021, TRPV1 was a novel receptor for temperature and touch42 and some studies have reported its link with cutaneous innate immune43, neuroinflammation44 and AA45. As a key regulator of TRPV1, the function of PIRT in this aspect deserves to be explored28. Among NIR genes, JAK2, TNF, IFNG and so on serve as key regulators in AA and are closely linked to ROS generation46–48. The pathways mediated by these genes, including JAK2/STAT3 signaling, may have constituted an intricate interplay bridging oxidative stress and immune responses46,47. All in all, we suggest that feature genes might mediate neural stress process in alopecia areata. The confirmation of expression of PIRT and KRT83 in AA and Con samples still need to be confirmed, on which our team will focus in the future.

The immune landscape of AA has always been a hot topic. The key driver of pathobiology of AA is secretion of IFN-γ by skin resident memory T cells, which induces ectopic and excessive expression of MHC class I and II molecules, leading to collapse of IP6,8. Hence, CD4 + memory T cells could be linked with recurrence of autoimmune status, with ROS being the underlying activator of memory T cells49,50. After that, CD8 + T cells would be the main force to attack hair follicles, leading to unpredictable hair loss6,8. Recently, more and more studies pointed out that AA is a multifactorial disease with disorders of many immune cells, every type of immune cells matters a lot in altering conditions of AA1,6–8. Neutrophils, γδT cells and M1 macrophages are main force of innate immune response, probably mediate the trigger of AA51,52. Apart from functions in autoimmune process, both γδT cells and macrophages could interact with cutaneous nervous systems. A study in psoriasis found that stimulation of cutaneous sympathetic nerve is a vital prerequisite for γδT cells secreting pro-inflammatory cytokine31. On the other hand, M1 macrophages could interact with cutaneous nervous systems (especially cholinergic system), also leading to immune and inflammation conditions, or even onset of AA5,53. Combined with ontological functions of feature genes, the “neuro-stress-immune” interplay might be a potential research topic in AA. For example, one of a possible hypothesis could be a “TRPV1-PIRT-PPP1R1C-Neuropeptide Axis”: Stress (such as oxidative stress) could activate cutaneous sensory nerve endings54,55, opening TRPV1 channels56. The diminished negative regulation of TRPV1 by PIRT57 leads to massive Ca2⁺ influx58 and release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides44,59,60. On the other hand, we speculate that downregulation of PPP1R1C, a phosphatase in sensory neurons, disrupts Ca2⁺-dependent phosphorylation balance, also promoting the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides41. Thus, release of ROS58,61 could be accelerated and many immune cells are stimulated to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, initiating perifollicular inflammation45. The combined effect of inflammation and niche disruption could accelerate the recruitment and activation of CD8+ T cells, resulting in collapse of immune privilege of follicles45. Nevertheless, further experiment is required to fully elucidate the roles of feature genes in this network.

As mentioned before, the train set had a gene count of 22,880 while the test set had a gene count of 14,979. Consequently, the DEGs identified in each set were not entirely identical. And the variation in gene expression is slightly less pronounced in the test set compared to the train set (Fig. 1b, d). This might implied heterogeneity between train set and test set, which could attribute to platform difference, biological difference across races and so on. Under this circumstance, our models still performed great in test set, namely manifesting excellent generalizing abilities.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to combine such an extensive collection of AA related-datasets in GEO and perform multifactor comparisons across five machine learning approaches. The integration of different datasets in GEO not only enabled us to conclude general AA pathobiology landscape, but also helpful to generalize the model. The table of the AA-related datasets we compiled can also provide relevant insights for subsequent research (Table 1). However, there is some limitation of our study. Despite integrating six independent GEO datasets (totaling 218 samples), the cohort sizes remained suboptimal for robust model training and validation (train set: n = 158; test set: n = 60). What’s more, further evidence such as in vivo or in vitro experiment are needed to validate the exact functions of feature genes and “neuro-stress-immune” interplay in AA, on which our team would focus in the future.

Method

Data sources

We scanned all datasets containing AA samples in GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) and extracted 6 datasets (GSE4551262, GSE6880163, GSE8034264, GSE5857362, GSE7476165, GSE14834666) from them to continue our subsequent analysis. The inclusion criteria were: specimen was scalp biopsy, profiling level was bulk-RNA, technology was microarray, the platform ID of GeneChip was Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array (GPL570).

Data processing

The probe expression matrices were converted to gene expression matrices by platform annotation file (GPL570-55,999) and applied the average value method to handle cases with multiple probes for a gene (Supplementary Fig. 1a–f). Then “limma” R package was exploited to normalize each dataset respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1 g–l) and combined 5 datasets (GSE45512, GSE68801, GSE80342, GSE58573, GSE74761) as train set (Supplementary Fig. 1 m). GSE148346 was appointed as test set. After combination, “combat” package was exploited to remove batch effect (Supplementary Fig. 1n), “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” packages were used to generate PCA lots.

Samples extraction

For sample extraction, we read the original articles and information from GEO series websites, manually selected control samples (Con samples) and alopecia areata samples (AA samples). Biopsies taken from the scalps of healthy individuals or from non-lesional areas of AA patients who had not been previously treated with drugs or other therapeutic interventions were recognized as Con samples. Biopsies taken from lesional scalp areas of AA patients who had never been treated with drugs or other therapeutic interventions were recognized as AA samples.

Differential expression genes (DEGs) screening

We screened DEGs between AA samples and Con samples in the train set using “limma” R package. The criteria of DEGs screening were: adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1.

Enrichment analysis

We conducted Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using “clusterProfiler” R package. For GO and KEGG analysis, p-value < 0.05 and q-value < 0.05 were the filter criteria of significantly different functions and pathways. Only the top 15 most significantly enriched GO terms were displayed. GSEA was conducted with “c5.go.v7.4.symbols.gmt” file obtained from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Enrichment scores (ES) for the top 10 and bottom 10 biological processes were displayed. R package “GSVA” was exploited to assess enrichment score for neural stress-related gene sets in train set with adjusted p-value < 0.05. Neural stress-related gene sets for GSVA were also gathered from MSigDB by searching for keywords “Neural Stress” within the Hallmark and C5 collections.

Immune filtrated analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to conduct an in-depth examination of the composition of infiltrating immune cells, along with an exploration of the correlation between immune cells and feature genes.

Feature genes determination

We exploited Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and Random Forest (RF) algorithm to determine candidate feature genes from DEGs. Firstly, R package “glmnet” was exploited to perform LASSO regression with tenfold-cross-validation to find the optimal lambda, and extracted feature genes with non-zero coefficients. Secondly, “randomForest” R package was exploited to conduct RF algorithm, building and optimizing a random forest model with 500 trees, then identifying and saving genes with importance scores greater than 2 as AA-related feature genes. Predictive potential of feature gens was validated using 100-iteration Bootstrap resampling strategy through ROC curves.

Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed on the train set. We focused on the top 50% most variable genes after Z-score normalization. After forming the cluster tree, 20 outlier samples were removed by setting the cutoff height at 147.813 (based on mean + 2*SD of the height values), the optimal soft threshold was 7 (scale-free R2 = 0.85).

Uncovering drugs targeting feature genes

Coremine Medical database (https://coremine.com/medical/#search) was chosen to obtain potential drugs targeting feature genes. Drugs linked with all feature genes were recognized as hub drugs. Establishment of gene-drug interaction network was completed in Cytoscape 3.10.3. Molecular formulas, 2D Structure images and 3D Conformers were obtained from Pubchem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Multicollinearity assessment

To evaluate the multicollinearity between feature genes, the variance inflation factor (VIF) between each feature gene was computed. Each feature gene was sequentially designated as the dependent variable, with the remaining two serving as explanatory variables. VIF < 5 indicates acceptable multicollinearity.

Model construction

Five machine learning algorithms were employed to build AA predictive models. All of them exhibit talents for building binary classification predictive models for diseases30,32,49,51.

Logistic Regression (LR) was used to construct models for binary-class classification tasks by estimating the likelihood of an event occurring based on a collection of predictor variables. The “rms” R package was applied to develop the LR model.

The K-nearest neighbors (KNN) constructed a model by categorizing a new data point according to the predominant class among its K-nearest neighbors within the train set. The “class” R package was exploited to construct KNN model (k = 5).

Elastic Net (ENet) was applied to develop a regression model that integrates L1 and L2 regularization terms. We fine-tuned the parameters to their optimal values by tenfold cross-validation for constructing the ENet model. The “glmnet” R package was utilized to construct ENet model.

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was employed to create a robust gradient boosting model based on level-wise algorithm. R package “xgboost” was employed to set XGBoost parameters, specifing 100 rounds of boosting and trained the final XGBoost model, with hyperparameter optimization performed via grid search combined with fivefold cross-validation to minimize the LogLoss metric. The grid systematically evaluated combinations of tree depth (max depth: 2–4), learning rate (eta: 0.01–0.1) and minimum loss reduction (gamma: 0 or 0.1). The optimal configuration was found to be max depth = 3, eta = 0.1, gamma = 0.1, with fixed parameters including min child weight = 1 and subsample = 0.8.

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) was also employed to build a gradient boosting model based on leaf-wise algorithm. Using the “lightgbm” R package, we configured model parameters, prepared training data, and trained the model for 100 rounds. The R package “lightgbm” was utilized to construct and train the final LightGBM model, specifying 100 rounds of boosting. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via Grid Search combined with fivefold cross-validation to minimize the LogLoss metric. The grid systematically evaluated combinations of the maximum number of leaves (15, 31, 63) and the learning rate (0.01, 0.05, 0.1). The optimal configuration was found to be: leaves = 31, learning rate = 0.1. Fixed parameters used in the model included: feature fraction = 0.9, bagging fraction = 0.8, bagging freq = 5, min data in leaf = 1, boosting type = “gbdt”, objective = “binary”.

Model performance evaluation and comparison

Models’ performance was evaluated and compared through classification performance metrics, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) curves. Confusion matrices were calculated using “caret” R package and extracted values of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN) and true negatives (TN) from the confusion matrices. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, G-mean from each confusion matrix were calculated. The 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and precision were calculated using Wilson score interval method (“Hmisc” package). For the F1 Score and G-mean, the 95%CI was determined via 1000-repetition Bootstrap resampling approach, utilizing the Percentile method on the resulting distribution of metrics. R package “pROC” was utilized to produce ROC curves and calculated area under the curve (AUC), The 95% CI for AUC values were calculated using the DeLong method. DCA curves were drawn using “dcurves” R package.

Evaluating model performance utilizing the train set was termed as internal validation while using the test set to do that was external validation. The internal and external validation bar charts and scatter plots for five-fold cross validation were all created by Microsoft Excel 2021.

Statistical power analysis was performed using Cohen’s f2 effect size, which was approximated from McFadden’s R2 (f2 = R2/(1—R2)) on the train and test set. The pwr. f2.test was then applied to estimate the power given the validation sample size and the f2 value. R package “pscl” was used for this process.

All five models were trained on incrementally larger subsets of the training data (10% to 100% of the total sample size) to form learning curves. Model performance at each increment was evaluated by calculating the LogLoss on the fixed test set, which determined the sufficiency of the current sample size for each algorithm.

Model interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used for interpreting XGBoost. The “shapviz” R package was applied to display feature importance as a bee swarm plot, the impact of each feature gene for model was displayed by dependence plot, the interaction of each feature gene was displayed by interaction plot. Feature interaction strength was quantified by calculating Mean Absolute SHAP Interaction Value (Mabs) across all samples for every non-self-interacting pair. An additional model was trained using the feature gene expression levels and the proportions of immune cells (those correlated with all feature genes) as variables. The impact of features on model predictions was visualized using waterfall plots and force plots for two randomly selected samples from AA and Con samples in train set respectively.

The final XGBoost diagnostic model was deployed as an interactive web application using the “Shiny” framework.

Immunohistochemistry

Scalp samples were collected from five AA patients and five healthy people. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections (4 µm) were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol. For antigen retrieval, sections were submerged in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% H₂O₂. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PPP1R1C polyclonal antibody (Biorbyt, United States, Catalog No. orb673323) at a dilution of 1:100.

For quantitative analysis, the 40 × images were analyzed using ImageJ to determine the Average optical density (AOD), which were subjected to statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism (10.6.0), and differences between groups were evaluated via the Mann–Whitney U test. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
