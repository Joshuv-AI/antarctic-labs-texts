Magic of discrete lattice gauge theories
Gianluca Esposito, Simone Cepollaro, Luigi Cappiello, Alioscia Hamma

Abstract
Simulation of quantum field theories and fundamental interactions are one of the most challenging tasks in modern particle physics. Classical computers generally fail to reproduce accurate results when it comes to strongly coupled theories such as QCD. Recent developments in quantum technologies open up the possibility of simulating such physical regimes by using quantum computers. In this paper, we study the quantum resource related to the simulability of a quantum theory, i.e. non-stabilizerness for Lattice Gauge Theory (LGT) with discrete symmetry gauge groups. We show that enforcing gauge constraints for ℤ l \mathbb{Z}_{l} LGTs has no cost in terms of this resource and discuss the relation between non-abelianity of the gauge group with the average non-stabilizerness of the gauge invariant Hilbert space.



I
Introduction

Gauge theories describe fundamental interactions through the principle of local gauge invariance. This principle requires that the laws of physics remain unchanged under certain local transformations of the fields, leading to the existence of gauge bosons that mediate the forces.
The development of gauge theories and the Standard Model represents one of the most significant achievements in twentieth-century physics, culminating in a unified description of fundamental particles and forces. The concept of gauge invariance emerged from Weyl’s attempt to unify electromagnetism and general relativity
Weyl 1929
. Although unsuccessful, his work laid the foundation for future developments. In particular, the quantum revolution led to the development of quantum mechanics and early quantum field theory. However, in quantum field theory, matter and gauge fields, the mediators of the interactions, are described by infinite numbers of degrees of freedom and this leads almost immediately to the appearance of divergencies in the calculations of perturbative corrections to physical quantities. The solution to the problem of infinities was obtained in the 50s by Tomonaga
Tomonaga 1946
, Schwinger
Schwinger 1948
, and Feynman
Feynman 1949
who independently developed renormalized QED, establishing it as the first successful quantum gauge theory, based on the Abelian group
U(1)
.

The discovery of new particles led to the application of non-Abelian gauge groups to classify them in (irreducible) representations thereof, and the proposal that quarks are the constituents of hadrons, which are the particles capable of interacting through strong interactions.

Yang and Mills
Yang and Mills 1954
proposed the extension of gauge theories to non-Abelian groups in order to describe the weak and strong nuclear forces. Finally, the works of Glashow
Glashow 1961
, Weinberg
Weinberg 1967
and Salam
Salam 1968
led to the formulation of the Standard Model, based on the gauge group
SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)
, encompassing three of the four known fundamental forces: electromagnetic force (QED, mediated by massless photons), weak force (mediated by massive W and Z bosons) and strong force (QCD, mediated by massless gluons).

Again, to ascertain the consistency of the model, it was necessary to study whether it was renormalizable,
i
.e. to tackle the problem of infinities in non-Abelian gauge theories calculations. This was accomplished by ‘t Hooft and Veltman
’t Hooft and Veltman 1972
, while Gross and Wilczeck
Gross and Wilczek 1973
and Politzer
Politzer 1973
discovering the asymptotic freedom of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) were finally able to clarify the puzzling properties of strong interactions.

In this short summary, we repeatedly mentioned the difficulties of quantum field theories in dealing with infinite degrees of freedom in perturbative calculations. Lattice gauge theory (LGT) was proposed as a powerful non-perturbative approach to studying quantum field theories, particularly quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Following the seminal papers of K. Wilson in 1974
Wilson 1974
, it consisted in discretizing the spacetime onto a lattice, allowing for numerical calculations of observables in strongly coupling, where perturbative expansion fails (see, for instance,
Creutz 1983
and references therein). Gauge fields are represented as links between lattice sites in a way that preserves gauge invariance.

Kogut and Susskind (KS)
Kogut and Susskind 1975
;
Kogut 1979
proposed one of the first formulations of LGT for discrete and continuous (compact) groups such as
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
D_{3}
,
U(N)
and
SU(N)
(see
Zohar and Burrello 2015
for a complete overview of the Hamiltonian formulation of this model).
Recent advances in algorithms and computing power have significantly improved the precision of lattice calculations
Martinez et al. 2016
, making them an essential tool in theoretical physics.

In particular, LGT is an essential tool for investigating phase transitions in spin models
Fradkin and Susskind 1978
;
Fradkin et al. 1980
and the emergence of gauge bosons from quantum order of vacuum
Wen 2005
;
Levin and Wen 2006
.
The engineering of digital simulations of lattice gauge theories

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

, allows us to study totally unexplored regimes in the context of Standard Model

In recent years, LGT has attracted increasing attention
because the amount of resources needed for a simulation on the lattice is
drastically reduced as local Hilbert spaces shrink from being infinite-dimensional to
being finite-dimensional.

The objective of this paper is to study the
non-stabilizer
properties of the Hilbert spaces in lattice gauge theories. Non-stabilizerness is a property of quantum states related to the simulability of a theory: it was proven by Gottesman
Gottesman 1998
that there exists a set of states and operations, called stabilizer states and Clifford operations, which can be efficiently simulated by a classical computer, whereas non-stabilizer states and non-Clifford operations require exponential time for simulation, necessitating the speed-up provided by a quantum computer. We focus on the amount of non-Clifford resources required to enforce the gauge constraint on a lattice with a discrete Abelian group. In order to
quantify
these resources we use Stabilizer Entropies
Leone et al. 2022a
;
Wang and Li 2023
as measures of non-stabilizerness.
Stabilizer Entropies have proven to be an important tool in the study of quantum chaos
Leone et al. 2021a
;
Oliviero et al. 2021
, in decoding algorithms from black hole’s Hawking radiation
Leone et al. 2022b
;
Oliviero et al. 2024
and in the holographic framework of AdS/CFT correspondence
White et al. 2021
;
Cao et al. 2024
.

In simulations of gauge theories, one typically works with fundamental degrees of freedom. Gauge invariance is then imposed by constructing gauge-invariant states via quantum circuits. We investigate the average cost of this operation in terms of non-Clifford resources and point out the case in which using fundamental degrees of freedom has no cost with respect to the gauge-invariant ones.

The main result of the paper is that lattice gauge theories with a discrete abelian group do not require resources beyond Clifford operations for simulations and can therefore be efficiently reproduced by a classical computer. In contrast, we argue that non-abelian gauge theories should be more complex to simulate, and provide an example that demonstrates this increased complexity.

Stabilizer Entropy

II.1
Stabilizer Entropy

Consider
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2\otimes n}
the Hilbert space of a
n
-qubit system and its Pauli group
\mathbb{P}_{n}=\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\otimes n}
with
\mathbb{P}_{1}=\{\mathbbm{1},X,Y,Z\}
the Pauli group of a single qubit. The
Clifford group

\mathcal{C}(n)\subset\mathcal{U}(n)
is defined as the normalizer of the Pauli group

, namely,
\mathcal{C}(n):=\{C\in\mathcal{U}(n)\,,\,\text{s.t. }\,\forall P\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\,,CPC^{{\dagger}}=P^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\}
; in short,
\mathcal{C}(n)
is the group of unitary operators that transform a Pauli string into an other.

We define the

\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d=2^{n}}
of
\mathcal{H}
as the common eigenbasis of the operators
\{\mathbbm{1},Z\}^{\otimes n}
, and the set PSTAB of pure
stabilizer states
as the Clifford orbit of the computational basis:

{\operatorname{PSTAB}}=\{C\ket{i}\,,C\in\mathcal{C}(n)\}\,.

(1)

Stabilizer states and Clifford operations play a central role in quantum computation: the Gottesman-Knill theorem
Gottesman 1998
states that any quantum process represented by Clifford operations acting on initial stabilizer states can be simulated by a classical computer in polynomial time.
This implies that one needs states and gates beyond the stabilizer formalism to obtain quantum advantage in computation: the resource needed is referred to as
non-stabilizerness
or
magic
.

Recently,
\alpha
-Stabilizer Rényi Entropy
Leone et al. 2022a
has been proposed as an entropic measure of non-stabilizerness.

Define the probability distribution associated with the tomography of a
n
-qubit pure state
\psi=\ket{\psi}\hskip-2.84544pt\bra{\psi}
, namely
\Xi_{P}(\ket{\psi}):=d^{-1}\tr^{2}(P\ket{\psi}\hskip-2.84544pt\bra{\psi})
, with
P
being a Pauli operator.
The
\alpha
-Stabilizer Rényi Entropy (SE) is

M_{\alpha}(\psi):=(1-\alpha)^{-1}\log\sum_{P}\Xi_{P}^{\alpha}(\ket{\psi})-\log d

(2)

while its linear counterpart, the linear stabilizer entropy (LSE) is

\displaystyle M_{\operatorname{lin}}(\psi):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-d\|\Xi_{P}(\ket{\psi})\|^{2}_{2}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-d^{-1}\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}{n}}\tr^{4}(P\ket{\psi}\hskip-2.84544pt\bra{\psi})

Both measures are: (i) faithful, i.e.
M(\psi)=0\Leftrightarrow\psi\in{\operatorname{PSTAB}}
, otherwise
M(\psi)>0
; (ii) invariant under Clifford operators, namely
M(C\psi C^{\dagger})=M(\psi)
.

These measures quantify the non-stabilizerness of qubit states by the entropy of the state tomography: states with high values of SE require an exponential amount of classical resources to be simulated.
Unlike other measures of non-stabilizerness (see for example
Wang et al. 2019
;
Veitch et al. 2014
), SE and LSE do not require any minimization processes and can be both computed and measured in experiments
Oliviero et al. 2022
.
In this work we will focus on
M_{\operatorname{lin}}
due to our interest in Haar and Clifford averages. The linearity of
M_{\operatorname{lin}}
makes it particularly suitable to average techniques discussed in detail in
Leone et al. 2021b
.
Moreover,
\alpha
-SE (for
\alpha\geq 2
) and LSE have recently been validated as
magic monotones
in multi-qubit systems
Leone and Bittel 2024
. These measures serve as effective measures for non-stabilizer resources in various settings, highlighting their utility in the analysis of quantum systems.

II.2
Stabilizer Entropy gap

Our goal is to quantify the resources required to obtain a gauge-invariant Hilbert space. Typically, gauge invariance is enforced by projecting onto the subspace of states that are invariant under the action of a gauge group. To address this, we first establish a general framework for measuring the resources associated with any projection map, not limited to those associated with gauge invariance. Then, we will apply this framework to the specific case of gauge-invariant subspaces, considering both abelian and non-abelian symmetry groups.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}=\Pi\mathcal{H}
, where
\Pi
is a projector, and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}^{\perp}
. Let us define the two basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}}=\{\ket{j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{\Pi}}
with
d_{\Pi}={\rm rank}(\Pi)\leq d
.
For prime dimension
l
, a natural generalization of the Pauli group is given by the Weyl-Heisenberg group
Gross 2006

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{1}=\left<\{D_{(p,q)}\}\right>=\{\tau^{a}D_{(p,q)}\}\quad\text{with}\quad a,p,q\in Z_{l}

(4)

i.e. the group generated by the
l^{2}
operators defined as

D_{(p,q)}=\tau^{-pq}Z^{p}X^{q}\quad\text{with}\quad\tau=e^{\frac{i}{l}\pi}\quad p,q\in Z_{d}

(5)

and

X\ket{j}=\ket{j\oplus_{l}1}\quad Z\ket{j}=\omega^{j}\ket{j}\quad\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{l}}\,.

(6)

The construction of Weyl-Heisenberg operators generalizes straightforwardly for
n
qudit systems via tensor product:

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{(l)}=\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{(l)}\otimes\underbrace{\dots}_{n}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{(l)}

(7)

For even or odd-nonprime dimensions, the set generated by the operators in expression (
5
) are not guaranteed to have an inverse, so the set fails to be a group: this is due to the fact that, for
l
prime,
\mathbb{Z}_{l}
is an
algebraic field
, so our qudits are actually described by a
l
-dimensional Hilbert space, which is not the case for non-prime
l

However, it is possible to obtain a description for generic number of levels by tensor product of Hilbert spaces of dimensions equal to the prime decomposition of the desired number, along with the corresponding Weyl-Heisenberg operators for each factor. Hence, for the remainder of this work we are going to stick with prime dimensions (2 included) without loss of generality.
The stabilizer entropies introduced in the previous subsection have an analogous form when the Pauli decomposition is substituted with the Weyl-Heisenberg decomposition
Wang and Li 2023
.

To facilitate future discussions, we introduce the projector

Q=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{D_{(p,q)}\in\mathcal{D}^{(l)}_{n}}(D_{(p,q)}\otimes D_{(p,q)}^{{\dagger}})^{\otimes 2}

(8)

with
d=l^{n}
.

This projector plays a crucial role in the subsequent analysis.
Indeed, we can use it to define a more concise expression for the Linear Stabilizer entropy:

M_{\operatorname{lin}}(\psi)=1-d\tr(Q\psi^{\otimes 4})

(9)

In general, the projection onto a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}
of a larger Hilbert space has a cost in terms of non-stabilizer resources. We are interested in quantifying the average amount of non-Clifford resources associated with the projection onto a
gauge-invariant
subspace. To this end, we use the
Stabilizer entropy gap
(SE gap)

\Delta M(\mathcal{H}_{G}):=M_{\operatorname{lin}}^{0}-M_{\operatorname{lin}}^{G}=\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}M_{\operatorname{lin}}^{0}(\psi_{U_{G}})-\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}M_{\operatorname{lin}}^{G}(\psi_{U_{G}})

(10)

with
\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}
denoting the Haar average over the unitary operators acting on the gauge invariant Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G}=\Pi_{G}\mathcal{H}
, and
\psi_{U_{G}}=U_{G}\psi U_{G}^{\dagger}

M_{\operatorname{lin}}^{i}(\psi):=1-d_{i}\tr(Q^{i}\psi^{\otimes 4})\,,i\in\{0,G\}

(11)

denoting the non-stabilizer resources calculated respectively in the Weyl-Heisenberg structures of the original Hilbert space and in the gauge-invariant subspace.

The SE gap measures the non-stabilizer resources required to project onto a subspace in a

manner. Given that the difference in SE is averaged over all possible gauge-invariant states, a nonzero value of the gap indicates an inherent incompatibility between the Weyl-Heisenberg operators structures of the ambient space and the projected gauge-invariant subspace.

Using the expression given by

9

\Delta M(\mathcal{H}_{G})=d_{G}\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}\tr(Q^{G}\psi_{U_{G}}^{\otimes 4})-d_{0}\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}\tr(Q^{0}\psi_{U_{G}}^{\otimes 4})\,.

(12)

By applying Lemma 1 from

\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}\tr(Q^{0}\psi_{U_{G}}^{\otimes 4})=c(d_{0},d_{G})\mathbb{E}_{U_{0}}\tr(Q^{0}\Pi_{G}^{\otimes 4}\psi_{U_{0}}^{\otimes 4})

(13)

c(d_{0},d_{G})=\binom{d_{0}+3}{4}\binom{d_{G}+3}{4}^{-1}\,.

(14)

For future convenience, we show the condition for a zero gap invariant subspace: simple manipulation of the previous expressions shows that

III
Short overview of Lattice Gauge Theory

In this work, we use a model of Lattice Gauge Theory (LGT) inspired by the Kogut-Susskind formulation
Kogut and Susskind 1975
, where matter fields reside on the vertices of a square lattice and gauge fields mediate interactions along the links. However, since our primary focus is on a pure gauge theory, we concentrate on the behavior and properties of the links Hilbert space. The model we investigate draws inspiration from the Toric code proposed by Kitaev
Kitaev 2003
. Kitaev proposes a model in which the gauge-invariant subspace is characterized by projectors
\Pi_{G}
that are written in terms of
star operators
which strictly depend on the gauge group G. This approach allows us to construct the gauge-invariant Hilbert space as
\mathcal{H}_{G}=\Pi_{G}\mathcal{H}
, thereby providing a robust framework for exploring the average resources within a quantum gauge theory.

III.1
Gauge invariant Hilbert space

The initial element required to formulate a lattice gauge theory (LGT) is a symmetry group G. In this discussion, we choose to focus on the case of
discrete groups
, whose finite nature permits a straightforward description of the Hilbert space associated with the lattice. This choice allows us to analyze the non-stabilizer resources that characterize a gauge invariant physical system within a very concise mathematical setting.

Each link is endowed with a local

\mathcal{H}_{l}

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{l}^{\otimes N}

III.2
Lattice orientation and non-abelian gauge groups

Consider a lattice
\mathcal{L}
composed of sites
s
and links
l
. Typically, it is essential to work with oriented graphs; this necessity arises from the physical interpretation of a link as a gauge current moving between sites. In this framework, the gauge invariant condition, enforced by the Gauss constraints at each site, ensures current conservation. Although the orientation of the lattice is not particularly important for abelian groups, it becomes a necessary technical tool for non-abelian gauge simmetry when it comes to the definition of local operators defined on the links. Nontheless, it is worth noticing that lattice orientation is a convention that bears no physical significance as long as it is coherently applied throughout the calculations. States in the Hilbert space should indeed incorporate this orientation information, as the change in orientation corresponds to the inversion of the current:

\ket{h}_{l}\to\ket{h^{-1}}_{l}

(24)

For non-abelian groups, altering the orientation of a link also modifies the group action, represented by the local adjoint operator
X(g)^{{\dagger}}
.
However, in the context of our current analysis, the orientation of the lattice does not significantly influence the results; since gauge invariance is achieved by summing the star operators over all group elements , changing the orientation does not influence the structure of the sum, hence leaving the gauge-invariant space invariant. The orientation would become particularly relevant when gauge-invariant operators, such as the plaquette operator or a Hamiltonian operator, are studied. Although this is not the main focus of this work, these considerations will be important in future developments of our research.

This means that the Pauli structure of the gauge-invariant Hilbert space can be faithfully reproduced by the Pauli structure of the total Hilbert space with no cost in terms of nonstabilizer resources. A non-vanishing gap would imply that a speed-up provided by a quantum processor should be necessary to efficiently simulate any process of the theory starting from the total Hilbert space.

V
LSE GAP of the
Z_{l}
lattice gauge theory

In this section we generalize the previous result, extending our consideration to the group
Z_{l}=\{0,g_{1},\ldots,g_{l-1}\}
,
\left|{G}\right|=l
.
Each link of the lattice
L
is now equipped with a qudit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{l}=\mathbb{C}^{l}=\text{span}\{\ket{0=e_{G}},\ket{1=g_{1}},\ldots\ket{l-1=g_{l-1}}
.

At this point, in order to study the non-stabilizer properties of this type of system, we need to restrict our analysis to prime
l
: by doing so, the Weyl-Heisenberg group is well defined
Gross 2006
and we can use the generalization of stabilizer entropies for a qudit Hilbert space.

Given the group algebra basis, we can write the representation of group elements
\{e,g,\ldots,g_{l-1}\}
as powers of the Weyl-Heisenberg operator
\operatorname{X}

\displaystyle X(e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{0}\bra{0}+\ldots\ket{l-1}\bra{l-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{l}

(33)

\displaystyle X(g_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{0}\bra{1}+\ldots\ket{l-1}\bra{1}=\operatorname{X}

(34)

\displaystyle\ldots

(35)

\displaystyle X(g_{l-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{0}\bra{d-1}+\ldots\ket{l-1}\bra{l-2}=\operatorname{X}^{l-1}

(36)

Accordingly, the star operators on a single site s of the square lattice read

\displaystyle A_{s}(e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{l_{1}^{(s)}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l_{2}^{(s)}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l_{3}^{(s)}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{l_{4}^{(s)}}

(37)

\displaystyle A_{s}(g_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{l_{1}^{(s)}}\otimes X_{l_{2}^{(s)}}\otimes X_{l_{3}^{(s)}}\otimes X_{l_{4}^{(s)}}

(38)

\displaystyle\ldots

(39)

\displaystyle A_{s}(g_{l-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{l_{1}^{(s)}}^{l-1}\otimes X_{l_{2}^{(s)}}^{l-1}\otimes X_{l_{3}^{(s)}}^{l-1}\otimes X_{l_{4}^{(s)}}^{l-1}

(40)

Therefore, we can define the group generated by the star operators at all sites of the graph as a subgroup of commuting Heisenberg Weyl operators
\mathcal{G}:=\langle\{A_{s}(g)\,,g\in Z_{l},s\in\mathcal{L}\}\rangle
. By doing so, the projector into the gauge invariant Hilbert space reads

\Pi_{G}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{G}}D\,.

(41)

To calculate the magic gap we need to compute the terms
\tr Q^{0}\Pi_{\text{sym}}^{0}\Pi_{G}^{\otimes 4}
and
\tr Q^{G}\Pi_{\text{sym}}^{G}
. Direct calculation (see
C
for the details) leads to:

\displaystyle\tr Q^{0}\Pi_{\text{sym}}^{0}\Pi_{G}^{\otimes 4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\left[\frac{3d_{0}^{2}}{\left|{G}\right|^{3}}+\frac{12d_{0}}{\left|{G}\right|^{2}}+\frac{9}{\left|{G}\right|}\right]

(42)

\displaystyle\tr Q^{G}\Pi_{\text{sym}}^{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\bigg[3d_{G}^{2}+(1-\text{mod}_{2}(l))(4^{n}-1)

(43)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 12d_{G}+9\bigg]

Notice that, in this calculation, we did not restrict to the case of odd dimensions, in order to prove, as a consistency check, that in the limit
l\to 2
, the formalism was consistent with the Pauli case of
Z_{2}
.

We conclude this analysis on abelian groups, by saying that the magic gap of
\mathbb{Z}_{l}
lattice gauge theory is:

\Delta M(\mathcal{H}_{Z_{l}})=d_{G}\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}\tr Q_{G}\psi_{U_{G}}^{\otimes 4}-d_{0}\mathbb{E}_{U_{G}}\tr Q^{0}\psi_{U_{G}}^{\otimes 4}=0

(44)

VI
LSE gap for a non-abelian group: SU(2)

We proceed with our discussion by analyzing a significant difference between what we obtained with abelian groups and an example coming from
Cepollaro et al. 2024
, concerning the
SU(2)
-gauge invariant Hilbert space on the site of a lattice.

Consider an open lattice composed of one site with four links. At each link, we attach a Hilbert space associated to an irreducible representation of
SU(2)
: in particular, choosing the fundamental (spin
\frac{1}{2})
representation leads to a qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}}
on each link, similar to the case of the
Z_{2}
gauge theory.

The non-gauge invariant Hilbert space is given by the tensor product of the Hilbert space of the four links, that is,
\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\otimes 4}
. Imposing gauge invariance requires Peter-Weyl decomposition of this tensor product structure, which reads:

\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\otimes 4}=2\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus 3\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}

(45)

which corresponds to two singlets, three triplets and one quintuplet Hilbert spaces.
The gauge invariant subspace is obtained via group averaging and corresponds to the singlet Hilbert space
2\mathcal{H}_{0}
, which is a
2
-dimensional Hilbert space. This means that the rank-
2
projector
\Pi_{SU(2)}
that realizes
2\mathcal{H}_{0}=\Pi_{SU(2)}\mathcal{H}_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\otimes 4}
can be used to calculate the magic gap, with the exact same procedure as we showed in
A
and
C
.
In
Cepollaro et al. 2024
, it was shown that this system with non-abelian gauge symmetry exhibits a non-vanishing magic gap.

In particular, for single site lattice, the LSE gap is

\Delta M(\mathcal{H}_{SU(2)})=8/45\,,

(46)

which means that
SU(2)
gauge invariance needs nonstabilizer resources to be implemented.

VII
Conclusions

Discrete abelian gauge theories seem to not require non-stabilizer resources for their simulation. This would mean that enforcing the gauge constraint on a lattice does not require computational costs, enabling efficient simulation of the theory by using only classical resources, by focusing on the non gauge-invariant degrees of freedom.

On the other hand, the same efficiency doesn’t seem to be achievable for non-abelian gauge theories. In this paper we shortly discussed a non-abelian counterexample (
SU(2)
fundamental representation) where the resource cost is non-zero, indicating a significant difference in the computational requirements. This distinction suggests that non-abelian gauge theories inherently demand more computational resources.

These observations may point towards a broader characterization of gauge theories based on the interplay between simulability and non-commutativity. We expect non-abelianity of the gauge group directly affects the computational cost in terms of non-stabilizer resource. This effect is expected to mirror the way in which non-abelian structures influence certain phenomena associated with another resource, namely entanglement, i.e. asymmetric behavior in the Page curve in many body systems with
SU(2)
symmetry
Bianchi et al. 2024
.
Further investigation would focus on formally proving this intuition and pointing out the feature of non-abelian theory increasing its computational cost by looking at the interplay between the Clifford group and continuous non-abelian symmetry groups
Mitsuhashi and Yoshioka 2023
. This potential characterization could provide valuable insight into the resource demands of different gauge theories and guide the development of more efficient simulation algorithms, as well as a more efficient resource use for experimental implementations of said LGTs, as it is known that magic also induces upper bounds on the maximum achievable fidelity of experimental realizations of quantum states
Cepollaro et al. 2024
;
Leone et al. 2023
.

\displaystyle=\ket{j\oplus_{l}1}

(65)

\displaystyle Z\ket{j}

\displaystyle=\omega^{j}\ket{j}\,,

(66)

D_{(p,q)}=\tau^{-pq}Z^{p}X^{q}\quad\text{with}\quad\tau=e^{\frac{i}{l}\pi}\quad p,q\in Z_{d}

(68)

D_{(a,b)}D_{(c,d)}=\tau^{bc-ad}D_{(b+c,a+d)}

(69)

\tr(D_{(a,b)}D^{\dagger}_{(c,d)})=l\delta_{a,c}\delta_{b,d}.

(70)

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{1}=\left<\{D_{(p,q)}\}\right>=\{\tau^{a}D_{(p,q)}\}\quad\text{with}\quad a,p,q\in Z_{l}

(71)

n

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{n}=\{\tau^{b}D_{(p_{1},q_{1})}\otimes D_{(p_{2},q_{2})}\otimes\ldots D_{(p_{n},q_{n})}\}\quad\text{with}\quad b,p_{i},q_{i}\in Z_{l}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(72)

We can define the Clifford group for n qudit as the normalizer of the discrete HW group

C_{n}^{(l)}:=\{C\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})\,\,:\,\,C\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{n}C^{{\dagger}}=\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{n}\}

(73)

The stabilizer entropies
M_{\alpha}
can thus be generalized for a
n
-qudit system and, in particular, if we define the quotient

\mathcal{D}^{(l)}_{n}=\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(l)}_{n}}{\tau^{b}\mathbbm{1}}

(74)

and the projector
Q=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{D_{(p,q)}\in\mathcal{D}^{(l)}_{n}}(D_{(p,q)}\otimes D_{(p,q)}^{{\dagger}})^{\otimes 2}

the usual definition of the Linear Stabilizer entropy still holds for qudits:

M_{lin(\psi)}=1-d\tr(Q\psi^{\otimes 4})

(75)
