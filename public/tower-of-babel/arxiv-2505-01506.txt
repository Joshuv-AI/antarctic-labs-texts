Microwave-field quantum metrology with error correction enabled by Rydberg atoms
Stanisław Kurzyna, Bartosz Niewelt, Mateusz Mazelanik, Wojciech Wasilewski, Rafał Demkowicz-Dobrzański, Michał Parniak

Abstract
Quantum sensing and metrology present one of the most promising near-term applications in the field of quantum technologies, with quantum sensors enabling unprecedented precision in measurements of electric, magnetic or gravitational fields and displacements. Experimental loss at the detection stage remains one of the key obstacles to achieve a truly quantum advantage in many practical scenarios. Here we combine capabilities of Rydberg atoms to both sense external fields and to be used for quantum information processing to largely overcome the issue of detection losses. While using the large dipole moments of Rydberg atoms in an ensemble to achieve a 39 nV cm − 1 Hz − 1 / 2 39\text{\,}\mathrm{nV}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1/2} sensitivity we employ the inter-atomic dipolar interactions to execute an error-correction protocol, that protects sensing information against conventional losses at the detection stage. Counterintuitively, the idea of the protocol is based on introduction of additional non-linear lossy quantum channel, which results in enhancement of Fisher information by a factor of 3.3. The presented results open up a path towards broader usage of quantum-information-inspired error correction in practical quantum metrology and communication, without the need for a general-purpose quantum computer.

For example, imperfections in experimental setups and non-ideal measurements introduce losses, which cause photon-loss error and are often difficult to mitigate. Optimal estimation in real conditions is captured by the Cramér-Rao bound
Kay 1993
, which ties the amount of Fisher information (FI) with the maximum possible precision of the measurement. In case of estimation of a parameter encoded in a quantum state
\rho_{\theta}
, the ultimate precision limit is set by the quantum Fisher information (QFI)
\mathcal{F}_{Q}\left(\rho_{\theta}\right)

Helstrom 1976
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
:

\Delta\theta\geq 1/\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{Q}\left(\rho_{\theta}\right)},

(1)

where optimization over all measurement schemes is taken into account. In typical situations where detection losses act independently on each sensing particle, and no error-correction procedure is implemented, the obtainable information is reduced to
\eta\,\mathcal{F}_{Q}
, where
\eta
is the detection efficiency.

In this paper we build on the idea that one may apply an additional quantum operation on the state, before it is subject to lossy measurement
Len et al. 2022
, making it less supceptible to losses and in this way protecting the encoded information. This operation may be viewed as a form of an error-correction protocol, as the applied operation modifies the way the sensing information is encoded in the state, so that even after experiencing losses it may still be recovered.

Remarkably, the operation we propose introduces in fact
additional non-linear losses
to the setup, that nevertheless, make the sensing particles more robust to subsequent detection losses, and effectively increases the FI.
Furthermore, using novel tools, designed to identify the optimal quantum metrological protocols in presence of noise, we show that the proposed approach is optimal in the simplest non-trivial case of two sensing particles, proving no other quantum protocol, no matter how general, could offer better performance given the level of detection losses present.

We demonstrate an experimental implementation of this protocol using collectively excited Rydberg states, where the key element are interatomic interactions and the sensitivity to weak microwave fields is enabled by the huge dipole moments. The collective states allow excellent coupling to light and the large size of the atomic ensemble enables usage of many sensors particles in each experimental shot. The error-correcting operation is based on dipolar Rydberg interactions, hence taking advantage of various aspects of intricate Rydberg physics simultaneously. We also take inspiration from the progress on lifetime prolongation methods and use a novel protocol that stops the thermal decoherence caused by atomic motion making long-lasting interactions possible.
With this, we achieve both excellent and quantum-enhanced sensitivity to an external microwave field, showing a direct application of error correction or mitigation in quantum metrology
Takagi et al. 2022
.

Results

II.1
Model

We will start by introducing a toy model that demonstrates an advantage in a practical protocol via a simple error-correcting operation, for a case of two sensor particles.
Let us consider a simple model, where we assume that we are working in Hilbert space limited to two or fewer bosonic excitations distributed among two modes with corresponding creation operators
\hat{d}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{p}^{\dagger}
.
We use spinwaves - collective Rydberg excitations, where one excitation can be represented as a collective superposition
\ket{1,0}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}\ket{0,0}=1/\sqrt{N}\sum_{k}\ket{g_{1}\ldots d_{k}\ldots g_{N}}
of
k
-th atom being in
\ket{d}
state and the rest in ground state
\ket{g}
. States
\ket{d}
and
\ket{p}
represent two Rydberg states of an atom, in particular in our case with the orbital quantum number
l=2
and
l=1
, respectively. Throughout the work, as a sensor particle we understand a single collective Rydberg excitation, which corresponds to a single delocalized and excited atom.

The protocol is represented by a diagram in Fig.
1
(a).
The system is initially prepared in state
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{2,0}=\hat{d}^{\dagger 2}/\sqrt{2}\ket{0,0}
. The estimated parameter
\theta
is encoded in a unitary operation taking the form
U(\theta)=\exp\left(-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\,\theta/2\sum_{k}\hat{\sigma}_{dp}^{k}+h.c\right)
, where
\hat{\sigma}_{dp}^{k}=\ket{g\ldots d_{k}\ldots g}\bra{g\ldots p_{k}\ldots g}
is a ladder operator acting on the
k
-th atom. This operation represents a Rabi rotation between internal states
\ket{p}
and
\ket{d}
which we associate with collective modes
p
and
d
. For the collective state, the parameter is effectively encoded in a Bogoliubov transformation of the operators:
\hat{p}\rightarrow\cos(\theta/2)\hat{p}+i\sin(\theta/2)\hat{d}
and
\hat{d}\rightarrow\cos(\theta/2)\hat{d}-i\sin(\theta/2)\hat{p}
. For our initial
|2,0\rangle
state, the parameter encoding in general results in a superposition of all states from the two-particle manifold:

|\psi\rangle=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|2,0\rangle+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(\theta)|1,1\rangle-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|0,2\rangle

(2)

Information about the parameter is now encoded in the amplitudes of the above state, and simple measurement of populations
\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}
and
\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}
is then enough to optimally estimate
\theta
and saturate the quantum Cramer-Rao bound (
1
). The FI in such measurement equals the QFI for this state
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{Q}=2
.

Let us now include detection losses in the model
and analyze their effect on various terms in Eq. (
2
) (see Fig. 1b).
In particular, provided at most single particle is lost, the
\ket{2,0}
and
\ket{0,2}
components
follow distinguishable decay paths, and hence if only these components were present, information encoded in amplitudes of these terms would not be lost. At the detection stage, this would lead to a lower number of photons detected but no loss of sensitivity. However, if
\ket{1,1}
component is present, its single-particle decay process leads to the same output states as in case of the remaining components, making it impossible to recover full information on the original amplitudes. In fact,
it is possible, that one may be better off preemptively removing the
\ket{1,1}
component (even if there was some non-zero information on the parameter in its amplitude), and in this way is able to recover full information encoded in the amplitudes of
\ket{0,2}
and
\ket{2,0}
components, despite presence of loss.

Hence, following this intuition, let us consider a non-unitary operation
\Lambda
, involving interaction (non-linear losses) between excitations in different modes, that is parametrized by Kraus operators
\hat{K}_{0}=\hat{p}\hat{d}
and
\hat{K}_{1}=\sqrt{1-\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}
and is applied after the parameter encoding stage.
The result of the interaction is depicted in Fig.
1
(b). The proposed operation removes the troublesome component
\ket{1,1}
,
while preserving components with distinct decay paths (
\ket{2,0}
and

\ket{0,2}
). A numerical evolution of the system for
\eta=2\%
results in a steepened expectation values of excitation numbers as shown in Fig.
1
(d). The output state exhibits a remarkable increase in the precision of estimation of
\theta
, which manifests in increased QFI for some values of the parameter, despite the lossy character of the interactions. Furthermore, the advantage is observed for various efficiencies, calculated results are shown in Fig.
1
(c). Finally, we are also able to use a semi-definite program
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
;
Dulian et al. 2025
to prove that the proposed error mitigation protocol is in fact optimal (see Methods).

II.2
Idea of the experiment

Schematic rendition of the experimental protocol.
(a) Atoms are initially prepared in a collective superposition of a Rydberg state
\ket{d}
. Later, a microwave field drives transition coupling a different Rydberg state. The strength of the microwave field encodes parameter
\theta
onto the atomic superposition. Dipolar interactions cause the atomic coherence to be dephased, forbidding their read-out.
(b) Same Rydberg state vdW interactions are considerably weaker than the dipolar interactions between different states. This is due to both the selection of the principle quantum number, as well as the large spatial extent of the atomic ensemble promoting the long range dipolar contribution as opposed to the short-range vdW contribution. Thus, decoherence is strongly suppressed if all excitations are in either
\ket{p}
or
\ket{d}
state.

We implement the described quantum error-correcting channel with a Rydberg-atom ensemble. Atoms in the same Rydberg states exhibit a short-range van der Waals (vdW) type interaction scaling with distance as
r^{-6}
. This manifests in their remarkable properties crucial for implementation of quantum gates
Evered et al. 2023
and single photon generation
Shi et al. 2022
. However, once two species of Rydberg atoms are introduced, due to their different electric dipole moments, they interact considerably stronger with dipole potential scaling with distance as
r^{-3}
, as shown in Fig.
2
(b). This state-dependent behavior can be used to engineer a protocol for the interaction-enhanced metrology.

Figure
2
(a) shows an exemplary experimental implementation. The atoms are initially prepared in a collective superposition (spinwave) of a Rydberg state
\ket{d}
. Then, a microwave (MW) field is applied, which drives the Rabi oscillation between the two internal Rydberg states
\ket{d}
and
\ket{p}
. The number of excitations is chosen in a way, that the dominant type of interactions are dipolar (see
Methods
). Once in a superposition, the collective excitations interact with each other and dephase, rendering them insensitive to the read-out. For example, a
\ket{2,0}
or
\ket{0,2}
component of the state remains unaffected by the dipolar interaction, while the
\ket{1,1}
component is subject to strong dephasing. Lastly, we probe the state of the spinwave via a lossy measurement.

II.3
Super Rabi oscillations

In the experiment, we use a weak coherent state to excite on average
n_{0}
Rydberg spin-wave excitations in the
|d\rangle
internal state, yielding
|\sqrt{n_{0}},0\rangle
. After the Rabi rotation, the system achieves a bi-coherent, separable state
\ket{\sqrt{n_{0}}\cos(\theta/2),{i\mkern 1.0mu}\sqrt{n_{0}}\sin(\theta/2)}
, still with mean number of excitations
n_{0}
. We then proceed to implement to operation
\Lambda
via interactions. The system will evolve with a complex many-body Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\sum_{\mu<\nu}\hat{\sigma}_{dp}^{\mu}\hat{\sigma}_{pd}^{\nu}V^{\mu\nu}_{dip}+h.c.

(3)

The energy of two resonantly interacting Rydberg excitations is equal to the energy of two dipoles separated by a distance
R
, oriented along the propagation axis
z
:

V_{dip}^{\mu\nu}(R,\theta)=\frac{C_{3}}{R^{3}}\frac{3\cos(2\theta)+1}{4}

(4)

With
\theta
being the angle between the axis connecting the two dipoles and the
z
axis.
The long-range character of the Hamiltonian does not allow for any simple treatment, but we may adopt a simplified model assuming that we are in fact only interested in the flat spin-wave state (
k=0
). Furthermore, we shall drop any correlations or entanglement arising in the system. Under these assumptions, we extend a simple two-particle solution to the many-body system, as detailed in the
Methods
section. In essence, we observe that each excitation in the
d
mode contributes to dephasing of
p
spin waves, and vice versa. Thus, the simplified solution for the excitation-number averages in both modes is:

\begin{cases}\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}=n_{0}\cos(\theta/2)^{2}\exp\left({-n_{0}\sin(\theta/2)^{2}\gamma\tau}\right)\\
\braket{\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}}=n_{0}\sin(\theta/2)^{2}\exp\left({-n_{0}\cos(\theta/2)^{2}\gamma\tau}\right)\end{cases}

(5)

Where
\tau
is the interaction time and
\gamma
is decay rate set by the dipole interaction strength and the geometry of the system. The total decay for mode
p
/
d
is thus a product of the basic decay rate
\gamma
and the number of ”control" excitations in the other mode. The simplified two-particle model (
Methods
section) allows us to make a prediction for the basic decay rate:

\gamma=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{9\sqrt{3}}\frac{C_{3}}{\hbar\mathcal{V}}

(6)

where
\mathcal{V}
is the effective volume of the atomic ensemble.
The magnitude of the interactions depends on the time
\tau
the two species of excitations interact with each other, which is limited by the memory lifetime. To obtain the greatest advantage over the detection losses, it is necessary that the interaction time is chosen as the longest possible. As a result, the Rabi oscillation of the population between the two collective Rydberg states becomes much steeper, we will refer to them as super Rabi oscillations. Normally, this would come at a cost of increased excitation number variance, however, the lossy measurement cancels it out.

II.5
Field estimation via Rabi oscillations

To benchmark the metrological advantage of the presented protocol, we utilized the FI approach. In this way, we can bound the limit of the estimation precision of the oscillation angle, which corresponds to the limit of the weakest possible detectable MW field. This is captured by the Cramér-Rao bound:

\Delta^{2}\theta\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{\theta}},\ \mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{P_{\theta}(n)}\left(\frac{\partial P_{\theta}(n)}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}

(7)

The analytical formula for the FI for the super Rabi oscillation can be derived from the phenomenological model of interacting spinwaves by calculating the probability distribution
P_{\theta}(n)
of the mean number of detected photons (see
Methods
).
The precision of the experimental measurements can be described with the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation, which allows to quantify how well the Cramér-Rao bound is saturated by the proposed scheme.
In Fig.
5
(a) we compare the mean value of the ML estimator with the value of the set oscillation angle. The red shaded area represents the inverse of the FI for the experimental parameters obtained from the analytical formula. Blue shaded area corresponds to the variance of the ML estimator. Both values are shown per 100 detected photons.
Fig.
5
(c) shows the bias of the estimator. The shaded area is the variance of the ML estimator, proving the unbiasedness of the estimator.
Fig.
5
(b) compares the theoretical value of FI with the inverse of the variance of the ML estimator. Values on the plot are normalized by the efficiency of the detection and per 1 photon. The black line represents the standard value of the classical FI, ie. without the interaction-induced enhancement.
The analytical formula for the FI shows the improvement of the amount of the information carried by the photon in the lossy detection (derivation in the
Methods
section). Here, it can be seen that the precision of the ML estimation obtained from the experimental values follows very well the theoretical predictions.
We achieved per photon FI
\mathcal{F}=$3.3(0.3)$
, over a threefold improvement in measurement precision compared to the standard limit of QFI for lossy measurement.

The improvement in the estimation precision allows to benchmark the detection capabilities of the presented protocol.
We can define the minimal variance of the MW Rabi frequency which translates to the minimal variance of the detectable electric field according to the formula

\sqrt{\Delta^{2}E_{MW}}=\Delta E_{MW}=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}\theta}}{T}\frac{\hbar}{d_{dp}}

(8)

where
T
is the MW pulse duration corresponding to the oscillation angle with the highest estimation precision and
d_{dp}
is transition dipole moment between
\ket{d}
and
\ket{p}
states. The minimal uncertainty for the phase
\Delta^{2}\theta
is defined per a single realization of the experiment, therefore it may be related to the FI as
\Delta^{2}\theta\geq 1/(F\bar{n})
, where
\bar{n}=$1.1(0.1)$
is the average number of detected photons per pulse.
We were able to achieve the estimation precision
\Delta E_{MW}=$44\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro V}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
per single experimental shot.

Rydberg sensors operating in a continuous mode are characterized by the noise spectral density rather than precision achieved per a single shot.
If we treat the atomic ensemble as the MW field detector, we can also define its best sensitivity
S_{E_{MW}}=\Delta E_{MW}\sqrt{T}=$39\text{\,}\mathrm{nV}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1/2}$
assuming a 100% duty cycle of signal acquisition. A high duty cycle of at least 50% would be practically feasible.

III
Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated the interaction enhanced metrology scheme which allows for error mitigation in lossy experiments.
We have theoretically showed the advantage of introducing the additional non-linear losses before the detection losses for two particles model. We quantified the advantage of this operation using QFI approach and showed that it’s the optimal operation for two excitation system.

To experimentally realize the postulated optimal operation, we have presented the model of two interacting dipoles, allowing to engineer the interaction-induced decay between the pair of Rydberg excitations. The theoretically predicted decay rate
\gamma
is consistent with the experimentally measured values allowing to propose the phenomenological model to quantify the evolution of coherent state stored in the quantum memory (see
Methods
for details), allowing for the interaction-enhanced metrology protocol.
The short lifetime of the Rydberg spinwaves is extended via momentum cancellation coherence transfer achieved with two crossed state-selective beams.
The resulting long coherence time allowed the interactions between Rydberg pairs resurface and the phenomenon of super Rabi oscillations was observed.
The theoretically predicted advantage manifested as steeper decay of oscillations, allows to estimate the precision of measurement. Using maximum likelihood estimation we compared the estimated precision with the theoretical limit introduced by Cramér-Rao bound.
We have achieved the FI per detected photon
\mathcal{F}=$3.3(0.3)$
, showing over threefold improvement as compared to standard procedure without additional losses before detection.

While current parameters allow to perform the protocol, there are numerous aspects that could be explored more to increase the advantage and improve the efficiency of the experiment.
The first obvious improvement could be increasing the optical depth of the atomic ensemble to increase the efficiency of the protocol. This would also allow to generate more Rydberg spin waves, allowing for a larger improvement in the FI per photon.
Also, the temperature of the atomic memory could be lowered to reduce the residual thermal motion.
At the end, fully harnessing the potential of Rydberg lifetime extension protocol would require a cryogenic environment
Cantat-Moltrecht et al. 2020
;
Muni et al. 2022
.

While the presented sensitivity of
39\text{\,}\mathrm{nV}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1/2}
is on par with the best Rydberg sensors based on hot atoms, it could be straightforwardly improved by using more atoms and exciting more spin waves in the ensemble, which requires better ensemble preparation and more power of the control beam than has been available in this study.
Apart from raw performance improvements, we envisage two major development directions for our research.
Firstly, more advanced sensing protocols and secondly, enhancing the role of interactions up to sensing singular excitations.
For a sufficiently strong decay rate
\gamma
, we will clearly be able to distinguish cases of one versus zero sensor particles being excited (Rabi-rotated) be the external microwave field. This presents an overarching opportunity to design quantum-enhanced microwave sensors operating in the fully quantum regime.

Methods

VII.1
Optimality of error mitigation

Here we consider the two-particle toy model introduced in the main text, described by Kraus operators
\hat{K}_{0}
and
\hat{K}_{1}
defined there.
One may wonder if the applied protocol is optimal.
To answer this question, let us consider a general quantum operation
\Lambda
on the sensing system (general completely positive map
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
), that acts after the parameter encoding stage
U(\theta)
and before the state experience detection losses
\Xi(\eta)
. Let us define the
ultimate
QFI as:

\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{\diamond}=\max_{\Lambda}\mathcal{F}_{Q}\left[\Xi(\eta)\circ\Lambda\circ U(\theta)\ket{\psi_{0}}\right],

(9)

which represents the maximal QFI that we can achieve if we optimize the scheme over general error-correcting operation
\Lambda
on the system—note that formally here, apart from including the detection losses, we do not assume any particular measurement being performed in the end, which makes the approach more general.

The above optimization problem can be solved via an iterative optimization procedure as described in
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
, where each step is a simple semi-definite programme—unlike in
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
, here we do not optimize the whole estimation strategy, including input probe and control operations described in a form of a quantum comb, but just the error-correcting operation
\Lambda
, with input probe state fixed and no additional ancillary systems allowed. We have used the code
Dulian et al. 2025
based on the procedure from Ref.
Kurdziałek et al. 2025
. Thanks to the relatively small dimension of the problem considered (states with at most two total excitations in both modes form a 6 dimensional Hilbert space) the optimization is very efficient. Remarkably, the numerically found optimal solution coincides perfectly with the simple error-correction scheme described above, proving that indeed the proposed operation is optimal!

Preparation

To conduct the experiment, we used a cloud of
N\approx 10^{8}
ultra-cold Rubidium-87 atoms acting as an atomic memory.
Atoms form an elongated cigar-shaped cloud measuring
$0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$\times$0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$\times$9\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$

The atomic ensemble is cooled to a temperature of
78\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}
and captured using a magneto-optical trap (MOT).
The experiments are performed in sequences lasting
12\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
each, synchronized with mains frequency.
To energetically separate relevant levels for the protocol, for the duration of the experiment, the trap is held in the bias magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}=B_{0}\hat{z}

\sigma_{+}
polarization and is tuned to the
\ket{5^{2}S_{1/2}\,F=2}\rightarrow\ket{5^{2}P_{3/2}F=3}

\ket{g}

Limits on number of Rydberg excitations

Lifetime highly depends on the number of created Rydberg atoms, the higher the number of incident photons, the lower the lifetime due to the
r^{-6}
interactions between Rydberg atoms. In the metrology-type experiment we want to avoid such interactions, thus we chose the number of excitations in such a way that apart from spontaneous emission, the spinwave decay is strictly Gaussian and comes from residual thermal motion of the atomic ensemble.
With higher power of signal, the number of Rydberg atoms increases and thus the
r^{-6}
interactions inducing the undesirable exponential decay reducing the memory lifetime. We also work in the regime where excitations are not affected by the dipole blockade.

VII.3
Derivation of the interaction model

The energy of two resonantly interacting Rydberg excitations is equal to the energy of the dipole in the electric field of the different dipole at the distance
R
:

\hat{V}_{\text{dd}}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{R}^{3}}\left(\hat{d}_{\mu}\cdot\hat{d}_{\nu}-\frac{3\left(\hat{d}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{R}\right)\left(\hat{d}_{\nu}\cdot\mathbf{R}\right)}{\mathbf{R}^{2}}\right)

(10)

For two dipoles with the
\sigma_{+}
polarization, the energy can be represented in spherical coordinates as:

V(R,\theta)=\frac{C_{3}}{R^{3}}\frac{3\cos(2\theta)+1}{4}

(11)

We can consider a pair of Rydberg excitations, one in the mode
d
and position
\xi
and the second in the mode
p
and position
\mu
.
We assume that the state of the single Rydberg excitation
\psi
is symmetric and thus the two-Rydberg state
\Psi
is also symmetric and invariant to swap in the position of the excitations.
We assumed that the initial two-Rydberg state
\Psi
is the product of a single Rydberg excitation
\psi(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{p(\mathbf{x})}
, each with a Gaussian probability density dictated by the normalized atomic distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
(i.e. the number density of atoms would be equal
Np(\mathbf{x})
). Thus, the two-Rydberg-spin-wave wavefunction is:

\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi&\hbox{\multirowsetup$0$}\\
\mu&\end{array}\right)=\psi(\xi)\psi(\mu)

(12)

And the evolution of the state according to the Schrodinger equation is

{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hbar\dot{\Psi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\mu&\end{array}\right)=V(\xi-\mu)\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mu&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\xi&\end{array}\right)\overset{\text{sym.}}{=}V(\xi-\mu)\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\mu&\end{array}\right)

(13)

Finally, the evolution of the two-Rydberg-spin-wave wavefunction can be found simply as the phase component:

\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\mu&\end{array}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}t}{\hbar}V(\xi-\mu)\right)\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi&\hbox{\multirowsetup$0$}\\
\mu&\end{array}\right)

(14)

The state of the two-Rydberg excitation is:

\ket{\Psi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi_{i}&\hbox{\multirowsetup$0$}\\
\mu_{j}&\end{array}\right)\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{i}_{dg}\hat{\sigma}^{j}_{pg}\ket{g_{1}\ldots g_{N}}}{\sqrt{p(\xi_{i})p(\mu_{j})}}

(15)

To characterize the strength of the interactions and the evolution of the state, we calculate the expected number of excitations in the flat spin-wave mode
d
for the pair collective state:

\langle\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{i}_{dg}\hat{\sigma}^{j}_{gd}\ket{\Psi}=\\
\frac{1}{N^{3}}\sum_{k,l,m}\Psi^{*}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi_{k}&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\mu_{m}&\end{array}\right)\Psi\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\xi_{l}&\hbox{\multirowsetup$t$}\\
\mu_{m}&\end{array}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(\xi_{k})p(\xi_{l})}p(\mu_{m})}

(16)

Into this equation, we plug in the result for the evolution (
14
). Furthermore, in order to evaluate the expression, we change summation into integration following a rule
\sum_{k}\rightarrow\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}Np(\mathbf{x})
, in which the atomic density is an integral measure. We further rewrite the integral, finally obtaining:

\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}=\int p(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}\mathbf{x}\left|\int p(\mathbf{y})\exp\left(-\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}t}{\hbar}V(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}\mathbf{y}\right|^{2}

(17)

We next change the variables and assume that
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\ll L
, where
L
is the size of the ensemble, while performing the internal integration. This means that we assume that the density of atoms does not change significantly in the potential range. With this, we get

\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}=\int p(\mathbf{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{y}}\left|\int p(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\exp\left(-\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}t}{\hbar}V(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{x}}\right|^{2}\approx\\
\int p(\mathbf{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{y}}\biggl|1-p(\mathbf{y})\underbrace{\int\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}t}{\hbar}V(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{x}}}_{A(t)}\biggr|^{2}

(18)

The internal integral
A(t)
can now be performed given the specific potential shape (Eq.
11
), with integration performed in spherical coordinates. Remarkably, we found that the real part of the integral is linear in time, i.e.
\mathfrak{Re}\left[A(t)\right]=Qt
. When expanded for small values of
Qt
, this can be transformed to the simple relation:

\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}\approx\int p(\mathbf{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{y}}\left[1-2p(\mathbf{y})Qt\right]

(19)

By integrating obtained formula with respect to
\mathbf{y}
, where
\int p^{2}(\mathbf{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{3}}{\mathbf{y}}=1/\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}
can be interpreted as the ensemble volume, we get:

\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}\approx 1-\frac{2Q}{\mathcal{V}}t\;\text{and}\;Q=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{9\sqrt{3}}\frac{C_{3}}{\hbar}

(20)

The factor
Qt
can be interpreted as volume excluded from the ensemble due to the interactions causing dephasing of spin waves. For only a single atom it is indeed small compared with the entire volume
\mathcal{V}=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times$80\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\times$4000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, which justifies the applied approximations. The
Q
itself is the rate at which this volume expands, and interestingly has a dimension of a volumetric flow. We can extrapolate this result to the regime of many (
n
) control atoms each generating such an exclusion volume. It is safe to assume that an interaction with multiple control atoms cannot restore the phase-matched readout. We can thus assume that each control atom acts independently. For large
n
and small excluded volume of any one atom, we have:

\braket{\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}\approx\left(1-\frac{2Q}{\mathcal{V}}t\right)^{n}\approx\exp\left({-n\frac{2Q}{\mathcal{V}}t}\right)

(21)

This of course takes into account that exclusion volumes can overlap, and for large time
t
even the entire ensemble may be dephased.
For the states chosen in the experiment we have
\frac{C_{3}}{2\pi\hbar}=$3.709\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$

Šibalić et al. 2017
. As indicated in the main text, we define a per-atom decoherence rate (Eq.
6
), which agrees remarkably well with an experimental estimate. Thus, the observation of this rate of decay as well as the profile of the decay being exponential (Fig.
4
(b)) strongly suggest an excellent viability of the proposed model.

VII.4
Phenomenological model

To fully understand the experimental situation we need a self-consistent treatment of the statistical properties of the system. We achieve this by introducting a phenomenological model, where each mode decays proportionally to the occupation of the other mode, which results in the behavior given by Eqs. (
5
). A set of quantum transformations of the annihilation operators (overall constituting operation
\Lambda
, expressed in the Heisenberg picture) resulting from the Rydberg interactions can be formally written as:

\begin{cases}\hat{d}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{}\hat{d}\exp{\left(-\frac{\gamma\tau}{2}\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}\right)}\\
\hat{p}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{}\hat{p}\exp{\left(-\frac{\gamma\tau}{2}\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}\right)}\\
\end{cases}

(22)

The number operator in the exponent always represents a total number of
p
or
d
excitations, which is equal to the number of excitation at
t=0
. The proposed quantum map is thus completely positive but does not belong to a quantum Markov semigroup. The operation
\Lambda
, in this case, could also be represented as a set
\cup_{k,m}\{\hat{K}_{k,m}\}
of Kraus operators:

\hat{K}_{k,m}=\hat{p}^{k}\hat{d}^{m}\sqrt{\frac{(e^{\gamma\tau\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}}-1)^{k}(e^{\gamma\tau\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}}-1)^{m}}{k!m!}}e^{-\gamma\tau\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d}\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p}}

(23)

The initial bi-coherent state
\ket{\psi}=\ket{\delta}\otimes\ket{\beta}
is a tensor product of two coherent states with amplitudes
\delta
and
\beta
in modes
d
and
p
respectively. The mean number of detected photons in the initial Rydberg state is:

\langle n_{d}^{\prime}\rangle=\bra{\delta,\beta}\hat{d}^{\prime\dagger}\hat{d}^{\prime}\ket{\delta,\beta}=|\delta|^{2}\exp{\left[-|\beta|^{2}(1-e^{-\gamma\tau})\right]}

(24)

Note that the value of the mean number of photons is almost identical to the one obtained in the two interacting excitations model. The difference in the phenomenological model comes from considering coherent states.
To calculate the FI, we will need the probability distribution of the number of detected photons. We can find it by calculating the Wigner distribution for the given initial state with the transformed annihilation operators:

W(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{2}}\lambda e^{-\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2}}e^{\lambda^{*}\alpha-\lambda\alpha^{*}}C_{N}(\lambda)=\\
=\frac{2e^{-|\beta|^{2}}}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\beta|^{2k}}{k!}e^{-2\left|\alpha-\delta e^{-\frac{\gamma\tau}{2}k}\right|^{2}}

(25)

Thanks to the Wigner distribution, we can calculate the probability-generating function
G(t)
using the following identity:

G(t)=\frac{2}{\pi(1-t)}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d^{2}}\alpha W(\alpha)\exp{\left(-2|\alpha|^{2}\frac{t+1}{t-1}\right)}

(26)

We get the explicit formula for the probability-generating function:

G(t)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\frac{|\beta|^{2k}e^{|\delta|^{2}(t-1)e^{-2\gamma k}-|\beta|^{2}}}{k!}

(27)

Finally, we obtain the probability distribution
P(n)
of the number of detected photons in the mode
d
:

P(n)=\left.\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}G(t)}{\partial t^{n}}\right|_{t=0}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\frac{|\beta|^{2k}\left(|\delta|^{2}e^{-2\gamma k}\right)^{n}e^{-|\delta|^{2}e^{-2\gamma k}-|\beta|^{2}}}{k!n!}

(28)

The probability distribution resembles Poissonian distribution affected by a random loss, the strength of which is also Poission-distributed. In the experiment, we rotate the state with the MW field, which transforms the coherent states amplitudes as functions of rotation angle
\theta
and incident mean number of photons
\bar{n}
(
|\delta|^{2}\rightarrow\bar{n}\cos^{2}(\theta/2),|\beta|^{2}\rightarrow\bar{n}\sin^{2}(\theta/2)
). Now with the unraveled
\theta
dependence, the probability distribution
P_{\theta}(n)
can be used to calculate the classical FI for a detected photon as in Eq. (
7
) and we can utilize the Cramér-Rao bound to get the ultimate precision of the estimation.

The probability distribution
P_{\theta}(n)
allows us to conduct the numerical simulations of the precision of the estimation for the different values of the parameter
\gamma
. To showcase the robustness of this framework, we give as simple example. In Fig.

9

\eta

Maximum likelihood estimation

To benchmark the improvement of the precision of the presented protocol, we used the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation. We define ML estimator as

\tilde{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(x)=\text{argmax}_{\theta}[l_{x}(\theta)],

(29)

where
l_{x}(\theta)=P_{\theta}(x)
is the likelihood function.
To calculate the ML estimator of the certain angle of oscillation, for each point we perform
N_{0}
experimental realization. We divide the realization into
k
samples with N experimental sub-realizations such that
N\times k=N_{0}
. For each
k
we measure the mean number of detected photons
\bar{n}
and we substitute
n
in Eq.
28
for
\bar{n}
. This way we can calculate the ML estimator for each angle
\theta
and obtain the precision of the protocol.
To recover the ML estimator errors, we bootstrap the data by changing the division method of
N_{0}
between
N
and
k
.
