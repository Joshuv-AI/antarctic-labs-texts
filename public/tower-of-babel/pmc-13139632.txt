Characterization of the Orphan Cytochrome P450 CYP107J1 From Bacillus subtilis Through Peroxygenase Activity Engineering
Authors: Hideki Kato, Takafumi Hashimoto, Tuhin Das, Daniel Z. Doherty, Stephen G. Bell, Toshiki Furuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytochrome P450 monooxygenases (P450s) not only play many physiological roles in oxidative metabolism but are also promising biocatalysts for the synthesis of organic molecules. Bacillus subtilis strain 168, which possesses eight P450 genes, has been extensively studied as a model bacterium, but the catalytic function of CYP107J1 remains to be fully elucidated. Here, we investigated the catalytic function of CYP107J1. Because the genes encoding the reductase components of CYP107J1 could not be identified from the genome sequence, putidaredoxin (Pdx) and its reductase (PdR) were first used to reconstitute the enzymatic activity of CYP107J1. The enzyme showed oxidation activity toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 3–8, although the activity was low. To enhance the activity of CYP107J1, over that observed when using heterologous redox partners, we next engineered CYP107J1 into a hydrogen peroxide–driven form (i.e., peroxygenase). Only two amino acid substitutions in the active site of CYP107J1 were required for a peroxygenase variant exhibiting 28 times higher catalytic activity toward 4‐hexylbenzoic acid than the wild‐type enzyme supported by Pdx and PdR. This highly active enzyme enabled detailed characterization of CYP107J1. Interestingly, the engineered enzyme not only oxidized 4‐alkylbenzoic acids but also efficiently produced the valuable dye indigo by simply mixing the enzyme with indole and hydrogen peroxide. Enzymes of the CYP107J subfamily are widely distributed among bacteria of genus Bacillus. The findings from this study will facilitate further exploitation of the catalytic potential of CYP107J subfamily enzymes.

Introduction

Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (P450s) are a superfamily of heme‐containing enzymes that introduce a single oxygen atom from molecular oxygen into organic compounds. P450s are widely distributed in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes and play many physiological roles in the oxidative metabolism of endogenous and exogenous compounds. These enzymes are also promising biocatalysts for the synthesis of organic molecules because they can catalyze oxygenation reactions at room temperature and ambient pressure (Bernhardt 2006; Nelson et al. 1996; Urlacher and Eiben 2006). Bacterial P450s are particularly advantageous in terms of applications as biocatalysts because they can be easily expressed heterologously (Urlacher and Eiben 2006). The model strain, Bacillus subtilis 168, possesses eight P450 genes, the products of which have been extensively characterized and studied for potential applications (Furuya et al. 2008). The P450s CYP102A2, CYP102A3, CYP107H1 and CYP152A1 reportedly function as fatty acid monooxygenases (Gustafsson et al. 2004; Matsunaga et al. 1999; Stok and De Voss 2000). CYP107K1 is involved in bacillaene metabolism (Reddick et al. 2007), whereas CYP109B1 hydroxylates various compounds including ionone (Girhard et al. 2010). CYP134A1 functions as an unusual cyclo‐L‐leucyl‐L‐leucyl dipeptide oxidase (Cryle et al. 2010). However, the catalytic function of the remaining P450, CYP107J1, has not been fully elucidated. In a compound screening study using mass spectrometry with terpenoids and polyketides as substrates, CYP107J1 exhibited activity only toward testosterone enanthate (Furuya et al. 2008). The genes encoding enzymes of the CYP107J subfamily are widely distributed in the genomes of bacteria of genus Bacillus (Figure S1; Furuya et al. 2009; Mthethwa et al. 2018; Padayachee et al. 2020).

A large number of P450s remain ‘orphans’ in the sense that their functions are unknown (Stark and Guengerich 2007). One reason why the catalytic functions of many P450s remain unknown is that the reductase components that partner with the P450s have not been identified. The heme moiety in the catalytic center of P450 enzymes activates molecular oxygen using electrons, which are transferred from NAD(P)H by the cooperating reductase components. The resulting active oxidant, designated ‘Compound I’, oxidizes the target substrate. Understanding of the reductase components of B. subtilis P450s remains limited because the P450 genes do not cluster with the reductase component genes in the genome (Furuya et al. 2008; Girhard et al. 2010). As such, other studies have employed ferredoxins or flavodoxins and corresponding reductases from other organisms (e.g., putidaredoxin [Pdx] and its reductase [PdR] from Pseudomonas putida ) to reconstitute the enzymatic activity of CYP107H1, CYP107J1, CYP109B1 and CYP134A1 in place of the unknown natural redox partners (Cryle et al. 2010; Furuya et al. 2008; Girhard et al. 2010; Stok and De Voss 2000). However, the incompatibility resulting from a poor fit between the P450 and its heterologous redox partner leads to weak electron transport and a consequent decrease in P450 oxidation activity. A technique for modifying P450s into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)‐driven forms (i.e., peroxygenases) that do not require reductase components was recently developed. Shoji et al. (2016) conferred peroxygenase activity upon P450BM‐3 by replacing residue T268 in the active center of the enzyme with Glu. The introduced Glu residue at position 268 holds a water molecule that acts as an acid–base catalyst, allowing the generation of Compound I from H2O2 (Figure S2). More recently, Bell and coworkers conferred peroxygenase activity upon CYP199A4 by replacing the T252 residue (equivalent to T268 of P450BM‐3) with a Glu. The peroxygenase activity of the resulting T252E variant was dramatically increased by the further introduction of a D251Q mutation (Podgorski et al. 2022, 2024). The D251Q/T252E variant of CYP199A4 exhibited similar substrate‐ and regioselectivity to the wild‐type enzyme supported by its reductase components (Podgorski et al. 2024). This technique has also been applied to thermostable P450s (Das et al. 2025; Gee et al. 2023). Peroxygenases are useful biocatalysts because they can catalyze oxygenation reactions using H2O2 as an oxidant without the need for expensive NAD(P)H and electron transfer proteins (Gao et al. 2025; Kawana et al. 2022).

In the present study, we characterized the catalytic function of CYP107J1 from B. subtilis strain 168. Whole‐cell assays using Escherichia coli co‐expressing CYP107J1 with Pdx and PdR revealed that CYP107J1 exhibits oxidation activity toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids, although the activity is low. We hypothesized that modifying CYP107J1 into a H2O2‐driven form would unlock the catalytic potential of this enzyme. We constructed a variant that does not require reductase components by rationally introducing two amino acid mutations into the active site, and then analyzed in detail the effects of the introduced mutations on the catalytic activity and substrate selectivity of CYP107J1. The engineered enzyme exhibited high activity toward not only 4‐alkylbenzoic acids but also indole in the presence of H2O2, producing the valuable dye indigo from indole. This study will provide a useful approach for characterizing orphan P450s whose natural redox partners are unknown.

Experimental Procedures

Chemicals

4‐Methylbenzoic acid, 4‐ethylbenzoic acid, 4‐propylbenzoic acid, 4‐butylbenzoic acid, 4‐hexylbenzoic acid, 4‐octylbenzoic acid, indigo, 2‐oxindole and isatin were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). 4‐Pentylbenzoic acid, 4‐heptylbenzoic acid and indole were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of Whole Cells and Cell‐Free Extracts

The previously constructed pETCYP107J1 and pMWpdRpdx plasmids (Furuya et al. 2008; Furuya and Kino 2009) carrying the CYP107J1 gene in the pET21d vector and the pdx and PdR genes in the pMW218 vector, respectively, were simultaneously introduced into E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting recombinant E. coli cells were cultivated at 30°C in lysogeny broth medium containing (per litre) Bacto tryptone (10 g), Bacto yeast extract (5 g) and NaCl (10 g) (pH 7.0), supplemented with ampicillin (50 μg mL−1) and kanamycin (50 μg mL−1). After cultivation for 6 h until reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8–1.0, isopropyl‐D‐thiogalactopyranoside (1 mM), 5‐aminolevulinic acid (0.5 mM) and FeSO4 (0.5 mM) were added to the medium, and cultivation was continued for an additional 15 h at 25°C. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation and washed with potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v).

For preparation of cell extracts, the washed cells were suspended in buffer and disrupted using a sonicator. After centrifugation at 20,400 g for 30 min at 4°C, the resulting supernatant was used as the cell extract. The protein concentration of the extract was determined using a Coomassie protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) with bovine serum albumin as the standard. The P450 concentration was measured based on carbon monoxide (CO)‐reduced difference spectra or the pyridine hemochromogen method, as described below.

Reactions Using Whole Cells and Cell Extracts

Reactions with 4‐alkylbenzoic acids using whole cells were carried out in 2 mL microtubes containing recombinant E. coli cells (20 mg wet cell weight mL−1), substrate compound (5 mM), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 4% v/v) and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) with glycerol (10% v/v) in a volume of 250 μL. Reactions using cell extracts were carried out in 2 mL microtubes containing cell‐free extract (5 mg protein mL−1, 9 μM P450), substrate compound (1 mM), DMSO (1% v/v), NADH (10 mM) and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) with glycerol (10% v/v) in a volume of 600 μL. Reactions with indole using whole cells were carried out in 1.5 mL microtubes containing recombinant E. coli cells (20 mg wet cell weight mL−1), the substrate (2 mM), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 2% v/v) and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) with glycerol (10% v/v) in a volume of 100 μL. Reactions were carried out at 30°C with vigorous shaking using a microtube shaker.

Construction of CYP107J1 Variants and Protein Purification

The gene encoding CYP107J1 was amplified from the pETCYP107J1 plasmid using PCR and ligated into the pETDuet‐1 vector (Novagen, St. Louis, MO, USA) using the oligonucleotide primers and restriction enzymes listed in Table S1. The E251 and T252 residues of CYP107J1 were substituted to construct E251Q, T252E and E251Q/T252E (QE) variants using a KOD‐plus mutagenesis kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) with the oligonucleotide primers listed in Table S2. In addition, the H95 and N98 residues of the QE variant were substituted to construct QE/H95A and QE/N98A variants (Table S2). Correct generation of the desired mutations was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

CYP107J1 and its variants with an N‐terminal His‐tag were purified from the soluble fraction of recombinant E. coli cells using a HisTrap HP column (1 mL; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), as described previously (Fujimaki et al. 2024; Kawana et al. 2022). The soluble fraction was applied to a column equilibrated with potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v), NaCl (300 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM) and imidazole (20 mM). The column was then washed with 10 volumes of the same buffer. Bound His‐tagged protein was eluted with potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v), NaCl (300 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM) and imidazole (200 mM). Enzyme‐containing fractions were combined and desalted using a HiTrap desalting column (5 mL; Cytiva) equilibrated with potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v). Protein purity was confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The concentration of CYP107J1 was determined based on CO‐reduced difference spectra, as described below. The concentration of each variant was determined using the pyridine hemochromogen method (Barr and Guo 2015).

Analysis of Substrate Binding

Substate binding was analyzed using a Spectra MAX ABS Plus (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) and a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length. The UV–vis spectrum of a solution of P450 (4 μM, 500 μL) in potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v) was recorded over the range 300–700 nm, after which substrate solution (100 mM in DMSO, 1 μL) was added. The high‐spin heme‐iron content of substrate‐bound P450 was estimated according to a method described previously (Maddigan and Bell 2017). To determine the dissociation constant, substrate solution (50 or 100 mM in DMSO, 0.2 μL) was added stepwise to a solution of P450 (4 μM, 1 mL), and the change in absorbance upon substrate addition was calculated over the range 300–700 nm. DMSO alone was also added stepwise, and the absorbance spectrum of the DMSO sample was subtracted from that of the substrate/DMSO sample. Finally, the peak‐to‐trough absorbance difference (ΔA) at 390 and 420 nm was plotted against the substrate concentration and fitted via hyperbolic regression using Excel (Kitaoka 2023).

CO Binding Assay

CO binding was assayed using a Spectra MAX ABS Plus (Molecular Devices) and a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length. The UV–vis spectrum of a solution of P450 (1 mg protein mL−1, 200 μL) in potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) containing glycerol (10% v/v) was recorded over the range 300–700 nm. Sodium dithionite (1 mg) was subsequently added to reduce the heme iron to the ferrous form. In addition, CO gas (2 mL) was gently bubbled through the P450 solution. The P450 concentration was calculated based on CO‐reduced difference spectra using an extinction coefficient of 91 mM−1 cm−1 at 450 nm (Omura and Sato 1964).

H2O2 ‐Driven Reactions Using Purified Enzyme

Reactions with 4‐alkylbenzoic acids and alkanoic acids were carried out at 30°C in 2 mL microtubes containing P450 (2 μM), substrate compound (2 mM, from a 100 mM stock solution in DMSO), H2O2 (10 mM) and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) with glycerol (10% v/v) in a volume of 600 μL. Reactions with indole were carried out at 20°C–60°C in 1.5 mL microtubes containing P450 (0.5 μM), indole (2 mM, from a 100 mM stock solution in DMSO), H2O2 (2.5–25 mM) and potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0–8.5) with glycerol (10% v/v) in a volume of 100 μL. Reactions were carried out with shaking using a microtube shaker.

Product Analysis

High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses were carried out using an LC20 system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an XTerra MS C18 IS column (4.6 × 20 mm; particle size, 3.5 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). When 4‐alkylbenzoic acids were used as the substrate, the reaction mixture (250 μL) was acidified by the addition of HCl (pH 2–3), followed by the addition of methanol (500 μL) and water (250 μL). When indole was used as substrate, the reaction was stopped by the addition of catalase (10 mg mL−1; 100 μL), followed by the addition of N,N′‐dimethylformamide (1.4 mL). The solution was then shaken vigorously and centrifuged. The resulting supernatant (10 μL) was injected onto the HPLC system. Mobile phases A and B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and methanol (B), respectively. A gradient of mobile phase B was programmed as follows: starting ratio of 5%, hold at 5% for 3 min, increase to 40% over 1 min, increase to 80% over 10 min, increase to 100% over 1 min, and hold at 100% for 3 min. The flow rate was 0.5 mL min−1.

When alkanoic acids were used as the substrate, gas chromatography (GC) analyses were carried out using a GC‐2014AF system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a DB‐5 capillary column (0.25 mm × 30 m, J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). The reaction mixture (250 μL) was acidified by the addition of HCl (pH 2–3), followed by the addition of ethyl acetate (500 μL). Octanoic acid (2 mM) was added as an internal standard. The analytes were extracted three times with ethyl acetate. After evaporation of ethyl acetate, the sample was dissolved in diethyl ether (1000 μL) and dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate. The sample was then derivatized with BSFTA + TMCS (99:1, 400 μL; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) by heating at 70°C for 30 min. The injection port temperature was 280°C. The column was held at 70°C for 1 min. The temperature was then increased to 280°C at a rate of 15°C min−1 and held at 280°C for 1 min. Finally, the temperature was increased to 300°C at a rate of 15°C min−1 and held for 10 min.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis was performed using a JMS‐T100CS TOFMS system (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an electrospray ionization source, as described previously (Nozawa et al. 2022). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was performed using a Bruker Spectrospin 400 system (Billerica, MA, USA), as described previously (Hashimoto et al. 2019). Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was performed using a Shimadzu GC‐2010 system equipped with a QP2010S GC–MS detector, as described previously (Doherty et al. 2023).

Molecular Modelling

Three‐dimensional structures of CYP107J1 and its variants were modelled using AlphaFold 3 software (Abramson et al. 2024). The three‐dimensional structures of 4‐alkylbenzoic acids and indole were constructed using ChemDraw (CambridgeSoft, Waltham, MA, USA). Docking of the substrates to the target proteins (CYP107J1 and CYP107J1‐QE) was performed using AutoDock Vina (Trott and Olson 2010). Docking was site‐directed, with grid boxes centered on the active sites. The grid centers were set at (−4.752, 1.054, 1.924) for the wild‐type enzyme and (−0.134, 1.335, −5.467) for the QE variant, with dimensions of both being 44 × 34 × 44 Å. Docking results were visualized using PyMOL 2.3.5 (https://pymol.org/2/). The top scoring (low energy) pose, which had 4‐alkyl group of 4‐alkylbenzoic acids or pyrrole ring of indole pointing down toward the heme, was chosen.

Results and Discussion

Catalytic Activity Toward 4‐Alkylbenzoic Acids of E. coli Cells Co‐Expressing CYP107J1 With Pdx and PdR

Escherichia coli cells co‐expressing CYP107J1 with Pdx and PdR reportedly oxidize the enanthate moiety of testosterone enanthate (Furuya et al. 2008). To identify new CYP107J1 substrates, various compounds structurally similar to the enanthate moiety were incubated with E. coli cells co‐expressing CYP107J1 with Pdx and PdR. HPLC analysis of the reactions revealed a marked decrease in the level of the substrate 4‐propylbenzoic acid (retention time, 16.6 min) after incubation with the recombinant E. coli cells (Figure S3A,B). A product with a retention time of 12.3 min was also detected. LC–MS analysis indicated that the product was formed from 4‐propylbenzoic acid via the addition of one oxygen (Figure S3C). Furthermore, 1H‐NMR and 13C‐NMR spectroscopy analyses identified the product as 4‐(2‐hydroxypropyl)benzoic acid (Figure S4) (Coleman et al. 2022).

Due to their simple molecular structures, 4‐alkylbenzoic acids are good model compounds for analyzing the catalytic function of CYP107J1, which may also provide insight into the physiological substrates of CYP107J1. The conversion products of 4‐alkylbenzoic acids have both carboxy and hydroxy groups and are thus potentially valuable building blocks for organic synthesis. We therefore investigated the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with an alkyl carbon chain length of 1–8 using whole‐cell assays with E. coli co‐expressing CYP107J1 with Pdx and PdR (Figures S5–S11). HPLC analysis of the reactions revealed that CYP107J1 generated products from 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 3–7 (Figures S3 and S7–S10). LC–MS analysis indicated that all of the major products were formed from the substrates via the addition of one oxygen (Figures S7–S10). In the reaction with 4‐pentylbenzoic acid, a small amount of the minor product was formed with a mass (+14 compared to the substrate) indicative of a ketone formed through further oxidation of the alcohol (Figure S8). By contrast, no products were detected for reactions involving 4‐methylbenzoic acid, 4‐ethylbenzoic acid, or 4‐octylbenzoic acid (Figures S5, S6 and S11). The catalytic activity of CYP107J1‐expressing E. coli cells increased with a carbon chain length up to 5, reaching a maximum with 4‐pentylbenzoic acid, and then decreased (Figure 1A). We also examined the catalytic activity of cell‐free extracts of CYP107J1‐expressing E. coli cells, taking into account that in whole‐cell reactions, the cell membrane may interfere with the reactions between the enzyme and the substrates. As expected, the cell‐free extracts showed a different chain‐length dependence of activity compared with whole cells, exhibiting higher activity toward 4‐hexylbenzoic acid and 4‐heptylbenzoic acid than toward 4‐pentylbenzoic acid (Figure 1B). However, the activity of CYP107J1 toward 4‐octylbenzoic acid was reduced in cell‐free‐extract assays, similar to that observed in whole‐cell assays (Figure 1). These results demonstrate that the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids is highly dependent on carbon chain length. In addition, the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 was only 1.8 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1 for the most efficiently converted 4‐hexylbenzoic acid (Figure 1B). One possible reason for the low activity is that the heterologous redox partners Pdx and PdR are not optimized for CYP107J1.

Binding Affinity of CYP107J1 for 4‐Alkylbenzoic Acids

We also examined the binding affinity of CYP107J1 for 4‐alkylbenzoic acids using purified enzyme. Binding of a substrate to P450 displaces water, the sixth ligand of the heme iron, changing the spin state of the iron from low to high. This displacement leads to a change in the P450 absorption spectrum, with a decrease in absorbance at 420 nm and an increase at 390 nm (type I shift) (Barry and Challis 2012; Isin and Guengerich 2008). CYP107J1 exhibited a type I shift (ca. 10% high spin) with 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 5–8 (Table 1, Figure S12E–H). By contrast, no shifts were induced by 4‐methylbenzoic acid, 4‐ethylbenzoic acid or 4‐propylbenzoic acid (Table 1, Figure S12A–C), and only a slight shift (5% high spin) was induced by 4‐butylbenzoic acid (Table 1, Figure S12D). These results are consistent with the fact that CYP107J1 exhibits relatively high catalytic activity toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 5–8 (Figure 1B).

We also evaluated the dissociation constants of CYP107J1 for 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with carbon chain lengths of 5–8 by measuring the type I shift while varying the substrate concentration (Barry and Challis 2012; Isin and Guengerich 2008). The dissociation constants of CYP107J1 for 4‐pentylbenzoic acid, 4‐hexylbenzoic acid, 4‐heptylbenzoic acid and 4‐octylbenzoic acid were 283, 213, 249 and 376 μM, respectively (Table 1, Figures S13–S16). CYP107J1 exhibited higher affinity (lower dissociation constant) for the compounds for which the enzyme exhibited higher activity in cell‐free‐extract assays (Figure 1B), indicating that the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 for 4‐alkylbenzoic acids correlates with the substrate affinity. Compared with the dissociation constants reported for other P450s, the dissociation constants of CYP107J1 for 4‐alkylbenzoic acids are approximately two orders of magnitude higher, implying lower affinity for these compounds (Coleman et al. 2022; Maddigan and Bell 2017). Thus, the physiological substrates of CYP107J1 are predicted to be similar to, but different from, 4‐alkylbenzoic acids.

Engineering CYP107J1 Peroxygenases and Their Spectroscopic Features

To improve the activity of CYP107J1, over that observed when using the heterologous redox partners (Figure 1B), we attempted to confer peroxygenase activity upon CYP107J1 via engineering. In previous studies, CYP199A4 residues D251 and T252 were substituted with Gln and Glu, respectively, to confer peroxygenase activity (Podgorski et al. 2024). These residues correspond to E251 and T252 of CYP107J1. Structural analysis of CYP107J1 using AlphaFold 3 revealed that E251 and T252 are located near the heme moiety (Figure S17). We therefore constructed an E251Q/T252E double variant (CYP107J1‐QE) along with CYP107J1‐E251Q and CYP107J1‐T252E single variants and investigated their spectroscopic features using purified enzymes.

CYP107J1 and its variants were purified from recombinant E. coli cells (Figure S18). By comparing the amount of holo‐P450 based on the pyridine hemochromogen method (Figure S19) with the amount of apo‐P450 based on the Coomassie protein assay, the heme occupancy was 54% for the wild‐type enzyme, 58% for the E251Q variant, 65% for the T252E variant and 53% for the QE variant. The UV–vis spectra of the T252E and QE variants differed subtly from the spectrum of the wild‐type enzyme. The Soret band maximum was red‐shifted by 1 nm, from 418 nm in the wild‐type enzyme to 419 nm in the variants (Figure S20A,C,D), suggesting that the sixth ligand water of the ferric heme iron exhibits more of a hydroxide character in the variants (Podgorski et al. 2022). The Soret band maximum in the E251Q variant was unchanged from the wild‐type enzyme. We also evaluated whether the substrate 4‐pentylbenzoic acid displaces the aqua ligand in the variants in a manner similar to that observed in the wild‐type enzyme (Figure S12E). Compared with the wild‐type enzyme (10% high spin, Figure S20A) and the E251Q variant (5% high spin, Figure S20B), the T252E and QE variants showed only a slight shift (< 5% high spin, Figure S20C,D). These results indicate that the Glu residue at position 252 interacts with the aqua ligand (Figure S2) and that this ligand is not readily displaced from the heme iron in the variants by the addition of substrate, as previously reported (Podgorski et al. 2022).

We also analyzed the ferrous CO spectra of the variants using sodium dithionite as the reducing agent. Treatment of the wild‐type enzyme and the E251Q variant with the reducing agent shifted the Soret band from 418 to 417 nm (Figure 2A,B), indicating that the sixth ligand water had been removed and a ferrous five‐coordinated high‐spin heme iron had been formed. Subsequent addition of CO to the solutions shifted the Soret band from 417 to 449–450 nm in response to CO binding (Figure 2A,B). By contrast, no shift in the Soret band of the T252E variant occurred when the enzyme was treated with reducing agent, and the Soret band shifted only slightly upon addition of CO (Figure 2C). Interestingly, introduction of the E251Q mutation into the T252E variant restored the Soret band shift; treatment of the QE variant with reducing agent shifted the Soret band from 419 to 418 nm, and subsequent addition of CO to the solution resulted in a greater shift in the Soret band of the QE variant than the T252E variant (Figure 2C,D). These results suggest that the aqua ligand that interacts with the Glu residue at position 252 (Figure S2) is more readily displaced from the heme iron in the QE variant than in the T252E variant.

Catalytic Activity of CYP107J1 Peroxygenases

The peroxygenase activity of the variants was examined in the presence of H2O2 using 4‐pentylbenzoic acid as the substrate (Figure 3). The variants were quantified using the pyridine hemochromogen method because the addition of CO only slightly shifted the Soret band, making measurements based on CO‐reduced difference spectra difficult (Barr and Guo 2015). The wild‐type enzyme and the E251Q variant showed no peroxygenase activity, whereas the T252E variant acquired moderate catalytic activity (2.1 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1). Furthermore, the activity of the QE variant was significantly enhanced (28.5 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1) compared with the T252E variant, confirming that peroxygenase activity was successfully conferred upon CYP107J1. Ferrous CO spectra revealed that the binding affinity of the aqua ligand to the heme iron was reduced in the QE variant compared with the T252E variant (Figure 2C,D), as described above. This reduced binding affinity of the QE variant could induce water to react with H2O2 and thus form Compound I (Figure S2), leading to higher peroxygenase activity in the QE variant than in the T252E variant.

We next investigated the peroxygenase activity of CYP107J1‐QE toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 1–8. HPLC analysis revealed that the QE variant exhibits peroxygenase activity toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids with a carbon chain length of 3–7 (Figure 4), similar to the monooxygenase activity of the wild‐type enzyme (Figure 1). However, unlike the wild‐type enzyme, the QE variant did not exhibit catalytic activity toward 4‐octylbenzoic acid. The QE variant exhibited the highest peroxygenase activity toward 4‐hexylbenzoic acid (50.7 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1), which was 28 times higher than the monooxygenase activity of the wild‐type enzyme (1.8 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1). These results suggest that conferring peroxygenase activity upon P450s using the method described in this study is an effective means of increasing the catalytic activity of P450s with unknown natural redox partners.

The hydroxylation positions in the products were determined based on the mass fragmentation patterns in GC–MS analysis of the trimethylsilyl‐derivatized products. The ω‐1, ω‐2 and ω‐3 hydroxylated products of the reaction of the QE variant with 4‐pentylbenzoic acid, 4‐hexylbenzoic acid and 4‐heptylbenzoic acid were identified based on the appearance of signals at m/z 117.05, 131.10 and 145.15, respectively (Maddigan and Bell 2017). These signals arise from cleavage at positions adjacent to OSi(CH3)3 and correspond to the aliphatic fragments of the trimethylsilyl‐derivatized products. The QE variant selectively oxidized the ω‐1 position of 4‐pentylbenzoic acid (Figure S21). Oxidation of 4‐hexylbenzoic acid produced two hydroxylated products, with the major product at ω‐2, followed by the minor product at ω‐1 (Figure S22). In addition, oxidation of 4‐heptylbenzoic acid gave ω‐2 and ω‐3 as major hydroxylated products and ω‐1 as the minor hydroxylated product (Figure S23). These results indicate that the preferred site of hydroxylation by the QE variant is four carbons away from the benzene ring. Furthermore, we confirmed that the hydroxylated products of 4‐alkylbenzoic acids were identical between the QE variant and the wild‐type enzyme based on the retention time in HPLC analysis, implying that introduction of the QE mutations does not significantly affect the regioselectivity of the enzyme, as previously reported for CYP199A4 (Podgorski et al. 2024).

We also examined the peroxygenase activity of CYP107J1‐QE toward decanoic acid and undecanoic acid. These two alkanoic acids have the same number of carbon atoms from the carboxyl group to the carbon chain terminus as 4‐pentylbenzoic acid and 4‐hexylbenzoic acid, respectively. The QE mutant exhibited catalytic activity toward decanoic acid (32.9 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1) and undecanoic acid (26.7 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1), which was on the same order of magnitude as its activity toward 4‐pentylbenzoic acid (28.5 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1) and 4‐hexylbenzoic acid (50.7 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1). GC–MS analysis revealed that the QE variant oxidized the ω‐1 or ω‐2 positions of decanoic acid and undecanoic acid (Figures S24 and S25). These results suggest that the presence or absence of an aromatic ring in the tested substrates does not significantly affect the activity or regioselectivity of CYP107J1. Regarding P450s from B. subtilis , CYP102A2, CYP102A3 and CYP107H1 have been reported to oxidize the ω‐1, ω‐2 or ω‐3 positions of myristic acid (Cryle et al. 2003; Furuya and Kino 2010; Gustafsson et al. 2004; Stok and De Voss 2000), whereas CYP152A1 has been reported to oxidize the α or β positions of this compound (Matsunaga et al. 1999).

Molecular Model of CYP107J1 and the QE Variant

The three‐dimensional structure of CYP107J1 was constructed using AlphaFold 3; the substrates, 4‐ethylbenzoic acid, 4‐pentylbenzoic acid or 4‐octylbenzoic acid, were docked into the structure using AutoDock Vina (Figure S26A–C). The docking model suggested that 4‐pentylbenzoic acid, for which CYP107J1 exhibited relatively high activity (Figure 1B), is fixed in the active site through electrostatic interactions between the carboxy group of the substrate and the side chains of the His95 and/or Asn98 residues, thus situating the substrate 4‐pentyl group over the heme iron (Figure S26B). The subterminal carbon of the 4‐pentyl group is 3.9 Å from the heme iron. In modelling with 4‐octylbenzoic acid, for which CYP107J1 exhibited catalytic activity (Figure 1B), the subterminal carbon of the 4‐octyl group is 4.3 Å from the heme iron (Figure S26C). In contrast, 4‐ethylbenzoic acid, for which CYP107J1 exhibited no activity (Figure 1B), does not appear to fit into the active site because its volume is smaller than the volume of the substrate pocket of CYP107J1 (Figure S26A). The 4‐ethyl group of this compound is located far (the subterminal carbon, 4.6 Å) from the heme iron. These modelling results are consistent with the substrate specificity of CYP107J1. As an example of another P450, CYP199A4, which exhibits catalytic activity toward 4‐alkylbenzoic acids like CYP107J1, has been reported to hydroxylate the α position on the alkyl groups located approximately 4.5–5.1 Å from the heme iron (Coleman et al. 2022).

We also examined the three‐dimensional structure of the QE variant (Figure S26D–F). The modelling results of the QE variant docked with 4‐pentylbenzoic acid or 4‐ethylbenzoic acid were similar to those of the wild‐type enzyme docked with these compounds (Figure S26D,E). The distances between the subterminal carbon of the 4‐pentyl or 4‐ethyl groups and the heme iron are 5.0 and 6.6 Å, respectively. In contrast, the structures of the QE variant and the wild‐type enzyme docked with 4‐octylbenzoic acid were different (Figure S26C,F), with the distance between the subterminal carbon of the octyl group and the heme iron being longer in the QE variant (9.7 Å) than in the wild‐type enzyme (4.3 Å). The wild‐type enzyme exhibited catalytic activity toward 4‐octylbenzoic acid, whereas the QE variant did not, likely because the QE mutations in the active site, especially T252E, prevented the octyl group from accessing the heme iron (Figure S26C,F).

The docking model suggested that the side chains of His95 and/or Asn98 residues are important for the recognition of the carboxy group of 4‐pentylbenzoic acid (Figure 5A), as described above. QE/H95A and QE/N98A variants were constructed to validate the generated molecular model. Peroxygenase activity assays revealed that the H95A mutation dramatically reduced the catalytic activity of the QE variant (Figure 5B). By contrast, the N98A mutation had minimal effect on catalytic activity. These results indicate that His95 plays an important role in substrate recognition by CYP107J1.

Indigo Production Using CYP107J1‐QE

We found that CYP107J1‐QE also exhibits catalytic activity toward indole, producing a blue dye (Figure 6A). HPLC analysis revealed that CYP107J1‐QE produced the valuable dye compound indigo from indole based on the retention time and UV–vis absorption spectrum (Figure 6B, Figures S27 and S28A,D). 2‐Oxindole and isatin were detected as by‐products in the enzymatic pathway leading to the generation of indigo from indole (Figures S27, S28B,C,E,F, S29). The retention times and UV–vis absorption spectra of these two by‐products were consistent with those of authentic samples of 2‐oxindole and isatin, respectively. Modelling of the QE variant docked with indole indicates that the C‐2 and C‐3 positions of indole are located near the heme iron (Figure S30), suggesting that this positioning contributes to the formation of indigo and the by‐products. In addition, we examined the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 toward indole using whole‐cell assays with E. coli co‐expressing CYP107J1 with Pdx and PdR. The whole cells produced 0.10 mM indigo from 2 mM indole for 4 h. However, the catalytic activity of CYP107J1 in whole cells (0.15 mol mol−1 P450−1 min−1) was much lower than that of the QE variant (30 mol mol−1 P450−1 min−1).

(C) P450 (0.5 μM) was incubated with 2 mM indole and 2.5–25 mM H2O2 at pH 8 and 30°C for 10 min. We also investigated the optimal reaction conditions of pH and temperature for the efficient production of indigo using the QE variant. Indigo was most efficiently produced from indole by the QE variant at pH 8.0 and 30°C (Figure S31A,B). The effect on indigo production of varying the H2O2 concentration under optimal reaction conditions (pH 8.0, 30°C) was also evaluated. The QE variant produced indigo at 30 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1 in the presence of 20 mM H2O2, with an indigo yield of 0.15 mM (39 mg L−1) in 10 min (Figure 6C). Regarding P450 peroxygenases, the A245E variant of P450SPα and the L87F variant of CYP105D18 reportedly produce indigo at 20 and 0.4 mol mol‐P450−1 min−1, respectively (Shoji et al. 2016; Pardhe et al. 2025). These results indicate that CYP107J1‐QE is a superior biocatalyst for the synthesis of indigo using H2O2 as the oxidant.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that CYP107J1 can catalytically oxidize 4‐alkylbenzoic acids. CYP107J1 preferentially hydroxylated these substrates at a position four carbons away from the benzene ring. Mutagenesis studies indicated that His95 in the active center plays an important role in substrate recognition by CYP107J1. We also found that CYP107J1 can convert indole to indigo. Although this paper was in preparation, it was reported that CYP107J3 catalyzes isophorone hydroxylation reactions (Dai et al. 2026). These results suggest that enzymes in the CYP107J subfamily exhibit a high degree of catalytic versatility. In addition, we were able to rationally modify P450 into a H2O2‐driven form that does not require reductase components. In the reaction with 4‐alkylbenzoic acids, the hydrogen peroxide‐driven variant exhibited similar substrate specificity and regioselectivity to the wild‐type enzyme and also showed higher catalytic activity than the wild‐type enzyme. Therefore, this approach will be useful for characterizing orphan P450s whose natural redox partners are unknown. However, since this modification caused CYP107J1 to lose catalytic activity toward 4‐octylbenzoic acid, it is important to note that the introduction of the mutations may have some effect on the active site structure of P450s. Overall, the findings from this study will facilitate further functional analyses of CYP107J subfamily enzymes and broaden their application in biocatalysis.
