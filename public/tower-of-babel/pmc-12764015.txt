Analysis of physiological and molecular mechanisms of cotton trichome aphid resistance based on SWS-1 mutant
Authors: Youzhong Li, Pengpeng Chen, Jianhua Zhao, Jincheng Zhu, Zengqiang Zhao, Hang Yu, Yongmei Dong, Yousheng Tian, Zhibo Li, Zongming Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton is a globally significant cash crop; however, its production is frequently compromised by aphid infestations. This study systematically investigated the regulatory mechanisms governing aphid resistance through leaf trichome development. A comprehensive investigation, encompassing multi-omics analysis, genetic validation, and physiological-biochemical experiments, was conducted to determine the impact of trichome density on aphid resistance. The findings revealed that trichome density exceeding 701.4/cm² significantly enhances aphid resistance. This mechanism involves the creation of physical barriers to aphid feeding and the induction of defensive metabolite accumulation (e.g., β-caryophyllene and D-limonene). After aphid feeding, there was a significant increase in soluble sugar content and POD activity in the high-trichome lines. Subsequent analysis, employing both BSA-seq and transcriptomic techniques, has identified four key genes (GhABCG32, GhV6Z11, GhRSD1, and GhSHMT3). The functional validation experiment demonstrated that the silencing of the GhABCG32 gene via the VIGS (virus-induced gene silencing) technology resulted in a significant reduction in leaf trichomes and a substantial decrease in stem trichomes. This finding serves to confirm the critical role of this gene in trichome formation and aphid resistance. The present study provides theoretical foundations and key gene targets for molecular design breeding of high-yielding aphid-resistant cotton varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07795-x.Keywords: Cotton, Aphid resistance, Trichome development, Defense metabolites, Molecular mechanisms

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.), a cornerstone of China’s agricultural economy, supplies more than 20% of global fiber output, with an industrial scale second only to food crops [1]. Severe yield losses (15–20% annually) stem from aphid infestations (Aphis gossypii, Acyrthosiphon gossypii, and Aphis atrata) that peak during flowering in Xinjiang’s core belt (36°–46°N) [2–4]. These pests cause leaf curling, photosynthetic impairment, and sooty mold via honeydew secretion, with affected areas reaching 1.23 million hectares (+ 7.2% Year) by 2020, and traditional insecticide reliance is unsustainable amid rising resistance in > 580 arthropod species [5–7]. Furthermore, aphids exacerbate crop losses by transmitting viruses, such as cotton leaf curl virus, particularly when infection occurs at the seedling stage, which can lead to complete crop failure [8]. The overuse of chemical controls not only enhances pest resistance but also disrupts the ecological balance of natural enemies, increasing environmental risks and compromising the safety of agricultural products [9]. In recent years, biological control and the application of aphid-resistant cotton varieties have become key research focuses; however, their large-scale field adoption still faces challenges such as inconsistent effectiveness and adaptability limitations [10]. Therefore, establishing an integrated pest management strategy has become an urgent requirement to ensure the sustainable development of the cotton industry [11].

Plant trichomes serve as the primary physical barrier against insect pests, significantly reducing pest pressure by obstructing feeding and oviposition behaviors [12]. In cotton, research focused on trichome-mediated insect resistance has become an important aspect of breeding programs. High-density and elongated trichomes have been shown to effectively suppress populations of piercing-sucking insects like planthoppers, with trichome length being significantly negatively correlated with plant damage [13]. Molecular studies have further elucidated the genetic basis of cotton trichome development, with key genes such as GhGIR1D [14] and GhMML3 [15] regulating the formation of stem and fiber trichomes, respectively, providing genetic engineering targets to enhance insect resistance. Moreover, trichomes often act in conjunction with secondary metabolites and volatiles, such as gossypol, to establish a joint defense mechanism against pests [16]. Currently, cotton varieties rich in trichomes are being integrated into integrated pest management systems through marker-assisted selection and trichome germplasm. However, the stability of trichome-mediated resistance still faces challenges due to environmental influences and complex multi-gene regulatory networks [17], highlighting an urgent need for further advancement through multi-omics research and large-scale field validation.

This study employed a hairy cotton material (SWS-1) and a glabrous line as parents to investigate the morphological differences in trichomes, aphid resistance, and physiological stress indicators. Through the development of a BC₁F₁ population and combined transcriptome analysis, we identified a key gene, GhABCG32, involved in the regulation of cotton leaf trichome development. Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) experiments further confirmed the functional role of GhABCG32 in trichome formation. These findings provide new genetic insights and candidate targets for breeding aphid-resistant cotton varieties.

Materials & methods

Experimental materials

More than 200 cotton accessions were field-planted for this study. Following morphological characterization and preliminary screening of trichome traits, thirteen materials exhibiting distinct trichome phenotypes were selected for detailed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of seedling leaves. Ultimately, five accessions-KS2611, 20Y01, 20Y03, HP565, and SWS-1-demonstrating pronounced trichome variation were chosen for subsequent experimentation.

Experimental methods

Trichome trait analysis

During the five-leaf stage, the third true leaf was collected; during the bud stage, the third apical leaf was collected; and during the bell stage, the third apical leaf was collected as experimental samples, with three leaves taken from each type of test material. Nine square millimeters of leaf segments were washed in isotonic buffer, fixed for 4 h in a 2% glutaraldehyde solution prepared with 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), and then repeatedly washed until no glutaraldehyde odor remained. The samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series of 30%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%, with each treatment lasting 30 min. After critical point drying, the samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope. The number of trichomes per field at 50x magnification was measured to calculate density, and their length was determined.

Measurement of leaf epidermal cell thickness

The upper leaves at the bud stage were fixed with an FAA solution, prepared by mixing 5 mL of 38% formaldehyde, 5 mL of glacial acetic acid, 90 mL of 70% ethanol, and 5 mL of glycerin, and then sent to Wuhan Service Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for paraffin sectioning. The thickness of the upper and lower epidermal cells was measured using an optical microscope.

Aphid resistance evaluation

Field evaluation

The experiment was conducted at the cotton breeding base of Shihezi University (Erlian Experimental Farm), using a “one-mulch, six-row” planting pattern with narrow-wide row spacing of 66 cm + 10 cm. Seeds were sown on April 9, 2021, with uniform irrigation, defoliant application at boll opening, and other management practices consistent with field standards. Resistance was evaluated at the seedling, bud, and boll stages by randomly selecting 10 plants per material to calculate the aphid damage index (ADI) [18]:

Resistance grades: highly resistant (ADI < 0.20), resistant (0.21–0.35), moderately resistant (0.36–0.50), susceptible (0.51–0.65), highly susceptible (> 0.66).

Laboratory evaluation

Aphid Rearing: Field-collected Aphis gossypii were acclimated and reared on cotton seedlings confined within 200-mesh nylon net cages in a controlled-environment growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark; 25 °C ± 1 °C day/18 °C ± 1 °C night; 60% ± 5% RH). Uniform-sized adult aphids were selected for subsequent bioassays.

Host Choice Assay: The fifth true leaves from each of the five selected cotton accessions (KS2611, 20Y01, 20Y03, HP565, and SWS-1) were evenly spaced around the perimeter of a 23-cm-diameter circular arena (wooden board). The arena edge was sealed with paper strips to prevent aphid escape. Fifty unfed adult aphids were released at the center of the arena. The number of aphids settled on each leaf was recorded at 10-minute intervals for five consecutive recordings (totaling 50 min). Each assay was replicated three times.

Aphid Inoculation and Resistance Assay: Individual plants of each accession were enclosed within separate 200-mesh nylon net cages. Thirty starved adult aphids were carefully transferred onto the young leaves of each plant using a soft brush. Aphid numbers (adults and nymphs) on each plant were counted daily for six consecutive days. The reproductive rate (RR) was calculated as the final aphid count/initial aphid count (30). Resistance levels were classified based on RR as follows: Highly Resistant (RR < 1.0), Resistant (RR = 1.0–2.0), Moderately Resistant (RR = 2.1–4.0.1.0), Low Resistance (RR = 4.1–5.0.1.0), and Susceptible (RR > 5.0). Three biological replicates were performed per accession.

Data were analyzed using a linear mixed model, where “biological replicates of aphid counts” served as a random effect to account for batch variation, and “cotton accessions” as a fixed effect. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Physiological mechanisms of aphid resistance

Uniform-sized adult Aphis gossypii reared in the laboratory were transferred onto the fourth true leaves of each plant material using a brush, with 30 aphids per material. Petioles were wrapped with double-sided tape to prevent aphid escape. After 48 h of feeding, aphid-infested leaves and control leaves were wrapped in aluminum foil, labeled, and frozen in liquid nitrogen for measuring soluble sugar content and activities of POD (Peroxidase), PPO (Polyphenol Oxidase), and PAL (Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase).

Soluble sugar content assay

Soluble sugar content [18] was determined by colorimetry. A standard curve was first plotted using optical density (OD) as the ordinate. Fresh plant leaves (5 g) were minced, ground with ethanol, transferred to a beaker, and heated in a water bath at 70–80 °C for 30 min with 30–40 mL of distilled water. After cooling, saturated neutral lead acetate was added dropwise until no further precipitate formed. The mixture was filtered into a flask, and 0.3 g of sodium oxalate was added to precipitate excess lead. The filtrate served as the soluble sugar extract. For quantification, 1 mL of extract was mixed with 5 mL anthrone reagent, boiled in a water bath for 10 min, cooled, and OD was measured at 620 nm.

Peroxidase (POD) activity assay

Fresh leaf tissue (1 g) was ground in 1 mL of distilled water on ice, centrifuged at 20,000×g for 3 min, and the supernatant was stored at 4 °C. In a test tube, 1.95 mL of 0.2 M acetate buffer (pH 5.0), 1 mL of 0.1% o-methoxyphenol, 0.02 mL enzyme extract, and 1 mL of 0.08% H₂O₂ were mixed (control: H₂O₂ was replaced with 1 mL distilled water). After 3 min, OD was measured at 470 nm. Activity was calculated as OD change per min per g fresh weight (1 unit = 0.01 change in OD).

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity assay

Fresh leaf tissue (1 g) was minced, ground with 4 mL phosphate buffer on ice, and centrifuged at 20,000×g for 3 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was diluted 2.5-fold, and 1 mL of the diluted extract was mixed with 3 mL phosphate buffer and 0.5 mL of 0.1 M catechol (control: enzyme extract was replaced with distilled water). The reaction mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 15 min, after which the OD was measured at 525 nm. Enzyme activity was expressed as the change in OD per min per g fresh weight (1 unit = 0.01 change in OD).

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity assay

Fresh leaf tissue (2 g) was minced, ground with four volumes of 0.1 M Tris-H₂SO₄ buffer on ice, incubated for 20 min, and centrifuged at 20,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. For the reaction, 0.5 mL supernatant was mixed with 2 mL 0.1 M Tris-H₂SO₄ buffer and 1 mL 0.02 M L-phenylalanine. Initial OD was measured at 290 nm, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 30-minute, after which the OD was measured again. Enzyme activity was expressed as the change in OD per min per g fresh weight (1 unit = 0.01 change in OD).

Metabolite extraction for leaf analysis

Collect the infested leaves 48 h after aphid feeding, stored on dry ice, and sent to Shanghai Biosciences Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Frozen leaf samples (200 ± 1 mg) from high- trichome SWS-1 (S group) and low-trichome HP565 (W group), were analyzed with three independent biological replicates per group (n = 3), where each replicate consisted of a pooled sample from five individually grown plants. These samples were placed in 20 mL headspace vials, with 10 µL of 2-octanol added as an internal standard for gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis. Helium was used as the carrier gas, with a sampling flow rate of 3 mL/min at the inlet and a column flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was initially maintained at 40 °C for 4 min, then increased at a rate of 5 °C per minute to 245 °C and held for 5 min. The injector, transfer line, ion source, and four-pole temperatures were set to 250 °C, 250 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. Analyses were performed in splitless injection mode. Solid-phase microextraction (SPME) was carried out using a PAL robot system, including a 15 min preheat, extraction at 60 °C for 30 min, and desorption for 4 min. GC-MS analysis was performed on a 5977B mass spectrometer equipped with a DB-Wax column, with electron impact energy set to −70 eV, scanning a mass range from m/z 20 to 400, with no solvent delay.

Physiological indicators data analysis

The data were organized using Excel 2010 and analysed via an independent-samples t test and correlation analysis in SPSS 20.0. The data were then visualized using Origin 2021. Differential metabolites between groups were identified using OPLS-DA, univariate analysis (t test), and filtering by fold change. Mass spectrometry data were processed using ChromaTOF software (version 4.3x, LECO) for peak extraction, baseline correction, deconvolution, integration, alignment, and MS spectrum matching against the NIST library.

Bulk Segregant analysis (BSA) and bioinformatics

Genomic DNA quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, which confirmed the presence of intact bands, alongside assessments using NanoDrop spectrophotometry, yielding OD₂₆₀/₂₈₀ ratios of 1.8–2.2, and Qubit fluorometry, which confirmed concentrations greater than 40 ng/µL. Following quality control, sequencing libraries were constructed with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) through a process involving fragmentation to a size range of 200–500 bp, end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR enrichment. Library quality was assessed by Qubit quantification, Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) before paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw reads were processed with fastp [19] to remove adapters and low-quality bases (Q ≥ 20). Clean reads were aligned to the Gossypium hirsutum reference genome (TM-1_v2.1) [20] using BWA-MEM [21], sorted with SAMtools [22], and variants (SNPs/InDels) were called with GATK HaplotypeCaller [23]. Variants were filtered (depth ≥ 5, bi-allelic sites, missing rate ≤ 5%) and annotated with ANNOVAR [24]. BSA analysis was performed using QTLseq [25] to calculate Δ(SNP-index) in 1 Mb sliding windows (p < 0.05).

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA integrity was confirmed by:

NanoDrop spectrophotometry (OD₂₆₀/₂₈₀ = 1.8–2.2), and (ii) agarose gel electrophoresis (absence of degradation). Poly(A) + mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation, cDNA synthesis with random hexamer priming, end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Qualified libraries (Qubit > 2 nM; Agilent 2100 DV200 > 80%; qPCR passed) underwent PE150 sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw reads were filtered to remove low-quality bases, and clean reads were aligned to the Gossypium hirsutum TM-1_v2.1 reference genomeusing HISAT2 [26]. Transcripts were assembled with StringTie [27] and quantified as FPKM values. Variant calling used bcftools [28] with filtering (QUAL ≥ 30, ≤ 4 SNPs/10 bp) and annotation via snpEff [29]. Alternative splicing p < 0.5 events were identified by rMATS [30] (FDR < 0.05, |ΔPSI| >0.1). Differential expression analysis employed DESeq2 [31] for replicated data (|log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05). Functional enrichment (GO, KEGG, COG) and transcription factor prediction (iTAK [32]) utilized Ensembl [33], NR [34], and UniProt [35] databases. All visualizations were generated in R v4.4 with ggplot2 [36].

Virus-Induced gene Silencing (VIGS) assay

Using cotton SWS-1 cDNA as template, target gene fragments GhA06G0645 (25EV230), GhA06G1167 (25EV231), and GhA06G1027 (25EV232) were amplified by high-fidelity PCR with primers listed in Table S1. After verification by agarose gel electrophoresis, the target bands were excised and purified using a gel extraction kit. The empty pTRV2 vector was linearized by double digestion with restriction enzymes BamHI and SacI, followed by purification via ethanol precipitation. The purified target fragments were mixed with the linearized vector at a 2:1 molar ratio, and recombinant plasmids (pTRV2-25EV230, pTRV2-25EV231, and pTRV2-25EV232) were constructed using a homologous recombination kit (2×Basic Assembly Mix) with a 15-minute incubation at 50 °C. Among them, the pTRV2-25EV230 vector targets the 324–623 bp region of the Gh_ABCG32 CDS, the pTRV2-25EV231 vector targets the 289–588 bp region of the Gh_A06G1167 CDS, and the pTRV2-25EV232 vector targets the 89–388 bp region of the Gh_A06G1027 CDS. Subsequently, 1 µL of the homologous recombination product was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells, which were spread onto LB agar plates containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were selected for colony PCR screening using the universal vector primer PTRV2-SEQS and gene-specific primers. PCR products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis to identify positive clones with expected band sizes. Positive clones were sent for commercial sequencing, and strains with confirmed sequences were preserved as glycerol stocks, with plasmids extracted for long-term storage. For agroinfiltration, when seedlings developed fully expanded cotyledons and exhibited pale green, robust hypocotyls, the hypocotyl epidermis was gently scratched with a sterile syringe needle. Agrobacterium suspension was then applied to the wound site using either a needle-free syringe or a sterile brush, allowing natural absorption. The infiltrated seedlings were maintained under cultivation conditions of 16,000 lx light intensity and 24 °C. Total RNA was subsequently extracted from plant tissues and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Gene expression levels were analyzed by qPCR (Table S4), normalized against the reference gene ubq7, and relative expression of GhA06G0645 and PDS was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Results

Dynamic characteristics of cotton leaf trichomes across developmental stages

Histological examination of cotton leaves revealed distinct structural patterns between high-trichome lines (SWS-1, HP565) and low-trichome lines (20Y01, KS2611, 20Y03) (Fig. 1c). While epidermal thickness showed no significant inter-line differences (upper epidermis: 83–87 μm; lower epidermis: 20–21 μm; Fig. 1b) and non-significant correlation with aphid resistance (p < 0.05), 20Y03 exhibited markedly thinner palisade tissue (28 μm; p < 0.01), potentially contributing to its reduced aphid resistance. Cross-sections further demonstrated compact mesophyll organization in low-trichome lines versus looser mesophyll tissue in high-trichome counterparts (Fig. 1a), suggesting that structural looseness may enhance defensive capacity (Table 1).

Trichome density dynamics diverged dramatically across developmental stages (p < 0.001; Fig. 1f). At the seedling stage, high-trichome lines (HP565: 3916.7; SWS-1: 4208.3 roots/cm²) exceeded low-trichome lines (KS2611: 27.8; 20Y01: 138.9 roots/cm²) by > 28-fold, with intermediate line 20Y03 (756.9 roots/cm²) differing significantly from both groups (p < 0.01). By the bud stage, densities converged among low/intermediate lines while high-trichome lines maintained elevated values (HP565: 3770.8; SWS-1: 3437.5 roots/cm²); at the boll-stage rankings reverted to seedling-stage patterns. Developmental trajectories were line-specific: KS2611 uniquely showed bud > boll > seedling progression, whereas others declined continuously (seedling > bud > boll) (Fig. 1d).

Trichome length exhibited minimal genotypic variation but clear stage-dependent shifts (Fig. 1e). KS2611 displayed maximal length at seedling and bud stages (0.057–0.058 cm), contrasting with 20Y01’s minimum at the seedling stage (0.046 cm). By the boll stage, lengths converged across lines (0.053–0.058 cm; p < 0.05), following a universal elongation trend: boll > bud > seedling. This pattern occurred independently of density changes, indicating developmental regulation of trichome morphology (Table 2).

Aphid resistance evaluation of tested leaves

Field resistance assessments demonstrated strong aphid resistance in high-trichome lines (HP565, SWS-1) across all developmental stages. At the seedling stage, these lines exhibited minimal aphid damage (ADI = 2.5%, classified as highly resistant), contrasting with low-trichome lines (20Y01, KS2611, 20Y03; ADI = 47.5%−60%, moderately resistant to susceptible). Although ADI values increased during the bud stage, high-trichome materials retained significant resistance. Notably, even under boll-stage conditions where environmental factors suppressed aphid reproduction, high trichome lines maintained significant resistance advantages (Fig. 2a; Table 3). Laboratory choice assays revealed time-dependent behavioral responses. Initial aphid settlement (t1) showed no preference, but later observations (t2-t5) demonstrated significant migration toward low-trichome lines (KS2611, 20Y01), resulting in 3.8- to 4.2-fold higher aphid counts compared with high-trichome leaves (Fig. 2b; Table 4). Reproduction assays further confirmed this pattern: aphid populations on high-trichome lines declined after day 2, whereas day-6 reproduction rates (HP565: 5.57; SWS-1: 5.63) were substantially lower than those on low-trichome lines, which showed a 166- to 167-fold increase.Visual assessment revealed contrasting trends between trichome traits and the aphid damage index (ADI) (Fig. 2c). Higher trichome density and greater trichome number consistently corresponded to lower ADI values, indicating reduced aphid damage. Conversely, trichome length exhibited no consistent trend with ADI variation, suggesting limited influence on damage severity.

Determination of physiological indexes of different test materials

Physiological analyses under aphid infestation revealed distinct metabolic divergence between high-trichome (SWS-1, HP565) and low-trichome lines (KS2611, 20Y01, 20Y03). In unchallenged seedlings, soluble sugar content showed no significant differences among low-trichome lines (p < 0.05; Fig. 3a), while high-trichome materials exhibited significantly elevated levels (1.8–2.2 × higher). Post-infestation, all genotypes accumulated soluble sugars, with SWS-1 and HP565 maintaining maximal concentrations and KS2611 showing minimal induction (p < 0.05), indicating that trichome density modulates sugar-based defense. Peroxidase (POD) activity was consistently lowest in low-trichome lines (KS2611, 20Y01) before and after infestation (38–45% lower than high-trichome lines; p < 0.01; Fig. 3b), while universal aphid-induced activation peaked in SWS-1/HP565, establishing POD as a trichome-dependent effector. Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) responses diverged: SWS-1 showed the strongest induction (2.8×, p < 0.001; Fig. 3c), but HP565 did not differ significantly from KS2611/20Y01 (p < 0.05), demonstrating PPO’s trichome independence. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) exhibited inverse patterns (Fig. 3d): low-trichome lines had minimal baseline activity but maximal post-infestation induction (KS2611: 4.1×; 20Y01: 3.3×; p < 0.01), whereas high-trichome HP565 showed negligible response, revealing PAL’s role in alternative defense pathways. To resolve metabolic mechanisms, untargeted metabolomics compared KS2611 (low-trichome) and SWS-1 (high-trichome) to identify trichome-linked and compensatory resistance metabolites (Table 5).

Screening of differential metabolites

Metabolomic analysis of aphid-infested cotton plants revealed significant differential regulation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) between trichome-rich (S) and trichome-sparse (W) groups, with terpenoids emerging as central mediators of trichome-dependent resistance mechanisms. Aphid feeding induced significant upregulation of 21 metabolites (p < 0.05), including seven terpenoids (e.g., D-limonene, caryophyllene), five aldehydes/alcohols (e.g., 2,4-nonadienal, phenylethyl alcohol), and two esters (e.g., sulfurous acid cyclohexylmethyl undecyl ester), with caryophyllene (log₂FC= 4.5) and D - limonene (log₂FC= 4.3) as dominant upregulated terpenoids (Fig. 4d); four compounds (3,4-dimethylcyclohexanol, styrene, 1 - pentanol, sulfurous acid ester) showed highly significant differences (p < 0.01). Concurrently, 14 metabolites were downregulated (p < 0.05), predominantly terpenoid derivatives (e.g., bicyclo heptane analogs, log₂FC = − 4.7) and gossypol-related compounds (Fig. 4e). The PCA plot (Fig. 4b) clearly separated S and W groups, confirming metabolic divergence, while the correlation heatmap (Fig. 4a) and hierarchical clustering (Fig. 4c) visualized distinct metabolite regulation patterns between groups. The terpenoid-enriched signature in multi-trichome materials, as reflected in these profiles, demonstrated a strong correlation with aphid resistance: caryophyllene disrupted herbivore physiology (suppressing larval growth, inducing pupal deformities), with (E)-β-caryophyllene exhibiting oviposition-deterring effects; D - limonene functioned as a tritrophic signal, attracting natural enemies (e.g., Microplitis mediator) via blends with ocimene/myrcene. Benzaldehyde showed concentration-dependent behavioral modulation (low concentrations promoting oviposition, high concentrations repelling pests). Coordinated upregulation of terpenoids/aldehydes in trichome-rich genotypes suggested a dual defense strategy (direct antixenosis, indirect enemy recruitment). Downregulated terpenoid derivatives (e.g., farnesene analogs) and gossypol-related compounds implied metabolic trade-offs, prioritizing specific defensive terpenes. Terpenoids accounted for 33% (upregulated) and 36% (downregulated) of metabolites (Fig. 4d and e), underscoring their pivotal, structure-specific (cyclic vs. acyclic) role in defense.

BSA and transcriptome sequencing reveal differential genes

To dissect genetic loci governing trichome development, six plants each from high-trichome genotype S and low-trichome genotype W were reciprocally crossed (Fig. 5a, b). Extreme-phenotype bulks were constructed by pooling 30 individuals from segregating progeny tails, followed by BSA-seq, generating 1.8 billion high-quality reads. Sequencing outputs comprised: high-trichome parent (361.2 million reads), low-trichome parent (344.9 million), high-trichome bulk (552.0 million), and low-trichome bulk (595.2 million). All libraries exhibited robust data quality: Q30 ≥ 90.5%, GC content 34.6–37.3%, and reference genome (G. hirsutum TM-1_v2.1) mapping rate > 99.3% (Table 6). Variant screening identified 3,707,058 polymorphic sites between parents, including 3,175,193 SNPs (Ts/Tv ratio = 2.02; Figure S1) and 531,865 InDels. QTLseq analysis of Δ(SNP-index) using 1-Mb sliding windows pinpointed major effect loci on chromosomes A02 and A06 (Fig. 5c), with the candidate interval harboring 75,330 SNPs and 3,036 InDels (Figure S2, (Table S6).). These metrics confirm the dataset’s suitability for high-confidence QTL mapping. BSA analysis identified two major loci, A02 and A06, associated with trichome development in cotton (Table 7). The A02 locus exhibited a typical clustered distribution pattern, comprising six non-contiguous candidate intervals within the 41.5–99 Mb chromosomal region. These intervals ranged from 1.2 to 1.7 Mb in length, collectively spanning approximately 9.18 Mb and encompassing 114 candidate genes. This clustering pattern suggests the presence of multiple genetically linked or functionally related genes regulating trichome formation. In contrast, the A06 locus displayed a distinct genetic architecture, characterized by a large linkage block. Its three candidate intervals covered a cumulative 116.47 Mb, with one prominent interval (15.18–124.74 Mb) alone spanning 109.56 Mb and containing 1,483 genes In total, this locus harbored 1,654 candidate genes. This extensive genomic region likely represents an ancestral haplotype block that has undergone limited historical recombination, potentially harboring one or more major trichome-related genes along with other agronomically important traits. Given the considerable number of genes within the A06 region, further functional screening through the integration of transcriptomic data and protein-protein interaction networks will be essential to pinpoint the core regulatory genes.

Gene function analysis via Virus-Induced gene Silencing (VIGS)

Notably, GhABCG32 is a homolog of Arabidopsis AtABCG32. It has been reported that AtABCG32 acts as a cuticular lipid transporter in Arabidopsis, participating in the formation and maintenance of the epidermal barrier while also affecting the development of surface structures such as trichomes. For the PPI interaction data of GhABCG32, the supporting evidence for its interactions includes experimental data (experimentally_determined_interaction = 0.414), database annotations (database_annotated = 0.320), and automated text mining (automated_textmining = 0.121), with a combined confidence score of 0.619. This classifies it as a predicted result with moderate confidence in the STRING database. To verify the function of the candidate gene GhABCG32, virus-induced gene silencing technology was employed to generate silenced plants of the gene and systematically observed its phenotypic changes. The experimental results showed that compared with the empty vector control TRV00, the GhABCG32-silenced plants (TRV:GhABCG32) exhibited significant morphological differences in young stems and leaves (Fig. 8a). Both trichome coverage on leaf and stem surfaces and trichome density and number were significantly reduced (Figs. 8b, c). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis further confirmed that the number of trichomes in the cross-section and longitudinal section of the silenced plant stems was significantly reduced compared to the control group (Figs. 8d, e, Table S3), indicating that GhABCG32 plays a crucial regulatory role in trichome formation. Notably, GhABCG32 is a homolog of Arabidopsis AtABCG32. It has been reported that AtABCG32 acts as a cuticular lipid transporter in Arabidopsis, participating in the formation and maintenance of the epidermal barrier while also affecting the development of surface structures such as trichomes. These findings suggest that GhABCG32 may retain the conserved functions of its homolog in lipid transport and epidermal development. Discussion

Physiological mechanisms of aphid resistance in High-Trichome leaves

We found that cotton plants 20Y01 and KS2611 with trichome density below 701.4 cm⁻² consistently exhibited an aphid-susceptible phenotype, while HSP565 and SWS-1 with density above this value always showed resistance; thus, this study identified 701.4 cm⁻² as a critical trichome density threshold conferring aphid resistance. While the aphid’s initial probing behavior was not affected by trichome density—consistent with previous observations that pre-ingestion host assessment relies more on chemical cues than on physical structure—sustained phloem feeding was significantly disrupted once density exceeded this threshold. This finding reconciles two key hypotheses: Butler’s classical barrier model [37], which emphasizes physical barriers to herbivore locomotion, and Zarpas’ density-dependent reproductive restraint framework [38], by demonstrating that high trichome density works through a dual mechanism: (i) mechanical interference with feeding appendages and (ii) chemosensory disruption of host recognition pathways [39]. Specifically, the dense trichome matrix may physically block aphid stylet penetration while simultaneously altering the volatile gradient that guides sustained feeding, creating a “sensory mismatch” that shortens the duration of ingestion. Notably, high-trichome accessions did not rely solely on physical barriers but achieved resistance through synergizing this trait with coordinated biochemical responses. Key biochemical traits showed distinct associations with aphid resistance in Fig. 3: Phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) activity, despite being a key enzyme in phenolic biosynthesis, central to wheat and coffee aphid resistance [40, 41], displayed only a weak correlation with resistance in cotton (Fig. 3d).

In contrast, peroxidase activity exhibited a strong positive correlation with resistance (P < 0.01, Fig. 3a), and soluble sugars also showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01, Fig. 3b). This divergence between the strong roles of peroxidase/sugars and the weak role of PAL highlights crop-specific defense strategies: whereas wheat [42] and coffee [43] prioritize phenolic-mediated chemical defense dependent on PAL [44], cotton integrates physical trichome barriers with peroxidase-driven oxidative defense and metabolic shifts involving soluble sugars. This integrated strategy likely reflects evolutionary adaptation to distinct aphid feeding strategies.

Metabolomic profiling further revealed 16 aphid-deterrent metabolites, dominated by terpenoids such as β-caryophyllene and D-limonene, consistent with their known roles in trichome-based defense. Volatile profiling confirmed directional metabolic reprogramming: 105 upregulated repellents (e.g., ocimene) and 226 downregulated attractants (e.g., hexanols) align with allelochemical defense theory [45]. This theory posits that plants strategically modulate volatile blends to deter herbivores. Importantly, this metabolic shift correlated tightly with trichome density, suggesting that trichomes themselves, likely glandular trichomes, serve as both physical barriers and biosynthetic sites for defensive volatiles [46, 47].

The main finding of this study is that cotton trichome density, a physical defense mechanism, influences the resistance of cotton leaves to aphids, and targeted analysis of volatile metabolites has not been conducted to date. This points to a direction for future research: we will integrate targeted GC-MS metabolite quantification with aphid behavioral assays to explore the potential function of this gene in chemical defense, thereby more comprehensively deciphering its multifunctionality.

This unique high-density trichome layer creates a dual defense system, and we propose that cotton trichomes may regulate aphid reproduction through two distinct pathways: on the one hand, high-density trichomes act as a physical barrier to impede aphid feeding and nutrient acquisition; on the other hand, they may influence the aphid juvenile hormone (JH) signaling pathway via β-caryophyllene. Song [48] identified juvenile hormone acid methyltransferase (JHAMT) as a core regulatory factor governing aphid reproduction, where decreased JH levels are associated with reduced aphid fecundity and nymph survival. We hypothesize that β-caryophyllene may interfere with JH biosynthesis by regulating JHAMT expression or modulating JH signaling activity. Supporting this view, Qiao [49] demonstrated that energy derived from NADH/ATP production is a critical prerequisite for JH synthesis and insect reproduction, as Nocturnin-mediated energy supply ensures the normal reproductive processes of aphids. We further speculate that β-caryophyllene may disrupt aphid energy metabolism by inhibiting NADH/ATP generation, thereby indirectly impairing JH biosynthesis and reproduction. This energy metabolism dimension supplements the metabolite-insect interaction mechanism, providing a more comprehensive theoretical basis for the observed suppression of aphid reproduction. Future studies will validate these two hypotheses through the quantification of JHAMT expression, JH content, NADH/ATP levels, and the expression of energy metabolism-related genes in aphids, thereby providing direct experimental evidence to support the proposed regulatory pathways.

Molecular mechanisms of aphid resistance in High-Trichome leaves

Through the combined analysis of BSA-seq and transcriptome datasets, this study identified major QTLs for trichome development on chromosomes A02 and A06 (Fig. 5c), which are consistent with the genomic locations of known trichome-related loci in cotton, such as GhMML3 and GhGIR1D. A total of 16,212 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected between high- and low-trichome materials, with functional enrichment in epidermal development and defense metabolism pathways (Fig. 6d, e). Integration of DEG screening with protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis (Fig. 7) identified GhABCG32 as a core regulator of trichome formation and aphid resistance [50–52]. GhABCG32 is a homolog of the Arabidopsis cuticular lipid transporter AtABCG32. Its function in cotton was directly validated using virus-induced gene silencing: silencing GhABCG32 significantly reduced trichome density and length on both leaves and stems, confirming that GhABCG32 is a key regulator of trichome initiation and morphogenesis. The reduction in trichome density impaired the physical barrier against aphids, further supporting that GhABCG32 indirectly mediates aphid resistance by modulating trichome development [53–55].

Mechanistically, based on the function of its Arabidopsis ortholog [60] and physiological observations in cotton, GhABCG32 is proposed to mediate the transport of cuticular lipids to the epidermal cell surface, thereby providing structural support for trichome base anchoring and maintaining trichome integrity. This function is consistent with the phenotypic characteristics of high-trichome materials such as SWS-1: high expression of GhABCG32 in SWS-1 not only corresponds to higher trichome density but is also accompanied by coordinated upregulation of defensive metabolites (e.g., β-caryophyllene). This suggests that GhABCG32 may indirectly support defense metabolism by maintaining the structural stability of specialized biosynthetic sites in trichomes (e.g., secretory cells of glandular trichomes), resulting in a synergistic “physical barrier–chemical defense” effect [56, 57]. This functional speculation is further supported by theoretical mechanisms from related studies and bioinformatic evidence. Yang [58] found in their study that OsTTG1, a WD40 repeat protein, can regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in rice, a typical secondary metabolic process, by modulating the expression of structural genes. Their research not only highlights the key role of regulatory networks in secondary metabolism but also indirectly reveals the core mechanism that secondary metabolites require the coordinated action of synthesis and transport pathways to achieve their biological functions. We also used the PSORTⅡ tool (https://www.genscript.com/psort.html) for bioinformatic prediction of the subcellular localization of GhABCG32, and the results indicate that it is predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum, a key organelle for lipid synthesis and intracellular transport, with 66.7% confidence from k-NN analysis, supported by its structural feature of 10 transmembrane segments consistent with transport-related proteins (Table S7). This localization feature is highly consistent with the functional properties of proteins involved in lipid metabolism and transport, further strongly supporting the speculation that GhABCG32 may act as a transporter to participate in the transport of lipids synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum to other cellular structures such as the plasma membrane and vesicles.

The critical role of GhABCG32 in trichome development has been directly demonstrated through virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) performed at the seedling stage. Silencing GhABCG32 at this stage significantly reduces trichome density and length. This functional importance is supported by transcriptomic data from the seedling stage, which shows a high expression level of GhABCG32 (Fig. 7a), consistent with its essential role in active trichome initiation during this developmental stage. While transcriptomic data across the entire developmental cycle is lacking, this further supports its regulatory role in seedling-stage trichome development (Fig. 8) and may explain why high-trichome materials exhibit the strongest aphid resistance at the seedling stage (Section Aphid Resistance Evaluation of Tested Leaves). At this stage, the high expression of GhABCG32 promotes the formation of dense trichomes, which physically hinders aphid stylet penetration and lays the foundation for the subsequent activation of chemical defenses.

However, several aspects still await verification. While these results confirm the involvement of GhABCG32 in trichome development and aphid resistance, several aspects still await further verification to clarify the underlying mechanisms. First, it is necessary to clarify whether GhABCG32 transports specific lipid components that serve as trichome precursors. Second, trichome development is a crucial quantitative trait—while GhABCG32 influences this process, it does not play a decisive role. Thus, further investigation is needed to identify other transcription factors involved in regulating trichome formation and to elucidate how these regulators specifically affect the anchoring efficiency of trichome bases. Third, although the research framework of this study facilitates the identification of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with cotton trichome development, the currently defined genetic intervals remain overly broad. Subsequent narrowing via linkage analysis is required to enable precise localization of key regulatory genes and lay a solid foundation for functional validation. Fourth, despite confirming the association between GhABCG32 and trichome development via VIGS, critical mechanistic data are still lacking. These include the silencing efficiency of GhABCG32, aphid population growth dynamics, and life table parameter measurements. To address these gaps, we plan to detect the in vivo interaction of GhABCG32 with its interacting proteins via bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and verify their binding relationship using co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) to supplement empirical evidence. This will ultimately confirm the functional role of GhABCG32 in cotton aphid resistance.

Notably, the current findings support GhABCG32 as a potential core gene regulating cotton trichome development, and further imply its role as a critical node linking trichome physiological traits to aphid resistance. As highlighted by Sohail [59], genome editing technologies such as CRISPR hold substantial promise for augmenting horticultural crop traits, but their practical application requires addressing technical bottlenecks (e.g., off-target effects, genotype-dependent editing efficiency) and regulatory considerations. Thus, GhABCG32 could serve as a promising candidate target for future research exploring trichome density enhancement and aphid-resistant cotton breeding via CRISPR-mediated genome editing—with follow-up studies needed to verify its editability, optimize editing strategies, and validate the phenotypic effects of targeted modifications in field conditions.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying aphid resistance in cotton accessions with high trichome density. A critical trichome density threshold of > 701.4 cm⁻² was identified, conferring resistance through a dual mechanism: physical obstruction of aphid feeding and disruption of chemosensory activity. These effects were further enhanced by biochemical responses, including increased peroxidase activity and soluble sugar accumulation, both of which showed a strong positive correlation with resistance (p < 0.01). Metabolomic and volatile profiling identified 16 aphid-deterrent metabolites, predominantly terpenoids such as β-caryophyllene and D-limonene, accompanied by extensive metabolic reprogramming involving the upregulation of 105 repellent metabolites and the downregulation of 226 attractants. Through BSA-seq, transcriptome analysis, and PPI network prediction, four candidate genes (GhABCG32, GhV6Z11, GhRSD1, and GhSHMT3) were identified as potential regulators of trichome development and resistance. These genes are involved in processes such as lipid trafficking, plastid metabolism, epidermal cell fate determination, and carbon allocation.

Among these candidates, GhABCG32 functions as a core gene associated with trichome development and aphid resistance. Its function in modulating trichome initiation and integrity is validated by VIGS, with endoplasmic reticulum localization further supporting its lipid transport activity. β-caryophyllene and other metabolites are hypothesized to potentially inhibit aphid reproduction via dual pathways: interfering with feeding/nutrient acquisition and disrupting the JHAMT-JH regulatory axis by impairing NADH/ATP-dependent energy metabolism. Despite these insights, gaps remain, including the specific lipid substrates of GhABCG32 and targeted volatile metabolite analysis, which will be addressed in future studies using GC-MS, behavioral assays, and protein interaction experiments. Collectively, GhABCG32 emerges as a key node linking trichome development to aphid resistance, offering a promising candidate target for CRISPR-mediated breeding of aphid-resistant cotton, with its full functional mechanisms to be elucidated by follow-up research.
