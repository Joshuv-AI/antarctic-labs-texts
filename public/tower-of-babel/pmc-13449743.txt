Hepatitis B virus-specific T cell receptor therapy for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma: lessons from Wu et al.
Authors: Rashmi Kumar, Irina Dobrosotskaya, Timothy L Frankel
Published: 2026

Introduction

Primary liver malignancies contribute significantly to the global cancer burden and remain the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs) comprise 75–85% of newly diagnosed liver cancers and arise as a result of chronic liver inflammation, secondary to hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, alcohol use, or cirrhosis due to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (2,3). Globally, HBV infection remains the leading cause of HCC despite ongoing efforts to expand vaccination (1). HCC carries an overall 5-year mortality rate of 18–21%, and surgical resection remains a mainstay of treatment (1,3). However, fewer than 20% of HCC patients are candidates for surgical resection at the time of presentation due to tumor location and size, as well as the severity of underlying liver disease (3). As a result, HCC remains a clinically challenging disease with limited treatment options and is in critical need of advancements in systemic therapy.

The unmet need in HCC therapy

Currently, few systemic therapies are available for HCC patients presenting with intermediate or late-stage disease (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage B or greater). The use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has provided only modest improvements in overall survival. The first systemic therapy approved for HCC was sorafenib in 2008; the SHARP trial reported that sorafenib, compared to placebo, extended overall survival by just 3 months in advanced HCC (4). The only other TKI to show non-inferiority to sorafenib in the frontline setting is the multi-receptor TKI lenvatinib (approved in 2018) (5), while additional second-line TKIs are also available (6).

While the advent of immunotherapy has changed the landscape of many solid tumor treatment algorithms, HCC remains a low mutational burden tumor without significant neo-antigenicity. While various combinations including immune checkpoint inhibitors have significantly improved survivals, a significant proportion of patients lack response to these therapies and do not benefit from them (7-9).

Given these limitations, the field of HCC management has expanded towards evaluating the potential of adoptive T cell therapy, with emphasis on exploiting oncogenic viruses such as hepatitis B as T cell targets.

T cell receptor therapies (TCR-Ts) in HCC

T-cell therapies have shown promise in solid tumors and come in two broad categories: chimeric antigen receptor-based therapies (CAR-T cell) and recombinant TCR-Ts (10). TCR-Ts utilize lentiviral or retroviral transduction in conjunction with the patient’s own T cells to express a specific TCR of interest.

In 2024, Wan et al. published the development of SCG101, an autologous, HBV-specific T cell product that expresses a high-affinity TCR isolated from an HLA-A*02:01 donor with resolved HBV infection (11). This high-affinity TCR was cloned into a viral vector, then used to transduce T cells to create highly potent SCG101 T cells that specifically recognized hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-expressing hepatocytes (11). In the initial publication, a single patient was selected for T cell recombination and subsequent TCR infusion. The patient from the index study (ST1206) had HBV-induced HCC, HBsAg levels exceeding 600 IU/mL during screening and was notably hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg; a marker of active, ongoing acute infection) negative (11). Clinically, the patient had a history of tumor and liver segment 8 resection, followed by trans-arterial chemoembolization (TACE), and treatment with sorafenib for 1 year (11). The patient underwent lymphodepletion followed by a single dose of adoptive T cell transfer, which resulted in stable disease for approximately 6.9 months (11).

Insights from Wu et al. (12): efficacy, safety, and TCR persistence

These results prompted the more recent investigator-initiated trial utilizing SCG101 published by Wu et al. in Gut in 2025 (12). In this study, six human patients with advanced HCC were enrolled to assess efficacy and safety of monotherapy SCG101 in patients with HBV-related HCC. The trial inclusion criteria required HLA-A*02:01 positivity, serum HBsAg positivity, and HBeAg negativity in patients with advanced HBV-HCC and Child-Pugh stage A liver disease (12). All patients had failed 1–3 lines of systemic therapies, and four out of six had prior exposure to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors. The subjects received a single dose of TCR-T cells three days after lymphodepletion; all had received TKIs and four out of six had additionally received immune checkpoint therapies (12). Patients were followed for a median of 12.5 months, with monitoring for systemic side effects, persistence of HBsAg, liver inflammation, and disease progression.

Assessing tolerability after infusion involved monitoring for acute toxicity. All six patients experienced grade 3 or 4 elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), over 80% experienced grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia, 66% suffered grade 3 or 4 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and all were successfully managed. Remarkably, none of the patients experienced neurotoxicity. Efficacy of the infusion was determined through several assays and imaging modalities. Tumor regression was measured by evaluating target lesions according to modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) on computed tomography (CT) imaging. Two of six patients had appreciable tumor regression on imaging, noted as partial response, two out of six had stable disease, and two out of six exhibited local disease progression in the liver or distant disease progression in the lung (a non-target site) (12). Notably, HBsAg levels were monitored during the period of peak expansion of T cells (days 4–16 post-infusion) and were reduced to less than 145 IU/mL in five out of six patients; only one patient had an elevated nadir of HBsAg levels (12).

Implications for patient selection

Median progression-free survival was 12.8 weeks; notably, one patient who had a partial response remained progression-free for over 2 years of data accrual (12). The patient (ST1301) with the best response to treatment had the greatest proportion of HBsAg+ hepatocytes in the liver area as measured on biopsies and had persistently detectable HBsAg+ cells in liver tissue. Moreover, serum HBsAg was also >1,000 IU/mL at the start of treatment. However, patients with progressive disease had similar pre-treatment clinical characteristics, suggesting response to therapy was not fully dependent on severity of HBsAg load or inflammation. The authors suggest that serum HBsAg may not fully reflect the extent of viral integration or disease severity (12). While drawing correlations between responses and patient characteristics in an early phase trial is premature, the correlation between treatment response and T cell persistence is definitely thought-provoking and biologically intuitive. Both patients with disease progression had poor persistence of HBV-TCRs, and two patients with notable partial response had persistent HBV-TCRs beyond study week 25. It would also be interesting to know if previous exposure to PD-1 drugs afforded any subsequent benefit in the subjects.

One limitation of the current HBV-specific TCRs is the relative paucity of HCC cells that are positive for HBsAg. In this study, the measurable range of HBsAg+ tumor cells in biopsies was 0–5%. In contrast, the total number of liver cells positive for HBsAg was much greater in the non-HCC liver, ranging from 10–50%. In 2018, a study assessing HBsAg positivity in matched non-cancerous liver vs. HCC tumor samples in over 88 patients using immunohistochemistry on tissue blocks noted a similar phenomenon (13). Intra-hepatic HBsAg positivity was present in 73.2% of non-cancerous tissue samples, but only in 10.7% of the HCC tissues, emphasizing that HBsAg positivity in HCC samples was significantly reduced in comparison to non-cancerous liver tissue from the same patient (13). This distinction is important in the context of HBV-specific TCR therapy; the effect of the treatment may be largely targeted towards hepatocytes positive for HBsAg, not necessarily HCC tumor cells that are HBsAg+.

The current trial designs for HBV-targeting TCRs also highlights the importance of barriers in patient pre-testing and treatment monitoring. Liver biopsy as a measure of HBsAg positivity is difficult in the setting of multi-modal care. While the optimal time to obtain the biopsy may be prior to exposure to any HBV specific treatments, patients on nucleoside/nucleotide analogs (NAs) may have reduced burden of HBV positivity. In this study, the biopsies were obtained 1–5 years prior to TCR infusion (12). Simultaneously, serial percutaneous liver biopsies to monitor treatment response is also challenging and not without risks in patients with concurrent cirrhosis. Newer technologies such as the use of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in HCC or use of HBV cell-free DNA (cfDNA) to monitor disease is an area of active investigation and may provide biopsy-independent modalities for treatment monitoring in the future.

HBV-specific TCR generation is an area of growing interest (10,14). A similar study utilizing HBV-specific TCR therapies was published by Zhao et al. in 2025 (15). Notable differences in this study included the use of HBV-TCRs specifically in patients with HCC recurrence post-liver transplantation, and the use of combination mRNA electroporation with lentiviral transduction to generate both short- and long-lived HBV-TCR-T cells (15). While similar rates of responders and non-responders were described, Wu et al. provide a more comprehensive overview of pre-treatment characteristics with tissue biopsies. In addition, the measurement of TCR persistence and long-term TCR expression allows for more in-depth assessment of key traits of long-term responders.

Path forward: potentials of HBV-targeted TCR therapy

In conclusion, SCG101 monotherapy in advanced HCC shows selective benefit in patients able to maintain TCR transduced T cell persistence, with durable antiviral activity and progression-free survival up to two years. Taken together, the utility of SCG101 can be considered in two discrete ways. First, as a means for disease control, it shows promise in select individuals capable of generating persistent TCRs; current methods for pre-therapy selection must be revisited, as HBsAg load and HBsAg+ cell percentage alone do not predict response to therapy. Second, the drug’s ability to control hepatitis B infection is also very intriguing and merits further, long-term investigation. While adjuvant therapy for HCC is still necessary, perhaps the short-term goal should be the application of HBV-TCR therapy for HBV infections in conjunction with NA therapies. Given that chronically infected liver biopsies contain a higher fraction of HBsAg positive hepatocytes, this could provide durable HBV infection control and treatment in chronically infected patients, ultimately leading to long-term HCC prevention.

Wu et al. (Gut, 2025) advance the field by demonstrating that SCG101 adoptive T cell therapy can elicit durable responses in selected patients with HBV-related advanced HCC, particularly those with sustained persistence of HBV-specific T cells (12). Moving forward, elucidating the mechanisms governing TCR persistence, refining patient selection criteria beyond serum HBsAg levels, and exploring preventive applications for chronic HBV infection could together catalyze the next wave of progress in HCC therapeutics.
