The Quantum Origin of Diffraction from Bright and Dark States
Jian-Jian Cheng, Jun-Ling Che, Lin Zhang, Ming-Liang Hu

Abstract
Diffraction, a cornerstone of wave optics, is reinterpreted through bright and dark collective states. In the continuous-mode framework, the diffraction pattern arises from projection onto a single bright mode, while dark-region photons populate orthogonal dark modes. Unlike the classical view of destructive interference as field cancellation, the quantum description shows photons persisting in detector-uncoupled states. Our approach thus resolves a key limitation of Glauber’s theory by identifying the detectable and undetectable modes, offering a complete particle-based explanation for diffraction.

In accordance with Glauber’s theory of quantum optical coherence, we model the measurement process at a microscopic level through energy exchange between the radiation field and a sensor atom. Within this framework, the quantum statistical properties of the electromagnetic field are captured by the electric field operator
[
16
;
17
]
:

\hat{E}(\mathbf{r},t)=\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)+\hat{E}^{(-)}(\mathbf{r},t),

(1)

where
\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)
and
\hat{E}^{(-)}(\mathbf{r},t)
denote the positive and negative frequency components, respectively. These correspond quantum mechanically to the photon annihilation and creation operators: specifically,
\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)\propto\hat{a}
and
\hat{E}^{(-)}(\mathbf{r},t)\propto\hat{a}^{\dagger}
.

The probability of detecting a photon from a single-mode field in a state
|\Psi\rangle
is proportional to the expectation value

\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{E}^{(-)}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)\middle|\Psi\right\rangle\propto\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\middle|\Psi\right\rangle,

(2)

which arises naturally from the energy-transfer mechanism between the field and sensor. This interaction is described under the rotating-wave approximation by the Hamiltonian
[
2
]
:

\hat{H}=\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{\sigma}^{+}+\hat{E}^{(-)}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{\sigma}^{-},

(3)

where
\sigma^{+}
(and
\sigma^{-}
) are the raising (and lowering) operators that induce transitions between the ground state
|g\rangle
and excited state
|e\rangle
of the sensor atom.

In contrast to the classical treatment of single-slit diffraction via the Huygens-Fresnel principle, which constructs the wavefront through superposition of elementary spherical subwaves, we develop a quantum formulation based on Glauber’s theory of optical coherence.
Here, each point
x\in[0,b]
corresponds to a localized mode operator
\hat{a}(x)
satisfying
[\hat{a}(x),\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})]=\delta(x-x^{\prime})
, with the single-photon state at position
x
defined as
|1_{x}\rangle=\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x)|0\rangle
. These states maintain orthogonality through
\langle 1_{x}|1_{x^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta(x-x^{\prime})
. Whereas the classical approach sums field amplitudes, our quantum construction builds the optical state from coherent superpositions of localized single-photon states, establishing a fundamentally particle-based description of diffraction from first principles.

Fraunhofer single-slit diffraction intensity distribution. The red envelope represents the diffraction pattern predicted by classical wave optics. Within this framework, the quantum interpretation is revealed: red dots denote photons in “bright” states that effectively couple to the detector and are thus detectable, while gray dots correspond to photons in “dark” states that, although physically present, remain undetectable due to their decoupling from the detector.

We accordingly construct the positive-frequency electric field operator for direction
\theta
as (see Fig.
1
)

\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\int_{0}^{b}\hat{a}(x)e^{i\phi(x,\theta)}dx.

(4)

This defines a collective mode, described by the operator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)
which satisfies
[\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta),\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)]=1
, that exhibits constructive interference toward direction
\theta
. This commutation relation underpins the detector-oriented quantum basis. In the far field, spherical wavefronts emitted from different points along the slit can be approximated as plane waves, with phases that depend linearly on the position. Thus,
\phi(x,\theta)=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x\sin\theta
represents the phase delay under the Fraunhofer approximation for a wave originating at slit point
x
and detected at angle
\theta
.

The interaction between the electromagnetic field and a localized detector, a sensor atom modeled as a two-level system, is governed by the light-matter interaction Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=g\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)\sigma^{+}+H.c.

(5)

Here,
g=g(\mathbf{r})
denotes the position-dependent coupling strength, taken as uniform across the relevant modes for simplicity, and
\mathbf{r}
specifies the location of the sensor atom. This Hamiltonian embodies a foundational principle of quantum measurement: the detector does not respond to the total photonic field, but only to that particular collective mode defined by the positive-frequency operator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)
. A field state is detectable, and can induce a transition from the atomic ground state
|g\rangle
to the excited state
|e\rangle
, only if it possesses a nonzero component along this specific mode. Conversely, any field component that lies in the kernel of
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)
, forming a dark state, remains completely decoupled from the sensor. This formulation offers an interpretation of directional interference within a quantum-mechanical framework, where the detection process is governed by the intrinsic properties of the sensor itself.

In the Fraunhofer diffraction regime with normal incidence, the incident plane wave illuminates the slit uniformly, resulting in a symmetric initial quantum state:

|S\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\int_{0}^{b}|1_{x}\rangle dx.

(6)

Here,
|1_{x}\rangle\equiv|\ldots,0_{x^{\prime}},1_{x},0_{x^{\prime\prime}},\ldots\rangle
denotes a single photon localized at position
x
, with all other points remaining in the vacuum state. In the classical picture, uniform illumination is described by a constant field amplitude; in the quantum picture, by contrast, it corresponds to a complete spatial delocalization of the photon, meaning that the photon simultaneously “explores” all possible paths through the slit. Each position eigenstate
|1_{x}\rangle
can be regarded as a quantized version of a Huygens secondary source, and their coherent superposition leads to the resulting diffraction pattern.

Glauber’s theory of optical coherence successfully predicts detection probabilities through the first-order correlation function. Specifically, the probability for a detector to absorb a single photon at an angle
\theta
is governed by
[
17
]
:

G^{(1)}(\theta)=\langle\psi|\hat{E}^{(-)}\hat{E}^{(+)}|\psi\rangle=\left|\left\langle 0\middle|\hat{E}^{(+)}\middle|\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2},

(7)

which quantifies the likelihood of exciting a sensor atom placed at that angular position.
While this approach correctly predicts the far-field diffraction pattern, it does not explicitly reveal the underlying quantum dynamics that determine which components of the optical state couple to the detector. In particular, for single-slit diffraction, it remains unclear how a localized sensor atom selectively interacts with a specific collective mode among the continuum emerging from the slit, and how the spatial relationship between the detector and the photon state governs the energy transfer.

To address these questions, we develop a quantum description of single-slit diffraction by introducing a detector-oriented basis. The central idea is to construct a complete orthonormal set of states
\{|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
associated with the detection direction
\theta
. These states are defined as

|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\int_{0}^{b}e^{-i\phi(x,\theta)}e^{ik_{n}x}|1_{x}\rangle dx,

(8)

with
k_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{b}
. Here, the phase factor
e^{-i\phi(x,\theta)}
compensates for the propagation phase from the slit to the detector, thereby linking the basis intrinsically to the detector’s directional sensitivity, while
e^{ik_{n}x}
provides a Fourier modulation that ensures orthogonality. The orthogonality relation
\langle\psi_{m}(\theta)|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle=\delta_{mn}
follows from Fourier series properties on
[0,b]
, confirming that
\{|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle\}
forms a complete orthonormal basis for expanding any single-photon state.

This basis diagonalizes the light–detector interaction Hamiltonian, enabling a clear distinction between bright states that strongly couple to the sensor and dark states that do not.
The
n=0
state defines the bright state:
|\text{Bright}_{\theta}\rangle=|\psi_{0}(\theta)\rangle
,
which satisfies

\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|\text{Bright}_{\theta}\rangle=|0\rangle.

(9)

This state is efficiently absorbed by the detector, corresponding to maximum light-matter coupling strength, and represents a coherent symmetric superposition of all localized modes with perfectly aligned phases in the
\theta
direction.

For
n\neq 0
, the states
|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle
define dark modes
|\text{Dark}_{n,\theta}\rangle
that satisfy

\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|\text{Dark}_{n,\theta}\rangle=0.

(10)

Although these states contain photons, their specific phase structure, associated with transverse wave number
k_{n}
, causes complete destructive interference in the
\theta
direction. As a result, they remain decoupled from the detector under the interaction Hamiltonian.

The incident single-photon state
|S\rangle
expands in this detector-defined basis as

\displaystyle|S\rangle=c_{0}|\text{Bright}_{\theta}\rangle+\sum_{n\neq 0}c_{n}|\text{Dark}_{n,\theta}\rangle.

(11)

The coefficients given by

c_{n}=e^{i(\beta-n\pi)}\,\frac{\sin(\beta-n\pi)}{\beta-n\pi}
.
Here,
\beta=\frac{\pi b}{\lambda}\sin\theta
is the normalized phase difference parameter.
The normalization
\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}=1
confirms that photons are redistributed among quantum states without loss. This expansion recasts the diffraction process as a projection onto a bright–dark basis determined by the detector. The correspondence with classical wave optics becomes evident in the bright-state projection. The detection probability
|c_{0}|^{2}=(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta})^{2}
reproduces the classical single-slit profile exactly. Where classical theory attributes this pattern to interference of field amplitudes, the quantum description identifies it with the probability of the photon occupying the bright mode.
as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Moreover, our continuum model reveals an infinite-dimensional dark subspace, a direct consequence of the slit’s finite width. This richness in dark states originates from the Fourier link between position at the slit and direction in the far field: any wave vector orthogonal to the bright mode forms a distinct dark state. Thus, a diffraction minimum simply indicates that all photons populate these dark states, remaining physically present yet invisible to the detector. This unitary, basis-dependent redistribution conserves photon number perfectly, demonstrating that the photons are not missing but unobserved, and that the diffraction pattern itself is an imprint of the detection process.

The distribution of dark-state weights
|c_{n}|^{2}
decays rapidly with increasing
|n|
, mirroring the suppression of high spatial frequencies in classical diffraction patterns. At
\beta=0.3\pi
, the bright-state population is
|c_{0}|^{2}\approx 0.737
, while the dark subspace constitutes only 26.3% of the total probability. Within this relatively small dark subspace, the
n=1
mode dominates with approximately 51.4% of the dark components (
|c_{1}|^{2}\approx 0.135
), followed by the
n=-1
mode at about 14.9% (
|c_{-1}|^{2}\approx 0.039
). The
n=2
and
n=-2
modes contribute approximately 8.7% and 4.8% respectively. This rapid fall-off in
|c_{n}|^{2}
confirms that the photon state is dominantly composed of the spectrally aligned bright mode and the few lowest-wave-number dark modes.

As discussed above, dark states correspond to eigenstates of the operator
\hat{E}^{(+)}(\mathbf{r},t)
with zero eigenvalue, which suggests that they may remain undetectable by conventional sensors such as two-level atoms. This property invites a natural question: to what extent can quantum mechanical dark and bright states account for the classical phenomena of diffraction? We now extend the detector-dependent mode eigenstates to an operator formalism that accommodates arbitrary photon numbers. The creation operators are defined through the fundamental relation

\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}|0\rangle=|\psi_{n}(\theta)\rangle
,
which leads directly to

\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\int_{0}^{b}e^{-i\phi(x,\theta)}e^{ik_{n}x}\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x)dx.

(12)

This construction ensures that each operator
\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}
generates precisely the corresponding single-photon state from the vacuum. The formalism thus transitions from describing individual particle wave functions to characterizing occupation numbers across different modes.

These operators satisfy the standard bosonic commutation relations
[\hat{J}_{m},\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{mn}
, confirming the mathematical independence of the corresponding modes. The total photon number operator then satisfies
\hat{N}_{\text{total}}=\int_{0}^{b}\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{a}(x)dx=\sum_{n}\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{J}_{n}
, manifesting the strict conservation of photon number during diffraction.
The case
n=0
defines the bright-state creation operator
\hat{B}_{\theta}^{\dagger}
, which produces photons that interfere constructively in the direction
\theta
and are efficiently detected. For
n\neq 0
, the dark-state operators
\hat{D}_{n,\theta}^{\dagger}
generate photons that remain decoupled from the detector due to destructive interference.
The extension to multi-photon states provides a robust demonstration of how the bright–dark state formalism accounts for classical diffraction phenomena. This framework offers a unified description spanning quantum and classical regimes, revealing how diffraction patterns emerge from the statistical distribution of photons across detector-defined modes.

We begin with an
N
-photon Fock state under uniform illumination:

\displaystyle|N\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\int_{0}^{b}\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x)dx\right)^{N}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\left(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\right)^{N}|0\rangle,

(13)

where the global creation operator satisfies
[\hat{A},\hat{A}^{\dagger}]=1
.

As detailed in the Supplemental Material (SM)
[
27
]
, or in Refs.
[
10
;
25
;
26
]
, the detector-oriented basis forms a complete basis in the single-slit geometry. Within this framework, the light-matter interaction leads to the fundamental relation:

\hat{H}|N\rangle|g\rangle=g\sqrt{N}c_{0}|N-1\rangle|e\rangle.

(14)

This captures the excitation of the sensor atom alongside the annihilation of one photon from the
N
-photon collective state, with a transition amplitude governed by
\sqrt{N}c_{0}
. The resulting dynamics mirror those of the
N
-excitation Dicke basis in multiatom systems
[
18
]
: the
\sqrt{N}
factor reflects the bosonic enhancement from
N
indistinguishable photons, while
c_{0}
encodes the directional interference that selectively activates the bright mode for detection.
This dynamical picture is further reflected in the mode occupations. In the detector-defined basis at angle
\theta
, the expectation values exhibit a clear structure:

\displaystyle\langle N|\hat{B}_{\theta}^{\dagger}\hat{B}_{\theta}|N\rangle

\displaystyle=N|c_{0}|^{2},

\displaystyle\langle N|\hat{D}_{n,\theta}^{\dagger}\hat{D}_{n,\theta}|N\rangle

\displaystyle=N|c_{n}|^{2}.

(15)

This distribution reflects the quantum statistics of indistinguishable bosons: each photon independently populates the bright mode with probability
|c_{0}|^{2}
, leading to an average bright-mode population of
N|c_{0}|^{2}
. The first-order correlation function consequently becomes:

\displaystyle G^{(1)}_{\text{Fock}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=g^{2}\langle N|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|N\rangle

\displaystyle=g^{2}N\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}.

(16)

For the Fock state, the classical diffraction profile (scaled by
N
) originates directly from the quantum probability amplitude of the bright state.

For a coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
with uniform amplitude
\alpha(x)=\alpha
, we begin by applying the displacement operator
\hat{D}(\alpha)
to the vacuum (see SM
[
27
]
):

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{D}(\alpha)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{b}\left[\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}(x)-\alpha\hat{a}(x)\right]dx\right)|0\rangle.

(17)

Rewriting this in the mode operator basis yields

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=\exp\left(\sum_{n}\left[\alpha\sqrt{b}\,c_{n}\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}-\alpha\sqrt{b}\,c_{n}^{*}\hat{J}_{n}\right]\right)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\otimes_{n}\exp\left(\alpha\sqrt{b}\,c_{n}\hat{J}_{n}^{\dagger}-\alpha\sqrt{b}\,c_{n}^{*}\hat{J}_{n}\right)|0\rangle_{n}.

(18)

The appearance of the direct product
\otimes_{n}
indicates that the total state factors into independent contributions from each mode. Defining the coherent amplitude of each mode as
\alpha_{n}=\alpha\sqrt{b}\,c_{n}
, we arrive at the decomposed form:

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=\otimes_{n}|\alpha_{n}\rangle,

(19)

where each
|\alpha_{n}\rangle
is a coherent state of mode
n
satisfying
\hat{J}_{n}|\alpha_{n}\rangle=\alpha_{n}|\alpha_{n}\rangle
. This factorization reflects a key property of coherent states: different modes remain uncorrelated. Thus, the bright and dark mode operators act as

\hat{B}_{\theta}|\alpha\rangle=\alpha_{0}|\alpha\rangle,\quad\hat{D}_{n,\theta}|\alpha\rangle=\alpha_{n}|\alpha\rangle,

(20)

implying that the detection signal stems solely from the bright mode. The first-order correlation function thus reduces to:

\displaystyle G^{(1)}_{\text{Coherent}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=g^{2}\langle\alpha|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=g^{2}|\alpha|^{2}b\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2},

(21)

where
|\alpha|^{2}b
denotes the total incident photon flux.
This
modal decomposition confirms that for a coherent state,
only the bright component is detected, despite the occupation of dark modes.

The decomposition into bright and dark modes uncovers fundamental distinctions between Fock and coherent states that remain hidden in conventional first-order measurements. Although both states yield identical diffraction patterns in the first-order correlation function, their underlying quantum structures differ markedly. Coherent states factorize into independent modes, whereas Fock states exhibit strong intermode correlations, a direct consequence of their fixed total photon number.

This distinction becomes apparent only through a deeper understanding of the field operator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)
. Rather than merely representing a local field amplitude, this operator encapsulates the entire optical mode detected in the far field at angle
\theta
. The quantum statistics of the source manifest as photon-number fluctuation correlations within the bright mode. Although the diffraction pattern is shaped by the bright–dark decomposition defined by the detector, higher-order correlations unveil the intrinsic quantum nature of the optical state.

Specifically, we evaluate the second-order correlation function for an
N
-photon Fock state:

\displaystyle G^{(2)}_{\text{Fock}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle N|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|N\rangle}{\langle N|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(-)}(\theta)\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{(+)}(\theta)|N\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{N}.

(22)

This result reveals clear photon antibunching, reflecting quantum correlations imposed by the fixed photon number. For
N=1
, the correlation vanishes completely, indicating perfect antibunching, while for large
N
it approaches the coherent-state limit. In contrast,

G^{(2)}_{\text{Coherent}}(\theta)=1

reflecting the Poissonian statistics and absence of photon correlations in a coherent state.

On the experimental front, platforms such as superconducting circuits and trapped-ion arrays offer a controlled setting to emulate a discretized version of a continuous slit using coupled electromagnetic or vibrational modes
[
28
]
. Quantum state tomography of the multimode field could then directly reveal the mode occupation distribution: at diffraction minima, the bright-mode population vanishes while the total excitation remains unity, fully distributed among the dark modes, providing clear evidence for the dark space.
Beyond foundational verification, the dark space offers intriguing practical prospects. As an infinite-dimensional subspace, it extends beyond the single dark state of discrete models, providing a naturally decoherence-free photonic memory. Encoding quantum information in this subspace protects it from radiative loss into the bright channel. More profoundly, the high dimensionality of the dark space allows redundant encoding across multiple dark modes, opening a path toward fault-tolerant quantum memory in structured photonic environments
[
29
;
30
]
.

In summary, we develop a continuous-mode quantum theory of single-slit diffraction that extends Glauber’s formalism to provide a complete particle-based interpretation of the phenomenon.
Our framework reveals that at each detection point, the optical field decomposes into a single bright mode accompanied by an infinite-dimensional dark subspace.
This formulation provides a unified particle-based picture where the classical intensity profile corresponds precisely to the population in the bright state, directly linking wave-optical patterns to detector excitation probability. Photons reaching intensity minima occupy the non-interacting dark subspace, remaining physically present while being undetectable by conventional means. The theory thus offers a quantum-mechanical account of how diffraction patterns emerge originate from particle behavior in optical systems.
