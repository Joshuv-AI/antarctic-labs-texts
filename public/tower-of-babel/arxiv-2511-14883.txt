Bernhard Aigner, Marcus Waurick

Abstract
. We extend a contraction mapping argument for ordinary state-dependent delay differential equations to evolutionary partial differential equations in the sense of R. Picard, that is, to equations of the form ( ∂ t M ( ∂ t ) + A ) u ( t ) = F ( t , u ( t ) ) \bigl(\partial_{t}M(\partial_{t})+A\bigr)u(t)=F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr) , where A A is an m \mathrm{m} -accretive (unbounded) linear operator and M M is a material law. We establish local well-posedness (in the sense of weak solutions) of generalized initial value problems that stem from a distributional formulation. We require prehistories in H 1 H^{1} with bounded derivative, a regularity increasing right-hand side and a consistency condition. We showcase the viability of our results by applying them to classical examples (heat, wave and Maxwell’s equations), examples from semigroup theory, port-Hamiltonian systems, as well as equations featuring fractional derivatives and convolutions (in time) with bounded operators.



1.
Introduction

In this article we study partial differential equations (PDEs) with state-dependent delay, which are PDEs of the form

(Du)(t)=F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)\qquad t>0\text{,}

where
D
is a (partial) differential operator in space-time and the right-hand side
F
is a function depending on the history

u_{(t)}\colon\,[-h,0]\rightarrow H\text{,}\qquad s\mapsto u(t+s)

of the state
u
. The parameter
h>0
describes a suitable backward time-horizon and
H
at this point denotes any Hilbert space. The dependence on prior histories demands — in contrast to equations without delay — a prescribed initial prehistory
\Phi
instead of traditional initial values:

u_{(0)}=\Phi\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u(t)=\Phi(t)\quad\text{for }-h\leq t\leq 0\text{.}

This article follows up on recent discoveries concerning the solution theory of initial value problems (IVPs) for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) (i.e.,
D=\nicefrac{{\operatorname{d}}}{{\operatorname{dt}}}
) with state-dependent delay utilizing a weak solution theory and exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces. We refer to
[
11
]
for the principal publication and to
[
1
]
for a summary of the main results and some elementary examples. We will extend the approach presented there to evolutionary equations in the sense of R. Picard, i.e., PDEs of the form

(1)

\bigl(\partial_{t}M(\partial_{t})+A\bigr)u(t)=f(t)+F\bigl(t,(u+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\qquad\,t>0\text{,}\\

where
M
is a holomorphic operator-valued function that is bounded on a right-half space,
M(\partial_{t})
is a so-called material law operator (cf.
section

2.2
or
[
32
, ch. 5.2]
) and
A
is an
\mathrm{m}
-accretive operator on the Hilbert space
H
. In particular, our results hold for skew-selfadjoint
A
. The function
f
is assumed to be suitably regular and integrable,
Z
is an extension of some initial prehistory
\Phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
and
F\colon[0,\infty)\times L_{2}(-h,0;H)\to H
is a continuous map incorporating the state-dependent delay
u_{(t)}
.
In the theory of evolutionary equations, IVPs of the form

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigl(\partial_{t}M(\partial_{t})+A\bigr)u(t)&=F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)&&\qquad\qquad\,t>0\text{,}\\
u(t)&=\Phi(t)&&\quad-h\leq t\leq 0\text{,}\end{aligned}

are equivalent to a reformulation as evolutionary equations on the entire line without initial value, albeit in a distributional setting, cf.
[
32
, thm. 9.4.3]
. The term
f
in
eq.

1
originates from this reformulation and suitably incorporates the initial prehistory
\Phi
. For more complex material laws, such a formulation is obtained using a projection argument. We provide some background in
section

2.3
. The appearance of a distributional formulation is also present in other approaches. Indeed, in the context of solution theory utilizing semigroup theory, well-posedness is understood in the sense of existence of mild solutions. It is a well-known fact, that mild solutions of a Cauchy problem are the classical solutions of the extrapolated Cauchy problem (cf.
[
9
, sec. VI.7]
or
[
31
, sec. 2.2]
).

In the context of delay differential equations (DDEs), semigroup theory has been utilized extensively, e.g., cf.
[
2
]
for a comprehensive overview and also
[
44
]
. There are results for the non-autonomous setting (cf.
[
12
]
) or with asymptotically autonomous delay (cf.
[
30
]
). Here, we offer a different perspective, but we compare our results to semigroup approaches in
section

6.4
and
section

6.5
.

In contrast to semigroup theory, the theory of evolutionary equations can make short work of mixed type problems (e.g., mixed eddy current models, cf.
section

6.3
) and the material law can incorporate operators that are even nonlocal in time (cf.
section

6.7
). The large applicability of evolutionary equations comes at the price of a more involved problem formulation for initial value problems. Indeed, partial differential algebraic equations are included; a subclass for which it is already a challenging task to identify a suitable space of initial values. We choose to forego this problem and focus on the well-posedness aspect. This approach is reminiscent of the foundational article of evolutionary equations
[
24
]
and the works
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
of S. Trostorff.

We want to transform the problem (
2
) into a fixed point problem (FPP) and then utilize a contraction mapping argument. To that end, we employ weighted Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces:

Definition 1.1
.

For
\rho>0
and
-\infty\leq a<b\leq+\infty
let

\displaystyle L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)

\displaystyle\coloneq\overline{\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}\bigl(a,b;H\bigr)}^{\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2,\rho}}\text{,}

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{2,\rho}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Bigl(\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pta}^{b}\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\text{,}

\displaystyle H_{\rho}^{1}(a,b;H)

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}}

The use of weighted Sobolev spaces, rather than the classical (unweighted) versions will allow us to force contractivity of a Lipschitz-mapping, reminiscent of a classical proof of the Picard–Lindelöf theorem for ODEs by Morgenstern, cf.
[
20

[

2.
Background on evolutionary equations

In the context of evolutionary equations, the left-hand side of
eq.

1
is split up into two parts, on one hand the “material dependence”
\partial_{t}M(\partial_{t})
, which is time-dependent, and on the other hand a purely spatial part
A
, which is assumed to be a densely defined, linear,
\mathrm{m}
-accretive operator on the Hilbert space
H
. To explain the first part, we first need to explain a central concept in evolutionary equations, the time derivative in exponentially weighted spaces.

2.1.
The time derivative

Let
\rho>0
and
-\infty<a<b\leq+\infty
. Let

\displaystyle I_{\rho}\colon\,L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)

\displaystyle\to L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)\text{,}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto\Bigl(t\mapsto\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pta}^{t}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)\text{.}

This operator is well-defined on
L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)
, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\rho>0
and
-\infty<a<b\leq+\infty
. Then
I_{\rho}\in\mathcal{L}\bigl(L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)\bigr)
with
\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}
.

The proof is a simple calculation and can be checked in
[
34
, prop. 2.2]
,
[
11
, prop. 4.7]
or
[
1
, prop. 2.3]
.
An easy corollary is the following result:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\rho>0
and
-\infty<a<b\leq+\infty
. For
I_{\rho}\colon\,L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rho}(a,b;H)
we have
\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}+1}
.

Proof.

For
v\in L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)
we infer
I_{\rho}v\in H^{1}_{\rho}(a,b;H)
and hence

\lVert I_{\rho}v\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}}=\lVert I_{\rho}v\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}}+\lVert(I_{\rho}v)^{\prime}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}}\leq\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}+1\bigr)\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}}\text{.}\qed

This allows us to define the time derivative with boundary condition in
L_{2,\rho}
. We could do this in general, but for the purposes of this article it suffices to consider the case
a=0
and
b=\infty
in this instance.

Definition 2.3
.

For
\rho>0
let

\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\colon\,L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)\supseteq\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\bigr)\to L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)\text{,}\qquad\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\coloneq I_{\rho}^{-1}\text{.}

Furthermore, let
\partial_{\rho}
be the standard (distributionally defined) derivative on
L_{2,\rho}
.

The operator
\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}

is injective and a bounded linear operator. In particular,
\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}

Definition 2.4

For

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rho}(-h,\infty;H)

\displaystyle\coloneq\bigl\{u\colon[-h,\infty)\to H\colon\,u\,\text{is continuous and}\,\,\lVert u\rVert_{\rho,\infty}<\infty\bigr\}\text{,}

Theorem 2.5

(Sobolev-embedding)

Let

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\rho>0

Remark 2.7
.

Completely analogously to the half-line case, one defines the operator

.

Let
\rho>0

\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\subseteq\partial_{\rho}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{\rho}=-\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\bigr)^{\ast}+2\rho\text{.}

\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}u(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}{}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}v(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}{}t=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}v(s)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\,\mathrm{d}{}t

We make an additional observation:

Remark 2.10

(
\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}
is
\mathrm{m}
-accretive)
.

We can appeal to
proposition

2.9
and infer for
u\in\operatorname{dom}(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho})

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\big\langle\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}u,u\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}u,u\big\rangle+\big\langle u,\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}u\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}u,u\big\rangle+\big\langle(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho})^{\ast}u,u\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u^{\prime},u\rangle-\langle u^{\prime},u\rangle+2\rho\langle u,u\rangle

i.e.,

is monotone/accretive. It is maximal, because the inverse

is a bounded linear operator; in particular,
\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}

\mathbb{R}_{<0}\subseteq\uprho(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho})

Having introduced the fundamental concept of the invertible time derivative, we can proceed to define material law operators.

2.2.
Material law operators

Definition 2.11
.

A map
M\colon\mathbb{C}\supseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)\to\mathcal{L}(H)
is called a material law if

i)

\operatorname{dom}(M)
is open and
M
is holomorphic and

ii)

there exists some
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}>\rho}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)
and
\lVert M\rVert_{\infty,\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}>\rho}}<\infty
.

Moreover, we call
s_{\mathrm{b}}(M)\coloneq\inf\{\rho\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}>\rho}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)\,\,\wedge\,\,\lVert M\rVert_{\infty,\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}>\rho}}<\infty\}
the abscissa of boundedness of
M
.

Utilizing a functional calculus, one can give meaning to
M(\partial_{\rho})
via the so-called
Fourier-Laplace transform

\mathcal{L_{\rho}}
which is the unitary extension of

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};H)&\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};H)\text{,}\\
\phi&\mapsto\Bigl(t\mapsto\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-(\mathrm{i}t+\rho)s}\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)\end{aligned}

to a map
\mathcal{L_{\rho}}\colon L_{2,\rho}\left(\mathbb{R};H\right)\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R};H)
, cf.
[
32
, ch. 5.2]
.

Definition 2.12
.

We define the material law operator

M(\partial_{\rho})\coloneq\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{\rho}M(\mathrm{i}\mathrm{m}+\rho)\mathcal{L}_{\rho}\in\mathcal{L}\bigl(L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\bigr)\text{,}

where
\mathrm{m}
denotes the multiplication with the argument operator.

This definition holds for the entire real line (i.e., the case
L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
). It is not immediately obvious what
M\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\bigr)
is supposed to be in case of the half-line (i.e., on
L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
). We will forego this problem later on by using a projection argument. Material laws exhibit the following important property:

Definition 2.13
.

Definition 2.17

is convex, by Mazur’s lemma:
\overline{V_{\alpha}}^{\!\!\phantom{.}{}_{\sigma(H^{1},(H^{1})^{\prime})}}=\overline{V_{\alpha}}^{\!\!\phantom{.}{}_{H^{1}}}
. Since
V_{\alpha}
is a closed subspace in
H^{1}(-h,0;H)
by
[
11
, rem. 3.1]
,
u\in V_{\alpha}
follows. For the convenience of the reader, we repeat the short proof of closedness of
V_{\alpha}
in
H^{1}
here:

Suppose
(u_{n})_{n}\in V_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
u_{n}\to u
in
H^{1}(-h,0;H)
. By the Fischer–Riesz theorem,
u_{n_{k}}^{\prime}\to u^{\prime}
pointwise almost everywhere for a subsequence and thus
\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
.
∎

3.2.2.
Projection

It will be critical to force the argument of
F
from
eq.

6
to be in
V_{\alpha}
in order to apply almost uniform Lipschitz-continuity of
F
. Expanding upon the proof strategy from
[
11
, thm. 4.1]
, we accomplish this by means of a projection:

Definition 3.7
.

Let the metric projection on
V_{\alpha}
be denoted by

\pi_{\alpha}\colon\begin{Bmatrix}L_{2}\\
H^{1}\end{Bmatrix}\to V_{\alpha}\text{,}\qquad v\mapsto\begin{cases}v&for $v\in V_{\alpha}$\text{,}\\
\operatorname{argmin}_{u\in V_{\alpha}}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2}\,\text{or}\,H^{1}}&for $v\notin V_{\alpha}\text{.}$\end{cases}

Since
V_{\alpha}
is a closed convex subspace and because
H^{1}
and
L_{2}
are Hilbert spaces, the metric projection exists. In either case, the metric projection is Lipschitz-continuous with constant
1
(cf.
[
43
, thm. V.3.2 & ex. V.6.15]
).

3.2.3.
Regularity preservation

For technical reasons, we will have to impose the condition that the right-hand side of
eq.

6
in some sense increases the regularity of the unknown
u
. We will achieve this by supposing that
F
is of a specific form and contains a nonlinearity that preserves regularity.

Definition 3.8
.

We call
F
regularity preserving if for
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}
with
\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}<\infty
, the function
t\mapsto F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)
defines an element of
H^{1}_{\rho}(0,\infty;H)
.

Example 3.9
.

Examples of such regularity-preserving functions include

•

autonomous functions
F\bigl(u_{(t)}\bigr)=\sigma\bigl(u(t)\bigr)
for any (globally) Lipschitz-continuous function
\sigma\colon H\to H
:

For
t,s\geq 0
we can estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert F(t,u_{(t)})-F(s,u_{s})\rVert

\displaystyle=\big\lVert\sigma\bigl(u(t))-\sigma(u(s)\bigr)\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}\lVert u(t)-u(s)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lvert t-s\rvert\text{,}

where
L_{\sigma}
denotes the Lipschitz-constant of
\sigma
. In particular,
t\mapsto F\bigl(t,u(t)\bigr)
is differentiable almost everywhere and we can bound the derivative by the
L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
-integrable constant
L_{\sigma}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}
.

•

the “standard” example of state-dependent delay,
F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)=u\bigl(t-\tau(u(t))\bigr)
for any (globally) Lipschitz-continuous
\tau\colon H\to[0,h]
:

Because
u^{\prime}\in L_{\infty}
,
u
is Lipschitz-continuous and we can calculate for
t,s\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\big\lVert F(t,u_{(t)})-F(s,u_{(s)})\big\rVert

\displaystyle=\big\lVert u\bigl(t-\tau(u(t))\bigr)-u\bigl(s-\tau(u(s))\bigr)\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lvert t-\tau(u(t))-s+\tau(u(s))\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\bigl[\lvert t-s\rvert+L_{\tau}\lVert u(t)-u(s)\rVert\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\bigl[1+L_{\tau}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\bigr]\lvert t-s\rvert\text{,}

where
L_{\tau}
denotes the Lipschitz-constant of
\tau
. In particular,
t\mapsto F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)
is differentiable almost everywhere and we can bound the derivative by the constant
\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\bigl[1+L_{\tau}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\bigr]
, which is an
L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
-integrable function.

The next result provides a more abstract class of examples.

Example 3.10
.

Let
F\colon[0,\infty)\times L_{2}(-h,0;H)\to H
be a function that is

•

an almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous map and

•

satisfies
\forall\alpha>0\exists L>0\forall\varphi\in V_{\alpha}\forall s,t\in[0,\infty)
:

(
\triangle
)

\lVert F(t,\varphi)-F(s,\varphi)\rVert_{H}\leq L\lvert t-s\rvert\text{.}

Then, the function
t\mapsto F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)
is an element of
H^{1}_{\rho}(0,\infty;H)
for any
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,\infty;H)
with bounded derivative.

Proof.

We let
0\leq s\leq t
and calculate for
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,\infty;H)
with
\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
:

\displaystyle\big\lVert F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)-F\bigl(s,u_{(s)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert F\bigl(t,u_{(t)}\bigr)-F\bigl(t,u_{(s)}\bigr)\big\rVert+\big\lVert F\bigl(t,u_{(s)}\bigr)-F\bigl(s,u_{(s)}\bigr)\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\alpha}^{(1)}\lVert u_{(s)}-u_{(t)}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{\alpha}^{(2)}\lvert t-s\rvert\text{,}

where
L_{\alpha}^{(1)}
denotes the Lipschitz-constant w.r.t. to the first variable provided by almost uniform Lipschitz-continuity and
L_{\alpha}^{(2)}
w.r.t. the second variable provided by (
\triangle
). We can further estimate the first summand in the following way:

\displaystyle\lVert u_{(s)}-u_{(t)}\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{0}\lVert u(s+r)-u(t+r)\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}r

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts-h}^{s}\lVert u(t-s+x)-u(x)\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts-h}^{s}\Big\lVert\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t-s}u^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}{}y\Big\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts-h}^{s}\lvert t-s\rvert^{2}\alpha^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}x=\lvert t-s\rvert^{2}\alpha^{2}h\text{.}

Hence, the Lipschitz-seminorm is bounded (almost everywhere) and the claim follows.
∎

3.2.4.
Integrability

Since the theory of evolutionary equations establishes well-posedness of a problem by applying the Picard operator
S_{\rho}
to the right-hand side of a problem, the associated solution theory requires an input on the entire (half-)line. Hence, we will also have to demand an integrability condition. It turns out that the following condition is sufficient:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
v\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,\infty;H)
and
F\colon[0,\infty)\times L_{2}(-h,0;H)\to H
be almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous. Suppose
F(\cdot,0)\in L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
. Then for all
\alpha>0
holds
F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\in L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
.

Proof.

We can simply estimate, using the simple inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
:

\displaystyle\big\lVert F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)}^{2}=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(t)})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert F(t,0)-F(t,0)+F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(t)})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\bigl[2\lVert F(t,0)\rVert_{H}^{2}+2L_{\alpha}\lVert\pi_{\alpha}(0)-\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(t)})\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\bigr]\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\bigl[2\lVert F(t,0)\rVert_{H}^{2}+2L_{\alpha}\lVert\underbrace{v_{(t)}}_{=\Theta_{t}(v)}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\bigr]\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\text{.}

All those integrals are finite, as our estimate for
\Theta
from
lemma

3.2
shows.
∎

3.3.
Reformulation of the problem

Let
A
and
M
satisfy the assumptions of
theorem

2.14
. As discussed in
section

2.3
, the problem we want to investigate is

(
6
)

\bigl(\overline{\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A}\bigr)v=F\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)+f\qquad\text{on}\ L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\text{,}

for some

with support on

and a continuation

of the prehistory
\Phi
. The closure of the operator on the left-hand side allows us to multiply this equation with the Picard operator

(cf.

This version of the problem lends itself to a reformulation as a fixed point problem (FPP) on

via the mapping

provided that the right-hand side of

2.15
.

4.
Well-posedness

Throughout this section, let
A\colon H\supseteq\operatorname{dom}(A)\to H
be an
\mathrm{m}
-accretive linear operator and let
M\colon\mathbb{C}\supseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)\to\mathbb{C}
be a linear material law, both of which together satisfy the assumptions of
theorem

2.14
for all
\rho>0
large enough. Then, utilizing the metric projection
\pi_{\alpha}
from
definition

3.7
, we transition from (
8
) to

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{\rho,\alpha}\colon\,L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)&\rightarrow L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)\text{,}\\
v&\mapsto S_{\rho}f+S_{\rho}F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}((v+Z)_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\text{,}\end{aligned}

where we will assume that
F
satisfies
F(\cdot,0)\in L_{2,\rho}
and the function on the right is therefore in
L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
by
lemma

3.11
.
The tricky part of our well-posedness theory will be to remove the projection
\pi_{\alpha}
again (at least locally in time). Suitable regularity assumptions on

will prove necessary for that as well. Before that, we introduce our notion of solution.

Definition 4.1
.

A local solution
v\in L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
of

6
is a fixed point of
\Gamma_{\rho,\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, that satisfies
\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)=(v+Z)_{(t)}
for

up to some positive
T>0
. A global solution of
eq.

6
is a fixed point of
eq.

8
.

Remark 4.2

(On the notion of solution)
.

Let
v
be a local solution of
eq.

6
, i.e.,
v
solves
eq.

7
up to some positive time
T>0
. Then

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}v

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}S_{\rho}\bigl[f+F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}((v+Z)_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}S_{\rho}\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}\bigl[f+F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}((v+Z)_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}S_{\rho}\bigl[\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}f+\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}((v+Z)_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}S_{\rho}\bigl[\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}f+\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}F\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}S_{\rho}\bigl[\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}f+\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}F\bigl(\cdot,(\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]\text{,}

where we used causality of the solution operator
S_{\rho}
, appealing to
theorem

2.14
. In that sense, a local solution

of
eq.

6
solves the FPP

(10)

L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)\ni w\mapsto S_{\rho}\bigl[\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}f+\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq T}}F\bigl(\cdot,(w+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]\in L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)

for some
T>0
. A global solution in particular solves the FPP above for all
T>0
.

To obtain a local solution of
eq.

6
and to not simply find a fixed point of
eq.

9
(which is necessarily defined on the entirety of
L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
), we will in addition have to impose a more specific shape of

. In order to do that, we introduce the short notation

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(v)(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq F\bigl(t,(v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\text{,}

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{\alpha}(v)(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\bigr)\text{.}

Note that the prehistory is integrated into the functions
\widetilde{F}
and
\widetilde{F}_{\alpha}
. We confine to the following two cases (which are relevant in applications, cf.
section

6
):

The case
\widetilde{F}(v)=I_{\rho}\widetilde{G}(v)

.

We assume
F\bigl(t,(v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)=I_{\rho}G\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)(t)
, which gives rise to the FPP

(11)

v=S_{\rho}\bigl[f+I_{\rho}G\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]\qquad\text{in}\,L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)\text{.}

The case
\widetilde{F}(v)=\widetilde{G}(I_{\rho}v)

.

We assume
F\bigl(t,(v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)=G\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)
, which gives rise to the FPP

(12)

v=S_{\rho}\bigl[f+G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]\qquad\text{in}\,L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)\text{.}

In either case, we again point out that the right-hand side has support only on

, because

and
G
have and the solution operator
S_{\rho}
is causal.

4.1.
Local Existence

Now we can state local existence and uniqueness results for both of these formulations. We will make use of some recurring assumptions:

Assumptions 4.3
.

.

(A)

Let

i)

be almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous (cf.
definition

3.5
),

ii)

regularity preserving (cf.
definition

3.8
)

iii)

and satisfy

(B)

Let

with

Theorem 4.4

(local existence and uniqueness for

11
)
.

Let

satisfy assumption
(A)
and let
\Phi
and

satisfy assumption
(B)

The last condition can be viewed as a consistency condition, which is reminiscent of results for ODEs with state-dependent delay, cf. e.g.,

Proof.

We let
\alpha>\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}
and
\rho>\max\bigl\{\tfrac{L_{\alpha}^{2}}{2c^{2}},\tilde{\rho}\bigr\}
, where

is the Lipschitz-constant of

associated to the parameter

3.5

2.14
.

We first show the existence of a fixed point of

by showing that

satisfies the requirements of the contraction mapping principle:

•

\Gamma_{\rho,\alpha}
is a self-mapping:

Since

maps into

•

\Gamma_{\rho,\alpha}
is Lipschitz-continuous:

Taking the norm

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\big\lVert\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}(v)-\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}(w)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)}^{2}

, we can estimate further:

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{c^{2}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}((v+Z)_{(t)})\bigr)-G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}((w+Z)_{(t)})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{L_{\alpha}^{2}}{c^{2}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)-\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((w+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\quad=\tfrac{L_{\alpha}^{2}}{c^{2}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\lVert v_{(t)}-w_{(t)}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{L_{\alpha}^{2}}{c^{2}}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert v-w\rVert_{H_{\rho}^{1}(-h,\infty;H)}^{2}\text{.}

\Gamma_{\rho,\alpha}
is a contraction:

.

\displaystyle\lVert v(t)\rVert_{H}\leq\Big\lVert\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\chi_{[0,t]}S_{\rho}\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}](s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Big\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\chi_{[0,t]}\big\lVert S_{\rho}\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}](s)\big\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\rho s}\chi_{[0,t]}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho s}\big\lVert S_{\rho}\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}](s)\big\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq\Bigl[\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Bigl[\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert S_{\rho}\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}](s)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}]\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\Bigl[\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert\chi_{[0,t]}[f^{\prime}+B^{\prime}](s)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\Bigl[\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)}+\Bigl(\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\big\lVert\chi_{[0,t]}B^{\prime}(s)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Bigr]

Now we can use the estimate for
B^{\prime}
from (
\circ
) to further estimate:

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\Bigl[\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}+\Bigl(\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}\bigl(L_{1}+L_{2}\lVert(v+Z^{\prime})_{(s)}\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}\bigr)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\Bigl[\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}\!+\!\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{2\rho}}L_{1}\!+\!\Bigl(2L_{2}^{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}2\bigl(\lVert v_{(s)}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\!+\!\lVert Z^{\prime}_{(s)}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\!\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\Bigl[\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}\!+\!\tfrac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{\rho}}\!+\!\Bigl(4L_{2}^{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}h^{2}\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{C}(0,s;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\!\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+4L_{2}^{2}\lVert\Theta Z^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,t;L_{2}(-h,0;H))}^{2}\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\!\leq\!\tfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}}{\sqrt{2\rho}}\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\!\Bigl[\!\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}\!\!\!+\!\tfrac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{\rho}}\!+\!\sqrt{\!\tfrac{2}{\rho}}L_{2}\lVert Z^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,\infty;H)}\!+\!2L_{2}h\!\Bigl(\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{C}(0,s;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\!\,\mathrm{d}{}s\!\Bigr)^{\!\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr]\text{.}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+C_{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t^{\ast}}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{C}(0,s;H)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+C_{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{C}(0,s;H)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\text{.}

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho_{1}}(0,\infty;H)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\lVert v(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho_{1}s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho s}\gamma(s)\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho_{1}s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{\infty}C_{1}\mathrm{e}^{(C_{2}+2(\rho-\rho_{1}))s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s<\infty\text{.}

Hence, the local solution

of

-function. It remains to verify that

is a fixed point of (

\displaystyle\big\lVert G\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho_{1}}(0,T;H)}=\big\lVert G\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho_{1}}(0,T;H)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert G(\cdot,0)\rVert_{L_{2,\rho_{1}}(0,T;H)}+L\Bigl[\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((I_{\rho_{1}}v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho_{1}s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

4.3.

The remaining part in the discusstion of well-posedness is continuous dependence on data. We will focus on

Theorem 4.15

(continuous dependence on the right-hand sides)
.

Under the assumptions of
theorem

4.6
let

be local solutions of

12

and

respectively up to some

Proof.

We estimate, utilizing the fixed point equation
eq.

10

\displaystyle\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}=\big\lVert S_{\rho}\bigl[f_{1}-f_{2}+\widetilde{G_{1}}(u)-\widetilde{G_{2}}(v)\bigr]\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\big\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}+\widetilde{G_{1}}(u)-\widetilde{G_{2}}(v)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\Bigl[\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}u+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)-G_{2}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\Bigl[\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}^{2}+2\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\bigl[\big\lVert G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}u+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)-G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\big\lVert G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)-G_{2}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert^{2}\bigr]\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\Bigr]\text{.}

The first integral term we can estimate using almost uniform Lipschitz-continuity of

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}u+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)-G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\quad=L_{G_{1}}^{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert(I_{\rho}(u-v))_{(t)}\big\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\quad=L_{G_{1}}^{2}\lVert\Theta(I_{\rho}(u-v))\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H^{1}(-h,0;H))}^{2}

We can estimate this term appealing to

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G_{1}}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert I_{\rho}(u-v)\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}

Making

\displaystyle\quad\leq\!C\Bigl[\lVert f_{1}\!-\!f_{2}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}\!+\!\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lVert G_{1}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v\!+\!Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\!-\!G_{2}\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}v\!+\!Z)_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\Bigr]\text{.}\qed

v\in\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\bigr)
follows. We obtain:

\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}M_{0}\!+\!M_{1}\!+\!A\bigr)v\!=\!-\!M_{0}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!(M_{1}\!+\!A)\bigl(\Phi(0)\!+\!I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\!+\!F\bigl(\cdot,(v\!+\!I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

Assuming that
F
has the same form as in
eq.

12
, the equation becomes

(17)

\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}M_{0}\!+\!M_{1}\!+\!A\bigr)v\!=\!-\!M_{0}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!(M_{1}\!+\!A)\bigl(\Phi(0)\!+\!I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\!+\!G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(v\!+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

Again, we obtain a corresponding well-posedness theorem:

Theorem 5.3

(local existence and uniqueness)
.

Under assumption
(A)
on
G
,
eq.

17
has a unique local solution up to some
T>0
provided that

•

\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(-h,0;H)
with
\Phi(0),\Phi^{\prime}(0)\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
and

•

the consistency condition
M_{0}\Phi^{\prime}(0)+(M_{1}+A)\Phi(0)=G(0,\Phi)
holds.

Proof.

We simply apply
theorem

4.6
and translate the consistency condition there:

f(0)=M_{0}\Phi^{\prime}(0)+(M_{1}+A)\Phi(0)=G\bigl(0,(I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(0)}\bigr)=G(0,\Phi)\text{.}\qed

For this formulation and the special case of simple material laws we also prove continuous dependence on the initial data:

Theorem 5.4

(continuous dependence on initial data)
.

Let
G
satisfy assumption
(A)
and let
\Phi,\Psi\in H^{2}(-h,0;H)
be two prehistories satisfying the assumptions of
theorem

5.3
. Let
\alpha>\operatorname{max}\{\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty},

\lVert\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for any solutions
u,v
to
eq.

17
with initial prehistories
\Phi,\Psi
respectively, that exist up to
T>0
such that both
(I_{\rho}(u+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(t)}
and
(I_{\rho}(u+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}))_{(t)}
remain in
V_{\alpha}
for all
t\leq T
:

\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,T;H)}\leq C\bigl[\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}(-h,0;H)}+\lVert\Phi(0)-\Psi(0)\rVert_{\operatorname{dom}(A)}\bigr]\text{.}

Proof.

We recall that the solutions are given by

u=w_{u}+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad v=w_{v}+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}\text{,}

where
w_{u}
and
w_{v}
are local solutions to the FPP (
17
), which
w_{u}
and
w_{v}
solve up to the common time
T
. We start by estimating the
L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)
-difference (
\rho
large enough) of
w_{u}
and
w_{v}
by using the formula provided by
eq.

17
. We estimate the nonlinearities and the additional terms separately.

i)

We start with the nonlinearity:

\displaystyle\big\lVert S_{\rho}\bigl[G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr]\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\big\lVert G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert G\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(t)}\bigr)-G\bigl(t,(I_{\rho}(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}))_{(t)}\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

Using almost uniform Lipschitz continuity of
G
we obtain:

\displaystyle\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}L^{2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert\bigl(I_{\rho}(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})\bigr)_{(t)}-\bigl(I_{\rho}(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}})\bigr)_{(t)}\big\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\text{.}

We obtain two integrals because of the
H^{1}
-norm, that we have to estimate:

a)

We start with the derivative:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert\bigl(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}\bigr)_{(t)}-\bigl(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}\bigr)_{(t)}\big\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\!\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\!\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{0}\big\lVert w_{u}(t\!+\!s)\!-\!w_{v}(t\!+\!s)\!+\!\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}(t\!+\!s)\!-\!\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}(t+s)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

Using
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
we continue:

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{T}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pts}^{s+h}\bigl(\lVert w_{u}(s)-w_{v}(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}(s)-\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{T}\bigl(\lVert w_{u}(s)-w_{v}(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}(s)-\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\rho}\biggl[\lVert w_{u}\!-\!w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\!+\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\!+\!\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}(0)\rVert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\!\,\mathrm{d}{}t\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho}\bigl[\lVert w_{u}\!-\!w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\!+\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\bigr]

The embedding
theorem

2.5
provides a constant
\tilde{C}(\rho)>0
such that

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho}\bigl[\tilde{C}(\rho)^{2}\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}+\lVert w_{u}-w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}\bigr]\text{.}

b)

For the term with an additional integral term we can make a similar estimate:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\big\lVert\bigl(I_{\rho}(w_{u}+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})\bigr)_{(t)}-\bigl(I_{\rho}(w_{v}+\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}})\bigr)_{(t)}\big\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{T}\bigl(\lVert I_{\rho}(w_{u}-w_{v})(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert I_{\rho}\bigl(\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}-\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}\bigr)(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

Using that
I_{\rho}
is a bounded linear operator, we continue as before:

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho^{3}}\biggl[\lVert w_{u}\!-\!w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\!+\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\!+\!\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}(0)\rVert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\!\,\mathrm{d}{}t\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho^{3}}\bigl[\lVert w_{u}\!-\!w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\!+\!\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\bigr]

The embedding
theorem

2.5
provides a constant
\tilde{C}(\rho)\!>\!0
such that

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\rho^{3}}\bigl[\tilde{C}(\rho)^{2}\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}^{2}+\lVert w_{u}-w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}\bigr]\text{.}

Alltogether this implies (assuming
\rho>1
if necessary),

\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert L\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\rho}}\bigl[\lVert w_{u}-w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}+\tilde{C}(\rho)\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}\bigr]\text{.}

The estimate
\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert\leq\frac{1}{c}
from
theorem

2.14
yields for some finite
C>0
,

\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{\rho}}\bigl[\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}+\tilde{C}(\rho)\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}\bigr]\text{.}

This takes care of the nonlinearity.

ii)

For the additional terms in
eq.

17
we have to estimate:

\displaystyle\big\lVert S_{\rho}\bigl[M_{0}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!+\!(M_{1}\!+\!A)\bigl(\Phi(0)\!+\!I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\!-\!M_{0}\Psi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!(M_{1}\!+\!A)\bigl(\Psi(0)\!+\!I_{\rho}\Psi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\bigr]\!\big\rVert_{\!L_{2,\rho}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert S_{\rho}\rVert^{2}\!\!\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{T}\!\lVert M_{0}(\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}(0))\!+\!(M_{1}\!+\!A)(\Phi(0)\!-\!\Psi(0))\!+\!t(\Phi^{\prime}(0)\!-\!\Psi^{\prime}(0))\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{4}{c^{2}}\bigl[\lVert M_{0}\rVert^{2}\lVert\Phi^{\prime}-\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}+\lVert M_{1}\rVert^{2}\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\lVert A(\Phi(0)-\Psi(0))\rVert_{H}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}+\tfrac{1}{4\rho^{3}}\lVert\Phi^{\prime}-\Psi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\bigr]\text{.}

The term
\lVert A(\Phi(0)-\Psi(0))\rVert_{H}
can be estimated with
\lVert\Phi(0)-\Psi(0)\rVert_{\operatorname{dom}(A)}
and thus we have proven a sufficient estimate for
\lVert w_{u}-w_{v}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}
.

To estimate the difference of
u-v=w_{u}-w_{v}+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}-I_{\rho}\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}
, it remains to estimate the difference
\lVert I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}-I_{\rho}\widehat{\Psi^{\prime}}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}
, but this can be done as above. Using the previous estimates and the fact that
\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\rho h}\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}(-h,0;H)}
, we have for
\rho\gg 0
a constant
\hat{C}(\rho)>0
such that:

\lVert u-v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\rho h}\hat{C}(\rho)\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}(-h,0;H)}+\lVert\Phi(0)-\Psi(0)\rVert_{\operatorname{dom}(A)}\text{.}

The equivalence of the norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{2}(0,T;H)}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}
produces the desired constant.
∎

6.
Examples

In this section, we visit examples highlighting the applicability of our results. In the process thereof, we will make use of several (weak) differential operators and their symmetrized versions. We provide their definitions in
appendix

A
and take the notation introduced there for granted subsequently. Furthermore, whenever convenient, we will assume that the prehistory
\Phi
(and if necessary, its derivatives) vanishes at
-h
, appealing to
remark

5.1
. We begin with three classical PDEs in which
A
is a skew-selfadjoint operator.

6.1.
Heat Equation

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and let
a\colon\,\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be measurable, bounded and
\operatorname{Re}a\geq c>0
. The heat equation with state-dependent delay is given as

(18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigl(\partial_{\rho}-\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigr)u&=F\bigl(\cdot,u_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}\\
u_{0}&=\Phi\text{.}\end{aligned}

Here, we simply took the evolutionary equation formulation of the heat equation from
[
32
, thm. 6.2.4]
and added a state-dependent right-hand side. Structurally, such a system has been studied e.g. in
[
18
]
(although for constant delay),
[
5
]
(in the context of control theory),
[
4
]
(for classical solutions) and
[
30
]
(for a semigroup approach).

Employing the ansatz
u=v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}
and immediately incorporating the prehistory into the equation in the style of
eq.

6
in form of a function
f
on the right-hand side, we can write the heat equation as a system in the following form:

(19)

\biggr[\partial_{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}_{0}\\
\operatorname{grad}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggl]\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f+F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

This system fits the setting of evolutionary equations (cf.
[
32
, ch. 6.2]
); in fact, we have a simple material law, that in addition satisfies the assumptions of
theorem

2.24
. We can prove well-posedness of the heat equation in both cases
\widetilde{F}(v)=I_{\rho}\widetilde{G}(v)
and
\widetilde{F}(v)=\widetilde{G}(I_{\rho}v)
, but we will focus on the latter case.

6.1.1.
Supposing
\widetilde{F}(v)=\widetilde{G}(I_{\rho}v)

If we simply suppose that the right-hand side is of the desired form, we can make use of the parabolic structure, cf.
definition

2.23
:

Theorem 6.1
.

Equation

19
is (locally) solvable under assumption
(A)
i) and iii) on
G
and assumption
(B)
on
\Phi
, provided that
f\in H^{1}_{\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and
f(0)=-G(0,\Phi)
.

Proof.

We observe that the left-hand side of
eq.

19
adheres to the conditions of
theorem

2.24
and we can therefore simply apply
theorem

4.8
.
∎

We remark that it is critical in our approach to delay equations that the equation is structurally regularity increasing. We can demand this by virtue of a right-hand side with built-in increase of regularity or an equation that structurally produces more regular solutions, e.g., by virtue of parabolic regularity.

6.1.2.
Generating the structure

Alternatively, we can also generate the desired formulation from
eq.

18
, showcasing that the assumptions on the form of the right-hand side are not as big of a restriction as they might seem at first glance. To this end, we make the ansatz
u=I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})
for a
v\in L_{2,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and obtain from
eq.

18
:

\bigl(1-\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}I_{\rho}\bigr)v+\Phi^{\prime}(0)-\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigl(\Phi(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)=F\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

We substitute
w=I_{\rho}a\operatorname{grad}v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and obtain:

v-\operatorname{div}_{0}w=-\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigl(\Phi(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)+F\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}

which can be written as the following system:

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\biggl[\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}_{0}\\
\operatorname{grad}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}\\
&\qquad=\begin{pmatrix}-\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigl(\Phi(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)\bigr)+F\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\end{aligned}

We therefore generated a formulation of the type (
12
), assuming we have a prehistory
\Phi\in H^{2}(-h,0;H)
(in particular, the first derivative is bounded in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm). This formulation is reminiscent of
[
33
, cor. 4.2]
, where stochastic problems were discussed.
We arrive at the following well-posedness theorem:

Theorem 6.2
.

Equation

20
is locally uniquely solvable in
H^{1}_{0,\rho}
under assumption
(A)
on
F
and assumption
(B)
on
\Phi
, provided that

•

a
is Hermitian (i.e.,
a=a^{\ast}
),

•

\Phi\in H^{2}(0,\infty;H)
with
\Phi(0),\Phi^{\prime}(0)\in\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigr)
and

•

the consistency condition
\bigl(\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigr)\Phi(0)+F(0,\Phi)=\Phi^{\prime}(0)
holds.

Proof.

We note that the left-hand side of
eq.

20
fits the setting of evolutionary equations, because
a=a^{\ast}
and we can simply apply
theorem

4.6
, translating the consistency condition there:

-\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\bigl(\operatorname{div}_{0}a\operatorname{grad}\bigr)\Phi(0)+F(0,\Phi)=0\text{.}\qed

6.2.
Wave Equation

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and let
T\colon\,\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be bounded, measurable and Hermitian, satisfying
T\geq c>0
. The wave equation is given as

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigl(\partial_{\rho}^{2}-\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}\bigr)u&=F\bigl(\cdot,u_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}\\
u_{(0)}&=\Phi\text{.}\end{aligned}

Again, we use the formulation from
[
32
, thm. 6.2.6]
for the wave equation in evolutionary equations and supplant the conventional righ-hand side with one that includes state-dependent delay as before. This system fits the formulation of many articles employing the theory of semigroups (for a very general resource we reference
[
2
]
or refer to
section

6.5
for an example), but there are other sources as well, e.g.
[
15
]
(where an actual solution is calculated) or
[
45
]
(in the context of attractors). It should be pointed out that the delayed wave equation has also been studied extensively by C. Pignotti and S. Nicaise, although in the context of delayed feedback on the boundary of a domain (e.g.
[
21
]
) or with the delay in the derivative of the state variable (e.g.
[
22
]
).

Making the usual ansatz
u=v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}}
with
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
, we can model the equation as the following system, where we include the prehistory as a function
f
into the equation as in
eq.

6
:

(22)

\biggl[\partial_{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&T^{-1}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}\\
\operatorname{grad}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f+F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

Again, this equation fits the setting of evolutionary equations (cf.
[
32
, ch. 6.2]
) and the material law is a simple material law. For both studied cases
\widetilde{F}(v)=I_{\rho}\widetilde{G}(v)
and
\widetilde{F}(v)=\widetilde{G}(I_{\rho}v)
we obtain a well-posedness theorem:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
f=I_{\rho}g
for some
g\in H^{1}_{\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and
F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)=I_{\rho}G\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)
and let
G
satisfy assumption
(A)
and
\Phi
assumption
(B)
. Then
eq.

22
has a unique local solution provided that
g(0)=-G(0,\Phi)
.

Proof.

Immediate from
theorem

4.4
.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
f\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and
F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)=G\bigl(\cdot,(I_{\rho}v+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)
and let
G
satisfy assumption
(A)
and
\Phi
assumption
(B)
. Then
eq.

22
has a unique local solution provided that
f(0)=-G(0,\Phi)
.

Proof.

Immediate from
theorem

4.6
.
∎

Because we are working with a simple material law, we can also be more explicit and use the following approach, generating the case
\widetilde{F}(v)=I_{\rho}\widetilde{G}(v)
:

We make the ansatz
u=v+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}
with
v\in H^{2}_{0,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
and obtain from
eq.

22

\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}^{2}-\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}\bigr)v=-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}+F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

Integration over the entire equation gives

\bigl(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}-\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}\bigr)v=-I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}+I_{\rho}F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

Substituting
w=T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}v
, we obtain the following system:

\displaystyle\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}v-\operatorname{div}w

\displaystyle=I_{\rho}\bigl[-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}\bigr]+I_{\rho}F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}T^{-1}w

\displaystyle=\operatorname{grad}_{0}v\text{.}

Written in matrix form, the system reads:

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\biggl[\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&T^{-1}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\operatorname{div}\\
\operatorname{grad}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}\\
&\qquad\qquad=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\rho}\bigl[-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}\bigr]+I_{\rho}F\bigl(\cdot,(v+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\end{aligned}

The left-hand side of this equation is unchanged from before and satisfies the requirements of Picard’s
theorem

2.14
. We thus produced an equation fitting the formulation (
11
). We also point out that this version of the delayed wave equation is reminiscent to the formulation in
[
28
, thm. 5.16]
, where stochastic problems were discussed.

Theorem 6.5
.

Equation

23
admits a unique local solution under assumption
(A)
on
F
and assumption
(B)
on
\Phi
, provided that

•

\Phi\in H^{2}(-h,0;H)
with
\Phi(0),\Phi^{\prime}(0)\in\operatorname{dom}\bigl(\operatorname{div}T\operatorname{grad}_{0}\bigr)
and

•

the consisteny condition

6.3.

Let

Remark 6.7

(Mixed-type problems)
.

One strength of the theory of evolutionary equations is the ability to treat mixed-type problems: For instance, on a split domain one may have hyperbolic behaviour on one part, on the other part parabolic behaviour, with both parts linked together by interface conditions. An example is the following eddy current system

\biggl[\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon&0\\
0&\mu\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-\operatorname{curl}\\
\operatorname{curl}_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}E\\
H\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-J\\
0\end{pmatrix}

on the space
(0,1)^{3}\eqcolon\Omega=C\dot{\cup}S
, where
\mu\equiv 1
,
\epsilon=1-\chi_{C}
,
\sigma=10^{3}\chi_{C}
,
J(x,t)=\sin(\pi t)\chi_{S}(x)
. This setting still fits the framework of evolutionary equations. For a precise treatment of mixed-type eddy current problems in the setting of evolutionary problems (without delay), we refer to
[
23
]
.

From our perspective of (generalized) initial value problems, such an increase in difficulty provides no problems for the solution theory (as long as we remain in the setting of evolutionary equations). Hence, we obtain well-posedness of the system above for the case of a state-dependent delay on the right-hand side in the vein of
theorem

4.4
or
theorem

4.6
.

The first three subsections were devoted to some classical PDEs to show the range of examples for our theory. The next two examples are dedicated to examples from semigroup theory. We shall demonstrate exactly how our theory can handle some typical problems.

Remark 6.9

(Comparison of results)
.

We point out that the assumptions in
[
7
]
differ slightly from ours. Here, the Hilbert space

[

. Hence, our Lipschitz assumptions are weaker than considered in
[

]
. In
[

, thm. 3.3]
, the authors prove existence and uniqueness of a local solution

satisfying

and they include a result on continuous dependence on initial datum, cf.
[
7
, prop. 3.4]
. For time continuity of the evolution of the prehistory, the authors consider initial values
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(-h,0;H_{-\frac{1}{2}})
satisfying a consistency condition

cf.
[

, rem. 3.6]
. We point out that solutions in this formulation from
[

]
can only be continuous across

.

6.5.
Second order evolution problems

Next, we consider a second-order problem from
[
6
]
:

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(t)+ku^{\prime}(t)+Au(t)+F(u(t))+M(u_{(t)})

\displaystyle=0\text{,}

\displaystyle u_{(0)}

\displaystyle=\Phi\text{,}

where
A
is a positive operator with discrete spectrum on a separable Hilbert space
H
;
k
is a constant;
F\colon\operatorname{dom}(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\to H
is locally Lipschitz-continuous and so is
M\colon W\to H
, where

W\coloneq\mathcal{C}\bigl(-h,0;\operatorname{dom}(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\bigr)\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(-h,0;H)\text{.}

The main example/application of the model in
[
6
]
is a nonlinear plate equation of the form

\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+k\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\Delta^{2}u(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+au\bigl(t-\tau(u(t)),x\bigr)=0

in a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with suitable boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
;
\tau
is a mapping defined on solutions with values in some interval
[0,h]
, satisfying a Lipschitz-like condition (cf.
[
6
, ass. M4]
);
k
and
a
are constants. The plate is assumed to be placed on some foundation; the term
au\bigl(t-\tau(u(t)),x\bigr)
models effects of the foundation with a delayed response;
F
is a potentially nonlinear force. The model in particular covers the wave equation with state-dependent delay. For more details and applications we refer to
[
6
]
and the references therein.

Since we want to solve a second order equation, we apply the usual ansatz to the derivative of
u
:
\partial_{\rho}u=I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})
, assuming that
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(-h,0;H)
. This means
u=I_{\rho}I_{\rho}(v+\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})
and consequently for
t>0
hold:

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=v(t)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\text{,}

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}v(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{0}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\,\mathrm{d}{}s=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt0}^{t}v(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s+\Phi^{\prime}(0)+t\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\text{,}

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=I_{\rho}(I_{\rho}v)(s)+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{t}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{s}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}(r)\,\mathrm{d}{}r\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle=I_{\rho}(I_{\rho}v)(s)+\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+t\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\Phi(0)\text{.}

The initial condition is trivially satisfied and substituting
b\coloneq I_{\rho}v
and
c\coloneq I_{\rho}A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}b
, we can rewrite these equations:

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}b)(t)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\text{,}

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=b(t)+\Phi^{\prime}(0)+t\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\text{,}

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=(I_{\rho}b)(s)+\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+t\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\Phi(0)=I_{\rho}(b+\Phi^{\prime}(0))+I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+\Phi(0)\text{.}

This allows us to write the differential equation as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}b+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+k\bigl(b+\Phi^{\prime}(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\bigr)+A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}I_{\rho}A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}b

\displaystyle\quad+A\bigl(I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\Phi(0)\bigr)+F\bigl(I_{\rho}(b+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})\bigr)+M\bigl((I_{\rho}(b+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}

which produces the system

(27)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \biggl[\mathring{\partial}_{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}&+\begin{pmatrix}k&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\\
-A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&0\end{pmatrix}\biggr]\begin{pmatrix}b\\
c\end{pmatrix}\\
&\quad=\begin{pmatrix}f-F\bigl(I_{\rho}(b+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}})\bigr)-M\bigl((I_{\rho}(b+I_{\rho}\widehat{\Phi^{\prime\prime}}))_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\end{aligned}

where

f\coloneq-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)-k\bigl(\Phi^{\prime}(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\bigr)-A\bigl(I_{\rho}I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+I_{\rho}\Phi^{\prime}(0)+\Phi(0)\bigr)\text{.}

We can prove well-posedness of the problem:

Theorem 6.10
.

Under assumption
(A)
on
F,M
and assumption
(B)
on
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(-h,0;H)
,
eq.

27
is locally uniquely solvable (in
H^{2}
), provided that the consistency condition
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)+k\Phi^{\prime}(0)+A\Phi(0)+F(\Phi(0))+M(\Phi)=0
holds.

Proof.

We note that the left-hand side of
eq.

27
satisfies the assumptions of
theorem

2.14
and therefore fits the framework of evolutionary equations. We can therefore simply appeal to
theorem

4.6
, translating the consistency condition there, which is:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=f(0)-F\Bigl(\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{0}\underbrace{b(s)}_{=0}+\Phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)-M\Bigl(\Bigl(\displaystyle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt-h}^{\cdot}\underbrace{b(s)}_{=0}+\Phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)_{(0)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)-k\Phi^{\prime}(0)-A\Phi(0)-F(\Phi(0))-M(\Phi)\text{.}\qed

Summary and Comparison

In this article, we considered “generalized” initial value problems in the form of
eq.

6
, that principally arise in a distributional formulation (motivated in
section

2.3
). Evolutionary equations in distributional spaces stemming from initial value problems have been studied before by S. Trostorff in
[
37
]
(cf. also the older publications
[
16
]
and
[
25
]
for ODEs). As mentiond in
section

2.3
, formulating suitable initial conditions tailored to a problem is nontrivial in general. We bypassed this aspect by rather examining the general formulation (
5
) that arises after transitioning from an initial value problem to a problem with non-homogeneity
f\in H^{-1}_{\rho}
(cf., e.g.,
[
36
,
37
]
). Rather than exploring this general setup, we further focussed on the issues occuring due to the presence of state-dependent delay; specifically, we demanded
f\in L_{2,\rho}/H^{1}_{\rho}
(cf. formulation (
6
)).

In any case, the main idea for the well-posedness results
theorem

4.4
and
theorem

4.6
is to extend the approach from
[
11
]
developed for the ODE case (we also refer to
[
1
]
for a more comprehensive overview) to the PDE case. There, a contraction mapping argument paired with a projection argument was used to prove a generalized Picard–Lindelöf theorem (cf.
[
11
, thm. 4.1]
) for ODEs with state-dependent delay. The idea for that result in turn stems from Morgenstern’s proof of the Picard–Lindelöf theorem (cf.
[
20
]
) utilizing exponentially weighted spaces in order to force a Lipschitz-continuous map to be a contraction. Observing that the delay operator
\Theta
scales with the inverse of the weight parameter
\rho
(cf.
lemma

3.2
) gave rise to
H^{1}
-solution theory for ODEs with state-dependent delay. Since exponentially weighted spaces naturally arise in the theory of evolutionary equations, it was an equally natural question to ask if the ODE approach could be generalized to PDEs. In the PDE case, there is the complication of the solution operator/Picard operator
S_{\rho}
though, which is a bounded linear operator on
L_{2,\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
and certainly not as convenient for the solution theory as the antiderivative
I_{\rho}
alone. In particular, there is no automatic increase in regularity due to the shape of the right-hand side of the fixed point problem any more. We had to remedy that fact by demanding that the right-hand side
f+F
in
eq.

6
is in some sense regularity-increasing. Naturally, an integrability condition also came into play, because the Picard operator is only defined on the entire real line. The increased demand on regularity introduces consistency conditions, more precisely the right-hand side
f+F
needs to be an element of
H^{1}_{0,\rho}(0,\infty;H)
.

Consistency conditions are prevalent in solution theories for state-dependent differential equations: In the case of classical solution theory (i.e., in the sense of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-solutions) for the ODE case, there is the concept of the
solution manifold
, cf.
[
41
]
, characterizing compatible initial prehistories via a consistency equation. In the case of evolutionary equations, the
space of admissible prehistories

\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)
(cf.
definition

B.3
) essentially takes the place of the solution manifold.

The increased regularity of the right-hand side
f+F
is used in a projection argument. In order to remove the projection locally and turn a solution of the modified problem (
9
) into a solution of the original problem (
8
), we made a continuity argument, cf. the proofs of
theorem

4.4
and
theorem

4.6
. The complication lies in the definition of the spaces

V_{\alpha}=\bigl\{u\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)\colon\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha\bigr\}\text{.}

The
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm in the definition of
V_{\alpha}
destroys the Hilbert space structure that is otherwise present everywhere in the theory of evolutionary equations and to the best of our knowledge, there are no bounded-input/bounded-output results for general evolutionary equations. Why define
V_{\alpha}
like that then? The simple answer is practical necessity: the “standard” example for state-dependent delay is of the form
-\tau(u(t))
for a Lipschitz-continuous
\tau
. To force Lipschitz-continuity of
u\bigl(t-\tau(u(t))\bigr)
one needs
u^{\prime}
to be bounded. Therefore, the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm in
V_{\alpha}
is necessary and it is precisely by virtue of the projection argument that we can accomodate state-dependent delay via a weakened notion of Lipschitz-continuity. If one replaces the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm with the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
-norm, the entire theory goes through much smoother, leads to well-posedness results in
L_{2,\rho}
without the need of consistency conditions or regularity-increasing right-hand sides, but is simply not applicable in relevant cases. Precisely because state-dependent delay differential equations in general do not exhibit Lipschitz-continuous right-hand sides, the weakening almost uniform Lipschitz-continuity was introduced in
[
11
]
and reused here (cf.
definition

3.5
).

To the best of our knowledge, there is little general solution theory for PDEs with state-dependent delay, general in the sense that the theory covers diverse physical phenomena modelled by parabolic or hyperbolic differential equations. Classical theory seems to have been done exclusively for the ODE case (cf. the classical reference
[
13
]
), the common approach for PDEs seems to be semigroup theory (cf. the textbooks
[
2
,
44
]
, but there is a multitude of authors and articles in our references). Semigroup theory holds some advantages and disadvantages over evolutionary equations. The advantage is that existence results usually have a simple way of inferring strong solutions under slightly stronger assumptions, e.g., that the prehistory takes values in the domain of the semigroup generator or a suitable interpolation space thereof. The theory of evolutionary equations on the other hand is a theory purely designed around a notion of weak solutions. The advantage of evolutionary equations is that the concept is applicable to a larger set of equations, in particular time-nonlocal implicit partial differential algebraic equations. Here lies the biggest advantage of our approach over semigroup results. There are some notable technical differences as well. For instance, in evolutionary equations there is no need to consider extended state spaces or demand (possibly difficult to verify) Lipschitz-conditions of the nonlinearity in case the semigroup generator is not a differential operator in divergence form.

This article was a foray into developing
H^{1}
-solution theory for DDEs in the framework of evolutionary equations. Many questions require further investigation though. For instance, do global solutions actually require the stricter assumption of almost uniform Lipschitz-regularity in
L_{2}
, or can this assumption be lowered, at least in certain cases?

), i.e.,

\bigl(\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A\bigr)v=f+F\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)

in
H^{-1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
with
f
and

t\mapsto F\bigl(t,(v+Z)_{(t)}\bigr)

having support on the right half-line
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
only (this is critically important for the well-posedness theory in
section

4
). For the latter map, this is an assumption on
F
. We only need to go into detail in regard to
f
. The key reference for this deliberation is
[
37
, sec. 3]
. There, IVPs of the form

(32)

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A\bigr)u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\qquad\text{on}\ \mathbb{R}_{>0}\text{,}

(33)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\text{,}

are studied for
g\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-\infty,0;H)
. The equation is given meaning by applying the projection
P_{0}
(cf.
definition

2.19
) to the problem for
u=v+g
on the entire real line. To give the accurate result, we briefly recall the necessary definitions and preliminary results from
[
37
]
.

Definition B.1
.

Let
M\colon\mathbb{C}\supseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)\to\mathcal{L}(H)
be a linear material law.
M
is called regularizing if there exists
\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}\geq\rho_{0}}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(M)
,
M|_{\mathbb{C}_{\operatorname{Re}\geq\rho_{0}}}
is bounded and for all
\rho\geq\rho_{0}
and
x\in H
,

\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}x\bigr)(0+)\quad\text{exists.}

This definition is required for the following useful fact:

Lemma B.2

(
[
37
, lem. 3.2.4]
)
.

Let
M
be a regularizing linear material law and
\rho>s_{\mathrm{b}}(M)
. Then for
g\in\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}}H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
holds:
\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})g\in\operatorname{dom}(P_{0})
.

Appealing to this lemma, for regularizing material laws we can define

K_{\rho}\colon\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}}H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R},H)\to H^{-1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\text{,}\quad g\mapsto P_{0}\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})g

and subsequently:

Definition B.3
.

Let
M
be a regularizing material law and
\rho>s_{\mathrm{b}}(M)
. Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)

\displaystyle\!\coloneq\!\bigl\{g\in\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}}H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\colon\exists x\!\in\!H\!\colon\!S_{\rho}(\delta_{0}x\!-\!K_{\rho}g)\!-\!\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}g(0-)\!\in\!H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\bigr\}

be the space of admissible histories for
M
and
A
. Furthermore let

\displaystyle\mathrm{IV}_{\rho}(M,A)

\displaystyle\!\coloneq\!\bigl\{g(0-)\colon g\in\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)\bigr\}

be the space of admissible initial values for
M
and
A
.

Remark B.4
.

It can be shown, that the element
x
in the definition of the space of admissible histories is uniquely determined and is given by

x=\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})g\bigr)(0-)-\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})g\bigr)(0+),

cf.
[
37
, lem. 3.2.5]
. Hence, it makes sense to define

\Gamma^{\rho}_{(M,A)}\colon\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)\to H\text{,}\quad g\mapsto\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho}g)\bigr)(0-)-\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho}g)\bigr)(0+)\text{.}

With these concepts, we can give the precise statement concerning the reformulation of the system (
32
) + (
33
) as an evolutionary equation in
H^{-1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
:

Theorem B.5

(
[
37
, prop. 3.2.6]
)
.

For a regularizing material law
M
and
\rho>s_{\mathrm{b}}(M)
let

v\coloneq S_{\rho}\bigl(\Gamma_{(M,A)}^{\rho}g-K_{\rho}g\bigr)\text{.}

Then the function
u\coloneq v+g
defines an element of
H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)
, satisfies the initial condition (
33
) and
eq.

32
in the sense that

\mathrm{spt}\bigl((\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A)u\bigr)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\text{.}

In the context of this article, we apply the projection operator
P_{0}
to the equation

\bigl(\overline{\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A}\bigr)(v+Z)=F\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{.}

Since
v+Z
is in the domain of the closure of
\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A
, appealing to
theorem

2.18
, solving the equation above is equivalent to the inclusion

\bigl(\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A\bigr)(v+Z)\subseteq F\bigl(\cdot,(v+Z)_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\text{,}

where we interpret the left-hand side as an element of
H^{-1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\cap L_{2,\rho}\bigl(\mathbb{R};H^{-1}(A)\bigr)
. Appealing to
remark

5.1
, we assume in the following that the initial prehistory
\Phi
satisfies
\Phi(-h)=0
. Now we can elaborate how
f
is obtained:

\bigl(\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})+A\bigr)Z
is a well-defined
L_{2,\rho}
-function, provided that
Z
takes values in
\operatorname{dom}(A)
; the projection
P_{0}
applied to it produces a function on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, cf.
remark

2.20
. We observe that under the conditions of Trostorff’s
theorem

B.5
,
v
is of the form

\bigl[\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})\tilde{Z}\bigr)(0-)-\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})\tilde{Z}\bigr)(0+)\bigr]\delta_{0}-P_{0}\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})\tilde{Z}\text{,}

where
\tilde{Z}
is the extension of
Z
by
0
on
(-\infty,-h)
. We observe that the first expression is purely distributional and has support only at
0
. The second term is

\partial_{\rho}\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\partial_{\rho}^{-1}\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})\tilde{Z}-\bigl[\partial_{\rho}^{-1}\partial_{\rho}M(\partial_{\rho})\bigr](0+)\delta_{0}\text{,}

the second summand of which only has support at
0
again, whereas the first part reduces to

\partial_{\rho}\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}M(\partial_{\rho})\tilde{Z}\text{,}

which is clearly supported on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
only, appealing to causality of material laws. Hence,
\operatorname{spt}(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

B.2.
Simple material laws

We conclude this section by providing a perspective on initial value problems for
simple
material laws, i.e., material laws of the form
M(z)=M_{0}+M_{1}z^{-1}
. In this case, the history space only depends on the values
g(0-)
of the prehistory
g
, and not the entire prehistory. This simpler dependency could already be observed in several examples we provided in
section

6
, but can be proven in Trostorff’s general framework:

Proposition B.6

(
[
37
, prop. 3.2.10]
)
.

Let
M_{0},M_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(H)
with
M_{0}
selfadjoint satisfying
M_{0}|_{\operatorname{ran}(M_{0})}\geq c_{0}>0
and
M_{1}
satisfying
\operatorname{Re}M_{1}|_{\operatorname{ker}(M_{0})}\geq c_{1}>0
. Then for
M(z)\coloneq M_{0}+z^{-1}M_{1}
and
\rho>s_{\mathrm{b}}(M)
holds

\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)=\bigl\{g\in\chi_{\mathbb{R}\leq 0}H^{1}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R};H)\colon g(0-)\in\mathrm{IV}_{\rho}(M,A)\bigr\}\text{.}

Furthermore, for
g\in\mathrm{His}_{\rho}(M,A)
with
g(0-)=0
holds

S_{\rho}\Bigl(\delta_{0}\bigl[\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})g\bigr)(0-)-\bigl(M(\partial_{\rho})g\bigr)(0+)\bigr]-K_{\rho}g\Bigr)=0\text{.}

It is an interesting observation, that in the context of formulating initial value spaces for evolutionary equations, one seems to obtain consistency conditions “from the left” for the initial prehistory. The consistency conditions required for solution theory of DDEs are akin to conditions on the history segments, which enforce a consistency condition “from the right”. This phenomenon might be subject to future investigation.
