1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Boltzmann equation and notations.

In the present paper, we study the spatially homogeneous Boltzmann equations with the exact collision kernels derived from the inverse power potentials
U(r)=\frac{1}{r^{1/s}},0<s<1
and the hard sphere model. The homogeneous Boltzmann equations read

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f^{s}=Q^{s}(f^{s},f^{s})\\
f^{s}(0,v)=f_{\textup{in}}(v),\end{cases}

and

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f^{0}=Q^{0}(f^{0},f^{0})\\
f^{0}(0,v)=f_{\textup{in}}(v).\end{cases}

Here,
f^{s}(t,v),f^{0}(t,v)\geq 0
are the density functions of collision particles which at time
t\geq 0
moving with velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The Boltzmann collision operator
Q^{s}
and
Q^{0}
are associated to the kernel
B^{s}(v-v_{*},\sigma),B^{0}(v-v_{*},\sigma)
respectively, which act only on velocity variable
v
, defined by

Q^{s}(g,f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}B^{s}(v-v_{*},\sigma)(g_{*}^{\prime}f^{\prime}-g_{*}f)\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}v_{*},

and

Q^{0}(g,f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}B^{0}(v-v_{*},\sigma)(g_{*}^{\prime}f^{\prime}-g_{*}f)\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}v_{*}.

Here we use the standard shorthand
f=f(v),g_{*}=g\left(v_{*}\right),f^{\prime}=f\left(v^{\prime}\right),g_{*}^{\prime}=g\left(v_{*}^{\prime}\right)
, where
v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{*}
are given by

v^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}+\frac{\left|v-v_{*}\right|}{2}\sigma,\quad v_{*}^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}-\frac{\left|v-v_{*}\right|}{2}\sigma,\quad\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

In the hard-sphere model, the collision kernel
B^{0}
depends only on the relative velocity and is given by (the radius of particle is
\frac{1}{2}
)

B^{0}(v-v_{*},\sigma)=\frac{1}{4}|v-v_{*}|.

For the inverse power law
U(r)=\frac{1}{r^{1/s}},0<s<1
,
explicit derivation of the Boltzmann kernel
B^{s}
was given in
[
7
]
. Here we recall the result in
[
7
]
and introduce adequate notations therein for our purpose. For any
0<s<1
,
the Boltzmann kernel
B^{s}
reads

(1.3)

\displaystyle B^{s}(v-v_{*},\sigma)=|v-v_{*}|^{\gamma}b_{s}(\theta)=|v-v_{*}|^{1-4s}b_{s}(\theta),\quad v,v_{*}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}\kern 0.0pt}\nolimits^{3},\theta\in[0,\pi],

where the
angular part is given by:

(1.4)

\displaystyle b_{s}(\theta)=\frac{2^{4s}}{2}\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\beta_{s}(y_{s}(\varphi))\beta_{s}^{\prime}(y_{s}(\varphi))y_{s}^{\prime}(\varphi).

Here
v-v_{*}
is the relative velocity and
\theta
is the deviation angle:
\cos\theta=\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}\cdot\sigma
. In (
1.4
), the two variables
\varphi
and
\theta
are related by
2\varphi+\theta=\pi
.
The formula (
1.4
) involves two
s
-dependent functions
\beta=\beta_{s}(y),y=y_{s}(\varphi)
. The function
\beta_{s}
is given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle\beta=\beta_{s}(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{y}{(1-y^{2})^{s}},\quad y\in[0,1].

By direct computation,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{s}^{\prime}(y)=2s\frac{1}{(1-y^{2})^{1+s}}+(1-2s)\frac{1}{(1-y^{2})^{s}}.

The function
y_{s}
is the inverse function of
\varphi_{s}
defined by

(1.7)

\displaystyle\varphi=\varphi_{s}(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}}\mathrm{d}z,\quad y\in[0,1].

Here, for the sake of simplicity, we define

(1.8)

\displaystyle g(s,y,z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1-z^{1/s}-y^{2}(z^{2}-z^{1/s}),\quad y,z\in[0,1],s\in(0,1).

As
s\to 0
, the inverse-power potential converges to the hard-core (hard-sphere) potential in the sense that

U_{s}(r)=r^{-1/s}\to U_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\infty\,\mathbf{1}_{0<r<1}
.
Consequently, the convergence of the singular Boltzmann kernel
B^{s}
to the hard-sphere kernel

\frac{1}{4}|v-v_{*}|
as
s\to 0
has long been expected in the kinetic theory literature.
As already anticipated in
[
4
, Remark 1.0.1]
, such a limit was suggested to exist in their discussion of the hard-sphere and short-range potential regimes, and it was rigorously established for the first time in the work of Jang–Kepka–Nota–Velázquez
[
7
]
.
The authors of
[
7
, Theorem 1(i)]
proved that the following limit

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}b(s,\theta)=\frac{1}{4},

holds for any
0<\theta\leq\pi
.
However, (
1.9
) is stated without an explicit
quantitative convergence rate uniform in
\theta
.
In this work, we provide an optimal convergence rate for this limit. Furthermore, the convergence result
f^{s}\to f^{0}
,
s\to 0
(in some suitable norms) will be proved.

1.2.
Main results

Our first main result provides a sharp and fully explicit quantitative version of the formal limit
b_{s}(\theta)\to\frac{1}{4}
as
s\to 0
.
Because the kernel is expressed through several implicitly defined functions whose
s
–dependence becomes singular near
\varphi=\pi/2
, obtaining an optimal estimate requires controlling all such compositions uniformly in
s
.
The following theorem establishes the optimal convergence rate
O(s)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
0<\theta\leq\pi,0<s<1
, it holds that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\left|b_{s}(\theta)-\frac{1}{4}\right|\leq 50000s\theta^{-2-2s}.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
is purely explicit and constructive so that we have the constant
50000
.
Comparison between Theorem
1.1
and the results in
[
7
]
is given in the following remark.

Remark 1.1

(Optimality)
.

We briefly explain why the estimate in Theorem
1.1
is optimal
both in the parameter
s
and in the angular singularity.
Recalling from
[
7
, Theorem 1(ii)]
that for any fixed
0<s<1
,

(1.11)

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\theta^{2+2s}b(s,\theta)=C_{s}=2^{4s}s\,\bigl(\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(1)\bigr)^{2s}=2^{4s}s\left(\frac{1}{s}W_{1/s}\right)^{2s},

where
W_{n}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{n}t\mathrm{d}t
denotes the classical Wallis integral.
Using the well-known asymptotic behavior of
W_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, one verifies that

(1.12)

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{C_{s}}{s}=1.

Combining (
1.11
) and (
1.12
), we see that for sufficiently
small
s
and
\theta
, the kernel satisfies

b_{s}(\theta)\sim s\,\theta^{-2-2s}.

Thus the estimate (
1.10
) in Theorem
1.1

achieves the optimal order in both variables
s
and
\theta
; no better decay in
s

or in the power of
\theta
can be expected in general.

We also note that the uniform upper bound

(1.13)

\sup_{s\in(0,1/2]}\;\sup_{\theta\in(0,\pi]}\;\theta^{2+2s}b(s,\theta)<\infty

was established in
[
7
, Theorem 1(iii)]
.
Our new estimate (
1.10
) implies (
1.13
) directly.

The above convergence estimate is significant in several respects. First, it clarifies the relationship between two fundamental physical models—the hard-sphere model and the inverse power model—by providing a precise rate at which the latter approaches the former. Such a quantitative link is essential for understanding scattering phenomena, since both models arise naturally in the derivation of microscopic scattering laws, and the convergence rate offers new insight into how hard-sphere interactions effectively approximate long-range collisions in inverse power potentials.

Second, the estimate serves as a basic ingredient for the study of the Boltzmann equation. In particular, Theorem
1.1
enables the derivation of a convergence rate for solutions
f^{s}\to f^{0}
in suitable norms, thereby providing a rigorous proof of the hard-sphere approximation at the equation level. This is exactly the following Theorem
1.2
.

Let us introduce adequate notations of function norms which we will use throughout the paper.

\displaystyle|f|_{L^{1}_{q}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(v)|\langle v\rangle^{q}\mathrm{d}v,\quad|f|_{W^{1,1}_{q}}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq 1}|\partial^{\alpha}f|_{L^{1}_{q}},

\displaystyle H(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(v)\log f(v)\mathrm{d}v,\quad|f|_{L\log L}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f\log f|\mathrm{d}v,

where
\langle v\rangle=(1+|v|^{2})^{1/2}
.

The optimal kernel estimate in Theorem
1.1
allows us to compare the full Boltzmann operators
Q_{s}
and
Q_{0}
in a quantitative manner.
To measure convergence at the level of solutions, we introduce the scaled difference

F^{s}(t,v)=\frac{f_{s}(t,v)-f_{0}(t,v)}{s},

which isolates the first-order deviation of
f_{s}
from the hard-sphere solution
f_{0}
.
Our second main result below shows that
F^{s}
remains uniformly bounded in weighted
L^{1}_{k}
norms for any
k\geq 2
, yielding the optimal rate

f_{s}(t)=f_{0}(t)+O(s)\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{k},\qquad t\in[0,T].

This establishes the optimal convergence of solutions in the hard-sphere limit.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
2\leq k\in\mathbb{R}
,
0<\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, suppose
0\leq f_{\textup{in}}\in L^{1}_{k+3+\delta}\cap W^{1,1}_{k+2+\delta}\cap L\log L
. For
0<s<\frac{1}{8}
, let
F^{s}=\frac{f^{s}-f^{0}}{s}
where
f^{s}
and
f^{0}
are solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) respectively. Then for any
T>0
, there exists a constant
C
depending on
k,\delta,|f_{\textup{in}}|_{L^{1}_{k+3+\delta}},|f_{\textup{in}}|_{W^{1,1}_{k+2+\delta}},|f_{\textup{in}}|_{L\log L},
and
T
, independent of
s
, such that

(1.14)

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|F^{s}(t)|_{L^{1}_{k}}\leq C(k,\delta,|f_{\textup{in}}|_{L^{1}_{k+3+\delta}},|f_{\textup{in}}|_{W^{1,1}_{k+2+\delta}},|f_{\textup{in}}|_{L\log L},T).

Remark 1.2

(Optimality of the solution convergence)
.

The
O(s)
convergence in Theorem
1.2
is also
optimal
at the solution level.
Indeed, Theorem
1.1
shows that the angular kernel admits a sharp
first-order expansion of the form

b_{s}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4}+s\,\bar{b}(\theta)+o(s),\qquad s\to 0,

with
\bar{b}(\theta)
not identically zero.
Since the collision operator
Q_{s}
depends linearly on
b_{s}
, one obtains the exact expansion

Q_{s}(f,f)=Q_{0}(f,f)+s\,\bar{Q}(f,f)+o(s),

and thus
Q_{s}(f,f)-Q_{0}(f,f)
cannot decay faster than order
s
in any weighted
L^{1}_{k}
norm.
Because the homogeneous Boltzmann flow is Fréchet differentiable with respect to the collision
kernel for general initial data, the corresponding solutions satisfy

f_{s}(t)=f_{0}(t)+sF(t)+o(s),

where the profile
F(t)
solves a nontrivial linearized equation.
Hence no rate better than
O(s)
can hold at the level of solutions.
Therefore the convergence rate in Theorem
1.2
is sharp.

Based on Theorem
1.1
,
we can also consider the convergence of solutions in other function spaces, for instance, in general Sobolev spaces. In this article, we refrain us to the weighted
L^{1}
framework because it is closely related to the physical quantities.
Indeed, the
L^{1}_{2}
norm corresponds mass and energy of solutions to the Boltzmann equation, and thus provides the most relevant convergence of
f^{s}\to f^{0}
.
To the best of our knowledge, this type of result on
optimal
convergence rate has not been previously available and may stimulate further developments, including potential extensions to the spatially inhomogeneous Boltzmann equation.

1.3.
Main difficulties

The analysis of the limit
s\to 0
presents several difficulties that originate
from the implicit structure of the exact angular kernel
b_{s}(\theta)
for inverse-power potentials and from a delicate interplay between geometry and singularity near the endpoint
y_{s}(\varphi)\to 1
.
Unlike the limit
s\to 1
to the Coulomb potential studied in
[
11
]
, where grazing collisions dominate, the hard-sphere limit
s\to 0
does not correspond to a concentration of small deflection angles.
Instead, the difficulty arises from the degeneracy of the impact-parameter map
y_{s}(\varphi)
near
\varphi=\pi/2
, together with the highly nonlinear
s
–dependence hidden in the implicit definition.

1.3.1.
Implicit structure and nested compositions.

The angular kernel for inverse-power potentials can be written as

b_{s}(\theta)=2^{4s-1}\,\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\,\beta_{s}(y_{s}(\varphi))\,\beta_{s}^{\prime}(y_{s}(\varphi))\,y_{s}^{\prime}(\varphi),\qquad\beta_{s}(y)=\frac{y}{(1-y^{2})^{s}},

where
y_{s}(\varphi)
is defined implicitly as the inverse function of

\varphi_{s}(y)=y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}},\qquad g(s,y,z)=1-z^{1/s}-y^{2}(z^{2}-z^{1/s}),

and
\theta
and
\varphi
satisfy
2\varphi+\theta=\pi
.
Thus
b_{s}(\theta)
involves a fourfold composition of

\beta_{s},\ \beta_{s}^{\prime},\ y_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{-1},\ y_{s}^{\prime},\quad\text{and the map }\theta\leftrightarrow\varphi.

Since none of these are available in closed form for
s\neq 0
, there is no simple direct comparison between
b_{s}(\theta)
and its hard-sphere limit value
1/4
.
The first main difficulty is to control these compositions uniformly in
s
as
s\to 0
.

1.3.2.
Endpoint degeneracy as
y_{s}(\varphi)\to 1
.

In the limit
s\to 0
, one has
y_{s}(\varphi)\to y_{0}(\varphi)=\sin\varphi
.
Hence the singularity in the limit arises from the fact that

y_{s}(\varphi)\to 1\quad\text{as}\quad\varphi\to\frac{\pi}{2}.

Controlling the quantities

(1-y_{s})^{-1},\qquad(1-y_{s}^{2})^{-1},\qquad y_{s}^{\prime}(\varphi)

requires a detailed analysis of the degeneracy of function
g(s,y,z)
near
z=1
and
y=1
.
We prove precise stability bounds,

\bigl|\varphi_{s}(y)-\arcsin y\bigr|\lesssim s\,y(1-y^{2})^{-1/2},\qquad\bigl|\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(y)-(1-y^{2})^{-1/2}\bigr|\lesssim s(1-y^{2})^{-3/2},

and then transfer these to
y_{s}(\varphi)
and
y_{s}^{\prime}(\varphi)
via inverse-function estimates.
This step is technically delicate because the inversion amplifies small errors in
\varphi_{s}
near the boundary
\varphi=\pi/2
.

1.3.3.
Nonlinear decomposition of the kernel error.

To obtain the optimal bound

\bigl|b_{s}(\theta)-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr|\lesssim s\,\theta^{-2-2s},

we decompose (see (
2.23
)-(
2.26
) for details)

I=\left|b_{s}(\theta)-\frac{1}{4}\right|\leq I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where each term isolates a structural contribution coming from:

(i)

the prefactor deviation
2^{4s}-1
,

(ii)

the deviation
y_{s}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\cos\varphi
,

(iii)

the deviation

\beta_{s}(y_{s}(\varphi))\,\beta_{s}^{\prime}(y_{s}(\varphi))\;-\;\beta_{0}(y_{0}(\varphi))\,\beta_{0}^{\prime}(y_{0}(\varphi))=\beta_{s}(y_{s}(\varphi))\,\beta_{s}^{\prime}(y_{s}(\varphi))-y_{0}(\varphi),

since
\beta_{0}(y)=y
and
\beta_{0}^{\prime}(y)=1
.

All three contributions contain mixtures of the small parameter
s
and the singular factor
(1-y_{s})^{-1}
.
A major part of the analysis is to show that each term is bounded by
Cs\theta^{-2-2s}
, using the uniform-in-s estimates for
y_{s}
,
y_{s}^{\prime}
, and the explicit structures of
\beta_{s}
and
\beta_{s}^{\prime}
.

1.3.4.
Passing from kernel convergence to solution convergence.

To compare the solutions
f_{s}
and
f_{0}
, we introduce the scaled difference
F^{s}(t,v)=\frac{f_{s}(t,v)-f_{0}(t,v)}{s}
.
It is easy to check that
F^{s}
satisfies the following equation.

(1.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F^{s}=Q^{s}(f^{s},F^{s})+Q^{s}(F^{s},f^{0})+\frac{Q^{s}-Q^{0}}{s}(f^{0},f^{0}).

The equation (
1.15
) for
F^{s}
involves the difference of collision operators,
the linear terms containing
F^{s}
and the Boltzmann operators
Q^{s}
with the angular singularity
\theta^{-2-2s}
.
Since the normalization by
1/s
cancels the leading singularity, all remaining bounds must be uniform in
s
.
This requires a refined angular decomposition, Povzner-type moment inequalities, and uniform propagation of weighted moments for both
Q_{s}
and
Q_{0}
.

1.3.5.
Large-data framework.

We work with general (possibly large) initial data in weighted
L^{1}_{k}
spaces preserving physical moments for some
k\geq 2
. In this setting, obtaining nonlinear estimates that remain stable as
s\to 0
requires delicate analysis. Consequently, deriving the
optimal

O(s)
convergence rate becomes a genuinely challenging task.

Outline of the paper

The remainder of the article is devoted to the proofs of
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
in Section
2
and
3
respectively.

2.
Convergence rate estimate of the kernel

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
with full details. The angular part

b_{s}(\theta)
contains the implicit function
y_{s}
which is the inverse function of
\varphi_{s}
given by the integral (
1.7
) and (
1.8
). We first give some basic facts about the two functions. The implicit relation between the impact parameter and the deviation angle is
classical in kinetic theory; see, for instance, Landau–Lifschitz
[
8
]

and the exposition of Cercignani
[
2
]
. We follow the same geometric
setup and obtain quantitatively uniform-in-s estimates.

By direct computation,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}_{s}(y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-z^{1/s}}{(1-z^{1/s}-y^{2}(z^{2}-z^{1/s}))^{3/2}}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-z^{1/s}}{g^{3/2}}\mathrm{d}z>0.

Therefore,
\varphi_{s}
is a strictly increasing function, and so
y_{s}
is well-defined and also strictly increasing. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\varphi=\varphi_{s}(y):y\in[0,1]\to\varphi\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}],\quad y=y_{s}(\varphi):\varphi\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]\to y\in[0,1].

One can
compare
\varphi_{s}(y)
to its limit
\varphi_{0}(y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\arcsin y
. For
s\in(0,1],y\in(0,1)
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}\left(y\right)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z^{1/s}-y^{2}(z^{2}-z^{1/s})}}\mathrm{d}z>y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}}}\mathrm{d}z=\arcsin y\mathrel{{=}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}}\varphi_{0}(y).

As a result, for
s\in(0,1],y\in(0,1),\varphi\in(0,\pi/2)
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}\left(y\right)>\arcsin y\iff\arcsin y_{s}(\varphi)<\varphi\iff y_{s}(\varphi)<\sin\varphi.

The authors in
[
7
]
proved that the limits

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}(y)\to\arcsin y,\quad\varphi^{\prime}_{s}(y)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}},\quad s\to 0,

hold locally uniform in
y\in[0,1)
. We further prove the limits with
explicit convergence rates.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
0\leq y<1,0<s<1
, it holds that

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{s}(y)-\arcsin y|\leq 2sy(1-y^{2})^{-1/2},

(2.5)

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime}_{s}(y)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}|\leq 2s(1-y^{2})^{-3/2}.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Recalling (
1.8
),
it is easy to check

(2.6)

\displaystyle(1-y^{2}z^{2})(1-z^{1/s})\leq g(s,y,z)\leq(1-y^{2}z^{2}),\quad z\in[0,1],y\in[0,1],s\in(0,1].

We will frequently use (
2.6
).
Recalling (
2.2
),
we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0\leq\varphi_{s}(y)-\arcsin y=&y\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}}}\right)\mathrm{d}z\\
=&y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y^{2}z^{2})-g(s,y,z)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}}\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}(\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}+\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}})}\mathrm{d}z\\
\eqref{def-of-g}=&y(1-y^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{1/s}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}}\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}(\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}+\sqrt{1-y^{2}z^{2}})}\mathrm{d}z\\
\eqref{estimate of g_1}\leq&y(1-y^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{1/s}}{(1-y^{2}z^{2})\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}(\sqrt{1-z^{1/s}}+1)}\mathrm{d}z\\
\leq&y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{1/s}}{\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}}\mathrm{d}z.\end{aligned}

Now by (
2.6
), using the change of variable
t=z^{1/s}
,

y\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{1/s}}{\sqrt{g(s,y,z)}}\mathrm{d}z\leq\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{1/s}}{\sqrt{1-z^{1/s}}}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}sB(s+1,\frac{1}{2})\leq\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}sB(1,\frac{1}{2}).

Here
B(a,b)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{0}^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\mathrm{d}t
is the Beta function.
Noting that
B(1,\frac{1}{2})=2
, we get (
2.4
).

We now prove (
2.5
) on the derivative
\varphi^{\prime}_{s}
. As

\varphi^{\prime}_{s}(y)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}
has no definite sign, we need to consider its absolute value

\displaystyle|v^{\prime}_{*}|^{2}=\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}|v|^{2}+\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}|v_{*}|^{2}-\sin\theta\sin\beta\cos\varphi|v||v_{*}|.

\displaystyle\sin^{k}\frac{\theta}{2}\langle v_{*}\rangle^{k}+C_{k}(\cos^{k-1}\frac{\theta}{2}\langle v\rangle^{k-1}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\langle v_{*}\rangle+\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\langle v\rangle\sin^{k-1}\frac{\theta}{2}\langle v_{*}\rangle^{k-1}).
