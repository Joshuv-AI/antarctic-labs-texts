Visual field and driving performance of individuals with inherited retinal disorders: a scoping review
Authors: Xanthe E Bell, John R Grigg, Clare L Fraser, Elisa E Cornish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMaintaining a drivers’ licence is an important patient-reported outcome. Conditions resulting in peripheral vision loss such as inherited retinal disorders (IRDs) can result in loss of licence due to an inadequate horizontal visual field (VF) extent. There are currently no international visual standards for licensing and current testing protocols outlined in licensing guidelines and conducted in out-patient clinics may not adequately reflect or predict on-road driving performance. The present study undertakes a scoping review of the VF of individuals with an IRD and their driving status and/or performance. It aims to determine the proportion of individuals who either meet the VF licensing requirements or pass a driving test and the impact of VF extent.Methods and analysis193 publications from online databases and other sources were identified, of which three publications met the inclusion criteria for the review. Publications were from two countries and included studies which measured driving performance based on collision records and driving simulator performance.ResultsOnly level 4 evidence was available on the topic, including two case-control studies and one cross-sectional study.ConclusionOne study reported 36% of retinitis pigmentosa patients met the VF licencing requirements. There was inconclusive evidence regarding the impact of VF extent on driving performance, with one study finding that VF extent was predictive of driving performance, and other findings did not correlate.Keywords: Dystrophy, Field of vision, Retina, Genetics

Introduction

Maintaining a drivers’ licence is an important patient-reported outcome, contributing to independence, particularly in working age patients. Loss of licence can negatively impact quality of life,1 potentially disrupting employment, caring responsibilities and social life. Inherited retinal disorders (IRDs) can impact licensing due to peripheral vision loss compromising the required visual field (VF) extent. In the working age population, retinitis pigmentosa (RP) the most common IRD2 is characterised by progressive VF constriction, night blindness, photophobia and a decline in visual acuity (VA).3

Formal VF assessment is required by the Austroads Australian fitness to drive (FTD) guidelines if there is clinical indication of a VF defect or a progressive eye condition.4 Monocular automated static perimetry is the minimum baseline standard; however, should a VF defect or progressive eye condition be noted a binocular Esterman is required. Original work from Esterman in 1967 introduced the concept of functional VF testing using a weighted grid approach whereby the central part of the VF is considered more valuable than the periphery, the lower hemisphere more useful than the upper and the horizontal meridian more important than other meridians, as would be the case for most daily activities.5 6 This was then adapted to create the binocular Esterman, a suprathreshold fixation-monitored test. An earlier functional VF iteration concluded that simply overlapping the two monocular VFs to calculate a binocular score was unsuitable.7 The concept of ‘binocular enhancement’ has also been described, where the VF plotted binocularly was larger than if the two monocular VFs were summed, largely attributed to greater sensitivity in the overlapping portion of the VF.7 Thus, the binocular Esterman was not specifically developed to assess driving, but has been adapted for this use. While previous research has found the odds of failing a driving test increase as more points are missed for most areas on the binocular Esterman, VF defect alone is a poor predictor of passing or failing a driving test at an individual level.8 While the Australian FTD guidelines also outline that adequate VFs are important for safe driving, the actual cut-off value that should be set remains unclear.4 Retrospective literature analysing kinetic VF data of 52 RP patients over a mean of 12 years found that 50% of RP patients would fail to meet Canadian VF standards by age 37 years and would be legally blind by 55 years.3

It is unclear how the driving performance of individuals with an IRD affecting peripheral vision correlates with their VF performance. This group is particularly important to consider as emerging genetic and supportive therapies for IRDs have the ability to rescue photoreceptors with potential for functional visual improvement.9 The phase 3 gene therapy trial found an improvement in navigation in low-to-moderate lighting conditions; however, it did not find a statistically significant effect on VF performance. However, therapy may have the potential to slow down the rate of VF loss.9 This contrasts with treatments for diabetic retinopathy (eg, pan-retinal photocoagulation) and glaucoma (intraocular pressure control) which aim to arrest disease progression. Current Australian FTD standards4 have limited information regarding IRDs and do not consider compensatory strategies such as increased eye and head movements. Further, the current testing protocol may not adequately predict on-road driving performance due to the limitations of the fixation-monitored binocular Esterman in predicting driving performance.8 International driving visual standards in countries where VF is included in the criteria also show considerable variability, while in many countries, this is completely disregarded (table 1). A scoping review was conducted to systematically map research in this area to identify gaps in knowledge with the objective of determining the VF status and driving status and/or performance of individuals with IRDs.

Materials and methods

A scoping review as outlined by Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) has the objective to provide an overview or ‘map’ of available evidence and is useful for examining evidence when it is still unclear what other more specific questions can be posed. As the aim of this review is to identify and analyse knowledge gaps and examine how research has been conducted on the topic, and due to the rarity of the condition and limited data available, a scoping review was deemed most suitable to map the evidence related to VF loss and the driving status and/or performance of people with an IRD. There are no previous reviews considering VF loss from IRDs resulting in peripheral VF loss and driving. The Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used, as well as the updated JBI methodology,10 incorporating the framework by Levac et al.11 The review was preregistered with the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/46NGP).

The scoping review considered any existing literature which fitted into the conceptual framework of the study, including both qualitative and quantitative study designs, from peer-reviewed journals and published grey literature. Review papers were excluded due to their broad nature, but individual papers from a review paper were considered if they met the inclusion criteria for this review. To identify relevant literature, MEDLINE Ovid, EMBASE Ovid, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and JBI Evidence Synthesis were searched from inception to 23 June 2025. Search strategies were drafted by the study authors, reviewed by an experienced librarian and further refined through team discussion. Controlled language index terms in the Medical Subject Heading database and free text keywords were used to search the following concepts: (1) IRDs; (2) VF measures; and (3) Driving. The search strategy was adapted for each database searched. The final search strategy for MEDLINE Ovid is given in online supplemental file 1. The reference list of eligible studies was hand searched for additional eligible papers. A separate search strategy of grey literature was undertaken in ProQuest Dissertation and Theses Global. Conference abstracts were also included. The results from the literature search were exported to EndNote 20 and then uploaded to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia).

Two authors (XB, JG) independently performed title and abstract screening based on the eligibility criteria (table 2). Abstracts with insufficient information to apply the eligibility criteria were considered for full-text review. Full-text review was performed independently (XB, JG) to select studies which met the inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the study authors.

A data extraction tool (JBI methodologies for scoping reviews format)10 was developed (XB) based on the research questions and cross-checked by a second reviewer (JG) (online supplemental file 2). It was first tested on a small sample of articles for appropriateness. Data extraction was performed by a single reviewer (XB) and reviewed by a second reviewer (JG). Studies are discussed in tabular and narrative form.

Driving ability in RP patients was first reported in 198112 comparing self-reported motor vehicle collision rates to controls. RP subjects had voluntarily limited themselves to daytime driving in 31/42 cases. Overall, the RP group were involved in significantly more collisions over a 5 year period (50%) compared with controls (29%). A subset of young (≤30) female RP subjects with 5–10 years driving experience accounted for a disproportionately high number of collisions. Excluding this group from the analysis found no difference in collision rates between the RP subjects and controls. There was no relationship between peripheral VF efficiency (Goldman) or central VA and the number of collisions. VF efficiency was calculated by totalling the degrees of VF in eight principal meridians (horizontal, vertical, oblique 45° and 225°, 135° and 315°) from the central fixation point, dividing by 500 (the total degrees of a normal VF as outlined by the American Medical Association)13 and multiplying by 100 to convert to an efficiency percentage. The American Medical Association outlines the normal VF extent in each of the eight principal meridians on a Goldman VF using a size III4e white target as 85° temporally, 60° superotemporally, 45° superiorly, 55° superonasally, 60° nasally, 55° inferonasally, 60° inferiorly and 80° inferotemporally, totalling to 500° when summed.13

A 1992 paper14 reported that RP drivers had significantly more collisions over a 5-year period (76%) compared with controls (39%). Collisions were attributed to peripheral vision (i.e. crashes during lane changes or at intersections) in the RP group (83%) compared with controls (31%). There was no difference between the groups for the proportion of daytime and nighttime collisions, when daytime only RP drivers (7/21 voluntarily limited) were excluded from the analyses. The RP group had a greater number of driving simulator collisions (4 vs 0). Logistic regression found horizontal field extent and binocular field area were the strongest predictors of collisions for the RP group, finding a statistically significant correlation with VF loss severity (p<0.03).

Finally, Heath Jeffrey reported on an Australian cohort.2 The group retrospectively analysed data from 228 RP patients (2013–2022). Of RP patients aged ≥16 years (Australian legal age for licensing) 36% met the Australian FTD standards based on better eye VA and a binocular Esterman VF. Logistic regression found the main predictors of meeting FTD visual standards were younger age at VF testing (p=0.004) and a rhodopsin genetic mutation (p=0.031); however, those with a rhodopsin genetic mutation only comprised 3.1% of the cohort. The rhodopsin genetic mutation has a more variable phenotype, often sector RP which was reported in two of the patients, leading to more reserve in the VF. However, the specific genetic mutations were not discussed. There was a non-significant effect of older age at symptom onset and meeting the FTD visual standards. Importantly only 52% of those who self-reported as still driving met the FTD visual standards. The reasons for failure to meet the minimum VF requirements was a horizontal VF extent <90 degrees (82%) or scotomas within the central 20-degree radius (83%), with 65% of patients failing both criteria. No patients failed due to VA. One serious drawback in this study was that the binocular Esterman VF was performed in dilated subjects which may have impacted performance, considering that driving with pharmacologically dilated pupils can lend itself to be considered an offence.

Discussion

This scoping review examines the VF status and driving performance in individuals with an IRD. This group is important to consider as current FTD guidelines have limited information regarding complex and/or progressive conditions, are not individualised, and do not consider compensatory strategies. The emergence of new treatments also makes this group an area of interest. A key finding is that when analysed at a group level, driving performance is often worse compared with controls; however, individually people may be rated as fit-to-drive.

There were both consistencies and discrepancies within the literature. Overall individuals with IRDs were involved in a greater number of collisions.12 14 Conflicting evidence was seen in the effect of VF extent on driving performance, with one paper finding no correlation between VF area and motor vehicle collisions12 versus binocular field area being the strongest predictor of collisions.14 One paper reported that 36% of RP patients met Australian FTD guidelines.2

The literature largely did not report on the effect of driving experience which may also affect learning compensatory strategies such as increased eye and head movements. However, IRDs are progressive, so while driving experience may positively impact performance, repeated assessments are required. As a result, practical tests which are under the remit of licensing bodies combined with clinical assessments are required to determine an individual’s capacity. In addition, in people with IRDs, driving licences should be issued for short-term fixed periods and renewed subject to driving visual standards being met (eg, by the Driver and Vehicle Licencing Agency in the UK or Austroads in Australia).

The paucity of literature considering IRDs and driving may be due to the fact that the same type of VFs that are undertaken by the licensing authority even in countries where VFs form part of the driving eligibility criteria is not always undertaken in the clinical context as part of standard of care. The roving binocular Esterman VF (a binocular Esterman VF without fixation monitoring) is an acceptable VF assessment for licensing in the Austroads guidelines,4 but is not discussed in the literature. Individuals can complete a binocular Esterman VF without fixation monitoring, which may more adequately resemble on-road driving. Notably, the retrospective Australian study2 commented that for those in their cohort who had a borderline binocular Esterman VF, they may have in fact passed if a roving binocular Esterman VF was performed. Also of note is a case-control study examining the predictive value of the fixation-monitored binocular Esterman on the outcome of an on-road driving test found that while the likelihood of passing an on-road driving test increases as more points are seen on the Esterman, the Esterman showed no discriminative ability to predict driving performance on an individual level.8 It is unknown whether the roving binocular Esterman VF assessment may better predict driving performance, compared with the fixation-monitored version.

There is limited evidence of the driving performance on RP patients, with only one simulator study from 1992.14 Since the 1990s when the papers looking at performance of RP drivers were published, there have also been significant developments in car design. Integrated safety features such as the lane departure warning system, lane keep assist, blind spot detection, lane change assist and rear cross-traffic alerts could all reduce collision rates. Given recent safety advancements, the emergence of therapies with potential to improve vision, persistent misconceptions about the fitness of individuals with RP to hold a driver’s licence2 and the scarcity of research on driving in this population, future studies should prioritise identifying and validating the most appropriate measures of visual function that accurately predict driving ability in this cohort.

Overall, the lack of literature examining the driving performance of individuals with an IRD, and the inconsistencies considering the impact of VF extent on performance, necessitates the need to individualise the FTD assessment. Much of the data is now over 30 years old and requires revaluation with modern perimetry techniques such as the Binocular Esterman in comparison to other measures of VF. The lack of evidence considering VF assessments which are included in the FTD guidelines, such as the roving binocular Esterman VF, also requires future research to understand if this better predicts driving performance.
