Fagonia indica mediated biosynthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles with enhanced antimicrobial and therapeutic potential
Authors: Asif Kamal, Moona Nazish, Khalid Kamal, Shaimaa A. M. Abdelmohsen, Sadeem F. Alarfj, Nazih Y. Rebouh, Muydinjon Muminov, Sarah Abdul Razak, Nasrulla Xakimov, Oktay Rzayev, Yaregal Damtie Mengistie, Wajid Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanotechnology provides innovative tools for medicine, agriculture, and environmental applications. Iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3 NPs) are of particular biomedical interest due to their biocompatibility, magnetic properties, and therapeutic potential. Harnessing the phytoconstituents of F. indica for nanoparticle fabrication offers a sustainable approach to generate biofunctional nanomaterials with enhanced therapeutic efficacyIn this study, Fe2O3 NPs were biosynthesized using F. indica extract through a green and cost-effective method. The nanoparticles were characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Ultraviolet–Visible Spectroscopy (UV–Vis), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), confirming their rectangular (average size of 65 nm) morphology, functional group interactions, and elemental composition of iron and oxygen. The bio-fabricated Fe2O3 NPs displayed broad pharmacological activities: strong antileishmanial (71%), anti-inflammatory (79%), antioxidant (total antioxidant capacity 78.4%, total reducing power 75.2%, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl 64.6%), and antidiabetic effects (α-amylase inhibition 55.2%, α-glucosidase inhibition 56.6%), along with protein kinase inhibition (14.4 ± 1.22), indicating anticancer potential. Biocompatibility was confirmed by low hemolysis (1.46 ± 0.10). The nanoparticles also showed significant antimicrobial activity, including inhibition zones of 24.0 mm against Escherichia coli and 70 ± 2.1% antifungal activity against Ascochyta rabiei. The integration of F. indica phytochemicals into Fe2O3 NP synthesis not only offers an eco-friendly production method but also yields multifunctional nanoparticles. These findings highlight their promise as candidates for future biomedical applications, particularly in infectious disease management, oxidative stress-related disorders, and metabolic regulation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Nanotechnology is a rapidly evolving interdisciplinary field, drawing contributions from various scientific domains including biological, natural, and physical sciences. The term “nano” refers to extremely small particles with diameters ranging from 1 to 100 nanometers. Nanoparticles (NPs) possess unique properties such as reduced size, high surface-area-to-volume ratio, structural diversity, and distinct chemical and physical characteristics, making them suitable for applications in environmental science, pharmaceuticals, and agriculture [1]. Due to their diverse morphologies, nanoparticles are classified into forms such as nanocrystals, nanoflowers, nanotubes, and nanowires [2]. Traditional physical and chemical methods for nanoparticle synthesis often require high energy input and involve toxic substances [3]. In contrast, biological or “green” synthesis methods utilize microorganisms [4, 5], algal biomass [6], and plant-based materials [7]. Previous studies have shown that plant-derived nanoparticles tend to be more effective than those synthesized using other biological sources, largely because of the rich phytochemical content present in plants. Furthermore, nanoparticles synthesized from plants generally exhibit higher stability and faster synthesis rates compared to those derived from fungi or bacteria [8].

Among various classes of NPs, metal-based nanoparticles (MO-NPs) are particularly valuable owing to their bio-friendly nature [9]. Metal oxide nanoparticles majorly contributed to various arenas, mainly in environmental management, agriculture, and nanomedicine [10, 11]. Currently, scientists are focusing on the bio-fabricated synthesis of nanoparticles using various metals such as zinc, iron, silver, platinum, and gold due to their applications in metal degradation, medical and pharmaceutical products, and consumer goods like shampoos, soaps, toothpaste, detergents, and cosmetics [12]. This growing interest in bio-fabrication methods reflects the potential for these nanoparticles to be environmentally sustainable while maintaining high efficiency in their diverse uses. The plant-based preparation of iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3 NPs) offers significant advantages, as the phytoconstituents in plant extracts act as powerful capping agents, playing a vigorous role in the synthesis of NPs. These capping agents stabilize the nanoparticles through various mechanisms, including electrostatic interactions, steric effects, and hydration stabilization.

Recently, iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3 NPs) have gained prodigious scientific attention. Together, Fe2O3 NPs are sporadic in showing colored arrival but are less soluble. Fe2O3 NPs play a vital role in many biomedical and industrial applications. The Fe2O3 NPs with greater antiferromagnetic potential are extensively applied in pigments, gas sensors, and catalysts [13]. Accordingly, it has been discovered that Fe2O3 NPs are among the widely studied transition metal oxides due to variations in crystallinity, magnetic properties, appropriate oxidation states, and their eco-friendly and cost-effective nature [14]. Fe2O3 NPs are extensively utilized in environmental management and the biomedical field because of the aforementioned properties [15]. Additionally, Fe2O3 NPs have been proven to be nontoxic, biocompatible, and easy to synthesize, making them a perfect material for applications in biological systems [16]. Their ability to be functionalized with various biomolecules further enhances their suitability for applications such as targeted drug transport and tomography. Ongoing research continues to explore their potential in innovative therapies, particularly in cancer treatment and neurological diseases. The inimitable characteristics of Fe2O3 NPs also make them auspicious applicants for the development of advanced biosensors and diagnostic devices.

Plant extracts synthesized from various portions have been used for their therapeutic purposes and to cure specific diseases since ancient times [17]. Fagonia indica (L.) is an outstanding pharmaceutical species, belonging to the genus “Fagonia” and is locally known as “sacchi booti”. F. indica is constrained to arid and warm zones globally. In Pakistan, F. indica is found typically in arid and semi-arid regions [18]. Previously, it has been reported that Fagonia species have antifungal and antibacterial potential [19]. The species is ethnobotanically used to cure allergy [20], urinary tract infection (UTI) [21], vomiting [22], and flu [23], while its ash is utilized in the treatment of anemia in children [24]. F. indica has been recommended as a plant with great efficacy to treat breast cancer [18]. The F. indica is contributing to different pharmacological activities because of the diverse phytochemical ingredients. Different types of phytochemicals, such as alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolics, terpenoids, and saponins, have been extracted from F. indica. As phytomedicines, the aforementioned compounds are noteworthy [18]. These diverse bioactive compounds make F. indica a promising candidate for the synthesis of novel therapeutic agents. Despite its rich and diverse phytochemical profile the use of F. indica, a medicinal halophytic plant rarely explored for nanoparticle synthesis. The combination of alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and saponins present in F. indica not only acts as a reducing and capping agent but may also synergistically enhance the potential of the synthesized Fe2O3 NPs.

Although a lot of research is done on plant-mediated synthesis of Fe2O3 NPs, most studies are concentrated on synthesis and general characterization, with little attention paid to multifunctional biomedical implementation and the mechanistic nature of the phytochemicals in stabilizing nanoparticles. In this regard, this paper discusses Fagonia indica, which is a well-known medicinal herb that contains a wide array of bioactive compounds but has not been extensively researched to be used as a nanoparticle-forming agent due to its bio-reducing and capping properties. This article highlights the synthesis of Fe2O3 NPs in an eco-friendly, cost-effective synthesis, devoid of toxic chemicals used and high energy input, and undergoes thorough characterization by using SEM, EDX, DLS, FTIR, and UV-Vis methods in an attempt to explain their morphology, surface chemistry, and optical characteristics. Moreover, in comparison with traditional studies, this study has given a complete assessment of the nanoparticles in most pharmacological areas, particularly antioxidants, anti-inflammatory, antileishmanial, antidiabetic, antimicrobial, biocompatibility, and protein kinase inhibition (anticancer) properties. This complementary strategy points to the synergistic role of F. indica phytochemicals in improving the stability of nanoparticles and their therapeutic effects. Consequently, the research makes contributions to the existing knowledge in the bridging of green synthesis with broad-spectrum biomedical uses, and it provides a more comprehensive and application-focused outlook of future nanotechnological and pharmacological studies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Ferric chloride (FeCl3, ≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.5% purity, Merck, Germany), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), ascorbic acid (AA, ≥ 99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, ≥ 95% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), methanol (HPLC grade, ≥ 99.9% purity, Merck, Germany), amphotericin-B (≥ 95% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), diclofenac sodium (≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), bovine serum albumin (BSA, ≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), Whatman filter paper No. 1 (Whatman International Ltd., UK), and Tris buffer (≥ 99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were used in this study. All chemicals employed were of analytical grade.

Collection and identification of plant species

The Fagonia indica plant was collected from Jhelum (32.94° N, 73.73° E) in 2024. The plant was identified by using scientific literature [25]. Subsequently, the plant was authenticated through its morphological characteristics and verified by a taxonomist, Dr. Moona Nazish , to ensure accurate species identification, and submitted to the National Herbarium at Quaid-i-Azam University, having voucher no. AFI-12346.

Synthesis of plant extract

The extract was made by collecting F. indica leaves and vigorously splashing them in running water to remove any dust or other material that might have adhered to their surface. Following cleaning, the leaves were all dried in a shady place and ground into fine particles via a grinder. 250 mL of double-distilled water (DDW) was mixed with the produced powder, heated for 15 min, and then incubated for another 15 min at 80 °C in a water bath. After cooling and filtering through muslin cloth and Whatman filter paper, the resulting mixture was set at 4 °C. The extract was then stored for further analysis and potential use in various applications.

Qualitative study on phytochemical constituents of phyto-extract

A qualitative phytochemical study includes the method of detecting and identifying different phytoactive components. It aims to assess the absence or occurrence of particular phytochemicals like flavonoids, phenolic compounds, alkaloids, protein, terpenoids, glycosides, saponins, oils, steroids, quinones, anthocyanins, and tannins. The Qualitative phytochemical assessment was achieved through the previous standard protocol [26]. This study provides essential information regarding the bioactive constituents of the plant, which can contribute to its potential.

Preparation of Fe2O3 NPs

To synthesize Fe2O3 NPs, 100 mL of F. indica leaf extract was gradually added to 100 mL of the FeCl3 solution (0.1 M). The product of the mixture was stirred and warmed on a magnetic hot plate at 200 rpm and heated up to 100 °C, and the color of the solution turned brown and then slowly turned dark brown, which indicated the formation of nanoparticles. The pH of the reaction mixture was maintained at 0.1 M NaOH to facilitate nanoparticle formation. At the end of the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 35 min to take out the nanoparticles. The pellet obtained was cautiously transferred into Petri plates and heated to 350 °C in a furnace instead of drying in air to kill all organic matter and increase the crystallinity. The obtained Fe2O3 NPs were then kept at 25 °C to be characterized further. The nanoparticles prepared were validated by different spectroscopic and microscopic methods before being utilized in biological studies.

Characterization of Fe2O3 NPs

UV–Vis study

The optical properties of the phyto-fabricated Fe2O3 NPs in a solution were analyzed via UV-Vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) at room temperature. The absorbance spectra were recorded in the range of 200–800 nm with a scan speed of 400 nm/min and a spectral bandwidth of 1 nm. This technique allowed for the determination of the surface plasmon resonance band, which provides insights into the optical properties of the NPs.

SEM and EDX study

SEM was executed to analyze the surface properties of the prepared Fe2O3 NPs. SEM (JEOL JSM-6380 A) was performed at 25 °C following the previously documented protocol [27]. SEM analysis of Fe2O3 NPs was performed using a SEM (JEOL JSM-6380 A) at 15 kV accelerating voltage, 8.9 mm working distance, high vacuum (7.54 × 10−10 mbar), using an SE2 detector at 10,000× magnification with a 1 μm scale bar. The EDX study was executed to assess the composition, the occurrence proportion, and the determination of the presence of iron and oxygen. EDX (EOL JSM-6380 A SEM equipped with an Oxford Instruments EDX detector) was used to assess the qualitative assessment of the constituents in Fe2O3 NPs in a dispersed manner at 25 °C using an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR analysis of Fe2O3 NPs was determined to identify structural characteristics and validate various functional groups. The FTIR examination of the Fe2O3 NPs sample was prepared following the former standard procedure with a little modification [28], using a Shimadzu IRTracer-100 FTIR spectrophotometer, 25 °C scanning in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

DLS study

DLS was performed to assess the zeta potential (ZP), hydrodynamic size distribution, and polydispersity index (PDI). DLS examination was achieved following the earlier procedure [27] using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK) at room temperature. This analysis provided crucial information about the stability and dispersibility of the Fe2O3 NPs in solution, which is essential for their potential biomedical applications. The results also indicated uniformity and surface charge, which are key factors influencing nanoparticle behavior in biological environments. All measurements were conducted in triplicate using water as the dispersing medium (refractive index n = 1.33; viscosity η = 0.8872 cP).

XRD analysis

The crystalline structure of the synthesized Fe2O3 nanoparticles was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The measurements were performed on a Advance, Bruker, Germany diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) as the X-ray source. The sample was prepared by finely grinding the dried nanoparticles and gently placing them on a glass sample holder to ensure a flat surface. The XRD patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20°–70° with a scanning rate of 2°/min. The operating voltage and current were set at 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. To reduce background noise, the measurements were conducted at room temperature. The obtained diffraction peaks were compared with standard data from the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card No. 33–0664 to confirm the phase and structure of Fe2O3 nanoparticles. The judgement of the antiparasitic potential of the prepared NPs was used against Leishmania tropica promastigotes, according to the previous standard procedure [29]. Every test tube has 5 mL of medium with 1 x 105 parasites. After that, 5 mL of every treatment (25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL) of the NPs was added to every tube and placed in an incubator at 28 °C. Throughout this assay, DMSO and amphotericin B were utilized as a negative and positive control, respectively. The number was counted following the hemocytometer in all tubes, including both NPs treated tubes and control tubes, at different time durations from 24 to 96 h, and the percentage (%) inhibition was determined using the formula (1):

Antioxidant potential

The antioxidant potential of the biosynthesized Fe2O3 NPs was systematically assessed using multiple complementary assays, including total antioxidant capacity (TAC), DPPH free radical scavenging activity (FRSA), and total reducing power (TRP), at concentrations ranging from 50 to 200 µg/mL [31]. The use of these assays enabled a comprehensive evaluation of the nanoparticles’ ability to scavenge free radicals, donate electrons, and mitigate oxidative stress, which are critical indicators of their therapeutic relevance in oxidative stress–related disorders. Ascorbic acid (AA) was used as the positive control, while dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) served as the negative control in all antioxidant experiments to ensure assay validity and comparative assessment. For TAC determination, 100 µL of each concentration of Fe2O3 NPs was individually mixed with the respective reagents following the previously reported protocol [31]. The TRP of the Fe2O3 NPs was evaluated using the potassium ferricyanide method as described earlier [31]. The absorbance of treated and control samples was recorded at 580 nm to determine the reducing capacity.

The FRSA of the nanoparticles was assessed using the standard DPPH method as previously reported [31]. Protein (PK) kinase inhibition

The protein kinase (PK) inhibitory property of the produced nanoparticles was tested on Streptomyces sp. strain 85E with a standard protocol with minor alterations [32]. The test organism was acquired from the National Institute of Health and grown on ISP4 agar medium in sterile conditions. The Streptomyces 85E fresh spores were spread on the sterile plates of ISP4 agar uniformly with a cotton swab to create a lawn. Various concentrations of the NPs (1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 µg/mL) were impregnated on sterile filter paper discs (6 mm diameter) and placed on the inoculated agar plates with a lot of care. As a positive control, Streptomycin was used, and discs with phosphate buffer were the negative control. The incubation of all plates was conducted at 28 °C of 48–72 h. The plates were then tested at the end of the incubation period for whether or not they contained bald areas around the discs, which was a sign of protein kinase inhibition. Measurement of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was in mm [33]. The bald zones and the not clear antimicrobial zones formed validate the fact that the nanoparticles disrupt the protein kinase-mediated signaling pathways that mediate morphological differentiation in Streptomyces 85E. The ZOI was calculated as described previously [33].

The antidiabetic efficacy of plant-mediated Fe2O3 NPs was assessed by means of ɑ-glucosidase and ɑ-amylase inhibition assessment. These assays were conducted to gauge the potential of Fe2O3 NPs in inhibiting the key enzymes involved in carbohydrate digestion, which can help in controlling postprandial blood glucose levels. The results demonstrated the promising antidiabetic properties of Fe2O3 NPs, indicating their potential as therapeutic agents for managing diabetes.

Estimation of the antimicrobial potential

Antifungal assay

To evaluate the antifungal activity, the prepared Fe2O3 NPs were tested against Ascochyta rabiei. The A. rabiei culture was obtained from the preserved culture at the National Agriculture Research Center, Islamabad. The preserved A. rabiei was re-cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) media and then incubated for one week at 25 ± 1 °C. PDA media was treated with varying doses of Fe2O3 NPs (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mg/mL). A 4 mm disc of A. rabiei was placed in the center of the Fe2O3 NPs-amended Petri plates. Amphotericin B was considered a positive control. The NPs’ amended plates were incubated for one week (7 days) at 25 ± 1 °C, and the antifungal activity was assessed by applying the subsequent formula.

Antibacterial assay

The antibacterial efficacy was estimated against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli, following a previously established procedure with slight modifications [36]. The bacterial culture was obtained from the preserved culture at the National Agriculture Research Center, Islamabad. The antibacterial assay was accomplished at various concentrations (200, 100, and 50 µg/mL) in accordance with the standard procedure, with a little modification [37]. Positive controls with antibiotics (Carbapenems) and negative controls containing less than 1% Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) were included. The plates were placed in an incubator at 30 °C for 24 h, and after incubation, the ZOI were determined in mm.

Statistical assessment

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 16.0 and Origin Pro 9. Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Characterization of Fe2O3 NPs

UV–visible spectroscopy

The plant extract and iron salts are responsible for the nanoparticle’s formation. The rapid reaction between the phytochemical constituents of F. indica extract and ferric chloride was indicated by a visible color change from brownish to dark brown, confirming the initiation of iron oxide nanoparticle (Fe2O3 NPs) formation. The UV–visible absorption spectrum of the prepared nanoparticles exhibited a strong peak at 350 nm (Fig. 1). This absorption band corresponds to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of iron oxide nanoparticles, which arises due to collective oscillations of electrons on the nanoparticle surface when excited by light. Such a characteristic peak at the 350 nm range has been consistently reported for Fe2O3 NPs synthesized using green approaches [41]. The absorption peak around 350 nm in the UV–Vis spectrum indicates the characteristic optical transition of Fe2O3 NPs, confirming their formation. This shift from bulk material absorption highlights the nanoscale nature and successful synthesis of Fe2O3 NPs [42]. Previous studies have shown that shifts in absorption maximum are influenced by particle size, crystallinity, and capping molecules present in the plant extract [43]. Plant-mediated Fe2O3 NPs prepared with extracts of Azadirachta indica [44], and Lepidium sativum [45] have been found to exhibit absorption maxima at 320–370 nm depending on particle size and conditions. Therefore, the maximum at 350 nm in the given study is sufficiently low to align with the given range, which proves the successful nanoparticles creation.

FTIR spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum of the biosynthesized Fe2O3 NPs, (Fig. 2) displayed a broad absorption band around ~ 3400 cm−1 corresponding to O–H stretching and hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl groups, weak signals at 2920–2850 cm−1 from aliphatic C–H stretching, and a distinct band at ~ 1630–1650 cm−1 attributed to C =O stretching or H–O–H bending. Additional peaks appeared between ~ 1380–1450 cm−1 (C–H bending/–CH3 deformation or residual –NO3) and ~ 1100–1000 cm−1 (C–O and C–O–C stretching of alcohols/ethers), while strong absorptions in the fingerprint region at ~ 570–590 cm−1 and ~ 420–460 cm−1 confirmed Fe–O stretching vibrations typical of iron oxide. These features verify successful nanoparticle formation and indicate that organic moieties from the plant extract remain associated with the surface, functioning as reducing and stabilizing agents. The retention of organic peaks suggests phytochemical capping, which enhances dispersion stability and may influence biological activity. Similar FTIR profiles have been reported for green-synthesized iron-oxide nanoparticles using plant extracts, where O–H, C=O, and C–O bands arise from phenolics, flavonoids, proteins, and polysaccharides, and Fe–O vibrations consistently occur within the 400–600 cm−1 range [46]. Minor variations in Fe–O peak positions or intensity across studies are typically due to differences in plant metabolites, particle size, crystalline phase (Fe2O3), or synthesis conditions [47]. Compared with calcined or extensively washed nanoparticles in earlier reports, which often show diminished organic signals, the strong phytochemical bands in Fe2O3 highlight that biomolecular capping was preserved, a feature advantageous for biomedical and catalytic applications. This finding is in agreement with reports by Amutha and Sridhar, who observed similar Fe–O stretching peaks and phytochemical signatures in plant-mediated Fe2O3 nanoparticles [48]. In contrast, studies using thermal or chemical synthesis often lack these organic bands, reflecting the absence of natural capping agents [49]. Therefore, the current results emphasize the advantages of green synthesis, where retained phytochemicals not only stabilize the nanoparticles but also impart potential bioactivity, making them superior to conventionally synthesized counterparts.

XRD

The XRD pattern of Fe2O3 NPs exhibited sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 24.2°, 33.2°, 35.7°, 40.9°, 49.5°, 54.1°, 57.5°, and 62.5°, corresponding to the (012), (104), (110), (113), (024), (116), (214), and (300) crystal planes, respectively. These peaks match well with standard JCPDS card No. 33–0664, confirming the formation of the rhombohedral hematite (α-Fe2O3) phase (Fig. 3). The sharpness and intensity of the peaks indicate high crystallinity of the nanoparticles, which is further enhanced by the calcination process at 350 °C. Thus, the average crystallite size of the synthesized Fe2O3 nanoparticles is approximately 10–11 nm, which is consistent with typical sizes obtained via green synthesis methods. For instance, previous studies using plant extracts for Fe2O3 NPs synthesis reported crystallite sizes ranging from 8 to 20 nm, depending on the source extract and calcination conditions. Compared to chemically synthesized Fe2O3 NPs, which often show larger crystallite sizes of 20–50 nm due to high-temperature processes, green synthesis offers a more controlled formation of nanosized particles with uniform morphology. Furthermore, the observed peak positions and intensities suggest minimal lattice strain and defects, which is favourable for applications requiring high surface reactivity, such as catalysis, antimicrobial activity, and adsorption [50, 51]. The similarity of the XRD pattern to other reported α-Fe2O3 nanoparticles synthesized via eco-friendly methods demonstrates the reproducibility and reliability of plant-mediated synthesis routes.

Overall, the XRD analysis confirms the successful formation of highly crystalline, nanosized hematite nanoparticles, comparable to other Fe2O3 nanoparticles synthesized both chemically and biologically, but with the added advantages of green synthesis, including environmental safety and biocompatibility.

SEM and EDX spectroscopy

SEM was executed to inspect the structural configuration and dispersal of the Fe2O3 NPs. The surface properties were clearly observed through SEM analysis (Fig. 4A). The SEM micrograph confirmed that the Fe2O3 NPs appeared as white-colored particles with a rod-shaped morphology having an average size of 65 nm, arranged in a clustered or clumped manner. The predominance of rectangular (rod)-shaped nanoparticles suggests controlled nucleation and growth during the biosynthesis process, likely mediated by the phytochemicals in F. indica extract, which acted as natural capping agents. Such bioactive compounds prevent excessive aggregation and help maintain shape integrity. The rod-shaped morphology observed in this study provides strong evidence for successful nanoparticle formation and allows assessment of surface topography, size distribution, and aggregation tendencies. While some degree of clustering is expected in biologically synthesized nanoparticles due to the presence of organic molecules on their surface, the relatively uniform shape and size indicate effective stabilization by plant-derived metabolites. Similar morphological characteristics have been reported for green-synthesized Fe2O3 nanoparticles in earlier studies [52], further validating our results. Aggregation occurs due to magnetic dipole–dipole attraction, high surface energy, and insufficient phytochemical stabilization, leading nanoparticles to cluster to minimize overall system energy [53]. Moreover, particle aggregation, as seen in clusters, can also be attributed to magnetic interactions among iron oxide nanoparticles, a common phenomenon that influences their surface energy and dispersibility [54].

The elemental conformation of the prepared NPs was inspected by means of the EDX study. The EDX study of the Fe2O3 NPs established the existence of iron (Fe) and oxygen (O), verifying the formation of Fe2O3 NPs, along with traces of other impurities such as carbon and sodium, likely originating from the plant extract (Fig. 4B). The weight percentages of Fe and O were found to be 35.0% and 17.9%, respectively. The high concentration of Fe and O confirms the formation of Fe2pt>2pt>O3 NPs. These findings align with previous studies conducted by nanotechnologists [55]. Additionally, the EDS elemental distribution was examined. Figure 5 shows that the NPs are uniformly dispersed on the carbon foil. These results highlight the ability of the EDX detector to rapidly identify Fe2O3 NPs. The presence of minor elements such as carbon, chlorine, potassium, and sulfur can be attributed to residual phytochemicals (e.g., polyphenols, proteins, and organic acids) from the plant extract acting as capping and stabilizing agents, as well as from the carbon-coated grid used during sample preparation. Such trace impurities are commonly reported in green synthesis approaches and do not significantly affect the core composition of the nanoparticles [56]. These results highlight the capability of the EDX detector to rapidly identify Fe2O3 NPs and confirm their formation with surface-stabilizing biomolecules characteristic of plant-mediated synthesis.

Antileishmanial potential

To evaluate the antileishmanial activity, the prepared NPs were tested at various concentrations over 72 h, and their effectiveness is shown in Fig. 7. The number of promastigotes was determined in both NPs-amended and control groups at 24, 36, 48, and 72 h. The antileishmanial activity increased with higher concentrations of Fe2O3 NPs. After 24 h of incubation, the antileishmanial effects were 20%, 32%, 35%, and 39% at 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL, respectively. At the same time, the positive control (Amphotericin B) exhibited a significantly higher inhibition (75–80%), and the negative control (DMSO) showed inhibition of < 5%. After 36 h, the Fe2O3 NPs showed efficacy of 30%, 34%, 39%, and 50% at the same concentrations. After 48 h of incubation, the inhibition rates were 38% at 25 µg/mL, 52% at 50 µg/mL, 62% at 100 µg/mL, and 71% at 200 µg/mL. A slight reduction in the antileishmanial effect was examined thereafter, which may be credited to the depletion of reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by the Fe2O3 NPs. The slight decline at later stages suggests that continuous ROS production is critical for sustaining the maximum anti-leishmanicidal activity, and depletion of ROS or aggregation of nanoparticles may reduce efficacy [60]. Similar findings have been reported for other metal oxide nanoparticles, where oxidative stress and membrane damage were the primary modes of parasite inhibition [61]. Fe2O3 NPs can generate reactive ions that form holes in the pathogen’s cell wall, disrupt membrane structure, cause seepage of intracellular materials, and ultimately lead to pathogen destruction. The significant results of this study suggest that Fe2pt>O3 NPs could be a promising tool for treating leishmaniasis, which is consistent with previous research [62]. These observations highlight that Fagonia indica-mediated Fe2pt>2O3 NPs combine both the metallic core activity and the phytochemical contribution, making them superior candidates for leishmanicidal applications [63]. Similarly, previous studies have reported approximately 70–75% antileishmanial activity using green-synthesized iron oxide nanoparticles, supporting the biological efficacy of such nanomaterials [64].

Anti-inflammatory potential

The synthesized Fe2O3 NPs demonstrated effective anti-inflammatory properties (Fig. 8). The Fe2O3 NPs helped reduce inflammation at various concentrations. The highest anti-inflammatory effect was observed after 48 h of incubation, with inhibition rates of 55% at 25 µg/mL, 60% at 50 µg/mL, 63% at 100 µg/mL, 70% at 200 µg/mL, and 79% at 400 µg/mL. Further incubation beyond 48 h did not result in more than 79% inhibition. In comparison, the positive control (Diclofenac sodium) exhibited significantly higher inhibition (~ 80–91%) under the same conditions, whereas the negative control (DMSO) showed minimal inhibition (< 5%), confirming the validity of the assay. A comparative analysis showed that Fe2pt>O3 NPs were more effective as an anti-inflammatory agent at specific concentrations. These results are consistent with previous studies [65]. The anti-inflammatory response may be attributed to the ability of Fe2O3 NPs to inhibit protein denaturation and stabilize membrane proteins, which are key events in the inflammatory cascade [66]. Phytochemicals adsorbed on the surface of the nanoparticles could also synergize with iron oxide to enhance suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators [67]. Previously, scientists have reported a 96.20% anti-inflamatory activity using Madhuca indica Fe2pt >2pt >O3 NPs [68], and 85.66% of the activity using Merremia quinquefolia mediated Fe2pt 2pt>2O3 NPs [69]. The difference in the activity of the Fe2O3 NPs in comparison may be due to differences in the plant extract. Additionally, the dose-dependent activity suggests that higher concentrations of Fe2O3 NPs provide stronger protection against protein denaturation and tissue damage.

Biocompatibility assay

The hemolytic test was directed to examine the toxicity of the Fe2O3 NPs using RBCs. RBCs were mixed with Fe2O3 NPs at concentrations ranging from 25 µg/mL to 200 µg/mL in a buffer solution prepared to simulate the extracellular environment. Ethical approval for the use of human blood samples was obtained from the institutional human ethics committee (Approval No.: 2024/R624); all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. The assay is based on the principle that hemoglobin is released into the medium when RBCs lyse, which only occurs if the tested sample induces cell rupture. The consequences of the hemolytic assay are presented in Table 3. Fe2O3 NPs showed minimal hemolysis (0.49–1.46%), compared to complete hemolysis in the positive control (97.1–99.3%) and negligible hemolysis in the negative control (0.08–0.11%) across all concentrations. According to the American Society for Testing and Materials, materials with hemolysis greater than 2% are considered non-hemolytic, those with hemolysis between 2 and 5% are mildly hemolytic, and materials with hemolysis greater than 5% are classified as hemolytic [76]. The results indicate that the phyto-fabricated Fe2O3 NPs displayed hemocompatibility, even at the highest dosage, with no significant hemolysis observed. The biocompatibility of these NPs is crucial for their potential biomedical applications. Consequently, our findings confirm the safety of the prepared NPs and support their use in therapeutic applications [77, 78]. Similar results were reported by scientists, where green-synthesized Fe2O3 NPs using Helianthemum lippii extract also showed excellent hemocompatibility with hemolysis below 2% [79]. In contrast, iron oxide nanoparticles synthesized using Cucurbita pepo extract exhibited mild hemolytic activity above 100 µg/mL, differing from our findings [80]. These comparisons suggest that while biogenic Fe2O3 NPs generally demonstrate blood compatibility, variations in plant phytochemicals can significantly influence hemolytic responses.

Anti-diabetic assay

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from insufficient insulin production and or impaired insulin action. One effective therapeutic strategy for controlling postprandial hyperglycemia involves the inhibition of key carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes, namely α-amylase (AA) and α-glucosidase (AG), in the gastrointestinal tract [85]. In the present study, the antidiabetic potential of the biosynthesized Fe2O3 NPs was evaluated by assessing their inhibitory effects on AA and AG at concentrations ranging from 25 to 400 µg/mL, as summarized in Table 5. The nanoparticles exhibited dose-dependent enzyme inhibition, with the highest inhibition observed at 400 µg/mL, reaching 55.23 ± 0.35% for AA and 56.59 ± 1.34% for AG. These findings are consistent with and further support previously reported studies demonstrating the inhibitory effects of Fe2pt>O3 NPs on carbohydrate-digesting enzymes.

The notable inhibition of both AA and AG suggests that Fe2O3 NPs may contribute to improved glycemic control by reducing glucose absorption, thereby offering a promising pharmacological approach for DM management [85]. Furthermore, the ability of Fe2O3 NPs to modulate these enzymes highlights their potential as adjunct therapeutic agents for regulating blood glucose levels and mitigating postprandial hyperglycemia. Comparable results were reported, who synthesized Fe2O3 NPs using Madhuca indica extract and observed strong α-glucosidase inhibition, in agreement with the present study [86]. Regarding Fe-ZnONPs, 30.60 ± 2.07% inhibition and 41.13 ± 1.58% inhibition were observed against α-glucosidase and α-amylase, respectively [87]. FeNPs synthesized using Securidaca longipedunculata demonstrated a high inhibition activity of up to 84.29% [88]. Similarly, Murraya koenigii exhibited 69.99% α-amylase inhibition at 500 µg/mL [89]. Solanum khasianum also showed significant antidiabetic potential by inhibiting α-glycosidase, with an inhibition rate of 85.02% [90]. Comparably, Oldenlandia diffusa displayed strong α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, reaching 84.82 ± 0.89 µg/mL [91]. These differences indicate that the phytochemical composition of plant extracts used in green synthesis significantly influences the antidiabetic efficacy of Fe2O3 NPs.

Antimicrobial activity

In vitro antibacterial activity

In the current study, the efficiency of the prepared phyto-coated Fe2O3 NPs was evaluated using P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. coli. The whole antibacterial efficiency of the Fe2O3 NPs is summarized in Table 6. Each Fe2O3 NPs dose (200, 100, 50 µg/mL) was applied to P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. coli, and the ZOI was determined. The largest ZOI of 24.0 mm was observed at 200 µg/mL, trailed by 19.0 mm at 100 µg/mL and 15.1 mm at 50 µg/mL for E. coli (Table 5). For P. aeruginosa, the highest ZOI was 22.0 mm at 200 µg/mL, followed by 17.0 mm at 100 µg/mL and 14.2 mm at 50 µg/mL. Against S. aureus, the ZOI was 21 mm at 200 µg/mL, 16 mm at 100 µg/mL, and 14 mm at 50 µg/mL (Table 6). In antibacterial trials, the bacterial membrane acts as the primary protective barrier against external agents.

Fe2O3 exhibits efficient antibacterial activity through multiple mechanisms. One of the prime mechanisms involves the reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, such as superoxide anions (O2⁻), hydroxyl radicals (OH−), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [92]. These ROS induce lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage and protein denaturation. This leads to seepage of intracellular components and ultimately the death of the cell. Furthermore, the nanoscale size and high surface-to-volume ratio of Fe2O3 improve their ability to interact with bacterial cells, facilitating greater ion release, which can further interfere with essential cellular functions and enzyme activity [80]. The cell membrane is the first line of defense in an antibacterial trial, which protects the bacteria from foreign agents. Fe2O3 with a larger surface-to-volume ratio causes more production of more reactive ions. These reactive ions lead to DNA damage, protein lysis, enzyme breakdown, and finally cause bacterial death [93]. Loss of integrity of the cell membrane is considered the main reason for the bactericidal influence of Fe2O3 on bacterial cells [94]. Our results are in good agreement with previous related studies of Jamzad et al. (2020) [83], who worked on the aqueous extract of Laurus nobilis L. leaves. A previous study comparing FeONPs synthesized using the aqueous extract of Borassus flabellifer seed coat showed a 26-mm inhibition zone against Bacillus subtilis [95]. Similarly, an inhibition zone of 22 mm was reported for Bacillus subtilis in studies involving FeONPs synthesized from aqueous extracts of Spinacia oleracea and Musa acuminata [96].

Antifungal efficacy

The antifungal potential of the bioinspired Fe2O3 NPs was evaluated via Petri plates containing PDA (Fig. 10). The maximum inhibition (70 ± 2.1%) was examined at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, followed by 0.75 mg/mL (59 ± 1.4%), 0.50 mg/mL (51.33 ± 0.5%), and 0.25 mg/mL (38.11 ± 0.5%) Increasing the Fe2O3 NPs concentration beyond these doses did not lead to additional inhibition, possibly due to nanoparticle aggregation at higher concentrations. With their smaller size and larger surface area, Fe2O3 NPs are more reactive and stable [97]. Physicochemical factors, including Brownian movement, size, and surface charge, can also inspire particle aggregation. Over the last decade, mycologists have successfully applied Fe2O3 NPs to inhibit the various pathogens (fungi), including Candida albicans, Fusarium graminearum, and Aspergillus niger [98]. It has been proposed that the antifungal potential of phyto-fabricated Fe2O3 NPs is associated with the generation of ROS. Additionally, nanomaterials have been shown to strongly intermingle with microbial cell walls, demonstrating antimicrobial effects [99]. Fe2O3 NPs form apertures in the cell wall, disrupting the plasma membrane’s configuration and causing changes in depolarization, elasticity, fluidity, and absorbency. This allows the Fe2O3 NPs to enter the cytoplasm, where they disrupt DNA, RNA, and proteins [100]. Consequently, the seepage of cellular machinery, including RNA, proteins, DNA, and enzymes, occurs, leading to cell death. Previously, the growth rate reductions of 90.4% for Aspergillus niger, 71.1% for Monilinia fructigena, 68.8% for Botrytis cinerea, and 84.2% for Penicillium expansum [101]. The biogenic IONPs showed a higher antifungal efficiency against the A. alternata OR236467 and A. alternata OR236468 strains at concentrations of 800 ppm compared to the metalaxyl– mancozeb fungicide, with relative growth inhibition percentages of 75.89 and 60.63%, respectively [102].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a successful and sustainable green synthesis of Fe2O3 NPs using Fagonia indica, yielding well-characterized, stable nanostructures with an average size of ~ 65 nm. The phytochemical-mediated fabrication not only ensured effective reduction and stabilization but also enhanced the biological performance of the nanoparticles. The synthesized Fe2O3 NPs exhibited strong, dose-dependent antimicrobial, antileishmanial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antidiabetic activities, alongside notable protein kinase inhibition and excellent biocompatibility (low hemolysis), confirming their multifunctional therapeutic potential. Scientifically, this work highlights the synergistic role of plant-derived phytoconstituents in improving the bioactivity and safety profile of metal oxide nanoparticles, reinforcing green nanotechnology as a viable alternative to conventional synthesis approaches. From a biomedical perspective, the pronounced antimicrobial efficacy against pathogenic bacteria and fungi, combined with antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory properties, underscores their potential application in infectious disease management, oxidative stress-related disorders, and metabolic diseases such as diabetes. However, to advance toward clinical translation, future research should focus on mechanistic studies at the molecular level, in vivo validation of therapeutic efficacy and toxicity, nanoparticle functionalization for targeted drug delivery, and large-scale synthesis optimization. Collectively, these findings position F. indica-mediated Fe2O3 NPs as promising candidates for next-generation nanomedicine and therapeutic interventions.
