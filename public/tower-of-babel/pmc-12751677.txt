The human umbilical cord–mesenchymal stem cell secretome regulates hair growth and cycle transition by promoting methylthioadenosine synthesis via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway
Authors: Yarui Zhang, Xueer Wang, Qimei Chen, Shan Zhao, Lingwei Bu, Shenhua Wu, Jianyuan Huang, Xunhong Xu, Xinyu Yang, Yong Miao, Lin Zhang, Min Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe human umbilical cord (hUC)–mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) secretome (SCT) is a cell-free therapy that may emerge as a novel therapeutic strategy for hair loss prevention. Here, we aimed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which SCT regulates hair growth and cycle transition.MethodsUsing C57BL/6 mice, ex vivo follicles, and cell experiments, we studied the effects and mechanisms of SCT on hair growth and cycling using untargeted metabolomics and phosphoproteomics. A three-month double-blind clinical study was conducted to validate the effects of SCT on human hair.ResultsSCT promotes the telogen-to-anagen transition, hair thickening, and elongation of the vibrissae in mice; regulates dermal papilla cells and hair matrix cells through cysteine and methionine metabolism; and stimulates methylthioadenosine synthesis in hair matrix cells by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Clinical studies demonstrated that SCT increased human hair density and average hair diameter. Scalp physiological tests and subjective feedback indicated no related adverse reactions on the scalp or hair.ConclusionsSCT promoted hair growth, thickening, and the hair follicle cycle via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. This research provides a basis for the application of cell-free alternatives in hair care and hair loss prevention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04806-4.Keywords: Human umbilical cord–mesenchymal stem cell secretome, Hair follicle, Hair cycle, Methylthioadenosine, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway

Background

Hair loss is a global problem that is becoming increasingly common, particularly among younger individuals. However, existing hair loss treatments, which include oral and topical drugs, hair transplants, and emerging platelet-rich plasma, often do not meet patient expectations [1]. Therefore, other anti-hair loss interventions should be explored.

Hair is produced by hair follicles, specialized accessory organs of the skin, which consists of the epithelium and dermis. These follicles exhibit self-renewal and maintain a regular cycle of hair growth, shedding, and regeneration. The hair follicle cycle in the scalp comprises three stages: anagen, catagen, and telogen [2]. During this cycle, the upper one-third of the hair follicle structure remains stable, with only the lower two-thirds, which includes the root, hair bulb (Hb), and dermal papilla at the bottom of the Hb, undergoing cyclic changes. Dermal papilla cells (DPCs) are a type of mesenchymal cell with pluripotent stem cell characteristics [3, 4]. Following the laser ablation of DPCs, hair follicles cannot re-enter the anagen phase [5]. Moreover, DPCs in the hair loss area exhibit a senescent phenotype, in which hair follicles also remain in the telogen phase [6]. These findings indicate that signals from DPCs, which serve as a signal-modulating center [7], can induce hair follicles to transition from the telogen to the anagen phase. In this process, hair follicle stem cells proliferate and differentiate into hair matrix cells (HMCs), which continue to proliferate, migrate, and regenerate the lower two-thirds of the hair follicle structure. During this period, close molecular signal communication between HMCs and DPCs effectively regulates the periodic regeneration of some hair follicle structures [8]. A decreased proliferation ability of HMCs affects the hair cycle, hair regeneration [9], and hair elongation [10]. Thus, maintaining and regulating DPC and HMC activity is of great significance for hair growth and regeneration and in the prevention of hair loss.

Hair-regenerative medicine represents a promising treatment for hair loss. Specifically, the human umbilical cord–mesenchymal stem cell (hUC-MSC)-derived secretome (SCT) is a cell-free therapy that offers a novel approach to the prevention and treatment of hair loss [11]. SCT refers to substances secreted by stem cells in the extracellular space through known or unknown secretion mechanisms under specific conditions and includes soluble proteins, free nucleic acids, lipids, extracellular vesicles, apoptotic bodies, particles, and exosomes. As a treatment for various degenerative diseases, SCT has therapeutic applications for skin wounds, light damage, and hair growth [11]. However, few studies have clarified the mechanism by which SCT affects hair growth and cycle transformation.

Therefore, we investigated the effects of the serum-free cultured hUC-MSC SCT on hair growth; the hair follicle cycle; and the proliferation, migration, and paracrine levels of DPCs and HMCs. Non-targeted metabolomic and phosphoproteomic analyses of a C57BL/6 mouse model of the hair follicle cycle were used to identify, screen, and verify the endogenous metabolites and signaling pathways regulated by SCT. The mechanisms of SCT-induced hair follicle growth and cycle transition were also evaluated. Furthermore, the role of SCT in human hair growth was explored in clinical subjects. This study provides a new basis for the application of the secretome in hair care and hair loss prevention.

Methods

Experimental animals and C57BL/6 mouse model of the hair follicle cycle

Fifteen 7 week-old, specific-pathogen free female and male wild-type C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University (SCXK [Guangdong] 2021–0059) and raised in the Animal Laboratory Department of Southern Medical University (SYXK 2021 [Guangdong] -0167). The approval and registration processes were carried out in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Within this protocol, the quantity of animals was ascertained based on our substantial experience with the models. One week acclimatization period in an open environment with controlled conditions, specifically a temperature of (24 ± 2) °C, a relative humidity of (50 ± 10)%, and a 12 hour light/dark cycle, the mice were individually housed in plastic cages with unrestricted access to food and water supply.

C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with 2% pentobarbital (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA; w/v; 0.01 mL/g body mass). A hair removal cream was used to remove the hair shaft from the back without damaging the hair root. Circles were drawn on the back of each mouse, with different concentrations of SCT (100 μL) applied in the treatment groups and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 100 μL) used in the control group, both of which were applied once daily.

Extraction of mouse vibrissae and ex vivo organ culture

Vibrissae were isolated from seven-week-old C57BL/6 mice [12]. The isolated vibrissae were grouped in Williams E medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10 ng/mL hydrocortisone, 10 mg/mL insulin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U/mL penicillin without phenol red at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3 d in an incubator for grouping treatments.

Hair follicle cell culture and inhibitors

Human DPCs (IH1006) were purchased from WgainBio. Human HMCs (BFN60803943) were purchased from Shanghai Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) high-glucose medium (all from ScienCell) containing 10% fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every 2 d.

Cells were starved under serum-free conditions for 8 h and subsequently treated with the PI3K inhibitor PI-103 (1 μM; Selleck, Houston, TX, USA; Catalog # S1038), AKT inhibitor MK-2206 2HCl (5 μM; Selleck; Catalog # S1078), or mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor rapamycin (1 μM; Selleck; Catalog # S1039) for 2 h.

Statistical analysis of data

Statistical significance was analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. All quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Data between two groups were analyzed using two independent sample t-tests and the paired samples Wilcoxon test, whereas multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance with multiple comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The clinical investigation was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University. Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants (Approval Number: NFEC-2024-323).

All animals used in this study were treated in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University [SMU L202310002]. In accordance with the regulations of the Laboratory Animal Management and Use Committee of Southern Medical University, a comprehensive animal study protocol was formulated prior to the study. This protocol encompasses personnel training, an experimental summary (comprising information regarding the rationale, species, quantity, and operational procedures of the animals employed in the study), the sedation process, and the alleviation of animal pain (details of all anticipated injuries or discomfort that may be inflicted upon the animals and the proposed prevention and control measures). The experiment adheres strictly to the 3R principle.

Results

Isolation and culture of hUC-MSCs for secretome acquisition

After successfully isolating and culturing primary hUC-MSCs using tissue adherence methods [13], we found that primary cells predominantly had a long and spindle-shaped morphology (Figure S1a). Flow cytometry (Figure S1d) showed high expression of MSC surface antigens, CD90, CD44, CD105, and CD73 (99.8%), but low expression of CD34, CD19, CD45, and HLA-DR (0.2%), meeting the criteria for MSCs that exhibited good adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential (Figure S1b, c). In addition, we cultured and observed the morphology of P0–P11 generations of hUC-MSCs, finding that P0–P5 cells were small, spindle-shaped, fully shaped, and uniform in size. When the proliferation reached 95%, the cells grew in a vortexed shape. P6–P11 generation cells became flatter and exhibited an increase in elongated filament antennae (Figure S1e). Conversely, the cell proliferation rate and proliferation density limit were reduced (Figure S2a). Therefore, we collected the secretome of P5-generation hUC-MSCs due to their greater cell-culture yield. The total protein concentration of SCT was 2.548 ± 0.103 mg/mL (n = 10; Figure S2b). Further analysis of SCT metabolites by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) indicated 638 metabolites (Table S1), including tyrosine, phenylalanine, indoline, pyroglutamic acid, nicotinamide, valine, methionine, theophylline, and dl-norleucine. The total ion chromatograms are shown in Figure S2c and d. The number of proteins and peptides, quantified by data-independent acquisition (DIA), was 183 ± 18 and 699 ± 40, respectively (n = 3; Table S2). The higher proteins included albumin, α2-HS-glycoprotein, lumican keratin 10, insulin, serum albumin, apolipoprotein A1, and keratin 1 (Table S1).

SCT promotes the transition from the telogen-to-anagen phase in mouse back skin

First, we used a C57BL/6 mouse model of the hair follicle cycle to explore the optimal concentration of SCT required to regulate back-skin hair follicles from the telogen-to-anagen phase. The treatment groups were treated with 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, or 100% SCT daily, whereas the control group was treated with the same volume of PBS daily (Fig. 1a).

Human umbilical cord–mesenchymal stem cell-derived SCT promotes the telogen to anagen transition in back-skin hair follicles. a Schematic of mouse treatment groups, detection index, and corresponding structure of the hair bulb (Hb) during anagen. b Gross and dermoscopic images of mouse back skin on days 0 and 7. c Stereoscopic microscope images showing the Hb structure of hair follicles on day 7. d Hb number per unit area of skin, n = 8. e Maximum diameter of 15 Hbs per unit area. f Hematoxylin and eosin staining of skin on day 7 (Epi, epidermis; Der, dermis; Sub, subcutaneous tissue; Ske, skeletal muscle; HF, hair follicle). g Proportion of hair follicles in each cycle stage for mice back skin from each group on day 7, n = 30 (Con, control group; 10%, 10%SCT; 30%, 30%SCT; 50%, 50%SCT; 70%, 70%SCT; 100%, 100% SCT). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

Compared to the control group, the back-skin color of mice treated with 50–100% SCT changed to gray and black after 7 d in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1b), indicating that hair follicles had entered the anagen phase. The skin color of control mice remained pink (Fig. 1b), indicating that hair follicles remained in the telogen phase; therefore, the Hb structure could not be seen under the stereomicroscope (Fig. 1c). For hair follicles in the SCT groups that entered the anagen phase, the Hb gradually increased, and melanocytes proliferated to synthesize melanin particles [14]. Moreover, in the 10% SCT treatment group, black dots (indicating hairball structures that had just entered the growth phase) were observed in the skin. With an increase in SCT concentration, the Hb structure gradually expanded and became more visible (Fig. 1c). The Hb number per unit area was substantially higher in the SCT treatment groups (Fig. 1d). With increasing SCT concentration, the number of hair follicles in the anagen phase also increased. Moreover, the maximum follicle diameter gradually increased with increasing SCT concentration (Fig. 1e), suggesting that hair follicles in different SCT groups experienced different stages of the anagen phase. Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that a large portion of hair follicles in the control group was completely within the dermis on day 7, whereas most hair follicles in the SCT groups were deep within the subcutaneous tissue (Fig. 1f). Quantification of hair follicles in the telogen and anagen phases [15] showed that follicles in the 100% SCT treatment group were mainly in anagen stage III to VI, whereas control follicles were mainly in the telogen stage and anagen stage I (Fig. 1g). The hair follicle cycle score differed substantially between the control and treatment groups, suggesting that SCT regulated the regeneration of the lower two-thirds of the hair follicle structure (Table S3).

SCT promotes DPC proliferation, migration, alkaline phosphatase activity, and paracrine levels

To investigate how SCT affected the biological activities of hair follicle cells at the beginning of the anagen phase, we tracked back-skin color and Hb changes in mice on day 3 of treatment. Despite no marked change in skin color (Fig. 2a), more black dots were observed in the 100% SCT group (Fig. 2b, c), suggesting that cells at the Hb site began to proliferate and that SCT-treated hair follicles began to enter the early anagen phase. Ki67+ cells were visible in the Hb of the SCT groups (Fig. 2d), whereas no Ki67+ cells were observed in Hb cells of the control group. This suggests that SCT regulates the proliferation of Hb cells during the telogen-to-anagen transition. After the hair follicle enters the catagen phase, HMC proliferation in the Hb stops, and the proliferative part of the hair follicle gradually degenerates to form an epithelial cell column, which links the Hb and hair follicle in the catagen phase. During the telogen phase, the epithelial cell column becomes further atrophic and shifts upward, whereas the free Hb remains below the epithelial cell column [2]. Thus, the Ki67+ cell population found in the Hb of hair follicles on day 3 of SCT treatment (Fig. 2d) was considered to represent DPCs.

Subsequently, we examined the effect of different SCT concentrations on the proliferation and viability of DPC using CCK8 and EdU assays. The 20% SCT group had the most marked effect on viability and cell proliferation (Fig. 2e, f; Figure S3a–c). Relative to the control group, the cell scratch assay and Transwell experiments showed that the migration ability of DPCs was substantially enhanced after 20% SCT treatment (Fig. 2g–j). The results suggest that DPC viability and migration ability were enhanced after SCT treatment.

Subsequently, we evaluated the ALP activity of DPCs cultured in conventional and SCT-supplemented conventional media (Fig. 2k and l), which is a specific molecular marker of hair follicle activity in DPCs [16] and a key factor that induces hair follicle regeneration and cyclic behavior. This further suggested that SCT promotes hair follicle growth and cell-cycle transition. Furthermore, the conditioned media of both groups were collected for 24 h and analyzed for the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1). VEGF concentration after SCT treatment was higher than that in the conventional culture group (Fig. 2m; Figure S3d, e). Moreover, Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Fig. 2n, o) and SHH/Gli-1 signaling (Fig. 2p, q) were markedly activated in DPCs after SCT treatment.

SCT promotes mouse hair growth and thickening and HMC proliferation and migration

To detect the influence of SCT on mouse hair growth, back skin was divided into two groups: a control group and a 100% SCT group, and the observation time was increased. On day 24, skin on both sides treated with SCT and belonging to the control group was black, with hair growth observed under dermoscopy (Fig. 3a). Forty regenerated hairs were extracted from each group to observe the hair shaft under a microscope. Mouse hair showed an obvious “thin–thick–thin” morphology from root to tail (Fig. 3b). The total hair shaft length and diameter at the thickest part were substantially higher in the SCT-treated group than in the control group (Fig. 3c, d). Moreover, after isolating and extracting mouse vibrissae for hair follicle organ culture in vitro, we found that 30% SCT promoted hair elongation in the cultured mouse vibrissae relative to the control group (Fig. 3e, f; Figure S4a, b).

SCT promotes hair growth, thickening, and HMC proliferation and migration in mice. a Gross and dermoscopic images of mouse back skin on day 24. b Stereoscopic microscope images showing back hair. c Length of mouse back hair, n = 40. d Diameter of mouse back hair, n = 40. e General images of mouse vibrissae cultured in vitro on days 0, 5, and 10. f Relative elongation of mouse vibrissae cultured in vitro, n = 5. g Immunofluorescent staining results of Ki67 in frozen sections of cultured vibrissae hair follicles on day 10 (Hb: hair bulb; Dp: dermal papilla). h EdU staining results of HMC proliferation. i EdU-positive cell rate, n = 8. j and k Cell scratch assay of HMC migration and cell healing rate, n = 4 (Con, control group). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

In frozen sections of vibrissae treated for 10 d, the number of Ki67-positive cells in the Hb of vibrissae was substantially higher in the SCT group (Fig. 3g), suggesting that SCT promoted the proliferation of HMC in vibrissae. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining detected the apoptosis of ex vivo organ cultures of hair follicle cells, which affected hair growth. No apoptotic cells were detected in either group of Hb cells, indicating that the culture system was suitable for the growth of hair follicle organs (Figure S4c). Furthermore, CCK8 and EdU showed that SCT increased HMC proliferation and activity and treatment with 20% SCT had the most marked effect (Fig. 3h, i; Figure S4d–f). The cell scratch experiment showed a gradual reduction in the cell-free area between scratches after 20% SCT treatment compared with controls (Fig. 3j, k). These results suggest that HMC viability and migration were promoted by SCT treatment.

Metabolomics indicates that SCT regulates cysteine and methionine metabolism in mouse skin and hair follicle tissues

The back skin of 7-w-old C57BL/6 mice was divided into two parts: one treated with PBS and the other with 100% SCT. After 7 d, the samples underwent untargeted metabolomic analysis (Fig. 4a). We used partial least squares discrimination analysis for supervised statistical data analysis of both groups to elucidate the metabolic changes. The model evaluation parameters reflected the good fit and predictability of the model (Table S4) [17]. The control and SCT groups exhibited different metabolic characteristics (Fig. 4b). We screened for metabolic differentials based on variable importance of projection (VIP) and p- values, with only those meeting both VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 considered as differential metabolites. The cluster heatmap also showed differences in metabolite concentrations between the control and SCT groups (Fig. 4c). Between the control and SCT groups, we identified 591 differential metabolites, of which 336 were upregulated and 255 were downregulated (Tables S5 and S6).

Metabolomic analysis indicates that SCT regulates cysteine and methionine metabolism as well as the citrate metabolic pathway in mouse skin and hair follicle tissue. a Sample processing and mode in the untargeted metabolomics experiment. b Partial least squares discrimination analysis of positive and negative ions. c Hierarchical clustering results of differential metabolites. d Bar chart of KEGG pathways in the second layer of metabolism. e Boxplots of methylthioadenosine and succinic acid contents in control (ConT) and SCT samples, n = 3. f and g CCK8 results of DPCs (f) and HMCs (g) treated with methylthioadenosine and succinic acid, n = 3. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

To determine the metabolic processes of differential metabolites in the two groups of samples, we performed a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 4d). The results showed changes in β-alanine; cysteine and methionine; purine; glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism; and lysine degradation pathways. We selected the most differential metabolites involved in the top ten pathways (methylthioadenosine, succinic acid [Fig. 4e], pantothenic acid, inosinic acid, betaine, ergothioneine, glutamine, and putrescine [Figure S5a]) and used CCK8 assays to preliminarily verify their regulatory effect on DPC and HMC proliferation. The exogenous addition of methylthioadenosine and succinic acid markedly promoted DPC proliferation (Fig. 4f, Figure S5b), whereas the addition of methylthioadenosine promoted HMC proliferation (Fig. 4g; Figure S5c). Accordingly, we speculated that SCT regulated the synthesis of methylthioadenosine or succinic acid through either the cysteine and methionine or citrate metabolic pathways, thus affecting the biological function of hair follicle cells.

SCT promotes methylthioadenosine synthesis by regulating cysteine and methionine metabolism, which in turn regulates hair follicle cell proliferation and migration

To explore the relationship between the synthesis of methylthioadenosine and succinic acid and SCT regulation of hair follicle cell proliferation and migration, we used N-(3-Aminopropyl) cycloheximide (APCHA), 4-Methylcycloheximide (4MCHA), and dimethyl malonate (DMM) to specifically inhibit the activities of spermine synthase, spermidine synthase, and succinic acid dehydrogenase, respectively, thus inhibiting the synthesis of methylthioadenosine in cysteine and methionine metabolism (Fig. 5a) and the synthesis of succinic acid in the citric acid cycle (Fig. 5b). Methylthioadenosine, succinic acid, and SCT had different effects on DPCs and HMCs. When DPCs were treated with either methylthioadenosine, succinic acid, or SCT, cell proliferation (Fig. 5c, d) and migration (Fig. 5e, f) were substantially enhanced. Subsequently, when the synthesis of methylthioadenosine was inhibited using APCHA and 4MCHA, or when succinic acid synthesis was inhibited using DMM, the increase of DPC proliferation and migration capacity following SCT treatment was considerably lower than that in the group with only SCT treatment. However, when HMCs were treated with methionadenosine or SCT alone, both promoted cell proliferation (Fig. 5g, h) and migration (Fig. 5i, j). When methylthioadenosine synthesis was inhibited using APCHA and 4MCHA, the increase in HMC proliferation and migration following SCT treatment was substantially lower than that in the group with only SCT treatment. Conversely, when HMCs were treated with succinic acid alone, their migration capacity increased but their proliferation capacity did not increase substantially. When succinic acid synthesis was inhibited with DMM, the increase in HMC migration following SCT treatment was substantially lower than with SCT treatment alone. Methylthioadenosine synthesis therefore affected the proliferation and migration of HMCs and DPCs, and inhibiting methylthioadenosine synthesis attenuated the ability of SCT to promote cell proliferation and migration. Thus, SCT may regulate methylthioadenosine synthesis through cysteine and methionine metabolism, which in turn regulates DPC and HMC proliferation and migration.

SCT regulates metabolic biological processes in hair follicle tissue through mTOR

To elucidate the mechanism by which SCT regulates methylthioadenosine synthesis, we selected three groups of samples for phosphorylated proteomic sequencing analysis. In the CONX group, the back skin of 7-w-old C57BL/6 mice was taken directly after hair removal, when hair follicles were completely in the telogen phase. In the CON group, the back skin was treated with PBS for 7 d. In the SCT group, the back skin was treated with 100% SCT for 7 d. The same mice were used for CON and SCT treatments, with different treatments performed on different sides of the back (Fig. 6a). Principal component analysis results of proteomics and phosphoproteomics showed that the CONX, CON, and SCT groups were substantially separated at the proteome and phosphoproteome levels (Fig. 6b), indicating that differences in the obtained proteins and phosphoproteins may better reflect the biological differences between groups. The relatively close clustering of the three samples in each group indicated good repeatability of the experiment. Figure 6c shows a clustered heatmap of the three samples with differentially phosphorylated modification sites. The k-means cluster analysis was performed on all groups of differentially phosphorylated proteins. Among these, the cluster showing a gradual increase in phosphorylation site expression across the three groups—telogen (CONX), CON, and SCT—was selected (Fig. 6d). KEGG enrichment analysis of 3,577 phosphoproteins (Table S7) in this group showed major enrichment in the mTOR pathway (Fig. 6e). Functional annotation and enrichment analyses of phosphorylated proteins showed that the phosphorylation sites were markedly enriched in metabolic biological processes (Fig. 6f), indicating that changes in the phosphorylated proteins were highly correlated with metabolic processes.

SCT regulates methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway

Subsequently, we investigated whether mTOR signaling mediated SCT to promote methylthioadenosine synthesis. First, we found that SCT treatment did not substantially alter methylthioadenosine synthesis in DPCs (Fig. 7a) but substantially increased methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs (Fig. 7b), which suggests that SCT mainly regulates methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs. Second, we found that SCT treatment substantially increased mTOR phosphorylation in HMCs relative to the control group (Fig. 7c and d).

SCT regulates methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. a and b Intracellular methylthioadenosine in DPCs and HMCs detected using ELISA, n = 3. c and d Phosphorylation expression and relative quantification of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR in HMCs, n = 4. e Intracellular methylthioadenosine content in HMCs detected using ELISA, n = 3. f–i Phosphorylation expression and relative quantification of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR in HMCs, n = 3. j Mechanism by which SCT regulates methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

mTOR is an important serine–threonine protein kinase downstream of PI3K/AKT. Therefore, we examined the phosphorylation status of PI3K and AKT, which are upstream signaling molecules of the mTOR signaling pathway. Both PI3K and AKT phosphorylation levels were increased in HMCs after SCT treatment (p < 0.05; Fig. 7c, d). Therefore, SCT is associated with increased methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Indeed, PI-103, MK-2206 2 HCL, and rapamycin, which are inhibitors of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR signaling pathways, respectively [18–20], inhibited the increased methylthioadenosine synthesis induced by SCT in HMCs (Fig. 7e).

Furthermore, western blot results (Fig. 7f–i) showed that PI-103 substantially inhibited the increase in the PI3K phosphorylation level caused by SCT, as well as the phosphorylation level of downstream AKT and mTOR. Similarly, MK-2206 substantially inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT induced by SCT, as well as the phosphorylation level of mTOR. However, rapamycin substantially inhibited the phosphorylation of mTOR induced by SCT. The combined application of PI-103 and MK-2206 reduced the levels of PI3K and AKT phosphorylation caused by SCT and substantially reduced the phosphorylation levels of mTOR. In summary, SCT regulated methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 7g).

SCT increases hair density and diameter in humans

To verify whether SCT affected human hair growth, we screened 129 participants (nine men and ten women) with normal scalp conditions and hair loss with a mean age of 37.47 ± 5.4 years for a three-month, double-blind clinical study. SCT treatment involved total scalp application (4 mL SCT) three times a week throughout the study period. After three months of SCT treatment, 84.21% of all participants (16/19) exhibited moderate or significant improvements (Table S8). The local hair density per square centimeter increased substantially (by 6.96 ± 4.55; Fig. 8b, Table S9), and the average hair diameter also increased substantially by 6.11 ± 3.39 μm (Fig. 8c; Table S9). According to the observed diameters, hair was divided into three types: terminal (> 60 μm), intermediate (30–60 μm), and vellus (< 30 μm) hair. Terminal hair increased substantially (9.6 ± 8.06%; Fig. 8d, Table S9), whereas vellus hair decreased substantially (− 5.90 ± 7.70%) after three months of SCT treatment (Fig. 8e; Table S9). Although intermediate hair decreased (− 3.46 ± 7.95%), the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 8f; Table S9). One participant exhibited a change in overall hair density, with a reduction in scalp exposure at the hair seam (Fig. 8g, h).

Discussion

The most common form of hair loss (alopecia) is attributed to abnormal hair follicle circulation and changes in hair follicle morphology. Thus, maintaining active hair follicle circulation, supporting cytological functions, and promoting hair follicle cycles are key to preventing hair loss. SCT includes the proteins secreted by MSCs, such as conditioned media, exosomes, and other substances [21]. Although the roles and specific mechanisms of various cell-derived exosomes in hair growth and regeneration have been investigated [22], the stem cell secretome has received comparatively less attention. Its mechanisms of action, the effects of smear application on human hair, and any potential adverse reactions have not been fully elucidated.

Therefore, in this study, we utilized a novel, optimized culture system for hUC-MSCs that, in contrast to that for other MSCs [23, 24], comprised a GMP-grade serum-free medium with a fully-defined chemical composition, containing no more than 20 protein components, with contents < 1.5 mg/mL, all derived from artificial reorganization systems without any animal origin. We refined the cell multiplication ability of different generations, obtained secretome from the optimal cells, and conducted a detailed analysis of secretome protein concentration and composition. Of the identified metabolites and proteins, nicotinamide [25], methionine [26], arginine [27], and basement membrane glycan [28] have been reported to promote hair follicle development, growth, and cycle transition. These cytokines and proteins have also been studied in secretomes of other cell sources, including the high expression of IGF, PDGF, bFGF, and Wnt7a in amniotic fluid-derived MSC conditioned medium [23]. These studies illustrate that secretomes containing these metabolites and proteins can effectively regulate hair growth.

However, few studies have detailed the therapeutic mechanism by which SCT regulates the hair follicle cycle and hair growth after percutaneous absorption. Here, we found that SCT promoted DPC proliferation, ALP activity, and paracrine VEGF levels during the telogen-to-anagen phases of hair follicle formation after skin application; SCT also promoted HMC proliferation, downward growth of hair follicles, and hair thickening during the anagen phase. As the “center” of signal communication, DPCs play a regulatory role in the hair follicle cycle. During the transition from telogen to anagen phases [5], DPCs control hair follicle growth in the dermis, bulb size, and maintenance of anagen [29]. When hair regeneration is initiated, DPCs activate adjacent HMCs and regulate cell proliferation. HMCs—a class of pluripotent epithelial cells known as the growth center of the hair—are related to the hair follicle cycle and hair shaft thickness and length [8]. Therefore, we targeted the biological functions of DPCs and HMCs to explore the specific mechanism by which SCT regulates hair growth.

Metabolism is closely related to the development of hair follicles throughout their life cycle. Glycolytic metabolism is essential for activating and maintaining hair follicle stem cells in the mouse bulb [30]. Taurine produced during cysteine and methionine metabolism has protective effects against human hair follicles in vitro [31]. Methionine is required for mouse hair maintenance [32], and disordered citrate metabolism inhibits hair growth [33]. Therefore, we first explored the metabolic changes in skin and hair follicle tissue after SCT treatment. We evaluated the major metabolic pathways regulated by SCT that were enriched using KEGG analysis. Furthermore, we screened the major differential metabolites in the metabolic pathways for verification. The exogenous addition of methylthioadenosine enhanced the proliferative viability of both DPCs and HMCs but was not as effective as SCT. Inhibition of methylthioadenosine synthesis inhibited the proliferation and migration of SCT cells in both cell types. Therefore, we propose that SCT regulates hair growth by regulating methylthioadenosine synthesis through cysteine and methionine metabolism in DPCs and HMCs.

Phosphoproteomic analysis indicated markedly enriched pathways in the mTOR signaling pathway, which is closely related to the hair follicle cycle [34] and hair follicle stem cell stabilization [35]. The mTOR signaling pathway also plays an important role in hair diseases; for example, inhibiting mTOR induces immunosuppression and immune tolerance, helping maintain the immune privilege of hair follicles and relieve alopecia areata [36]. Our findings indicated that SCT regulated the cysteine and methionine metabolic pathways through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, affecting methylthioadenosine synthesis in HMCs and subsequently promoting hair growth and hair follicle cycle transitions. Therefore, HMC metabolic activity plays an important role in regulating DPC proliferation and migration. Although this study provides new insights, some aspects still require further investigation, such as the role of SCT in other hair follicle cell types (e.g., hair follicle stem cells and melanocytes [37]) and whether it regulates cysteine and methionine metabolism in these cells. Although we explored which key components of SCT's complex composition might primarily contribute to these effects, an in-depth analysis was not conducted in this study. Through LC–MS, we analyzed the top nine components (phenylalanine, tyrosine, theophylline, nicotinamide, valine, methionine, L-Tryptophan, thiamine, Remerine, arginine, and proline) of SCT. Upon processing both individual and combined components on DPCs and HMCs, we found that while certain components (e.g., theophylline, nicotinamide, valine, methionine, arginine and proline) or their combinations showed modest proliferative effects (Figure S6), their efficacy remained substantially lower than that of the composite SCT. In future studies, we plan to further investigate the specific functions of individual SCT components to identify novel regulators of hair growth.

Injection therapy is mostly used with newly developed platelet-rich plasma and stem cell-derived exosomes as hair loss treatment, which has limited applicability and causes adverse effects, such as pain. Conditioned media or the stem cell SCT is also typically injected in animal studies, which have shown that the subcutaneous local injection of hypoxia-induced adipose mesenchymal stem cell-conditioned media can promote the proliferation of human DPCs and hair growth [38]. Furthermore, a conditioned medium for subcutaneous local injection of keratinocytes and human DPCs can promote mouse hair growth [39] and hair follicle regeneration [40], respectively. Our animal experiments showed similar results to those achieved by the local application of MSC-conditioned medium from amniotic fluid [23], which accelerated telogen-to-anagen transformation and increased hair density in mice. We also found that SCT promoted hair thickening in mice. Additionally, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of SCT in a population-based proof-of-concept study. After SCT treatment, the local hair density and diameter of the subjects increased; 63.16% and 73.68% exhibited a 5% increase in local hair density and diameter, respectively. We further subdivided hair types and found that 89.47% of subjects had improved proportions of terminal hair, and 84.21% had reduced proportions of vellus hair. Measurements of scalp physiological indices, including oil content, moisture content, barrier function, pH, and elasticity, indicated no adverse effects of SCT treatment (Table S9). Subjective feedback also indicated no related adverse reactions on the scalp or hair. Therefore, according to our proof-of-concept study, SCT is an effective and safe treatment option for maintaining hair health and preventing hair loss in both men and women.

However, several aspects of this clinical trial should be improved through further research. First, the sample size was small. Second, no experiments were performed to confirm the absorption rate of the hUC-MSC SCT applied to mouse and human skin by smearing. However, current studies have reported that the follicular infundibulum with normal pores represents an effective reservoir for the penetration of locally applied substances and its increased surface area is conducive to absorption [41]. Through this route, SCT may penetrate hair follicle tissue and regulate the activity of the hair follicle and surrounding cells. Third, if SCT were used as long-term hair care treatment, would it affect the hair cycle? To address this concern, we are currently conducting animal experiments to evaluate the effects of prolonged SCT use on subsequent hair follicle cycles and hair wave patterns. Meanwhile, follow-up calls with participants who discontinued SCT after three months revealed no increase in hair loss or whitening following cessation. However, this efficacy test constitutes a proof-of-concept study. The sample size is relatively small, the follow-up study period is short, and long-term safety data in humans are lacking. Moreover, some components, such as secretory metabolites, may exert off-target effects. These factors may influence the overall effectiveness of SCT when applied to a larger population.

Conclusions

SCT is a stem cell secretome with high efficiency and safety that can effectively regulate the hair follicle cycle, promote hair growth, and improve hair follicle density and diameter through non-invasive application. SCT provides a safe and simple treatment option for preventing hair loss and maintaining hair health.
