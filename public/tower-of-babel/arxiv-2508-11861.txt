A novel approach to generate distributions
Subhankar Dutta, Roberto Vila and Terezinha K. A. Ribeiro

Abstract




Abstract

A novel approach to adding two additional parameters to a family of distributions for better adaptability has been put forth.
This approach yields a versatile class of distributions supported on the positive real line. We proceed to analyze its mathematical characteristics, such as critical points, modality, stochastic representation, identifiability, quantiles, moments, and truncated moments.
We present a new regression model for unimodal continuous data based on a submodel of the newly proposed family of distributions, in which the distribution of the response variable is reparameterized in terms of the median. We use the maximum likelihood method to estimate the parameters, which was implemented through the

gamlss
package in
R
. The proposed regression model was applied to a real dataset, and its adequacy was validated through quantile residual analysis.

1
Introduction

Experts are becoming more interested in creating novel lifetime distributions to get around complex models as the variety of real-world problems along with applications encompassing complex phenomena grows. As a result, substantial improvements have been achieved in creating a variety of novel distribution classes that will produce more adaptable distributions rather than the traditional ones and offer improved information for modeling. The very first individual to propose a novel distribution by using a baseline probability was
Kumaraswamy, 1980
. After that
Azzalini, 1985
followed with a method for creating novel distributions with modifying a symmetric distribution through the addition of a skewing factor. There are five alternative ways to generate an entirely novel class of distributions. The initial method involves applying differential equations, and the subsequent one involves creating a weighted version of the baseline probability. The third concept is about supplementing the baseline probability with a number of extra parameters required. The discretization of the continuous probability densities is the subsequent strategy. The final strategy is the distribution transforming technique that creates a probabilistic representations of the baseline probability to alter the existing probability distribution models. The resulting connection ought to satisfy the distribution theory postulates.

The final approach for developing an entirely novel type of probability distribution is of relevance to us in this current piece of work. Various generators depending on various quantitative functional connections have been suggested in the existing literature. In this regard,
Mudholkar and Srivastava, 1993
used a positive parameter to exponentiate an existing baseline distribution in order to construct the exponentiated class of probability distributions.
Marshall and Olkin, 1997
added an extra shape parameter to the transforming technique in order to apply it to the survival function.
Eugene et al., 2002
developed the Beta-G class of distributions by using beta distribution as a generating function.
Zografos and Balakrishnan, 2009
proposed novel family of distributions using gamma distribution.
Tahir et al., 2016
suggested a transmuted family of distribution using logistic distribution.
Alizadeh et al., 2017
introduced a generalized family of transmuted distributions.
Aslam et al., 2018
proposed a cubic transmuted family of distributions.

Alzaatreh et al., 2013
recently put up a technique for creating continuous distribution families, known as T-X family of distributions.
Maurya et al., 2016
proposed log transformed family of distributions. Further,
Kumar et al., 2015a
introduced DUS family of distributions. Some researchers established some family of distributions using trigonometric functions as well. For instance,
Kumar et al., 2015b
introduced Sine-g family of distributions.
Bakouch et al., 2018
proposed new exponential trigonometric function to generate family of distributions.
Souza et al., 2019
introduced cosine-G class of distributions. In statistical literature, to find a novel generator of a family of distributions, numerous authors have made extensive modifications. For more details see
Nassar et al., 2019
,
Aslam et al., 2020
,
El-Morshedy and Eliwa, 2019
,
Chakraborty et al., 2022
,
Alsolmi, 2023
,
Atchadé et al., 2023
and
Makubate and Musekwa, 2024
.

The purpose of this study is to increase the flexibility of a particular family of distribution functions by adding one additional parameter. We frame this transformation as the Dutta transformation (DT) method. Since the suggested DT approach is so straightforward, it may be applied to data analysis rather successfully. In this transformation we can replace any distribution as a baseline function to generate new family of distributions. The newly formed special models built on the DT can contend with certain other competing models or the underlying models that are currently in use. In contrast to the currently available models, the models based on the DT can generate adaptable shapes of the density and hazard rate in certain situations.

In many fields, random phenomena are influenced by various factors, making it reasonable to assume that the data observed by the experimenter may not be identically distributed. One approach to handling such scenarios is to employ regression frameworks that model the parameters of the distribution of random phenomena (response variable) as functions of the covariates. To extend and demonstrate the applicability of a submodel generated by the DT approach, we propose a new regression model for unimodal continuous data. In this model, the regression structure is assigned to the distribution parameters, one of which represents the median.
This approach offers flexibility and allows for a direct interpretation of the relationships between response and covariates. The median is a measure of central tendency that is easy to interpret, robust to outliers, and more representative of asymmetric data. Median regression models have attracted considerable attention over the years. A few examples are as follows. The median regression is a special case of regression quantiles proposed by
Koenker and Bassett Jr, 1978
. Median regression models for estimating how covariates affect the median survival time were proposed by
Ying et al., 1995
. Median-dispersion reparameterizations of the Kumaraswamy distribution, which facilitate its use in regression models, were introduced by
Mitnik and Baek, 2013
.
A flexible class of models called power logit regression models, indexed by three parameters—one of which represents the median—was developed by
Queiroz and Ferrari, 2024
.
Bourguignon and Gallardo, 2025
proposed a quantile regression model based on the inverse Gaussian distribution, discussing the special case of median regression and comparing it with the classical mean regression model. Additionally, several probability distributions used in the generalized additive model for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS)
(
Stasinopoulos and Rigby, 2008
;
Stasinopoulos et al., 2017
)
are parameterized in terms of the median, facilitating the interpretation of regression coefficients.

The study is broken down in the following manner. In Section
2
, the new generator called Dutta transformed-G (DT-G) family has been proposed.
In Section
3
, we investigate several mathematical properties of the proposed DT-G model, including critical points, modality, stochastic representation, identifiability, quantiles, moments, and truncated moments.
In Section
4
, a new biparametric distribution, which is a particular case of the DT approach called the Dutta transformed exponential distribution (DTED), is presented.
In Section
5
, we introduce an alternative parameterization of the DTED that allows one of its parameters to represent the median. We then develop a new regression model based on the DTED and discuss parameter estimation and diagnostic methods. In Section
6
, we present an application to real data that illustrates the usefulness of the proposed regression model. Finally, in Section
7
, we provide some concluding remarks.

2
Proposed DT-G model

Similarly, with the goal to offer an entirely novel type of parametric lifetime distribution, the present paper will recommend a family of distributions, called the Dutta family (DF) of distributions. For any arbitrary baseline distribution function
G(x)
, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and probability density function (PDF) of DF distributions can be expressed as

\displaystyle F(x)=G(x)e^{-\bar{G}(x)^{\beta}},~~x>0;\beta>0,

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle f(x)=g(x)\left[1+\beta G(x)\bar{G}(x)^{\beta-1}\right]e^{-\bar{G}(x)^{\beta}},

respectively, where
g(x)
is the pdf of the distribution
G(x)
,
\bar{G}(x)=1-G(x)
and
\beta
is the shape parameter.
A random variable with DT-G distribution will be called DT-G random variable.
It is clear that
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0
. The survival function (SF) and hazard rate function (HRF) can be obtained as

\displaystyle S(t)=1-G(t)e^{-\bar{G}(t)^{\beta}};~~t>0,

and

\displaystyle h(t)=\frac{g(t)\left[1+\beta G(t)\bar{G}(t)^{\beta-1}\right]e^{-\bar{G}(t)^{\beta}}}{1-G(t)e^{-\bar{G}(t)^{\beta}}},

respectively.

3
Some structural properties

In this section some mathematical properties associated with the proposed DT-G model are investigated.

3.1
Critical points and modality

A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle[\log(f(x))]^{\prime}={g^{\prime}(x)\over g(x)}+\beta g(x)[1-G(x)]^{\beta-2}\left\{V(x)+1-G(x)\right\},

where

\displaystyle V(x)\equiv{1-G(x)-(\beta-1)G(x)[1-G(x)]^{-1}\over 1+\beta G(x)[1-G(x)]^{\beta-1}}.

Therefore,
x
is a critical point for
f
if and only if
x
satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle U(x)=\beta g(x)[1-G(x)]^{\beta-2}\left[V(x)+1-G(x)\right],

(3.1)

where we are using the notation

\displaystyle U(x)\equiv-{g^{\prime}(x)\over g^{2}(x)}.

Now let us assume that
\beta\geqslant 2
.
Let
A
be the set of distributions
G
satisfying the conditions:

•

U(x)

is increasing in

x
,

•

V(x)

is decreasing in

x
.

If
G\in A
, then the equation (
3.1
) has a unique solution, denoted by
x_{0}
. In other words,
x_{0}
is the unique critical point for the function
f
. But since
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0
, it follows that
x_{0}
is the unique mode of the density
f
. That is, under the above assumptions, the DT-G model is unimodal.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that the set
A
is non-empty. Just consider
G
as the exponential distribution with unitary rate, and with
\beta=3
, for example.

3.2
Stochastic representation

It is simple to observe that
F
in (
2.1
) can be written as (for
x>0
)

\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{0}^{G(x)}f_{Y}(y){\rm d}y=\mathbb{P}(Y\leqslant G(x)),

where
f_{Y}(y)=1-F_{W}(1-y)+yf_{W}(1-y)
,
0<y<1
, is the PDF of a random variable
Y
, with
W\sim{\rm Weibull}(1,\beta)
, that is,
F_{W}(w)=1-e^{-w^{\beta}}
and
f_{W}(w)=F_{W}^{\prime}(w)
,
w>0
.
Hence, if
X
has CDF
F
, then

\displaystyle F_{X}(x)=F(x)=\mathbb{P}(G^{-1}(Y)\leqslant x),\quad\forall x>0.

In other words,

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G^{-1}(Y),

(3.2)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
means equality in distribution of two random variables.

3.3
Identifiability

Let
\bm{\varrho}
be the parameter vector corresponding to the CDF
G
in (
2.1
).
In this section we will denote
G(x;\bm{\varrho})
instead of
G(x)
, and
F(x;\beta,\bm{\varrho})
instead of
F(x)
.

Suppose the choice of
G
is such that the function
\bm{\varrho}\mapsto G(x;\bm{\varrho})
,
\forall x>0
, is injective.

In what follows we will show that the function
(\beta,\bm{\varrho})\mapsto F(x;\beta,\bm{\varrho})
,
\forall x>0
, is injective.

Indeed, suppose that

\displaystyle F(x;\beta_{1},\bm{\varrho}_{1})=F(x;\beta_{2},\bm{\varrho}_{2}),\quad\forall x>0.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})^{\beta_{1}}}=G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})^{\beta_{2}}},\quad\forall x>0.

(3.3)

First, suppose that
G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})=G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})
,
\forall x>0
. As
\bm{\varrho}\mapsto G(x;\bm{\varrho})
is injective we have
\bm{\varrho}_{1}=\bm{\varrho}_{2}
. Moreover, from the above identity it follows that
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
. And the proof follows.

Second, suppose that
G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})\neq G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})
, for some
x>0
. Without loss of generality let us assume that
G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})<G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})
, for some
x>0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})^{\beta_{1}}}<G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})^{\beta_{1}}}.

As the function
(y,\beta)\mapsto e^{-(1-y)^{\beta}}
is increasing in
y
and in
\beta
separately, the above expression is at most

\displaystyle G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})^{\beta_{2}}}.

In short, we have

\displaystyle G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})^{\beta_{1}}}<G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})e^{-\bar{G}(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})^{\beta_{2}}},

for some
x>0
, which contradicts the initial assumption (
3.3
). Therefore the second case
G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{1})\neq G(x;\bm{\varrho}_{2})
, for some
x>0
, cannot occur. This completes the proof.

The quantile function of

\displaystyle Q_{X}(p)=G^{-1}(Q_{Y}(p)),

3.5
Moments and truncated moments of DT-G random variables

Depending on the choice of the CDF
G
, in this subsection we obtain explicit formulas for moments and truncated moments of a DT-G random variable. To this end, we state and prove the following result which is valid for random variables with arbitrary support contained in the real numbers.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable
and
p>0
be a real number. For all
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
and
\delta>0
, the following identity is satisfied:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}})=\varepsilon^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\varepsilon)-\delta^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\delta)+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X>u){\rm d}u.

Proof.

For all
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
and
\delta>0
, note that the random variable
X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}}
is non-negative and integrable. Then, by using the well-known formula
\mathbb{E}(Z)=p\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(Z>z){\rm d}z
, with
\mathbb{P}(Z>0)=1
and
p>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}})

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}}>u){\rm d}u

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}}>u){\rm d}u+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}}>u){\rm d}u.

(3.4)

Note that

\displaystyle X\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}}>u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X>u\ \text{and}\ \varepsilon<X<\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\max\{u,\varepsilon\}<X<\delta.

Hence, (

) can be written as

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(\max\{u,\varepsilon\}<X<\delta){\rm d}u+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(\max\{u,\varepsilon\}<X<\delta){\rm d}u

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon<X<\delta){\rm d}u+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(u<X<\delta){\rm d}u

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\varepsilon)-\delta^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\delta)+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X>u){\rm d}u.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, if
X
has a DT-G distribution, by (

) and by Proposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}})=\varepsilon^{p}\left[1-G(\varepsilon)e^{-\bar{G}(\varepsilon)^{\beta}}\right]-\delta^{p}\left[1-G(\delta)e^{-\bar{G}(\delta)^{\beta}}\right]+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\left[1-G(u)e^{-\bar{G}(u)^{\beta}}\right]{\rm d}u.

(3.5)

Assuming that
\mathbb{E}(|X|^{p})<\infty
and

•

Letting

, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{X>\varepsilon\}})=\varepsilon^{p}\left[1-G(\varepsilon)e^{-\bar{G}(\varepsilon)^{\beta}}\right]+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}u^{p-1}\left[1-G(u)e^{-\bar{G}(u)^{\beta}}\right]{\rm d}u.

(3.6)

•

Letting
\delta\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p})=p\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{p-1}\left[1-G(u)e^{-\bar{G}(u)^{\beta}}\right]{\rm d}u.

(3.7)

Then, from (

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{p}\left[1-(1-e^{-\lambda\varepsilon})e^{-e^{-\lambda\beta\varepsilon}}\right]-\delta^{p}\left[1-(1-e^{-\lambda\delta})e^{-e^{-\lambda\beta\delta}}\right]

4
DT-exponential distribution

Replacing
G(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}
in (
2.1
), we have obtained a specific sub-model which is called Dutta transformed exponential distribution (DTED) with two parameters
(\beta,\lambda)
. Then the CDF and PDF of DTED are given by

\displaystyle F_{DTED}(x)=(1-e^{-\lambda x})e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda x}},~\mbox{where}~x>0,\beta,\lambda>0;

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle f_{DTED}(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\left[1+\beta(1-e^{-\lambda x})e^{-(\beta-1)\lambda x}\right]e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda x}},

respectively. The survival and hazard rate functions for DTED are given by

\displaystyle S(x)=1-(1-e^{-\lambda x})e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda x}},~\mbox{and}~h(x)=\frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\left[1+\beta(1-e^{-\lambda x})e^{-(\beta-1)\lambda x}\right]e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda x}}}{1-(1-e^{-\lambda x})e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda x}}}.

5
DT-exponential regression model

5.1
Reparametrization

In the formulation of the DT-exponential distribution in Section
4
, the parameters
\beta
and
\lambda
do not represent the mean or any other straightforward characteristic of the distribution and, therefore, lack a direct interpretation. In regression modeling, it is more common to model the mean or median parameter of the distribution directly. Here, we introduce a median-based reparameterization of the DT-exponential distribution.

Let
q_{\tau}=Q_{DTED}(\tau)
,
0<\tau<1
represent the
\tau
-th quantile of DT-exponential distribution. When
\tau=0.5
,
\mu=q_{0.5}
corresponds to the median of the distribution.
From (
4.1
), we have that the quantile
q_{\tau}
satifies:

\displaystyle\tau=(1-e^{-\lambda q_{\tau}})e^{-e^{-\beta\lambda q_{\tau}}}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \beta=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\left[\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-\lambda q_{\tau}}}{\tau}\right)\right)^{1/q_{\tau}}\right].

(5.1)

To achieve a simple regression structure, we will proceed with a different parameterization of the DT-exponential distribution. Let

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\sigma+\log(2)}{\mu},\quad\beta=-\frac{1}{\sigma+\log(2)}\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right),

where
\mu
denotes the median of DT-exponential distribution.
The expression for
\beta
is obtained from equation (
5.1
) with
\tau=0.5
, and the relationship between
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\sigma
was determined such that the reparameterized density of the DTED is well defined.

The CDF e PDF can be written, in
the new parameterization, as

\displaystyle F_{RDTED}(x)=\left(1-\exp\left\{-\frac{(\sigma+\log(2))x}{\mu}\right\}\right)\exp\left\{-\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma+\log(2)))}}{0.5}\right)\right)^{x/\mu}\right\},

(5.2)

where
x>0
,
\mu>0
,
\sigma>0
, and

\displaystyle f_{RDTED}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sigma+\log(2))}{\mu}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\sigma+\log(2))x}{\mu}\right\}\Bigg[1-\frac{1}{\sigma+\log(2)}\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1-\exp\left\{-\frac{(\sigma+\log(2))x}{\mu}\right\}\right)\exp\left\{\frac{(\sigma+\log(2))x}{\mu}\right\}\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)^{\frac{x}{\mu}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)^{\frac{x}{\mu}}\right\}.

We will denote by
X\sim~RDTED(\mu,\sigma)
.

This approach will result in a simpler and more interpretable regression model based on the DT-exponential distribution, with regressions structures assigned to the parameters
\mu
and
\sigma
.

5.2
Regression structure

To establish the regression structure, consider the following. Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
be
n
independent random variables representing
n
response variables, where
Y_{i}\sim{\rm RDTED}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i})
.
The
model is obtained by assuming that
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle g_{\mu}(\mu_{i})={\bm{W}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}w_{ij}\alpha_{j},

\displaystyle g_{\sigma}(\sigma_{i})={\bm{Z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\gamma}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{2}}z_{ij}\gamma_{j},

where
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p_{1}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p_{2}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
are vectors of unknown regression parameters,
{\bm{W}}_{i}=(w_{i1},\ldots,w_{ip_{1}})^{\top}
and
{\bm{Z}}_{i}=(z_{i1},\ldots,z_{ip_{2}})^{\top}
are vectors of covariates of lengths
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
(
p_{1}+p_{2}=p<n
), respectively, which
are assumed fixed and known. In addition,
g_{\mu}(\cdot):(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g_{\sigma}(\cdot):(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are strictly monotonic and twice differentiable link functions.
There are several possible choices for the link functions. For instance,
one can use the identity, inverse, square root, and logarithm link function defined as
g(x)=x
,
g(x)=1/x
,
g(x)=\sqrt{x}
, and
g(x)=\log(x)
, respectively. Since that
\mu>0
, and
\sigma>0
, the logarithm link is the most appropriate, i.e,
g_{\mu}(x)=g_{\sigma}(x)=\log(x)
. In particular, when using the logarithmic link function, each regression coefficient represents the log-relative change in the parameter (
\mu
or
\sigma
) for a one-unit increase in the covariate, holding other variables constant; that is, exponential of the coefficient gives the multiplicative effect on the parameter.

5.3
Inference and diagnostics

The vector of unknown parameters for the RDTED regression model is represented as
{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{\alpha}}^{\top},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
The inference procedure adopted here will be based on maximum likelihood estimation.
The log-likelihood function based on a sample of
n
independent observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\bm{\theta}),

(5.3)

where
\ell_{i}(\bm{\theta})=\log[f_{RDTED}(y_{i})]
with
f_{RDTED}(y_{i})
denoting the PDF of RDTED evaluated in the parameters
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is obtained by maximizing the log-likelihood function in (
5.3
) with respect to
\bm{\theta}
. The MLE for
\bm{\theta}
is denoted by
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
.

The contribution of the
i
-th observation in the log-likelihood function is given by

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{i}+\log(2)}{\mu_{i}}\right)-\frac{(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))y_{i}}{\mu_{i}}+\log\Bigg[1-\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}+\log(2)}\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(1-\exp\left\{-\frac{(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))y_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\right\}\right)\exp\left\{\frac{(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))y_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\right\}\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)^{\frac{y_{i}}{\mu_{i}}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-(\sigma_{i}+\log(2))}}{0.5}\right)\right)^{\frac{y_{i}}{\mu_{i}}}.

As solving the first-order conditions of the log-likelihood function yields a complex system of nonlinear equations, numerical methods are required to compute the maximum likelihood estimate of
\bm{\theta}
. To obtain the estimates, we use
the
gamlss
function in the package with the same name
Stasinopoulos and Rigby, 2008
contained in
R

(
R Core Team, 2025
)
. Such framework considers maximum likelihood estimation procedure based on the Rigby and Stasinopoulos algorithm for the GAMLSS
(
Stasinopoulos and Rigby, 2008
;
Stasinopoulos et al., 2017
)
.
As the
gamlss
function requires initial values to the parameters, we consider
\mu_{i}^{(0)}={\rm med}(y)
and
\sigma_{i}^{(0)}=1
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, with
{\rm med}(y)
denoting the sample median.
The
gamlss
package offers a user-friendly interface for computing estimates and their standard errors, quantile residuals, predictions, and more. Additionally, the
gamlss.ggplots
package
(
Stasinopoulos et al., 2024
)
available in
R
provides a collection of functions for visualizing GAMLSS objects with high-quality and customizable graphics.
Further details on how to implement a new probability distribution as a
gamlss.family
object for use in GAMLSS modeling can be found in
Stasinopoulos et al., 2017
.

To obtain standard errors of the maximum likelihood estimates and to perform hypothesis testing, we will rely on the asymptotic distribution of the maximum likelihood estimators. Under usual regularity conditions for maximum likelihood estimation, when the sample size
n
is large,
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm a}}}{{\sim}}{\rm N}_{p}(\bm{\theta},J^{-1}(\bm{\theta})),
where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm a}}}{{\sim}}
denotes an approximate distribution,
{\rm N}_{p}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the
p
-dimensional normal distribution, and
J^{-1}(\bm{\theta})
is the inverse of the observed information matrix. Based on this, consider testing the hypotheses
H_{0}
:
\theta_{j}=\theta_{j}^{0}
versus
H_{1}
:
\theta_{j}\neq\theta_{j}^{0}
, where
\theta_{j}^{0}
is a specific value for the unknown parameter
\theta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,p
. Under
H_{0}
,
z_{j}=(\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j}^{0})/{\rm se}(\widehat{\theta}_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm a}}}{{\sim}}{\rm N}(0,1),
where
\widehat{\theta}_{j}
is the MLE of
\theta_{j}
, and
{\rm se}(\widehat{\theta}_{j})=\sqrt{J_{jj}}
is the asymptotic standard error of
\widehat{\theta}_{j}
, with
J_{jj}
the
j
-th element of the diagonal of
J^{-1}(\bm{\theta})
. The test statistic
z_{j}
is the square root of Wald’s statistic
(
Wald, 1943
)
, which is widely used to perform hypothesis tests in regression models. In the next section, we will apply this test to evaluate the significance of the parameters in the RDTED regression model, setting
\theta_{j}^{0}=0
.

To assess the goodness of fit of the RDTED regression model, we will use the quantile residual introduced by
Dunn and Smyth, 1996
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle r_{i}=\Phi^{-1}(\widehat{F}_{RDTED}(y_{i})),

where
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)
is the CDF of the standard normal distribution, and
\widehat{F}_{RDTED}(y_{i})
denotes the CDF in (
5.2
) evaluated in
y_{i}
,
\widehat{\mu}_{i}
, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{i}
. If the RDTED regression model is well-fitted,
r_{i}
is approximately distributed as a standard normal distribution.
To evaluate the empirical distribution of quantile residuals within the GAMLSS framework, it is common practice to use normal probability plots (QQ-plots) and worm plots, the latter being a de-trended QQ-plot of the residuals
Buuren and Fredriks, 2001
. These diagnostic plots can be created using the functions
resid_qqplot
and
resid_wp
from the package
gamlss.ggplots
.

7
Concluding remarks

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, it introduces a new generator, referred to as the DT-G family. Second, it investigates several mathematical properties of the proposed DT-G model, including critical points, modality, stochastic representation, identifiability, quantiles, moments, and truncated moments.
Third, it presents a parameterization of the DTED that guarantees the interpretability of parameters in regression models by offering a clear interpretation based on the median response. These models can be used to measure the relationship between variables and to predict new values of the response variable. Fourth, it presents a method for parameter estimation in DTED regression models using maximum likelihood estimation within the GAMLSS framework. Fifth, it illustrates the application of the developed theory to foliage biomass data of small-leaved lime trees, accompanied by its adequacy based on the analysis of quantile residuals, and the direct interpretability of the regression coefficients.
