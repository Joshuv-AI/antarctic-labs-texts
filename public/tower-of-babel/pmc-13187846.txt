How is health equity considered in scale-up guidance? A scoping review of process models for scaling public health innovations
Authors: Sarah Marshall, Ekaterina Frolov, Roberta de Carvalho Corôa, Harriet Koorts, Andrew Milat, Karen Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScale-up guidance is valuable to guiding efforts to increase the reach and impact of public health innovations. Health equity, that is, ‘the absence of systematic disparities in health between groups’ is often a motivator for scale-up though it is unclear how ‘equity’ is considered within existing scale-up guidance. This scoping review aimed to identify the equity considerations articulated within existing scale-up models. Five databases and grey literature were systematically searched. Articles were included if they explicitly developed a model/framework to guide the process of scaling-up public health innovations. Search results were screened, and content analysis was undertaken. Of the 29 scale-up models identified, 24 included mention of equity terms or equity-related characteristics, however there was variation in how equity was conceptualized and the extent to which it was incorporated. Seven domains were developed to describe how equity was considered: (1) Equity acknowledged in the supporting text as an important factor when scaling-up; (2) Equity positioned as a guiding principle or cross-cutting theme; (3) Equity considered in scalable innovation design or intended population; (4) Equity incorporated into scale-up planning and strategy development; (5) Equity assessed within context and environment assessments; (6) Equity factored into resource allocation and workforce planning; (7) Equity considered in stakeholder engagement. There was considerable variation in how equity was considered which offers opportunities for enhancements. Existing and future scale-up models can be strengthened by applying a more consistent equity focus, and providing more comprehensive, actionable guidance for undertaking equitable scaling of public health innovations.Keywords: scale-up, health equity, frameworks, public health innovations

Introduction

Scaling-up effective health innovations is an opportunity to improve the health of populations. The scale-up of innovations is commonly defined as ‘deliberate efforts to increase the impact of successfully tested health innovations so as to benefit more people and to foster policy and programme development on a lasting basis’ (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009, p. 1), where an ‘innovation’ refers to service components, practices or products that are new or perceived as new (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009, p. 1). ‘Population health’ or ‘public health’ refers to an ‘emphasis on actions or activities organised by societies to protect people from disease and to promote their health’ (Baum 2015, p. 666). However, there have been many well-documented barriers to scaling-up public health innovations (Milat et al. 2015, Ben Charif et al. 2017, Zomahoun et al. 2019. de Carvalho Corôa et al. 2023, McCrabb et al. 2023). Barriers include resource limitations and challenges adapting to local contexts, resulting in many effective innovations never being scaled-up in real-world contexts (Milat et al. 2015, McCrabb et al. 2023).

Expanding the reach of an innovation among individuals or beneficiaries is frequently cited as both a key driver and a marker of successful scale-up (WHO 2009, Mangham and Hanson 2010, Woltering et al. 2019). More recently, equitable reach has been highlighted as a core element within definitions of scale-up ‘success’ (Koorts et al. 2025). However, the challenge of ensuring that population-level health interventions effectively reach priority populations who may be at greater risk or face structural barriers to access has long been recognized within the field of public health (Frohlich and Potvin 2008, Marmot et al. 2010).

Health equity is an ethical principle, and while it can be conceptualized in many ways, can be defined as ‘the absence of systematic disparities in health (or in the major social determinants of health) between groups with different levels of underlying social advantage/disadvantage—that is, wealth, power, or prestige’ (Braveman and Gruskin 2003, p. 254). Achieving greater health equity has been recognized as a key challenge in scale-up, as has balancing efficiency with equity trade-offs (i.e. replicating an innovation for broad use versus targeting and tailoring for identified groups with social disadvantage) (Mangham and Hanson 2010, Zomahoun et al. 2019, Weber et al. 2022). While scaled-up innovations aim to benefit entire populations and efforts are frequently motivated by addressing reach, too often, those who could benefit most do not gain the benefits of scaled-up innovations (Flavel et al. 2024).

Equity in scale-up is often framed in terms of reaching underserved populations and ensuring universal access to health interventions, with the aim of achieving equitable health outcomes (Zomahoun et al. 2019). The PROGRESS-Plus framework remains the most widely used tool for conceptualizing equity impacts (Hollands et al. 2024), particularly by highlighting differential impacts across characteristics such as socioeconomic status, geographic location, and other social determinants. While the framework is useful for encouraging attention to population-level disparities, it does not prompt critical reflection on the societal-level drivers of health inequity. Equity must therefore be understood and addressed not only in terms of outcomes or access, but also in relation to imbalances in power, wealth, and resources (Commission on Social Determinants of Health 2008, Marmot et al. 2008). Achieving equity in scale-up may require more transformative approaches, such as redistributing opportunity, resources, and influence, promoting shared governance, and embedding community-led decision-making into systems and policy (McLoughlin and Salmon 2024). However, despite growing efforts to address social determinants of health and promote equity, equity is often not systematically considered in scale-up processes (Zomahoun et al. 2019, Weber et al. 2022).

Process models, sometimes also described as frameworks, are used to guide or describe the process of applying research in real-world practice (Nilsen 2015). For simplicity, hereafter, we refer to both process models and frameworks as ‘models’. Scale-up models are valuable for guiding efforts to increase the reach and impact of health innovations. Guidance to support the practice of scale-up has emerged since the mid-2000s, e.g. the World Health Organization (WHO) ExpandNet framework for scaling up presents scale-up as a system of interconnected components (the innovation, environment, resource team, user organization, and scale-up strategy) and outlines four core principles to guide the process (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009). It is important to note that scale-up process models are part of a subset of a larger body of translation and implementation and/or implementation science frameworks (McKay et al 2019). Scale-up process models provide practical steps for the planning and execution of the scale-up which includes implementation strategies (Nilsen 2015). Importantly, scale-up is often distinguished from implementation science as the former focusses on translating interventions to a population under real world conditions while the latter is focussed on testing and/or optimizing strategies in controlled trials to improve implementation (Lee et al 2024).

While prior studies have explored the incorporation of health equity frameworks and theory to support the equity-focused implementation of health innovations (Kerkhoff et al. 2022, McLoughlin and Martinez 2022, Baumann et al. 2023, Gustafson et al. 2023) none have yet explored this in relation to scale-up. A recent 2024 review of scaling frameworks and models investigated attributes of the framework components broadly; however, the review did not explore equity aspects (Kothari et al. 2024). To address this gap, this review aims to identify how equity and equity-related factors are considered in scale-up process models across the health spectrum.

Materials and methods

Study design

A scoping review was chosen to align with our research aim, to extract, synthesize, and describe data as it occurs in the literature. We took a systematic approach guided by current best-practice recommendations for conducting scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2020). The review protocol and reporting are guided by the Preferred Reporting for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (Tricco et al. 2018). We also consulted the PRISMA extension for equity-focused reviews (PRISMA-E) (Welch et al. 2016) to strengthen our reporting. The protocol was prospectively published on Open Science Framework (Frolov et al. 2024). Ethics approval was not required for this review because it synthesized data from previously published reports and no new data were generated.

Search strategy

A search strategy of academic and grey literature to identify scale-up models was developed in consultation with University of Sydney librarians specializing in health and medicine research. Peer-reviewed literature searches were conducted in August 2024, and the grey literature search was conducted in September 2024. We searched research databases Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), PsycINFO (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), and SCOPUS (Elsevier). The key concepts of ‘scale-up, process model, public health and scalable unit’ were defined (Table 1) and formed the basis of the search strategy. Full search string can be found in Supplementary File 1. Using key terms aligned with those in Table 1, a grey literature web search was conducted using an advanced Google Search. We examined the first 100 hits, following guidance to search the first 5–10 pages (Aromataris et al 2024).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to the search results. The searches were limited to 1990 onwards to ensure coverage of scale-up literature, which gained traction in the early 2000s.

Inclusion criteria

Articles or reports published in English that developed or adapted a model or framework that guides the process of scaling up a public health innovation.

Exclusion criteria

Reference to the concept of scale-up without providing a framework or model, scale-up of initiatives not related to public health, reports of facilitators and/or barriers to scale-up within specific interventions or general experiences of scale-up that did not provide process guidance. We excluded ‘scalability assessment’ tools as these are primarily used to determine the suitability of an innovation for scale, prior to scale-up. Scalability assessment tools are generally complementary but distinct and separate tools.

Study selection

Search results from academic literature were uploaded to Covidence systematic review management software (https://www.covidence.org), where duplicates were removed. The title and abstract screening were completed by two reviewers (pairing of K.L., S.M., R.D., A.M., E.F.) with disagreements resolved by a third independent reviewer (E.F., S.M., or A.M.). Full-text screening was completed by two reviewers (E.F., S.M.) with discrepancies resolved by a third independent reviewer (K.L.). The grey literature search conducted in Google was exported as an Excel document (E.F.). Two reviewers independently screened grey literature titles, full texts, and resolved conflicts through discussion (E.F., S.M.). When multiple editions or versions of an identical record were available, only the most recent version was included.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a template in Excel developed for this review. To summarize the characteristics of the identified models, we extracted the following data: primary author and year, model name, focus area within public health, context and process of model development/adaptation, publication format (research article or grey literature user guide), and a brief summary of the model.

Focusing on equity, we extracted any excerpts of text in the reports that included (i) equity terms, (ii) equity-related characteristics, or (iii) a definition of equity. Equity terms included: equity/inequity, unfair/unjust, injustice, equality/inequality, social determinants of health, disparity, and disadvantaged or vulnerable. These terms were informed by the authors’ expertise and Braveman’s work in defining health equity (Braveman 2016). This approach enabled the identification of steps that were explicitly framed as equity-related within the scale-up models, rather than relying on subjective interpretation of potential health equity concepts.

Broad terms related to intended populations but not explicitly related to equity were not coded, such as ‘target audience’, ‘those in need of the intervention’ or ‘intended users’.

Equity-related characteristics were defined using PROGRESS Plus, a well-known framework for globally recognized characteristics that can contribute to inequities or unfair and unjust differences (O'Neill et al. 2014, Cochrane Methods 2025). PROGRESS Plus characteristics include place of residence, race/culture, occupation, gender/sex, religion, education, socioeconomic status, and social capital. The ‘Plus’ expands characteristics to include temporal, relational, and physiological characteristics contributing to inequity. General terms not explicitly aligned to equity-related characteristics, such as ‘social and political context’ or ‘local considerations’, were not coded.

We also extracted any definition of equity provided in the text. During data extraction, we also categorized where the data was extracted from in the report: (i) within the supporting text (e.g. pre-amble, background, discussion), (ii) within the steps, dimensions or explanation of the model itself.

Data analysis

To investigate equity considerations within the identified models, a qualitative content analysis was undertaken (Elo and Kyngäs 2008) of (i) content ‘within’ the models themselves and (ii) ‘supporting text’ (e.g. Introduction and Discussion sections) included in the published reports. Content analysis aligns with the aim of this research as the approach enables a structured, replicable analysis to identify patterns in the data based on previous knowledge related to equity. Our steps included: (1) familiarization with the included reports (the data); (2) development of a codebook informed by equity literature [i.e. the Health Equity Framework (Peterson et al. 2021), the Equity-based framework for Implementation Research (Eslava-Schmalbach et al. 2019), and the Health Equity Implementation Framework (Woodward et al. 2019)]; (3) coding the data deductively according to the codebook and inductively as new codes were identified in the data; (4) development of domains (higher-level categories) to represent the coded data. This process was undertaken by EF in consultation with S.M. and K.L. S.M. then cross-checked and further coded of all reports using NVivo software for data management. The domains and corresponding coding were cross-checked independently by K.L. The domain labels, descriptions and illustrative quotes were finalized in consultation with the whole author team.

Results

Search results

Our systematic search resulted in 32 reports representing 29 scale-up models (Fig. 1). These reports were from research literature (n = 16), grey literature (n = 7) and previous literature reviews (Lee 2021) (n = 6) and (Kothari et al. 2024) (n = 3).

PRISMA flow diagram of search results.

Summary characteristics of identified scale-up models

The summary characteristics of the identified scale-up models are presented in Supplementary File 2. The models were developed for a range of focus areas in health; the majority related to women’s and children’s health topics (n = 10). Most models were developed from low- to middle-income country contexts (n = 22) and first published prior to 2020 (n = 25). Most reports were research articles (n = 24), six were user guides identified as grey literature (typically longer documents with more detailed advice). The model by Milat et al. was both an original research article (Milat et al. 2016) and a more detailed user guide adopted by a government health department (Centre for Epidemiology and Evidence 2023). The model from Management Systems International comprised a framework (Cooley et al. 2020) and companion toolkit guide (Cooley 2021). Two WHO ExpandNet framework resources that offer guidance for scaling were included [‘Nine steps for developing a scaling-up strategy’ (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2010) and ‘Practical guidance for scaling up health service innovations’ (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009)].

Equity-related data extracted from scale-up models

Twenty-four of the 29 scale-up models (83%) included a combination of either equity terms, PROGRESS Plus characteristics, or a definition of equity (Table 2). Specifically, 16 models included mention of equity terms, 22 models mentioned equity-related characteristics defined by the PROGRESS Plus framework, and two models included a definition of equity (Table 2). The main PROGRESS plus characteristics referred to were socioeconomic status, race/culture, and place of residence. Four models were developed based on scaling up innovations among populations differentiated by a PROGRESS Plus characteristics. For example, three models reflected the PROGRESS Plus factor ‘a personal characteristic associated with discrimination’; two models were developed for populations affected by HIV/AIDS (human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) and one model was developed for women with complications of unsafe abortion (refer Table 2). A fourth model reflected the PROGRESS Plus factor ‘socioeconomic status’, as it was developed based on ‘scale-up of innovative maternal and newborn health interventions targeting mothers and newborns within poor, vulnerable populations’ (Table 2).

Equity domains describing how equity was considered in the models and published reports

From the content analysis, discrete domains were developed to describe how equity was considered in the included scale-up model reports (e.g. Introduction and Discussion sections). One domain was specific to equity-related data extracted from the supporting text of identified models (n = 13 models). Six domains were derived from equity-related data extracted from the steps, dimensions or explanation of the model itself (n = 23 models). These domains are summarized in Table 3 and presented in the text with illustrative quotes. Report supporting text

Domain 1: Equity acknowledged within the supporting text as an important factor when scaling up

Thirteen scale-up models included acknowledged or highlighted equity in the supporting text (e.g. background, discussion) as an important factor or challenge to address. This was clearly expressed by some, e.g. ‘As countries push to achieve Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) 4 and 5, increasing attention is being paid to the equitable scale-up of proven high-impact interventions for reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health’ (Smith et al. 2015, p. S4). Uniquely, Hirschhorn et al. (2013, p. 6) included in the report discussion that ‘the vision for scaled programs is increasingly focused on transition to the public sector as the main pathway to sustainability, an emphasis on equity (i.e. a focus on ensuring access that includes the most vulnerable) needs to be an increasingly important driver of decisions and delivery models to ensure that this sector is able to fulfill the role of serving the entire population’ and proposed a future additional equity domain (‘emphasizing equity’) for their model, but did not describe this in their model dimensions or steps.

Model dimension/steps

Domain 2: Equity positioned as a guiding principle or cross-cutting theme

Three models referred to equity as a guiding principle or cross-cutting theme underpinning the scale-up effort. Bryce and colleagues include ‘reducing inequities’ as a cross-cutting theme in their model: ‘The conceptual model (Fig. 1) reﬂects the importance of addressing equity across the entire pathway from inputs to impact, and the need to stratify outcomes (coverage) and impact results by population subgroups’ (Bryce et al. 2011, p. 143). While not presented in the model depiction, the models from World Health Organization ExpandNet (2010) and Bergh et al. (2015) clearly state equity as a guiding principle or cross-cutting component of their models and underpinning any scale-up effort.

Domain 3: Equity considered in scalable innovation design or intended population

Ten models articulated equity aspects as an attribute of the scaled up innovation’s intended population. Of note, Pelletier and colleagues explicitly included this as a discrete step in their model (Step 3: Reaching the most vulnerable): ‘Identifying the most vulnerable and hard-to-reach groups, their special situations and needs, and the additional contact points, strategies, and resources needed to reach them’ (Pelletier et al. 2011, p. 2087). The other 10 models articulate the need to consider equity in design or the intended population of the innovation within a scale-up step or dimension. Koorts and colleagues, e.g. pose direct questions to prompt thinking about the intervention intended recipients, ‘How will you ensure equity of access for disadvantaged subgroups?’ (Koorts et al. 2018, p. 5). Of the ten scale-up models coded for this domain, all prompt for considering equity in the innovation design and intended populations at the outset of the scale-up process, and one model referred specifically to adapting the innovation to address ‘different geographical, socioeconomic and cultural contexts’ (Spicer et al. 2014, p. 32).

Domain 4: Equity incorporated into scale-up planning and strategy development

Consideration of equity within scale-up planning, i.e. considering scale-up pathways and organizational roles was articulated by six models. This was clearly conveyed by the WHO ExpandNet model: ‘Rapid expansion can often result in a loss of essential components of the innovation. Those lost are often the components most diﬀerent from prevailing practices and most diﬃcult to implement, for example interventions to promote quality of care or values such as equity and gender perspectives. A more gradual, phased process allows learning about how sustainable expansion of all aspects of the innovation can be attained’ (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2010, p. 27). Pelletier et al. (2011, p. 2087) gave more specific action in relation to the goals for scaling ‘integrate equity into the goal statement [of the intervention delivered at scale].’

Domain 5: Equity assessed within context and environment assessments

Equity considerations were included as a factor to understand the context and environment where scaled-up innovations will be implemented, or broader contexts that interact with the innovation by seven models. Edwards clearly articulate this as an explicit dimension in their model: ‘The first axis of implementation has proximal and socio-structural determinants as its end points. Proximal determinants are individual characteristics such as socioeconomic status, household income, or education level. Socio-structural determinants reflect embedded social conditions such as class, stigma, or discrimination, and power relationships’ (Edwards and Barker 2014, p. S157). The other five models incorporate this into a model step or dimension, e.g. ‘Little is known about how maternal, household, and community contexts shape intervention effectiveness, however, and the need to invest more research resources in identifying these contextual issues is recognized (19). Successful interventions usually explicitly consider the cultural and household context and develop tailored strategies to overcome potential barriers’ (Gillespie et al. 2015, p. 445).

Domain 6: Equity factored into financing, resource allocation and workforce planning

Four models considered financing, resource allocation and workforce planning in relation to achieving equity. Bergh et al. (2015) and Gillespie et al. (2015) discuss financial aspects: ‘Balancing the greater cost of outreach to very remote areas (which are also likely to have higher levels of undernutrition) with that of reaching more accessible communities represents another form of trade-off, highlighting the need to link equity with discussions of scale-up’ (Gillespie et al. 2015, p. 447). Whereas Ploeg and WHO include that equity aspects need to be reflected within the members of the ‘resource team’ to promote and facilitate wider adoption of the innovation (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009, Ploeg et al. 2014).

Domain 7: Equity considered in stakeholder engagement

Five models considered equity in stakeholder engagement related to the scale-up process and presented this in varying ways. The models from Bradley (Bradley et al. 2012) and Yamey (2011) provided an example of equity-informed stakeholder engagement, Koorts presented a prompt question to consider ‘how will you engage communities with disadvantaged subgroups?’ (Koorts et al. 2018, p. 5), and Gillespie emphasize engagement across all levels, including populations differentiated by an equity-related characteristic, as an integral part of scale-up (Gillespie et al. 2015). The fifth model, from Weber, indicated that they ‘identiﬁed ‘encourage participation and ownership’ as an important key component that contributes to the development of partnerships and the acceptance of initiatives for scaling up community-based health promotion with a focus on health equity’ (Weber et al. 2022, p. 15), however they did not articulate this as such in the description of their model dimension. No models provided instruction about how to engage stakeholders in relation to achieving equity.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a systematic examination of how equity and/or equity-related factors have been considered in public health scale-up models. Most of the models (83%) identified in this review included equity terms or equity-related characteristics. This is a promising finding, as most of the models identified in this review were published from 2007, with the majority published around 2015–20; indicating the historical and current attention that equity requires in scaling public health innovations. Also, positively, equity was embedded in some capacity as a step or dimension in 79% of included models. However, while we identified examples of clear positioning of equity in scale-up guidance, there was variation in how equity in scale-up was defined and in the extent to which it was incorporated into the models.

Equity in scalable innovation design and intended population

Equity considerations in the design of scalable innovations and the intended populations was a commonly coded domain, appearing in ten models. This is not unsurprising as equity in scale-up, and indeed success in scale-up, is often framed in terms of expanding reach, including among underserved populations and ensuring universal access to health interventions (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2009, Mangham and Hanson 2010, Woltering et al. 2019, Koorts et al. 2025). Even prior to commencing the scale-up process, the innovation design and intended population are key factors in assessing the scalability of an innovation (i.e. ‘the ability of a health intervention shown to be efficacious on a small scale and or under controlled conditions to be expanded under real-world conditions to reach a greater proportion of the eligible population, while retaining effectiveness’ (Milat et al. 2013 pp. 285, 289). One model used question prompts to actively encourage users to consider this (e.g. Are there subgroups that experience disparities? How will you ensure equity of access for disadvantaged subgroups?’) (Koorts et al. 2018, p. 5). The need to consider equity in the innovation design and intended population is essential for mitigating the possible pitfall of some people being left out and therefore not benefiting from the scaled innovation (Zomahoun et al. 2019). Further, it strives to ensure that scale-up does not inadvertently widen disparities but instead uses an appropriate innovation to target groups with the greatest need.

Equity in scale-up strategy and context assessments

Scale-up planning and strategy development, along with context and environmental assessments, were identified across several models as key avenues for advancing health equity. These steps are critical for embedding equity considerations from the outset of the scale-up process (World Health Organization ExpandNet 2010, Milat et al. 2015). For example, defining clear scale-up goals and pathways is an important initial step that will shape the subsequent pursuit of equity-related outcomes during scaling. Only one model in this review explicitly stated in their guidance that equity should be integrated into the goal statement (Pelletier et al. 2011). Similarly, conducting context assessments (e.g. local social, cultural, political, economic, and organizational factors) and the broader environment assessments (e.g. policy landscape, governance structures, and system capacity) is essential for understanding the conditions under which scale-up will be effective and sustainable (Koorts et al. 2022). When these assessments are undertaken through an equity lens, they can also help to identify equity gaps and inform equity-focused scale-up (Ganakas et al. 2025). Importantly, these assessments can encourage consideration of broader determinants of equity that shape equity outcomes, such as power relations and discrimination, as specified by a model in this review (Edwards and Barker 2014).

Equity in scale-up resource allocation

Resourcing and costs are a common issue raised in pursuing an equitable, inclusive approach versus standard one-size-fits-all scaling of health innovations (Mangham and Hanson 2010, Zomahoun et al. 2019), however financing, resource allocation and workforce planning related to achieving equity were discussed in only five models identified in this review. Consistent with previous research that has shown lack of or inconsistent reporting of scaling costs (Johns and Baltussen 2004, Brown et al. 2020) no models discussed the cost-benefits of scaling-up equitable health innovations, which could assist with the allocation of resources and design of the innovation. Scale-up assumes extended reach or coverage will align with lower average costs, however it is likely that an equitable, inclusive approach to reach the most disadvantaged populations will have greater financial costs (Carter and Remme 2018). In a recent scale-up umbrella review of health and social care, de Carvalho Corôa et al. (2023) discussed that even when scale-up is perceived as an opportunity to reduce costs and target inequities by policymakers, a priori costs and measures were considered less in reviews of scale-up studies, along with equity. The economic aspects of scaling preventive health initiatives require considerable development generally (Bankolé et al 2025), and there is opportunity for equity to be embedded in future advancements.

Equity in community involvement throughout scale-up

Several models identified in this review recommended community involvement as a key strategy for integrating equity into the scale-up process. Community participation, including engaging end-users and diverse interest-holders, in the planning, implementation, and monitoring of innovations was recognized as a critical factor for successful scale-up (Yamey 2011, Milat et al. 2015, Bulthuis et al. 2020, Bottorff et al. 2021). Community-oriented approaches and interest-holder engagement have great potential for promoting health equity and tackling the underlying determinants (Gilbert et al. 2023, Shelton and Brownson 2024). There has been increasing attention to the importance of consumer engagement in healthcare research, implementation and practice (Wiles et al. 2022, Crane et al. 2023), and there is an opportunity to further develop and articulate this in scale-up models and action.

Strengths and limitations

Our systematic approach to this scoping review resulted in a comprehensive summary of scale-up models. Our structured analysis, involving multiple coders and cross-checking via discussion and independently, as well as team involvement in domain development, strengthened the methodological rigour and trustworthiness of our results (Leung 2015). There are several limitations to note. First, as equity can be conceptualized in many ways, we attempted to overcome this using common equity-related terms and equity-related characteristics to identify data for analysis. In doing so, we adopted a semantic approach to analysis, and as a result, we may have overlooked concepts that address equity abstractly. Second, it is possible that the models identified did incorporate equity considerations during development; however, they did not articulate this in the published papers and therefore were not coded and analysed in this study. Aligned with this, it is possible that equity considerations may be embedded or implicitly assumed in some scale-up models, particularly models developed from lower- and middle-income (LMIC) country contexts. For example, the well-known framework from Yamey was developed for LMIC contexts and is suggestive of equity considerations throughout however does not articulate equity terms coded in this review (Yamey 2011). Similarly, models that mentioned the importance of physical, social, and political environment, while important for considering equity, were not explicitly related to addressing equity or paired with equity-related terms. Third, while multiple independent reviewers undertook screening, it is possible that scale-up models may have been missed if all four search concepts were not included in the title and abstract. Furthermore, we did not search books or other publication formats that could identify additional models, as this was outside the scope of this research.

Future research

As a next step from this review, there is an opportunity for existing and future scale-up models to more fully integrate direct equity-focused actions across all stages of scale-up processes. This aligns with calls to apply a ‘health-equity lens’ and integrate health equity frameworks to achieve equity-focused scale-up (McKay et al. 2024, McLoughlin and Salmon 2024). An example of this in implementation science is from Fort et al. (2023), who developed a context-specific equity approach to the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model for translating research into practice. While we recognize that in practice many practitioners use implementation frameworks for scaling, there are nuances in scale-up that make it a different process from implementation alone and require distinct guidance (Lee et al. 2024). We also value the increased inclusion of equity in implementation and implementation frameworks (McLoughlin and Martinez 2022, Baumann et al. 2023, Gustafson et al. 2024, Shelton and Brownson 2024) and therefore advocate for scale-up models to be updated in line with the advances in equitable implementation science. This study provides a nomenclature and methods to examine of how equity considerations are articulated in health models and frameworks, that could be applied in other areas of public health practice.

Additionally, as a next step from this review, further research could investigate how equity is addressed, operationalized and evaluated during scale-up in practice when applying models that embed equity concepts or steps, and whether their application contributes to reducing health inequities. Exploring the application of these models from an equity perspective in practice may uncover additional insights and guidance for addressing equity more effectively during scale-up. Successful scaling examples such as immunization programs in LMIC country contexts (Shattock et al. 2024) are likely to offer learning opportunities that may be used to improve models for equitable scale-up.

Conclusion

Though equity has been considered to some extent in most of public health scale-up models to date, there is variation in how it has been considered and incorporated. Based on the findings of this review, there is an opportunity to enhance existing scale-up models to offer more comprehensive and consistent guidance on considering equity throughout scale-up processes. Recent advancements in equity within implementation science present a valuable opportunity to inform and advance equity-oriented scale-up guidance. Strengthening the equity focus of scale-up guidance has the potential to enhance efforts to reduce health inequities when scaling public health innovations.
