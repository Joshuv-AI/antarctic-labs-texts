Family screening for genetic cardiomyopathies in ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents: a joint clinical and genetic approach
Authors: Mark Abela, Jessica Debattista, Jeanesse Scerri, Tiziana Felice, Victor Grech, Christian Scerri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adolescents may present with abnormal ECG findings despite having no structural or electrical evidence of cardiomyopathy. The value of extending family screening to this subgroup remains uncertain. To evaluate the impact of family screening in ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents, and determine whether a joint clinical-genetic approach improves the diagnostic yield. In this prospective cohort study, adolescents with an abnormal ECG identified through a nationwide screening program underwent comprehensive clinical/genetic evaluation. Phenotype-negative individuals were included. Relatives underwent clinical screening, with cascade genetic testing offered when probands carried a likely/definite pathogenic (LP/P) cardiomyopathy variant. Abnormal screening outcomes included relatives with (i) de novo cardiomyopathy diagnosis, (ii) early cardiomyopathic traits, or (iii) gene-positive/phenotype-negative (G + P −) status. Sixteen adolescents (0.6%) met inclusion criteria, and 3 (18.8%) were G + (MYBPC3, MYH7, ACTC1). One in five relatives (n = 13/71, 18.3%; 95% CI 11.0–28.9%) were referred, most having an abnormal ECG (12.7%). Nine relatives (12.7%; 95% CI 4.9–20.4%) exhibited early cardiomyopathy traits at baseline. One (1.4%) relative was G + /P − . Cardiomyopathy was diagnosed in 3 (4.2%, 95% CI 0.9–11.8%) relatives at baseline (n = 2 HCM, n = 1 DCM). One relative with early cardiomyopathy traits underwent phenoconversion (NDLVC) during follow-up at 77 months, yielding an overall diagnostic rate of 5.6% (95% CI 2.0–13.8%) in 4 unrelated families. A joint clinical-genetic approach enabled personalized surveillance in 6 relatives (8.5%), including 2 (2.8%) diagnoses that would otherwise have been missed with conventional clinical screening.Conclusion: One in five relatives of ECG‑positive phenotype‑negative adolescents received a cardiomyopathy diagnosis or had early cardiomyopathy traits. A joint clinical-genetic approach in this subgroup may aid early cardiomyopathy detection in selected families, though findings remain exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. What is Known – What is New:•Resting ECG can identify individuals harboring conditions linked to sudden cardiac death•The penetrance of cardiomyopathy is age dependent, which explains why screened adolescents may exhibit abnormal ECG patterns in the absence of a cardiomyopathy diagnosis•Our data indicate that extending screening to relatives of ECG-positive, phenotype-negative adolescents is justified, with one in five relatives receiving a new cardiomyopathy diagnosis (5.6%) or exhibiting early cardiomyopathy traits (11.3%)•A joint clinical/genetic evaluation in this context may help personalize screening strategies

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young individuals is a tragic unexpected event that provokes profound emotional stress for the victim’s loved ones. Up to 10% of all sudden deaths in the UK occur in adolescent individuals, with sudden arrhythmic death syndrome and myocardial disorders being the most common conditions diagnosed at autopsy [1]. There are no prewarning symptoms or a relevant family history in up to 80% of cases [2, 3]. Most deaths are preventable, with the pathologies linked to SCD often identified during cardiac screening with a resting 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) [4]. Several groups have reported an increased risk of SCD during elite sports [5–8]. This has led to most sporting bodies mandating ECG-based cardiac screening in their athletes [9].

The benefits of such an approach in athletes have paved the way to extending cardiac screening programs to non-athletes [10, 11]. Screening younger individuals however has caveats. Pre-clinical phenotypes in this age bracket are frequently encountered, and phenotypic markers of disease are often subtle and non-diagnostic [12]. False negatives using this approach are also inevitable, as disease penetrance, notably in cardiomyopathies, is age dependent. These screening caveats were experienced first-hand in a nationwide cardiac screening program for adolescents, with most individuals with an abnormal ECG having no clinical diagnosis [11]. Repeated screening in young athletic individuals has however been shown to improve the diagnostic yield [13], highlighting the importance of surveillance in grey zone cases.

Cardiomyopathy is a leading cause of SCD, hence why family screening is one of the key pillars in the management of these families. Guidelines currently advocate pursuing family screening and genetic testing in individuals fulfilling criteria for a hereditary disorder [12, 14]. This extended familial evaluation better informs physician of disease expressivity in families, and also helps diagnose other affected individuals [15–17]. Phenotype and diagnosis are often used interchangeably in consensus documents. Phenotype is actually from the Greek word “phainein” meaning “appear” and “typos” meaning “type,” referring to morphological traits of a disease. Criteria for diagnosis are often very stringent, following a binary metric. Disease expression in cardiomyopathy is however highly heterogenous, with ECG anomalies, and morphofunctional or electrical abnormalities frequently reported in cardiomyopathy gene carriers, even though they may not be fulfilling diagnostic criteria for cardiomyopathy [18–24]. Data emerging from genetic repositories has reinvigorated interest in precision medicine, with some groups now advocating for a genotype-first approach [18, 25]. Some have floated the idea that a combined clinical and genetic evaluation may be reasonable in individuals harboring non-diagnostic clinical phenotypes [26]. A gene-positive (G +) status is also an important predictor for early onset and more aggressive disease [17, 25, 27, 28]. Data from the UK biobank has also shown that 1 in 6 adults with putative pathogenic variants in dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) genes exhibited early DCM features on ECG or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), abnormalities which in isolation may not necessarily be diagnostic [29]. Most cardiomyopathies present in the 3rd or 4th decades of life, which is why the penetrance of cardiomyopathy for most genetic variants is low in children and adolescents [30]. There is also minimal and infrequent progression to an overt phenotype in the short term [31–36]. The penetrance is however higher in families with a genetic variant compared to gene elusive families [32, 35]. Naturally, not all G + _ individuals harboring a LP/P variant end up with a diagnosis [19, 37–39], which is why one should exercise caution when counseling gene carriers. The ramifications of a positive genetic result can undoubtedly led to significant emotional disturbances, alongside potential future impacts on career prospects, financial stability, exercise routines, and family planning [18, 40].

Family screening has traditionally not been adopted in individuals harboring pre-clinical phenotypes. Early detection and management of asymptomatic young individuals which may potentially be harboring Mendelian cardiovascular disease through screening may on the other hand improve outcomes. This may help determine the most appropriate surveillance and treatment strategy, while also better informs clinicians and families on the potential risks [41].

Despite increasing recognition of early phenotypic markers and the potential value of genetic testing, there remains limited evidence to guide family screening in adolescents who present with an abnormal ECG but do not meet diagnostic criteria. To address this knowledge gap, the main objective of this study was to explore the diagnostic yield of a family screening program in ECG-positive phenotype-negative probands, potentially suggestive of pre-clinical cardiomyopathy. A secondary aim was to determine whether incorporating genetic testing in these probands led to an improved overall diagnostic rate among their family members.

Methodology

This was a single-center, prospective cohort study [11]. Adolescents were systematically invited to undergo cardiac screening as part of a nationwide cardiac screening program (BEAT-IT) in 2017–2018) [11]. The screening protocol consisted of a health questionnaire and resting 12-lead ECG. Individuals who gave consent (including parental consent) were enroled into the screening program. The health questionnaire and ECG were reviewed by a physician on site during school hours. The international criteria for ECG interpretation were used for reporting ECG [42]. All ECG were reviewed by two independent cardiologists. Adolescents with an abnormal health questionnaire and/or an abnormal ECG were referred for comprehensive clinical evaluation, as previously published [11]. For the purpose of this study, adolescents with an abnormal ECG suggestive of cardiomyopathy, in the absence of a clinical diagnosis (pre-clinical phenotype), were included. The principal investigator informed these adolescents of the possibility that their relatives could undergo family screening. Relatives who wished to be evaluated were asked to contact the research team directly. Those with an abnormal screen underwent a multi-modality assessment (next section).

An abnormal evaluation in screened relatives included those with (a) a definite cardiomyopathy diagnosis fulfilling guideline criteria [12, 14, 43–45]; (b) early cardiomyopathy traits (Fig. 1) requiring surveillance (pathological ECG patterns, morphofunctional anomalies on cardiac imaging, and/or abnormalities during cardiopulmonary exercise testing [CPET]); and (c) and gene-positive phenotype-negative (G + /P −) harboring a LP/P cardiomyopathy variant in the absence of early cardiomyopathy traits or diagnosis. Probands – screening protocol

Adolescents with a resting 12-lead ECG suggestive of cardiomyopathy, without a clinical diagnosis, were included for this study. An abnormal ECG suggestive of cardiomyopathy included individuals with two or more borderline ECG abnormalities (axis deviation, atrial enlargement) and/or pathological ECG patterns (anterior T wave inversion (≥ 16 years), inferior/lateral TWI, pathological Q waves [excluding lead III, aVR], ST segment depression, left bundle branch block, right bundle branch block [QRS duration ≥ 140 ms], ≥ 2 premature ventricular/atrial beats [PVBs/PACs]) [42]. The multi-modality assessment performed to rule out cardiomyopathy included a repeat resting 12-lead ECG, an echocardiogram, exercise tolerance test, 24-h ambulatory ECG monitoring, and CMR imaging. Part of the work-up also included a cardiac gene panel. Expert genetic counseling led by a multidisciplinary team (consisting of a cardiologist with expertise in inherited cardiac conditions, geneticists, and genetic counselors) was carried out. These subjects were counseled on the possible advantages and negative ramifications of genetic testing. Participants were informed that surveillance remained imperative, irrespective of the outcome of genetic testing. Individuals were however informed that the presence of a LP/P cardiomyopathy variant may alter the surveillance strategy during follow-up (frequency and advanced imaging modalities). The presence of a LP/P variant would also play a role in cascade testing of family members. Parental and patient consent was mandatory before testing. A 218 cardiac gene panel (Health in Code S.L., A Coruna, Spain) (supplementary text) was performed on all included probands. Variants were classified as variants of unknown significance (VUS), LP, or P according to the ACMG criteria [46], using Franklin Genoox to compute criteria supporting pathogenicity.

Relatives—screening protocol

First-degree relatives of probands with an abnormal ECG suggestive of cardiomyopathy, without a clinical diagnosis, were consequently informed of the possibility of undergoing screening (recruited between 2018 and 2020). This consisted of a clinical assessment (history and examination), a resting 12-lead ECG and an echocardiogram. ECG were reported using the same criteria for probands [42]. Abnormal ECG findings suggestive of early cardiomyopathy were defined as previously described for probands. Second-line investigations were also organized for relatives with an abnormal screen. Internationally recognized diagnostic criteria were applied for those labeled with a cardiac disorder [12, 14, 45]. Following clinical testing, first-degree relatives of probands with a LP/P cardiomyopathy variant were counseled on the implications of cascade genetic testing. This approach was also extended to non-immediate family members if first-degree (or next generation) relatives were given a clinical diagnosis, harbored early cardiomyopathy traits, and/or tested positive for the familial cardiomyopathy (LP/P) variant. Probands and their relatives with an abnormal evaluation were followed up yearly in a dedicated inherited cardiac conditions clinic. Adolescent relatives were defined as individuals ≤ 18 years at the first evaluation. Relatives and probands with a LP/P genetic variant (in the absence of a diagnosis) had a repeat CMR at 5 years from baseline.

Data collection

Medical records were reviewed for demographics, family history, baseline clinical characteristics, cardiac symptoms, diagnostic tests, and genetic evaluation. Data on those given a diagnosis or advised to undergo surveillance was recorded. Information on relatives given lifestyle advice and early medical interventions (medical therapy and cardiac interventions) was also collected. Follow-up data about evolving phenotypes and clinical outcomes were also recorded using electronic health records. A major adverse cardiac event (MACE) was defined as (i) cardiac-related hospitalization related to a genetic cardiomyopathy (diagnosed at baseline or during follow-up); (ii) cardiac implantable electronic device implantation; and/or (iii) cardiac-related death.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the University of Malta Faculty of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number 34/2017) and conforms to the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for screening was obtained from all relatives.

Statistical analysis

Statistical computations were carried out with SPSS V.23 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data is presented as the mean ± SD and data ranges. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

The baseline clinical evaluation was normal in most screened relatives (n = 58/71, 81.7%). The ECG and echocardiogram were abnormal in 9 (12.7%; 95% CI 6.8–22.4%) and 8 (11.3%; 95% CI 6.8–22.4%) individuals respectively. Seven individuals with an abnormal ECG also had an abnormal echocardiogram (n = 7/13, 53.8%). Family members (n = 25/71, 35.2%) of ECG-positive phenotype-negative probands who also harbored a LP/P genetic variant were also counseled on cascade testing. Ten (14.1%) family members were G + for the familial LP/P genetic variant.

Referred family members

Thirteen (18.3%; 95% CI 11.0–28.9%) were referred for a more comprehensive evaluation based on their preliminary clinical evaluation (Fig. 2). Relatives with a LP/P genetic variant (n = 9/71, 12.7%) were also referred for second-line investigations, irrespective of the outcome of the initial clinical screen. One of these individuals refused further testing and was excluded from further analysis.

The ECG was the commonest reason for referral (n = 9/13, 69.2%), including two symptomatic individuals. The commonest ECG abnormality was TWI (n = 3 inferolateral, n = 1 anterolateral), followed by ST depression (n = 2), PVBs (n = 2), and Q waves (n = 1). Several individuals had an abnormal echocardiogram (n = 8/13, 61.5%). Echocardiography identified left ventricular hypertrophy in 7 relatives. A dilated left ventricle with reduced ejection fraction was observed in another relative. The outcomes of second-line investigations are tabulated in Table 2.

Screening adolescent relatives

Six (8.%) adolescent relatives were screened in the program. Most were female (n = 4/6, 66.7%) and first-degree relatives (n = 5/6, 83.3%). Two (33.3%) of these individuals from the same family were G + and exhibited early cardiomyopathy traits (n = 1 inferolateral TWI, n = 1 evidence of cardiac limitation during CPET). The remaining adolescent relatives had a normal ECG and echocardiogram (gene-negative families).

Incidental diagnosis

An incidental diagnosis was also identified in another 3 (4.2%) relatives, all unrelated to genetic cardiomyopathy (n = 2 hypertensive cardiomyopathy, n = 1 ischaemic cardiomyopathy, n = 1 LVOT ectopy). The relative with LVOT ectopy had his PVB burden suppressed early on during exercise testing. CMR imaging was also reassuringly normal.

Management strategy

All three (4.2%) relatives diagnosed with cardiomyopathy required medical therapy at the time of diagnosis (Fig. 2). Two underwent invasive coronary angiography because of symptoms and were both found to have concomitant coronary artery disease. One required revascularization with complete resolution of symptoms. One patient with HCM received an implantable cardioverter defibrillator because of high-risk clinical markers.

Screening non-immediate family members

Consistent with current recommendations [12, 17], cascade genetic testing was performed in families of patients with a LP/P cardiomyopathy genetic variant. As per the study protocol, the presence of a LP/P variant in first-degree family members (even in the absence of a diagnosis or early cardiomyopathy traits) led to cascade genetic testing in non-immediate (to proband) relatives (n = 14, 19.7%). This approach led to a second-degree relative with a new DCM diagnosis (n = 1, 1.4%). A further 3 (4.2%) individuals also harbored early cardiomyopathy traits (Fig. 5).

Follow-up

All relatives with a diagnosis or those having early cardiomyopathy traits underwent active follow-up (59 ± 23 months). One individual with early cardiomyopathy traits at baseline (n = 1/9, 11.1%) underwent phenoconversion at 71 months (Figs. 2 and 6). This individual harbored a MYBPC3 P variant, together with cardiomyopathy traits during exercise testing. A repeat CMR identified a new isolated non-ischemic left ventricular mid wall scar in the lateral wall. At the end of the follow-up period, the diagnostic yield for cardiomyopathy diagnosis increased to 5.6% (95% CI 2.0–13.8%). Follow-up CMR in the remaining relatives undergoing surveillance did not identify any new pathology. Gene-positive ECG-positive adolescent probands–how does it all come together?

Three (18.8%) ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents probands were identified with a LP/P genetic variant (Table 1). These individuals exhibited inferior, inferolateral, and anterior/inferior TWI. Genotype (LP/P) was not associated with any specific ECG pattern. At the end of the follow-up period, the presence of a LP/P genetic variant in screened relatives was however associated with a higher likelihood of a diagnosis (n = 4/10 [40.0%) vs n = 0/61 [0.0%)]) or cardiomyopathy traits (n = 4/10 [40.0%] vs n = 4/61, [6.6%]) (p < 0.001). Cascade testing enabled personalized surveillance in 6 relatives (8.5%). These individuals either demonstrated a normal clinical screen (ECG and echocardiography) or were non-immediate relatives who would not have required screening by conventional criteria. Two (2.8%) of these individuals were given a diagnoses, and these would otherwise have been missed.

Discussion

Electrocardiographic abnormalities may represent one of the earliest phenotypic expression of cardiomyopathy, preceding the morphofunctional changes that typically underpin diagnosis [47, 48]. Current guidelines recommend family screening only when a proband fulfils diagnostic criteria for cardiomyopathy [12, 49]. Cascade genetic testing is traditionally reserved for families in whom a LP/P variant has been identified [12, 30, 49]. Repolarization anomalies suggestive of underlying cardiomyopathy are uncommon when screening adolescents [9, 11, 42, 48, 50], hence why caution is warranted in such cases. Cardiomyopathies often present in the fourth or fifth decade of life [13, 21, 22, 30, 37, 51, 52]. A diagnosis in ECG-positive adolescents is understandably infrequent. Family screening in this context may be reasonable, though its broader applicability remains uncertain. Our observations indicate that clinically relevant anomalies may emerge in selected families, even when adolescents are ECG-positive phenotype-negative on standard screening modalities. These signals are preliminary and may simply reflect cohort enrichment, warranting further evaluation in larger studies.

As a follow-up to the BEAT-IT study [11], we report the clinical impact of family screening in ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents. Nearly one in five relatives (18.3%, 95% CI 11.0–28.9%) required second-line investigations. A cardiomyopathy diagnosis was present in 3 individuals (4.2%; 95% CI 0.9–11.7%) at baseline, increasing to 5.6% (95% CI 2.0–13.8%) at the end of the follow-up period. Another 8 individuals (11.3%; 95% CI 4.9–21.0%) had early cardiomyopathy phenotypic traits. An abnormal screening outcome was more prevalent in families where the ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescent proband had lateral TWI or a LP/P genetic variant, in families with a history of SCD, symptomatic relatives, and in relatives with lateral TWI. Most referred relatives had ECG anomalies, reinforcing the central role of the ECG as an early marker of disease expression within families [51]. Relatives given a diagnosis underwent lifestyle modification, medical therapy, or invasive procedures (diagnostic or preventive therapies), underscoring the clinical relevance of detecting disease in this unconventional clinical context.

Genetic testing in this cohort added further value to the screening process. A fifth of probands harbored rare LP/P variants, not previously identified in the local population. This relatively high yield likely reflects the composition of the cohort, consisting of ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents referred for specialist evaluation, rather than an unselected population that is reported in genetic biobank studies [19, 52]. This should not be interpreted as representative of the general adolescent population. Incorporating genetic data in this cohort enabled a more personalized surveillance strategy in 8.5% of relatives. Several relatives with normal ECG and echocardiographic findings demonstrated early cardiomyopathy traits on advanced testing, including morphofunctional and tissue characterization abnormalities (CMR), arrhythmias (holter monitoring/exercise testing), evidence of a cardiac limitation during CPET (Table 2). Two (2.8%) family members were diagnosed with cardiomyopathy (n = 1 DCM in non-immediate family member; n = 1 NDLVC in a first-degree relative), findings that would have been missed using the conventional screening approach. A diagnosis or early cardiomyopathy traits were also more frequent in G + families (80.0 vs 4.4%, p < 0.001), suggesting that a joint clinical-genetic approach may offer additional diagnostic yield in selected contexts. These observations remain exploratory, and should be interpreted cautiously. Our findings are directionally consistent with emerging genotype-first data indicating that carriers of cardiomyopathy-associated variants may exhibit subtle or concealed phenotypes detectable only with advanced imaging or functional assessment [18, 19, 53–55]. Variant carriers have also been reported to have an increased risk of heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and arrythmias [55], reinforcing the concept that G + individuals may benefit from more comprehensive evaluation and closer surveillance. Validation in larger, unselected cohorts will be essential, as genotype-specific or family-specific factors may underlie the differences observed in our cohort.

Although clinically appealing, several barriers may limit implementation. Clinical penetrance among G + individuals is highly heterogenous and often unpredictable. While predictive testing has been shown to be a cost-effective in other settings [56], the financial implications of cascade genetic testing in the absence of established familial cardiomyopathy remains uncertain. The emotional impact of genetic testing in young individuals must also be considered, as a positive result, whether diagnostic or equivocal, may carry biological, psychological, and social consequences [57, 58]. Repeat screening, particularly in G + families, may ultimately increase the diagnostic yield [13, 39], potentially strengthening the long-term economic justification for this approach. Nonetheless, our data should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, given the modest sample size and small number of events.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The proband sample size is modest, reflecting the low prevalence of ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents in the screening program. The population is predominantly of white ethnicity, limiting generalizability, particularly given the higher prevalence of TWI in Afrocarribean individuals [42, 59–61]. The absence of a comparator group also makes it difficult to quantify the incremental diagnostic yield above background detection rates.

The genetic architecture of the Maltese population, including potential founder effects, may also influence variant distribution and partly explain the relatively high genetic yield, with the impact of founder mutations in island communities already described previously by local and international academic groups [62–64]. Despite the clinically actionable genetic variants identified in this study carrying a low prevalence in the local population, founder effects may possibly explain the comparatively high LP/P yield in phenotype-negative probands.

Penetrance of LP/P genetic variants is incomplete and age-dependent. We did not perform a cost-effectiveness analysis to justify this novel screening approach. Although our findings support the feasibility and potential direction of genotype-guided surveillance in selected families, these findings should be considered as hypothesis-generating. The role of routine genetic testing in all ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents require confirmation in larger cohorts.

The cohort of relatives was drawn from 16 families, meaning that certain clinical and genetic characteristics were shared across individuals. Within-family clustering was not modeled, as the study was descriptive rather than inferential. This may reduce statistical independence and attenuate p-values; however, it does not alter the descriptive patterns observed nor the exploratory intent of the study.

Although no MACE occurred during follow-up, this study was neither powered nor designed to asses prognostic benefit. Our results merely highlight the potential benefit of this approach to identify more family members with early phenotypic cardiomyopathy traits or a diagnosis. Longer surveillance may in turn reveal further phenotypic evolution. Extended follow-up will also shed light on the long-term implications of harboring a LP/P cardiomyopathy variant, in the absence of overt cardiomyopathy.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight the potential value of a combined clinical and genetic approach in families of adolescents with ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents. Early identification of at-risk relatives may enable timely surveillance, risk stratification, and intervention. Larger studies are needed to refine and validate the role of genetic testing in this setting and to determine which subgroups derive the greatest benefit from extended family evaluation.

Conclusion

This exploratory, proof of concept study examined the yield of clinical and genetic screening in families of ECG-positive, phenotype-negative adolescents that may be harboring pre-clinical cardiomyopathy. Repolarization ECG changes suggestive of cardiomyopathy are rare in adolescents [9, 11, 13, 48, 50], hence why these findings warrant referral for more advanced testing. Given the age-dependent penetrance of inherited cardiomyopathies, adolescents rarely meet diagnostic criteria, making long-term surveillance a reasonable consideration. In this context, family screening may be justified. The presence of a family history of SCD, lateral TWI or a LP/P cardiomyopathy variant in ECG-positive phenotype-negative adolescents, and symptomatic relatives in this cohort was associated with a higher yield when screening relatives. In this enriched cohort, family screening identified a small proportion of individuals with a new cardiomyopathy diagnosis (5.6%) and early cardiomyopathic traits (11.3%). Our findings support that a combined clinical-genetic approach may offer additional insight and could support more individualized screening strategies in selected families. These observations remain preliminary and hypothesis-generating. These should be validated in larger, unselected cohorts, as genotype-specific or family-specific factors may have contributed to these findings.
