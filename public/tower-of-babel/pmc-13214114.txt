Dendritic cell-derived exosomes from PDT-treated glioma cells enhance antitumor immunity as a subcellular vaccine
Authors: Fang Wang, Shuai Ma, Zhihui Liu, Mengzhao Feng, Dengpan Song, Mengyuan Li, Yuan An, Kaiyuan Zhang, Yan Du, Shaoshan Hu, Xinbin Guo, Fuyou Guo, Han Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioma is a highly aggressive brain tumor with a poor prognosis. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) induces antitumor immunity, a key mechanism of its efficacy. Dendritic cell-derived exosomes (Dexs) are crucial for tumor antigen presentation and T-cell activation. Dexs represent a promising cell-free strategy for cancer immunotherapy. Understanding the role of Dexs in PDT-induced immunity may enhance PDT efficacy.MethodsGL261 glioma cells were subjected to PDT, followed by co-culture with dendritic cells (DC) to obtain PDT-Dexs. Flow cytometry and ELISA were used to assess the effects of PDT-Dexs on DC maturation and T-cell activation. MicroRNA sequencing was performed on PDT-Dexs. The effect of PDT-Dexs on tumor growth and survival was evaluated in immune-competent and immune-deficient glioma model mice.ResultsCo-incubation with PDT-Dexs significantly increased DC maturation and T-cell activation. Sequencing revealed 156 up-regulated microRNAs, miR-152-3p was validated as a key functional miRNA that promotes DC maturation by targeting DNMT1 to upregulate MyD88 expression. PDT-Dexs entered the systemic circulation, increasing the serum IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α levels. In immunocompetent mice, PDT-Dexs increased CD3 + CD4+ and CD3 + CD8+ T-cell percentages, slowed tumor growth, prolonged survival, and were associated with enhanced CD8 + T-cell infiltration and tumor cell apoptosis. Crucially, PDT-Dexs failed to inhibit tumor growth or improve survival in immunodeficient nude mice.ConclusionsPDT-Dexs promote DC maturation and T-cell activation, improving antitumor immunity and PDT efficacy. Combining of PDT with PDT-Dexs further improves antitumor immunity and PDT efficacy. PDT-Dexs warrants further investigation as a potential strategy for enhancing antitumor immunity in glioma.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08111-3.Keywords: Dendritic cell, Exosomes, Photodynamic therapy, Glioma, Vaccine

Introduction

Glioma is the most common malignant tumor in the central nervous system, with glioblastomas (GBM) comprising over 50% of all cases [1, 2]. It is characterized by a high recurrence rate, extremely aggressive and highly immunosuppressive features. Despite standard combined treatment with surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, patients prognosis remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of ~ 5% [3]. Therefore, there is a urgent need for explore more effective treatments for glioma.

Cell-based vaccine is a critical therapeutic approach for eradicating various tumors and preventing their relapse via activation of the patient’s immune system [4, 5]. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy and biosafety of dendritic cell (DC) vaccines in glioma [6–11]. However, it still has several limitations and deficiencies for clinical application. For example, long-term in vitro culture reduces DC migration and function [12]. Moreover, DC vaccines cannot be readily stored, and their life span in vivo is short [13, 14]. In contrast, DC-derived exosomes (Dexs) offer several advantages: greater stability, longer half-life in vivo, and ability to present whole tumor cell antigens in the form of peptide-major histocompatibility complexs (pMHCs). These pMHCs on the surface of the Dexs could be bound and “collected” by antigen-specific T cell, resulting in their activation [15]. Moreover, as an indirect antigen presentation mechanism, pMHCs on the Dexs can be captured and processed by DCs, which then transmit antigen information to T cells, which is considered one of the main mechanisms of T-cell activation in vivo [16]. Thus, Dexs represents a promising cell-free vaccine strategy, has promising potential clinical application prospects [17].

The successful development of subunit vaccines depends on the selection of effective tumor antigens and the induction of robust anti-tumor immune response [18, 19]. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a highly effective method for treating various cancers including glioma [20, 21]. In addition to direct cytotoxic effects and vascular injury, an important mechanism of PDT is its ability to induce inflammatory responses that activate systemic immunity [22, 23]. Tumor cells exposed to PDT display surface proteins and release diverse immunostimulatory molecules, including danger-associated molecular patterns, such as calreticulin, heat shock proteins, and adenosine triphosphate. These molecules act as natural adjuvants, driving inflammation and the activation of antigen-presenting cells [24]. The immunogenic cell death induced by PDT thus provides an ideal source of tumor antigens and danger signals for dendritic cell uptake. We therefore proposed that exosomes derived from DCs pulsed with PDT-treated glioma cells (PDT-Dexs) could harness these signals to amplify antitumor immunity.

In this study, PDT was used to expose the tumor antigens of glioma cells. A co-culture system was then established in which DCs engulfed these antigens and processed them for antigenic peptides presentation. Antigen-loaded exosomes secreted by DCs (PDT-Dexs: Dexs pulsed with PDT-treated glioma cells) were collected from the culture supernatant. We confirmed that PDT-Dexs promote homologous DC maturation and T cells activation in vitro. PDT combined with PDT-Dexs can improve the anti-tumor immunity of mice, and improve the efficacy of PDT treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The GL261 mouse glioma cell line was purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany) and cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). Mouse DCs were derived from the bone marrow of C57BL/6J mice as previously described [25]. T cells were also isolated from C57BL/6J mice via a T-cell isolation kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (B90021, Selleck Chemicals, Shanghai, China). Dendritic cells and T cells were cultured in the corresponding complete RPMI 1640 medium containing the same supplements (10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin). These cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Animal model

C57BL/6J and immunocompromised nude mice (6–8 weeks, weighing 18–22 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratories Company (Vital River, Beijing, China). Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions under standard 12-hour light/dark cycle, at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and humidity of 50 ± 10%, with free access to autoclaved food and water. Mice were acclimatized for at least one week prior to experiments. Both male and female mice were used in initial pilot studies, and no sex-dependent differences were observed in tumor take rate or growth kinetics. Intracranial orthotopic glioma model and subcutaneous glioma tumor model were constructed as previously described [26, 27]. Briefly, GL261-luc cells (5 × 10^4 in 2 µL PBS) were stereotactically injected into the right striatum (coordinates: 2.0 mm anterior, 1.8 mm lateral to bregma, 3.0 mm depth) of mice with 2% isoflurane inhalation. Anesthesia was maintained throughout the procedure via a nose cone. The sample size of six mice per group (n = 6) was selected based on established standards in glioma immunotherapy studies.

Tumor engraftment was confirmed using bioluminescence imaging (BLI) on day 7 post-implantation. After confirmation of tumor engraftment, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. The primary outcome was overall survival, defined as the time from tumor implantation to the point of humane endpoints. The secondary outcome was tumor growth, monitored using weekly BLI. Mice that died within 48 h of surgery (due to operative complications) or failed to show BLI signal on day 7 were excluded from the final analysis. All other mice were included in an intention-to-treat analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Animal ethics approval was obtained from the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Number: A20220013).

Photodynamic treatment

In vitro PDT: Adherent cells were cultured until they reached 80% confluence, after which the photosensitizer (Hematoporphyrin monomethyl Ether, HMME; 0.5 µg/ml) (H302937, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was added to the media and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in the dark. After washes to remove unincorporated HMME, the cells were irradiated with 630 nm laser (100 mW/cm2) at a dose of 4 J/cm2.

In vivo PDT: HMME (5 mg/kg body weight) was injected via the tail vein. After 24 h, the mice were treated with a 630 nm laser at a laser fluence rate of 1 W/cm2 and a total fluence of 50 J/cm2. The laser spot size was adjusted to fully cover the tumor region.

CCK8

GL261 cell suspensions (100 µL/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in a 37℃ incubator for 12 h to allow them to adhere to the wall. The groups were set, different concentrations of HMME were added, and the mixture was incubated for 4 h in the dark. Different groups of cells were then treated with PDT. Then, 10µL of CCK-8 solution (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated in the incubator for 3 h at 37 ℃. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate the relative survival rate of cells.

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested and washed with 3 mL of PBS, and then, resuspended in 100 µl of 3% BSA at a concentration of 1 × 10 7 cells/ml. The samples were incubated with antibodies at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. The antibodies used for flow cytometric analyses were as follows: PE-conjugated anti-mouse I-A/I-E (107607,1:50, Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c (117310, 1:50, Biolegend), APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3 (100236, 1:50, Biolegend), FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD25 (102005, 1:50, Biolegend), PE/Cyanine7-conjugated anti-mouse CD69 (104512, 1:50, Biolegend), PerCP-conjugated anti-mouse CD4 (100538, 1:50, Biolegend), and PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD8a (100708, 1:50, Biolegend) antibodies. Apoptotic tumor cells following PDT exposure was examined using an Annexin V-PE Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1065L, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Flow cytometric Data acquisition was performed on a BD fortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). Detailed gating strategies for flow cytometry analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Figure S1).

Transwell co-culture system

The co-culture of dendritic cells and tumor cells was performed via a Transwell chamber (6-well plates, 0.4-µm pore size). Tumor cells were inoculated into the upper compartment at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/ml, and dendritic cells were inoculated into the lower compartment at a concentration of 4 × 105 cells/ml.

Isolation and characterization of exosomes

The cell supernatant was collected to extract the exosomes through an exosome extraction kit (UR52121, Umibio, Shanghai, China). In addition, specific exosome surface markers, including CD9, Alix, and CD81, were detected via western blotting (WB). The primary antibodies used were as follows: CD9 polyclonal antibody (20597-1-AP, 1:2000, Protientech, Wuhan, China), Alix polyclonal antibody (12422-1-AP, 1:3000, Protientech), CD81 monoclonal antibody (66866-1-Ig, 1:3000, Protientech). The WB procedure for proteins was carried out as described previously [28]. Purified exosome samples were examined by transmission electron microscopy using a Hitachi HT7800 instrument at HaoKe Biotechnology Company Limited (HaoKe, Hangzhou, China). The size distribution and concentration of the exosomes were analyzed via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a Malvern NanoSight NS300 system by Umibio Co. Ltd (Umibio, Shanghai, China).

Cell fluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde for 15 min, and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After three washes with PBS, the cells were blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min, and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used were as follows: CD86 polyclonal antibody (13395-1-AP, 1:300, Protientech, Wuhan, China) and anti-mouse MHC Class II (65122-1-Ig, 1:200, Protientech). Secondary antibody incubation was performed at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). The samples were photographed with an SP8 Leica microscope (Leica, Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, USA).

ELISA analysis

ELISAs were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ABclonal Technology, Wuhan, China). Mouse IFN-gamma ELISA Kit (ABclonal Technology, RK00019), Mouse IL-2 ELISA Kit (ABclonal Technology, RK00007), Mouse IL-10 ELISA Kit (ABclonal Technology, RK00016), Mouse TNF-alpha ELISA Kit (ABclonal Technology, RK00027), and Mouse Transforming Growth Factor Beta 1 ELISA Kit ((ABclonal Technology, RK00057) were used. Serum samples were collected at the experimental endpoint (day 14). Briefly, 100 µL of samples or serially diluted standards were added to pre-coated wells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing, detection antibodies were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with streptavidin-HRP for 30 min at 37 °C. The reaction was developed using TMB substrate for 15–20 min in the dark and terminated with stop solution. Absorbance was immediately measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader.

Lactate dehydrogenase cytotoxicity assay

The direct cytotoxic capacity of T cells pulsed with PDT-Dexs was evaluated using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay. GL261 glioma target cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, in vitro-activated T cells (effector cells) were added to the target cells at specified effector-to-target (E: T) ratios (20:1, 10:1, 5:1, and 1:1). After a 6-hour co-culture period, culture supernatant was collected. LDH activity in the supernatant was quantified using a commercial Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (LDH) (MCE, HY-K1090), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of specific cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula: Specific Lysis (%) = (Experimental Release – Effector Spontaneous Release – Target Spontaneous Release) / (Target Maximum Release – Target Spontaneous Release). All assays were performed with triplicate technical replicates and repeated in at least three independent biological experiments.

MicroRNA sequencing and analysis

Exosomes extracted from the experimental group and control groups (3 samples/group, 200 µl/case) were sent to Lianchuan Biotechnology Company (Lianchuan Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China) for sequencing analysis of micro-RNA of exosomes. Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (version 0.23.2) to remove low-quality bases and adapters. Library preparation for miRNA sequencing was performed using the TruSeq Small RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform with a single-read 50 bp (1 × 50 bp) configuration. The miRNA data analysis software ACGT101-miR (LC Sciences, Houston, Texas, USA) provided by Hangzhou Lichuan Biological Institute was used to screen and process the original data, so as to obtain effective data, which could be used for subsequent small RNA data analysis. The R-package “limma” was used to screen differentially expressed microRNA genes in different groups. The truncation criterion was set as | log2-fold change (FC) | > 1, with an adjusted p-value (False Discovery Rate, FDR) < 0.05. The R package “clusterProfiler” was subsequently used for GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Quantitative real-time PCR for miRNA expression

Total RNA was extracted from PDT-Dexs and control-Dexs. For cDNA synthesis, RNA was reverse-transcribed using the SweScript RT I First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (G3330, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) with gene-specific stem-loop RT primers according to the manufacturer’s instructions in a 20 µL reaction volume.

Quantitative PCR was performed using the 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3326, Servicebio) on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System. The miRNA-specific forward primers were synthesized by Servicebio (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The primer sequences were: mmu-miR-152-3p forward: 5’-ACACTCCAGCTGGGTCAGTGCATGACAGA-3’. U6 snRNA forward: 5’-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3’; U6 snRNA reverse: 5’-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3’. The universal reverse primer provided with the cDNA synthesis kit was used for amplification. All reactions were run in triplicate. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with U6 snRNA serving as the endogenous control for normalization.

Cell transfection with miRNA mimics and inhibitors

Bone marrow-derived DCs were seeded in culture plates 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with 50 nM of either mmu-miR-152-3p mimic, miR-152-3p inhibitor, or their respective negative controls (mimic NC and inhibitor NC; all synthesized by GenePharma, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following a 6-hour transfection period, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were then cultured for an additional 24 h before stimulation with PDT-Dexs.

Immunohistochemical

IHC was performed on 4-µm-thick formalin-fixed sections according to the experimental protocol as previously described [28]. The primary antibodies used were as follows: CD8 antibody (AF5126, 1:200, Affinity, Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA), Foxp3 antibody (AF6544, 1:200, Affinity), and Ki67 antibody (AF0198, 1:400, Affinity). Sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature, and visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine as the chromogen. Finally, images were captured with an Olympus BX41 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Positive cells (brown DAB staining) were quantified manually or using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) by an investigator blinded to the treatment groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

No data were excluded from the analyses. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare two groups with a normally distributed data, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple groups. Data from at least two independent experiments are presented as the mean ± SEM. Mouse survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between treatment groups were assessed for statistical significance using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0, and R language (version 4.2.2) was used for additional analyses. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Details of the biological replicates are provided in the figure legends wherever necessary.

Results

DCs are key immune cells in the glioma immune microenvironment

The Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) online platform was used to examine the relationships between immune cell infiltration (B cells, CD4, CD8, macrophages, neutrophils and DCs) and the prognosis of patients with glioma. In patients with low-grade glioma, infiltration of all six types of immune cells was significantly correlated with prognosis (P < 0.01) (Figure S2A). In contrast, in patients with GBM, only DC infiltration showed was significant correlation with prognosis (P < 0.01) (Figure S2B). These data highlight the key role of DCs in shaping the tumor microenvironment and influencing the prognosis of patients with glioma.

Parameters used for PDT

A CCK8 assay was used to determine the specific parameters for PDT, including photosensitizer concentration and energy density. As both the concentration of photosensitizer and energy density increased, the survival rate of tumor cells decreased progressively (Fig. 1A). To avoid potential interference from the photosensitizer, a concentration of 0.5 µg/mL HMME was selected for subsequent experiments. At a light energy density of 4 J/cm2, approximately 50% of the cells died. Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis further confirmed these findings (Fig. 1B, C).

Identification of DCs and establishment of a transwell co-culture system

The purity of mouse bone marrow-derived DCs was tested by flow cytometry. The results revealed that DC purity remained stable at approximately 95% across different time points (Figures S3A, B). The glioma cells/DC co-culture system was established as shown in Fig. 1D. GL261 cells were seeded in the upper chamber and treated with PDT. Because immature DCs exhibit greater endocytic capacity than mature DCs [29], they were seeded into the lower chamber for 24 h to take up PDT-derived antigens. The DCs were then transferred into exosome-free medium and cultured for additional 48 h. Finally, the PDT-Dexs were isolated from the DC culture supernatant.

Characterization of exosomes

In accordance with the guidelines of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles [30], exosomes were quantified and characterized using electron microscopy, WB, and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Electron microscopy revealed vesicles with diameters of approximately 60–150 nm and a cup-shaped morphology (Fig. 1E). Several specific exosome marker proteins, including CD9, CD81 and Alix were confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 1F). NTA revealed that exosome sizes ranged from 80 to 150 nm, with a peak diameter of approximately 120 nm (Fig. 1G).

PDT-Dexs promote DC maturation in vitro

PKH26-labeled PDT-Dexs were cultured with immature DCs for 24 h. Fluorescence imaging revealed that PDT-Dexs were engulfed by immature DCs and distributed uniformly within the cytoplasm (Fig. 2A). Activated DCs produce IL-12 and TNF-α, which promote immune responses [31, 32]. Compared with control Dexs, PDT-Dexs effectively enhanced the secretion of DCs (Fig. 2B). Mature DCs expressed high levels of MHC-II and CD86 [33]. Flow cytometric analysis showed that the percentage of mature DCs (CD11c+ CD86+, and CD11c + MHC II+) was significantly greater when DCs were co-cultured with PDT-Dexs than when they were co-cultured with control Dexs (Fig. 2C-E). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed these findings (Fig. 2F, G). These results demonstrate that PDT-Dexs promote DC maturation and trigger the release of the pro-inflammatory cytokines.

PDT-Dexs promoteT-cell activation in vitro

As early as 2003, studies reported that Dexs activate T cells by directly presenting antigenic peptide bound to MHC-I and MHC-II proteins on the exosome surface to the T-cell receptor [34]. Therefore, we examined the in vitro activation effects of PDT-Dexs on T cells. Flow cytometry results revealed that the purity of the T cells extracted from the spleen was stable at approximately 90% (Figures S4A, B). CD25 and CD69 are classic markers of T-cell activation [35]. To define the activation kinetics of T cells stimulated by our vaccine, we analyzed the expression of the early activation marker CD69 and the sustained activation marker CD25 at serial time points. Flow cytometry revealed that PDT-Dexs induced a rapid and robust upregulation of CD69, with a significantly higher proportion of CD3 + CD69+ T cells compared to control Dexs as early as 6 h post-stimulation; this elevated expression persisted through 24 h. In parallel, PDT-Dexs promoted a progressive and sustained increase in CD25 expression. The percentage of CD3 + CD25+ T cells was significantly higher in the PDT-Dexs group from 12 h onward, and this difference was maintained through 48 h (Fig. 2H-J). These findings demonstrate that PDT-Dexs not only potently initiate early T-cell activation but also maintain a prolonged state of T-cell activation. Notably, this effect was achieved by pulsing PDT-Dexs with GL261 glioma cells lysates treated with PDT. Collectively, these results confirm that PDT-Dexs significantly enhance T cell activation in vitro.

To assess the cytotoxic function of PDT-Dexs-activated T cells, we performed an in vitro killing assay. T cells primed with either PDT-Dexs or Control-Dexs were co-cultured with GL261 glioma target cells at variying effector-to-target (E: T) ratios. Morphological examination revealed that activated T cells actively engaged with tumor cells, forming characteristic immune synapses and inducing target cell membrane disruption (Figures S4C). Cytotoxicity was quantified using an LDH release assay. T cells primed with PDT-Dexs exhibited significantly higher specific lysis of GL261 cells compared to control T cells at all E: T ratios tested (20:1, 10:1, 5:1, and 1:1) (Fig. 2K). The cytotoxic effect was dose-dependent, with lysis efficiency increasing proportionally with the E: T ratio. These results provide direct functional evidence that PDT-Dexs not only activate T cells phenotypically but also endow them with potent, specific cytotoxicity against glioma cells in vitro.

MicroRNA sequencing of Dexs

Exosome-encapsulated miRNAs play important roles in translational regulation following uptake by recipient cells [36]. To characterize the distinct miRNA constituents of Dexs and PDT-Dexs, we performed microRNA sequencing. The proportion of miRNAs detected in the PDT-Dexs group was significantly greater than that in the Dexs group (Figures S5A-C). A Venn diagram illustrating the differential miRNAs between the two groups was generated (Figure S5D). These results confirm that the miRNAs composition of Dexs changs markedly after co-culture with PDT-treated GL261 cells.

A heatmap showing differentially expressed miRNAs between the two groups was generated (Fig. 3A), and the number of significantly differentially expressed miRNAs is shown in Fig. 3B. miRNA-31-3P, miRNA-152-3P, and miRNA-10b-5P were significantly upregulated in the PDT-Dexs group, whereas miRNA-9-5P, miRNA-103-3P, and miRNA-147-3P were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3C).

Finally, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the common differentially expressed miRNAs to explore their potential functions. GO analysis revealed that these miRNAs are mainly involved in biological processes such as RNA transcription, protein trafficking, endocytosis and transmembrane transport (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment in pathways including the Ras signaling, Wnt signaling, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and inositol phosphate metabolism (Fig. 3E). Overall, these findings confirm distinct difference in miRNAs between the two groups.

Integration and functional validation of a key exosomal miRNA

To elucidate the functional component of PDT-Dexs, we analyzed miRNA-seq data and identified miR-152-3p as one of the most significantly upregulated species, a finding that was independently validated by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3F). To determine its causal role, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in DCs. Overexpression of miR-152-3p in DCs enhanced PDT-Dexs-induced secretion of IL-12 and TNF-α, whereas its inhibition significantly attenuated cytokine production (Figure S5E), establishing miR-152-3p as a key mediator of DC maturation. Mechanistically, we investigated the downstream DNMT1/MyD88 axis, a conserved pathway in which miR-152-3p targets DNMT1 to epigenetically regulate MyD88 expression. WB analysis confirmed reciprocal regulation: miR-152-3p mimics suppressed DNMT1 and elevated MyD88 protein levels, whereas inhibitors produced the opposite effect (Fig. 3G-I). Collectively, these data identified the miR-152-3p/DNMT1/MyD88 signaling axis as an important mechanism through which PDT-Dexs empower DCs to initiate anti-tumor immune responses.

PDT-Dexs/Dexs enter murine circulation and cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB)

PDT-Dexs/Dexs were labeled with DiR fluorescent dye and then intravenously injected into mice through the tail vein. Separate batches of mice were euthanized at 24 and 48 h after injection. In vivo imaging indicated that both PDT-Dexs and Dexs rapidly entered circulation following injection (Figures S6A, B). To directly evaluate the biodistribution and BBB-penetrating capacity, DiR-labeled PDT-Dexs or control Dexs were administered intravenously to mice bearing orthotopic GL261 gliomas. In vivo imaging revealed a distinct cranial fluorescent signal as early as 12 h post-injection, which persisted for 48 h, with significantly higher signal intensity in the PDT-Dexs group (Figures S6C, D). To confirm parenchymal delivery and exclude vascular contamination, mice were perfused at 48 h to remove circulating blood-borne exosomes prior to organ harvest. Ex vivo imaging confirmed robust accumulation of DiR-labeled PDT-Dexs within the brain parenchyma, alongside expected uptake in the liver and spleen (Figure S6E). These findings provide direct evidence that PDT-Dexs efficiently cross the BBB and achieve systemic distribution.

PDT-Dexs induce host immune response in vivo

To validate whether PDT-Dexs can induce a host immune response in vivo and to examine the potential effect of combination therapy with PDT, four treatment groups were established: control (Dexs), PDT-Dexs, PDT, and the PDT-PDT-Dexs combination. Mice were administered booster injections of PDT-Dexs/Dexs at 4-day intervals, following previously published. The mice in the PDT group were treated with PDT on day 7 after tumor modeling. All mice were euthanized on day 14, and tissues and blood were collected for analysis (Fig. 4A).

Flow cytometric analysis was performed to assess CD3 + CD4+ and CD3 + CD8+ cell populations in the spleen. The proportion of CD3 + CD4+ cells in the PDT-Dexs group was significantly higher than that in the Control-Dexs group (23.9% ± 0.6% vs. 25.7% ± 0.5%, P < 0.01), with the combination treatment group showing the highest proportion. A similar trend was observed for CD3 + CD8+ cells (20.9% ± 1.0% vs. 23.1% ± 0.8%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). Serum ELISA revealed that serum levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2 were significantly elevated in the PDT-Dexs group compared with that in the Control-Dexs group (all P < 0.05). In contrast, the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β were highest in the Control-Dexs group (both P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrate that PDT-Dexs elicit a robust immune response in mice and that combination therapy with PDT-Dexs and PDT produces stronger effects than PDT-Dexs or PDT alone.

PDT-Dexs inhibit glioma cell growth in vivo

Tumor growth in the four groups was monitored using an IVIS imaging system on day14 after tumor modeling. PDT-Dexs monotherapy inhibited glioma growth to a certain extent (Control-Dexs vs. PDT-Dexs, P < 0.001), although the effects were not as significant as those observed in the combination treatment group (Fig. 5A, B). Thin brain slices from each group were imaged, and the tumor volumes were measured. The combined PDT + PDT-Dexs group treatment exhibited the strongest antitumor effect, with tumor volume significantly reduced compared to all other groups (10.9 ± 3.1 mm³, all P < 0.001 vs. Other groups). The results were consistent with the IVIS findings (Fig. 5C, D). These results demonstrate that both PDT-Dexs monotherapy and PDT alone effectively inhibit glioma growth in vivo, and their combination exerts a synergistic antitumor effect.

Histopathological examination of orthotopic tumors

Representative HE staining results are shown in Fig. 5E. IHC staining revealed greater CD8 + T-cell infiltration in the tumor tissue from mice in the combined treatment group than in those in the other groups (82.3 ± 7.3, all P < 0.001 vs. Other groups). In contrast, the number of infiltrating FoxP3 + Treg cells was significantly lower in the combined treatment group than that in the other groups (5.2 ± 1.2, all P < 0.001 vs. Other groups) (Fig. 5F, G). Finally, IHC for Ki-67 and TUNEL assays were performed on the tumor sections. The proliferative activity of the tumor cells in the combined treatment group was significantly lower than that in the PDT/PDT-Dexs monotherapy groups (PDT + PDT-Dexs 16.5 ± 5.3% vs. PDT-Dexs 41.8 ± 3.1%, PDT 38.5 ± 2.4%, both P < 0.001) (Fig. 5H, I). TUNEL assays revealed that PDT-Dexs induced tumor cell apoptosis compared with the controls, although the effect was lower than that observed with PDT (Control-Dexs 4.5 ± 1.9% vs. PDT-Dexs 10.8 ± 2.5%, PDT 18.8 ± 2.3%, both P < 0.001) (Fig. 5J, K). These findings illustrate that PDT-Dexs suppress tumor growth by affecting immune cell infiltration and inducing tumor cell apoptosis.

Survival analysis and in vivo experiments in nude mice

Survival experiments and in vivo experiments using a nude mouse model were subsequently conducted (Fig. 6A). The survival of C57BL/6J mice was prolonged in all treatment groups compared with the controls with the PDT + PDT-Dex group exhibiting the longest survival period (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). However, PDT-Dexs did not inhibit tumor growth in vivo in immunodeficient nude mouse (Control-Dexs vs. PDT-Dexs, P = 0.82) (Fig. 6C, D). In addition, immunodeficient nude mice treated with PDT-Dexs did not exhibit better survival outcomes relative to control mice (Control-Dexs vs. PDT-Dexs, P = 0.94) (Fig. 6E). These results indicate that PDT-Dexs exert antitumor effects in immune-competent mice, indirectly confirming that their efficacy is achieved primarily through immune system regulation and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Finally, long-term safety was examined in vivo. HE staining revealed no damage to major organs in PDT-Dex-treated mice, demonstrating that PDT-Dexs are safe in vivo (Figure S7).

Discussion

In this study, we successfully generated glioma-antigen-loaded Dexs through PDT and investigated their functionality in vitro and in vivo. Our findings suggest that PDT-Dexs exert anti-tumor effects through the following mechanism: (1) PDT-Dexs directly promote the maturation of immature DCs, leading to the release of immune-stimulating cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α. (2) PDT-Dexs are phagocytosed by receptor DCs in vivo. Peptide-MHC complexes on exosome membrane are directly internalized into the MHC of receptor DCs or antigenic peptides are processed within the DCs and re-presented via surface MHC complexes. These DCs then present antigens to naive T cells in regional draining lymph nodes, promoting their differentiation into effector T cells, which then infiltrate the tumor microenvironment to kill tumor cells. (3) PDT-Dexs directly present antigens to T cells, promoting their activation. Activated T cells secrete cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, thereby enhancing systemic immune response. (4) These actions collectively facilitate the killing of tumor cells by effector T cells, leading to immunogenic cell death and the release of molecules such as HMGB1, ATP, and CRT. These danger-associated molecules are then phagocytosed and processed by antigen-presenting cells, triggering a new round of immune response (Fig. 7).

Graphical abstract for illustrating the mechanism underlying the antitumor immunogenicity of PDT-Dexs

Malignant gliomas, especially GBM, are characterized by rapid infiltrative growth, which makes complete surgical resection impossible [37, 38]. Therefore, surgery must be combined with additional therapies concurrent chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy to achieve effective tumor control. Immunotherapeutic approaches mainly include immune checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell transfer, tumor vaccines and oncolytic viruses [39]. However, the highly immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment remains a major obstacle to the success of immunotherapy in glioma [40]. Ameliorating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and eliciting anti-tumor immune response are therefore essential for the success of immunotherapy against gliomas.

DCs are the most potent antigen-presenting cells and are characterized by their ability to efficiently process antigens and stimulate T cells [41]. Immune cells involved in both the innate and adaptive immune systems, such as DCs, T cells, and B cells, can release large quantities of exosomes via complex regulatory mechanisms [42]. The recent phase 3 clinical trial demonstrated that autologous DC vaccine DCVax-L, when added to standard care, significantly extended median overall survival to 22.4 months from surgery in patients newly diagnosed with GBM [43]. While this validates the clinical potential of DC-based immunotherapy, its personalized cell therapy format presents inherent challenges in manufacturing scalability and logistical complexity.

Dexs express high levels of functional MHC class-I and class-II peptide complexes, which effectively induce neoantigen-specific CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell activation [44]. Several advantages of Dexs over DC vaccines have been proposed in the literature: (1) Dexs display 10–100 times more than surface peptide-MHC-II complexes DCs; (2) Additionally, the lipid composition of Dexs provides high stability, allowing storage at -80 °C for up to six months; (3) Dex membranes contain NK receptor ligands; (4) Their optimal tissue compatibility and small size enable broader distribution in lymph nodes and delivery across various immune cell types without reliance on chemotactic signals; (5) Importantly, Dexs shows no response to immunosuppressive molecules [45]. Thus, this study introduces PDT-Dexs as a novel ‘subcellular’ vaccine paradigm. By recapitulating and potentially amplifying the immunogenic effects of DC vaccines in a stable, cell-free vehicle, PDT-Dexs may overcome scalability challenges.

Recent clinical evidence confirms that PDT improves GBM outcomes, yet paradoxically associates with a higher rate of distant recurrence, highlighting a gap between local treatment efficacy and the establishment of systemic, protective immunity [46]. Mechanistically, PDT is known to induce immunogenic cell death and modulate the tumor microenvironment [23]. Our PDT-Dexs strategy builds on this foundation by engineering a systemic immune response: by using the antigenic and immunostimulatory cargo from PDT-treated tumor cells to generate a DC-derived exosome vaccine, thereby transforming a localized, transient immunogenic event into a potent and systemic “vaccine” effect.

PDT combination therapies play a major role in glioma management. Intraoperative PDT can selectively kill tumor cells that infiltrate the brain parenchyma. The effectiveness of chemotherapy in glioma is limited because the BBB and blood-brain tumor barrier prevent the delivery of most chemotherapeutic drugs to the brain [47]. Moreover, PDT has been shown to open the BBB and blood-brain tumor barrier to a certain extent, allowing improved chemotherapeutic into the brain [48]. In our study, combination treatment with PDT and PDT-Dexs in glioma-bearing mice induced more persistent systemic anti-tumor effects and significantly prolonged survival of the mice.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the finding that PDT-Dexs enhance anti-glioma T cell activation is based on tumor lysate. Future studies using defined antigen models (e.g., OVA/OT-I) are needed to confirm enhanced cross-presentation of specific antigens. Second, while PDT was employed for its ability to induce immunogenic cell death, a direct comparison between PDT-generated tumor lysate and conventional lysate (e.g., freeze-thaw) was not performed. Future studies directly comparing the immunogenicity of these approaches in the context of exosome vaccines would further elucidate the specific benefits of PDT. Third, therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in a syngeneic mouse model, which does not fully replicate the genetic and immunological complexity of human GBM. Validation in patient-derived xenografts or humanized models would improve clinical relevance. Fourth, although HMME is a well-established photosensitizer with known tumor-targeting properties, direct monitoring of its biodistribution was not performed, which would have provided more definitive evidence of photosensitizer delivery. Then, the safety of combining PDT with an immune-stimulating vaccine requires further assessment. Finally, critical translational parameters - including stability, scalability, and batch-to-batch consistency of PDT-Dexs production - remain to be established prior to pursuing further clinical development.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an antigen-loaded exosome (PDT-Dexs) derived from DCs pulsed with PDT-treated glioma cells. PDT-Dexs promoted the maturation of homologous DCs and the activation of T cells in vitro. PDT combined with PDT-Dexs improved anti-tumor immunity, and enhanced the efficacy of PDT treatment. Our results not only elucidate the mechanisms underlying PDT-induced antitumor immunity in glioma but also highlight the potential of PDT-Dexs as an exosome-based vaccine for patients with glioma.
