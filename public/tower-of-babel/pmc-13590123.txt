Nuclear speckle-associated perispeckle networks partition active chromatin into gene expression niches
Authors: Neha Chivukula Venkata, Jiah Kim, Saireet Misra, Gabriel P Faber, Atabek Bektash, Pankaj Chaturvedi, Gabriela Andrea Hernandez Gonzalez, Joseph Dopie, Masato T Kanemaki, Kyu Young Han, Yaron Shav-Tal, Andrew S Belmont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYA subset of genes, reproducibly positioned adjacent to nuclear speckles (NS), amplify their expression only with NS contact. However, genome-wide gene expression differences inversely correlate with differences in NS distance. To resolve this paradox, we hypothesized the existence of additional “niches” away from, but spatially correlated with NS, that would enhance gene expression of adjacent genes. Analyzing NS-related proteins led us to identify two dynamic multi-protein perispeckle patterns extending outwards from NS. Highly active chromosome regions weakly associated with NS show close, NS-independent association with these perispeckle patterns. These perispeckle patterns persist after NS are eliminated. Whereas NS-associated genes are downregulated upon NS depletion, perispeckle-pattern associated genes are upregulated. Transcripts expressed from model intron-containing versus intronless genes associate differentially with these two patterns. We suggest that the interchromatin space is partitioned into additional gene expression “niches”, surrounding and extending from NS, that may be involved in mRNA and gene dynamics.

INTRODUCTION

Nuclear speckles (NS) were first suggested to act as a gene expression hub for a subset of active genes based on a survey of ~20 active genes using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH).1,2 This model has now been validated by multiple orthogonal genome-wide approaches, including TSA-seq, SPRITE, and highly multiplexed immuno-FISH.3–8 More specifically, ~50–60% of the highest expressed (top 5%) genes in the genome localize in the top decile of nuclear speckle proximity,4,7 showing mean distances to NS of just several hundred nm.4 Moreover, the subset of genes localized near NS corresponds to higher GC-content, shorter genes with fewer introns, and housekeeping genes.4,8 Previous models of genome organization include the binary model of low gene expression in lamina-associated domains (LADs) and higher expression in inter-LAD regions (iLADs)9,10 and the radial position model in which levels of gene expression were correlated to nuclear radial position.11–13 We recently proposed a new model in which gene expression is more tightly correlated with distance to NS, which are themselves nonrandomly radially positioned.4,7,8

The HSPA1 locus is in the top ~1% of the genome in speckle proximity in both mouse and human cells based on TSA-seq scores. Approximately 90–95% of HSPA1 alleles are prepositioned at NS prior to heat shock.7,14,15 However, for the ~5–10% of alleles positioned away from NS, live cell imaging revealed a striking, multi-fold increase in heat-shock-induced HSPA1 transgene expression occurring within several minutes of HSPA1 transgene movement and contact with NS.14,16 We described this phenomenon as “gene expression amplification with NS contact”. Similar amplification of gene expression with NS association was inferred for several genes flanking the HSPA1 locus,14 for the HSPH1 gene,7 and for several hundred p53-responsive genes.17 Additionally, NS proximity has been shown to increase the efficiency of RNA splicing.18

Together, these observations suggested that actual contact with NS was required to boost gene expression. However, a genomic comparison of four human cell lines instead showed that differences in distances of genes to NS among these cell lines were inversely correlated with differences in the expression levels of these genes.7 Importantly, this inverse correlation was observed for changes in the relative differences in NS distances rather than absolute differences. These two sets of observations therefore created an apparent paradox. Whereas the microscopy approaches suggested close proximity or actual contact with NS was required for NS-related boosting of gene expression, the cell line comparative genomic analysis instead found that genes positioned far versus moderate distances to NS showed a similar increase in gene expression as genes positioned moderate versus close distances to NS.

To resolve this paradox, we hypothesized that just as certain genes might need to actually contact NS to enhance their gene expression, other genes might need to contact a different nuclear body/compartment to enhance their gene expression. If these putative additional nuclear bodies/compartments were themselves spatially correlated with NS, then a gene movement leading to actual contact with these nuclear bodies/compartments would appear by SON TSA-seq as a relative movement toward but not close to NS, resolving the apparent paradox.

Here we describe identification of several markers for each of two interwoven perispeckle patterns that surround NS but also spread outwards throughout the nuclear interior from NS and are flanked by transcriptionally active chromosome regions. Both perispeckle patterns persist after NS elimination. Together, these perispeckle patterns meet our predictions of additional novel compartment(s) serving as alternative gene expression hubs for a subset of highly active genes not associated with NS.

RESULTS

Screening for potential markers of alternative “gene expression hubs”

Previously, we described two types of chromosome regions with similarly elevated levels of gene expression. Both types of these “gene expression hot-zones”, averaging hundreds of kbp in size, aligned with SON TSA-seq local maxima.4,8 Whereas the high-amplitude, “Type I” SON TSA-seq peaks corresponded to chromosome regions strongly associated with NS, low-amplitude, “Type II” SON TSA-seq peaks corresponded to chromosome regions with weak association with NS.8

In the Introduction, we postulated the existence of a nuclear compartment(s), itself spatially correlated with NS, that might act as an alternative gene expression hub for genes not closely associated with NS, including those lying within Type II gene expression hot-zones (Figure 1A). We hypothesized that such a compartment(s) might lie adjacent to and/or extend outwards from NS. Thus, shifts of chromosome regions toward this compartment(s) would score as increases in NS TSA-seq scores.

We also previously applied tyramide signal amplification coupled to mass spectrometry (TSA-MS) to identify NS-enriched proteins.19 Similarly to TSA-seq, TSA-MS exploits the exponential decay of tyramide-biotin free radicals as they diffuse outwards from the immunostained target. Given the measured ~1-μm radius of this spreading,4 we realized that tyramide labeling of proteins would extend beyond the NS to proteins in a significant volume surrounding each NS.

Whereas 74% of the top 91 ranked proteins had been previously reported as colocalizing with NS, the next 92–200 top ranked proteins showed a sharp decrease in previously reported NS colocalization to ~13% yet showed a similar significant enrichment in proteins linked functionally to various steps of gene expression.19 We reasoned these moderately-ranked proteins were likely enriched in transcriptionally active regions surrounding NS and thus were candidates for proteins marking our hypothesized alternative gene expression compartment(s) (yellow regions in Figure 1A).

We identified 25 candidates from this list of moderately enriched proteins based on their non-diffuse distribution within the interchromatin space (ICS), as reported in the literature and/or the Human Protein Atlas.20 As a first screen, we used transient expression or immunostaining to identify candidate proteins exhibiting non-diffuse staining patterns extending out from NS into areas of low DNA-staining (Figure 1B, left).

Among these 25 proteins, essentially all showed some type of nondiffuse pattern, with foci of higher intensity throughout the nucleoplasm (Figure S1). However, there was a continuum in the extent of the nuclear area occupied outside of NS by higher than background intensities. Proteins such as SRRM2 and RBM25 showed little staining outside of NS, while proteins such as DDX41 and PNN showed staining occupying most of the nucleus (Figures S1A–S1C). We focused on proteins which showed intermediate occupancy between these extremes (Figures S1B and S1C), concentrated within and/or surrounding NS, and showed nondiffuse staining extending outwards from NS, in some cases all the way to the nuclear periphery (Figure 1B; Figures S1A–S1E).

Among these proteins, we observed two distinct categories of staining, as exemplified by DDX39B and THRAP3, which both occupied ~40% of the nuclear area outside of NS (Figure S1C) and were previously reported as overlapping with and/or surrounding NS, respectively.21,22 Here we show THRAP3 and DDX39B also form thread-like projections that radiate outwards from NS and throughout the nuclear interior (Figures 1C–1F). Additionally, DDX39B thread-like projections frequently connect neighboring NS (Figure 1D, inset box 2). In the first “THRAP3” category, protein concentrations were high surrounding but not concentrated within NS (Figure 1C; Figure S1A). In the second “DDX39B” category, protein concentrations were highest within NS (Figure 1D; Figure S1A). These differences in protein concentrations within versus outside NS were distinguished quantitatively by cumulative protein intensities (Figure S1A, bottom). Additional proteins (i.e., SAFB, HNRNPM) showed non-diffuse nuclear staining uncorrelated with NS (Figures S1A and S1E) that we therefore did not consider further.

Next, we asked whether proteins within the same category would colocalize extensively—thus marking the same nuclear compartment. We first used CRISPR-mediated knock-ins to construct U2OS cell lines expressing either HaloTag-SON plus EGFP-THRAP3 or EGFP-SON plus mCherry-DDX39B from their endogenous loci. We then used immunofluorescence to ask which other candidate proteins overlapped the DDX39B or THRAP3 patterns (Figures 1B–1D). For MAGOH, due to the lack of a suitable antibody, we instead used lentivirus transduction to express mCherry-MAGOH and THRAP3 and DDX39B immunostaining.

Through this secondary screening, we identified additional proteins with intermediate occupancy values sharing high colocalization with either THRAP3 or DDX39B. THRAP3 (BCLAF2) and its paralog BCLAF1 overlapped extensively (Figures 1B and 1E). Additionally, PLRG1 overlapped with THRAP3 outside of NS but concentrated within NS similarly to DDX39B (Figure S1F). MAGOH and RBM8A (Y14) instead overlapped extensively with DDX39B (Figure 1F; Figure S1G). Members of the same pattern (THRAP3 versus BCLAF1, RBM8A versus DDX39B) showed a higher degree of overlap outside NS than the overlap between members of the two different patterns (THRAP3 versus DDX39B), as measured by both their correlation coefficient and average fraction of colocalization (Mander’s coefficient) outside of NS (Figures 1G and 1H; Figure S1H). Moreover, the overlap between members of the same pattern approached that of our positive control-endogenously tagged mCherry-DDX39B versus DDX39B immunostaining. In contrast, several proteins (SF3B1, SNRNPA1, SNRPB2) only partially overlapped both DDX39B and THRAP3; we did not characterize these further.

In conclusion, we identified two intertwined multi-protein staining patterns subdividing the ICS. Moving forward, we focused on the differential localization of THRAP3 versus DDX39B as markers of these two different patterns that we named Perispeckle THRAP3 Pattern (PSTP) and Perispeckle DDX39B Pattern (PSDP).

THRAP3 and DDX39B networks are spatially adjacent within chromatin-depleted nuclear regions

At the higher resolution of STORM super-resolution light microscopy, THRAP3 and DDX39B staining still appear closely juxtaposed but more spatially distinct (Figure 2A) than by deconvolution wide-field microscopy (Figure S1H). THRAP3 and DDX39B networks extend beyond NS and occupy a significant area within the nucleus. We next asked how much of the network overlaps with DNA by expressing H2B-HaloTag and comparing the distribution of chromatin relative to the endogenous distribution of THRAP3, DDX39B and SON, using deconvolution wide-field microscopy. Both THRAP3 and DDX39B concentrations predominantly occupy low-intensity regions of H2B staining (Figures 2B–2D). Quantitatively, the median fraction of overlap of protein with H2B is < 0.25 for both THRAP3 and DDX39B, suggesting that these networks occur largely in the DNA-depleted ICS (Figure 2D).

THRAP3 and DDX39B perispeckle patterns are maintained independently of NS and vary independently of each other after transcriptional inhibition

We next asked whether the maintenance of these perispeckle patterns was dependent or independent of NS. Previous work concluded that SON and SRRM2 together act as core scaffolding proteins of NS,23 based on the observation that simultaneous SON and SRRM2 knockdown led to the simultaneous loss of the co-concentration of several other known NS proteins within NS-like bodies. However, none of the PSTP or PDSP markers were examined in that previous study.

To address this, we used simultaneous biallelic knockdown of SON and SRRM2 using an improved auxin-inducible degron (AID2) system24 in HCT116 cells (Figure 2E; Figure S2A) (see STAR Methods). NS, identified by SON and SRRM2 concentrations, shrink and then largely disappear within 0–4 h of 5-Ph-IAA treatment (Figure 2F; Figure S2A). Live-cell imaging shows that SON concentrations inside NS remain elevated as NS initially shrink (Figure 2F, 20–40 min), consistent with SON existing as a condensate within NS (Video S1).

We next expressed a HaloTag-DDX39B construct and performed live-cell imaging. Within 20–25 min after 5-Ph-IAA treatment, cells showed a ~20% reduction of DDX39B intensity within the regions formerly occupied by NS after SON and SRRM2 double knockdown (DKD) while showing a concomitant increase in DDX39B intensities in the network outside of NS (Figure 2F). At later time points (>45 min), NS SON levels drop to a few percent of their value at 20 min (Figure 2F insets top), while DDX39B levels within the same NS decrease by ~25% (Figure 2F insets bottom).

Importantly, the overall nuclear DDX39B intranuclear distribution is largely maintained, including the DDX39B concentrations over regions previously occupied by SON in NS. Both THRAP3 and DDX39B maintain similar staining networks before and after NS depletion (Figure 2G). Using a skeletonized representation (Figure S2B), we observed either no or minor changes in total skeleton lengths for THRAP3 and DDX39B after elimination of NS (Figure 2H), further suggesting perispeckle networks are maintained independently of NS.

Additionally, we identified physiological perturbations that uncouple the spatial and temporal adjacency of the THRAP3 and DDX39B perispeckle patterns (Figures S2C–S2K). Between 0 and 2 h of transcriptional inhibition by DRB (5,6-dichloro-1-beta-D-ribofuranosylbenzimidazole), the thread-like, diffuse network of THRAP3 staining outside of NS consolidates into fewer but more distinct threads, that then breakup into distinct foci aligned along linear paths, plus larger concentrations adjacent to and surrounding NS (Figure S2C, Video S2), whereas the DDX39B distribution remains relatively unchanged (Figure S2). Similarly, the skeletonized representations of these perispeckle patterns show reductions in total skeleton length and connection numbers for THRAP3 but little change for DDX39B (Figures S2E and S2F). We observed similar disruption for BCLAF1, another marker for PSTP, as for THRAP3 (Figures S2G and S2I–S2K), while RBM8A, another marker for PSDP, showed slight differences from DDX39B in the perturbation of the network (Figures S2H, S2J, and S2K).

We conclude that THRAP3 and DDX39B perispeckle patterns largely persist even after elimination of NS and thus are not dependent on NS for their maintenance. We further conclude that although the two perispeckle patterns are normally spatially adjacent, they are not mutually dependent on each other.

THRAP3 and DDX39B networks are both spatially and temporally adjacent but form recurrently within the same nuclear DNA-depleted spaces

We next examined the temporal dynamics of THRAP3 and DDX39B networks (Figure 3; Figure S3), starting with the previously described U2OS cell lines expressing the combinations of HaloTag-SON and EGFP-THRAP3 or EGFP-SON and mCherry-DDX39B. To contrast the dynamics of proteins from the same perispeckle pattern with the dynamics of proteins from the two different perispeckle patterns, we used CRISPR to tag a third protein in these cell lines-either SNAP-tag-BCLAF1, SNAP-tag-DDX39B, or SNAP-tag-RBM8A-expressed also from their endogenous gene loci.

We first used fixed samples to identify low-light, live-cell imaging conditions allowing visualization of perispeckle pattern dynamics above background noise (see STAR Methods). A running average of ±2 time frames-each separated by 2.5 seconds-facilitated visualization of perispeckle dynamics over tens of seconds to minutes time scales (Video S3) (See STAR Methods).

Measuring THRAP3, DDX39B, and SON dynamics in live cells over a 75 s timescale, using this running average approach (Figures 3A–3C; Figures S3A and S3B, Video S4), confirmed the minimal overlap but juxtaposed positions of DDX39B and THRAP3 foci outside of NS previously visualized in fixed samples (Figures 2A–2D). DDX39B and THRAP3 foci dynamics appeared asynchronous over time (Figure 3A; Figures S3A and S3B, S3G), in contrast to the more extensive spatial overlapping and synchrony observed between members of the same perispeckle patterns (THRAP3 versus BCLAF1 or DDX39B versus RBM8A) (Figure 3C; Figures S3C–S3F, S3H, and S3I; Video S5 and S6). An increased overlap between THRAP3 and DDX39B was observed in temporal projections covering the entire 75 s interval (Figures 3B and 3C). Thus, THRAP3 and DDX39B perispeckle patterns can occupy the same nuclear spaces but at times separated by tens of seconds.

Finally, we examined dynamics of NS relative to both DNA and THRAP3 or DDX39B perispeckle patterns over 12–14 h (Figures 3D and 3E). NS dynamics included both fusion and fission events (Figures 3D and 3E, arrows, yellow outlines), while the thread-like protrusions of THRAP3 and DDX39B surrounding NS moved with the NS and continued to extend and retract from these NS, largely within DNA-depleted regions (Video S7; Figures 3D and 3E, dashed outlines). Overall positions of DNA-depleted versus DNA-enriched nuclear regions appear stable over multiple hours.

Thus, we conclude that PSDP and PSTP are dynamic on a timescale of tens of seconds, remaining generally both spatially and temporally adjacent to each other while showing asynchronous dynamics.

Alignment of chromatin domains with high gene expression adjacent to rather than overlapping with perispeckle patterns

To address the positioning of transcriptionally active sites relative to the perispeckle patterns, we compared the position of perispeckle patterns to two proxies of sites of high gene expression-RNA Pol II foci and chromosome sites with high histone acetylation.

Immunostaining against RNAPII C-terminal tail modifications reveals ~60 nm diameter foci, originally termed “RNA Pol II factories,” that map ultra-structurally near sites of transcription.25–27 Initially proposed as actual transcription sites,28,29 recent work suggests Pol II clusters interact with other co-regulators within transcriptional condensates.30 Multiple studies have shown the close proximity of these Pol II clusters to sites of active transcription, with some correlating increased numbers of these clusters with higher gene expression levels.14,31 While an increased density of RNA Pol II Ser5p and Ser2p foci surrounds the NS periphery,4,32 large numbers of RNA Pol II Ser2p and Ser5p foci away from NS distribute throughout the nuclear interior in no obvious pattern.4

However, immunostaining and 3D SIM super-resolution imaging now reveal the spatial proximity of RNA Pol II Ser2p foci localized away from NS near the THRAP3 perispeckle pattern (Figure 4A), as further supported by comparison of distance distributions of the nearest THRAP3 foci to these RNA Pol II Ser2p foci versus the overall DNA staining (Figure 4A, right image).

Sequence-based mapping has shown high levels of H3K27ac and H3K9ac over chromosome regions with high gene expression levels,33 with the highest levels of histone acetylation mapping to NS-associated chromosome regions,4,34,35 consistent with older reports of hyperacetylated histone staining adjacent to NS.36

Immunostaining now reveals concentrations of H3K27ac and H3K9ac localized adjacent to but mostly non-overlapping with THRAP3 and DDX39B concentrations (Figures 4B–4F; Figure S4). More specifically, segmented images of THRAP3 and DDX39B versus H3K27ac and H3K9ac signals (Figures 4B–4E) show substantial acetylated histone concentrations adjacent to rather than overlapping with the perispeckle patterns, while line profiles show peaks of H3K9ac and K3K27ac often alternating with peaks of THRAP3 and DDX39B (Figure 4F). Again, Euclidean distance measurements of pixels within the hyperacetylated histone foci support this closer association to THRAP3 and DDX39B foci as compared to overall DNA (DAPI) staining (Figure S4, right images). Compared to the total H2B signal, regions of elevated H3K27ac and H3K9ac signals showed a slightly but significantly higher overlap with DDX39B foci; an increased overlap of H3K9ac but not H3K27ac concentrations was also seen for THRAP3 foci (Figure 4G). However, the overlap of THRAP3 and DDX39B foci with both H3K27ac and H3K9ac signals was still <0.35 (Mander’s coefficient), showing that the majority of hyperacetylated histone foci positioned adjacent to rather than centered over the perispeckle patterns.

In summary, two markers of sites of high gene expression-RNA Pol II and hyperacetylated histone foci-largely align adjacent to perispeckle patterns and closer than bulk chromatin.

Gene expression hot zones localize near PSTP and PSDP as measured by TSA-seq

We next used TSA-seq to ask whether chromosomal regions positioned away from NS but with high levels of gene expression would localize as postulated near these perispeckle patterns. We combined the use of dithiothreitol (DTT) to improve the TSA-seq spatial resolution4 with the “super-saturation” TSA-seq 2.0 protocol,7 which minimizes the cell numbers required for TSA-seq.

Both in HCT116 (Figures 5A–5C) and U2OS cells (Figures S5A and S5B), DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq peak and valley patterns qualitatively parallel the SON TSA-seq pattern. SON TSA-seq valleys typically correspond to LADs low in both gene density and gene expression; SON TSA-seq peaks represent subregions within the “inter-LAD (iLAD)” genome domains between LADs.4,9,10 Type I and Type II SON TSA-seq local maxima align with genomic regions showing strong versus weak NS association, respectively; both Type I and II regions are locally elevated in gene expression levels relative to flanking iLAD regions but vary in the characteristics of the genes that they contain.8 Thus, both Type I (yellow highlights) and Type II (blue highlights) peaks align with peaks of nascent transcripts (Bru-seq) and transcriptional initiation (RNA Pol II Ser5p ChIP-seq) (Figures 5A and 5D). Additionally, both Type I and Type II peaks align with peaks of H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation over this resolution scale of 100s of kbp (Figure 5A).37

Quantitatively, however, the patterns of SON, DDX39B, and THRAP3 local maxima differ. We interpret DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq peaks as corresponding to genomic regions that associate closest to foci of DDX39B and THRAP3 staining. Consistent with our microscopy findings, the highest amplitude DDX39B and THRAP3 scores and peaks do align with the highest SON TSA-seq scores (Figure 5B) and Type I SON TSA-seq peaks (Figures 5A and 5C; Figure S5A). However, we observe a disproportionately higher amplitude of DDX39B and THRAP3 relative to smaller Type II SON TSA-seq peaks (Figure 5A; Figure S5A), as appreciated by comparing SON, DDX39B, and THRAP3 TSA-seq pile-up plots for Type I versus Type II peaks (Figure 5C). Whereas the average local maxima for SON are near +1.5 for Type I peaks versus < −0.25 for Type II peaks, the average local maxima for DDX39B and THRAP3 correspond to small positive peaks that show less disparity in magnitude between Type I versus II peaks. This disproportionality can also be appreciated in DDX39B or THRAP3 versus SON TSA-seq scatterplots, calculated over the entire genome, showing a lower slope for higher versus lower values of SON TSA-seq (Figure 5B). Finally, DDX39B and THRAP3 peaks are significantly broader and flatter-extending over most of the iLADs-than their corresponding SON TSA-seq peaks (Figures 5A and 5C; Figure S5A).

We interpret this broader shape of DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq peaks relative to SON TSA-seq peaks as reflecting the extent to which DDX39B and THRAP3 networks extend throughout the nuclear interior such that iLAD regions are never that distant from the nearest DDX39B or THRAP3 foci. Additionally, we interpret the overall similarity in the patterns of DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq over Type II peaks as consistent with the spatial resolution of TSA-seq (several 100s of nm d1/2 decay constant), the close juxtapositioning and partial overlapping in space and time of the DDX39B and THRAP3 patterns (Figures 1, 2, 3, and 4), and the averaging over millions of cells used per TSA-seq sample.

In summary, the TSA-seq results predict that Type I and II SON TSA-seq peaks are most closely positioned at NS (Type I) and/or perispeckle patterns (Type II) relative to flanking iLAD regions but that iLAD chromosome regions flanking Type II peaks are nearly as close to the perispeckle patterns as the peaks themselves.

Close proximity of perispeckle patterns to sites of elevated gene expression

To validate the localization of sites of high gene expression near perispeckle patterns predicted by TSA-seq, we used OligoPAINT DNA FISH to localize 7 Type I or 8 Type II SON TSA-seq peaks (Table S1) relative to DDX39B and THRAP3 perispeckle patterns in HCT116 and U2OS cells (Figure 5B, highlighted regions).

As predicted, the aggregated Type I peaks localized close to NS while the aggregated Type II peaks localized at greater and more variable distances from NS (Figures 5E and 5F; Figures S5C–S5F). In contrast, both Type I and II peaks were positioned close to THRAP3 or DDX39B foci, including alleles positioned away from NS (Figures 5E and 5F; Figures S5C and S5D). This proximity of Type I and II peaks to THRAP3 or DDX39B foci was significantly closer than that observed over the entire genome, as shown by the distance distributions of the DNA staining (DAPI) from THRAP3 or DDX39B foci (Figure 5F; Figure S5D).

Next, we painted chromosome “trajectories” from the nearest LAD/iLAD boundary to either the apex of a Type I peak or to the apex of a Type II peak in U2OS cells (Figure S6). We designed probes to target 7–9 100 kb regions spaced approximately evenly along these trajectories (Figures S6A and S6C). In most cells (70–90%), both chromosome trajectories showed multiple, individual DNA FISH spots consistent with a partly extended iLAD chromosome trajectory. Interestingly, a high fraction of these extended trajectories aligned relative to the THRAP3 (40–51%) and, especially, the DDX39B staining foci (55–73%) (Figures S6B and S6D). While Type I trajectories were oriented with the TSA-seq peak trajectory apex positioned at least as close or closer to the nearest NS as compared to the flanking arm (Figure S6B), Type II trajectories were not obviously oriented, with all spots approximately equidistant from the nearest NS or THRAP3/DDX39B concentrations (Figure S6D), consistent with the smaller differences in distances predicted by TSA-seq between peak and flanking trajectory regions.

To compare the association of different trajectory regions with the perispeckle patterns and NS quantitatively, we segmented and divided the DNA-FISH arm signal into three parts- “Arm-close”, “Arm-center”, and “Arm-far”—based on their proximity to the trajectory apex (See STAR Methods). For the Type I trajectory, the apex was closer to SON, THRAP3 or DDX39B foci compared to more distal arm regions (Figure S6E). Moreover, the Arm-close, Arm-center, and Arm-far regions showed progressive increases in distances, respectively, relative to the nearest NS or protein foci edge. In contrast, the entire Type II iLAD chromosome trajectory typically was within several hundred nm of some part of the DDX39B and THRAP3 networks (Figure S6F), again consistent with the wider DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq peaks observed over Type II SON TSA-seq local maxima (Figure 5C). The Type II peak apex compared to the arm-far region was significantly closer to the THRAP3 foci (Figure S6F). However, the distances to the edge of the nearest DDX39B foci were similar for the apex and all three arm regions (Figure S6F), which might reflect the apparent higher alignment of the Type II overall trajectory with the DDX39B network noted previously.

Thus, both the TSA-seq and microscopy mapping support the proximity of chromosomal sites of high transcriptional activity with PSTP and PSDP. Additionally, the immuno-FISH mapping of individual chromosome trajectories is consistent with a close proximity of large iLAD regions flanking Type II peaks to perispeckle patterns as suggested by the TSA-seq.

Nuclear genome positioning relative to PSTP and PSDP is largely maintained after SON and SRRM2 DKD elimination of NS

We next asked whether chromosome positioning relative to PSTP and/or PSDP was dependent on maintenance of NS.

We performed DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq after 4 h of 5-Ph-IAA induced SON and SRRM2 DKD (Figure 6A; Figure S7A), the earliest time point showing maximum KD of both SON and SRRM2 expression (Figure 2E; Figure S2A) and shrinkage of NS (Figure 2F). NS were previously defined as polyadenylated RNA-rich “domains.”38,39 Indeed, we observed the dispersion of locally concentrated polyA-RNA after SON and SRRM2 DKD (Figure 6B), suggesting that loss of NS scaffolded by SON and SRRM2 condensates also functionally perturbed the local concentration of polyA-RNA.

Overall, NS elimination over this 4-h time period resulted in little change to THRAP3 and DDX39B TSA-seq patterns (Figure 6A; Figure S7A). Quantitative comparisons show a slight but largely statistically insignificant increase in DDX39B TSA-seq values after NS elimination (Figure S7A, bottom images). While THRAP3 TSA-seq shows a small but statistically significant overall decrease in signal after NS elimination (Figure S7A), this decrease appears to reflect a systematic variation in scaling across the genome, with slightly more negative values over both THRAP3 peaks and valleys (Figure S7A, top images). The reasons for these subtle genome-wide changes in DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq remain unclear but may be related to small shifts in DDX39B and THRAP3 intranuclear distribution after NS KD.

Despite these small changes in scaling, we did not see significant or specific changes in the overall patterns of peaks corresponding to NS and perispeckle pattern-associated domains (Figure 6A; Figures S7A–S7D). Pearson’s correlation coefficients of DDX39B and THRAP3 TSA-seq scores comparing 5-Ph-IAA versus DMSO treatments are similar to those comparing experimental replicates (Figures S7B and S7D), while TSA-seq score percentile differences between datasets reveal small changes overall, with a low fraction of bins showing ±20 percentile shifts (Figure S7C).

Finally, we asked how genes differentially positioned relative to NS might change their expression as a function of SON and SRRM2 DKD. SON depletion is known to arrest cell growth by perturbing the cell cycle, particularly during mitosis.40,41 To minimize changes in gene expression due to changes in cell cycle progression, we applied an extended 16-h DKD of SON and SRRM2 as cells progressed from a G1-arrest into a late G1/early S phase block.

RNA-seq analysis revealed NS-proximal genes (differentially expressed genes (DEGs)) within the top SON TSA-seq decile were downregulated while genes within deciles 3–7 were upregulated (Figure 6C; Figure S7E). More specifically, ~89% of all DEGs within the top SON decile were downregulated (Figure S7E). Mapping DEGs within ±500 kb of Type I SON TSA-seq peaks showed an ~5.5-fold systematic bias toward decreased (897 genes) versus increased (162 genes) gene expression after SON and SRRM2 DKD (Figures 6D–6F; Table S1). In contrast, genes within ± 500 kb of Type II SON TSA-seq peaks showed a ~2-fold bias of increased (123 genes) versus decreased (62 genes) DEGs (Figures 6D–6F).

We conclude that chromosome positioning relative to PSTP and PSDP networks, including regions originally near NS, remains unchanged for at least several hours after NS elimination, suggesting that perispeckle patterns may maintain genome organization independent of NS, as defined by SON and SRRM2 concentrations. Despite this maintenance of genome organization, a significant number of genes in Type I peaks do appear dependent on NS rather than the remaining perispeckle patterns to sustain their normal levels of gene expression.

Interactions of specific RNAs with PSTP and PSDP

Most NS-enriched factors are involved in RNA processing and/or export, suggesting proximity of genes and RNAs to NS might increase overall gene expression levels through facilitating post-transcriptional RNA processing.18,42–45 Additionally, older cytological studies2,46 and newer sequencing-based approaches43,44,47 have suggested trafficking of certain RNAs through NS, possibly linked to their export.

Because marker proteins for PSTP and PSDP have similarly been connected to various aspects of RNA processing, stability, and export, we lastly asked whether PSTP and/or PSDP networks might also associate with specific pre-mRNAs and mRNAs. Because Type I versus Type II chromosome regions are enriched in genes with low versus high intron-content, respectively,8 we specifically compared the localization of RNAs from intronless versus intron-containing genes using two experimental systems (Figure S8).

Firstly, we used two cell lines carrying different, stably integrated, multi-copy transgene arrays48,49 that express at high levels producing 100s of nuclear RNPs. The “E6” cell line contains a synthetic transgene with β-globin derived sequences including 6 exons and 5 introns, whereas the “Mini-dystrophin” cell line (“mini-Dys”) contains a truncated dystrophin transgene with a single exon and no introns. Both transgene RNAs contain 24 MS2 repeats and both cell lines express the MS2 coat protein (MCP) tagged with YFP that binds MS2 RNA repeats. Secondly, to complement our transgene array results, we used HSPA1 and HSPH1 endogenous genes that also produce a high number of transcripts. Whereas the highly homologous HSPA1A and HSPA1B genes expressed from the HSPA1 locus (situated within a SON Type I peak) produce short transcripts less than 1kb with no introns, the 27 kb HSPH1 gene (resident within a SON Type II peak) contains 17 introns. Because the shorter HSPA1 genes are induced more quickly after HS than the much longer HSPH1 gene, we chose 30 min after HS for HSPA1 genes versus 60 min after HS for HSPH1, which maximized the number of nuclear RNPs present in the nucleus away from their transcription sites.

Both the intron-rich E6 and HSPH1 RNAs localized very close to THRAP3 foci in absolute distances and closer than both the intronless mini-dystrophin and HSPA1 RNAs (Figures S8A, S8B, S8E, S8F, S8I, and S8J). However, both intron-rich and intronless transcripts showed very close and similar distances to the nearest DDX39B foci (Figures S8C, S8D, S8G, S8H, S8I, and S8J). Distances to the nearest DDX39B and THRAP3 foci for all transcripts were significantly lower than their distances to NS (SON and SRRM2), indicating that a significant fraction of the transcripts associate with DDX39B and THRAP3 foci away from NS. Whereas both THRAP3 and DDX39B foci show a high degree of overlap with the intron-containing HSPH1 RNAs, the intronless HSPA1 RNAs show a pronounced higher fractional overlap specifically with DDX39B versus THRAP3 foci (Figure S8K).

Overall, these results suggest that within the ICS transcripts from intron-containing genes preferentially associate with PSTP while transcripts from both intron-containing and intronless genes show close association with PSDP.

DISCUSSION

Summary

Here we identified two “perispeckle patterns”, PSTP and PSDP, that both spatially correlate with NS (Figure 7A). Whereas PSDP components concentrate within NS, PSTP components surround but do not concentrate within NS, as defined by SON and SRRM2 concentration. Chromosomal regions with high gene expression levels not localized near NS frequently localize adjacent to the thread-like patterns of PSTP and PSDP. Additionally, transcripts from two model intron-rich genes associate with PSTP and PSDP while transcripts from two model intronless genes associate preferentially with PSDP. Both PSTP and PSDP persist and genome organization relative to both perispeckle patterns is largely maintained after NS elimination by SON and SRRM2 DKD (Figure 7B). Together, these perispeckle patterns meet our predictions of additional novel gene expression hub(s) that may spatially integrate transcription, RNA processing, and nuclear export for a subset of active genes away from NS (Figure 7C). The previous association of perispeckle marker proteins with RNA transport out of NS45,50–54 and the more recent association of PSDP with long-range, directional gene movement and movement of proteins between NS further support the possible role of perispeckle patterns as gene expression hubs.15,55

Model shows organization of genes relative to NS and DDX39B (PSDP) and THRAP3 (PSTP) perispeckle patterns

(A) Interchromatin (IC) space is further divided into two perispeckle patterns, which are flanked by transcriptionally active chromosomal loci and associate differentially with different RNAs as they move through IC space.

(B) After NS depletion, genome organization relative to PSTP and PSDP remains largely the same, although many Type I genes are downregulated.

(C) Positive regulation of gene expression may result from one set of genes associating with NS and another set of genes associating with perispeckle patterns either by gene movement toward hubs (top), movement or extension of NS and/or perispeckle patterns toward genes (middle), a combination of gene and hub movements (bottom), or nucleation of NS and/or perispeckle patterns near genes (not shown).

Perispeckle patterns are dynamic networks rather than stable nuclear bodies

Although we originally hypothesized Type II SON TSA-seq peaks as interacting with an alternative nuclear body/compartment of comparable stability to NS, instead both perispeckle networks are dynamic over tens of seconds to minutes. Perhaps these fast perispeckle dynamics are linked to their still largely unknown physiological function(s). Interestingly, DDX39B previously was suggested to regulate the entry and release of RNAs from phase-separated condensates in an ATP-dependent manner,56 while electron microscopy showed DDX39B associating with both the periphery of IGCs and thread-like projections extending outwards from IGCs into the ICS.22 DDX39B mediates assembly of export-competent mRNPs57 and is also essential for the export of spliced and intronless RNA.22,45,50–52 Finally, a time-resolved interactome of RNA-binding proteins on nascent mRNA showed the peak binding of multiple proteins within PSTP and PSDP networks occurs sequentially,58 consistent with RNPs moving sequentially through different “processing” condensates before and during nuclear export.

In a companion study, we observed directional bulk transfer of SON protein as small condensates between NS in ATP-dependent “connections” marked by dynamic DDX39B, MAGOH, and SON multi-phase protein concentrations.55 In a second companion study, we showed HSF1-dependent directional movements of HSPA1 genes to and from NS that both spatially and temporally correlate with extensions and retractions of DDX39B concentrations from NS.15

On a longer timescale, we visualized recurrent dynamics and coordinated reorganization of perispeckle patterns relative to the movements of NS within DNA-depleted spaces. The temporal stability of these DNA-depleted spaces contrasts with the known “constrained” but rapid diffusion of chromatin,59 suggesting that some still unidentified but more stable perispeckle structure(s) may restrict chromatin diffusion.

Relationship of perispeckle patterns to NS

SON and SRRM2 were considered to be required for NS integrity because their DKD led to dispersal of several other known NS proteins.23 Should the perispeckle patterns that largely persist after NS elimination then be considered as components of NS that persist after SON and SRRM2 DKD, with SON and SRRM2 necessary only for retention of a subset of NS proteins? Just as nucleolar GC proteins such as nucleolin can mix with the FC,60 we suggest PSDP components have the ability to mix with NS components and that SON and SRRM2 condensates might lie within larger PSDP condensates.

What functions might NS impart separately from PSDP? The observation of gene expression amplification with NS contact,14,17,61 plus the observed reduced expression specifically of a subset of genes positioned very close to NS after SON and SRRM2 KD, suggests that NS are indeed required for the increased expression of thousands of NS proximal genes. Conversely, genes close to NS whose expression remains unchanged after SON and SRRM2 DKD may be independent of the SON/SRRM2 nucleated NS but might still depend on perispeckle patterns. After SON and SRRM2 DKD, the small increase in perispeckle protein concentrations within the perispeckle networks away from NS may account for the increased expression of certain NS-distant perispeckle-associated genes.

Limitations of the study

Although we focused here on PSTP and PSDP, our initial screening suggested additional perispeckle patterns/protein condensates may exist that further subdivide the ICS. Even for PSTP and PSDP, we only identified several marker proteins for each. The initial screening itself was limited by the use of transgene expression and/or antibody staining, both of which may deviate in some cases from the true distribution of the endogenously expressed, unmodified proteins. Future studies are needed to more comprehensively survey the likely tens to hundreds of other proteins and RNAs comprising the PSTP, PSDP, and possibly other perispeckle patterns. While both perispeckle patterns showed fast dynamics, as visualized using a subset of marker proteins, we cannot exclude that other perispeckle components are more stable, conferring a higher degree of stability to an underlying perispeckle structure not yet visualized. How all these protein condensates and their components correlate with each other and other previously described nuclear bodies and condensates,62 as well as important nuclear functions, remains far from complete given the necessarily limited scope of our initial investigations reported here. Additionally, although we initiated some functional studies by examining effects of nuclear speckle elimination, we have not yet moved beyond correlation to test causality between chromosomal and RNA association with PSTP and PSDP networks and modulation of gene expression. Beyond gene expression, exploring the possible linkage between perispeckle patterns and genome stability is also anticipated, as multiple components of both PSTP and PSDP have been implicated in both R-loop resolution (THRAP3 and DDX39B)63,64 and DNA repair and genome stability (THRAP3, BCLAF1, RBM8A, DDX39B, PLRG1).53,65–68 Finally, we also anticipate explorations into the role of perispeckle patterns in gene motion,15 transport of proteins between NS,55 and possibly transport of proteins between NS and active genes.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell lines and cell culture

U2OS and HCT116 cells were authenticated by ATCC. Upon receiving the cell lines from ATCC, they were expanded using pyramid stocking as per the 4D Nucleome SOP (https://data.4dnucleome.org/resources/experimental-resources/cell-lines). After pyramid stocking, cell vials were thawed and cultured for no more than 10–12 passages before disposal, removing the need for additional authentication. The cell lines derived from these cells by genetic manipulation were validated after flow-sorting by genotyping and fluorescence microscopy of tagged proteins and comparing these distributions to endogenous protein distributions. After clone derivation, cells were frozen and after thawing, cells were cultured for no more than 10–12 passages. For these tagged cell lines, additional authentication was provided by the visualization of the fluorescently tagged proteins they expressed.

Cell lines were frequently monitored for mycoplasma contamination by DAPI staining.

U2OS cell lines containing E6 β-globin and mini-Dystrophin A1 transgenes were described previously (Mor et al., 2010; Brody et al., 2011). All cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Specifically, both U2OS and HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (UIUC cell media facility) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (VWR, cat# 97068–091, Lot #035B15). Cells were passaged (or harvested) at 70%–80% confluency using Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 25300062). U2OS cell lines with the E6 β-globin and mini-Dystrophin transgenes were cultured in low glucose containing DMEM (UIUC cell media facility). The E6 β-globin cell line was maintained with 10 μg/mL Puromycin (Puromycin Dihydrochloride, Gibco, cat# A1113803) and 0.54 mg/mL Zeocin (Gibco, cat# R25001). The mini-dystrophin A1 cell line was cultured with 50 μg/mL Hygromycin (Gibco, cat#10687010) and 0.54 mg/mL Zeocin (Gibco, cat# R25001). Cells were passaged using Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 25300062).

METHOD DETAILS

cDNA constructs and screening

For the screening of selected protein candidates, PCR amplicons were prepared from cDNA. For genes with multiple transcript variants, the longest protein-coding transcript sequence was chosen for PCR amplification and cloned into pEGFP-C1 vector as described previously (Dopie et al., 2020). Briefly, total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, cat#74104) and 1μg total RNA was reverse transcribed using the Protoscript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, cat# M0368S) to generate cDNA. Using primers containing the appropriate restriction enzyme sites (Table S1), cDNA amplicons were PCR amplified with Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, cat# M0491S), purified and cloned into pEGFP-C1 (originally obtained from Clonetech). The sequences of all plasmids generated were validated by Sanger sequencing before their use.

For transfections, ~120,000 U2OS cells were seeded per well in a 6-well plate containing 12mm diameter #1.5 coverslips (Electron Microscopy Sciences, cat#72230–01) and the next day, 500 ng of plasmid was transfected using 5 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#11668019) in OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 31985070) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, cells were fixed with freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, cat#P6148) in calcium- and magnesium-free phosphate buffered saline (CMF-PBS) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Cells were washed for 5 min 3 times with CMF-PBS and then either mounted directly using Mowiol-DABCO antifade mounting medium or processed further.

For H2B-HaloTag (Addgene #247338) transfections, U2OS EGFP-SON knock-in cells were seeded at ~50,000 cells per well of a 24-well plate containing 12mm diameter #1.5 coverslips (Electron Microscopy Sciences, cat#72230–01) and the next day, 500 ng of plasmid was transfected using 5 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#11668019) in OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 31985070) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were fixed 48h post-transfection using freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, cat#P6148) in calcium and magnesium free phosphate buffered saline (CMF-PBS) for 10 min at room temperature (RT).

Antibody generation

A rabbit polyclonal anti-DDX39B antibody (Pacific Immunology) was generated against the 24 amino acid synthesized peptide (Cys)-DRFEVNISELPDEIDISSYIEQTR, conserved across human, mouse and Chinese hamster DDX39B protein sequences. Briefly, two rabbits were immunized once with AdjuLiteTM Complete Freund’s Adjuvant followed by three rounds of AdjuLiteTM Incomplete Freund’s Adjuvant immunizations. The first production bleed was validated by ELISA against the peptide used for immunization. After validation, serum from both rabbits was pooled prior to affinity purification. 25 mL pooled serum was subjected to affinity purification using the same target peptide. This affinity-purified antibody was diluted in 50% glycerol for long-term storage at a concentration of 0.6 mg/mL. The antibody was further validated by demonstrating its colocalization after immunostaining with the mCherry-DDX39B signal in a U2OS cell line with the endogenous DDX39B gene tagged with mCherry (See “Cell Line Construction” Section, below).

Cell line construction

Knockin cell lines containing endogenous gene sequences tagged with different fluorescent proteins were constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 as described previously (Gholamalamdari et al., 2025). For gRNA synthesis, a 2-step overlap PCR strategy was employed. In the first step, a forward primer including the T7 promoter sequence followed by the target specific sequence 20bp immediately upstream of the PAM sequence and a reverse primer containing the tracrRNA template sequence (5′-AAAAGCACC-GACTCGGTGCCACTTTTTCAAGTTGATAACGGACTAGCCTTATTTTAACTTGCTATTTCTAGCTCTAAAAC −3′) (Table S1) were used. This first step generated a 120bp T7-gRNA-tracr fragment. In the second step, we amplified this DNA template using a T7 promoter sequence forward primer (5′-TAATACGACTCACTATAG-3′) and a reverse primer sequence (5′-AAAAGCACCGACTCG-3′).

This DNA template was gel purified (QIAGEN # 28704) and used for an in vitro-transcription reaction to generate gRNA. 1 μg of DNA template was incubated with 2.5mM ribonucleotides (NTP, New England Biolabs cat#N0466S), 1U/μL Ribolock RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# EO0381), 8 μL T7 RNA Polymerase (purified in the laboratory) in 50 μL reaction volume in IVT buffer (50mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 15mM magnesium chloride, 5mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 2mM spermidine). The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 6–16 h in a thermocycler. In-vitro transcribed product was treated with 2.5 U DNase I (2 U/μL; New England Biolabs cat# M0303S) at 37°C for 15 min to remove the DNA template prior to purification. gRNA was purified using the RNeasy plus kit (QIAGEN cat# 74134). Purified gRNAs eluted in RNase-free water (Accugene cat#51200) were stored as aliquots at −80°C.

2 μg of target-specific gRNAs were complexed with 3μg purified Cas9 Nuclease V3 (Integrated DNA Technologies, cat# 1081058). gRNA sequences and primers used for homology arms of Donor DNA sequences are provided in Table S1. Primers 70–90 bp long with their 5′ end modified with an amine group coupled to a C6 linker (Yu et al., 2020) were used for generating donor DNA with homology arms including sequences upstream and downstream of the target genomic DNA cut site; (Table S1). PCR amplification of the donor DNA used Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, cat# M0491S) and was applied to a plasmid or BAC template DNA containing sequences coding for the fluorescent protein and linker peptide to be added to the NH2-terminus of the target protein (Table S1). This donor DNA was purified after the PCR using magnetic beads (MAGBIO, HighPrep PCR, cat#AC-60050). Nucleofection was performed using an Amaxa Nucleofector II device (Lonza) as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

After 5 days of recovery, fluorescence-positive cells were sorted using the FACS ARIA II sorter at the UIUC Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center. From the fluorescence-positive sorted cells (mixed population), further clones were generated by single-cell dilutions whenever specified. Using this approach, the “U2OS EGFP-SON C5” cell clone was first generated. U2OS EGFP SON C5 was then further modified to generate the “U2OS EGFP-SON C5 mCherry-DDX39B C11” cell clone and the “U2OS EGFP-SON C5 mCherry-THRAP3 gRNA#1” mixed population cell line. Similarly, a U2OS HaloTag-SON” mixed population cell line was generated and then further modified to generate the “U2OS HaloTag-SON EGFP-THRAP3 gRNA#1″ mixed population cell line (Table S1). U2OS EGFP-SON C5 mCherry-DDX39B C11 and U2OS HaloTag-SON EGFP-THRAP3 gRNA#1 were further modified to generate a third knock-in with SNAPtag-RBM8A and SNAPtag-BCLAF1 or SNAPtag-DDX39B respectively.

HCT116 SON and SRRM2 double-degron cell line (expressing mAID-Clover-SON and SRRM2-mAID-mCherry) was generated by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing as previously described (Yesbolatova et al. Nat. Commun., 2020; Saito et al. Current Protocols, 2021). Briefly, we initially fused mAID-mCherry to the C terminus of SRRM2. For this, we designed a CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid for targeting the SRRM2 locus (5′-AGGTCTCCATAAATTGTCTT(TGG)-3′). A donor plasmid harboring mAID-mCherry and a selection marker (BSD) with two homology arms (approximately 500 bp each) was constructed. HCT116 cells stably expressing OsTIR1(F74G) from the AAVS1 locus were transfected with the CRISPR and donor plasmids. Clones surviving blasticidin selection were screened for biallelic by genomic PCR, and expression of the fusion protein was confirmed by immunoblotting. Subsequently, we fused mAID-Clover to the N terminus of SON. For this, we designed a CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid for targeting the SON locus (5′-AGA GAACGGAGCGGACGCCA(TGG)-3′). A donor plasmid harboring HygroR-P2A-mAID-mClover with two homology arms was constructed. The SRRM2-mAID-mCherry cells were transfected with the CRISPR and donor plasmids for tagging SON. Clones surviving hygromycin selection were screened for biallelic insertion of the tagging construct by genomic PCR, and expression of the fusion protein was confirmed by immunoblotting. To induce degradation of SON and SRRM2, 5-Ph-IAA was added to the final concentration of 1 μM.

Confirmation of endogenous knock-in tags by genotyping (PCR) and western blot for clonal cell lines can be seen in Table S1 (see 14_Cell_line_confirmation).

Tyramide Signal Amplification-sequencing (TSA-seq)

A detailed protocol for TSA-seq is described elsewhere.71 For high-resolution mapping, we combined the use in the original TSA-seq 1.0 procedure4 of both sucrose and Dithiotheitol (DTT) to increase spatial resolution with the higher tyramide concentration and longer TSA reaction time used in the TSA-seq 2.0 protocol, which saturated the protein TSA labeling but increased the DNA tyramide labeling without saturation.7 More specifically, we used a TSA reaction buffer prepared in CMF-PBS with 50% sucrose, 1.5 mM DTT (Millipore Sigma, cat#233155–10GM), and 0.33 mM tyramide-biotin (Apex-Bio, cat# A8011) with a reaction time of 30 min. By boosting the tyramide concentration beyond previous TSA-seq 2.0 TSA reaction conditions, we compensated for the decreased TSA-labeling caused by the addition of the DTT, while keeping the nucleic acid labeling undersaturated as judged by microscopy as described previously.7

For each TSA-seq sample in U2OS cells, cells were grown in two T-300 flasks (~15 million cells per T-300 flask). For each TSA-seq sample in HCT116 cells, cells were grown in one T-150 flask (~30–40 million cells). For the TSA-seq of the HCT116 mAID-Clover-SON SRRM2-mAID-mCherry CMV-OsTIR1(F74G) cell line, cells in T-150 flasks were first treated with either DMSO (Avantor # IC0219605590) or 5μM 5-Ph-IAA (Tocris Biosciences #7392) for 4 h before proceeding to the TSA-seq procedure.

All TSA-seq libraries were prepared using 3–10 ng of purified DNA using the TruSeq ChIP Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, cat# IP-202–1012) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were PCR-amplified for 8 cycles and purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, cat# A63881). Concentrations of individual libraries were estimated by qPCR and then pooled accordingly. They were then sequenced from one end of the fragments on a NovaSeq X Plus with V1.0 sequencing kits. FASTQ files were generated and demultiplexed with the bcl-convert v4.1.7 Conversion Software (Illumina). TSA-seq normalization, scores and identification of changed regions were performed using a previously reported pipeline.4,7

For all related analysis, Type I and Type II peaks as defined previously4,8 were filtered such that only local maxima with SON score >0 were considered as Type I peaks (n = 218 peaks) and local maxima with SON score ≤0 were considered as Type II peaks (n = 215 peaks).

Oligolibrary design and probe synthesis

For DNA FISH of TSA-seq peaks, we used an oligolibrary containing ~500 FISH probes targeted to DNA sequences within each ~100 kb targeted peak region (Table S1). Oligo probes (Table S1) were designed using PAINTSHOP (https://paintshop.io/) as per published recommendations.72,73 Briefly, selected genomic region coordinates were uploaded and advanced probe settings specified as: no repeats allowed, max off-target score 15, max K-mer count 5, min prob value 0 and probe set optimized to generate ~500–523 probes per 100 kb target with “trim” setting for each region. Each probe set was appended with universal outer primers for each TSA-seq peak type, and a unique inner primer set for each 100 kb genomic peak region. The 3′ inner and outer primer was followed by the T7 promoter sequence.

For trajectory mapping, the distances between two consecutive ~100 kb regions were kept at least 200 kb apart between the nearest edges of two neighboring regions. The outer primer was unique for “Apex” and “Arm” regions and each 100 kb genomic region had a unique inner primer set. The 3′ inner and outer primer was followed by the T7 promoter sequence. Otherwise, settings for probe selection were kept the same as for the TSA-seq peak probe selection.

The detailed protocol used for probe preparation is described elsewhere.73 Briefly, probes were amplified using PCR with Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase for 7–8 cycles, followed by in-vitro transcription using HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs cat# E2040S) and reverse-transcription using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat# EP0751) as described previously.73

2 μL of reactions were loaded on a urea-TBE gel to check for the desired ~70–80% incorporation of reverse transcription RT primer (20bp) into the probes (~150 bp). Probes were purified using Zymo DCC-25 columns as described previously.73

2 μg purified probes were end-labelled using 12U Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase (TdT 20U/μL; Thermo Scientific, cat# EP0161) in TdT buffer (Thermo Scientific, cat# EP0161) with 108μM unlabelled dATP or dTTP and 54μM Biotin-dATP (Invitrogen #19524016) or Digoxigenin-11-dUTP (Roche #11093088910) respectively in a 25 μL reaction volume. Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 1hr in a thermocycler. Probes were purified using the QIAquick Nucleotide Removal kit (QIAGEN, cat# 28306). Probes were eluted in 100 μL molecular biology grade water (Accugene, cat#51200) and human Cot-1DNA (Invitrogen #15279011) was added to each probe such that human Cot-1 DNA was 10x higher than the probe concentration. The resulting probe solutions were concentrated using a Vacufuge concentrator (Eppendorf) with the settings for aqueous solutions at 45°C for 45 min until the probe solutions were reduced to ~2–5 μL volumes. To these solutions, equal volumes of 100% formamide (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#F9037) and 2x hybridization buffer (20% w/v dextran sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, cat# D8906) in 4x Saline Sodium Citrate (SSC) buffer) are added such that the final probe concentrations are 25 ng/μL in hybridization buffer (10% w/v dextran sulfate in 2X SSC buffer with 50% formamide).

RNA-seq sample preparation and analysis

Cell synchronization and protein depletion

For synchronization at the G1/S boundary,80 HCT116 mAID-Clover-SON SRRM2-mAID-mCherry CMV-OsTIR1(F74G) cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to approximately 80% confluency, followed by treatment with 20 μM Lovastatin (Mevinolin; LKT Laboratories, cat# M1687) for 24 h. To release the G1 arrest but block cells at the G1/S boundary, cells were rinsed three times with pre-warmed, CO2-equilibrated culture medium supplemented with 2 mM Mevalonic acid (Mevalonolactone; Millipore-Sigma cat# M4667) and 2 mM thymidine (Millipore-Sigma, cat# T9250).

Degradation of AID-tagged proteins was induced by the addition of 5 μM 5-Ph-IAA simultaneously with the release of the G1 block by addition of the Mevalonic acid/thymidine-containing media. Cells were incubated for 16 h after this G1 block release. Control cells were treated the same except that an equivalent volume of DMSO without added 5-Ph-IAA was added. The G1 arrest followed by addition of 5-Ph-IAA coincident with the release of this block for the G1/early S synchronization was used to minimize cell cycle-related variability between DMSO-treated controls and the SON/SRRM2-depleted samples.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Cells in a 6-well plate were rinsed twice with ~1 mL of cold CMF-PBS per well, and total RNA was extracted immediately using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat# 74134), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified RNA eluted in RNase-free water was treated with 2U DNase I (New England Biolabs, cat# M0303S) for 20 min at 37°C, followed by DNase I inactivation by adding 5 μM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# J15694.AE) and 10 min incubation at 75°C.

RNA integrity was assessed at the DNA Services Facility, Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign), using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. All samples exhibited RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) of 10. Ribosomal RNA was depleted using the FastSelect HMR kit (Qiagen, cat# 334386). RNA-seq libraries were prepared with the KAPA HyperPrep Stranded mRNA Library Kit (Roche, cat# KK8580), quantified by qPCR, pooled, and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus instrument using V1.0 sequencing chemistry for 151 paired-end cycles. Adapter trimming was performed on the 3′ ends of reads, using the first 33 nucleotides of the adapter sequences for trimming. FASTQ files were generated and demultiplexed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq v2.20 software.

RNA-seq analysis

The quality of trimmed reads was assessed using FastQC with default parameters. High-quality reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using STAR aligner,81 with the corresponding GTF annotation file obtained from Ensembl. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package69 using DMSO-treated samples as controls. Genes with an adjusted p-value (Padj) < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) were considered significantly differentially expressed (DEGs) (Table S1). All genes with raw counts >100 were considered as “expressed” and showed no change between DMSO and 5-Ph-IAA samples were considered as “nonDEGs”.

Image analysis

General conventions used

All fixed-cell images shown are single optical sections unless indicated otherwise. Non-linear exponential gamma scaling of 0.7–0.8 was applied to all images of proteins where speckle-enriched fraction was higher than surrounding network to reduce the intensity dynamic range and thus facilitate the visualization of low-intensity nuclear regions. Images presented in the live-cell imaging videos are 2D maximum-intensity projections of the original optical section z-stacks acquired at each time point. All images were processed using Fiji using Fiji (ImageJ).82

Protein-intensity quantification within and outside NS

A single optical z section containing the max-signal over the entire z stack for NS (SON or SRRM2) was selected using the np.max function in the Python NumPy package.83 The intensity distribution of each candidate protein inside versus outside of NS was assessed as follows: Pixels with intensities <10% of the maximum signal of the protein were considered “noise” and discarded. A nuclear mask, established by DAPI staining, was applied to both the NS and candidate protein images. NS were defined by SON/SRRM2 intensity thresholds established using Otsu thresholding.84 The remaining pixels showing candidate protein “positive” signals (>10% maximum intensity threshold) were then divided into two categories: “Protein within NS” for pixels overlapping with the NS segmented regions and “Protein outside NS” for remaining positive pixels outside the NS segmented regions. The resulting images were displayed using the “viridis” LUT color-map. Cumulative-intensity percentile plots of the “Protein within NS” and “Protein outside NS” staining distributions were generated using the Python package Matplotlib.85

For correlation coefficients and Mander’s coefficient calculations, we used single z-slices. A nuclear mask was generated using the DAPI signal and Otsu thresholding84 and applied to the protein signals. These nuclear-masked images were then used for calculating the coefficients using the JaCoP plugin86 in Fiji.82 To assess the correlation coefficients and Mander’s coefficient of markers within a network and markers of two different networks, only protein outside NS (marked by SON) was considered. For live-cell imaging, correlation coeffiecient between two markers for each frame were calculated using a custom script (see data and code availability). For this data, plotting and statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Network changes upon transcriptional inhibition with DRB and speckle depletion

A Fiji macro (“Skeletonize”, See data and code availability Section) was created for this quantification. Briefly, for each image, the SON channel was used to generate an NS mask. Then, all pixels of the protein channel that overlapped with the NS mask were set to zero intensity to generate an image of only the protein outside NS. The resultant image was converted into a binary image using Otsu thresholding.84 Using the Fiji Skeletonize function (fiji.sc/Skeletonize3D), “protein skeletons” were generated, and branch lengths and number of branches/skeleton were calculated. Total skeleton length was computed by summing the lengths of all the skeletons within a nucleus. Number of connections was the number of unique skeletons (length >5 pixels) identified within a single nucleus.

For SON-SRRM2 depletion, total network with DMSO and 5-Ph-IAA treatments were considered for the analysis without applying the speckle mask.

Distance measurements for histone, RNA Pol II Ser2p, and DAPI distributions relative to THRAP3 and DDX39B perispeckle patterns

Single z-slices were used for the analysis. Perispeckle protein network signal was thresholded using Otsu thresholding84 and converted to a binary image. All pixels with DAPI signal or marker signal (acetylated histones (H3K27ac/H3K9ac) or H2B or RNAP II Ser2p) greater than their corresponding Otsu thresholds were considered for the analysis. A Euclidean distance map was generated for each marker and DAPI using the distance_transform_edt function of Python scipy.ndimage package. For the histone marks and DAPI comparisons, the distance measurements were weighted by intensity-quantiles. Distances from multiple cells were aggregated and used to generate normalized kernel density plots as shown.

Distance measurement for Type I and Type II DNA-FISH spots relative to SON, THRAP3 and DDX39B

To measure distance of a spot to SON, THRAP3 and DDX39B, we used the Loci_Compartment_Distance ImageJ plugin described elsewhere.87 Since the THRAP3 and DDX39B networks are widely distributed within the nucleus, and due to limited Z-resolution, we used a single optical section for THRAP3 and DDX39B analysis. For NS detection, we used the sum-projection of 3 optical sections centered over the brightest spot of the locus. To define the edge of the compartment boundary, the intensity drop of 50% of the local maximum intensity (after background correction) was used to locally segment the image. The center of mass of the DNA FISH spot signal was used as the spot center location and the shortest distance between the spot center to the nearest compartment edge was measured.

Categorizing and assessing alignment of DNA-FISH trajectories relative to NS, THRAP3 and DDX39B foci

To eliminate DNA FISH background staining signals, for the DNA FISH mapping of chromosome trajectories, only the “Apex” biotin signals that colocalized adjacent to DNA-FISH signals for the “Arm” regions were considered as true positive Apex signals. Additionally, the Apex signal had to be at least 2-fold higher intensity than single biotin spots seen elsewhere in the nucleus to be considered for analysis. For analysis, a sum-projection was generated of all the consecutive optical sections which contained an individual trajectory FISH signal consisting of positive DNA-FISH Apex and Arm signals. Chromosome trajectories were categorized as condensed if the Arm region appeared as a single spot or blob next to an Apex DNA-FISH spot. Any Arm region appearing as 2 or more spots was termed “decondensed”. For alignment, a trajectory was considered aligned only if all the spots of the Arm and Apex were either overlapping or adjacent and in-contact to THRAP3 or DDX39B foci. In all other cases, the chromosome trajectory was classified as not aligned.

Distance measurements of trajectory apex versus arm regions from nearest NS, THRAP3, or DDX39B foci

DNA-FISH spots for Trajectory Apex and Arm were detected using the “blob log” function within the scikit-Image Python package88 and were clustered using DBSCAN.89 For this clustering, an iterative threshold estimation method was employed such that the number of clusters per nucleus was 3–4. For each cluster, the cluster center was calculated and a box of 30 × 30 pixels (x-y) from the center was cropped; within this cropped volume, 7 optical sections (z-slices) were sum intensity projected to create a new 2D image, within which the center of mass of the Apex signal was calculated. Within the same 2D cropped projection, the Arm region signal was segmented and the pixels within the segmented region were split into three categories, for each of which their center-of-mass determined: 1) “Arm-close”: 25% pixels closest to Apex; “Arm-far”: 25% pixels farthest from Apex; “Arm-middle”; remaining pixels. The DDX39B or THRAP3 and NS signals were thresholded using the Otsu function.84 The shortest Euclidean distance between the center of each DNA-FISH spot (Apex or Arm) to the nearest edge of the segmented protein/speckle was measured.

Distance measurements of E6 and mini-dystrophin transgene RNAs relative to SON, THRAP3 and DDX39B foci

Distance measurements for transgene RNAs, tagged with MS2 repeats and MS2-binding proteins, relative to NS (SON/SRRM2), DDX39B, or THRAP3 foci were performed by manually selecting nuclear regions. RNA spots within each nuclear region were identified using the ‘Find Maxima’ function. Detected locations were assigned a pixel value of 1, while all other pixel values were set to 0, converting the RNP image into a binary image containing only RNP locations. The binary image was then imported into a custom MATLAB code for distance measurements relative to NS, THRAP3 and DDX39B (https://github.com/Jiahkim/distancebtwlocusNS.git). NS and perispeckle (THRAP3 and DDX39B) areas were segmented using the Otsu’ thresholding algorithm84 which effectively defined NS and perispeckle boundaries. RNP-positive pixels were first classified based on whether they overlapped with NS regions. For RNP-positive pixels outside NS area, distances to the nearest pixel at the NS boundary were as calculated. For this data, plotting and statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Distance measurement of HSPH1 and HSPA1 RNAs relative to SON, THRAP3, and DDX39B foci

For HSPH1 and HSPA1 RNA-FISH analysis, images were denoised using the “despeckle” function in ImageJ and a Gaussian filter of 1 pixel radius applied afterward Distance measurements relative to SON, THRAP3 and DDX39B foci were performed using the ImageJ plugin as described for DNA-FISH measurements of Type I and Type II SON TSA-seq peaks.

For Mander’s coefficient calculations, we used a maximum-intensity projection of 3 optical sections of the “despeckle” denoised and Gaussian fitered images. A nuclear mask was generated using the DAPI signal and Otsu thresholding84 and applied to the RNA and protein signals. These nuclear-masked images were then used for calculating Mander’s coefficient using the JaCoP plugin86 in Fiji.82

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All plots were generated using either the ggplot2 package in R version R4.2.2 or GraphPad Prism. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) used type 3 comparison using the “car” package. The options were set as options (contrasts = c (“contr.sum”, “contr.poly”)). We used a generalized linear model with gamma distribution followed by the Tukey posthoc test for datasets which deviated from normality assumption.

All boxplots represent the interquartile range with the upper line indicating 75% and the bottom line indicating 25%. Central line indicates median, and whiskers represent the range of data shown. Squares indicated within boxplots and violin plots represent mean. All figure legends mention the statistical test used and the exact numbers of n used for each analysis. p values indicated for all figures: ns: p > 0.05; *: p ≤ 0.05; **: p ≤ 0.01; ***: p ≤ 0.001; ****: p ≤ 0.0001.
