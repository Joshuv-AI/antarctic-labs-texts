Development and Validation of a Self-Management Motivation Scale for Children with Chronic Diseases
Authors: Xiaojing Ye, Xin Zhang, Cong Chen, Xiaonan Wu, Yulu Ren, Xulu Wang, Xiaofen Zheng, Runping Wang, Chintana Wacharasin, Chunmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-Determination Theory highlights the role of motivation in sustaining self-management behaviors among children with chronic diseases, but validated instruments for assessing this construct in pediatric populations are limited. This study developed and validated a self-management motivation scale for children aged 8–18 years with chronic diseases.MethodsScale development proceeded in sequential phases. First, the initial item pool was developed by integrating evidence from relevant literature with findings from semi-structured interviews (n = 22). Second, items were refined through a two-round Delphi consultation with 20 experts to evaluate item importance and relevance, with expert authority and consensus assessed, followed by two rounds of cognitive interviews (n=13) and a pilot survey, yielding a 24-item preliminary scale. Third, item analysis was conducted in 143 children to remove poorly performing items. Finally, psychometric evaluation was performed in 386 children, including content validity, factor structure through EFA (n = 155) and CFA (n = 231), convergent and discriminant validity, internal consistency, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability (n=30).ResultsThe final scale comprised 21 items across four dimensions: autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation. Content validity was strong (I-CVI=0.875–1.000; S-CVI/Ave=0.988). A four-factor structure emerged from the exploratory factor analysis, explaining 75.84% of the variance. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated a good model fit (χ2/df=1.755, RMSEA=0.057, CFI=0.965, IFI=0.965, TLI=0.960). AVE ranged from 0.612 to 0.778 and CR from 0.887 to 0.942, supporting convergent validity; discriminant validity was also satisfactory. At the subscale level, Cronbach’s α, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability coefficients ranged from 0.879 to 0.941, 0.865 to 0.932, and 0.763 to 0.944, respectively.ConclusionThe scale demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity for assessing self-management motivation in children with chronic diseases. Further longitudinal and multicenter studies are needed to examine its predictive validity and intervention responsiveness.Keywords: children with chronic diseases, self-management, motivation, self-determination theory, scale development, reliability, validity

Background

In recent years, the prevalence of chronic diseases among children and adolescents has continued to rise, making them an important public health concern that affects physical health, psychological well-being, and social functioning.1–3 Effective disease self-management is therefore essential for optimizing long-term health outcomes in this population.4–8 However, the current management of pediatric chronic conditions remains largely parent-led, with children often insufficiently involved in day-to-day disease management, limited in their initiative, and not fully supported in exercising agency in their own care.7,9 As they grow older, this dependency-based pattern may undermine treatment adherence, impede the development of self-management capacity, and ultimately place long-term health outcomes at risk.6,10 Against this background, motivation is particularly critical because it directly shapes whether children are willing to engage in and sustain self-management behaviors.11–14 Self-Determination Theory suggests that different forms of motivation are associated with distinct patterns of behavioral engagement and health outcomes; accordingly, accurate assessment of children’s motivational characteristics is of considerable importance.15–17

Nevertheless, instruments specifically designed to assess self-management motivation in children with chronic diseases remain scarce. Existing measures tend to focus on specific disease categories or isolated domains of health behavior, or they are adapted from adult scales, which limits their ability to reflect children’s developmental level, psychological needs, and behavioral regulation characteristics.18–22 Thus, we developed a Self-management Motivation Scale for Children with Chronic Diseases and assessed its psychometric properties in a sample of children aged 8–18 years.

Methods

Children and experts played distinct but complementary roles in this scale development. Children with chronic diseases contributed to item generation through semi-structured interviews (n = 11), item comprehensibility through cognitive interviews (n = 13), and psychometric testing through pilot and validation surveys (n = 30, 143, and 386). Experts (n = 20) participated in the two Delphi rounds and the final content validity assessment. In this methodological sequence, expert validation was used to evaluate theoretical relevance, clinical importance, and content representativeness, whereas children’s participation was used to ensure that the items reflected their disease-management experiences, were understandable to the intended age group, and were appropriate for the pediatric chronic disease context.

Phase 1: Initial Scale Development

Development of the Item Pool

This study was guided by Self-Determination Theory (SDT), proposed by Deci and Ryan, to construct the conceptual framework of disease self-management motivation in children with chronic diseases.23 SDT conceptualizes motivation as a continuum of internalization, including six regulatory types: amotivation, external regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, integrated regulation, and intrinsic regulation.24 It explains how external motivation may gradually become internalized, thereby providing a psychological basis for understanding children’s transition from passive compliance with treatment to active participation in disease management.24 However, in scale development, the six regulatory types of SDT are not always operationalized in the same way. Previous studies have shown that researchers often adapt or integrate regulatory types according to the research context, behavioral domain, and target population to improve interpretability and applicability.22,25,26

These regulatory types can also be organized according to their level of self-determination into two broader categories: autonomous motivation and controlled motivation. The former encompasses identified, integrated, and intrinsic regulation, whereas the latter consists of external and introjected regulation.23,24 Autonomous motivation reflects behavioral engagement based on personal values, interest, or self-endorsed choice, whereas controlled motivation reflects engagement driven by internal pressure, external demands, rewards, punishment, or expectations from others. Classic Self-Determination Theory research suggests that, compared with subtle distinctions among specific regulatory subtypes, the higher-order distinction between autonomous and controlled motivation is often more useful for predicting behavioral persistence and well-being.27,28 This distinction has also been widely applied in health behavior research.29,30 Specifically, autonomous motivation is generally associated with better adherence, more stable maintenance of health behaviors, and more favorable health outcomes, whereas controlled motivation tends to result in less stable and less sustainable behavior because it lacks sufficient self-endorsement.31,32 However, amotivation is also a theoretically distinct state within the Self-Determination Theory continuum, reflecting a lack of behavioral intention or a lack of perceived value in performing a behavior. Therefore, the initial framework of this study was not reduced to autonomous and controlled motivation only; instead, amotivation was retained as an independent component. Taking into account the theoretical structure of Self-Determination Theory, the developmental characteristics of children and adolescents, and the need for a parsimonious framework during the early stage of scale development, this study constructed an initial three-dimensional conceptual framework comprising autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation. This framework was intended to capture the main motivational states within the Self-Determination Theory continuum and to provide a basis for subsequent item generation and scale validation.

The item pool was developed through theoretical derivation and empirical evidence. First, Chinese and English databases were searched using terms such as “children,” “chronic diseases,” “self-management,” “motivation,” and “self-determination theory” to identify relevant scales, theoretical models, and empirical studies. Initial items were then generated based on the literature review. Second, to enhance the clinical applicability and cultural relevance of the items, semi-structured interviews were conducted in September 2024 with 11 children aged 8–18 years with chronic diseases and their primary caregivers from pediatric wards of a tertiary hospital in Wenzhou, Zhejiang Province. The interviews explored disease management experiences, sources of motivation, and related influencing factors. Thematic analysis was applied to the interview transcripts to extract children’s motivational expressions in daily disease management, which further refined the item pool.

Delphi Expert Consultation Method

A two-round Delphi consultation was undertaken to enhance the methodological rigor and content validity of the scale. 20 experts from four Chinese cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Wuhan, and Wenzhou) were recruited, meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) a minimum of 10 years of professional experience in pediatrics, child psychology, nursing education, or related disciplines; (2) an intermediate or higher professional title; (3) at least a bachelor’s degree; and (4) providing written informed consent to participate voluntarily.

In both Delphi rounds, experts evaluated each item for importance on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important, 5 = very important) and for relevance on a 4-point scale (1 = irrelevant, 4 = highly relevant). After each round, descriptive statistics—including the mean, standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and interquartile range (IQR) for importance ratings—were calculated, along with the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) derived from the relevance ratings. The expert authority coefficient (Cr) was also computed, with a value of ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable.33

Several rules were applied for item retention: an importance mean > 3.5, a CV < 0.25,33 and an I-CVI ≥ 0.78.34,35 Additionally, an IQR ≤ 1.5 was adopted as a supplementary threshold for good consensus, in line with Delphi methodology recommendations.36,37 Items that satisfied all criteria were kept; those failing any criterion were flagged for revision or removal based on the experts’ qualitative comments. Expert consensus was evaluated using Kendall’s W coefficient.

Cognitive Interviews and Pilot Testing

Cognitive interviews were conducted after the Delphi expert consultation to ensure the scale items were semantically clear, consistently understood, and culturally appropriate. Using verbal probing techniques, two rounds of interviews were performed with children meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria for chronic diseases until informational saturation was reached.38 The interviews aimed to delve into the children’s semantic understanding of the item wording, their response process, and the logic behind their interpretations, thereby identifying potential issues such as ambiguous phrasing, comprehension difficulties, or contextual inappropriateness.39,40

A pilot survey was subsequently conducted among 30 children with chronic diseases who met the eligibility criteria and were conveniently recruited from relevant departments of a tertiary hospital in Wenzhou, Zhejiang Province. The survey captured several aspects of the process, such as the time taken to complete it, along with participants’ comprehension of the items and their feedback. The scale was finalized, and an initial draft was produced following revisions.

The results of the cognitive interviews and pilot testing were integrated with the preceding Delphi findings. When children reported difficulty understanding an item or when their interpretation differed from the intended meaning, the wording was revised to improve readability and contextual fit while retaining the theoretical meaning confirmed by the expert panel. Thus, expert validation and children’s feedback served complementary purposes: the former supported the theoretical and professional appropriateness of the items, whereas the latter provided response-process evidence that the items were understandable, meaningful, and contextually grounded for children with chronic diseases.

Phase 2: Refinement of Items and Development of the Predictive Model

During item refinement, participants were selected through convenience sampling from relevant pediatric departments at a tertiary hospital in Wenzhou, Zhejiang Province. Children were considered eligible if they: (1) had been clinically diagnosed with a chronic disease; (2) were aged 8–18 years; and (3) provided assent together with written informed consent from their legal guardians. Children were excluded if they were in a critical or clinically unstable condition, or if they had severe intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder that would prevent them from understanding or completing the questionnaire. In total, 170 questionnaires were distributed, of which 143 were valid.

Subsequently, item performance in the predictive version was examined using four approaches: dispersion trend method, critical ratio method, item-total correlation analysis, and factor analysis.41,42 During analysis, reverse-scored items were converted to scores as appropriate per the respective method, while the remaining items retained their original scores.

The specific criteria for item screening were as follows:43 (1) Dispersion Trend Method: The discriminative ability of each item was evaluated by calculating its standard deviation. An item was excluded if its standard deviation fell below 0.75. (2) Critical Ratio Method: According to the total scale scores, participants in the upper 27% and lower 27% were classified as the high-score and low-score groups, respectively. Each item was then compared between the two groups using independent-samples t tests. Items showing insufficient discrimination, defined as a critical ratio <3 or p > 0.05, were deleted. (3) Correlation Coefficient Method: Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between each item and the total scale score. Items showing weak item-total correlations (r < 0.40 or p > 0.05) were removed. (4) Factor Analysis Method: Judgment was based on communality and factor loadings. Items were deleted by applying dual criteria: a communality threshold of 0.30 and a factor loading threshold of 0.40, as values below these indicate insufficient explanatory power for the common factors.

Phase 3: Evaluation of Scale Performance and Development of Final Version

To comprehensively evaluate the reliability and validity of the scale, an independent validation sample was recruited using the same eligibility criteria described in Phase 2. Of the 420 questionnaires distributed, 386 were valid and included in the final analysis. All participants completed the revised scale and a general demographic information questionnaire.

The validation sample was randomly divided into two subsamples: one for exploratory factor analysis (EFA; n = 155) and the other for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; n = 231). The final scale was subsequently examined through reliability and validity testing to assess its psychometric performance.

Reliability Assessment

Reliability testing included internal consistency and test-retest reliability.44 Internal consistency was estimated using Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability, with Cronbach’s α >0.80 and split-half coefficients ≥0.70 considered acceptable at the dimension level according to established criteria.45 To assess the scale’s applicability across age groups, internal consistency was examined not only in the full sample but also separately in two age subgroups: children aged 8–12 years and adolescents aged 13–18 years.

Temporal stability was assessed using test-retest reliability.44 After an interval of two weeks, 30 children were conveniently sampled to complete the scale again. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was computed from the two assessments; values above 0.75 were interpreted as good stability, and values above 0.90 as excellent reliability.44,46

Because the scale was designed to assess distinct motivational regulation types rather than a single homogeneous construct, reliability was interpreted primarily at the dimension level, as internal consistency coefficients are more appropriate for relatively homogeneous constructs or subscales.47 The total-scale reliability coefficient was reported for transparency but was not used as the primary indicator of internal consistency.

Validity Assessment

Content Validity

After cognitive interviews, pilot testing, and item analysis, the retained 21 items were further reviewed for content validity to ensure that the final scale adequately represented the target construct. Eight experts in pediatric chronic diseases, nursing or medical education were asked to assess the relevance of each item. Based on these ratings, we computed the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI), the Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI), and the Average S-CVI (S-CVI/Ave). Acceptance of the scale’s content validity was determined by meeting these thresholds: I-CVI ≥ 0.78, S-CVI ≥ 0.90, and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90.48

Construct Validity

For construct validity testing and to reduce potential overfitting, the 386 participants were randomly divided into two independent subsamples. EFA was conducted in Subsample 1 (n = 155) to explore the latent structure, while CFA was performed in Subsample 2 (n = 231) to verify the stability and generalizability of the factor structure identified in Subsample 1. This split-sample cross-validation approach is consistent with recommendations that factor structures identified in an exploratory stage should be tested in an independent sample whenever possible.49,50

EFA was conducted on Subsample 1 using principal component analysis with varimax rotation.51,52 Factors were retained if their eigenvalues exceeded 1.0 and the cumulative variance explained was >50%.53 Items with factor loadings <0.40 or cross-loadings >0.40 on multiple factors were considered for removal.49,51

CFA was subsequently conducted in Subsample 2 with maximum likelihood estimation to examine how well the hypothesized model corresponded to the observed covariance structure.54,55 Model fit was evaluated using several indices, with acceptable fit defined as χ2/df <3, RMSEA <0.08, AGFI >0.85, and GFI, IFI, CFI, and TLI >0.90.55,56

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity refers to the extent to which indicators belonging to the same dimension show sufficient association and consistency, while discriminant validity concerns whether the dimensions of the scale are adequately distinct from each other. AVE values >0.50 and CR values >0.70 were interpreted as supporting adequate convergent validity.57 Adequate discriminant validity requires each dimension’s AVE square root to exceed its inter-dimension correlations.58

Data Collection

This study adopted a mixed-methods design. For the qualitative component, uniformly trained researchers conducted semi-structured interviews in teaching rooms within the hospital wards. Interviews lasted approximately 20–40 minutes and continued until data saturation was reached. During the interviews, researchers used flexible probing and active listening to foster a supportive environment and help participants describe their experiences more openly.

Quantitative data were collected using on-site questionnaires. After obtaining informed consent, trained investigators guided children to complete the questionnaire independently whenever possible. For children who had difficulty reading or writing, the investigator or the primary caregiver read the items aloud, and the child’s verbal responses were recorded. The average completion time was approximately 10 minutes.

To minimize missing data, all questionnaires were checked on site by trained investigators immediately after completion. If any missing responses were identified, the investigator asked the participant to complete the omitted item before the questionnaire was collected. Questionnaires that remained incomplete after checking or failed quality control were excluded from the final analysis. Therefore, the final datasets used for item analysis and psychometric validation contained no item-level missing data, and no statistical imputation was performed.

Data Analysis

Microsoft Excel was used for data management, while SPSS 26.0 and Amos 26.0 were used for statistical analysis. Before analysis, all questionnaires were screened for completeness and response validity. Reverse-scored items were recoded before analyses requiring a consistent scoring direction.

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the characteristics of participants and the findings from the expert consultations. Continuous variables, where applicable, were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Item analysis, content validity assessment, reliability testing, exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were conducted according to the procedures and criteria described in the relevant subsections above.

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to determine whether the data were suitable for exploratory factor analysis before factors were extracted. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted in Amos 26.0. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (IRB No. 2024-K-152-01), and all study procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given that the participants were minors, written informed consent was provided by their parents or legal guardians prior to participation. Children received an age-appropriate explanation of the study aims and procedures and provided assent before completing the questionnaire or interview. Participation was voluntary, and participants were free to withdraw at any time, with no adverse consequences for their ongoing medical care. All collected data were de-identified and used solely for the purposes of this study.

Results

Phase 1: Initial Scale Development

Development of the Item Pool

The construction of scale items followed a systematic process. Initially, 21 preliminary items were created following a systematic review of 10 established scales, including the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire and the Health Behavior Motivation Scale. Subsequently, thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews conducted with 11 children with chronic diseases and their primary caregivers supplemented this pool with an additional 6 items, yielding an initial item pool of 27.

Delphi Expert Consultation Method

A two-round Delphi consultation was conducted with 20 experts from four Chinese cities. The experts had a mean age of 48.25 ± 6.22 years. Their characteristics are presented in Table 1. Expert participation was high, with questionnaire response rates of 90.91% and 100% in the first and second Delphi rounds, respectively. The expert authority coefficient (Cr) increased from 0.813 to 0.875, and Kendall’s W rose from 0.317 to 0.406, with both values reaching statistical significance (p < 0.001). These results suggested that the degree of expert consensus improved across the two rounds.

After the first round, the research team revised the initial 27-item pool according to the predefined retention criteria, namely an importance mean >3.5, CV <0.25, I-CVI ≥0.78, and IQR ≤1.5, together with the experts’ qualitative comments.

Six items were deleted. In the autonomous motivation dimension, A6 (“…consistent with my personal goals and values”) and A8 (“…because it is interesting”) were removed because the experts considered them too abstract for younger children or insufficiently consistent with the clinical reality of long-term disease management. In the controlled motivation dimension, B2 was deleted because it overlapped with B1 and could be confused with autonomous motivation, while B7 was removed because its meaning was similar to that of B3. In the amotivation dimension, C1 was deleted due to semantic redundancy, and C7 was removed because it did not adequately reflect the construct of amotivation.

Twelve items were revised to improve clarity, readability, and contextual relevance to disease management. For example, expressions such as “taking care of myself” were replaced with “disease management” where appropriate. A2 was modified to emphasize children’s willingness to learn how to care for their own health. A5 was reworded to highlight that disease management may help children participate more effectively in daily activities, such as playing and learning. Several items in the controlled motivation dimension were also rewritten to more accurately capture external pressure, family expectations, fear of criticism, and concerns about disease deterioration.

Four new items were added based on expert suggestions. These items were designed to reflect children’s motivation to gain recognition from healthcare professionals, make family members feel happy, avoid deterioration in health status, and respond to peer influence. The added items included the following expressions: “I manage my disease well because I want medical staff to think I am great,” “I participate in disease management to make my family happy,” “I participate in disease management because I am afraid that my health will get worse if I don’t,” and “I participate in disease management because other children in the hospital do so, making me feel I should too.”

Following these revisions, the initial 27-item pool was refined into a 25-item pool for the second round of consultation. The original three-dimensional structure, comprising autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation, remained unchanged.

In the second round, all items met the retention criteria except A3, “I want to participate in disease management because I want to better understand my health status.” This item had a mean importance score of 2.00 and a CV of 0.487. Experts considered the item insufficiently rigorous and partially overlapping with A2. After discussion, the research team decided to delete it. In addition, six items were further revised to enhance clarity and age appropriateness. For example, A7 was changed to “I enjoy the process of gradually improving my health through participating in disease management,” because the expression “sense of achievement” was considered difficult for younger children to understand. In the controlled motivation dimension, B10 and B11 were revised to better represent family approval and concern about disease deterioration, respectively. In the amotivation dimension, C5 was simplified as “I do not want to participate in disease management because I feel that I cannot do it.” No further changes were made to the dimensional structure.

Accordingly, the pretest version of the scale included 24 items across three dimensions. Table 2 summarizes the iterative content validity indicators across the two Delphi rounds. The S-CVI/Ave increased from 0.898 in the first round to 0.975 in the second round, and the proportion of items with an IQR ≤1.5 increased from 70.4% to 100%, indicating improved expert agreement. Overall, the Delphi process showed that controlled motivation was the domain requiring the greatest contextual refinement, followed by autonomous motivation. Controlled motivation items were substantially revised or added because children’s disease self-management is closely embedded in family caregiving, healthcare supervision, concerns about disease deterioration, and peer influence. Accordingly, the revisions strengthened items reflecting external pressure, family approval, recognition from healthcare professionals, fear of worsening health, and peer-related motivation. Autonomous motivation items were mainly adjusted to translate abstract SDT constructs, such as personal values, interest, and sense of achievement, into developmentally appropriate expressions for children. In contrast, changes to amotivation mainly involved removing redundant items and simplifying wording. These shifts helped ensure that the scale was not only theoretically consistent with SDT but also understandable and contextually grounded for children with chronic diseases.

Cognitive Interviews and Pilot Testing

To evaluate item comprehensibility across age groups, two rounds of cognitive interviews were conducted with 13 pediatric patients living with chronic conditions. The first round identified six items with problematic wording, which were subsequently revised based on research team discussions and expert feedback, with no items added or deleted. During the second round, the children raised no objections to the content, response options, or format of the items. A pre-survey was then administered to 30 children, and the results supported the scale’s acceptability. Integrating the Delphi results with the children’s cognitive interview and pilot testing feedback, the initial version of the Self-management Motivation Scale for Children with Chronic Diseases was established, consisting of three dimensions and 24 items.

Phase 2: Item Refinement and Formation of the Pilot Version

The distribution of 170 preliminary scales in this phase resulted in 143 valid returns, achieving a response rate of 84.12%. Participants had an average age of 13.27 years with a standard deviation of 2.71 years. Supplementary Table S1 outlines the children’s fundamental characteristics.

The item screening results indicated that: (1) Item B3 had a standard deviation < 0.75; (2) Item B12 had a critical ratio (t) < 3; (3) The item-total correlation coefficients for Items B3 and B12 were both lower than 0.40. Based on these findings, items B3 and B12 were removed. The remaining 22 items were subsequently subjected to exploratory factor analysis. The analysis indicated that all items exhibited communalities > 0.40 and factor loadings > 0.40. However, item B11 was deleted as it loaded above 0.40 on two factors. As shown in Figure 1, the scree plot leveled off after the fourth factor, providing support for retaining a four-factor structure. Through the above screening process, the final Chronic Illness Self-Management Motivation Scale for Children (Predictive Version) was formed, consisting of 21 items across 4 dimensions.

Phase 3: Evaluation of Scale Performance and Development of Final Version

This study’s predictive scale consisted of 21 items. Considering the basic sample size requirement for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (generally > 200) and allowing for an estimated 10% of invalid questionnaires, the minimum sample size needed was determined to be 234. Using convenience sampling, a questionnaire survey was conducted among children with chronic diseases at a tertiary hospital in Wenzhou from October 2024 to January 2025. Among the 420 questionnaires distributed, 386 were valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 91.90%.

The demographic and disease-related characteristics of the validation sample are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The validation sample covered several common pediatric chronic diseases. Because the included conditions differ substantially in their disease-specific staging and severity criteria, a unified disease severity grading system was not applied. Therefore, disease-related clinical burden indicators, including complications, comorbidity with other chronic diseases, and number of hospitalizations, were reported instead.

Validity Testing

For the final content validity assessment of the 21-item scale, eight experts were selected from the original Delphi panel (n = 20) to assess item relevance using a 4-point scale, ranging from 1 “not relevant” to 4 “highly relevant”. These experts comprised five specialists in pediatric chronic diseases and three experts in nursing/medical education. The mean age of the experts was 48.25 ± 6.14 years, and their mean length of professional experience was 27 ± 8.35 years. All eight experts held associate senior or senior professional titles. Good content validity was established for the scale, as evidenced by an I-CVI range of 0.875–1.000 and an S-CVI/Ave of 0.988, supporting its relevance and representativeness.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was initially conducted on Subsample 1 (n=155) to assess structural validity. Factorability of the data was supported by the KMO value of 0.907 and the statistically significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2 = 2614.102, p < 0.001). Based on the eigenvalue-greater-than-one criterion and supported by the scree plot (Figure 2), a four-factor structure was retained. This model explained 75.84% of the total variance, and the items’ communalities ranged from 0.735 to 0.909 (see Table 3). Because of the high explained variance and item communalities, the factor solution was further evaluated using CFA in an independent subsample rather than interpreted solely on the basis of EFA results. Given that the EFA yielded a four-factor solution and that these factors corresponded to theoretically distinguishable motivational regulation types within the SDT continuum, autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation were specified as four latent variables in the CFA model, with the 21 items as observed indicators. CFA was then conducted using Subsample 2 (n = 231). The model fit indices were acceptable: χ2/df = 1.755, RMSEA = 0.057, GFI = 0.883, AGFI = 0.853, CFI = 0.965, IFI = 0.965, and TLI = 0.960, indicating a good model fit. Discussion

Background and Clinical Significance of Scale Development

In line with the “Healthy China 2030” initiative, which emphasizes comprehensive life-cycle health services, improving self-management capacity among children with chronic diseases remains an important issue in pediatric healthcare. Motivation is a key psychological factor underlying children’s engagement in disease self-management, and its systematic assessment can help healthcare professionals and researchers better understand why children participate in, persist in, resist, or withdraw from long-term disease management behaviors.11,59 Despite this need, few motivation assessment instruments have been specifically designed for children with chronic diseases while also being grounded in theory and appropriate for their developmental stage.

Existing Self-Determination Theory-based instruments, such as the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ)60 and the Behavioral Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire-2 (BREQ-2),22,61 provide useful references for assessing motivational regulation. However, these instruments differ from the present scale in both target population and behavioral context. The TSRQ was developed to assess self-regulation in treatment or health-related behaviors more broadly, whereas the BREQ-2 focuses on exercise behavior.22,60,61 In contrast, the present scale was developed specifically for children with chronic diseases and focuses on disease self-management behaviors embedded in long-term treatment, family caregiving, healthcare interactions, and daily school or peer contexts. Directly applying existing adult- or behavior-specific SDT instruments to pediatric chronic disease populations may be limited by differences in cognitive development, language comprehension, family involvement, and disease-management responsibilities.62,63 Children’s self-management motivation is often shaped not only by personal values or interests, but also by parental expectations, caregiving relationships, healthcare guidance, and peer experiences.64,65 Therefore, an assessment tool tailored to the developmental and clinical characteristics of children with chronic diseases is needed.

Using Self-Determination Theory as the conceptual foundation, we developed the Self-management Motivation Scale for Children with Chronic Diseases and examined its psychometric properties. The initial conceptual framework was organized around autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation, while the empirical analyses further differentiated controlled motivation into introjected regulation and external regulation. In developing the items, we sought to translate abstract motivational constructs into wording that could be more easily understood by children aged 8–18 years and that reflected common pediatric disease-management situations. For example, items were formulated to capture children’s perceived value of disease management, pressure from family or external requirements, and lack of motivation in everyday self-management contexts.

The potential clinical value of this scale lies in its ability to provide a structured, theory-based assessment of motivational regulation among children with chronic diseases. By describing motivational profiles and identifying possible motivational strengths or weaknesses, the scale may offer a useful measurement basis for future research, health education, supportive communication, and the development of targeted motivational support strategies. For example, children with higher autonomous motivation may benefit from support that further strengthens self-endorsed goals and self-management confidence, whereas children with prominent external regulation or amotivation may require additional attention to motivation internalization, illness understanding, and emotional support.66 These potential applications should be interpreted as directions for future research and practice rather than as clinical effects demonstrated by the present study. Further longitudinal, multicenter, and intervention-based studies are warranted to examine the scale’s predictive validity, responsiveness to motivational interventions, and associations with self-management behaviors, treatment adherence, quality of life, and other clinically meaningful outcomes.

Reliability Assessment of the Scale

The four dimension-level scores showed satisfactory reliability. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four dimensions ranged from 0.879 to 0.941, split-half reliability coefficients ranged from 0.865 to 0.932, and test-retest ICCs exceeded 0.70, indicating acceptable internal consistency and temporal stability at the dimension level. Reliability analyses conducted separately among children aged 8–12 years and adolescents aged 13–18 years also showed that all dimensions maintained good internal consistency across the two age groups.

The relatively low total-scale Cronbach’s α (0.648) should be interpreted in light of the theoretical structure of Self-Determination Theory. Cronbach’s α assumes that items collectively measure a relatively homogeneous construct.67,68 However, this scale was developed to assess multiple motivational regulation types rather than a single unidimensional trait.69 Autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation represent distinct regulatory processes along the self-determination continuum, with different degrees of autonomy and different theoretical implications for self-management behavior.28 Therefore, items across these dimensions are not necessarily expected to show uniformly high inter-item correlations. In this context, the lower total-scale α may reflect the multidimensional and theoretically heterogeneous nature of self-management motivation rather than poor reliability of the dimension-level scores. Accordingly, the scale should be interpreted primarily through its four dimension scores rather than as a single undifferentiated total score.

This dimension-level interpretation is also consistent with the psychometric reporting practices of many Self-Determination Theory-based motivation instruments. Because different regulatory styles represent theoretically distinct motivational processes, these instruments commonly report Cronbach’s α coefficients separately for each subscale rather than relying exclusively on a total-scale reliability coefficient. Examples include the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire,21 the Academic Self-Regulation Questionnaire for children,60 and the Behavioral Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire series.22,61 For this reason, the dimension-level Cronbach’s α coefficients were regarded as more appropriate and theoretically meaningful evidence of internal consistency than an overall α for the full scale.

Validity Assessment of the Scale

Content validity was supported throughout the iterative process of item development and refinement. During the two-round Delphi consultation, experts evaluated the importance and relevance of the items, and the content validity indicators improved after revision, with the S-CVI/Ave increasing from 0.898 in the first round to 0.975 in the second round. After item refinement and psychometric screening, the final 21-item scale was further evaluated by eight experts, yielding I-CVI values ranging from 0.875 to 1.000 and an S-CVI/Ave of 0.988. These findings indicate that the scale items had good relevance and representativeness for assessing self-management motivation in children with chronic diseases.34,35

An issue that warrants further discussion is the relationship between the initial conceptual framework and the empirically derived factor structure. The scale was initially developed using a three-dimensional conceptual framework consisting of autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation. In the exploratory factor analysis, however, the items were grouped into a four-factor structure comprising autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation. This result does not necessarily indicate a contradiction between the theoretical framework and the empirical structure. Rather, it suggests that the original controlled motivation dimension was empirically differentiated into two distinct regulatory forms: introjected regulation and external regulation. To further clarify this relationship, Figure 4 illustrates how the original Self-Determination Theory motivational continuum was mapped onto the initial three-dimensional conceptual framework and how the empirically derived four-factor structure emerged during scale validation. This visual mapping shows that the four-factor solution did not depart from the theoretical foundation of SDT, but rather provided a more refined empirical representation of controlled motivation in the context of pediatric chronic disease self-management.

Conceptual mapping of the Self-Determination Theory motivational continuum, initial conceptual framework, and empirically derived four-factor structure.

This differentiation is theoretically defensible within Self-Determination Theory. Although introjected regulation and external regulation are both controlled forms of motivation, they differ in the source of perceived pressure. Introjected regulation is driven by internal pressure, such as guilt, self-blame, obligation, or the need to maintain self-worth, whereas external regulation is driven by external demands, rewards, punishment, or the expectations of others.11 In the initial conceptual framework, these two forms of regulation were combined under controlled motivation to provide a parsimonious structure suitable for pediatric scale development. The empirical differentiation of these two forms of regulation suggests that, in the context of pediatric chronic disease self-management, introjected and external regulation may represent distinguishable motivational experiences. Therefore, separating introjected regulation from external regulation provides a more refined representation of controlled motivation within the SDT continuum.

The four-factor structure is also meaningful in the context of pediatric chronic disease management. Children’s self-management behaviors are often embedded in family caregiving, healthcare supervision, school life, and peer interactions.70–72 Some children may engage in disease management because they recognize its value or wish to regain health, reflecting autonomous motivation. Others may participate because they feel guilty, worry about disappointing family members, or want to prove that they are “good” patients, reflecting introjected regulation. Still others may comply mainly because of parental reminders, healthcare requirements, or external consequences, reflecting external regulation. Children with amotivation may lack perceived meaning, confidence, or intention to participate in disease management. Distinguishing these motivational patterns allows the scale to describe not only the level of motivation, but also the quality and source of motivation.

The statistical findings further supported this four-factor interpretation. All items met or exceeded the standard factor loading threshold of 0.40 on their designated factors, and the four factors accounted for 75.837% of the cumulative variance. The clear factor pattern, acceptable communalities, and subsequent CFA results in an independent subsample together suggest a coherent factor structure. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively high explained variance and consistently strong factor loadings may partly reflect the relatively homogeneous clinical source of the sample or the close conceptual proximity among items within each motivational dimension. Confirmatory factor analysis further supported the four-factor model, with all model fit indices meeting acceptable standards.56 In addition, CR values greater than 0.70 and AVE values greater than 0.50 supported the convergent validity of each dimension, while the discriminant validity results indicated that the four dimensions were empirically distinguishable.73,74

Scoring and Interpretation of the Scale

Motivation scales developed based on Self-Determination Theory commonly use either a relative autonomy index or separate dimension scores to interpret motivational regulation.75 The relative autonomy index summarizes different regulatory styles into a single weighted score reflecting the overall degree of autonomy, whereas dimension-level scoring preserves the distinct meanings of different motivational types. In the present study, mean scores were calculated separately for each of the four dimensions because autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation represent theoretically distinct regulatory processes and may have different associations with self-management behaviors. This scoring approach is consistent with the multidimensional nature of Self-Determination Theory-based motivation assessment and allows researchers and clinicians to identify not only the overall level of motivation, but also the quality and source of children’s self-management motivation. Therefore, the scale should be interpreted primarily through its four dimension scores rather than through a single total score or autonomy index.

Research Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were selected through convenience sampling at one tertiary hospital, which may affect the extent to which the findings can be generalized. Although the sample size met the requirements for the main psychometric analyses, the relatively homogeneous clinical source of the sample should be considered when interpreting the high explained variance and strong factor loadings observed in the EFA. Future multicenter studies with larger and more heterogeneous samples are needed to further examine the applicability and structural stability of the scale. Second, although the sample included several common pediatric chronic diseases, a unified disease severity classification was not applied because the included conditions have different disease-specific staging and severity criteria. Instead, disease-related burden indicators, including complications, comorbidity with other chronic diseases, and number of hospitalizations, were reported. Future studies may further validate the scale in disease-specific samples and across different levels of disease severity or disease burden. Third, this study focused on initial scale development and psychometric validation. Although reliability analyses were conducted separately among children aged 8–12 years and adolescents aged 13–18 years, the subgroup sample sizes were insufficient for robust subgroup CFA or formal measurement invariance testing. In addition, criterion validity was not examined because no established criterion-referenced instrument is currently available for assessing self-management motivation in children with chronic diseases. Future longitudinal, multicenter, and intervention-based studies should further examine the scale’s measurement invariance, predictive validity, responsiveness to motivational interventions, and associations with actual self-management behaviors, treatment adherence, and health outcomes.

Conclusion

Guided by Self-Determination Theory, this study developed the Self-management Motivation Scale for Children with Chronic Diseases. The final scale consists of 21 items across four dimensions: autonomous motivation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation. The findings provide preliminary psychometric evidence supporting the scale’s content validity, construct validity, and reliability in the current sample. The scale may serve as a useful measurement tool for assessing different motivational regulation types and describing motivational profiles among children with chronic diseases. By identifying potential motivational strengths and weaknesses, it may provide a measurement basis for future research and for the development of targeted motivational support strategies. Future longitudinal, multicenter, and intervention-based studies are warranted to further examine the scale’s predictive validity, responsiveness to motivational interventions, and applicability across broader pediatric chronic disease populations.
