IGA Laplace Eigenfrequencies Distributions and Estimations: Impact of Reparametrization on Eigenfrequency Behavior


Abstract
This work addresses the Galerkin isogeometric discretization of the one-dimensional Laplace eigenvalue problem subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a bounded interval. We employ GLT theory to analyze the behavior of the eigenfrequencies when a reparametrization is applied to the computational domain. Under suitable assumptions on the reparametrization transformation, we prove that a structured pattern emerges in the distribution of eigenfrequencies when the problem is reframed through GLT-symbol analysis. Additionally, we establish results that refine and extend those of [ 3 ] , including a uniform discrete Weyl’s law. Furthermore, we derive several eigenfrequency estimates by establishing that the symbol exhibits asymptotically linear behavior near zero.



Keywords—
Laplace operator, Isogeometric Galerkin discretization, Reparametrization, GLT theory, eigenfrequencies, Behavior, Spectral symbol.

Introduction

Isogeometric Analysis (IGA), introduced in
[
29
]
, is a powerful paradigm for analyzing and approximating partial differential equations
[
30
]
. The main purpose of IGA is to bridge computer-aided design (CAD) and finite element analysis (FEA), see
[
30
,
29
]
. This connection gives IGA an advantage over classical (FEA). In particular, the high smoothness of B-spline and NURBS basis functions enables higher accuracy per degree of freedom
[
6
,
21
,
23
]
and provides a better description of the spectrum of the involved operator compared to classical (FEA), see
[
7
,
17
,
18
]
.

Several authors have considered the analysis of the IGA Laplace operator problem as a foundational study for solving and investigating IGA problems; see, for instance,
[
7
,
9
,
14
,
20
,
22
]
. From the works
[
21
,
23
]
, the authors deduce that B-splines of maximal smoothness on uniform grids are preferable for addressing the Laplace eigenvalue problem. However, despite this accurate description and estimation of the spectrum, there are always a few eigenvalues, called outliers
[
7
]
, that are poorly approximated, with their corresponding values being significantly larger than the exact ones. In
[
20
]
, the authors overcame this problem by constructing subspaces of the full IGA spaces subject to the three standard boundary conditions and proving that these spaces filter out the outliers while yielding the optimal approximation error. Compared to other outlier-free spaces, such as reduced spaces
[
16
]
, the authors in
[
20
]
provide a mathematical confirmation that guarantees the outlier-free property of these spaces.

Although optimal spline subspaces
[
20
]
are the best spaces for addressing the Laplace eigenvalue problem, the construction of these spaces depends on selective uniform grids that vary according to the chosen boundary conditions and the parity of the interpolation degree. However, some applications require the use of non-uniform grids. In particular, for the uniform boundary observability of the wave equation, it is widely acknowledged that almost all classical discretizations of the
\displaystyle 1d
wave equation using uniform grids result in the divergence of the discrete controls; see
[
15
,
19
]
and the survey paper
[
27
]
. In this context, the authors in
[
10
]
propose restoring the uniform observability property by utilizing non-uniform grids. The main idea involves constructing various non-uniform finite-difference and finite-element semi-discretizations of the wave equation through the use of an appropriate concave diffeomorphism, referred to as a reparametrization. It is important to mention that in
[
10
]
, the authors avoided addressing the uniform gap property related to the eigenfrequency of the discretized Laplace operator, even though, in general, this condition simplifies the study. This omission was due to the complexity of deriving information about the eigenfrequencies (or eigenvalues); thus, this property was identified only through numerical analysis. The first theoretical investigation of the uniform gap condition in this particular direction is attributed to the work
[
4
]
, which demonstrated that it is indeed possible to derive information about the asymptotic distribution of the eigenfrequencies. Moreover, it was proved that using concave or convex reparametrizations of the domain results in a specific behavior of these eigenfrequencies, named the average gap property, which is a necessary condition for the uniform gap property, as established in
[
4
]
. Furthermore, the study in
[
4
]
is not confined to classical discretization, as in
[
10
]
, but deals with various schemes, including IGA. However, it should be mentioned that this average gap condition is insufficient to establish the wave equation’s uniform boundary observability.

The main issue in both
[
10
]
and
[
4
]
is the lack of information on the eigenfrequency spectrum of the discretized Laplace operator when using reparametrization. In this work, we address the following question:
How much information can be derived about the eigenfrequency spectrum when using a strictly concave or strictly convex reparametrization of the domain?
As in
[
4
]
, we use Generalized Locally Toeplitz (GLT) theory to analyze the impact of these reparametrizations on the eigenfrequency distributions. The GLT theory was initially introduced in
[
26
]
and further developed in
[
24
,
25
]
. The main advantage of GLT theory lies in its capacity to provide a general conceptual background for addressing problems within a broader context. Additionally, it can help determine whether a discretization method effectively approximates the spectrum; see
[
14
,
3
]
. Equipped with this theory and building on the results in
[
3
]
, we illustrate the potential to gain valuable and previously undiscovered insights into the relationship between these specific reparametrizations and eigenfrequency behavior. Furthermore, we obtain more favorable outcomes than those in
[
3
]
, including a uniform convergence version of
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
and various new estimations of the eigenfrequencies.

2
Preliminaries

Consider the following one-dimensional Laplace eigenvalue problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\partial_{xx}u=\lambda u,\hskip 9.24994ptx\in(0,1),\\
u(0)=u(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

(1)

It is well-known that the system (
1
) has a set of exact, non-trivial solutions given by
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\left(k\pi\right)^{2}
,
\displaystyle u_{k}(x)=\sin(k\pi x)
, for
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Here,
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}
is the
\displaystyle k
-th eigenvalue of the operator
\displaystyle-\partial_{xx}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, and
\displaystyle u_{k}
is the corresponding eigenfunction.

This paper investigates the behavior of the IGA approximation of eigenfrequencies
\displaystyle(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}})_{k\geq 1}
when using strictly convex or concave reparametrizations to create a non-uniform spatial mesh. Specifically, we aim to understand how these mappings affect the distribution of the approximate eigenfrequencies and their estimation using uniform sampling of the IGA spectral symbol. The necessary background for our analysis is provided in this section, starting with the IGA Galerkin discretization of (
1
) in Subsection
2.1
, followed by a brief overview of GLT theory in Subsection
2.2
. Finally, we present the GLT symbol of the IGA eigenfrequencies in Subsection
2.3
.

2.1
Galerkin B-spline IGA Discretization

System (
1
), employing
\displaystyle B
-spline functions
[
5
]
, involves discretizing the weak form of problem (
1
) stated as follows: for
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, find
\displaystyle u_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

A(u_{k},v)=\lambda_{k}\;L(u_{k},v),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1),

(2)

where

A(u_{k},v)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}^{\prime}(x)\;v^{\prime}(x)\,dx,\;\;\text{and}\;\;L(u_{k},v)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}(x)\;v(x)\,dx.

The next step involves constructing a finite-dimensional subspace to approximate the solution space
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
. This subspace is defined by a finite set of basis functions. In the standard IGA discretization, these functions are constructed using
\displaystyle B
-spline functions.

We consider non-periodic and uniform knot vectors of the form

(t_{j})_{0\leq j\leq 2p+n}=\left(\displaystyle\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{p+1},\dfrac{1}{n},\dfrac{2}{n},\cdots,\dfrac{n-1}{n},\displaystyle\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{p+1}\right),

where
\displaystyle n,p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. The
\displaystyle B
-spline functions of degree
\displaystyle p
on these knots are defined recursively as follows (for instance, see
[
5
]
): for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq p
,

N_{j}^{k}(t)=\dfrac{t-t_{j}}{t_{j+k}-t_{j}}N_{j}^{k-1}(t)+\dfrac{t_{j+k+1}-t}{t_{j+k+1}-t_{j+1}}N_{j+1}^{k-1}(t),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\;\;0\leq j\leq 2p+n-1-k,

(3)

with

N_{j}^{0}(t)=\mathcal{X}_{[t_{j},t_{j+1})}(t),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\;\;0\leq j\leq 2p+n-1.

(4)

Here,
\displaystyle p+n
is the number of
\displaystyle B
-spline functions, and in cases where a fraction has a zero denominator, we assume it to be zero. We can then define the Schoenberg space

\mathbb{S}^{p}=span\left\{N_{j}^{p}:\;j=0,\dots,p+n-1\right\}.

(5)

In classical spline approximation theory (see
[
5
]
), it is well-known that
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{p}
coincides with the space of splines of degree
\displaystyle p
and smoothness
\displaystyle p-1
, namely

\mathbb{S}^{p}=\left\{s\in\mathcal{C}^{p-1}([0,1]),\,\,s|_{\left[i/n,\,(i+1)/n\right)}\in\mathbb{P}_{p},\;\;i=0,\dots,n-1\right\},

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
\displaystyle p
.

The isogeometric approximation of
\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}([0,1])
is given by

\mathbb{S}^{p}_{0}=\left\{s\in\mathcal{C}^{p-1}([0,1]),\,\,s|_{\left[i/n,\,(i+1)/n\right)}\in\mathbb{P}_{p},\;\;\text{and}\;\;s(0)=s(1)=0,\;\;i=0,\dots,n-1\right\}.

(6)

Then, our discrete solutions
\displaystyle(u_{k,h},\lambda_{k,h})\in\mathbb{S}^{p}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfy the approximate weak formulation

A(u_{k,h},v_{h})=\lambda_{k,h}\;L(u_{k,h},v_{h}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall v_{h}\in\mathbb{S}^{p}_{0},

(7)

where
\displaystyle h
refers to the discretization parameter defined by
\displaystyle h=1/n
. We use the standard basis for
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{p}_{0}
formed by the
\displaystyle B
-spline functions
\displaystyle\{N_{1}^{p},\cdots,N_{p+n-2}^{p}\}
that vanish at the boundary.
Equation (
7
) can be expressed as a finite-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\left[\left(M_{n}^{p}\right)^{-1}K_{n}^{p}\right]\mathbf{u}_{k,h}=\lambda_{k,h}\mathbf{u}_{k,h},

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k,h}
is the coefficients vector of
\displaystyle u_{k,h}
with respect to the basis
\displaystyle\{N_{1}^{p},\cdots,N_{p+n-2}^{p}\}
and
\displaystyle M_{n}^{p}
and
\displaystyle K_{n}^{p}
are the mass and stiffness matrices, respectively

(M_{n}^{p})_{i,j}=\int_{0}^{1}N_{i}^{p}(x)N_{j}^{p}(x)\,dx,\hskip 9.24994pt(K_{n}^{p})_{i,j}=\int_{0}^{1}(N_{i}^{p})^{\prime}(x)(N_{j}^{p})^{\prime}(x)\,dx,

for
\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq p+n-2.

As previously stated, this work aims to derive insights into the approximate eigenfrequencies when applying strictly convex or concave reparametrizations of the domain
\displaystyle[0,1]
. For the case without reparametrization, we refer the reader to
[
9
]
. We then introduce the following test space:

\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}=\left\{\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1]):\;\phi^{{}^{\prime}}>0,\;[\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}>0\text{ or }\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}<0],\;\phi(0)=0\text{ and }\phi(1)=1\right\}.

(8)

For
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, we define the following basis functions by pullback under transformation
\displaystyle\phi
:

B_{j}^{p}=N_{j}^{p}\circ\phi^{-1},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }1\leq j\leq p+n-2,

and aim to approximate the exact eigenpairs
\displaystyle(u_{k},\lambda_{k})
using the standard Galerkin method, where the discrete solution space is given by
\displaystyle span\{B_{1}^{p},\cdots,B_{p+n-2}^{p}\}
. Simple computations lead to the following expressions for the mass and stiffness matrices:

(M_{\phi,n}^{p})_{i,j}=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\phi^{\prime}(x)\right|N_{i}^{p}(x)N_{j}^{p}(x)\,dx,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }1\leq i,j\leq p+n-2.

(9)

(K_{\phi,n}^{p})_{i,j}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\left|\phi^{\prime}(x)\right|}(N_{i}^{p})^{\prime}(x)(N_{j}^{p})^{\prime}(x)\,dx,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }1\leq i,j\leq p+n-2.

(10)

The numerical eigenvalue problem in this case is described by:

L_{\phi,n}^{p}\mathbf{u}_{k,h}^{\phi}=\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}\mathbf{u}_{k,h}^{\phi},

where
\displaystyle L_{\phi,n}^{p}=\left(M_{\phi,n}^{p}\right)^{-1}K_{\phi,n}^{p}.

2.2
Preliminaries on GLT Sequences

This subsection provides a brief overview of the essential background on the Generalized Locally Toeplitz (GLT) sequences theory. More details can be found in the pioneering works
[
3
,
12
]
, and the references therein.
In what follows,
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
represents a sequence of matrices of size
\displaystyle N=N(n)
, with
\displaystyle N\rightarrow+\infty
as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow+\infty
. Furthermore, the eigenvalues of each matrix
\displaystyle L_{n}
, denoted by
\displaystyle(\lambda_{k}(L_{n}))
, are assumed to be real, positive, and sorted in increasing order, specifically:

0<\lambda_{1}(L_{n})<\lambda_{2}(L_{n})<\cdots<\lambda_{N}(L_{n}).

In the current subsection, all the definitions and results have been adjusted to better align with our particular context, including the following definition of the spectral symbol.

Definition 2.1

(Spectral symbol)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of continuous functions with compact support over
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, and let
\displaystyle\omega:[0,1]\times[0,\pi]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We say that
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
has a spectral (or eigenvalue) distribution described by
\displaystyle\omega
, and we write

(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\omega,

if for all
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\lim_{N\longrightarrow+\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}F(\lambda_{k}(L_{n}))=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]\times[0,\pi]}F(\omega(x,\theta))\,dx\,d\theta,

(11)

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(L_{n})
,
\displaystyle k=1,\cdots,N
are the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle L_{n}
. In this case,
\displaystyle\omega
is referred to as the spectral symbol of
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
.

For insights into the largest set of test functions
\displaystyle F
for which (
11
) holds, we refer to
[
28
]
.

With the chosen discretization in Subsection
2.1
, whether or not reparametrization is employed, an issue arises concerning outliers. A few eigenvalues are poorly approximated by the uniform sampling of the symbol
\displaystyle\omega
, and their corresponding values are notably larger than the exact values. The following definition provides the mathematical definition of these outliers.

Definition 2.2

(Outliers)
.

Let
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\omega
, and let
\displaystyle R_{\omega}
represent the essential range of
\displaystyle\omega
, defined as

R_{\omega}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\;\mu_{2}\left(\{(x,\theta)\in[0,1]\times[0,\pi],\;\;|\omega(x,\theta)-y|<\epsilon\}\right)>0,\;\forall\epsilon>0\right\},

(12)

where
\displaystyle\mu_{2}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
. An eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(L_{n})
is considered an outlier if
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(L_{n})\notin R_{\omega}
.

It can be proved that
\displaystyle R_{\omega}
is a closed set (
[
12
, Lemma 2.1]
). Furthermore, if the function
\displaystyle\omega
is continuous and since the domain
\displaystyle[0,1]\times[0,\pi]
is compact, we can demonstrate that the essential range of
\displaystyle\omega
coincides with the image of
\displaystyle\omega
.

In general, and specifically in our case, the symbol is defined on a multidimensional domain, which complicates the mathematical and numerical study of the distribution of the eigenvalues. However, we can derive a new one-dimensional symbol from the original symbol, as explained in the following

Definition 2.3

(Monotone rearrangement of the symbol)
.

Let
\displaystyle\omega:[0,1]\times[0,\pi]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\omega
. We assume that the essential range of
\displaystyle\omega
is bounded. The extension function
\displaystyle\xi:[0,1]\longrightarrow R_{\omega}
is defined by

\xi(x)=\inf\left\{y\in R_{\omega}:\;\;\Psi(y)>\pi x\right\},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall x\in(0,1),

where

\Psi(y)=\mu_{2}(\{(x,\theta)\in[0,1]\times[0,\pi],\;\;\omega(x,\theta)\leq y\}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall y\in\mathbb{R},

(13)

is called the monotone rearrangement of
\displaystyle\omega
.

The following result
[
3
]
shows that the monotone rearrangement of a symbol remains a spectral symbol for the same sequence of matrices.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\omega
with
\displaystyle\omega:[0,1]\times[0,\pi]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
having a bounded essential range. Let
\displaystyle\xi
be the monotone rearrangement of
\displaystyle\omega
. Then, we have

(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\xi.

We conclude this subsection by introducing the discrete Weyl’s law and some of its consequences. These results describe the asymptotic behavior of eigenvalues and are essential for our IGA spectral analysis presented in the next section.

Theorem 2.1

(Discrete Weyl’s law,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sim_{\lambda}\omega
with
\displaystyle\omega:[0,1]\times[0,\pi]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
having a bounded essential range. Define
\displaystyle\Psi:\,\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
as the function given by (
13
). Then, at every point of continuity
\displaystyle y
of
\displaystyle\Psi
, the eigenvalues of the matrices
\displaystyle L_{n}
satisfy

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\dfrac{\left|\left\{k=1,\dots,N:\;\lambda_{k}(L_{n})\leq y\right\}\right|}{N}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi(y),

(14)

where, for a generic set
\displaystyle A
,
\displaystyle|A|
denotes the number of elements in set
\displaystyle A
.

Furthermore, if we assume that
\displaystyle\Psi
and
\displaystyle\xi
are continuous, then for every sequence

)), we obtain

\left(\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\right)^{\prime}(0)=\pi\dfrac{1}{\left(\Psi_{\phi}^{p}\right)^{\prime}\left(\left(\Psi_{\phi}^{p}\right)^{-1}(0)\right)},

which concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4
Impact of reparametrization on the behavior of eigenfrequencies

When choosing a reparametrization
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
in
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, we generate through IGA discretization (see Subsection
2.1
) a family of eigenfrequencies, denoted as
\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}}\right)_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}
, which depends, by construction, on the mapping
\displaystyle\phi_{1}
. Similarly, when using another reparametrization
\displaystyle\phi_{2}\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, we generate a new family of eigenfrequencies, denoted as
\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}}\right)_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}
. In this section, we aim to analyze the impact of changing the mapping on the distribution of eigenfrequencies. Specifically, we aim to locate each family with respect to the other. Additionally, we want to understand if choosing a reparametrization that results in a particular property of the symbol influences the behavior of the eigenfrequencies. We begin our analysis with a lemma that allows us to approximate the non-outlier eigenfrequencies by the symbol sampling. Following this, we establish an ordering relation between the eigenfrequencies and the reparametrizations set
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
. Finally, we analyze how the symbol’s convexity impacts the eigenfrequency behavior.

In this part, we chose to present our findings using conditions on
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
due to its simpler definition compared to
\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}
. However, it is important to note that the two are linked through (
24
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, such that
\displaystyle\sqrt{n^{-2}L_{\phi,n}^{p}}\sim_{\lambda}\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}-\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{k\pi}{N+1}\right)\right|=0.

(32)

Proof.

The proof follows a similar approach to that used in the proof of Corollary 3.3 in
[
3
]
. We assume that (
32
) is false. Under this assumption, we observe that all subsequences
\displaystyle(k(n))_{n\geq 1}
are contained within
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(p,n)
, which implies, by construction, that
\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is contained within
\displaystyle Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)
. Applying (
15
) directly leads to a contradiction, which completes the proof.
∎

We now present the first main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1

(Distribution of eigenvalues)
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle Rg(\omega_{\phi_{1}}^{p})\cap Rg(\omega_{\phi_{2}}^{p})

Then, there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

and
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\displaystyle n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}},n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}\in I

\sqrt{\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}}<\sqrt{\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}},

where, for
\displaystyle i=1,2

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{i}}}\right)_{k}

\displaystyle\phi_{i}

Proof.

For all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and

\displaystyle z_{k,h}^{\phi^{1}}
and
\displaystyle z_{k,h}^{\phi^{2}}

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{1,2}(n,p)}\left|z_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}-z_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}\right|=0.

Proof.

(of Lemma

Let
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and

. Then,
\displaystyle n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}\in Rg(\omega_{\phi_{1}}^{p})
,
\displaystyle n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}\in Rg(\omega_{\phi_{2}}^{p})
. Since, for

\displaystyle\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{i}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle z_{k,h}^{\phi^{1}}
and
\displaystyle z_{k,h}^{\phi^{2}}

\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}}=\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{1}}\right)^{-1}(z_{k,h}^{\phi_{1}}),

\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}}=\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{2}}\right)^{-1}(z_{k,h}^{\phi_{2}}).

, we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{\phi_{i}}(p,n)}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi_{i}}}-\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{i}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{k\pi}{N+1}\right)\right|=0,\hskip 9.24994pti\in\{1,2\}.

(34)

Using the mean value theorem and Proposition

which concludes the proof of Lemma
4.2

Now, we can proceed to the proof of the theorem. Let
\displaystyle(z_{k,h}^{\phi^{1}})
and
\displaystyle(z_{k,h}^{\phi^{2}})
be the two sequences constructed in Lemma

, and let
\displaystyle\varepsilon=\min_{y\in I}\left(\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})-\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{2}}(\sqrt{y})\right)
. Note that the continuity of
\displaystyle y\mapsto\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{i}}(y)
,
\displaystyle i=1,2
, ensures that
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0

, there exists

. To conclude the proof, it is sufficient to prove that

The previous theorem established a crucial relationship: it demonstrated that the ordering of eigenfrequencies is precisely the inverse of the ordering of the functions
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}
and
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}
. However, it is important to note that accessing and manipulating the functions
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}
and
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}
can be complex and difficult in practice. Therefore, there is a need for a more general relationship that connects the ordering of reparametrizations to the order of the associated eigenfrequencies.

The following theorem demonstrates the possibility of exerting control over the distribution of eigenfrequencies through the reparametrization set
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, offering a valuable tool for managing their behavior more effectively.

.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
that are strictly convex, and
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\phi_{2}^{\prime}(0)
. Let
\displaystyle x_{0}\in(0,1)
to be the first zero of the function
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\phi_{2}^{\prime}
. Then, if

\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x)\geq\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall x\in[0,x_{0}],

we have,

\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})>\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}(\sqrt{y}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall y\in\left(\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}},\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0))^{2}}\right).

Proof.

First, we observe that

Rg(\omega^{p}_{\phi_{1}})=Rg(\omega^{p}_{\phi_{2}})=\left[0,\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(0))^{2}}\right],

which implies that the restrictions of
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}
and
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}
on the interval
\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}(\pi)}}{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0})},\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}(\pi)}}{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)}\right)
are well-defined. On the other hand, Rolle’s theorem ensures the existence of the zero
\displaystyle x_{0}
in the open interval
\displaystyle(0,1)
since
\displaystyle\phi_{1}(0)-\phi_{2}(0)=\phi_{1}(1)-\phi_{2}(1)
.

Now, let us assume that there exists
\displaystyle y\in\left(\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}},\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0))^{2}}\right)
such that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})\leq\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})
. From
[
4
]
(see pages
\displaystyle 20
and
\displaystyle 21
), we have

\begin{split}\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})&=\pi-\displaystyle\int_{S_{1}(y)}\left(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right)\;d\theta-\mu_{1}(A_{1})\\
\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})&=\pi-\displaystyle\int_{S_{2}(y)}\left(\phi_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right)\;d\theta-\mu_{1}(A_{2}),\end{split}

(36)

where

S_{i}(y)=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\hskip 9.24994pt\phi_{i}^{\prime}(0)\leq\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\leq\phi_{i}^{\prime}(1)\right\},

and

A_{i}(y)=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\;\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}>\phi_{i}^{\prime}(1)\right\}\cup\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\;\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}<\phi_{i}^{\prime}(0)\right\},\;i\in\{1,2\}.

We claim that

S_{1}(y)=S_{2}(y)=\left[e_{p}^{-1}\left(y\left(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(0)\right)^{2}\right),\pi\right],

(37)

and

A_{1}=A_{2}=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\;\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}<\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\right\}.

(38)

Indeed, considering that
\displaystyle y\in\left(\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{0}))^{2}},\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(0))^{2}}\right)
together with
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\phi_{2}^{\prime}(0)
and
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0})=\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{0})
, we obtain

\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}(0)\leq\sqrt{\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{y}}\leq\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{0}),\hskip 9.24994pti=1,2.

Then, using the fact that
\displaystyle e_{p}(\theta)\leq e_{p}(\pi)
for all
\displaystyle\theta\in[0,\pi]
(see Corollary
2.3
), we obtain

\sqrt{\frac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\leq\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{0})\leq\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}(1),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\theta\in[0,\pi],\hskip 9.24994pti=1,2.

We conclude

S_{1}(y)=S_{2}(y)=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\hskip 9.24994pt\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(0)\leq\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\leq\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{0})\right\},

and

A_{1}=A_{2}=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\;\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}<\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\right\}.

Moreover, since
\displaystyle y(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}>e_{p}(\pi)
, it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\hskip 9.24994pt\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\leq\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\leq\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0})\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\{\theta\in[0,\pi]:\hskip 9.24994pty\left(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\right)^{2}\leq e_{p}(\theta)\leq y\left(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0})\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left[e_{p}^{-1}\left(y\left(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(0)\right)^{2}\right),\pi\right],

which prove the claim (
37
)-(
38
). On the other hand, the assumption
\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})\leq\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})
implies

\displaystyle\int_{S_{1}(y)}\left(\phi_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right)-\left(\phi_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right)\;d\theta\leq 0.

(39)

Since
\displaystyle 0<y(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0))^{2}<e_{p}(\pi)
, we have
\displaystyle\mu_{1}\left(S_{1}(y)\right)>0
. Then, using fact that
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x)\geq\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x)
for all
\displaystyle x\in[0,x_{0}]
, the above inequality (
39
) gives

\left(\phi_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right)=\left(\phi_{2}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{e_{p}(\theta)}{y}}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\theta\in S_{1}(y).

(40)

To end the proof, it is enough to demonstrate that (
40
) contradicts the fact that
\displaystyle x_{0}\in(0,1)
is the first zero of the function
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\phi_{2}^{\prime}
. In fact, by the intermediate-value theorem and (
37
), one can find
\displaystyle x_{1}\in(0,x_{0})
and
\displaystyle\theta_{0}\in S_{1}(y)
such that

\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})=\sqrt{\frac{e_{p}(\theta_{0})}{y}},

and, using (
40
), we obtain

x_{1}=\left(\phi_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{e_{p}(\theta_{0})}{y}}\right)=\left(\phi_{2}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{e_{p}(\theta_{0})}{y}}\right)=\left(\phi_{2}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\left(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})\right).

Consequently,
\displaystyle\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1})=\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})
, signifying that
\displaystyle x_{1}
is a zero for the function
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\phi_{2}^{\prime}
. This concludes the proof as
\displaystyle x_{1}<x_{0}
.
∎

Similarly, the next theorem addresses strictly concave reparametrizations. The proof follows a similar approach to that of Theorem
4.2
and is thus omitted.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
that are strictly concave, and
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(1)=\phi_{2}^{\prime}(1)
. Let
\displaystyle x_{0}\in(0,1)
to be the last zero of the function
\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}-\phi_{2}^{\prime}
. Then, if

\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x)\geq\phi_{2}^{\prime}(x),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall x\in[x_{0},1],

we have,

\Psi_{\phi_{1}}^{p}(\sqrt{y})>\Psi_{\phi_{2}}^{p}(\sqrt{y}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall y\in\left(\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}},\frac{e_{p}(\pi)}{(\phi_{1}^{\prime}(1))^{2}}\right).

A family of examples of such convex reparametrization functions is

\phi_{a,b}=e^{ax+b}-e^{b}+(\gamma-ae^{b})x,

(41)

where
\displaystyle a>0
,
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
and
\displaystyle b
is given by

b=-\ln\left(\frac{e^{a}-(a+1)}{1-\gamma}\right).

Notably, these reparametrization functions satisfy the conditions specified in Theorem
4.2
, including the property
\displaystyle\phi_{a,b}^{\prime}(0)=\gamma
.

In the case of strictly concave reparametrization, the family (
41
) is replaced by

\phi_{a,b}=\ln(ax+b)-\ln(b)+\left(\gamma-\frac{a}{a+b}\right)x.

(42)

Here, the parameters are similar:
\displaystyle a>0
,
\displaystyle 0<\gamma<1
. However, the value of
\displaystyle b
is calculated as
\displaystyle b:=a/x^{*}
, where
\displaystyle x^{*}\in(0,1)
represents the unique solution to the equation

\gamma=1-\left(\ln(x^{*}+1)-\frac{x^{*}}{x^{*}+1}\right).

Once again, these reparametrization functions satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
4.3
, and they specifically verify
\displaystyle\phi_{a,b}^{\prime}(1)=\gamma
.

At this stage, we have demonstrated the feasibility of ordering the families of eigenfrequencies based on the corresponding reparametrizations’ ordering. In the following result, our focus will be on the distribution of packed eigenfrequencies and how the convexity of the symbol influences the behavior of these eigenfrequencies. More precisely, we will show that the number of eigenfrequencies can indeed be ordered, and this order depends on the convexity of the symbol. In the case of a strictly convex symbol, the frequency spectrum shifts to the right, whereas a strictly concave symbol results in a leftward shift.

Theorem 4.4

(Distribution of pack-eigenvalues)
.

Let
\displaystyle p\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
. Suppose there exists a closed interval
\displaystyle I
in which
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
is of class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}(I)
. Let
\displaystyle\{y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}\}
be a uniform discretization of
\displaystyle I
and define
\displaystyle E_{I}^{i}
by

E_{I}^{i}=\left|\left\{k=1,\dots,N:y_{i-1}<\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\leq y_{i}\right\}\right|,\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\dots r-1.

Then, for sufficiently large
\displaystyle n
, the following hold

1.

If
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
is strictly convex on
\displaystyle I
, then for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}:

E_{I}^{i}<E_{I}^{i+1}.

2.

If
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
is strictly concave on
\displaystyle I
, then for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}:

E_{I}^{i}>E_{I}^{i+1}.

Proof.

We will focus on proving assertion 1, as the proof of assertion 2 follows a similar argument. For all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
, we apply Discrete Weyl’s law
2.1
to obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\dfrac{E_{I}^{i}}{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i})-\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i-1}),

(43)

and by the mean value theorem, there exist
\displaystyle x_{i}\in(y_{i-1},y_{i})
such that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i})-\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i-1})&=\frac{1}{\pi}(y_{i}-y_{i-1})\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime}(x_{i})\\
&=\frac{y_{r}-y_{0}}{r\pi}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime}(x_{i}).\end{split}

(44)

Now, for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\cdots,r-1\}
, let us fix
\displaystyle\epsilon
such that

0<\epsilon<\frac{(y_{r}-y_{0})\delta}{2r\pi}\inf_{I}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime\prime},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with }\delta:=\inf_{1\leq i\leq r-1}(x_{i+1}-x_{i}).

Using equations (
43
) and (
44
), we deduce that for sufficiently large
\displaystyle n
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\left(E^{i}_{I}-E^{i+1}_{I}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon+\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i})-\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i-1})\right]-\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i+1})-\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\epsilon+\frac{y_{r}-y_{0}}{r\pi}\left(\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{i})-\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}(x_{i+1})\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon-\frac{y_{r}-y_{0}}{r\pi}\inf_{I}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime\prime}\;(x_{i+1}-x_{i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon-\frac{(y_{r}-y_{0})\delta}{r\pi}\inf_{I}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime\prime}<0.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Improved eigenfrequency estimations

In this section, we leverage Proposition

). Specifically, we use the properties of
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
from Proposition

The first result of this section reflects that the normalized count of eigenfrequencies less than a given value
\displaystyle y
in
\displaystyle Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)
converges uniformly to
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y)
.

Theorem 5.1

(Uniform Discrete Weyl’s law)
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n^{-2}L_{\phi,n}^{p}}\sim_{\lambda}\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
. Then the sequence of functions

G_{n}^{p}(y)=\dfrac{\left|\left\{k=1,\dots,N:\hskip 9.24994pt\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\leq y\right\}\right|}{N+1},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall y\in Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right),

converges uniformly, as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow+\infty
, to
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. We observe that the range of the function
\displaystyle\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}
is decomposed as

\begin{split}Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)&=\left[0,\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{1,h}^{\phi}}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N-OUT(p,n)-1}\left[\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}},\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k+1,h}^{\phi}}\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt\cup\left[\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}},\max Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)\right].\end{split}

Hence,

\max_{y\in Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)}\left|G_{n}^{p}(y)-\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y)\right|=\max\left\{T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}\right\},

(45)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{1}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y):\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq y<\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{1,h}^{\phi}}\right\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{2}=\max\left\{\left|\dfrac{k}{N+1}-\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y)\right|:1\leq k\leq N-OUT(p,n)-1\text{ and }\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\leq y<\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k+1,h}^{\phi}}\right\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle{and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{3}=\max\left\{\left|\dfrac{N-OUT(p,n)}{N+1}-\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y)\right|:\;\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}\leq y\leq\max Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)\right\}.

Now, we will estimate the three terms
\displaystyle T_{1}
,
\displaystyle T_{2}
, and
\displaystyle T_{3}
. For
\displaystyle T_{1}
, using the monotonicity of the function
\displaystyle y\mapsto\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(y)
, we have:

T_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\left(\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{1,h}^{\phi}}\right).

(46)

Applying the mean value theorem, Proposition
2.2
and the relation (
24
), we obtain:

T_{2}\leq\frac{C}{\pi}\max_{1\leq k\leq N-1-OUT(p,n)}\left(T_{2,k}\right),

(47)

and

T_{3}\leq\frac{C}{\pi}T_{3,k},

(48)

where

C:=\sup_{y\geq 0}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{\prime}(y),

T_{2,k}=\left(\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|+\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k+1,h}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|\right),

and

\begin{split}T_{3,k}&=\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{N-OUT(p,n)}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}\right|\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt+\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}-\max Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)\right|.\end{split}

We now establish that all the terms
\displaystyle T_{1}
,
\displaystyle T_{2}
, and
\displaystyle T_{3}
tend to zero as
\displaystyle n
tends to infinity.

From (
32
), we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{1,h}^{\phi}}-\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi}{N+1}\right)\right|=0.

Since the function
\displaystyle\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{-1}
is continuous, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi}{N+1}\right)=\left(\Psi^{p}_{\phi}\right)^{-1}\left(0\right)=0.

Hence,

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{1,h}^{\phi}}=0.

(49)

Injecting the above limit (
49
) in (
46
) and using the fact that
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}(0)=0,

we get

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}T_{1}=0.

(50)

For the term
\displaystyle T_{2},

we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{2,k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|+\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k+1,h}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|+\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k+1}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k+1,h}^{\phi}}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt+\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k+1}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)\right|.

Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{2}\leq\frac{C}{\pi}\Bigg\{3

\displaystyle\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\max_{k=1,\cdots N-1-OUT(p,n)}\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k+1}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)\right|\Bigg\}.

(51)

On the other hand, since the function
\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}
is continuous over
\displaystyle[0,1]
, it is also uniformly continuous. This implies that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\max_{k=1,\cdots N-1-OUT(p,n)}\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k+1}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{k}{N+1}\right)\right|=0.

(52)

Inserting the limit (
52
) along with the estimate (
32
) into inequality (
5
) leads to

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}T_{2}=0.

(53)

We now, check the limit of the last term
\displaystyle T_{3}
. Using (
32
), we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\dfrac{N-OUT(p,n)}{N+1}\right)-\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}\right|=0.

(54)

From Corollary
2.1
, we have
\displaystyle OUT(p,n)=o(N)
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{N-OUT(p,n)}{N+1}=1.

(55)

Injecting (
55
) in (
54
) and employing the continuity of
\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
, we get

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}=\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}(1).

Observe that
\displaystyle\max Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega^{p}_{\phi}}\right)=\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}(1)
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{N-OUT(p,n),h}^{\phi}}-\max Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega_{\phi}^{p}}\right)\right|=0.

(56)

Taking the limit in (
48
) and using the two limits (

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}T_{3}=0.

(57)

Applying the limit in (

) and incorporating the three derived limits (

), (

In the following, we leverage the symbol’s asymptotic behavior in the zero neighborhood to derive new estimates for the eigenfrequencies. The next result indicates an equivalence between the eigenfrequency behavior near zero and the order of approximation in (

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, such that
\displaystyle\sqrt{n^{-2}L_{\phi,n}^{p}}\sim_{\lambda}\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
. Suppose there exists a constant
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, such that for any subsequence
\displaystyle(k(n))\subseteq\mathcal{I}(p,n)
satisfying
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{k(n)}{N+1}=0
, the following holds:

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}}{\gamma\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}=\beta.

(58)

where
\displaystyle\gamma
is given by (
30
). Then, the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

\displaystyle\beta=1.

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}\left\{\frac{N+1}{k}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k,h}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k}{N+1}\right)\right|\right\}=0.

Proof.

Assuming that (
58
) holds, we proceed with the first implication, showing that
\displaystyle(i)
implies
\displaystyle(ii)
using proof by contradiction. Specifically, we assume
\displaystyle(i)
holds and then suppose, for contradiction, that
\displaystyle(ii)
is false. Then, there exists a subsequence
\displaystyle((k(n))\subset\mathcal{I}(p,n)
, such that

\frac{N+1}{k(n)}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)\right|>\varepsilon,

(59)

for some
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
independent of
\displaystyle n
.

Since
\displaystyle\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)
is bounded, there exists a subsequence, which we also denote by
\displaystyle\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)
, such that (
59
) holds and

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{k(n)}{N+1}=x_{0}\in[0,1].

By the continuity of
\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
and (
29
), we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}(x_{0})}{x_{0}},&\hskip 9.24994ptx_{0}\neq 0,\\
\gamma,&\hskip 9.24994ptx_{0}=0.\end{array}\right.

(60)

Using the assumption (
58
) along with the Discrete Weyl’s law (
15
), we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}(x_{0})}{x_{0}},&\hskip 9.24994ptx_{0}\neq 0,\\
\gamma,&\hskip 9.24994ptx_{0}=0.\end{array}\right.

(61)

By substituting (
60
) and (
61
) in (
59
), we arrive at
\displaystyle 0\geq\varepsilon
, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof of the first implication.

We now focus on establishing the second implication, namely, that
\displaystyle(ii)
implies
\displaystyle(i)
. Let
\displaystyle(k(n))\subset\mathcal{I}(p,n)
, such that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{k(n)}{N+1}=0
. From
\displaystyle(ii)
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{N+1}{k(n)}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}-\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)\right|=0.

(62)

On the other hand,

\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}}{\gamma\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda_{k(n),h}^{\phi}}}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}-\frac{\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}\right]+\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{\sqrt{\xi_{\phi}^{p}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}.

(63)

By taking the limit in the equality (
63
) and using (
62
) along with the linear behavior of the symbol near zero (
29
), we obtain
\displaystyle\beta=1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

The next result gives an equivalence estimate to (

), illustrating the asymptotic equivalence between the sequence of eigenfrequencies and the sequence generated by the uniform sampling of the symbol, up to an additional sequence term.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathbf{C}_{[0,1]}
, such that
\displaystyle\sqrt{n^{-2}L_{\phi,n}^{p}}\sim_{\lambda}\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)}\left\{\frac{k}{N+1}\left|\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda^{\phi}_{k,h}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k}{N+1}\right)}-1\right|\right\}=0.

(64)

Proof.

We will establish only the first implication, namely, that (
32
) implies (
64
). The second implication can be derived using similar steps. Let
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathcal{I}(p,n)

\frac{k}{N+1}\left|\frac{\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda^{\phi}_{k,h}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k}{N+1}\right)}-1\right|=\frac{\frac{k}{N+1}}{\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k}{N+1}\right)}\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda^{\phi}_{k,h}}-\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k}{N+1}\right)\right|.

64

\displaystyle(i)
implies
\displaystyle(ii)
in the above corollary, we can construct a subsequence
\displaystyle(k(n))\subset\mathcal{I}(p,n)

for some
\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle n
. Then, using (
65

\left|\sqrt{n^{-2}\lambda^{\phi}_{k(n),h}}-\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)\right|>\varepsilon\frac{\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}\left(\frac{k(n)}{N+1}\right)}{\frac{k(n)}{N+1}}.

For the second implication, one can deduce that for all

6
Conclusions, further comments, and future work

We have shown that GLT theory can provide complex and novel insights into the distribution of eigenfrequencies and the effect of reparametrization on the eigenfrequency spectrum. Using the regularity of
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
established in
[
4
]
, we developed an ordering relation among different mappings and their associated families of eigenfrequencies. Furthermore, we showed that this regularity leads to uniform convergence for the discrete Weyl’s law (Theorem
2.1
). Additionally, we illustrated how the convexity of
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi}
can be leveraged to shift the eigenfrequency. Moreover, we established a linear asymptotic behavior of the symbol
\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi^{p}_{\phi}}
near zero, enabling us to derive new estimations for eigenfrequencies.

The analysis presented in this study is related to the IGA discretization of the Laplace operator, utilizing regular B-splines. Throughout, our investigation of eigenfrequency distribution reveals that our analysis is independent of the specific discretization method and the particular operator under consideration. What remains crucial is the matrix symbol resulting from a numerical discretization of any given operator, and in this direction, the GLT theory is very flexible, as already emphasized in the seminal paper [
[
24
]
, pp. 376-377, Remark 2.1, Remark 2.2, Section 6, Theorem 6.2, Corollary 6.3, Corollary 6.4, Remark 6.4]. Consequently, we can extend the same study to finite difference discretization, higher-order Lagrangian finite element approximation, and optimal spline subspaces
[
20
]
.

To derive the estimations in both Corollary
5.1
and Corollary
5.3
, we assumed that the eigenfrequencies exhibit the same behavior as the symbol near zero, which can be seen as a hypothesis equivalent to assumption (i) in [Theorem 3.3,
[
3
]
]. In future work, we aim to investigate and formally establish this assumption. Furthermore, in Theorem
4.1
, we have avoided the intersection points of the functions
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\Psi^{p}_{\phi_{2}}
; studying the behavior of eigenfrequencies at these points would be interesting. Moreover, in the alternative theorems to Theorem
4.1
, namely Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
, we have provided only a subset of reparametrizations that allow ordering of eigenfrequencies within a portion of
\displaystyle Rg\left(\sqrt{\omega^{p}_{\phi}}\right)
. Further investigation into the eigenfrequency behavior in the rest of the range, along with the construction of a reparametrizations subset that permits ordering across the entire range, remains necessary.

Lastly, it is worth noting that all the findings presented in this paper, which encompass the improvement of the results in
[
3
]
, along with our analysis of eigenfrequencies distribution, can be extended to domains of higher dimensions (see
[
25
]
,
[
1
]
, and
[
13
]
).
