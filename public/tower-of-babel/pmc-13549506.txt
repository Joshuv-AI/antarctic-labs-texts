Real-World Safety and Efficacy of Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for Chronic Subdural Hematoma: A Retrospective Cohort Study of 38 049 Patients
Authors: Ariel Sacknovitz, Isaac B. Thorman, Dinusha Wanniarachchige, Eris Spirollari, Ankita Jain, Michael C. Schubert, Sauson Soldozy, Ilya Frid, Gurmeen Kaur, Stephan A. Mayer, Chirag D. Gandhi, Fawaz Al-Mufti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Chronic subdural hematoma is a complex but commonly treated neurosurgical condition, particularly affecting the elderly. Traditional treatment involves open surgeries, but middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) has emerged as an effective, minimally invasive adjunctive. The objective of this study is to assess the safety and efficacy of MMAE in combination with surgery using real-world data.METHODS:This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the TriNetX Research Network. Outcomes of mortality, reoperation, and hospital readmission were studied using Cox proportional hazard models controlling for age, sex, indications of severity, type of surgical intervention, antiplatelet/anticoagulant use, comorbidities, and hospitalization events. Patients were followed for up to 6 years.RESULTS:This study included 38 049 patients with chronic subdural hematoma, of which 486 received surgery with MMAE and 5362 received surgery monotherapy. At 90 days after surgery, mortality was 5.2% in the MMAE group and 7.7% in the surgery monotherapy group (P=0.0467). After 6 years, mortality was 11.2% in the MMAE group and 19.0% in the surgery monotherapy group (P<0.0001). However, the hazard of 90-day mortality was not significantly lower in the MMAE group after controlling for covariates (hazard ratio, 0.739 [95% CI, 0.479–1.141]; P=0.173). Patients with MMAE had significantly lower rates of rehospitalization at 1 year (2.1% versus 4.4%; P=0.0195), even after adjustment (hazard ratio, 0.414 [95% CI, 0.203–0.845]; P=0.015).CONCLUSIONS:Among patients with chronic subdural hematoma who received surgery, patients receiving adjunctive MMAE experienced lower mortality rates out to 6 years of follow-up, but the difference was not significant after full adjustment. Beginning 1 year after surgery, patients receiving combination therapy had significantly lower long-term rehospitalization compared with patients receiving surgery monotherapy. Future prospective studies will help identify strengths and weaknesses of this highly promising treatment modality.

Chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is one of the most common pathologies seen in the neurosurgical service. cSDH has an incidence of 1.7 to 20.6 per 100 000 patients per year, predominantly affecting the elderly population.1 The anticipated demographic shift towards an aging population is projected to result in a higher prevalence of cSDH as well.2 While the pathophysiology of nontraumatic cSDH is poorly understood, it is thought that subtraumatic injuries lead to splitting of the dural tissue, resulting in the release of angiogenic factors.1,3 A neovascular membrane forms over the subsequent weeks to years as inflammatory cytokines and cells incrementally expand the area, creating an increasing vulnerability to rupture.1,3

Although the standard of care intervention has long included open neurosurgical procedures such as Burr holes and craniotomies,4,5 endovascular middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) has emerged as an adjunct therapy to surgery or a stand-alone treatment. Recently, a trio of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in the New England Journal of Medicine compared the safety and efficacy of the combination therapy to surgery monotherapy.6–8 The EMBOLISE trial (Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery With Onyx Liquid Embolic System in the Treatment of Subacute and Chronic Subdural Hematoma) showed a significant reduction in cSDH recurrence or regression within 90 days of operation.6 The MAGIC-MT trial (Managing Non-Acute Subdural Hematoma Using Liquid Materials: A Chinese Randomized Trial of Middle Meningeal Artery Treatment) showed a smaller, nonsignificant reduction of 90-day recurrence or regression.7 The STEM trial (Squid Trial for the Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery for the Treatment of Chronic Subdural Hematoma) showed a significant reduction in a composite outcome including cSDH recurrence, death, and stroke at 180 days postoperation.8

Although these trials were exceptionally well designed and produced invaluable results, their exclusion of more clinically severe patients limits their generalizability. The objective of this study is to assess the safety and efficacy of surgery monotherapy compared with surgery with MMAE in a large and diverse patient population with cSDH. Because we did not exclude any patients, based on severity or other criteria, we hypothesized that our results would support those of EMBOLISE, MAGIC-MT, and STEM, but would offer a more tempered assessment of MMAE.

Methods

Study Population and Exposures

This retrospective cohort study used data accessed between December 4, 2024 and February 17, 2024, from the TriNetX Research Network, which provided access to electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values) from ≈142 million patients from 105 healthcare organizations.9 The data reviewed are a secondary analysis of existing data, do not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and are deidentified per the deidentification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) Privacy Rule. Updates and new patients are added daily, so the number of patients present at the moment of each individual analysis may fluctuate. The data that support the findings of this study are available from TriNetX, LLC, but third-party restrictions apply to the availability of these data. TriNetX data are available for licensing at TriNetX, LLC. Data access may require a data sharing agreement and may incur data access fees.

Among the TriNetX Research Network, patients were included if their medical record included the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition diagnosis for nontraumatic chronic subdural hemorrhage (I62.03) when at least 18 years old. We did not exclude any patients. Patients were followed for up to 6 years, the maximal time with substantive results from all patient groups.

In line with a prior study of cSDH in TriNetX, patients underwent surgery within 30 days of their cSDH diagnosis.10 As in prior literature, surgery was defined by either of the following Current Procedural Terminology codes: 61312: craniectomy or craniotomy or 61154: burr hole(s) with evacuation or drainage of hematoma.10 Similar to EMBOLISE, which allowed 5 days after surgery for embolization to occur, patients receiving combination therapy with embolization were embolized within 7 days on or after their surgery.6 We defined embolization by either of the Current Procedural Terminology codes (61624 or 61626) for transcatheter permanent occlusion or embolization. Full codes and descriptions are listed in Table S1. All patients were indexed on their first surgery. Embolizations that occurred after 7 days postsurgery were considered unplanned and, therefore, did not affect whether a patient was considered to be in the surgery monotherapy or combination therapy groups. For illustration, we also included patients who received neither surgery nor embolization (no intervention) and patients who received embolization but no surgery at any time (embolization monotherapy), but these groups were used for illustration purposes only and were not included in analytic comparisons. Patients in the no intervention and embolization monotherapy groups were indexed on their cSDH diagnoses.

Outcomes

The outcomes for this study were mortality, reoperation, and rehospitalization. Mortality was determined by a vital status of deceased and could occur at any time starting on the day of surgery. Reoperation was defined as either of the surgery codes above (61312 or 61154) or either code for decompressive craniectomy or craniotomy (61322 or 61323). Full code descriptions are listed in Table S1. Rehospitalization was defined by the Current Procedural Terminology code 1013652: new or established patient initial hospital observation care services. As this code had a relatively low frequency in the analysis, it is likely to have high specificity but poor sensitivity. In contrast to the mortality outcome, the outcomes of reoperation and rehospitalization were studied beginning the day after surgery.

Potential Confounders/Covariates of Interest

As has been previously published, we controlled for 95 covariates, including measures of demographics, comorbidities, medications, surgical procedures, and hospitalization events (Table S2).11,12 Covariates were ascertained in the lifetime leading up to and including the day of surgery. Briefly, we controlled for age at index, sex, factors included in EMBOLISE, criteria from the National Inpatient Sample–Subarachnoid Hemorrhage Severity Scale, type of neurosurgical intervention (craniectomy/craniotomy versus burr hole), comorbidities of the Charlson Comorbidity Index, measures presented in prior studies of severity in neurosurgical patients, and others.6,13–17

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using TriNetX’s Advanced Analytics Platform, which uses R version 3.4.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Python version 3.6.5 (Python Software Foundation, Centrum voor Wiskunde en Informatica, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Statistical significance was established at α<0.05. To protect patient confidentiality, the Advanced Analytics Platform does not return patient counts from queries with 1 to 10 patients, reporting all such values as ≤10.

We used Cox proportional hazard models to compare patients receiving surgery and embolization combination therapy to patients receiving surgical monotherapy. All models included full covariate adjustment as described above. Each of the 3 outcomes—mortality, reoperation, and hospital readmission—was analyzed separately, with mortality analyses beginning the day of surgery and reoperation and readmission analyses beginning the day after surgery. In line with EMBOLISE and MAGIC-MT, the primary outcome and timeframe of interest was 90-day mortality, but other timeframes were studied as well.6,7

In a subanalysis, to further replicate the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria of the RCTs, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with severe presentations: hydrocephalus, paralytic strabismus, sequelae of cerebrovascular disease, stupor, coma, and mechanical ventilation (see Table S1 for codes). 1:1 propensity score matching was used on the full set of covariates from the multivariable Cox proportional hazard model (Table S2). Propensity score matching was performed using a nearest-neighbor method with a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs, without replacement. Cox proportional hazard models were then used to compare patients with surgery and embolization to patients with surgery alone on 90-day mortality, as above.

Results

Study Population

We identified 38 049 patients with chronic subdural hematoma, of which 486 received combination therapy with surgery and embolization, 5362 received surgery monotherapy, 1510 received embolization monotherapy, and 22 507 received neither surgery nor embolization. Patients receiving the combination therapy were significantly older than patients receiving surgery monotherapy (70.71±12.24 versus 68.88±12.88 [mean±SD], P=0.0024; Table 1). Combination therapy patients had significantly lower prevalences of nicotine dependence (12.9% versus 16.6%, P=0.0388), heart failure (12.9% versus 17.4%, P=0.0137), hyperosmolality and hypernatremia (3.2% versus 6.1%, P=0.0103), and coma (17.4% versus 25.2%, P=0.0002) compared with patients receiving surgery monotherapy (Table S2). Combination therapy patients had significantly higher prevalences of chronic ischemic heart disease (32.0% versus 27.4%, P=0.0359) and deep vein thrombosis (10.1% versus 6.3%, P=0.0018).

Mortality

Combination therapy patients had significantly lower mortality at 30 days (3.2% versus 5.3%, P=0.0481) and 90 days (5.2% versus 7.7%, P=0.0467) compared with patients receiving surgery monotherapy (Table 2). The difference was not significant from 180 days (6.9% versus 9.4%, P=0.0675) to 1 year (8.6% versus 11.5%, P=0.0597), but was significant at 3 years (10.5% versus 16.0%, P=0.0019) and 6 years (11.2% versus 19.0%, P<0.0001; Figures 1 and 2). After full adjustment, combination therapy was associated with a nonsignificant decrease in the hazard of 90-day mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 0.739; [95% CI, 0.479–1.141]; P=0.173; Table S3). Intracranial hypertension (HR, 3.361; [95% CI, 2.097–5.387]; P<0.001), ventilation for 24 to 96 hours (HR, 2.442; [95% CI, 1.763–3.383]; P<0.001), and ventilation for >96 hours (HR, 2.853; [95% CI, 2.050–3.971]; P<0.001) were among the most significant factors contributing to 90-day mortality. In comparison, mortality after MMAE monotherapy was 3.7% at 30 days, 9.1% at 180 days, 10.5% at 1 year, and 15.2% at 6 years.

Reoperation and Rehospitalization

The frequency of reoperation did not significantly differ between the patients in both groups (Table 2). After full adjustment, combination therapy was associated with a nonsignificant increase in the hazard of 90-day reoperation (HR, 1.064 [95% CI, 0.789–1.437]; P=0.6833).

Patients receiving surgery monotherapy had a steady increase in rehospitalization throughout follow-up, whereas patients receiving combination therapy plateaued in rehospitalization after 30 days (Table 2). As a result, patients receiving combination therapy did not have a significant difference in rehospitalization at 30 days (2.1% versus 1.3%, P=0.1158), 90 days (2.1% versus 2.5%, P=0.6262), and 180 days (2.1% versus 3.5%, P=0.1248). There was significantly lower rehospitalization at 1 year (2.1% versus 4.4%, P=0.0195), 3 years (2.1% versus 6.4%, P=0.0002), and 6 years (2.1% versus 7.3%, P<0.0001). After full adjustment, combination therapy remained nonsignificant through 180 days and had a significantly reduced hazard of rehospitalization at 1 year (HR, 0.414 [95% CI, 0.203–0.845]; P=0.015). Hydrocephalus (HR, 1.801 [95% CI, 1.031–3.146]; P=0.039) and tracheostomy (HR, 3.849 [95% CI, 1.242–11.926]; P=0.020) were complications associated with an increased hazard of rehospitalization. In comparison, patients who underwent MMAE monotherapy were rehospitalized at rates of 0.7% at 30 days, 1.8% at 180 days, 2.0% at 1 year, and 2.8% at both 3 and 6 years.

Sensitivity Analysis

After applying the additional exclusion criteria to more closely mimic the RCT criteria, there were 312 matched patients in each treatment group. Of these, 3.2% of combination patients died, and 5.4% of surgery monotherapy patients died (P=0.1684). On proportional hazards modeling, combination therapy was again associated with a nonsignificant decrease in 90-day mortality (HR, 0.521 [95% CI, 0.232–1.170]; P=0.1079).

Discussion

We present a real-world analysis of 38 049 patients with cSDH. Combined surgery with adjunctive MMAE, although nonsignificant, was associated with decreased mortality and rehospitalization, but a slight increase in reoperation, compared with surgery monotherapy. After applying additional exclusion criteria to more closely mimic the recent RCTs, we demonstrated a larger, though still nonsignificant, reduction in mortality. To our knowledge, this is the first study to date that uses a large and diverse sample with real-world data to directly test the conclusions of the 2024 landmark RCTs.6–8

The clinical trials, while groundbreaking, were limited in generalizability by their exclusion criteria. EMBOLISE excluded patients with a life expectancy of <1 year, a premorbid modified Rankin Scale score >3/6, a Markwalder Grading Scale score >2/4, leukemia, renal insufficiency, or continued use of antithrombotic agents.6 MAGIC-MT excluded patients with a premorbid modified Rankin Scale score of at least 3 of 6 or if either a craniotomy or emergency hematoma evacuation was required.7 STEM excluded patients with premorbid modified Rankin Scale score >1/6 or if they received a craniotomy for their hematoma.8 In contrast, just over half of our sample who received either surgery monotherapy or combination therapy underwent a craniectomy or craniotomy. It is therefore unsurprising that there were differences in outcome frequencies between our cohort and those of the clinical trials. Our unadjusted 90-day mortality was 5.2% for combination therapy and 7.7% for surgery monotherapy, whereas 90-day mortality was 4.6% and 2.0%, respectively, in EMBOLISE.6

Our results build on the RCTs by showing that patients receiving combination therapy experienced a significant decrease in long-term rehospitalization rates compared with surgery monotherapy patients. No significant differences in hospital readmission were found in EMBOLISE and MAGIC-MT, whereas STEM did not include rehospitalization as an outcome.6–8 In our cohort, rehospitalization was only significantly lower in combination therapy patients beginning 1 year after surgery. EMBOLISE capped follow-up at 90 days, whereas the median follow-up time in MAGIC-MT was 91 days (interquartile range, 86–96 days).6,7 As our patients were followed for up to 6 years, it is possible that the significant association shown here is due to the increased follow-up time enabled by the TriNetX data set. Longitudinal follow-up of the RCT study participants will be critical in assessing these secondary safety outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings, some of which are inherent to using a large, international, federated database. First, this study uses International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition codes. It is not known what type of medical professional recorded the codes or for what reason, and there was likely significant heterogeneity between coders and healthcare organizations. In addition, there is likely a severity bias in which more severe diagnoses were recorded. Second, there was no conformity in treatment choices; patients received any combination of surgery and embolization based on the best judgment of clinicians and the capability of medical centers. Third, despite best efforts to control for severity, it is highly likely that more severe patients received more interventions or died before potentially life-saving interventions could be provided, introducing an inherent bias. Specific imaging characteristics such as hematoma size, thickness, location, volume, midline shift, and brain compression were unavailable for analysis in this database. Furthermore, although we used International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition codes in an attempt to piece together the baseline state of the patients, gold-standard measures, such as modified Rankin Scale score and Markwalder Grading Scale score, were not available for analysis. Nevertheless, this is the largest and most diverse analysis of MMAE and neurosurgical interventions to date, and our findings closely align with those shown in previous clinical trial literature.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study of 38 049 patients with cSDH, MMAE was a safe and effective adjunctive treatment to traditional neurosurgical approaches. Embolized patients experienced decreases in both mortality and hospital readmission, consistent with recent clinical trials and published literature. Prospective studies with longer follow-up, radiographic analysis, and direct patient evaluation are still warranted to ascertain the true strengths and weaknesses of this promising therapy.
