Suicidal ideation as an additional risk for violence against women residing in shelters: a clinical sample analysis in Hong Kong
Authors: Ko Ling Chan, Qianru Wu, Qiqi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntimate partner violence (IPV) is a major public health issue associated with adverse mental health outcomes, including suicidal ideation. However, limited research has examined the correlates of suicidal ideation among shelter-seeking women. This study analyzed data from a clinical sample of 597 women who sought services from three major shelters for battered women in Hong Kong between 2020 and 2024. Among the participants, 48.1% reported suicidal ideation, and 21.3% were classified as having high-risk suicidal ideation based on its frequency in the preceding year. Logistic regression analyses identified several factors associated with high-risk suicidal ideation across victim, perpetrator, and family domains. Among participants’ characteristics, lower educational attainment, being divorced or separated, chronic illness, and diagnosed mental illness were significantly associated with high-risk suicidal ideation. Perpetrator-related factors, including histories of childhood abuse or neglect, jealousy, and depressive symptoms, were also significantly associated with high-risk suicidal ideation. In addition, family members’ chronic illness, mental illness, and gambling problems were associated with high-risk suicidal ideation. These findings underscore the importance of holistic and trauma-informed approaches in shelter settings that consider individual, relational, and family-level circumstances. Future research should further examine these associations over time and explore ways to strengthen shelter-based mental health support for this vulnerable population.Keywords: Suicide ideation, Intimate partner violence, Battered women shelters, Risk factorsSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Intimate partner violence as a global public health issue

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is one of the most pervasive violations of human rights and a major public health concern worldwide, affecting women across cultural, social, and economic contexts1. Violence against women (VAW) encompasses a wide range of abuses directed at women and girls, with particular emphasis on its gender-based nature, as it is predominantly perpetrated by men against women2,3. According to the World Health Organization4, IPV is among the most common forms of VAW and includes physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, as well as controlling behaviours perpetrated by a current or former intimate partners. Globally, approximately 30% of women aged 15 years and older have experienced IPV in their lifetime, although prevalence varies substantially across regions5. The prevalence is lowest in East Asia (16.3%) and highest in sub-Saharan Africa (65.6%). In Hong Kong, a large-scale telephone survey revealed that one in five women had experienced violence since the age of 16, and nearly one in ten had been assaulted by an intimate partner, with sexual violence reported more frequently than physical violence2. In clinical and service settings, the burden appears even greater. A recent study in Hong Kong showed that more than half of battered women using shelter services reported both IPV and child abuse, and nearly one-quarter had endured abuse for more than 10 years6.

The health consequences of IPV are both immediate and long term. Physical and sexual violence can result in acute injuries such as fractures, burns, head and facial trauma, brain injuries, and, in severe cases, death7. A growing body of evidence shows that IPV is associated with substantial long-term physical health consequences, including more severe menopausal symptoms, increased risk of diabetes, sexually transmitted infections, chronic pain, and other chronic health conditions. IPV is also associated with greater engagement in health-risk behaviors, including alcohol and drug misuse7.

The psychological impacts of IPV are equally profound. Research has consistently documented strong associations between IPV victimization and depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder8. Psychological abuse, although less visible than physical assault, may be particularly damaging because it reinforces coercive control and gradually erodes women’s mental well-being over time8.

IPV and suicidal ideation

One of the most serious mental health consequences associated with IPV is suicidal ideation. Previous studies have consistently shown that exposure to IPV is associated with suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts. For example, a study of university students found that approximately 26% had experienced dating violence, and 32% reported suicidal ideation. The relationship between intimate partner rape and suicidal ideation was mediated by posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression, suggesting that mental health symptoms may be an important pathway linking violence exposure to suicide risk9. Similarly, Renner and Markward10 found that 43% of women residing in a domestic violence shelter reported suicidal ideation. Their findings further suggested that both past and current traumatic experiences contribute to suicide risk, with childhood physical and sexual abuse, greater IPV severity, and recent IPV exposure identified as significant predictors10. These findings highlight the cumulative effect of trauma across the life course.

The association between IPV and suicidal ideation is also shaped by broader family vulnerabilities. Higher rates of IPV have been observed in families facing multiple disadvantages, including households affected by schizophrenia, disability, or chronic mental illness11–13. The relationship between IPV and suicidal ideation is unlikely to be purely unidirectional. Devries et al.14 proposed three possible pathways: (1) IPV leads to subsequent psychological symptoms and suicidal behaviors; (2) pre-existing mental health conditions increase vulnerability to IPV; and (3) IPV and suicidality share common risk factors. Consistent with this framework, women with severe mental illness have been found to be more vulnerable to violent victimization than women without such conitions15. Psychological vulnerabilities may also influence partner selection and relationship dynamics, contributing to insecure attachment, reduced relationship support, greater conflict, and prolonged involvement in abusive relationships14,16. Regardless of causal direction, the co-occurrence of IPV and suicidal ideation signals heightened vulnerability and a pressing need for targeted assessment and intervention.

Shelter use among women experiencing IPV

For women experiencing IPV, the decision to leave an abusive relationship and seek shelter is shaped by multiple structural and interpersonal factors. Social networks and neighborhood context can influence quality of life, housing satisfaction, and women’s likelihood of seeking help17. However, poverty remains one of the most commonly identified barriers to leaving and remaining separated from an abusive partner, as relocation and housing costs create substantial obstacles to safety and stability18. Indeed, IPV is a major contributor to women’s housing instability and homelessness19,20.

Women use different strategies to cope with abuse. Some rely on informal support, such as staying temporarily with relatives or friends, whereas others seek formal services such as shelters. Women who seek formal support may differ from non-help-seekers in important ways or may face more severe and complex circumstances that compel them to pursue external assistance. Shelters for battered women are therefore critical resources, providing both immediate safety and opportunities to seek support and plan for future living arrangements21. A systematic review by Yakubovich and affiliates emphasized the importance of housing interventions for improving the physical, psychological, and economic well-being of women experiencing IPV18.

Nevertheless, many victims do not seek shelter services. Leaving an abusive partner is often constrained by fear of escalating violence, economic dependence, limited social support, concerns for children, and fear of stigma22. Prior research suggests that help-seeking tends to increase with the severity and frequency of violence. Women exposed to severe physical violence are more likely to leave home than those experiencing less severe abuse. Educational and marital status may also influence help-seeking, with single women and those with higher education more likely to leave than older married women22.

Children are another important factor shaping women’s decisions to seek help. Dufort23 found no significant difference in violence severity between shelter users and non-users, but women who sought shelter were more likely to have children, particularly children living with the perpetrator. Concern that violence may extend to children, or that children may be harmed by witnessing abuse, is a major motivator for help-seeking among mothers24. These concerns are well-founded, given the high co-occurrence of IPV and child abuse and neglect (CAN). In Hong Kong, nearly half of women in shelter services have reported both IPV and CAN involving their children; these women were also more likely to be younger, unemployed, and recent immigrants6. Thus, by the time women enter shelter services, they often face multiple, accumulated risks beyond IPV itself, including physical and mental health problems6, adjustment difficulties21, and possibly suicidal risk.

Knowledge gap and significance of the study

Although the association between IPV and suicidal ideation is well documented, important gaps remain in the literature. Relatively little is known about suicidal ideation among battered women residing in shelters, despite the likelihood that this group represents one of the most vulnerable subpopulations of IPV survivors. The present study aims to examine the prevalence and patterns of suicidal ideation among battered women using shelter services in Hong Kong. Specifically, it seeks to: (1) examine the prevalence of suicidal ideation among battered women in shelters; and (2) identify factors associated with suicidal ideation, including abuse-related and psychosocial factors. A better understanding of these patterns is crucial for the early identification of women at heightened risk of suicide and for informing targeted screening and intervention strategies in shelter settings.

Methods

Study setting and sample

This was a cross-sectional study based on routinely collected risk assessment data collected from three of the five major refuge centers for battered women in Hong Kong operated by Po Leung Kuk, a local charitable organization dedicated to the protection of women and children. These refuge centers provide temporary accommodation, conduct risk assessments, and offer professional support services to assist women in coping with emotional distress, developing safety plans, and reducing the impact of family violence on their children.

Risk assessment records were obtained for 597 women who sought assistance from or resided in these refuge centers between 2020 and 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Approval ID: HSEARS20190704001).

Measures

Demographic characteristics and health conditions of victims

Participants provided demographic information about themselves. Variables included age, educational attainment, employment status, and marital status. Health conditions included the presence of chronic illness, diagnosed mental illness, and physical or emotional problems, such as insomnia, headaches, muscle pain, loss of appetite, fatigue, substance misuse, panic or fear, feeling depressed, anxiety, and general emotional problems. All health-related information was self-reported by participants.

Suicidal ideation

Participants were asked about suicidal ideation and related concerns. Specifically, they reported the frequency of suicidal thoughts during the preceding year using a four-point scale: 1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = occasionally, 4 = frequently. For analysis, participants who responded “never” or “rarely” were classified as having low-risk suicidal ideation, whereas those who responded “occasionally” or “frequently” were classified as having high-risk suicidal ideation. Additional items assessed whether participants had: considered suicide methods, attempted suicide, thought about dying by suicide together with their children or family members, and worried about who would care for their children or family after their death. The four-point scale was chosen to assess suicidal ideation directly, rather than indirectly through general depressive symptoms. The measure incorporates key domains of the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)25, including suicidal thoughts and behaviors, while allowing greater cultural and contextual relevance for this population. By specifying the preceding year, the measure captures a clear and meaningful timeframe. It also includes culturally salient concerns related to children and family responsibilities. The reliability and validity of this approach have been supported in previous peer-reviewed studies9,26, indicating its appropriateness for this population.

Risk assessment

Risk profile among battered women in the refuge centers was assessed by trained social workers using a previously validated checklist6,27. The assessment covered the context of victimization, characteristics of the abuse, perpetrator-related factors, and family circumstances. It should be noted that information on perpetrators’ psychological characteristics (e.g., jealousy, controlling behavior) was based on victims’ reports during interviews. Direct clinical assessment of perpetrators was not feasible because safety protocols prohibited contact between refuge staff and the perpetrators. The assessment included the relationship between the participant and the perpetrator (spouse or former spouse), frequency of physical assault in the preceding year, and duration of violence. Participants were also asked about injuries, forced sex, threats, whether they were afraid of the perpetrator, and whether they were currently being harassed by the perpetrator. Based on the available information, social workers rated the case’s overall risk level as mild, moderate, or severe. Although this approach is more clinically judgment-based than actuarial, it has demonstrated strong practical utility for risk management, with reported interrater reliability exceeding 98%6.

Family disadvantage factors were also assessed to identify vulnerabilities that might intensify women’s risk. These included socioeconomic and psychosocial stressors such as disability, mental illness, chronic illness, indebtedness, and gambling. We also examined perpetrator-related factors, including a history of violence, childhood abuse or neglect, witnessing parental violence during childhood, difficulties in stress management, jealousy, controlling behavior, and depressive symptoms.

Intimate partner violence

Participants’ experiences of IPV victimization were assessed using items modified from the Chinese version of the Abuse Assessment Screen (AAS)28. These items measured experiences of physical assault, psychological aggression, sexual coercion, and fear related to IPV. Responses were recorded in a yes/no format.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, victimization experiences, and risk assessment factors. Comparisons were then made between women classified as having low-risk versus high-risk suicidal ideation. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, and independent-samples t-tests were used for continuous variables. Missing data were examined prior to analysis. As the proportion of missing data was small, analyses were conducted using complete-case methods, with cases excluded only from analyses involving variables with missing values.

Binary logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with suicidal ideation risk. First, univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each potential correlate. Multivariable analyses were then performed using a domain-specific modeling strategy rather than a single fully adjusted model. Specifically, demographic characteristics of victims were entered simultaneously in one model, and separate models were fitted for victims’ health conditions, perpetrators’ characteristics, and family disadvantage factors, with each domain adjusted for demographic characteristics. This approach was selected to identify a broad range of factors associated with suicidal ideation while minimizing overadjustment, reducing model complexity, and maintaining interpretability. It also reflects the study aim of examining correlates across multiple domains, rather than testing a single etiological model incorporating all variables simultaneously. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were reported. Prior to model fitting, logistic regression assumptions, including independence of observations and absence of problematic multicollinearity, were assessed. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics Version 29.

Results

Demographic characteristics and health conditions

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics and health conditions of the service users. Among the 597 battered women seeking shelter services, 78.7% (n = 470) were classified as having low-risk suicidal ideation in the preceding year, whereas 21.3% (n = 127) were classified as having high-risk suicidal ideation during the same period. Thus, more than one-fifth of the women reported high-risk suicidal ideation in the past year, suggesting a substantial burden in addition to the trauma associated with abuse. Overall, 287 participants (48.1%) reported suicidal ideation at some point in their lifetime. Among these women, 82 (28.6%) had considered suicide methods, 16 (5.6%) had attempted suicide, 12 (4.2%) had thought about dying by suicide together with their children or other family members, and 109 (38.0%) reported worry about who would care for their children or family after their death. Across these indicators, the high-risk suicidal ideation group generally reported higher proportions than the low-risk group. We further compared the characteristics of the low- and high-risk suicidal ideation groups. Compared with the low-risk group, women in the high-risk group more often had lower educational attainment, were divorced, and reported poorer physical health, including chronic illness, insomnia, headache, and loss of appetite. They also more frequently reported poorer mental health, including diagnosed mental illness, depressive feelings, and emotional problems, as well as higher levels of substance use.

Regression analyses of factors associated with suicidal ideation

Table 4 summarizes the factors associated with high-risk suicidal ideation, including both crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs). Interpretation focused primarily on the adjusted models. Among participants’ characteristics, lower educational attainment (aOR = 0.546), being divorced or separated (aOR = 2.380), having a chronic illness (aOR = 2.206), and having a diagnosed mental illness (aOR = 2.079) were significantly associated with high-risk suicidal ideation. Perpetrators’ characteristics and family disadvantages were also associated with participants’ suicidal ideation. After adjustment for demographic characteristics, perpetrators’ history of childhood abuse or neglect (aOR = 2.519), jealousy (aOR = 1.678), and symptoms of depression (aOR = 2.134) were significantly associated with greater odds of being in the high-risk suicidal ideation group. Difficulty with stress management and controlling behavior toward a partner showed marginal associations in the adjusted models, although these variables were not significant in the bivariate or crude analyses. With respect to family disadvantages, having a family member with mental illness (aOR = 1.802), chronic illness (aOR = 1.676), or a gambling problem (aOR = 1.711) was significantly associated with greater odds of belonging to the high-risk suicidal ideation group.

Discussion

The present study provides important insights into the prevalence and correlates of suicidal ideation among a clinical sample of battered women using shelter services in Hong Kong, a population characterized by prolonged exposure to IPV. Battered women may endure violence for extended periods before seeking help, often in the context of social norms, stigma, and family obligations6. In our sample, by the time women entered shelters, levels of vulnerability appeared substantial: approximately 90% were assessed as being at moderate to high risk of harm, and 21.3% were classified in the high-risk suicidal ideation group. This proportion is notable given the co-occurrence of IPV exposure and suicidal ideation. In addition, 28.6% of participants who reported lifetime suicidal ideation had considered specific methods of suicide, suggesting considerable severity in suicidal thoughts among some women in this sample. Although shelter residence may provide temporary protection from perpetrator violence, it does not necessarily eliminate suicidal ideation9,29,30. These findings underscore the importance of assessing and managing not only IPV-related danger, but also suicidality within shelter settings.

Our study identified a range of factors associated with high-risk suicidal ideation, including participants’ characteristics, perpetrator-related characteristics, and family disadvantages. Women in the high-risk group more often reported chronic illness and psychological distress, including insomnia, headaches, and loss of appetite, all of which may reflect substantial physical and emotional strain. The high prevalence of insomnia is particularly noteworthy, as prior research has linked sleep disturbance with suicidal ideation and suicide mortality31. In the present study, these health conditions should be understood as correlates of high-risk suicidal ideation rather than causal antecedents.

We also found that perpetrators’ histories of childhood abuse or neglect were more common among women in the high-risk suicidal ideation group. This pattern is consistent with literature suggesting that early trauma may be associated with later psychological vulnerability and dysregulated stress responses, which in turn may be linked to violent behavior in intimate relationships32. Some prior studies have also suggested that childhood adversity in abusive partners may be relevant to women’s suicidality within violent relationships33. Although our data do not permit conclusions about causal pathways, the findings suggest that trauma-informed perspectives may be useful in shelter assessment and intervention, including attention to trauma histories within the broader relational context.

Regarding perpetrator characteristics, jealousy and depressive symptoms were significantly associated with high-risk suicidal ideation among participants. This finding is broadly consistent with previous research suggesting that psychological abuse and coercive control may be more closely related to suicidality than physical violence alone8,34. Behaviors such as threats, intimidation, and scolding may be associated with helplessness and hopelessness, which are well-established correlates of suicidal ideation34. Psychological control may be particularly salient, as it can operate through domination, surveillance, and intimidation. Importantly, even after physical separation in shelter settings, some women may continue to experience threats, harassment, or emotional abuse through phone calls or other forms of contact. This may help explain why suicidality can remain pronounced despite temporary physical safety.

Family-related burdens also emerged as important correlates. Caring for family members with chronic illness or mental illness may continue, or even intensify, while women are in shelters. These ongoing responsibilities may be associated with elevated stress, emotional exhaustion, and hopelessness, which may in turn co-occur with suicidal ideation. In many Asian contexts, caregiving expectations shaped by cultural norms such as filial piety may place considerable emotional, practical, and financial pressure on women35, particularly in families affected by disability, gambling problems, or unemployment. These responsibilities may require career disruptions, ongoing financial contributions, and complex caregiving arrangements, all of which have been described as contributing to caregiver burden36,37. Our findings therefore highlight the importance of situating women’s suicidality within a broader family context, rather than examining individual-level factors alone.

Although the high prevalence of suicidal ideation in our sample suggests persistent vulnerability, the findings also point to the potential role of shelter services as supportive environments for IPV survivors. Housing interventions are central to providing immediate physical safety and stability38, while counselling services and peer support within shelters may help address emotional distress. Trauma-informed approaches, including safety planning and support network development, may also be relevant to residents’ mental well-being. At the same time, the persistence of high-risk suicidal ideation suggests that shelter services alone may be insufficient for some women and that more intensive or specialized mental health support may be warranted. This interpretation is consistent with previous research indicating that shelter-based interventions may be associated with improvements in mental health among IPV survivors18, while also suggesting that suicide-related concerns require ongoing attention within these settings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study drew on a clinical sample with a relatively modest sample size, and the non-representative nature of the sample may limit generalizability to the broader population of IPV survivors. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow examination of temporal ordering between suicidal ideation and associated factors. Third, the measurement and coding of suicidal ideation necessarily involved simplification. Distinguishing transient or infrequent suicidal thoughts from those indicating elevated clinical concern remains challenging, and categorical coding systems such as the C-SSRS may not fully capture this complexity. In this exploratory analysis, we grouped “never” and “rarely” as low-risk ideation and “occasionally” or “frequently” as high-risk ideation. Although this approach has limitations, it may still offer a clinically useful framework for examining variation in suicidal ideation severity in this sample. Fourth, the study relied primarily on self-report data, which may be subject to recall or reporting bias, particularly given the sensitivity of IPV and suicidality. In addition, perpetrator psychological characteristics were based on participants’ reports rather than direct clinical assessment. While these reports are highly relevant to the women’s lived experiences, they may not fully reflect perpetrators’ clinical status. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs to examine how suicidal ideation changes over time and whether shelter services are associated with changes in mental health outcomes. It would also be valuable to examine additional protective factors, including cultural and family support systems, which may be particularly relevant in Chinese contexts. Where feasible and safe, future studies might also incorporate perpetrator self-reports or clinical assessments to provide a fuller picture of relational dynamics.

Despite these limitations, the present study adds to understanding of suicidal ideation among shelter-seeking women experiencing IPV in the Chinese context. The findings suggest several directions for strengthening shelter services and suicide prevention efforts for this population. From a clinical perspective, the results support more comprehensive screening protocols in shelter settings that include not only individual characteristics, but also family-related burdens, trauma histories, and perpetrator-related dynamics. In addition, the observed associations between health conditions and high-risk suicidal ideation suggest the need for better integration of physical and mental health support within shelters. From a policy perspective, the association between educational attainment and suicidal ideation points to the possible value of education and skill-building opportunities for women in shelter services. Resources may also need to be directed toward healthcare access, caregiving support, education, and resilience-oriented services. Overall, the findings suggest that responses to suicidality in shelter settings may benefit from a holistic approach that addresses individual, relational, and family-level circumstances together. Future practice and research should continue to develop and evaluate integrated interventions tailored to the shelter context.
