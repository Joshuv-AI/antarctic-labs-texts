Credit Fairness: Online Fairness In Shared Resource Pools
Seyed Majid Zahedi, Rupert Freeman

Abstract
. We consider a setting in which a group of agents share resources that must be allocated among them in each discrete time period. Agents have time-varying demands and derive constant marginal utility from each unit of resource received up to their demand, with zero utility for any additional resources. In this setting, it is known that independently maximizing the minimum utility in each round satisfies sharing incentives (agents weakly prefer participating in the mechanism to not participating), strategyproofness (agents have no incentive to misreport their demands), and Pareto efficiency ( 9 ) . However, recent work ( 19 ) has shown that this max–min mechanism can lead to large disparities in the total resources received by agents, even when they have the same average demand. In this paper, we introduce credit fairness , a strengthening of sharing incentives that ensures agents who lend resources in early rounds are able to recoup them in later rounds. Credit fairness can be achieved in conjunction with either Pareto efficiency or strategyproofness, but not both. We propose a mechanism that is credit fair and Pareto efficient, and we evaluate its performance in a computational resource-sharing setting.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider a set of agents who contribute resources to a shared pool of homogeneous resources.
Each agent owns a fixed endowment but faces time-varying demands.
In such settings, pooling resources naturally increases utilization by accommodating agent-level demand spikes
(
14
)
.

Our primary motivation is the sharing of computing resources
(
10
;
17
)
, such as supercomputers for scientific computing, network bandwidth, and datacenters for internet services.
More broadly, our model accommodates a wide range of applications, including shared energy systems (e.g., microgrids), transportation infrastructure (e.g., airport runways), research infrastructure (e.g., telescopes), and organizational resources shared across departments or units within a company.

In all of these settings, several requirements naturally arise as fundamental to system stability and success.

Pareto efficiency (PE)
requires that it be impossible to make some agent better off without making another agent worse off, and is essential for any system whose central goal is maximizing resource utilization.

Sharing incentives (SI)
requires that agents prefer participating in the shared system over relying solely on their own resources in every round.
SI serves as both a fairness property and a stability guarantee: without SI, systems risk collapse as agents opt out.
Finally,
strategyproofness (SP)
requires that agents maximize their utility by truthfully reporting their demands to the system.
Because system performance can only be optimized with respect to reported demands, any discrepancy between reports and true demands can jeopardize efficiency.

While SI is a compelling property,
19
point out that system fairness may fail even for mechanisms that satisfy SI.
Focusing on resource utilization, they observe that the classic
static max-min fairness (SMMF)
mechanism—which lexicographically maximizes the minimum utility in every round—can lead to large disparities in agent utilities, despite satisfying all three desirable properties discussed above.
Intuitively, this occurs because SMMF is memoryless and therefore does not prioritize agents who contributed resources to others in earlier rounds.
As a result, agents may lend resources to the system without ever being repaid.
To address this issue,
19
propose
Karma
, which guarantees each agent a fraction
\alpha
of their contributed resources in each round and allocates the remaining resources by prioritizing agents with low cumulative allocations.

Our goal in this work is to formally capture, via an axiomatic property, the sense in which SMMF is unfair.
To this end, we introduce
credit fairness
.
Intuitively, credit fairness posits the existence of an accounting framework (a “credit system”) that tracks lending and borrowing and ensures that agents who are net donors are not deprioritized in favor of agents who are net borrowers.
Moreover, when combined with PE, credit fairness constitutes a strengthening of SI: it preserves the stability interpretation of SI while enhancing it from a fairness perspective.
This strengthening, however, comes at a cost—we show that credit fairness is no longer jointly compatible with PE and SP, although any two of the three properties can be satisfied simultaneously.

To our knowledge, all existing allocation mechanisms in this setting violate credit fairness, including Karma.
We design a simple mechanism,
LendRecoup
, that is both credit fair and PE.
Additionally, we show that
LendRecoup
is
online strategyproof (OSP)

(
2
)
, a weaker notion of strategyproofness that assumes agents do not account for future rounds when strategizing.
While this is a relatively weak incentive guarantee, it is, in some sense, the strongest one can hope for, as our impossibility result rules out full SP.

Related Work

In recent years, there has been renewed interest in repeated allocation mechanisms in dynamic settings where agents have time-varying demands
(
9
;
12
;
15
;
18
;
19
;
7
;
8
;
6
)
.
The work most closely related to ours is that of
9
, who study the online allocation of multiple resource units from a shared pool among agents who both contribute to and draw from the pool over multiple rounds.
Agents are assumed to have piecewise-linear utilities that are high up to a demand and low beyond it.
They consider two settings: (I) zero utility beyond demands and (II) strictly positive utility beyond demands.
For (I), they analyze two max–min fair mechanisms: dynamic max–min fair (DMMF), which maximizes the minimum cumulative utility up to the current round, and static max–min fair (SMMF), which maximizes the minimum utility in the current round independently of previous rounds.
They show that SMMF satisfies SP, SI, and PE, while DMMF violates SP and SI.
For (II), they show that no online mechanism can satisfy PE along with either SI or SP.

Another closely related work is that of
19
, who study setting (I) of
9
and propose
Karma
, an online mechanism parameterized by
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, which specifies the fraction of each agent’s endowment that is guaranteed when demanded.
The remaining resources, together with donations from agents whose demand falls below their fair share, are allocated to agents with excess demand by prioritizing those with the smallest cumulative allocations.
Agents gain credits when others borrow their donated resources and lose credits when borrowing from the pool.
They derive theoretical guarantees only for
\alpha=0
, in which case
Karma
reduces to DMMF, violating SP and SI.

Repeated allocation has also been studied in settings where indivisible items arrive over time and must be allocated immediately without revocation.

21
and
4
study the trade-off between fairness and efficiency in this setting, while
1
examine online fair division under monotonicity constraints and analyze how fairness properties can be preserved.

2
further show that, for agents with additive utilities, SP, envy-freeness, and PE cannot be simultaneously achieved against worst-case adversaries.

5
provide a polynomial-time deterministic algorithm with vanishing envy over time.

3
study a partial information setting, where ordinal but not cardinal information is elicited and values are drawn from i.i.d. distributions.
Finally,
13
study the repeated allocation of indivisible goods and chores, providing guarantees on both per-round and cumulative fairness and efficiency.

3.
Preliminaries

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we denote
[k]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Consider a system with
n
agents and discrete rounds
t=1,2,\ldots
.
(All definitions apply to any finite prefix of rounds.)
At each round, each agent
i
contributes a fixed
endowment
of
e_{i}>0

E=\sum_{i}e_{i}
. We denote by
E_{-i}=\sum_{j\neq i}e_{j}
the total number of units not including
i

We assume a dynamic setting in which agents have time-varying demands.
At round

has a true demand of
d_{i,t}\geq 0

d^{\prime}_{i,t}\geq 0

Let
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}=(d_{i,t^{\prime}})_{1\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}

denote the vectors of agent

Further, let
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
denote the reported demands of all agents other than
i

t
.
At every round
t
, an
online allocation mechanism

M
(henceforth simply
mechanism
) assigns each agent an allocation
a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{1,1:t},\ldots,\bm{d}^{\prime}_{n,1:t})=a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})
with
\sum_{i}a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\leq E
.
We will typically write simply
a_{i,t}
when the exact mechanism and the demands are clear from context.
Let
\bm{a}^{M}_{i,1:t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})
(often simply
\bm{a}_{i,1:t}
) denote the vector of agent
i
’s allocations up to round
t
.
Agents derive one unit of utility for every unit of resource they receive up to their demand and do not value additional units; that is, the utility of agent
i
with demand
d_{i,t}
at round
t
is
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t},d_{i,t})=\min(d_{i,t},a_{i,t})
.
We additionally assume that utilities are additive over rounds, so that agent
i

t

\bm{a}_{i,1:t}
is
U_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}_{i,1:t})=\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}u_{i,t^{\prime}}(a_{i,t^{\prime}},d_{i,t^{\prime}})
.
For notational simplicity, we write
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})
or
U_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t})
when the dependence on
d_{i,t}
or
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}
is clear.
It will often be convenient to reason about an agent’s utility with respect to their reported, rather than true, demand; for this purpose, we use the shorthand
u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=u_{i,t}(a_{i,t},d^{\prime}_{i,t})
and
U^{\prime}_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t})=U_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t})
.
Although this quantity does not represent an agent’s actual utility, it appears often because our definition of credit fairness will disregard incentive considerations and implicitly assume that reported demands equal true demands, as is standard for fairness properties.
Note that all of our results extend to the case where every agent has a (potentially different) utility multiplier
s_{i}>0
, i.e., when resource units are valued differently across agents.

Following prior work
(
9
)
, we allow the allocations
a_{i,t}
to be real-valued even though resource units and demands are discrete. We interpret real-valued allocations as being implemented via randomization where agent
i
is allocated
a_{i,t}
units in expectation.

Properties of Mechanisms.

In addition to the new property that we will define later, we focus on three fundamental desiderata: Pareto efficiency (PE), sharing incentives (SI), and strategyproofness (SP).
PE requires that if any agent receives fewer units than their demand, then all units must be allocated to agents who derive positive utility from them, i.e., the sum of agents’ utilities equals
E
.

Definition 0.

A mechanism
M
is
Pareto efficient
if, for every round
t
and every report profile
(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{1,1:t},\ldots,\bm{d}^{\prime}_{n,1:t})
, the following holds: whenever there exists an agent
i
such that
a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})<d^{\prime}_{i,t}
, then it must be the case that:

\sum_{j}\min\!\left(a^{M}_{j,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{j,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-j,1:t}),\;d^{\prime}_{j,t}\right)=E.

SI requires that agents receive at least as much utility from (truthfully) participating in the system as they would have received by not participating.

Definition 0.

A mechanism
M
satisfies
sharing incentives
if, for every round
t
, every agent
i
, all true demands
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}
, and any report profile
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
of the other agents, it holds that:

U_{i,t}\!\left(\bm{a}^{M}_{i,1:t}(\bm{d}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\right)\;\geq\;U_{i,t}(\bm{e}_{i}),

where
\bm{e}_{i}=(e_{i},\ldots,e_{i})
denotes the allocation in which agent
i
receives their endowment in every round.

SP requires that agents never benefit from misreporting.

Definition 0.

A mechanism
M
is
strategy-proof
if, for every agent
i
, every round
t
, all true demands
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}
, all report profiles
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t}
of agent
i
, and any report profile
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
of the other agents, it holds that:

U_{i,t}\!\left(\bm{a}^{M}_{i,1:t}(\bm{d}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\right)\;\geq\;U_{i,t}\!\left(\bm{a}^{M}_{i,1:t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\right).

Finally,
online strategyproofness (OSP)

(
2
)
requires that, fixing reports of all agents in rounds
1,\ldots,t-1
, no agent can increase their utility in the current round by misreporting.

Definition 0.

A mechanism
M
is
online strategy-proof
if for every agent
i
, every round
t
, all demands
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}
, all report profiles
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t}
of agent
i
such that
\bm{d}_{i,1:t-1}=\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t-1}
, and any report profile
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
of the other agents, it holds that:

u_{i,t}\left(a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\right)\;\geq\;u_{i,t}\left(a^{M}_{i,t}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t},\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t})\right).

4.
Credit Fairness

It is known that static max-min fairness (SMMF) satisfies Pareto efficiency, sharing incentives, and strategyproofness
(
9
)
. However, as
19
note, SMMF can produce highly inequtable allocations in terms of the number of resources received—as large as a factor of
\Omega(n)
—even between users with the same average demand.
Let us more closely examine the shortcomings of SMMF with the following example, heavily inspired by
19
’s proof of the aforementioned result. Suppose that there are 3 rounds and 3 agents, each with endowment
e_{i}=1
, and the following demands:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&2&2&2\\
\text{Agent 2}&2&2&2\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&0&6.\\
\end{array}

Note that the total demand of all three agents is the same. Under SMMF, the agents receive the following allocations:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&1.5&1.5&1\\
\text{Agent 2}&1.5&1.5&1\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&0&1.\\
\end{array}

Agents 1 and 2 receive utility
4
while agent 3 receives utility 1.
Agent 1 could argue that, having lent their endowment to other agents in the first two rounds, they should have the right to reclaim those resources in round 3 when they need them.
Since it is memoryless, SMMF denies this possibility.
In this section, we formally encode the undesirable behavior of SMMF on this example by defining
credit fairness
, an axiomatic property to capture the idea that agents should have priority over their previously lent resources.

4.1.
Formal Definition

To define credit fairness, we require the notion of a
credit system
. A credit system
(c_{i,t})_{i\in[n],t\in\mathbb{N}}
is simply a balance of credits for every agent. We denote by
c_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}
the number of credits that agent
i
has at the start of round
t
, and require that
c_{i,1}=0
for every agent
i
. Let
\Delta c_{i,t}\triangleq c_{i,t+1}-c_{i,t}
. Furthermore, for every agent
i
and round
t
, let
A_{-i,t}=A_{-i,t}^{M}(\bm{d}^{\prime}_{1,1:t},\ldots,\bm{d}^{\prime}_{n,1:t})\triangleq\sum_{j\neq i}a_{i,t}
denote the total round
t
allocation of agents other than
i
under mechanism
M
.

Definition 0.

A mechanism
M
is
credit fair
if there exists a credit system
(c_{i,t})_{i\in[n],t\in\mathbb{N}}
for which the following properties hold for all agents
i
and rounds
t
:

(CF1)

\min(0,e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t}))\leq\Delta c_{i,t}\leq\max(0,e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t}))
;

(CF2)

If
\sum_{j\neq i}u^{\prime}_{j,t}(a_{j,t})>E_{-i}
, then
\Delta c_{i,t}\geq\sum_{j\neq i}u^{\prime}_{j,t}(a_{j,t})-E_{-i}
;

(CF3)

\sum_{i\in[n]}\Delta c_{i,t}\leq 0
;

(CF4)

a_{i,t}\geq\min(d^{\prime}_{i,t},e_{i}+\min(0,c_{i,t}))
; and

(CF5)

If
a_{j,t}>\max(0,e_{j}+c_{j,t})
for some agent
j
, then for all agents
i
it holds that either
a_{i,t}\geq d^{\prime}_{i,t}
or
a_{i,t}\geq e_{i}+c_{i,t}
.

Let us unpack this definition. Condition (CF1) states that agents who receive less than
e_{i}
utility (informally, these agents are donating recources to the system) should not lose credits but should gain no more than
e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})
. Likewise, agents who receive more than
e_{i}
utility (informally, borrowers) should not gain credits but should lose no more than
u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})-e_{i}
. Agents who receive utility exactly equal to their endowment should neither gain nor lose credits. While this condition does not require that credits correspond one-to-one to resources, it imposes bounds on how far divorced credits can be from allocations. At a high level, this condition justifies us thinking of agents with positive credits as being owed resources while agents with negative credits owe resources to the system.

Condition (CF2) requires that if an agent
i
lends resources to other agents, i.e., if the agents other than
i
collectively receive utilty greater than the number of resources that they contribute to the system, then agent
i
should gain at least one unit of credit for each such resource lent. This condition captures the idea that agents should be rewarded when they are pivotal to other agents receiving (valued) resources.

Condition (CF3) requires that the credit system is deflationary in the sense that the total number of credits does not increase over time. Intuitively, credits track claims on resources, and allowing the total amount of credit to increase would imply that agents can collectively obtain resources without anyone bearing a corresponding cost.

To understand condition (CF4), consider an agent with
d_{i,t}>e_{i}
. If this agent has positive credits, then they should receive at least their endowment
e_{i}
. On the other hand, if the agent has negative credits, we only guarantee them
e_{i}+c_{i,t}
units of resources. The intuition is that the system is justified in reclaiming from the agent up to a number of resources equal to the amount of credit they owe.

Finally, condition (CF5) builds on the notion that each agent has a
credit-adjusted endowment
, defined as
e_{i}+c_{i,t}
, which reflects both the agent’s baseline endowment and whether they are a net borrower or donor of resources. The condition requires that if some agent
j
receives strictly more than their credit-adjusted endowment in a given round, then every other agent with sufficient demand must receive at least their own credit-adjusted endowment. Interpreting the credit-adjusted endowment as an agent’s “fair share” in a round, this condition ensures that no agent receives more than their fair share while another receives strictly less.

4.2.
Properties

We begin with a useful lemma that characterizes credit updates for any credit-fair and Pareto-efficient mechanism in settings where resources are overdemanded.

Lemma 0
.

Fix a round
t
with
\sum_{j}d^{\prime}_{j,t}>E
. Let
M
be a mechanism that
is
credit fair
and
Pareto efficient
. Then, for every agent
i\in[n]
,
it holds that
\Delta c_{i,t}\;=\;e_{i}-a_{i,t}
.

We now show that, when combined with Pareto efficiency, credit fairness implies sharing incentives. The proof is omitted for space constraints, but the main idea is to show that if an agent receives less than their SI baseline utility
u^{\prime}_{i,t}(e_{i})
in some round, then that agent must have nonpositive credits, even after the round-
t
allocations, i.e.,
c_{i,t+1}\leq 0
. This fact is sufficient to guarantee that the agent’s cumulative utility still does not fall below their SI baseline.

Theorem 3
.

Let
M
be a credit-fair and Pareto efficient mechanism.
Then
M
satisfies sharing incentives.

As a corollary of Theorem
3
, any Pareto efficient mechanism that violates sharing incentives cannot be credit fair.
Since DMMF is Pareto efficient but violates sharing incentives, it follows that DMMF is not credit fair.

Corollary 0
.

The dynamic max-min fair mechanism violates credit fairness.

Furthermore, we show that SMMF, despite satisfying sharing incentives and Pareto efficiency, violates credit fairness, which further validates the necessity of our definition.

Proposition 0
.

The static max-min fair mechanism violates credit fairness.

To our knowledge, SMMF is the only known mechanism that satisfies SP, PE, and SI, yet it violates credit fairness. This naturally raises the question of whether there exists a mechanism that satisfies the stronger combination of SP, PE, and credit fairness. Our next result shows that the answer is no, at least under the mild restriction of anonymity. A mechanism is
anonymous
if permuting the agents results in the allocation being permuted in the same way; in other words, the mechanism does not depend on the identities of the agents.

Theorem 6
.

No mechanism is anonymous, strategyproof, credit fair, and Pareto efficient.

Proof.

Consider
n=3
agents, each with endowment of
e_{i}=1
.
We examine the first
5
rounds with the following demands:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}&\text{$t=4$}&\text{$t=5$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&1&2&0&0&3\\
\text{Agent 2}&3&0&1&1&2\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&2&2&2&0.\\
\end{array}

Let
M
be a credit fair, PE, and anonymous mechanism (and therefore also satisfies SI). Then
M
must produce the following allocations:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}&\text{$t=4$}&\text{$t=5$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&1&1&0&0&2\\
\text{Agent 2}&2&0&1&1&1\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&2&2&2&0,\\
\end{array}

and the following credit balances:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}&\text{$t=4$}&\text{$t=5$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&0&0&0&1&2\\
\text{Agent 2}&0&-1&0&0&0\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&1&0&-1&-2.\\
\end{array}

Note that, given the demands and allocations, these credit balances are implied by Lemma
2
.

The first-round allocations are implied by SI and PE.
In the second round, SI requires that both agents 1 and 3 receive at least one unit, and (CF5) then requires that the additional unit be allocated to agent 3.
The third- and fourth-round allocations are dictated by PE.
Finally, the fifth-round allocation is implied by (CF5), which does not allow agent 2 to receive more than one unit unless agent 1 receives at least three.

Now suppose agent 1 instead reports a demand of 0 in the first round.
In this case, the allocations must be:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}&\text{$t=4$}&\text{$t=5$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&0&1.5&0&0&3\\
\text{Agent 2}&3&0&1&1&0\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&1.5&2&2&0,\\
\end{array}

with the following credits:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&\text{$t=1$}&\text{$t=2$}&\text{$t=3$}&\text{$t=4$}&\text{$t=5$}\\
\hline\cr\text{Agent 1}&0&1&0.5&1.5&2.5\\
\text{Agent 2}&0&-2&-1&-1&-1\\
\text{Agent 3}&0&1&0.5&-0.5&-1.5.\\
\end{array}

To verify the allocations, observe that the allocations in the first, third, and fourth rounds are required by PE, the second-round allocation follows from anonymity, and the fifth-round allocation is implied by (CF5).
As a result of the misreport, agent 1’s total utility increases by 0.5, from 4 to 4.5, demonstrating a profitable deviation.
Therefore,
M
is not SP.
∎

While SP, PE, and credit fairness cannot all be satisfied simultaneously, any two of them can. SMMF is SP and PE but not credit fair. The static mechanism that allocates each agent their endowment
a_{i,t}=e_{i}
at each round is SP and credit fair (SP is obvious; see Appendix
A
for a formal statement and proof of credit fairness). In the next section we will introduce a mechanism that is credit fair and PE.

5.
LendRecoup
Mechanism

In this section, we present an online allocation mechanism,
LendRecoup

, that is credit fair and Pareto efficient.
At each round,
LendRecoup
allocates resources by first satisfying agents’ demands as much as possible up to their credit-adjusted endowment
e_{i}+c_{i,t}
.
If resources remain after every agent receives
\min(d^{\prime}_{i,t},e_{i}+c_{i,t})
, the surplus is distributed among agents with remaining demand, prioritizing agents with a low cumulative allocations.

Results

As can be seen in Table

attains near-frontier performance despite its stronger individual-level protections.

For SIx,
LendRecoup
and SMMF stand out by fully avoiding harm from sharing in these runs: both report a minimum value of
1.00
and
0\%
of agents with

These patterns matter because

LendRecoup
provides a stronger practical value proposition than mechanisms that achieve similar welfare but do so by shifting costs onto a substantial subset of agents.

For WMM and WEq, DMMF performs best, with Karma essentially tied, while
LendRecoup
achieves roughly 60% of DMMF’s performance; SMMF trails by a significant margin.
In contrast, Karma and DMMF perform relatively worse under the normalized versions NMM and NEq, with SMMF performing best for NMM, followed by

LendRecoup

It is interesting to note the significant disparity between mechanism performances as measured by the weighted and normalized versions of these metrics; exploring the underlying reasons for this phenomenon would be a valuable question for future work. Of the four mechanisms we consider,
LendRecoup
is the most robust across metrics, again suggesting that it yields an even distribution of utilities across agents.

7.
Conclusion

We introduce the notion of credit fairness in resource-sharing systems, motivated by the idea that agents who lend resources to the shared system in early rounds should receive priority in later rounds.
When paired with Pareto efficiency, credit fairness strengthens sharing incentives, establishing it as both a fairness and a stability notion for shared systems.
Although this strengthening comes at a cost—credit fairness cannot be simultaneously achieved with both Pareto efficiency and strategyproofness—we introduce
LendRecoup
, a novel mechanism that is credit fair, Pareto efficient, and online strategyproof.
Using experiments on real data from a computational resource-sharing setting, we demonstrate that
LendRecoup
achieves performance comparable to existing state-of-the-art baseline mechanisms.
Combined with its formal credit-fairness guarantee, these empirical results position
LendRecoup
as a compelling candidate for practical deployment.

Our work raises several open questions.
We focus on a setting with a single resource type, capped linear utility functions, and a fixed set of agents and resource units.
Extending the notion of credit fairness to more general online settings is an interesting direction for future work.
Even within the setting studied here, the design space of mechanisms and desiderata remains far from fully explored.
For instance, are there mechanisms that achieve stronger theoretical guarantees and/or improved empirical performance compared to the four mechanisms considered in this paper?

d_{i,t}>e_{i}
, then
u_{i,t}(e_{i})=e_{i}
, and
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=d_{i,t}>e_{i}
.
By (CF1), we have:
e_{i}-u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})\leq\Delta c_{i,t}\leq 0
, and therefore:

u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})+\Delta c_{i,t}\;\geq\;e_{i}=u_{i,t}(e_{i}).

Thus, the lemma holds in Case A.

Case B:

\sum_{j}d_{j,t}\geq E
(i.e., shortage).
By Pareto efficiency, the full supply is allocated, and no agent receives more resources than their demand.
Therefore,
\sum_{j}a_{j,t}=E
, and for all
j
,
u_{j,t}(a_{j,t})=a_{j,t}
(equivalently,
d_{j,t}\geq a_{j,t}
).
Fix any agent
i
. Substituting
\Delta_{i,t}=e_{i}-a_{i,t}
from Lemma
2
gives the following:

u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})+\Delta c_{i,t}=a_{i,t}+\Delta c_{i,t}=e_{i}\geq\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})=u_{i,t}(e_{i}).

Thus, the lemma holds in Case B as well.
∎

We next identify the rounds in which an agent can fall below their per-round baseline, and how this interacts with their credit balance.

Lemma 0
.

Let
M
be a credit-fair and Pareto efficient mechanism with associated credit system
(c_{i,t})_{i\in[n],t\in\mathbb{N}}
. Fix an agent
i
and a round
t
such that
u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})<u^{\prime}_{i,t}(e_{i})
.
Then, it must be that (1)
c_{i,t}<0
, and (2)
c_{i,t+1}\leq 0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
1
, we again suppress the distinction between true demands and reports (and therefore between
u^{\prime}_{i,t}
and
u_{i,t}
).
Suppose
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})<u_{i,t}(e_{i})
.
First, if
\sum_{j}d_{j,t}<E
, then, as in the proof of Lemma
1
, Pareto efficiency implies that
a_{j,t}\geq d_{j,t}
for all
j
.
Hence,
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=d_{i,t}\geq\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})=u_{i,t}(e_{i})
, which contradicts the assumption.
Thus, it must be that
\sum_{j}d_{j,t}\geq E
; that is, round
t
is a shortage round.

We first show that
c_{i,t}<0
.
In a shortage round,
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=a_{i,t}
and
u_{i,t}(e_{i})=\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})
. The inequality
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})<u_{i,t}(e_{i})
translates
to
a_{i,t}<\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})
. If
c_{i,t}\geq 0
, then (CF4) implies that:

a_{i,t}\;\geq\;\min(d_{i,t},\,e_{i}+\min(0,c_{i,t}))=\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})=d_{i,t},

a contradiction.

We next show that
c_{i,t+1}\leq 0
.
Consider a shortage round with
a_{i,t}<\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})
and
c_{i,t}<0
. This implies that
a_{i,t}<e_{i}
and
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=a_{i,t}
.
By (CF4), it must hold that:

a_{i,t}\;\geq\;\min(d_{i,t},\,e_{i}+\min(0,c_{i,t}))=\min(d_{i,t},\,e_{i}+c_{i,t}).

As in Case B of Lemma
1
, shortage together with

a_{i,t}<e_{i}
implies, via (CF1) and (CF2), that
\Delta c_{i,t}=e_{i}-a_{i,t}
.
Moreover, from
a_{i,t}\geq\min(d_{i,t},e_{i}+c_{i,t})
and the fact that
a_{i,t}<\min(d_{i,t},e_{i})
, it follows that
a_{i,t}\geq e_{i}+c_{i,t}
. Hence, we have:

\Delta c_{i,t}=e_{i}-a_{i,t}\;\leq\;-c_{i,t}.

Therefore,
c_{i,t+1}=c_{i,t}+\Delta c_{i,t}\leq 0
.
∎

We can now show that sharing incentives holds.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Fix an agent
i
, a demand sequence
\bm{d}_{i,1:t}
, a report profile
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
of the other agents, and a round

.
We need to show that
U_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t})\geq U_{i,t}(\bm{e}_{i})
for all

.
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a round

such that
U_{i,t}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t})<U_{i,t}(\bm{e}_{i})
.
Let

be the smallest such round.
Then
U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t^{\star}})-U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{e}_{i})<0
and
U_{i,t^{\star}-1}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t^{\star}-1})-U_{i,t^{\star}-1}(\bm{e}_{i})\geq 0
.
In particular,
u_{i,t^{\star}}(a_{i,t^{\star}})-u_{i,t^{\star}}(e_{i})<0
.
Therefore, the condition of Lemma

is satisfied for round
t^{\star}
and agent

.
By that lemma we have
c_{i,t^{\star}+1}\leq 0
.

On the other hand, Lemma

applied at round

gives

U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t^{\star}})+c_{i,t^{\star}+1}\;\geq\;U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{e}_{i}).

Equivalently,

U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{a}_{i,1:t^{\star}})-U_{i,t^{\star}}(\bm{e}_{i})\;\geq\;-\,c_{i,t^{\star}+1}\;\geq\;0,

where the last inequality follows from

This contradicts the assumption that

A.4.
Static Mechanism is Credit Fair

We provide a proof that the static mechanism that always allocates every agent their endowment
a_{i,t}=e_{i}
is credit fair.

Proposition 0
.

Let
M^{\text{static}}
be the mechanism that, in every round
t
and for every
report profile, allocates each agent exactly their endowment:

a_{i,t}\;=\;e_{i}\qquad\text{for all }i\in[n],\ t\geq 1.

Then
M^{\text{static}}
is credit fair.

Proof.

We exhibit a credit system
(c_{i,t})_{i\in[n],t\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying conditions
(CF1)–(CF5). Define

c_{i,t}\coloneqq 0\qquad\text{for all }i\in[n],\ t\geq 1,

so that
\Delta c_{i,t}=c_{i,t+1}-c_{i,t}=0
for all
i,t
.

We verify each condition.

(CF1).

Since
a_{i,t}=e_{i}
, we have
u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=u^{\prime}_{i,t}(e_{i})
, which implies:

e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})=e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(e_{i})\geq 0.

(CF1) requires the following:

\min\!\bigl(0,\,e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})\bigr)\;\leq\;\Delta c_{i,t}\;\leq\;\max\!\bigl(0,\,e_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i,t}(a_{i,t})\bigr).

The left-hand side equals
0
, and the right-hand side is nonnegative.
Therefore,
\Delta c_{i,t}=0
satisfies (CF1).

(CF2).

Fix an agent
i
and a round
t
. The premise of (CF2) is

\sum_{j\neq i}u^{\prime}_{j,t}(a_{j,t})\;>\;E_{-i}.

However, we have
a_{j,t}=e_{j}
, which implies
u^{\prime}_{j,t}(a_{j,t})=u^{\prime}_{j,t}(e_{j})\leq e_{j}
.
Therefore, we have:

\sum_{j\neq i}u^{\prime}_{j,t}(a_{j,t})\;\leq\;\sum_{j\neq i}e_{j}\;=\;E_{-i}.

Hence, the premise can never hold, so (CF2) is satisfied vacuously.

(CF3).

We have
\sum_{i}\Delta c_{i,t}=\sum_{i}0=0\leq 0
, so (CF3) holds.

(CF4).

Since
c_{i,t}=0
, we have
\min(0,c_{i,t})=0
, and thus:

\min\!\bigl(d^{\prime}_{i,t},\,e_{i}+\min(0,c_{i,t})\bigr)=\min(d^{\prime}_{i,t},e_{i})\leq e_{i}.

Because
a_{i,t}=e_{i}
, we obtain:

a_{i,t}=e_{i}\;\geq\;\min(d^{\prime}_{i,t},e_{i})=\min\!\bigl(d^{\prime}_{i,t},\,e_{i}+\min(0,c_{i,t})\bigr),

so (CF4) holds.

(CF5).

Since
c_{j,t}=0
, the threshold in (CF5) is
\max(0,e_{j}+c_{j,t})=\max(0,e_{j})=e_{j}
.
But
a_{j,t}=e_{j}
, so the antecedent
a_{j,t}>\max(0,e_{j}+c_{j,t})
is never satisfied. Thus (CF5) holds vacuously.

All five conditions are satisfied, so
M^{\text{static}}
is credit fair.
∎

The following lemma regarding the behavior of the

Lemma 0
.

Fix an instance: weights
\bm{w}
, resource capacity

, and constraints
(\bm{m},\bm{\ell})
, and denote the output of

for the instance by
\bm{a}
. Suppose that there exists an agent

m_{i}<a_{i}<\ell_{i}

(\bm{m}^{\prime},\bm{\ell}^{\prime})

\ell^{\prime}_{j}=\ell_{j}

m^{\prime}_{i}<a_{i}<\ell^{\prime}_{i}

(A,\bm{w},\bm{m}^{\prime},\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
by
\bm{a}^{\prime}

a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{i}
.
In particular, increasing
\ell_{i}
or decreasing

cannot increase

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that the theroem does not hold: there exist
m^{\prime}_{i}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{i}
for which
a^{\prime}_{i}\neq a_{i}
.
From the fact that
a_{i}
is interior (i.e.,
m_{i}<a_{i}<\ell_{i}
), it follows that
a_{i}=xw_{i}
for some

that defines the outcome of
PSWC
on this instance.
Suppose without loss of generality that
m_{i}<a_{i}<a^{\prime}_{i}\leq\ell^{\prime}_{i}
.
Then, it is either the case that
a^{\prime}_{i}=x^{\prime}w_{i}
(if
x^{\prime}w_{i}<\ell^{\prime}_{i}
) or that
a^{\prime}_{i}=\ell^{\prime}_{i}
(if
x^{\prime}w_{i}\geq\ell^{\prime}_{i}
), where
x^{\prime}
denotes the scalar defining the output of

on
(A,\bm{w},\bm{m}^{\prime},\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
.
In the former case, it holds that
x^{\prime}w_{i}=a^{\prime}_{i}>a_{i}=xw_{i}
, while in the latter case it holds that
x^{\prime}w_{i}\geq\ell^{\prime}_{i}>a_{i}=xw_{i}
.
In both cases,

, which implies that all agents’ allocations have weakly increased, while agent

’s allocation has strictly increased (from

to

We can now prove Theorem

.

3
.

t\geq 1
, an agent
i
with reports
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{i,1:t-1}=\bm{d}_{i,1:t-1}
in rounds
1,\ldots,t-1
. Let
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,1:t}
denote the report profile of all the other agents and write
\bm{d}^{\prime}_{-i,t}
for their reports in round
t
. Finally, let
d\triangleq d_{i,t}
denote agent
i
’s true demand in round
t
.

The history up to round
t-1
fixes the credit vector
\bm{c}_{t}
and the past allocations
(\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{t-1})
, hence fixes the quantities:

A^{\mathrm{past}}_{j}\;:=\;\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}a_{j,t^{\prime}}

for all
j\in[n]
.
Therefore, the only effect of any misreport by agent
i
in round
t
is through their report
r\triangleq d^{\prime}_{i,t}
, which enters the round-
t
branch conditions and the
PSWC
constraints.

Write
S\triangleq\sum_{j\neq i}d^{\prime}_{j,t}
, so the total reported demand at round
t
is
D(r)\triangleq r+S
.
We compare agent
i
’s utility in round
t
under truthful reporting
r=d
with that under an
arbitrary misreport
r\geq 0
.
Throughout this proof, we treat several quantities as functions of the report in order to clarify the effect of agent
i
’s report; for example, we write
a_{i,t}(d)
and
a_{i,t}(r)
for agent
i
’s allocation in round
t
under reports
d
and
r
, respectively, holding all other reports fixed.

Case 1:
D(d)\leq E
(truth is in the no-shortage branch).

When
D(d)\leq E
,
LendRecoup
calls
PSWC
with minima
\bm{m}=\bm{d}^{\prime}_{t}
and no upper limits.
In particular, under truthful reporting we have:

a_{i,t}(d)\;\geq\;d,

so
u_{i,t}(a_{i,t}(d))=\min(d,a_{i,t}(d))=d
, which is the maximum possible utility in round
t
.
Therefore, no deviation
r
can strictly improve utility.

Case 2:
D(d)>E
(truth is in a shortage branch).

Define the
credit-adjusted cap
(fixed under the history):

\kappa_{i}\triangleq\max(0,e_{i}+c_{i,t}),

and the corresponding capped report
\bar{d}_{i}(r)\triangleq\min(r,\kappa_{i})
.

We split by whether the deviation changes the outer branch, i.e., from shortage to no shortage.

Case 2a: Deviation chooses
r
with
D(r)\leq E
(switching to no-shortage).

Define

as follows:

r^{\prime}\;:=\;r+(E-D(r))\;=\;E-\sum_{j\neq i}d_{j,t},

so that
D(r^{\prime})=E
.
We first show that the deviation
r^{\prime}
weakly dominates
r
in round
t
, and hence it suffices to analyze the boundary deviation with
D(r^{\prime})=E
.

In the no-shortage branch,
LendRecoup
runs
PSWC
with total resource
A=E
, weights
\bm{w}=\bm{e}
, minimum constraints
\bm{m}=\bm{d}^{\prime}_{q}
, and no upper limits.
Thus, every agent
j
receives at least
m_{j}=d^{\prime}_{j,t}
, and the total resource left after satisfying all minima is
E-D(r)
.
Consequently, agent
i
’s allocation in round
t
satisfies the following:

(2)

a_{i,t}(r)\;\leq\;r+(E-D(r))\;=\;r^{\prime}.

Now consider the deviation
r^{\prime}
. Since
\sum_{j}m_{j}=D(r^{\prime})=E
, feasibility
forces
PSWC
to return
a_{j,t}(r^{\prime})=m_{j}=d^{\prime}_{j,t}
for every agent
j
, and in particular
a_{i,t}(r^{\prime})=r^{\prime}
.
Combining with (
2
) and using that

is nondecreasing in the allocation, we obtain:

u_{i,t}\!\left(a_{i,t}(r)\right)\;\leq\;u_{i,t}\!\left(a_{i,t}(r^{\prime})\right).

Therefore, among deviations that switch to the no-shortage branch, it suffices to consider
r^{\prime}
with

It remains to compare truthful reporting with the deviation

.
Since

As shown in Case 2b below, no deviation that remains in the shortage branch, and in particular, no deviation with

than truthful reporting.
(Note that we can treat reports with
