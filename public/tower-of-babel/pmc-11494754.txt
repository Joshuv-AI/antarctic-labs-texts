Prognostic value of platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio in patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Li Huang, Wenke Wu, Ge Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesNumerous studies have investigated the predictive significance of the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) in oral squamous cell carcinoma(OSCC). However, the results of studies on its prognostic value remain controversial. This study aims to evaluate the prognostic significance of the PLR in patients with OSCC.Materials and methodsWe conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for all studies investigating the association between PLR and prognosis in OSCC, from the inception of each database up to November 2023. The outcome measures included overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). To identify potential sources of heterogeneity, sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed, alongside an assessment of publication bias. The analyses in this study were conducted using RevMan and Stata software.ResultsAccording to the inclusion criteria, 20 articles were included, including 5,714 patients.The meta-analysis revealed that an elevated PLR adversely affects OS (HR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.29–1.94; p < 0.0001), DFS (HR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.20–2.42; p < 0.003), and DSS (HR = 2.41; 95% CI: 1.79–3.25; p < 0.00001). The results of the subgroup analysis showed that a preoperative high PLR was associated with reduced OS when the PLR threshold was ≤ 150 (HR = 1.49, P = 0.0003).When the PLR threshold was > 150, Preoperative PLR was not significantly associated with the prediction of overall survival (OS) in OSCC. (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe prognostic significance of PLR in patients with oral cancer is evident in terms of OS, DSS, and DFS. Nonetheless, it is crucial to note that the predictive value of PLR might depend on specific threshold values.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05026-7.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is recognized as a leading malignancy within the head and neck region. Data from 2020 show that the global incidence of OSCC exceeded 370,000 new cases, leading to over 170,000 death [1]. The likelihood of oral cancer development is significantly increased in individuals with prolonged histories of smoking and alcohol consumption. This group also faces a higher risk of cancer recurrence and a less favorable prognosis [2]. The current standard treatment for OSCC includes radical surgery, often supplemented by adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Despite advancements in diagnostic and treatment approaches, the prognosis for those affected by OSCC remains poor [3, 4].

Inflammation is identified as a critical characteristic of cancer, with literature indicating that inflammatory markers may serve as prognostic indicators for cancer progression [5]. Recent studies on hematological prognostic assessment in OSCC patients reflect ongoing advancements in understanding blood-related factors influencing disease prognosis [6]. A study involving 600 patients, with an average follow-up of 33.1 months, observed that a PLR greater than 167.3 is an independent prognostic factor for overall survival (HR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.029–1.824; p = 0.031). In another study, which included 825 OSCC patients with a follow-up period of one year or more [7], demonstrated that PLR is not only associated with tumor staging, lymph node infiltration, and perineural invasion but also serves as a significant predictive factor for prognosis (p < 0.001). As an inflammatory biomarker, Growing evidence suggests that the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) serves as a prognostic indicator for various malignancies [8, 9]. In OSCC, an elevated PLR may reflect tumor-associated inflammation and immunosuppression, indicating a higher risk of recurrence and poorer survival outcomes for patients [10]. As a readily accessible and non-invasive biomarker, the prognostic value of PLR in OS, DFS, and DSS in cancer patients is increasingly being recognized [11].

Numerous studies have reported the impact of preoperative variations in PLR on prognostic of OSCC. However, there has been no recent meta-analysis that thoroughly compiles and evaluates the latest findings on this topic. Our objective is to integrate these contemporary data to generate a higher level of evidence-based medical insights. In this study, we aim to assess the prognostic significance of elevated PLR on survival outcomes in OSCC patients.

Materials and methods

Literature search

This meta-analysis was performed according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) 2020 statement [12] and has been prospectively registered in the PROSPERO (CRD42023496409). A systematic review of the literature was conducted in the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases to identify studies on the relationship between PLR and OSCC prognosis, from the inception of these databases until November 2023. Search terms included “Blood Platelets,” “Lymphocytes,” and “Squamous Cell Carcinoma of Head and Neck.” Abstracts that discussed the influence of PLR on the prognosis of OSCC and included follow-up data were meticulously reviewed. The literature search and selection of relevant studies were independently carried out by researchers. Any discrepancies will be addressed through discussion and negotiation with a third party.

Identification of eligible studies

The inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: (a) study participants must be pathologically diagnosed with OSCC. (b) the study design must be a randomized controlled trial, case-control study, or cohort study. (c) PLR must be measured preoperatively. (d) the literature must report data on the association between PLR and OSCC survival outcomes, and provide sufficient data to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI).The exclusion criteria are as follows: (a) duplicate articles. (b) studies that are unrelated to the topic, reviews, non-clinical research, non-English articles, or retracted publications. (c) studies that do not provide estimated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) data. (d) insufficient data extraction. For articles with discrepancies regarding inclusion, resolution will be coordinated through communication with senior researchers.

Data extraction

The researchers, Huang and Wu, independently extracted and compiled data, methodically documenting relevant details from each selected study. This included information such as the name of the primary author, the duration of the study, the geographical location, the design of the study, the size of the sample, ages of participants, Body Mass Index (BMI), dimensions of tumors, clinical staging, and the defined PLR threshold.In cases where both univariate and multivariate data are available, priority will be given to the latter.

Quality assessment

The evidential quality of the aggregated studies, which numbered 20, was independently assessed by Huang and Wu using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Studies scoring between 7 and 9 points were unequivocally considered to be of high quality, representing a total of 15 studies. The quality and level of evidence for the study will be independently assessed by two researchers, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consultation with a third party.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis utilized Revman 5.4 and Stata14 for statistical computations. The analysis included the count of subjects with high or low PLR expression, alongside survival-related HR and their 95% CI. The outcome measures analyzed were OS, DFS, and DSS. A fixed-effects model was employed in cases where heterogeneity was minimal (I2 < 50%). In contrast, a random-effects model was adopted in the presence of significant heterogeneity (I2 > 50%). Sensitivity analysis was carried out for studies numbering more than three in the context of marked heterogeneity (I2 > 50%), to evaluate the reliability of the findings. Additionally, for outcome indicators with more than three included studies, Egger’s test and funnel plot analyses will be performed to assess publication bias. A P-value < 0.05 from Egger’s test will be interpreted as evidence of significant publication bias.

PLR and OS

The prognostic significance of PLR for OS was analyzed across 19 studies using a random-effects model. This analysis revealed a significant link, indicating that patients with elevated PLR had significantly shorter OS compared to those with lower PLR levels (HR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.29–1.94; p < 0.0001), as shown in Fig. 2A. However, the analysis displayed a high degree of heterogeneity (I2 = 86%; p < 0.0001). Results from the sensitivity analysis confirmed the findings’ stability and reliability, as depicted in Fig. 3A. Although the funnel plot for OS did not show marked asymmetry (Fig. 4A), the Egger’s test suggested a potential for publication bias in this prognostic factor (P = 0.04).

Subgroup analyses for OS stratified by sample size, PLR threshold, and geographical region were conducted. These analyses demonstrated the continuous prognostic relevance of PLR for OS across various sample sizes (> 200 and ≤ 200) and regions (Asia, Europe, South America). Nonetheless, the subgroup analysis based on the PLR threshold indicated that for thresholds > 150 (HR = 1.66; 95% CI: 0.97–2.86; p = 0.07) (Table 2), PLR’s predictive capacity for OS did not achieve statistical significance. Conversely, for thresholds ≤ 150 (HR = 1.49; 95% CI: 1.20–1.85; p = 0.0003), its predictive value remained statistically significant. Therefore, the predictive effectiveness of PLR for OS appeared to diminish in studies adopting a threshold > 150, while it was maintained in studies with a threshold ≤ 150.

PLR and DFS

The prognostic evaluation of PLR for DFS was extracted from a total of 8 studies. This pooled analysis highlighted a significant relationship, with patients having elevated PLR experiencing significantly shorter DFS compared to those with lower PLR levels (HR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.20–2.42; p < 0.003) (Fig. 2B). A notable level of heterogeneity was observed in the analysis (I2 = 82%; p < 0.003). The outcomes of the sensitivity analysis demonstrated the stability and reliability of these findings (Fig. 3B). While the funnel plot for DFS did not show significant asymmetry (Fig. 4B), the Egger’s test indicated a potential for publication bias in this prognostic marker (P = 0.002).

Subgroup analyses for DFS were conducted, stratified by sample size, PLR threshold, and geographical region. These analyses confirmed the consistent prognostic relevance of PLR for DFS across varying sample sizes (> 200 and ≤ 200), PLR thresholds (> 150 and ≤ 150), and regions (Asia, Europe, South America). However, the analysis identified that a sample size greater than 200 (I2 = 0%; p = 0.02) (Table 2) acted as a potential source of heterogeneity for the predictive value of PLR for DFS.

PLR and DSS

The examination of the predictive value of PLR for DSS was based on a pooled analysis of 4 studies, employing a fixed-effects model. The results demonstrated a significant association, indicating that patients with elevated PLR had significantly reduced DSS compared to those with lower PLR levels (HR = 2.41; 95% CI: 1.79–3.25; p < 0.00001) (Fig. 2C). Notably, the analysis showed minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 26%; p < 0.00001). Furthermore, the funnel plot for DSS exhibited no significant asymmetry (Fig. 4C), and the Egger’s test revealed no evidence of publication bias for this prognostic factor (P = 0.951).

Discussion

Oral cancer includes lip cancer and other malignancies originating from the oral cavity. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) represents the majority of histopathological types of oral cancer and is one of the most common malignancies in the head and neck region. Most patients present with advanced-stage disease due to the absence of prominent early symptoms. The recurrence rate is high within the first two years after initial surgery, and the tumor is prone to distant metastasis [31]. OSCC profoundly affects various dimensions of patients’ lives, including psychological well-being, underscoring the critical need for early detection and intervention. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that inflammation plays a critical role in tumor initiation and progression. As an inflammatory marker, PLR has been shown to be closely associated with the prognosis of OSCC [32, 33]. Cho et al. identified a marked difference in 5-year DSS rates between patients with low PLR (81.2%) and those with high PLR (58.4%) (p = 0.0004) [17]. In 2019, Zhang et al. conducted a meta-analysis confirming the utility of PLR in prognosticating OSCC outcomes [34]. This research expanded upon previous findings by encompassing a larger patient cohort and incorporating a wider range of high-quality clinical studies published in the last 1 to 2 years. Notably, the current investigation included a total of 20 studies, exceeding the previous count by 15. Furthermore, detailed subgroup analyses for OS and DSS were performed, stratified by sample size, PLR threshold, and geographical region. This comprehensive approach was designed to enhance the precision and scientific rigor of the findings.

In numerous studies focused on cancer prognosis, PLR has consistently demonstrated a significant correlation with clinical characteristics of oral cancer patients, as well as with OS, DFS, and DSS [33]. This alignment with the findings of the current meta-analysis further supports the strong association of PLR with the prognosis of OSCC. PLR exhibits a positive correlation with inflammation, and platelets, serving as mediators in angiogenesis and immune regulation, play a pivotal role in influencing cancer progression. Platelets influence cancer cells through TGF-β [35], facilitating the adhesion of cancer cells to microvascular endothelium and promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [36]. Additionally, lymphocytes play a crucial role in the host’s defense against tumors. It is suggested that a decreased lymphocyte count may impair the tumor immune surveillance function, providing a conducive environment for tumor metastasis and development [37–39]. Elevated PLR in patients might indirectly indicate poor lymphocyte-mediated immune response against tumors, leading to accelerated tumor progression and poor prognosis.Therefore, PLR can serve as a predictive indicator for the prognosis of various cancers [40–43].

The study highlights that the meta-analysis outcomes underscore a notable and consistent predictive value of PLR for OS, DFS, and DSS, contrary to the findings of Tsai et al., who did not reveal an independent role of PLR in predicting DFS [15]. In the sensitivity analysis, the results remained unchanged after the exclusion of each individual study, indicating that the data is stable and reliable. Notably, Egger’s test indicated the presence of publication bias for PLR in relation to OS and DSS, while no such bias was observed for PLR and DFS. Subgroup analysis was conducted using a PLR threshold of 150 as the cutoff value, The results indicated that studies where the PLR > 150, there was no predictive value for OS. However, in studies with PLR ≤ 150, a predictive value for OS was observed.Consequently, future endeavors in developing prognostic risk prediction models based on PLR thresholds should consider lowering the threshold to below 150 for enhanced predictive accuracy. The PLR cutoff value was established based on the median from the included data and referenced from previous literature, We believe this method enhances the reliability of our findings [44, 45].

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, despite the implementation of sensitivity and subgroup analyses, the precise source of heterogeneity has not been identified.This heterogeneity may be attributed to regional variations and the differing cutoff values for PLR.Second, The majority of the included studies are retrospective in nature, which typically rely on pre-existing data that often lack comprehensive documentation of confounding factors influencing the relationship between exposure and outcomes, such as gender, age, and tumor staging. These uncontrolled factors may impact PLR and survival outcomes, thereby affecting the validity of the conclusions. Therefore, it is essential to incorporate more rigorously designed prospective studies to validate our findings, while also adhering to standardized protocols for PLR sample collection.Third, the primary inclusion of Asian populations introduces a degree of selection bias, Furthermore, the Egger’s test indicated significant publication bias, which we speculate may be related to the majority of Asian populations. Due to the most of populations comes from the same region, the positive findings of this research may lack applicability to other regions. Future multicenter studies should be conducted to evaluate the influence of regional variations on outcome measures.Despite these limitations, to the best of current knowledge, this analysis represents the most extensive meta-analysis concerning the predictive value of PLR in oral cancer to date. With a total of 5714 patients from 20 studies, the association of elevated PLR with reduced OS, DFS, and DSS has been substantiated. These findings provide theoretical support and direction for future research aimed at developing prognostic models for clinical application in oral cancer.

Conclusion

The results of this meta-analysis suggest that PLR is associated with OS, DFS, and DSS in patients with OSCC, with elevated PLR indicating poorer OS, DFS, and DSS outcomes. Therefore, using PLR as a prognostic predictor for OSCC holds certain clinical value. However, considering the limitations of this study, including heterogeneity, publication bias, and potential selection bias, the exact predictive value of PLR in OSCC patients requires further validation through large-scale, prospective, multicenter clinical studies.
