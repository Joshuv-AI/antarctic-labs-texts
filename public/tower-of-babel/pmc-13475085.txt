BLACK-ARTS: a qualitative study exploring the insights of black young people with psychiatric disorders and the uses of creative arts therapies to improve mental well-being
Authors: Briana Applewhite, Susanne Birnkammer, Valerie Hartland, Oliver Campbell, Cara Horsley, Rose Meghen-Prudhoe, Jamie Crane, Melanie Berry, Ilina Singh, Joshua E J Buckman, Morten Kringelbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlack people experience persistent inequalities in mental health outcomes, access and care quality compared with their white counterparts. These disparities are compounded by structural racism and a lack of culturally competent care within National Health Service (NHS) services. Creative arts therapies (CATs), including music, art, dance and drama therapy, may offer culturally relevant, non-verbal approaches to mental health support for this population.ObjectiveTo explore how black young people with mental health challenges and NHS clinicians perceive the usefulness and cultural relevance of CATs and to examine their potential as culturally competent interventions for improving mental well-being.MethodsA qualitative, multicentre study was conducted across five NHS Trusts in England. 20 black service users (aged 16–24) and 15 clinicians participated in 10 online focus groups (45–60 min each). Transcripts were analysed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis method constructed with NVivo V.12 software. Coding was led by the first author, with regular discussions with a second researcher to refine interpretations and develop themes.FindingsFive overarching themes were identified: (1) creative arts as vehicles for emotional healing, identity and agency, (2) challenges and barriers to CAT implementation, (3) disconnection and mistrust within clinical encounters, (4) discovering therapeutic potential through creative experience and (5) creative expression as a bridge between culture and care. Participants viewed CATs as emotionally regulating, accessible and aligned with black cultural identity though limited by underfunding, stigma and insufficient cultural competence within the NHS.ConclusionsCATs may provide a culturally congruent and emotionally accessible approach to mental healthcare for black youth. Embedding culturally competent, creative modalities within NHS pathways could enhance engagement, trust and equity in service outcomes.Clinical implicationsCATs should be integrated early in NHS pathways (eg, Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services, Talking Therapies) to improve engagement among black youth who may be hesitant to engage in verbally dominant therapies. However, scaled implementation requires meaningful coproduction with black communities, cultural competence training for multidisciplinary teams and recognition within NHS and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence frameworks.Keywords: Mental Health, Music Therapy, Art Therapy, Dance Therapy, Psychiatry

Background

Black populations in the UK continue to experience persistent and well-documented inequalities in mental health access, care quality and outcomes compared with their white counterparts. These disparities occur across the care pathway, including delayed access to services, higher rates of compulsory detention, disproportionate exposure to crisis-oriented care and poorer treatment experiences once engaged with services. National reports and systematic reviews consistently identify structural racism, institutional bias and a lack of culturally responsive care as central drivers of these inequities within National Health Service (NHS) mental health services.1–4

Black people are more likely to access mental health services through adverse or coercive routes, including police involvement and compulsory admission under the Mental Health Act, rather than via primary care or early intervention pathways.3 5 They are also more likely to receive pharmacological treatment rather than psychological therapies and to report dissatisfaction, mistrust and feelings of being misunderstood by clinicians.4–6 These patterns contribute to poorer engagement, reduced treatment adherence and worse long-term outcomes.

Barriers to accessing and sustaining mental healthcare among black communities include stigma surrounding mental illness, fear of discrimination, prior negative experiences with health systems and a lack of culturally informed therapeutic approaches.6–8 The NHS Race and Health Observatory has highlighted that these barriers are reinforced by institutional practices that prioritise standardised, ‘culture-neutral’ models of care, despite evidence that such approaches inadequately serve racially minoritised populations.2 4

Mental health inequalities among black children and young people

Children and young people from black and other minoritised backgrounds are particularly vulnerable to these inequities. In the UK, approximately one in five young people experience a probable mental disorder, with half of all lifetime mental health conditions emerging by the age of 14.9 Despite this, black children and adolescents are less likely to access early mental health support and are more likely to disengage from services once care begins.10 11

Black young people are under-represented in Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS) relative to need, yet over-represented in crisis care and compulsory admissions.3 10 They are also significantly more likely to be referred to services through social care rather than primary care, reflecting delayed help-seeking and systemic barriers to early intervention.3 These patterns contribute to fragmented care trajectories and reduced opportunities for preventative or relationally grounded therapeutic work.

A further challenge lies in the limited specificity of existing research. Studies frequently collapse diverse ethnic identities into broad categories such as ‘Black, Asian, and minority ethnic’, obscuring within-group differences and limiting understanding of the unique barriers faced by black youth in the UK.11 As a result, there is a critical gap in evidence regarding culturally appropriate interventions that can improve engagement, trust and outcomes for this population.

Cultural competence in mental healthcare

Cultural competence refers to the capacity of health systems and practitioners to deliver care that is responsive to service users’ cultural identities, histories and lived experiences.12 13 In mental healthcare, cultural competence extends beyond awareness of difference to include reflective practice, relational sensitivity and organisational adaptation.12 14 In this paper, cultural competence is treated as both relational and structural. At the relational level, it concerns how clinicians recognise and respond to cultural identity within therapeutic encounters; at the structural level, it relates to how services organise care in ways that either mitigate or reproduce inequities.

Evidence indicates that culturally adapted psychological interventions can improve engagement and outcomes for minoritised groups, particularly when adaptations occur at both individual and organisational levels.15 16 However, UK mental health services continue to rely heavily on standardised, verbally oriented therapeutic models that may feel inaccessible or alienating to some black service users, particularly those with prior experiences of mistrust or coercion within care systems.5 6

Clinicians have also reported limited training and confidence in addressing race, racism and cultural identity within therapeutic encounters, further constraining their ability to deliver equitable care.4 14 Together, these factors underscore the need for therapeutic approaches that are clinically effective and are culturally congruent and emotionally accessible.

Creative arts therapies as culturally responsive interventions

Creative arts therapies (CATs) encompass music therapy, art therapy, dance movement therapy and drama therapy. These approaches use creative and non-verbal modalities to support emotional expression, regulation and meaning making within a therapeutic context.17 Already in the UK, CATs are recognised by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) as effective adjunctive treatments for conditions such as psychosis and schizophrenia, particularly for individuals who struggle with traditional talking therapies.18

CATs may be especially relevant for black young people, for whom creative expression through music, movement and visual art often plays a central role in cultural identity, community connection and coping.19–22 Unlike verbally dominant therapeutic models, CATs offer alternative pathways for expressing distress, processing trauma and exploring identity, which may feel more accessible for individuals who find verbal disclosure difficult or culturally incongruent.

A growing body of evidence supports the effectiveness of CATs for improving emotional well-being and reducing symptoms across a range of mental health conditions, including depression, psychosis and trauma-related disorders.23 24 A recent meta-analysis found that creative arts-based interventions may be particularly beneficial for young people from non-Western and Global Majority backgrounds, highlighting their potential as culturally responsive treatments.25

Importantly, CATs may also support engagement and therapeutic alliance, key predictors of positive outcomes in youth mental healthcare. By incorporating culturally familiar modes of expression, CATs may help bridge the gap between black youth’s lived experiences and clinical care, fostering trust, agency and sustained participation in treatment.

Objective

Despite their promise, the cultural relevance and perceived usefulness of CATs for black young people within NHS mental health services remain underexplored. There is limited qualitative research capturing the perspectives of black service users alongside those of clinicians working within NHS systems. Understanding these perspectives is essential for informing the development of more culturally competent, equitable and engaging mental health services.

The present study addresses this gap by exploring how black young people with mental health challenges and NHS clinicians perceive the use of CATs as culturally competent interventions. By foregrounding lived experience and clinical insight, this study aims to inform service design and contribute to ongoing efforts to reduce mental health inequalities among black youth in the UK.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a qualitative, multicentre design using semistructured focus groups to explore perceptions of CATs among black young people with mental health challenges and NHS clinicians. Data were collected between December 2023 and September 2024 across five NHS Trusts in England: South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust (SLaM), Berkshire Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust (BH), Central and North West London NHS Foundation Trust (CNWL), Oxford Health NHS Foundation Trust (OH) and the North Central and East London Improving Access to Psychological Therapies Service Research Network (NCEL IAPT). The inclusion of five NHS Trusts was intentional to capture variation in service structure, geography and demographic context across different settings. While the sample size within each Trust was modest, the multisite design enabled exploration of patterns that transcended local organisational differences and strengthened the transferability of findings across NHS contexts.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited via clinician referral and professional networks using purposive sampling to ensure representation across service types, age ranges and black ethnic backgrounds. This minimised the risk of ineligible participants joining sessions. All participants were able to communicate in English and provided written informed consent prior to participation. Black service users received a £20 voucher in appreciation of their time.

Demographic information (including age, gender, ethnicity, treatment setting and diagnosis where provided) was collected via a brief structured questionnaire completed prior to the focus group sessions.

Data collection

10 virtual focus groups were conducted using Microsoft Teams, each lasting approximately 45–60 min to balance depth of discussion with participant comfort, particularly given the sensitive nature of topics, including race, stigma and mental health experiences.

Focus groups were selected as the primary method of data collection because the study aimed to explore shared experiences around CATs and cultural competence. Given the cultural nature of the topic, group discussion was considered appropriate to elicit dialogue among participants. Virtual focus groups were chosen based on findings from previous qualitative research with minority patients as they were cited to be flexible for both participants and the research team and could be conducted in relaxed environments like one’s home, leading to more involvement in the group discussion.26 Participants were encouraged, but not required, to keep cameras on.

Black service users and clinicians participated in separate groups to minimise power imbalances and facilitate open discussion. Ground rules were established at the start of each session to promote respectful and open discussion. Each focus group included four to five participants and was moderated by the lead researcher (BA). The moderator actively invited contributions from quieter participants and used structured prompts to ensure balanced participation across the group.

Prior to each session, participants received an overview of the study aims and a brief explanation of CATs and culturally competent care. This included examples of music therapy, art therapy, dance movement therapy and drama therapy and clarified that CATs may be delivered in group or individual formats, typically combining creative activity with facilitated reflection or discussion led by a trained therapist. Two semistructured topic guides, one for service users and one for clinicians, were developed based on existing literature on cultural competence, CATs and mental health service engagement.

Discussion topics included experiences of current mental healthcare, perceptions of CATs, cultural relevance, perceived benefits and challenges, and views on how creative approaches might better meet service users’ needs.

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and checked against recordings by the lead researcher for accuracy prior to analysis. Identifying information was removed during transcription to ensure confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.27 Reflexive thematic analysis was selected due to its flexibility in exploring shared meanings across participant groups while allowing reflexive engagement with issues of race, identity and power. The analytic team consisted of two researchers (BA and SB), with BA leading the coding process. Transcripts were read repeatedly to support familiarisation, and initial codes were generated inductively across the dataset. In line with reflexive thematic analysis, coding was treated as an active, interpretive process, with themes understood as constructed through researcher engagement with the data rather than discovered as objective categories. The second researcher (SB) reviewed transcripts and engaged in collaborative discussions to explore alternative interpretations and refine the developing thematic structure, with the aim of deepening reflexive engagement rather than achieving coding consensus or reliability.

Codes were iteratively reviewed, refined and organised into broader patterns of meaning, involving recursive movement between data, codes and emerging themes, leading to the generation of five overarching themes. A summary of coding development is provided in online supplemental appendix table 1. NVivo V.12 software was used to support data management and organisation.28 Ongoing reflexive dialogue within the research team enhanced analytic depth and transparency. This study was conducted within a constructivist-interpretivist paradigm, recognising knowledge as co-constructed between researchers and participants.

We used the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline29 to guide manuscript preparation and enhance transparency in reporting qualitative methods and analysis.

Reflexivity and researcher positionality

Given the study’s focus on race, culture and identity, reflexivity was integral to the research process. The lead researcher (BA) identifies as part of the black community and has professional experience within mental health services. This positionality facilitated rapport and sensitivity to cultural nuance but required ongoing reflexive awareness to minimise bias.

The second analyst (SB) is a white European doctoral researcher with training as an arts therapist and with culturally competent and trauma-informed approaches. Differences in cultural backgrounds within the analytic team were treated as an analytic resource rather than a source of bias to be eliminated. Regular discussions were held to interrogate assumptions and question emerging interpretations.

Analytic decisions were made through iterative dialogue rather than consensus seeking. Reflexive diaries were maintained throughout data collection and analysis to document positional reflections, analytic shifts and decisions regarding theme development. Peer debriefing sessions were conducted between the two analysts (BA and SB) who are doctoral researchers with shared experience in creative arts and mental health research.

We were particularly attentive to issues of power in representing participants’ voices and reflexive engagement supported efforts to centre black young people’s perspectives while situating clinician accounts within their institutional context.

Patient and public involvement

We consulted a Young People’s Advisory Group (YPAG) at the University of Oxford composed of young people aged 14 and 25 in the Oxford community. The YPAG contributed to the overall study design as well as assisting in the development of the focus group schedule and topic guide for both service users and clinicians.

Findings

Overview of findings

35 participants took part in the study, including 20 black service users aged 16–24 and 15 clinicians working across NHS mental health services. Clinician participants included creative arts therapists, psychologists and multidisciplinary practitioners working across CAMHS, IAPT and Early Intervention Services (EIS). Those trained in CATs tended to describe CATs in terms of therapeutic mechanism and clinical legitimacy, whereas other clinicians more frequently positioned CATs within broader discussions of service hierarchy and resource allocation.

Participants’ quotes are labelled using pseudonymous codes (SU01–SU20 for service users; CL01–CL15 for clinicians with their broader professional roles) to preserve confidentiality.

Although themes were developed across the dataset, variation in emphasis emerged between participant groups. Black youth more frequently foregrounded the emotional accessibility of CATs and mistrust in clinical encounters, drawing on personal experiences of stigma and mistrust of traditional NHS services. In contrast, clinicians tended to frame CATs in relation to service feasibility, workforce limitations and institutional barriers. Participant groups diverged also in how cultural competence was understood, with black youth emphasising lived experience, while clinicians often discussed training and professional awareness.

Clinician demographics.

Some participants (n=6 service users; n=6 clinicians) did not specify treatment setting. In several cases, service users were on waitlists or receiving care across multiple pathways, and clinicians worked across integrated service models, making categorisation difficult. Non-disclosure was also respected where participants chose not to provide this information.

Of the 20 service users, seven reported prior experience of formal CAT within NHS settings, while the remaining participants described engagement primarily through informal creative practices (eg, listening to music, dancing recreationally). Among clinicians, five were trained creative arts therapists, with others working in psychology, psychiatry or multidisciplinary roles.

Creative arts as vehicles for emotional healing, identity and agency

Participants consistently described CATs as supportive tools for emotional regulation and expression. Black youth emphasised the role of music, art and movement in helping them manage anxiety and communicate feelings that were difficult to express verbally. Clinicians echoed these accounts, highlighting CATs as accessible alternatives for young people who struggle with conventional talking therapies. Notably, several clinicians were trained creative arts therapists or worked within services where CATs were already embedded.

It breaks barriers—almost every patient can relate to some form of art or music, regardless of background or religion. (CL02, Art therapist)

Several clinicians described therapeutic breakthroughs emerging through creative engagement rather than verbal exploration.

Sometimes being heard isn’t about your voice. It can be through art, through your body, through music. (CL09, Clinical psychologist)

Participants also emphasised the sense of agency afforded by creative approaches, describing CATs as flexible, empowering and aligned with individual needs.

However, not all participants experienced CATs as inherently accessible. Some black youth described discomfort with group sessions and fears of judgement, suggesting that creative modalities may not be equally supportive for all young people, particularly those with social anxiety or low confidence in group spaces.

What if someone judges my dancing or the art that I’m making? That might make me feel worse. (SU04)

Challenges and barriers to CAT implementation

Despite strong interest in CATs, participants identified significant barriers to their implementation within NHS services. Clinicians described limited funding, insufficient staffing and the marginalisation of creative approaches within clinical hierarchies.

It’s often devalued for its purpose. We (arts therapists) know the power of the intervention, but other clinicians are more likely to disrupt seeing somebody engaging in drama or music or art and thinking, oh, “I can disrupt that in the way that they wouldn’t disrupt if it was a one-on-one conversation.” It’s seems to be viewed as nonessential for our CBT [Cognitive Behavioural Therapy] Cognitive behavioural therapy] therapists. (CL13, Music therapist)

Black youth described personal barriers to engagement, including shyness and fear of judgement in group-based creative settings.

I think for me the difficulty would be just interacting with new people. I always find it hard to make new friends. What if they’re judging my dance movements or what I decide to create in an arts session? (SU01)

Participants noted that these challenges were compounded by broader systemic constraints, including long waiting times and limited access to therapeutic options beyond talking therapies.

Discovering therapeutic potential through creative experience

Although not all black youth had accessed formal CAT sessions, all reported engaging in creative practices in their everyday lives to support their mental health. Participants described singing, dancing and listening to music as coping strategies, particularly during periods of waiting for services.

I would say I’ve used singing as like a coping strategy. (SU03)

I sing and dance and it really helps to some extent. It’s helped me deal with a lot of mental things on my own. (SU20)

Clinicians described recognising similar patterns in practice, noting that many young people already use creative expression as a form of self-regulation before entering therapy.

Disconnection and mistrust within clinical encounters

Black youth frequently described feelings of disconnection and dissatisfaction within conventional therapy settings. Talking therapies were often experienced as impersonal, repetitive or misaligned with their lived experiences.

I feel sometimes this therapy lacks some personal approaches. Generally, I don’t feel they are addressing my issues. Sometimes I even end up treating my own self without the therapy. Sometimes it kind of feels like they’re trying to put words in my mouth or explain my experience through their own. (SU06)

Sometimes therapy just feels like another job I have to do. There was nothing personal about it and it seemed like false promises. (SU03)

Clinicians often framed black young people’s disengagement in terms of systemic barriers. While clinicians acknowledged structural racism, their accounts tended to focus on training gaps and resource limitations, reflecting their positioning within NHS systems. Clinicians acknowledged that black young people often enter services following crisis or coercive experiences, which can undermine trust and engagement.

We expect young people to dive straight into heavy therapy, but for many, especially if they’ve had bad experiences, it’s too much too soon. (CL12, Psychotherapist)

Black youth also discussed stigma surrounding mental health within black communities, which contributed to delayed help seeking.

That’s just not a thing. Talking about our emotions, I do think that has a lot to do with oppression and what we’ve been through as a as a people anyway in general. So we’re told to keep quiet and things like that. I feel like that’s just rippled on to now. (SU15)

Creative expression as a bridge between culture and care

Participants consistently described creative expression as deeply embedded within black cultural identity. Music, art and dance were framed as culturally familiar and meaningful forms of expression that could support engagement with mental healthcare. Participants described identity alignment in several distinct ways. For some, creative expression connected them to diasporic heritage and cultural memory, particularly through music rooted in African or Caribbean traditions. For others, identity alignment referred less to cultural tradition and more to a sense of self-expression that felt authentic and self-directed, in contrast to structured clinical dialogue.

It’s something that’s taught me something about myself and my roots. I know how much art, music, and dance means to us. It’s ingrained. I would love to see that happening in our services. (SU11)

I have a personal value of it by growing up in the Caribbean half my life and half my life in the UK, and I guess it helped to shape my identity as a Black person… it helped me to build a core anyway. (SU04)

Growing up in the Caribbean, (music) is something that taught me about myself, about my roots. It’s something that gave me a sense of self and it gives me a reenergised feeling like, ah, yes, I’m at home. (SU13)

It’s something my people need, especially growing up in a European place. I do think it helps connect us to our identity and could really help those that struggle growing up in a place that’s so dissimilar to our home countries. And if there were creative arts offered at my service, I would definitely be signing up for it today. (SU17)

While many participants described creative expression as culturally meaningful, this was not presented as a wholly ubiquitous experience. Cultural identity was diverse and layered, and some young people’s relationship to the arts was shaped also by generational or personal experience not by racial identity alone. Cultural resonance therefore appeared contingent rather than automatic.

Music (is) definitely something I find helpful, but I don’t tie any cultural meaning to it personally. (SU08)

Clinicians emphasised the importance of cultural competence in building therapeutic relationships.

Just being aware of different cultures and experiences is really important. We cannot expect a one-size-fits-all model to work for everyone, especially in therapy. If we’re naïve to cultural competence, we’re not going to build relationships with patients and they’re not going to get anything from our treatments. I try to learn from my patients just as they are trying to learn from me. But as clinicians, we should all be doing this. (CL09, Clinical psychologist)

When you understand more about their culture, where they come from, you will know where you’re going. If you understand that culture and try to incorporate that into the session, you will probably get more information and a deeper connection with that client. (CL03, Clinical psychologist)

Some black youth described the emotional labour involved in addressing race within therapy.

In the therapy where things have come up about race, it’s like they don’t know how to handle these situations, especially in a group setting… sometimes I have to explain it to them, which is a form of labour on my part. I have to educate people who should be helping and educating me. They need to educate themselves about our cultures before they can help us fully. (SU01)

Discussion

This study explored how black young people with mental health challenges and NHS clinicians perceive CATs as culturally competent interventions within mental healthcare. The findings indicate that CATs are viewed as emotionally accessible, culturally resonant and potentially effective alternatives or complements to traditional talking therapies. As outlined in table 1, participants described CAT in either individual or group settings with reflective discussion, as aligning more closely with lived experience, and preferred modes of expression, particularly for black youth who often feel misunderstood or alienated within conventional clinical encounters.

Although perspectives from clinicians are presented alongside those of black service users, the analytic emphasis of this study is centred on the lived experiences of black young people.

CATs as accessible and culturally congruent care

Black youth consistently described CATs as tools for emotional regulation, expression and agency, particularly when verbal communication felt limiting or inadequate. These findings align with existing evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of creative arts-based interventions in supporting emotional well-being and engagement among young people with mental health difficulties.25 30 Recent literature focused on CATs specifically for black youth populations globally has found leading evidence for creative arts’ beneficial uses with this population, highlighting their usefulness as culturally aligning and accessible tools for black young people struggling with mental health difficulties.25 By offering non-verbal and embodied modes of expression, CATs may be particularly well suited to individuals who struggle with introspection-based or cognitively demanding therapeutic models. Importantly, CATs should not be positioned solely in opposition to traditional talking therapies. Participants’ accounts suggest that creative approaches may function most effectively alongside broader psychosocial supports, including social prescribing initiatives, community-based well-being programmes, peer support networks and recovery colleges.

The perceived value of CATs was not simply that they supported emotional regulation, but that they offered an alternative relational dynamic to traditional NHS encounters. For many black youth, creative approaches disrupted the interrogative structure of clinical dialogue, enabling more embodied forms of participation. In this sense, CATs were experienced as therapeutic tools and as altering the power dynamics of care.

Participants framed CATs not simply as therapeutic techniques but as culturally meaningful practices. Music, dance and visual art were described as embedded within black cultural identity and everyday coping, suggesting that CATs may offer a form of cultural congruence often lacking in standardised NHS therapies. This cultural alignment may help explain why participants viewed CATs as more engaging and authentic than conventional talking therapies, supporting the argument that therapeutic effectiveness is closely tied to cultural relevance.

However, it is important to note that a minority of participants expressed uncertainty about whether creative approaches would feel therapeutic in formal clinical contexts, suggesting that everyday engagement with music or art does not automatically translate into comfort within structured therapeutic settings.

Addressing disengagement and mistrust in mental health services

The study also highlights persistent experiences of disconnection and mistrust within NHS mental health services. Consistent with recent reporting,2 4–6 10 16 black youth frequently described feeling unheard, misunderstood or burdened by impersonal therapeutic approaches, echoing wider evidence that black young people often experience mental healthcare as alienating or misaligned with their needs.

Clinicians acknowledged that many black service users enter care through crisis pathways or after coercive experiences, which can undermine trust and engagement from the outset.

Within this context, CATs may offer a less threatening entry point into therapy by prioritising relationship building, creativity and emotional safety over immediate verbal disclosure. Participants suggested that creative approaches could support early engagement, particularly for young people who are hesitant to access services or who have disengaged from previous treatment. These findings reinforce the importance of flexibility and relational sensitivity in addressing engagement disparities within youth mental healthcare.

The significance of CATs in this study lies less in their novelty as therapeutic tools and more in how participants positioned them within a system marked by mistrust. CATs were framed as counterpoints to standardised NHS care. This suggests that their perceived effectiveness may derive from modality and from the ways they reconfigure participation and agency within institutional contexts.

Cultural competence beyond individual awareness

Participants’ accounts underscore that cultural competence must extend beyond individual clinician awareness to encompass systemic and organisational change. While clinicians expressed commitment to culturally responsive practice, many also described limited training, institutional constraints and a lack of support for implementing creative approaches within NHS services. Black service users, in turn, described the emotional labour of explaining cultural or racial experiences to clinicians who felt unprepared to engage with these topics.

CATs alone cannot resolve structural inequities in mental healthcare; however, they may function as one component of a broader culturally competent system. By embedding creative approaches within standard care pathways and supporting clinicians through training and reflective practice, services may be better equipped to meet the needs of black young people. Importantly, participants did not present CATs as a solution to structural racism or systemic failures within NHS care. Experiences of coercive pathways and mistrust were understood as institutional problems that require policy-level reform. CATs may improve engagement and emotional accessibility, but they cannot substitute for structural change.

Within current NHS pathways, CATs may function as relational and engagement-enhancing interventions rather than structural remedies. Their contribution appears most evident in improving emotional accessibility, strengthening therapeutic alliance and fostering identity affirmation. However, their implementation remains contingent on institutional support and integration within existing service models.

These findings align with calls from national policy bodies and recent research to move beyond ‘one-size-fits-all’ models and develop culturally informed, community-grounded mental health services.2 16

Implications for NHS mental health services

The findings have several implications for service design and delivery within the NHS. First, CATs could be integrated in EIS, CAMHS and NHS Talking Therapies, particularly for young people referred through crisis social care routes who may be hesitant to engage in verbally dominant therapies. Integrating CATs earlier in care pathways may support engagement, reduce dropout and foster trust among black young people who are less likely to benefit from conventional approaches. Additionally, CATs could be integrated within clinical services and within social prescribing frameworks, recovery colleges and community-led mental health initiatives. Embedding CATs within these relational and non-clinical spaces may reduce stigma and support early engagement without requiring immediate entry into formal psychotherapy.

Finally, meaningful coproduction with black communities is essential. Involving black youth in the design, delivery and evaluation of creative interventions may help ensure that services are culturally sensitive and responsive to lived experience. Such approaches align with broader NHS commitments to reduce health inequalities and promote person-centred care.

However, implementing CATs at scale requires structural change. This includes (1) commissioning recognition of CATs within NHS service specifications, (2) increased resources for creative arts therapist posts, (3) integration of CATs into NICE care pathways where appropriate and (4) mandatory training in cultural competence for multidisciplinary teams.

Responsibility for these changes lies at multiple levels: NHS Trust leadership and service managers in workforce planning and service design; commissioners and Integrated Care Boards in funding decisions; and national bodies such as NHS England and NICE in recognising culturally congruent modalities within formal guidance frameworks.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength lies in the study’s inclusion of both service user and clinician perspectives across multiple NHS Trusts, which enabled a more holistic understanding of how cultural factors, systemic barriers and therapeutic engagement intersect in practice. The focus group format fostered collective reflection, allowing participants to build on one another’s experiences and identify shared challenges in accessing and delivering culturally competent care. Conducting separate focus groups for clinicians and black service users also helped ensure that participants could speak freely without fear of judgement or professional repercussions.

However, qualitative research of this nature carries inherent limitations. Focus group discussions may be influenced by social desirability bias, with participants potentially presenting views they believe to be acceptable to others in the group. Given the sensitive nature of race and mental health, some participants, particularly those in mixed-ethnicity clinician groups, may have moderated their responses.

The sample size, while appropriate for qualitative inquiry, limits generalisability. Participants were drawn from NHS Trusts in England, and their views may not represent the full diversity of black young people’s experiences across the UK.

Furthermore, while there are clear benefits of CATs, outlined by both black youth and clinicians, there were expressed limits on who these therapies may not work well for, namely those with social anxieties when group creative arts sessions are conducted. Service users expressed the fear of being judged for the art they created or their movements in dance sessions, highlighting the unease some individuals might have in using these methods. Clinicians highlighted the lack of resources allocated to creative arts therapists within the NHS and also convincing other clinical staff to see the value in these interventions and how these would be carried out.

Additionally, while clinician perspectives aligned closely with those of black service users, these accounts must be interpreted cautiously. Several clinician participants were trained creative arts therapists or worked within services where CATs were embedded. Their enthusiasm may therefore reflect both experiential knowledge and professional commitment to the legitimacy of creative modalities. This underscores that clinician endorsement should not be interpreted as independent validation of effectiveness.

Future research should build on these findings using mixed-method or longitudinal designs to assess the sustained impact of CATs on engagement, well-being and treatment outcomes among black youth. Quantitative studies could complement this qualitative evidence by measuring the effectiveness of culturally adapted CATs in comparison to standard talking therapies. Further, participatory and community-led research models would help ensure that interventions are coproduced with black communities, fostering authenticity, accessibility and trust.

Conclusion

This study provides new evidence that CATs may offer a culturally relevant and emotionally accessible approach to improving mental well-being among black young people with mental health challenges. By combining clinical efficacy with cultural congruence, CATs have the potential to address persistent inequities in engagement and outcomes within NHS services. Embedding culturally congruent creative approaches, alongside broader organisational efforts towards cultural competence could represent a critical step towards more equitable, responsive and person-centred mental health provision for black youth.
