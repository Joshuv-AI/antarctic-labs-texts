Changes in nutrition-related health outcomes following a minimum wage increase
Authors: Caitlin E. Caspi, Maria F. Gombi-Vaca, Molly De Marco, Rebekah Pratt, Thomas Durfee, Samuel L. Myers, Julian Wolfson, Lisa J. Harnack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Minimum wage laws have the potential to promote economic stability and may have beneficial health effects. This study evaluated nutrition-related effects of a city ordinance increasing the minimum wage to $15 an hour in Minneapolis, Minnesota (MN). The study followed a cohort of low-wage workers in Minneapolis (n = 490) and a comparison group with no minimum wage increase in Raleigh, North Carolina (n = 479) from 2018 to 2022. Main outcomes included body mass index (BMI) assessed annually by objective height/weight measures; grocery spending assessed in 2018 and 2022 by food receipts; and food insecurity and food assistance program participation, and benefit amounts assessed annually by a survey. Other outcomes included stress, physical activity, sleep, and healthcare utilization. The study tested changes in each outcome between cities and within cities adjusting for covariates and inflation. From 2018 to 2022, the mean hourly wage increased in both cities. There was no change in BMI or in the proportion of food spending on fruits and vegetables or foods high in added sugars between or within cities. There was a decrease in food insecurity and an increase in Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) participation and benefit amounts within both cities, with a greater magnitude of change in Raleigh. In this study, there was no evidence of minimum wage policy effect on wages, and no subsequent policy-related effects on nutrition-related or health outcomes. Unanticipated COVID-19 pandemic changes may have contributed to the observed results.

Introduction

Economic stability is a social determinant of health (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, n.d.). While there are many dimensions of economic stability, sustained low-wage earnings and frequent employment fluctuations are associated with all-cause mortality and excess deaths (Kezios et al., 2023). This makes strengthening work-related policies for low-wage earners, including minimum wage laws, a promising policy strategy for improving health (Berenson et al., 2017; Leigh, 2016, 2018; Rigby & Hatch, 2016). Income-related policies might influence health-related outcomes through material and psychosocial pathways (Adler et al., 1994; Link & Phelan, 1995). For instance, wage increases have the potential to translate into health benefits by increasing access to basic necessities, including enough food and stable housing, as well as other health-promoting resources (Braveman, 2006). Raising the minimum wage also has the potential to address racial/ethnic health inequities given persistent wage gaps affecting minoritized racial/ethnic groups (Berenson et al., 2017; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024).

There is evidence from high-income countries that a higher minimum wage is associated with more favorable outcomes for some dimensions of health (Leigh et al., 2019; Neumark, 2023). These include infant and birth outcomes (Bhatia & Katz, 2001; Komro et al., 2016; Wehby et al., 2020), mental health and suicide outcomes (Bai & Veall, 2023; Gertner et al., 2019), and premature deaths (Bhatia & Katz, 2001; Tsao et al., 2016). This association between higher minimum wage and better health status reflects a well-established socioeconomic gradient in health (Fuhrer et al., 2002; Marmot et al., 1991). At the same time, studies of the relationship between minimum wage and other behaviorally-mediated outcomes, including weight outcomes, have demonstrated conflicting results (Andreyeva & Ukert, 2018; Buszkiewicz et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2020; Horn et al., 2017; Kim & Leigh, 2010; Leigh et al., 2019; Meltzer & Chen, 2009; Palazzolo & Pattabhiramaiah, 2021). Higher minimum wage has been associated with lower body mass index (BMI) in some studies (Clark et al., 2020; Kim & Leigh, 2010; Meltzer & Chen, 2009), but not all (Andreyeva & Ukert, 2018; Buszkiewicz et al., 2020). There is also inconclusive evidence as to whether increasing minimum wage has positive effects on diet quality (Clark et al., 2020; Horn et al., 2017; Narain & Zimmerman, 2019; Palazzolo & Pattabhiramaiah, 2021) or physical activity (Dallmeyer & Breuer, 2024; Horn et al., 2017; Lenhart, 2017), as would be expected if increasing minimum wage resulted in access to health-promoting resources. There is a smaller body of research suggesting that higher minimum wage is associated with a lower food insecurity (Freije et al., 2025; Men et al., 2021; Winkler et al., 2025) and that wage gains offset decreases in federal food assistance benefits like the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) that are sensitive to changes in income (Rodgers, 2016). There are many enduring questions about the relationship between income changes and weight-related outcomes, including the causal direction and drivers of a putative relationship (Kim and von dem Knesebeck, 2018).

The Minneapolis Minimum Wage Ordinance, passed by the city council of Minneapolis, MN, USA in June 2017, offered an opportunity to study the effects of weight-related outcomes in a sample of low-wage workers followed prospectively during the implementation of a wage increase. The WAGE$ study tests the hypothesis that Minneapolis workers likely to be affected by a minimum wage ordinance would have more favorable trends in BMI, food insecurity, healthy food purchases, and other health outcomes – even accounting for potential decreases in federal food assistance program support – compared with low-wage workers in a comparison city with no increase in minimum wage (Caspi, De Marco, Durfee, et al., 2021). Although the effects of an increase in the minimum wage on economic outcomes such as job loss and hours worked are a topic of much debate (Cengiz et al., 2019; Godoey et al., 2019; Neumark & Yen, 2021), the current study focuses primarily on the health-related consequences of a minimum wage increase. An early-impact analysis found no beneficial or adverse effects of the minimum wage ordinance on BMI, food insecurity or dietary intake at the study midpoint in 2020 (Caspi et al., 2023). This study presents complete findings on primary and secondary outcomes collected over five time points from 2018 to 2022.

Methods

Study overview

The WAGE$ study evaluated the diet-related health effects of a city ordinance, the Minneapolis Minimum Wage Ordinance, using a difference-in-difference (DiD) approach. The primary outcome was change in BMI. Intermediary diet-related outcomes included changes in food purchases, food insecurity status, and participation in government-supported food assistance programs; other intermediary outcomes included stress, physical activity, sleep, and healthcare utilization. A cohort of workers likely to be affected by an increase in minimum wage in Minneapolis and comparable workers in a matched comparison city (Raleigh, North Carolina, USA) were enrolled in 2018 and followed annually through 2022. During this time, the Minneapolis minimum wage increased incrementally each year on July 1, from $10 to $15 per hour for employees of large businesses (defined by the ordinance as firms with ≥100 employees) and from $7.75 to $13.50 for employees of small businesses (<100 employees) in Minneapolis. The ordinance applied to all time worked within the geographic boundaries of the city of Minneapolis if at least 2 h a week were worked. Independent contractors and federal and state employees were excluded from the ordinance, but part-time, joint, and temporary workers were included. There was no exclusion of the minimum wage for tipped workers. Details of the study design and measures (Caspi, De Marco, Durfee, et al., 2021), site selection and recruitment (Shanafelt et al., 2021), baseline sample characteristics (Caspi, De Marco, Durfee, et al., 2021, Durfee et al., 2021), and preliminary results (Chapman et al., 2021, Caspi et al., 2023, Chapman et al., 2024) have been previously published. The study was preregistered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03962712).

Comparison site

Raleigh was identified as the most appropriate matched comparison city (Shanafelt et al., 2021). To determine the best comparison city, the first step was to identify all U.S. cities with a population size of ± 50% of Minneapolis that were located in states with a minimum wage preemption law (Melton-Fant, 2023) in effect that prohibited local minimum wage increase (n = 41 cities). Second, demographics between each city and Minneapolis were matched, including median household income, four racial/ethnic categories, poverty, percent foreign born, percent with greater than a high-school degree, employment rate, total businesses, and median rent based on U.S. Census Bureau data. Raleigh was a match with Minneapolis on all demographics except percent poverty and percent Black. A common approach in DiD analyses is to assess differential pre-study trends using an event study framework, which evaluates whether outcome trends are similar across groups prior to the intervention or policy. While our study sample did not include multiple pre-policy observations, we used BRFSS SMART data to examine BMI trends between 2006 and 2015 in both cities. These trends indicated that BMI was slightly higher in Raleigh in all years, with a small and comparable net increase in BMI across years in both cities, supporting the plausibility of the parallel trends assumption (Supplementary Fig. 1). As a final step, we compared the obesity prevalence, the five largest industries, cost-of-living measures, and historical and projected earning trends and annualized job growth in major low-wage industries, all of which were of a similar in Minneapolis and Raleigh.

Participant eligibility

The participant sample for the study was defined as those likely to be affected by the minimum wage in Minneapolis and comparable workers in Raleigh in 2018. Eligible participants: (1) were 20 years old or older, (2) worked at least 10 h a week at a wage of less than or equal to $11.50/hour (15% above the Minneapolis minimum wage at the start of the study) in either city OR were employed at that wage within the last six months and were currently seeking work in either city (to avoid conditioning on employment), (3) planned to serve in the workforce for at least five years, (4) could be contacted for follow-up, and (5) spoke English or Spanish. Participants were excluded if they were federal or state workers, full-time students, or planned to retire or move more than 100 miles away.

Participant data collection

Participants were recruited in community settings in Minneapolis and Raleigh in 2018 (Shanafelt et al., 2021) and followed annually through 2022. Time points are labeled in the results with the year in which most participants completed the measure to provide better context for interpretation. Baseline (T1) data were collected from February 2018 through October 2018 and are labeled “2018.” In subsequent periods (T2-T5), data collection started after July 1, after each incremental annual wage increase was implemented in Minneapolis. From T2 to T5, nearly all measures (99%) were collected from July through December, with the remainder (1%) occurring in January or February of the subsequent year. For example, T3 results are labeled “2020,” and reflect data collected from July 8, 2020 to January 25, 2021.

In T1 and T2, data collection measures occurred through in-person appointments at community locations with trained research staff. At T3, due to pandemic-related restrictions on in-person appointments, remote appointments replaced in-person ones. In T4 and T5, both in-person and remote appointments were offered. In T4, 18% of participants opted for in-person appointments; this rose to 86% of participants in T5. These flexibilities were necessary for two reasons. First, offering appointment flexibility helped to bolster participation in a transient and hard-to-reach cohort. Second, demographically, the cohort also carried a higher than average risk of adverse effects from COVID-19 illness (Lopez et al., 2021; Moore et al., 2020) and so the research team was reluctant to require in-person visits.

The study sample size was based on detecting a mean difference in change of 0.44 BMI units between the two groups (Meltzer & Chen, 2009) with an alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, assuming a standard deviation of change in BMI of 1.9 units (Albrecht et al., 2013; Watson et al., 2016) and 60% retention rate at T5. A consort table (Supplementary Fig. 2) shows the number of participants who were screened for eligibility, enrolled at baseline, and completed measures at each time point. If participants could not be reached at any follow-up time point, the study team attempted to reach them again in subsequent time points, unless the participant withdrew from the study or was deceased. Participants received an incentive for completing each study activity, which totaled $70 to $100 per time point.

Measures

Hourly wage

At each annual visit, participants were asked to provide a paystub of their hourly wage or other documentation from their employer. Self-reported hourly wage was used when verification was not provided. If a participant was not currently employed at the time of data collection, they were marked as not employed. Aligned with baseline screening processes, at any time point, if a participant had been employed in the last six months, they could provide a paystub or self-reported hourly wage reflecting their most recent employment in the last six months. Mean hourly wage was calculated in two ways: (1) using current or most recent hourly wage in the last six months, and (2) using zero-wage imputation by setting current hourly wage to $0 if not currently employed at the time of data collection. All wages are adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Price Index for all items (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024a), and reported in 2022 dollars.

BMI

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms/(height in meters)2. At in-person appointments, trained research staff recorded participant height and weight in duplicate on a portable digital scale (Seca model) and portable Schorr stadiometer (Schorr Production, Olney, MD) in person. When remote appointments were required due to the COVID-19 pandemic (T3-T5), remote weight measures were collected via scales (American Weight Scales OM-200 or Etekcity EB4473C) mailed to participants and reported to data collectors. For remote measures, the most recent height data for each participant was used to calculate BMI, as all participants had at least one in-person baseline measurement.

Diet-related intermediary outcomes

To measure food purchases, at T1 and T5 participants were trained to submit all household food purchase receipts (groceries, restaurants, carry out, and other food vendors) for two weeks using standardized protocols (Valluri et al., 2020). Two variables were derived by summing receipt values for each participant: (1) the proportion of total food spending in dollars on foods classified as fruits and vegetables, and (2) the proportion of total food spending in dollars on foods classified as high in added sugars (sugar sweetened beverages, sweet baked goods, and candy). Spending was adjusted for inflation (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024a) and reported in 2022 dollars.

Food insecurity was measured by the 6-item Household Food Security Survey Module (United States Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service, 2021) with a 12-month reference period. For the analysis, food insecurity was coded as binary variable (yes/no) where households that report having low or very low food security (i.e., they respond affirmative to more than 1 question on the 6-item module) were categorized with the positive response (yes). For SNAP and WIC, participation in the last month (30 days) was measured as yes, no, or unsure, with unsure responses coded as no.

Monthly federal food assistance benefit amounts from SNAP in the last 30 days were measured in 9 ordinal categories and self-reported on the participant survey. For the highest reported amount category (more than $750), we substituted that year's maximum SNAP benefit amount for the number of individuals that were reported to live in the household (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024). For the lowest amount category ($1-$25), we substituted that year's minimum SNAP amount (e.g., $15 at T1). For all other categories, the middle-range value of the category was assigned (e.g., for a self-reported benefit receipt in the $26-$50 category, a value of $38 was assigned). All values were then adjusted for inflation (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024a) and reported in 2022 dollars.

Other intermediary outcomes

Level of stress was calculated by summing the responses of a 4-item Perceived Stress Scale with a 30-day reference period (Cohen et al., 1983) and a possible score range of 0 to 16. Hours of sleep were calculated as a continuous measure by the difference in hours between participants' responses to their usual time they go to bed and the usual time they wake up with a 7-day reference period; values of 15 h or more were considered implausible and excluded from the analysis (n = 5 observations). Physical activity was measured as a continuous measure using used the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise scale (Pereira et al., 1997). The formula for calculating the physical activity score was based on the sum of participant's self-reported frequency of mild, moderate, or strenuous/vigorous exercise for 15 min or more during a typical 7-day period. Physical activity scores in the 95th percentile and above (n = 168 observations, n = 19 participants) were excluded to remove likely implausible scores that could act as influential points in the analysis. Healthcare utilization was measured by questions from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2013), coded yes if participants responded that in the last 12 months they “always had access to healthcare if they needed care right away” or if they reported they “had no illness that needed care right away.”

Covariates

Time-invariant covariates measured at baseline were age (in years), gender (male; female; non-binary), race/ethnicity (Hispanic; Non-Hispanic White; Non-Hispanic Black; Non-Hispanic Asian; Non-Hispanic Other), and education (Less than high school; High school completed; Some college; Associate/Technical degree; Bachelor's degree or higher). Time-variant covariates measured at each time point were being pregnant (yes/no), household size (a count variable), employment sector (Food preparation & serving related; Office and Administrative Support; Transportation and Material Moving; Building and Grounds Cleaning & Maintenance; Sales & Related Occupations; Healthcare Support; Protective Service; Other), month of participation (January to December), and having more than one job (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for key sociodemographic variables reported by site for the baseline sample. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported for continuous variables, and the number and frequency of each category for categorical variables. We compared the sample characteristics at each time point (i.e., T2, T3, T4, T5) to the baseline sample (T1), and to subsequent time points (i.e., T2 to T3, T4, and T5; T3 to T4 and T5; T4 to T5) to look for differences in demographic factors (age, gender, race/ethnicity, education) in each study site. Chi-square, Fisher exact, or independent t-tests were used to test whether samples differed by these characteristics between time points.

In all models, the analytic sample includes those with non-missing outcome data (Supplementary Table 1). A DiD analysis was used to detect statistically significant changes in key measures among Minneapolis participants compared with Raleigh participants during the period from T1 (2018) to T5 (2022). DiD models were run for exposure variables (two measures of mean hourly wage and non-employment), our primary outcome variable (BMI), diet-related intermediary outcomes (food insecurity status, SNAP and WIC participation, monthly benefits from SNAP), and in other psychosocial and behavioral intermediary outcomes (stress, sleep, physical activity, and healthcare utilization).

For all outcomes, we used a linear mixed-effects regression model of the form: Yi,c,t = λc + μt + δDc,t +βXi + ζi+ ϵi,c,t where Yi,c,t is the outcome of interest, λc is the city effect (Minneapolis vs. Raleigh), μt is the time effect in years, Dc,t is the city-by-year interaction, δ is the interaction coefficient, βXi captures the effects of adjustment covariates, ζi is the random effect for participants, and ϵi,c,t is the error term. The random effect for participants was included to account for correlation between repeated measurements of participants and captures time invariant characteristics of participants. Model adjustments included baseline demographic factors (age, gender, race/ethnicity, education), and time-varying factors (number of jobs worked, employment sector, pregnancy status, household size, and month of participation).

We first tested for overall changes in each measure within cities with a joint hypothesis test from T1 to T5. The joint hypothesis test identified statistically significant changes in each measure, even if changes were non-linear. Next, we tested for statistically significant DiD effects between cities. We tested DiD at consecutive time points (T1 and T2, T2 and T3, T3 and T4, T4 and T5), and then conducted a joint hypothesis DiD test from T1 to T5. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Changes within cities

Results of within-city changes in average hourly wage and all outcomes from 2018 to 2022 are presented in Table 2 for Minneapolis and Table 3 for Raleigh. Hourly wage distribution graphs by year and city are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. In Minneapolis, mean hourly wage for current or most recent wage increased from $12.20 in 2018 to $17.47 in 2022; mean hourly wage increased from $9.53 to $16.46 when those without current employment were set to $0. This was a statistically significant increase in adjusted models (p < 0.001). Trends in employment were not linear, with non-employment increasing from 22.9% in 2018 to a high of 46.4% in 2020 before subsequently declining (p < 0.001). Food insecurity decreased from 74.7% in 2018 to 54.2% in 2021, before increasing in 2022; this was overall a statistically significant change (p < 0.001). Changes in SNAP participation changes were statistically significant (p = 0.044), with 61.8% participating in 2018, which rose to 66.4% in 2021 before declining in 2022. Mean SNAP benefit amounts increased overall (p < 0.001), rising from $142.34 in 2018 to $243.44 in 2022. Statistically significant but non-linear changes were observed in stress scores (p = 0.001), and physical activity (p < 0.001). In adjusted models, there were no overall statistically significant changes within Minneapolis in BMI, the proportion of spending on fruits and vegetables or foods high in added sugar, WIC participation, sleep hours, or healthcare utilization. In Raleigh, mean hourly wage for current or most recent wage increased from $10.90 in 2018 to $16.00 in 2022; mean hourly wage increased from $10.16 to $14.88 when those without current employment were set to $0. Using both measures, this was a statistically significant overall increase (p < 0.001). Trends in employment were not linear, with non-employment increasing from 6.7% in 2018 to a high of 21.4% in 2020 before declining (p < 0.001). Food insecurity decreased from 75% in 2018 to 48.2% in 2022. SNAP participation increased from 42.9% in 2018 to 46.5% in 2022. SNAP benefits amounts averaged $111.96 in 2018 and $277.76 in 2022. Food insecurity reached its lowest point in 2021 (43.6%) when both SNAP participation (57.1%) and SNAP benefit amounts ($288.32) were at their highest. Overall changes in food insecurity, SNAP participation, and SNAP benefit amount were all statistically significant (p < 0.001). Statistically significant but non-linear changes were also observed in stress scores (p < 0.0001), sleep hours (p = 0.018), and healthcare utilization (p = 0.02). There were no statistically significant changes in BMI, the proportion of spending on fruits and vegetables or foods high in added sugar, WIC participation, or physical activity.

Discussion

In this natural experiment of minimum wage effects among low-wage workers in two U.S. cities from 2018 to 2022, results did not provide evidence of an effect of minimum wage policy on study nutrition-related outcomes. While mean hourly wages increased in both cities, there was no differential wage increase in Minneapolis. In this sense, the ordinance did not offer an income shock distinct from broader labor market forces. Without a measurable minimum wage policy effect, downstream changes in health outcomes would not be expected. Indeed, there were no overall changes in BMI or food purchases between or within cities. There was a decrease in food insecurity and an increase in SNAP participation and benefit amounts within both cities; these trends may have been related to unexpected social and policy changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.

The magnitude of wage increases over the years was mostly similar in both Minneapolis, where a minimum wage ordinance was incrementally implemented, and Raleigh, where there was no minimum wage increase. The average real wage increase was $5.27 (2022 USD) in Minneapolis in 2022 compared with $5.20 in Raleigh. Based on previous estimates of wage elasticity among workers affected by minimum wage (Neumark, 2023), a real-wage increase of around $5.66 would have been expected in Minneapolis only. The rise in both cities likely reflects national labor market forces, which saw a steady rise in hourly earnings during the study period (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, n.d.), rather than the Minneapolis Minimum Wage Ordinance.

When the mean hourly wage for participants without current employment was set to $0, overall wage gains were greater in Minneapolis compared with Raleigh. This measure of hourly wage captures changes in employment status, but could overestimate wage effects in years following high unemployment and underestimate them in years following low unemployment. Indeed, while participants in both cities experienced substantial loss of employment in 2020 and 2021, these losses were higher in Minneapolis, likely reflecting both the pandemic and the area effects of the civil uprising in Minneapolis in the summer of 2020, which resulted in both short and long-term business closures (Caspi et al., 2023). Notably, by 2022, non-employment rates in Minneapolis were similar to baseline levels. It could be that the minimum wage ordinance contributed to a particularly strong rebound in hourly wages between 2021 and 2022 in Minneapolis compared with Raleigh, if workers earned higher wages upon re-entering the job market.

Changes in food security and SNAP benefits observed at both sites could be attributable to a robust COVID-19 pandemic policy response that rapidly expanded SNAP eligibility and increased benefits by $154 per month for the average household (Schanzenbach, 2023). Expansions that states could opt-in to were rolled out (and receded) in roughly the same way across the two study sites (Killion et al., 2022; US Department of Agriculture, Food and Nutrition Service, 2021). Qualitative interviews conducted with a subset of WAGE$ participants in 2019 (Caspi et al., 2022) suggest that the greater magnitude of change in SNAP outcomes in Raleigh may reflect state-level participation barriers that were present in North Carolina prior to the pandemic, such as stricter work requirements (North Carolina General Assembly, 2016), that were relaxed during the pandemic. Other economic supports, including emergency rental assistance programs and the expanded Child Tax Credit were in place when food insecurity reached its lowest point in 2021 and were in various stages of de-implementation by 2022 when food insecurity began to rise again. Trends in the study sample align in expected ways with changes in these broader policies. Notably, analyses restricted to the earliest phase of implementation (2018–2019) did not show that wage changes were associated with food insecurity and diet-related outcomes, suggesting that the null policy effect is not solely attributable to COVID-19-era economic disruptions in factors like food security and food assistance (Chapman et al., 2024, Caspi et al., 2023).

Regardless of the explanation for improvements in wages, food insecurity, and SNAP benefits across both cities, we did not observe measurable changes in food purchases or BMI in the current study. In the years since the study was initiated, new studies have emerged that present conflicting evidence about the impact of minimum wage on health (Neumark, 2023), including diet quality and obesity (Andreyeva & Ukert, 2018; Buszkiewicz et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2020; Narain & Zimmerman, 2019); this evidence has been based on data collected prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Mixed findings for behavioral outcomes might be explained by the various ways that higher income is ultimately spent, and whether such changes are health-promoting. Added income could, for example, be spent on food that may not promote health, such as purchases from restaurants or other food away-from-home. Conflicting findings could also be attributable to differences in study populations, as there is abundant evidence that minimum wage effects are heterogeneous (Brown-Podgorski et al., 2023; Buszkiewicz et al., 2020, 2023; Narain & Zimmerman, 2019; Sigaud et al., 2022). For example, Buszkiewicz et al. (2020) found some less favorable weight-related outcomes associated with minimum wage among those who were male and non-White or Hispanic (Buszkiewicz et al., 2020). Narain et al. (2019) also found that minimum wage increases were associated with improvements in diet quality among whites, but not among Latino and Black men (Narain & Zimmerman, 2019). The current study, while not powered to test subgroup differences, was comprised of overwhelmingly non-Hispanic Black participants, who may experience diminished returns on supportive policies due to mechanisms of broader structural racism (Brown-Podgorski et al., 2023).

Precarious employment could also contribute to the lack of positive findings in the current study (Oddo et al., 2021; Piekarz-Porter et al., 2024). Participants in the study were not stably employed (Caspi et al., 2023), and precarious employment introduces other risks and requires employment protections that go beyond minimum wage increases. Such employment may include contract-based or gig work in the informal labor market, which is typically excluded from minimum wage policies. These less formal labor markets expanded during the study time period (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024b), and workers in these markets may have experienced attenuated wage gains in the current study. Protections from precarious employment include secure scheduling, paid leave, insurance benefits, collective bargaining, and others. In one difference-in-difference analysis, while minimum wage was not associated with health outcomes, other work-related policies were, like the Earned Income Tax Credit (Spencer et al., 2020). This suggests the need to explore other work-related policies beyond or in conjunction with minimum wage increases.

Small wage gains unrelated to the minimum wage policy were observed across cities, but these did not translate into improved living conditions, or may have been offset by increased spending. For instance, increases in income may have been spent on paying off debts rather than contributing to disposable income. Though we adjusted for inflation for the Consumer Price Index for all goods, rising food prices during this period may have exceeded wage gains. The effect of higher doses of income increase on nutrition-related outcomes, such as living wage initiatives (Mulcahy & Trautner, 2022), are unknown.

Still, the benefits of even high-dose income interventions for health are not conclusive (Liebman et al., 2022; Noble et al., 2025). A recent randomized controlled study of a cash transfer intervention in which participants were provided $1000 for 36 months did not result in improvements in body mass index; improvements in stress and food security were noted, but were short-lived (Miller et al., 2024). The study noted that health-focused interventions, rather than cash transfers, were likely to be better for targeting health disparities and improving health among those facing poverty. Even so, studies like the one conducted by Miller et al. conclude that cash transfer interventions may be effective at reducing poverty or improving financial security, even if they do not result in measurable improvements in health or behavioral outcomes.

Overall, findings from this study suggest that minimum wage policies alone may not be sufficient to yield measurable improvements in nutrition-related health outcomes in low-income populations. For policymakers seeking to improve health outcomes among low-income populations in the U.S., this study indicates that minimum wage increases likely have a limited impact on diet-related health outcomes when implemented in isolation and under strong labor market conditions. The study also highlights the importance of safety net programs like SNAP to address food security in times of crisis. Taken together with other previous studies, minimum wage policies may still play a role as part of a broader set of strategies to address economic stability among low-wage workers, along with anti-poverty programs, worker protection policies, and a robust safety net system.

Strengths and limitations

This prospective cohort study of low-wage workers in two U.S. cities overcomes limitations of a number of prior studies by offering a well-defined treatment and control group, panel data, and avoiding self-reported weight outcomes (Leigh et al., 2019; Neumark, 2023). At the same time, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced unexpected period changes that were likely to affect measured study outcomes at both sites. The pandemic also required flexibility in the study procedures, including a switch to remote data collection. Given the study design and the nature of the disruptions, these pandemic effects cannot be precisely modeled, but instead must serve as the context in which to interpret results. The study was relatively small, conducted in only two U.S. cities and not powered to detected heterogeneous effects in participant subgroups. While the mixed model approach uses all available data, bias related to missing data cannot be ruled out. However, sample demographics were consistent across time points, and an analysis of the early-impact results of the study using a balanced sample produced similar findings (Caspi et al., 2023). Baseline differences in the city sample characteristics in Minneapolis and Raleigh were also present and suggest the potential for residual confounding by participant characteristics. However, a previous analysis found that baseline differences in BMI between Black women in the two cities could be explained by demographic differences that were controlled for in the current study (Durfee et al., 2021).

Conclusion

The findings from this difference-in-difference study examining the effects of a minimum wage policy on diet and nutrition-related outcomes from 2018 to 2022 do not provide evidence of beneficial health effects. We did not observe a differential wage increase in Minneapolis relative to Raleigh, and in the absence of a measurable policy-related wage increase, downstream changes in nutrition-related health outcomes would not be expected.

At the same time, the study provides some support for the important role of federal food assistance programs in reducing food insecurity during periods of expanded eligibility and increased benefit levels. Our findings suggest that changes to federal safety net programs may have shaped observed outcomes, and point to the role that these programs can play in times of economic uncertainty.

Overall, results contribute to a growing body of research testing whether policies and interventions targeting income result in improved health outcomes. While such policies may improve economic conditions, they may not consistently translate into measurable improvements in health outcomes. As a next step for research, studies that pair high-dose income changes with other supportive work policies and targeted health supports are warranted.
