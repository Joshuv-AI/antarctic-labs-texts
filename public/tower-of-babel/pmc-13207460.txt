Multi-scale spatial-temporal remote sensing fusion for phenology identification in rice germplasm resources
Authors: Huimin Wang, Wei Guo, Yue Mu, Yang Zhang, Haozhou Wang, Yandong Yang, Hu Xu, Yanfeng Ding, Shirong Zhou, Ganghua Li, Seishi Ninomiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crop phenology is a critical determinant for yield prediction and germplasm evaluation. However, precise phenological monitoring in large-scale rice breeding trials faces significant challenges due to the inherent phenological asynchrony among hundreds of cultivars and the trade-off between spatial resolution and temporal continuity in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) remote sensing. To address these issues, this study proposes a multi-scale temporal deep learning framework that integrates high-frequency medium-resolution (MR) images as temporal anchor with sparse high-resolution (HR) images as spatial enhancement. We introduce a Missing Aware Gated Fusion (MAGF) mechanism to dynamically integrate multi-resolution features on non-aligned timelines, enabling robust modeling under irregular sampling conditions. Validated on a massive dataset covering approximately 500 rice cultivars and over 100,000 images across 2023 and 2024 growing seasons, the proposed method significantly outperformed single-temporal-scale baselines despite multiple growth stages coexisting within the same dates. The integration of multi-spatial-scale fusion with LSTM temporal modeling yielded superior performance considering efficiency, achieving an Overall Accuracy (OA) and F1-score of 0.873, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.84. A hybrid sampling strategy (daily MR image combined with weekly HR image) demonstrates that weekly flight time can be reduced from 28 h to approximately 6 h while maintaining high accuracy. Notably, even when HR acquisition was reduced to a once every 14 days frequency, the fusion performance remained significantly superior to that of daily MR monitoring alone. The model exhibited strong generalization capabilities. When directly applying the model trained on 2024 data to the 2023 dataset, it maintained an OA of 0.774 and an F1-score of 0.738 under a 3-day error tolerance, with recall for the maturity stage consistently exceeding 0.96. This framework offers a flexible, scalable, and cost-effective solution for high-throughput phenotyping in precision breeding.

Introduction

Crop phenology is a fundamental biological attribute of plants, representing the integrated result of physiological development throughout the growth cycle and reflecting the combined effects of genetic background, environmental conditions, and management practices [1,2]. It is not only a key component of crop growth models for yield prediction [3,4] but also an essential parameter for crop classification and mapping [5]. In major food crops such as rice (Oryza sativa L.), key phenological stages, including tillering, jointing, heading, flowering, and maturity, not only influence yield formation and define optimal management windows but also serve as important phenotypic traits for germplasm evaluation and variety improvement [6,7]. Accurate identification of these phenological stages provides decision support for agricultural management practices such as fertilization, irrigation, and pest control [8], making it a fundamental issue for precise rice management and regional breeding.

Particularly in rice germplasm trials, hundreds of cultivars with diverse genetic backgrounds frequently exhibit significant phenological asynchrony [9], rendering traditional single-temporal observations insufficient to comprehensively characterize phenological diversity at the population level. In large-scale field germplasm trials, phenological surveys have long relied on manual field observations. Although intuitive, manual surveys are labor-intensive, prone to high cumulative subjective errors, and often lack the temporal continuity required to capture rapid phenological transitions when dealing with numerous cultivars [[10], [11], [12]]. On the other hand, crop growth models—such as ORYZA2000, CERES-Rice, APSIM, and WOFOST—simulate developmental processes primarily based on meteorological drivers [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, these models typically require accurate sowing dates and variety-specific parameter calibration, which is impractical for trials involving high genetic diversity. Furthermore, they generally assume canopy homogeneity, failing to capture the spatial heterogeneity of growth dynamics under actual field conditions [18,19]. In rice germplasm trials with extreme genetic diversity, calibrating models individually for each variety is virtually unfeasible in practice, and limited parameter sets struggle to adequately characterize phenological differences at the population scale.

Driven by the evolution of remote sensing and high-throughput phenotyping, the integration of proximal, satellite, and aerial platforms has revolutionized the monitoring of crop phenology. In particular, UAV remote sensing is establishing itself as a cornerstone technology for growth stage characterization at the breeding scale, characterized by its unparalleled flexibility and spatial resolution [20,21]. UAVs offer centimeter-level spatial resolution and flexible revisit schedules, enabling the capture of fine canopy structures and color dynamics [22]. By combining UAV multispectral or RGB data with machine learning techniques, recent studies have achieved significant progress at the field plot scale. For instance, multi-temporal index sequences combined with Random Forest algorithms have been successfully employed to estimate specific heading dates or perform stage discrimination [23,24]. However, most conventional methods still rely on image features—such as vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI, NDRE) and texture/color metrics—combined with empirical rules or shallow models. Consequently, these approaches are sensitive to feature selection, threshold settings, sampling frequency, and missing data, leading to insufficient robustness in complex backgrounds or across different growing seasons.

Phenology is intrinsically a continuous developmental process that evolves over time [25], and where the identification of key stages depends heavily on the shifting patterns before and after critical transitions. Consequently, single-date images often fails to reliably distinguish between adjacent growth stages, let alone robustly pinpoint transition points—such as heading or flowering—under conditions of non-synchronous cultivar development [26]. To characterize these temporal dynamics, early studies predominantly employed physiological time models based on Growing Degree Days (GDD) or constructed time series of vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI, EVI, NDRE). These approaches typically infer phenological stages through thresholding, inflection point detection, or curve fitting [27,28]. However, their performance remains highly sensitive to index selection, fitting strategies, sampling frequencies, and data gap patterns. In scenarios involving complex backgrounds or multi-modal curves, stage boundaries are prone to shifting, which limits their transferability across different cultivars and years.

Recently, deep learning has introduced a new paradigm for spatio-temporal feature mining. In particular, the integration of convolutional neural networks (CNN) and recurrent neural networks (RNN) has proven effective for end-to-end sequential modeling: the front-end CNN extracts spatial features from canopy textures and organs [29], while the back-end long short-term memory (LSTM) captures the temporal evolution patterns and long-term and short-term dependencies in these features [30,31]. For example, PhenoNet, a two-stage lightweight deep learning framework, achieved real-time classification of winter wheat phenological stages using near-ground RGB time series by sequentially fusing spatial and temporal features, reporting an overall classification accuracy exceeding 93% [32]. These end-to-end deep temporal models effectively capture stage-specific features and their evolution [20,25,33].

Despite the success of deep temporal models, a fundamental trade-off persists between spatial resolution and temporal continuity in UAV-based phenotyping [34,35]. Low-altitude aerial photography provides the HR images essential for discerning subtle inter-cultivar variations; however, it is inherently constrained by operational efficiency and cost, resulting in observations that are temporally sparse and irregularly sampled [36]. Conversely, increasing flight altitude enables cost-effective, high-frequency coverage to construct dense time of MR images, albeit at the expense of fine-grained morphological details. From a modeling perspective, these two scales exhibit significant complementarity: MR images serves as a stable “temporal backbone” for capturing continuous phenological trajectories, while sparse HR images provides irreplaceable information for resolving microscopic phenotypic differences during critical developmental transitions [37]. To leverage this complementarity, various multi-scale fusion strategies have been developed, primarily focusing on spatial feature fusion (e.g., FPN), multi-stream architectures, and adaptive mechanisms such as cross-attention. Nevertheless, research dedicated to multi-scale temporal fusion for phenology identification remains scarce. Although existing studies have explored spatial-scale integration for classification or trait estimation, they have yet to fully address the synergistic interplay between high-frequency MR images and low-frequency HR image [38,39]. Most established methods assume that multi-source data are spatio-temporally aligned (i.e., paired data), thereby failing to mitigate the temporal discontinuity induced by high-resolution sparsity or to capture the nuanced phenological diversity of germplasm through principled temporal modeling. Consequently, under practical operational constraints, there is a pressing need for a robust and scalable modeling framework that prioritizes temporal continuity while preserving fine-scale spatial discriminative power, thereby accommodating the pronounced heterogeneity and irregular sampling inherent in large-scale rice germplasm trials.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this study proposes a multi-scale time-series deep learning framework designed for high-throughput phenotyping in large scale rice breeding trials is proposed. Materials and methods

Study site and experimental design

The experiments were conducted in 2023 and 2024 at the Baima Teaching and Research Base of Nanjing Agricultural University (Fig. S1), located in Lishui District, Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China (31°37′25″N, 119°9′15″E). A total of 520 rice germplasm resources, including 340 japonica and 180 indica cultivars from China, India, Thailand, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, and Japan, were cultivated under the same environmental conditions.

In 2023, three blocks were arranged for both japonica and indica cultivars, providing three replicate plots for each cultivar. In 2024, the design was optimized: the majority of cultivars were planted with two replicates, while selected representative cultivars were maintained at three replicates to ensure reference stability. Plot configurations varied by cultivar type and year. For japonica, the plot size was 1.20 m × 1.20 m with 0.5 m spacing between plots, five rows per plot with 0.3 m row spacing, eight plants per row, and 0.15 m plant spacing. For indica cultivars, the plot size in 2023 was 1.20 m × 1.26 m with 0.5 m spacing between plots, five rows per plot with 0.3 m row spacing, nine plants per row, and 0.18 m plant spacing. In 2024, the spacing between plots was increased to 0.8 m to mitigate lodging observed in the previous season. Field operations and crop management followed seasonal schedule. In 2023, rice was sown on May 24, transplanted on June 23, and harvested from October 16 to November 17. In 2024, sowing was carried out on May 22, transplanting on June 23, and harvest from October 31 to November 18. Fertilization management was uniform across years with an N: P: K ratio of 1:0.2:0.4. Each plot was managed uniformly following local standard agronomic practices to ensure that observed differences were attributable to genetic background and experimental layout rather than field management.

Data description and preprocessing

UAV image acquisition

Time series UAV images were acquired using a DJI Mavic 3M (DJI-Innovations, Shenzhen, China). The UAV was equipped with an RTK module for centimeter-level accuracy and a 20 MP RGB camera (5280 × 3956 pixels). In this study, only RGB images were used for rice phenology identification. UAV surveys were conducted during the 2023 and 2024 growing seasons using a multi-altitude flight strategy. In both years, flights at 20 m altitude were performed for orthophoto acquisition, yielding a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 0.93 cm/pixel, with a flight speed of 1 m/s, 80% forward overlap, and 70% side overlap. In terms of operational efficiency, completing a full-field UAV survey at 20 m altitude took approximately 20 min, whereas a survey at 4 m altitude required about 4 h when approximately 1000 plots were imaged individually. In 2023, the field layout comprised 1560 plots in total. Under this configuration, acquiring high resolution imagery at 4 m would have markedly prolonged the total survey duration and increased the time gap between the first and last plots captured within the same survey. Such a prolonged acquisition window could introduce additional variability from changing illumination conditions and other short term environmental fluctuations. Therefore, low altitude images in 2023 were acquired at 6 m, allowing a single image to simultaneously cover four adjacent plots. This strategy improved acquisition efficiency, reduced within survey inconsistency, and yielded a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 0.26 cm/pixel. In 2024, the field layout was reduced to 1040 plots in total, as replication was two, which made individual plot imaging at 4 m operationally feasible, and provided a finer GSD of 0.17 cm/pixel. Regarding temporal sampling, images at 20 m altitude were collected 2–3 times per week before August 22 and almost daily thereafter, excluding rainy days. Low altitude flights were conducted daily during the grain filling stage and 2–3 times per week from late grain filling to maturity. All UAV flights were performed under clear or slightly cloudy conditions between 10:00 and 14:00 to minimize variations in illumination.

To ensure spatial consistency and reduce geometric distortion, nine ground control points (GCPs) were evenly distributed across the study area and surveyed using high-precision GNSS equipment. Image preprocessing was conducted using Agisoft Metashape (v2.0.3), including georeferencing, radiometric calibration, dense point cloud reconstruction, digital elevation model (DEM) generation, and orthomosaic production. All data were referenced to the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS 1984) coordinate system.

Data preprocessing and annotation

This study developed introduce a UAV-based phenology dataset comprising HR RGB images acquires at low flight altitiudes). The raw images were annotated into five distinct phenological stages according to the Biologische Bundesanstalt, Bundessortenamt and Chemische Industrie (BBCH) scale [40]. All annotations were conducted by domain experts with extensive agronomic experience, ensuring label accuracy and biological consistency. The annotation process was supported by a lightweight custom annotation tool that enabled efficient browsing of time-series images. Phenological stages were assigned by jointly considering temporally adjacent images, allowing annotators to exploit the continuity of crop development and reduce ambiguity during stage transitions.

The five phenological stages were defined as Jointing–Booting Stage (JBS), Heading–Flowering Stage (HFS), Milky Stage (MkS), Dough Stage (DS), and Maturity Stage (MS). JBS represents the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth and is characterized by rapid internode elongation, panicle differentiation, increased plant height, and visible stem swelling. Due to the subtle morphological differences and limited distinguishability of these phases in UAV images, the jointing and booting stages were merged into a single class. HFS corresponds to panicle emergence and flowering, during which heading and flowering typically occur almost simultaneously (within half a day to one day) and persist for approximately one week. Given their strong temporal overlap and similar canopy-level phenotypes, these two phases were combined. MkS is characterized by grains filled with milky white endosperm and high moisture content, accompanied by the onset of leaf yellowing and active grain filling. During DS, grain moisture gradually decreases, the endosperm transitions from milky white to yellowish, and plant senescence becomes pronounced. At MS, grains are fully mature, hardened, and dry, while plants are largely withered, making this stage suitable for mechanical harvesting. To further ensure annotation reliability, a subset of the labeled dataset was cross-validated against manually recorded phenological observations available for 60 rice cultivars (Fig. S4).

As shown in Fig. 1(a and b), phenological time distributions indicate that inter-annual variability mainly manifests as shifts in absolute timing rather than disruptions in stage sequences. Despite this temporal offset, phenological stages remain reasonably separated in their overall temporal distributions, with noticeable overlaps between consecutive stages, indicating that different cultivars may occupy different phenological stages at the same time point. The high level of agreement confirms the reliability of the annotations and provides robust ground truth for rice phenological recognition tasks. Specifically, the final dataset covers 498 cultivars (340 japonica and 158 indica) with 1485 plots in 2023, and 499 cultivars (338 japonica and 161 indica) with 1108 plots in 2024. Although the genetic diversity remained consistent, the variation in plot numbers reflects our rigorous quality control standards. Multi-scale time-series deep learning framework

This study proposes a novel crop phenology classification framework that integrates multi-resolution UAV images (MR images and HR images) with time-series deep learning (Fig. 2). The overall workflow includes data alignment, multi-scale feature extraction, temporal modeling, and phenological stage prediction. The multi-scale time-series framework consists of four key modules:(1) Time-series construction: MR images are used as the temporal anchor to build synchronized multi-resolution sequences. A missing-data mask is introduced to explicitly indicate the absence of HR images while maintaining temporal alignment. (2) Feature extraction: A lightweight CNN backbone [42] is employed to extract spatial features independently from the MR and HR images. The MR features emphasize temporal continuity, while the HR features capture fine-grained texture and structural information. (3) Feature fusion and Missing-Aware Gated Fusion (MAGF): The extracted features are linearly projected into a shared embedding space and combined using a Missing-Aware gating mechanism. This ensures adaptive fusion across modalities and mitigates the impact of missing HR data. (4) Temporal modeling: LSTM networks are applied to capture temporal dependencies across consecutive time steps, modeling the phenological dynamics. A classification head outputs the final crop phenology stage.Fig. 2The overall flowchart of multi-scale spatial-temporal deep learning framework. The proposed design has two major advantages: (i) the MR images provide a stable and long-term temporal anchor, ensuring sequence continuity; and (ii) the HR images, when available, enriches the representation with detailed spatial information. Even in the absence of high-resolution data, the framework maintains robustness through its multi-scale design.

Multi-resolution temporal input construction

Let t∈{1,2,…,T} denote the discrete time indices of a phenological observation sequence of fixed length T. At each time step t, the input comprises a widely available MR images, denoted as X(MR,t), and an intermittently available high-resolution image, denoted as X(HR,t).

To maintain temporal alignment without discarding data, we employ a zero-padding strategy [43,44] for missing HR observations:(1)X(HR,t)={HRimage,ifavailable0,otherwiseConcurrently, to prevent the network from interpreting missing data as valid features, a binary availability mask mt is generated:(2)mt={1,ifHRimageisavailable0,otherwise

This mask explicitly encodes the temporal sparsity pattern and acts as a critical control signal, allowing the subsequent layers to mathematically distinguish between invalid zero-padding and actual black image pixels.

Feature extraction module

To capture complementary phenological cues, X(MR,t) and X(HR,t) are processed by two parallel feature extraction branches. We employ MobileNetV3 as the backbone architecture due to its proven efficiency in processing high-throughput phenotypic data [42]. Although the architectures are identical, the networks do not share weights, allowing them to specialize in extracting resolution-specific patterns (e.g., color texture from MR image vs. leaf and panicle morphology from HR image).

Following the convolutional stages, Global Average Pooling (GAP) is applied to generate compact feature vectors:(3)G(MR,t)=GAP(MobileNetV3(X(MR,t)))(4)G(HR,t)=GAP(MobileNetV3(X(HR,t)))

To align these representations in a common latent space and reduce dimensionality, linear projection layers are applied:(5)Z(MR,t)=WMRG(MR,t)(6)Z(HR,t)=WHRG(HR,t)where Z(MR,t),Z(HR,t)∈Rd represent the projected embeddings in a d-dimensional real vector space. Two critical design aspects are noteworthy here: (1) unified dimension: By projecting disparate backbone outputs to a fixed dimension d, we ensure dimensional compatibility, which is a prerequisite for the element-wise operations in the fusion module. (2) shared weights across time steps: The weight matrices WMR and WHR are shared across all time steps t. This time-distributed design ensures consistent feature extraction throughout the growing season and prevents parameter explosion.

Multimodal feature fusion: missing-aware Gated Fusion (MAGF)

To effectively synthesize the heterogeneous features while addressing the issue of missing high-resolution data, we propose a Missing-aware Gated Fusion (MAGF) mechanism. This module adopts a residual learning paradigm, treating the continuous MR features as the “anchor” and the HR features as a conditional refinement. Unlike standard masked fusion, which simply uses HR features when available and ignores them when missing, MAGF incorporates missing awareness and asymmetric fusion to adaptively regulate the contribution of HR features according to the current MR context, HR feature representation, and modality availability, while preserving MR features as a stable temporal backbone. Detailed comparisons with several conventional gating-based fusion baselines are presented in Appendix Table S1, where MAGF consistently outperforms the alternative fusion strategies.

First, to determine the relevance of the HR features at the current time step, the feature vectors from both branches are concatenated to form a joint context representation:(7)Z(cat,t)=Concat(Z(MR,t),Z(HR,t))

This joint vector is passed through a learnable gating network to generate an adaptive attention score αt. The gating network consists of a weight matrix Wg that projects the high-dimensional context into a scalar importance metric, and a bias term bg that adjusts the activation threshold:(8)αt=σ(WgZ(cat,t)+bg)Where σ(·) denotes the Sigmoid activation function that normalizes the score to αt∈(0,1).

Crucially, to prevent noise from zero-padded missing observations from contaminating the feature space, the binary availability mask mt is explicitly introduced. The fusion process is formulated as a mask-gated residual addition:(9)Z(fuse,t)=Z(MR,t)+(·αt)⊙Z(HR,t)where ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. In this formulation, when valid HR data is available (=1), the HR features are adaptively weighted by αt and added to the MR baseline to enhance spatial discriminability. Conversely, when HR data is missing (=0), the fusion term vanishes entirely, forcing the model to rely solely on the robust MR anchor.

LSTM-based temporal modeling and classification

The sequence of fused embeddings {Z(fuse,1),Z(fuse,2),…,Z(fuse,T)} encapsulates the spatiotemporal characteristics of the crop. To decode the underlying growth dynamics, this sequence is fed into a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network [45]. The LSTM is well-suited for phenological monitoring as its gated memory mechanism—comprising input, forget, and output gates—effectively manages long-term dependencies. This allows the model to accumulate cumulative growth information while suppressing short-term fluctuations caused by environmental variability. The recurrent update at time step t is formalized as:(10)(ht,ct)=LSTM(Z(fuse,t),ht−1,ct−1)where ht represents the hidden state encoding the current phenological status, and ct serves as the cell state preserving long-term context.

The final hidden state hT, which aggregates the comprehensive phenological trajectory of the crop throughout the monitoring period, is used for classification. It is passed through a fully connected layer and normalized by a softmax function to produce the predicted probability distribution over phenological stages:(11)yˆ=softmax(WchT+bc)where Wc denotes the classifier weight matrix, and bc denotes the classifier bias vector. Note that bc is distinct from the gating bias bg and is used to shift the decision boundary for optimal class separation.

Rationale for different temporal intervals

In the multi-temporal modeling framework, the MR image sequence was treated as the temporal anchor, while the HR images was aligned to it via nearest-neighbor matching. To regulate the temporal sparsity of the HR data, we introduced a temporal interval parameter Δ, and conducted experiments with Δ∈{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,10,14}. Specifically, Δ defines the data frequency: Δ=1 corresponds to a daily series, Δ=2 indicates sampling every other day (2-day interval), and Δ=3 indicates sampling every three days.

The rationale behind this setting lies in both phenological time scales and agricultural observation practices:(a) Small intervals (Δ=1,2,3) capture fine-grained transitions over several days, such as the rapid shift from jointing to booting. (b) Medium intervals (Δ=4–7) correspond to weekly to biweekly rhythms, consistent with field inspections and short-cycle management practices. (c)Larger intervals (Δ=10,14) align with conventional satellite remote sensing monitoring frequencies, such as dekadal (10-day) and biweekly (14-day) observations, enabling the coverage of long-term developmental dynamics.

Given a fixed Time sequence length of T=5, the temporal coverage expands as (T−1)Δ days. For example, it spans 4 days when Δ=1, whereas it spans 56 days whenΔ=14. By selecting consecutive integers from 1 to 7, we ensure a monotonic refinement of temporal resolution, while adding 10 and 14 provides typical “application scales” used in remote sensing. This design establishes a balanced exploration between dense and sparse temporal inputs, and further validates the robustness of the proposed MAGF mechanism under varying temporal sparsity conditions. For the ablation experiments, all single-scale and multi-scale sequence-based models were evaluated using the same sequence length (T = 5), the same temporal interval (Δ), and thus the same temporal coverage and sampling density.

Implementation details and model evaluation

All experiments were conducted on a workstation running Ubuntu 16.08/4, equipped with Intel Xeon E5 CPUs (2.20 GHz per core, 32 GB RAM) and an NVIDIA Tesla V100-DGXS GPU (16 GB memory). The models were implemented in PyTorch 2.0 with CUDA 12.1 as the backend. The AdamW optimizer was adopted with an initial learning rate of 1×10−4 and a weight decay of 1×10−4. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler was employed to adaptively adjust the learning rate during training. The batch size was fixed at 64, and each model was trained for 100 epochs. To improve computational efficiency, automatic mixed precision (AMP) training was enabled.

The model performance on the testing dataset was comprehensively evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Cohen's Kappa coefficient. In addition, the numbers of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) were calculated as the basis for performance evaluation. The metrics were defined as follows:(12)Accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN(13)Precision=TPTP+FP(14)Recall=TPTP+FN(15)F1=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+RecallFurthermore, to account for the possibility of agreement occurring by chance, we also computed Cohen's Kappa coefficient (κ), defined as:(16)κ=po−pe1−pewhere po represents the observed agreement, and pe represents the expected agreement by chance. The Kappa coefficient ranges from −1 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating stronger agreement between predictions and ground truth.

Temporal robustness and biological consistency of the learned phenology representation

To evaluate the framework's robustness, we simulated temporal misalignment between MR and HR images collected in 2024 using offsets (delta) from 1 to 14 days. Overall, increasing temporal offsets resulted in a gradual degradation of classification performance across all evaluation metrics (Fig. S6). When the temporal offset was small (delta1–delta3), the model achieved consistently high performance, with OA and F1-score remaining above approximately 0.85. As the offset increased beyond delta4, a moderate decline in OA, precision, recall, and F1-score were observed. For larger offsets (delta7–delta14), performance metrics stabilized at a lower level, with OA and F1 converging to approximately 0.83–0.84. In contrast, the Kappa coefficient exhibited a more pronounced decline with increasing temporal offset, decreasing from approximately 0.84 at delta1 to below 0.80 at larger offsets. The model using only MR data with LSTM achieves an OA of 0.828, which is lower than the performance obtained when HR images are incorporated with Δ = 14 (Fig. S6). This indicates that, even in the presence of a 14 day temporal lag, the high-resolution spatial details provided by the HR data can still significantly enhance model performance, thereby making the fusion approach superior to the MR-only method. Stage specific error patterns also changed with temporal offset. Across all tested Δ values, most predictions remained concentrated along the diagonal, indicating generally stable recognition of the 5 phenological stages (Fig. 4). However, misclassification between adjacent stages increased as Δ became larger, especially between HFS and MkS and between MkS and DS. By contrast, JBS and MS remained relatively stable across temporal offsets, suggesting that stages with more distinct visual characteristics were less sensitive to temporal misalignment. Overall, these results indicate that temporal misalignment mainly affects phenological transition stages, while stages with clearer visual boundaries remain comparatively robust.Fig. 4Confusion matrices in the different temporal intervals (Δ) in 2024.

Discussion

Synergistic mechanism of temporal modeling and multi-scale spatial information

Crop phenology is inherently a dynamic process that evolves continuously over time, with individual phenological stages connected through gradual changes in morphological, physiological, and structural traits rather than existing as isolated states. Consequently, phenological stage classification based solely on single-date images often struggles to accurately delineate stage boundaries, particularly during transitional phases such as JBS and HFS, where visual differences are subtle and temporally progressive [46,47]. At the scale of rice breeding plots, pronounced genetic diversity and phenological asynchrony among cultivars further exacerbate the uncertainty associated with single-temporal discrimination [48]. To address these challenges, this study integrates explicit temporal modeling with multi-scale spatial information, aiming to jointly exploit temporal continuity and spatial complementarity to improve the robustness of phenological recognition under complex field conditions.

From the perspective of temporal modeling, the incorporation of an LSTM module consistently improved classification performance across multiple key phenological stages, with particularly pronounced gains observed during early and middle growth phases (Fig. 3).This finding indicates that phenological stage discrimination depends not only on instantaneous spatial phenotypic cues but also on growth trends embedded in preceding and subsequent observations [49].For transitional stages such as JBS and HFS, canopy color and structural differences within a single image are often limited; by explicitly modeling temporal dependencies, the network can integrate continuous phenotypic changes across adjacent time points, thereby forming more discriminative feature representations and effectively reducing confusion between neighboring stages [22,29].These results are consistent with findings reported for PhenoNet in wheat phenology monitoring [32], further supporting the general applicability of explicit temporal modeling in crop phenological classification.

Regarding spatial information utilization, HR UAV images offer clear advantages in capturing fine-scale phenotypic traits, such as panicle structure, leaf texture, and local canopy morphology, whereas MR images are better suited for stably characterizing overall canopy conditions at the population level. In rice breeding plots, substantial variation in plant architecture, leaf posture, panicle morphology, and canopy closure exists among cultivars, even within the same phenological stage [18].This high degree of visual heterogeneity renders phenological classification based on a single spatial resolution susceptible to local noise and atypical samples. To mitigate this issue, the proposed framework employs MR images to construct a continuous and stable temporal backbone and introduces HR images at key time points as a source of spatial detail, thereby achieving a balance between temporal continuity and spatial discriminability. Ablation results (Table 3) demonstrate that, compared with single-resolution inputs or naïve multi-scale fusion strategies, the proposed multi-scale approach yields superior overall performance and improved stage-level stability. Although the dense HR-only setting achieved the highest OA, this configuration relies on intensive HR acquisition and mainly serves as a high-cost benchmark. In contrast, the proposed HR + MR + LSTM model achieved an OA of 0.873 with substantially lower acquisition time and operational cost. Therefore, the small reduction in accuracy should be interpreted as a practical trade-off between performance and efficiency, rather than a negative effect of MR data itself. Notably, the benefits of HR images are not uniformly distributed across phenological stages: its contribution is most pronounced during early and middle stages, while performance gains during late stages such as the dough and maturity phases are relatively limited. This stage-dependent pattern aligns well with the biological characteristics of rice development, where in phenotypic traits become increasingly stable and visually distinct during later growth stages.

The synergistic effects of temporal modeling and multi-scale spatial representation are further corroborated by visualization analyses. Grad-CAM results (Fig. 5a) show that, with the integration of multi-scale inputs and temporal modeling, the network focuses more consistently on panicles and key canopy regions during early and middle stages, rather than on background or non-discriminative areas. Feature embedding visualizations (Fig. 5b) further reveal a marked reduction in overlap among phenological stages in the low-dimensional feature space, with substantially improved separability between JBS and HFS. The strong correspondence between these feature-space patterns and the observed stage-specific performance gains suggests that the performance improvements arise from effective exploitation of complementary temporal and spatial information, rather than from incidental effects.

Overall, the results indicate that temporal modeling and multi-scale spatial information are not simply additive but interact synergistically during feature learning. On the one hand, multi-scale spatial features provide LSTM with stable and complementary phenotypic inputs across multiple spatial levels, reducing the sensitivity of temporal modeling to local noise. On the other hand, temporal dependency modeling amplifies the discriminative value of HR images at critical phenological transition points by propagating its information across time [31,32].Without relying on auxiliary meteorological or management data, the proposed framework leverages UAV-based temporal images alone to robustly capture crop responses to genetic and environmental variability, offering a practical and scalable solution for accurate and sustainable phenological monitoring in large-scale breeding trials.

Advantages and design trade-offs of lightweight CNNs for UAV-based temporal phenological modeling

In UAV-based temporal phenological modeling, the choice of the spatial feature extraction network influences not only single date classification accuracy but also directly constrains the computational efficiency, stability, and scalability of the multi-temporal modeling framework. A systematic comparison between classical deep convolutional neural networks, including VGG16, ResNet50, and GoogLeNet, and lightweight architectures (Table 2) indicates that deeper networks do not exhibit performance gains commensurate with their substantially higher computational complexity in phenological recognition tasks. In contrast, MobileNetV3 [42,50] achieved stable and competitive classification performance under both HR and MR, and attained leading overall accuracy, agreement metrics, and F1-scores under high-resolution conditions. These results suggest that phenological stage discrimination primarily relies on mid and low level visual features such as canopy texture, panicle structure, and color variation, rather than highly abstract deep semantic representations, which is consistent with existing understanding of the hierarchical nature of phenological phenotypic features [51]. Within a temporal modeling framework, spatial feature extraction must be repeatedly executed across multiple time steps, causing network parameter size and computational complexity to be further amplified along the temporal dimension (Fig. 2). Under such conditions, heavyweight networks tend to introduce substantial computational redundancy and increase the risk of overfitting in multi-temporal settings, particularly in breeding scenarios characterized by limited sample sizes and pronounced interannual variability [52]. By contrast, MobileNetV3 employs lightweight architectural designs, such as depthwise separable convolutions, to substantially reduce parameter counts, FLOPs, and memory consumption while preserving sufficient feature representation capacity (Table 2), making it more suitable as a backbone for multi-temporal phenological modeling.

Furthermore, the compact feature representations produced by lightweight networks help suppress redundant information and encourage the model to focus on phenotypic patterns closely associated with phenological progression, thereby exhibiting more stable generalization performance across years and flight altitudes (Fig. S6; Table 3). It should be emphasized that the selection of MobileNetV3 does not imply a dismissal of deeper or more complex networks in other computer vision applications, but rather reflects a task specific trade off tailored to phenological recognition, where temporal evolution, operational efficiency, and sustainability are critical considerations. Overall, MobileNetV3 achieves a more favorable balance between classification performance and computational efficiency, providing a stable and efficient spatial feature foundation for subsequent multi-scale feature fusion and temporal modeling, and offering methodological support for large-scale and sustainable UAV-based crop phenology monitoring.

Asynchronous multi-scale fusion strategy: trade-off between monitoring frequency and operational efficiency

Given the long acquisition time of HR UAV images, which makes high frequency phenological monitoring impractical, this study proposes an asynchronous multi-scale fusion scheme within the MAGF framework. Unlike conventional multi-modal fusion that assumes strict temporal alignment across modalities [53], the proposed approach allows HR images to be collected at discrete intervals (Δ = 1–14) and introduces a missing-aware gating module at the feature level to adaptively allocate contributions from MR and HR streams. When HR images were unavailable, the model infers informative cues by integrating macro-scale temporal trends from MR observations with the historical memory of the LSTM; when HR images were available, they serve as periodic calibration at critical stages. Mechanistically, this design parallels agronomic practice: frequent MR observations maintain continuous situational awareness, while sparse HR observations provide stage-wise refinement [54,55].

Sampling-interval experiments reveal a clear operational optimum (Fig. S6; Fig. 4). Classification performance is highest when HR is acquired every 1–3 days, and even when the interval extends to 5–7 days, the degradation remains within 1.5% while still substantially outperforming the MR-only baseline. This is consistent with prior findings from within-season phenological monitoring using UAV RGB time series [29] and suggests that the marginal gains of ultra-high spatial resolution can be offset by its temporal and logistical costs [24]. Building on these 2024 results, the strategy also yields tangible operational benefits: a full-coverage HR flight requires approximately 4 h, compared with about 20 min for an MR flight. Accordingly, a practical hybrid protocol with MR acquisition daily or every other day to preserve temporal continuity, complemented by one HR flight every 5–7 days for spatial refinement and stage-wise calibration, reduces total weekly flight time from ∼28 h (daily HR) to ∼6 h while maintaining high phenological recognition accuracy. This estimate is based on the 2024 field scale (0.53 ha) and flight configuration, and assumes seven feasible sampling opportunities per week during the intensive monitoring period, excluding rainy days. Although the exact time cost depends on field scale and local operating conditions, the proposed hybrid strategy provides a practical and scalable reference for large-scale breeding applications. Cross-year evaluation (Fig. 6; Fig. S7) further tests robustness by directly applying the model trained in 2024 to an independent 2023 dataset. The model still achieves an OA of 0.774 at Δ = 3; however, robustness remains limited and a “performance saturation” pattern emerges. Notably, both the 2023 and 2024 results exhibit a consistent sensitivity to temporal offset for HFS, with its accuracy dropping sharply once Δ exceeds 7 days. This indicates that cross-year errors are dominated by systematic domain shifts rather than sampling frequency alone. inter-annual meteorological variability can advance or delay phenological rhythms, causing misalignment between calendar time and true developmental progress and thereby exacerbating confusions between adjacent stages (e.g., JBS vs. HFS) [56].In addition, cross-year changes in canopy appearance and imaging conditions, together with a double out of distribution setting (i.e., simultaneous shifts across years and across resolutions/sampling strategies) may weaken the transferability of gating priors learned in the source domain. Consequently, simply increasing sampling frequency is insufficient to substantially mitigate these errors, consistent with known limitations in cross domain generalization for remote-sensing time-series tasks [57].

Implications, limitations, and future directions

This study establishes a new paradigm for phenological monitoring of rice germplasm resources that balances identification accuracy with operational costs. From a practical perspective, the proposed asynchronous multi-scale fusion strategy successfully addresses the critical bottleneck of poor timeliness often associated with HR UAV remote sensing in large-scale breeding trials. By demonstrating the feasibility of spatiotemporal complementarity and synergy, this study provides breeders with a reproducible, cost-effective digital phenotyping tool. Consequently, the time cost of manual field surveys is significantly reduced, the screening efficiency for superior germplasm (e.g., specific maturity varieties) is accelerated, and the transition from “empirical breeding” to “precision breeding” is promoted. However, certain limitations remain in this study. First, the model was primarily trained on data from years with normal climatic conditions. Crop appearance may rapidly turn yellow while internal physiology remains immature, posing a risk of misclassification for pure vision-based models under scenarios of heat forced maturity induced by extreme heat or drought [58]. Second, the current research focused on a germplasm bank at a single site, and large-scale generalization validation across multi-site networks spanning different latitudes and ecological zones has not yet been conducted. Future research will focus on the construction of a “Visual-Environmental” multi-driven mechanism. Responding to the suggestions of [59], meteorological factors (such as Growing Degree Days and Photosynthetically Active Radiation will be explored as covariates to be introduced into the LSTM temporal module, utilizing environmental data to correct visual deviations under extreme climate conditions. Furthermore, the association between deep features and genotypes will be further explored, with the aim of constructing a comprehensive evaluation model connecting genotype-phenotype-environment (G × P × E). Thereby, the genetic and environmental interaction mechanisms of crop development can be deciphered at a deeper level.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a multi-scale time-series deep learning framework to address the challenges of phenological asynchrony and the trade-off between spatial resolution and temporal frequency in large-scale rice germplasm trials. By integrating high frequency MR images as temporal anchors and sparse HR images as spatial enhancement via a MAGF mechanism, we achieved robust phenology identification under irregular sampling conditions. The integration of temporal modeling significantly enhanced performance, improving F1-scores by 1.7% to 4.7% during ambiguous transition stages (before DS) compared with single-temporal-scale baselines. The integration of multi-spatial-scale fusion with LSTM temporal modeling yielded superior performance considering efficiency, achieving an Overall Accuracy (OA) and F1-score of 0.873, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.84. Furthermore, independent cross-year validation on the 2023 dataset (acquired at a different flight altitude of 6 m) yielded an overall accuracy of 0.774 within a 3 day tolerance, confirming the model's robustness against inter-annual environmental variations and sensor altitude changes. Quantitative analysis further identified a sampling interval of 5 to 7 days for high-resolution images as an optimal balance point, maintaining accuracy degradation within 1.5%. Based on these findings, we validated the hybrid sampling strategy that reduces weekly flight duration from 28 h to approximately 6 h, representing a fourfold efficiency gain in efficiency. Ultimately, this study not only offers a scalable solution for high throughput phenotyping but also provides a scientific reference for agricultural practitioners to flexibly tailor UAV operational strategies that best balance specific budget constraints and precision requirements.
