Preparation and in vitro property evaluation of NO-HA@ICG self-assembled nanoparticles for gas therapy synergized with phototherapy
Authors: Jiamin Huang, Jianhu Jia, Qian Zhang, Xiaohui Tang, Qi Shen, Wenjun Su, Jing Zhao, Mei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The synergistic effect of gas therapy and phototherapy is an innovative therapy in the field of cancer treatment with a unique therapeutic mechanism and application characteristics. NO improves the tumor blood vessels and facilitates the drugs to reach the cancer site, and the heat and ROS generated by phototherapy promote the release of NO and synergistically induce cancer cell Apoptosis. In this study, a self-assembled nanoparticle constructed by NO donor and HA was prepared by microfluidic equipment, and ICG was encapsulated in the nanoparticle and evaluated for its phototherapeutic effect and NO release performance. And the results showed that NO-HA@ICG NPs + laser group produced stronger ROS and NO than Free ICG and NO-HA@ICG NPs groups, which had a synergistic effect. The nanoparticle NO is expected to improve the tumor vascularity to facilitate the drug to reach the cancer site, and not only did the heat and ROS released by phototherapy promote the release of NO, but also synergistically induced the apoptosis of cancer cells with NO.

Introduction

Among the many directions explored for cancer therapy, nitric oxide, as an endogenous lipid-soluble gas molecule, has also gained increasing attention for its use in breast cancer treatment, but it enters the body mainly as a NO donor due to its short and unstable half-life, which is rapidly scavenged once it binds to oxygen or hemoglobin(Tang et al., 2024). NO released by NO donors can damage biological macromolecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids of cancer cells. For example, high concentrations of NO can break the DNA of cancer cells, damage their genetic material, induce apoptosis or necrosis of cancer cells, and directly inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells. In addition, tumor blood vessels are usually abnormally proliferated and dysfunctional, NO released from NO donors can regulate the diastole and contraction of tumor blood vessels, improve blood perfusion to the tumor, and make it easier for drugs to reach the inside of cancer tissues(Chen et al., 2025), and at the same time, activate the macrophages, causing them to release more necrosis factors, such as TNF-alpha, to enhance the killing effect on cancer cells. However, in anticancer therapy, NO donors suffer from the defects of difficult-to-control NO concentration in vivo and lack of targeting, while different concentrations of NO at the treatment site show their different effects, with high concentrations of NO having anticancer effects, while low concentrations of NO show pro-cancer effects(Tang et al., 2024). As a result, cancer treatment based on NO gas therapy is limited, and gas therapy with NO is often combined with other therapeutic strategies, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and photoacoustic therapy, in order to achieve better therapeutic outcomes(Duan et al., 2025; Qiao et al., 2024).

Phototherapy, as one of the means of cancer treatment, also has unique therapeutic mechanisms and application characteristics. Phototherapy includes Photodynamic Therapy (PDT), Photothermal Therapy (PTT), and Photoactive Therapy(Cuadrado et al., 2024). Among them, PDT therapy is to treat cancer by using specific wavelength laser irradiation of the cancer site with photosensitive drugs and a laser activation method, which can activate the photosensitive drugs selectively enriched in the cancerous tissues, trigger the photochemical reaction, and destroy the cancerous tissues(Wang et al., 2025a). When the laser irradiates the cancerous area, the photosensitive drug in the cancerous area can transfer the energy to the oxygen around the cancerous tissue, generating highly active mono-linear oxygen, which interacts with the nearby biological macromolecules to destroy the function of cells and cell organelles, and then kills the cancerous cells(Li et al., 2025b). PDT is widely used in the treatment of solid tumors (e.g., bladder, esophagus, skin, and breast cancers), vascular disease, dental, and other diseases due to its low toxicity, lack of drug resistance, and mild adverse effects. PTT mainly utilizes photothermal conversion materials with strong absorption ability in the near-infrared light region (wavelength range of 700–1300 nm) (Luo et al., 2025), which efficiently convert the absorbed light energy into heat energy under near-infrared laser irradiation to raise the temperature of local cancer tissues to 40–45 °C (thermotherapy) or higher than 45 °C (thermoablation), resulting in the denaturation of the cancer cells into necrosis, in order to achieve the purpose of cancer treatment. Most cancer cells are thermally lethal at temperatures between 42 and 45 °C, which normal cells can tolerate for long periods of time, so the localized heat therapy generated by PTT can selectively kill cancer cells and cause irreversible damage without damaging normal cells(Bai et al., 2025). Among them, indocyanine green is a second-generation photosensitizer commonly used in clinical diagnosis and treatment of tumors(Li et al., 2025a), which exhibits strong light absorption and fluorescence properties in the near-infrared region(Wang et al., 2025b), and is commonly used in the treatment of breast cancer, endometrial cancer, and cervical cancer. Especially in the operation, ICG is often used to locate the sentinel lymph nodes before judging whether lymph node dissection should be performed(Wang et al., 2025c), thus avoiding the complications brought about by systematic purging and making the operation faster and safer. In addition, ICG has a phototherapeutic effect, which can induce the death of cancerous tissues by generating ROS and exerting a thermotherapeutic effect under radiation irradiation. However, ICG suffers from defects such as water instability, photodegradability, thermal degradation, extremely short circulatory life, and easy to bind to lipoproteins, resulting in rapid in vivo clearance and short half-life, which greatly limits its clinical application, but this problem can be overcome by synthetic modification or by utilizing drug delivery systems(He et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

Nanodrug delivery systems can precisely deliver anticancer drugs in cancer therapy and reduce the damage to normal cells. Among them, Self-assembling Nanoparticles (SANPs) have received increasing attention in cancer therapy due to their advantages of small particle size, narrow particle size distribution, wide range of drug loading, high drug loading capacity, stable structure, and long in vivo retention time etc. SANPs have good biocompatibility, and they are usually constructed with biodegradable materials such as proteins, lipids, polysaccharides, etc. These materials can be naturally metabolized in vivo and are less likely to trigger an immune response. SANPs are biocompatible and usually constructed with biodegradable materials, such as proteins, lipids, polysaccharides, etc., which can be naturally metabolized in vivo and are not prone to triggering immune reactions. SANPs can be accurately controlled in terms of nanoparticle size by altering the assembly conditions or the composition of the materials to satisfy the different drug delivery needs. For example, the size of nanoparticles can be adjusted to 10–200 nm, which makes it easier for them to penetrate biological membranes, and their shapes can also be controlled, such as being made into spheres, rods, or disks, with different shapes of nanoparticles having different distributions and movement characteristics in the body, which provides more choices for targeted delivery of drugs. With the significant advantages of being green, inexpensive, and easy to expand, SANPs are not only highly efficient in the encapsulation of drugs with different physicochemical properties, but also can be used at specific sites to deliver drugs and other substances. SANPs are not only capable of efficiently encapsulating drugs with different physicochemical properties, but can also be functionally modified at specific sites(Huang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024) by introducing stimuli-responsive molecules with diverse targeting motifs and taking advantage of the unique microenvironments of the cancer site, such as acidic interstitial pH, altered redox state due to vigorous cellular metabolism, and enhanced oxygen perfusion(Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025), while at the same time leveraging on the molecules or receptors overexpressed on the surface of the cancer cells such as growth factors, interleukins, transferrin, etc., to achieve targeted drug delivery(Dong et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024), and thus is a very promising vehicle in cancer therapy.

Therefore, in this study, we proposed to prepare NO donor [3-benzenesulfonyl-4-(1-hydroxy ether)-1,2,5-diazole-2-oxide] and hyaluronic acid to construct a drug-macromolecule drug coupling, and use microfluidic method to encapsulate ICG into the coupling to form self-assembled into nanoparticles (NO-HA@ICG NPs), which improves the stability and phototherapeutic effect of indocyanine green photosensitizers, and also makes them with the ability to release NO, which further improves the water solubility of the coupling, and the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles were investigated, and the phototherapeutic effect and NO therapeutic effect of the nanoparticles were preliminarily evaluated by cellular experiments with a view to providing experimental bases for the subsequent studies. Materials

Laboratory instruments

The obtained nanoparticles were synthesized using the Microflow S microfluidic nanomedicine preparation system [Mingtai Pharmaceutical Equipment (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.], Characterization was performed using a Laser808-4 W laser (NBeT Group Corp.), absorption spectra were measured on a UV-2700 UV–Vis spectrophotometer [Shimadzu, Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan], and cellular experiments were conducted in a Herasafe™ KS Class II biosafety cabinet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Experimental material

Methanol is an analytical grade reagent. DPBF was purchased from Aldrich Chemical Company, DMEM medium was purchased from Gibco Life Technologies (USA), the NO detection kit and DAF-FM DA were purchased from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., the CCK-8 assay kit was purchased from OBiO Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Indigo carmine was purchased from Bide Pharmatech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and the NO donor was produced in-house by this laboratory.

Methods

Synthesis and characterization of oxidized furazan-like NO donors with hyaluronic acid

Synthesis of Oxidized furazan-like NO donors and hyaluronic acid

A solution of NO donor (500 mg, 1.02 mmol), DCC (840 mg, 4.07 mmol), and DMAP (165 mg,1.35 mmol) in DMF (20 mL) was stirred at 0 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere for 3 h. Dissolve HA (50 mg, 1.00 mmol) in formamide (10 mL) and react with the above solution at 40 °C for 36 h. The solution was dialyzed in DMSO for 2 days and in distilled water for 3 days using a dialysis membrane (MWCO:14000) and lyophilized to a powder(Wang et al., 2018). IR/cm−1: 723, 842, 892, 1045, 1068, 1170, 1185, 1345, 1732, 3033. 1H NMR (600 MHz, DMSO‑d6) δ 8.23 (s, 1H), 8.01 (s, 1H), 7.89 (s, 1H), 7.74 (s, 1H), 5.57–5.52 (m, 1H), 4.48 (s, 1H), 4.18 (s, 1H), 3.93 (d, J = 12.3 Hz, 1H), 3.47 (s, 1H), 3.29 (s, 5H), 3.18 (s, 1H), 2.90 (s, 2H), 2.72 (s, 1H), 2.66 (s, 1H), 2.50 (s, 2H), 2.13–2.09 (m, 1H), 1.82 (d, J = 13.5 Hz, 6H), 1.75–1.70 (m, 7H), 1.68–1.64 (m, 7H), 1.60 (s, 3H), 1.55 (s, 2H), 1.50 (d, J = 13.0 Hz, 4H), 1.42 (d, J = 11.8 Hz, 2H), 1.27 (d, J = 12.3 Hz, 9H), 1.24 (s, 8H), 1.17 (s, 3H), 1.15–1.10 (m, 4H), 1.07–1.00 (m, 4H). 13C NMR (151 MHz, CDCl3) δ 171.98, 165.27, 158.83, 156.80, 153.71, 129.72, 128.54, 77.25, 77.04, 76.82, 67.96, 60.66, 55.74, 49.08, 41.02, 34.92, 32.68, 30.95, 27.92, 26.24, 26.20, 25.45, 24.67.

Ultraviolet Spectral Analysis of NO-HA

Take solutions (0.5 mg/mL, 2 mL) containing NO donor, HA, NO-HA, and NO-HA@ICG NPs, respectively, and perform full-wavelength scanning in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm using ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy.

Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis of NO-HA

Take 2 mg of NO-HA powder and compress it with an appropriate amount of KBr to form a sample. Measure using an IR Prestige-21 spectrometer with a scanning range of 400–4000 cm−1.

1H NMR in DMSO‑d₆ and D₂O

Take 20 mg each of HA and NO-HA powder and dissolve thoroughly in a defined volume of DMSO‑d₆ and D₂O. Following dissolution, transfer the solutions into NMR tubes and proceed with measurement using a Unity-Inova 600 nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer.

13C NMR in CDCl3 and D₂O

Take 50 mg each of HA and NO-HA powder and dissolve them completely in a defined volume of CDCl3 and D₂O. Following dissolution, transfer the solution into NMR tubes and perform the measurement using a Unity-Inova 600 nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer.

Establishment of a method for the determination of ICG content

Using water as a solvent, an appropriate amount of ICG standard was weighed to prepare the ICG reserve solution (50 μg/mL), and this standard solution was placed on a UV spectrophotometer, and spectral scanning was carried out in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm to investigate the maximum absorption wavelength of ICG. The ICG stock solution was diluted into a series of standard solutions with concentration gradients of 12.5, 6.25, 3.12, 1.56, 0.78, 0.39 μg/mL, and the absorbance was measured at the maximal absorption wavelength and the standard curve was obtained by linear regression of the concentration (C) on the absorbance (A).

Preparation of NO-HA@ICG NPs by microfluidic devices

Self-assembled nanoparticles were prepared using microfluidic equipment(Maged et al., 2022). NO-HA 4 mg was precisely measured and dissolved with 2 mL of methanol, which was used as the organic phase for spare. ICG 200 μg was precisely measured and dissolved with 4 mL of deionized water, which was used as the aqueous phase. The parameters of the microfluidic device were set with a total flow rate of 14 mL/min, a flow rate ratio of 2:1, and a total volume of 5 mL. The organic and aqueous phases were mixed rapidly, and then the resulting solution was dialyzed overnight in a dialysis bag to remove the unassembled Free ICG solution and organic solvents, and to obtain a green solution of self-assembled nanoparticles.

Prescription screening of NO-HA@ICG NPs

Different factors such as total flow rate (10, 12, 14, 16, 18 mL/min), flow rate ratio (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1), NO-HA concentration (1, 2, 3 mg/mL), and ICG concentration (30:1, 40:1, 50:1) were investigated to determine the effect of particle size, PDI and zeta potential on self-assembled nanoparticles to obtain a better prescription. On self-assembled nanoparticles to obtain a better prescription.

Screening of total flow rate

Dissolve 20 mg of NO-HA in 10 mL of methanol and reserve as the organic phase. Dissolve 1 mg of ICG in 20 mL of deionized water as the aqueous phase. The parameters of the microfluidic equipment were set, the total flow rate was 10, 12, 14, 16, 18 mL/min, the flow rate ratio was 2:1, and the total volume was 5 mL. The organic phase and the aqueous phase were mixed rapidly, and the resulting solution was dialyzed overnight in a dialysis bag to remove the unassembled Free ICG solution and organic solvent, and obtain the self-assembled nanoparticles. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles were determined using a particle size analyzer, and the drug loading and encapsulation rate were calculated.

Screening of Flow velocity ratios

Dissolve 20 mg of NO-HA in 10 mL of methanol and reserve as the organic phase. Dissolve 1 mg of ICG in 20 mL of deionized water as the aqueous phase. The parameters of the microfluidic equipment were set, the total flow rate was 14 mL/min, and the flow rate ratios were 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, and the total volume was 5 mL. The organic and aqueous phases were mixed rapidly, and then the resulting solution was dialyzed overnight in a dialysis bag, and the unassembled Free ICG solution and organic solvents were removed to obtain the self-assembled nanoparticles. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles were determined using a particle size analyzer, and the drug loading and encapsulation rate were calculated.

Screening of NO-HA content

Dissolve 30 mg of NO-HA in 10 mL of methanol and prepare a stock solution with a concentration of 2 mg/mL. Measure the appropriate amount of reserve solution and dilute to 1, 2, 3 mg/mL NO-HA methanol solution, as the organic phase standby. Dissolve 1 mg of ICG in 20 mL of deionized water, as the aqueous phase. The parameters of the microfluidic equipment were set with a total flow rate of 14 mL/min, a flow rate ratio of 2:1, and a total volume of 5 mL. The organic and aqueous phases were rapidly mixed, and the resulting solution was dialyzed overnight in a dialysis bag to remove the unassembled Free ICG solution and organic solvent, and the self-assembled nanoparticles were obtained. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles were determined using a particle size analyzer, and the drug loading and encapsulation rate were calculated.

Screening of ICG content

Dissolve 20 mg of NO-HA in 10 mL of methanol and reserve as the organic phase. Dissolve 1 mg of ICG with 10 mL of deionized water, and then use it as a stock solution with a concentration of 100 μg/mL. The appropriate amount of reserve solution was diluted into ICG aqueous solution according to the concentration ratios of NO-HA to ICG of 30:1, 40:1, and 50:1, respectively, and used as the aqueous phase for spares. The parameters of the microfluidic equipment were set with a total flow rate of 14 mL/min, a flow rate ratio of 2:1, respectively, and a total volume of 5 mL. The organic and aqueous phases were rapidly mixed, and the resulting solution was dialyzed overnight in a dialysis bag to remove the unassembled Free ICG solution and organic solvent, and the self-assembled nanoparticles were obtained. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles were determined using a particle size analyzer, and the drug loading and encapsulation rate were calculated.

Characterization of NO-HA@ICG NPs

Appearance and micro-morphological characterization

Transmission electron microscopy was used to observe the apparent morphological characteristics of self-assembled nanoparticles(Zhao et al., 2023). A 10 μL drop of NO-HA@ICG NPs solution was taken onto the copper mesh with carbon film, naturally evaporated to dryness, and then stained using 10 % phosphotungstic acid for 10 min. The samples were placed under a transmission electron microscope to observe the morphology of the nanoparticles.

Measurement of particle size and zeta potential

The samples of NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs were diluted 10-fold, and the particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles were determined using a Malvern particle sizer, which was repeated three times for each sample.

Determination of encapsulation rate and drug loading capacity

After 10-fold dilution of NO-HA@ICG NPs samples, the absorbance A was measured by a UV spectrophotometer, and substituted into the standard curve equation to calculate the drug concentration. The encapsulation rate and the amount of drug load were calculated according to the following equations, and the determination was repeated three times for each sample.

Encapsulation rate (%) = (amount of drug administered - amount of free drug)/amount of drug administered × 100 % [1].

Amount of drug loaded (%) = [amount of drug loaded/(amount of drug administered + carrier)] × 100 % [2].

Stability evaluation

Three batches of NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs were prepared and placed at 4 °C for 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 14, 21, and 28 days, and the particle sizes, PDIs, potentials, encapsulation rates, and drug loadings of the NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs were measured, and the change curves were plotted.

Evaluation of photothermal performance

Prepare a NO-HA@ICG NPs solution with an ICG concentration of 35 μg/mL. Divide the experiment into four groups labeled A, B, C, and D. Each group's solution receives 15 min of 808 nm laser irradiation at different power densities: Group A: 500 mW/cm2, Group B: 1000 mW/cm2, Group C: 1500 mW/cm2, Group D: 2000 mW/cm2. Solution temperature was recorded every 30 s, plotted for analysis, and the optimal power density was determined. All subsequent experiments employed this power density. Laser irradiation was applied to solutions of Free ICG, NO-HA NPs, and NO-HA@ICG NPs for 15 min. The solution temperature was recorded every 30 s, and the data were plotted to analyze the photothermal conversion efficiency of the nanoparticles. Laser irradiation was applied to solutions of Free ICG and NO-HA@ICG NPs. Temperature sensors monitored and recorded the thermal response of each formulation during five consecutive thermal cycles (laser on for 10 min, off for 15 min) in aqueous solution. Graphical analysis was performed to compare photothermal stability.

ROS Detection

The reactive oxygen species yield of self-assembled nanoparticles was detected using a chemical probe assay.1,3-and phenylisobenzoruran (DPBF) is a fluorescent probe that has long been recognized for its high specific binding to certain reactive oxygen species types(Zheng et al., 2019). DPBF binds to ROS, irreversible oxidation, and a rapid decrease in absorption intensity at 415 nm in the UV–Vis.

Using ethanol as a solvent, the DPBF masterbatch was prepared at 2 mmol/L, diluted to 40 μmol/L, and placed in a dark place for backup. The concentrations of Free ICG, NO-HA NPs, and NO-HA@ICG NPs solutions were adjusted to 35 μg/mL. Take 0.5 mL of the prepared solution and 4.5 mL of DPBF solution, respectively, and mix them well (the control group was a blank DPBF solution), place the mixed solution in a quartz cuvette, and irradiate it with a laser at 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 s for full wavelength scanning.

NO release detection

In vitro NO release studies were performed using the Griess method(Girotti and Fahey, 2020). The concentrations of Free NO-HA, NO-HA NPs, and NO-HA@ICG NPs solutions were adjusted to 10 μg/mL, and 50 μL was taken at different time points (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 150, 180, 210, and 240 min) and mixed with 50 μL of the Griess I and II reagents. The solutions were kept at room temperature for 10 min and then measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The NO concentration was calculated using the regression equation of the NaNO2 standard solution.

In vitro cellular evaluation of the properties of NO-HA@ICG NPs

Evaluation of photodynamic effect

The photodynamic therapy efficacy of NO-HA@ICG NPs against HeLa cells was determined by CCK-8 assay. The HeLa cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at densities of 104 per well, respectively, and cultured for 24 h. Different concentrations (15.00, 7.50, 3.75, 1.88, 0.94, 0.47, 0.23 μg/mL) of NO-HA@ICG NPs and Free ICG in serum-free medium solutions were added, and a blank control group was established. Subsequently, after 48 h of incubation under light-free conditions, the well plates were processed according to the CCK-8 method. In the light group, cells were irradiated with a laser for 1.5 min/well after 5 h of drug administration, and then the OD value at 450 nm was measured, and the cell survival rate was calculated according to the formula.

Cell survival rate (%) = (absorbance of experimental group – absorbance of blank) / (absorbance of control group - absorbance of blank) × 100 %. [3].

In vitro ROS detection

Intracellular ROS production was assessed using a ROS fluorescent probe (DCFH-DA)(Auti et al., 2022). The HeLa cells were inoculated in 24-well plates (7 × 104/well). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were incubated with NPs for 5 h, and then 5 μM DCFH-DA was added and incubated for 30 min. After DAPI staining, fluorescence images of ROS release from HeLa cells were observed by a fluorescence inverted microscope at excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively.

In vitro NO release assay

Intracellular NO production was assessed using an NO fluorescent probe (DAF-FM DA)(Huysseune et al., 2023a). The HeLa cells were inoculated in 24-well plates (7 × 104/well). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were incubated with NPs for 5 h, and then 5 μM DAF-FM-DA was added and incubated for 30 min. After DAPI staining, fluorescence images of NO release from HeLa cells were observed by a fluorescence inverted microscope at excitation and emission wavelengths of 495 nm and 515 nm, respectively.

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 software was utilized for data processing, and the measurement information was expressed as (x̄ ± s). Comparisons between multiple groups were made by one-way ANOVA, and further two-by-two comparisons were made by the LSD-t-test. The test level α = 0.05.

Result

Structural characterization of NO-HA

The synthesis process of the NO donor with HA is shown in Fig. 2A. To confirm the structure of the product NO-HA, we characterized it using nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy, with the results shown in Fig. 2B. First, the proton signal of the methylene group at the ortho position of the alcohol hydroxyl group involved in the reaction in HA was originally located at δ 3.4–3.8 ppm. Following the formation of the ester bond, the signal shifts significantly to δ 3.93 ppm due to the strong electron-withdrawing effect of the ester oxygen. Second, the broad proton peak at δ 12.06 ppm in NO donor molecules is completely absent in the NO-HA spectrum. The consistent changes in these two key signals indicate that HA and the NO donor have been successfully linked via an ester bond. The NMR carbon spectrum characterization results are shown in Fig. S1. First, in the spectrum of the NO donor, the carboxyl carbon signals at δ 175.71 ppm and δ 171.65 ppm show significantly reduced intensity in the spectrum of the NO-HA product, indicating consumption of the carboxyl group during the reaction. Second, the characteristic signal corresponding to the primary alcohol carbon in HA (δ 60–65 ppm) is significantly weakened or even absent in the NO-HA spectrum. Concurrently, a new enhanced signal appears at δ 67.96 ppm, which is assigned to the methylene carbon in the formed ester bond. The observed weakening of the carboxyl signal is consistent with the shift of the primary alcohol carbon to the ester bond carbon, collectively confirming that the HA and NO donor have been successfully covalently linked via an ester bond. The infrared spectra of NO-HA are shown in Fig. 2C, in which both HA and NO-HA showed carbon‑oxygen double-bond peaks at 1626 cm−1, and NO-HA had a stronger carbon‑oxygen double-bond peak and a carbon‑nitrogen double-bond peak at 1573 cm−1. HA showed a hydroxyl peak at 3424 cm−1, and NO-HA showed a carbon‑nitrogen double-bond peak at 1573 cm−1. Shows a hydroxyl peak, while NO-HA also shows a hydroxyl peak at 3326 cm−1. When the hydroxyl group reacts with the carboxyl group, the chemical environment of the hydroxyl group changes, and the formation of a new chemical bond or the change of the hydrogen bonding environment will reduce the vibration frequency of the hydroxyl group, resulting in the shift of the IR absorption peaks to the lower wavelengths. In addition, after the esterification reaction, the hydroxyl group is transformed from a state in which there may be a variety of hydrogen bonding interactions to a part of a relatively uniform ester structure, making its infrared absorption peak narrow. Moreover, the relative increase in the concentration of hydroxyl-related structures around the newly formed ester bonds resulted in enhanced absorption in the IR detection region, and these changes in peak shapes were consistent with the expected changes in chemical bonding due to the chemical reaction. In addition, The UV spectrum of NO-HA is shown in Fig. 2D, NO donor has maximum absorption at 225.5 nm and 250 nm, while NO-HA shows maximum absorption at 220 nm, which is blue-shifted compared to NO donor, and the absorption at 250 nm is weakened, which indicates that HA was introduced into the molecule, which produces a conjugation effect, and the molecule has a change in the electronic structure, which indicates that NO donor was successfully coupled with HA. In addition, when NO-HA was prepared as nanoparticles with ICG, it was found that there was no significant change in NO-HA at 200–400 nm, indicating that this dosage preparation method does not affect the physicochemical properties of NO-HA. Characterization of self-assembled nanoparticles

The prescription screening for nanoparticle preparation ultimately yielded nanoparticles with a favorable particle size distribution, as shown in Fig. 4A. The solution of NO-HA NPs was milky white, the surface of NO-HA NPs was presented as smooth spherical or spheroidal, and its particle size distribution was relatively uniform. The ROS release performance of the nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 5D. When the NO-HA@ICG NPs solution was added to the DPBF solution, the absorption of DPBF at 415 nm gradually decreased with the increase of the laser irradiation time. This indicates that the prepared NO-HA@ICG NPs continuously produced ROS under the laser. After 5 min of laser irradiation, the absorbance of the NO-HA nanoparticle group remained unchanged compared to the DPBF blank control, while the absorbance of both the Free ICG group and the NO-HA@ICG nanoparticle group decreased significantly. The NO-HA@ICG NPs group decreased to a greater extent, probably because NO-HA wrapped around ICG can improve the stability of ICG in the system and reduce its aggregation. When NO interacts with ICG, it may change the electronic structure of ICG, which prolongs the lifetime of the ICG excited state or increases the probability of the electron leaps. So more molecules can effectively absorb the NO-HA@ICG NPs solution when it is illuminated with light energy to produce ROS, which suggests that NO-HA@ICG NPs are more efficient in producing ROS compared to the Free ICG group.

In addition, NO release from NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs was detected by the Griess method, as shown in Fig. 5E. The regression equation for sodium nitrite was A = 0.00627C + 0.04563 (R2 = 0.999). Within 3 h, the NO release rates of Free NO-HA, NO-HA NPs, NO-HA@ICG NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs + laser groups were respectively were about (97.49 ± 1.85)%, (64.44 ± 1.07)%, (69.46 ± 0.30)% and (78.04 ± 0.51)%, after which they showed slow-release characteristics, which could prolong the action time of NO and ensure its continuous release in the target tissues, and effectively reduce the toxic side effects produced by high concentrations of NO. And at the 4th h, compared with the Free NO-HA group, which could release 100 % NO, the dosage form group could only release about 70–80 %, and each dosage form group was significantly different from the Free NO-HA group, respectively (p < 0.0001), which demonstrated that the self-assembled nanoparticles made the NO donor more tightly packed. In the NO release process, the NO donor needs to overcome the obstruction of the surface layer of the nanocarrier first, so the release rate is slow. Compared to Free NO-HA, NO release in the NO-HA NPs group decreased by 0.44-fold, while NO release in the NO-HA@ICG NPs group decreased by 0.41-fold. No significant difference was observed between the NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs groups (p > 0.05). The NO release in the NO-HA@ICG NPs + laser group decreased by 0.24-fold, significantly differing from both the NO-HA NPs and NO-HA@ICG NPs groups. The photothermal and photodynamic effects generated by laser irradiation of ICG may directly activate the NO donor. Its chemical bonds become more susceptible to breaking and releasing NO during heating, causing structural changes in the nanocarrier. This accelerates nanoparticle degradation, exposing more active sites of the NO donor and further promoting NO release. 808 nm laser irradiation activates the NO donor through the photothermal/photodynamic effect of ICG, and this strategy realizes the synergistic effect of photo-enhanced release and PTT/PDT/NO therapy, which significantly improves the precision and efficacy of the therapy, and can be applied to tumor, antibacterial, or anti-angiogenic applications.

Intracellular ROS production was assessed using a ROS fluorescent probe. The fluorescent probe DCFH-DA itself is non-fluorescent and can freely pass through the cell membrane of cells. After entering cells, it can be hydrolyzed by intracellular esterase to generate DCFH, which cannot pass through the cell membrane; thus, the probe is loaded in cells, and the reactive oxygen species in cells can oxidize the non-fluorescent DCFH to generate the fluorescent DCF(Rajneesh et al., 2017). Therefore, we investigated the production of ROS in HeLa cells by the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe, and the amount of fluorescence produced was statistically analyzed by using ImageJ software, as shown in Figs. 6C-E. Blue fluorescence indicates cell nuclei, while green fluorescence indicates ROS. Under dark conditions, no green fluorescence was detected in HeLa cells across any formulation group. Under lighting conditions, both the Free ICG group and the NO-HA@ICG NPs group exhibited green fluorescence within cells. The green fluorescence intensity in the NO-HA@ICG NPs group was significantly stronger than that in the Free ICG group (p < 0.0001), consistent with the previous findings of ROS detection. This indicates that encapsulating ICG within NO-HA improves ICG's instability, enhances ROS release, and achieves synergistic anticancer effects between ICG and NO.

DAF-FA DA is the newest generation of fluorescent probe for NO detection, which can cross the cell membrane of cells, and after entering the cells, it can be catalyzed by intracellular esterases to form DAF-FM, which cannot cross the cell membrane. DAF-FM exhibits only weak fluorescence, but reacts with NO to produce strong fluorescence(Huysseune et al., 2023b). So we investigated the release of NO in HeLa cells by the DAF-FA DA fluorescent probe and statistically analyzed the amount of fluorescence produced by using the ImageJ software, and the results are shown in Figs. 7A-B, where the blue fluorescence represents the nucleus, and the green fluorescence represents NO. Green fluorescence was produced in the NO-HA NPs group, NO-HA@ICG NPs group, and NO-HA@ICG NPs + laser group. The green fluorescence intensity in the NO-HA@ICG NPs + laser group was significantly stronger than that in the NO-HA NPs group and NO-HA@ICG NPs group (p < 0.001), and there was no significant difference between the NO-HA NPs group and NO-HA@ICG NPs group (p > 0.05). Consistent with previous findings using the Griess assay to detect NO release, these results demonstrate that laser irradiation of nanoparticles excites ICG to generate reactive oxygen species. Discussion

In cancer treatment, PTT/PDT has gained widespread application for solid tumors due to its low toxicity, lack of drug resistance, and minimal adverse effects, making it a research hotspot in oncology(Li et al., 2025c; Zhao et al., 2025). This study proposes a novel approach for targeted delivery of self-assembled nanomedicines by combining PTT/PDT with NO gas therapy. In this study, a nitric oxide donor containing hyaluronic acid and the photosensitizer indocyanine green was prepared via a microfluidic device to self-assemble into nanoparticles in water. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that NO-HA@ICG NPs exhibit a smooth spherical morphology with a hydrodynamic size of 150 nm, demonstrating high dispersibility in water. NO-HA@ICG NPs achieve an encapsulation efficiency exceeding 85 % and a drug loading capacity of approximately 5 %, enabling effective delivery of NO. In addition to preparation and characterization, optical and chemical characterization of NO-HA@ICG NPs was conducted. In vitro non-cellular studies of photothermal performance and ROS release demonstrated that compared to conventional single PTT/PDT therapies, NO-HA@ICG NPs undergo thermal ablation at 45 °C, generating elevated levels of reactive oxygen species and prolonging the half-life of free ICG, thereby achieving synergistic therapeutic effects. In vitro cell-free nitric oxide release assays demonstrate that NO-HA@ICG NPs exhibit a more sustained NO release profile, maximizing overall NO delivery. This effectively addresses the limitations of sudden NO bursts and insufficient total NO levels commonly encountered in therapeutic applications. In vitro experiments demonstrate that NO-HA@ICG NPs exhibit significant phototoxicity toward HeLa cells, while exhibiting low cytotoxicity under dark conditions. Concurrently, their IC50 value under illumination is 1.35 μg/mL, indicating that the nanoparticles can be specifically activated at tumor sites. Furthermore, at the in vitro cellular level, NO-HA@ICG NPs efficiently generated ROS and NO within HeLa cells, with intracellular ROS and NO fluorescence intensities reaching 55 % and 57 %, respectively. This breakthrough overcomes the limitations of monotherapy and significantly enhances the synergistic antitumor therapeutic efficacy of PTT/PDT combined with NO gas therapy. Overall, this study provides innovative and highly effective research approaches for gas therapy, phototherapy, and self-assembled nanoparticles.

Conclusions

In this study, self-assembled NO-HA@ICG nanoparticles were prepared using a microfluidic method. The optimized nanoparticle formulation was studied and evaluated in terms of appearance and morphology, particle size, PDI, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, drug loading capacity, and initial stability. The prepared NO-HA@ICG NPs exhibited smaller particle sizes and higher drug encapsulation efficiency. Stability testing results indicated that all parameters demonstrated stability. At the cellular level, nanoparticles exhibit lower toxicity toward cancer cells under non-illuminated conditions. NO-HA@ICG NPs demonstrate photoresponsiveness, generating substantial amounts of NO and ROS within HeLa cells, thereby significantly inhibiting the survival of HeLa cells. NO-HA@ICG nanoparticles exhibit synergistic effects between PTT/ PDT and NO gas therapy. The incorporation of NO donors not only enhances the PTT/PDT activity of the nanoparticles but also endows them with the ability to release NO. Subsequent animal studies will further validate the safety and targeting efficacy of NO-HA@ICG NPs in vivo for cervical cancer, thereby investigating the effectiveness of this nanoparticle design. In summary, NO-HA@ICG NPs can be used for the delivery of NO and photosensitizers, enhancing antitumor activity and safety.
