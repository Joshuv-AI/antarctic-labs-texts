POMC neuron METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1/YTHDF2 pathways safeguard energy balance, body weight, and metabolism
Authors: Yuan Li, Min-Hyun Kim, Decheng Ren, Liangyou Rui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
METTL14 mediates N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, while YTHDC1 and YTHDF2 specifically bind m6A-methylated RNA to regulate RNA fate. POMC neurons constitute the core of the central melanocortin circuit, and POMC deficiency causes obesity in both mice and humans. However, how m6A-based epitranscriptomics regulates melanocortin circuit function remains unclear. Here, we generated and characterized POMC neuron-specific knockout mice lacking Mettl14 (Mettl14ΔPOMC), Ythdc1 (Ythdc1ΔPOMC), or Ythdf2 (Ythdf2 ΔPOMC). Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice develop hyperphagia, obesity, glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis in both sexes under standard chow conditions, accompanied by POMC downregulation. Conversely, POMC neuron-specific overexpression of METTL14 or YTHDC1 protects against diet-induced obesity. In contrast, Ythdf2 ΔPOMC mice are resistant to obesity, revealing an m6A-dependent balance between YTHDC1 and YTHDF2. Mechanistically, the METTL14/YTHDC1 pathway is indispensable for embryonic POMC neurogenesis, while in adults YTHDC1 maintains melanocortin circuit integrity/function. METTL14 and YTHDC1 directly target POMC and ISL1 transcripts to regulate protein expression. POMC neuron-specific restoration of POMC reverses obesity and metabolic phenotypes in Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice, defining an anti-obesity METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1/POMC axis. These findings identify METTL14 as the m6A writer for POMC/ISL1 and YTHDC1 and YTHDF2 as their readers, uncovering a critical role of m6A epitranscriptomic regulation in melanocortin circuit development and maintenance.

Introduction

Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) send projections to neurons in the paraventricular hypothalamus (PVH), constituting the core of the central melanocortin circuit that controls energy balance, body weight, and metabolic homeostasis. POMC is cleaved to produce α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) that binds and activates melanocortin receptor type 4 (MC4R) and MC3R to suppress appetite1–4. POMC deficiency causes obesity and metabolic disease in both mice and humans1,2,5,6. Transcription factor ISL1 plays a critical role in stimulating POMC expression7–9; moreover, it also regulates embryonic POMC neurogenesis2,10. The melanocortin circuit, including POMC protein expression, represents an important therapeutic target for obesity treatment.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of RNA has emerged as a critical mechanism governing cell proliferation, differentiation, and function11. METTL14 binds METTL3 to form an RNA m6A methyltransferase catalyzing m6A methylation (referred to as m6A writer)11,12. YTH domain-containing proteins YTHDC1, YTHDF2, and other family members, referred to as m6A readers, specifically bind m6A-methylated RNA to regulate RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA splicing, RNA stability, and mRNA nuclear export and translation11,12. Global deletion of Mettl14, Mettl3, Ythdc1, or Ythdf2 causes embryonic lethality13–16, highlighting the essential role of the m6A system in supporting development and survival. Despite their importance, the role of m6A writers and readers in the central melanocortin circuit remains largely unexplored.

To investigate their role, we have generated and characterized POMC neuron-specific knockout mice lacking Mettl14 (Mettl14ΔPOMC), Ythdc1 (Ythdc1ΔPOMC), or Ythdf2 (Ythdf2 ΔPOMC). In the current study, we demonstrate that both Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice develop spontaneous obesity and metabolic disease in both sexes on standard chow conditions, accompanied by downregulation of POMC expression. In contrast, Ythdf2 ΔPOMC mice exhibit the opposite phenotype, revealing an m6A-controlled balance between YTHDC1 and YTHDF2. Mechanistically, deletion of Mettl14 or Ythdc1 disrupts embryonic POMC neurogenesis, thereby decreasing POMC neuron numbers. Notably, adult-onset deletion of POMC neuron Ythdc1 also causes spontaneous obesity; conversely, POMC neuron-specific overexpression of METTL14 or YTHDC1 in adult mice protects against high fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity and metabolic disorders. These results uncover a pivotal role of m6A-dependent YTHDC1 pathways in the maintenance of the melanocortin circuit in adult animals. Furthermore, we have identified ISL1 and POMC mRNAs as key targets of the METTL14 and YTHDC1. POMC neuron-specific restoration of POMC expression reverses obesity and metabolic phenotypes in both Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice, thus revealing a previously unrecognized METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1/POMC axis and its role in restraining hyperphagia and excessive body weight gain. Taken together, these findings unveil a critical role of POMC neuron-intrinsic METTL14/YTHDC1/YTHDF2 pathways in governing both the development and the maintenance of the central melanocortin circuit.

Both mRNA and protein levels of METTL14 and YTHDC1 were significantly reduced in the hypothalamus (Fig. 1a–c). Histological analysis unveiled significant reductions in ARC METTL14-expressing neurons and YTHDC1-expressing neurons in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1a). To investigate the pathological role of m6A dysfunctions, we tested if METTL14 restoration might rescue diet-induced obesity. To this end, we generated a Cre-dependent AAV-CAG-DIO-METTL14 vector (Fig. 1e). Male POMC-Cre mice were bilaterally microinjected into the ARC with AAV9-CAG-DIO-METTL14 or AAV9-CAG-DIO-mCherry (control) vector and were subsequently fed an HFD for 12 weeks. Immunoblot and histological analyses verified an ARC- and POMC neuron-specific overexpression of METTL14 (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). METTL14 overexpression profoundly reduced diet-induced obesity, as evidenced by a progressive and profound reduction of body weight in AAV9-CAG-DIO-METTL14-transduced mice compared with AAV9-CAG-DIO-mCherry mice (Fig. 1g). METTL14 overexpression significantly reduced fat mass (Fig. 1h). Consistently, adipocytes in both epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and inguinal WAT (iWAT) were smaller in METTL14-overexpressing mice (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 1e), and the weights of the liver, eWAT, iWAT, and brown adipose tissue (BAT) were significantly reduced (Fig. 1j). POMC neuron-specific overexpression of METTL14 substantially reduced the number and size of hepatic lipid droplets, as revealed by H&E and Oil red O staining, as well as liver triacylglycerol (TAG) levels (Fig. 1i–k). METTL14 overexpression also markedly improved glucose intolerance and insulin resistance, as assessed by glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT), respectively (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Fig. 1f). Together, these results reveal a previously unrecognized anti-obesity strategy through activation of METTL14 pathways in POMC neurons.

POMC neuron-specific deletion of Mettl14 results in obesity and metabolic disorders

To study the role of endogenous METTL14, we generated POMC neuron-specific Mettl14 knockout mice (Mettl14ΔPOMC) by crossing Mettl14f/f mice with POMC-Cre drivers. RNAscope analysis confirmed efficient and specific deletion of Mettl14 in POMC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Strikingly, both male and female Mettl14ΔPOMC mice developed severe obesity despite being maintained on a normal chow diet (Fig. 2a). Fat mass and the weights of iWAT, eWAT (in males), gonadal WAT (gWAT; in females), and BAT were significantly elevated in Mettl14ΔPOMC mice compared with Mettl14f/f littermates (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Histological analyses revealed adipocyte hypertrophy in iWAT and eWAT, as well as enlarged lipid droplets in BAT, indicative of BAT whitening and dysfunction (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Mettl14ΔPOMC mice exhibited hyperinsulinemia (Fig. 2d), a hallmark of insulin resistance. As expected, Mettl14ΔPOMC mice developed glucose intolerance and insulin resistance in both sexes, as assessed by GTT and ITT, respectively (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Hepatic insulin signaling was severely impaired in Mettl14ΔPOMC mice, as evidenced by reduced AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 2g, h). Hepatic lipid droplets, as assessed by H&E and Oil red O staining, and liver TAG levels were significantly elevated in Mettl14ΔPOMC mice (Fig. 2c, i). In addition, body length was increased in Mettl14ΔPOMC mice (Supplementary Fig. 2f), consistent with previous reports that disruption of central melanocortin signaling promotes increased linear length17. Pituitary morphology, size, and POMC cell numbers were comparable between Mettl14f/f and Mettl14ΔPOMC mice (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), suggesting that the observed phenotypes are not attributable to pituitary dysfunction. To examine the role of METTL14 in adult mice, we generated an adult-onset, POMC neuron–specific Mettl14 deletion model. Mettl14f/f mice were crossed with tamoxifen-inducible POMC-CreERT drivers, which have been previously validated18, and the resulting Mettl14f/f;CreERT+/− mice were treated with tamoxifen. However, deletion efficiency was low, with a substantial proportion of POMC neurons retaining METTL14 expression, as assessed by RNAscope analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Consistent with this limited deletion, body weight, fat content, glucose tolerance, and insulin sensitivity were largely unchanged in tamoxifen-treated Mettl14f/f;CreERT+/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). It is likely that limited accessibility of CreERT to the Mettl14 floxed allele may underlie the inefficient recombination observed in this model.

POMC neuron-specific overexpression of YTHDC1 protects against obesity and metabolic disease

We sought to determine whether YTHDC1 mediates METTL14’s anti-obesity function. We generated Cre-dependent AAV9-CAG-DIO-YTHDC1 vector (Fig. 3a) and bilaterally injected the vector into the ARC of male POMC-Cre mice (AAV9-CAG-DIO-mCherry as control). The transduced mice were subsequently fed a HFD to induce obesity. Immunoblotting and histological analysis confirmed that YTHDC1 was modestly overexpressed selectively in POMC neurons (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Remarkably, POMC neuron-specific overexpression of YTHDC1 progressively and significantly decreased body weight (Fig. 3d), accompanied by a significant reduction in fat but not lean mass (Fig. 3e) and reduced adipocyte size in eWAT and iWAT (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 5c). Liver, eWAT, and iWAT weights, hepatic lipid droplet content, and liver TAG levels were all reduced by YTHDC1 overexpression (Fig. 3f–h). YTHDC1 overexpression modestly improved glucose intolerance but markedly mitigated insulin resistance (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 5d). It is likely that YTHDC1 overexpression influences glucose clearance by an additional insulin-independent mechanism. Embryonic POMC neuron–specific deletion of Ythdc1 results in obesity and metabolic dysfunction

We next aimed to investigate the functions of endogenous YTHDC1. To this end, we generated Ythdc1f/f mice using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 4a) and subsequently produced POMC neuron-specific Ythdc1 knockout (Ythdc1ΔPOMC) mice by crossing Ythdc1f/f mice with POMC-Cre mice. Histological analysis confirmed a POMC neuron-restricted deletion, as evidenced by the loss of YTHDC1 expression in a large proportion of tdTomato lineage-labelled POMC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Strikingly, Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice of both sexes developed severe obesity despite being maintained on a normal chow diet (Fig. 4b). Consistently, fat mass and weights of iWAT, eWAT (in males), gWAT (in females), and BAT were significantly elevated in Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice compared with Ythdc1f/f littermates (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6c). Histological examination revealed marked adipocyte hypertrophy in iWAT and eWAT, along with pronounced BAT whitening in Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice (Fig. 4d). Body length was also increased (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Similar to Mettl14ΔPOMC mice, Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice exhibited hyperinsulinemia, glucose intolerance, and insulin resistance in both sexes (Fig. 4e–g and Supplementary Fig. 6e). Insulin-stimulated phosphorylation of AKT in the liver was impaired in both male and female Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice (Fig. 4h–i and Supplementary Figs. 6f, g). Liver weight, hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, and liver TAG levels were all significantly elevated in Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice (Fig. 4d, j). Adult-onset Ythdc1 deletion in POMC neurons causes obesity and metabolic disease

Embryonic deletion might perturb developmental programs in Ythdc1 ΔPOMC mice. To circumvent developmental adaptations, Ythdc1f/f mice were crossed with POMC-CreERT drivers to produce Ythdc1f/f;CreERT+/− mice (i.e., wild-type). Adult Ythdc1f/f;CreERT+/− mice were treated with tamoxifen at 12 weeks of age to selectively disrupt Ythdc1 in POMC neurons. Histological analysis confirmed POMC neuron-specific ablation of Ythdc1, as evidenced by the loss of YTHDC1 expression in a large proportion of tdTomato lineage-labeled POMC neurons (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Tamoxifen-induced adult-onset deletion of Ythdc1 resulted in obesity even under normal chow diet conditions (Fig. 5a). In addition, YTHDC1-deficient mice exhibited glucose intolerance and insulin resistance, as assessed by GTT and ITT, respectively (Fig. 5f). The POMC neuron METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1 pathway critically regulates food intake and energy expenditure

To interrogate how the m6A system protects against obesity, we assessed food intake and energy expenditure in both gain-of-function (METTL14 and YTHDC1 overexpression) and loss-of-function (Mettl14 and Ythdc1 knockout) models described above. POMC neuron-specific overexpression of either METTL14 or YTHDC1 significantly reduced food intake while increasing core body temperature, particularly during the dark phase (Fig. 6a–d). Conversely, ablation of either Mettl14 or Ythdc1 in POMC neurons resulted in hyperphagia accompanied by a reduction in core body temperate (Fig. 6e–h). Consistently, adult-onset deletion of Ythdc1 in POMC neurons also induced hyperphagia and decreased core body temperature (Fig. 6i, j). We further evaluated energy balance using CLAMS. The transcription factor ISL1 is known to play a critical role in embryonic POMC neurogenesis as well as in the regulation of Pomc transcription7,8. To determine whether RNA m6A modification links METTL14 and YTHDC1 to POMC and ISL1 synthesis, we measured m6A enrichment on Isl1 and Pomc transcripts using methylated RNA immunoprecipitation assays (MeRIP). As expected, overexpression of the m6A writer METTL14 increased the abundance of m6A-modified Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs, whereas ablation of endogenous Mettl14 produced the opposite effect (Fig. 7j, k). Consistently, in N2a neuronal cells, METTL14 overexpression similarly enhanced m6A-methylation of Isl1 and Pomc transcripts (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Importantly, HFD feeding―which suppresses hypothalamic METTL14 expression―significantly reduced m6A methylation of Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs in the ARC (Fig. 7l). These findings uncover a previously unrecognized diet-METTL14-m6A-Isl1/Pomc regulatory axis.

We next examined whether YTHDC1 directly binds Isl1 and Pomc transcripts in an m6A-dependent manner using YTHDC1-linked RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP). In N2a neuronal cells overexpressing YTHDC1, YTHDC1 robustly associated with Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs, and co-overexpression of the m6A writer METTL14 further enhanced YTHDC1 binding to both transcripts (Fig. 7m). These results indicate that METTL14 and YTHDC1 function, respectively, as the m6A writer and reader for Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs in neurons.

To define the functional consequences of YTHDC1 binding, we next measured POMC and ISL1 synthesis in N2a cells. Co-overexpression of METTL14 and YTHDC1 synergistically increased POMC protein levels (Fig. 7n), whereas METTL14 overexpression alone was sufficient, likely via endogenous YTHDC1, to elevate ISL1 protein abundance (Supplementary Fig. 10d). In line with these findings, overexpression of METTL14 and YTHDC1 increased Pomc mRNA stability, as determined by transcription inhibition using actinomycin D; in contrast, METTL14 exerted a limited effect on Isl1 mRNA stability(Supplementary Fig. 10e). We next measured POMC and ISL1 translations using O-propargyl-puromycin (OPP) pulldown assays19,20. Overexpression of METTL14 and YTHDC1 markedly enhanced POMC translation in N2a cells, as evidenced by increased levels of OPP-labeled POMC protein (Fig. 7o and Supplementary Fig. 10f). Similarly, METTL14 overexpression significantly promoted ISL1 translation (Fig. 7o and Supplementary Fig. 10g). Collectively, these results uncover a METTL14-m6A-YTHDC1-ISL1/POMC axis.

To directly examine embryonic POMC neurogenesis, we employed tdTomato-based lineage-tracing approaches. Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice were crossed with Rosa26LSL-tdTomato+/- reporter mice to genetically label POMC-expressing cells. Histological analysis revealed a significant reduction in tdTomato+ POMC neurons in Mettl14ΔPOMC pups at P1 and P10, as well as in Ythdc1ΔPOMC pups at P10 (Fig. 9c, d). Because no differences in apoptosis were detected between knockout and control groups, we next assessed POMC progenitor proliferation using BrdU incorporation assays. POMC progenitors were labelled at embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5), and BrdU+tdTomato+ POMC neurons were quantified at P1. The number of BrdU+tdTomato+ cells were significantly reduced in both Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC animals (Fig. 9e-f). Collectively, these findings indicate that the progenitor-intrinsic METTL14/YTHDC1 pathway critically supports embryonic POMC neurogenesis and melanocortin circuit development.

We next explored the underlying molecular mechanisms by assessing m6A modification and expression of key hypothalamic transcriptional regulators that govern POMC neurogenesis, including ISL1, SIX3, Nkx2.1, MASH1, TBX3, and PRDM121,22,23. m6A methylation of Isl1, Six3, Prdm12, and Pomc mRNAs was significantly inhibited in Mettl14ΔPOMC embryos (Fig. 9g), indicating that METTL14 serves as the m6A writer for these transcripts. Moreover, Isl1, and Six3 mRNA expression were significantly suppressed in both Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC embryos at E18 (Fig. 9h). Consistently, ISL1 protein levels were significantly reduced in Mettl14ΔPOMC mice at both P1 and 8 weeks of age (Fig. 9i and Supplementary Fig. 12c). Together, these findings suggest that the METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1 pathway safeguards POMC neurogenesis and melanocortin circuit development, at least in part, by sustaining ISL1 and POMC expression.

Among these, YTHDF2 is known to mediate m6A-dependent mRNA decay. To test whether YTHDF2 functionally counteracts YTHDC1, both proteins were expressed in N2a neuronal cells. While YTHDC1 alone increased ISL1 and POMC levels, and coexpression of YTHDF2 attenuated these effects (Supplementary Figs. 13a-b). Discussion

While the central melanocortin system is well known to regulate energy balance and body weight in both mice and humans, a key knowledge gap remains regarding the role of RNA modifications and RNA-binding proteins in regulating embryonic melanocortin circuit development and in maintaining its integrity and function in adulthood. In the current study, we defined distinct functions of the METTL14/YTHDC1 epitranscriptomic pathway in embryonic melanocortin circuit development and its maintenance in adulthood, and uncovered opposing functions for YTHDC1 and YTHDF2 in POMC neurons. We demonstrated that embryonic deletion of either Mettl14 or Ythdc1 substantially reduced POMC neuron numbers due to impaired progenitor cell proliferation, resulting in severe obesity and metabolic dysfunction in adult mice. METTL14 and YTHDC1 directly targeted ISL1 mRNA and enhanced ISL1 protein expression, suggesting that a METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1/ISL1 epitranscriptomic axis contributes, at least in part, to the metabolic phenotypes observed in Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice. Because POMC-expressing progenitor cells also give rise to AgRP and other hypothalamic neuron subpopulations10,24,25, and future studies will be required to determine whether alterations in these lineages contribute to the observed phenotypes. Moreover, as Mettl14 and Ythdc1 are also deleted in nucleus of the solitary tract (NST) POMC neurons, we cannot exclude a contribution of the NST METTL14/YTHDC1 pathway to certain aspects of the phenotype. We also observed, unexpectedly, that Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice were prone, whereas Ythdf2ΔPOMC mice were resistant, to obesity. In cell culture, YTHDC1 competed with YTHDF2 for binding to POMC mRNA,with YTHDC1 enhancing POMC synthesis and YTHDF2 antagonizing this effect. These findings support an m6A-dependent balance between YTHDC1 and YTHDF2 that shapes melanocortin circuit function (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Importantly, adult-onset deletion of Ythdc1 in POMC neurons caused hyperphagia, obesity, and metabolic disorders, whereas overexpression of YTHDC1 or METTL14 in adult mice―overexpression was restricted to ARC but not NST POMC neurons―protected against HFD-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction. These findings firmly establish a pivotal role of the METTL14/YTHDC1 epitranscriptomic pathway in adulthood by maintaining melanocortin circuit function. METTL14 and YTDHC1 directly targeted POMC mRNA to enhance POMC protein expression, and hypothalamic POMC expression was markedly reduced in Mettl14ΔPOMC and Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice. Restoration of POMC expression reversed obesity and metabolic phenotypes, uncovering a previously unrecognized anti-obesity METTL14/m6A/YTHDC1/POMC epitranscriptomic pathway. Nonetheless, additional non-POMC RNA targets of METTL14 and YTHDC1 may also contribute to these phenotypes and remain to be identified.

During preparation of this manuscript, Shen et al reported mice with pan-hypothalamic deletion of Mettl14, Ythdc1, or Ythdf226. Although both studies identified a similar role of METTL14 and YTHDC1 in embryonic POMC neurogenesis, differences emerge between pan-hypothalamic and POMC neuron-specific loss of Ythdc1 and Ythdf2. In Shen et al., pan-hypothalamic deletion of Ythdc1 produced only mild metabolic alterations, and pan-hypothalamic Ythdf2 knockout mice appeared normal. In contrast, POMC neuron-specific ablation of Ythdc1 in the present study caused severe obesity and metabolic dysfunction, whereas POMC neuron-specific deletion of Ythdf2 conferred resistance to obesity and metabolic disorders. We propose that YTHDC1 and YTHDF2 exert cell type–specific and opposing functions in POMC versus non-POMC hypothalamic neurons. Consequently, deletion of Ythdc1 or Ythdf2 in non-POMC neurons in pan-hypothalamic models may mask or counteract the phenotypes arising from their loss in POMC neurons. Further studies will be required to directly test this hypothesis.

We further show that obesogenic signals from HFD feeding reduced expression METTL14 and YTHDC1 in the hypothalamus, as well as m6A modification of Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs. These findings indicate that the METTL14 -m6A-YTHDC1-ISL1/POMC epitranscriptomic pathway is disrupted by obesogenic environments, thereby promoting obesity and its associated diseases (Supplementary Fig. 14). Thus, this m6A-centered epitranscriptomic pathway represents a potential therapeutic target for obesity and related diseases.

Methods

Animals

Mettl14f/f and Ythdf2f/f mice were previously described16,19,27. Mettl14f/f and Ythdf2f/f mice were crossed with POMC-Cre drivers (JAX #005965) to obtain Mettl14ΔPOMC (Mettl14f/f;POMC-Cre+/-) and Ythdf2ΔPOMC (Ythdf2f/f;POMC-Cre+/-) mice (C57BL/6 background), respectively. Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice (JAX 007914) were crossed with POMC-Cre or Mettl14ΔPOMC mice to obtain tdTomatoPOMC mice (Rosa26LSL-tdTomato+/-;POMC-Cre+/-) or tdTomatoPOMCMettl14ΔPOMC mice (Rosa26LSL-tdTomat +/-;Mettl14f/f;POMC-Cre+/-), respectively. POMC-CreERT2 mice were crossed with Mettl14f/f mice to generate Mettl14f/f;CreERT mice. Mice were housed on a 12 h light-dark cycle at ambient temperature of 25 0C and humidity of 30% and were fed ad libitum a normal chow diet (9% fat; TestDiet, St. Louis, MO) or a HFD (60% fat in calories; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ).

Ythdc1 knockout mice

Ythdc1f/+ ES cells were obtained from the European Mouse Mutant Cell Repository (EuMMCR, EPD0113_2_D09) to generate Ythdc1f/f mice (C57BL/6 J background). Ythdc1f/f mice were crossed with POMC-Cre or Mettl14f/f;CreERT drivers to obtain Ythdc1ΔPOMC mice (Ythdc1f/f;POMC-Cre+/-) and Ythdc1f/f;CreERT (Mettl14f/f;POMC-CreERT+/-) mice, respectively.

Adult-onset deletion of Mettl14 or Ythdc1

Mettl14f/f;CreERT and Ythdc1f/f;CreERT males were treated with tamoxifen (100 mg/kg body weight, 5 times, 5 days apart) starting at 8 or 12 weeks of age. Mettl14f/f and Ythdc1f/f mice were treated with tamoxifen as control.

AAV9-CAG-DIO-METTL14, AAV9-CAG-DIO-METTL14, AAV-DIO-POMC, and AAV-DIO-α-MSH vectors and stereotaxic microinjection

We prepared Cre-dependent AAV-CAG-DIO-METTL14 (human), AAV-CAG-DIO-YTHDC1 (human), or AAV-CAG-DIO-mCherry vector. AAV-DIO-POMC and AAV-DIO-α-MSH vectors (dj serotype), which are available from Addgene (POMC: Cat# 239042; α-MSH: Cat#239044), were provided by Dr. Qingchun Tong (University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston). POMC-Cre males (7 weeks old) were isoflurane-anesthetized and mounted on an Ultra Precise Small Animal Stereotaxic Alignment System (David KOPF Instruments, Tujunga, CA). A small opening was made in the skull. AAV vector (0.3 µl) was bilaterally microinjected into the ARC (mm: −1.5 a-p, ±0.3 m-l, −5.9 d-v), using an UltraMicroPump with SYS-Micro4 Controller (UMP3-2, World Precision Instruments Inc, Sarasota, FL). One week later, mice were fed an HFD for 12 weeks.

RNAScope

RNAscope assays were performed by the Microscopy, Imaging, and Cellular Physiology Core, Michigan Diabetes Research Center, as described previously28. Mettl14 and Pomc transcripts were visualized by Pomc (ACD #314081) and Mettl14 probes (ACD #1166331-C2), respectively, using RNAscope® Multiplex Fluorescent in situ assay Reagent V2 Kit (Cat# 323110).

CLAMS

Body composition was measured using EchoMRI (Houston, TX). Energy expenditure was measured using the Comprehensive Laboratory Monitoring System (CLAMS, Columbus Instruments) in the University of Michigan Metabolic, Physiological, and Behavioral Core. Data was analyzed using ANCOVA by Notational Mouse Metabolic Phenotyping Center (MMPC): http://www.mmpc.org/shared/regression.aspx.

Fat and lean mass

Fat content and lean mass were measured using a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry pDexa (Norland Stratec). Heads, necks, and tails were not included in data analysis.

GTT and ITT

Mice were fasted overnight (GTT) or 6 h (ITT) and intraperitoneally injected with glucose (2 g/kg body weight, GTT) or human insulin (1 unit/kg body weight, ITT). Blood samples were collected from tail veins to measure blood glucose levels at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after injection. Plasma insulin levels were measured using mouse insulin ELISA kits (CRYSTAL CHEM, Downers Grove, IL).

Liver TAG

Liver samples were homogenized in 1% acetic acid and extracted using 80% chloroform/methanol (2:1). The organic fraction was dried in a chemical hood, resuspended in a KOH (3 M)/ethanol solution, incubated at 70 °C for 1 h, and resuspended in MgCl2 (0.75 M). Aqueous fractions were used to measure TAG levels using a TAG assay kit (Pointe Scientific Inc., Canton, MI).

Immunoblotting

Tissues or cell cultures were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM Na3VO4, 100 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin). Tissue or cell extracts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies (Supplementary Table 1). Full uncropped scans were provided in the Source data file.

Immunostaining

Brain sections were prepared using Leica cryostat (Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Nussloch, Germany), and immunostained with the indicated antibodies (Supplementary Table 1). Images were visualized using BX51 Microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a DP72 digital camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). POMC fiber density in the PVH was quantified using ImageJ and normalized to view area. H&E and Oil red O staining were described previously28.

BrdU incorporation

Dams were intraperitoneally injected with BrdU (80 mg/kg body weight, in PBS) on E10.5, E11.5, and E12.5. Brains were dissected at P1, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4 °C, and stained with anti-BrdU antibody.

Quantitative real time RT-PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagents (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). The first-strand cDNAs were synthesized using random primers and M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI). qPCR was performed using Radiant™ SYBR Green 2X Lo-ROX qPCR Kits (Alkali Scientific, Pompano Beach, FL), a StepOnePlus RT PCR System (Life Technologies Corporation, NY, USA), and respective primers (Supplementary Table 2).

MeRIP

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagents. RNA (10 μg) was incubated at 4 °C with anti-m6A antibody (1 μl) for 2 h and then with protein-A agarose beads (10 μl) for an additional 2 h (with rotations). The beads were washed with PBS, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent to measure m6A-marked Isl1 and Pomc mRNAs by qPCR (normalized to inputs).

O-propargyl-puromycin (OPP)-mediated pulldown assay

N2a neurons (ATCC, CCL-131) were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Mouse Isl1 cDNA was prepared from hypothalamic mRNA using RT-PCR and inserted into pcDNA3.1(+) vectors. Forward: 5′-ATGGATCCACAGATATGGGAGACATGGGCGATC-3′; reverse: 5′-ATATCTAGACATTAGAACAGACTTCATGCGCTT-3′. Isl1, POMC, METTL14, and YTHDC1 plasmids were cotransfected into N2a cells using Lipofectamine 3000. Two days later, POMC and ISL1 protein translations were measured by OPP pulldown assays as we described previously19.

RNA stability assay

Isl1 or POMC plasmids were cotransfected into N2a cells with METTTL14 and YTHDC1 plasmids. After 20 h, cells were treated with actinomycin D (5 µg/ml) for various durations. Total RNA was extracted to measure Isl1 or POMC mRNAs levels by qPCR (normalized to 36B4 levels).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± SEM. Differences were analyzed by a 2-tailed Student’s t-test (2 groups) and ANOVA/Bonferroni post-test (>2 groups) using GraphPad Prism 8. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival rates were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method.
