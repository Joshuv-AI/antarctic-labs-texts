2
Prerequisites

The implicit (also: backward) Euler method

E:{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:y=E(t,m,x)

(9)

integrates systems of ordinary differential equations

\frac{dx}{dt}=G(t,x(t))

(10)

with differentiable right-hand side

G:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
from a given initial state

x=x(0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to a given target time
t=\check{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}
as
y=x(\check{t})=E(\check{t},\check{m},x(0))
using an
equidistant discretization of the time interval
[0,\check{t}]
with a given number
m=\check{m}>0
time steps.
In the following,
E
is required to be
differentially invertible

at
\check{t},\check{m},x(0)
, that is, it needs to be differentiable and its
Jacobian must be invertible.
The corresponding
differential inverse

\left(\frac{dE}{dx}(\check{t},\check{m},x(0))\right)^{-1}\cdot v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is, for example, required in the context of the inverse problem, aiming to
estimate the initial state
x(0)
for given observations of
x(\check{t}).

Feasibility of this inversion depends on a number of conditions,
which are assumed to be satisfied, see, for example,
[
3
]
. Generalization for scenarios that
require regularization is the subject of ongoing investigations.

2.1
Integration (Outer Iteration)

The implicit Euler method approximates the time derivative in
Equation (
10
) with a backward finite difference obtained by
truncation of the Taylor expansion

x(t)=x(t-\Delta t)+\frac{dx}{dt}\cdot\Delta t+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta t^{2})

of
x
at
t-\Delta t
after the first-order term, yielding the linearization

\frac{dx}{dt}\approx\frac{x(t)-x(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\;.

Equation (
10
) is replaced by its discrete equivalent

\frac{x(t)-x(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}=G(t,x(t))\;.

Time steps of size
0<\Delta t\leq t
yield a sequence of iterates

x_{i},

i=1,\ldots m,
such that

\frac{x_{i}-x_{i-1}}{\Delta t}=G(i\cdot\Delta t,x_{i})

(11)

and where
x_{i}=x(i\cdot\Delta t)
implying
x_{m}=x(m\cdot\Delta t)=x(t).
Uniform time stepping yields
\Delta t=\frac{t}{m}.

Convergence of the implicit Euler method
depends on a number of conditions,
which are assumed to be satisfied. See, for example,
[
2
]
for details.

2.2
Root Finding (Inner Iteration)

Equation (
11
) implies the system of nonlinear equations

F(x_{i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)=x_{i}-x_{i-1}-\Delta t\cdot G(i\cdot\Delta t,x_{i})=0\;.

(12)

It needs to be solved
m
times to obtain the solution
x(t).

Linearization of
F
at
x_{i}
yields

\displaystyle 0=F(x_{j+1,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)

\displaystyle=F(x_{j,i}+\Delta x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)

\displaystyle=F(x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)+\frac{dF}{dx_{i}}(x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)\cdot\Delta x_{j,i}\;.

Setting

F^{\prime}\equiv\frac{dF}{dx_{i}},

the solution of the resulting linear system

F^{\prime}(x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)\cdot\Delta x_{j,i}=-F(x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)

is followed by updates

x_{j+1,i}=x_{j,i}+\Delta x_{j,i},\quad j=0,\ldots,p\;,

where, for the purpose of cost analysis, convergence is assumed to be reached
after (at most)
p
Newton steps for all
m
iterations of the implicit Euler method. W.l.o.g., we use
x_{0,i}=x_{i-1}.

Differentiation of Equation (
12
) with respect to
x_{i}

yields

F^{\prime}(x_{j,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)=I_{n}-x_{i-1}-\Delta t\cdot G^{\prime}(i\cdot\Delta t,x_{j,i})=0

which is solved by Newton’s method

N:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\quad x_{i}=N(x_{0,i},x_{i-1},\Delta t)

for
given
G^{\prime}\equiv\frac{dG}{dx_{i}}.

Implicit Euler integration
amounts to the solution of
m
systems of nonlinear equations at the expense
of
\mathcal{O}(p\cdot n^{3})
each. Both accumulation and factorization of
(the dense)

F^{\prime}
induce a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
assuming evaluation of

F
at a cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}).
Potential sparsity of
F^{\prime}
can and should
be
exploited.
The total cost of the implicit Euler method can, hence, be estimated as

\mathcal{O}(m\cdot p\cdot n^{3})
.

6
Conclusion

Conceptually, differential inversion of a differentially invertible composite function

f(x)=f_{m}(f_{m-1}(\ldots f_{1}(x)\ldots):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

involves two steps:

1.

accumulation of the Jacobian
f^{\prime}=\frac{df}{dx}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n};

2.

solution of the linear system
f^{\prime}\cdot w=v
for a given
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

yielding

w=(f^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot v.

The computational effort is typically dominated by the former, as the cost
of evaluating
f
often exceeds
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}).

Symbolic (as well as structural) analysis of
f
may yield options for
avoiding the accumulation of the Jacobian. A notable gain in computational
performance can be expected.
Implicit Euler integration
of initial value problems represents one prominent example, where
f
amounts
to the sequence of Euler steps. Even the solution of the linear system becomes
obsolete in this case. The computational cost of the naive approach can thus be reduced by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}).
