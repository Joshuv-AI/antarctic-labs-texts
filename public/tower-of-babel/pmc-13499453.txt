Diagnosis, management, and prevention of Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever: a Delphi-based consensus study in Türkiye

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCrimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a widely distributed viral haemorrhagic fever with expanding geographic range. As new regions encounter the disease, clinicians must make high-stakes decisions despite limited standardised management frameworks.MethodsIn this multicentre, modified Delphi study, infectious diseases specialists were recruited from 19 centres in Türkiye via an open call through the professional communication channels of the Turkish Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (known as KLİMİK). Eligibility was determined based on self-reported clinical experience. Eligible participants reported managing more than ten patients with CCHF as primary physicians, with no additional demographic or professional exclusion criteria applied. The Delphi process included three sequential rounds of anonymous online surveys (between Sept 26 2025, and Jan 1, 2026) and an interim structured clarification meeting (Nov 28–29, 2025), to map areas of agreement and heterogeneity across diagnosis, monitoring, therapeutics, and exposure management. Statements were analysed using Likert-scale methodology. Responses were summarised using medians, interquartile ranges, and proportions of agreement. Consensus levels were defined a priori.FindingsOf 45 ID specialists invited to participate in Round 1, 35 completed the questionnaire and were included in the analysis (78% response rate). Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar from database inception to September 2025 without language restrictions to identify studies on the diagnosis, management, and prevention of Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever. Available evidence is largely based on observational studies and expert opinion, with substantial variability in clinical practice and limited standardised management frameworks.

Added value of this study

This multicentre Delphi study systematically captures areas of agreement and heterogeneity among experienced infectious diseases specialists managing CCHF in a high incidence setting. By quantifying consensus across diagnosis, monitoring, therapeutics, and exposure management, it provides a structured overview of prevailing clinical practice patterns and highlights domains of convergence and ongoing uncertainty.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings support a clearer understanding of current clinical practice in the absence of robust evidence and may inform clinicians in both endemic and newly affected regions. Prospective studies are needed to validate key therapeutic and management strategies.

Introduction

Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is one of the most widespread viral haemorrhagic fevers worldwide, with a geographic distribution spanning Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and southeastern Europe.1 CCHF is maintained in enzootic cycles involving ticks and vertebrate hosts. Despite Hyalomma spp. are the primary and epidemiologically dominant vectors, viral RNA has also been detected in other tick genera.2 Human infection occurs mainly through tick bites or contact with infected animal blood or tissues, and less commonly via exposure to body fluids of infected patients.3 Clinically, CCHF ranges from nonspecific febrile illness to rapidly progressive haemorrhagic disease, with reported case fatality rates reaching up to 30% in some settings.3

By the last two decades, climate change, ecological disruption, and shifts in animal husbandry have contributed to the expansion of tick habitats, facilitating the northward and westward spread of Hyalomma species.4 Recently, emerging CCHF infections were reported from Spain,5 Portugal,6 and Greece.7 These developments underscore that CCHF is no longer confined to historically recognised endemic zones and highlight the urgent need for preparedness in regions with limited prior clinical experience of the disease.

Despite its growing geographic footprint and potential for nosocomial and household transmission, clinical management of CCHF remains largely experience-driven. Although national and international authorities have issued case definitions and surveillance guidance, there are still gaps in almost all steps of case management including diagnosis, therapeutics and surveillance of the cases. Accordingly, recent global CCHF research priorities emphasise the need for standardised case definitions and harmonised, severity-based clinical management guidance for both routine care and clinical research.8

Türkiye represents one of the countries with the highest cumulative experience of CCHF globally, having managed around 20,000 confirmed cases since 2002.9 National surveillance data indicate that CCHF in Türkiye predominantly affects rural agricultural populations. Approximately two-thirds of cases report tick exposure, and a similar proportion occur among individuals engaged in farming or animal husbandry.10,11 In contrast, exposures consistent with human-to-human transmission account for around four percent of the reported cases.10

This sustained exposure has generated substantial knowledge among clinicians, particularly infectious diseases (ID) specialists working in endemic and referral centres. However, much of this expertise remains undocumented and inconsistently applied, so that it limits its global utility. By this Delphi study among Turkish ID specialists, we aimed to capture expert judgements, quantifying consensus and disagreement. Areas that failed to achieve consensus emerged as indicators of clinical uncertainty and were interpreted as priority domains requiring further prospective research.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

The aim of this project was to characterise areas of expert agreement and disagreement regarding the diagnosis, therapeutics, and surveillance-related aspects of CCHF, including prevention and protection measures. We applied a modified classical Delphi method to achieve structured consensus on key clinical and public health decision points for CCHF.

The classical Delphi method is a structured, iterative approach designed to achieve convergence of expert opinion in areas where empirical evidence is limited or heterogeneous. It is grounded in anonymity of responses, iterative rounds of rating, controlled feedback between rounds, and predefined quantitative criteria for determining consensus.12 We selected the Delphi method because the evidence base for several high-impact areas in CCHF remains incomplete, and real-life practices vary in endemic centres. In this context, experimental designs are not feasible for many questions, and observational analyses alone cannot provide adequate evidence for critical clinical decisions. The Delphi method is widely used in health sciences and provides a rigorous, transparent approach to synthesise expert judgement, quantify agreement and disagreement, and iteratively refine statements to support harmonised recommendations.13

Given the clinical focus of the study, ID specialists with expertise in the management of CCHF were included. To ensure broad national representation, recruitment was not restricted to members of the core consortium. An open call was disseminated through the professional communication channels of KLİMİK (Turkish Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases; ESCMID-affiliated), inviting any ID specialist to participate who had personally followed patients with CCHF. Participants were asked to report the number of patients with CCHF they had managed as the primary physician. Eligibility was determined based on self-reported clinical experience, and Delphi analyses included all ID specialists who reported managing more than ten patients with CCHF as primary physicians, with no additional demographic or professional exclusion criteria applied. Data on race and ethnicity were not collected, as these characteristics were not considered relevant to the study objectives. Participant characteristics were described to reflect the geographic distribution and clinical experience of the panel.

Ethics

Ethical approval was not sought for this study, as it involved voluntary participation of healthcare professionals in a Delphi survey and did not include patient data. Participation was entirely voluntary, and responses were collected anonymously; therefore, written informed consent was not required. All participants in the Delphi panel are listed as authors on this manuscript.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Before initiation of the Delphi process, a focused narrative review was undertaken to map key domains relevant to CCHF and to identify areas of clinical uncertainty. Searches were conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar in September 2025, without date restrictions, using combinations of terms including “Crimean-Congo h(a)emorrhagic fever”, “CCHF”, “epidemiology”, “diagnosis”, “case definition”, “admission”, “monitoring”, “antiviral therapy”, “ribavirin”, “supportive care”, “follow-up”, “discharge”, “protection”, “post-exposure prophylaxis”, “nosocomial infection”, “healthcare worker”, “occupational exposure”, and “infection prevention and control”. Relevant national and international guidance documents, as well as publicly available surveillance reports, were also reviewed. Two authors (DG and DY) screened relevant publications without language restrictions. Extracted information was cross-checked within the study team. No formal quality assessment was undertaken. Instead, emphasis was placed on widely accepted guidelines, national datasets, and larger observational studies.

Survey rounds

The Delphi process consisted of three sequential online rounds, each open for two weeks, with three-week intervals between rounds. All Delphi rounds were conducted electronically using a secure, web-based survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA).

Round 1 was conducted between Sept 26 and Oct 6, 2025, using a structured survey developed based on the literature and input from the coordination committee. The coordination committee comprised authors (ÖE, DG, DY, FP, ŞK, BM) who were responsible for the methodological design and development of the survey. Round 1 comprised 47 multiple-choice questions, one matrix-format table with seven sub questions, and 18 open-ended questions covering clinical and public health domains. Prior to dissemination, the survey was pilot tested by three external specialists not involved in the Delphi panel, and the final version was refined based on their feedback. Responses from Round 1 were used to generate candidate consensus statements. The number of candidate statements was not predefined. In Delphi studies, the aim is comprehensive coverage of the relevant domains. These statements were assessed both quantitatively and qualitatively: quantitative analysis was based on levels of agreement with multiple-choice items, and qualitative analysis involved thematic interpretation of open-ended responses to determine the frequency with which concepts were mentioned or supported. Statements endorsed by more than 90% of participants or by fewer than ten percents of participants were predefined as having reached consensus and were not reassessed in subsequent rounds. The remaining statements were reviewed for overlap, clarity, and conceptual similarity, and were merged or revised as needed.

In Round 2, conducted between Nov 3–17, 2025, statements generated from Round 1 were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Responses were summarised to assess agreement and dispersion. During this phase, it became apparent that certain statements were interpreted inconsistently across participants, and clarifying questions were received from several experts. A face-to-face meeting was held in Ankara on Nov 28–29, 2025, to ensure that all survey items were understood clearly and consistently. This in-person session formed part of the study design and extended the classical Delphi structure. The meeting was moderated by an impartial facilitator and focused on identifying ambiguities in wording and interpretation, addressing questions raised by participants, and discussing emerging research areas highlighted during the survey process. Statements were revised for clarity, and selected items were split, merged, or removed where necessary. No voting took place during the meeting, individual responses were not challenged, and discussions that could influence subsequent ratings were deliberately avoided. The sole purpose of the meeting was to ensure a shared and clearly defined understanding of all survey statements before the final round.

In Round 3, held between Dec 15, 2025, and Jan 1, 2026, participants re-rated the refined statements using the same 5-point Likert scale, with structured feedback provided in the form of aggregated results from the previous round. Two weeks after dissemination of the Round 3 questionnaire, participants who had completed the first two rounds but had not yet responded to the final round were contacted by telephone and reminded to complete the survey. Following completion of Round 3, responses from experts who participated in all three rounds were analysed for the final time. Based on predefined consensus criteria and statement subdomains, results were synthesised into final summary tables and organised into a structured clinical framework reflecting areas of agreement among experts.

Data analysis

Statements evaluated in Rounds 2 and 3 were analysed using Likert-scale methodology. Responses were summarised using medians, interquartile ranges (IQRs) as measures of dispersion, and proportions of agreement. Consensus levels were defined a priori. Very high consensus was defined as an IQR of less than 1, a median score of 5, and more than 95% of experts selecting a score of 5. High consensus was defined as an IQR of less than 2, a median score of 4–5, and more than 80% of experts selecting scores of 4–5. Consensus achieved was defined as an IQR of less than 3, a median score of 4–5, and 65–79% of experts selecting scores of 4–5. An IQR greater than 3, a median score below 4, or fewer than 64% of experts selecting scores of 4–5 was considered insufficient for consensus. Analyses were performed using complete-case data only, including only fully completed questionnaires.

Role of the funding source

No funding was received for this work.

Results

Participants

The Round 1 survey was initiated with a total of 45 ID specialists, of whom 35 completed the questionnaire and were included in the analysis (78% response rate). All 35 eligible participants were invited to Round 2, and 32 (91%) completed the questionnaire. All 32 participants completed Round 3, with no attrition between Rounds 2 and 3.

Overview of the Delphi process

In Round 1, responses were synthesised into 146 candidate consensus statements. Of these, 72 statements met predefined criteria for early consensus and were not re-evaluated in subsequent rounds (Supplementary Appendix 2). The remaining 74 statements addressed areas of greater clinical variability or uncertainty and were carried forward for further evaluation.

In Round 2, 57 consolidated statements were assessed. Thirty-eight statements (66·7%) met predefined criteria for consensus, including seven reaching very high consensus, 15 high consensus, and 16 moderate consensuses. Nineteen statements did not meet consensus thresholds and were classified as demonstrating insufficient agreement.

Between Rounds 2 and 3, a face-to-face consensus meeting was convened in Ankara, Türkiye, to review areas of heterogeneity. A key outcome of this meeting was agreement on a unified framework of three clinical case definitions: suspected, probable, and definitive CCHF. Following this framework, statements were restructured: 23 remained unchanged, 10 were removed, 15 were revised, and eight were divided into 18 distinct statements. After consolidation, 56 statements were included in the final Round 3 questionnaire.

In Round 3, 39 of 56 statements (69·6%) met predefined criteria for consensus, including eight reaching very high consensus, 22 high consensus, and nine moderate consensuses. Seventeen statements did not reach consensus and were classified as demonstrating persistent heterogeneity of expert opinion.

Clinical suspicion and case definitions

Early consensus in Round 1 supported prompt tick removal in all cases. The application of molecular testing in patients presenting from endemic regions with a history of tick exposure and compatible clinical symptoms, was agreed upon. Following the face-to-face meeting, a unified three case-definition framework was formally adopted. A suspected case was defined as the presence of at least one epidemiological risk factor together with compatible clinical symptoms. A probable case was defined as a suspected case with either documented tick exposure or supportive laboratory abnormalities. A definitive case required laboratory confirmation by molecular or serological testing (Fig. 3). In highly endemic regions, hospitalisation and diagnostic sampling for suspected cases were supported, whereas in subendemic referral centres with rapid diagnostic capacity, outpatient management of selected suspected cases was considered acceptable. Regardless of endemicity, consensus supported hospitalisation and diagnostic testing for all probable cases. Persistent heterogeneity was observed regarding hospital admission of asymptomatic individuals following tick bite, for which consensus was not achieved.

Hospital admission and inpatient monitoring

Early consensus supported prompt laboratory evaluation at presentation, including complete blood count (CBC), liver enzymes (ALT, AST, LDH, CK), and coagulation parameters (PT and aPTT). Following subsequent Delphi rounds, measurement of fibrinogen was emphasised as a marker for severity assessment. Monitoring of renal function was also supported for early identification of multiorgan involvement and potential treatment-related toxicity. Some experts favoured performing comprehensive laboratory testing at the initial blood draw to minimise repeated invasive procedures, particularly in patients at risk of evolving coagulopathy (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Therapeutic management

Early consensus was rarely achieved within the therapeutic domain. After iterative evaluation, consensus supported initiation of ribavirin within the first four days of disease in patients with confirmed CCHF. Agreement was also reached for initiation in probable cases, yet, at a lower consensus level. No consensus was reached regarding initiation of ribavirin irrespective of symptom onset.

For supportive care, consensus supported a risk-adapted transfusion approach in which the presence and severity of bleeding guided intervention (Table 2, Fig. 3). Lower platelet thresholds were considered acceptable in the absence of bleeding, whereas higher thresholds were supported in the presence of haemorrhagic manifestations. Continued platelet support after initiation for active bleeding was encouraged until stabilisation.

Selective use of corticosteroids in severe cases or in patients with rapidly worsening severity scores reached consensus. No agreement was observed regarding a specific corticosteroid agent or dosing regimen. Routine use of immunomodulatory therapies did not reach consensus.

Follow-up and discharge

Consensus was reached on structured clinical and laboratory reassessment during hospitalisation to guide therapeutic decisions and detect early deterioration. The use of formal severity scoring approaches was supported as part of this process. The Dokuzoğuz Severity Scoring Index (SSI) was the most applied tool in this cohort.15 The use of SSI in this study represents prevailing practice rather than a preferential recommendation.

Virological clearance was not required for discharge. Discharge decisions were based on clinical stability, resolution of active bleeding, and sustained improvement in key laboratory parameters over consecutive days.

Occupational exposure

Early consensus was reached on routine application of standard personal protective equipment (PPE) for patients with any type of patient with CCHF. Standard PPE included gloves, gowns, and surgical masks during patient contact. In addition, use of eye protection and appropriate barrier precautions was supported in any situation with potential exposure to blood or bodily fluids including risk of mucosal splash. Use of N95 respirators was agreed upon for healthcare workers (HCW) involved in aerosol-generating procedures, including airway bleeding management, endotracheal intubation, bronchoscopy, and laboratory handling of specimens. In clinical scenarios where the likelihood of exposure to blood or body fluids is increased, such as massive haemorrhage, enhanced protective measures were reported by several centres, including use of full-face shields, fluid-resistant gowns, and double gloves.

Consensus was achieved for administration of ribavirin post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following high-risk occupational exposure, primarily defined as direct contact with blood or other bodily fluids. Agreement was also reached for PEP when appropriate respiratory protection (N95 mask) was not used during aerosol-generating procedures involving confirmed CCHF cases. The agreed dosing regimen was 500 mg four times daily for one week.

Protection of household contacts

Consensus was reached on precautionary measures aimed at reducing exposure to potentially infectious bodily fluids following hospital discharge. Experts agreed that patients should avoid sexual intercourse for at least two weeks after discharge. For patients who received ribavirin therapy, avoidance of unprotected sexual intercourse for six months was agreed upon.

Additional measures included avoiding sharing personal items such as towels, bedding, and eating utensils for two weeks. Use of separate toilet facilities for two weeks after discharge was also agreed upon where feasible. Adherence to airborne precautions during close contact was supported as a precautionary measure.

Overall, strongest agreement was observed in domains of early recognition, structured monitoring, and discharge criteria. Greater heterogeneity persisted in therapeutic decision-making, particularly regarding antiviral initiation, transfusion strategies in selected scenarios, and role of immunomodulatory drugs.

Discussion

We present a Delphi-based consensus on CCHF diagnosis, therapeutics, and surveillance, based on more than two decades of experience in Türkiye. We gathered 32 ID specialists with extensive hands-on experience in patient care. Around 85% of participants have managed at least 50 confirmed cases over 20 years. This structured Delphi process was undertaken to clarify where experienced clinicians converge and where uncertainty persists. The methodology combined anonymous iterative survey rounds with a structured face-to-face meeting to harmonise interpretation and systematically identify both agreement and persistent heterogeneity in real-life practice.

Frameworks and evidence hierarchies (such as GRADE) can be useful for the development of evidence-based recommendations. However, the aim of this study was not to generate or grade recommendations, but to map areas of agreement, heterogeneity, and uncertainty in routine clinical practice using a Delphi approach. Given the limited and predominantly observational evidence base in CCHF, particularly in therapeutic domains, formal evidence grading was not feasible or appropriate for many of the questions addressed. As such, findings should be interpreted as consensus-derived observations rather than evidence-based recommendations.

The Delphi panel comprised infectious diseases specialists from 15 cities across Türkiye, representing regions where the burden of CCHF is greatest (Fig. 2). Recruitment was conducted through an open national call and was not restricted to members of the core consortium. Participants also represented diverse healthcare settings, including university hospitals, city hospitals, training and research hospitals, and state hospitals. The panel consisted of highly experienced clinicians, with a median of 16.5 years of clinical experience; more than 80% had managed at least 50 patients with CCHF as primary physicians, and half had managed over 500 patients (Table 1). Although participation was voluntary and some centres contributed more participants than others, the panel captured a broad range of clinical experience from the regions where CCHF is most frequently encountered. This recruitment strategy enabled the panel to capture a broad range of expertise and practice patterns from centres routinely managing patients with CCHF across Türkiye.

A central contribution of this work lies in the operational refinement of case definitions. International frameworks from WHO and CDC primarily provide surveillance-oriented classifications and do not specify structured clinical actions following categorisation.16,17 Similarly, the current Turkish Ministry of Health framework distinguishes only two case categories, which may complicate triage decisions, particularly in subendemic referral centres where diagnostic confirmation and monitoring capacity vary.9 In this study, we defined three pragmatic case categories with the aim of streaming clinical assessment as early as possible. Compared to international classifications, this framework was designed to link categorisation with typical diagnostic and management considerations. By integrating epidemiological risk, clinical features, and laboratory confirmation, it provides a structure for triage and initial decision-making (Fig. 3). Beyond classification, strong agreement emerged in structured recognition and monitoring. Experts consent on early laboratory evaluation, including haematological and coagulation parameters, and serial reassessment during hospitalisation. This approach is consistent with cohort evidence linking thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, and coagulation abnormalities with disease severity.19,20 Emphasis on fibrinogen level measurement and renal function monitoring further refines bedside risk stratification in severe disease. During hospitalisation, use of a pragmatic severity scoring tool to support routine assessment was supported. Agreement was also observed for discharge decisions based on sustained clinical stability and improving laboratory parameters rather than virological clearance (Table 2, Fig. 3). These areas of convergence suggest that diagnostic and monitoring pathways are relatively mature across high-volume centres.

In contrast, therapeutic decision-making represented the domain of greatest complexity and heterogeneity. Ribavirin remains the only antiviral agent available for CCHF,3 yet its efficacy continues to be debated. Observational cohort studies and systematic reviews suggest that any potential benefit may be time dependent, particularly when initiated early in the disease course.11,21,22 However, the available evidence is derived predominantly from observational studies which are inherently susceptible to selection bias, residual confounding, heterogeneity in disease severity, differences in timing of treatment initiation, and variations in supportive care across centres. Together with the absence of adequately powered randomised controlled trials and ethical challenges of conducting placebo-controlled studies in outbreak settings, these limitations preclude definitive conclusions regarding antiviral efficacy.23 Within this evidentiary landscape, the consensus observed here reflects prevailing clinical reasoning in endemic settings rather than resolution of the antiviral controversy.

Agreement emerged around early initiation of ribavirin in confirmed disease and, with lower consensus strength, in probable cases. Divergence persisted regarding initiation irrespective of symptom timing. This lack of convergence likely reflects differing interpretations of the time-dependent hypothesis. Some clinicians favour empirical initiation when CCHF is suspected, particularly given potential recall bias in determining symptom onset. Others prioritise careful risk-benefit assessment, emphasising that ribavirin-associated toxicity may outweigh uncertain benefit when presentation suggests later-stage disease.

While clinicians reported adherence to WHO24 and CDC-recommended25 dosing frameworks, treatment duration was commonly abbreviated in routine practice, generally to around five days. This reflected the assumption that antiviral efficacy is time-sensitive and the need to carefully balance uncertain benefit against recognised adverse effects.

Supportive care decisions demonstrated a similar pattern of partial convergence and contextual variability. Bleeding-guided transfusion strategies reached agreement, reinforcing the principle that haemorrhagic manifestations rather than platelet count alone should guide intervention (Table 2, Fig. 3). This approach is consistent with the pathophysiologic centrality of coagulopathy and endothelial dysfunction in CCHF.26 At the same time, specific transfusion thresholds varied across institutions. Differences in access to blood products, response capacity, and institutional infrastructure likely influence clinical thresholds alongside individual judgement.23,24 These findings highlight that therapeutic heterogeneity is shaped not only by evidence gaps but also by infrastructural constraints.

Corticosteroid therapy emerged as an area of continued clinical uncertainty in the Delphi process. While selective corticosteroid use in severe or rapidly progressive disease was acknowledged, no consensus was reached to support its routine use. This pattern is consistent with the current evidence base which does not support routine corticosteroid therapy and is limited to observational studies suggesting potential benefit in selected subgroups.15,27 Accordingly, these findings reflect expert practice in selected scenarios but should not be interpreted as evidence supporting routine corticosteroid therapy in patients with CCHF. Participants also highlighted the need for more biologically informed strategies, including prospective evaluation of inflammatory pathways such as interleukin-1 and interleukin-6. Immunomodulatory therapies such were used pragmatically in certain clinical contexts, but there was no agreement to support their routine application in the absence of stronger prospective evidence (Supplementary Appendix 4). No agreement was reached on specific agents, dosing strategies, or treatment duration, reflecting continued uncertainty in this area.

CCHF remains one of the viral haemorrhagic fevers with documented human-to-human transmission, placing HCWs at particular risk of infection. Numerous outbreaks and case series from both endemic and non-endemic settings have reported nosocomial transmission, often linked to delayed recognition or inadequate infection prevention measures.28, 29, 30, 31 Infection prevention practices described in this study were broadly consistent with existing WHO32 and CDC33 recommendations for CCHF in healthcare settings. The experts in our survey were aligned with the international standard precautions for all suspected or confirmed cases, use of respirators during aerosol-generating procedures, and application of appropriate barrier protection based on exposure risk. In scenarios with increased likelihood of contact with blood or bodily fluids, such as high-volume fluid exposure, escalation of protective measure was reported.

In addition to preventive measures during patient care, the panel reached agreement on exposure-based criteria for ribavirin PEP. Although WHO guidance does not currently include formal PEP recommendations,32 published reports suggest a potential protective effect following high-risk exposure,34,35 and agreement was reached for defined high-risk occupational scenarios. No consensus was achieved for lower-risk exposures, demonstrating continued uncertainty in balancing potential benefit against toxicity.

Beyond healthcare environments, documented household transmission and reports of sexual transmission,36, 37, 38 together with detection of viral RNA in non-blood specimens after serum clearance,39 informed precautionary measures during the post-discharge period. These considerations extend prevention strategies across the continuum of care and acknowledge that transmission risk may persist beyond acute hospitalisation in selected contexts.

Taken together, areas of strongest agreement clustered around early recognition, structured monitoring, discharge criteria, and clearly defined high-risk exposure management. Persistent heterogeneity was concentrated in therapeutic initiation thresholds, transfusion practices in selected scenarios, and lower-risk prophylaxis. Notably, these domains of divergence correspond to areas where prospective comparative evidence remains limited. The findings therefore reflect the inherent uncertainty surrounding therapeutic decision-making in CCHF rather than inconsistency in clinical reasoning.

A principal strength of this study is the breadth and depth of clinical experience represented. The panel included 32 ID specialists practising across diverse healthcare settings in Türkiye, one of the countries with the highest cumulative case numbers globally. This sustained exposure to CCHF across both endemic and subendemic regions allowed the consensus to reflect real-life clinical decision-making shaped by variability in infrastructure, diagnostic capacity, and patient volume. The structured Delphi methodology enhanced transparency by incorporating predefined consensus thresholds and allowing participants to review anonymised aggregated responses, including measures of agreement and dispersion, before subsequent rating rounds. This approach facilitated systematic identification of both areas of strong convergence and domains of persistent heterogeneity. By explicitly distinguishing between agreement and uncertainty, the framework avoids presenting all outputs as uniform directives and instead allows interpretation of consensus strength within local clinical contexts.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study relies on expert opinion in areas where prospective comparative evidence remains limited, and consensus cannot substitute for formal evidence grading. Second, participation was voluntary, and not all centres may have been equally represented. In addition, the panel consisted exclusively of ID specialists; inclusion of critical care, haematology, emergency medicine, public health, and infection prevention disciplines might have influenced selected domains, however ID specialists are the primarily responsible physicians in care of patients with CCHF in Türkiye. Third, although the modified Delphi design incorporated a face-to-face meeting to harmonise interpretation, the potential for interaction effects cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the outputs have not undergone prospective validation. Importantly, domains demonstrating substantial heterogeneity, particularly in therapeutic initiation thresholds, and transfusion practices, should be viewed as priority areas for future research. As CCHF incidence expands geographically, transparent mapping of expert convergence and divergence may help inform clinical pathways while higher-quality evidence is developed.

In conclusion, this Delphi-based consensus delineates areas of strong expert convergence in early recognition, triage, and structured monitoring in CCHF, while transparently identifying domains of persistent therapeutic uncertainty. By explicitly presenting the strength of agreement across statements, the framework enables clinicians to interpret consensus outputs considering local epidemiology, diagnostic capacity, and resource availability. The areas with weak or no consensus highlighted the priority targets for prospective studies aimed at strengthening the evidence base for CCHF management.
