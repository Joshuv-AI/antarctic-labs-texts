STARK denoises spatial transcriptomics images via adaptive regularization
Sharvaj Kubal, Naomi Graham, Matthieu Heitz, Andrew Warren, Michael P. Friedlander, Yaniv Plan, Geoffrey Schiebinger

Abstract
We present an approach to denoising spatial transcriptomics images that is particularly effective for uncovering cell identities in the regime of ultra-low sequencing depths, and also allows for interpolation of gene expression. The method – Spatial Transcriptomics via Adaptive Regularization and Kernels (STARK) – augments kernel ridge regression with an incrementally adaptive graph Laplacian regularizer. In each iteration, we (1) perform kernel ridge regression with a fixed graph to update the image, and (2) update the graph based on the new image. The kernel ridge regression step involves reducing the infinite dimensional problem on a space of images to finite dimensions via a modified representer theorem. Starting with a purely spatial graph, and updating it as we improve our image makes the graph more robust to noise in low sequencing depth regimes. We show that the aforementioned approach optimizes a block-convex objective through an alternating minimization scheme wherein the sub-problems have closed form expressions that are easily computed. This perspective allows us to prove convergence of the iterates to a stationary point of this non-convex objective. Statistically, such stationary points converge to the ground truth with rate 𝒪 ( R − 1 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2}) where R R is the number of reads. In numerical experiments on real spatial transcriptomics data, the denoising performance of STARK, evaluated in terms of label transfer accuracy, shows consistent improvement over the competing methods tested.

Introduction

The substantial success of
spatial transcriptomics

[
31
]
has led to massive new datasets, with high dimensional
images of gene expression
.
The tissue of interest is placed on a chip that captures RNA molecules from individual cells; material from each pixel is tagged with a unique ‘DNA barcode’ that allows everything to be sequenced in parallel.

Recent technological breakthroughs (e.g. Stereo-seq
[
12
]
) have dramatically increased the area of tissue which can be profiled and have achieved finer spatial resolutions.
As new technologies increase the scale of what can be profiled, the raw cost of sequencing RNA from cells over large areas will quickly become the limiting factor.
The cost of sequencing is paid on a
per read
basis, where a
read
refers to a captured RNA molecule being sequenced, and large scales typically require a large number of reads.
Reducing the number of reads as a cost reduction strategy results in excessive noise in the measured gene expression data, including the well-known problem of
dropouts

[
30
]
, where the observed
counts matrix
contains excessive zero entries. Hence, there is a need to develop denoising algorithms for spatial transcriptomics.

In this work, we present an adaptive regularization scheme for denoising spatial transcriptomics images in the regime where the number of reads per pixel, also known as
sequencing depth
, is extremely low—around 100 reads per pixel. Our method is based on kernel ridge regression and employs ideas from
Facciolo et al. 2009
,
Peyré et al. 2011
and
Pang and Cheung 2017
to achieve accurate image recovery with provable asymptotic guarantees.

1.1
Mathematical formulation

In this section, we formulate the problem of denoising a gene expression image.

1.1.1
Gene expression images and denoising

Our main object of interest is the transcriptional state of the cells in a tissue.
Consider a spatial domain
\mathcal{Q}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(with straightforward extensions to
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3
). A gene expression image (or
spatial transcriptomics image
)
F:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
associates to each spatial location
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, a
gene expression vector

F(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
denotes the number of genes in the genome
1
1

1

This is usually around
d\approx 10^{4}
.

Figure

Given a ground truth gene expression image
F^{\star}
and a finite set of pixels

\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
whose rows are
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}

The

\displaystyle\text{Estimate}

\displaystyle F^{\star}(q_{1}),\dots,F^{\star}(q_{m})

(1)

\displaystyle\text{from}

\displaystyle Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}.

A more general problem is that of interpolating

at arbitrary locations
q\in\mathcal{Q}
; here, one is effectively
learning

F^{\star}
in the sense of nonparametric regression:

\displaystyle\text{Estimate}

\displaystyle F^{\star}\,\,\text{on }\mathcal{Q}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{from}

\displaystyle Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}.

Image denoising
[
45
,
11
,

and nonparametric regression

18

4

53

have been studied extensively in general settings, but there exist additional challenges in the context of spatial transcriptomics. Gene expression vectors are high-dimensional (
d\simeq 10^{4}
), and contain important biological information on cell identities that must be retained. Next, gene expression images are susceptible to high levels of multinomial noise in low-reads regimes
[
20
,
51

, leading to very sparse observed matrices
\mathbf{Y}

1.1.2
Multinomial sequencing noise

Multinomial sequencing models are standard in the literature
[
20
,
51
]
, and are closely related to the popular Poisson model
[
56
,
64
]
.
The noisy expression vectors
Y_{i}
are generated via a two-step procedure. In the first step, we select the number of measurements for each pixel:

(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(R,\mathbf{u}),

(3)

where
R
denotes the total number of reads,
R_{i}
denotes the number of reads for pixel
i
, and
the vector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\Delta_{m}
models the normalized sampling frequencies of the pixels, specifying how often a read is sampled from each cell.
In the second step, the gene-expression profiles
Y_{i}
are generated conditional on
R_{1},\ldots,R_{m}
as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})\sim\frac{1}{R_{i}}\mathds{1}_{\{R_{i}\geq 1\}}\mathrm{Multinomial}(R_{i},F^{\star}(q_{i})),

(4)

with
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
being independent conditional on the reads
(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})
. The vector
Y_{i}
consists of empirically observed relative abundances of genes at pixel
q_{i}
, giving an empirical gene expression vector. The corresponding noise is then defined as

\displaystyle V_{i}:=Y_{i}-F^{\star}(q_{i}),

whose expected squared norm can be shown to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{i}-F^{\star}(q_{i})\right|^{2}\mid(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})\right]=\frac{1}{R_{i}}(1-\left|F^{\star}(q_{i})\right|^{2})

(5)

for
R_{i}\geq 1
. (Note that we use
\left|\cdot\right|
to denote Euclidean norm.)

Remark 1
.

The vector
\mathbf{u}
of normalized sampling frequencies is unspecified, and may be thought of as a nuisance parameter. Regardless, our denoising algorithm is agnostic of
\mathbf{u}
and simply assuming
u_{\min}:=\min_{i}u_{i}>0
is sufficient for our asymptotic bounds. To achieve good denoising performance in practice however, we would like close-to-uniform sampling frequencies in the sense
u_{i}\geq c/m
for some absolute constant
c>0
.

Remark 2
.

The two-step procedure above can also be packaged as one-step multinomial sampling, with an appropriately scaled matrix of parameters.

Remark 3
.

Our results also allow for the Poisson noise model, which is obtained by replacing (
3
) with
R_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Poisson}(u_{i}R)
, while keeping (
4
) the same. Here,
R
would be the expected total reads, instead of the actual number of reads which is stochastic.

1.2
Method overview

Our goal is to design a denoising strategy for problems (
1
) and (
2
) that spatially smooths the initially rough noisy image, but does so in an anisotropic manner to preserve inter-region boundaries between distinct cell types. Along these lines, we propose a variant of kernel ridge regression with additional graph Laplacian regularization that iteratively adapts to the structure of
F^{\star}
. This is realized by jointly optimizing an objective over the space of gene expression images
F
and graph weights
W
that determine the Laplacian regularizer. We describe the various components of this optimization problem below.

The image
F
is constrained to
\mathcal{H}^{d}
, a
vector-valued Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(RKHS) with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
that we detail in
Section

2.1
(see (
13
)). To specify
W
, we first fix a (directed) graph
\mathcal{G}
on the set of pixel locations
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\}
, with an edge set
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
(
\tau>0
) consisting of all pairs
(i,k)\in[m]\times[m]
such that
\left|q_{i}-q_{k}\right|\leq\tau
.
Then, the weights of all edges in
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
are described by
W
which is constrained to the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\tau}:=\left\{W\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}\mid\textstyle\sum_{k:(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}W_{(i,k)}=1\,\,\forall i\in[m]\right\}.

(6)

We will always identify
W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
with a row-stochastic matrix
[W_{ik}]_{i,k=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times m}
such that
W_{ik}=0
if
(i,k)\notin\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
.
The objective that we minimize is

\displaystyle J(F,W):=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|Y_{i}-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}}_{\text{data fit}}+\underbrace{\lambda\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}}_{\text{ridge regularization}}

(7)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\omega\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}W_{ik}\left|F(q_{i})-F(q_{k})\right|^{2}}_{\text{graph Laplacian regularization}}+\underbrace{\omega E(W)}_{\text{graph entropy}},

where
E(\cdot)
is an entropy functional (following
Facciolo et al. 2009
,
Peyré et al. 2011
) defined as

\displaystyle E(W):=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}\left\{s_{1}^{2}\,W_{ik}\left(\log W_{ik}-1\right)+\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\,W_{ik}\left|q_{i}-q_{k}\right|^{2}\right\}.

Note that
\lambda>0
,
\omega\geq 0
, and
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are hyperparameters. Note also that
J(\cdot,\cdot)
is block-wise convex, i.e. convex
separately
in both
F
and
W
. Let us tabulate the optimization problem as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{F\in\mathcal{H}^{d},W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}\quad J(F,W).

(P)

We briefly describe the roles of the four terms in (
7
) below.

Data fit

This term simply encourages the values of
F
at the pixels
q_{1},\dots q_{m}
to be close to the noisy observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
in a least-squares sense.
2
2

2

A likelihood-based loss function under of multinomial noise would be the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. We found in experiments, however, that using the least squares loss leads to better denoising performance in certain biological meaningful metrics. It also results in simpler minimization algorithms.

Ridge regularization

Regularizing with the squared RKHS norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}
makes the objective
J
strongly convex and coercive in
F
. Importantly, it also allows for a variant of the
representer theorem
, which boils down the (potentially) infinite-dimensional problem of minimizing
J(\cdot,W)
over
F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
to a tractable finite-dimensional problem. Lastly, depending on the particular choice of RKHS, the ridge regularization term can also promote smoothness in
F
.

Graph Laplacian regularization

This term promotes smoothness in
F
by getting nearby pixels
q_{i},q_{k}
to have similar gene expressions
F(q_{i}),F(q_{k})
. Such smoothing can be done anisotropically, by using image gradient information, to prevent excessive blurring of inter-region boundaries. The graph weights
W
are meant to learn and encode such anisotropy.
In
Section

2.2
, we interpret the graph Laplacian regularizer as a discretization of an anisotropic Dirichlet energy.

Graph entropy

A typical way to adapt
W
to the structure of
F
is via Gaussian weights

\displaystyle W=\mathrm{RowNormalize}\left[\exp\left(-|F(q_{i})-F(q_{k})|^{2}/s_{1}^{2}\right) \exp\left(-|q_{i}-q_{k}|^{2}/s_{2}^{2}\right)\right]_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}

(8)

(here,
\mathrm{RowNormalize}(\cdot)
scales the rows to sum to 1).
Directly prescribing such a nonlinear dependence of
W
on
F
, however, makes the optimization problem nonconvex in
F
, and difficult to solve. On the other hand, incorporating the graph entropy term into the objective produces these Gaussian weights as an optimality condition, without prescribing any relationships a priori. More precisely, fixing
F
and minimizing
J(F,\cdot)
over
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
results in the weights (
8
).

The function
J(\cdot,\cdot)
is in fact block-wise strongly convex on
\mathcal{H}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
.
Theorem

5
in
Section

3
shows that all stationary points
(\bar{F},\bar{W})
of
J
yield consistent estimators of the ground truth
as the number of reads
R\uparrow\infty
.
Theorem

8
on the other hand shows that the sequence of iterates
(F^{t},W^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the alternating updates

\displaystyle F^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\arg\min}_{F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}}J(F,W^{t})

(U)

\displaystyle W^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\arg\min}_{W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}J(F^{t+1},W),

does in fact converge to a stationary point
(\bar{F},\bar{W})
of
J
. Finally, closed-form solutions are available for both subproblems resulting in
Algorithm

.

1.4
Summary of contributions

Our main contributions are listed below.

•

We develop the method Spatial Transcriptomics via Adaptive Regularization and Kernels (STARK) – an iteratively adaptive approach for denoising spatial transcriptomics data that is particularly effective in low sequencing depth regimes. We place STARK into a broader context by connecting it to anisotropic smoothing and regularization.

•

We prove that the iterates converge to a stationary point of the objective, and establish asymptotic bounds on the statistical estimation error of such stationary points.

•

We propose a testing strategy for denoising performance on real spatial transcriptomics data, that is simple, general and practically relevant. Briefly, given a (high sequencing depth) counts matrix, we first
downsample
it to significantly fewer reads. The downsampled counts matrix is fed into the denoising method being tested, and the output is compared with the original (normalized, fully-sampled) counts matrix.

•

Our numerical experiments show STARK’s improved denoising performance over competing methods as evaluated in biologically relevant metrics such as
label transfer accuracy
, which measures the extent to which cell identities are preserved.

1.5
Related work

We review related work on the denoising problem originating in single-cell analysis as well as in the more traditional field of image processing.

Denoising gene expression matrices

There has been substantial work in the last few years on denoising or imputing counts matrices resulting from single-cell RNA sequencing
[
38
]
.
MAGIC
[
54
]
uses data diffusion to learn an underlying manifold in gene expression space, and performs denoising by effectively projecting the measured cells onto the manifold.
SAVER
[
24
]
employs
an empirical Bayes-like approach to denoising. scImpute
[
30
]
uses a targeted imputation strategy instead of globally denoising the counts matrix.
McImpute
[
34
]
uses a slightly relaxed version of the targeted approach via low-rank matrix completion. More recent work has also used autoencoders
[
16
,
65
]
for denoising. Despite their success on single-cell RNA sequencing datasets however, these methods face a severe disadvantage in the context of spatial transcriptomics because they do not use any spatial information.

Denoising spatial transcriptomics images

Pioneering work on denoising methods tailored to gene expression images with spatial structure was carried out by
Wang et al. 2022b
. Their method SPROD uses a t-SNE-like procedure to learn a spatially-aware latent graph on the pixels. This graph then denoises the observed data via a regularized least squares step

\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}}\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\omega\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}),

(9)

where
\mathbf{L}
is the (unnormalized) graph Laplacian of the learnt latent graph. We realized though numerous experiments that graph Laplacian regularization as in (
9
) is very effective on real spatial transcriptomics data, often outperforming competing regularizers such as total variation. Additionally, its simplicity allows for closed-form solutions which are particularly convenient for quick testing and tuning even on large datasets. This provided motivation for including graph Laplacian regularization in our own objective function (
7
).

SPROD also adapts its regularizer to the image
F^{\star}
, but unlike in the current work, employs a one-shot strategy: it directly uses the noisy gene expression matrix
\mathbf{Y}
to learn its latent graph of pixel similarities. This strategy, which is similar to bilateral filtering
[
37
]
, generally performs well, but is naturally susceptible to problems in learning the graph when
\mathbf{Y}
is very noisy. Intuitively, it is harder to detect inter-region boundaries and edges in noisier images. Our method, on the other hand, adapts incrementally by alternating between estimating
F^{\star}
and adapting the weights
W
to the current estimate of
F^{\star}
. Similar adaptive techniques have appeared in the image denoising and regularization literature in the form of iterative steering kernel regression
[
49
]
, non-local regularization
[
41
]
and optimal graph Laplacian regularization
[
36
]
, as well as in other areas such as terrain modelling
[
27
]
and optical flow estimation
[
33
]
, providing a basis for this work.
Traditional PDE-based methods on image smoothing and edge detection
[
40
,
10
,
52
]
also contain notions of such incremental adaptivity.

Many recently developed methods for spatial transcriptomics denoising are based on dimensionality reduction and latent representations of gene expression vectors. Linear dimensionality reduction in the form of low-rank factorization is featured in SpatialPCA
[
47
]
, GraphPCA
[
63
]
, nonnegative spatial factorization
[
50
]
and the Master’s thesis
[
26
]
. GraphPCA in particular can be interpreted as a low-rank variant of SPROD, and benefits from the availability of closed-form solutions. MIST
[
57
]
on the other hand uses a low-rank matrix completion approach following
Mongia et al. 2019
.
On the nonlinear side of latent representations, (graph-based) autoencoders play a key role in STAGATE
[
15
]
, ADEPT
[
23
]
, STGNNks
[
39
]
, SEDR
[
61
]
, DenoiseST
[
13
]
and DiffuST
[
25
]
. We observed through experiments, however, that methods based on low-dimensional representations tend to perform worse in terms of label transfer accuracy, meaning that cell identities fail to be sufficiently preserved. This agrees with the results in
Section

4
, where we compare GraphPCA and STAGATE with SPROD and STARK.

Interpolating the image at new points

Most existing denoising methods for spatial transcriptomics do not provide a direct scheme for estimating
F^{\star}
at an arbitrary point
q\in\mathcal{Q}
that is different from the measured pixels
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
. Notable exceptions include SpatialPCA
[
47
]
, which uses distance kernels, and DIST
[
66
]
, which constructs a variational learning network. By framing our method through a variational problem over a space of functions
F
(an RKHS specifically), we get evaluation at arbitrary points
q\in\mathcal{Q}
essentially for free.

Evaluating denoising performance

Various testing strategies for denoising performance have been employed in the literature surveyed above, but no standardized framework appears to have emerged.
Wang et al. 2022b
add iid Gaussian noise to a simulated gene expression matrix, which then goes through an exponential transformation and scaling step. The resulting noisy matrix is then denoised, and a relative
\ell^{1}
denoising error is recorded.
Dong and Zhang 2022
simulate dropouts by randomly setting 30% of the non-zero entries in a gene expression matrix to 0. After denoising, they compute the Pearson correlation coefficients of the denoised values to a reference ground truth. Recent work by
Cui et al. 2024
also simulates dropouts in a similar fashion, but evaluates clustering performance instead.
Here, we propose a denoising test based on downsampling reads that is simple, general and practically relevant (see
Section

4.1
).

2
Kernel ridge regression with graph-Laplacian regularization

We derive our method by first considering a fairly generic variational problem and specifying the components needed for its well-definedness and tractability. By considering a regularizer that depends both on space and gene expression information, we obtain an adaptive scheme for edge-aware denoising.

2.1
Variational formulation of the regression problem

We will start with a bare-bones variational problem geared towards recovering
F^{\star}
, and determine the structures necessary for well-definedness. Consider the following regularized problem

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|Y_{i}-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}+\omega S(F),

(10)

where
\mathcal{F}
is an appropriate choice of function class (which will end up being a vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}^{d}
), and
\omega\geq 0
is the regularization strength for the Dirichlet energy

\displaystyle S(F)=\|DF\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}^{2}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\,\nabla F_{j}(q)\cdot\nabla F_{j}(q)dq.

Here,
DF
denotes the (weak) Jacobian of
F
.
Note that
S(\cdot)
penalizes the gradients of the image
F
, thereby encouraging smoothness. This is closely related to Gaussian blurring
[
60
]
. Smoothing isotropically can potentially blur edges, however. Following
[
40
,
60
,
36
]
, one can replace the Dirichlet energy above by its anisotropic counterpart

\displaystyle S_{G}(F):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\,\nabla F_{j}(q)\cdot G(q)^{-1}\nabla F_{j}(q)\,dq,

(11)

where
G:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a matrix-valued function such that
G(q)
is positive definite for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
G
should depend on local image gradients, see
Section

2.2
.

To determine the function class
\mathcal{F}
, we examine the requirements for a well-defined problem (see also
Green et al. 2021
). Firstly, since the Dirichlet energy depends on first-order derivatives, we need
F\in\mathcal{F}
to be at least (weakly) once-differentiable. This suggests the Sobolev space

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid DF\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d\times 2})\right\}.

However, in
H^{1}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, elements
F
need not have a continuous representative, and so pointwise evaluation
q\mapsto F(q)
need not be well-defined everywhere

[
19
]
, which makes the problem (
10
) ill-defined.
This can be resolved by imposing additional smoothness via choosing the space
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
s>1
, where up to
s
(weak) derivatives of
F
are required to be in
L^{2}
. If
s>1
, pointwise evaluation
is
in fact well-defined (and bounded) in
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

In moving to
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
however, the objective function in (
10
) loses coercivity, and hence, may not admit minimizers. This can be corrected by regularizing additionally with a squared Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}^{2}
(which also provides strong convexity), to give the
spline
-like problem

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{F\in H^{s}(\mathcal{Q},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|Y_{i}-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}+\omega\,S_{G}(F)+\lambda\,\|F\|_{H^{s}}^{2},

(12)

for a hyperparameter
\lambda>0
. Another advantage of working in the space
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, given
s>1
, is that it is a (vector-valued)
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(RKHS)
[
2
]
, which is effectively generated by a single positive semidefinite kernel function
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
. This injects computational tractability into the problem.
Sobolev spaces
H^{s}
are generated by Matérn kernels
[
42
]
(depending on
s
), where the case
s=3/2
in particular corresponds to a simple exponential kernel
\mathcal{K}(q,q^{\prime})=\exp(-\left|q-q^{\prime}\right|/l)
where
l>0
. (Precise statements and technical details regarding domain restrictions are provided in
Appendix

A
.)

From here on, we would like to abstract away from particular Sobolev spaces and work directly with some general (vector-valued) RKHS.

Vector-valued Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

A vector-valued RKHS
[
46
,
32
]
generalizes its scalar counterpart by considering functions into a vector space such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as opposed to just scalar-valued functions. Given a scalar-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, the Cartesian product space

\mathcal{H}^{d}=\{F:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid F_{j}\in\mathcal{H}\text{ for all }j\in[d]\},

(13)

with inner product and norm

\langle F,\tilde{F}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}:=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle F_{j},\tilde{F}_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\langle F,F\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{1/2},

is a vector-valued RKHS. Now, we replace
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
in problem (
12
) with
\mathcal{H}^{d}
; the corresponding Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}
should then be replaced by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
. This provides great computational advantages, as we may now use variants of
kernel ridge regression
to attack the problem (given a suitable discretization of
S_{G}
).

2.2
Adaptive Dirichlet energy and discretization

So far, we have formulated an optimization problem (
12
) with a precise choice of
\mathcal{F}
, and what remains is to choose
G
in the anisotropic Dirichlet energy
S_{G}
defined in (
11
).
The key function of the anisotropic Dirichlet energy term is to smooth while preserving image edges or inter-regional boundaries by making use of information on local image gradients. Suppose an oracle returns image gradients of the ground truth
F^{\star}
; then, one can set
G
to be the corresponding
oracle
structure tensor
[
52
,
36
]

\displaystyle G^{\star}(q):=I_{2}+\tfrac{s_{1}^{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}DF^{\star}(q)^{T}DF^{\star}(q)

(14)

where
I_{2}
is the 2-by-2 identity matrix, and
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are hyperparameters.

Approximating the Dirichlet energy with the graph Laplacian regularizer

Recall the (directed) graph
\mathcal{G}
from
Section

1.2
with vertices
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\}
and edge set
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
. Given edge weights
W_{ik}
, for
(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
, consider the graph Laplacian regularizer

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{\mathcal{G}}(F)=\tfrac{1}{2m}\textstyle\sum_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}}W_{ik}\,\left|F(q_{k})-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}.

(15)

Pang and Cheung 2017
show that
\widehat{S}_{\mathcal{G}}
provides a good approximation to
S_{G^{\star}}
, under certain conditions, when
m
is large. The underlying principle is that the graph
\mathcal{G}
with weights
3
3

3

Note that the weights
W^{\star}
here follow a slightly different normalization than that in
Pang and Cheung 2017
; row-normalized weights prove useful in the context of our algorithm.

\displaystyle W^{\star}=\mathrm{RowNormalize}\left[\exp\left(-|F^{\star}(q_{i})-F^{\star}(q_{k})|^{2}/s_{1}^{2}\right) \exp\left(-|q_{i}-q_{k}|^{2}/s_{2}^{2}\right)\right]_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}

serves as a discrete approximation to the Riemannian manifold

\displaystyle\mathrm{graph}\left(\tfrac{s_{1}}{s_{2}}F^{\star}\right)=\left\{\left(q,\,\tfrac{s_{1}}{s_{2}}F^{\star}(q)\right)\mid q\in\mathcal{Q}\right\},

whose Dirichlet energy functional is closely linked to
S_{G^{\star}}

[
5
,
21
]
.
Following this discretization, we end up replacing (
12
) with the problem,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}}\quad\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|Y_{i}-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}+\frac{\omega}{2m}\sum_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}}W^{\star}_{ik}\,\left|F(q_{k})-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}+\lambda\,\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}.

(16)

Importantly, constructing
W^{\star}
would require information about the ground truth
F^{\star}
provided by an oracle (as is the case for the oracle structure tensor
G^{\star}
). In practice, one needs to estimate
W^{\star}
.
Thus, following
Peyré et al. 2011
, we can iteratively adapt
W
to the current denoised estimate
F
as per (
8
), update the associated regularizer, and then resolve the optimization problem under the new regularizer. This alternating procedure is, in fact, equivalent to performing block coordinate minimization on
eq.

7
. We demonstrate this in the next section.

3
Theoretical results

We now present some theoretical results regarding the statistical and computational convergence of our denoising method.
Recall from
Section

2.1
, the vector-valued Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}^{d}=\{F:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid F_{j}\in\mathcal{H},\,\forall j\in[d]\}
generated from a scalar-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, and let
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the positive semidefinite kernel function for this RKHS.
Our statistical convergence result states that stationary points of the objective function
J
of (
P
) converge to the ground truth, while the computational convergence guarantees that the alternating updates (
U
) converge to a stationary point of
J
.

Definition 4
.

A point
(\bar{F},\bar{W})\in\mathcal{H}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is a stationary point of
J
over
\mathcal{H}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
if

\displaystyle-\nabla_{W}J(\bar{F},\bar{W})\in N_{\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}(\bar{W}),

\displaystyle\qquad\nabla_{F}J(\bar{F},\bar{W})=0,

where
N_{\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}(\bar{W})
is the normal cone to
{\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}
at
\bar{W}
. Let
\mathrm{Stat}(J)\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
denote the set of all such stationary points.

3.1
Convergence

We start with statistical convergence, showing that as the reads
R\uparrow\infty
, all stationary points of
J
approach the ground truth
F^{\star}
in the empirical
L^{2}
norm defined via

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}_{m}}^{2}:=\tfrac{1}{m}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|F(q_{i})\right|^{2}.

Notice that this empirical
L^{2}
norm is simply the scaled Frobenius norm of a matrix
\mathbf{F}
with rows
F(q_{1}),\dots,F(q_{m})
, i.e.
\|F\|_{L^{2}_{m}}=\|\mathbf{F}\|_{F}/\sqrt{m}
.

Suppose the measurements
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
are generated according to the noise model from
Section

1.1.2
, with
R
total reads. The precise statement is as follows.

Theorem 5
(Asymptotic rate with respect to the reads).

Consider the objective function
J
from
eq.

7
with strictly positive hyperparameters
\lambda=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1})
and
\omega=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1})
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\sup\{\|\bar{F}-F^{\star}\|_{L^{2}_{m}}\mid(\bar{F},\bar{W})\in\mathrm{Stat}(J)\}=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2}).

The proof
relies on several definitions and results to follow, and is presented towards the end of the paper in
Section

5
.

Theorem

5
provides a notion of consistency as the reads
R\uparrow\infty
, or in other words, the noise level goes to 0 by (
5
). Note also that this result is
asymptotic
– the constant hidden in
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
, while fixed with respect to
R
, is problem-dependent in general.

The empirical
L^{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}_{m}}
as an error metric in
Theorem

5
, although standard, is concerned only with the behaviour of
\bar{F}
at the points
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
where measurements
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{m}
are available. A more global metric that is natural in our setting is provided by the RKHS norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
. The corollary to follow obtains a similar bound in
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
, but with an additional interpolation error term. To set it up, first define the finite-dimensional subspace

\displaystyle M(q_{1},\dots,q_{m}):=\left\{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{K}(q_{i},\cdot)\theta_{i}\mid\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{d}

(
Lemma

9
to follow implies that for any stationary point
(\bar{F},\bar{W})
,
\bar{F}
lies in this subspace). The approximation (or interpolation) error of
F^{\star}
with respect to this subspace is defined as

\mathcal{A}(F^{\star};\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\}):=\inf\left\{\|F^{\star}-F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\mid F\in M(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})\right\}.

Corollary 6
(Error in the RKHS norm).

Consider the objective function
J
with strictly positive hyperparameters
\lambda=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1})
and
\omega=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1})
. We have

\mathbb{E}\,\sup\{\|\bar{F}-F^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\mid(\bar{F},\bar{W})\in\mathrm{Stat}(J)\}\leq\mathcal{A}(F^{\star};\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\})+\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2}).

See
the proof
in
Appendix

B
. This error bound consists of an approximation error and an estimation error. The approximation error
\mathcal{A}(F^{\star};\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\})
would depend on the number of pixels
m
and their placement in
\mathcal{Q}
. We do not attempt to further quantify it here.

Remark 7
.

The error bound of
Corollary

6
has a couple of interesting consequences. First, for any
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, it holds by RKHS properties that
\left|\bar{F}(q)-F^{\star}(q)\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{K}}\|\bar{F}-F^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
,
where the constant
C_{\mathcal{K}}:=\sup_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{K}(q,q)^{1/2}
. This, together with
Corollary

6
, leads to some simple pointwise estimates of the error. Second, we obtain estimates on the RKHS norm of
\bar{F}
via the triangle inequality as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup\{\|\bar{F}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\mid(\bar{F},\bar{W})\in\mathrm{Stat}(J)\}

\displaystyle\leq\|F^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}+\mathcal{A}(F^{\star};\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\})+\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|F^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}+\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2}).

Such estimates can be interpreted as quantifying the expected regularity of
\bar{F}
.

Notice that the suprema in the bounds of
Theorem

5
and
Corollary

6
are over
all
stationary points of the objective
J
, including, in view of
Theorem

8
, the limiting point returned by the updates (
U
).
We remark that this is not very surprising, mainly because the input noisy counts themselves satisfy
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{F}^{\star}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2})
, and the updates have certain stability properties as can be inferred from the proofs.

The convergence of the iterates generated by (
U
) is established by the following result.

Theorem 8
.

The sequence
(F^{t},W^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the updates (
U
) converges to a stationary point of
J
over
\mathcal{H}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
.

The proof
is presented in
Section

5.2
.

3.2
Updates of the block coordinate descent algorithm

In this section, we demonstrate that both of the updates in (
U
) have closed-form expressions. In particular, for the
F
-update, we establish a useful lemma that identifies the infinite-dimensional problem with an associated finite-dimensional problem. To state these results, one needs to define a few objects.

Given pixel locations
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
, and the kernel function
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
for our RKHS, the corresponding
kernel matrix
is a symmetric, positive semidefinite matrix defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}:=\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\mathcal{K}(q_{i},q_{k})\right]_{i,k=1}^{m}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.

(17)

The following subspace of matrices corresponding to
\mathbf{K}
will play a role later:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\equiv\{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\}.

Next, given a row-stochastic weights matrix
W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
, define the effective graph Laplacian

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W}:=\frac{1}{2}({I}_{m}+\mathrm{Diag}(W^{T}\mathbf{e}))-\frac{1}{2}\left(W+W^{T}\right)\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

(18)

where
\mathbf{e}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This is the usual unnormalized graph Laplacian of the symmetric weights matrix
\frac{1}{2}(W+W^{T})
, and satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{ik}W_{ik}\left|F(q_{k})-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W}\mathbf{F}).

(19)

Consequently,
\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W}
is also symmetric and positive semidefinite. Finally, define the finite-dimensional counterpart to
J
, denoted by
\mathbf{J}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}\to\mathbb{R}
, as follows:

\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},W):=\frac{1}{m}\|\mathbf{Y}-\sqrt{m}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)+\omega\left\{\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\mathbf{K}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)+E(W)\right\}.

(20)

We have a variant of the
representer theorem
from RKHS theory,
proved
in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 9
(A representer theorem).

Given
W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
,
\lambda>0
,
\omega\geq 0
, a function
\hat{F}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
satisfies
\hat{F}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}}\,J(F,W)

if and only if
\hat{F}
can be represented as
\hat{F}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{K}(q_{i},\cdot)\hat{\theta}_{i}
,
where the vectors
\hat{\theta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\theta}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the rows of the unique matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbf{K}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}}\,\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},W)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(\mathbf{K}^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{K}+\omega\mathbf{K}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W}\mathbf{K})^{+}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Y}.

(21)

See
the proof
in
Appendix

B
.

Remark 10
.

The function
\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},W)
is strongly convex on
\mathbf{K}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
with modulus (at least)
\Lambda_{r}(\mathbf{K})^{2}+\lambda\,\Lambda_{r}(\mathbf{K})
, where
\Lambda_{r}(\mathbf{K})
denotes the smallest strictly positive eigenvalue of
\mathbf{K}
.
Note, additionally, that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in (
21
) also minimizes
\mathbf{J}(\cdot,W)
over the whole space
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
.

Given an iterate
W^{t}\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
, we obtain
F^{t+1}
, the updated
F
-iterate from (
U
), using
Lemma

9
.
Now that we have a closed-form expression for
F^{t+1}
, we can go on to solve for
W^{t+1}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}\,J(F^{t+1},W)
.
The following proposition establishes that the weighting procedure introduced in
eq.

8
is equivalent to solving this
W
-update subproblem. We remark that the additional entropy term
E(W)
is required in order to establish this equivalence.

Proposition 11
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
,
\lambda>0
and
\omega>0
. The function
W\mapsto J(F,W)
is
(\omega/2m)
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
, and the unique minimizer
\hat{W}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}\,J(F,W)

is given by
\hat{W}=\mathrm{RowNormalize}(\tilde{W})
, where

\tilde{W}_{ik}:=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s_{1}^{2}}\left|F(q_{k})-F(q_{i})\right|^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s_{2}^{2}}\left|q_{k}-q_{i}\right|^{2}\right),&\text{if }|q_{k}-q_{i}|\leq\tau,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(22)

See
the proof
in
Appendix

B
.

The above results, together with an initialization choice
W^{0}
, are summarized into
Algorithm

.

4
Numerical experiments

The recently-developed Stereo-seq technology
[
12
]
has produced massive, high-quality spatial transcriptomics datasets, including the Mouse Organogenesis Spatiotemporal Transcriptomic Atlas (MOSTA). This dataset consists of gene expression image snapshots of mouse embryos at various stages of development. The high quality of the data is owed to a large field of view, cellular-scale resolution and a larger number of total reads, as compared to previous technologies.

We report on several numerical experiments evaluating our method STARK on the MOSTA dataset, including comparisons with SPROD
[
58
]
, GraphPCA
[
63
]
and STAGATE
[
15
]
.
STARK is implemented in python, and is available at
https://github.com/schiebingerlab/STARK
. The inputs to STARK are (1) a pixels-by-genes noisy gene expression matrix
\mathbf{Y}
whose rows are normalized to sum to 1, (2) spatial
(x,y)
coordinates specifying the locations of the pixels
q_{1},...,q_{m}
from which the reads were observed, and optionally, (3) the number of reads
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}
sequenced from each of the pixels, which help auto-tune regularization strengths.
We do any additional transformations on the counts, e.g.
log1p
,
after
denoising.

The main output of the algorithm (see
Algorithm

1
) is a function
\bar{F}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which can be evaluated at any
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Given spatial coordinates specifying (possibly new) locations
q_{1}^{\prime},\dots,q^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}
, the gene expression vectors at those locations can be estimated as
\bar{F}(q_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,\bar{F}(q^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}})
. Noting that gene expression vectors should ideally lie in the simplex
\Delta_{d}
, we additionally threshold
\bar{F}(q_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,\bar{F}(q^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}})
to the simplex via the map
X\mapsto{X_{+}}\,/\,{|X_{+}|_{1}}
,
where
X_{+}:=\max(X,0)
is defined component-wise. This thresholding map is more of a simple heuristic and can be replaced by Euclidean (or other) projections onto
\Delta_{d}
if desired.

Details on suitable hyperparameter settings for STARK are provided in
Section

C.1
.
Importantly, in the experiments to follow, we pick the exponential kernel
\mathcal{K}(q,q^{\prime}):=\exp(-|q^{\prime}-q|/l)
, where
l>0
is a tunable length-scale. Recall that for this choice of kernel, the resulting vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}^{d}
coincides with the Sobolev space
H^{3/2}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, up to equivalent norms, provided that
\mathcal{Q}
is an extension domain (see
Appendix

).

4.1
Denoising and interpolation tests

We describe here the denoising and interpolation tests based on downsampling reads and subsampling cells that we use to evaluate our method. Denote by
\mathbf{C}_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m\times d}
a pixels-by-genes counts matrix of unique molecular identifier (UMI) reads from a real spatial transcriptomics dataset. The entry
(\mathbf{C}_{0})_{ij}
counts the number of UMI reads of gene
j
that were collected at pixel
q_{i}
. If the total number of reads
R_{0}
is high, then
\mathbf{F}_{0}=\mathrm{RowNormalize}(\mathbf{C}_{0})
is a good approximation to the underlying ground truth gene expression matrix
\mathbf{F}^{\star}
. In particular, the rows of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
, which are the observed gene expression vectors at pixels
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
, are
close to
F^{\star}(q_{1}),\dots,F^{\star}(q_{m})
.
Given
\mathbf{C}_{0}
, we downsample it to
R<R_{0}
reads, which means that we retain
R
reads from
\mathbf{C}_{0}
uniformly at random and discard the rest, resulting in a downsampled counts matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{DS}}
. The matrix
\mathbf{Y}=\mathrm{RowNormalize}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{DS}})
has rows
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
that are much noisier than those of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
when
R\ll R_{0}
.

Denoising test

Input
\mathbf{Y}
into the denoising algorithm being studied, which outputs denoised gene expression vectors
\bar{F}(q_{1}),\dots,\bar{F}(q_{m})
. Compare these to the rows of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
.

Interpolation test

Subsample
\mathbf{Y}
to
\tilde{m}<m
pixels by retaining
\tilde{m}
pixels

\{q_{i_{1}},q_{i_{2}},\dots,q_{i_{\tilde{m}}}\}
selected uniformly at random and discarding the rest. The resulting subsampled matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}
has the corresponding rows
Y_{i_{1}},Y_{i_{2}},\dots,Y_{i_{\tilde{m}}}
. Input
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}
into a denoising algorithm that estimates gene expression vectors
\bar{F}(q_{1}),\dots,\bar{F}(q_{m})
at all
m
pixels. Compare these to the rows of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
.

4.2
Performance metrics

The performance metrics
label transfer accuracy
,

and

are described below. Recall the matrix
\mathbf{F}_{0}
of originally observed gene expression vectors, and denote by
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
a denoised gene expression matrix with rows
\bar{F}(q_{1}),\dots,\bar{F}(q_{m})
.

Label transfer accuracy

An important piece of information contained in gene expression is cell identity; cells of distinct types tend to occupy different regions in gene expression space. The metric of label transfer accuracy intends to quantify how well the denoising process preserves cell identities. At a high level, we use a
k
-nearest neighbour procedure to transfer cell type labels from the original gene expression vectors at the pixels (rows of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
) to the denoised gene expression vectors (rows of
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
), and record the fraction of the transferred labels that are correct. This fraction clearly lies between 0 (completely inaccurate) to 1 (completely accurate). The degree to which the labels can be transferred successfully indicates the quality of the denoised matrix. Details on the steps involved in this label transfer procedure (e.g. log-transform, PCA) are provided in
Section

C.2
.

We remark that label transfer accuracy captures the essential geometry of gene expression space, and treat it as our primary metric for evaluation and hyperparameter tuning.

kNN overlap

A fundamental data structure that encodes essential information in a set of gene expression vectors is their
k
-nearest neighbour (kNN) graph (notice that similar graphs have already played a role in our denoising strategy, and also in the above metric of label transfer accuracy). Important clustering algorithms for cells in gene expression space also take the kNN graph as an input.
This motivates our kNN overlap metric, which is closely related to the corresponding metric from
Ahlmann-Eltze and Huber 2023
. It evaluates the denoised gene expression vectors (rows of
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
) by comparing their kNN graph against the kNN graph of the original gene expression vectors (rows of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
) post some transformations (e.g. log-transform, PCA) as detailed in
Section

C.2
. From the resulting adjacency matrices
A_{0},\bar{A}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
, compute the overlap as
\frac{1}{m}\langle A_{0},\bar{A}\rangle_{F}
.
With the choice
k=50
as in
Ahlmann-Eltze and Huber 2023
, the kNN overlap lies between 0 (no overlap) to 50 (full overlap).

Relative error

The relative error metric directly compares the original gene expression matrix
\mathbf{F}_{0}
with the denoised matrix
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{RelErr}(\mathbf{F}_{0},\bar{\mathbf{F}}):=\frac{\|\bar{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}_{0}\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{F}_{0}\|_{F}}.

This is a standard metric that is popular in the matrix denoising literature. Relative error can sometimes disagree with label transfer accuracy and kNN overlap, and we suspect this is because relative error fails to capture the geometry of gene expression space.
We expect there to be certain directions or substructures in gene expression space containing disproportionately more information on cell identities, but the relative error metric is agnostic to these.

While tuning hyperparameters of the denoising methods, we observed that the hyperparameter choices leading to the smallest relative errors often led to substantially worse label transfer accuracies and kNN overlaps. We also noticed that methods based on low-dimensional representations (e.g. low rank factorization) often did very well in terms of relative error, but struggled in the other metrics, likely because of misalignments between the low-dimensional representations involved and the biologically important directions or substructures.

6
Discussion

We have addressed the problem of denoising and interpolating spatial transcriptomics images
via regression in a vector-valued RKHS. By additionally employing incrementally adaptive graph Laplacian regularization, our proposed method STARK is able to estimate the ground truth effectively at sequencing depths as low as 14 UMI reads per pixel. The method is supported with asymptotic statistical error bounds as the reads
R\uparrow\infty
, as well as a convergence result on the alternating minimization scheme. When evaluated in label transfer accuracy and kNN overlap, the denoising performance of STARK on a slice of the MOSTA dataset shows consistent improvement over the competing methods tested.

The spatial transcriptomics denoising problem stipulates multiple directions for further theoretical investigation.
A key avenue is the finite-sample analysis of such a denoising method. Nonasymptotic error bounds would highlight the relationships and tradeoffs between the number of genes, pixels and reads.
On the other hand, modeling gene expression images by functions in an RKHS opens up connections to Gaussian process theory. Describing distributions of such images by Gaussian processes could enable a Bayesian analysis of spatial trajectory inference, providing a theoretical base for the development of new algorithms.

Finally, in
fig.

3
, we observe that STAGATE and GraphPCA, the two methods based in low-dimensional representations appear to perform the best in relative error, while simultaneously performing worse than STARK and SPROD in both label transfer accuracy and kNN overlap. This discrepancy across metrics calls into question the most appropriate choice of error measurement for testing performance. It also suggests that evaluating methods based solely on Frobenius norm relative error will create a bias towards low-dimensional representations that may perform worse in terms of classification results.

Appendix A
Background information on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS)

Fix a ground set
\mathcal{Q}
. An RKHS
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
in which pointwise evaluation
f\mapsto f(q)
is continuous for any
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Given the RKHS, there exists a unique positive semidefinite kernel function
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
such that (1) the reproducing property holds i.e.
f(q)=\langle f,\mathcal{K}(q,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, and (2) the space of finite linear combinations

\left\{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}\mathcal{K}(q_{k},\cdot)\mid K\in\mathbb{N},\,q_{1},\dots,q_{K}\in\mathcal{Q},\,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

is dense in
\mathcal{H}
. Conversely, starting from a positive semidefinite kernel
\mathcal{K}
, there exists a unique RKHS satisfying (1) and (2).

A.1
Linking Sobolev spaces and Matérn kernels

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a Matérn kernel defined as

\mathcal{K}(q,q^{\prime})=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{l}}\left|q-q^{\prime}\right|\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{l}}\left|q-q^{\prime}\right|\right)

(28)

where
\nu>0
is a smoothness parameter,
l>0
is a length-scale, and
K_{\nu}
is a modified Bessel function; see
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
. Setting
\nu=1/2
in (
28
) gives the exponential kernel

\mathcal{K}(q,q^{\prime})=\exp(-\left|q-q^{\prime}\right|/l).

Letting
\nu\to\infty
on the other hand yields a Gaussian kernel.

If
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the RKHS generated by the Matérn kernel
\mathcal{K}
coincides with the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
s=n/2+\nu
(possibly up to equivalent norms)
[
43
,
35
,
42
]
. The same goes for vector-valued RKHSes and Sobolev spaces respectively.
Letting
n=2
and
\nu=1/2
, we conclude that the exponential kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
generates
H^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(and consequently,
H^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{d})
).

A.2
Restricting to subdomains

In
Section

2.1
, we consider
H^{s}(\mathcal{Q};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for a subdomain
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and assert that it is still generated by a Matérn kernel provided
s=1+\nu
with
\nu>0
. Thus, it remains to verify that the link between Matérn kernels and Sobolev spaces is preserved under restriction.

In the case of an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, restricting the kernel to values in a subdomain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
gives rise to an RKHS
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
over
\Omega

[
6
, Section 1.4]
with norm

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}=\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}:f\upharpoonright_{\Omega}=\tilde{f}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

On the other hand, for domains
\Omega
which are “extension domains”, it holds that there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
\Omega,n,s
such that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}:f\upharpoonright_{\Omega}=\tilde{f}}\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|\tilde{f}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}.

In particular,
Leoni 2017
verifies that any
\Omega
which is bounded and has Lipschitz boundary is an extension domain, when
s>0
is an integer. The case of general
s>0
is then obtained from
Leoni 2017
, who establishes the corresponding extension theorem for Besov spaces by interpolation (see also
[
8
]
), together with
[
14
, Theorem 6.7]
which shows that the class of Besov spaces contains the class of fractional Sobolev spaces (under assumptions on
\Omega
which are satisfied in this case). Altogether this shows that the RKHS norm
\|\cdot\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}
is equivalent to the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}
in our case where
\Omega=\mathcal{Q}
, with the same therefore also holding in the vector-valued case.

\|A^{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}\to\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq B,

for a constant
B
that does not depend on
t
.

To conclude the proof, recall that
F^{t+1}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{F\in\mathcal{H}^{d}}J(F,W^{t})
and use first-order optimality conditions together with (
35
) to obtain

A^{t}F^{t+1}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}^{*}Y_{i}.

Evaluating the gradient at
F^{t}
now gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{F}J(F^{t},W^{t})=A^{t}F^{t}-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}^{*}Y_{i}=A^{t}F^{t}-A^{t}F^{t+1},

so that the norm

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{F}J(F^{t},W^{t})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\|A^{t}(F^{t}-F^{t+1})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq B\|F^{t}-F^{t+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}.

P
roof of
Lemma

13
.

First suppose
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(R,p)
. By the Chernoff inequality for left tails (see
Vershynin 2018
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{X\leq\tau Rp\}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Rp}\left(\frac{e}{\tau}\right)^{\tau Rp}

\displaystyle=e^{-Rp}\left(e^{1+\log(1/\tau)}\right)^{\tau Rp}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-Rp+\tau Rp+\tau Rp\log\frac{1}{\tau}\right),

as desired.

Now let
X\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)
for
\lambda=Rp
and define
X_{N}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,\lambda/N)
. By the Poisson limit theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{X\leq\tau\lambda\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{X_{N}\leq\tau\lambda\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{X_{N}\leq\tau\cdot N(\lambda/N)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\exp\left(-\lambda\left(1-\tau-\tau\log\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\lambda\left(1-\tau-\tau\log\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\right),

where the second inequality uses the bound for Binomial random variables that we just obtained.

P
roof of
Lemma

14
.

Since
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is a bounded set and
W^{t}\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\ \forall t
, it is clear that
(W^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. For proving boundedness of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, by (
21
) we observe that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(\mathbf{K}^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{K}+\omega\mathbf{K}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W^{t}}\mathbf{K})^{+}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Y}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}||\mathbf{K}\|_{\text{op}}\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{F}\|\left(\mathbf{K}^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{K}+\omega\mathbf{K}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W^{t}}\mathbf{K}\right)^{+}\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{m}}||\mathbf{K}\|_{\text{op}}\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{F}\|\left(\mathbf{K}^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{K}\right)^{+}\|_{\text{op}},

where the last inequality uses the fact that
\mathbf{K}\overline{\mathbf{L}}_{W^{t}}\mathbf{K}
is positive semidefinite together with properties of the pseudoinverse.
We further get that
(F^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded since for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,

\displaystyle\|F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle_{F}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}\|\mathbf{K}\|_{\text{op}}.

P
roof of
Lemma

15
.

We start by showing that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})-(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})\|^{2}<\infty.

(36)

To do this, we use the extended-valued function
\mathbf{J}_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}_{\tau}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, which coincides with
\mathbf{J}
if
W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
and outputs
+\infty
otherwise.

By
Remark

10
, the function
\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\cdot,W^{t})
is
\mu
-strongly convex on
\mathbf{K}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. This guarantees that

\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t})\geq\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})+\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

(37)

By (
U’
) and
Remark

10
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbf{K}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},W^{t})\subseteq\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},W^{t})
and
so
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t})=0
. Combining this with strong-convexity (
37
), we get

\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})-\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t}).

Similarly,
by
Proposition

11
, for each
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, the function
\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},\cdot)
is
(\omega/2m)
-strongly convex. For any
Z\in\partial_{W}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})

\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})\geq\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t})+\langle Z,W^{t+1}-W^{t}\rangle_{F}+\frac{\omega}{4m}\|W^{t+1}-W^{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

(38)

Since
W^{t+1}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{W}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W)
, we have
0\in\partial_{W}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})
. Now,

\frac{\omega}{4m}\|W^{t+1}-W^{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t})-\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1}).

Thus, there exists
C>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})-(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})\|^{2}\leq C(\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})-\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})).

Summing up over all

, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})-(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})-\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t+1},W^{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle=C\left[\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1},W^{1})-\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{J}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})\right]<\infty,

because the iterates
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are feasible, and
\mathbf{J}_{\infty}
is bounded below.

Now, let
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\bar{W})
denote an arbitrary limit point of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t},W^{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and let
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t_{s}},W^{t_{s}})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
be a subsequence converging to
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\bar{W})
.
By (

), we know that

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t_{s}+1}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
W^{t_{s}+1}\to\bar{W}
as

. Again by (

) and
Remark

10
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t_{s}+1},W^{t_{s}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},W^{t_{s}})\ \forall\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t_{s}+1},W^{t_{s}+1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t_{s}+1},W)\ \forall W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}.

Taking the limit of both sides and using the fact that
\mathbf{J}
is continuous, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\bar{W})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\bar{W})\ \forall\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\bar{W})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{J}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},W)\ \forall W\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}.

Recall the

-update
W^{t+1}=\text{RowNormalize}(\tilde{W}^{t+1})
, where

\tilde{W}^{t}_{ik}=\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{s_{1}^{2}}|\mathbf{F}^{t}_{k\cdot}-\mathbf{F}^{t}_{i\cdot}|^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{s_{2}^{2}}\left|q_{k}-q_{i}\right|^{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{|q_{k}-q_{i}|\leq\tau\}}.

Noting that
\mathbf{F}^{t}=\sqrt{m}\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{t}
and using the uniform bound on

from the proof of

\left|\mathbf{F}_{k\cdot}^{t}-\mathbf{F}_{i\cdot}^{t}\right|\leq\left|\mathbf{F}_{i\cdot}^{t}\right|+\left|\mathbf{F}_{k\cdot}^{t}\right|\leq 2\|\mathbf{F}^{t}\|_{F}\leq 2\|(\mathbf{K}^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{K})^{+}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{K}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{F}

Appendix C
Supplementary information on numerical experiments

C.1
Hyperparameter settings for STARK

Our method STARK depends on several hyperparameters: the length scale
l
of the kernel function, the values
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
and
\tau
involved in the graph construction, the regularization strengths
\lambda
and
\omega
, and the number of iterations
N
of the block coordinate descent scheme.
We employ useful heuristics that automatically fix many of these hyperparameters so that minimal additional tweaking is required.
The choices involved are based on various numerical experiments and parameter sweeps.

The kernel length scale
l>0
is set to be a neighbourhood radius on the set of pixel locations such that a pixel has 7 neighbours on average that are at a distance at most
l
from it.
Naturally, this length scale is also meaningful in constructing the weighted graph on the pixels. Set
s_{2}=l
and
\tau=\frac{3}{2}l
. As (
22
) suggests,
\tau
sets a hard cutoff on the graph weights whereas
s_{2}
defines a softer threshold, justifying
s_{2}<\tau
.

The parameter
s_{1}>0
represents a length scale in gene expression space instead. A strategy for choosing it as follows.

•

Compute the first iterate
F^{1}
in Algorithm 3.1, which does not require
s_{1}
. This is already a decent estimate of the ground truth
F^{\star}
(see the performance of the
spatial
variant in
Figure

5
).

•

Set
s_{1}
to be the 0.75-th quantile of the set
\{\left|\mathbf{F}_{i\cdot}^{1}-\mathbf{F}^{1}_{k\cdot}\right|\mid(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau},\,i\neq k\}
.

For regularization strengths
\lambda
and
\omega
, it is convenient to set
\lambda=\alpha\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}
,
\omega=\alpha\omega_{\mathrm{rel}}
, where
\alpha>0
quantifies an ‘overall’ regularization strength, and
\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}},\omega_{\mathrm{rel}}>0
determine the relative strengths of the ridge regularization and the graph Laplacian regularization terms in (
7
) respectively. We fix
\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}=\|\mathbf{K}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
as a natural scale, and tune
\omega_{\mathrm{rel}}=6
.

The overall strength
\alpha
should be set according to the noise level in the measurements; noisier data typically require more regularization. By (
5
), we know that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Fit}(F^{\star})\mid R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\right]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|Y_{i}-F^{\star}(q_{i})\right|^{2}\,\Big|\,R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\right]\simeq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min\left(\frac{1}{R_{i}},1\right).

Therefore, it is reasonable to adjust the strength
\alpha
such that our estimate
\bar{F}
exhibits a comparable noise level i.e.
\mathrm{Fit}(\bar{F})\simeq\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{Fit}(F^{\star})
. To avoid running the full iterative algorithm multiple times while adjusting
\alpha
however, we actually use the first iterate
F^{1}
as a proxy for
\bar{F}
. Briefly, we employ a bisection search to find a value for
\alpha
such that

\mathrm{Fit}(F^{1})\simeq p\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min\left(\frac{1}{R_{i}},1\right),

where
p
is a value close to 1. Tuning
p
between 0.6 and 0.8 worked well in experiments.

Finally, the number of iterations
N
is set to 7. A large number of iterations is not required here because the first iterate
F^{1}
, as discussed earlier, is already a reasonable estimate of the ground truth. Additionally, we also observe fairly quick convergence of the iterates.

C.2
Details on computing label transfer accuracy and kNN overlap

We need to following transformations on the original and denoised gene expression matrices
\mathbf{F}_{0}
and
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
in order to compute both metrics:

•

Transform the rows
(\mathbf{F}_{0})_{1\cdot},\dots,(\mathbf{F}_{0})_{m\cdot}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\mathbf{F}_{0}
elementwise by the function
t\mapsto\log(1+10^{4}\,t)
, center the resulting vectors by their mean, and perform principal component analysis (PCA) with
r
components to yield eigenvectors
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and principal component scores
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
for the pixels
i=1,\dots,m
.

•

Transform the rows
\bar{\mathbf{F}}_{1\cdot},\dots,\bar{\mathbf{F}}_{m\cdot}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\bar{\mathbf{F}}
elementwise by the function
t\mapsto\log(1+10^{4}\,t)
, center the resulting vectors by their mean, and compute their corresponding scores
\bar{Z}_{1},\dots,\bar{Z}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with respect to the eigenvectors
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
from the previous step. Keeping the eigenvectors the same across these two steps allows for meaningfully computing distances between
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\{\bar{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.

We set
r=30
in our experiments.

Computing the label transfer accuracy

The precise steps are as follows.

1.

At each pixel
i
, the original score
Z_{i}
is paired with a cell type label
l_{i}
. Train a
k
-nearest neighbour classifier on the pairs
\{(Z_{i},l_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, using the Euclidean metric.

2.

Test this classifier on the scores
\bar{Z}_{1},\dots,\bar{Z}_{m}
resulting from the denoised matrix to generate predicted labels
\bar{l}_{1},\dots,\bar{l}_{m}
.

3.

Compute the label transfer accuracy as the proportion of correctly predicted labels:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}|\{i\in[m]\mid l_{i}=\bar{l}_{i}\}|.

We use
k=9
for the
k
-nearest neighbour classifier.

Computing the kNN graphs

The precise steps are as follows.

1.

Construct a directed graph on the points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
by connecting each
Z_{i}
to
k
other
Z_{i^{\prime}}
that are closest to it in Euclidean distance
\left|Z_{i}-Z_{i^{\prime}}\right|
. Define the adjacency matrix
A_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
such that
(A_{0})_{ii^{\prime}}=1
if
Z_{i^{\prime}}
is one of the
k
closest vectors to
Z_{i}
, and
(A_{0})_{ii^{\prime}}=0
otherwise.

2.

Repeat this construction on
\bar{Z}_{1},\dots,\bar{Z}_{m}
, and compute the corresponding adjacency matrix
\bar{A}
.

3.

Compute the overlap

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle A_{0},\bar{A}\rangle_{F}.

We set
k=50
for this metric, which is reasonable following
Ahlmann-Eltze and Huber 2023
.
