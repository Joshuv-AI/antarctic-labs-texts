Feed color modifies hypothalamic appetite gene regulation and gut function in broiler chickens
Authors: Hamid Basoo-Gandomani, Behnam Ahmadipour, Fariborz Khajali, Hossein Hassanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to study the impact of feed color on hypothalamic appetite gene regulation, gut function, and performance of broiler chickens. A total of 280 male broiler chicks (Ross 308) were randomly assigned to 20 pens (14 chicks/pen; 5 pens/treatment) and fed a corn-soybean meal control diet or the same diet supplemented with 1.5 g/kg green, purple, or orange colorant. Intestinal histomorphology, serum biochemical markers, and hypothalamic gene expression (NPY, AGRP, POMC, CART) were assessed at the end of trial (42 days of age). Performance criteria were also recorded during the trial. Across the growing and finishing phases, colored feeds increased feed intake and weight gain relative to the control, with the orange group consistently showing the largest effects. The largest improvement in body weight gain and feed conversion ratio (FCR) was recorded in the orange feed group, which was associated with greater improvements in villus height and absorptive surface area in different segments of the small intestine. Green feed showed intermediate benefits, improving feed intake, weight gain, and FCR over the entire trial (1–42 d) but to a lesser extent than orange, while purple feed failed to significantly improve growth performance despite showing comparable improvements in intestinal morphology. Serum cholesterol and HDL concentrations were higher in the green group compared to the control and purple groups, total protein was higher in the orange group compared to the control, and albumin was higher in both green and orange groups compared to the control and purple groups, while hepatic enzymes, MDA, NO, LDL, triglyceride, and glucose did not differ among treatments. Colored feeds stimulated appetite through up-regulation of orexigenic genes (NPY, AGRP) and down-regulation of anorexigenic genes (POMC, CART), with the purple group showing the most pronounced orexigenic gene expression despite its poorer performance. This dissociation may be partly attributed to the reduced feed lightness index of the purple diet. The results of this study could have significant potential for practical application in commercial poultry production.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Feed color, Intestinal morphology, Appetite-regulating genes, Growth performance

Introduction

The global poultry industry seeks sustainable strategies to enhance broiler production efficiency. While nutritional optimization particularly early feed intake is critical for digestive development and growth (Medras and Al-Khalaifah, 2026), research has largely overlooked sensory attributes of feed. Feed color remains a largely underexplored modulator of broiler feeding behavior and performance.

Birds possess a highly developed and complex visual system that is fundamentally different from that of mammals. Unlike humans, which are trichromatic, avian species including domestic fowl, have a tetrachromatic color space (Cuthill et al., 2000). This means that chickens possess four types of cone photoreceptor cells, which allow them to perceive a broader spectrum of light, including ultraviolet wavelengths (Iwaniuk and Wylie, 2020; Martin, 2007). Furthermore, unique oil droplets in their retinal cones, containing carotenoid pigments, act as spectral filters that enhance color discrimination and protect the eye from phototoxic damage (Toomey et al., 2015). This advanced visual capability suggests that birds can perceive and differentiate between subtle color variations in their environment, including their feed, in ways that are largely invisible to humans (Martin, 2022). Such enhanced sensitivity suggests feed color as a potential natural stimulus to boost feed intake and growth performance (Golawski and Sytykiewicz, 2021).

Beyond behavioral changes in feeding, the mechanism by which feed color might influence the performance is not fully elucidated. The hypothalamus serves as the primary integrator of peripheral signals and nutritional status, functioning as the "center of satiety and hunger control" to regulate feeding behavior and energy homeostasis (Mahdavi et al., 2023; Volyanskaya et al., 2024). The infundibular nucleus (IN) within avian hypothalamus is the equivalent of the mammalian arcuate nucleus (ARC) and contains two distinct populations of neurons. One population co-expresses the orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) neuropeptides Y (NPY) and Agouti-related peptide (AGRP), while the other population expresses the anorexigenic (appetite-suppressing) neuropeptides Proopiomelanocortin (POMC) and Cocaine- and Amphetamine-Regulated Transcript (CART) (Volyanskaya et al., 2024). The balance between the expression of these two sets of neuropeptides ultimately dictates the state of hunger or satiety (Ahmadi et al., 2016). The role of these neuropeptides in chickens has been studied. Administration of NPY and AGRP, when centrally injected, consistently stimulates feed intake in broiler and layer chickens, confirming an orexigenic role (Greene et al., 2022; Tran et al., 2019). Conversely, POMC-derived peptides such as α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) send anorexigenic signals, which suppress feed intake when administered centrally (Scanes and Pierzchała-Koziec, 2021). Furthermore, the expression of these genes is highly sensitive to nutritional status. For example, feed restriction upregulates hypothalamic NPY and AGRP expression whereas it downregulates POMC. Nevertheless, the specific response pattern varies depending on the breed, age, and duration of fasting (Lees et al., 2017; Volyanskaya et al., 2024). Although the role of nutrient composition and energy status as modulators of this neuropeptide network has been established, the role of sensory stimuli such as feed color on hypothalamic expression of genes regulating feed intake remains largely unexplored.

Given the neuroanatomical connections between the avian visual system and hypothalamic feeding centers, we hypothesized that feed color could modulate central appetite-regulating pathways. The avian retina projects via the retinohypothalamic tract to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and lateral septal region, which in turn have reciprocal connections with the infundibular nucleus (Brandstätter and Abraham, 2003). This pathway provides a potential neuroanatomical route through which color signals detected by retinal cone photoreceptors could reach and influence the orexigenic and anorexigenic neuropeptide systems (Hanlon et. al. 2020). Therefore, we hypothesized that feed color could modulate central appetite-regulating pathways.

This study evaluated the impact of feed color on growth performance, intestinal morphology, blood biomarkers and hypothalamic appetite-regulating gene expression (NPY, AGRP, POMC, CART) in broilers chickens.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and bird management

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shahrekord University. A total of 350 male broiler chicks (Ross 308) were obtained from a commercial hatchery. Following the removal of chicks within the extreme body weight range, a number of 280 chicks with an average body weight of 41.0 ± 0.5 g were selected to be used in the study. Fourteen chicks were randomly assigned to one of 20 floor pens (1.25 × 1.25). Five such pens were randomly allotted to each treatment group. The experiment was conducted at the Shahrekord University poultry research facility (2100 m).

Throughout the 42-day trial, chicks had free access to feed and water. Environmental factors including temperature, humidity, and lighting followed according to the strain guidelines. A corn-soybean meal diet was formulated according to the strain nutrient recommendations for three distinct feeding phases: starter (1–10 d), grower (11–24 d), finisher 1 (25–33 d), and finisher 2 (34-42 d) (Table S1). A diet composed of corn and soybean meal was prepared and served as the control. Three additional diets were prepared by incorporating certified synthetic feed colorants (1.5 g/kg diet from Ajanta Food Colors, Ajanta Chemical Industries, Rajasthan, India) to the control. The colors included green, purple, and orange. Colorimetric analysis of the experimental diets (Table 1) confirmed distinct spectral characteristics for each group. A preliminary study was performed to determine the appropriate dose to give a distinct and visually consistent hue. All feed colorants were of food-grade standard.

Growth performance

Performance criteria including body weight gain (WG), feed intake (FI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were obtained during 1–10, 11–24, 25–33, 34–42, as well 1–42-day periods. Feed conversion ratio was calculated by taking the weight of mortalities into account.

Blood and serum analysis

On day 42, two birds per pen (10 birds per treatment) were randomly selected for blood collection. Blood samples (5 mL) were collected from the brachial vein into non-heparinized tubes. A portion of the blood was transferred into capillary tubes for hematocrit determination. An aliquot of blood was also taken on a glass slide to prepare a blood smear for differential white blood cell counting. Serum samples were separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C and stored at −20°C until further biochemical analyses. Biochemical variables including cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride, total protein, albumin, globulin, and glucose concentrations, as well as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities were determined in serum samples by means of an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Hitachi 902, Roche Diagnostics, Germany) using commercially available kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran).

Hematocrit (%) was measured by the microhematocrit method; blood samples were centrifuged in heparinized capillary tubes at 12,000 × g for 5 minutes, and the packed cell volume was expressed as a percentage of total blood volume. Differential leukocyte counts were determined from blood smears prepared on glass slides. Following a 2 to 4-hour fixation in methyl alcohol, the smears were stained sequentially with May-Grünwald and Giemsa stains. The heterophil to lymphocyte (H:L) ratio was then calculated based on a count of 100 leukocytes (Maxwell and Robertson, 1998).

Serum malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, a key biomarker of lipid peroxidation, was measured using the assay of thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) by the method described elsewhere (Bahadoran et al., 2025). Serum nitric oxide (NO) concentration was determined by measuring its nitrate and nitrite metabolites. The method has been given in details elsewhere (Behrooj et al., 2012).

Intestinal histomorphologic measurement

Ten birds per treatment were slaughtered for gut morphological assessments. Tissue samples of approximately 2 cm in length were taken from duodenum (at the midpoint of the duodenal loop), jejunum (midpoint between the duodenal loop and Meckel's diverticulum), and ileum (midpoint between Meckel's diverticulum and the ileocecal junction). Each intestinal specimen was gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) to eliminate residual digesta and subsequently preserved in the Clark fixative solution for 45 min. Tissue samples were then transferred to 50% ethyl alcohol for longer storage. Tissue segments were periodically put in acid-Schiff reagent for 2 to 3 min for staining (Pat et al., 2025).

Villus height (VH) and villus width (VW) were assessed using a calibrated light microscope (Olympus CX23) fitted with an ocular micrometer at 10× magnification. Three intact villi from each intestinal segment (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) were randomly chosen per each sample. Villus height was measured as the perpendicular distance from the villus tip to the crypt-villus junction. Villus width was recorded at the broadest point of the villus, perpendicular to the height axis. To improve the accuracy of measurements, three independent readings were obtained per each villus, and a mean value was obtained. The thickness of the lamina propria was also evaluated at the villus base. Villus surface area (VSA) was calculated using the formula (Khajali et al., 2011): VSA = 2π × VW/2 × VH, where VW and VH show villus width and height, respectively.

Carcass characteristics and organ weights

All carcass characteristics were recorded and expressed as a percentage of live body weight at slaughter. Carcass yield was determined following feather removal, evisceration, and removal of the head, neck, and feet. The breast muscle (pectoralis major and minor) and both thighs (including drumstick and thigh) were carefully dissected and weighed for breast and thigh yield determination, respectively. The abdominal fat pad, including fat surrounding the gizzard and cloaca, was removed and weighed. The heart, liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were carefully excised, blotted dry, and weighed individually.

Forebrain (Ventral basal region) tissue collection for gene expression analysis

On day 42, two birds per pen (10 birds per treatment) were randomly selected from euthanized birds for forebrain tissue collection. The brain was rapidly excised, and a tissue block containing the hypothalamus was carefully dissected from the ventral basal region of the diencephalon (forebrain) (Cerreta and McEntire, 2025; Kuenzel, 2018). The dissected tissue block, which encompasses the hypothalamic region, was immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70°C until RNA extraction.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and gene expression assay by RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the snap-frozen forebrain tissue samples using RNX-Plus reagent (Sinaclon Bioscience, Karaj, Iran) according to the manufacturer's protocol. This protocol was based on the acid guanidinium thiocyanate-phenol-chloroform single-step extraction method. To eliminate any potential contamination with genomic DNA, the extracted RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I (Sinaclon Bioscience). RNA purity and integrity were assessed by measuring the ratio of absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm (A260/A280 ratio) using a spectrophotometer. Samples with A260/A280 ratios from 1.8 to 2.2 were selected for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. First-strand cDNA was subsequently synthesized from the purified RNA using the Easy cDNA Synthesis Kit (Parstous Co., Mashhad, Iran) following the supplier's recommended procedures. The resulting cDNA products were stored at −20°C until further processing (Hassanpour et al., 2023).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted to evaluate the relative mRNA expression levels of the appetite-regulating genes NPY, AGRP, POMC, and CART. Tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein zeta (YWHAZ) was selected as the reference gene for normalization, as its expression remained stable across all treatment groups in preliminary validation experiments. Each PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 10 µL containing 10 ng of cDNA template, 400 nM of each forward and reverse gene-specific primer (Table S2), and SYBR Green Master Mix-Plus (Amplicon, Odense, Denmark). All reactions were run in technical triplicate for each biological sample. The thermal cycling program comprised an initial denaturation step at 94°C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 15 seconds and combined annealing and extension at 60–62°C for 20–30 seconds, with the annealing temperature optimized for each primer pair. To ensure the reliability of the results, no-template controls and no-reverse transcriptase controls were included in each run. Amplification data were analyzed using LinRegPCR software to determine the cycle threshold values and amplification efficiencies for each primer pair. Relative quantification of target gene expression was performed using the Pfaffl method, which accounts for differences in amplification efficiency between the target genes and the reference gene (Ahmadipour et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.1) with the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure applied under a completely randomized design. Gene expression data were normalized prior to analysis to satisfy model assumptions. When significant treatment effects were identified, mean separation was conducted using Duncan's multiple range test, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). To assess the magnitude of treatment effects, Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for pairwise comparisons between treatment groups and the control group using PROC TTEST, defined as the difference between group means divided by the pooled standard deviation.

Discussion

In general, live performance of the broiler chickens was lower than the strain performance objectives. This difference may partly reflect the high-altitude conditions of the experimental site. Hypobaric hypoxia can impose cardiopulmonary and metabolic stress in broilers and may reduce growth efficiency (Khajali, 2022). Because hypoxia-related pathways, including HIF signaling, were not measured in the present study, this explanation should be regarded as a contextual interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism. High-altitude stress may also have influenced feeding behavior and hypothalamic gene expression, potentially modifying the magnitude of the response to feed color. This possibility requires direct comparison across altitudes.

In this study, the color of feed had a significant impact on live performance. Feed color, particularly the orange treatment, was associated with consistent improvements in feed intake, body-weight gain, and FCR. The concurrent changes in hypothalamic appetite-related gene expression suggest that altered central appetite signaling may have contributed to this response; however, the present design does not establish a causal relationship. Nevertheless, green and purple colors were not as effective as orange in terms of improving live performance. Green feed resulted in a weight gain inferior to the orange, while purple feed caused a slight negative change in FCR, indicating color-specific and not universally beneficial effects. These differential responses to specific colors are aligned with the avian visual system's tetrachromatic capacity, which allows birds to perceive and discriminate between colors in ways that fundamentally differ from mammalian perception (Tanaka, 2015). The color-specific responses may partly reflect differences in the spectral and photometric properties of the diets. The orange diet showed reflectance predominantly in the long-wavelength region, whereas the green and purple diets differed in their relative contributions to medium- and short-wavelength regions. Because chickens possess multiple cone classes with spectral sensitivities that differ from those of humans (Hart and Hunt, 2007), these diets may have generated distinct visual signals under the lighting conditions used in the present study. Nevertheless, reflectance measurements alone cannot determine cone activation or establish that the orange diet produced a stronger retinal signal. Controlled studies that quantify spectral reflectance under experimental illumination and directly assess feeding preference are therefore needed. The retinohypothalamic tract projects to the suprachiasmatic nucleus and lateral septal region, which reciprocally connect with the infundibular nucleus, providing a theoretically plausible route through which visual information could influence hypothalamic regions involved in feeding regulation. Whether feed-color signals engage this pathway, and whether they affect NPY/AGRP or POMC/CART neurons directly or indirectly, remains unknown. Future work could combine controlled lighting and color conditions with behavioral monitoring, neuronal activation markers, and measurements of circulating appetite-related hormones to test this hypothesis.

Cooper (1971) reported that turkey poults preferred a green colored feed over a non-colored control, which was associated with a greater weight gain. Gulizia and Downs (2021) reported an inconsistent impact of feed color on chicken performance. They found that blue and purple colors had a significant impact on broiler performance. However, their study was limited to 1 to 21 days of age. In another study, Vargas et al. (2025) reported that broilers preferred uncolored feed over blue and purple diets. This study was done in the starter stage and did not cover the entire growing cycle of broiler production. Mesmarian and Toghyani (2024) reported that broilers preferred green feed. Farghly and Abdelfattah (2017) identified orange as the most effective color for improving growth performance, which was in line with the findings of our experiment. The inconsistency among studies may reflect differences in bird age, lighting spectrum, diet composition, colorant concentration, colorimetric properties, feeding experience, and whether preference or growth performance was assessed. Importantly, a color that is preferred during a short-term choice test may not necessarily improve cumulative feed efficiency, because preference, intake stimulation, nutrient utilization, and growth response represent distinct outcomes. Feed color had no significant influence on carcass characteristics.

Feed color significantly improved intestinal morphology across all three intestinal segments, with the orange had a profound effect in terms of villus height and villus absorptive surface area. Similarly, Liu et al. (2023) reported that blue and blue-green LED light improved ileal morphology in broilers, supporting the broader concept that spectral manipulation, whether via feed color or environmental light, can influence intestinal development. However, the optimal color may differ depending on stimulus modality (feed vs. lighting), reflecting distinct sensory pathways.

The reduction in lamina propria thickness in the colored groups also warrants caution. As this tissue contains blood vessels and gut-associated lymphoid tissue, a thinner lamina propria is not necessarily beneficial and could reflect either reduced inflammation or reduced lymphoid mass (Dal Pont et al., 2023). Without histological assessment of immune infiltration, we cannot distinguish between these possibilities, and this change should not be automatically equated with improved intestinal morphology. Whether these morphological changes translate into enhanced nutrient transporter expression and absorptive function remains to be determined.

While the orange diet produced the greatest improvements in live performance criteria, the purple diet failed to improve performance to such an extent, despite comparable improvements in gut morphometry. This dissociation indicates that intestinal development alone is insufficient for improved performance; central appetite regulation, feed intake patterns, and metabolic efficiency also play critical roles (Te Pas et al., 2020). Purple-fed birds exhibited greater intestinal surface area without a corresponding improvement in performance. This finding suggests that structural changes in the intestine alone were insufficient to improve growth under the conditions of this study. Feed intake pattern, nutrient utilization, metabolic efficiency, and unmeasured peripheral signals may have contributed to this dissociation (Browning et al., 2026).

The expression profile was generally consistent with a shift toward orexigenic signaling in some colored-feed groups, although the response was gene- and color-specific. The clearest increase in NPY and AGRP occurred in the purple group, whereas POMC expression was reduced in the orange and purple groups. These transcriptional changes may indicate altered appetite-related signaling, but they cannot be equated directly with increased appetite or peptide activity. While this expectation happened for green and orange colors, purple-fed birds did not achieve superior live performance to the extent observed in other colored feeds. The findings suggest feed color may influence hypothalamic neuropeptide expression via visual pathways projecting to feeding-related brain regions (Volyanskaya et al., 2024), with distinct spectral inputs activating different neural circuits. This color-specific gene expression pattern has not been previously reported and it represents a significant contribution to understanding sensory-metabolic integration in birds. The mismatch between hypothalamic signaling and performance in purple-fed birds may have several explanations. First, gene expression was measured at a single point (day 42) and may reflect a transient state rather than the full pattern of appetite regulation across the rearing period. Second, one possibility is that the darker purple diet reduced visual attractiveness or induced a transient neophobic response; however, this interpretation remains speculative because feed preference and feeding behavior were not measured. Third, peripheral satiety signals (e.g., CCK, GLP-1, PYY) or altered vagal signaling may have counteracted central orexigenic drive; these were not measured here. Fourth, post-transcriptional regulation may uncouple NPY/AGRP mRNA levels from functional peptide activity, and fifth, the synthetic colorants themselves may have direct physiological effects on gut function or metabolism that are independent of their spectral properties. Collectively, these possibilities indicate that intestinal development alone is insufficient for improved performance and that central appetite regulation, peripheral satiety signaling, learned color preferences, and post-transcriptional regulation must be considered together. Future studies incorporating automated feeding behavior monitoring, serial gene expression profiling, gut hormone measurements, and short-term preference tests are needed to disentangle these factors.

Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. Only one sex and one broiler strain were evaluated, which limits generalizability. The experimental unit for performance was the pen (five pens per treatment), whereas blood, morphological, and gene-expression data were obtained from two birds per pen; statistical analyses should therefore be based on pen means where appropriate. Gene expression was measured from a ventral basal forebrain tissue block rather than a microdissected infundibular nucleus; consequently, the measured transcripts cannot be assigned exclusively to appetite-regulating neurons. Feed color stability during storage and after mixing was not assessed, and the chemical composition of the colorants beyond colorimetric properties was not characterized. Light intensity and spectral composition at feeder level, which strongly influence color perception, were not reported. Finally, no measurements of feed digestibility, nutrient transporter expression, brush-border enzyme activity, gut barrier function, or microbiota were performed.

In conclusion, under the conditions of this study, feed color influenced broiler performance, intestinal morphology, and the expression of selected hypothalamic appetite-related genes. The orange diet produced the most consistent improvement in feed intake, body-weight gain, and FCR and was accompanied by greater villus height and surface area. These morphological changes provide a possible structural basis for improved intestinal function, although nutrient absorption was not directly measured. The purple diet increased NPY and AGRP expression and improved intestinal morphology but did not improve growth performance, emphasizing that transcriptional and structural responses do not necessarily predict whole-animal performance. The mechanisms underlying these color-specific responses remain uncertain and may involve feed acceptance, visual properties, central and peripheral appetite signaling, and effects of the colorants independent of visual perception. Further studies incorporating feed-choice tests, behavioral monitoring, serial molecular sampling, gut hormones, digestibility, nutrient transporter expression, microbiota, and multiple lighting and altitude conditions are needed before practical recommendations can be made.
