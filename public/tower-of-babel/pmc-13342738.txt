Effect of prophylactic antimicrobial therapy in orthodontic tooth extractions: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Prakhar Mittal, Amiya Agrawal, Arunesh K. Tiwari, Akhilesh K. Pandey, Vibha Singh, Geeta Singh, Sumit Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The use of antibiotics postdental extraction is quite prevalent, leading to widespread healthcare crisis, antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Judicious use of these antibiotics can prevent the onset of adverse events and enhance the positive patient outcome. The role of antibiotic therapy in orthodontic extraction of premolars among the healthy cohorts is unclear, requiring due consideration.Materials and Methods:The prospective study explores the role of prophylactic antibiotic in orthodontic extraction of premolar teeth. Forty-four patients were randomly divided into two groups: group A included 23 patients receiving Amoxicillin 500 mg, while group B included 21 patients receiving Lactose capsule (placebo), for 3 days postextraction. Postextraction the patients were evaluated for postoperative wound healing using Landry scale at day 3, day 10, and day 21. Pain scores were evaluated using 100 mm Visual Analog Scale at day 10.Results:This study showed better wound healing in group A (3.74 ± 0.45) compared to group B (3.05 ± 0.22) (P < 0.001) at postop day 3. However, the second and third follow-up demonstrated similar wound healing with no significant difference noted (P = 0.862 and P = 1.0, respectively). The pain scores demonstrated similar results across both group A (mean 8.78 ± 6.95 mm) and group B (mean 9.05 ± 6.70 mm).Conclusion:Similar wound healing progression across both the groups along with similar pain control challenges the need for antibiotic therapy in orthodontic extraction of healthy teeth. The rising AMR has led to equivocal increase in opportunist infections, requiring the healthcare professionals to judiciously use the antibiotics.

INTRODUCTION

Dental extraction is the most common minor oral surgical procedure performed in outpatient clinic.[1] Common indications for tooth extraction include nonsalvageable tooth or to facilitate orthodontic or prosthodontic and traumatic indications accounting for a smaller but important proportion.[23] In orthodontics, extraction of permanent tooth-most often the premolars is an established treatment option for managing malocclusions, severe crowding, and facial discrepancies.[45]

Although frequently performed, tooth extraction is not without risk of surgical site infection. Postoperative complications, including pain, swelling, dry socket, abscess and local infection, trismus and neurosensory disturbances, are often documented.[67] The use of systemic antibiotics to reduce the incidence of anticipated surgical site infection is termed antibiotic prophylaxis. Prophylaxis may be justified when the infection to be prevented is associated with high morbidity and mortality, or when it is relatively common but clinically significant.[8] According to a survey of knowledge and practice of antibiotic prescription, about 79.23% of the dental practitioners were unaware of the antimicrobial stewardship concepts, and 72.31% were not aware of the WHO’s access, watch, reserve (AWaRe) guidelines.[9] In developed countries, the incidence of antibiotic prescription ranges from 7 to 10% of the total antibiotics prescribed in healthcare, and a major proportion of them have been found to be inappropriate.[10] The widespread abuse of antibiotics has significantly contributed to antimicrobial resistance, leading to a major healthcare menace.

The scientific literature on the use of prophylactic antibiotics postdental extraction is highly controversial, focusing largely on the removal of impacted third molars. Even within this group, the results are contradictory. Sekhar et al. found that antibiotic prophylaxis did not reduce the postextraction morbidity.[11]

The standard guidelines support more of a restrictive approach in antibiotic use postdental extraction. The American Heart Association significantly restricts antibiotic prophylaxis for infective endocarditis, limiting it only to the highest risk cardiac patients.[12] Also, a recent Cochrane review concluded that the evidence for antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent infective endocarditis after dental procedures is limited and uncertain.[13]

The role of postextraction antimicrobials following orthodontic tooth extraction remains unclear. Several studies have attempted to address the issue, with results largely lying in the grey zone. The present study aims to evaluate whether postextraction systemic antimicrobials improve socket healing compared with placebo in healthy patients undergoing orthodontic extractions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical approval

A prospective double-armed, triple-blinded, parallel-group, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted according to CONSORT guidelines after getting the ethical approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. XIX-PGTSC-IIB-IMR-S/P5), dated 6th September 2023.

Settings and consent

This study was conducted in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at a tertiary hospital from September 2023 to December 2023 [Figure 1].

The eligibility criteria were followed for recruitment into the study for evaluation. All the procedures done for this study complied with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 (revised in 2000) and written informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using Cochran’s formula for proportions with a conservative estimate of prevalence (P = 0.5), a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a desired margin of error (precision) of 5% (e = 0.05). Cochran’s initial estimate (n0) was 384; applying the finite population correction for an OPD population of N = 50 resulted in a final sample size of approximately 44. This sample size provides an estimate of proportions with ±5% margin of error (95% CI).

Randomization and blinding

Each eligible patient was assigned a consecutive study number (1–44) at enrollment. A random number table determined group allocation (A or B). The departmental pharmacist (not part of the study), prepared identical packets labeled “A” or “B” and maintained a confidential code list.

Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) containing group codes (A or B) as per the randomization list. These envelopes were given to a nursing staff member unaware of the contents. After extraction, the investigator referred each participant to the nurse, who opened the assigned envelope and dispensed the corresponding medication packet. The study was triple blinded and CONSORT guidelines for randomized controlled trial were followed.

Interventions

Patients indicated for orthodontic extraction of tooth were randomly divided into two groups:

Group A (Antibiotic group, N = 23): Amoxicillin 500 mg thrice daily, Diclofenac 50 mg twice daily was given for 3 days postextraction.

Group B (Placebo group, N = 21): Lactose capsule thrice daily, Diclofenac 50 mg twice daily was given for 3 days postextraction.

Data collection

Postorthodontic extraction of premolars, each patient was followed up for a total of 21 days. The first follow up was on third postoperative day, the second was on day 10, and the third follow up was on day 21 postextraction. At each follow up the patient’s extraction site was graded for the healing using the wound healing index, adopted from Landry et al. The index was based on five-point evaluation (1-very poor, 2-poor, 3-good, 4-very good, and 5-excellent) measured at the set follow-ups [Figure 2a and b]. The pain was evaluated at postop day 10 using the 100 mm VAS.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For between-group comparisons: Independent samples t-test was applied to compare normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., age, VAS pain scores). Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonparametric data (e.g., healing index scores at each recall point). Chi-square test compared categorical variables such as gender distribution and healing score categories.

For within-group comparisons of healing progression over follow-up intervals, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used.

Effect size for continuous variables was calculated using Cohen’s d to estimate the magnitude of difference between groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The study involved 44 participants, divided into Group A (n = 23, 52.3%) who received postoperative antibiotics and Group B (n = 21, 47.7%) who received a placebo. The overall sample consisted of 18 males (40.9%) and 26 females (59.1%). There was no statistically significant difference in gender distribution in Group A and Group B; however, there was a statistically significant difference in the mean age of group A (15.96 ± 1.19) and group B (18.14 ± 1.53) (Z = −4.314, P < 0.001) [Table 1]. None of the patients reported any major complications or needs for rescue analgesia or antibiotics.

DISCUSSION

The rising misuse of antibiotics worldwide has highlighted the pressing issue of antibiotics resistance across the healthcare. About 10% of the medicines prescribed from the dental clinics amount to antibiotics prescriptions.[14] Their indiscriminate use along with easily available as over the counter medication has led to serious concerns regarding the antibiotic-resistant infections, risk of serious adverse events like anaphylaxis along with antibiotic colitis.[15] There is dearth of sufficient literature regarding the judicious use of antibiotics in orthodontic extraction, with no evident justification of the prophylactic antibiotics use.

Routine dental extractions can temporarily impact a patient’s quality of life, with issues such as changes in speech, difficulty communicating at work, reduced work efficiency, and postextraction pain.[16] However, the decision to prescribe prophylactic antibiotics for simple, nonsurgical extractions should be made cautiously, ensuring that any potential benefits truly justify their use. The present study evaluated the efficacy of prophylactic antibiotics in orthodontic extraction of premolar teeth. At the first follow-up on day 3, patients in the antibiotic group showed significantly better wound healing than those in the placebo group. A higher proportion of participants in the antibiotic group (73.9%) achieved a healing score of 4 compared with only 4.8% in the placebo group; this disparity may be explained by the recruitment of a greater number of younger patients in the antibiotic arm, representing a potential confounding factor. The superior scores observed on the third postoperative day in the antibiotic group are more likely attributable to inflammatory responses rather than true biological healing of the extraction socket. Furthermore, the lack of pain assessment at day 3 limits the generalizability of conclusions regarding improved early healing in patients receiving antibiotics.

The second follow-up at day 10, and the third follow-up at day 21 demonstrated no significant difference across the groups, as both showed near-complete healing. Cinquini et al., in a similar study, reported no significant difference in terms of surgical site infection among the antibiotic and placebo groups.[17] Hurkat et al. conducted a prospective study on 100 patients to assess the necessity of postoperative antibiotics following orthodontic premolar extractions in healthy individuals. They found no significant benefit of antibiotic use in preventing postoperative infections, thereby questioning their role as prophylactic antimicrobial.[18]

Yi et al., in their retrospective study of 1821 extractions over 6 years, noted postoperative infection in only 1.4% of the cases, thereby negating the justification of need for prophylactic antibiotics in noninfected extraction cases.[6]

The intragroup comparison across the various follow-up periods in the placebo group demonstrated significant improvement in wound healing, indicating that wound healing eventually occurred without any complications. A similar trend was observed in the intragroup comparison of the antibiotic group, demonstrating no added advantage of antibiotics in orthodontic extraction of premolar teeth. Agrawal et al., in a cross-sectional study involving 112 patients undergoing surgical removal of noninfected, impacted third molars for orthodontic purposes, found no statistically significant difference between the antibiotic and placebo groups with respect to postoperative morbidity, including pain, swelling, and trismus.[19]

Sologova et al., in a systematic review assessing the effectiveness of prophylactic antibiotics in reducing postoperative infection rates, found no meaningful benefit of antibiotic use in third molar extractions. Instead, they noted that the potential adverse drug reactions may outweigh the advantages.[15]

The present study also aligns with the findings of Yousuf et al., who assessed the role of antibiotics in simple extractions among 146 patients. In their study sample, only 11 patients developed dry socket, with an equal number occurring in both the antibiotic and nonantibiotic groups. One patient developed a postoperative infection, and this was in the nonantibiotic group.[20] Their results clearly indicate that prophylactic antibiotics offer no added benefit in routine simple extractions.

In contrast, Torof et al., in their systematic review and meta-analysis, reported that antibiotic use during third molar extractions reduced the risk of postoperative infection by 69%. However, they did not support the routine use of antibiotics as prophylaxis in noninfected cases, as no significant reduction was observed in postoperative inflammation or wound dehiscence.[21] Pérez-Amate et al. conducted a study evaluating the patient’s perception on the need of antibiotics postdental extractions. Approximately 76.6% of patients believed antibiotics were necessary after extraction to prevent infection. However, this perceived need was significantly associated with lower education levels, younger age, and limited awareness of antibiotic resistance making them more prone for self-medication.[22]

The evaluation of postextraction pain showed marginally better pain control in antibiotic group (mean VAS 8.78 ± 6.95) of patients compared to the placebo group (mean VAS 9.05 ± 6.70), although the results were not statistically significant. Similar results were reported by Cinquini et al., where the authors evaluated three group of patients, antibiotic group, antibiotic with probiotics, neither antibiotic nor probiotics. They concluded that at first week postextraction, pain was significantly higher in control group compared to antibiotic with probiotics group (P = 0.016); however, at subsequent follow up, the pain scores were similar amongst all the groups with no surgical site infection noted in any of the group.[17]

In our tertiary care hospital, the most common antibiotic prescription is Amoxcillin with similar prescription being reported in other studies as well.[1023] The indiscriminate use of these antibiotics is not without adverse events including penicillin related allergy (incidence of 10–20%), antibiotic-related colitis.[24] The worldwide rise in antimicrobial resistance has led to a major healthcare crisis and significant financial burden, contributing to higher morbidity and prolonged hospital stays. This ongoing challenge is further aggravated by the absence of any newly discovered antibiotic classes since the 1980s.[1]

Antibiotic prophylaxis is generally advised only for a small group of patients who are at high risk for serious infectious complications, such as for infective endocarditis, prosthetic joint infections, medication-related osteonecrosis of jaws, particularly in medically compromised patients. However, their use in healthy individuals undergoing routine dental extractions is not recommended, in order to limit the development of antimicrobial resistance and its associated consequences.[25]

The results of the study have demonstrated similar wound healing progression across both the groups along with adequate pain control. The lack of adverse events and complications throughout the study further challenges the necessity of prophylactic antibiotics for orthodontic premolar extractions in healthy individuals.

The strengths of the present study include its robust design, objective assessment, and comprehensive analysis of wound healing. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size, calculated using Cochran’s formula, may not be optimal for repeated-measures outcomes such as longitudinal wound healing indices. Correlating analgesic use with extraction socket healing at the third postoperative day could have further strengthened the study’s inferences. Future studies with larger sample sizes, multicentric designs, and incorporation of quality-of-life assessments are warranted to further validate these findings.

CONCLUSION

The rise of antibiotic resistance has created major challenges for global healthcare, largely influenced by poor adherence to established guidelines. In healthy individuals undergoing orthodontic premolar extractions, prophylactic antibiotic therapy did not confer sustained or clinically meaningful benefits in terms of wound healing or pain control when compared with placebo. Although improved healing scores were observed at the early postoperative assessment, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are likely influenced by inflammatory responses and demographic confounding rather than true biological healing. The absence of differences at subsequent follow-up points reinforces the limited role of routine antibiotic use in such procedures. Judicious, evidence-based antibiotic prescribing is therefore essential to mitigate the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance.
