Reply to: The efficacy and safety of antidepressants as alternative treatment of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in pediatric populations
Authors: Joshua Caballero, Julio Chavez
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

In their recent literature review, “The efficacy and safety of antidepressants as alternative treatment of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in pediatric populations,” Hilo and colleagues1 describe the available literature on antidepressant use in pediatric attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), but appear to have methodologic inconsistencies.

First, the inclusion criteria state “Only trials comparing antidepressants against first-line stimulants or placebo were included.”1 However, 25% of their included studies (Table 2)1 did not have a direct comparator, limiting internal validity and clinical inference. Additionally, the authors list 4 venlafaxine studies, but at least 5 are published.2 The missing article is a 6-week, double-blind study comparing venlafaxine vs methylphenidate in children with ADHD.3 In summary, venlafaxine produced statistically significant reductions in ADHD Rating Scale IV scores, with response rates of 33% (parent-rated) and 44% (teacher-rated), reflecting modest to moderate clinical improvement. In comparison, methylphenidate demonstrated a higher response rate of 95% within 2 weeks (P < 0.001) across both parent and teacher scales, indicating faster and greater symptom control, reinforcing stimulants as first-line therapy.3 We ask the authors to clarify their study inclusion and exclusion criteria and edit the results, as needed.

Second, the review’s stated scope of included antidepressants appears internally inconsistent. The methods in 1 section state a focus on “FDA-approved antidepressants for pediatric ADHD,” while in another section, it states a focus on “studies evaluating the efficacy of medications that are FDA approved for depression in the antidepressant class.”1 Additionally, they state at the end of their methodology, “Because this review focuses specifically on FDA-approved antidepressants for pediatric ADHD, studies related to these 2 medications fall outside the defined scope.” As it stands, the included evidence provides results listing 11 of 12 studies evaluating antidepressants not FDA-indicated in pediatric populations in the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry guidance they cite. We ask the authors to clarify their inclusion criteria for antidepressants and adjust the results as needed.

The article’s framing, while not intentional, may inadvertently imply broader support for serotonin-reuptake inhibitors in ADHD than warranted. Their discussion, stating most (11/12) studies show efficacy compared to placebo or stimulant, without delineating which antidepressants had more data in either the abstract or discussion, should be interpreted cautiously. In fact, this statement is incorrect, as 4 of 12 studies lacked a direct comparator. Additionally, although only 1 small, open-label fluoxetine study with no comparator is included, their article highlights serotonergic dysregulation as emerging evidence without clearly separating mechanistic hypotheses from clinical efficacy. Although the authors cite serotonergic activity,4 this does not establish serotonin-reuptake inhibitors as effective ADHD treatment. Clinically, serotonin-reuptake inhibitors are more plausibly relevant to comorbid anxiety or depression than core ADHD symptoms.5

Finally, the discussion of neurotransmitter activity and adverse effects (eg, increased blood pressure) is clinically relevant but does not clearly distinguish general pharmacologic data from pediatric findings. Pediatric venlafaxine trials used lower doses (∼40–75 mg/day) with limited and inconsistent cardiovascular effects, creating a risk of overinterpretation. Overall, efficacy and safety differences across antidepressant subclasses should be more clearly delineated for pediatric populations.

In summary, clearer methods and sharper distinctions among antidepressant subclasses should be elucidated. For example, discussing which antidepressant subclasses offer more evidence for ADHD should be clearly stated. While Hilo et al1 note limitations (eg, small samples, limited head-to-head comparisons, incomplete safety reporting), we encourage the authors to (1) clarify their methodology (eg, explicit criteria for antidepressants used, inclusion/exclusion of studies) and revise results as needed, (2) delineate efficacy and safety differences across antidepressant subclasses, and (3) revise conclusions as needed. In conclusion, our aim is not to mischaracterize the authors’ conclusions, but rather to encourage clearer differentiation of the evidence base to support accurate interpretation by readers.
