Impact of social determinants of health on rehabilitation service use and outcomes in adults in the USA: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Jordan Wickstrom, Emma J Leone, Noa Sasson, Hailey C Morris, Alicia A Livinski, Nathan D Camarillo, Justine Lely, Rajpriya Venkatarajan, Julie Goldberg, Afua Asante-Otoo, Elizabeth K Rasch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMany Americans fail to receive equitable access to or positive outcomes from rehabilitation services. These disparities result from broader societal inequities that disproportionately affect certain groups of people due to non-medical factors such as race, gender, education and living status. This scoping review aims to describe research papers that examine social determinants of health (SDOH) that underlie disparities in access to, use of, or outcomes related to rehabilitation (in occupational therapy, physical therapy, recreational therapy, rehabilitation medicine/physiatry and speech-language pathology) among adults in the USA. Identifying SDOH-related factors that drive inequities in rehabilitation will inform the development of potential solutions to improve access, professional education and patient outcomes.Methods and analysisThe Joanna Briggs Institute methodological framework will be used to conduct this scoping review. To meet the inclusion criteria, articles will need to be published in English since 2013, be classified as full-length original research, include at least 100 sampled adults, be conducted in the USA or its territories, and investigate SDOH-related disparities in rehabilitation. Search terms will include SDOH, rehabilitation and disparities. Five databases (Embase, PubMed, Scopus, Sociological Abstracts and Web of Science: Core Collection) will be searched for articles published in English between 2013 and 2023. Records from these databases as well as reference lists of included papers and review papers will be screened for eligibility. Two people will independently review each record during title and abstract screening and full-text screening, and three people will independently extract data. Screening and data extraction processes will be piloted. The results will be presented in tables, graphs and narrative summaries.Ethics and disseminationFindings will be disseminated through scientific conferences, professional networks, stakeholder meetings, invited workshops and webinars, email listservs of professional associations, academic social media and in a peer-reviewed journal. Ethics approval is not required as the review will only include previously published data.Keywords: Health Equity, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Review, Health Services Accessibility, Research Design

Introduction

Background and rationale

With its shifting age structure and ever-growing population, the USA is experiencing a rise in chronic conditions, illnesses and injuries, ultimately leading to a steep demand for rehabilitation services.1 However, despite global2 efforts to address this problem, a surprisingly small number of Americans in need of services are able to receive them;3 4 among those who do receive services, many fail to achieve favourable outcomes. For example, racial-ethnic minority groups are less likely to receive postacute rehabilitation after stroke, traumatic brain injury, hip fracture and spinal cord injury.5 Black adults with traumatic brain injury have been shown to have lower functional ability at discharge from rehabilitation compared with non-Hispanic white adults.6 These disparities are inequitable, directly related to the historical and current unequal distribution of resources, and disproportionately affect certain groups of people (eg, factors such as race, gender, education and living status).5 Identifying the social and economic factors contributing to health disparities in rehabilitation is an essential step in navigating the barriers preventing Americans from achieving optimal health and functioning.

This mission aligns with the priority areas identified by Healthy People 2030, an initiative striving to address the social determinants of health (SDOH) in the USA to facilitate advancements in health equity and health literacy.7 This initiative defines SDOH as, ‘the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age that affect a wide range of health, functioning, and quality-of-life outcomes and risks,’ which are organised into five domains: economic stability, education access and quality, healthcare access and quality, neighbourhood and built environment and social and community context.7 SDOH are associated with opportunities and resources that maintain or improve one’s health, and they have a major influence on general well-being.8 Health inequities driven by SDOH occur when underlying social structures and systemic processes unjustly assign people to different social positions. These opaque yet impactful allocation processes evolve over time and are reinforced by social ideologies and practice,9 resulting in unintentional or unrecognised disparities. Thus, for Americans in need of rehabilitation services, it is essential to examine the SDOH-related barriers contributing to disparate access to, use of, or outcomes related to rehabilitation services.

Several review papers have been published in recent years regarding SDOH in the context of rehabilitation; however, these reviews have generally had a narrow focus on a single SDOH category (race/ethnicity10 11 and non-urban settings),12,14 condition (anterior cruciate ligament injury,15 intellectual and developmental disabilities,16 pelvic floor issues,17 persistent pain,18 rotator cuff repairs,19 voice and upper airway disorders20 and neurological conditions)21 or rehabilitation discipline (occupational therapy,12 14 physical therapy15 18 22 and speech-language pathology).20 While the literature has described SDOH as they broadly relate to general health outcomes in the US healthcare system, it fails to comprehensively or systematically describe the complex, multifactorial interplay between SDOH and rehabilitation services. The current scoping review aims to explore this gap by summarising SDOH-related disparities examined in relation to rehabilitation access, use and outcomes. With a broader scope than the aforementioned reviews, this review will include all SDOH categories and conditions examined across a variety of rehabilitation disciplines: occupational therapy, physical therapy, recreational therapy, rehabilitation medicine/physiatry and speech-language pathology. Rehabilitation settings considered in this review will also be all-encompassing, spanning from acute hospital care to postacute inpatient rehabilitation, home health, telehealth and outpatient care.

We chose to conduct a scoping review rather than another method of data synthesis because scoping reviews are designed to identify and map the available evidence regarding a broad topic and its related concepts.23 Thus, in alignment with our primary objective, a scoping review is appropriate because we aim to investigate a broad, exploratory question with expansive inclusion criteria and provide a clear indication of the volume of literature on this topic.23 Since our objective is to characterise the literature on SDOH in the context of rehabilitation, a scoping review will help us better understand what research has been done, recognise the current gaps in knowledge and identify subsequent steps to address inequities.

As the number of people requiring rehabilitation services continues to rise, it is crucial to expand our understanding of the intricate and dynamic relationships between SDOH and rehabilitation services. This scoping review will allow us to identify these relationships using the Healthy People 2030 framework,7 the methods used to explore them (eg, study designs, data sources and study location), and the samples in which they have been examined (eg, sample size, race/ethnicity, sex/gender, conditions and age). Improved insight into such nuanced relationships will allow for the development of innovative solutions that address the factors contributing to rehabilitation-related disparities and will inform the education of key stakeholders, collaboration with public health organisations and creation of equitable health policy and practice.

Objective

The aim of this scoping review is to collate and describe research papers published since 2013 that investigate SDOH-related disparities in access to, use of, or outcomes related to rehabilitation. Specifically, the relationship between SDOH and rehabilitation—in the context of occupational therapy, physical therapy, recreational therapy, rehabilitation medicine/physiatry and speech-language pathology—will be investigated in adults in the USA.

Planning for this project began in October 2023, and the expected end date (journal submission) is January 2025.

Participants

Studies that include adult participants (ages 18 years and older) who have tried to access or have used rehabilitation services will be included. A sample size of at least 100 will be required to ensure sufficient power (generalisable results).24

Concept

Sources must examine SDOH, rehabilitation and disparities. Specifically, each source must include (1) at least one SDOH (as defined by Healthy People 2030Healthy People 2030),7 (2) at least one rehabilitation discipline: occupational therapy, physical therapy, recreational therapy, rehabilitation medicine/physiatry and speech-language pathology and (3) an SDOH-related disparity in access to, use of, or outcomes related to rehabilitation.

Context

This review will only consider studies that have been conducted in the USA and/or its territories due to vast global differences in the structure of healthcare and health policies. Studies conducted outside of (or in addition to) the USA and/or its territories will be excluded. We will limit to articles published in English since it is the predominant language in our setting of interest.

Types of sources

Only full-length original research articles will be considered for inclusion. Examples of publication types that will be excluded can be found in table 1. Articles must be published since 2013 to capture the most recent literature on this topic.

Search strategy

To write this protocol, we followed guidance from Lely and colleagues (2023)25 and abided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).26 The proposed scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews27 and reported using the PRISMA-ScR.26 The results of the search will be reported in full in the final scoping review and presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.28

The comprehensive search strategy will be developed by a biomedical librarian (AAL) through iterative consultation with the review team. The final strategy will be validated through the retrieval of a key set of relevant records.

The databases (vendors) to be searched are Embase (Elsevier), PubMed (US National Library of Medicine), Scopus (Elsevier), Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest) and Web of Science: Core Collection (Clarivate Analytics). The searches will be limited by publication year (January 2013–December 2023), language (English) and type (full-length original research). This will allow us to capture the most recent literature published in the predominant language of our region of interest. Controlled vocabulary terms (eg, MeSH, EMTREE) and keywords to be used for the main concepts of our research question include SDOH, rehabilitation and disparities. See online supplemental appendix A for the anticipated full search strategy for one database (PubMed).

In addition to searching databases, the reference lists of (1) included records (articles that passed full-text review) and (2) relevant review papers (systematic, scoping, meta-analysis, narrative) will be prescreened to identify other potentially relevant articles. To identify the review papers, a separate search will be conducted in PubMed using a modified version of the original search strategy. Resulting review papers will be prescreened for eligibility (to ensure the review papers are relevant to our topic). For the review papers that pass this prescreening phase, the reference lists of each review paper will be inspected for potential relevance. References that pass this initial inspection will then be uploaded to Covidence, and similar to papers identified by the main search strategy, these records will go through title and abstract screening and full-text screening (as appropriate).

Professional rehabilitation association websites will also be searched to identify any non-indexed grey literature (see details in ‘Study Selection’ for how this will be done). The websites to be searched include: American Physical Therapy Association (APTA), American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), American Occupational Therapy Association (AOTA), Association of Academic Physiatrists (AAP), American Academy of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (AAPM&R), American Therapeutic Recreation Association (ATRA), American Congress of Rehabilitation Medicine (ACRM) and National Rehabilitation Information Centre (NARIC).

Study selection

Following the searches, all records will be collated and imported into EndNote V.20 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA), and duplicates will be identified and removed. The unique records from the literature searches will be exported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening, and any newly identified duplicate records will be removed. Articles will be screened using Covidence in two phases: title and abstract screening and full-text screening.

For title and abstract screening, the process will be piloted until 80% voting agreement is achieved across all voters (not just within voter pairs); the first pilot round will include approximately 20 records, and subsequent rounds will consist of 10 records each until the threshold is reached. A screening schedule will be implemented based on the total number of search results; the schedule will include a list of articles, due dates and meeting dates to discuss discrepancies. Two independent votes will be required per record, and voting discrepancies will be resolved by a third reviewer. If a record does not include an abstract (ie, title only), it will be passed onto the full-text stage.

For full-text screening, records that pass title and abstract screening will have the corresponding full-texts uploaded into Covidence. All team members will complete an iterative piloting process for full-text screening until a 90% voting agreement is attained. A voting and meeting schedule will be implemented. Two team members will vote on each record independently, and discrepancies will be resolved through consensus with a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion will be recorded and reported in the final scoping review.

One team member will independently prescreen the reference lists of (1) records included after full-text screening and (2) review articles (systematic, scoping, meta-analysis and narrative) related to our topic. Reference snowballing will not be conducted. Records will be excluded if published prior to 2013 or if the title is not written in English, only mentions children, lists a country other than or in addition to the USA, does not mention an SDOH or a rehabilitation discipline/disparity or clearly indicates a publication type that is not original research. All records that are selected during reference list prescreening will be uploaded into Covidence and will go through the same screening process as records retrieved from the literature databases, as detailed in the previous paragraph.

One team member conducted a preliminary search of 24 websites representing government agencies, non-profit organisations and professional rehabilitation associations to identify which websites might contain relevant evidence that addresses our research question. It was determined that the websites for professional organisations provided the best yield of relevant information and will form the basis for the grey literature search. One team member will search eight professional websites (APTA, ASHA, AOTA, AAP, AAPM&R, ATRA, ACRM and NARIC). The team member will independently search using the websites’ native search feature for records published since 2013 using various combinations of terms including SDOH, rehabilitation and health disparities. Records that appear relevant will be uploaded into Covidence and will go through full-text screening.

Data charting

A data extraction form will be created by the data analyst (ie, first author) in Qualtrics XM (Qualtrics, Provo, Utah, USA); a draft of this form is provided in online supplemental appendix B. Repeated pilots of the form will be conducted on up to 5 articles per round until extractors and senior team members have a shared understanding of elements to extract (80% agreement). Full-text articles for the pilot will be selected at random by the data analyst. The data extraction form will be modified with each pilot round based on extraction discrepancies and feedback from team members. After piloting is complete, a weekly extraction schedule will be developed based on the total number of included articles; this schedule will include a list of assigned articles, due dates and meeting dates to discuss discrepancies. Three team members will independently extract information from each record in accordance with the schedule; discrepancies will be reviewed and resolved in the presence of the data analyst during weekly meetings by referring to the original text and reaching consensus through discussion.

The following data will be extracted: study objectives, SDOH—rehabilitation discipline relationships (eg, insurance status—physical therapy), data source information (eg, Burn Model Systems), geographical location (eg, Nebraska), rehabilitation setting (eg, inpatient care), sample race/ethnicity, sample sex/gender, group label (condition or description), sample size (n), sample age in years (mean, SD, median, IQR). These extracted data are subject to change based on included sources and the ongoing process of screening; modifications will be detailed in the final scoping review. Rules regarding data extraction will be documented directly in the data extraction form. There are no plans to contact the authors of articles to obtain missing or unclear information; instead, this information will be coded accordingly. Supplemental files of all articles will be checked for relevant information associated with our data elements. In cases in which articles refer to previous publications for information, we will not check those referenced publications and will indicate that the information is ‘Not Reported’ in the extraction form. If multiple articles from the same single study are identified, we will follow the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (chapter 5) guidance.29

Data analysis and presentation

After data extraction is complete, a quality assurance check will be conducted for all extracted data. Specifically, each data element will be checked by a team member for accuracy by comparing the extracted data against the original full-text article using our final agreed-on rules (documented in the extraction form). This process will help ensure that our extracted data is accurate and is not affected by coder drift or misunderstanding.30

After the quality assurance check, data will be cleaned and coded by the data analyst in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). Data elements with strictly closed-ended responses will not require any coding (ie, data source, rehabilitation setting) unless they contain write-in categories, such as ‘other: _________’ (ie, geographical location, race/ethnicity, sex/gender), in which case the extracted text will need to be grouped into its own categories or combined with already existing ones. Data elements with numerical data entry (ie, sample size, age) will not require coding either. For open-ended response data elements, formatting changes will be necessary to achieve a consistent appearance and improve readability (ie, study objectives, SDOH—rehabilitation discipline; group labels) and/or will need categorised into appropriate subgroups (ie, SDOH—rehabilitation discipline, geographical location; see table 2 for potential subgroups).

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics

Ethical approval is not required for this scoping review as it will only include data from previously published papers.

Dissemination

Findings from this scoping review will be disseminated through scientific conferences, professional networks, stakeholder meetings, invited workshops and webinars, email listservs of professional associations, academic social media and in a peer-reviewed journal.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not included in the planning of this scoping review (ie, writing this protocol), but stakeholders will be involved throughout the course of this review to enhance the usefulness/application of the findings of our scoping review publication. Specifically, they will assist with interpreting the results, disseminating the findings and identifying funding priorities to include in the final scoping review.
