ABSTRACT
BackgroundSustaining evidence-based HIV prevention interventions remains a challenge, particularly among at-risk youth in Africa. The 4 Youth by Youth (4YBY) sustainment study is a hybrid type 2 cluster randomized controlled trial that evaluates the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of a crowdsourced approach to sustaining a package of evidence-based HIV prevention services. The study will be conducted across 40 community sites, supported by 18 community-based organizations (CBOs) providing HIV prevention services in Nigeria. Given their longstanding engagement with young people, CBOs serve as key partners in assessing long-term sustainability strategies for the 4YBY intervention, which has proven effective in increasing HIV prevention service uptake among youth.MethodsThis protocol describes a cluster randomized controlled trial that will be conducted across 40 sites, all randomized into two arms. Half of the sites (20) will be assigned to receive the standard 4YBY intervention (4YBY-S), while the other 20 sites will receive the standard 4YBY intervention plus an enhanced sustainability strategy (4YBY-S + 4YBY-E). While the 4YBY-S group will continue implementing core intervention activities, the 4YBY-E group will receive additional sustainability-focused support. The Enhanced Sustainability Strategy in the 4YBY-E arm consists of four key components. First, “People” involves identifying and training sustainability teams within CBOs to champion intervention longevity. Second, “Learning”, where bi-weekly collaborative sessions will be established to foster knowledge-sharing and problem-solving among stakeholders. Third, “Adaptation Monitoring” will focus on continuously tracking and modifying intervention strategies to better align with local needs. Finally, “Nurturing Coaches” includes dedicated coaches who will provide technical assistance, conduct audits, and offer feedback to strengthen sustainability efforts. Evaluation will be guided by Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR), the PEN-3 Cultural Model, Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes Framework, and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). The study hypothesizes that sites receiving the enhanced sustainability strategy (4YBY-S + 4YBY-E) will exhibit greater sustainability of core intervention components, defined as the continued delivery, adaptation, and integration of the intervention within community-based organizations—compared to sites receiving only the standard 4YBY intervention (4YBY-S). Additionally, the 4YBY-S + 4YBY-E arm is expected to achieve a higher uptake of HIV prevention services at 24 months, reflecting both the intervention’s effectiveness and its long-term viability within community settings.DiscussionThis protocol represents one of the first cluster randomized controlled trials evaluating intervention sustainment strategies for a youth-led, evidence-based HIV prevention program in Africa. Findings will advance implementation science by establishing a threshold for sustainable strategies and identifying the key factors that support the long-term integration and continuation of HIV prevention services for at-risk youth. The results critically impact scaling up community-led interventions and shaping policy frameworks to strengthen the global HIV response. Introduction

Sustaining evidence-based HIV prevention interventions remains a significant global challenge [1]. While many interventions along the HIV prevention continuum demonstrate initial effectiveness, their long-term viability is often uncertain. This issue is particularly pronounced among adolescents and young adults (AYA) aged 14–24 in African countries like Nigeria, where HIV risk is high for certain subgroups, yet prevention services remain underutilized [2]. Globally, only 23% of evidence-based interventions are sustained two years post-implementation, underscoring a critical gap in long-term intervention effectiveness [3–5]. In this study, sustainability refers to the continued delivery, adaptation, and integration of an intervention within existing systems for long-term impact. It is measured across three dimensions: sustained activities (ongoing implementation of core components), sustained benefits (continued effectiveness in improving health outcomes), and sustained capacity (the ability of community-based organizations to independently support and scale the intervention). Failure to prioritize sustainment can lead to diminished health benefits, discontinued services, wasted resources, and erosion of stakeholder trust, ultimately undermining efforts to control the HIV epidemic [5–8].

One way to address these sustainment challenges is through participatory approaches, which foster inclusive, iterative, and collaborative engagement to improve the sustainability of HIV prevention efforts [9]. Crowdsourcing, a bottom-up approach that leverages the collective wisdom of communities—such as AYA—to generate innovative solutions, has emerged as a promising strategy for sustaining public health interventions [10–12].

Our group has iteratively used crowdsourcing to co-develop and implement a multicomponent intervention bundle to increase the uptake of HIV self-testing (HIVST) and other prevention services among AYAs [12–14]. We conducted a pragmatic stepped-wedge cluster randomized controlled trial to assess whether a youth-led, crowdsourced intervention, youth by youth (4YBY), improves the uptake of HIVST and other HIV prevention services, including sexually transmitted infections (STI) testing, treatment linkage, and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) uptake [13]. Since its launch in 2018, 5,062 Nigerian AYAs through crowdsourcing open calls, designathons, and participatory learning communities to co-develop and refine intervention strategies [12].

While this approach effectively increased HIV testing and STI screening, its long-term sustainment within community-based organizations remains a critical challenge, necessitating this trial [13].

In the stepped-wedge cluster randomized controlled trial, we demonstrated that a crowdsourced HIV prevention intervention successfully generated implementation strategies that dramatically enhanced HIVST uptake [15]. Study findings demonstrated an uptake of HIVST by 89%, with increased testing rates maintained up to 2 years of follow-up. However, this effective implementation package may not be sustained without dedicated sustainment approaches. As a result, a large-scale study is needed to determine the minimum viable strategies needed to sustain this intervention over time.

This study aims to identify the essential strategies required to sustain 4YBY within community-based organizations that deliver HIV prevention services to at-risk youth in Nigeria. Drawing from similar studies, we define the minimum sustainment threshold as the added value of a more intensive sustainability strategy and the optimal duration needed to ensure long-term program viability [16, 17]. Our study is intended to support community-based organizations in implementing and sustaining the 4YBY intervention to increase the uptake of HIV prevention services among youth while optimizing the allocation of resources to achieve sustainability. The need to focus on sustainability comes from expert consensus research, which highlighted the need to know how well or under what conditions health interventions such as 4YBY are sustained and their gains maintained once they are put into practice [18–20].

Methods

This protocol provides an overview of the intervention, our associated frameworks, study settings, study design, and planned analysis for this study.

Overview and objectives

This study collaborates with the Washington University School of Medicine, the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Georgia State University, George Washington University, Monash University, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, and the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (NIMR).

Three specific aims drive the study protocol: (1) to use participatory approaches (crowdsourcing, designathons and participatory learning communities (PLCs)) to develop strategies to sustain 4YBY in participating community sites served by recruited community-based organizations; (2) to test the effects of the standard 4YBY implementation (4YBY-S, control arm) versus standard implementation plus an enhanced sustainability strategy (4YBY-E, intervention arm) on site-level adoption and sustainability of the 4YBY intervention across 24 months in participating community sites (n = 40) and community-based organizations (n = 18); and (3) to characterize and estimate the impact and cost-effectiveness of sustaining 4YBY over time. We hypothesize that sustainment will be greater in communities and CBOs using 4YBY-E compared to 4YBY-S.

Conceptual frameworks

The study protocol is guided by youth participatory action research (YPAR), the PEN-3 cultural model, Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes Framework, and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) (See Fig. 1). YPAR positions young people as co-creators of knowledge and supports their active involvement in identifying and addressing social problems such as HIV [21]. The PEN-3 cultural model will be used to examine positive, existential, and negative factors influencing the sustainability of 4YBY within communities and to distinguish high- and low-sustaining sites [22, 23]. Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes Framework will guide the assessment of implementation outcomes, including acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, fidelity, penetration, cost, and maintenance [24]. CFIR will guide the evaluation of multilevel barriers and facilitators of intervention sustainability across intervention characteristics, inner and outer settings, individual characteristics, and implementation processes [25].

Study setting and sociodemographic profile of participants

Nigeria’s population is estimated at over 200 million, of which 49.3% are female. The country has 36 states mapped into six geopolitical zones with a Federal Capital territory [26]. There are also 374 distinct ethnic groups and over 500 indigenous languages throughout its 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory [26]. In Nigeria, AYAs face a significant HIV prevention gap, with only 1 in 5 being tested for HIV despite being at high risk [12, 13, 27]. Barriers exist at multiple levels, including individual (fear and low perceived risk), social (insufficient social support), and structural (poor access to testing) [13]. Our partnership with the Nigerian Institute for Medical Research is critical for enhancing the sustainability of the 4YBY intervention at participating community-based organizations. As the key research hub within the Nigerian Ministry of Health, NIMR is well-positioned to design and deliver the intervention across the 40 recruited local government areas within the six geopolitical zones of Nigeria (See Fig. 2).

Rationale for community-based organizations

We will recruit 18 community-based organizations (CBOs) across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones, with each CBO supporting multiple community sites. The 40 community sites will be randomized into two arms: 20 sites receiving the standard 4YBY intervention (4YBY-S) and 20 sites receiving 4YBY-S plus the enhanced sustainability strategy (4YBY-S + 4YBY-E). Eligible CBOs must be non-profit organizations providing HIV prevention or other youth-related health services, willing to link at-risk youth to STI testing services, have at least 50% of their clientele aged 14–24 years, recruit and follow 35 at-risk youth for 24 months, and agree to study procedures. We will also recruit CBO representatives, supervisors, and at least two CHWs per site, yielding an estimated sample of 18 CBO representatives, 16 supervisors, and 80 CHWs.

Rationale for community sites

Community sites will be selected in collaboration with recruited CBOs and NIMR to ensure diverse and representative settings. Where feasible, sites will be stratified by size and community type, urban or rural, to enhance comparability across study arms. Sustainability outcomes will be assessed at both the community level (n = 40) and CBO level (n = 18). Eligible sites must have access to a participating CBO, availability of HIV prevention, STI testing, or youth-friendly health services, no existing 4YBY services, and willingness to adhere to study procedures.

Rationale for at-risk youth participants

This study will recruit 1,400 at-risk adolescents and young adults (AYAs) from 40 community sites served by 18 CBOs. Participants will be followed for 24 months to assess the sustainability and effectiveness of the 4YBY intervention, with eligibility criteria detailed in Table 1. To support implementation, 80 CHWs and 16 supervisors will be trained on the 4YBY intervention, good clinical practice, and human subject research. Research design

This study is a hybrid type 2 implementation-effectiveness trial utilizing a cluster-randomized design with a parallel group approach. CBOs will facilitate phased enrollment, with all sites initially receiving the standard 4YBY intervention (4YBY-S). Following this, the sites will be randomized into two study arms, with one group continuing with 4YBY-S alone while the other receives 4YBY-S plus an enhanced sustainability strategy (4YBY-E).

The study will recruit at-risk youth from 40 community sites across Nigeria. The 4YBY intervention will be implemented over six months, followed by a two-year study-supported sustainment phase to evaluate the effectiveness of sustainability strategies. In this context, sustainability is defined as the continued delivery, adaptation, and integration of the intervention within existing systems. It will be assessed across three key dimensions: sustained activities, which measure the ongoing implementation of core intervention components; sustained benefits, which evaluate the long-term impact on HIV prevention outcomes; and sustained capacity, which assesses the ability of CBOs to independently integrate and maintain the intervention over time [6, 29] (See Table 2).

Randomization

Cluster randomization will occur in a parallel group approach at the community level, with communities and their corresponding CBOs randomized 1:1 to two clusters, 4YBY-S or 4YBY-E. Randomization will be performed by a study statistician not involved with the NIMR to avoid site-selection bias.

This training aims to equip them with the necessary skills to implement HIVST bundles, facilitate STI screening linkages, and provide peer support to youth participants. Measures

The two main outcomes are: (1) primary data comparing the sustainability of 4YBY in the 4YBY-S versus 4YBY-E arms at the 40 community sites and CBOs; (2) secondary data on health impact for at-risk youth and overall impact in all communities and CBOs at 24 months. The primary aim is to compare the implementation and health outcomes of 4YBY-S versus 4YBY-E. The primary implementation outcome is percent sustainability (i.e., sustained activities, benefits, and capacity over time) of 4YBY at 24 months. The primary health outcome is the uptake of HIV prevention services at 24 months. Evaluation of other secondary outcomes, including sustainability plans, cost outcomes, and health-related data, will involve collecting and analyzing quantitative and qualitative data. High- and low-sustaining sites

Using a triangulation mixed-methods design (QUAN + QUAL), we will examine factors influencing sustainability, guided by the PEN-3 cultural model [22, 45]. CBOs and communities will be classified as high- or low-sustaining based on implementation and effectiveness outcomes. Successful sustainability at 12 months is defined a priori as 75% sustainment and 80% uptake of HIV prevention services. Surveys and interviews at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months will assess implementation climate, readiness for change, CHW and CBO characteristics, community relationships, and youth engagement.

Sample size and power considerations

The primary analysis compares the study outcomes between the intervention and control groups. We used a binary outcome within a cluster-RCT design for sample size calculation, where the unit of randomization is the geographic site (cluster). Thus, there are two important sample estimates: N, the number of participants, and K, the number of sites. Specifically, the N participants will be nested within the K clusters. This nesting will likely create dependency on the data. The degree of this dependency depends on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). It will require a larger sample to maintain the power available if the unit of randomization had been individuals rather than clusters. The required sample size is calculated for the primary outcomes: (1) the percent sustainment of 4YBY (activities, benefits, and capacity) and (2) the HIV prevention services uptake rate. We planned a total of 40 sites (30 participants per site, N = 40 × 30 = 1200) with 20 sites randomly assigned to the intervention arm and another 20 sites assigned to the control arm, 2-sided alpha = 0.05 and ICC = 0.1, we determine whether we have sufficient power (> 80%) to detect the differences in the primary outcomes between the intervention arm and control arm. Based on existing literature, including our own studies in Nigeria [49–51], we expect the HIV prevention services uptake rate in the intervention arm to be 65% and that in the control arm to be 47%, the power to detect the difference is 83%. Similar to existing sustainment studies [52], we expect the percent sustainment of core 4YBY elements to be 75% and that in the control arm to be 53%. Given qualitative data from our ongoing RCT research in Nigeria, the power to detect the difference is 96%. To account for the estimated 15% attrition rate, we increased the sample size to 1400 participants (~ 35 for each site). The calculation was performed using the software PASS 2019 (Power Analysis and Sample Size Software (2019)).

Data analysis

Quantitative data

Primary analysis will examine a hypothesis comparing the superiority of the implementation and health outcomes of 4YBY-S versus 4YBY-E. This analysis will be accomplished with generalized linear mixed models to account for the primary between-person factor (randomization site dummy code as 0 = control and 1 = intervention). The 2-level model is structured as subjects nested within local government areas. Multilevel modeling software (SAS, version 9.4, PROC GLIMMIX with a binomial distribution and a logit link function) will be used to compute full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimates of the model parameters. The model will include intervention status as fixed effects and site as random effects. Other covariates (e.g., age, gender, education, occupation, marital status, number of current partners), will be included in the model as potential confounders. The estimated intervention effects will be reported with 95% CIs and p-values. Descriptive analysis will summarize the characteristics and the study outcomes in the local areas in the intervention arm and the control arm. All analyses will be conducted using SAS version 9.4 [53] with a significant level set at alpha = 0.05.

Qualitative data

Qualitative data analysis will occur iteratively using thematic analysis informed by the approach of Braun and Clarke [54] with six stages for thematic analysis: (1) familiarization of interview data; (2) production of initial deductive and inductive codes by multiple coders; (3) review generated codes for consensus among coders; (4) search for themes and explore relationship between codes; (5) revise and summarize themes using thematic mapping to explore relationship between themes and discuss with multiple teams; and (6) written report. We will compare evidence to determine the key factors affecting intervention uptake for high, mid, or low sites. We will then develop descriptive cross-site summaries based on integrated data analysis. Should the data be divergent, we will assign higher credence to the qualitative data with a richer explanation of participants’ attitudes and behaviors. We will incorporate best practices in data visualization to transform these large bodies of disparate data into timely, digestible, and actionable insights that will be disseminated to key stakeholders.

Methods for data management

Study data will be collected, managed, and securely maintained using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at NIMR. REDCap is a secure, web-based application that supports data capture for multiple purposes, including research and quality improvement. Non-electronic personal information on potential and enrolled youth will be kept in a locked fireproof cabinet at the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research. It will be accessible only to study researchers and staff. The quality and completeness of data entry will be systematically checked by a data manager at regular intervals during the study. All study data will be archived in REDCap as well. All data collection and storage will be undertaken under strict confidentiality.

Methods for handling missing data

Although we will attempt to retain as high a fraction of participants as possible, we acknowledge that some attrition is likely, leading to missing outcome values. The generalized linear mixed models proposed for the primary and secondary analyses incorporate an assumption of data missing at random, meaning that the likelihood of a value being missing depends on observable characteristics (e.g., sex or age). In sensitivity analyses, we will assess the impact of different assumptions about the missing data mechanism and will determine the robustness of trial results to these different assumptions. We will consider using multiple imputations of missing data as an alternative sensitivity analysis.

Ethical considerations

The protocol was registered with Clinical Trials.gov, under registration NCT07072481.

Discussion

Innovative approaches to sustain evidence-based HIV prevention services among at-risk youth in Africa are needed. Yet, many interventions repackage old ideas and services and are not created or delivered with the end users in mind [1]. Our goals are to move from a deficit mindset of what Nigeria is doing poorly to an asset-based mindset that is positive about what Nigerians can do to end the HIV epidemic [55]. The existing assets for HIV prevention services provided by the 4YBY (HIVST at reduced cost, free STI tests, free PrEP, and mobile platforms to verify test results) as well as national recommendations on partnerships with communities and community-based organizations to implement health interventions offer unique opportunities to implement and sustain evidence-based HIV prevention services to end the HIV epidemic in an African setting. Study findings will lay the groundwork for how asset-based implementation science can best be used to sustain evidence-based HIV prevention programs for at-risk Nigerian youth. Additionally, by using participatory implementation science, we will enhance the sustainment of HIV prevention services in Africa [56]. Many implementation research studies in Nigeria and Africa have more broadly focused on educating stakeholders [57] instead of specifically co-designing innovative strategies for implementation and sustainability [58]. From the beginning, our 4YBY program has used bottom-up participatory implementation strategies (crowdsourcing open calls and desiganthons [11]) to engage Nigerians, spur and implement innovations that have the greatest salience to youth, and create an enabling environment to build the capacity of youth, creating the community-level ripple effects that will reduce the burden of HIV in Nigeria. We believe that our continued use of participatory strategies to implement and sustain HIV interventions that resonate with youth preferences will allow us to be among the first to examine how Nigerians themselves, including Nigerian youth, can be prime movers and leaders in developing, implementing, and sustaining evidence-based strategies for HIV prevention and control among the youth population. Finally, we advance the state of the science on sustainment as a priority topic for equitable HIV prevention and control in low-and middle-income countries [59, 60]. No empirical work has systematically examined the minimum necessary to sustain evidence-based HIV prevention “with” youth populations and community-based organizations in African settings as partners or leaders and not solely as beneficiaries [60, 61]. In a comprehensive review of the literature in Africa, we found that few studies are sustained, and most have no plans beyond initial research funding [60]. The potential public health impact and return on investment of implementing evidence-based HIV prevention interventions in Africa will not be fully realized unless the interventions can be sustained in the context in which they are implemented [62]. Thus, we present a rare opportunity to systematically contribute knowledge on the factors that will influence the transition to ownership and sustained use of the 4YBY program in Nigeria. We also gain insight into the relative effects of planning for sustainability with attention to the people, continuous learning, adaptation, and nurturing factors that matter to local areas over time.

Nonetheless, there are limitations worth mentioning. Recruiting and retaining young people in research may be a challenge over time. We will use tailored, low-burden, mobile text and email messages as check-ins/reminders with youth participants, as well as leverage resources from ongoing crowdsourcing activities such as the annual World AIDS Day contest carried out on the 4YBY platform (which includes gifts that appeal to target youth) to facilitate key youth engagement in this phase. The project team has extensive experience engaging with and retaining AYAs in research studies. Additionally, there may be turnover among CHWs, which may act as a barrier to intervention sustainment. We will continue to build maintenance activities into the implementation process, including recruiting and training new CHWs in the event of turnovers and organizing crowd-sourcing events among the CHWs that build their capacity or retool them with skills necessary for sustaining the 4YBY program.

Additionally, some model parameters for the return on investment analysis may be difficult to obtain or not currently measured in Nigeria. We will continue collaborating closely with colleagues at NIMR and the World Health Organization to obtain best-guess estimates for Nigeria. These estimates will allow us to generate Nigeria-specific model parameters that will be useful for scaling up the crowdsourced intervention. If certain model inputs are unavailable for Nigeria, we will substitute them with estimates from other West African countries with health systems and HIV/STI epidemiology like Nigeria’s (e.g., Ghana). We will follow best-practice guidance within health economics to test for the impact of the uncertainty of these model parameters (e.g., using wide confidence intervals in univariate and probabilistic sensitivity analyses).

Conclusion

Sustaining 4YBY will reconfigure essential HIV preventive services in Nigeria, drawing on the creativity and power of Nigerian youth, communities, and community-based organizations traditionally underrepresented in implementation science research. Importantly, we stand to advance the state of the science about factors that influence sustained activities, sustained benefits, and sustained capacity of a youth-driven HIV prevention intervention with a sustainability PLAN targeting at-risk youth in Africa’s most populous country [1]. Our multi-disciplinary team and history of collaboration increase the likelihood of success and scale-up in Nigeria and other African countries.
