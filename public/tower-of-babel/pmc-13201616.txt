Research on vibration prediction method of aircraft lens frame based on CPO-BP
Authors: Xuejian Zheng, Tieneng Guo, Hongyan Chu, Shuangfu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the engineering challenge of installing sensors directly on aircraft lens frames for vibration measurement, this paper proposes a vibration response prediction model (CPO-BP) based on a Crested Porcupine Optimizer Algorithm (CPO)-enhanced Backpropagation (BP) neural network. The model employs a two-step optimization strategy: first, CPO optimizes the number of hidden layer neurons and the learning rate; subsequently, it refines the initial values of the network’s weights and biases, thereby improving both the model’s convergence speed and generalization capability. Experimental results demonstrate that the CPO-BP model achieves a high coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.97976, with a mean absolute error (MAE) as low as 0.02020 and a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.10112. Its predictive performance significantly outperforms COA-BP, DA-BP, PSO-BP, and traditional BP models. The predicted response curves closely match the actual responses across acceleration, velocity, and displacement dimensions. This study provides a highly accurate and reliable solution for vibration condition monitoring at critical locations within optical equipment.Keywords: Crested porcupine optimizer (CPO), BP neural network, Vibration prediction, Parameter optimization, Aircraft lens frameSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

When flying equipment operates at high speeds, it is subjected to various forms of vibrational loads. In particular, during abrupt turns of the aircraft, equipment vibrations can lead to blurring in the images captured by the lens, thereby impairing the pilot’s ability to observe tracked objects. Given the high vibration sensitivity of optical lenses, sensors cannot be deployed directly at the lens mounting position. To accurately obtain vibration information at the lens mounting position, sensors must be placed at appropriate adjacent locations. These locations should ensure that the vibration data measured by the sensors truly reflect the vibrational characteristics at the lens mounting position.

Therefore, developing an effective method for predicting vibration signals is of crucial significance to flight equipment. Liu Jialu et al. established a nonlinear vibration response prediction model using a BP neural network from a time-series perspective, and found that the smaller the prediction step, the higher the degree of agreement between the predicted and measured data1. Zhou Jianhui et al. integrated Kalman filtering technology with deterministic analysis and stochastic considerations, and adopted a minimum variance unbiased estimation strategy to realize the prediction of longitudinal vibration displacement at any position of the propulsion shafting under the condition of unknown propeller excitation force2. Han Qichao et al. focused on the coupling effect between the robot and the spindle system, and proposed a corresponding method for predicting the frequency response function of the blade tip. This method constructs a tool tip frequency response function prediction model that incorporates the stiffness characteristics of the robot, thereby effectively predicting the natural frequency of the tool tip in the robot milling system under a specific machining path3. Based on the frequency response function obtained from experiments, Li Feng calculated its response transfer rate, which was combined with the test data of the basic vibration response to achieve the prediction of vibration response during the rotation of the turntable4.

With the advancement of intelligent algorithms, some scholars have applied machine learning methods (e.g., neural networks) to construct prediction models in fields such as cutting, bearings, lenses, and robotic arms, aiming to achieve high-performance prediction of machining performance indicators, including cutting force, thermal error, and machining quality. Gao Xing et al. proposed an artillery muzzle vibration prediction model based on a deep belief network, which exhibits favorable prediction accuracy but demands more computational time and resources5. Wang Mingyue et al. combined genetic algorithms with neural networks to predict crack location and depth using natural frequencies, enabling accurate identification of structural damage6. Yang Shuo et al. investigated the influence of neural network learning rates on the prediction results of structural crack length and location, demonstrating the feasibility of neural networks in crack parameter identification7. Jiang Shuang et al. improved the BP neural network using the PSO algorithm and established a prediction model (PSO-BP) to predict tool cutting vibration acceleration based on cutting speed, back engagement, and feed rate8. Xue Zenghong et al. adopted the CPO-SVDD model to identify and diagnose three typical faults (pitting, spalling, and fracture) in low-speed heavy-duty bearings, revealing that the identification efficiency of the CPO-SVDD model can reach up to 98%, which is 29.8% higher than that of the traditional SVDD model9.

Traditional methods for vibration transmission or prediction, such as transfer path analysis based on frequency response functions (FRFs)4 or linear regression models, perform well in simple systems where the structural dynamic characteristics are known or exhibit high linearity. However, for structures like aircraft lens frames—characterized by complex couplings, nonlinear connections (e.g., bolted joints)14, and constrained sensor placement—these conventional approaches often struggle to accurately capture their intricate vibration transmission relationships. Particularly when data can only be acquired from a limited number of adjacent measurement points, a key challenge arises: how to extract an effective representation of the target point’s vibration state from the highly correlated and informationally redundant signals at the available sensor locations.

In summary, while intelligent algorithms have shown considerable promise in vibration prediction5,8,16,17 existing studies still exhibit two major limitations. First, the majority of “optimization algorithm–neural network” models only conduct single-phase parameter optimization11–13, lacking a systematic and sequential optimization process for both network architecture and initial parameters. Second, research on achieving synchronous, high-precision vibration prediction at multiple points and in multiple directions for complex structures, particularly for aircraft optical lenses which have constrained sensor placement, remains inadequate. These shortcomings hinder the reliability and applicability of such predictive models in real industrial settings15.

To address these gaps, this paper proposes a two-stage optimization framework using the Crested Porcupine Optimizer (CPO) to enhance a BP neural network. Unlike existing single-stage methods (e.g., PSO-BP, GA-BP), our approach first optimizes the network’s structural parameters (number of hidden neurons and learning rate) and then refines the initial weights and biases. This sequential optimization ensures a more balanced trade-off between global exploration and local exploitation, leading to improved generalization. Furthermore, we extend the model to multi-point synchronous prediction on both end-face and radial surfaces of an aircraft lens frame, a scenario rarely addressed in previous work.

Among various heuristic algorithms, this paper adopts the Crown Porcupine Optimization Algorithm (CPO)10 to optimize the BP neural network, mainly due to the following strengths. CPO simulates the four escalating defense behaviors of the crown porcupine—visual intimidation, vocal intimidation, scent attack, and physical attack—thereby establishing a dynamic equilibrium optimization mechanism that shifts from broad global exploration to refined local exploitation. Unlike the particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm, which is susceptible to local optima, and genetic algorithms (GA), which often converge slowly11–13, CPO incorporates a unique cyclic population reduction (CPR) strategy that periodically adjusts the population size. This approach effectively mitigates premature convergence and exhibits stronger robustness and higher convergence accuracy when optimizing parameters for complex nonlinear problems10. As a result, CPO is particularly suitable for addressing vibration prediction challenges characterized by intricate transmission paths and limited training data, such as those encountered in aircraft lens frame structures.

To address these limitations, this paper proposes a CPO-BP prediction model for aircraft lens frames. The model utilizes the Crested Porcupine Optimizer (CPO)10 to conduct a two-stage optimization of the Backpropagation neural network, enhancing its performance at both the macro-architectural and micro-parametric levels. This study applies the CPO algorithm to the vibration transmission prediction of aircraft lens frames, establishing a multi-point synchronous prediction model for both end faces and radial cylindrical surfaces. Systematic experiments and comparisons demonstrate the method’s effectiveness and generalization capability in addressing such complex spatial structures under confined sensor placement conditions15,18.

BP neural network

The BP neural network is one of the most fundamental and widely adopted models in artificial neural networks. As a multi-layer feedforward neural network trained via the backpropagation algorithm, it emulates the information processing mechanisms of biological neural networks through computer technology. With neurons as the fundamental units, it constructs a network architecture comprising an input layer, hidden layers, and an output layer. The inter-layer connections are defined by weights, and each neuron (except those in the input layer) incorporates a bias term.

The core operational mechanism of neural networks involves forward propagation for output computation. The error between the output results and target values is calculated via the backpropagation algorithm, which utilizes the Sigmoid activation function to compute gradients. This process continuously adjusts the weights and biases of each layer until the error falls within an acceptable range or the iteration limit is reached. This distinctive training method enables neural networks to exert significant efficacy in fields such as machine learning, pattern recognition, and image processing. The computational process of a BP neural network can be summarized as follows: (1) Conduct data training by inputting data into the network to enable the network to initially learn data features; (2) Calculate the error of neural network nodes to reflect the deviation between predicted values and actual values; (3) Calculate the mean squared error to quantify the overall error level; (4) Calculate and adjust parameters such as weights and sensitivities of the neural network system; (5) Adjust the weights of the hidden layer based on the sensitivities of the hidden layer. Repeat the above steps until the error is within an acceptable range or the maximum number of iterations is reached.

CPO-BP prediction model

In the prediction process of a BP neural network, parameter configuration is a critical factor influencing prediction accuracy. Among these parameters, the number of hidden layer nodes is closely associated with the network’s predictive performance and inherently correlated with the number of input and output nodes in the system.The number of hidden layer nodes does not display a simple linear positive correlation with the system’s generalization ability: when the number of nodes is excessive, although it may reduce the training error, it can lead to a significant increase in the testing error, markedly prolong the system’s learning duration, and even induce overfitting; conversely, when the number of nodes is insufficient, the system’s fault tolerance is diminished, causing the testing error to approximate the training error and resulting in computational results that are difficult to converge. Thus, both an excessive and an insufficient number of hidden layer nodes will adversely impact the system’s generalization ability.

The learning rate of a neural network is typically confined to the range (0, 1). Variations in its value modulate the network weights, thereby influencing the learning speed of the system. Increasing the learning rate can expedite the training process; however, an excessively high learning rate may induce weight oscillations and even result in training stagnation. Conversely, an excessively low learning rate will significantly prolong the training duration and substantially decelerate the convergence speed.

To effectively enhance the system’s generalization ability and convergence speed, this paper adopts the CPO algorithm to optimize the BP neural network through two sequential steps: the first step involves optimizing the number of hidden layer neurons and the learning rate, while the second step focuses on optimizing the initial values of weights and biases. The CPO algorithm’s parameters are chosen based on problem complexity. For the single-point prediction task (end-face), the search space is relatively small, so a population size of 8 and 20 iterations are sufficient to obtain a good solution. For the multi-point synchronous prediction task (radial surfaces), the network has three outputs, requiring optimization over a higher-dimensional parameter space; therefore, the population size is increased to 20 and iterations to 30 to ensure adequate exploration. The algorithmic workflow of the CPO-BP model is primarily divided into three components: determining the structure of the BP neural network, performing iterative optimization via the CPO algorithm, and conducting training and prediction using the BP neural network. Experimental study on aircraft lens frames

To simulate the high-speed maneuvering conditions of an aircraft, the lens frame structure was excited in a laboratory setting. Both training and testing data were generated by applying broadband pulse excitation using a force hammer (Model 8206-002) at multiple positions on the frame base, exciting the overall structural modes within the 0–1000 Hz range. Data were acquired while the structure was freely suspended to ensure that vibration responses were not influenced by external constraints. The experiment captured multiple sets of time-history data, including steady-state, transient, and residual vibration responses. The specific training dataset used in this study consisted of 750 time steps (corresponding to approximately 0.18 s at a sampling frequency of 4096 Hz), with 80% allocated for network training and the remaining 20% reserved for validation. Although 750 points may appear limited, the vibration signals contain rich frequency content and are sampled at a high rate, providing sufficient temporal patterns for the neural network to learn the underlying dynamics. Cross-validation during training further confirms the model’s stability.

This paper takes the lens frame of a specific aircraft model as the research object, whose structure and sensor layout are illustrated in Fig. 2. The experiment employs four DeltaTron4533-B accelerometers, characterized by a sampling range of 0.2 Hz to 12,800 Hz, an operating voltage of 13 ± 1 V, an operating temperature range of − 55 °C to 128 °C, and a mass of 8.6 g. These accelerometers are utilized to collect response data from both test points and prediction points, respectively. The excitation device adopted is an 8206-002 force hammer, with a sensitivity of 2.167 mV/N and a frequency range of 10 to 10,000 Hz. Vibration data acquisition is conducted using the LMS system to achieve precise on-site data collection, and the acquired experimental data are employed for model training and comparative analysis of prediction results.

Aircraft lens frame.

The experiment selected two sets of data for neural network training and prediction, respectively, with training data accounting for 80% of the total dataset and validation data comprising the remaining 20%. The first dataset uses the responses from sensors 1, 2, and 3 as inputs and the response from sensor 4 as the output for training the CPO-BP neural network. The second dataset employs the responses from sensors 1, 2, and 3 as inputs for model prediction validation, while the response from sensor 4 is used to compare results with the predicted response.

Their amplitude distributions and temporal evolution are representative of the structural dynamics, supplying the neural network with comprehensive learning samples and ensuring the model’s capacity to capture complex vibration features.

Analysis of the Pearson correlation coefficients, as presented in the table, reveals a complex and predominantly weak linear relationship among the sensor signals. Notably, correlations between some input sensors exhibit negative values (e.g., − 1.0842 between Sensor 1 and Sensor 2), while others show positive but modest strengths (e.g., 0.7223 between Sensor 3 and Sensor 4). Similarly, the correlations between input sensors and the target point signals remain limited in magnitude. This pattern of low and variably signed correlations underscores the highly complex nature of vibration transmission paths within the aircraft lens frame. During propagation, vibration energy not only attenuates but may also undergo phase shifts, modal coupling, and nonlinear interactions influenced by joint stiffness, leading to significant alterations in signal waveforms. Consequently, despite spatial proximity, the linear similarity in vibration responses between measurement points is markedly low. This observation strongly justifies the necessity of employing nonlinear models like neural networks, which are capable of learning complex dynamic mappings beyond simple linear relationships. Subsequent optimization results demonstrate that even under these conditions of weak and heterogeneous correlation, the neural network optimized via the two-step CPO process achieves high prediction accuracy, highlighting the robustness and superiority of the proposed method for addressing vibration transmission in complex structures.

To more accurately assess the operational status of the lens, this study expands the prediction beyond end-face vibrations to specifically include the radial cylindrical surface vibrations at the lens mounting position. Conclusion

This study has addressed the challenge of vibration measurement at aircraft lens mounting positions by introducing a prediction model based on a CPO-optimized BP neural network. The principal conclusions are as follows:

The proposed two‑step CPO optimization strategy effectively improves the performance of the BP neural network. Compared with the conventional BP network, the CPO‑BP model reduces the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by 8.31% (from 0.022035 to 0.020204) and the Mean Squared Error (MSE) by 5.24% (from 0.10671 to 0.10112).

The proposed two‑step CPO optimization strategy effectively improves the performance of the BP neural network. Compared with the conventional BP network, the CPO‑BP model reduces the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by 8.31% (from 0.022035 to 0.020204) and the Mean Squared Error (MSE) by 5.24% (from 0.10671 to 0.10112).

The CPO-BP model exhibits comprehensive superiority over other heuristic-optimized models. As shown in Table 3, it achieves the highest R2 value while maintaining the lowest MAE and MSE among all compared models (including PSO-BP, DA-BP, and COA-BP). This leading performance across all key metrics is primarily attributed to the inherent characteristics of the CPO algorithm. Unlike PSO and GA algorithms, which are prone to local optima or slow convergence, CPO establishes a more effective dynamic balance between global exploration and local exploitation by simulating the crested porcupine’s four-phase defense behaviors (visual intimidation, vocal intimidation, odor attack, and physical attack). Furthermore, its Cyclic Population Reduction (CPR) strategy helps suppress premature convergence. Consequently, CPO can identify more robust network architecture parameters (including the number of hidden neurons and learning rate) as well as more suitable initial weights and biases for the BP neural network, thereby significantly enhancing the final network’s prediction accuracy, stability, and generalization capability.
