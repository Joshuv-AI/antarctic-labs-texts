Emergence From the Complex Interactions of Epidemics, Droughts, and Floods: Insights From Ming and Qing Dynasties of China During 1470–1911
Authors: Jianbo Gao, Xin Hou, Yang Cheng, Yu Ye, Yuxiao Wang, Jingsong Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the many health implications of droughts and floods known, and the many adverse secondary and tertiary effects of the Covid‐19 pandemic still lingering, it is important to study the complex interactions of epidemics, droughts, and floods. To gain insights into this issue, we have constructed epidemic, drought, and flood indices for the Ming and Qing dynasties of China in a period of more than 400 years. Using adaptive fractal analysis, we find that the time series of epidemic, drought, and flood indices possess long‐range correlations of different degrees in different regions of China. More importantly, the scaling behavior for the cross correlations between the epidemic and the drought indices in Northern China is characterized by a non‐stationary emergent behavior rather than by a long‐range correlation, while this scaling behavior is close to the boundary of stationarity and non‐stationarity in the Central China. This scaling is up to about 16 years, highlighting that on average, outbreak of large‐scale epidemics may occur not shorter than once every 32 years. Interestingly, the emergent behavior can be characterized as a Zipf's law for the ranked size of the epidemics, mostly in the Northern China, and sometimes also involving some regions in the Central China.

Introduction

Albeit the Covid‐19 pandemic has been declared over more than a year, many of the induced adverse secondary and tertiary effects may remain in the world for years to come. Does the pandemic have anything to do with the ever worsening climate changes, including more frequent and more severe flooding and drought, although considerable efforts have been made by the global community to decelerate the pace of global warming? In other words, is there any interaction between extreme weather and epidemics that can be theoretically characterized?

In recent years, with the intensification of global climate change, floods and droughts have become more frequent and severe worldwide (Gage et al., 2008). Climate factors can affect the geographical distribution and population richness of disease vectors, such as promoting the dynamic transmission and distribution of vector ‐borne diseases. More concretely, droughts and floods can lead to the proliferation of vector‐borne diseases such as mosquitoes, which can increase the spread of diseases like dengue fever, malaria, and Zika virus (Brown et al., 2014; Dhimal et al., 2021; Shokri et al., 2020). Floods can contaminate drinking water sources and lead to the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera, typhoid fever, and dysentery. Floods can even increase the risk of infectious diseases such as tetanus, hepatitis A, and leptospirosis (Ding et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2023).

Some existing research has explored the historical spatial distribution of epidemics and the relationship between the occurrence of epidemics and climate change based on historical data. The frequency of epidemics corresponded well with temperature change and responded to the drop in food supply per capita with a 5‐ to 30‐year time lag. By analyzing the data from 800 to 1900 CE in the Northern Hemisphere during the pre‐industrial era, D. D. Zhang, Lee, Wang, Li, Zhang, et al. (2011) found that famines, wars and epidemics were the dominant triggers of population collapse and a 10% decrease in temperature produces on average 2.2%, 1.2%, and 1.1% increases in famines, wars and epidemics, respectively. By using the indices of 10‐year Epidemic Disaster Index and Epidemic Disaster Frequency, Gong (2003) studied the temporal variation of epidemic disasters and the trend of spatial distribution of epidemics in the past 3,000 years in China, and found that the cold era of the Wei, Jin, and Southern & Northern dynasties from the 3rd to the 6th century was the first epidemic climax, while the Little Ice Age of the Ming and Qing dynasties from the 14th to the 19th century formed the second epidemic climax. The frequency of epidemics in China during 770–1911 CE showed a long‐term increasing trend with colder climate, and the affected areas were expanding from the middle and lower Yellow River and the core of epidemics showed a shifting trend both from north to south and from east to west. However, these studies focused more on the impact of the temperature changes on the outbreak of epidemics rather than the impact of precipitation. Note while the above studies all used data from historical China, the association between number of malaria outpatients and climatic variability in the East African highlands from 1978 to 1998 has also been studied and the findings show that climate variability played an important role in initiating malaria epidemics in the East African highlands (Zhou et al., 2004).

Q. Pei et al. (2015) investigated the impacts of temperature, precipitation, and socio‐economic fluctuations on epidemics in the Ming and Qing Dynasties in China (1368–1901 CE) using a variety of statistical methods including correlation analysis, Granger causality analysis, ARX and Poisson‐ARX modeling. They found that the depressed economic well‐being was the direct trigger of epidemics at the national and long‐term scale in historical China, while climate change was often directly responsible for the change in rice price (temperature had a larger effect than precipitation in this respect), which in turn would trigger epidemics. Chen et al. (2023) used long‐term epidemiological data from historical books to analyze the relationship between epidemics and solar activity. Xu et al. (2011) used the spatial and temporal human plague records in China from 1850 to 1964 to investigate the association of human plague intensity (plague cases per year) with proxy data of climate condition (specifically an index for dryness/wetness), and found that the responses of plague intensity to dry/wet conditions were different in northern and southern China. In northern China, plague intensity generally increased when wetness increased, while in southern China, plague intensity generally decreased when wetness increased. The results suggest that associations between human plague intensity and precipitation are nonlinear: positive in dry conditions, but negative in wet conditions. Based on the historical documents from 1390 to 1909, X. Gao et al. (2021) reconstructed grades and counties affected by locust plagues to examine their spatiotemporal characteristics, environmental significance and social impact. The results show that climatic extremes in Ming‐Qing China, such as droughts and floods, frequently triggered epidemic outbreaks through cascading ecological and social disruptions. Droughts spurred locust plagues (r = 0.378** with epidemics), causing famine that forced populations to consume contaminated food, enabling diseases like bubonic plague (e.g., the outbreak in Beijing in 1643). Floods, like the one in Yellow River breach in 1878, spread waterborne cholera through polluted waterways. These climate‐driven disasters compounded by poor sanitation and limited medicine, created vicious cycles where natural shocks escalated into demographic catastrophes, particularly in agriculturally dependent pre‐modern societies.

Analysis of complex time series is a central issue in science. Chaos theory and random fractal theory are two of the most insightful theories for complex time series analysis. Chaos theory states that some complex behaviors in nature, laboratories, and society may stem from deterministic systems that appear to be random. Chaotic systems are predictable on short time scales, as errors grow exponentially; however, on long time scales, the behavior of the system may appear indistinguishable from purely random systems (J. Gao et al., 2007). In contrast, random fractal theory offers excellent means of studying interactions across a vast range of temporal and spatial scales through scaling laws in systems that are intrinsically random. The scaling laws usually take the form of power laws, and are particularly convenient to study the distribution and correlation structures in the data. Although chaos may not be rare in many fields, including ecology (Rogers et al., 2022), it is usually difficult to observe in historical data with poor temporal resolutions, since information on exponential growth of errors, which is the defining property of chaos, may not be contained in the reconstructed data at all. Therefore, random fractal theory becomes the major theory that may help one uncover certain salient features in historical data, such as the type of distribution or correlation structure and emergent behavior of the system that has generated the data.

The essence of fractal theory is that part of a system (including geometrical objects or patterns of dynamical variation) is similar to other parts or to the whole of the system. Mathematically, this is characterized by a power‐law. In the discrete‐time case, a power‐law becomes the celebrated Zipf's law, which originally was used to characterize the inverse relationship between the usage frequency of any word in some corpus of natural language and its rank (Zipf, 1949). Zipf's law has also been found in many other phenomena of physical and social sciences, including firm‐size distribution (Axtell, 2001) and city size‐distribution (Gabaix, 1999).

When distributions only are considered, one may not be able to define the concept of fractal dimension. However, in the case of power‐law decaying autocorrelations, one not only has the power‐law, but also has the fractal dimension that is related to the exponent of the power‐law characterizing the decay of the autocorrelation. For example, the celebrated fractional Brownian motion (fBm) has a power‐law decaying autocorrelation function with the exponent of 2H − 2, where H is called the Hurst exponent, and depending on the scales concerned, a dimension of 1, 1/H, or D − H, where D is the dimension of the space the fBm is in (for more details, please refer to Section 6.4 of J. ao et al. (2007)). In general, this type of data is often called 1/f noise. Its dimensionality cannot be reduced by popular methods such as principle component analysis (J. Gao et al., 2003). When 0 < H < 1/2, the process is said to have anti‐persistent correlation. When H = 0.5, the process is said to be completely random without any memory, or only have short‐term memory. When 1/2 < H < 1, the process is said to possess long‐range correlation—the larger the H, the stronger the correlation. Processes with H > 1 are often associated with non‐stationarity. Such processes include significant social processes or natural processes having significant and lasting impact on society, as reflected by the usage of google ngram (J. Gao et al., 2012). In other words, such processes are often associated with emergence. A great number of interesting 1/f processes have been reported in the literature. For a partial list of interesting examples of such processes, we refer to our recent review article (J. Gao & Xu, 2021).

According to statistics, there were 375 droughts, 388 floods, and 138 epidemics occurred in the Ming and Qing dynasties. The number and influence of disasters have greatly increased, and the number of deaths caused by disasters was increasing (Meng, 1999). With so many health implications of droughts and floods known, in this research, we aim to understand the complex interactions between historical droughts and floods and epidemics from Ming and Qing dynasties of China during 1470–1911. To gain insights into the important question of how extreme weather affects the occurrence of epidemics, we derive three indices characterizing flood, drought, and epidemic in the period of 1470–1911 during the Ming and Qing dynasties of China. We will examine interactions between floods, droughts, and epidemics using adaptive fractal analysis (J. Gao, Hu, Buckley et al., 2011; J. Gao, Hu, & Tung, 2011; J. Gao et al., 2010, 2012; Tung et al., 2011).

Data and Methods

Data

In this study, epidemics are defined as the occurrence of infectious disease outbreaks that spread rapidly across human populations within a defined geographic area and time period, causing significant morbidity or mortality, as documented in historical records. The original data of epidemics in this study come from the “General Collection of Chinese Meteorological Records for 3,000 Years” (D. Zhang, 2004), the “History of Famine in China” (Meng, 1999) and the “Chronicle of Human Disasters” (Fan, 1998).

The data of drought and flood disasters come from the “Atlas of Drought and Flood Distribution in China in the Past 500 Years” (The Academy of Meteorological Sciences, 1980). This study adopts the standardized drought/flood classification system established by Shi and Zhang (1996), specifically, drought severity is classified based on precipitation deficits and documented socioecological impacts (e.g., historical records of “severe famine with cannibalism”). Flood severity is categorized by spatial scale (e.g., county‐level vs. regional inundation) and archival damage descriptors (e.g., “countless houses swept away” in local chronicles). This system was developed through rigorous analysis of historical documents (e.g., local gazetteers, official disaster reports), meteorological observations, and paleoclimate proxies. This established framework was applied to ensure methodological consistency with China's historical climatology research standards. The definition of level of droughts and floods is as follows:Level 1, “flood” refers to long‐lasting and intense precipitation, or large‐scale floods, or heavy rain caused by typhoons along the coast.Level 2, “partial flood” refers to the continuous precipitation with less severe disasters in a season, or local flood in a season.Level 3, “normal” refers to the year of normal crop harvest, or the year without any records of drought or flood disasters.Level 4, “partial drought” refers to a drought that is less severe in a season or month.Level 5, “drought” refers to a drought that lasts for several months or across seasons, or a drought on a large scale.

According to the collected data, the study period of this research is from 1470 to 1911. The study area includes 18 provinces (cities), namely Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Jiangsu, Shanghai, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Fujian, Shanxi, Shaanxi, Guizhou, Hubei, Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hunan. These areas are divided into three parts, northern, central, and southern China (Figure 1). The northern region (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Shanxi, and Shaanxi) is characterized by semi‐arid temperate climate and loess plateau ecosystems, making it particularly vulnerable to drought‐induced locust plagues and experiencing increased extreme droughts during the Little Ice Age (LIA) (e.g., 1637–1641). The central region (Jiangsu, Shanghai, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Hubei, and Hunan), comprising the Yangtze River floodplain with dense water networks, faced frequent monsoon‐driven flooding that led to waterborne diseases, with LIA impacts enhancing summer rainfall variability. The southern region (Fujian, Guizhou, Guangdong, and Guangxi) features subtropical climate and mountainous terrain, experiencing tropical cyclone‐related flooding and maintaining unique disease ecology favoring malaria and other tropical diseases.

The map of the study area, where Southern China includes Guangxi, Guangdong, Guizhou, and Fujian Provinces, Central China includes Hunan, Jiangxi, Zhejiang, Hubei, Anhui, and Jiangsu provinces and Shanghai, and Northern China includes Shaanxi, Henan, Shandong, Shanxi, and Hebei provinces, and Beijing and Tianjin.

Methods

The Indices of Epidemics, Floods, and Droughts

Yearly Epidemic Impact Index (IE) has been constructed and defined as the number of counties with epidemics occurring in counties within a certain spatial range for a year. It reflects the scope of influence of epidemics in the study area in the year (Cheng, et al., 2009). China's administrative hierarchy comprises four main levels: provinces, prefecture‐level cities, counties/districts, and townships. As a cornerstone of this system, counties serve as vital governance units, particularly for rural management. They play pivotal roles in managing agriculture, environment and rural development. The series established by this index is a dynamic time series. In a certain period of time, the impact of flooding and drought in a region is associated with the severity of flooding and drought at each station and also related to the area affected by flooding and drought. The Index of flood (IF) and index of drought (ID) are used to reveal the flooding and drought in a certain period of time. The formula is as follows: (1) IE:Ni/N (2) IF:F=1T∑KmN1+KnN2 (3) ID:D=1T∑KmN5+KnN4

In the formula, Ni refers to the number of counties with epidemics in a certain year; N refers to the total number of counties in the region; T refers to the number of stations in the region; N1, N2, N4, N5 refer to the number of observation stations with flood and drought level 1, 2, 4, 5; Km is the coefficient for the severity of floods and droughts, taking 1; Kn is also the coefficient, taking 0.5 (Shi & Zhang, 1996).

For ease of discussing results in Section 2, we will denote the epidemic, droughts, and flood time series by X, Y, and Z. Furthermore, we will use subscript N, C, and S to denote northern, central, and southern regions of China. For example, XN denotes the time series of epidemic index in Northern China.

Adaptive Fractal Analysis (AFA)

In the past three decades, many efforts have been made to estimate the Hurst exponent H, the most important parameter for random fractals. As a result, many excellent methods for estimating H have been proposed. Among them is the celebrated detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) (Peng et al., 1994). It works as follows: To analyze a time series, x(1), x(2), x(3), …, one first determines its mean x, then constructs a random walk process, (4) u(k)=∑i=1kx(i)−x‾

By doing so, one has assumed that the data is like a noise or increment process (J. Gao, et al., 2006). One then partitions the random walk into non‐overlapping segments of length l (therefore, the number of distinct segments is not larger than N/l, where N is the length of the time series). One further determines the local trend in each segment by using the best linear or polynomial fitting. Finally, one obtains the difference between the original “walk” and the local trend. Denote it by u(n). H is then estimated by (5) Fd(l)=〈∑i=1lul(i)2〉1/2∼lH where the angle brackets is a short‐hand notation for averages over all the segments.

Although DFA is very good in many applications, when a signal has a strong nonlinear trend, such as an oscillatory component or a rhythmic activity, which is the case with the droughts and floods indices to be presented below, there may exist large discontinuities in adjacent segments of DFA. These discontinuities can cause big problems. This problem can be readily solved by the adaptive fractal analysis (AFA) (J. Gao, Hu, Buckley, et al., 2011; J. Gao, Hu, & Tung, 2011; J. Gao et al., 2012). The difference with AFA and DFA is that AFA uses a globally not only continuous but also almost everywhere differentiable trend (J. Gao et al., 2010; Tung et al., 2011). Denote it by v(i). The difference between the original random walk process u(i) and v(i) can be used to accurately estimate H. The formula is given by (J. Gao, Hu, Buckley, et al., 2011; J. Gao, Hu, & Tung, 2011) (6) F(m)=1N∑i=1N(u(i)−ν(i))21/2∼mH.

This can be generalized to multifractal analysis, which however, will not be used in the following analysis.

Results

Long‐Rang Correlations in Epidemic, Drought, and Flood Indices

First, we examine the time series of epidemic, drought, and flood indices of the Northern China. They are denoted as XN, YN, and ZN, and plotted in Figure 2(a1), 2(b1), and 2(c1), respectively. We observe that there are four large epidemic outbreaks, occurring around 1588, 1641, 1821, and 1862. We also observe that the drought and flood time series have quite large values. Having a semi‐arid climatology in Northern China, a large drought time series is easy to comprehend. Why is the flood time series also large in Northern China? This is because a large swath of the inhabitable land in Northern China belonged to the loess plateau ecosystems and the drainage system in Northern China was largely poor since overall the climate was semi‐arid. As a result, almost any large precipitation would cause floods.

The drought and flood time series shown in Figure 3(b1) and 3(c1) are smaller than those shown in Figure 2(b1), and 2(c1). Having a subtropical climate in Southern China, a smaller drought time series is easy to understand. The small flood time series can be attributed to the effective drainage systems partly naturally formed and partly cultivated by man in the region and the rareness of cyclones in Southern China.

Finally, we examine the time series of epidemic, drought, and flood indices of Central China. They are denoted as XC, YC, and ZC, and plotted in Figure 4(a1), 4(b1), and 4(c1), respectively. It appears that in response to the frequent monsoon‐driven flooding, people in the Yangtze River floodplain of Central China had effectively utilized the dense water networks there to effectively reduce the impact of flooding.

We have analyzed these epidemic, drought, and flood time series using AFA. The results for the epidemic, drought, and flood time series in Northern China are shown in Figure 2(a2, b2, c2). Recalling that the slope of log2F(m) versus log2m curve, when it is straight, is the Hurst parameter. We thus find that for the epidemic time series in Northern China, log2F(m) versus log2m is a single scaling, with H = 0.62. The drought time series however possesses double scaling, with the Hurst parameter being 0.89 when the time scale m is smaller than 24 years and 0.44 when the time scale m is larger than 24 years. The behavior of the flood time series is similar to that of the drought time series, except that the two Hurst parameters are 0.78 and 0.59, rather than 0.89 and 0.44.

The results for the AFA of the epidemic, drought, and flood time series of Southern China are shown in Figure 3(a2, b2, c2). We observe that the scaling is simpler, since all three curves basically exhibit single scaling, with the Hurst parameter for the epidemic, drought, and flood time series being 0.72, 0.59, and 0.57, respectively. The only complexity here is that the scaling for the flood time series in Southern China is finite—it is valid up to a time scale m of 26 years.

The results for the AFA of the epidemic, drought, and flood time series of Central China are shown in Figure 4(a2, b2, c2). Interestingly, the epidemic time series in the Central China possesses double scaling, with two Hurst parameters being 0.83 and 0.29, corresponding to whether the time scale m is smaller or larger than 24.5 years. The drought and flood time series in Central China are largely characterized by a single scaling, with the additional complexity exhibited by the flood time series in that the scaling is valid up to a time scale m of 26.5 years.

Special attention should be paid to the time scales dictating double scaling and finite scaling identified as types 2 and 3 above. For oscillatory signals with irregular amplitude, such as sunspot numbers, it has been found that scaling for fractal long‐range correlation usually breaks at half of the period (Hu et al., 2009). For brain wave EEG signals, the fractal scaling is intrinsically finite due to rhythmic firing of dendritic neurons (J. Gao, Hu, Buckley, et al., 2011; J. Gao, Hu, & Tung, 2011). Therefore, the actual time scale associated with the cases of double fractal scaling and finite fractal scaling is twice the time scales listed in Table 1. These time scales reflect the general pattern change in the time series studied here: the patterns of the time series are different for time scales longer or shorter than 2 m identified here, where m is the time scale where the fractal scaling changes.

We observe that the scaling exponent for the cross‐long‐range correlation between drought and flood index time series decreases from the Northern, to the Central, and finally to Southern China. In fact, the scaling exponent for Southern China is close to 0.5, and therefore, the cross‐long‐range correlation is quite weak. It is quite strong in Northern China, since the scaling exponent is as high as 0.91. The situation is quite similar with the cross‐long‐range correlation between epidemic and drought, in the sense that the scaling exponent decreases from the Northern, to the Central, and finally to Southern China. However, the values of the scaling exponent are quite different from those for the cross correlation between drought and flood, since now the exponent is larger than 1 for Northern China, 0.97 (which is close to 1) for Central China, and 0.75, and thus still much bigger than 0.5, for Southern China.

It is important to point out why the cross‐long‐range correlations between epidemics and drought as well as between drought and flood decrease from the Northern, to the Central, and finally to Southern China. This is largely due to the different climatology and environment in the three regions. As we have mentioned, Northern China is semi‐arid, basically comprising the ancient Yellow River basin (including areas such as Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Shanxi, and Shaanxi), where floods frequently occurred mainly due to the combined effects of natural and human factors. The Yellow River carried a large amount of sediment, forming a “suspended river” above the ground, making it prone to breaches and floods. Northern China often experienced concentrated summer rainfall, coupled with severe soil erosion in the Loess Plateau, which exacerbated flood disasters along the Yellow River basin. Additionally, excessive cultivation had led to deforestation and inadequate water management, causing the Yellow River to change its course multiple times, resulting in widespread flooding. Moreover, when some parts of Northern China were experiencing flooding, droughts may be going on in other parts of the region, since Northern China was huge. The grave problem facing Northern China did not exist in Southern China, and was of a lesser degree in Central China. As a result, the cross‐long‐range correlations between epidemics and drought as well as between drought and flood decrease from the Northern, to the Central, and finally to Southern China.

The fact that the scaling exponent for the cross‐long‐range correlation between the epidemic and the drought index time series is larger than 1 is of particular interest and significance. It highlights that from time to time, the interaction between epidemics and droughts may make epidemics to spread into a wide range of areas. In other words, emergence of large scale epidemics may appear from time to time. Indeed, the drought index in Northern China shown in Figure 2(a1) is somewhat “synchronized” with the epidemic index in the region, in the sense that the four epidemic outbreaks correspond to the four local maxima of the drought index shown in Figure 2(b1), and the four large epidemic outbreaks in Ming and Qing dynasties of China, which occurred around 1588, 1641, 1821, and 1862, respectively, all involved many counties in Northern China. Two examples are shown by the maps in Figures 8 and 9, for the outbreaks which occurred around 1641 and 1821. In the maps, different color shows the locations of counties with epidemics in a certain year. The different color grids show the locations of counties with epidemics in various years. Note that two large epidemic outbreaks which occurred around 1588 and 1641 also involved many counties in Central China. This can be partly inferred from the fact that the scaling exponent for the cross‐long‐range correlation between the epidemic and the drought index time series is close to 1, which is at the very boundary between emergence and no emergence. Although we have emphasized that the scaling between the epidemic and flood index time series is not very good, it is still interesting to note that the scaling exponents are all quite large. This means that from time to time, their interaction could give rise to emergence as well. This can be readily appreciated by noticing the many large peaks in Figure 3(a1) after around 1820.

Zipf's Law and Large Epidemic Outbreaks

Now that we have understood that the four large epidemic outbreaks in Ming and Qing dynasties of China, which occurred around 1588, 1641, 1821, and 1862, respectively, can be viewed as the consequence of emergent behaviors arising from the complex interactions between epidemics and drought, it is interesting to examine if these outbreaks have quantifiable characteristics. For this purpose, it is better to work with the actual size (in terms of number of counties affected) instead of the normalized epidemic index data shown in Figure 2(a1), Figure 3(a1), and Figure 4(a1). For this purpose, it is sufficient to only consider half of the “cycle” of the epidemic. The data for the four epidemics are shown in Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, respectively. To find interesting patterns in the data, we have ranked the number of incident counties of plagues in each of Tables 3, 4, 5, 6 in descending order. Therefore, the number “85” in Table 3 is ranked as the 1st, and the number “43” in Table 3 is ranked as the 2nd. We have then plotted these ranked numbers of incident counties of plagues with their ranks in double‐logarithmic scale. The results are shown in Figures 10a–10d. We observe that the plots can be well described by the Zipf‐like law: (8) rankednumberofincidentcountiesofplague∼k−α where k is the rank and α is the scaling exponent. When α = 1, Equation 8 is the celebrated Lipf's law, originally used to describe the inverse relationship between the usage frequency of any word in some corpus of natural language with its rank (Zipf, 1949). Zipf's law later has been found in many phenomena of physical and social sciences, including firm‐size distribution (Axtell, 2001) and city size‐distribution (Gabaix, 1999). Its generalization, Zipf‐Mandelbrot law, recently has been to describe the global hierarchy and revealed comparative wealth (RCW) in the world well (J. Gao et al., 2024).

Discussion

During the 15th to the 20th century, the climate in eastern China was in a relatively cold period, which was also the longest and lowest of the four low temperature periods in the past 5,000 years. The air temperature reached the lowest point in the second half of the 17th century (Shi & Zhang, 1996). With the change of cold and warm climate, the humidity also changed. Droughts in the Yellow River basin were particularly frequent from the second half of the 15th century to the end of the 17th century, while floods happened more frequently in the south, forming a wet period in the south from the 18th century to the end of the 19th century. It entered the dry period in the 20th century. Natural disasters formed two larger peak periods from 1650 to 1670 (peak year in 1660) and 1730 to 1750 (peak year in 1740), and it increased obviously after 1820 and maintained a sharp upward trend (Ge & Wang, 1995). By analyzing 14 fine‐grained variables from 1500 to 1800 CE in Europe, D. D. Zhang, Lee, Wang, Li, Pei, et al. (2011) found that cooling from 1560 to 1660 CE caused agro‐ecological, socioeconomic, and demographic catastrophes, leading to the General Crisis of the Seventeenth Century.

One of the contributions of this study is to analyze the long‐range correlations between epidemic, drought and flood and find the regional differences in China. Some studies explored the relationship between the outbreak of epidemic and temperature (Lee, et al., 2017; Y. Pei et al., 2022). Xu et al. (2011) analyzed the spatial and temporal human plague records in China from 1850 to 1964. The findings show that the associations between human plague intensity and precipitation are nonlinear. The intensity of plague increased when precipitation increases in Northern China. In southern China, plague intensity generally decreased when precipitation increased. The findings partly support the results in this study. The study conducted by Li and Yuan (2003) also showed the correlation between the outbreak of epidemics and droughts.

This is also one of the first studies to show the outbreak cycle of epidemic is no shorter than once every 32 years based on long‐term historical data, which provides policy implications for public health. The time scale of 32 years found here compares favorably with the average time interval of almost 30 years between two successive suspected outbreaks of epidemic typhus documented by Socolovschi and Raoult (2009), if the case occurred in Russia in 1989 with a small casualty of 29 cases is excluded. For convenience of appreciating this comparison, the data from Socolovschi and Raoult (2009) are listed in Table 7 below. Understandably, the time scale of about 30 years is larger than the average time interval between two “successive” outbreaks of large epidemics that occurred on Earth during 1601–1945, as documented by Marani et al. (2021), since the spatial domain studied by Marani et al. (2021) is much larger than that studied here, and many of the epidemics studied by Marani et al. (2021) could more or less be considered concurrent epidemics occurring in different parts of the earth.

Some limitations have to be addressed in this study. First, the data are collected from historical records and incompletions are inevitable. The attributes of historical epidemic data are not infinite, such as the type of epidemics and the death number of each epidemics, which does not support more in depth analysis. Second, this study only analyzed the correlation of epidemics and drought or floods, other influencing factors such as economic well‐being, population pressure, wars are not considered. Despite of the two points, this study provides important insights to understand the correlation between the outbreak of historical epidemics and climate change, as well as the outbreak cycle of epidemics. More importantly, Adaptive fractal analysis (AFA) is proved to be an effective tool for understanding the complex correlations.

Conclusions

This study constructed epidemic, drought, and flood indices for the Ming and Qing dynasties of China for analyzing their long‐range correlations in Northern, Southern and Central China by using adaptive fractal analysis. There were four large‐scale outbreaks during the Ming and Qing dynasties, which occurred around 1588, 1641, 1821, and 1862 respectively. The drought index shows a certain synchronization with the epidemic index, however, the flood index does not show good correlation with the epidemic index. The long‐term correlation of epidemic index time series shows regional differences, which is the largest in Central China and the smallest in Northern China, while the long‐term correlation of drought and flood index time series gradually weakens from Northern China to Central China and then to Southern China.

The scaling exponent of the cross‐long‐term correlation between the epidemic and drought index time series indicates that the interaction between historical epidemics and drought may lead to a pandemic. It also finds that the outbreak of large‐scale epidemics may occur not shorter than once per 32 years. The size sorting regions of the epidemics follows Zipf's law are usually in Northern China, and also involving some regions in Central China.
