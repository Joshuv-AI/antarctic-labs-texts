Application of Ultrasonic Osteotome in Segmental Resection and Removal of Cage During Revision Surgery for Intervertebral Disc Space Infection After Lumbar Fusion
Authors: Junhui Liu, Yunyun Ouyang, Xuyang Zhang, Qibin Zhang, Chudi Fu, Fengdong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCage removal is essential in revision surgery for intervertebral disc space infection (IVDS). The aim of this study is to investigate the effectiveness and safety of using ultrasonic osteotome in segmental resection and removal of cage during revision surgery for IVDS after lumbar fusion.MethodWe retrospectively reviewed 32 patients who underwent revision surgery for IVDS infection after lumbar fusion in our hospital from June 2018 to November 2024. The operation time, blood loss, nerve damage, success rate of removal, infection control, and fusion rate were compared between the traditional whole removal method (n = 15) and the ultrasonic osteotome‐based segmental excision method (n = 17).ResultsThe average incision length and hospital stay were not significantly different between the two groups (p > 0.05). Nevertheless, the time taken for removing the cage, the success rate of cage removal, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative drainage were significantly improved in the ultrasonic osteotome group compared with the traditional surgery group (p < 0.05). The assessment of postoperative recovery at 7 days and 1, 3, 12 months postoperation demonstrated significant improvements in VAS and ODI scores compared with the preoperative values in both groups. However, within‐group and between‐group analyses did not reveal significant differences at these intervals, even up to the 12‐month evaluation (p > 0.05). Contrast‐enhanced lumbar MRI scans conducted for the 32 patients at 3 months postoperation confirmed successful infection control among all patients. Both groups demonstrated no incidence of dural tears, cerebrospinal fluid leakages, or wound infection. In contrast, incisional fat liquefaction occurred in one and two patients in the ultrasonic osteotome and traditional surgery group, respectively, with all patients exhibiting satisfactory resolution following appropriate wound care. Moreover, a slight deterioration in the extensor muscle strength of the great toe was observed in two patients and an exacerbation of foot dorsum numbness was detected in one patient in the traditional surgery group, with all symptoms fully resolving within the 3‐month follow‐up period. In contrast, the ultrasonic osteotome group showed no such significant neurological function impairments. At 1‐year postoperation, bone fusion was confirmed in both groups via CT reconstructions of the lumbar spine.ConclusionCompared with the traditional whole removal method, the ultrasonic osteotome‐based segmental excision method has the advantages of shorter and controllable operation time, lower blood loss, higher success rate of removal, and lower risk of nerve damage, making it uniquely valuable in the revision surgery for IVDS infection after lumbar fusion.Keywords: cage, postoperative IVDS infection, segmental resection, the ultrasonic osteotome

Introduction

The widespread adoption of posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) has led to an increase in associated complications. Among them, postoperative IVDS infection is one of the most severe complications, with a reported incidence rate of 0.7%–12% [1, 2]. Furthermore, the utilization of internal fixation and fusion devices, as well as the complexity of their anatomical integration, has rendered it exceedingly challenging to manage IVDS infection following lumbar fusion. Such infection can lead to extended hospital stay, increased healthcare cost, higher risks of readmission and need for additional surgeries, and even death in severe cases.

Postoperative IVDS infection can also cause varying degrees of bone erosion and destruction. The initial management strategies for these infections focus on empirical antimicrobial therapy and nonoperative immobilization. However, the treatment strategy shifts to surgical procedures when conservative treatments fail to achieve adequate infection control. The standard surgical method includes the removal of the cage, followed by iliac bone grafting and replacing the internal fixation screws [3]. The cage, being a foreign body, is the primary infection source within the IVDS, making its removal a critical step during revision surgery [4, 5]. Traditional methods for removing the cage primarily employ a whole removal approach via instruments such as pedicle probe, osteotome, and curettes to loosen the cage, followed by cage removal using various grasping instruments, such as curettes and nucleus pulposus forceps. However, cage removal has become a complex and challenging procedure, mainly due to the lack of cage‐specific extraction tools and the limited efficacy of available instruments, which is further compounded by the issues of scar adhesion and elevated risks of neurological damage.

Medical technology advancements have led to the extensive application of ultrasonic osteotome in clinical, owing to its high bone‐cutting efficiency and diminished operative trauma [6, 7, 8, 9]. The ultrasonic osteotome, with its unique selective cutting capability and significant advantages in reducing nerve complications, minimizing bleeding, and enhancing surgical safety, has become an indispensable tool in modern spinal surgery, particularly for high‐risk and complex procedures [10, 11, 12, 13]. In this study, we report on the use of the ultrasonic osteotome in segmental extraction techniques during revision surgery for IVDS infection after lumbar fusion, wherein the ultrasonic osteotome was utilized to fragment and remove the cage in pieces. This technique considerably reduced operation time and blood loss, as well as reduced the risk of nerve damage and achieved a high success rate in cage removal. This study compared the traditional whole removal and ultrasonic osteotome‐based segmental excision methods in terms of operation time, blood loss, nerve damage, success rate of removal, infection control, and bone fusion rate after revision surgery. Here, we aim to determine the effectiveness and safety of the ultrasonic osteotome in the segmental excision and removal of cage during revision surgery for IVDS infection after lumbar fusion.

Materials and Methods

Patient Information

From June 2018 to November 2024, the study included 32 patients who had developed IVDS infection after lumbar fusion. All included patients were referred to our hospital from primary care facilities, comprising 18 men and 14 women (age range: 47–77 years; mean age: 66 years). The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) A diagnosis of IVDS infection after lumbar fusion confirmed via contrast‐enhanced lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT) scans, and biochemical analysis of inflammatory indicators. (2) A history of inadequate response to conventional conservative treatment lasting longer than 1 month. Patients who showed an adequate response to conventional conservative treatment and refused revision surgery were excluded. The 32 patients were subsequently divided into two groups, where the ultrasonic osteotome group (n = 17) underwent ultrasonic osteotome‐based segmental excision and removal of the cage and the traditional surgery group (n = 15) underwent whole cage removal via traditional tools such as pedicle probe, osteotome, curettes, and nucleus pulposus forceps. Both surgical procedures were performed by the same medical team. Comparative analysis of the patients' baseline characteristics, including gender, age, disease duration, and affected segments, revealed no significant differences (p > 0.05; Table 1). All patients were informed about their surgical options. This comparative research was conducted after receiving approval from the Ethics Committee of our hospital (SRRSH2026‐2073).

Postoperative Treatment

Following surgery, patients were prescribed absolute bed rest. Typically at 3 months postoperation, patients underwent comprehensive CT and MRI evaluations and were allowed to walk with the assistance of a supportive brace. Drainage tubes placed during surgery were removed after 1 week. Routine pathogen cultures and next‐generation sequencing (NGS) testing were also conducted. Before identifying the pathogens, empirical anti‐infection treatment (vancomycin [1000 mg ig q12h] + levofloxacin [0.6 g ig qd] + rifampin [0.45 mg po qd]) was initiated. The anti‐infection treatment plan was then adjusted after pathogen identification based on the NGS result. The antibiotic treatment duration was generally 3 months, after which antibiotic administration was continued according to the values of inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C‐reactive protein (CRP) and the complete blood count (CBC) and contrast‐enhanced lumbar MRI results.

Statistical Analysis

Data were input into Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and quantitative results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were transferred to the SPSS 20.0 software (PASW, Statistics, IBM, USA). Continuous data such as age, disease duration, incision length, the time taken for removing the cage, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume and hospital stay were compared using the Student's t‐test. VAS and ODI scores obtained preoperatively, at 1 week, and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after the procedure were compared with one‐way analysis of variance. The differences between different time points were compared with Fisher's least significant difference test. Categorical data including gender, segment, and the success rate of cage removal were analyzed with the chi‐squared test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Postoperative Infection Control

Contrast‐enhanced lumbar MRI scans conducted for the 32 patients at 3 months postoperation confirmed successful infection control among all patients. Subsequently, antibiotic treatments were discontinued after normal CBC and CRP levels in all patients. However, four patients presented with elevated ESR and required an additional month of antibiotic therapy. Eventually, their ESR returned to normal values.

Postoperative Complications and Fusion Status

Both the ultrasonic osteotome and traditional surgery groups demonstrated no incidence of dural tears, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, or wound infection. In contrast, incisional fat liquefaction occurred in one and two patients in the ultrasonic osteotome and traditional surgery group, respectively, with all patients exhibiting satisfactory resolution following appropriate wound care. Moreover, a slight deterioration in the extensor muscle strength of the great toe was observed in two patients, and an exacerbation of foot dorsum numbness was detected in one patient in the traditional surgery group, with all symptoms fully resolving within the 3‐month follow‐up period. Conversely, the ultrasonic osteotome group showed no such significant neurological function impairments. Discussion

Prevention of IVDS Infection After Lumbar Intervertebral Fusion

Postoperative infection following lumbar intervertebral fusion surgery poses significant challenges during the postoperative period, potentially resulting in long‐term sequelae including chronic pain, internal fixation failure, pseudoarthrosis development, and irreversible neurological damage. In the worst‐case scenario, these complications escalate to sepsis, multiorgan failure, and even mortality. Accumulating research has highlighted multiple risk factors associated with postoperative infection in such cases, comprising patient‐specific (e.g., age, obesity, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], and osteoporosis) and surgical (e.g., operation time, blood loss, dural tear, and blood transfusion) risk factors [14, 15]. Consequently, careful attention should be paid to the procedural details of the applied surgical protocols to prevent severe infection outcomes, including extensive irrigation with saline and diluted povidone‐iodine solutions [16, 17, 18]. In our practice, we routinely employ thorough irrigation with more than 2000 mL of both 0.9% NaCl and 0.35% povidone‐iodine solution before wound closure in lumbar fusion surgeries to effectively prevent postoperative infection. Additionally, the strategic use of vancomycin powder in the operative field, particularly in patients with high‐risk factors such as advanced age, diabetes, and compromised immunity and those undergoing extensive surgical procedures, has been found to reduce infection risks [19, 20, 21], thereby markedly lowering the incidence of postoperation infection. It is important to note that postoperative infections did occur among our surgical patients. Based on our clinical experience, we initiated early empirical vancomycin therapy (1000 mg IV q12h) whenever postoperative infection was suspected. This standardized approach enabled effective infection control at an early stage, ultimately preventing the need for surgical revision in all documented cases.

Conservative Treatment Versus Surgical Treatment

Infection following lumbar intervertebral fusion surgery can be classified into early and delayed infection based on the timing of the infection occurrence and into superficial and deep infection according to the infection depth. Superficial infection is often managed effectively with antibiotics and appropriate wound care. However, deep infections, particularly those associated with implanted devices such as internal fixation and cage, present considerable treatment challenges. In such cases, surgical intervention to remove the infected tissue and infection source significantly reduces the bacterial load within the body. Moreover, removing the internal fixation and cage facilitates further thorough debridement, minimizes the risk of residual infection foci, and reduces the risk of infection recurrence and the need for multiple debridement procedures. Hence, surgical treatment is the ideal approach for controlling or eradicating infections, shortening treatment duration, facilitating faster alleviation of patient discomfort and expedited recovery [22]. Lastly, surgical management has notable advantages over conservative treatment in addressing postoperative IVDS infection caused by bacterial pathogens.

Postoperative Infection Control

Prior studies indicated that the most common pathogens in postoperative IVDS infections are Staphylococcus aureus ( S. aureus ) accounting for 45.2% of infections (of which methicillin‐resistant S. aureus [MRSA] constitutes 15.5%) and S. epidermidis that represents 31.4% of the infections [23]. In line with these findings, our study also found that S. aureus (including MRSA) was the most common pathogen causing infections in the IVDS after lumbar fusion surgery, comprising 43.7% of infection cases. Therefore, broad‐spectrum antibiotics targeting S. aureus and MRSA can be empirically selected for patients with IVDS infections following lumbar fusion surgery before the pathogen results are obtained. The antibiotic regimen adopted in this study consisted of vancomycin (1000 mg ig q12h) + levofloxacin (0.6 g ig qd) + rifampin (0.45 mg po qd). Pathogen identification via NGS is a routine procedure used for surgical patients to guide the formulation of targeted anti‐infection strategies. Additionally, the antibiotic treatment period is generally 3 months, after which the decision to continue antibiotic administration is based on the values of inflammatory markers such as ESR, CRP, CBC, and contrast‐enhanced lumbar spine MRI findings. In this study, the contrast‐enhanced lumbar spine MRI results obtained 3 months postoperatively showed controlled infections in all 32 patients, along with normal CBC and CRP levels in all patients. Four patients exhibited elevated ESR, which normalized after extending the antibiotic treatment for an additional month. At 1‐year postoperation, all patients achieved bone fusion, as evidenced by their lumbar spine CT scans with sagittal and coronal reconstructions.

Of the 32 patients who underwent follow‐up contrast‐enhanced MRI at 3 months, imaging confirmed successful infection control in all cases. We attribute this favorable outcome to four key factors: (1) strict adherence to absolute bed rest for 3 months, consistent with the fundamental principle of immobilization in the management of pyogenic spondylitis; (2) rigid internal fixation during revision surgery, including supplemental screw placement at adjacent vertebral levels in cases of initial screw loosening, to enhance construct stability; (3) targeted antibiotic therapy guided by pathogen identification through NGS; and (4) administration of antibiotics at full therapeutic doses for an adequate duration. These elements collectively formed the cornerstone of our successful infection control protocol.

Choice of Surgical Approach and the Advantages of Ultrasonic Osteotome

The anterior approach has been noted as advantageous during the selection process for the surgical approach to address IVDS infection after lumbar fusion [24]. This approach offers clear surgical access directly to the IVDS and evades significant tissue adhesion, thereby facilitating thorough removal of infected lesions. Additionally, the anterior approach does not involve traversing the spinal canal, thus avoiding disturbing the spinal canal, eliminating the need to deal with dural and nerve root scars, and substantially reducing the risk of intraoperative nerve damage. Nonetheless, this approach requires surgeons experienced in anterior spinal surgery, and cage removal can be challenging due to its possible oblique, posterior, or right‐lateral placement. Conversely, the posterior approach is indispensable in cases where the spinal canal has substantial abscess formation, requiring decompression and abscess evacuation. Furthermore, given that cages are typically placed via the posterior approach, the posterior approach becomes the preferred choice for most surgeons because it allows them to manage the situation using the same approach and technique. However, the posterior approach involves working within the spinal canal with limited surgical visualization and carries the potential for scar formation and adhesions around the dura mater and nerve roots, increasing the likelihood of triggering neurological complications and making it challenging to remove inflammatory necrotic tissue. Despite these challenges, the posterior approach remains the preferred strategy for adequate abscess clearance in cases of extensive spinal canal abscesses.

The conventional strategy for cage extraction predominantly relies on a complete removal technique using instruments such as a pedicle probe, osteotome, and curettes to loosen the cage, followed by extraction using curettes, nucleus pulposus forceps, and other grasping instruments. However, this conventional extraction process is considerably complicated owing to the lack of extraction tools specialized for various cage types, combined with the issue of scar adhesion at the decompression site and the increased risk of nerve damage. The ultrasonic osteotome operates on the principle of high‐frequency microvibration, demonstrating “intelligent” tissue selectivity and precise cutting control. This technology significantly reduces the risk of damage to soft tissues (particularly nerves and blood vessels) while maintaining temperatures substantially lower than those generated by high‐speed drills, effectively preventing thermal necrosis of bone and neural tissues. Furthermore, its precision cutting capability facilitates complex surgical maneuvers, such as segmented resection of spinal cages. Our study described the application of the ultrasonic osteotome in a segmental removal approach during revision surgeries for managing IVDS infection following lumbar fusion. We used the ultrasonic osteotome to fragment the cage for subsequent extraction, thereby effectively reducing operation time and blood loss, minimizing nerve damage risk, and achieving a high success rate in cage removal.

Strengths and Limitations

This investigation constitutes the first direct comparison between the ultrasonic osteotome segmental excision technique and the conventional whole removal method for managing intervertebral disc space infection (IVDS) after lumbar fusion. The results demonstrate that the ultrasonic osteotome surpasses traditional methods with marked clinical advantages. It still has certain limitations. First, in obese patients, the standard handle length of the ultrasonic osteotome may be insufficient, which can restrict the instrument's operative space during cage fragmentation and consequently prolong surgical duration. Second, this method is not suitable for managing IVDSs caused by the metallic material of the cage itself. Third, that is indeed an accurate observation; the limited sample size and the single‐center retrospective design are limitations of this study, as they may affect the accuracy and generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, we believe that the results of the present study have clinical significance. Evidently, further studies are needed.

Prospects of Clinical Application

The ultrasonic osteotome offers significant advantages over traditional methods in revision surgery for IVDS after lumbar fusion, including shorter operation time, less blood loss, a higher cage removal success rate, and a lower risk of nerve damage. It is particularly suitable for posterior revision surgery, effectively addressing challenges such as scar adhesion, limited surgical visibility, and the lack of specialized cage removal tools. Therefore, the ultrasonic osteotome holds important value for clinical application and has the potential to be extended to other complex spinal revision procedures in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, compared with the conventional surgical method, ultrasonic osteotome‐based segmental extraction offers advantages in terms of shorter and more controllable operation time, reduced blood loss, higher success rate in cage removal, and lower nerve damage risk. This study underscores the unique value of utilizing ultrasonic osteotome in revision surgeries for IVDS infection after lumbar fusion.
