Predicting of tunneling conductivity for polymer-carbon black nanocomposites by interphase percolation
Authors: Mohammad Boomhendi, Mostafa Vatani, Yasser Zare
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarlier studies primarily focused on the experimental aspects of percolation onset and conductivity in polymer/carbon black (CB) nanocomposites, but the modeling approach was largely underexplored. In this study, a new equation is developed to determine the CB percolation threshold, taking into account the superficial properties of CB and polymer, as well as crucial terms such as CB radius (R) and interphase thickness (t). Also, a model is advised to simulate the conductivity of CB-filled examples by accounting for R, contact circle diameter, the number of contacts, and “t”. Validation by laboratory data confirms the reliability of the proposed model. The results indicate that a smaller “R” and a higher volume portion of CB significantly enhance the conductivity, achieving 7.7 S/m at minimum R = 5 nm and the highest CB volume fraction of 0.036. A direct correlation is also found between the number of CB contacts (m) and composite conductivity, where the highest network volume portion of 0.88 and supreme m = 37 produce the supreme conductivity of 2.45 S/m.Keywords: Polymer nanocomposites, Carbon black nanoparticle, Conductivity, Tunneling effect, Percolation thresholdSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Carbon black (CB) is a meticulously processed variant of carbon, characterized by its nearly spherical particles and aggregates1–7. It is generated by the incomplete combustion or thermal breakdown of hydrocarbons8. CB is utilized in various industrial applications particularly to enhance the physical, electrical, and optical properties of materials9–12. Its distinct structure and characteristics render it a valuable resource across multiple areas including pigments, fuel cells, and batteries5,7,13. CB executes a significant function in enhancing the structural properties of elastomer nanocomposites, and its reinforcement effect is influenced by morphology14. It is imperative to create conductive network within polymer matrices critical for the applications that require electrical conductivity. Mondal et al.15 examined thermal ageing and EMI-shielding in chlorinated polyethylene filled with carbon black, highlighting how network evolution affects conductivity. Also, Huang16 reviewed conducting polymer/CB composites and reported how polymer structure, filler dispersion, and interfacial interactions govern the percolation behavior. More recently, Paul et al.17 discussed the recovered CB from tire pyrolysis as a sustainable filler, underlining the role of surface properties in conductivity.

Electrical conductivity in nanocomposites boosts at a specific filler volume, known as the percolation onset, which allows for charge transport pathways18–21. This point is affected by the size, dispersion, and the aggregation of particles22–27. Additionally, the interphase, which is the interaction zone between the polymer medium and CB, significantly affects the composite performance24,28–34. The interfacial layer around CB nanoparticles facilitates for forming a conductive network in polymer nanocomposites (PNCs). The interphase influences the connectivity of nanoparticles, where thicker interphases enhance the conductivity by providing more conducting pathways (Fig. 1).

CB in nanocomposite with interphase layer.

The phenomenon of electron tunneling within nanocomposites that incorporate CB is instrumental in augmenting electrical conductivity35–37. The amalgamation of CB with additional conductive substances has enabled the creation of a tunneling region that markedly grows the conductivity of composite38. This effect is predominantly influenced by the arrangement and dispersion of conductive fillers within the polymer matrix35. Ji et al.39 introduced a computational method for evaluating nanocomposite electrical conductivity using equivalent resistances in a resistive network. They recommended that factors such as CB concentration and particle morphology enhance the accuracy of simulation. Payandehpeyman et al.40 also explored simulation techniques for CB polymer nanocomposites to model electrical conductivity and percolation onset. Their physical model assumed randomly distributed ellipsoidal nanoparticles and effectively predicted the low percolation thresholds.

In this article, we present a developed method for the conductivity of CB-filled nanocomposites that incorporates the percentage of networked CBs, nanoparticle conductivity, CB dimension, interphase depth and the number and diameter of contacts among nanoparticles. To validate our model, we utilized experimental data from various samples, allowing us to assess the accuracy in predicting conductivity across different formulations. We also explored the impact of each parameter on the predicted conductivity, providing a detailed discussion that support the model’s validity. This investigation enhances the understanding of the ways in which these factors collectively influence the electrical performance. By combining an interphase-adjusted effective volume fraction with a contact/tunneling conductivity expression, and validating the framework against several reported polymer/CB systems, we aim to provide a practical predictive tool for material design and to identify which microstructural parameters most strongly control the nanocomposite conductivity.

The percolation threshold in CB-containing nanocomposites is largely determined by three key factors: nanoparticle radius, interphase thickness, and surface tension38,45. Nanoparticles with smaller radii typically result in a lower percolation threshold as their increased surface area promotes stronger particle interactions and facilitates the development of conductive network. In contrast, larger nanoparticles typically need higher concentrations, resulting in an elevated percolation threshold. The thickness of the interphase plays a crucial role as well; a thicker interphase can enhance particle connectivity and decrease the percolation threshold by creating a more conducive environment for interactions. Furthermore, higher surface tension can negatively impact nanoparticle dispersion, facilitating the formation of conductive pathways and consequently reducing the percolation threshold. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of these factors is crucial for minimizing the percolation behavior44. A general caveat is warranted regarding the presumed monotonic influence of interphase thickness on the percolation onset and DC conductivity. In CB-dominated systems with near-spherical aggregates, a thicker interphase tends to increase the effective filler volume (Eq. 5) and facilitate geometric connectivity, which can lower in Eq. (6). However, this trend is not universal. For high-surface-area and anisotropic fillers such as CNTs and graphene, thicker amorphous interphases may increase the effective tunneling barrier height and/or separation distance between conductive domains. Because tunneling resistance depends exponentially on barrier width/height, a thicker or more disordered interphase can suppress charge transport, elevate , and reduce overall conductivity. Additional dielectric phenomena (e.g., interfacial polarization and trap states) can further diminish DC conduction in such systems. Accordingly, Eq. The present model assumes spherical CB nanoparticles and their random orientation in the samples as well as the dominated tunneling conduction. So, it does not treat anisotropic fillers such as CNTs46,47, graphene48,49, carbon fiber50. The present framework treats the conductive network as an assembly of 0D, nearly spherical CB particles connected by tunneling contacts parameterized by an average contact number (m) and contact diameter (dc). Equation 11 does not consider (i) the full inter-particle distance distribution, (ii) interfacial polarization and frequency-dependent dielectric effects, (iii) percolation network topology statistics beyond average connectivity, or (iv) local electric field enhancement and field-induced alignment. These simplifications make the model most applicable to DC conductivity of CB-dominated systems with random orientation, above the percolation onset, where tunneling conduction is dominant. Consequently, Eq. 11 should not be extrapolated to systems governed by strong polarization, AC frequency dispersion, field-induced structuring, or highly anisotropic filler morphologies (e.g., CNTs or graphene) without further modification.

Roles of parameters in composite conductivity

The validated model was utilized to evaluate and record the power of each variable on the electrical conductivity of PCB samples. Additionally, it is feasible to investigate the effects of critical variables on the conductivity of the nanocomposites to substantiate the predictive validity of the proposed model. The parameters were assumed for comprise median measurements of R = 20 nm, t = 15 nm, m = 20, dc = 20 nm, f = 0.02,, γp = 35 mN/m and γf = 55 mN/m.

Figure 4 illustrates the influence of the parameters “ϕf” and “R” on the conductivity of PCB, as foreseen by the new model. Notably, at ϕf = 0.036 and R = 5 nm, the highest conductivity observed is 7.7 S/m. This indicates that optimal conductivity is achieved when the amount of the filler is maximized and the CB size is the lowest. Conversely, when the CB radius is maximized and its amount is minimized, the composite behaves as an insulator, resulting in the lowest possible conductivity. This highlights the critical roles of both filler content and size in determining the electrical properties of polymer nanocomposites.

Influences of CB radius and volume fraction on PCB conductivity as illustrated through (a) three-dimensional and (b) contour drawings.

Low filler content may not reach the percolation threshold, which prevents significant enhancement in the conductivity of the resulting nanocomposites. When the filler concentration is insufficient, the conductive pathways necessary for effective electron transport are not adequately formed, leading to a limited electrical performance. This underscores the standing of filler concentration to establish a network inside a polymer matrix. As the filler content increases, the likelihood of forming interconnected pathways also increases, causing an upgraded conductivity. This relationship highlights the pivotal role of the filler concentration in optimizing the electrical properties of nanocomposites, emphasizing the need for careful consideration of filler loading during the material design process to achieve the intended conductivity level.

The size of CB particles plays an essential character in determining the conductivity of samples. Smaller particles possess higher surface-area-to-volume ratios, which significantly enhance their interactions with the polymer matrix. This increased surface area aids in charge transport by providing more contact points between the particles and the polymer. The improved interfacial connectivity facilitates more efficient electron transfer, thereby increasing the conductivity of the nanocomposite. In contrast, larger CB particles grow the percolation threshold, condensing the network within the composite material. Actually, bigger nanoparticles produce the shorter network weakening the charge transfer. Therefore, it is essential to control the particle size to enhance the electrical properties of PCB.

The relationship between the conductivity of PCB and the parameters including “f” (the proportion of percolated CB) and “m” (the number of contacts) is illustrated in Fig. 5. As these parameters increase, the conductivity of the composite also rises significantly. This trend indicates that a higher fraction of percolated CB and an increased number of contacts facilitate better electron transport within the material, leading to enhanced conductivity. Specifically, the optimal conductivity value of 2.45 S/m is achieved at (f = 0.88) and (m = 37). In contrast, when the values of these parameters are low, particularly when f < 0.2 or m < 12, the conductivity drops considerably which results in poor electrical performance. This behavior underscores the critical role that the proportion of percolated CB and the number of contacts play in determining the electrical properties of nanocomposites. Overall, the findings confirm that the achievement of a sufficient level of network portion and contacts among CB particles is essential for optimizing the conductivity.

“f” serves as a crucial indicator of the magnitude and the concentration of the filler network within PCB. Specifically, it quantifies the percentage of CBs, integrated into the conductive network, particularly beyond the established percolation threshold. A high “f” value is indicative of a significant presence of nanoparticles within interconnected regions, which suggests the establishment of a dense and wide network. In contrast, a lower “f” value reflects a more dispersed arrangement of CB throughout the PCB, leading to a less compact and smaller network. This relationship is critical because larger networks are generally more effective in facilitating charge transport. Consequently, the electrical conductivity of the PCB is directly influenced by the size of the conductive network. Thus, understanding and optimizing the “f” parameter is essential for advancing the development of high-performance conductive materials.

The number of contacts between CB particles significantly affects the PCB conductivity by enhancing the formation of conductive pathways within the polymer matrix. An increased number of contacts lead to a more interconnected conductive network, facilitating better charge transport and improving overall conductivity. This is particularly important for reaching the percolation threshold, which is the minimum concentration of conductive filler required for effective conductivity. Smaller CB particles typically create more contact owing to their larger surface area, aiding in achieving this threshold at lower filler concentrations. Consequently, the established model clearly explains how “m” manages the conductivity of PCBs.

The elevated conductivity of CB significantly enhances the overall conductivity of the composites. This effect is particularly important because, in general, polymers are considered insulators. Therefore, the conductivity of PCBs is predominantly influenced by the conductive properties of the incorporated nanoparticles. In essence, a higher conductivity of these nanoparticles serves to facilitate and promote the conduction pathways through the polymer matrix. This interaction not only improves the electrical performance of the nanocomposite but also suggests that optimizing the concentration and distribution of conductive nanoparticles within the polymer can lead to enhanced conductive networks, which ultimately results in the improved performance characteristics of the PCB. Thus, the relationship between nanoparticle conductivity and the overall electrical properties of the composite is crucial for the development of a tailored conductivity for various requests. Conversely, a scenario, where there is poor conduction alongside a high percolation level of CB, does not lead to significant enhancement in the overall composite conductivity.

The percolation threshold is a crucial element that influences the electrical conductivity of PCB, because it marks the concentration at which a continuous conductive network forms within the insulating polymer matrix. Below this threshold, the material behaves as an insulator; however, once the threshold is reached, there is a dramatic increase in conductivity owing to the establishment of interconnected pathways that facilitate the movement of charge carriers. Several factors, including particle size, interphase thickness, and surface tension of the polymer with filler, can affect the percolation onset26,61,62. Consequently, the proposed model accurately illustrates an inverse relationship between PCB conductivity and the onset of percolation.

The results indicate a clear trend: the highest conductivity values are achieved when both “t” and “dc” are at their maximum levels. Specifically, the maximum conductivity reaches a value of 4.41 S/m when “t” is set at 29 nm and “dc” at 38 nm. This finding highlights the essential function that such factors play in enhancing the electrical performance of the nanocomposite. The data suggest as the interphase thickness and contact diameter increase, the pathways for electron transport become more efficient, leading to improved conductivity. Conversely, when the values of “dc” fall below 4 nm or “t” is less than 13 nm, the conductivity approaches zero. This phenomenon indicates that insufficient interphase thickness and contact diameter can severely hinder the movement of charge carriers, resulting in a state akin to electrical insulation.

The dimensional characteristics of the interphase, which are developed between a polymeric matrix and its corresponding filler, have a profound influence on the electrical conductivity of PCB. An augmented interphase thickness fosters enhanced connectivity and interaction between the conductive filler and the polymeric substrate. This augmented interaction is critical for the establishment of more efficient electron transport pathways, which ultimately culminate in elevated overall conductivity. A precisely delineated interphase promoted the development of a coherent conductive network throughout the composite material. This structural continuity is imperative for the enhancement of the charge carrier mobility, which is intrinsically linked to improved electrical performance. As the mobility is augmented, electrons afford greater freedom of movement, thereby reducing the probability of charge trapping or recombination phenomena that could impede conductivity. Hence, a synergistic relationship was established between the interphase thickness and overall conductivity of the nanocomposites.

The analysis highpoints the main role of the “dc” in improving the conductivity of PCB. The increased diameters of contact rings are associated with enhanced conductivity as they create a larger contact area that reduces resistance during the tunneling process driven by CB nanoparticles. A rise in the “dc” value leads to an expanded contact area, which in turn decreases resistance and improves overall conductivity. However, a reduced “dc” value condenses the contact area, that grows the resistivity but decreases the conduction. Thus, the proposed model successfully illustrates the relationship between the conductivity of the PCB and the contact area.

The conductivity of the nanocomposite, in relation to parameters “γp” and “γf”, is illustrated in Fig. 8. Notably, at γp = 20 mN/m and γf = 60 mN/m, the conductivity reaches a value of 0.64 S/m. In contrast, the lowest observed conductivity, approximately 0.15 S/m, occurs in the regions characterized by high γp and low γf. The influence of polymer surface tension on electrical conductivity is profoundly interconnected with the circulation and distribution attributes of conductive particles. A decrease in polymer surface tension deteriorates the wettability of nanoparticles by the polymer chains. In such scenario, the arrangement of nanoparticles within the polymer matrix exhibits irregularities that frequently cause the suboptimal dispersion. Notwithstanding, this diminished wettability incites the clustering of nanoparticles, and, thus, it augments their direct interactions and promotes the formation of interconnected networks. This aggregation and networking of CB particles ultimately results in an enhancement of the nanocomposite electrical conductivity as the conductive pathways, engendered by the interactions between nanoparticles, are imperative for electron mobility. This phenomenon is encapsulated in the proposed theoretical framework, which posits an inverse relationship between the composite conductivity and the surface tension of the polymer medium. Fundamentally, polymers, characterized by diminished surface tension, engender enhanced electrical conductivity by fortifying the interactions of the nanoparticles through diminished dispersion. Additionally, when the surface tension of the filler is increased, the tendency for network formation and aggregation within the nanocomposite rises. This increase in interface tension between the polymer and nanoparticles causes the nanoparticles to come closer together and aggregate, thereby enhancing the conductivity of PCB. Accordingly, the model effectively represents the relationship between the conductivity and the surface tensions of its components.

Conclusions

The study introduces a framework for the PCB conductivity, filling a gap in the literature that has largely focused on experimental findings. The model incorporates key parameters such as nanoparticle radius (R), interphase thickness (t), volume fraction of CB (f), and the number of contacts between nanoparticles (m). Key findings include that a smaller nanoparticle radius (R = 5 nm) and a higher volume fraction of CB (f = 0.036) enhance conductivity significantly, achieving a maximum of 7.7 S/m. The nanocomposite conductivity directly correlates to network fraction and m, and σ = 2.45 S/m is seen at supreme levels of f = 0.88 and m = 37. The interphase thickness is crucial for conductivity as thicker interphases provide more tunneling pathways for electron transport. The model also highlights the influence of surface tension, indicating that lower polymer surface tension (γp) and higher filler surface tension (γf) enhance conductivity. Specifically, at γp = 20 mN/m and γf = 60 mN/m, the conductivity reaches 0.64 S/m, while higher γp and poorer γf result in reduced conductivity. Overall, this model provides valuable insights into the factors affecting conductivity and serves as a practical instrument for augmenting conductive materials for various requests in physical and chemical sensors, bio-electrochemical system, and energy storage.
