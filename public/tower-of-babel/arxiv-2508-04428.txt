SimInstruct : A Responsible Tool for Collecting Scaffolding Dialogues Between Experts and LLM-Simulated Novices
Si Chen, Izzy Molnar, Ting Hua, Peiyu Li, Le Huy Khiem, G. Alex Ambrose, Jim Lang, Ronald Metoyer, Nitesh V. Chawla

Abstract
High-quality, multi-turn instructional dialogues between novices and experts are essential for developing AI systems that support teaching, learning, and decision-making. These dialogues often involve scaffolding —the process by which an expert supports a novice’s thinking through questions, feedback, and step-by-step guidance. However, such data are scarce due to privacy concerns in recording and the vulnerability inherent in help-seeking. We present SimInstruct , a scalable, expert-in-the-loop tool for collecting scaffolding dialogues. Using teaching development coaching as an example domain, SimInstruct simulates novice instructors via LLMs, varying their teaching challenges and LLM’s persona traits, while human experts provide multi-turn feedback, reasoning, and instructional support. This design enables the creation of realistic, pedagogically rich dialogues without requiring real novice participants. Our results reveal that persona traits—such as extroversion and introversion—meaningfully influence how experts engage. Compared to real mentoring recordings, SimInstruct dialogues demonstrate comparable pedagogical relevance and cognitive depth. Experts also reported the process as engaging and reflective, improving both data quality and their own professional insight. We further fine-tuned a LLaMA model to be an expert model using the augmented dataset, which outperformed GPT4o in instructional quality. Our analysis highlights GPT4o’s limitations in weak reflective questioning, overuse of generic praise, a condescending tone, and a tendency to overwhelm novices with excessive suggestions.



Introduction

Scaffolded multi-turn dialogues, where support is gradually adjusted based on learner need, form the basis of effective learning in many domains
(
Wood, Bruner, and Ross 1976
;
Van de Pol, Volman, and Beishuizen 2010
)
. These interactions foster metacognition, adapt to individual needs, and encourage reflective reasoning rather than direct answer delivery. Scaffolding plays a central role not only in formal education like mathematics instruction
(
Collins, Brown, and Newman 1989
)
, but also in informal and professional development contexts—including clinical supervision, counseling, peer mentoring, and coaching—where nuanced feedback and situated reasoning are essential
(
Tran et al.
)
.

As interest in AI for education grows, so does demand for language models that provide multi-turn scaffolding rather than straightforward fact delivery. OpenAI’s recently introduced Study Mode is one such example: a tutoring-like interface that uses Socratic prompts and scaffolded feedback across multiple turns to foster active reflection and deeper learner engagement

. Other promising systems—such as CLASS and KG-RAG—demonstrate scaffolded, stepwise QA and measurable gains in education
(
Sonkar et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2025
)
.

Despite the demand, authentic scaffolding dialogues remain elusive at scale. Because these interactions are often private, ethically sensitive, and highly context-dependent—especially in education—they are rarely documented or generalizable
(
Lee, Kim et al. 2022
)
. Moreover, traditional data collection relies on post-hoc annotation of small recordings or static LLM pipelines that omit expert intent and situated pedagogical reasoning
(
Lee, Kim et al. 2022
;
Geiger et al. 2021
)
. As a result, coaching-style instructional dialogue datasets remain scarce.

In this work, we introduce
SimInstruct
, a responsible and scalable tool for generating expert–novice scaffolding dialogues using arge language model(LLM)-simulated personas. Human experts engage in multi-turn conversations with a wide range of LLM-simulated novices, offering guidance, reflection, and instructional reasoning. This expert-in-the-loop design produces pedagogically rich, contextually grounded dialogue data while avoiding the ethical and logistical challenges of recruiting real novices. We evaluate the quality of 123 dialogues from 18 experts by comparing them to real coaching interactions in teacher development programs and find that
SimInstruct
achieves comparable quality.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose
SimInstruct
, a novel expert-in-the-loop and responsible data collection tool that leverages LLM-simulated novices to elicit rich, multi-turn instructional dialogues grounded in scaffolding theory.

•

We evaluate the collected dialogues through both qualitative feedback and quantitative analysis. By comparing our data with real expert–novice recordings and fine-tuned LLMs on the augmented dataset, we demonstrate improved instructional quality and pedagogical relevance.

•

We distill practical lessons for involving human experts in high-quality dataset creation and offer actionable insights for building domain-specific AI.

Among the many domains and subjects where scaffolding is critical, we focus on
teacher development
as our case study. In both preservice training and ongoing professional learning, expert–novice coaching helps educators interpret classroom events, make instructional decisions, and develop their pedagogical identity
(
Hennessy and Davies 2019
;
Wallin and Boggan 2015
)
. Recent research shows that existing tools often fall short in addressing the real-world complexities of teaching, with educators expressing a preference for AI systems that offer more contextualized and reflective support
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. We answer the research question–
How effectively does the use of LLM-simulated novices support the generation of high-quality scaffolding dialogues?

Background and Related Work

Human–AI Collaboration in Dataset Collection

Human–AI collaboration has been widely applied in dataset creation, especially in annotation workflows where humans curate or label AI-generated content, as seen in projects like Soylent and Garbage-in, Garbage-out
(
Bernstein et al. 2010
;
Geiger and Adamic 2022
)
. With the rise of large language models (LLMs), newer pipelines generate synthetic data across domains including healthcare
(
Li, Wu et al. 2023
)
, math tutoring
(
Tang et al. 2023
)
, and instruction tuning
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

Despite these advancements, many pipelines treat LLMs as autonomous generators, with humans only reviewing or filtering content after generation
(
Lee, Kim et al. 2022
)
. Approaches such as GLAN
(
Li et al. 2024
)
and MIDI-Tuning
(
Wang, Leong et al. 2024
)
improve consistency through structured prompting and multi-round tuning but still fall short in integrating domain expertise or pedagogical objectives.

In contrast, recent HCI research demonstrates how domain experts can play a more central role in AI-supported workflows. Yang et al.
(
Yang, Steinfeld, and Zimmerman 2021
)
emphasize the co-construction of datasets with AI to enhance interpretability and contextual alignment. Collaborative tools like DocuViz
(
Kim et al. 2014
)
visualize writing dynamics to scaffold expert coordination, while Mechanical Novel
(
Kim, Cheng, and Bernstein 2017
)
shows how experts and non-experts can co-author content through iterative reflection and revision.

Scaffolding in Teaching and Learning Systems

Effective teaching involves more than delivering correct answers. Scaffolding strategies—such as metacognitive prompting, formative feedback, and adaptive coaching—help learners construct knowledge and build autonomy
(
Van de Pol, Volman, and Beishuizen 2012
;
Chi and Wylie 2014
)
. Yet many conversational AI systems prioritize factual accuracy or task completion, reinforced through training methods like reinforcement learning from human feedback
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
.

Such systems risk “answer-leaking,” which can bypass productive struggle and hinder learning
(
Koedinger, McLaughlin, and Stamper 2012
)
. Although datasets like MathDial incorporate step-by-step reasoning
(
Tang et al. 2023
)
, they fall short of modeling the coaching-based interactions essential in teacher development. Our work builds on these insights by simulating expert–novice scaffolding dialogues that reflect authentic instructional goals. Recent benchmarks like ClarifyBench assess multi-turn reasoning but lack pedagogical context
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
.

Expert–Novice Dialogues in Professional Development

Expert–novice interactions are central to professional learning in fields such as education, counseling, and clinical supervision
(
Schön 1983
;
Eraut 2000
)
. In
teacher development
, coaching conversations help novices interpret classroom events, navigate uncertainty, and cultivate their pedagogical identity. However, capturing these dialogues at scale remains difficult due to privacy concerns and logistical constraints
(
Perell et al. 2021
)
.

Beyond supporting novice growth, such interactions benefit experts as well by promoting reflection and reinforcing instructional judgment. This dual impact aligns with the learning through teaching paradigm, which suggests that teaching others enhances the teacher’s own understanding by requiring knowledge reorganization, articulation of tacit reasoning, and anticipation of learner needs
(
Bargh and Schul 1986
;
Chi and Wylie 2014
)
. These benefits have been documented across teacher preparation, medical supervision, and design-based learning
(
Eraut 2000
;
Schön 1983
)
. Recent work has begun to explore how LLM-simulated learners can support expert tasks. For example, Generative Students
(
Lu and Wang 2024
)
introduces simulated student profiles to aid item evaluation in assessment design, illustrating how synthetic learners can enhance expert workflows.

Method

We adopted a human-centered design approach to develop
SimInstruct
, a web-based tool for collecting multi-turn instructional dialogues between human experts and LLM-simulated novice instructors. From January to June 2025, we conducted weekly design sessions with two senior teacher development experts to iteratively refine the tool’s interface, persona generation, and study protocol. In July 2025, human experts used
SimInstruct
asynchronously, generating dialogues. We evaluated the tool through two lenses: (1) content analysis of the collected dialogues and their suitability for fine-tuning AI models (AI perspective); and (2) expert feedback on usability and perceived realism (HCI perspective). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Tool Design & Development

Design Goals

G1: LLM-Simulated Novices to Mitigate Privacy Risks

We initially aimed to collect traditional expert–novice teaching dialogues via audio recording, but privacy concerns, scheduling difficulties, and worries that being recorded might inhibit authentic and often vulnerable help-seeking limited us to being able to collect just four sessions (around 200 minutes) over two months. To address ethical and logistical barriers,
SimInstruct
uses LLMs to simulate novice instructors to talk with human experts, enabling the scalable collection of realistic dialogues without relying on sensitive or identifiable data. These four real dialogues were later used to check the quality of the dialogues collected with our tool, for example in Fig
2
.

G2: Conversational Data Collection to Capture Expert Reasoning Naturally

We chose a conversation-based approach to data collection because it better captures the dynamics of real instructional interactions and allows experts to express their reasoning more naturally, engagingly and flexible than through static annotations or scoring tasks.

G3: Persona-Based for Diverse Contexts and Questions

We created diverse, pedagogically meaningful personas to simulate novices with varied subject areas, experience levels, and instructional challenges, enabling the generation of dialogue data across a broad range of teaching contexts. We also varied personality traits, such as introversion and extroversion, to further diversify conversational behaviors and content. The goal was to make the dialogues resemble authentic novice–expert interactions.

Data Collection Tool Details

SimInstruct
is designed to simulate realistic novices, enabling human teaching experts to engage in conversations that resemble typical coaching interactions—offering an alternative to real instructors, thereby addressing
G1
. To support
G2
, we developed a web-based conversational user interface (UI) (Full version in Fig.
7
) where experts interact with an LLM-simulated novice through multiple dialogue turns. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the simulated novices are assigned diverse personas with traits relevant to the teacher coaching domain, addressing
G3
. Prompts can be found in Appendix A.

Below, we provide more details on the three key components of LLM-Simulated Novice.
1

Persona Profile Generator

Each simulated novice is assigned a persona comprising nine randomly selected attributes for
Domain Profile
: first name, last name, classroom context, teaching experience, discipline, course level, semester context, teaching style, and conversation style. Then for
Personality Profile
: Teaching and conversation styles are defined using four of the Big Five personality traits—openness, conscientiousness, extroversion, and agreeableness
(
McCrae and Costa 1987
)
—which are randomly assigned. Neuroticism was excluded as it was considered irrelevant to our task. A
Domain Challenge
was then randomly selected from a 40 item list created by human expert. To ensure internal consistency, a verification function using GPT-4 checks each generated profile for logical coherence (e.g., avoiding mismatches like a law professor assigned to a laboratory classroom).
2

Initial Question

Once the persona is generated, GPT-4 is used to produce an initial question, which is presented to the human expert. The expert must respond based solely on the information provided, eliciting further details through follow-up questions. This interaction design reflects a realistic coaching scenario and supports
G2
.
3

Follow-up Responses

For subsequent turns, GPT-4-turbo-preview generates responses aligned with the persona profile and initial question to behavior coherence. The expert continues the dialogue with the simulated novice until they determine the conversation is complete and a viable teaching strategy has been identified—or that the novice appears ready to implement and satisfied with experts’ suggestion.

When engaging with the tool, experts are presented with a welcome message from the simulated novice, which includes the novice’s first and last name along with the initial question. Experts interact with a unique set of novice profiles, with the option to delete any conversations they find unrealistic. To refine the generation process, the two human expert was asked to review and evaluate 30 randomly selected novice profiles which included the persona and initial question. The expert’s feedback was incorporated to enhance realism and coherence of the generated profiles.

Data Collection Study with Human Experts

Eighteen human experts based in the United States, all with extensive coaching experience in higher education, participated in the study. They were recruited through email and word-of-mouth to ensure their expertise and experiences. All expert had advanced degree. Human experts completed their dialogues asynchronously over a two-week period. Compensation was based on the number of completed dialogues, with per-dialogue pay calculated according to the estimated time taken (5 to 20 minutes) and the participant’s hourly rate of
50
USD. Throughout the study, participants were encouraged to communicate with the research team to ask questions, raise concerns, and share positive experiences. At the end of the study, all participants were invited to complete a brief post-hoc survey to provide feedback on the tool, the quality of the dialogues, and their experience.

Overview

Across 123 dialogues, the LLM-simulated novice produced a total of 65,004 words, and the human expert contributed 38,444 words, with 1,848 total dialogue turns. On average per dialogue, the LLM contributed 528.49 words (SD = 378.40), the human expert contributed 312.55 words (SD = 242.41), and there were 15.02 turns (SD = 7.77). The majority dialogues collected through our tool followed a three-part scaffolding structure: Problem Identification, where the novice raised a specific teaching challenge; Reason Exploration, where the expert guided reflection on underlying causes; and Strategy Development, where practical solutions were discussed. Findings

We further analyzed dialogue turns by examining novice’s different persona input across different aspects. A Welch two-sample t-test was conducted to examine whether LLM persona extraversion influenced the human expert’s word count in dialogues. As shown in Fig.
3

Human experts used more words when interacting with extroverted LLM personas (M = 385.46, SD = 276.28, n = 54) compared to introverted personas (M = 293.78, SD = 181.20, n = 60). The 95% confidence interval for the difference in means ranged from –179.65 to –3.71, indicating that persona design significantly influences expert engagement during dialogue but not conversation turns and LLM-simulated novice word counts. In contrast, the other three personality traits—openness, conscientiousness, and agreeableness—did not significantly affect the number of dialogue turns or word counts. Dialogues with fewer than 3 turns were removed to meet testing prerequisites.

Evaluation of
SimInstruct

SimInstruct Collected Data vs. Real Dialogues

We compared four dialogues collected using our tool with four real, face-to-face coaching sessions totaling of 200 minutes. While this comparison is limited in scope due to the small sample size, it offers preliminary insights into both the promise and limitations of using LLMs to simulate novice behavior. As shown in Fig.
2
, approximately half of the collected dialogues fall within the turn range observed in real dialogues, while the other half exhibit fewer turns. We did not compare word counts, as face-to-face oral dialogues often include filler words and self-correction language, that are not typically present in text-based communication.

Content Evaluated using LLM-as-Judge

To systematically evaluate the quality of the dialogues, we employed an LLM-as-a-Judge approach, a common method in which a separate LLM is prompted to rate each dialogue using human-developed evaluation criteria
(
Gu et al. 2024
)
. Four evaluation criteria were developed collaboratively by team members with experience in pedagogy and educational research, and served as a rubric for determining how well the simulator was able to replicate realistic and meaningful novice questions. These criteria were created for overall pedagogical relevance, cognitive depth of the simulated question, instructional contextualization of the question, and the coverage of pedological concerns. A list of these evaluation criteria and prompts is found in Appendix E.

Findings

We found that the structural scaffolding in both types of dialogues was similar. In particular, both data included the three-step scaffolding structure: identifying a problem, reasoning about it, and suggesting a strategy. When evaluated using the LLM-as-a-Judge method, the average scores across the four criteria were consistently lower for the
SimInstruct
collected data compared to the face-to-face recorded data. As shown in Fig.
4
, recorded dialogues consistently received scores of 3 across all criteria, while the
SimInstruct
collected data had slightly lower averages.

A closer look at the score distribution of the
SimInstruct
data revealed more specific patterns as seen in Fig.

. While the LLM-as-Judge generally recognized the tool-generated dialogues as containing high-quality pedagogical features, the LLM-simulated novices in these dialogues sometimes appeared overly straightforward and lacked contextual grounding of own challenges. This suggests that the simulated novice responses may not yet fully replicate the complexity of human novices.

Collected Data to Finetune Expert Model

While our tool collected a set of high quality expert-novice dialogues, the dataset still remains relatively small and insufficient for fine-tuning of language models. To address this limitation, we decided to augment the data using synthetic generation strategies.

Data Augmentation

Starting with a seed dataset of 123 collected dialogues, we use GPT‑4o mini to generate over 1,000 synthesized dialogues within a consistent domain. In each iteration, we randomly sample three real dialogues from the seed set as examples and prompt the language model to generate a new multi-turn dialogue in the same format and with a similar tone and style, but beginning with a different initial question. This process leverages the model’s in-context learning capabilities. After synthesis, we filter out any dialogues that do not match the required format, resulting in a final set of 1,415 dialogues.

Finetune Expert Model using Augmented Data

We fine-tuned the
Llama-2-7b-chat-hf
model using the full augmented dataset described previously, consisting of 1,415 multi-turn scaffolding dialogues. After splitting these into individual turns, we obtained a total of 9,271 training examples. Fine-tuning was performed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU for 435 steps, taking approximately 1.5 hours. We used a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, weight decay of 0.01, warmup ratio of 0.05, and cosine learning rate scheduling. Optimization was done using the AdamW optimizer from PyTorch.

Human Annotators’ Rating of Expert Model

We conducted a human evaluation of 220 AI-generated instructional dialogues, with the source model of each dialogue kept hidden and shuffled. Two human annotators independently rated the expert responses for each dialogue in 5 hours. We used a rubric developed by our research team with extensive expertise in teaching and AI. The rubric focuses on how effectively expert responses coach novice instructors, using four criteria on a 1-3 scale (full definitions provided in Appendix D): Clarity of Expression, Supportive & Appropriate Tone, Reflective Prompting, and Appropriateness of Validation. To assess inter-rater reliability across these dimensions, we computed quadratically weighted Cohen’s
\kappa
on the pooled ratings. LLaMA achieved a
\kappa
of 0.65, indicating substantial agreement, while GPT reached a moderate agreement level with a
\kappa
of 0.53. See Fig.
10
for detailed agreement matrices.

Approximately 20% of the dialogues were excluded from the analysis and did not receive a score because the model repeatedly generated the same self-identifying name, making its origin easily recognizable and thus unsuitable for blind evaluation. Unlike in our evaluation of novice responses, we did not rely on LLM-as-Judge for assessing expert quality. Instead, we used human-annotated scores, as preliminary analysis showed that LLM-as-Judge scores did not align well with expert ratings across the four criteria.

Findings

As show in Fig
6
, LLaMA outperformed GPT4o across all four evaluation dimensions. The most pronounced difference was in reflective prompting, where LLaMA received a substantially higher mean score (M = 2.67, SD = 0.55) than GPT4o (M = 1.76, SD = 0.56), indicating that LLaMA was significantly more effective at encouraging novices to reflect or reason. In terms of clarity of expression and supportive tone, LLaMA again showed stronger performance, achieving mean scores of 2.62 on both, compared to GPT4o’s 2.12 and 2.00, respectively. Finally, for appropriateness of validation, LLaMA scored 2.59 (SD = 0.57) while GPT4o scored 1.92 (SD = 0.49), suggesting LLaMA provided more balanced and contextually appropriate validation without overusing generic praise. These results indicate that LLaMA’s responses were consistently clearer, more supportive, more reflective, and more appropriately validating than those of GPT4o.

Human Expert’s Experience of Using the Tool

Expert participants reported a positive experience using the tool, as reflected in both informal interactions and a post-study survey. The lightweight, asynchronous interface was described as easy to navigate, and the conversation-based format aligned with their coaching practices. Survey responses indicated that most participants found the simulated scenarios realistic and the task intellectually engaging. Several noted that the process prompted reflection on their own coaching styles and approaches to feedback. Experts appreciated the flexibility to skip scenarios, and many expressed interest in using similar tools for training or professional development.

Encouraging Expert Participation Through Framing and Flexible Task Design

Framing the task as a meaningful contribution to the future of AI in education was key to recruiting and sustaining expert engagement. We aligned the task with familiar elements of their daily routines and avoided presenting it as a generic data labeling exercise. Instead, we emphasized that their participation would directly inform how AI tools support real-world instruction—positioning their expertise as central to AI design.
To accommodate diverse work styles and schedules, we decided to do flexible compensation options, including task-based (per-dialogue) payment. This flexibility signaled respect for expert labor and contributed to higher engagement and completion rates.

Engaging with AI as Reflective Learning Opportunity

Creating novel and intellectually engaging data collection tools was essential for meaningful expert participation. Rather than assigning routine annotation tasks, we invited experts to engage in authentic, multi-turn instructional dialogues that mirrored real coaching practice. This positioned them as co-designers contributing pedagogical insight, rather than passive data labelers. Participants had full autonomy to skip, revise, or decline prompts they found unproductive, reinforcing respect for their professional judgment. This opt-out flexibility supported sustained engagement and high-quality contributions.

Many human experts described the task as more reflective and rewarding than traditional data collection efforts. The interactive format encouraged deeper professional reflection and engagement. These findings suggest that expert-centered design—grounded in authenticity, flexibility, and intellectual value—is essential for responsible and effective data collection in education and beyond.

Refining Evaluation Criteria (and Usage) Through Expert Collaboration

Unlike the LLM-simulated novice, the initial discrepancies between LLM-as-a-Judge scores and human expert ratings for the expert model revealed critical misalignments. This raised the question of whether the issue lay in the rubric itself or in the LLM’s inability to interpret nuanced pedagogical language. To disentangle these possibilities and ensure the rubric’s validity, we conducted a series of two-hour workshops with six domain experts, organized into three pairs. Each group reviewed sample dialogues alongside the evaluation criteria and engaged in iterative revisions of the rubric. The goal was to better align the criteria with established pedagogical principles and authentic instructional practice. Despite several rounds of refinement, the gap between LLM-generated scores and expert ratings persisted. As a result, we ultimately chose to rely on human annotators for final evaluation, acknowledging the current limitations of LLMs in applying instructional rubrics with the necessary depth and nuance.

Across workshops, experts flagged criteria that appeared objective but lacked instructional nuance. For instance, a relevance item asking whether the expert directly answered the novice’s question was unanimously rejected, as effective teaching often involves redirecting or prompting deeper reflection. All groups independently converged on similar revisions, underscoring consistent pedagogical reasoning. This process highlights the value of LLMs not as final evaluators, but as prompts for expert reflection that strengthened the evaluation framework.

Promises and Challenges of Using LLM for Teaching and Learning

Our results show that dialogue turns and word counts span a wide range. Notably, Fig.
3
illustrates how extroverted and introverted LLM-simulated novice personas influenced expert behavior—even when the dialogues were similar in length and number of turns. This highlights the importance of persona design in creating realistic data and suggests that personality traits can shape how human experts engage with LLMs and the kinds of responses they provide.

Another challenge, illustrated in Fig.
5
and reinforced by expert feedback, is that the LLM-simulated novice sometimes posed overly straightforward instructional challenges—ones that could be resolved with a single suggestion. Experts observed that human novices are more likely to say “no” or push back on suggestions, whereas the LLM consistently agreed, even when they are prompted to decline when appropriate. As a result, coaching the LLM felt easier but less authentic than coaching real novices for human experts. These gaps underscore the need for future LLM systems to better simulate disagreement and dialogic complexity, especially since LLMs are typically optimized to be agreeable responders.

Third, Fig.
6
shows that GPT was less capable of asking meaningful, reflective questions—an essential aspect of effective teaching that supports metacognitive thinking in learners. This raises important concerns about the extent to which AI, especially current GPT, can serve as a good teacher. In addition to limited reflective prompting, we observed other problematic behaviors such as overusing generic praise, adopting a condescending tone, and overwhelming learners with an excessive number of suggestions. These patterns may hinder the learning process.

Last, human-human face-to-face dialogues also often included more turns as shown in Fig.
2
and shorter utterances like “keep going,” which were absent in any dialogues collected in our tool. These brief responses, often tied to non-verbal cues, signaled idea-seeking and created natural pauses. It opens a new discussion on how human-human data can or cannot can be used to train LLM doing similar things as human.

Limitations

For this study, we selected teacher coaching as the example domain, rather than subjects like mathematics. While math-based tasks often emphasize factual correctness, teacher coaching focuses on reasoning an reflection that are more broadly transferable across instructional contexts.
That said, transferring
SimInstruct
to other domains still requires domain-specific adaptation. Persona profiles and challenges must be defined in collaboration with domain experts to ensure realism. Additionally, the domain-specific language—including how challenges are described, what counts as effective feedback, and how success is framed—would need to be re-authored to reflect norms in fields such as law, medicine, or engineering. We intentionally excluded gender and age from persona prompts to avoid introducing potential biases in simulated behavior—an area that merits further research.

Conclusion

SimInstruct
offers a responsible and scalable approach for collecting expert–novice scaffolding dialogues using LLM-simulated personas. Our findings demonstrate that expert engagement, thoughtful persona design, refined evaluation criteria, and expert-in-the-loop dialogue generation can yield pedagogically meaningful data while addressing common privacy and ethical concerns. Fine-tuning LLaMA with this augmented dataset improved its instructional quality over GPT, particularly in reflective prompting—its ability to ask thoughtful questions. However, challenges remain: LLM behaviors such as excessive agreement, user-pleasing tendencies, and limited contextual nuance hinder realistic simulation.

Appendix D: Human Annotator Criterion for Expert Model

Criterion

Guiding Question

3

2

1

Clarity of Expression

Is the expert’s language clear, free of jargon, and concise enough to be quickly understood by instructors with varied teaching experience and English proficiency?

Clear and informative: Well-phrased, avoids jargon, easily understood.

Understandable: Some jargon or assumptions of prior knowledge; could be more concise.

Vague or confusing: Hard to follow, overly technical, or verbose.

Supportive & Appropriate Tone

Does the expert use an encouraging tone that feels appropriate for adult learners, without sounding overly directive or condescending?

Invitational and facilitative: Warm, respectful, and encourages reflection.

Slightly directive: Helpful but somewhat prescriptive or expert-centered.

Dominant, flat, or dismissive: Authoritative, discouraging, or patronizing.

Reflective Prompting

Does the expert encourage the instructor to reflect or reason?

Strong probing: Encourages deep reasoning or exploration.

Mildly reflective: Prompts some reflection but not in-depth.

None or purely directive: No reflection encouraged, just advice or direction.

Appropriateness of Validation

Does the expert appropriately acknowledge and validate the user’s input without overusing generic praise?

Balanced and situational: Validation feels genuine and meaningful.

Mildly generic or frequent: Some feedback feels formulaic or excessive.

Excessive or inauthentic: Overused or insincere validation.
