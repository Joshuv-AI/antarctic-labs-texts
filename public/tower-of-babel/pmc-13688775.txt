Socioeconomic status and demographic factors influencing pedestrian safety behaviors: baseline results from the Persian traffic cohort
Authors: Amirmohammad Azizzadeh, Elham Davtalab Esmaeili, Mina Golestani, Leila Vahedi, Homayoun Sadeghi-bazargani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePedestrian safety remains a vital public health issue, with pedestrians representing a disproportionate share of traffic-related injuries. While socioeconomic status (SES) is a recognized health determinant, its connection to pedestrian behavior has been relatively understudied. This study explores how SES and demographic factors influence pedestrian behaviors in Tabriz, a busy urban area in northwest Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional, population-based study was carried out using data from 1,442 participants in the Persian Traffic Cohort. Participants aged 14 and above from Tabriz’s second municipal district were randomly sampled during summer 2019. The study employed the Pedestrian Behavior Questionnaire and SESIran to evaluate safety behaviors (such as traffic violations, distractions, rule-following, positive actions, and aggression) and socioeconomic status. Linear regression analyses, controlling for potential confounders, identified factors associated with pedestrian behaviors, with statistical processing done using STATA v17.ResultsHigher levels of education consistently correlated with safer pedestrian habits across all behavior categories. Younger individuals were more prone to risky behaviors like frequent traffic violations, distractions, and aggression, while older adults tended to act more safely. Female pedestrians adhered more closely to traffic rules and were less distracted than males. Walking for 60–120 min daily was linked to increased violations and overall riskier behavior compared to walking under 30 min. Greater SES was associated with more positive behaviors and better rule compliance. The association between SES and behaviors appeared to be largely accounted for by variables such as education level, age, and walking duration.ConclusionThe study recommends targeted interventions for groups at higher risk, such as young people and those with lower education, through educational campaigns and infrastructural improvements. Tackling SES-related disparities could enhance pedestrian safety, decrease injuries, and foster fairer road safety practices in urban Iran.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28809-9.Keywords: Traffic Accidents, Pedestrian behavior, Epidemiology, Persian Traffic Cohort

Introduction

Pedestrian safety is a significant global public health concern. The World Health Organization reports that around 1.19 million people die annually due to road traffic collisions, with pedestrians accounting for nearly a quarter (23%) of these fatalities. Although approximately 95% of individuals consider themselves pedestrians at some stage, only about 21% of roads worldwide comply with basic safety standards [1]. In Iran, pedestrian safety is a pressing concern, with road traffic injuries being the third leading cause of death. Pedestrians make up 24% of road traffic injuries and 34% of traffic-related deaths [2]. Due to the nature of pedestrian movement and their lack of physical protection relative to other road users’ higher velocities, pedestrians bear a disproportionate burden of injuries and fatalities, which highlights the need to address their safety and safe behaviors. Road safety fundamentally hinges on the interplay between individual behaviors, infrastructure, and the wider cultural, social, and political context. Pedestrian behavior within the transportation system is a critical element, and understanding these behaviors is vital for enhancing safety interventions. Unlike other road users, pedestrians exhibit higher flexibility and interaction with their environment, often adjusting their trajectory, speed, or direction. They are also more prone to traffic rule violations, with their decision-making influenced by personal behaviors and environmental factors [3]. Currently, research on road user behavior predominantly focuses on drivers, leaving a notable gap in the traffic safety literature regarding pedestrian behavior and its underlying factors. Various methodologies have been employed to assess pedestrian behaviors, with direct observation and self-report questionnaires being among the most widely used. Self-report instruments have become central to measuring pedestrian behaviors at a large scale. A key family of tools is the Pedestrian Behavior Survey (PBS), originally developed by [4]. Further development of this tool took place in the United States, where [5] created and validated the Pedestrian Behavior Questionnaire (PBQ) focused on crosswalk behaviors. This version had five distinct subscales (violations, errors, lapses, aggressive and positive behaviors). Subsequently, a psychometrically sound 20-item short form was also developed [6]. used this 20-item PBQ across six countries and after item reduction, reported a robust 12-item, three-factor structure (violations, lapses, aggressive behaviors). In parallel, and distinct from those international PBS and PBQs [7], developed an Iranian PBQ through consolidating multiple existing tools, de novo item-generation, and reduction process that incorporated expert review, test-retest reliability, and exploratory factor analysis. The final instrument comprised of five subscales of violations, aggressive behaviors, distractions, Adherence to rules, and positive behaviors. Although conceptually aligned with the broader PBQ tradition, it is uniquely validated and widely used in the Iranian context. As mentioned, self-reported instruments have identified key dimensions such as intentional violations, positive safety behaviors, errors stemming from inadequate traffic regulations, lapses primarily caused by distractions, and aggressive behaviors [8]. Errors, defined as failures in planned actions to achieve intended outcomes, encompass unintentional deviations such as slips and lapses. The boundary between errors and violations is blurred, as they can simultaneously occur in a single action sequence. Driver errors and violations are especially critical due to their potential to cause road accidents [9]. Against this background, we used the Iranian PBQ to assess the safety-related behavior among pedestrians. Prior research has identified factors such as personality traits, age, gender, and alcohol consumption as predictors of traffic-related behaviors [5, 10–12].

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a complex, multidimensional construct that encompasses various factors such as income, education, and employment status, which collectively determine an individual’s or group’s position within a societal hierarchy [13]. SES is recognized as a social determinant of health and has been linked to traffic injuries; notably, 92% of road traffic fatalities occur in middle- and low-income countries [1]. Even in high-income countries, residents of poorer areas face higher chances of traffic-related harm or death [14]. SES can influence access to safe walking environments and risk perception and in turn may affect the individual’s travel behavior. Research in public health has operationalized SES using indicators such as occupational class [13], household wealth and asset ownership [15], housing tenure and quality [16], and neighborhood-level measures like deprivation indices [17]. These measures can also include indicators like household health expenditure, which reflect disposable income and financial vulnerability [18]. Subjective measures such as perceived social standing have also been used [19]. Most health studies that consider SES, use a single socioeconomic variable, income or education, measured at a single period and often, with very few response categories [20]. This in turn limits our ability to capture the complexity of socioeconomic position, particularly in middle- and low-income countries with high economic volatility and informal employment. In such settings, multidimensional measures can improve classification and better reflect lived socioeconomic realities. Composite indices, which combine several of these dimensions can provide a broader picture of SES and its relationship to health and behavior. In the present study, we use the SESIran tool [18], which is a composite of six domains of education, occupation, household income, housing value, vehicle value, and healthcare expenditure, to provide a more comprehensive and contextually relevant assessment of socioeconomic status. Furthermore, studies on the relationship between pedestrian behavior and their SES have been limited, with most research focusing on the SES of drivers [21].

There remains a significant research gap regarding comprehensive investigations into how SES interacts with demographic factors to influence pedestrian safety-related behaviors. This study aims to examine the relationship between socioeconomic and demographic characteristics and pedestrian safety behaviors.

Methods

Setting and participants

This cross-sectional study is a part of the baseline results of the Persian Traffic Cohort (PTC), a large population-based cohort study [22] focusing on Tabriz in northwestern Iran. The second municipal district was selected as it reflects many characteristics typical of large Iranian cities such as dense mixed-use development, major amenities such as shopping centers, hospitals, and universities, and a substantial volume of both motorized and pedestrian traffic. Participants were residents of this district for at least six months, over 14 years of age, and provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to participate or withdrawal from the study.

Sampling and data collection

Cluster random sampling was employed during summer 2019. Households and their members within district two were selected from a database maintained by the Tabriz Primary Healthcare Organization. Healthcare centers served as clusters, and households within each were randomly chosen proportionally to their population size. To account for the effects of cluster sampling, the sampling structure was defined using the svyset function in STATA. The minimum sample size for the multiple regression analysis was calculated a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9.6, which indicated a required sample of 363 participants. While 363 was the minimum for power, we utilized the full baseline sample of the Persian Traffic Cohort to maximize precision. A post hoc power analysis confirmed a statistical power of 90% for the regression models after adjusting for sample size and study design.

The data were collected during the baseline data collection phase of the PTC and included demographic information, the Pedestrian Behavior Questionnaire (PBQ) and the Socioeconomic Status Questionnaire (SESIran). Demographic data included gender (dichotomized as male or female), age group (divided into six categories), marital status (single, married, widowed, divorced), Daily Walking Duration (less than 30, 30–60, 60–120, or over 120 min), main method of transportation (personal car, taxi, public transport including bus or metro, motorcycle, bicycle, or walking), level of education (illiterate, primary education, secondary education, diploma, bachelor’s degree, master’s degree, and doctorate or higher). For illiterate participants, trained research assistants read the questionnaire items aloud in a neutral tone and recorded the participants’ verbal responses to ensure data accuracy and minimize comprehension bias. PBQ is a standardized self-reported assessment tool employed to evaluate pedestrians’ behavior related to Traffic Safety [7]. Although some conceptual overlap is present between the international and the Iranian version of PBQ, this questionnaire was prepared and validated for the Iranian context. The questionnaire is scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (most of the time). The total score ranges from 29 to 145, with higher total scores indicating a higher propensity for engaging in risky traffic safety behaviors. This structured questionnaire comprises five subscales, encompassing a total of 29 questions. The first subscale focuses on adherence to traffic rules and consists of seven items. The second subscale addresses pedestrian traffic violations and comprises 10 items. The third subscale assesses positive pedestrian behaviors and includes six items. The fourth subscale measures pedestrians’ distraction and consists of four items. Lastly, the fifth subscale evaluates pedestrians’ aggressive behaviors with two items. The questionnaire achieved a Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.84 in the original validation study and 0.799 in the current sample. SESIran is a reliable and valid tool used for assessing socioeconomic status while accounting for volatility of the Iranian economic system. The ultra-short version of this instrument consists of six questions that cover various aspects [18]. These questions include the level of education, occupation, monthly household income, net worth of the family home, net worth of the family car, and health expenditure ratio. The questionnaire was scored strictly according to the original validation protocol. The six domain scores were weighted and summed to generate a continuous composite score, with higher scores indicating greater socioeconomic advantage. The results are then categorized into five sample-based quintiles of socioeconomic status levels: very low, low, medium, high, and very high. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this questionnaire was reported to be 0.84 and the intra-rater correlation coefficient for all questions was reported to be 0.6 in the validation study. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the SESIran form in this sample was 0.718, which is acceptable for a brief six-item composite instrument.

Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17. Continuous variables were summarized by mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Pedestrian behavior, assessed via PBQ and its subscales, was modeled based on SES and demographic factors using linear regression and models were adjusted for the cluster sampling and study design. Assumptions for linear regression including multicollinearity were verified prior to analysis. Multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) for all multivariate regression models. All individual VIF values were below 5, and the mean VIF across all models remained below 2, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Complete VIF statistics for the main total-score model are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Variables were included in multivariate regression analyses based on prior conceptual works and also those with a p value of less than 0.05 in univariate analysis. Results are reported as regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values, with significance set at 0.05. To examine the moderation effects between variables, we specified interaction models. Predicted marginal means were estimated and plotted with 95% confidence intervals to visualize moderation effects. The study protocol received ethical approval from the internal review board (ID: IR.TBZMED.REC.1401.759) and adhered to STROBE guidelines for observational research.

Results

A total of 1442 participants were included in the final analysis. The demographic characteristics of the study cohort exhibited a balanced distribution of gender, albeit with a slight male predominance. The age brackets of 31–40 and those over 60 were the most prevalent. A considerable segment of the respondents was married (69.83%), and the majority attained at least a basic level of education. 40% of respondents reported having secondary schooling. 50% of the participants indicated engaging in walking activities for more than 30 min daily, while the primary mode of transportation for over half the population was personal automobiles. 4.65% reported occupational driving. Notably, only 17.48% of participants stated walking as their main method of transportation. Participant characteristics are described in detail in Table 1. The majority of households fell within the middle-income brackets, with 45.28% reporting monthly incomes between 8.25 and 17.7 million Iranian Riyals (635 to 1362$ USD in 2019). Most family cars and home values fell within the mid-range of estimated values. 44.25% reported the family car to be worth between 100 and 300 million riyals (7692 to 23076$ USD in 2019) and 46.53% reported their house to be worth between 1360 and 2720 million riyals (104615 to 209230$ USD in 2019). Expenditure on healthcare was noteworthy, with 72.05% of participants reporting the allocation of at least 20% and 28.01% allocating over 40% of household income to health-related costs. The educational levels of households were mainly at the high school tier (40.15%), suggesting a basic level of educational attainment. Despite the aforementioned differences in subscales of SESIran, the overall socioeconomic status presented a balanced distribution. The multivariate linear regression model identified several significant associations related to pedestrian safety behaviors. Detailed information is presented in Table 4. Analysis demonstrated a lower tendency for females to engage in distractions (β = -0.60, P-value = 0.001) and a higher compliance with traffic regulations compared to males (β = -0.74, P-value = 0.014). Moreover, occupational driving correlated with a lower tendency for distracting pedestrian behaviors (β = -0.84, P-value = 0.033). Single individuals (β = 1.22, p-value = 0.032) and the participants engaging in 60–120 min of walking daily (β = 1.72, p-value = 0.007) reported more violations compared to the respective reference group. Respondents using motorcycles as their main method of transportation demonstrated less adherence to pedestrian traffic rules compared to individuals commuting by personal vehicles (β = 2.20, p-value = 0.046). Age emerged as a notable predictor. The 15–20 years age stratum demonstrated higher tendency for distractions (β = 1.18, P-value = 0.015) and fewer positive behaviors (β = 1.23, p-value = 0.025). Individuals 21 to 30 years old reported more distracting behaviors (β = 1.42, p-value < 0.001), aggressiveness (β = 0.40, p-value = 0.008), more traffic violations (β = 1.46, P-value = 0.022), and fewer positive behaviors (β = 1.40, P-value = 0.006) when compared to the reference group of 41–50 years old. Lower distractions while walking was reported by individuals 51 to 60 years old (β = -0.77, P-value = 0.013) and those over 60 years of age (β = -1.35, P-value < 0.001) compared to the reference group. Individuals over 60 years of age also demonstrated less violations (β = -1.62, P-value = 0.007).

Discussion

This study explored how demographic factors and socioeconomic status (SES) influence pedestrian safety-related behaviors. The results indicated that SES primarily predicted adherence to traffic rules and positive behaviors but was less associated with traffic violations, distractions, or aggression. Key demographic predictors included age, gender, education, and walking duration: individuals aged 21–30 engaged in riskier behaviors, whereas those over 60 demonstrated safer practices. Higher education correlated with fewer violations and distractions, and greater rule adherence. Women and occupational drivers exhibited fewer distractions, with women also showing higher compliance with traffic regulations. Overall, SES and education emerged as the strongest determinants of pedestrian behavior.

When compared to prior Iranian research, these findings align with and expand existing evidence. For example, studies have identified significant gender differences, with males reporting more frequent transgressions, lapses, and aggressive behaviors. Age also plays a critical role, as younger pedestrians (under 35 years) exhibit more transgressions and fewer positive behaviors compared to older groups [23]. A 2024 study of elderly pedestrians in Iran found that higher SES and intact cognitive function were associated with safer behavior across PBQ domains [24], while another local investigation documented greater unsafe pedestrian behaviors among lower-SES pedestrians [25]. A nationwide qualitative study identified pedestrian ignorance of traffic rules and low safety literacy as direct contributors to pedestrian collisions in Iran [26].

Our report of reduced distracting behaviors among females supports earlier findings from observational studies that men are more prone to distractions, particularly cellphone use [27]. Observational studies in a similar region to this study have identified cellphone use as the most common distraction [28]. However, conflicting reports have also been published, such as reports of higher cellphone-related distractions among women while walking [29], which may reflect regional or temporal differences. Fewer violations among women and older adult pedestrians have also been observed [27]. While we categorize cellphone use as a distraction, following the convention of the PBQ, it may also be viewed as a deliberate violation of safe walking norms. Additionally, research highlighting lower pedestrian bridge usage (a marker of rule adherence) among less educated and younger individuals aligns with our emphasis on targeting these groups for interventions [30]. Individuals walking 60–120 min daily exhibited more unsafe behaviors. Even after controlling for SES and other variables, this pattern still held true in most socioeconomic categories. One possible explanation is greater exposure; individuals who walk more frequently or for longer durations may encounter a wider range of traffic situations which increases their likelihood and opportunities for risky behaviors [4]. It is also possible that frequent walkers become desensitized to traffic hazards through repeated exposure, normalizing certain violations or distractions.

The findings of this study also align with the global research on pedestrian safety, revealing consistent demographic and behavioral predictors across diverse contexts. Studies consistently link younger age and male gender to riskier behaviors, including traffic violations, distractions, and aggression, as well as fewer positive behaviors [12, 31]. Higher education levels similarly have been found to be associated with safer pedestrian behaviors, more positive behaviors, and fewer violations [31]. A potential explanatory mechanism for the education–behavior relationship is health literacy, particularly traffic or safety literacy. A recent study found that health literacy was positively associated with pedestrian safety behavior, even though education itself was not significantly associated with safety behavior in that sample [32]. This divergence suggests education’s protective effect on pedestrian safety may operate less through formal schooling and more through downstream literacy-related capacities such as interpreting risk information, following safety instructions, or evaluating traffic situations that education typically confers but does not guarantee. Future research should formally test health literacy as a mediator in the relationship between SES, education, and pedestrian behavior using structural equation modeling [33].

SES emerged as one of the strongest standalone predictors of overall pedestrian behaviors in our study. Although univariate analysis indicated a positive correlation where higher SES aligned with safer pedestrian actions, this association lost statistical significance in the multivariate model. This finding suggests that the influence of SES as a multifaceted construct on pedestrian safety might be indirect, potentially mediated by related factors such as educational level. This interpretation aligns with previous research; for instance, income level, a component of SES, has been shown to correlate with pedestrian choices, with higher incomes linked to fewer self-declared traffic transgressions and more positive behaviors [23]. Furthermore, SES has been linked to disparities in pedestrian injury rates. Research has highlighted low-income status and lower education levels as critical factors in pedestrian injury risk, possibly because individuals from lower SES backgrounds may have less awareness of safety issues or experience greater exposure to high-traffic environments as pedestrians [34]. Environmental context is also crucial; inadequate pedestrian infrastructure in lower SES areas might force residents into adopting riskier behaviors due to a lack of safer options [35]. Psychosocial factors could be at play too. The chronic stress associated with poverty has been shown to impact cognitive executive functions [36], which are needed for assessing risk and controlling impulses in traffic situations. Similarly, observations have reported that higher-income pedestrians are more likely to utilize bridges or subways when available compared to more hazardous level crossings [37]. Lastly, it is also possible that constant exposure to hazardous environments may lead to a normalization of risk, or a different calibration of risk perception compared to those in safer surroundings. Furthermore, findings from multinational studies complicate any simple assumptions about SES and pedestrian behavior. Research comparing various countries has not revealed a straightforward relationship between nationality and specific pedestrian behavior subscales [6, 38]. Specifically, the expectation that individuals from nations considered more developed would consistently report safer behaviors was not substantiated by the data. This highlights that socioeconomic status (SES) must be treated as a more intricate index than single indicators might suggest. Limiting the understanding of SES to just one dimension, such as a country’s overall development, individual education level, or personal income, is unlikely to adequately represent the diverse factors influencing pedestrian safety differences. By contrast, the present study employed a multidimensional SES measure, incorporating six domains to capture a broader and more contextually relevant picture of socioeconomic position. Although we analyzed this measure as a categorized composite score and did not explore the predictive role of its individual components separately. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving pedestrian safety need to be carefully tailored, acknowledging this complexity rather than relying on broad demographic categorizations.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s comprehensive approach, utilizing multiple behavioral subscales and a large, diverse sample, enhances the validity and relevance of the findings. However, its cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or social desirability biases. We did not conduct formal mediation analyses to test whether the effects of SES on pedestrian behaviors were explained by education or age, as such analyses require specialized modeling approaches and ideally longitudinal data to establish temporal sequence, which were beyond the scope of this cross-sectional design. Future studies may employ structural equation modeling to more formally test the potential mediating role of variables in the relationship between SES and pedestrian behavior.

The composition of our sample warrants careful interpretation. 17.48% of participants identified walking as their primary transportation mode, indicating that the majority were occasional or casual pedestrians rather than transport-dependent walkers. This distribution is representative of general urban populations in automobile-oriented cities but introduces an exposure consideration that casual pedestrians have fewer opportunities to engage in both safe and unsafe traffic behaviors. This may have potentially compressed the variance in PBQ scores and yielded conservative estimates of risky behavior at the population level. The PBQ instructs respondents to consider all walking episodes, which partially mitigates context specificity. In our multivariate models, primary transportation mode itself was not a significant predictor of PBQ scores, suggesting that the inclusion of casual pedestrians did not systematically bias the total or subscale scores in a direction attributable to walking frequency alone. Daily walking duration, rather than walking as one’s primary mode, appears to be the more relevant exposure variable, as reflected in its independent association with riskier behavior. Nonetheless, the mean scores reported herein reflect a population dominated by low-exposure individuals and may not fully capture the behavioral profile of high-exposure pedestrian commuters. Future studies should incorporate objective exposure metrics such as GPS-derived walking data.

Data were collected prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, and subsequent changes in travel patterns may affect the generalizability of these findings to the current context. The pandemic subsequently produced profound disruptions in urban mobility, including altered pedestrian volumes, shifting transportation mode preferences, and increased pedestrian fatalities in many jurisdictions despite reduced vehicular traffic [39, 40]. In Iranian cities, public transit ridership declined sharply during the pandemic, and walking patterns were affected by mobility restrictions and changing activity spaces [40]. While these shifts may have modified exposure patterns and the absolute frequency of certain behaviors, the fundamental sociodemographic gradients identified in this study, wherein younger age, male gender, lower education, and lower SES predict riskier pedestrian practices, are expected to remain consistent across pre, during, and post pandemic research globally. Nevertheless, we caution that the walking duration and transportation mode findings may be less generalizable to the post-pandemic urban environment, and future research should assess whether these behavioral patterns have shifted. Additionally, environmental factors and cultural norms were not explored, which could significantly influence behaviors. Future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs, incorporate qualitative methods like interviews, and employ emerging technologies such as wearable sensors, connected databases, and machine vision for objective behavior tracking. Focused investigations into SES’s mediating factors, like access to education, infrastructure, and healthcare, are especially pertinent in settings with high income inequality. We used a comprehensive composite score for assessing SES, future studies should also strive to assess the relations between pedestrian behaviors and multiple social and economic position indicators and their interactions. Accordingly, we cannot rule out residual confounding or reciprocal relationships between SES, education, and pedestrian behaviors. For example, unmeasured factors such as personality traits, neighborhood safety, or cultural norms may influence both educational attainment and safety practices. Likewise, while it is plausible that higher education promotes safer pedestrian behaviors, the reverse, where individuals with inherently safer behavioral tendencies are more likely to attain higher education cannot be excluded. Future longitudinal or cohort-based studies are needed to clarify the directionality and causality of these associations.

Finally, because this study was conducted in one urban district of Tabriz, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Iranian settings. Although district 2 was chosen because of its population density, land-use variety, and socio-economic diversity, it certainly cannot reflect all the economic and infrastructural differences across Tabriz. We acknowledge that we do not provide empirical evidence of representativeness across all municipal districts. Peripheral districts of Tabriz may markedly differ in pedestrian behavior patterns and road safety conditions. Large metropolitan areas in Iran share similarities in mixed land use, high pedestrian volumes, and vehicle–pedestrian interactions, making our results broadly transferable to other urban contexts. However, smaller cities and rural areas differ in road infrastructure, traffic density, cultural norms, and enforcement levels, which may influence pedestrian behaviors in distinct ways. Future studies across multiple cities and rural settings with multi-district sampling frames are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study identifies age, education, and daily walking duration as the primary factors associated with pedestrian safety behaviors in urban Iran. While higher socioeconomic status is linked to better rule adherence and positive behaviors, this relationship is significantly explained by educational attainment. Specifically, young adults and those with lower education levels represent high-risk groups requiring targeted safety interventions. Incorporating these insights into policymaking can foster targeted strategies to reduce pedestrian injuries and fatalities, ultimately contributing to safer, more sustainable urban environments. Future research should utilize longitudinal designs to establish temporal sequences and incorporate objective measures, such as wearable sensors or video observation, to validate self-reported data. Additionally, investigating the interplay between individual behaviors and built-environment characteristics remains essential for developing comprehensive urban safety policies.
