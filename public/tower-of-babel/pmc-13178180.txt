First-line sunitinib treatment modification in patients with mRCC: nationwide analysis of the Swedish population
Authors: Maria Jakobsson, Fredrik Nilsson, Angela Strambi, Johannes Arpegård, Johan Dalén
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: Assess first-line sunitinib dosing for treatment of metastatic renal cell carcinoma in Swedish clinical practice (2006–2019).Materials & methods: Retrospective analysis of three sunitinib dosing regimens: 2-weeks on, 1-week off (2:1 Start); standard 4-weeks on, 2-weeks off (4:2) and 4:2 start with switch to 2:1 (2:1 Switch).Results: Time-to-treatment discontinuation (95% CI) differed significantly (p < 0.001): 6.2 (5.6–7.2), 13.9 (8.1–20.6) and 4.6 (4.3–5.6) months for 2:1 Start (n = 320), 2:1 Switch (n = 71) and 4:2 (n = 704), respectively. Overall survival (95% CI) differed significantly (p < 0.001): 21.8 (18.1–26.1), 32.2 (25.1–48.3) and 13.5 (12.3–15.8) months for 2:1 Start (n = 320), 2:1 Switch (n = 71) and 4:2 (n = 704), respectively.Conclusion: Alternative dosing does not compromise clinical efficacy and may provide advantages in terms of improved treatment outcomes. However, due to the changing treatment patterns during this long-term study, and the absence of patient risk category data, caution is required when interpreting the main outcomes.

Introduction

Each year there are an estimated 430,000 new cases of kidney cancer world wide and 180,000 associated deaths [1]. Approximately 90% of kidney cancers are renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [2]. RCC is a heterogenous disease; in the advanced/metastatic stage (mRCC), the appropriate systemic treatment is mainly determined by histologic subtype and the presence of established prognostic risk factors [3–6].

The approval of several new immuno-oncology (IO)-based combinations is revolutionizing the first-line (1L) treatment of mRCC [3–6]. In the context of controlled randomized Phase III trials and in real-world studies, these new regimens have demonstrated improvement in efficacy compared with sunitinib (the standard therapy in the control arm of all registrational studies) [3–8]. However, at the time of writing, the access to IO-based treatments varies in a global context, this is especially true for low- and middle-income countries [9]. As such, sunitinib, as well as pazopanib and other single-agent targeted therapies, remains an important 1L treatment option when an IO-based regimen is not suitable or not available, and as a recommended second-line (2L) option after failure of an IO-based combination [3,4]. Additionally, IO-based combinations may improve progression-free survival (PFS) but may have limited benefit on overall survival (OS) in patients with favorable risk based on criteria from the International mRCC Database Consortium. As such, single-agent VEGFR-TKI remains a reasonable option for these patients [10]. Optimizing treatment with these agents is therefore still of considerable importance in ensuring that all patients are provided with the best possible management of their systemic therapy.

Sunitinib, an oral receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, exerts an anti-angiogenic effect through inhibition of VEGFRs and PDGF [11]. Sunitinib was approved in Europe in July 2006 (and included in the Swedish reimbursement system in November 2006) at a recommended dose of 50 mg once daily on a 4-weeks on, 2-weeks off (4:2) dosing schedule. However, several studies, both real-world and clinical trials, suggest that alternative dosing schedules and/or modifications do not compromise effectiveness and may help manage treatment-related toxicity [12–20]. These studies underscore the heterogenous nature of mRCC and the potential benefits of individualized treatment regimens.

The study described herein provides a comprehensive nationwide real-world analysis of all patients with mRCC treated with 1L sunitinib in Sweden, with a focus on alternative sunitinib dosing schedules. To our knowledge, there are no similar studies available in a Swedish population, nor studies that comprehensively capture data on a nationwide level. This analysis was possible due to the existing national healthcare registries in Sweden, including the Swedish Cancer Register [21], and complements the previous RENal COMParison (RENCOMP) analyses [12,22,23]. As almost all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) utilize the approved 4:2 sunitinib dosing schedule, the use of real-world evidence to complement and expand on the data provided by RCTs is important for optimizing clinical outcomes in the mRCC patient population [24].

Materials & methods

Data sources

This was a retrospective analysis utilizing the population-based Swedish health data registers, run by the National Board of Health and Welfare (NBHW). Individual patient data were extracted from the Swedish National Patient Register, National Prescribed Drug Register (NPDR), Swedish Causes of Death Register and Swedish Cancer Register. Individual patient-level data were linked between all data sets via the personal identifier given to all citizens across the entire Swedish population. Sunitinib dosing schedules (initial and changes) were reviewed manually and then merged with the original datasets. All data were anonymized prior to delivery from the NBHW. The study was approved by the Swedish ethical review authority (2020-00434).

Patients & study design

Eligible participants were patients with ≥1 filled prescription of relevant mRCC treatment between January 2006 and December 2019, aged ≥18 years, diagnosed with RCC and with ≥1 filled prescription of sunitinib for 1L treatment. Figure 1 summarizes how the patient population was defined. Metastatic status was based on the assumption that sunitinib is indicated in metastatic disease and prescribed accordingly. The 1L sunitinib treatment group was identified from the full mRCC cohort described above; patients who had sunitinib as the first filled prescription (the earliest date) were selected from the cohort of patients with prescriptions of the relevant drugs: sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, axitinib, everolimus, cabozantinib, tivozanib, lenvatinib and cytokines medication. As cytokines were a common treatment in patients with mRCC in the beginning of the investigated period, this group was needed to identify the correct treatment line.

Three sunitinib dosing schedules were analyzed: regimens starting on a 2:1 dosing schedule with no modifications (2:1 Start); regimens starting on a 4:2 dosing schedule with a switch to 2:1 (2:1 Switch) and regimens starting on a 4:2 dosing schedule with no succeeding modifications (4:2). Dosing regimens were determined based on instructions to the patient provided by the prescriber available in the NPDR and reviewed manually. If no instructions were provided at the start of treatment, a 4:2 dosing scheme was assumed. Patients were included in the 2:1 Switch group if the dosing instructions clearly indicated a switch from 4:2 to 2:1. Other dosing schedules were not included in this analysis.

Outcomes & statistical analysis

The primary outcomes of this analysis were time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) and OS. TTD was defined in accordance with the terminology and definitions suggested by the Medication Compliance Work Group at the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research, as the time elapsed from treatment initiation (the date of the first filled prescription) to TTD [25]. Allowing for accumulation of medicine and a grace period of 90 days between filled prescriptions (Figure 2), the number of days covered was determined based on information in the summary of product characteristics [26], the volume of dispensed drug, and by using the prescriber’s dosing instruction. If no dosing instructions were available, a defined daily dose and a 4:2 dosing schedule was assumed. TTD was defined as the end of the prescribed supply; or a switch to another treatment, or death, if either of the two occurred before end of supply. Initiation of other treatments was defined as a subsequent filled prescription of either sorafenib, pazopanib, axitinib, everolimus, cabozantinib, tivozanib or lenvatinib and did not include combination treatment. As the NPDR does not cover treatment administered in a hospital setting, potential IO treatment with nivolumab subsequent to sunitinib could not be accounted for. OS was defined as the time from the date of the first dispensed prescription to the date of death; death was defined as all-cause death. The potential impact on OS of subsequent lines of therapy was not considered in this analysis.

Accumulation of medicine and identifying treatment discontinuation. (A) Discontinuation is identified prior to the gap in filled prescriptions, as the gap exceeds 90 days. (B) Discontinuaiton is identified following the gap in filled prescriptions, as the gap was shorter than 90 days.

* Discontinuation date was also defined as when a new filled prescription of another relevant oral targeted therapy or death occurred. If no end of prescribed supply was recorded, data were censored at the latest available date.

TTD and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with right censoring at the end of data availability (31 December 2019). Estimation of PFS was not possible due to the nature of the available data. Previous studies have indicated a patient-level association between TTD and PFS, thus supporting the use of TTD as a pragmatic real-world evidence end point when PFS is not available [27,28]. However, it should be noted that there may be a discrepancy between TTD and PFS as patients frequently are maintained on treatment beyond objective RECIST progression both in clinical practice and in randomized clinical trials as this may lead to a prolonged time to clinical progression [29,30]. Moreover, TTD can underestimate PFS when discontinuation occurs for safety, tolerability or other issues different from disease progression [31]. Safety was not analyzed in this study due to the characteristics of the available data.

There are no data available on the clinical characteristics/prognostic factors or any other rationale for choice of dosing schedule. Hence, it may be argued that 2:1 Switch patients should be considered as either 2:1 Start patients or 4:2 patients rather than a standalone cohort. Additionally, both 2:1 Start and 2:1 Switch was more common in later years when several additional post-progression treatments became available. Therefore, to assess the sensitivity around dosing schedule and treatment period, four complementary analyses for OS were carried out: 2:1 start and switch (2:1 All) versus 4:2 for the full follow-up period; 2:1 Start vs 4:2 (2:1 switchers included) for the full follow-up period; 2:1 start and switch (2:1 All) versus 4:2 from 2016 and onwards; 2:1 start versus 4:2 (switchers included) from 2016 and onwards.

Results

Patients

Overall, 1095 patients with mRCC were identified as initiating 1L sunitinib: 320 started on the 2:1 schedule, 71 in the 2:1 Switch group and 704 on the 4:2 schedule. Available baseline characteristics, age at RCC diagnosis, gender and age at treatment initiation were deemed similar between subgroups (Table 1).

Outcomes

TTD (95% confidence interval [CI]) was significantly different between subgroups (p < 0.001): median 6.2 (5.6–7.2), 13.9 (8.1–20.6) and 4.6 (4.3–5.6) months for the 2:1 Start, 2:1 Switch, and 4:2 dosing groups, respectively (Figure 3). OS (95% CI) was also significantly different between subgroups (p < 0.001): median 21.8 (18.1–26.1), 32.2 (25.1–48.3) and 13.5 (12.3–15.8) months for the 2:1 Start, 2:1 Switch, and 4:2 dosing groups, respectively (Figure 4).

Discussion

This study provides a nationwide, real-world analysis of all patients with mRCC treated with 1L sunitinib in Sweden from 2006 to 2019, focusing on alternative sunitinib dosing schedules. The results from the analysis show that modified 1L sunitinib schedules (2:1 Switch and 2:1 Start) were associated with prolonged TTD and OS.

We also report a change in how 1L sunitinib was initiated in Sweden. From 2006 to 2013, the majority of patients initiated sunitinib on the standard 4:2 dosing schedule; however, from 2014, the proportion of patients who initiated sunitinib on 2:1 dosing increased substantially. By 2017, approximately two thirds of patients in Sweden initiated sunitinib on the 2:1 dosing schedule.

The greatest benefit in OS and TTD was observed in patients who had 2:1 Switch dosing; however, it should be noted that data from patients with 2:1 Start dosing were comparatively immature and the number of observations were few.

Complementary analyses assessing the sensitivity around different definitions of treatment groups and time periods were carried out and the results support that alternative dosing schedules have a favorable impact on OS compared with a 4:2 schedule. While the benefit of the 2:1 Start dosing schedule compared with the 4:2 schedule is confirmed in both sensitivity analyses (Figure 5), the advantage of the 2:1 Switch observed in our original analysis remains of interest. Indeed, even if interpretation requires caution due to the limitations highlighted, the greater clinical benefit observed in the 2:1 Switch dosing group has also been observed in other retrospective real-world studies [14,20,32].

As increased sunitinib exposure has been associated with improved outcomes [33,34], the initial prolonged exposure to sunitinib during 4:2 dosing may partly account for the improved effectiveness. This approach may ensure that only the occurrence of unmanageable toxicity triggers the switch, maximizing exposure initially and then prolonging time on treatment, thanks to the higher tolerability of the 2:1 schedule [33]. Alternative dosing schedules have indeed been extensively investigated with the aim of prolonging the duration of treatment for a greater proportion of patients. This represents a particularly pressing need as patients in real-world clinical practice are more likely to discontinue sunitinib early due to treatment-related adverse events (AEs) compared with patients in RCTs [35].

Our results are generally consistent with those from other studies, including both real-world studies and clinical trials [12–20]. An analysis of 297 patients from the German registry STAR-TOR reported that sunitinib dose modification reduced the frequency of AEs, prolonged treatment duration, and significantly increased median progression-free survival (PFS; 15.1 vs 6.0 months) and OS (38.1 vs 13.7 months) versus standard 4:2 dosing [13]. A meta-analysis that aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of the 4:2 and 2:1 dosing schedules, found that the 2:1 schedule was associated with longer PFS, higher disease control rate, fewer dosage interruptions and specific severe AEs, but similar OS and objective response rate [17].

A multicenter, randomized, open-label, Phase II trial (RESTORE) compared the efficacy and safety of 4:2 sunitinib dosing with that of the 2:1 dosing schedule [18]. The 2:1 dosing schedule was associated with a higher 6-month failure-free survival rate compared with the 4:2 schedule (44 vs 63%) and certain AEs were observed at a lower frequency with 2:1 than 4:2 dosing [18]. Consistently, our analysis found that both OS and TTD were improved with the 2:1 schedule compared with the 4:2 schedule. Another Phase II trial investigated individualized sunitinib dosing utilizing toxicity-driven changes to dosing and dose schedule as opposed to rigid scheduled dosing [36]. This study found individualized sunitinib dosing to be feasible and safe, with potential improvements to treatment outcomes [36]. Overall, our analysis provides further support for the use of modified or individualized sunitinib dosing to help optimize clinical outcomes.

The main strength of this study is the capture of real-world clinical data for the entire nationwide Swedish population treated with sunitinib in first line. Patients seen in everyday practice represent a more heterogenous population, whereas clinical trials often under-represent patients with comorbidities, ethnic minorities and elderly patients [37,38]. The use of real-world data to support clinical decisions for those patients not wholly represented by RCTs is therefore of great value. Several analyses of real-world data demonstrate that well-designed real-world studies can provide a valuable complement to data from RCTs to support clinical decisions [24,39].

The limitations in this study include those common to real-world retrospective studies, including unknown treatment compliance, potential treatment selection/channeling bias and absence of information regarding AEs. The reason for dose schedule modification (e.g. information on potential safety issues) was not available and may have contributed to differences between dosing schedule outcomes, although the sensitivity analyses carried out supported the results. As mRCC is a heterogenous disease with clinical outcomes linked to the presence of risk factors [40,41], it is possible that other factors may also have contributed to differences between dosing schedule outcomes. Specific to this analysis, mRCC risk group stratification according to either Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center or International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium prognostic factors [41] were not recorded in registers and may potentially impact data on OS and TTD. Furthermore, a complete profile of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics, including tumor histology, was not available. The NPDR does not include drugs that are administered at the hospital. Therefore, for some patients moving on to nivolumab after sunitinib, there is a possibility that TTD was overestimated if treatment with nivolumab was initiated before the end of supply of sunitinib. This possibility is considered small since usually one packet of sunitinib is dispensed at a time. Also, nivolumab became available as a 2L option in Sweden toward the end of the study period (April 2016). It is possible that bias in the estimation of OS may have been introduced over time as more 2L therapies became available. In addition, we observed a greater proportion of patients initiating the 2:1 schedule later in the observation period as doctors gained more experience in this regimen and additional data on alternative dosing schedules were published. Sensitivity analyses were carried out based on data starting from 2016 and onwards with results in line with the main results presented here. Some patients included in this analysis may potentially have been misclassified; patients who may have started treatment in a hospital setting, where treatment is not reported in the NPDR, and later moved on to prescription could be captured as belonging to the 2:1 Start group whereas, in fact, they belong to the 2:1 Switch group. Further, considering the inclusion criteria, this implies that patients who received treatment exclusively in a hospital setting may not have been included in study. However, the impact on the results from potential misclassification of patients and/or not including all patients treated with sunitinib is deemed nominal. It was also not possible to control for potential regional differences in treatment preferences/treatment patterns. Finally, the recent introduction of new, predominantly IO-based, mRCC treatments may reduce the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, given the continued use of sunitinib in clinical practice, these findings may still be important, in complementing previous research and further informing treatment decisions in mRCC.

Conclusion

This study provides real-world clinical data representative of all patients treated with 1L sunitinib in a nationwide Swedish cohort. In Swedish clinical practice, alternative 1L sunitinib dosing regimens may be associated with prolonged treatment duration and OS compared with standard 4:2 dosing. The greatest clinical benefit was observed with the 2:1 Switch group. Over time, there was an increase in the proportion of patients in Sweden receiving alternative sunitinib dosing regimens. These results are in line with previous research and provide further support that the use of alternative dosing does not compromise clinical efficacy and may provide advantages in terms of improved treatment outcomes. However, due to the changing treatment patterns during this long-term, real-world observational study and the absence of patient risk category data, caution is required when interpreting the main outcomes.
