Grain-Aware Data Transformations: Type-Level Formal Verification at Zero Computational Cost
Nikos Karayannidis

Abstract
. Data transformation correctness is a fundamental challenge in data engineering: how can we systematically verify that pipelines produce correct results before executing on production data? Traditional approaches rely on expensive iterative testing, requiring data materialization and manual reconciliation to discover errors. The root cause is the absence of formal methods for reasoning about grain —the level of detail or granularity of data—causing transformations to inadvertently change granularity in unexpected ways, producing incorrect results that include well-known pathologies like fan traps (metrics duplication) and chasm traps (data loss). We provide the first formal, mathematical definition of grain —previously existing only as an informal concept in dimensional modeling ( Kimball 1996 ) . We generalize grain from fact table design to a universal type-theoretic framework applicable to any data type , enabling systematic correctness verification across all transformation paradigms. By encoding grain into the type system, our framework enables compile-time verification of transformation correctness, shifting validation from runtime to design time. We establish three fundamental grain relations (equality, ordering, incomparability) and prove a general grain inference theorem for systematically computing the grain of equi-join results from input grains using only type-level operations, covering all cases (comparable and incomparable join key portions). Combined with inference rules for the complete set of relational operations, this enables zero-cost verification through schema analysis alone. This enables engineers to verify that entire pipeline DAGs preserve correctness properties, detecting all grain-related errors—including fan traps, chasm traps, and aggregation inconsistencies—before processing any data, while allowing them to focus on grain—the essential characteristic that matters —rather than tracking all data details during transformations. We provide machine-checkable formal proofs in Lean 4 and demonstrate 98-99% cost reduction ($8-$50 per pipeline vs. $415-$2,750 per bug). Critically, large language models can automatically generate correctness proofs, shifting humans from proof writing to proof verification—democratizing formal methods for data engineers and enabling confident deployment of AI-generated pipelines with machine-checkable guarantees.



1.
Introduction

Consider a data engineer building a sales report showing total sales count and amount per customer and date. The data comes from two fact tables at different grains:
SalesChannel
(one row per customer-channel-date) and
SalesProduct
(one row per customer-product-date). The natural implementation joins on common dimensions, then aggregates:

SELECT sc.customer_id, sc.date,
SUM(sc.sales_count) + SUM(sp.sales_count) AS total_sales
FROM sales_channel sc
JOIN sales_product sp
ON sc.customer_id = sp.customer_id AND sc.date = sp.date
GROUP BY sc.customer_id, sc.date

This query executes successfully and produces the expected result grain (customer
\times
date). Yet it
systematically doubles all sales figures
—a fan trap that could inflate financial reports by 200% and cost tens of thousands of dollars in incident response at enterprise scale. The bug is invisible to schema validation, type checking, and unit tests with small datasets. Traditional detection requires running the query on production-scale data, discovering inflated totals through reconciliation with source systems, debugging the transformation logic, and iterating—a process costing $415–$2,750 per bug in compute and engineering time (Section
6.1.2
). The root cause? A
grain inconsistency
: joining at customer-date level creates a Cartesian product over channels and products (result grain: customer
\times
channel
\times
product
\times
date), causing rows from
both
inputs to be duplicated before aggregation. Despite the final grain matching requirements, the intermediate grain violates correctness.

1.1.
Motivation: Grain as the Root Cause of Pipeline Bugs

Grain-related bugs—including fan traps (metric duplication from one-to-many joins), chasm traps (data loss from incomplete join paths), and incorrect joins or aggregations due to wrong granularity assumptions—plague data pipelines across industries. These bugs arise because data engineers lack formal tools to reason about
grain
: the level of detail or granularity of the data. When transformations inadvertently change grain in unexpected ways, results become incorrect, often without obvious symptoms.
The concept of grain has existed for decades in dimensional modeling, particularly in Ralph Kimball’s work defining fact table grain
(
Kimball 1996
)
. However, Kimball’s grain is
informal
(descriptive business definitions),
narrow
(limited to fact tables in star schemas), and
non-systematic
(provides no inference rules for how transformations change grain). Consequently, engineers rely on ad-hoc testing and post-execution debugging to catch grain errors—expensive, reactive, and error-prone approaches that don’t scale to complex pipelines.
This problem is increasingly critical in the AI era. Large language models now generate data pipelines at scale, requiring
machine-checkable correctness guarantees
before deployment. Manual review of AI-generated code is impractical; we need formal verification methods that can prove correctness automatically. Moreover, the barrier to formal verification has historically been too high (requiring expertise in proof assistants like Coq or Lean), limiting adoption to academic settings. The convergence of AI-generated pipelines and AI-generated proofs creates an opportunity to
democratize formal verification
for data engineers.

1.2.
Problem Statement

We address the following problem:
How can we systematically verify the correctness of arbitrary data transformations and entire pipeline DAGs before executing any queries, using only type-level schema analysis?

This problem is challenging because it requires:

(1)

Formal grain definition
: A mathematically rigorous definition of grain applicable to
any data type
, enabling type-level reasoning about granularity for any transformation operation.

(2)

Universal grain inference
: Systematic methods to compute the grain of transformation results (joins, aggregations, filters, windows, unions, etc.) from input grains, covering the complete space of data transformations.

(3)

Correctness verification formulas
: Grain relations and ordering that enable verification of whether a transformation preserves required correctness properties, detecting all classes of grain-related errors.

(4)

Zero-cost verification
: Detection at compile/design time through type-level schema analysis alone, avoiding expensive iterative data processing.

(5)

Machine-checkable proofs
: Formal correctness guarantees that can be verified by proof assistants, enabling AI-generated proofs for AI-generated pipelines.

Existing approaches fall short: Kimball’s grain lacks formal foundations; functional dependencies don’t provide grain inference rules; type theory is too abstract for practical use; and ad-hoc testing is reactive rather than preventive. No existing work provides a formal framework for verifying transformation correctness through type-level grain reasoning.

1.3.
Our Solution: Type-Level Grain Theory

We introduce
grain theory
, the first formal, mathematical framework for reasoning about data granularity and transformation correctness. By encoding grain into the type system, we enable compile-time verification of whether any transformation or pipeline DAG produces correct results, shifting verification from runtime to design time. Our grain inference theorem systematically computes result grains for any transformation through zero-cost schema analysis. Combined with grain relations (equality, ordering, incomparability), this enables verification of arbitrary correctness properties—automatically detecting all classes of grain-related errors including fan traps (metrics duplication), chasm traps (data loss from incomplete join paths), incorrect joins or aggregations (wrong granularity assumptions), and grain mismatches in complex pipeline DAGs—before processing any data.

2.
Related Work

This section reviews related work in areas relevant to our grain theory framework. We examine dimensional modeling approaches to grain, join pathology detection, functional dependency theory, type-theoretic foundations, formal verification methods, categorical databases, and modern data engineering tools, highlighting how our work extends and unifies these concepts.

2.1.
Dimensional Modeling and Grain

The concept of grain has its roots in dimensional modeling, particularly in Ralph Kimball’s work on data warehousing
(
Kimball 1996
)
. In Kimball’s framework, the grain of a fact table is defined as “the finest level of detail represented by the data in that table,” determining what a single row represents.
While Kimball’s grain concept provides valuable intuition, it has several limitations for general data pipeline design:

•

Limited scope
: Focused exclusively on fact tables in dimensional models, not applicable to general data types or transformations.

•

Descriptive rather than formal
: Provides business-oriented descriptions rather than mathematical definitions.

•

No inference rules
: Does not provide systematic methods for determining how transformations change grain.

•

Table-level focus
: Defined at the table (collection) level rather than the type level, limiting its applicability to pipeline design.

Our work extends Kimball’s grain concept to a formal, type-theoretic framework applicable to any data type and transformation operation.

2.2.
Data Transformation Pathology Detection

A fundamental challenge in data pipelines is that engineers lose track of data grain after a series of transformations (joins, aggregations, filters, etc.), causing grain mismatches with target tables and incorrect results (double counts, duplicates, missing rows). The dimensional modeling literature identifies well-known examples of this problem:
fan traps
occur when joining tables with one-to-many relationships creates unintended Cartesian products, duplicating metrics during aggregation
(
Kimball Group 2024
;
dbt Labs 2024
;
sho 2024
)
—one of the most common and costly bugs in data warehouse queries, as it silently produces incorrect aggregate results that may pass basic validation checks.
Chasm traps
arise in normalized dimension hierarchies (snowflake schemas) when INNER JOINs along chains with nullable foreign keys cause data loss by eliminating rows where foreign keys are NULL
(
Sisense 2024
;
ThoughtSpot 2024
;
dat 2024
;
dat 2019
)
.
Traditional detection methods include:

•

Manual ER diagram inspection
: Requires expertise in recognizing pathological patterns in entity-relationship models, prone to human error in complex schemas.

•

Runtime testing with production data
: Expensive iterative execution ($415–$2,750 per bug) requiring reconciliation with source systems to discover discrepancies.

•

Heuristic linting in BI tools
: Modern BI platforms (Sisense, ThoughtSpot) provide warnings for potential traps but lack formal guarantees and mathematical foundations.

Gap
: No existing work provides
type-level, pre-execution detection
through systematic grain inference for arbitrary transformations. Our work fundamentally changes this paradigm: we enable systematic grain tracking across all transformation operations, detecting fan traps via grain ordering (
G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{i}]
indicates row duplication), chasm traps through partial grain functions in join chains, and any grain mismatch—all at design time through zero-cost schema analysis before any data processing. This enables a shift from reactive debugging to proactive verification.

2.3.
Functional Dependencies and Normalization

Functional dependencies (FDs) and Armstrong’s axioms
(
Armstrong 1974
)
provide a formal foundation for reasoning about attribute relationships in relational databases. The connection between FDs and grain ordering is significant: when
G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}]
, there exists a functional dependency from the finer grain to the coarser grain.
However, FDs alone are insufficient for our purposes:

•

No grain inference
: FDs describe relationships but do not provide formulas for computing result grain in transformations.

•

Attribute-level focus
: FDs operate at the attribute level, while grain theory operates at the type level, providing a higher-level abstraction.

•

Limited to relational model
: FDs are primarily concerned with normalization in relational databases, while grain theory applies to any data type.

Our grain ordering relation generalizes functional dependencies to the type level, enabling systematic reasoning about grain relationships.

2.4.
Type Theory and Formal Methods

Type theory, particularly dependent types
(
Martin-Löf 1984
)
and category theory
(
Mac Lane 1998
)
, provides the mathematical foundations for our approach. Category-theoretic approaches to databases
(
Spivak 2012
;
Rosebrugh and Wood 2000
)
model schemas and transformations using categories, but focus on schema evolution and query composition rather than grain reasoning.

Phantom types

(
Leijen and Meijer 1999
)
and
GADTs (Generalized Algebraic Data Types)

(
Peyton Jones et al. 2006
)
enable encoding type-level properties without runtime overhead, successfully applied in domains like dimensional analysis
(
Kennedy 1994
)
and security typing
(
Pottier and Simonet 2002
)
. Our grain encoding (Section
6.1.4
) follows this pattern: grain is a type parameter that enables compile-time verification with zero runtime cost. Type systems with phantom types (Haskell, Scala) or generic types (Python, TypeScript) can encode grain as
DataFrame[G]
or
GrainedDataFrame[G]
, enabling the type checker to catch grain mismatches at compile time.

Dependent types

(
Brady 2013
;
de Moura and Ullrich 2021
)
allow types to depend on values, enabling rich specifications with machine-checkable proofs. Our grain relation
\texttt{IsGrainOf}\ G\ R
carries a proof of isomorphism, similar to dependent records in Lean 4
(
de Moura and Ullrich 2021
)
and Agda
(
Team 2024
)
. Dependent type systems enable three complementary encoding patterns: (1) parameterized types (
\texttt{DataFrame}\ Grain
) where grain is a type parameter, (2) type classes that enable automatic grain inference via instance resolution, and (3) dependent records (
DataWithGrain
) that bundle data with its grain proof.
However, existing type-theoretic database work
(
Cheney et al. 2013
)
focuses on schema evolution and type safety, not grain-level reasoning. Related work in database theory has explored type-theoretic approaches to data modeling, but these have been primarily concerned with schema compatibility rather than granularity inference.

Gap
: While type theory provides the foundations, no existing work formalizes
grain
as a first-class type-level concept with systematic inference rules for data transformations. Our categorical interpretation (grain as canonical minimal representative of isomorphism class
[R]=\{T:T\cong R\}
) provides universal applicability to any data type—not just relational tables but also graphs, documents, streams, and arbitrary algebraic data types—while remaining practically useful for data engineering.

2.5.
Formal Verification in Data Engineering

Existing approaches to verification in data engineering fall into several categories, each addressing different aspects but none providing type-level grain inference:

Query Equivalence Checking.
Work on proving SQL query equivalence focuses on semantic equivalence of entire queries
(
Chaudhuri and Vardi 2014
)
. Approaches to query explainability
(
Chu et al. 2021
)
provide fine-grained analysis of query results but do not address grain reasoning or transformation correctness at intermediate pipeline stages. Query optimization work
(
Neumann and Radke 2020
)
focuses on performance improvements for large join queries but operates at the query level rather than providing grain inference for individual transformations.

Constraint-Based Verification.
Approaches using functional dependencies and integrity constraints verify data quality
(
Fan et al. 2009
;
Chu et al. 2013
)
but operate reactively on actual data, requiring expensive execution rather than type-level analysis. Data profiling methods
(
Abedjan et al. 2017
)
detect constraint violations statistically but cannot provide guarantees before data processing.

Static Analysis Tools.
SQL linters and data quality frameworks
(
Heinrich et al. 2018
)
provide rule-based checks but lack formal mathematical foundations for systematic grain reasoning and, more fundamentally, misclassify grain mismatches as data quality issues rather than data transformation code bugs. Type-safe database systems
(
Cheney et al. 2013
)
ensure schema compatibility but do not address grain inference or transformation correctness.

AI-Assisted Formal Verification.
Recent work explores using large language models to generate formal proofs
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
First et al. 2023
;
Azerbayev et al. 2023
)
, demonstrating that LLMs can assist with theorem proving in systems like Lean 4, reducing barriers to formal verification adoption. However, these approaches focus on general mathematics and software verification, not data pipeline correctness. Similarly, program synthesis work
(
Solar-Lezama 2020
;
Polikarpova et al. 2016
)
generates code from specifications but doesn’t address grain-specific correctness properties or provide domain-specific formal semantics for data transformations.

Gap.
Traditional approaches treat grain inconsistencies reactively: either through costly data tests during development ($415–$2,750 per bug) or data quality checks on production data. Both are reactive and expensive. No existing work provides
type-level grain inference
that reveals grain mismatches as transformation code bugs at
design time
through the type system, before any testing or data execution. Our grain theory framework fills this gap by encoding grain semantics into types, enabling compile-time detection of grain inconsistencies with
machine-checkable formal proofs
. Domain-specific formal semantics enable LLMs to automatically generate correctness proofs from grain inference specifications. This democratizes formal verification: data engineers shift from
writing proofs
(high barrier, requires theorem-proving expertise) to
verifying proofs
(low barrier, simply confirming that AI-generated proofs type-check and correspond to intended pipeline semantics). The combination of AI-generated pipelines and AI-generated grain proofs creates a new paradigm for confident deployment of data transformations at machine speed.

2.6.
Categorical Databases and Algebraic Data Integration

The categorical database literature
(
Spivak 2012
;
Schultz and Wisnesky 2017
;
Schultz et al. 2023
;
Daimler and Wisnesky 2020
)
provides rigorous foundations for data integration using category theory. Schemas are defined as categories (or algebraic theories), instances as functors (or their initial algebras), and data migrations as adjoint functors (
\Sigma
,
\Delta
,
\Pi
). This framework enables compile-time correctness guarantees through type-theoretic constructions. Our work shares this categorical foundation: grain is defined as a universal property (canonical representative of an isomorphism class) applicable to any data type. However, because categorical databases address
arbitrary
integrity constraints through equational logic, they face fundamental computability challenges—the mathematical objects are “almost always infinite and not computable”
(
Schultz and Wisnesky 2017
)
, requiring specialized algorithms (e.g., Knuth-Bendix completion) to decide equality in equational theories. In contrast, grain-correctness is
always decidable
: grain inference reduces to simple set operations (union, intersection, projection on key sets), and grain mismatch manifests as a type error in any strongly-typed compiler. This computational simplicity makes grain theory practical for day-to-day data engineering, complementing categorical databases’ more general (but computationally expensive) framework.

2.8.
Data Integration and Schema Matching

The generalized grain inference theorem (Theorem
5.6
) connects to several areas of data integration research, particularly schema matching and data exchange.

Schema Matching.
Schema matching identifies correspondences between attributes in different schemas
(
Rahm and Bernstein 2001
;
Shvaiko and Euzenat 2013
)
. The isomorphism
\phi:J_{k1}\cong J_{k2}
in our generalized join theorem corresponds to the “exact match” case of schema matching, where two attributes are semantically equivalent and have a bijective mapping. Schema matching algorithms
(
Doan et al. 2003
;
Madhavan et al. 2001
)
typically produce similarity scores or probabilistic mappings, while our framework requires explicit isomorphism witnesses for type-level grain inference. The categorical perspective of grain theory—working with types up to isomorphism—aligns naturally with schema matching’s goal of identifying semantically equivalent structures across heterogeneous schemas.

Data Exchange.
Data exchange
(
Fagin et al. 2005
;
Libkin 2004
)
studies the problem of transforming data from a source schema to a target schema according to schema mappings. Grain-preserving mappings are a special case of data exchange settings where the transformation preserves the granularity semantics. Our generalized join theorem enables grain inference when joining data across schema boundaries, which is a common scenario in data exchange. However, data exchange focuses on instance-level transformations and query answering, while our work provides type-level grain inference formulas that enable correctness verification before data processing.

Entity Resolution.
Entity resolution (also known as record linkage or deduplication) identifies records that refer to the same real-world entity across different data sources
(
Elmagarmid et al. 2007
;
Christen 2012
)
. The isomorphism between join keys in our generalized theorem captures identity correspondences: when
J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
via
\phi
, the isomorphism represents a bijective mapping between entity identifiers in different systems. Entity resolution typically operates at the instance level using similarity functions, while our framework requires type-level isomorphism witnesses. The grain inference formulas ensure that joins across resolved entities preserve correct granularity semantics.

Connection to Our Work.
The generalized grain inference theorem demonstrates that grain theory’s categorical foundations—working with types up to isomorphism—naturally enable correct grain reasoning across schema heterogeneity, system integration boundaries, and schema evolution. This aligns with data integration’s core challenges while providing a formal, type-level framework for grain-aware transformation correctness that complements existing instance-level approaches.

2.9.
Positioning of Our Work

Grain theory differs fundamentally from prior work in scope and approach. While previous research addresses specific classes of data quality issues (schema matching, constraint validation, query optimization, trap detection), grain theory provides a
universal mathematical foundation
for verifying correctness of
any
data transformation through type-level grain reasoning. This allows us to verify data pipeline correctness during the design phase, before any data processing occurs. The grain inference theorem and grain relations enable systematic verification across the complete space of transformation operations—joins, aggregations, filters, windows, unions, and arbitrary compositions—not merely detection of known pathologies. By encoding grain into the type system, we shift verification from reactive data-level testing (existing approaches) to proactive type-level analysis, enabling zero-cost correctness guarantees (no data processing). The categorical interpretation (grain as canonical representative of isomorphism class) ensures applicability to any data type, from relational tables to graphs, streams, and arbitrary algebraic types. This universality, combined with machine-checkable formal verification in Lean 4 and AI-assisted proof generation, positions grain theory as a foundational framework for data engineering rather than a specialized tool for specific bug classes.

3.
Grain Theory Foundations

This section outlines the formal foundations of grain theory. We define grain as a fundamental relation between data types, establishing the mathematical framework that supports our correctness verification approach.

3.1.
Preliminaries

We work in a type-theoretic setting where data types
T
are sets (
T:Set
), and values
t
are inhabitants of these types (
t:T
). A
record type
(or
product type
)
R:Set
is a collection of other types, written as
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
, where each
R_{i}
is a type. By convention, uppercase letters denote types. We follow functional programming style for function application:
foo\ "hello"\ 3\ false
rather than
foo("hello",3,false)
.
We assume semantically rich domain-driven data types (e.g.,
Customer=CustomerId\times CustomerName\times Email
rather than
Integer\times String\times String
), enabling correctness assessment through domain-specific meaning and constraints. Product types can be viewed as sets of types (the field types), allowing type-level set operations:

•

Union
(
\cup_{typ}
): The union of two types contains all fields from both types.

•

Intersection
(
\cap_{typ}
): The intersection contains only fields common to both types.

•

Difference
(
-_{typ}
): The difference contains fields in the first type but not in the second.

Example
: If
Customer=CustomerId\times CustomerName\times Email
and
Order=OrderId\times CustomerId\times OrderDate
, then
Customer\cup_{typ}Order=CustomerId\times CustomerName\times Email\times OrderId\times OrderDate
(common fields appear once).
We define type-level set relations that capture structural relationships between types:

Definition 3.1 (Type-Level Subset Relation).

Given two data types
A
and
B
, we say that the fields of
A
are a
subset
of the fields of
B
, and write
A\subseteq_{typ}B
, if and only if there exists a
surjective
projection function
p:B\rightarrow A
such that for any element
a:A
there exists an element
b:B
such that
p\ b=a
.

Definition 3.2 (Type-Level Proper Subset Relation).

Given two data types
A
and
B
, we say that the fields of
A
are a
proper subset
of the fields of
B
, and write
A\subset_{typ}B
, if and only if
A\subseteq_{typ}B
and
\neg(B\subseteq_{typ}A)
.

Examples
:

•

Let
Customer=CustomerId\times CustomerName\times Email
,
CustomerProfile=CustomerId\times CustomerName
, and
CustomerBasic=CustomerId
.

•

Then
CustomerProfile\subseteq_{typ}Customer
and
CustomerBasic\subset_{typ}Customer
.

A function
f:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
is an
isomorphism
, denoted
f:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
, if there exists a function
g:R_{2}\rightarrow R_{1}
such that
f\circ g=id_{R_{2}}
and
g\circ f=id_{R_{1}}
, where
id_{X}:X\rightarrow X
is the identity function on type
X
such that
\forall x:X,id_{X}\ x=x
. The function
g
is the
inverse
of
f
. If there exists an isomorphism
f:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
, then
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are
isomorphic sets
and we write
R_{1}\cong R_{2}
.

3.2.
Definition of Grain

The grain defines the
level of detail
(granularity) of a data type by specifying the
minimum
information needed to
uniquely identify
each element. Intuitively, the grain of
R
is the
simplest type isomorphic to
R
—carrying only the essential identifying information while discarding redundant structure. This granularity determines the semantic interpretation of the data.

Definition 3.3 (Grain).

Given a data type
R
, the
grain
of
R
, denoted
G[R]
, is a type such that:

(1)

Isomorphism
:
G[R]
and
R
are isomorphic sets
G[R]\cong R
, meaning there exists an isomorphism
f_{g}:G[R]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
, referred to as the
grain function
.

(2)

Irreducibility
: No proper subset of
G[R]
can serve as the grain of
R
.

Examples
:

•

For a
Customer
type
Customer=CustomerId\times CustomerName\times Email
, the grain is
G[Customer]=CustomerId
(internal: grain fields are part of the type).

•

For a collection type
List\ MonthlyAccountBalance
representing monthly snapshots of all bank account balances, the grain is
G[List\ MonthlyAccountBalance]=BalanceDate
(external: the snapshot date is not a field of the collection type). Each date uniquely determines a snapshot, and vice versa.

The minimalistic nature of this definition—requiring only an isomorphism with irreducibility—enables universal application of grain to
any
data type, not merely record types. While record types are the most common case in data transformations over tabular data,
R
can be
any
type: sets, lists, maps, arrays, trees, graphs, streams, or arbitrary algebraic data types. Crucially, the grain can be: (1)
derived or computed
from the data rather than obtained through simple field projection, (2)
external
to the type structure (not necessarily a field of
R
), and (3)
any type
that establishes an isomorphism with
R
. This generality allows grain theory to reason about temporal snapshots, event streams, aggregated collections, and other non-tabular structures encountered in modern data systems.

Categorical Interpretation
: The grain
G[R]
can be understood as a
canonical minimal representative
of the isomorphism class
[R]=\{T:T\cong R\}
. For any type
G^{\prime}
isomorphic to
R
via
g:G^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
, there exists a unique morphism
p:G^{\prime}\rightarrow G[R]
such that
g=f_{g}\circ p
—
the grain factors all isomorphic representations
. You can view
p
as a projection that discards the extra structure of
G^{\prime}
to arrive at the grain
G[R]
. This universal property explains why the definition applies uniformly to any data type (not just record types), and why grains are unique up to isomorphism (Theorem
3.4
). The irreducibility condition ensures
G[R]
is the “simplest” such representative, carrying only the information necessary for unique identification.

3.3.
Grain Function

The grain establishes an isomorphism
f_{g}:G[R]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
(the
grain function
) that maps each grain value to a unique element of
R
. The inverse
f_{g}^{-1}
(also called
grain
) :
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R]
is the projection that extracts the grain value from an element of
R
.

3.4.
Grain Determines Data Semantics

Grain specifies the
level of detail
(granularity) at which each element represents information, fundamentally determining the
semantic interpretation
and
behavioral properties
of data. The same data type with different grain definitions yields completely different semantics and requires different transformations and constraints. Unclear or incorrect grain definitions are a primary source of data transformation bugs, leading to incorrect assumptions about uniqueness, ordering, and valid data instances.
Consider a
Customer
type with fields (of type):
CustomerId
,
Name
,
Email
,
Address
,
CreatedOn
,
EffectiveFrom
, and
EffectiveTo
. The
same type
with
different grain definitions
yields fundamentally different semantics:

•

G[Customer]=\texttt{CustomerId}
:
Entity semantics
—one distinct customer per
CustomerId
, no inherent ordering.

•

G[Customer]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{EffectiveFrom}
:

Versioned/temporal semantics
—multiple time-stamped versions per customer with

causal ordering.

•

G[Customer]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Address}\times\texttt{EffectiveFrom}
:
Relationship versioning
—permits multiple addresses per customer-time combination.

•

G[Customer]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{CreatedOn}
:
Event semantics
—each creation event, allowing
CustomerId
reuse across events.

The grain determines: (1)
uniqueness constraints
(enforced by the grain isomorphism), (2)
ordering relationships
(when temporal fields are in the grain), (3)
valid data instances
(e.g., multiple versions per customer), and (4)
appropriate transformations
(e.g., versioned tables require different read/write patterns than non-versioned tables).

Key Insight
: Grain defines granularity, and granularity determines semantics. The same Customer type can represent entities (one row per customer), versions (multiple time-stamped rows per customer), or events (each creation/modification event). Each granularity level yields fundamentally different semantics, constraints, and valid transformations.

Incorrect grain assumptions lead to transformation bugs: joining tables with mismatched grain semantics, aggregating without recognizing versioning, or applying uniqueness constraints incorrectly. Our grain inference framework (Section
5
) enables systematic verification that transformations preserve or correctly transform grain semantics, preventing such bugs at the type level before data processing.

3.5.
Grain as First-Class Type-Level Property

A fundamental insight of our approach is that grain should be a
first-class citizen
in data transformations. By encoding grain semantics directly into data types, we make grain explicit and visible to data engineers rather than an implicit assumption buried in documentation or comments. This allows for
zero-cost
,
compile-time
verification of grain correctness, catching bugs during development rather than during data testing or in production.

The Paradigm Shift
: This paper makes grain
declarative
—shifting it from an implicit assumption buried in code or data model designs to an explicit, central element of data transformations. Our validation method requires data engineers to explicitly declare the grain of source (input) and target (output) for every transformation, and to monitor grain evolution through each transformation step. This declarative approach makes the possibility of grain errors negligible, with the type checker serving as a valuable assistant to verify correctness automatically.
When grain is encoded in the type system, the type checker automatically enforces that:

•

Functions expecting data with grain
G_{1}
reject inputs with grain
G_{2}\neq G_{1}

•

Transformations claiming to produce grain
G_{1}
cannot return data with grain
G_{2}\neq G_{1}
without explicit conversion

•

Pipeline compositions are type-checked for grain consistency across all stages

This compile-time safety provides value
even without applying grain inference formulas
(Section
5
): simply encoding grain in types catches mismatches that current practice detects only through expensive runtime testing. Such tests may fail due to data quality issues rather than transformation errors, and they incur computational costs for every execution. In contrast, type-level grain checking has
zero computational cost
and catches semantic errors before any data processing. Section
6.1.4
discusses specific encoding mechanisms across type systems of varying expressiveness, from gradual typing to dependent types.

3.6.
Fundamental Theorems

Theorem 3.4 (Multiple Grains Isomorphism).

If
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are both grains of
R
, then
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic sets.

Example
: A
WeatherObservation
type can have two alternative grains:
G_{1}=ObservationId
or
G_{2}=ObservationDateTime\times WeatherStationId
. Both are valid irreducible subsets isomorphic to
WeatherObservation
. The Multiple Grains Isomorphism Theorem implies
G_{1}\cong G_{2}
: observation identifiers correspond one-to-one with
(ObservationDateTime,WeatherStationId)
pairs

Theorem 3.5 (Grain Uniqueness).

If
G
is the grain of
R
, then
G
uniquely identifies any element
r:R
. In other words, for any
r_{1}\ r_{2}:R
such that
grain\ r_{1}=grain\ r_{2}
it follows that
r_{1}=r_{2}
, which means that the projection function
grain:R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R]
is injective.

Theorem 3.6 (Grain of a grain / Grain Operator Idempotency).

If
G[R]
is the grain of
R
, then
G[G[R]]=G[R]
.

Theorem 3.7 (The Grain of Product Types).

The grain of a product type
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
is the product of the grains of the component types:

G[R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}].

Theorem 3.8 (The Grain of Sum Types).

The grain of a sum type
R=R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
is the sum of the grains of the component types:

G[R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}].

Example
: For
Vehicle=Car+Motorcycle
, we have
G[Vehicle]=G[Car]+G[Motorcycle]
.
The proofs of these theorems appear in the appendix. These theorems establish that grain is well-defined and provides a stable foundation for reasoning about data granularity.

4.
Grain Relations and Lattice

This section introduces the three fundamental grain relations that capture all possible relationships between data types: grain equality for one-to-one relationships, grain ordering for one-to-many relationships, and grain incomparability for many-to-many relationships. We establish theorems characterizing each relation and present the grain lattice structure that enables systematic reasoning about incomparable types.

4.1.
Grain Equality

Grain equality captures one-to-one relationships between data types.

Definition 4.1 (Grain Equality).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, we say they have
equal grain
and write
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
if and only if there exists an isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
between their grains.

Theorem 4.2 (Grain Equality Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
,
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
if and only if
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets.

Corollary 4.3 (Grain Equality Preservation).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:

R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]

That is, grain equality between types is equivalent to grain equality between their grains.

This corollary follows directly from the Grain Operator Idempotency Theorem (Section
3
) and shows that grain equality is a “stable” relation under the grain operator.
Grain equality is an
equivalence relation
, satisfying:

•

Reflexivity
:
R\equiv_{g}R

•

Symmetry
:
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}\Rightarrow R_{2}\equiv_{g}R_{1}

•

Transitivity
:
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\equiv_{g}R_{3}\Rightarrow R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{3}

Example
: If
Customer
and
WebCustomer
have equal grain, then each customer corresponds to exactly one web account, and vice versa.

4.2.
Grain Ordering

Grain ordering captures one-to-many relationships between data types.

Definition 4.4 (Grain Ordering).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, we say
R_{1}
has
lower grain
than
R_{2}
(equivalently,
R_{2}
has
higher grain
than
R_{1}
), and write
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
, if and only if there exists a function
f:G[R_{1}]\rightarrow G[R_{2}]
between their grains.

Example
: For
Order
with
G[\texttt{Order}]=\texttt{OrderId}
and
OrderDetail
with
G[\texttt{OrderDetail}]=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
, the projection
f:G[\texttt{OrderDetail}]\rightarrow G[\texttt{Order}]
mapping
(orderId,lineItemId)\mapsto orderId
establishes
OrderDetail\leq_{g}Order
.

Theorem 4.5 (Grain Ordering Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
,
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
if and only if there exists a function
h:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
that establishes a one-to-many correspondence between the elements of the two types.

This theorem shows that grain ordering implies a functional dependency: if
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
, then
R_{1}
functionally determines
R_{2}
.
Grain ordering can be established in two ways:

(1)

Subset-based
: When
G[R_{2}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{1}]
, e.g.,
G[\texttt{Order}]=\texttt{OrderId}
and
G[\texttt{OrderDetail}]=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
.

(2)

Functional dependency
: When there exists a functional dependency from
G[R_{1}]
to
G[R_{2}]
, e.g.,
G[\texttt{Employee}]=\texttt{EmployeeId}
and
G[\texttt{Department}]=\texttt{DepartmentId}
with
\texttt{EmployeeId}\rightarrow\texttt{DepartmentId}
.

The subset-based case is a special instance where the function from
G[R_{1}]
to
G[R_{2}]
is a projection (as in the example above).

Remark (Surjectivity Not Required)
: The function
f:G[R_{1}]\to G[R_{2}]
in the grain ordering definition is not required to be surjective. This correctly models data semantics where finer-grained entities need not cover all coarser-grained entities (e.g., orders may exist without order details, customers without orders). For subset-based grain ordering, surjectivity follows automatically from the definition of
\subseteq_{typ}
(Definition
3.3
). For functional dependency-based ordering, surjectivity is not required; the ordering captures
functional determination
at the type level rather than instance-level coverage constraints. The partial order structure is preserved because antisymmetry yields
isomorphism
(
\equiv_{g}
), not strict equality—the categorically appropriate notion of “sameness” for type-level reasoning (see the categorical perspective below).

Corollary 4.6 (Grain Ordering Preservation).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:

R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}]

Theorem 4.7 (Grain Subset Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, if
G[R_{1}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{2}]
, then
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
.

Corollary 4.8 (Grain Determines All Type Subsets).

If
G
is the grain of
R
, then for any
R^{\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}\subseteq_{typ}R
, we have
G\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
. In other words, the grain of a type functionally determines any subset of that type’s fields.

Theorem 4.9 (Grain Inference).

For any data type
R
, if there exists a type
G
such that:

(1)

G\subseteq_{typ}R
(G is a type-level subset of R)

(2)

G\leq_{g}R
(G has lower grain than R)

(3)

G[G]=G
(G is its own grain, i.e., irreducible)

then
G
is a grain of
R
, i.e.,
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
.

This theorem provides sufficient conditions for inferring the grain of types after data transformations (see Section
5
). The conclusion
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
means
G
and
G[R]
are isomorphic, consistent with the principle that grains are unique up to isomorphism.
Grain ordering is a
partial order
, satisfying:

•

Reflexivity
:
R\leq_{g}R

•

Antisymmetry
:
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}\Rightarrow R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}

•

Transitivity
:
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{3}\Rightarrow R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{3}

Categorical Perspective
: The antisymmetry property states
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}\land R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}\Rightarrow R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
, where
\equiv_{g}
denotes grain equivalence (isomorphism), not strict type equality. This is
antisymmetry up to isomorphism
: the relation
\leq_{g}
is a
preorder
on types that induces a
partial order
on grain-equivalence classes
[R]=\{R^{\prime}:R^{\prime}\equiv_{g}R\}
. This categorical formulation—working with types up to isomorphism—is the natural level of abstraction for data transformation correctness: two types with isomorphic grains represent the same granularity and admit the same valid transformations, regardless of superficial structural differences.

4.3.
Armstrong Axioms for Grain Ordering

The grain ordering relation
\leq_{g}
satisfies the
Armstrong axioms

(
Armstrong 1974
)
, a sound and complete inference system for functional dependencies introduced by Armstrong in 1974. We treat
\leq_{g}
as a functional dependency relation at the type level: just as functional dependencies describe how one set of attributes determines another, grain ordering describes how one type’s grain functionally determines another type’s grain.

4.3.1.
Basic Axioms

(1)

Self-determination
:
R\leq_{g}R
for any type
R
.

(2)

Reflexivity
: If
G[R_{1}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{2}]
, then
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
(Grain Subset Theorem).

(3)

Augmentation
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
, then
R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{3}\leq_{g}R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{3}
for any type
R_{3}
.

(4)

Transitivity
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{3}
, then
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{3}
.

4.3.2.
Derived Rules

The following rules are derivable from the basic axioms:

•

Union
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{3}
, then
R_{1}\leq_{g}(R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{3})
.

•

Decomposition
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}(R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{3})
, then
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{3}
.

•

Composition
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{3}\leq_{g}R_{4}
, then
(R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{3})\leq_{g}(R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{4})
.

•

Pseudotransitivity
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
(R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{4})\leq_{g}R_{3}
, then
(R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{4})\leq_{g}R_{3}
.

4.3.3.
Advanced Theorems

•

Darwen’s Theorem
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{3}\leq_{g}R_{4}
, then
(R_{1}\cup_{typ}(R_{3}-_{typ}R_{2}))\leq_{g}(R_{2}\cup_{typ}R_{4})
, where
R_{3}-_{typ}R_{2}
denotes the type difference.

4.3.4.
Additional Properties

•

Union with intersection
: If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
(R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{2})\leq_{g}R_{3}
, then
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{3}
. This property, specific to grain ordering, is derivable from
Transitivity
and
R_{1}\leq_{g}(R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{2})
(via the Grain Subset Theorem).

Combined with the Grain Inference Theorem (Theorem
4.9
), these axioms enable systematic inference of grain relationships after data transformations.

Example
: Consider collections
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
where
R_{1}=Account\times Customer
with
G[R_{1}]=Account
, and
R_{2}=Customer\times Address
with
G[R_{2}]=Customer\times Address
. After joining on
Customer
, we obtain
R_{3}=Account\times Customer\times Address
.
To infer
G[R_{3}]
: (1) Since
G[R_{1}]=Account
, we have
Account\leq_{g}Customer
. (2) By
Augmentation
with
Address
:
Account\times Address\leq_{g}Customer\times Address
. (3) By
Augmentation
with
Account
:
Account\times Address\leq_{g}R_{3}
. (4) Since
Account\times Address\subseteq_{typ}R_{3}
,
Account\times Address\leq_{g}R_{3}
, and
G[Account\times Address]=Account\times Address
(as
Account
is irreducible), by the
Grain Inference Theorem
we conclude
G[R_{3}]=Account\times Address
.

4.3.5.
Distinction from Functional Dependencies

While grain ordering
\leq_{g}
satisfies Armstrong’s axioms, it differs fundamentally from traditional functional dependencies in one key respect: grain ordering forms a
partial order
(on equivalence classes), whereas functional dependencies do not.
For functional dependencies,
A\to B
and
B\to A
implies only that
A
and
B
have the same attribute closure (
A^{+}=B^{+}
), not that
A=B
as attribute sets. In contrast,
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
implies
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
—the types are isomorphic and thus
equal up to isomorphism
. This stronger property arises because grain theory operates on types (which carry structure) rather than attribute sets, and because grain equivalence is defined via isomorphism rather than syntactic equality.

This partial order structure is what enables grain theory to provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for data transformation correctness that goes beyond traditional functional dependency theory. The key insight is that working with

4.4.
Grain Incomparability

When two types’ grains are neither equal nor ordered, they are incomparable, meaning they can only be related through a many-to-many relationship.

Definition 4.10 (Grain Incomparability).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, we say they are
grain-incomparable
and write
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}
if and only if:

•

\neg(R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2})
(not equal)

•

\neg(R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2})
(not ordered)

•

\neg(R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1})
(not ordered in reverse)

Theorem 4.11 (Grain Incomparability Preservation).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:

R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]

Grain incomparability satisfies:

•

Irreflexivity
:
\neg(R\langle\rangle_{g}R)
(no type is incomparable with itself)

•

Symmetry
:
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow R_{2}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{1}

•

Non-transitivity
:
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}
and
R_{2}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{3}
does not imply
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{3}

Example
:
SalesChannel
with grain
\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{Date}
and
SalesProduct
with grain
\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{Date}
are incomparable, representing a many-to-many relationship.

4.5.
The Grain Lattice

The grain ordering relation
\leq_{g}
forms a
bounded lattice
structure, enabling systematic reasoning about grain relationships.
For any two grains
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
:

•

Greatest Lower Bound (glb)
:
G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}]
is the coarsest grain that is finer than both
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
.

•

Least Upper Bound (lub)
:
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]
is the finest grain that is coarser than both
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
.

The lattice structure enables reasoning about incomparable types: even when types cannot be directly compared, we can determine their relationship through lattice operations. This is crucial for understanding many-to-many joins and complex transformations.

(

, grain theory applies lattices at the type level to enable compile-time correctness verification of data transformations, rather than runtime query optimization.

5.
Grain Inference for Data Transformations

5.1.
Overview

A critical challenge in data pipeline design is understanding how transformations change data granularity. This section presents systematic,
type-level
formulas for
grain inference
—determining the grain of transformation results from the grains of inputs.
We focus particularly on
equi-joins
, as joins are among the most complex and error-prone operations in data pipelines. Our grain inference formulas enable
correctness verification
by allowing data engineers to compute the expected grain of join results and verify that actual results match expectations, preventing common bugs proactively.

5.2.
Grain Inference for Equi-Joins

Our main contribution is a general formula for calculating the grain of equi-join results. We assume an abstract concept of a
collection
of data elements of a type
R
, represented by a container type
C:Set\to Set
, which is referred to as the type
C\ R
.

Theorem 5.1 (Grain Inference for Equi-Joins).

For any two collections of type
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
sharing common fields of type
J_{k}
(i.e.,
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
), performing an equi-join on
J_{k}
produces a result
C\ Res
with type
Res
and grain
G[Res]
such that:

•

Res=(R_{1}-_{typ}J_{k})\times(R_{2}-_{typ}J_{k})\times J_{k}
(the result type contains fields from
R_{1}
not in
J_{k}
, fields from
R_{2}
not in
J_{k}
, and
J_{k}
once; note that fields common to both types but not in
J_{k}
appear twice, once from each side, since their values may differ)

•

(G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}])\leq_{g}G[Res]\leq_{g}(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])

The
G[Res]
formula depends on the relationship between the
J_{k}
-portions of the two grains. Let:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{1}]
that overlaps with
J_{k}
)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{2}]
that overlaps with
J_{k}
)

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are comparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\\
\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Case B
: When neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are incomparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\\
\times((G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Key Insight
: The key insight is that when one grain’s
J_{k}
-portion is contained in the other’s, a “bootstrapping” mechanism allows redundancy elimination: the containing grain can determine the full
J_{k}
value, which in turn determines the contained grain’s
J_{k}
-portion. However, when the
J_{k}
-portions are incomparable (each has fields the other lacks), no such bootstrapping is possible, and both portions must appear in
G[Res]
.
Note that
J_{k}
is a
type
—the common subset of fields where
(J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1})\land(J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2})
—not a join predicate. The join predicate for an equi-join is implicitly equality on
J_{k}
values. Because our formula operates entirely at the
type level
, using only type operations (
\cap_{typ}
,
-_{typ}
,
\times
,
\cup_{typ}
) on types (
G[R_{1}]
,
G[R_{2}]
,
J_{k}
), it applies uniformly to
inner and outer joins
(left, right, full), which produce results containing fields from both input relations. Note that semi-joins and anti-joins, while using equi-join predicates, return only one side of the join (typically
R_{1}
), so their result grain is simply
G[R_{1}]
rather than the combined structure given by this formula.

5.2.1.
Examples Illustrating Case A and Case B

We illustrate the two cases of Theorem
5.1
with concrete examples:

Example 1 (Case A: Comparable
J_{k}
-portions)
: Consider the Armstrong axioms example:

•

R_{1}=\texttt{Account}\times\texttt{Customer}
with
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{Account}

•

R_{2}=\texttt{Customer}\times\texttt{Address}
with
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{Customer}\times\texttt{Address}

•

J_{k}=\texttt{Customer}
(the common field used for the join)

Computing the
J_{k}
-portions:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{Account}\cap_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=\emptyset

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=(\texttt{Customer}\times\texttt{Address})\cap_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=\texttt{Customer}

Since
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\emptyset\subseteq_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}
, Case A applies:

•

G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=\texttt{Account}-_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=\texttt{Account}

•

G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=(\texttt{Customer}\times\texttt{Address})-_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=\texttt{Address}

•

G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\texttt{Account}\cap_{typ}(\texttt{Customer}\times\texttt{Address})\cap_{typ}\texttt{Customer}=\emptyset

Therefore:
G[Res]=\texttt{Account}\times\texttt{Address}\times\emptyset=\texttt{Account}\times\texttt{Address}
.

Note
: Many data engineers would intuitively assume the result grain is
\texttt{Account}\times\texttt{Address}\times\texttt{Customer}
, but the grain inference theorem correctly shows it is
\texttt{Account}\times\texttt{Address}
. This illustrates the importance of formal grain inference: while
Customer
is part of
R_{2}
’s grain, it is also a field in
R_{1}
, making it fully determined by
Account
(the grain of
R_{1}
). Through the join relationship, it maintains the same value for
R_{2}
and can therefore be safely omitted from the result grain. The theorem accurately calculates the level of detail after the data transformation, preventing grain mismatches that could lead to incorrect aggregations.

Example 2 (Case B: Incomparable
J_{k}
-portions)
: Consider:

•

R_{1}=A\times B\times C\times E
with
G[R_{1}]=A\times C

•

R_{2}=A\times B\times C\times D
with
G[R_{2}]=A\times B

•

J_{k}=A\times B\times C
(the common fields used for the join)

Computing the
J_{k}
-portions:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=(A\times C)\cap_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=A\times C

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=(A\times B)\cap_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=A\times B

Since neither
A\times C\subseteq_{typ}A\times B
nor
A\times B\subseteq_{typ}A\times C
, Case B applies:

•

G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=(A\times C)-_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=\emptyset

•

G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=(A\times B)-_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=\emptyset

•

(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k}=((A\times C)\cup_{typ}(A\times B))\cap_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=A\times B\times C

Therefore:
G[Res]=\emptyset\times\emptyset\times(A\times B\times C)=A\times B\times C
.
Note: Using the Case A formula incorrectly would give
A
, which fails to uniquely identify join results.

Example 3 (Join on subset of common fields)
: Consider joining on only some of the common fields:

•

R_{1}=A\times B\times C
with
G[R_{1}]=A\times B

•

R_{2}=B\times C\times D
with
G[R_{2}]=C\times D

•

J_{k}=B
(joining only on
B
, not on all common fields
B\times C
)

The result type is
Res=A\times B\times C_{1}\times C_{2}\times D
, where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
indicate that
C
appears twice (once from each side) since
C
is common to both types but not in
J_{k}
. Computing the
J_{k}
-portions:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=(A\times B)\cap_{typ}B=B

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=(C\times D)\cap_{typ}B=\emptyset

Since
\emptyset\subseteq_{typ}B
, Case A applies:

•

G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=(A\times B)-_{typ}B=A

•

G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=(C\times D)-_{typ}B=C\times D

•

G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=(A\times B)\cap_{typ}(C\times D)\cap_{typ}B=\emptyset

Therefore:
G[Res]=A\times(C\times D)\times\emptyset=A\times C\times D
.

5.3.
Special Cases

The general formula simplifies for the three main grain relationship scenarios:

5.3.1.
Equal Grains (One-to-One Joins)

Corollary 5.2 (Equal Grains).

When
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
and the equi-join is performed on common fields
J_{k}
(where
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
), Case A of Theorem
5.1
always applies (since equal grains have equal
J_{k}
-portions).
Note that
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
means the grains are isomorphic (equal up to an isomorphism), not necessarily the same type. In the practical case of equi-joins, the grains are typically the same type (e.g., both have
CustomerId
), but this corollary also holds in the more general case where the grains are isomorphic but distinct types (e.g., one has
CustomerId
and the other has
ClientId
, with an isomorphism between them). The relationship between
J_{k}
and the grains determines the result:

•

Case 1
: If
J_{k}=G[R_{1}]
(or equivalently
J_{k}=G[R_{2}]
since the grains are equal), then
G[Res]=J_{k}
. Example:
Customer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}
) and
LoyalCustomer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}
yields
G[Res]=\texttt{CustomerId}
.

•

Case 2
: If
J_{k}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
(proper subset of the grain), then
G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times J_{k}
. Example:
Customer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
) and
CustomerSegment
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{SegmentId}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}
yields
G[Res]=\texttt{RegionId}\times\texttt{SegmentId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}
.

•

Case 3
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]\neq\emptyset
but
J_{k}\not\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
(non-empty intersection but not a subset), then the Case A formula from Theorem
5.1
applies. Example:
Customer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
, with
ProductId
as a non-grain field) and
Order
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{OrderDate}
, with
ProductId
as a non-grain field), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ProductId}
yields
G[Res]=\texttt{RegionId}\times\texttt{OrderDate}\times\texttt{CustomerId}
.

•

Case 4
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]=\emptyset
(empty intersection with the grain), then
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
. Example:
Customer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}
) and
LoyaltyCustomer
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{Email}
(which appears in both types but is not in either grain) yields
G[Res]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}
.

5.3.2.
Ordered Grains (One-to-Many Joins)

Corollary 5.3 (Ordered Grains).

When
G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}]
(where
G[R_{1}]
is the finer grain) and the equi-join is performed on common fields
J_{k}
(where
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
), Case A of Theorem
5.1
applies because the grain ordering ensures the
J_{k}
-portions are comparable.
The relationship between
J_{k}
and the coarser grain
G[R_{2}]
determines the result:

•

Case 1
: If
G[R_{2}]\subseteq_{typ}J_{k}
(
J_{k}
contains the coarser grain), then
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]
. Example:
OrderDetail
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
) and
Order
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{OrderId}
yields
G[Res]=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
.

•

Case 2
: If
J_{k}\subset_{typ}G[R_{2}]
(
J_{k}
is a proper subset of the coarser grain), then the Case A formula from Theorem
5.1
applies. Example:
OrderLineItem
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}\times\texttt{ProductId}
) and
OrderDetail
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
), where
G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}]
since
G[R_{2}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{1}]
, joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{OrderId}
(proper subset of
G[R_{2}]
) yields
G[Res]=\texttt{LineItemId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}\times\texttt{OrderId}
. Note that
LineItemId
appears twice because it is not part of the join condition
J_{k}=\texttt{OrderId}
, so the
LineItemId
values from
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
can differ and both must appear in the grain to uniquely identify result rows.

•

Case 3
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\neq\emptyset
but
J_{k}\not\subset_{typ}G[R_{2}]
and
G[R_{2}]\not\subset_{typ}J_{k}
(partial overlap with coarser grain), then the Case A formula from Theorem
5.1
applies. Example:
OrderDetail
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
) and
Order
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{OrderDate}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}
(where
CustomerId
is a non-grain field in both types) yields
G[Res]=\texttt{LineItemId}\times\texttt{OrderDate}\times\texttt{OrderId}
.

•

Case 4
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]=\emptyset
(empty intersection with the coarser grain), then the Case A formula from Theorem
5.1
applies. Example:
OrderDetail
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
) and
Order
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}
), joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}
(which appears in both types but is not in either grain) yields
G[Res]=(\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId})\times\texttt{OrderId}=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
, since
G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]
,
G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{2}]
, and
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\emptyset
.

Key Insight
: The result grain is
G[R_{1}]
(the finer grain) whenever
J_{k}
contains the coarser grain
G[R_{2}]
, which ensures the join condition aligns with the grain ordering relationship. This applies whether the grain ordering is established by a subset relation (e.g.,
OrderId\subseteq_{typ}(OrderId\times LineItemId)
) or by a functional dependency between disjoint types (e.g.,
EmployeeId\rightarrow DepartmentId
).

Example
: Joining
OrderDetail
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
) with
Order
(
G=\texttt{OrderId}
) on
OrderId
yields
G[Res]=\texttt{OrderId}\times\texttt{LineItemId}
.

5.3.3.
Incomparable Grains (Many-to-Many Joins)

Corollary 5.4 (Incomparable Grains).

When
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
(the grains are incomparable), the equi-join result grain depends on the relationship between the
J_{k}
-portions of the grains.
Let
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
.

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are comparable):

G[Res]=G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Example:
SalesChannel
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{Date}
) and
SalesProduct
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{Date}
), where
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
, joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
. Here,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
, so
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(trivially comparable). Applying Case A:
G_{1}^{rest}=\texttt{ChannelId}
,
G_{2}^{rest}=\texttt{ProductId}
, and
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
, yielding
G[Res]=\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
.

Case B
: When neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are also incomparable):

G[Res]=G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times((G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Example:
Sales
(
G=\texttt{SalesId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{StoreId}
) and
Product
(
G=\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{SupplierId}\times\texttt{CategoryId}
), where
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
, joining on
J_{k}=\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{StoreId}\times\texttt{SupplierId}
. Here,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{StoreId}
(since
SupplierId
is not in
G[R_{1}]
) and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{SupplierId}
(since
StoreId
is not in
G[R_{2}]
). Since neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, Case B applies. Applying Case B:
G_{1}^{rest}=\texttt{SalesId}
,
G_{2}^{rest}=\texttt{CategoryId}
, and
(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{StoreId}\times\texttt{SupplierId}
, yielding
G[Res]=\texttt{SalesId}\times\texttt{CategoryId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{StoreId}\times\texttt{SupplierId}
.
where
G_{1}^{rest}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}
and
G_{2}^{rest}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}
.

Key Insight
: Incomparable grains may still have comparable
J_{k}
-portions, in which case the intersection formula (Case A) applies. However, when the
J_{k}
-portions are also incomparable, the union formula (Case B) is required because neither side can bootstrap the determination of the other’s
J_{k}
-portion through the join condition.

5.3.4.
Natural Join

Corollary 5.5 (Natural Join).

When the equi-join is a
natural join
, meaning
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}
(joining on all common fields between the two types), and
Res=R_{1}\cup_{typ}R_{2}
, Theorem
5.1
applies based on the comparability of the
J_{k}
-portions.
Let:

•

G_{1}^{unique}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}R_{2}
(grain fields of
R_{1}
that are not in
R_{2}
)

•

G_{2}^{unique}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}R_{1}
(grain fields of
R_{2}
that are not in
R_{1}
)

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}R_{2}
(grain fields of
R_{1}
that are in
R_{2}
)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}R_{1}
(grain fields of
R_{2}
that are in
R_{1}
)

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])

Example:
Customer
(
R_{1}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{CustomerName}\times\texttt{RegionId}
,
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
) and
CustomerSegment
(
R_{2}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}\times\texttt{SegmentId}
,
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
), natural join on
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
. Here,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
, so
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(trivially comparable). Applying Case A:
G_{1}^{unique}=\emptyset
,
G_{2}^{unique}=\emptyset
, and
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
, yielding
G[Res]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
. Verifying with the general formula:
G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=\emptyset
,
G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=\emptyset
, and
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
, confirming
G[Res]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{RegionId}
.

Case B
: When neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

Example:
R_{1}=A\times B\times C
with
G[R_{1}]=A\times B
and
R_{2}=B\times C\times D
with
G[R_{2}]=C\times D
, natural join on
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}=B\times C
. Here,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}R_{2}=(A\times B)\cap_{typ}(B\times C\times D)=B
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}R_{1}=(C\times D)\cap_{typ}(A\times B\times C)=C
. Since neither
B\subseteq_{typ}C
nor
C\subseteq_{typ}B
, Case B applies. Applying Case B:
G_{1}^{unique}=A
,
G_{2}^{unique}=D
, and
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}=B\cup_{typ}C=B\times C
, yielding
G[Res]=A\times D\times B\times C
. Verifying with the general formula:
G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=A
,
G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=D
, and
(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k}=B\times C
, confirming
G[Res]=A\times D\times B\times C
.

Key Insight
: In a natural join, the result grain consists of three components: grain fields unique to
R_{1}
, grain fields unique to
R_{2}
, and the
J_{k}
-portion contribution. Case B arises when both grains have fields in the common type
R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}
that are not present in the other grain. In such cases, neither grain can “bootstrap” the other’s
J_{k}
-portion through the join condition, so both must be included.

5.4.
Generalized Equi-Join: Isomorphic Join Keys

Theorem
5.1
assumes that both input types share the
same
join key type
J_{k}
. However, the categorical perspective of grain theory—where types are considered
up to isomorphism
—naturally suggests a generalization: what if the join keys are
isomorphic but not identical
?

Theorem 5.6 (Generalized Grain Inference for Equi-Joins).

For any two collections of type
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
with join key types
J_{k1}
and
J_{k2}
respectively, where:

•

J_{k1}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k2}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
(join keys are extractable via projection)

•

J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
, i.e., there exists an isomorphism
\phi:J_{k1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}J_{k2}

Performing an
equi-join up to isomorphism
—matching rows where
\phi(\pi_{1}(r_{1}))=\pi_{2}(r_{2})
for projections
\pi_{i}:R_{i}\to J_{ki}
—produces a result
C\ Res
with type
Res
and grain
G[Res]
such that:

•

Res=(R_{1}-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(R_{2}-_{typ}J_{k2})\times J_{k}
, where
J_{k}
is a canonical representative of the isomorphism class
[J_{k1}]=[J_{k2}]
(typically
J_{k1}
or
J_{k2}
)

•

The grain
G[Res]
follows the same structure as Theorem
5.1

Let:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k1}
(the part of
G[R_{1}]
overlapping with the join key)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k2}
(the part of
G[R_{2}]
overlapping with the join key)

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are grain-comparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\\
\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

where
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
denotes the grain
meet
(the coarser of the two comparable grains).

Case B
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\langle\rangle_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are grain-incomparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\\
\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

where
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
denotes the grain
join
(least upper bound in the grain lattice).

Relationship to Theorem
5.1
: When
J_{k1}=J_{k2}=J_{k}
(identical join keys), the isomorphism
\phi
is the identity function, and the generalized theorem reduces to the original. The key generalization is twofold:

(1)

Isomorphic Join Keys
: The join keys need only be grain-equivalent (
\equiv_{g}
), not type-identical. This captures the categorical insight that semantically equivalent structures should be interchangeable.

(2)

Grain Ordering for Comparability
: The case conditions use
\leq_{g}
instead of
\subseteq_{typ}
. By the Grain Subset Theorem,
A\subseteq_{typ}B\Rightarrow B\leq_{g}A
, so this is a strict generalization—subset-based comparability implies grain-ordering comparability, but not vice versa. This captures functional dependency relationships that establish grain ordering without subset relationships.

5.4.1.
Practical Applications

The generalized theorem enables grain inference in scenarios that the original theorem cannot handle:

Example 1 (Cross-System Integration)
: Consider joining customer data from two systems with different key representations:

•

System A:
R_{1}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Name}\times\texttt{Email}
with
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{CustomerId}
(where
CustomerId
is a 32-bit integer)

•

System B:
R_{2}=\texttt{ClientGuid}\times\texttt{Address}\times\texttt{Phone}
with
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{ClientGuid}
(where
ClientGuid
is a UUID)

•

Isomorphism:
\phi:\texttt{CustomerId}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\texttt{ClientGuid}
(a bijective mapping maintained by the integration layer)

Joining on
\phi
yields
G[Res]\equiv_{g}\texttt{CustomerId}\equiv_{g}\texttt{ClientGuid}
(Case A with equal grain portions).

Example 2 (Schema Evolution)
: Consider joining historical data with current data after a schema change:

•

Historical:
R_{1}=\texttt{OrderDate}\times\texttt{Amount}
with
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{OrderDate}
(where
OrderDate
is
String
in “YYYY-MM-DD” format)

•

Current:
R_{2}=\texttt{OrderTimestamp}\times\texttt{Amount}\times\texttt{Status}
with
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{OrderTimestamp}
(where
OrderTimestamp
is a proper
DateTime
type)

•

Isomorphism:
\phi:\texttt{OrderDate}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\texttt{OrderTimestamp}
(parsing/formatting)

Joining on
\phi
preserves grain semantics despite the structural difference.

Example 3 (Composite Key Restructuring)
: Consider joining data with differently-structured but isomorphic composite keys:

•

R_{1}=(\texttt{Year}\times\texttt{Month}\times\texttt{Day})\times\texttt{Sales}
with
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{Year}\times\texttt{Month}\times\texttt{Day}

•

R_{2}=\texttt{Date}\times\texttt{Inventory}
with
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{Date}

•

Isomorphism:
\phi:(\texttt{Year}\times\texttt{Month}\times\texttt{Day})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\texttt{Date}
(date composition/decomposition)

The join on
\phi
yields
G[Res]\equiv_{g}G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
(equal grains up to isomorphism).

Example 4 (Functional Dependency-Based Ordering)
: Consider a case where grain ordering exists via functional dependency rather than subset:

•

R_{1}=\texttt{EmployeeId}\times\texttt{Name}\times\texttt{DeptId}
with
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{EmployeeId}

•

R_{2}=\texttt{DepartmentId}\times\texttt{DeptName}\times\texttt{Budget}
with
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{DepartmentId}

•

Join key types:
J_{k1}=\texttt{DeptId}
and
J_{k2}=\texttt{DepartmentId}
with
\phi:\texttt{DeptId}\cong\texttt{DepartmentId}

•

Grain ordering:
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
via the functional dependency
\texttt{EmployeeId}\to\texttt{DeptId}

Here,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=\emptyset
(since
DeptId
is not in
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{EmployeeId}
) and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{DepartmentId}
, so
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
trivially. Case A applies:
G[Res]=\texttt{EmployeeId}\times\emptyset\times\emptyset=\texttt{EmployeeId}
.

5.4.2.
Theoretical Significance

The generalized theorem aligns grain inference with the categorical perspective of grain theory:

(1)

Consistency with “Up to Isomorphism”
: The grain definition identifies types up to isomorphism (Theorem
3.4
). The generalized join theorem extends this principle to join operations, ensuring that semantically equivalent structures produce equivalent results.

(2)

Partial Order Preservation
: The grain ordering
\leq_{g}
forms a partial order on equivalence classes (Section
4
). Using
\leq_{g}
for comparability in the generalized theorem preserves this structure through join operations.

(3)

Functional Dependency Generality
: While the original theorem implicitly assumes subset-based key extraction, the generalized theorem accommodates functional dependency-based relationships where
G[R]\leq_{g}J_{k}
without
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R
.

The generalized theorem demonstrates that the categorical foundations of grain theory yield practical benefits: by working with types
up to isomorphism
, we enable correct grain reasoning across schema heterogeneity, system integration boundaries, and schema evolution—precisely the scenarios where data transformation bugs are most likely to occur.

5.5.
Grain Inference for Other Data Transformations

Beyond equi-joins, grain inference applies to the full set of relational algebra operations. Since grain is a
type-level notion
, the key principle is:
if the result type is unchanged, the grain is unchanged
. For unary operations, if all grain component types are preserved in the result, the grain remains the same.

5.5.1.
Unary Operations

•

Selection
(
\sigma_{condition}(C\ R)
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
. Since selection only filters rows without changing the type structure,
G[Res]=G[R]
.

•

Projection
(
\pi_{S}(C\ R)
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=S
and
S\subseteq_{typ}R
. If
G[R]\subseteq_{typ}S
(all grain columns preserved), then
G[Res]=G[R]
. If
G[R]\not\subseteq_{typ}S
(some grain columns removed), the grain becomes coarser; without additional functional dependency information,
G[Res]=S
in the worst case. Since relations in relational algebra are sets by definition, projection automatically eliminates duplicates. However, in practical implementations (e.g., SQL), where relations may be bags/multisets, explicit duplicate elimination (e.g.,
DISTINCT
,
ROW_NUMBER(), GROUP BY
) may be required when grain columns are removed. This is another example of a subtle bug that can be detected at design time through formal grain inference.

•

Extension
(adding derived column
D=f(R)
to
C\ R
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R\times D
. Since
D
is functionally dependent on
R
(and thus on
G[R]
), the new column does not contribute to the grain. By the Grain of Product Types theorem and functional dependency,
G[Res]=G[R]
. Window functions (e.g.,
ROW_NUMBER()
,
RANK()
,
LAG()
,
LEAD()
) are typical examples of Extension operations, as they add derived columns computed over partitions without changing the grain of the input data.

•

Rename
(
\rho_{a\to b}(C\ R)
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R^{\prime}
(the renamed type). Renaming preserves structure, so the result grain is equivalent to the input grain:
G[Res]\equiv_{g}G[R]
.

•

Grouping and Aggregation
(
\gamma_{G_{cols},agg(A)}(C\ R)
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=G_{cols}\times AggResult
. Since each unique combination of grouping columns produces exactly one row,
G[Res]=G_{cols}
. The relationship between
G_{cols}
and
G[R]
affects the semantics:

–

If
G[R]\subseteq_{typ}G_{cols}
: Each group contains at most one row; aggregation is trivial.

–

If
G_{cols}\subseteq_{typ}G[R]
: True aggregation; grain is coarsened from
G[R]
to
G_{cols}
.

–

If
G_{cols}\cap_{typ}G[R]=\emptyset
: True aggregation; grouping is orthogonal to grain.

–

Otherwise (partial overlap): True aggregation with mixed grain/non-grain grouping.

5.5.2.
Set Operations

Set operations require
union-compatible
types (identical schemas). For collections
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
where
R_{1}=R_{2}=R
(by type compatibility), the result type equals the input type, so the grain is preserved:

•

Union
(
C\ R_{1}\cup C\ R_{2}
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
. Since the type is unchanged,
G[Res]=G[R]
.

•

Intersection
(
C\ R_{1}\cap C\ R_{2}
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
. Since the type is unchanged,
G[Res]=G[R]
.

•

Difference
(
C\ R_{1}-C\ R_{2}
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
. Since the type is unchanged,
G[Res]=G[R]
.

5.5.3.
Non-Equi-Join Operations

•

Theta Join
(
C\ R_{1}\bowtie_{\theta}C\ R_{2}
where
\theta
is a non-equality condition): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}\times R_{2}
(no columns eliminated since no equality constraint deduplicates fields). Without equality, neither grain can “bootstrap” the other. By the Grain of Product Types theorem:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
.

•

Semi-Join
(
C\ R_{1}\ltimes C\ R_{2}
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}
(returns rows from
R_{1}
that have at least one match in
R_{2}
). Since semi-join only filters
R_{1}
rows without changing the type:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]
.

•

Anti-Join
(
C\ R_{1}\triangleright C\ R_{2}
): Produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}
(returns rows from
R_{1}
that have no match in
R_{2}
). Since anti-join only filters
R_{1}
rows without changing the type:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]
.

6.
Correctness Verification and Practical Applications

6.1.
Practical Value for Enterprise Data Engineering

Modern enterprise data landscapes feature complex data pipelines—often represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with hundreds of transformation nodes—processing tables with hundreds of millions or billions of rows. In cloud-based platforms such as Databricks, Snowflake, or BigQuery, each query execution incurs computational cost. Finding bugs through testing is expensive: a single end-to-end pipeline run may cost hundreds of dollars and take hours to complete, yet grain-related errors (double-counting, fan traps, incorrect joins) remain among the most common and difficult-to-detect bugs in data engineering.
Grain theory fundamentally shifts this paradigm by: a) placing grain at the center of data pipeline development, transforming it from an implicit assumption into the most essential data transformation property, and b) enabling
correctness verification at zero computational cost
. Since grain is a
type-level property
, the grain inference rules in Table
2
can be applied to pipeline specifications—before any data is processed. A data engineer can verify that every transformation in a 500-node pipeline produces the expected grain by symbolic computation alone, without executing a single query or scanning a single row.

6.1.1.
Type-Level Verification Methodology

The verification methodology proceeds in five steps, all operating at the type level:

(1)

Identify grains
: Determine
G[R]
for all types
R
in the pipeline at the type level.

(2)

Determine grain relations
: Establish
\equiv_{g}
,
\leq_{g}
, or
\langle\rangle_{g}
relationships between types.

(3)

Apply grain inference
: Use the appropriate grain inference rule from Table
2
(which includes Theorem
5.1
for equi-joins and rules for all other relational algebra operations) to compute expected result grain for each transformation.

(4)

Verify grain consistency
: Compare expected grain with actual result grain to verify correctness.

(5)

Detect violations
: Identify discrepancies indicating potential bugs or incorrect transformations.

This methodology enables
correctness verification before testing
—bugs are detected at design time, not after expensive pipeline executions. For enterprise pipelines processing terabytes of data, this represents a fundamental shift from “run and debug” to “verify and deploy.”

6.1.2.
Illustrative Example: Pipeline Design Validation

Consider a data engineer tasked with producing a sales report showing aggregate figures (total sales count and amount) per customer and date.

Step 1 (Identify grains)
: The pipeline involves three types—two sources and one target:

•

SalesChannel
(source):
G[R_{1}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{Date}

•

SalesProduct
(source):
G[R_{2}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{Date}

•

SalesReport
(target):
G[Target]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}

Step 2 (Determine grain relations)
: The engineer must establish grain relations between all types—both among sources and between sources and target.

•

Source-to-source
: Since
G[R_{1}]
contains
ChannelId
(absent from
G[R_{2}]
) and
G[R_{2}]
contains
ProductId
(absent from
G[R_{1}]
), neither is a subset of the other:
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
(incomparable grains).

•

Source-to-target
: Both
G[Target]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{1}]
and
G[Target]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{2}]
, meaning
R_{1}\leq_{g}Target
and
R_{2}\leq_{g}Target
. The sources are at finer grains than the target—aggregation is required.

The engineer considers three pipeline alternatives:

Pipeline 1: Direct join.

Join
C\ R_{1}\bowtie_{J_{k}}C\ R_{2}
where
J_{k}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
.

Step 3 (Apply grain inference)
: Using Theorem
5.1
with
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
(Case A applies):

G[Res]=\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}

Step 4 (Verify grain consistency)
:
G[Res]\neq G[Target]
—the result is at a finer grain than required.
Step 5
: The methodology detects a
grain mismatch
: direct join alone cannot produce the target report.

Pipeline 2: Direct join, then aggregate.

First join, then group by
CustomerId
,
Date
with
SUM
aggregations.

Step 3
: The join produces
G[Res_{join}]=\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{ProductId}\times\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}
. The grouping operation yields
G[Res_{final}]=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{Date}=G[Target]
.

Step 4 (Verify grain consistency)
:
G[Res_{final}]=G[Target]
—the final grain matches!

Step 5 (Detect violations)
: The methodology detects a
metrics duplication hazard
by comparing intermediate grains. Observe that
G[Res_{join}]<_{g}G[R_{1}]
and
G[Res_{join}]<_{g}G[R_{2}]
(the join result is
finer
than both inputs). This triggers the following grain-theoretic rule:

Metrics Duplication Rule
: If a join produces
G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{i}]
(result finer than input
R_{i}
), then each row from
R_{i}
appears multiple times in the result. Aggregating any metric from
R_{i}
after the join will sum duplicated values, causing double-counting.

Since both
G[Res_{join}]<_{g}G[R_{1}]
and
G[Res_{join}]<_{g}G[R_{2}]
, metrics from
both
inputs are duplicated. The methodology flags this as a
fan trap
(Section
6.2.1
) without requiring the engineer to visualize the Cartesian product—it follows mechanically from grain comparison.

Pipeline 3: Aggregate first, then join.

First aggregate each source to the target grain, then join:

Complementary Analysis: Element Preservation.

Grain analysis verifies structural correctness (type-level): the result has the expected grain. Element preservation (data-level completeness) is a complementary concern that grain theory helps identify. At the type level,

should

C\ R_{1}^{\prime}\bowtie^{full}_{J_{k}}C\ R_{2}^{\prime}

6.1.4.
Encoding Grain in the Type System

Grain verification involves two complementary aspects:
grain computation
(determining what grain a transformation produces) and
grain enforcement
(ensuring data is used correctly given its grain). Grain computation applies the inference formulas from Section
5
using set operations on field names—this can be implemented in any language and answers “what grain does this transformation produce?” Grain enforcement, in contrast, requires encoding grain in the type system itself, making the compiler automatically catch violations where functions receive data with incorrect grain—this answers “is this data being used correctly given its grain?” Together, they enable zero-cost verification: computation happens at compile/build time without data processing, while enforcement prevents grain mismatches before code execution. The grain inference formulas (Table
2
) provide the computational foundation, while type-level encoding provides the enforcement mechanism.
The power of grain-aware type checking depends on the expressiveness of the underlying type system. We identify a
progressive power spectrum
across language categories:

(1)

No type support
(SQL): Grain checking requires external static analyzers operating on comment annotations or metadata
(
Chaudhuri and Vardi 2014
)
. No compile-time guarantees from the language itself.

(2)

Declarative metadata
(dbt): Grain definitions in YAML configurations enable compile-time macro validation
(
dbt Labs 2024
)
. Custom tests enforce grain constraints before query execution.

(3)

Gradual typing with generics
(Python/PySpark): Parameterized types like
GrainedDataFrame[G]
enable optional static checking via tools like mypy
(
Lehtosalo et al. 2024
)
, with runtime validation as a safety net. Gradual typing
(
Siek and Taha 2007
)
allows incremental adoption of type annotations. We provide a complete implementation demonstrating this approach (see Section
8
).

(4)

Strong static typing
(Haskell, Scala): Phantom type parameters, type families
(
Peyton Jones et al. 2007
)
, and GADTs
(
Peyton Jones et al. 2006
)
enable compile-time grain computation and verification with zero runtime overhead. Scala’s type system
(
Odersky et al. 2014
)
provides similar capabilities through higher-kinded types.

(5)

Dependent types
(Lean 4, Agda): Grain relations like
IsGrainOf G R
carry mathematical proofs of isomorphism, enabling formal verification with machine-checkable correctness guarantees. Lean 4
(
de Moura and Ullrich 2021
)
and Agda
(
Team 2024
)
support dependent types where types can depend on values, enabling rigorous grain verification. We provide equivalent implementations in both languages using type classes (see Section
8
).

At the highest level, dependent type systems support three complementary encoding patterns: (1)
parameterized types
(
DataFrame Grain
) where grain is a type parameter, (2)
type classes
that enable automatic grain inference via instance resolution, and (3)
dependent records
(
DataWithGrain
) that bundle data with its grain proof. Each pattern trades off explicitness against automation.
The key insight is that
any
type system supporting parameterized types can encode grain at the type level, enabling compile-time verification. More expressive systems add formal proofs, but even simple generic types (Python’s
Generic[G]
) catch grain mismatches before runtime. This democratizes grain-aware development: production systems can use practical encodings (Python, Scala) while formal verification uses rigorous systems (Lean 4). Our artifacts (Section
8
) provide concrete implementations demonstrating all three approaches: Python with
GrainedDataFrame[G]
, Lean 4 with type classes, and Agda with equivalent type class patterns, all enabling compile-time grain verification.

Fan Traps (Metrics Duplication).

A fan trap
(
Kimball Group 2024
;
dbt Labs 2024
;
sho 2024
)
occurs when a join produces a Cartesian product between incompatible grain levels, causing row multiplication that duplicates metrics upon aggregation. In dimensional modeling, this typically arises when joining fact tables with many-to-many relationships through shared dimensions. The fan trap is one of the most common and costly bugs in data warehouse queries, as it silently produces incorrect aggregate results that may pass basic validation checks.

Grain-Theoretic Characterization
: Given collections
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
joined on
J_{k}
producing
C\ Res
, a fan trap occurs when the result grain is strictly finer than an input grain:

G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{i}]\quad\text{for some }i\in\{1,2\}

This strict grain ordering (
<_{g}
) means each row from
R_{i}
appears multiple times in
Res
, causing metrics from
R_{i}
to be duplicated. The
Metrics Duplication Rule
(Section
6.1.2
) formalizes this relationship between grain ordering and data semantics.

Detection via Grain Inference
:

(1)

Apply Theorem
5.1
to compute
G[Res]
from
G[R_{1}]
,
G[R_{2}]
, and
J_{k}

(2)

Check if
G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{1}]
or
G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{2}]
using the grain ordering relation

(3)

If either condition holds, a fan trap exists—
detection completes without data processing

(4)

Incomparable grains (
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}
) are a strong indicator: such joins typically produce
G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{1}]\land G[Res]<_{g}G[R_{2}]
, meaning metrics from
both
sides are duplicated

Example
: The illustrative example (Section
6.1.2
) demonstrates fan trap detection. Joining
SalesChannel
(
G=\texttt{CustomerId}\times\texttt{ChannelId}\times\texttt{Date}

SalesProduct

yields:

Grain inference reveals
G[Res]<_{g}G[\texttt{SalesChannel}]
(channel counts duplicated per product) and
G[Res]<_{g}G[\texttt{SalesProduct}]
(product counts duplicated per channel). Grain theory detects this fan trap
before any data processing
, enabling prevention through pipeline redesign.

Prevention
: Aggregate each input to the target grain
before

G[R_{i}^{\prime}]=G[Target]\quad\Rightarrow\quad G[Res]\not<_{g}G[R_{i}^{\prime}]

This guarantees no row multiplication occurs, eliminating metrics duplication. Pipeline 3 in the illustrative example demonstrates this pattern.

Chasm Traps (Data Loss from Incomplete Join Paths).

A chasm trap
(
dat 2019
;
Sisense 2024
;
ThoughtSpot 2024
;
dat 2024
)
occurs in normalized dimension hierarchies (common in snowflake schemas) when joining through intermediate tables with nullable foreign keys. INNER JOINs along such chains cause data loss by eliminating rows where the foreign key is NULL, creating a “chasm” (gap) in the join path. This silently loses significant portions of the dataset, often without obvious symptoms in query results.

Grain-Theoretic Characterization
: A chasm trap manifests in join chains
R_{1}\rightarrow R_{mid}\rightarrow R_{end}
when:

(1)

A grain ordering chain exists:
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{mid}\leq_{g}R_{end}
(each table is progressively coarser-grained)

(2)

A foreign key in
R_{mid}
referencing
R_{end}
is nullable, making the
dependency function

f:R_{mid}\rightarrow R_{end}

partial
(undefined for NULL values)

(3)

INNER JOINs from
R_{1}
through
R_{mid}
to
R_{end}
eliminate all
R_{1}
rows where the dependency function is undefined

The partial dependency function breaks the
transitivity property
of the partial order
\leq_{g}
: although
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{mid}
and
R_{mid}\leq_{g}R_{end}
hold,
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{end}
holds only partially (fails for rows with NULL foreign keys).

Detection via Grain Theory
:

(1)

Identify join chains
R_{1}\rightarrow R_{mid}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow R_{end}
in normalized dimension hierarchies

(2)

Verify grain ordering: check that
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{mid}\leq_{g}\cdots\leq_{g}R_{end}

(3)

Analyze foreign key constraints: detect nullable foreign keys that make grain functions partial

(4)

If nullable foreign keys exist in the chain, INNER JOINs will cause data loss—a chasm trap exists

Example
: Consider a normalized dimension hierarchy
Sales

\rightarrow

Product

\rightarrow

Category
where:

•

Sales.ProductId
is NOT NULL (every sale has a product)

•

Product.CategoryId
is nullable (uncategorized products exist)

•

Grain ordering:
\texttt{Sales}\leq_{g}\texttt{Product}\leq_{g}\texttt{Category}

The query:

SELECT SUM(s.Amount), c.CategoryName
FROM Sales s
INNER JOIN Product p ON s.ProductId = p.ProductId
INNER JOIN Category c ON p.CategoryId = c.CategoryId
GROUP BY c.CategoryName

loses all sales for uncategorized products
(where
p.CategoryId IS NULL
). The intermediate join
Product

\bowtie

Category
drops products with NULL
CategoryId
, and subsequent aggregation omits their sales entirely. Grain analysis reveals the partial grain function from
Product
to
Category
, flagging the chasm trap before execution.

Prevention
:

•

Use LEFT OUTER JOINs to preserve rows with NULL foreign keys:
LEFT JOIN Category c ON p.CategoryId = c.CategoryId

•

Enforce referential integrity (NOT NULL + FOREIGN KEY) to make grain functions total, eliminating the possibility of NULL foreign keys

•

Use “unknown” surrogate keys
(
Kimball 1996
)
: replace NULL foreign keys with a special key value pointing to an “Unknown” entity row in the parent table (e.g.,
CategoryId = -1
for uncategorized products). This Kimball best practice makes dependency functions total while preserving all rows during INNER JOINs

•

Denormalize dimension hierarchies to avoid intermediate tables (star schema instead of snowflake)

Grain Theory’s Contribution
: Unlike traditional detection methods (manual ER diagram inspection, runtime testing, or post-execution debugging), grain theory
systematically identifies
partial dependency functions through zero-cost, type-level analysis of schema constraints. By analyzing whether foreign key columns permit NULLs (schema-level constraint checking, not data inspection) in grain ordering chains, the formalism reveals chasm traps as violations of dependency function totality, enabling principled detection before any query execution or data processing.

6.2.2.
Verification of AI-Generated Pipelines

With the rise of AI-assisted development (large language models, coding assistants, agentic workflows), data engineers increasingly rely on AI agents to generate SQL queries, Spark transformations, or dbt models. While AI can produce syntactically correct code, it may introduce subtle grain-related bugs—incorrect joins, missing aggregations, or fan traps—that are difficult to detect through inspection alone. Grain theory provides a
formal verification layer
: by computing the expected grain at each transformation step and comparing it to the target grain, we obtain a mathematical proof of correctness independent of the AI’s reasoning. We have demonstrated this capability through machine-checkable formal proofs in Lean 4 (Section
6.1.3
), where all three pipeline alternatives are formally verified before any data processing. This enables “trust but verify” workflows where AI-generated pipelines can be deployed with confidence, knowing they have passed rigorous grain-based verification.

6.2.3.
Pipeline Migration and Refactoring Correctness

When migrating data pipelines between platforms (e.g., from on-premises to cloud, or from one cloud vendor to another), grain theory provides a verification framework: if the migrated pipeline produces the same grain at each node as the original, semantic equivalence is preserved. Similarly, when refactoring complex SQL queries for performance optimization, grain analysis ensures the refactored version maintains correctness.

6.2.4.
Schema Evolution Validation

When source schemas change (new columns, renamed fields, modified relationships), grain analysis identifies which downstream transformations are affected. If a change alters a table’s grain or grain relationships, all dependent transformations must be re-verified.

6.2.5.
Incremental Development and Code Review

As new transformation steps are added to a pipeline, each addition can be verified in isolation: does the new step produce the expected grain given its inputs? This enables continuous verification during development. During code review, grain annotations serve as executable documentation that reviewers can validate.

6.2.6.
Data Catalog Integration

Grain metadata (
G[R]
for each table, grain relationships between tables) can be integrated into enterprise data catalogs, enabling self-service analytics users to understand table granularity and avoid common join pitfalls without deep technical knowledge.

Conclusion

7.1.
Summary

This paper introduces grain theory, the first formal, mathematical framework for systematic verification of data transformation correctness through type-level grain reasoning. We formalize grain—previously existing only as an informal concept in Kimball’s dimensional modeling—into a universal type-theoretic framework applicable to any data type. By encoding grain into the type system, we enable compile-time verification of whether any transformation or pipeline DAG produces correct results, fundamentally shifting verification from runtime to design time.
Our key contributions are: (1) formal grain definition with three fundamental relations (equality, ordering, incomparability) capturing all grain relationships, (2) grain inference theorem that systematically computes result grains for any transformation through zero-cost schema analysis, (3) systematic correctness verification enabling comprehensive detection of
all
grain-related errors at design time through type-level analysis—including well-known pathologies (fan traps causing metrics duplication, chasm traps causing data loss) as special cases of grain relation violations, as well as arbitrary grain inconsistencies in complex pipeline DAGs, and (4) machine-checkable formal verification demonstrated through Lean 4 proofs.

7.2.
Main Result: Zero-Cost Verification Paradigm

The central insight of grain theory is that
grain is the essential characteristic that matters
in data transformations. By focusing on grain alone rather than tracking all data details, engineers can verify pipeline correctness through type-level analysis—without processing any data.
This enables a paradigm shift from “run and debug” to “verify and deploy.” Traditional debugging requires 3–7 expensive pipeline executions plus 4–12 hours of engineering time per bug ($415–$2,750), with no guarantees that all grain errors are caught. Grain theory detects all grain inconsistencies at design time through schema analysis alone ($8–$50 per pipeline), achieving
98–99% cost reduction
. When grain bugs reach production, costs escalate to $5,000–$50,000 in incident response plus potential millions in business impact. Type-level verification eliminates this production risk entirely.

7.3.
Impact in the AI Era

The timing of grain theory is critical. Large language models now generate data pipelines at scale, requiring machine-checkable correctness guarantees before deployment. Manual review of AI-generated code is impractical. Grain theory provides the missing verification layer: formal proofs of correctness independent of the AI’s reasoning.
Crucially, the barrier to formal verification has historically been too high (requiring expertise in proof assistants like Coq or Lean), limiting adoption to academic settings. In the AI era, large language models can
generate

writing proofs

verifying proofs

This democratizes formal verification: data engineers without theorem-proving expertise can now obtain machine-checkable correctness guarantees for their pipelines. 7.5.
Future Work

Several promising research directions extend grain theory:

Entity-aware data integration
: While this work focuses on grain inference within transformations, future research could explore entity-aware integration across types with different grains. The entity key

Streaming and temporal data

Probabilistic grain reasoning
: Handling uncertain or approximate data where grain relationships may be probabilistic rather than deterministic, enabling grain verification for machine learning pipelines and data quality scenarios.

Grain theory for non-relational data models
: While grain theory applies universally to any data type (as established in Section
3

Future work includes developing grain inference formulas for graph operations (MATCH, traversals), document operations ($unwind, $group), and demonstrating grain-based trap detection in NoSQL contexts.

Comprehensive pipeline verification framework
: While grain theory addresses granularity correctness, complete pipeline verification requires formalizing additional concepts that have existed informally in data warehousing for decades. Future work includes developing formal mathematical foundations for: (1)
ordering dependencies

temporal semantics

lineage constraints

Tool integration

Appendix A
Proof of Grain Uniqueness Theorem

Theorem A.1 (Grain Uniqueness).

If
G
is the grain of
R
, then
G
uniquely identifies any element
r:R
. In other words, for any
r_{1}\ r_{2}:R
such that
grain\ r_{1}=grain\ r_{2}
it follows that
r_{1}=r_{2}
, which means that the grain projection
grain:R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R]
is injective.

Proof.

From the definition of grain (Definition
3.3
), the grain function
f_{g}:G[R]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
is an isomorphism. The grain projection
grain:R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R]
is the inverse of
f_{g}
, i.e.,
grain=f_{g}^{-1}
. Since
f_{g}
is an isomorphism, its inverse
grain
is also an isomorphism. Because
grain
is an isomorphism, it is injective. As a result,
grain
being injective indicates that
G
uniquely identifies any element
r:R
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Multiple Grains Isomorphism Theorem

Theorem B.1 (Multiple Grains Isomorphism).

If
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are both grains of
R
, then
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic sets.

Proof.

To prove that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic sets, we need to show that there exists an isomorphism
g_{12}:G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G_{2}
.
Since
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are grains of
R
, by the definition of grain, there exist isomorphisms:

•

f_{g1}:G_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
(the grain function for
G_{1}
)

•

f_{g2}:G_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
(the grain function for
G_{2}
)

Since
f_{g1}
and
f_{g2}
are isomorphisms, they have inverse functions that are also isomorphisms:

•

f_{g1}^{-1}:R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G_{1}

•

f_{g2}^{-1}:R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G_{2}

Consider the composition of functions:

g_{12}=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ f_{g1}:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}

To show that
g_{12}
is an isomorphism, we need to show it has an inverse. The inverse of
g_{12}
is:

g_{12}^{-1}=f_{g1}^{-1}\circ f_{g2}:G_{2}\rightarrow G_{1}

We verify that
g_{12}^{-1}
is indeed the inverse:

\displaystyle g_{12}\circ g_{12}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(f_{g2}^{-1}\circ f_{g1})\circ(f_{g1}^{-1}\circ f_{g2})

\displaystyle=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ(f_{g1}\circ f_{g1}^{-1})\circ f_{g2}

\displaystyle=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ id_{R}\circ f_{g2}

\displaystyle=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ f_{g2}

\displaystyle=id_{G_{2}}

Similarly,
g_{12}^{-1}\circ g_{12}=id_{G_{1}}
. Therefore,
g_{12}
is an isomorphism, and
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic sets.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Grain Operator Idempotency Theorem

Theorem C.1 (Grain Operator Idempotency).

If
G[R]
is the grain of
R
, then
G[G[R]]=G[R]
. In other words, the grain of a type that is a grain is the type itself.

Proof.

Let
G=G[R]
be the grain of
R
. To prove
G[G[R]]=G[R]
, it suffices to show
G[G]=G
.
By Definition
3.3
, if
G^{\prime}
is the grain of
G
, then:

•

G^{\prime}\subseteq_{typ}G

•

G^{\prime}\cong G
(i.e., there exists an isomorphism
f_{g^{\prime}}:G^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G
)

•

G^{\prime}
is irreducible

Since
G\subseteq_{typ}R
and
G
is irreducible as the grain of
R
, no proper subset of
G
can serve as the grain of
R
. If
G^{\prime}\subset_{typ}G
(proper subset), then
G^{\prime}\subset_{typ}R
. However, since
G^{\prime}\cong G
and
G\cong R
(by the grain definition), we have
G^{\prime}\cong R
by transitivity of isomorphism. This would contradict the irreducibility of
G
as the grain of
R
, because
G^{\prime}
would be a proper subset of
G
that is isomorphic to
R
.
Therefore,
G^{\prime}
cannot be a proper subset of
G
, so
G^{\prime}=G
. Hence
G[G]=G
, and by substitution
G[G[R]]=G[R]
.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of The Grain of Product Types Theorem

Theorem D.1 (The Grain of Product Types).

The grain of a product type
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
is the product of the grains of the component types
R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{n}
. In other words,

G[R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}].

Proof.

Let
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
be a product type. We need to show that
G[R]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
by verifying both conditions of Definition
3.3
: isomorphism and irreducibility.

Isomorphism

: We need to show that
G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
and
R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
are isomorphic sets.
By Definition
3.3
, for each
R_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
), there exists an isomorphism
f_{gi}:G[R_{i}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{i}
(the grain function for
R_{i}
).
We construct an isomorphism
f_{g}:G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
as the product of the grain functions:

f_{g}(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n})=(f_{g1}(g_{1}),f_{g2}(g_{2}),\ldots,f_{gn}(g_{n}))

where
g_{i}:G[R_{i}]
for each
i
.
Since each
f_{gi}
is an isomorphism, it has an inverse
f_{gi}^{-1}:R_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{i}]
. The inverse of
f_{g}
is:

f_{g}^{-1}(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n})=(f_{g1}^{-1}(r_{1}),f_{g2}^{-1}(r_{2}),\ldots,f_{gn}^{-1}(r_{n}))

where
r_{i}:R_{i}
for each
i
.
We verify that
f_{g}^{-1}
is indeed the inverse:

\displaystyle f_{g}\circ f_{g}^{-1}(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n})

\displaystyle=f_{g}(f_{g1}^{-1}(r_{1}),f_{g2}^{-1}(r_{2}),\ldots,f_{gn}^{-1}(r_{n}))

\displaystyle=(f_{g1}(f_{g1}^{-1}(r_{1})),f_{g2}(f_{g2}^{-1}(r_{2})),\ldots,f_{gn}(f_{gn}^{-1}(r_{n})))

\displaystyle=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n})

Similarly,
f_{g}^{-1}\circ f_{g}=id_{G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]}
.
Therefore,
f_{g}
is an isomorphism, establishing that
G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]\cong R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
.

Irreducibility

: We need to show that no proper subset of
G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
can serve as the grain of
R
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a proper subset
G^{\prime}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
such that
G^{\prime}
is the grain of
R
. Then
G^{\prime}\cong R
by the grain definition.
Since
G^{\prime}
is a proper subset, there exists at least one component
i
(
1\leq i\leq n
) such that the projection of
G^{\prime}
onto the
i
-th component is a proper subset of
G[R_{i}]
, or
G^{\prime}
omits at least one component entirely.
However, since
G^{\prime}\cong R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
and each
G[R_{i}]
is irreducible (by Definition
3.3
), any proper subset of
G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
cannot be isomorphic to the full product
R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
. This is because:

•

If
G^{\prime}
omits a component
G[R_{i}]
entirely, then
G^{\prime}
cannot be isomorphic to
R
since
R
contains
R_{i}
as a component.

•

If
G^{\prime}
has a proper subset of
G[R_{i}]
in the
i
-th component, then by the irreducibility of
G[R_{i}]
, this proper subset cannot be isomorphic to
R_{i}
, and thus
G^{\prime}
cannot be isomorphic to
R
.

This contradicts the assumption that
G^{\prime}
is the grain of
R
. Therefore,
G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
is irreducible and is the grain of
R
.
Hence,
G[R_{1}\times R_{2}\times\cdots\times R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\cdots\times G[R_{n}]
.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of The Grain of Sum Types Theorem

Theorem E.1 (The Grain of Sum Types).

The grain of a sum type
R=R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
is the sum of the grains of the component types
R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{n}
. In other words,

G[R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}].

Proof.

Let
R=R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
be a sum type (disjoint union). We need to show that
G[R]=G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
by verifying both conditions of Definition
3.3
: isomorphism and irreducibility.

Isomorphism

: We need to show that
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
and
R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
are isomorphic sets.
By Definition
3.3
, for each
R_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
), there exists an isomorphism
f_{gi}:G[R_{i}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{i}
(the grain function for
R_{i}
).
We construct an isomorphism
f_{g}:G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
by case analysis on the sum type:

f_{g}(g)=\begin{cases}f_{g1}(g)&\text{if }g:G[R_{1}]\\
f_{g2}(g)&\text{if }g:G[R_{2}]\\
\vdots\\
f_{gn}(g)&\text{if }g:G[R_{n}]\end{cases}

where
g
is an element of the sum type
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
.
Since each
f_{gi}
is an isomorphism, it has an inverse
f_{gi}^{-1}:R_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{i}]
. The inverse of
f_{g}
is:

f_{g}^{-1}(r)=\begin{cases}f_{g1}^{-1}(r)&\text{if }r:R_{1}\\
f_{g2}^{-1}(r)&\text{if }r:R_{2}\\
\vdots\\
f_{gn}^{-1}(r)&\text{if }r:R_{n}\end{cases}

where
r
is an element of the sum type
R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
.
We verify that
f_{g}^{-1}
is indeed the inverse. For any
r:R_{i}
(where
1\leq i\leq n
):

\displaystyle f_{g}\circ f_{g}^{-1}(r)

\displaystyle=f_{g}(f_{gi}^{-1}(r))

\displaystyle=f_{gi}(f_{gi}^{-1}(r))

\displaystyle=r

Similarly, for any
g:G[R_{i}]
:

\displaystyle f_{g}^{-1}\circ f_{g}(g)

\displaystyle=f_{g}^{-1}(f_{gi}(g))

\displaystyle=f_{gi}^{-1}(f_{gi}(g))

\displaystyle=g

Therefore,
f_{g}
is an isomorphism, establishing that
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]\cong R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
.

Irreducibility

: We need to show that no proper subset of
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
can serve as the grain of
R
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a proper subset
G^{\prime}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
such that
G^{\prime}
is the grain of
R
. Then
G^{\prime}\cong R
by the grain definition.
Since
G^{\prime}
is a proper subset of the sum type, there exists at least one component
i
(
1\leq i\leq n
) such that
G^{\prime}
omits some elements from
G[R_{i}]
, or
G^{\prime}
omits the entire component
G[R_{i}]
.
However, since
G^{\prime}\cong R=R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
and each
G[R_{i}]
is irreducible (by Definition
3.3
), any proper subset of
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
cannot be isomorphic to the full sum
R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}
. This is because:

•

If
G^{\prime}
omits the entire component
G[R_{i}]
, then
G^{\prime}
cannot be isomorphic to
R
since
R
contains
R_{i}
as a component, and there would be no way to map elements from
R_{i}
to
G^{\prime}
.

•

If
G^{\prime}
has a proper subset of
G[R_{i}]
(i.e.,
G^{\prime}\cap G[R_{i}]\subset_{typ}G[R_{i}]
), then by the irreducibility of
G[R_{i}]
, this proper subset cannot be isomorphic to
R_{i}
. Since
R
contains
R_{i}
as a component,
G^{\prime}
cannot be isomorphic to
R
.

This contradicts the assumption that
G^{\prime}
is the grain of
R
. Therefore,
G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
is irreducible and is the grain of
R
.
Hence,
G[R_{1}+R_{2}+\cdots+R_{n}]=G[R_{1}]+G[R_{2}]+\cdots+G[R_{n}]
.
∎

Appendix F
Proof of Grain Equality Theorem

Theorem F.1 (Grain Equality Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
,
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
if and only if
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets.

Proof.

This is a biconditional statement, so we need to prove both directions.

Forward direction (
\Rightarrow
):

If
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
, then there exists an isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
(i.e.,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets).
From the definition of grain equality, if
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
, then there exists an isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
between their grains.
From the definition of grain, we know that:

•

G[R_{1}]
and
R_{1}
are isomorphic sets, which means there exists an isomorphism
f_{g1}:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}
(the grain function for
R_{1}
)

•

G[R_{2}]
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets, which means there exists an isomorphism
f_{g2}:G[R_{2}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
(the grain function for
R_{2}
)

Since
f_{g1}
and
f_{g2}
are isomorphisms, their inverse functions
f_{g1}^{-1}:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{1}]
and
f_{g2}^{-1}:R_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
also exist and are isomorphisms.
Now, we construct the isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
as the composition:

h=f_{g2}\circ f\circ f_{g1}^{-1}:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}

This function
h
takes an element
r_{1}:R_{1}
, maps it to its grain value in
G[R_{1}]
via
f_{g1}^{-1}
, then maps that grain value to a grain value in
G[R_{2}]
via
f
, and finally maps that grain value to an element in
R_{2}
via
f_{g2}
.
Since
h
is a composition of three isomorphisms (
f_{g2}
,
f
, and
f_{g1}^{-1}
), it is itself an isomorphism. Therefore, we have constructed the required isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
, establishing that
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets.

Backward direction (
\Leftarrow
):

If there exists an isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
(i.e.,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets), then
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
.
Assume that
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets, meaning there exists an isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
.
From the definition of grain, we know that:

•

G[R_{1}]
and
R_{1}
are isomorphic sets, with isomorphism
f_{g1}:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}

•

G[R_{2}]
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets, with isomorphism
f_{g2}:G[R_{2}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}

Since
h
is an isomorphism, its inverse
h^{-1}:R_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}
exists and is also an isomorphism. Since
f_{g2}
is an isomorphism, its inverse
f_{g2}^{-1}:R_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
exists and is also an isomorphism.
Now, we construct the isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
as the composition:

f=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ h\circ f_{g1}:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]

This function
f
is a composition of three isomorphisms (
f_{g2}^{-1}
,
h
, and
f_{g1}
), and is therefore an isomorphism.
Since we have constructed an isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
between the grains of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, by the definition of grain equality, we have
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
.
Therefore,
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
if and only if there exists an isomorphism
h:R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
(i.e.,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are isomorphic sets).
∎

Appendix G
Proof of Grain Equality Preservation Corollary

Corollary G.1 (Grain Equality Preservation).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:

R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]

Proof.

This is a biconditional statement, so we prove both directions.

Forward direction (
\Rightarrow
):

Assume
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
. By the definition of grain equality, there exists an isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
.
To show
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
, we need an isomorphism
h:G[G[R_{1}]]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[G[R_{2}]]
.
By the Grain Operator Idempotency Theorem,
G[G[R_{1}]]=G[R_{1}]
and
G[G[R_{2}]]=G[R_{2}]
.
Therefore, the isomorphism
f:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
is exactly the required isomorphism
h:G[G[R_{1}]]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[G[R_{2}]]
.
By the definition of grain equality,
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
.

Backward direction (
\Leftarrow
):

Assume
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
. By the definition of grain equality, there exists an isomorphism
h:G[G[R_{1}]]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[G[R_{2}]]
.
By the Grain Operator Idempotency Theorem,
G[G[R_{1}]]=G[R_{1}]
and
G[G[R_{2}]]=G[R_{2}]
.
Therefore,
h:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[R_{2}]
is an isomorphism between the grains of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
.
By the definition of grain equality,
R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
.
∎

Appendix H
Proof of Grain Ordering Theorem

Theorem H.1 (Grain Ordering Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
,
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
if and only if there exists a function
h:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
that establishes a one-to-many correspondence between the elements of the two types.

Proof.

This proof follows the same structure as the Grain Equality Theorem proof, with the key difference that we work with functions rather than isomorphisms.

Forward direction (
\Rightarrow
):

If
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
, then by definition there exists a function
f:G[R_{1}]\rightarrow G[R_{2}]
. Using the grain function isomorphisms
f_{g1}:G[R_{1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{1}
and
f_{g2}:G[R_{2}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{2}
, we construct:

h=f_{g2}\circ f\circ f_{g1}^{-1}:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}

Since
f_{g1}^{-1}
and
f_{g2}
are isomorphisms and
f
is a function,
h
is a function. The one-to-many correspondence follows from
f
not being injective in general.

Backward direction (
\Leftarrow
):

If there exists a function
h:R_{1}\rightarrow R_{2}
, we construct:

f=f_{g2}^{-1}\circ h\circ f_{g1}:G[R_{1}]\rightarrow G[R_{2}]

Since
f_{g1}
and
f_{g2}^{-1}
are isomorphisms and
h
is a function,
f
is a function. By the definition of grain ordering,
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}
.
∎

Proof of Grain Ordering Preservation Corollary

Corollary I.1 (Grain Ordering Preservation).

and
R_{2}
:

.

.

Appendix J
Proof of Grain Subset Theorem

Theorem J.1 (Grain Subset Theorem).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, if
G[R_{1}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{2}]
, then
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
.

Proof.

From the definition of type-level subset relation (Definition in Section
3
), if
G[R_{1}]\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{2}]
, then there exists a surjective projection function
p:G[R_{2}]\rightarrow G[R_{1}]
.
By the definition of grain ordering,
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
if and only if there exists a function
f:G[R_{2}]\rightarrow G[R_{1}]
.
Since the projection function
p:G[R_{2}]\rightarrow G[R_{1}]
exists and is a function, we have
R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}
.
∎

Appendix K
Proof of Grain Determines All Type Subsets Corollary

Corollary K.1 (Grain Determines All Type Subsets).

If
G
is the grain of
R
, then for any
R^{\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}\subseteq_{typ}R
, we have
G\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
. In other words, the grain of a type functionally determines any subset of that type’s fields.

Proof.

We prove this by constructing a function from
G
to
R^{\prime}
, which by the definition of grain ordering establishes
G\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
.
Since
G
is the grain of
R
, there exists an isomorphism
f_{g}:G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R
(the grain function). Since
f_{g}
is a function from
G
to
R
, we have
G\leq_{g}R
.
Since
R^{\prime}\subseteq_{typ}R
, there exists a surjective projection function
p:R\rightarrow R^{\prime}
. Since
p
is a function from
R
to
R^{\prime}
, we have
R\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
.
By transitivity of grain ordering, if
G\leq_{g}R
and
R\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
, then
G\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
.
Alternatively, we can directly construct the function
p\circ f_{g}:G\rightarrow R^{\prime}
, where
f_{g}:G\rightarrow R
is the grain function and
p:R\rightarrow R^{\prime}
is the projection function. Since the composition of two functions is a function,
p\circ f_{g}
is a valid function from
G
to
R^{\prime}
, establishing that
G\leq_{g}R^{\prime}
by the definition of grain ordering.
∎

Appendix L
Proof of Grain Inference Theorem

Theorem L.1 (Grain Inference).

For any data type
R
, if there exists a type
G
such that:

(1)

G\subseteq_{typ}R
(G is a type-level subset of R)

(2)

G\leq_{g}R
(G has lower grain than R)

(3)

G[G]=G
(G is its own grain, i.e., irreducible)

then
G
is a grain of
R
, i.e.,
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
.

Proof.

We show that
G
is grain-equivalent to
G[R]
by establishing both
G\leq_{g}G[R]
and
G[R]\leq_{g}G
, then applying antisymmetry.

Step 1: Establish
G\leq_{g}G[R]
.

From condition 2,
G\leq_{g}R
. By the Grain Ordering Preservation Corollary (Section
4
), this implies
G[G]\leq_{g}G[R]
. Since
G[G]=G
(condition 3), we have:

G\leq_{g}G[R]

Step 2: Establish
G[R]\leq_{g}G
.

From condition 1,
G\subseteq_{typ}R
, meaning there exists a surjective projection
p:R\rightarrow G
. By the Grain Subset Theorem, if
G\subseteq_{typ}R
, then
R\leq_{g}G
. Applying the Grain Ordering Preservation Corollary:

G[R]\leq_{g}G[G]=G

Step 3: Apply antisymmetry to conclude
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
.

From Steps 1 and 2, we have
G\leq_{g}G[R]
and
G[R]\leq_{g}G
. By the antisymmetry property of grain ordering (which states that
R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2}\land R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1}\Rightarrow R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2}
), we conclude:

G\equiv_{g}G[R]

Step 4: Verify
G
satisfies the grain definition.

To confirm
G
is a valid grain of
R
:

•

Isomorphism
: Since
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
and
G[R]\cong R
(by the grain definition), we have
G\cong R
by transitivity of isomorphism.

•

Irreducibility
: From condition 3,
G[G]=G
, meaning
G
is its own grain and therefore irreducible—no proper subset of
G
can serve as a grain.

Therefore,
G
satisfies both conditions of the grain definition and is a grain of
R
, with
G\equiv_{g}G[R]
.
∎

Appendix M
Proof of Grain Incomparability Preservation Theorem

Theorem M.1 (Grain Incomparability Preservation).

Given two data types
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:

R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}\Leftrightarrow G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]

Proof.

This proof follows the same pattern as the Grain Equality and Grain Ordering Preservation corollaries, using the fact that incomparability is defined as the negation of equality and ordering.

Forward direction (
\Rightarrow
):

If
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}
, then by definition:
\neg(R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2})
,
\neg(R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2})
, and
\neg(R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1})
.
By the Grain Equality Preservation Corollary:
\neg(R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2})\Rightarrow\neg(G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}])
.
By the Grain Ordering Preservation Corollary:
\neg(R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2})\Rightarrow\neg(G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}])
and
\neg(R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1})\Rightarrow\neg(G[R_{2}]\leq_{g}G[R_{1}])
.
Therefore, by the definition of grain incomparability,
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
.

Backward direction (
\Leftarrow
):

If
G[R_{1}]\langle\rangle_{g}G[R_{2}]
, then by definition:
\neg(G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}])
,
\neg(G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}])
, and
\neg(G[R_{2}]\leq_{g}G[R_{1}])
.
By the Grain Equality Preservation Corollary:
\neg(G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}])\Rightarrow\neg(R_{1}\equiv_{g}R_{2})
.
By the Grain Ordering Preservation Corollary:
\neg(G[R_{1}]\leq_{g}G[R_{2}])\Rightarrow\neg(R_{1}\leq_{g}R_{2})
and
\neg(G[R_{2}]\leq_{g}G[R_{1}])\Rightarrow\neg(R_{2}\leq_{g}R_{1})
.
Therefore, by the definition of grain incomparability,
R_{1}\langle\rangle_{g}R_{2}
.
∎

Appendix N
Proof of Grain Inference for Equi-Joins Theorem

Theorem N.1 (Grain Inference for Equi-Joins).

For any two collections
CR_{1}
and
CR_{2}
sharing common fields of type
J_{k}
(i.e.,
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
), performing an equi-join on
J_{k}
produces a result
CRes
with type
Res
and grain
G[Res]
such that:

•

Res=(R_{1}-_{typ}J_{k})\times(R_{2}-_{typ}J_{k})\times J_{k}
(the result type contains fields from
R_{1}
not in
J_{k}
, fields from
R_{2}
not in
J_{k}
, and
J_{k}
once; note that fields common to both types but not in
J_{k}
appear twice, once from each side, since their values may differ)

•

(G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}])\leq_{g}G[Res]\leq_{g}(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])

The formula depends on the relationship between the
J_{k}
-portions of the two grains. Let
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
.

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are comparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\\
\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Case B
: When neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are incomparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\\
\times((G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Proof.

We are given two collections
CR_{1}
and
CR_{2}
with common fields of type
J_{k}
, where
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
. We perform an equi-join on
J_{k}
, producing a result collection
CRes
. Each element in
Res
is a pair
(r_{1},r_{2})
where
r_{1}:R_{1}
,
r_{2}:R_{2}
, and their
J_{k}
values match.
To determine the grain of
Res
, we need to identify the minimal irreducible set of fields that uniquely identifies each element in the join result.

Step 1: Decomposing the grains

For convenience, let us denote:

•

G_{1}^{rest}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{1}]
not overlapping with
J_{k}
)

•

G_{2}^{rest}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{2}]
not overlapping with
J_{k}
)

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{1}]
that overlaps with
J_{k}
)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the part of
G[R_{2}]
that overlaps with
J_{k}
)

•

G^{J_{k}-comm}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cap_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the common part of
J_{k}
in both grains)

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}-_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(the part of
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
not in
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}-_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the part of
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
not in
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
)

Thus we have:

•

G[R_{1}]=G_{1}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}\cup_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}

•

G[R_{2}]=G_{2}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}

Step 2: The bootstrapping principle

A key observation is that knowing
G[R_{1}]
allows us to determine the full
r_{1}
tuple, which includes all
J_{k}
fields (since
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
). Due to the equi-join condition
r_{1}.J_{k}=r_{2}.J_{k}
, this also determines the
J_{k}
value on the
R_{2}
side. Symmetrically,
G[R_{2}]
determines the
J_{k}
value on both sides.
This means:

•

If
G[R_{1}]
is fully present in
G[Res]
, then
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
is redundant (it can be derived from
r_{1}.J_{k}
)

•

If
G[R_{2}]
is fully present in
G[Res]
, then
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
is redundant (it can be derived from
r_{2}.J_{k}
)

Step 3: Case analysis

Case A:
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}

Without loss of generality, assume
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
. This means
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}=\emptyset
.
Since
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}=\emptyset
, we have
G[R_{1}]=G_{1}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}
. Therefore, the candidate grain
G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G^{J_{k}-comm}
contains all of
G[R_{1}]
.
With
G[R_{1}]
fully present:

•

We can determine
r_{1}
, and thus
r_{1}.J_{k}

•

By the equi-join condition, we know
r_{2}.J_{k}=r_{1}.J_{k}

•

This includes
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
(the part of
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
not in
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
)

•

Combined with
G_{2}^{rest}
from our grain, we have all of
G[R_{2}]=G_{2}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}

•

Thus
r_{2}
is uniquely determined

Therefore,
G[Res]=G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G^{J_{k}-comm}=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})
.

Case B: Neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}

In this case, both
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}\neq\emptyset
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}\neq\emptyset
. This means:

•

G[R_{1}]=G_{1}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}\cup_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
(contains
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
)

•

G[R_{2}]=G_{2}^{rest}\cup_{typ}G^{J_{k}-comm}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
(contains
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
)

The candidate grain
G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G^{J_{k}-comm}
is a
proper subset
of both
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
:

•

It’s missing
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
from
G[R_{1}]

•

It’s missing
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
from
G[R_{2}]

This creates a circular dependency:

•

To derive
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
, we would need
G[R_{2}]
(to get
r_{2}.J_{k}
)

•

To derive
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
, we would need
G[R_{1}]
(to get
r_{1}.J_{k}
)

•

But neither
G[R_{1}]
nor
G[R_{2}]
is fully present in
G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G^{J_{k}-comm}

Therefore, both
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
must be explicitly included in the grain:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G^{J_{k}-comm}\times G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}\times G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}

This can be rewritten as:

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times((G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Step 4: Irreducibility

In both cases, we need to verify that no proper subset of the proposed grain uniquely identifies join result elements:

•

If we omit
G_{1}^{rest}
: Two elements
r_{1}
and
r_{1}^{\prime}
from
R_{1}
can differ in
G_{1}^{rest}
while sharing the same
J_{k}
value. Joining both with the same
r_{2}
produces distinct results indistinguishable without
G_{1}^{rest}
.

•

If we omit
G_{2}^{rest}
: By symmetry, we lose uniqueness.

•

If we omit
G^{J_{k}-comm}
when
G^{J_{k}-comm}\neq\emptyset
: Elements can differ only in
G^{J_{k}-comm}
, making them indistinguishable.

•

In Case B, if we omit
G_{1}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}-uncomm}
: Without one of these, we cannot fully determine the respective grain, and thus cannot uniquely identify the corresponding tuple.

Step 5: Verifying the bounds

Lower bound
(G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}])\leq_{g}G[Res]
:
The finest possible grain would require both full grains
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
. In both Case A and Case B,
G[Res]\subseteq_{typ}(G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}])
. By the grain subset theorem, if
A\subseteq_{typ}B
, then
B\leq_{g}A
. Therefore
(G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}])\leq_{g}G[Res]
.

Upper bound
G[Res]\leq_{g}(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])
:
The grain
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]
contains only fields common to both grains. Since
G[Res]
is constructed from fields that are subsets of
G[R_{1}]
and/or
G[R_{2}]
, we have
G[Res]\subseteq_{typ}(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])
. By the grain lattice structure,
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]
is the least upper bound of the two grains, and
G[Res]\leq_{g}(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])
.
∎

Appendix O
Proof of Natural Join Corollary

In Corollary 11.4 (Natural Join), we have seen that when the equi-join is a
natural join
, meaning
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}
(joining on all common fields between the two types), Theorem
5.1
applies based on the comparability of the
J_{k}
-portions.
Let
G_{1}^{unique}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}R_{2}
,
G_{2}^{unique}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}R_{1}
,
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}R_{2}
, and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}R_{1}
.

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])

Case B
: When neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

Proof.

We substitute
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}
into Theorem
5.1
’s formula.

Preliminary: Set-theoretic identity

For any types
A
and
B
, we have
A-_{typ}(A\cap_{typ}B)=A-_{typ}B
.

Justification
: Let
x\in A-_{typ}(A\cap_{typ}B)\Leftrightarrow x\in A\land x\notin(A\cap_{typ}B)\Leftrightarrow x\in A\land x\notin B\Leftrightarrow x\in A-_{typ}B
.

Step 1: Compute the
J_{k}
-portions

Since
J_{k}=R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}
:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}(R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2})=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}R_{2}
(since
G[R_{1}]\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}(R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2})=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}R_{1}
(since
G[R_{2}]\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
)

Step 2: Compute the rest portions

Using the set-theoretic identity:

•

G_{1}^{unique}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}(R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2})=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}R_{2}

•

G_{2}^{unique}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}(R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2})=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}R_{1}

Step 3: Determine which case applies

We must check whether
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
.

Case A: The
J_{k}
-portions are comparable

If
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, apply Case A of Theorem
5.1
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Since
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2}=J_{k}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}])

Case B: The
J_{k}
-portions are incomparable

If neither
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
nor
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, apply Case B of Theorem
5.1
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times((G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k})

Since
(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}(R_{1}\cap_{typ}R_{2})=(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}R_{2})\cup_{typ}(G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}R_{1})=G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
:

G[Res]=G_{1}^{unique}\times G_{2}^{unique}\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

∎

Appendix P
Proof of Equal Grains Corollary

In Corollary 11.1 (Equal Grains), we have seen that when
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
and the equi-join is performed on common fields
J_{k}
(where
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
), Case A of Theorem
5.1
always applies (since equal grains have equal
J_{k}
-portions). The relationship between
J_{k}
and the grains determines the result:

•

Case 1
: If
J_{k}=G[R_{1}]
, then
G[Res]=J_{k}

•

Case 2
: If
J_{k}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
, then
G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times J_{k}

•

Case 3
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]\neq\emptyset
but
J_{k}\not\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
, then the Case A formula from Theorem
5.1
applies

•

Case 4
: If
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]=\emptyset
, then
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]

Proof.

Why Case A always applies:

Since
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
, we have
G[R_{1}]=G[R_{2}]
(as types). Therefore:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}

Since
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}
, the
J_{k}
-portions are trivially comparable (they’re equal), so Case A of Theorem
5.1
applies.

Case 1:
J_{k}=G[R_{1}]
(joining on the full grain)

Since
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
and
J_{k}=G[R_{1}]=G[R_{2}]
, applying Case A:

•

G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}G[R_{1}]=\emptyset

•

G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}G[R_{2}]=\emptyset

•

G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]=J_{k}

Therefore:
G[Res]=\emptyset\times\emptyset\times J_{k}=J_{k}
.

Case 2:
J_{k}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
(joining on a proper subset of the grain)

Since
J_{k}\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{1}]=G[R_{2}]
, applying Case A:

•

G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}\neq\emptyset
(since
J_{k}
is a proper subset)

•

G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}\neq\emptyset

•

G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=J_{k}
(since
J_{k}\subseteq_{typ}G[R_{1}]
)

Therefore:
G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times J_{k}
.

Case 3:
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]\neq\emptyset
but
J_{k}\not\subset_{typ}G[R_{1}]
(non-empty intersection but not a subset)

Let
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
. Since
G[R_{1}]\equiv_{g}G[R_{2}]
, we have
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}
. Applying Case A:

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k})\times(G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k})=G_{1}^{rest}\times G_{2}^{rest}\times G_{1}^{J_{k}}

where
G_{1}^{rest}=G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k}
and
G_{2}^{rest}=G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k}
.

Case 4:
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]=\emptyset
(empty intersection with the grain)

Since
J_{k}\cap_{typ}G[R_{1}]=\emptyset
and
G[R_{1}]=G[R_{2}]
, applying Case A:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}=\emptyset

•

G_{1}^{rest}=G[R_{1}]

•

G_{2}^{rest}=G[R_{2}]

•

G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}=\emptyset

Therefore:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]\times\emptyset=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
.
∎

Appendix S
Proof of Generalized Grain Inference for Equi-Joins Theorem

Theorem S.1 (Generalized Grain Inference for Equi-Joins).

For any two collections of type
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
with join key types
J_{k1}
and
J_{k2}
respectively, where:

•

J_{k1}\subseteq_{typ}R_{1}
and
J_{k2}\subseteq_{typ}R_{2}
(join keys are extractable via projection)

•

J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
, i.e., there exists an isomorphism
\phi:J_{k1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}J_{k2}

Performing an
equi-join up to isomorphism
—matching rows where
\phi(\pi_{1}(r_{1}))=\pi_{2}(r_{2})
for projections
\pi_{i}:R_{i}\to J_{ki}
—produces a result
C\ Res
with type
Res
and grain
G[Res]
such that:

•

Res=(R_{1}-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(R_{2}-_{typ}J_{k2})\times J_{k}
, where
J_{k}
is a canonical representative of the isomorphism class
[J_{k1}]=[J_{k2}]
(typically
J_{k1}
or
J_{k2}
)

•

The grain
G[Res]
follows the same structure as Theorem
5.1

Let:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k1}
(the part of
G[R_{1}]
overlapping with the join key)

•

G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k2}
(the part of
G[R_{2}]
overlapping with the join key)

Case A
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are grain-comparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\\
\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

where
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
denotes the grain
meet
(the coarser of the two comparable grains).

Case B
: When
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\langle\rangle_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(the
J_{k}
-portions are grain-incomparable):

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\\
\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

where
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
denotes the grain
join
(least upper bound in the grain lattice).

Proof.

We are given two collections
C\ R_{1}
and
C\ R_{2}
with join key types
J_{k1}
and
J_{k2}
respectively, where
J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
via isomorphism
\phi:J_{k1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}J_{k2}
. We perform an equi-join up to isomorphism, matching rows where
\phi(\pi_{1}(r_{1}))=\pi_{2}(r_{2})
.
The key insight is that the isomorphism
\phi
establishes a semantic equivalence between
J_{k1}
and
J_{k2}
, allowing us to reduce the generalized case to the original theorem by working with a canonical representative
J_{k}
of the isomorphism class
[J_{k1}]=[J_{k2}]
.

Step 1: Isomorphism preserves grain relationships

Since
J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
via
\phi
, by the Grain Equality Preservation Corollary, we have
G[J_{k1}]\equiv_{g}G[J_{k2}]
. This means there exists an isomorphism
\psi:G[J_{k1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}G[J_{k2}]
that preserves the grain structure.
The join condition
\phi(\pi_{1}(r_{1}))=\pi_{2}(r_{2})
establishes a semantic equivalence: for any
j_{1}:J_{k1}
and
j_{2}:J_{k2}
where
\phi(j_{1})=j_{2}
, they represent the same logical join key value, just in different representations.

Step 2: Reduction to canonical representative

Let
J_{k}
be a canonical representative of the isomorphism class
[J_{k1}]=[J_{k2}]
(typically
J_{k1}
or
J_{k2}
). Since
J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k}
and
J_{k2}\equiv_{g}J_{k}
, we can work with
J_{k}
as the common join key type, using the isomorphisms to translate between representations.
The grain portions
G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k1}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k2}
are defined relative to their respective join key types. However, since
J_{k1}\equiv_{g}J_{k2}
, the grain portions are also grain-equivalent:
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\equiv_{g}G_{1}^{J_{k}^{\prime}}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\equiv_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}^{\prime}}
where
G_{1}^{J_{k}^{\prime}}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}^{\prime}}
are the corresponding portions relative to
J_{k}
.

Step 3: Case analysis with grain ordering

Case A:
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
or
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{1}^{J_{k}}

Without loss of generality, assume
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
. This means there exists a function
f:G_{1}^{J_{k}}\to G_{2}^{J_{k}}
establishing the grain ordering.
By the Grain Subset Theorem, if
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, then
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
follows automatically. However, the generalized case also captures functional dependency relationships where
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
without a subset relationship.
The grain
meet

G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
is defined as the coarser of the two comparable grains. Since
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
, we have
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(the coarser grain).
The bootstrapping principle from the original proof applies: if
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
is fully present in
G[Res]
, then
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
can be determined through the grain ordering relationship
f:G_{1}^{J_{k}}\to G_{2}^{J_{k}}
combined with the join condition.
Therefore:

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\times G_{2}^{J_{k}}

Since
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
(the meet), this matches the Case A formula.

Case B:
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\langle\rangle_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}

When the
J_{k}
-portions are grain-incomparable, neither can be determined from the other through grain ordering. This creates the same circular dependency as in the original proof: to determine
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, we would need
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
, and vice versa.
The grain
join

G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
is defined as the least upper bound in the grain lattice. Since
G_{1}^{J_{k}}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}
are incomparable, their join is the union:
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
.
Both portions must be explicitly included in the grain:

G[Res]=(G[R_{1}]-_{typ}J_{k1})\times(G[R_{2}]-_{typ}J_{k2})\times(G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}})

This matches the Case B formula with
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G_{1}^{J_{k}}\cup_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
.

Step 4: Reduction to original theorem

When
J_{k1}=J_{k2}=J_{k}
(identical join keys), the isomorphism
\phi
is the identity function. In this case:

•

G_{1}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
and
G_{2}^{J_{k}}=G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(same
J_{k}
)

•

The grain ordering
\leq_{g}
reduces to subset-based comparability: if
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
, then by the Grain Subset Theorem,
G_{2}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{1}^{J_{k}}
, which is equivalent to
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
in the subset case

•

The meet
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\wedge_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
reduces to
G[R_{1}]\cap_{typ}G[R_{2}]\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the intersection)

•

The join
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\vee_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
reduces to
(G[R_{1}]\cup_{typ}G[R_{2}])\cap_{typ}J_{k}
(the union)

Therefore, the generalized theorem reduces to Theorem
5.1
when join keys are identical.

Step 5: Strict generalization

By the Grain Subset Theorem,
A\subseteq_{typ}B\Rightarrow B\leq_{g}A
. Therefore, subset-based comparability (
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\subseteq_{typ}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
) implies grain-ordering comparability (
G_{1}^{J_{k}}\leq_{g}G_{2}^{J_{k}}
), but not vice versa. The generalized theorem using
\leq_{g}
is strictly more general than the original theorem using
\subseteq_{typ}
.

Step 6: Irreducibility

The irreducibility argument from the original proof applies: no proper subset of the proposed grain uniquely identifies join result elements. The isomorphism
\phi
preserves the uniqueness properties, so the grain structure is maintained across the isomorphic join keys.
∎

Appendix T
Proofs of Grain Inference for Other Operations

This section provides proofs for grain inference rules of relational operations beyond equi-joins.

T.1.
Grain Preservation under Selection

Theorem T.1 (Selection Preserves Grain).

For any collection
CR
with grain
G[R]
and any selection condition
\theta
, the result
\sigma_{\theta}(CR)
produces a collection
C\ Res
where
Res=R
and
G[Res]=G[R]
.

Proof.

Selection filters rows without changing the type structure. The result type equals
R
, so
Res=R
. Since grain is a type-level property and the type is unchanged,
G[Res]=G[R]
.
At the data level: if
G[R]
uniquely identifies elements in
CR
, then for any subset obtained by filtering,
G[R]
still uniquely identifies elements (a subset of a set with unique identifiers retains unique identifiers).
∎

T.2.
Grain Inference for Projection

Theorem T.2 (Projection Grain Inference).

For any collection
CR
with grain
G[R]
and projection
\pi_{S}(CR)
where
S\subseteq_{typ}R
, the result produces a collection
C\ Res
where
Res=S
:

•

If
G[R]\subseteq_{typ}S
, then
G[Res]=G[R]
.

•

If
G[R]\not\subseteq_{typ}S
, then without additional functional dependency information,
G[Res]=S
(the entire projected type becomes the grain).

Proof.

Case 1
:
G[R]\subseteq_{typ}S
. All grain columns are preserved in the projection. Since
G[R]
uniquely identifies elements in
CR
, the projected grain values still uniquely identify projected elements. Therefore
G[Res]=G[R]
.

Case 2
:
G[R]\not\subseteq_{typ}S
. Some grain columns are removed. This may create duplicates in the projected result. Without functional dependencies guaranteeing that a subset of
S
uniquely identifies rows, the worst case is that all columns in
S
are needed. Therefore
G[Res]=S
.
Note: In practice, if functional dependencies are known (e.g., a column in
S
determines other columns), the grain may be finer than
S
.
∎

T.3.
Grain Inference for Extension

Theorem T.3 (Extension Preserves Grain).

For any collection
CR
with grain
G[R]
, extending with a derived column
D=f(R)
where
f:R\rightarrow D
is a function, the result produces a collection
C\ Res
where
Res=R\times D
and
G[Res]=G[R]
.

Proof.

The result type is
R\times D
, so
Res=R\times D
. By the Grain of Product Types theorem,
G[Res]=G[R]\times G[D]
in general.
However, since
D=f(R)
, the value of
D
is completely determined by
R
, and thus by
G[R]
(by the Grain Uniqueness theorem,
G[R]
determines all of
R
). Therefore,
D
is functionally dependent on
G[R]
, meaning
G[D]
adds no information for unique identification.
Formally: for any two elements
(r_{1},d_{1})
and
(r_{2},d_{2})
in the extended collection, if
G[r_{1}]=G[r_{2}]
, then
r_{1}=r_{2}
(by grain uniqueness), so
d_{1}=f(r_{1})=f(r_{2})=d_{2}
. Thus
G[R]
uniquely identifies elements in the extended collection, and
G[Res]=G[R]
.
∎

T.4.
Grain Equivalence under Rename

Theorem T.4 (Rename Preserves Grain Equivalence).

For any collection
CR
with grain
G[R]
and rename operation
\rho_{a\to b}(CR)
that renames attribute
a
to
b
, the result produces a collection
C\ Res
where
Res=R^{\prime}
(the renamed type) and
G[Res]\equiv_{g}G[R]
.

Proof.

Renaming an attribute changes the attribute name but not the underlying structure or values. The result is
C\ Res
where
Res=R^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}=\rho_{a\to b}(R)
is the renamed type.
The rename operation establishes a bijection between elements of
CR
and elements of
C\ Res
: for each
r:R
, there is exactly one
r^{\prime}:Res
with identical values (just different attribute names). This bijection preserves the grain structure.
If
G[R]=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}
, then
G[Res]=A^{\prime}_{1}\times\cdots\times A^{\prime}_{n}
where
A^{\prime}_{i}=B
if
A_{i}=A
(the renamed attribute), and
A^{\prime}_{i}=A_{i}
otherwise.
Since the bijection preserves uniqueness properties,
G[Res]
uniquely identifies elements in
C\ Res
if and only if
G[R]
uniquely identifies elements in
CR
. By Definition of grain equivalence (
\equiv_{g}
), which captures structural isomorphism between grains, we have
G[Res]\equiv_{g}G[R]
.
∎

T.5.
Grain Inference for Grouping and Aggregation

Theorem T.5 (Grouping Determines Grain).

For any collection
CR
with grain
G[R]
and grouping operation
\gamma_{G_{cols},agg(A)}(CR)
where
G_{cols}\subseteq_{typ}R
, the result produces a collection
C\ Res
where
Res=G_{cols}\times AggResult
and
G[Res]=G_{cols}
.

Proof.

Grouping partitions the input collection by the grouping columns
G_{cols}
. For each unique combination of values in
G_{cols}
, exactly one row is produced in the result (containing the group key and aggregate values). The result type is
Res=G_{cols}\times AggResult
.
By definition, the grouping columns uniquely identify result rows. No proper subset of
G_{cols}
can serve as the grain (removing any grouping column would create duplicates if that column varies within the data). Therefore
G[Res]=G_{cols}
.

Relationship between
G_{cols}
and
G[R]
:

While the result grain is always
G_{cols}
, the relationship between the grouping columns and the input grain affects the semantics:

•

Case 1
:
G[R]\subseteq_{typ}G_{cols}
(grain included in grouping). Since
G[R]
uniquely identifies input rows and
G_{cols}
contains all of
G[R]
, each group contains at most one row. The aggregation is “trivial” (aggregating single values). The result grain
G_{cols}
is coarser than or equal to
G[R]
.

•

Case 2
:
G_{cols}\subseteq_{typ}G[R]
(grouping on subset of grain). Multiple input rows with different grain values can fall into the same group. This is true aggregation where the grain is “coarsened” from
G[R]
to
G_{cols}
.

•

Case 3
:
G_{cols}\cap_{typ}G[R]=\emptyset
(disjoint). Grouping on columns completely outside the grain. Multiple rows per group; aggregation is orthogonal to the grain structure.

•

Case 4
: Partial overlap (neither subset, non-empty intersection). Multiple rows per group with mixed grain/non-grain grouping.

In all cases,
G[\gamma_{G_{cols},agg}(R)]=G_{cols}
, but the semantic interpretation differs.
∎

T.6.
Grain Inference for Theta Join

Theorem T.6 (Theta Join Grain).

For any two collections
CR_{1}
and
CR_{2}
with grains
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
, a theta join
CR_{1}\bowtie_{\theta}CR_{2}
where
\theta
is a non-equality condition produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}\times R_{2}
and
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
.

Proof.

The result type of a theta join is
R_{1}\times R_{2}
(a full Cartesian product structure with the
\theta
condition filtering rows but not eliminating duplicate columns as in equi-join), so
Res=R_{1}\times R_{2}
.
By the Grain of Product Types theorem:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
.
Unlike equi-joins, theta joins with non-equality conditions do not create equality constraints between fields of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. Without such constraints, there is no “bootstrapping” effect where one grain can determine the other’s contribution to the join key. Each combination of
(G[R_{1}],G[R_{2}])
values can independently appear in the result.
Therefore:
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]\times G[R_{2}]
.
∎

T.7.
Grain Inference for Semi-Join and Anti-Join

Theorem T.7 (Semi-Join and Anti-Join Preserve Grain).

For any two collections
CR_{1}
and
CR_{2}
with grains
G[R_{1}]
and
G[R_{2}]
:

•

Semi-join:
CR_{1}\ltimes CR_{2}
produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}
and
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]

•

Anti-join:
CR_{1}\triangleright CR_{2}
produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R_{1}
and
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]

Proof.

Semi-join
:
CR_{1}\ltimes CR_{2}
returns rows from
R_{1}
that have at least one matching row in
R_{2}
. The result type is
R_{1}
(only columns from
R_{1}
are returned), so
Res=R_{1}
. This is equivalent to a selection on
CR_{1}
with the condition “exists a match in
CR_{2}
”. By the Selection Preserves Grain theorem,
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]
.

Anti-join
:
CR_{1}\triangleright CR_{2}
returns rows from
R_{1}
that have no matching row in
R_{2}
. The result type is
R_{1}
, so
Res=R_{1}
. This is equivalent to a selection on
CR_{1}
with the condition “no match exists in
CR_{2}
”. By the Selection Preserves Grain theorem,
G[Res]=G[R_{1}]
.
∎

T.8.
Grain Preservation under Set Operations

Theorem T.8 (Set Operations Preserve Grain).

For any two collections
CR_{1}
and
CR_{2}
of the same type
R
(union-compatible), with grain
G[R]
:

•

Union:
CR_{1}\cup CR_{2}
produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
and
G[Res]=G[R]

•

Intersection:
CR_{1}\cap CR_{2}
produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
and
G[Res]=G[R]

•

Difference:
CR_{1}-CR_{2}
produces a result
C\ Res
where
Res=R
and
G[Res]=G[R]

Proof.

Set operations require union-compatible types, meaning
R_{1}=R_{2}=R
(same schema). The result type of all three operations is
R
, so
Res=R
.
Since grain is a type-level property, and the result type equals the input type, the grain is unchanged:
G[Res]=G[R]
.
At the data level: any subset or superset of a collection maintains the same grain, as the grain’s uniqueness property holds for all subsets (elements in subsets remain uniquely identified) and supersets of union-compatible collections (elements from either source are uniquely identified by the same grain).
∎
