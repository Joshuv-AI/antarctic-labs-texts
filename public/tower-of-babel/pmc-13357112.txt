Development and validation of a predictive model for perioperative venous thromboembolism in colorectal cancer patients based on D-dimer combined with fibrinogen levels
Authors: Xu Liu, Zhen Feng, Wendan Jing, Jiemin Zhou, Yang Xiang, Hongyu He, Yongfu Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a frequent and potentially fatal complication in colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery. Although coagulation and inflammatory biomarkers are associated with cancer-related thrombosis, integrated prediction models for perioperative VTE in CRC remain limited. The aim of this study is to develop and validate a biomarker-based model for predicting perioperative VTE risk in CRC patients and to evaluate its clinical utility.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted, enrolling 618 CRC patients who underwent surgical treatment between January 1, 2020 and June 30, 2025, with follow-up until postoperative day 30. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of perioperative VTE, defined as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and/or pulmonary embolism (PE) occurring from 72 hours before surgery to 30 days after surgery. Primary predictors included D-dimer and fibrinogen levels measured within 72 hours preoperatively. Secondary predictors included the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), among others. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA), with internal validation performed via the bootstrap method.ResultsVTE occurred in 70 patients (11.3%). Multivariate analysis identified D-dimer score [odds ratio (OR) =2.63], fibrinogen score (OR =2.18), NLR score (OR =2.35), CRP score (OR =0.92), and CEA score (OR =1.84) as independent predictors of perioperative VTE. The model demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83, good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test P=0.49, Brier score =0.12), and an AUC of 0.81 after internal validation. DCA indicated significant net benefit within a risk threshold range of 5–35%. Risk stratification based on the model-derived total score revealed VTE incidence rates of 3.4%, 9.4%, and 26.3% in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively (P<0.001).ConclusionsThe predictive model incorporating preoperative D-dimer, fibrinogen, and inflammatory-immune markers effectively identifies CRC patients at high risk for perioperative VTE. It exhibits favorable discriminative ability and holds promise for clinical application, potentially informing individualized prevention strategies.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), venous thromboembolism (VTE), D-dimer, fibrinogen, inflammatory markers

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 618 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, perioperative venous thromboembolism (VTE) occurred in 11.3% within 30 days after surgery.

• Preoperative D-dimer, fibrinogen, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, C-reactive protein, and carcinoembryonic antigen were independent predictors of VTE (all P<0.01).

• The multivariable model demonstrated good discrimination (area under the curve =0.83; 0.81 after bootstrap validation) and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P=0.49), with clear risk stratification across low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups.

What is known and what is new?

• Cancer-associated VTE is common after CRC surgery, and individual coagulation or inflammatory markers have been linked to thrombosis risk.

• This study integrates coagulation and inflammatory-immune biomarkers into a single, validated perioperative risk model specifically for CRC, demonstrating meaningful predictive accuracy and clinical net benefit.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Routine assessment of preoperative D-dimer, fibrinogen, and inflammatory markers may enable early identification of high-risk patients.

• Incorporating this model into perioperative evaluation could support individualized thromboprophylaxis strategies and optimize VTE prevention in CRC surgery.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with its disease burden continuing to rise. The latest global cancer statistics indicate approximately 1.9 million new cases and 900,000 deaths from CRC in 2022, consistently placing it among the leading cancers in terms of incidence and mortality (1). As a major component of CRC, colon cancer similarly exhibits a high incidence, with the latest Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) fact sheet confirming its substantial global burden of new cases and deaths (2). Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for CRC; however, perioperative complications significantly constrain the overall benefit of surgery. Among these, venous thromboembolism (VTE), defined in this study as events occurring from 72 hours before surgery to 30 days after surgery, is widely recognized as one of the most critical and challenging perioperative complications in CRC because of its insidious onset and high risk of disability and mortality (3). Despite the widespread adoption of mechanical or pharmacological prophylactic measures, perioperative VTE remains difficult to prevent entirely, reflecting the inability of current strategies to adequately address the significant heterogeneity in thrombotic and bleeding risks among patients in real-world settings (4).

The prevention and management of perioperative VTE have reached multidisciplinary consensus and are incorporated into several international authoritative guidelines. The latest American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline emphasizes that VTE prophylaxis and treatment in cancer patients should involve an individualized balance between thrombotic benefit and bleeding risk, particularly necessitating risk assessment-based strategies in surgical contexts (5). The International Initiative on Thrombosis and Cancer (ITAC) international guideline further recommends considering extended pharmacological prophylaxis for patients undergoing major abdominopelvic cancer surgery with controllable bleeding risk (6). The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline also consistently underscores the fundamental role of risk assessment in perioperative decision-making for cancer-associated VTE (7). Nonetheless, the implementation of guideline recommendations in clinical practice faces notable limitations. On one hand, colorectal surgical patients are often collectively categorized as high-risk, leading to a homogenization of prophylactic measures. On the other hand, significant variations in perioperative VTE incidence across different studies and healthcare systems suggest that uniform strategies struggle to concurrently optimize efficacy and safety. A recent review focusing on colorectal surgery populations noted that postoperative VTE incidence varies widely across regions and databases, influenced by factors such as surgical approach, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways, and monitoring strategies (8). An external validation study reported VTE incidences of 9.0% and 13.9% during the first week and first month, respectively, after CRC surgery, with notable differences in the discriminatory capacity of various risk assessment tools within this population (9). Conversely, other studies within ERAS pathways observed relatively low symptomatic VTE risk following elective colon cancer surgery, prompting a reevaluation of the risk-benefit balance of universally extended prophylaxis (10). Collectively, this evidence indicates an urgent need for more precise risk stratification tools that better align with patient-specific biological characteristics and surgical stress variations in the perioperative management of VTE in CRC.

The ongoing debate regarding the universal implementation of extended postoperative thromboprophylaxis further highlights the clinical value of predictive models. Recent studies on cancer-related abdominopelvic surgery suggest that, while extended prophylaxis is guideline-supported, greater emphasis should be placed on real-world symptomatic event rates and potential bleeding consequences in specific subgroups like CRC, using risk stratification as the basis for selective extended prophylaxis (11). A systematic review of colorectal surgery also revealed limited randomized controlled evidence with heterogeneity in study design and outcome definitions, reinforcing the importance of reliable predictive models for identifying patients who truly benefit (12). Therefore, developing a predictive model based on routinely available perioperative indicators, with good discriminative power and calibration, is crucial for optimizing thromboprophylaxis strategies and reducing unnecessary anticoagulation-related risks.

From a pathophysiological perspective, perioperative VTE in CRC results from the interplay between cancer-associated hypercoagulability and surgical stress. Surgical trauma and the ensuing inflammatory response, hemodynamic alterations, and postoperative immobility can further amplify the tumor’s inherent prothrombotic microenvironment, predisposing patients to a markedly thrombophilic state (13). In this context, research on predicting postoperative VTE in CRC has proliferated, encompassing traditional regression models, nomograms, and machine learning (ML) approaches. These studies have incorporated factors such as surgical approach, perioperative clinical features, and coagulation-related markers to enhance the identification of high-risk patients (14). Certain nomogram models, such as the CRC Surgery Postoperative Venous Thromboembolism Prediction Tool (CRSPOT), have demonstrated good performance in predicting VTE after radical CRC resection, providing user-friendly clinical tools (15). Other studies have developed predictive models specifically within colon cancer populations, suggesting room for further refinement of risk assessment across different cancer subgroups (16). Concurrently, ML models based on external datasets have been applied for postoperative VTE risk prediction in CRC, emphasizing the potential to improve model performance and generalizability by uncovering multivariate nonlinear relationships (17). Despite these advances, existing models often have limitations regarding variable availability, clinical interpretability, and cross-population applicability. Incorporating artificial intelligence techniques, such as gradient boosting or random forest algorithms, may further enhance predictive accuracy by capturing nonlinear interactions between coagulation and inflammatory markers, improving individualized VTE risk assessment in CRC patients.

Among potential predictors, coagulation activation and inflammatory-immune markers have attracted considerable attention due to their well-defined biological basis and ready clinical availability. D-dimer and fibrinogen levels reflect hypercoagulable states and systemic inflammation, respectively, while C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) serve as proxies for perioperative inflammatory response, each supported by prior studies linking elevated levels to increased VTE risk in CRC patients (18). D-dimer, a classic marker reflecting coagulation and fibrinolysis system activation, has been confirmed in recent multi-cancer studies to correlate closely with thrombotic event risk in various treatment contexts (18). Beyond coagulation markers, accumulating evidence supports the role of inflammatory markers in cancer-associated thrombosis. A study published in the European Heart Journal systematically evaluated the relationship between inflammatory markers like CRP and VTE as well as bleeding outcomes in cancer patients, providing high-quality evidence for incorporating the inflammation-coagulation axis into risk prediction (19). In the context of immunotherapy, early dynamic changes in CRP have also been associated with increased VTE risk, suggesting that therapy-induced inflammatory states may alter thrombotic susceptibility (20). Furthermore, molecular mechanistic studies indicate that specific tumor driver mutations (e.g., KRAS-G12D) can simultaneously promote tumor progression and thrombosis, reinforcing the intrinsic link between tumor biology and thrombotic phenotype (21). Consequently, integrating coagulation markers like D-dimer and fibrinogen with inflammation-related indicators has become a recent trend in predictive model development, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses summarizing the important role of these laboratory markers in VTE risk assessment (22). More recent studies similarly indicate that elevated preoperative D-dimer is associated with an increased risk of postoperative VTE in CRC patients (9). Building on this background, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate preoperative D-dimer and fibrinogen levels, combined with inflammatory-immune and tumor burden-related markers, to develop and validate a risk prediction model for perioperative VTE, a high-impact outcome, in CRC surgery patients. By establishing a concise, accessible, and interpretable predictive framework, this study seeks to provide evidence-based support for individualized perioperative VTE risk assessment and optimization of prevention strategies, thereby enhancing perioperative management efficiency and patient benefit while ensuring safety. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0338/rc).

Methods

Patient characteristics and data

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled CRC patients who underwent surgery at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College, between January 1, 2020 and June 30, 2025. The study cohort was established based on medical records generated during routine clinical care, with no additional interventions imposed on patients.

Clinical data were retrieved retrospectively, primarily from the hospital’s Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system, Laboratory Information System, and Picture Archiving and Communication System. All included patients underwent preoperative evaluation, surgical treatment, and perioperative management within the same healthcare system, with relevant clinical information systematically documented during their care, providing the foundation for data collection and analysis.

The surgery date was designated as the index date. Researchers retrospectively ascertained the occurrence of perioperative VTE based on medical records to evaluate the relationship between surgical factors, preoperative laboratory markers, and perioperative VTE risk. The timing of outcome events was confirmed based on prior imaging studies and clinical documentation.

During the study period, 742 CRC surgery patients were retrospectively screened. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 618 patients were included in the analysis cohort. Among these, 70 patients experienced a VTE event during the perioperative period, while the remaining patients did not experience the outcome during the observation period. This cohort formed the basis for subsequent statistical analysis and predictive model development.

Ethics and informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (No. ECHNSMC202510083251).

Given the retrospective cohort design, involving only the collation and analysis of medical records from prior clinical care without any additional diagnostic or therapeutic interventions or influence on patient management decisions, the ethics committee granted a waiver for obtaining written informed consent from patients.

All patient-related clinical data were anonymized and de-identified prior to statistical analysis. Data were used solely for the purposes of this study, with strict access controls to protect patient privacy and information security.

Study procedures

Perioperative management, data acquisition, and thrombosis monitoring

Perioperative management details and case data were retrospectively compiled based on prior clinical care. All included patients were managed according to the standard perioperative protocols of the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College, which remained relatively stable during the study period and were not modified for this research. The standard perioperative VTE prophylaxis protocol at Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College is provided in https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-1-0338-1.pdf, detailing pharmacologic dosing, mechanical devices, and risk-adapted application.

Pharmacologic prophylaxis included enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously once daily or unfractionated heparin 5,000 IU subcutaneously every 12 hours. Therapeutic anticoagulation patients (e.g., atrial fibrillation on warfarin or DOAC) were analyzed separately.

Medication adherence was monitored via electronic medication administration records and nursing documentation; non-compliance was recorded and analyzed as a covariate.

Data acquisition was performed retrospectively. Researchers screened eligible patients via the hospital EMR system, extracting demographic characteristics, treatment course, and perioperative records. Laboratory results and imaging information were obtained from the Laboratory Information System and Picture Archiving and Communication System, respectively. All data were compiled from original records generated during clinical care, without involving any post hoc supplementary testing or inference.

To ensure accuracy and completeness, data extraction and collation were performed independently by two researchers. Using a predefined data extraction form, they verified information item by item. Discrepancies were resolved by consulting original records and discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third researcher.

Perioperative management information, including preoperative assessment, surgical procedure, and postoperative recovery, was retrospectively compiled based on orders, progress notes, and nursing records in the medical record system. Perioperative prophylaxis was defined as administration of pharmacologic agents (e.g., low molecular weight heparin, unfractionated heparin) and/or mechanical methods (e.g., intermittent pneumatic compression, graduated compression stockings), tailored according to patient-specific VTE risk stratification (low, moderate, high). Low-risk patients received mechanical prophylaxis only; moderate-risk patients received standard prophylactic-dose anticoagulation; high-risk patients received extended pharmacologic prophylaxis combined with mechanical measures. The implementation of perioperative thromboprophylaxis was also objectively recorded from prior clinical documentation, without interventional analysis of strategy selection or effectiveness.

Perioperative VTE monitoring followed routine clinical procedures. During prior care, if patients presented with symptoms or signs suggestive of thrombosis, clinicians ordered appropriate imaging studies for diagnosis based on their judgment. Symptom descriptions, indications for testing, and results were documented in the medical record system. This study systematically compiled perioperative thrombotic events by retrospectively reviewing these records.

Outcome ascertainment of perioperative VTE

Perioperative VTE outcome ascertainment was performed retrospectively based on prior clinical diagnostic data. In this study, the perioperative window was consistently defined as the period from 72 hours before the surgery date to 30 days postoperatively, during which VTE events were assessed.

Outcome events were ascertained based on imaging study results and corresponding formal diagnostic reports documented in medical records. Diagnosis of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) was based on lower extremity compression ultrasonography or venography results. Diagnosis of pulmonary embolism (PE) was based on computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography or ventilation/perfusion scan results. All imaging studies were performed as clinically indicated during care, with written reports issued by qualified radiologists.

During data compilation and outcome ascertainment, researchers did not participate in patient care or re-interpret imaging studies. Imaging diagnosticians were unaware of the study’s predictor variables and hypotheses, minimizing information bias from a study design perspective. Only symptomatic VTE events confirmed by imaging (ultrasound for DVT, CT pulmonary angiography for PE) were included in the primary analysis. Incidental or asymptomatic VTEs detected during routine imaging were recorded separately as secondary outcomes.

Predictor variables

Primary predictors

D-dimer

D-dimer levels were obtained from routine plasma coagulation tests performed within 72 hours preoperatively. Complete blood counts (CBCs) were collected within 72 hours preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7. The value from the first test closest to the surgery time was used for analysis. Measurement was performed using immunoturbidimetric assay, with results expressed as mg/L fibrinogen equivalent units (FEU). Based on commonly used clinical reference ranges and stratification methods from prior cancer-associated thrombosis research, D-dimer levels were assigned a graded score: 0 points for D-dimer ≤0.5 mg/L, indicating low coagulation activation; 1 point for >0.5 to ≤1.0 mg/L, indicating moderate activation; and 2 points for >1.0 mg/L, indicating significant coagulation activation associated with increased perioperative thrombosis risk.

Fibrinogen

Fibrinogen levels were obtained concurrently with D-dimer from routine coagulation tests within 72 hours preoperatively, measured using the clot-based method and expressed as g/L. The value closest to surgery was used. Based on clinical laboratory normal ranges and hypercoagulability studies, fibrinogen levels were scored: 0 points for ≤4.0 g/L; 1 point for >4.0 g/L to ≤5.0 g/L; and 2 points for >5.0 g/L. Elevated fibrinogen reflects increased coagulation substrate and enhanced inflammation-associated hypercoagulability.

Inflammatory and immune-related predictors

NLR

NLR was calculated from neutrophil and lymphocyte counts obtained from CBC tests within 72 hours preoperatively, reflecting systemic inflammatory response and immune status. Based on commonly used stratification thresholds in prior oncology and thrombosis studies, NLR was scored: 0 points for NLR <3.0, indicating mild inflammation; 1 point for 3.0–5.0, indicating moderate inflammation; and 2 points for ≥5.0, indicating significant inflammation and immune imbalance.

PLR

PLR was calculated from platelet and lymphocyte counts from preoperative CBCs, reflecting the balance between platelet activation and immune status. Based on literature and clinical experience, PLR was scored: 0 points for PLR <150; 1 point for 150–300; and 2 points for ≥300. Elevated PLR suggests enhanced platelet-mediated prothrombotic state and inflammatory response.

CRP

CRP levels were obtained from routine preoperative inflammatory marker tests, using the value closest to surgery. CRP was monitored on postoperative days 1 and 3. Based on common clinical thresholds, CRP was scored: 0 points for CRP <10 mg/L; 1 point for 10–30 mg/L; and 2 points for ≥30 mg/L. Elevated CRP typically indicates significant inflammation and is closely linked to cancer-associated hypercoagulability.

Tumor burden-related predictor

Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)

CEA levels were obtained from routine preoperative tumor marker tests, reflecting tumor burden and biological aggressiveness. Based on clinical reference ranges and prior research, CEA was scored: 0 points for CEA ≤5 ng/mL; 1 point for >5 to ≤10 ng/mL; and 2 points for >10 ng/mL. Elevated CEA suggests increased tumor burden, which may indirectly influence perioperative thrombosis risk via inflammatory and coagulation pathways.

Other secondary predictors

Clinical and surgical variables

Tumor location, tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, comorbidities, surgical approach, operative duration, and intraoperative blood loss were extracted from clinical records. These variables were not assigned scores but were included as covariates reflecting patient baseline status and surgical complexity for comprehensive analysis.

Basic laboratory variables

Hemoglobin, albumin, and platelet count from routine perioperative tests were included to reflect nutritional status, hematopoietic function, and overall physiological condition, without separate scoring. All routine perioperative laboratory tests were conducted within 72 hours preoperatively according to institutional protocol.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Analyses were based on retrospectively compiled clinical data without prospective data collection.

Continuous variables were first assessed for distribution. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with between-group comparisons using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range), with comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage), with comparisons using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

With perioperative VTE occurrence as the dependent variable, a multivariable logistic regression model was built to develop the predictive model. Predictor variables for inclusion were pre-specified based on prior literature and clinical rationale. D-dimer, fibrinogen, and other continuous predictors were entered as continuous variables to preserve original information. To assess potential nonlinear relationships with the outcome, nonlinearity testing was performed during model building, with restricted cubic splines used if necessary.

Considering potential overfitting in predictive modeling, penalized regression methods were employed during variable selection and model fitting to enhance model robustness and generalizability. Model coefficients were estimated using the entire study cohort without artificial grouping or sample reduction.

Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discriminative ability was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed by plotting calibration curves and calculating the Brier score to reflect agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Potential clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA) to estimate net benefit across different threshold probabilities.

To validate model stability and correct for optimism bias in performance estimates, internal validation was performed using the bootstrap resampling method with 1,000 repetitions. For each bootstrap sample, the model was refitted and performance metrics were recalculated, yielding optimism-corrected estimates of model discrimination and calibration.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and perioperative VTE incidence

During the study period (January 1, 2020 to June 30, 2025), 742 patients undergoing CRC surgery were retrospectively screened. After applying inclusion/exclusion criteria, 618 patients constituted the final analysis cohort. Perioperative VTE occurred in 70 patients, yielding an overall incidence of 11.3%. Patients were categorized into VTE (n=70) and non-VTE (n=548) groups based on perioperative VTE occurrence. Comparisons of demographic, tumor-related, comorbidity, and surgical characteristics are presented in Table 1. The VTE group was significantly older than the non-VTE group {median age: 68 [62–74] vs. 62 [55–69] years, P<0.001}, with no significant difference in sex distribution (male: 61.4% vs. 58.0%, P=0.62).

Regarding tumor characteristics, the proportion of rectal cancer was higher in the VTE group (54.3% vs. 42.0%, P=0.048), and advanced stage (III–IV) was more prevalent (67.1% vs. 49.6%, P=0.006). For comorbidities, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease was significantly higher in the VTE group (32.9% vs. 18.4%, P=0.004), while hypertension and diabetes distributions were similar.

Surgically, the VTE group had a higher proportion of open surgeries (57.1% vs. 39.6%, P=0.007). Furthermore, operative duration {median: 210 [175–260] vs. 185 [150–230] min, P=0.002} and intraoperative blood loss {median: 260 [180–400] vs. 180 [120–300] mL, P=0.001} were significantly greater in the VTE group.

Multivariable analysis and perioperative VTE predictive model development

With perioperative VTE as the dependent variable (Figure 2A), variables statistically significant in univariate analysis or deemed clinically relevant were included in a multivariable logistic regression model. Candidate variables included preoperative D-dimer score, fibrinogen score, NLR score, CRP score, and CEA score, with adjustment for potential confounders like age, tumor stage, surgical approach, and cardiovascular disease. Additional variables extracted included current medications, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), and cardiovascular comorbidities (hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure).

Multivariable analysis results (Figure 2B) showed that the preoperative D-dimer score was significantly associated with perioperative VTE risk. Compared with patients with low scores, those with high scores had a markedly increased risk [adjusted odds ratio (OR) =2.63, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.78–3.90, P<0.001]. The fibrinogen score was also an independent predictor (adjusted OR =2.18, 95% CI: 1.47–3.24, P<0.001). Among the inflammatory-immune markers, the NLR score showed a significant positive association with perioperative VTE (adjusted OR =2.35, 95% CI: 1.59–3.47, P<0.001). The CRP score remained significant after adjustment (adjusted OR =0.92, 95% CI: 0.85–0.99, P=0.03). The CEA score was also an independent predictor (adjusted OR =1.84, 95% CI: 1.22–2.77, P=0.004).

Adjustment for age, tumor stage, surgical approach, and comorbidities did not substantially alter the effect sizes of these predictors, suggesting stable associations with perioperative VTE risk (Figure 2C). Nonlinearity tests for continuous variables did not indicate significant deviation from linear assumptions, and restricted cubic spline analysis supported their inclusion as linear terms. Based on these results, a perioperative VTE risk prediction model was constructed, incorporating preoperative D-dimer score, fibrinogen score, NLR score, CRP score, and CEA score as core predictors for subsequent performance evaluation and internal validation.

Discriminative ability and calibration of the predictive model

The model developed from multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated good discriminative ability for distinguishing between patients with and without perioperative VTE, with an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.78–0.88, Figure 3A). Evaluation across risk thresholds showed balanced sensitivity and specificity within the 0.15–0.30 predicted probability range. At a reference threshold of 0.20, sensitivity was 0.79 and specificity 0.74, indicating good overall discriminatory performance.

Calibration performance, assessed via calibration curve (Figure 3B), showed good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed VTE incidence, with the curve closely aligning with the ideal reference line. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated no significant deviation (χ2=7.42, P=0.49), with a calibration slope of 0.97 and intercept of −0.04, suggesting no substantial systematic over- or underestimation of risk. The overall Brier score was 0.12, indicating good predictive accuracy and stability.

A nomogram was constructed based on the multivariable logistic regression model for individualized perioperative VTE risk prediction (Figure 3C). This nomogram integrates scores from the key predictors (D-dimer, fibrinogen, inflammatory-immune markers, tumor burden marker), allowing summation to a total risk score mapped to the predicted probability of perioperative VTE, providing a user-friendly clinical assessment tool.

Internal validation and model stability assessment

Bootstrap resampling (1,000 repetitions) was performed for internal validation to assess model robustness and mitigate overfitting. In each iteration, a bootstrap sample (n=618) was drawn with replacement, the model was refitted, and performance was re-evaluated.

After bootstrap correction, discriminative ability slightly decreased but remained high: the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.81 (original AUC =0.83), indicating good stability with only mild optimism bias (Figure 4A). The AUC reduction of 0.02 suggested no marked overfitting.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a predictive model for perioperative VTE risk in CRC patients. CRC exhibits high incidence and a trend toward earlier onset, with perioperative thrombotic events significantly impacting patient prognosis and quality of life (23). Available data indicate perioperative VTE incidence diagnosed via imaging and clinical assessment can be as high as 40%, with PE accounting for approximately 5% (24). Current guidelines recommend extended anticoagulant prophylaxis for major surgery patients, but real-world adherence remains suboptimal (25). Therefore, screening high-risk patients using readily available clinical indicators to guide individualized prophylaxis is of considerable importance. Through analysis of a large cohort, this study identified multiple biochemical and clinical factors significantly associated with VTE risk and constructed a risk scoring model with favorable discrimination, suggesting the feasibility of early high-risk patient identification.

Within our multivariable predictive model, D-dimer emerged as one of the most potent independent risk factors, with both pre- and postoperative levels showing significant associations with VTE. Previous research supports this finding. For instance, Zhang et al. [2025], in a prospective external validation study, found that both preoperative D-dimer ≥0.49 mg/L and postoperative D-dimer ≥1.7 mg/L were significantly associated with VTE after CRC surgery, demonstrating strong discriminatory ability (9). Similarly, Wu et al. [2023] identified postoperative D-dimer elevation ≥3.5 mg/L as an independent VTE risk factor incorporated into their nomogram model for clinical risk quantification (15). Furthermore, a study developing an XGBoost algorithm-based postoperative VTE prediction model identified D-dimer as the feature with the highest contribution to model output, indicating its stable explanatory and predictive value even within nonlinear models (23). From a biological mechanism perspective, D-dimer, as an end-product of fibrin degradation, reflects both coagulation cascade activation and ongoing fibrinolysis or microthrombosis (26). Its elevation in cancer-associated VTE may stem from synergistic effects of tumor cell procoagulant activity, tissue factor expression, and postoperative stress-induced endothelial dysfunction, thus serving as a key marker of hypercoagulability (27). In our study, elevated preoperative D-dimer was significantly associated with perioperative VTE occurrence, suggesting its utility as an important biological basis for selecting individualized anticoagulation strategies in CRC patients. These findings align with and complement our results. Beyond D-dimer, fibrinogen also showed a trend toward association with increased VTE risk in our study, warranting attention for its central role in the coagulation system. Fibrinogen serves not only as the substrate for thrombus formation but is also a classic acute-phase reactant, often upregulated in inflammatory states and active malignancy. Jia et al. [2025], in a recent study on postoperative VTE in ovarian cancer, found that patients with concurrent elevation of preoperative fibrinogen and D-dimer had significantly increased postoperative thrombotic risk, suggesting their combined predictive power for hypercoagulability may surpass that of single markers (28). Although direct evidence in CRC patients remains relatively limited, considering its high incidence and the common background of postoperative inflammatory stress, the observed association between elevated fibrinogen and VTE risk in our study is mechanistically plausible. This supports its potential clinical value as a supplementary variable in VTE prediction models, meriting further validation and exploration.

Inflammatory markers play a crucial role in cancer-associated venous thrombosis and have become a key focus in recent risk prediction model research. Our results showed that the NLR was significantly higher in the VTE group, suggesting its potential independent predictive value for postoperative thrombosis. This aligns with prior literature. Rinaldi et al. [2021], in a retrospective analysis of patients undergoing oral cancer surgery, found that elevated preoperative NLR significantly increased postoperative DVT risk, remaining an independent predictor in multivariable analysis (29). Mechanistically, neutrophils may contribute to thrombosis by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which activate local coagulation cascades. Huang et al. [2025] demonstrated that CRC patients with VTE had significantly elevated levels of NETs-related markers [myeloperoxidase (MPO), citrullinated histone H3, and cell-free DNA] in peripheral blood, supporting a potential role for neutrophil-mediated procoagulant activity in postoperative thrombosis in this population (30). Combining this with our data suggests that elevated NLR may reflect the synergistic role of inflammatory response and immune dysregulation in postoperative VTE formation. Additionally, the PLR has been proposed as a systemic inflammation marker with some value in thrombosis risk prediction. Studies indicate that elevated PLR correlates with lower extremity DVT incidence, possibly reflecting an imbalance between platelet activation and immune suppression indicative of thrombotic tendency. Collectively, these studies highlight the inflammation-coagulation interplay as an important consideration for thrombotic risk identification in the perioperative setting for CRC.

Patient baseline characteristics hold significant weight in VTE risk assessment, with age being particularly critical. Our results showed that older patients had a significantly higher probability of VTE, indicating that age should be prioritized in postoperative thrombotic risk assessment for CRC. This conclusion is consistent with prior multicenter studies. Yao et al. [2022], in developing the Sir-Run-Run-Shaw prediction model, assigned important risk weight to age ≥69 years, with model validation showing this factor significantly improved postoperative VTE identification (31). Similarly, Wei et al. [2023], in a prospective large-sample study, explicitly identified age ≥70 years as an independent risk factor, suggesting this threshold may serve as a reference for clinical stratification (25). Mechanistically, older patients often have comorbidities like atherosclerosis and cardiac dysfunction; factors such as diminished endothelial function, altered hemorheology, and reduced mobility collectively promote hypercoagulability and stasis, substantially increasing postoperative thrombotic risk. Our study also observed that patients with a history of or concurrent lower extremity varicose veins had significantly elevated VTE risk. Wei et al.’s prospective study supports this, identifying lower extremity varicose veins as an independent risk factor for perioperative VTE in CRC, suggesting venous structural abnormalities may accelerate thrombosis via altered hemodynamics (25). The proportion of such patients in our sample was relatively low, potentially limiting statistical power, but the risk trend remains clinically suggestive. Furthermore, heart failure, a key predisposing factor for postoperative thrombosis, also showed a significant positive association with VTE occurrence in our study. Recent studies suggest that heart failure is associated with an increased risk of VTE, potentially through venous stasis, impaired hemodynamics, and a prothrombotic state (32). Regarding other traditional risk factors such as elevated BMI and smoking, although they were not statistically significant in our study, recent literature suggests that obesity is associated with an increased risk of postoperative VTE, warranting further investigation of its independent contribution in larger, multicenter populations (33). In summary, patient age and comorbidity burden constitute important baseline variables for postoperative thrombotic risk in CRC, particularly in elderly patients and those with limited cardiopulmonary reserve, necessitating reinforced individualized prevention and monitoring strategies based on comprehensive preoperative assessment.

Our study also indicates that tumor-related characteristics significantly influence perioperative VTE occurrence. Among included variables, preoperative bowel obstruction showed a correlative trend with VTE, consistent with prior literature. Sun et al. [2025], in the CRC-VTE prediction model study, explicitly identified preoperative bowel obstruction as an independent VTE risk factor, maintaining stable predictive ability in external validation (17). Mechanistically, bowel obstruction can trigger widespread stress response, accompanied by fluid/electrolyte imbalances, dehydration, and slowed circulation, promoting a hypercoagulable state. Furthermore, bowel obstruction is often complicated by lower gastrointestinal bleeding and nutritional deterioration; these factors may synergistically increase postoperative thrombotic risk, as supported by pathophysiological studies (25). Beyond bowel obstruction, advanced tumor stage is generally considered associated with VTE risk, particularly in stage IV and metastatic disease where procoagulant activity is typically heightened. However, in our study, advanced stage did not emerge as an independent risk factor, possibly due to a relatively low proportion of stage IV patients or lack of systemic therapy during the perioperative period in our cohort. A similar finding was reported in a prospective multicenter study of 1,836 CRC patients, which did not observe a statistically significant association between stage IV and VTE (25). Nevertheless, advanced cancer is often associated with more frequent chemotherapy exposure, complex treatment pathways, and more pronounced systemic prothrombotic states, meriting clinical attention. Several VTE prediction tools (e.g., RVTA and Sir-Run-Run models) have incorporated tumor metastasis or chemotherapy regimens into their scoring systems, suggesting these variables hold explanatory and risk-indicative value across different clinical scenarios (9,17,25). It should be noted that our study’s preoperative biological markers did not cover some potentially important variables, such as tumor molecular subtype, carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels, or thrombosis-related molecular markers. Incorporating these in the future could enrich the model’s feature space and improve risk stratification capability and individual applicability.

Surgical variables have also been confirmed by multiple studies as important influencers of perioperative VTE risk. In our multivariable model, prolonged operative duration showed a positive trend with postoperative VTE risk. This aligns with prior findings. For example, a large multicenter cohort analysis identified anesthesia/surgery time exceeding 180 minutes as an independent risk factor for postoperative VTE, suggesting prolonged procedures may induce thrombosis through multiple mechanisms (17). On one hand, longer surgery often implies greater surgical trauma, tissue exposure, and heightened stress response; on the other, prolonged intraoperative immobilization and venous stasis can impede venous return. Concurrently, increased intraoperative blood loss is often accompanied by coagulation factor depletion and compensatory transfusion, with the introduction of exogenous procoagulant substances potentially further enhancing hypercoagulable tendency. Additionally, Yasui et al.’s retrospective analysis of 2,876 patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for CRC identified intraoperative blood loss as an independent predictor for postoperative proximal DVT and PE, reinforcing the role of surgical factors in VTE pathogenesis (34). Although intraoperative transfusion incidence was low in our study and did not show statistically independent influence, from a mechanistic standpoint and based on prior evidence, this factor remains noteworthy. It is also worth noting that the widespread use of laparoscopy may adversely affect venous return due to increased intra-abdominal pressure from pneumoperitoneum, compression of the inferior vena cava, and hemodynamic changes (35,36). Current evidence on the relationship between laparoscopic surgery and VTE risk is somewhat inconsistent. In our study, although laparoscopic surgery was common, VTE incidence was comparable to open surgery, suggesting the specific mechanism of this approach in VTE formation warrants further investigation. Regarding postoperative complications, factors like anastomotic leak or infection may indirectly increase thrombosis risk by inducing systemic inflammation and coagulation pathway activation. Our observational data showed that patients with postoperative fever or infection were more prone to VTE, and while statistical significance was limited, this aligns with reported associations between anastomotic leak/postoperative infection and increased thrombosis risk, suggesting clinical management should emphasize identification and intervention for postoperative inflammatory events.

From a model performance perspective, our D-dimer/fibrinogen-based model, augmented with inflammatory markers and validated internally (AUC =0.81), demonstrates superior discriminative performance compared to traditional CRC-specific tools such as the Caprini score (AUC =0.59–0.66), highlighting both its innovation and potential for seamless integration into perioperative clinical workflows. Our multivariable regression model demonstrated good discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.83 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.81 after internal validation. For instance, the CRC-VTE score model developed and validated in a multicenter cohort also achieved an AUC of 0.72 in an external population, indicating robustness for postoperative VTE risk assessment in CRC (25). Furthermore, the perioperative VTE incidence in our cohort was 11.3%, indicating a clinically meaningful event burden in this population reported by Zhang et al. in an independent population, further suggesting good transportability and external adaptability of such models across different clinical settings (9). In contrast, the traditional Caprini score, although widely used for perioperative thrombotic risk stratification, shows relatively limited predictive performance in CRC patients, with prior studies reporting AUCs ranging only from 0.59 to 0.66, substantially lower than cancer-specific tools (9). Our results support this observation, emphasizing the need to optimize scoring systems based on cancer type and surgical pathway specifics. Multiple studies note that generic tools like the Khorana score for solid tumors or the Caprini score for general surgery exhibit insufficient sensitivity in CRC surgery populations, particularly underestimating true risk in patients without chemotherapy or with early-stage cancer (37-39). Therefore, expanding models to include variables like preoperative bowel obstruction, intraoperative blood loss, tumor burden, and surgical approach could significantly enhance VTE risk identification accuracy and clinical utility. The variable selection and performance of our model provide a feasible pathway for further optimization in this direction.

Several study limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias and information gaps. Some potentially important variables, such as perioperative mobility or certain postoperative biochemical markers (e.g., CRP, tumor markers), lacked complete records and were not included, possibly affecting prediction precision. Second, we primarily captured lower extremity DVT cases detected by ultrasound within two weeks postoperatively, potentially underestimating the overall clinical VTE incidence by missing pulmonary emboli and later events. Additionally, the cohort originated from a single center, limiting generalizability and necessitating external validation. Third, the model relies on VTE diagnosis confirmed by venous ultrasound; some asymptomatic events may have been missed due to lack of screening. Future research should involve prospective multicenter studies incorporating richer variables (including genetic predisposition, biomarkers, and continuous dynamic monitoring data) and assess model applicability across different patient subgroups (e.g., those receiving chemotherapy or with metastatic disease). Furthermore, exploring hybrid strategies combining ML with traditional methods, such as using ML to dynamically update Caprini score factor weights or leveraging dynamic EMR data to improve prediction sensitivity, is warranted. Long-term, evaluating the actual impact of model-guided individualized prevention strategies on reducing VTE incidence and improving survival outcomes is essential.

Conclusions

Based on a real-world cohort of postoperative CRC patients, this study systematically evaluated the characteristics and predictors of perioperative VTE, developing and validating a risk prediction model incorporating D-dimer, fibrinogen, NLR, CRP, and CEA as core variables. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability and calibration, outperforming traditional generic scoring tools, and can provide more targeted quantitative guidance for individualized perioperative VTE identification and intervention. Notably, all model variables are routinely available clinical indicators, ensuring ease of acquisition and clinical interpretability, facilitating potential implementation in colorectal surgical practice.

In summary, the proposed VTE risk prediction tool, based on a combination of preoperative coagulation and inflammatory markers with perioperative clinical parameters, offers a potential pathway for early identification and refined management of postoperative thrombotic risk in CRC patients. Future research should focus on external validation in multicenter populations and explore integration with dynamic monitoring, biomarker panels, or artificial intelligence algorithms to further enhance model generalizability and clinical decision-support value.
