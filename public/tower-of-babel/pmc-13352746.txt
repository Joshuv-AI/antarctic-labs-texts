Perceived vs. actual cigarette and waterpipe dependence in Lebanon: a cross-sectional study of social desirability across demographic subgroups
Authors: David Karam, Chadia Haddad, Hala Sacre, Rana Hatem, Fouad Sakr, Sandrella Bou Malhab, Clara Gerges, Dalal Youssef, Roy Eid, Georgia Chkayban, Aline Hajj, Marguerite Ghostin, Pascale Salameh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco use is a significant global public health issue, increasingly affecting low- and middle-income countries. In culturally sensitive contexts like Lebanon, self-reported smoking may be unreliable, particularly among socially restricted groups such as women and youth. This study aims to evaluate the discrepancy between perceived and actual dependence on cigarettes and waterpipes in the Lebanese population and its association with social desirability, while also examining gender differences and patterns among self-identified non-smokers and younger individuals.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between February and March 2025, enrolling a total of 377 participants. Data were collected through an online self-administered survey distributed via Google® Forms, using previously validated scales for smoking dependence and social desirability assessment.ResultsThe present study showed a substantial gap between self-perception and actual nicotine dependence among Lebanese youth and young adults: despite scoring high on the Lebanese Cigarette Dependence and Waterpipe Dependence scales, 10.6% of cigarette smokers and 28.6% of waterpipe smokers did not perceive themselves as dependent. Underestimation of tobacco dependence was more prominent among waterpipe smokers; according to the model, it was overall associated with higher waterpipe dependence (ORa = 1.24; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.14–1.34) and cigarette dependence (ORa = 1.12, 95% CI 1.08–1.18), more smokers at home (ORa = 1.53; 95% CI 1.24–1.90), depression (ORa = 6.44; 95% CI 1.38–30.08), and social desirability components, mainly the reversed independent thinking (ORa = 1.09; 95% CI 1.00–1.18). Underestimation of cigarette and waterpipe dependence was linked to lower virtuous perfectionism in both sexes. Additionally, males living outside Beirut were more likely to underestimate waterpipe dependence, while in females, underestimation was also associated with depression.ConclusionThese findings should be interpreted within Lebanon’s unique cultural and religious context. Given the important role of social and psychological factors in tobacco dependence underestimation, future interventions should go beyond physical health messaging. Targeted campaigns should encourage self-awareness, challenge conformity, and embrace acknowledging addiction and seeking help.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01934-y.Keywords: Cigarette, Waterpipe, Smoking, Dependence, Lebanon, Underestimation, Social desirability, Independent thinking

Background

Tobacco use is a major public health concern worldwide, with this burden shifting to low- and middle-income countries. Earlier World Health Organization (WHO) projections suggested slower declines or potential increases in tobacco use in the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) compared with other regions [1]. Lebanon, in particular, reports among the highest smoking rates globally [2]. Moreover, recent national studies report high tobacco use among Lebanese adults, with approximately 35% identified as cigarette users and 39.5% reporting waterpipe use. In a 2019 household survey, waterpipe smoking was particularly common among women, who represented 46% of users compared with 33% among men [3, 4]. This widespread use of both cigarettes and waterpipes in Lebanon highlights the importance and need for understanding tobacco dependence in this context.

In Lebanon and the Middle East, smoking and the reporting of this behavior could be influenced by individual behaviors, as well as deeply rooted social and cultural norms. Within Lebanon, the degree of social permissiveness varies across religious groups and rural settings. Religious and societal norms would thus shape these behaviors, with some conservative circles frowning upon smoking cigarettes yet normalizing waterpipe smoking in social events [5]. Waterpipe smoking is viewed as a social activity that is associated with hospitality and leisure, even among the youth and women [5, 6], while this is not the case for cigarette smoking. Conversely, in other communities, smoking could be an acceptable form of recreation and venting instead of alcohol, leading individuals to underestimate its addictive potential [7].

The underreporting of socially stigmatized behaviors, known as social desirability bias (SDB), is a well-known methodological challenge in self-reported data and varies according to the context. SDB refers to the tendency to present oneself in a socially acceptable manner by minimizing stigmatized behaviors or endorsing culturally approved responses. It could represent a limitation in behavioral surveys, particularly among subgroups such as women or younger individuals: SDB can cause studied individuals to downplay the frequency of tobacco use or deny their use altogether, especially among women and younger users, where judgment is more likely to be experienced [5]. This underreporting and distortion would affect prevalence estimates and also contribute to the under-recognition of nicotine dependence. For example, many waterpipe users might deny addiction but meet dependence criteria on standardized scales [5].

Culturally relevant dimensions of impression management are thus hypothesized to influence the likelihood of acknowledging nicotine dependence, and different dimensions of social desirability may influence the perception of dependence through distinct psychological mechanisms. Traits such as virtuous perfectionism reflect a tendency to maintain a socially acceptable image, which in turn may encourage individuals to deny or minimize stigmatized behaviors. On the other hand, independent thinking could reflect resistance to social norms and external judgments, which may influence how individuals label their smoking dependence. Indeed, international research has consistently shown discrepancies between self-reported and verified smoking behavior [8], with individuals often underreporting use or misclassifying their dependence status due to stigma, social desirability, or misperception. Studies in diverse settings demonstrate that both cigarette smokers and waterpipe users frequently underestimate their dependence or deny smoking altogether [9, 10]. However, while global research has extensively examined misreporting of smoking status, far fewer studies have explicitly explored the divergence between perceived and objectively measured dependence, a construct that is clinically relevant and more closely tied to treatment-seeking behavior. This gap is particularly important in Lebanon, where both cigarette and waterpipe use are highly prevalent and socially normalized within a complex cultural, political, and religious context.

Despite these conditions, research in Lebanon has primarily focused on prevalence and demographic predictors of smoking, with limited attention to discrepancies between perceived and assessed dependence. Moreover, there is limited literature examining how social desirability may affect individuals’ self-assessments of addiction in Lebanon. This is a significant gap, as underestimation of one’s dependence may reduce perceived need for treatment, as smoking identity and self-perception have been shown to influence quit motivation and engagement with cessation behaviors. This is particularly concerning among dual users, where dependence levels are often high and unrecognized [11].

Previous international studies have primarily examined discrepancies between self-reported smoking status and biochemical verification, highlighting the impact of social desirability and misreporting [12–14]. In contrast, research in Lebanon and the EMR has largely focused on prevalence, demographic predictors, and patterns of use [3, 4]. Far less attention has been given to discrepancies between perceived and scale-based nicotine dependence or to psychological mechanisms underlying such misperceptions. The present study addresses this gap by examining the possible divergence between perceived and scale-based dependence and its association with social desirability and psychological factors in a Lebanese sample.

The primary objective of this study is to assess the potential underestimation of cigarette and waterpipe dependence among the Lebanese population and its relationship with social desirability aspects. The secondary objectives are to compare these associations among gender subgroups and to assess them among those who consider themselves non-smokers and younger individuals.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted between February and March 2025, enrolling a total of 377 participants. Data were collected through an online self-administered survey distributed via Google® Forms. The survey employed a snowball sampling technique and was disseminated through social media platforms, including WhatsApp. Eligible participants were individuals aged 18 years or older, from diverse demographic backgrounds (no restriction on inclusion of adult age groups, genders, religions, education levels, and socioeconomic status), and living in Lebanon. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no financial compensation was offered.

Sample size calculation

The minimum sample size was calculated using the G*Power software 3.1.9.7 for Windows (Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Multiple regressions would be used to assess the correlates of dependent variables. Assuming a calculated effect size is f2 = 0.05 (small effect size) and expecting a squared multiple correlation of 0.05 (R2 deviation from 0) related to the Omnibus test of the multiple regression, the minimum necessary sample would be n = 371, considering an alpha error of 5%, a power of 80%, and allowing 15 predictors to be included in the model.

Translation procedure and piloting

The questions were translated from English to Arabic using a forward–backward translation approach. The forward translation was carried out by two of the authors, and the backward translation by an independent translator. The original and back-translated English versions were then compared to identify discrepancies, which were resolved through consensus among the authors. The resulting questionnaire was piloted on ten individuals with sociodemographic characteristics that are similar to the targeted population and using a similar selection method (online with no specific inclusion criterion except for adult age): unclear questions were clarified before dissemination, except for questions taken from validated scales that were used verbatim (Supplement 1). Pilot study participants were not included in the final study sample.

Questionnaire

The online questionnaire was available in both Arabic, the official language of Lebanon, and English. Each question was presented in both languages to ensure clarity and ease of comprehension. The survey comprised two sections and required approximately 10–15 min to complete (Supplement 1).

The first section collected sociodemographic information, including age, gender, marital status (single, married, divorced, or widowed), nationality (Lebanese or non-Lebanese), residency status (resident or visitor), governorate, living area (urban or rural), monthly income, employment status (working or not working), and education level (school or university level). Monthly income was categorized into four brackets: no income, less than $500, $500–$2000, and more than $2000. The eight Lebanese governorates included were Akkar, Baalbeck-Hermel, Beirut, Beqaa, Mount Lebanon (including Keserwan), North Lebanon, Nabatiyeh, and South Lebanon.

The second section of the questionnaire included the other validated scales and additional items of cultural importance, particularly related to religious bans and social acceptance of smoking.

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) scale

The AUDIT is a 10-item scale created by the WHO as a screening tool to evaluate drinking habits, alcohol use, and alcohol-related issues. The psychometric properties of an Arabic version of the AUDIT scale were evaluated among 789 Lebanese adults [15]. This scale consists of ten questions, each scored 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4. Questions 9 and 10 are attributed scores of 0, 2, or 4. The scores range from 0 to 40, with 0 denoting abstinence and no history of alcohol-related issues. A score of eight or above is considered a sign of dangerous or harmful alcohol use. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.940.

Tobacco dependence scales

The Lebanese Cigarette Dependence (LCD) [16] and Lebanon Waterpipe Dependence Scale (LWDS-11) [17] were used because they are locally developed, culturally relevant tools that capture smoking patterns unique to Lebanon, such as intermittent use, social-context cues, and culturally embedded smoking habits.

The LCD is a comprehensive tool used to assess nicotine dependence and cigarette smoking behaviors among Lebanese adults [16]. It comprises 14 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores calculated by summing all responses. Higher scores indicate higher cigarette dependence (LCD Cronbach’s alpha = 0.935). The LWDS-11 was created and developed in Lebanon by Salameh et al. to assess nicotine dependence among adult waterpipe smokers [17]. It consists of eleven items with ratings on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. Higher LWDS-11 scores indicate higher waterpipe dependence levels (LWDS-11 Cronbach’s alpha = 0.910).

In the current study, the LCD and LWDS-11 categories refer to levels of dependence severity.

The Lebanese Social Desirability Scale (LSDS)

The LSDS consists of 36 items grouped in three factors. Factor 1 includes 15 items, with higher scores indicating perfectionism, virtue, and impression management (Virtuous Perfectionism; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.945). Factor 2 comprises 8 items; higher scores are associated with lower conscientiousness, lower impression management, and possibly higher openness to experience (Independent Thinking; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.747; note that this scale was reversed to be in parallel with factors 1 & 3 and show lower independent thinking/higher dependent thinking). Factor 3 includes 13 items; higher scores reflect a more realistic, socially adaptive profile, with lower honesty and moderately self-serving tendencies (Socially Strategic Pragmatism; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.856). Overall, the LSDS showed an acceptable validity structure and good reliability (Supplement 2).

The Socioeconomic Status Composite Scale (SES-C)

The SES-C scale, developed and validated among 448 Lebanese adults [18], is a composite instrument measuring socioeconomic status within the Lebanese context. The scale comprises ten items addressing well-being (InCharge Financial Distress/Financial Well-Being Scale (IFDFW)) [19], income, external aids, debt magnitude, household monthly income, social class, crowding index, and education and work status of both the participant and family head. The total scores ranged from 6 to 28, with higher scores indicating higher socioeconomic status. The total score was then divided into very low, low, moderate, and high SES, as per the initial scale. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.457.

Data management and definition of tobacco underestimation

After questions were asked about the subjective reporting of individual smoking and the estimation of dependence (subjective classification), categories of smoking dependence were also assessed using the LCD and the LWDS-11, according to the scale suggested categorization by the developers (objective measure classification). Afterwards, subjects who subjectively classified themselves below the category of the objective measure were considered smoking dependence “underestimators.” Thus, “underestimation” refers to the discrepancy whereby an individual’s subjective classification of dependence falls below the objective dependence category determined using the LCD or LWDS-11; it is a perceptual mismatch between the measured assessment and the perceived concept. Individuals who subjectively declared themselves as “non-smokers” but who had an objective dependence measure higher than zero were considered “extreme underestimators”; in other words, these participants denied smoking and could be social or intermittent smokers. These operational definitions are a proxy for misclassification but are essential given the cultural context, where many individuals engage in occasional or social smoking yet do not perceive themselves as smokers or do not publicly declare their smoking status [20, 21], sometimes also considered as “phantom smokers.”

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 30. For a descriptive analysis, averages and standard deviations (SD) were used for quantitative measurements, and absolute frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Due to the usage of the snowball sampling approach and the online questionnaire, no missing data were found. Quantitative variables were checked for normality before proceeding with the analysis.

For bivariate analysis, independent samples t-tests were employed to compare means between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons across three or more groups. Additionally, associations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients.

For multivariable analyses, two multiple logistic regression models were performed using the ENTER method, taking cigarette and waterpipe dependence underestimation as dependent variables. Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle variables, other substance dependence measures, and social desirability concepts. In addition, stratification by gender was conducted to further take into account the cultural aspect of gender and differences in the acceptability of smoking.

Additionally, two subgroup analyses were conducted among self-declared non-smokers and younger participants (emerging adults). For each subgroup, two logistic regression models were performed: one with cigarette dependence underestimation and one with waterpipe dependence underestimation as the dependent variable. These subgroups were selected because they are particularly vulnerable to social desirability bias and underreporting due to stigma, normalization of waterpipe use, or misperception of occasional use as non-use. These analyses were exploratory and aimed to assess potential differences in associations across these predefined subgroups.

A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, and social desirability measures of the sample

Table 1 presents a comprehensive overview of the sociodemographic and related characteristics of 377 participants. The results showed that the mean age of the participants was 25.35 ± 9.17 years; the sample was predominantly female (74.3%) and unmarried (92.6%), with nearly all individuals (97.6%) having attained university-level education. Parental education levels are also relatively high, with over 40% of fathers and 50% of mothers having university degrees. Most participants reside outside Beirut (72.9%) and report average socioeconomic status (72.7%), while a large majority are unemployed (87.8%). Given these characteristics, the sample consists mostly of young, educated, unemployed Lebanese adults, and the findings may not be generalizable to the broader population.

Discussion

The present study shows a substantial gap between self-perception and actual nicotine dependence among Lebanese youth and young adults. Despite scoring high on the LCD and LWDS-11 scales, 10.6% of cigarette smokers and 28.6% of waterpipe smokers did not perceive themselves as dependent. These findings are consistent with previous literature showing that people frequently underestimate or deny nicotine dependence due to cultural norms, stigma, or cognitive biases [10, 20, 21]. International studies have similarly documented that self-reported smoking behavior often diverges from biochemical verification, particularly among socially constrained groups such as women and younger adults [22, 23]. In particular, higher objective dependence scores were positively correlated with the underestimation of addiction in the waterpipe group. This reflects evidence suggesting that individuals who are most dependent are often the least willing to admit their dependence, due to defense mechanisms such as denial [24]. In addition, the waterpipe’s cultural image as a harmless social tradition exacerbates this dynamic [25]. An alternative explanation, particularly among younger participants, is limited literacy regarding the addictive potential of waterpipe smoking, whereby underestimation may reflect misperception or lack of awareness rather than intentional impression management.

Environmental exposure also played a crucial role. Underestimation rates were more likely among participants with a greater number of smokers in their environment, such as the home or workplace. This converges with prior findings that social normalization of smoking tends to blunt the perception of risk and addiction [26]. Notably, sociodemographic variables (i.e., age, sex, education level, and income) were not significantly associated with underestimation. This goes hand in hand with the idea that psychological and behavioral traits are more relevant in shaping perceptions around smoking than demographic context [7].

Participants who scored high on “ Reversed Independent Thinking” (were more dependent thinkers) were more likely to underestimate their cigarette dependence, particularly among men. These findings suggest that social conformity and self-image management may be associated with lower levels of acknowledgement of nicotine dependence, particularly within specific subgroups, while individuals with higher independent thinking may resist externally imposed labels such as “dependent,” reinterpret symptoms as voluntary behavior, or perceive their smoking as a personal choice rather than an addiction. Moreover, this is consistent with data showing that self-exempting beliefs, such as “I’m not like other smokers,” and social desirability biases have been identified as important barriers to smoking cessation [24, 27]. In contrast, for waterpipe dependence, different patterns were observed, with more virtuous perfectionism being associated with lower smoking underestimation; this was especially true for women, who are more likely to accurately assess and declare their waterpipe smoking and dependence status. These subgroup-specific associations should be interpreted cautiously, as several estimates were accompanied by wide confidence intervals, reflecting limited precision due to reduced effective sample sizes.

The present data also highlights the role of mental health, particularly depression. Depression scores were significantly associated with the underestimation of dependence. This may reflect a coping mechanism, as individuals with depressive symptoms could rationalize tobacco use as mood regulation or a form of venting, rather than an addiction. Prior studies have confirmed that depression is both a predictor and a consequence of increased tobacco use [28].

Furthermore, a robust cross-substance effect was another important finding in this study. Participants, particularly those under 26 years old, with waterpipe dependence and problematic alcohol use, were more likely to underestimate their cigarette dependence. One possible explanation is that dual or poly-tobacco use may dilute perceived dependence on any single product: individuals may frame cigarette use as “occasional,” while waterpipe use sustains habitual nicotine intake, or they may normalize one product relative to another (e.g., “at least I’m not a cigarette smoker”), reducing the likelihood of labeling their use as dependence. This cross-normalization mechanism may be particularly relevant in Lebanon, where waterpipe smoking is culturally framed as a social and less harmful practice. However, in the absence of biochemical verification, it remains unclear whether these discrepancies primarily reflect differences in nicotine exposure or misperception of dependence severity. In agreement, prior research has indicated that dual users tend to exhibit higher cumulative dependence symptoms in comparison to exclusive users, eventually increasing health risks [11].

A subgroup worth mentioning is the participants who self-identify as non-smokers despite having used tobacco products and scoring high on the dependence scales. They scored lower on “Virtuous Perfectionism” and “Independent Thinking” scores, which suggests a combination of low internal health standards and high social conformity. Participants who considered themselves “social smokers” may dismiss dependence symptoms as situational rather than pathological [16], leading to systematic underestimation.

The findings of the current study are particularly relevant in the context of Lebanon, where cultural factors can shape perceptions of substance use and mental health. The results support targeted public health interventions that address both psychological well-being and the influence of the immediate social environment, especially among younger populations. This also aligns with broader public health interests in how cultural factors shape substance use behaviors in Lebanon. Also, the results have important implications for clinical management and treatment of nicotine dependence. When individuals underestimate their dependence, they are less likely to seek cessation support, to recognize early symptoms of addiction, or to respond accurately to screening tools used in primary care. Misperception may also lead to delayed treatment initiation, lower adherence to cessation interventions, and a reduced likelihood of accepting pharmacological or behavioral therapies. For waterpipe users in particular, under-recognition of dependence may prevent clinicians from assessing withdrawal risk or offering appropriate cessation pathways. Addressing dependence misperception in routine clinical encounters through validated tools and culturally tailored counseling may therefore be essential to improving treatment outcomes.

Comparison with international findings

The current results are consistent with international evidence showing that individuals who smoke occasionally or socially often fail to categorize their behavior as smoking, despite measurable nicotine intake [21]. A key finding requiring clarification is the presence of “underestimation” among individuals who self-identified as non-smokers. This does not reflect underestimation among true non-users; instead, it highlights individuals who declared non-smoking yet had dependence scores above zero. This subgroup likely includes intermittent smokers, misclassified occasional users, or individuals minimizing their smoking behavior due to stigma or social desirability. Thus, their classification as “extreme underestimators” is methodologically justified and agrees with the literature showing that denial of smoking status is common when stigma is high [21]. Our findings closely parallel international evidence demonstrating that self-reported smoking behavior is often inaccurate. Underestimation of dependence has been documented in Western and Asian contexts, particularly among occasional or intermittent smokers [13, 29, 30]. However, unlike studies from high-income countries where cigarette stigma drives misreporting, the Lebanese results appear more strongly shaped by waterpipe normalization and cultural acceptance. In contrast to countries where biochemical verification significantly reduces underestimation [29, 31], the absence of such measures in Lebanon may amplify discrepancies between perceived and actual dependence. Furthermore, very few global studies have examined dependence misperception specifically, making the present findings a valuable contribution.

Strengths and limitations

This study has many strengths, such as its relatively large sample size, use of standardized tools to assess the measured concept, and various statistical models that take into account potential confounding. LCD and LWDS-11 are culturally adapted and validated for Lebanon, offering stronger accuracy for assessing waterpipe smoking compared to international measures such as the Fagerström Test, which does not adequately capture waterpipe-specific dependence characteristics. Both the LCD and LWDS-11 have been validated and widely used in Lebanese epidemiological and clinical research, making them the most appropriate and reliable instruments for assessing cigarette and waterpipe dependence in this population.

However, the present study has additional limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional study design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, although the total sample size of 377 slightly exceeded the calculated minimum, a larger sample would have been preferable. The effective sample size for subgroup analyses (e.g., by gender or emerging adults) was smaller, which may have reduced statistical power for these comparisons. Accordingly, subgroup-specific associations are best viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of effect modification. Also, some predictors in the regression models showed wide confidence intervals, reflecting potential instability in the estimates due to the limited sample size in certain subgroups. Third, the use of an online snowball sampling strategy introduces selection and homophily bias. Participants tend to recruit individuals similar to themselves, resulting in clusters of young, educated, urban respondents. Thus, representativeness is limited. Fourth, social media recruitment favors individuals with stable internet access, which is unevenly distributed in Lebanon due to electricity shortages, high connectivity costs, and poorer infrastructure in rural areas. Vulnerable populations such as older adults, low-income groups, and rural communities are likely underrepresented. Also, the sample’s socioeconomic profile was skewed toward higher SES and higher education, which limits generalizability, partly due to digital recruitment and uneven internet access in Lebanon (among vulnerable populations, for example). Highly educated individuals may be more aware of socially acceptable responses and better able to manage self-presentation, potentially inflating social desirability scores and distorting associations between social desirability traits and dependence underestimation. The underrepresentation of older adults (despite eligibility beginning at age ≥ 18) further restricts external validity. Furthermore, self-report measures are subject to social desirability bias, especially in Lebanon, where stigma surrounding smoking remains high. Although the Lebanese Social Desirability Scale was used to adjust results, its presence does not eliminate biased reporting. Fifth, the absence of biochemical verification (e.g., cotinine testing) limits the interpretation of “objective” dependence, as LCD and LWDS-11 capture self-reported dependence symptoms rather than biological exposure. Accordingly, the study compares perceived dependence labels with scale-based dependence severity, rather than self-perception versus biological reality. All the above-cited limitations warrant a cautious interpretation of the results. Finally, while culturally validated Lebanese dependence scales (LCD and LWDS-11) were used, international comparison with other standardized scales (e.g., the Fagerström Test) may be limited. Nevertheless, these culturally adapted tools provide greater accuracy than international instruments for tobacco dependence. Additional studies that overcome the stated limitations are also suggested, with biological measures, a prospective design, a larger sample size, and higher diversity to confirm the current findings.

Conclusion

These findings should be interpreted within Lebanon’s unique cultural and religious context. The results highlight the potential importance of social and psychological factors in shaping dependence underestimation. In this context, the findings may inform future public health efforts aimed at moving beyond purely physical health messaging. Public health campaigns could potentially encourage self-awareness, challenge conformity, and promote acknowledgment of addiction and help-seeking behaviors. Moreover, integrating mental health screening and support into smoking cessation initiatives may be beneficial, particularly for younger populations.
