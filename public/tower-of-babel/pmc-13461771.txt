Increased CCR5 expression in lymphoma cells and M2 macrophages is associated with poor prognosis in primary intestinal diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma
Authors: Wei‐Li Ma, Tsai‐Yun Chen, Pei‐An Fu, Jo‐Pai Chen, Ming Yao, Hsiu‐Po Wang, Been‐Ren Lin, Chung‐Wu Lin, Chia‐Lang Hsu, Chung‐Yu Huang, Ann‐Lii Cheng, Sung‐Hsin Kuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFew studies have evaluated the impact of organ‐specific tumor microenvironments (TMEs) on clinical outcomes in diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL). This study investigated potential molecular markers, the immune cell composition within the TME, and their associations with clinical outcomes in patients with primary intestinal DLBCL (PI‐DLBCL). We initially analyzed RNA expression in tumor cells from 19 patients with PI‐DLBCL in the experimental cohort. Candidate biomarkers were then assessed in lymphoma tissue from a total of 48 patients in the same cohort, including the 19 with RNA‐expression data, and validated in an independent cohort of 29 patients with PI‐DLBCL. Associations between these markers and clinicopathological features, event‐free survival (EFS), and overall survival (OS) in the two cohorts were analyzed. In the RNA expression panel, CCL5 expression was higher in patients with advanced‐stage PI‐DLBCL and was associated with inferior EFS and OS. Its principal receptor, CCR5, expressed in approximately one‐third of patients, was associated with lower complete response rates to first‐line immunochemotherapy: 50% in the experimental cohort and 10% in the validation cohort and correlated with the 7‐year worse OS rate in both the experimental (49.8% versus 71.4%, p = 0.082) and validation (0% versus 76.2%, p < 0.001) cohorts. In both the experimental and validation cohorts, CCR5‐positive tumors exhibited decreased CD86‐positive (M1‐like) and increased CD206‐positive (M2‐like) macrophage infiltration, consistent with an immunosuppressive TME. Multivariate analyses revealed that CCR5 expression was independently associated with worse EFS (p < 0.001) and OS (p = 0.004) in patients with PI‐DLBCL (combined experimental and validation cohorts). CCR5 expression indicates a biologically aggressive subtype of PI‐DLBCL with poor clinical outcomes and M2 macrophage–dominant immunosuppression.Keywords: CCL5, CCR5, tumor‐associated M2 macrophages, primary intestinal diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, prognosis, chemotherapy

Introduction

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract accounts for approximately 10–15% of all non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) cases and 30–40% of extranodal cases [1]. Koch et al reported that the distribution of primary GI NHLs was 74.8% in the stomach, 8.6% in the small intestine, and 7.0% in the ileocecal region [2]. Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most prevalent histological subtype, accounting for approximately 40% of NHLs, and more than 80% of patients with GI NHL are diagnosed with stage I or II disease [3]. The mainstay of treatment for primary intestinal DLBCL (PI‐DLBCL) is systemic therapy, particularly rituximab‐based immunochemotherapy [4]. In contrast, surgical intervention is often used to manage complications such as intestinal bleeding or perforation in PI‐DLBCL [4, 5].

Genome‐wide gene expression analysis of lymphoma cells has identified three subtypes of DLBCL: germinal center B‐cell (GCB), activated B‐cell (ABC), and unclassifiable DLBCL [6, 7]. The prognosis of patients is not solely determined by cell of origin (COO) classification, as both ABC‐ and GCB‐DLBCL are biologically heterogeneous [8, 9]. Moreover, nodal DLBCL exhibiting concurrent MYC and BCL2 overexpression on immunohistochemical (IHC) labeling is referred to as double‐expressor lymphoma and is associated with a poor prognosis [10]. Even after adjusting for COO in multivariate Cox regression analysis, the detrimental prognostic effect of MYC and BCL2 co‐expression on survival persisted in patients with nodal DLBCL [11]. However, the association between MYC/BCL2 co‐expression and clinical outcomes in patients with PI‐DLBCL has rarely been investigated.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) of B‐cell lymphomas comprises a diverse array of immune cells, stromal cells, blood vessels, and the extracellular matrix [12]. Poor prognosis in patients with DLBCL is also associated with M2‐polarized tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) [13]. Nevertheless, the relationship between chemokines produced by immune cells in the TME and clinical outcomes in DLBCL remains poorly characterized. Additionally, approximately 40% of DLBCL cases are extranodal, which may have distinct clinical outcomes compared with nodal DLBCL [14, 15]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the organ‐specific TME of DLBCL would influence patient outcomes, despite limited data.

In this study, we assessed patterns of co‐expression, chemokine expression, and associated immune cell types within the TME, and their correlation with clinical outcomes in patients with PI‐DLBCL. We further validated the prognostic biomarkers identified in the experimental cohort in an independent cohort of patients with PI‐DLBCL.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Research Ethics Committee of the National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) (IRB number: 201610074RIND and 202102033RINC; approved on 29 December 2017 and 19 April 2021, respectively) and the IRB of the Research Ethics Committee of the National Cheng Kung University Hospital (NCKUH) (IRB number: A‐ER‐111‐465; approved on 19 December 2022). All experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patient selection and diagnosis of PI‐DLBCL

The experimental cohort comprised patients diagnosed with PI‐DLBCL who had complete clinical data and adequate tissue for pathological analysis at the NTUH, a medical center in Northern Taiwan, between January 2001 and December 2020. To validate our findings, we collaborated with attending physicians at another medical center, the NCKUH in Southern Taiwan, and analyzed corresponding pathological tissues and clinical outcome data collected from January 2008 to December 2022 (validation cohort).

Dawson et al proposed the following diagnostic criteria for primary GI lymphoma: (1) absence of palpable superficial lymphadenopathy; (2) no radiographic evidence of mediastinal lymphadenopathy; (3) normal total and differential white blood cell counts; (4) predominant GI tract involvement without lymph node dissemination beyond the regional nodes; and (5) no hepatic or splenic involvement [16]. Lewin et al and Herrmann et al further refined the criteria, emphasizing that lymphomas should primarily involve the intestine, be associated with GI symptoms, and not exhibit features of widespread systemic lymphoma at diagnosis [17, 18]. In addition to the criteria suggested by Dawson et al, Lewin et al, and Herrmann et al, we rigorously defined primary intestinal lymphoma using criteria established by d'Amore et al, who defined primary intestinal lymphoma by ensuring that >75% of the total tumor volume was confined to the intestine and its regional lymph nodes on clinical imaging, excluding non‐GI lymphoma with intestinal involvement [19].

Diagnosis of DLBCL was confirmed according to the fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumors: Lymphoid Neoplasms and the International Consensus Classification of Mature Lymphoid Neoplasms [20, 21]. Patient age, sex, lymphoma involvement sites, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, and bone marrow findings at diagnosis were extracted from the medical records. PI‐DLBCL was staged according to the Lugano classification [22]. The International Prognostic Index (IPI) score was calculated based on the following factors: age >60 years, elevated LDH levels, Ann Arbor stage III–IV, ECOG performance status ≥2, and extranodal involvement >1 site (excluding primary intestinal involvement) [23]. Most patients underwent four to six cycles of first‐line therapy, with subsequent treatment adjusted based on clinical status. Follow‐up imaging included whole‐body computed tomography (CT) every 3 months, with additional CT or positron emission tomography‐CT (PET‐CT) performed as needed.

RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction from human tumor samples

Using TaqManTM OpenArray® technology, the ACT TMETM platform (developed by ACT Genomics Co., Ltd., Taipei, Taiwan) was used to measure the expression of 102 immune‐related genes involved in immune cell population profiling, antigen presentation, immune checkpoint regulation, and other TME‐related modulators [24]. Additionally, 10 genes related to assay integrity and internal controls were included [24].

We extracted RNA from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissues of patients with PI‐DLBCL; samples yielding <100 ng of RNA was excluded. RNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using the GeneAmp PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The resulting cDNA was pre‐amplified using a Veriti™ 96‐Well Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to ensure sufficient templates for subsequent quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Following pre‐amplification, the PCR sample mix was transferred to a TaqMan OpenArray ACTCHIP01 plate. The sample and master mixture were subsequently loaded onto a QuantStudio™ 12 K Flex OpenArray® chip containing 48 subarrays using the QuantStudio™ 12 K Flex AccuFill™ System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The loaded OpenArray® chip was placed into the QuantStudio™ 12 K Flex Real‐Time PCR System for data acquisition. Only samples meeting the following quality control criteria were included in downstream analyses: (1) detection of at least two integrity control assays; (2) detection of at least one internal control assay; (3) detection of >50% of target genes; and (4) scaling factor within ±5.

Bioinformatics analysis of qPCR results

The t‐test was used to compare significantly different gene expression levels between groups. The resulting values were visualized using a volcano plot, with the x‐axis representing log2 fold change (log2FC) and the y‐axis representing –log10(p value), allowing simultaneous assessment of the effect size and statistical significance. Genes with a p value <0.05 were determined as significantly differentially expressed.

Immunohistochemical staining and scoring

FFPE tissue samples obtained at diagnosis were immunohistochemically stained for the C‐C chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5)‐C‐C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) axis and macrophage polarization markers, including CCR5, CD86 (M1 macrophages), and CD206 (M2 macrophages). Double‐expressor lymphoma was defined as concurrent MYC nuclear expression in >40% of lymphoma cells and BCL2 membranous expression in >50% of lymphoma cells by IHC [11]. Sections (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated in xylene and ethanol. Anti‐human primary antibodies included CCR5 (catalog no. MAB181; R&D Systems, Bio‐Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA), CD86 (catalog no. sc‐28347; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), CD206 (catalog no. sc‐376108; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), MYC (clone Y69; ab32072; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and BCL2 (clone SP66; ab236221; Abcam) [11, 25, 26, 27]. After incubation with biotin‐conjugated secondary antibodies, antibody binding was detected using avidin–biotin enzyme reagent. We initially quantified the percentage of CCR5‐positive lymphoma cells among all lymphoma cells, as there is no established criterion for defining CCR5 expression as positive or negative in lymphoma cells. We then determined the optimal cutoff percentage of CCR5 expression to assess its association with event‐free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS). TAMs positive for CD86 and CD206 were counted under a light microscope, and the average cell count was calculated from five high‐power fields (HPF; 400× magnification) per tissue section [28].

Statistical analysis

Fisher's exact test was used to evaluate categorical data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables. The analysis included follow‐up data available as of 31 December 2025. EFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to relapse, progression, or death, while OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause [29]. Survival curves for EFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Potential prognostic factors for EFS and OS were analyzed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value <0.05.

Results

Characteristics and treatment modalities of patients with PI‐DLBCL

Table 1 summarizes the clinicopathological features and treatment characteristics of 48 patients with PI‐DLBCL in the NTUH cohort (experimental cohort) and 29 patients in the NCKUH cohort (validation cohort). Median age (p = 0.777), sex distribution (p = 1.000), and rates of elevated LDH levels (p = 0.348) were comparable between the two cohorts. The stage distribution, IPI score, and primary tumor location (small or large intestine) did not differ between the experimental and validation cohorts (Table 1). More than 80% of patients in both cohorts received first‐line rituximab‐based immunochemotherapy regimens, with approximately 60% receiving anthracycline‐containing regimens [e.g. rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisolone (R‐CHOP)] [30]. Furthermore, the complete response (CR) rate after first‐line therapy was 73% (35/48) in the experimental cohort and 62% (18/29) in the validation cohort (Table 1).

Analysis of clinical outcomes and different gene expressions in PI‐DLBCL

Nineteen tumor specimens from patients with PI‐DLBCL (experimental cohort) were analyzed. Supplementary material, Table S1 summarizes the clinical features and therapeutic options of the patients. The cohort comprised 6 female and 13 male patients, aged between 37 and 76 years. At diagnosis, 12 patients had stage II disease, one had stage III disease, and six had stage IV disease. First‐line treatment consisted of R‐CHOP in 15 (79%) patients and CHOP in 4 (21%) patients. As shown in the volcano plot, CCL5 expression was higher in patients with stage III–IV (late‐stage disease) than in those with stage I–II (early‐stage disease) (Figure 1A). Several genes were differentially expressed between patients with favorable (longer than the median) and unfavorable (shorter than the median) EFS or OS (supplementary material, Figure S1A,B). High BCL2 expression was associated with worse EFS (p = 0.11) and OS (p = 0.14) in the Cox regression analysis of the top 10 differentially expressed genes by EFS and OS. Likewise, higher CCL5 expression was associated with worse EFS (p = 0.17) and OS (p = 0.18) (Figure 1B,C).

Expression patterns of CCR5, CD86, CD206, and MYC/BCL2 co‐expression in patients with PI‐DLBCL

Given the association of CCL5 with advanced disease stage and inferior EFS and OS, we evaluated the prognostic relevance of its primary receptor, CCR5 [31], in both cohorts. Because MYC was not included in the gene expression panel and MYC/BCL2 co‐expression has prognostic relevance, we additionally performed IHC staining to assess MYC and BCL2 expression in tumor cells from both cohorts. Representative images of CCR5‐expressing lymphoma cells, CD86‐positive cells, and CD206‐positive cells in the TME of patients with PI‐DLBCL are shown in Figure 2A–F. CCR5 expression in >20% of lymphoma cells was observed in 18 patients (38%) in the experimental cohort and 10 patients (34%) in the validation cohort, with no significant difference observed between the two cohorts (p = 0.812). Furthermore, therapeutic regimens were assessed based on CCR5 expression status across the experimental, validation, and combined cohorts, with patients classified as receiving first‐line rituximab with anthracycline, rituximab without anthracycline, or no rituximab. Over 70% of patients in both the CCR5‐positive and CCR5‐negative cohorts were administered rituximab‐based therapies, which are essential for the treatment of DLBCL (supplementary material, Table S2).

To examine the association between CCR5 expression in lymphoma cells and TAMs within the TME, we assessed the densities of CD86‐ and CD206‐positive cells according to the CCR5 status in lymphoma cells. As shown in Figure 2G, in the experimental cohort, CCR5‐positive lymphoma tissues contained significantly fewer CD86‐positive cells than CCR5‐negative tissues [median, 6 (range, 1–46) versus 15 (range, 3–58) cells per HPF; p = 0.012]. Similar findings were observed for CD86 in the validation cohort [median, 6 (range, 1–48) versus 28 (range, 2–66) cells per HPF; p = 0.001]. When the two cohorts were combined, CCR5‐positive tumors consistently exhibited lower CD86‐positive cell densities than CCR5‐negative tumors [median, 6 (range, 1–48) versus 22 (range, 2–66) cells per HPF; p < 0.001]. As shown in Figure 2H, in the experimental cohort, the median number of CD206‐positive cells was higher in CCR5‐positive tumors than in CCR5‐negative tumors [median, 11 (range, 2–48) versus 3 (range, 0–10) cells per HPF; p < 0.001]. A similar pattern was observed in the validation cohort [median, 37 (range, 12–66) versus 14 (range, 3–48) cells per HPF; p = 0.001]. Pooled analysis of both cohorts confirmed a significantly higher CD206‐positive cell density in CCR5‐positive tumors than in CCR5‐negative tumors [median, 24 (range, 2–66) versus 6 (range, 0–48) cells per HPF; p < 0.001].

MYC/BCL2 co‐expression (double‐expressor lymphoma) was observed in 10% (5/48) of patients in the experimental group, 28% (8/29) in the validation cohort, and 17% (13/77) overall, with no statistically significant differences between cohorts (p = 0.065).

Clinical outcomes of patients with PI‐DLBCL according to prognostic factors

In the experimental cohort, after a median follow‐up of 8.8 years, patients with IPI scores of 0–1 (low risk), 2–3 (intermediate risk), and 4–5 (high risk) had 7‐year EFS rates for first‐line therapy of 59.3%, 29.2%, and 17.9%, respectively (p = 0.049). The corresponding 7‐year OS rates were 76.2%, 49.5%, and 35.7% for IPI scores of 0–1, 2–3, and 4–5, respectively (p = 0.079) (Figure 3A,B). Likewise, in the validation cohort, the 7‐year EFS rates for first‐line therapy were 57.1%, 50.9%, and 0%, respectively (p = 0.067), and the 7‐year OS rates were 66.7%, 50.9%, and 0%, respectively, for the same risk groups (p = 0.032) (Figure 3C,D). When both cohorts were combined, the 7‐year EFS rates for first‐line therapy were 58.3%, 37.4%, and 13.6%, respectively (p = 0.007), whereas the 7‐year OS rates were 73.3%, 48.0%, and 25.5%, respectively (p = 0.006) for IPI scores of 0–1, 2–3, and 4–5 (Figure 3E,F).

To further explore the clinical relevance of CCL5, we evaluated CCR5 expression using IHC and quantified the proportion of CCR5‐positive lymphoma cells in tumor tissues. In the experimental (supplementary material, Figure S2A,B) and validation (supplementary material, Figure S3A,B) cohorts, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that a cutoff of >20% CCR5‐positive lymphoma cells defined CCR5 positivity. The distribution of intestinal involvement and disease stage did not differ significantly between the CCR5‐positive and CCR5‐negative groups in the experimental, validation, or combined cohorts (Figure 4A,B). Irrespective of CCR5 status, approximately 50%, 40%, and 10% of cases involved the small intestine, large intestine, and multiple sites, respectively, and approximately 60% of patients presented with stage I–II disease compared with 40% presenting with stage III–IV disease (Figure 4A,B). In contrast, CCR5‐positive lymphoma was significantly associated with a lower CR rate after first‐line immunochemotherapy than CCR5‐negative lymphoma across all cohorts (Figure 4C).

In the experimental cohort, CCR5‐positive patients had worse 7‐year EFS (19.6% versus 59.1%, p = 0.004) and OS (49.8% versus 71.4%, p = 0.082) than CCR5‐negative patients (Figure 4D,E). In the validation cohort, CCR5‐positive patients had significantly poorer 7‐year EFS (0% versus 65.9%, p < 0.001) and OS (0% versus 76.2%, p < 0.001) than CCR5‐negative patients (Figure 4F,G). Finally, when both cohorts were combined, patients with CCR5‐positive tumors demonstrated significantly worse 7‐year EFS (13.9% versus 61.9%, p < 0.001) and OS rates (34.4% versus 73.0%, p < 0.001) than their CCR5‐negative counterparts (Figure 4H,I). Furthermore, patients with MYC/BCL2 co‐expression (double‐expressor lymphoma) did not demonstrate significantly worse 7‐year EFS or OS in the experimental, validation, or combined cohorts (supplementary material, Figure S4A–F).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of EFS and OS

In the univariate analysis, IPI scores of 4–5 were significantly associated with worse EFS [hazard ratio (HR) = 3.641; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.532–8.653; p = 0.003] and OS (HR = 4.206; 95% CI: 1.633–10.835; p = 0.003). Patients with CCR5‐positive lymphomas had worse EFS (HR = 4.527; 95% CI: 2.338–8.763; p < 0.001) and OS (HR = 3.566; 95% CI: 1.724–7.380; p = 0.001). Tumor site and MYC/BCL2 co‐expression (double‐expressor status) were not associated with poor clinical outcomes in PI‐DLBCL. Because the IPI scores of the 2–3 group were not significantly associated with worse EFS or OS compared with the IPI scores of the 0–1 group in the univariate analysis, we combined IPI scores of 0–3 into one category and compared this group with IPI scores of 4–5 in the multivariate model. Multivariate analysis revealed that CCR5 positivity was significantly associated with worse EFS (HR = 4.106; 95% CI: 2.064–8.170; p < 0.001) and OS (HR = 3.091; 95% CI: 1.444–6.614; p = 0.004) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we identified that elevated CCL5 expression is associated with advanced‐stage disease and inferior clinical outcomes in a small experimental cohort using RNA expression analysis. We subsequently extended these findings to larger cohorts, from both the experimental and validation cohorts, demonstrating that a high‐risk subset of PI‐DLBCL is characterized by expression of its major receptor, CCR5. This subtype of CCR5‐positive PI‐DLBCL is characterized by an immunosuppressive TME with decreased CD86‐positive M1‐like and increased CD206‐positive M2‐like TAMs, poor treatment response, inferior survival outcomes, and prognostic relevance independent of the IPI score.

CCL5 (also known as RANTES), a member of the C‐C motif chemokine family, which also includes CCL3 (MIP‐1α) and CCL4 (MIP‐1β), primarily binds to CCR5 but also interacts with CCR1, CCR3, and CCR4 [31, 32]. The CCL5–CCR5 signaling axis promotes the development of an immunosuppressive TME and cancer progression [33]. NF‐κB activation in breast cancer induces CCL5 expression, thereby recruiting CCR5‐positive macrophages to the tumor site. These TAMs subsequently produce collagen, promoting tumor recurrence [34]. In colorectal cancer, the CCL5–CCR5 axis regulates the immunosuppressive activity of TAMs within the TME. These TAMs can be reprogrammed into anti‐cancer phenotypes by CCR5 inhibition, which has been linked to objective clinical responses [35].

CCL5 expression is strongly associated with CD68‐positive macrophage infiltration in classical human Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) specimens, and patients with elevated CCL5 levels have worse clinical outcomes [36]. The TME exhibits elevated CCL5 levels, and cHL tumor cells express functional CCR5 receptors, suggesting that CCL5–CCR5 signaling contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME and tumor progression in cHL [36]. Certain CCL5‐expressing tumor cells in DLBCL selectively recruit circulating monocytes via CCR1 and CCR5. The majority of TAMs in DLBCL exhibit a non‐canonical gene expression profile characterized by co‐expression of M1‐ and M2‐associated genes [37]. High expression of CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, and CCR5 in pediatric B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia promotes terminal differentiation of exhausted CD8+ T cells and is associated with poor prognosis [38]. Wen et al reported that high expression of monocyte chemotactic protein‐1 (MCP‐1), also known as CCL2, was significantly correlated with high expression of CD68, a macrophage marker, or CD168, an M2 macrophage marker, in the TMEs [39]. High expression of each of these markers was also significantly associated with worse progression‐free survival (PFS) and OS in patients with DLBCL [39]. Furthermore, CCR2 antagonist injection reduced tumor growth in an A20 murine B‐lymphoma cell‐derived xenograft BALB/C mice model by increasing CD8+ cells and reducing M2 macrophage infiltration in the TME [39]. These findings, together with literature reviews by Aldinucci et al and Zeng et al on the role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in hematologic malignancies and solid cancers and on therapeutic approaches targeting CCL5/CCR5 [32, 40], support the importance of chemokine axes, including MCP‐1/CCR2/M2 and particularly CCL5/CCR5, in tumor cells, the TME, and DLBCL progression.

In the present study, we found that CCR5 expression was significantly associated with increased CD206 infiltration and decreased CD86 cell density in the TME of patients with PI‐DLBCL. These findings corroborate previous reports indicating that elevated CCL5 levels in the TME, together with increased CCR5 expression in tumor cells, may promote M2 macrophage enrichment and are associated with poorer clinical outcomes in patients with PI‐DLBCL. We acknowledge that Manfroi et al previously showed that the DLBCL microenvironment is characterized by noncanonical macrophages recruited by tumor‐derived CCL5, providing the most directly relevant precedent for the CCL5/CCR5 axis in general DLBCL [37]. In the present study, we extend this concept to the rare organ‐specific subtype of PI‐DLBCL by demonstrating the prognostic significance of the CCL5/CCR5 axis and by showing CCR5 expression on lymphoma cells, rather than only on TAMs. The CCL5/CCR5 axis was strongly associated with worse 7‐year EFS in the experimental cohort through discovery of a 102‐gene RNA‐expression panel paired with IHC confirmation. In the validation cohort, CCR5 retained poor prognostic significance for 7‐year EFS. Furthermore, CCR5 remained an independent adverse prognostic factor after adjustment for IPI scores. The poor prognosis associated with CCR5 in PI‐DLBCL was mechanistically coherent with the observed downregulation of M1 macrophages and upregulation of M2 macrophages in the DLBCL TMEs.

Hu et al reported that MYC/BCL2 co‐expression was more common in the ABC subtype. In the absence of co‐expression, there was no difference in prognosis between the ABC and GCB subtypes, suggesting that MYC/BCL2 co‐expression, rather than COO, is a more reliable prognostic indicator in patients with DLBCL undergoing R‐CHOP [10]. Chen et al found that the clinical outcomes of PI‐DLBCL did not differ significantly between GCB and non‐GCB subtypes [41]. Therefore, we did not classify patients with PI‐DLBCL into the GCB or non‐GCB subtypes. In our PI‐DLBCL cohort (combined experimental and validation cohorts), double‐expressor status was observed in 17% of patients, which is slightly lower than the previously reported rate of 21% [11]. In our patients with PI‐DLBCL, double‐expressor status was not significantly associated with worse EFS and OS. We did not perform double‐hit analysis because double‐hit DLBCL primarily presents with nodal disease at diagnosis, less frequent extranodal involvement, and rare small‐intestinal involvement [42, 43]. Further studies with larger PI‐DLBCL cohorts are needed to elucidate the interaction between genomic alterations and the TMEs, although the two cohorts in our investigation consistently linked CCR5 expression in PI‐DLBCL, CCL5 in the TME, and M2 macrophage enrichment to poor prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, bias may have been introduced owing to its retrospective design and the heterogeneity of treatment regimens, including variations in dosages, cycles, and imaging follow‐up intervals. Differences in the therapeutic regimens of first‐line treatments may have contributed to residual confounding in the link discovered between CCR5 expression and clinical outcomes in our populations. Patient outcomes may have been influenced by changes in clinical practice and the introduction of newer therapies during the study period, which ranged from 2001 to 2022. Furthermore, we performed qPCR rather than more comprehensive approaches, such as RNA sequencing, to assess gene expression in the TME of PI‐DLBCL. Although the numbers of patients at risk were provided below the Kaplan–Meier curves, the ROC‐derived optimal cut‐off for CCR5 positivity and the wide confidence intervals, particularly during long‐term follow‐up of the small CCR5‐positive subgroup, warrant cautious interpretation and independent confirmation.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that CCR5 expression is significantly associated with M2 macrophage–dominant immunosuppression and poor clinical outcomes in patients with PI‐DLBCL. These results highlight potential therapeutic targets and prognostic biomarkers related to the CCL5–CCR5 axis in PI‐DLBCL.
