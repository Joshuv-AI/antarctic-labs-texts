Clinical importance of the 5-Step Precision Medicine (5SPM) method in shared decision-making to choose the antipsychotic treatment for a patient with first-episode psychosis: a case report
Authors: Óscar Gonzalo, Carmen García Cerdán, David Heredero Jung, Sandra Lorenzo Hernández, Alejandro de la Sota Pérez, Rocío García García, Pablo Salas Aranda, Carmen Martín Gómez, Concha Turrión Gómez, María Isidoro-García, Jesus Perez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse effects can disrupt early treatment in first-episode psychosis and precipitate discontinuation. We report the use of the five-step precision medicine (5SPM) method in personalised precision psychiatry to guide antipsychotic decision-making in a patient with first-episode psychosis.Case presentationA 26-year-old woman with regular tobacco and cannabis use was admitted to an acute psychiatric ward with persecutory delusions, behavioural dysregulation, anxiety and insomnia. Olanzapine (7.5 mg) was initiated and aripiprazole (20 mg) added to transition to aripiprazole monotherapy. Soon after aripiprazole introduction, she developed pain and cramps circumscribed to her arms, rejected treatment, and symptoms did not remit. A 5SPM-guided assessment, including pharmacogenetic testing and a structured review of pharmacokinetic interactions and environmental factors, provided a coherent basis to interpret an uncommon tolerability signal and to rationalise a change in antipsychotic strategy. Following shared decision-making, aripiprazole was withdrawn and olanzapine optimised; arm symptoms resolved within days, psychotic symptoms ceased, and she was discharged. At six months, she remained adherent to pharmacological treatment, which was eventually switched to long-acting injectable olanzapine following the patient’s preference, had stopped cannabis and returned to work.ConclusionsIn this case, precision-guided shared decision-making was associated with treatment continuity in early psychosis by resolving an atypical adverse effect and improving clinical and functional outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08158-y.

Introduction

Discontinuation rates for antipsychotic therapy exceed 40% within the first year after a first-episode psychosis [1]. Insufficient clinical response and adverse drug reactions constitute major drivers of treatment withdrawal [2]. This supports the need to individualise antipsychotic selection and dose from the earliest stages of a psychotic illness, not only to optimise symptomatic outcomes, but also to prevent adverse effects and preserve treatment adherence. Personalised precision psychiatry seeks to address these limitations through a systematic integration of biological and environmental determinants to inform individualised prescribing decisions from the earliest phases of the illness.

The Unit of Pharmacogenetics and Precision Medicine at the University Hospital of Salamanca (HUS) has developed an internationally pioneering award-winning framework, known as the 5-Step Precision Medicine (5SPM) method, to implement pharmacogenetics across different clinical specialties [3–4]. The 5-Step Precision Medicine (5SPM) method is a structured pharmacogenetics-informed workflow that integrates clinical and epidemiological information, review of pharmacokinetic interactions, targeted genotyping, and treatment adjustment with subsequent reassessment. Its implementation in first-episode psychosis has been described in detail elsewhere [5].

At the Salamanca University Healthcare Complex (CAUSA), Spain, the PRevention and early INTervention (PRINT) in psychosis service was created to provide comprehensive care to adolescents and young adults experiencing a first-episode psychosis. Building on previous applications of the 5SPM method in psychiatric settings [4, 6], this pharmacogenetics-informed approach has been incorporated into PRINT through the CLUMP (CLinical Utility of early intervention including the 5-Step Precision Medicine Method in first-episode Psychosis) project [5], thereby establishing a pharmacogenetically enriched early-intervention setting that provides the clinical context for this case report.

We present a first-episode psychosis case characterised by an adverse effect with significant impact on treatment acceptance, and a challenging pharmacogenetic profile. We use this case to illustrate how a structured 5SPM evaluation can help distinguish adverse drug reactions from illness-related or contextual symptoms, generate a plausible pharmacokinetic explanation in patients receiving polypharmacy, and support shared decision-making when an unexpected adverse effect threatens treatment adherence and symptomatic recovery.

Case description

In November 2024, a 26-year-old woman with psychotic symptoms was admitted to the acute psychiatric ward at the HUS. She had a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism and no relevant personal or family psychiatric history. She had not received previous psychiatric treatment. She lived alone, had little contact with her parents, had no social circle, and voluntarily quit her last job. She reported regular tobacco and cannabis use. Laboratory analyses revealed no relevant alterations, except for abnormalities in lipid metabolism (HDL cholesterol 31 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol 137 mg/dL) and history of anemia (currently folate 3.6 ng/mL, haemoglobine 15.1 g/dL). TSH levels were normal (2.1 mIU/L). Toxicological screening was positive for cannabis and benzodiazepines. Cranial computed tomography showed no relevant radiological abnormalities.

Several weeks prior to admission, a natural disaster occurred in Valencia, Spain, resulting in substantial human and material losses, with hundreds of deaths, injuries, and missing people. The patient expressed beliefs concerning “the preparation of a coup d’état and the approval of the regional government to allow the natural disaster to create chaos in the area and facilitate the execution of the coup.” She was admitted with persecutory delusions. She was aggressive, suspicious, hostile, hypervigilant, irritable, and distressed by the content of her delusions. She presented with ideational and somatic anxiety, insomnia, hyporexia, and impaired reality testing. The patient was clinically assessed as presenting with a first-episode psychosis. Differential diagnostic considerations included substance-induced psychosis related to cannabis use and psychosis secondary to a medical condition, particularly given the history of hyperthyroidism. Nevertheless, the available laboratory and neuroimaging findings did not identify a clear acute organic explanation for the presentation, and the overall clinical picture was considered most consistent with first-episode psychosis.

The patient was initially treated with olanzapine 7.5 mg/day to manage hostility and aggressive behavior, while continuing her medical treatment with thiamazole 5 mg, propranolol 10 mg and folic acid 5 mg. At that stage, she was informed about the potential adverse effects of olanzapine, including increased appetite and weight gain. When she was admitted to the psychiatric ward, aripiprazole 20 mg/day was added with the intention of reducing olanzapine and maintain aripiprazole in monotherapy. However, the patient was reluctant to try other antipsychotic options, as she believed they would be less well tolerated. In order to respect her preferences and support treatment adherence, olanzapine was maintained alongside aripiprazole during the transition period.

Following the introduction of aripiprazole, she began to experience pain and cramps exclusively circumscribed to her arms, which led her to develop a negative attitude towards pharmacological treatment. She also exhibited persistent delusional ideation with marked behavioural and affective impact. Because the patient was being managed within the PRINT/CLUMP clinical pathway [5], the treating team undertook a 5SPM-informed evaluation as part of routine care. In view of the adverse effects observed and the partial clinical recovery, they sought to characterise her pharmacogenetic profile in order to explore whether pharmacokinetic factors could be contributing to poor tolerability and limited response. Briefly, the analysis covered key CYP450 genes involved in antipsychotic metabolism, together with ABCB1/MDR1 [7, 8]. The patient was identified as a highly inducible phenotype for CYP1A2, a poor metaboliser for CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A5, and exhibited altered activity of the MDR1 transporter protein (Table 3). Aripiprazole was identified as a potential inhibitor of CYP2D6 [9–10], with the capacity to partially reduce the metabolism of CYP2D6 substrate drugs in polypharmacy situations like this. Olanzapine is predominantly metabolised via CYP1A2, with only a minor contribution from CYP2D6 [11–13]; in this patient, habitual tobacco smoking is expected to induce CYP1A2 transcription [14], further increasing olanzapine clearance in the context of a genetically determined highly-inducibility CYP1A2 phenotype. Even in a patient genotypically classified as a CYP2D6 normal metabolizer (*1/*41), functional CYP2D6 capacity may become clinically relevant, and effectively limited, under conditions of high substrate load and limited alternative metabolic routes for concomitant agents. In this context, aripiprazole, acting as a high-affinity CYP2D6 substrate [9–10], may have contributed to competitive inhibition at CYP2D6, thereby potentially affecting the pharmacokinetics of co-administered CYP2D6-dependent drugs such as propranolol and, to a lesser extent, olanzapine, in a patient with limited availability of alternative efficient metabolic pathways. However, propranolol had been prescribed as part of the management of the patient’s hyperthyroidism, and the relevant specialist was consulted and preferred to maintain the existing treatment unchanged. Therefore, the immediate therapeutic adjustment focused on withdrawing aripiprazole, which was considered the most plausible driver of the newly emergent tolerability problem.

Following the results of pharmacogenetic analyses, the patient was consulted to find out her opinion regarding her polypharmacy situation. The findings were explained to her in plain clinical language by a psychiatrist (JP) with expertise in pharmacogenetics, using a colour-coded table to facilitate understanding of the potential interactions between her genes and medications (see Table 3). This was intended to support shared decision-making and ensure that the patient could meaningfully participate in the discussion of treatment options. After the explanation, the patient wanted to stop aripiprazole. She said that since she started taking it, “something was not right.” As previously stated, she reported the onset of pain and cramps in her arms since she began taking aripiprazole. With all this genetic, clinical, and environmental information, and in light of the potential drug-drug interactions observed, the persistence of symptoms, and the emergence of adverse effects, the treating multidisciplinary team, along with the patient, decided to withdraw aripiprazole in order to reduce metabolic competition at CYP2D6 and optimize overall pharmacokinetic performance. In addition, a formal adverse drug reaction causality assessment using the Naranjo algorithm [15] yielded a score of 5, indicating a probable relationship between aripiprazole and the arm pain/cramps.

Follow-up and outcomes

After a few days on psychopharmacological treatment with oral olanzapine alone, the pain and cramps in the patient’s arms resolved and psychotic symptoms improved, allowing discharge to outpatient follow-up. Over the following 6 months, she remained engaged with treatment, and oral olanzapine was later switched to long-acting injectable olanzapine 450 mg according to her preference. During follow-up, she stopped cannabis use, improved her relationship with her family, and returned to work. Weight gain was observed during the first months of olanzapine treatment, but this plateaued after month 4, together with lifestyle changes including a healthier diet and joining a gym. No clinically relevant abnormalities were identified in routine monitoring tests. Overall, this course suggested a favourable short- to medium-term prognosis.

The patient consistently linked the onset of arm pain and cramps to the introduction of aripiprazole and stated that since starting it, “something was not right.” Tolerability and adherence were assessed through the patient’s subjective reports, clinical observation during admission, her attitude towards medication, and follow-up psychiatric interviews. The adverse effects and negative attitude towards treatment emerged after aripiprazole introduction and improved after its withdrawal, while continued follow-up engagement and subsequent preference for long-acting injectable olanzapine supported improved treatment acceptance.

Discussion

This is the first report describing the application of the 5SPM method in personalised precision psychiatry to guide antipsychotic decision-making in a patient with first-episode psychosis, resulting not only in clinical remission of psychotic symptoms, but also in improved functionality and unusual side effects cessation.

This case illustrates how the 5SPM method, within an early intervention for psychosis service, contributed to a clinically coherent interpretation of possible pharmacogenetic and pharmacokinetic factors relevant to uncommon side effects of aripiprazole [16–18], which ceased by introducing a new 5SPM-based treatment plan. Although aripiprazole is usually preferred as a maintenance antipsychotic in patients with first-episode psychosis due to fewer side effects than other antipsychotics, such as olanzapine [19–20], in this patient the pharmacogenetic profile, clinical context, and patient preferences were considered in revising the antipsychotic strategy and retaining olanzapine as the maintenance antipsychotic. This treatment revision was followed by adherence and positive effects on the patient’s clinical and functional outcomes, thus avoiding discontinuation [2]. Cannabis cessation should also be considered as a potential confounder. However, cannabis levels were already negative when blood was drawn for the 5SPM assessment, at a stage when psychotic symptoms and adverse effects were still persisting. This temporal pattern suggests that cannabis cessation alone was unlikely to explain either the subsequent clinical improvement or the resolution of the adverse effects, although it may still have contributed to the broader course of recovery.

Another striking aspect of this case is that the patient noticed from the outset that the introduction of aripiprazole was not beneficial. The multidisciplinary team considered this complaint, which could otherwise have been interpreted as an expression of reluctance to continue treatment, and used the 5SPM framework to assess whether her concern was clinically plausible, thus integrating the patient’s views into the pharmacological choice and shared decision-making process.

In conclusion, this case supports the potential clinical utility of embedding a structured precision-psychiatry workflow within early intervention services to address unexpected tolerability problems that threaten treatment continuity in first-episode psychosis. In a phase when adherence is particularly fragile, precision-guided shared decision-making may help reduce avoidable discontinuation and its associated clinical consequences. The 5SPM method provided an integrated interpretation of symptom chronology, pharmacokinetic interactions and patient-reported experience, which supported a change in antipsychotic strategy that was followed by symptom remission, functional improvement, cessation of the adverse effect and sustained adherence.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of the use of the 5SPM method to guide antipsychotic treatment in first-episode psychosis, and a rare report of pain and cramps exclusively circumscribed to the arms after aripiprazole introduction in this clinical context. A key strength of this case is the clinically coherent temporal sequence, together with the integration of the 5SPM method to interpret an unusual adverse effect and translate that information into shared therapeutic decision-making. A key limitation of this case is that the proposed attribution of these symptoms to aripiprazole, as well as the underlying pharmacokinetic explanation, remains inferential. Although the adverse effect emerged soon after aripiprazole initiation and resolved after its withdrawal, the interpretation was based on clinical, pharmacogenetic, and contextual information gathered in routine care and was made in the context of polypharmacy and other potentially relevant clinical factors.

An additional limitation is that pharmacogenetic results were not available before aripiprazole was introduced. Consequently, in this case the 5SPM assessment informed treatment revision rather than initial antipsychotic selection, and earlier availability of these results might have helped anticipate and possibly avoid the adverse effects experienced by the patient.

A further limitation is that plasma drug levels were not measured in this case during follow-up. Therefore, the proposed pharmacokinetic mechanism remains hypothetical and should be understood as a clinically reasoned interpretation based on pharmacogenetic, clinical, and contextual information rather than on direct therapeutic drug monitoring data.

Patient perspective

This case highlights the importance of considering the patient’s subjective experience when adverse effects threaten treatment adherence in first-episode psychosis. In this case, the patient clearly identified the introduction of aripiprazole as the moment when “something was not right”, associating it with pain and cramps in her arms and with a reduced willingness to continue treatment. The subsequent shared decision-making process, supported by the 5SPM assessment, allowed her concerns to be incorporated into treatment planning. After aripiprazole withdrawal, she reported improved tolerability and greater confidence in continuing treatment, later expressing preference for long-acting injectable olanzapine as part of her maintenance strategy.
