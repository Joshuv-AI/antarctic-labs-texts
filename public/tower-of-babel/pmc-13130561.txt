Clinical outcomes and complications of the indirect modified arthroscopic posterior transseptal approach at a minimum 2-year follow-up
Authors: Christian Arras, Georg Gaentzsch, Karl-Heinz Frosch, Markus T. Berninger, Selina Loose, Alexander Korthaus, Matthias Krause, Jannik Frings
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe arthroscopic posterior transseptal approach (TSA) allows improved visualization of the posterior compartment of the knee joint. However, it can be associated with increased risks particularly regarding potential injury to the neurovascular bundle or postoperative scarring, which may contribute to extension deficits of the knee joint. We hypothesized that the modified TSA does not increase the risk of postoperative extension deficit or neurovascular complications while providing safe access to the posterior compartment.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed prospectively collected data from patients who underwent arthroscopic knee surgery via TSA between 01/2018 and 02/2024. The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of perioperative complications. The secondary outcome measures were postoperative complications, postoperative range of motion compared with the contralateral (control) knee, and functional outcomes measured by the subjective International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score and Tegner score.ResultsA total of 82 patients were eligible for the study. No perioperative complications were observed. 48 patients (10 females, 38 males; mean age, 37.8 ± 13.5 years) completed follow-up and were available for secondary outcome analysis at a mean follow-up of 45.9 ± 20.7 months. Indications for the TSA included posterior and/or posterolateral knee instability (n = 36), chronic knee dislocation (n = 8), posterior compartment synovectomy for tenosynovial giant cell tumors (n = 2), posterior meniscal pathology (n = 3), and posterior cruciate ligament cysts (n = 2). Patient-reported outcomes were good (IKDC 77.9 ± 17.4; Tegner 4.5 ± 1.7). There were no relevant side-to-side (SSD) differences in knee extension (mean SSD = 0.39, p = 0.098).ConclusionsThe indirect posterior transseptal approach is a safe arthroscopic technique that can be associated with moderate clinical outcomes depending on the underlying pathology. The modified TSA appears to be safe when performed by experienced surgeons and was not associated with neurovascular complications in this cohort.

Background

Advances in arthroscopic techniques over the past decades have led to an expansion of the range of knee pathologies that can be treated via knee arthroscopy [1]. However, especially when assessing the posterior aspects of the knee conventional anterior portals often provide insufficient visualization and limited access [2–4]. In this context, posterior arthroscopic approaches are often utilized and can be regarded as standard approaches in complex knee surgery. However, for some indications, the addition of a posterior transseptal approach (TSA) is needed. Common indications for the TSA are the treatment of posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) lesions, arthroscopic reconstructions of the posterolateral corner (PLC) of the knee or posterior compartment synovectomy for the resection of tenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCTs) [5–8].

In addition to the advantages of the posterior transseptal approach in terms of better visualization and access to posterior structures, the establishment of the TSA carries certain risks. Owing to its proximity to critical posterior neurovascular structures the risk of iatrogenic damage particularly to the popliteal and middle geniculate arteries has been described [9]. Additionally, potential postoperative scarring of the posterior capsule resulting from the approach could contribute to an extension deficit of the knee joint. Ahn et al. proposed a surgical technique for the development of the TSA, which involves transcondylar preparation and visualization between the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and PCL. They reported no complications associated with the TSA in “over 150 knees”; however, no functional outcomes were reported [10]. Furthermore, while the aforementioned technique was deemed safe with respect to complications, it involves the removal of a significant portion of the septum during direct posteromedial transcondylar visualization of the septum during preparation. To reduce the extent of preparation and allow for precise placement of the TSA, we propose a modified technique for the TSA, which includes indirect posterolateral visualization of the septum and, thus, indication-dependent placement of the TSA portal within the septum. Despite the increasing use of posterior knee arthroscopy in recent years, there is a paucity of evidence on clinical and functional outcomes after TSA [11].

The purpose of this study was therefore to evaluate the safety of the modified arthroscopic TSA and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for posterior knee pathologies, with a particular focus on neurovascular complications and postoperative range of motion (ROM). We therefore hypothesized that the modified arthroscopic TSA does not increase the risk of postoperative extension deficit or neurovascular complications while providing safe access to the posterior compartment.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study analyzed prospectively collected data from patients who underwent arthroscopic knee surgery at a single center via TSA between January 2018 and February 2024. Postoperative assessment of the outcome parameters and ROM was performed in clinical follow-up examinations. Knee range of motion was measured via long-arm goniometer following standardized goniometric techniques [12], with the patient in the supine position. Specifically, the fulcrum was centered over the lateral femoral condyle, the proximal arm was aligned with the femoral axis using the greater trochanter as a reference point, and the distal arm was aligned with the lateral malleolus. The ROM was assessed according to the neutral-zero method, with hyperextension coded as negative values and values > 0° indicating an extension deficit. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were obtained during the course of the follow-up examination or postally.

Patients were included if they underwent surgical intervention via TSA for PCL injury, posterior compartment synovectomy in cases of TGCT, treatment of posterior meniscal lesions or posterolateral rotatory instabilities (PLRIs), with a minimum follow-up of 24 months. The exclusion criteria were a diagnosis of arthrofibrosis of the knee joint prior to TSA, congenital or posttraumatic deformities of the knee joint or total knee replacement (TKA).

The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of perioperative complications. The secondary outcomes included postoperative complications, range of motion (ROM), and validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) [13] score and Tegner [14] score.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by fellowship-trained senior orthopedic surgeons (KF, MB, MK, JF) with special expertise in complex knee surgery [9]. For all the cases, the knees were stored in an electric leg holder. A torniquet was placed but was not routinely activated. First, standard diagnostic arthroscopy was performed through a high anterolateral (AL) portal and an anteromedial (AM) portal. The posteromedial compartment was then accessed by carefully advancing the arthroscope through the intercondylar notch while the knee was slightly flexed and under valgus stress. The knee was then flexed to 90 degrees, allowing direct visualization of the posteromedial capsule. Under direct arthroscopic visualization and with careful attention to surrounding neurovascular structures, the posteromedial portal was created after initial probing (Fig. 1A, B). A cannula was inserted if needed, depending on the specific indication (Fig. 1C). All further preparations were performed via a radiofrequency ablation device (RF) to prevent excessive bleeding without the use of a torniquet (Fig. 1D).

The next few steps were carried out to allow for precise positioning of the transseptal portal. With the RF placed directly adjacent to the septum (Fig. 2A, B, C), the arthroscope was retracted from the posteromedial compartment. The knee was then stored in a figure-of-4-position, advancing the camera through the intercondylar notch and into the posterolateral compartment while the RF was left in place. The planned portal position was determined by indirect blunt probing using the inactive RF (Fig. 2D), followed by subsequent establishment of the transseptal portal under direct arthroscopic control (Fig. 2E, F). The following steps were performed with regard to the specific surgical indication (Fig. 3A), allowing for an indication-dependent expansion of the portal, if needed (Fig. 3B). In the case of focal findings such as solitary TGCT (Fig. 3C), no further extension of the TSA was required (Fig. 3D, E). In the case of PCL reconstruction, further preparations toward the tibial PCL insertion site were performed, allowing preservation of the posterior and superior aspects of the septum. Through the TSA, a posterolateral portal was developed under direct visualization of the posterolateral joint capsule (Fig. 3F).

Rehabilitation

All patients followed a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol adapted to the underlying pathology. For patients with PCL and/or PLC involvement, a brace (Jack PCL Brace; Albrecht, Bernau am Chiemsee, Germany) with posterior support to prevent hyperextension was used for 12 weeks. Flexion was limited to 45° during the first 2 postoperative weeks, increased to 60° in weeks 3–4, and increased to 90° in weeks 5–6. Standardized physiotherapy was started 48 h after surgery. Patients treated for TGCTs had no postoperative restriction of range of motion.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively. Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) as well as median. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages.

Normal distribution was tested via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in nonnormally distributed data were analyzed via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Mean intraindividual differences were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. The presence of an extension deficit was analyzed as a binary paired variable and compared between sides via McNemar’s exact test. Correlations between ROM parameters and PROMs were calculated via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient due to non-normal data distribution. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the statistical significance level was set to p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.14.2 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA).

Ethical statement

Institutional Review Board approval was obtained (reference number: blinded for submission) and written informed consent was provided by all participating patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 82 patients underwent arthroscopic knee surgery via the TSA during the study period. No perioperative complications, such as injury to neurovascular structures, were observed in this cohort. After the application of the predefined exclusion criteria, 48 patients were included in the analysis of secondary outcomes. Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that the indirect modified posterior transseptal approach appears to be a safe surgical technique with a low perioperative complication rate. We found no significant loss of extension in the operated knees compared with the contralateral side, whereas there was a slight loss of flexion in the operated knees. Patient-reported outcome measures revealed moderate clinical outcomes, which correlated with the knee flexion deficits.

The posterior transseptal approach was first described by Kim et al. in 1997 [15]. Ahn and Ha later described an “optimized” version of the TSA, stating that there were no complications in a series of “more than 150 patients” [10]. Louisia et al. later described another modification of the TSA, suggesting blunt perforation of the posterior septum in order to further reduce the risk of neurovascular injury [16]. In addition to the anatomic considerations made in these articles, however, evidence to underline the low complication rates was not provided. Technically and anatomically, the risk of injury to the neurovascular bundle, especially of the popliteal artery, persists and may be increased in the case of dorsal extension of the TSA [9, 16–18]. In contrast, the modified indirect TSA allows precise indication-specific placement of the transseptal portal under direct visualization of the septum, thus, reducing the risk of misguided (posterior or superior) preparation. In this regard, our findings are consistent with those of described studies in terms of the low rate of intraoperative complications. Yet, another relevant complication of the TSA is scarring of the posterior aspects of the knee joint capsule, which can be responsible for a limited postoperative range of motion, particularly a loss of extension [19, 20]. According to the results of our study, the TSA was not associated with a relevant loss of knee extension, when compared to the healthy contralateral side. However, the ROM analysis revealed restrictions in knee flexion, with a mean side-to-side difference of 9°. Few other studies have reported the ROM following posterior knee arthroscopy. Shahibullah et al. investigated the postoperative outcomes of patients who underwent PCL reconstruction via a TSA. The authors reported an average range of motion of 115.6° after a mean follow-up of 41 months, which is generally in line with our findings [6]. In contrast to our study, however, the authors concluded that no limited range of motion had occurred, comparing the pre- and postoperative ROMs of the injured knees only, without the contralateral reference [6]. According to numerous studies, restrictions in the range of motion following posterior knee injuries are considered typical complications. In particular, PCL reconstruction is commonly associated with a loss of knee flexion [21]. Fanelli and Monahan investigated a series of 120 patients undergoing PCL reconstruction (predominantly in the context of concomitant multiligament surgeries) and reported a flexion deficit of 10° in the operated knees [22]. In a series of 25 patients who underwent isolated anterolateral bundle PCL reconstruction, Hermans et al. reported a mean loss of flexion of 8° after long-term follow-up [23]. For combined PCL and PLC reconstruction, Zorzi et al. described a mean flexion deficit of 10° (SSD) but symmetrical knee extension after a mean follow-up of 38 months [24]. Taken together, the findings of these studies align closely with our findings regarding flexion loss of motion.

Pathogenetically, the TGCT is also known to decrease the ROM due to the proliferation of synovial-lined tissue [25] and, when located in the posterior compartment of the knee, it may cause a flexion deficit. Dines et al. reported on 26 patients who were followed up after surgical treatment for TGCTs, without observing a decreased range of motion. Since none of the patients had a posterior-compartment TGCT [26], the comparability to our findings is limited. Shekhar et al. described excellent outcomes in a study of 10 patients undergoing surgical treatment for posterior-compartment TGCT, but did not report the ROM at final follow-up [27]. In the collective of our study, two patients who underwent surgery for TGCT using the TSA showed only a slight reduction in postoperative knee flexion. With regard to the small number of cases in this study and the current evidence on literature, the influence of the TSA on postoperative knee flexion remains uncertain. In this context, the observed loss of flexion may also very well be attributed to the underlying pathologies, rather than the TSA. Nevertheless, the occurrence of a flexion deficit is of clinical relevance, as it may influence the postoperative functional outcome. In this study, postoperative knee flexion was moderately positive correlated with the subjective IKDC score, whereas a weak and nonsignificant tendency toward an association was observed between knee flexion and the Tegner activity score. Accordingly, a reduced knee flexion was associated with inferior functional outcomes, which corresponds to the findings of previous clinical studies [28]. Other studies have shown that a knee flexion angle of 110° may be sufficient for everyday life, which is contrary to our findings [29]. Taken together, the observed loss of flexion in our study may reflect the underlying pathology rather than the modified TSA itself. However, because no subgroup analysis was performed, this interpretation remains speculative.

With regard to the functional outcomes, we found good overall postoperative PROMs in our study cohort. Jacobsen et al. reported comparable results for the Tegner activity score in a series of patients who underwent anatomic PLC reconstruction [30]. Camarda et al. demonstrated a slightly greater postoperative IKDC score of 88.48 points after isolated PLC reconstruction [31]. Another study investigating mostly multiligament injuries with PLC involvement observed a mean postoperative IKDC score of 70.4 and a Tegner activity score of 5.5 [32]. Kim et al. compared conventional and remnant-preserving transtibial single-bundle posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction combined with posterolateral corner reconstruction and reported mean subjective IKDC scores between 64.5 and 70.6 and Tegner activity scores between 3.5 and 4.3 [33]. The patient cohort in our study comprised a high proportion of PCL reconstructions. Therefore, despite the heterogeneity of underlying pathologies in our study, the reported functional outcome parameters can be categorized within the literature.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design of the analysis limits the ability to draw causal inferences and is susceptible to selection and information bias. Furthermore, the study cohort is comparably small, and a relatively high loss to follow-up rate must be acknowledged. Furthermore, the lack of preoperative PROMs and ROM analysis limits the interpretability of the postoperative outcomes as the effect of surgery could not be quantified. In this regard, the study cohort comprised various surgical indications for the TSA, which caused a certain heterogeneity. This may have reduced the statistical power, precluded subgroup analysis, and complicated the attribution of differences in postoperative range of motion specifically to the TSA. In addition, a potential surgeon-related bias must be considered, as the procedures were performed by experienced surgeons at a specialized center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the absence of a control group treated with an alternative surgical approach limits the scope for comparative interpretation.

Conclusions

The modified indirect posterior transseptal approach appears to be a safe technique for posterior arthroscopic knee surgery when performed by experienced surgeons. Crucially, our findings suggest that, regardless of the underlying indication, the use of the posterior transseptal approach is not associated with a clinically relevant loss of knee extension, whereas a certain loss of flexion may occur depending on the underlying pathology.
