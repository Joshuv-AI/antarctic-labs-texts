Weak-Memory Dynamics in Discrete Time
Hugues Meyer and Kay Brandner

Abstract
Discrete dynamics arise naturally in systems with broken temporal translation symmetry and are typically described by first-order recurrence relations representing classical or quantum Markov chains. When memory effects induced by hidden degrees of freedom are relevant, however, higher-order discrete evolution equations are generally required. Focusing on linear dynamics, we identify a well-delineated weak-memory regime where such equations can, on an intermediate time scale, be systematically reduced to a unique first-order counterpart acting on the same state space. We formulate our results as a mathematical theorem and work out two examples showing how they can be applied to stochastic Floquet dynamics under coarse-grained and quantum collisional models.



The dynamical principles of classical and quantum mechanics imply that physical systems across all scales evolve continuously in time.
Nonetheless, processes with broken temporal translation symmetry can often be naturally understood as sequences of discrete steps.
Isolated systems subject to rapidly oscillating fields, for example, admit a stroboscopic description in terms of time-independent effective Hamiltonians.
These Floquet Hamiltonians can be engineered through the applied driving, making it possible to realize phenomena with no equilibrium counterparts
Bukov et al. 2015
;
Eckardt 2017
;
Oka and Kitamura 2019
.
In open systems, periodic driving typically leads to the formation of limit cycles, where state variables converge to stable fixed points in stroboscopic time
Moskalets and Büttiker 2002
;
Ray and Barato 2017
;
Menczel and Brandner 2019
;
Mori 2023
.
This behavior arises from dissipation balancing energy absorption and can be observed, for instance, in mesoscopic devices such as stochastic pumps, which generate directed currents through cyclic variations of control parameters
Sinitsyn and Nemenman 2007
;
Rahav et al. 2008
;
Esposito and Parrondo 2015
;
Raz et al. 2016
;
Funo et al. 2020
, see Fig.
1
.

Discrete-time lattice models, such as cellular automata and quantum circuits, for instance, offer elegant frameworks to explore how macroscopic features of many-body systems such as entanglement spreading and hydrodynamic behavior emerge from local interactions
Nahum et al. 2017
;
Nahum et al. 2018
;
Von Keyserlingk et al. 2018
;
Khemani et al. 2018
;
Chan et al. 2018
;
Rakovszky et al. 2018
;
Rakovszky et al. 2019
;
Bertini et al. 2019
;
Klobas et al. 2021
;
Klobas and Bertini 2021a
;
Klobas and Bertini 2021b
;
Buča et al. 2021
;
Bertini et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2025
.
In a similar spirit, collisional models enable an elegant description of open quantum systems, even beyond the weak-coupling regime, by replacing continuous environments with discrete collections of ancillas that interact sequentially with the observable
system

A prototypical discrete evolution equation is

X_{n+1}=\mathsf{V}X_{n},

(1)

where the vector
X_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
characterizes the state of the system after
n
time steps, for example, in terms of a probability distribution, and
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is the one-step propagator or generator.
Such first-order recurrence relations provide suitable models for systems whose future is, at any point in time, fully determined by their present state.
This condition is satisfied if all relevant degrees of freedom are accessible to the observer.
In particular in mesoscopic systems, however, it is typically only possible to resolve coarse grained variables, while details of internal and environmental structures remain inaccessible
Pigolotti and Vulpiani 2008
;
Esposito 2012
;
Yvinec et al. 2014
;
Hummer and Szabo 2015
;
Wachtel et al. 2018
;
Lapolla and Godec 2019
;
Strasberg and Esposito 2019
;
Busiello et al. 2020
;
Seiferth et al. 2020
;
Loos and Klapp 2020
;
Straube et al. 2020
;
Ayaz et al. 2021
;
Hartich and Godec 2021
;
Lapolla and Godec 2021
;
Hartich and Godec 2023
;
Coghi 2024
;
Blom et al. 2024
;
Vollmar et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Wiśniewski and Spiechowicz 2025
.
These hidden degrees of freedom can, if they evolve sufficiently slowly, retain information about the observable system over several time steps, while constantly influencing its dynamics.
Accounting for this effect requires more general evolution equations of the form

X_{n+1}=\mathsf{V}X_{n}+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathsf{K}_{m}X_{n-m},

(2)

where the memory kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}
can either be extracted from experimental or simulation data, or derived by projecting the microscopic state space of a discrete-time model onto a lower-dimensional space of mesostates
Lesnicki et al. 2016
;
Jung et al. 2017
;
Meyer et al. 2021
;
Vrugt and Wittkowski 2020
;
Klippenstein et al. 2021
;
Schilling 2022
;
Hilder and Sharma 2024
.

Higher-order recurrence relations of the type of Eq. (
2
) can provide significantly more accurate models for systems with hidden degrees of freedom than their first-order counterparts.
However, they are also less physically transparent and more difficult to solve both analytically and numerically, especially when the accessible state space remains large.
In the following, we show how this trade-off can be systematically overcome in settings where memory effects play a subdominant role.
To this end, we adapt a recently developed theory of weak-memory dynamics from continuous to discrete time, thereby substantially broadening the scope of this framework
Brandner 2025a
;
Brandner 2025b
.

The key idea of our approach is to construct an effective generator
\mathsf{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
such that the solution
Y_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
of the first-order recurrence relation

Y_{n+1}=\mathsf{G}Y_{n}

(3)

converges to the solution of Eq. (
2
) at sufficiently long times.
The short-time dynamics of the system are described as an effectively instantaneous change of its initial state,
Y_{0}=\mathsf{D}X_{0}
, induced by the slippage matrix
\mathsf{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, which, for any
X_{0}
, has to satisfy

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathsf{G}^{-n}X_{n}=\mathsf{D}X_{0}.

(4)

Here,
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{D}
must be non-singular to ensure that the long-time approximation
Y_{n}
is non-trivial for any
X_{0}\neq 0
.
The physical intuition behind this ansatz is that, after a brief transient period, all hidden degrees of freedom reach a near-stationary state, which then evolves almost adiabatically under the influence of the observable ones.
As a result, the backflow of information from inaccessible to accessible components of the system gradually diminishes and the impact of memory effects becomes insignificant.

The validity of this picture depends on three characteristic time scales, which we define by imposing the bounds

\lVert\mathsf{V}^{-1}\rVert\leq 1/v,\qquad\lVert\mathsf{K}_{n}\rVert\leq Mk^{n-1}

(5)

on the free generator and the memory kernel, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the spectral norm.
Here,
v
corresponds to the slowest mode of the adiabatic dynamics, which would be observed if the evolution of all hidden degrees of freedom was frozen.
The parameters
M
and
k<1
quantify, respectively, the coupling strength between accessible and inaccessible parts of the system and the relaxation rate of the latter.
Thus, when
M
and
k
are small compared to
v
, the hidden degrees of freedom evolve nearly autonomously, with the observable ones acting only as a weak and slow perturbation.
Under these conditions, memory effects are expected to be subdominant and an effective generator satisfying Eq. (
4
) may exist.

Our main achievement is to translate the qualitative analysis above into a mathematical theorem, which can be formulated as follows.
If the parameters
v,M
and
k
entering the bounds (
5
) satisfy

k<v,\quad M<M^{\ast}=\begin{cases}(v-k)^{2}/4,&v+k<2\\
(1-k)(v-1),&v+k\geq 2\end{cases},

(6)

there exists a unique effective generator
\mathsf{G}
such that, for any initial state
X_{0}
, the long-time approximation
Y_{n}
of the solution
X_{n}
of Eq. (
2
) satisfies

\lvert X_{n}-Y_{n}\rvert\leq\frac{\zeta-k}{\eta-\zeta}\lvert X_{0}\rvert\zeta^{n}

(7)

with

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=(v+k)/2-\sqrt{(v-k)^{2}/4-M}<1,

(8)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=(v+k)/2+\sqrt{(v-k)^{2}/4-M}

(9)

and
\lvert\cdot\rvert
indicating the Euclidean norm.
The corresponding slippage matrix is given by the formula

\mathsf{D}^{-1}=1+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{H}^{-m}\mathsf{K}_{m+n-1}\mathsf{G}^{-n},

(10)

where
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
are the unique solutions of the fixed-point equations

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{V}+\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{K}_{n}\mathsf{G}^{-n}=T(\mathsf{G}),

(11)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{V}+\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{H}^{-n}\mathsf{K}_{n}=U(\mathsf{H})

(12)

in the set
B=\bigl\{\mathsf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\;:\;\lVert\mathsf{X}^{-1}\rVert\leq 1/\eta\bigr\}
.

The proof of this theorem uses techniques similar to those recently developed for continuous dynamics
Brandner 2025a
;
Brandner 2025b
, with a few key modifications.
A detailed account of the underlying mathematical framework is provided in Refs.
Meyer and Brandner 2025
;
Burton 2005
;
Rivas and Huelga 2012
;
Horn and Johnson 2013
;
Agarwal et al. 2018
.
At this point, we add only three remarks before turning to specific classes of systems for illustration.
First, Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) define the weak-memory regime for discrete dynamics.
These conditions do not require a strong separation of time scales, but can be met even when
M,k
and
v
are of the same order of magnitude.
Second, Eq. (
7
) provides a strong bound on the error of the long-time approximation, thereby enforcing the uniqueness of the effective generator.
That is, while there may exist a whole family of generators that define a non-singular slippage matrix, there is only one such pair whose corresponding long-time approximation satisfies

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert X_{n}-Y_{n}\rvert\zeta^{-n}<\infty

(13)

for any
X_{0}
.
Finally, Eqs. (
10
), (
11
) and (
12
) provide an explicit scheme to construct this optimal approximation.
To this end, the fixed-point equations (
11
) and (
12
) may be solved with a suitable ansatz or by iteration, where the latter approach exploits the fact that
T
and
U
are contractions on
B
under the metric
d(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=\lVert\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{Y}\rVert
.

As a first application, we show how theory can be employed in the coarse graining of Markov processes.
We focus on systems with a finite space of microstates
\mathbb{M}=\{1,\dots,M\}
and denote by
P_{n}=[P_{n}^{1},\dots,P_{n}^{M}]
the time dependent vector that assigns an occupation probability to every microstate.
Dynamics are governed by the discrete-time master equation

P_{n+1}=\mathsf{L}P_{n},

(14)

where
(\mathsf{L})_{ij}\geq 0
is the probability to transition from the microstates
i
to the microstate
j
in one step.
A coarse graining is induced by a partitioning of
\mathbb{M}
into
N
disjoint subsets
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}
, which correspond to the operationally distinguishable configurations of the system.
The occupation probabilities
X_{n}^{\alpha}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}}P^{i}_{n}
of these mesostates are collected in the lumped vector
X_{n}=[X_{n}^{1},\dots,X_{n}^{N}]
.
Under minor technical conditions, this vector follows an evolution equation of the form (
2
), where
\mathsf{V}
and
\mathsf{K}_{n}
can be expressed in terms of the microscopic transition matrix
\mathsf{L}
using projection operator techniques
Vrugt and Wittkowski 2020
;
Klippenstein et al. 2021
;
Schilling 2022
;
Hilder and Sharma 2024
.

Our master theorem extends this framework by establishing rigorous conditions for the existence of, and systematic procedures to construct, an effective generator of the lumped dynamics.
These results apply to broad classes of physical systems, including in particular ones with continuous underlying dynamics.
Systems such as electronic nano-devices, for example, are typically described by a continuous-time master equation
Seifert 2012
;
Benenti et al. 2017
,

\dot{p}_{t}=\mathsf{W}_{t}p_{t}.

(15)

Here,
p_{t}=[p^{1}_{t},\dots,p^{M}_{t}]^{\mathsf{T}}
is a vector of occupation probabilities,
(\mathsf{W}_{t})_{ij}\geq 0
is the transition rate from the microstate
j
to the microstate
i
, which becomes time dependent under external driving, and
(\mathsf{W}_{t})_{ii}=-\sum_{j\neq i}(\mathsf{W}_{t})_{ji}
to ensure probability conservation.
If the driving is periodic,
\mathsf{W}_{t+\tau}=\mathsf{W}_{t}
, Floquet’s theorem implies that the stroboscopic vector
P_{n}=p_{n\tau}
follows the discrete-time master equation (
14
) with the transition matrix
\mathsf{L}=\exp_{\mathrm{O}}[\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\mathsf{W}_{t}]
.
This observation shows that our theory opens a path to systematically derive effective generators for coarse-grained, periodically modulated dynamics, a task that is otherwise difficult to address without additional assumptions on the driving time scale.

Two remarks are in order before we illustrate this result with a simple example.
First, if
\mathsf{L}
is a stochastic matrix, as assumed above, then so is the free generator
\mathsf{V}
of the lumped dynamics.
Consequently, the Perron-Frobenius theorem implies
v\leq 1
for this entire class of models.
Second, while we have so far focused on classical Markov jump processes, our conclusions extend directly to open quantum systems with a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, upon replacing probability vectors, transition matrices, and rate matrices by density matrices, quantum channels, and Lindblad generators, respectively.

As our second main application, we consider collisional models, which offer a versatile platform to describe open or monitored quantum dynamics in discrete time
Ciccarello et al. 2022
.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, such models consist of a system of interest that couples sequentially to a collection of identical environmental units, or ancillas.
Memory effects can be added to this framework, for instance, through interactions between successive ancillas.
The state of the system after
n
collisions is then given by

\rho_{n}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{U}_{n}\;\cdots\;\mathcal{U}_{2}\mathcal{Q}_{12}\mathcal{U}_{1}(\rho_{0}\otimes\xi^{n})],

(18)

where
\xi^{n}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
is the initial state of the ancillas, and
\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[\circ]
indicates the partial trace over their joint Hilbert space.
The maps
\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{ij}
describe bipartite interactions between the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
ancilla and the system, and between the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
and the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
ancillas, respectively.

For concreteness, we now assume that the system and the ancillas are qubits with ground and excited states
\lvert 0_{i}\rangle
and
\lvert 1_{i}\rangle
, where
i=S,1,\dots,n
.
System-ancilla and ancilla-ancilla interactions are described as unitary and incoherent partial swaps,

\mathcal{U}_{i}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\circ=\mathsf{U}_{i}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\circ\mathsf{U}_{i}^{\dagger},\quad\mathcal{Q}_{ij}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\circ=(1-k)\circ+k\mathsf{S}_{ij}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\circ\mathsf{S}_{ij}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},

(19)

where
\mathsf{U}_{i}=\sqrt{1-u}-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{u}\mathsf{S}_{Si}
and
0<u,k<1
.
The swap matrices are defined such that
\mathsf{S}_{ij}\lvert\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\rangle=\lvert\beta_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle
for
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}
.
If all ancillas are initially prepared in the same state, the dynamics of this model are governed by the recursion relation

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{K}_{0}\rho_{n}+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}_{m}\rho_{n-m}+\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\rho_{0},

(20)

with
\mathcal{K}_{n}=(1/k-1)\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}\circ=k^{n}\mathrm{Tr}_{1}^{\vphantom{n}}[\mathcal{U}^{n}_{1}(\circ\otimes\xi_{1}^{\vphantom{n}})]
, see Refs.
Meyer and Brandner 2025
;
Ciccarello et al. 2022
.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is a linear map on the operator space of the system, it can be represented as a four-dimensional matrix
1
1

1

In our discussion of collisional models, we formally treat density matrices as vectors and quantum maps as matrices.
For this approach to be consistent,
\lvert\cdot\rvert
must, when applied to a density matrix, be evaluated as the Hilbert-Schmidt norm;
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the operator norm induced by
\lvert\cdot\rvert
throughout.
.
Thus, Eq. (
20
) has the structure of Eq. (
2
), up to an inhomogeneous term, which can, however, be accounted for.
If
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
are the effective generator and slippage map of the homogeneous dynamics, then the long-time approximation of
\rho_{n}
reads
Meyer and Brandner 2025

\chi_{n}=\mathcal{G}^{n}\mathcal{D}\rho_{0}+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}^{n-m}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}_{m}\rho_{0}.

(21)

To evaluate this expression, we solve the fixed-point equations (
11
) and (
12
) numerically by iteration using the initial guesses
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{K}_{0}
; the corresponding slippage map then follows from Eq. (
10
).
As we show in Fig.
3
, the error of the resulting approximations first decays exponentially with time, obeying the bound

\lvert\rho_{n}-\chi_{n}\rvert\leq\frac{\zeta-k}{\eta-\zeta}\lvert\rho_{0}\rvert\zeta^{n}\Bigl(1+\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{n}\lVert\mathcal{F}_{m}\rVert\zeta^{-m}\Bigr),

(22)

which follows from Eq. (
7
) and holds for the exact effective generator and slippage map
Meyer and Brandner 2025
, before approaching a plateau whose value decreases exponentially with the number of iterations used to determine the generator.

This observation shows that iterating the fixed-point equations (
11
) and (
12
) provides an efficient numerical method for determining the effective generator and the associated slippage matrix.
However, due to their nonlinear structure, these equations generally do not provide a convenient starting point for analytical perturbation schemes.
To this end, it is often more useful to focus instead on the memory function
\mathsf{E}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, which, for any given
\mathsf{G}
, is uniquely defined by requiring that the inhomogeneous recurrence relation

X_{n+1}=\mathsf{G}X_{n}+\mathsf{E}_{n}X_{0}

(23)

is equivalent to Eq. (
2
).
If the weak-memory conditions (
5
) and (
6
) are fulfilled, and
\mathsf{G}
is the optimal effective generator of our master theorem, this function satisfies the linear summation equation

\mathsf{E}_{n}=-\sum\nolimits_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\mathsf{K}_{m}+\sum\nolimits_{l=1}^{m-1}\mathsf{E}_{l-1}\mathsf{K}_{m-l}\Bigr)\mathsf{V}^{n-m},

(24)

which can be solved by iteration.
In this way, the optimal memory function
\mathsf{E}_{n}
of a given model can be systematically constructed from an initial guess
\mathsf{E}^{0}_{n}
, which may be obtained from an exact solution in a suitable limit.
If the free solution satisfies the bound
\lVert\mathsf{E}^{0}_{n}\rVert\leq S\sigma^{n}
with
\zeta\leq\sigma<\min\{\eta,1\}
and
S\geq\sigma-k
, this procedure converges.
Once the optimal memory function has been determined, the corresponding effective generator and slippage matrix follow from the relations

\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{V}-\mathsf{E}_{0},\qquad\mathsf{D}=1+\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{G}^{-n}\mathsf{E}_{n-1}.

(25)

For the trivial initial guess
\mathsf{E}^{0}_{n}=0
, for instance, we obtain an expansion of
\mathsf{G}
in powers of the coupling strength
M
.

These results, whose derivation we provide in Ref.
Meyer and Brandner 2025
, complete our theory of weak memory effects in discrete time.
We now invite the classical and quantum nonequilibrium communities to extend and apply the techniques developed here to more complex and physically richer settings.
Promising directions for future work include the role of dynamical memory in many-body circuit models, the emergence of metastability in systems with hidden degrees of freedom, and the thermodynamics of discrete dynamics under coarse graining.
