The experiences of pregnant women during pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum period about bad news: A qualitative study protocol
Authors: Zohreh Abbasi, Sakineh Nazari, Soheila Mohamadirizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Disseminating bad news in the field of medical sciences poses challenges for patients and their families. This is particularly important in cases of high-risk pregnancies, where cultural differences across cities and countries can impact the attitudes and experiences of pregnant women when receiving and coping with unpredictable and stressful situations. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the experiences of pregnant women in hearing bad news during pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum period in 2024.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This qualitative study will utilize a conventional content analysis approach. The sample will consist of 30 pregnant women in the perinatal period, and data collection will continue until data saturation is achieved. The research will take place in both public and private hospitals in Isfahan city. Data will be collected through individual, in-depth, open, and semistructured interviews as well as through observation and field note-taking. Participants will be selected through purposeful sampling, specifically targeting pregnant women attending public and private hospitals in Isfahan. Data analysis will be conducted using the inductive analysis method developed by Granheim and Lundman.CONCLUSION:This study will shed light on the experiences of mothers with high-risk pregnancies during the perinatal period, regarding the communication of bad news by healthcare professionals. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have employed this approach, making the findings of this study valuable in informing health policies and improving the quality of care, particularly within the sociocultural context of Iran.

Introduction

Disseminating bad news to patients or their loved ones is a complex task that can have a significant impact on their outlook and psychological well-being. Sensitively preparing the patient and their family, providing ample time for counseling and careful disclosure, and using effective communication methods can improve their experience and foster hope.[1,2] According to a study by Choe et al.[3] (2019), patients and their family members who received bad news from doctors experienced inner turmoil and emotional distress, highlighting the importance of emotional and informational support. Bad news can involve chronic illnesses or high-risk pregnancies, which bring about significant changes[4,5] High-risk pregnancies pose risks to both the mother and the fetus, potentially endangering their lives.[6] The unexpected nature of this news often leads to depression, anxiety, and trauma among parents.[7] Healthcare providers, including doctors, midwives, and nurses, face challenges in delivering bad news, requiring training and expertise in this area. Likewise, recipients of bad news, such as pregnant mothers, encounter difficulties that result in unique experiences.[8] A study by Aein and Delaram[9] (2014) identified four main categories of mothers’ experiences in conveying bad news about their children’s illnesses: shock and discomfort, emotional burden, the responsibility of informing other family members, and handling information overload. Similarly, the findings of a study by Brann et al.[10] (2019) indicated that mothers with a history of abortion emphasized the importance of empathy, allowing patients time to process information and actively assessing patients’ comprehension. They strongly recommended healthcare providers to use appropriate language, avoiding medical jargon and emotionally charged terms. In fact, a key aspect of clinical communication skills lies in delivering unfavorable news. Despite the progress of medical technology, the relationship between the healthcare team and the patient is considered a crucial factor that impacts patient satisfaction and engagement.[11,12] Nurses and midwives, being integral members of the healthcare community, often spend more time with patients than doctors. Consequently, they are frequently the primary source of information for patients.[13,14] The failure to establish a reassuring relationship with the patient is deemed a significant issue.[15] Although the current state of communicating unfavorable news in our country’s healthcare system remains largely unknown, in many countries, it is common practice to disclose the diagnosis of incurable diseases to the patient and their family. In other words, the benefits of conveying information and disclosing the truth to the patient and their family outweigh the downsides of withholding it.[16] Furthermore, research on clinical professionals, including doctors and paramedics, reveals that they have not received formal training in effectively delivering unfavorable news and lack the necessary proficiency in doing so.[17] The field of midwifery, being a substantial part of the paramedic community, is not exempt from this deficiency. However, note 3 of the regulation on the scope of midwives’ competence stipulates that “midwives” must adhere to occupational standards and professional ethics in providing their services.[18] Midwives, who directly interact with pregnant mothers and women of reproductive age, often encounter situations such as unwanted pregnancies, fetal demise, fetal screening tests, and fetal abnormalities.[19,20] Communication skills are included in the midwifery curriculum, but it fails to address the issue of communicating unfavorable news to patients.[21] Consequently, significant concerns persist among midwifery personnel regarding the delivery of unfavorable news.[17] Given the significance of psychological outcomes in high-risk pregnancy[22] and how the healthcare team transmits unfavorable news in high-risk pregnancies and considering the absence of qualitative studies in Iran on the experiences of pregnant women upon receiving unfavorable news during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period, it is essential to conduct such a study. Moreover, cultural differences across cities and countries can influence the attitudes and experiences of pregnant women in hearing about and coping with various unpredictable and stressful issues during this period. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the experiences of pregnant women upon receiving unfavorable news during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period (including experiences of fetal or infant death, abnormal clinic and paraclinical test results, fetal abnormalities, pregnancy complications, and postpartum hemorrhage after delivery). The study is scheduled to be conducted in 2024.

Materials and Methods

The present study is a qualitative study utilizing an inductive content analysis approach consisting of two phases: a literature review phase and a qualitative phase, both to be conducted in 2024.

Phase 1: Literature review

During this stage, a literature review was conducted to investigate the experiences of pregnant mothers regarding receiving negative news about their fetuses in different contexts. The search was conducted in internationally recognized databases such as ProQuest, PubMed, Elsevier, Web of Science, Scopus, Science Direct, and CINAHL. The search keywords included “bad news”, “fetus”, “pregnant mother”, “pregnancy”, “delivery”, “abortion”, “ectopic pregnancy”, “fetal anomalies”, and “experiences”, using Boolean operators (and, or, *). The search covered the years 2010 to 2024 and included articles in both Farsi and English languages. The search was limited to the title and abstract of the articles, and different study types were selected. Initially, a list of article titles and abstracts was compiled, and the inclusion criteria for the studies were reviewed, focusing on articles with specific objectives, methodologies, and results. Exclusion criteria included articles without full-text access, irrelevant content, articles presented at conferences, letters to the editor, and articles with insufficient data. Textual data analysis was employed in this step for data extraction and analysis.

Phase 1: Qualitative research (experiences of pregnant mothers receiving bad news)

The current qualitative research aims to investigate the experiences of pregnant mothers upon receiving negative information about their fetus. The research will be conducted using in-depth interviews and field note-taking. To analyze and condense the qualitative data, the researcher employed the qualitative content analysis method. This method is suitable for research projects focused on describing significant issues within a specific group of individuals.[23] Qualitative content analysis is a research approach utilized to analyze textual information obtained through various means such as narrative responses, open-ended questions, interviews, focus groups, observations, articles, and books.[24] Conventional content analysis is commonly employed for studies aiming to describe a phenomenon when limited existing theories or research literature is available. In this type of design, researchers refrain from using pre-established categories and instead allow categories to emerge from the data. They immerse themselves in the data to gain new insights and knowledge, inducing the formation of categories directly from the data. This approach is referred to as inductive categorization.[25] In this research, the main focus of the first stage is to explain the experiences of pregnant women when receiving negative news during different stages of pregnancy and after delivery. Due to a lack of knowledge in this area, particularly in Iranian society, a conventional qualitative content analysis approach will be employed to gain a comprehensive understanding and description of the concepts being studied. In qualitative studies, the selection of participants should be based on a clear logic and aligned with the study’s purpose and research question.[26] The choice of participants depends on the desired knowledge, stakes, benefits, and credibility. Generally, participants in qualitative studies are selected based on personal experience or knowledge relevant to the subject being studied.[27] In this study, purposive sampling will be used. Purposive sampling involves selecting participants who have experience in the field of the phenomenon or key concepts being explored. Various strategies have been proposed for this type of sampling, with one common approach being maximum diversity, which involves selecting individuals with diverse perspectives on the issue being studied. This enhances the credibility of the study. The criteria for achieving maximum diversity will depend on the study type and can include factors such as gender, level of education, or any other factors that distinguish participants from each other. The viewpoints of these participants highlight existing differences and contribute to the development of a solid and appropriate qualitative study.[28] Accordingly, the present study aims to interview mothers of varying ages and ethnicities who are susceptible to receiving news for any reason and have experienced negative news about themselves or their fetuses and babies. The research participants will be selected based on the study goals and criteria for inclusion, and data collection will continue until saturation is reached. This phase of the study will be conducted in Isfahan hospitals, and all interviews will take place in a quiet and private environment based on the participants’ preferences. The experiences will be extracted from the data using conventional content analysis methods. The interview will be guided by questions derived from the literature review. Some example questions include “Describe your experiences when receiving negative news about your fetus?” and “What challenges did you face upon receiving negative news about your fetus?” Additional questions will be tailored to the interview process. The duration of the sessions will vary based on participant characteristics, typically ranging from 45 to 60 minutes. The interviews will be recorded to allow for a thorough analysis of the data. The researcher will guide the discussion in line with the study objectives, ensuring feedback provision, trust-building, avoidance of leading the interviewee’s opinions, and refraining from correcting the interviewee’s statements. If necessary, additional interviews may be conducted with participants or other individuals with key information related to the phenomenon under study. After each session, the recorded conversations will be played in full. The researcher will also take note of verbal and nonverbal interactions (field notes) during the interview. The researcher will observe and document participants’ behavior in public and private hospitals, perinatology specialists’ offices, and any relevant information-gathering locations based on the cultural norms of the society.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The entry criteria for this study include obtaining informed consent from participants, the ability to communicate and be interviewed, and having personal experience with receiving negative news about the fetus or baby based on clinical and paraclinical diagnoses. Exclusion criteria also include a lack of willingness to continue participating at any stage of the study.

Data analysis

The qualitative data analysis will utilize conventional qualitative content analysis.[24] This entails directly obtaining data from study participants, with categories not predetermined but instead derived from the text of the data. The analysis process begins with repeated readings of all the data, immersing the researcher in the information and gaining an overall understanding. Then, the data are read word by word, identifying key concepts and extracting corresponding codes. The researcher offers initial interpretations of the text and conducts preliminary analysis. As the process continues, labels are assigned to codes representing multiple key concepts, often taken directly from the text and later converted into an initial coding scheme. The codes are then organized into clusters based on their relationships. Emerging categories are utilized to organize and group codes into meaningful clusters. Definitions are established for each category, subcategory, and code. When presenting the findings, examples of each code and data category are provided.[24] If there are gaps in information, the interviews are reorganized to obtain the necessary data.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the accuracy of the research findings, the following five criteria (validity or acceptability, trustworthiness or similarity, transferability, verifiability, and authenticity) suggested by Strobert and Carpenter (2011)[29] will be implemented:

To validate the findings, in-depth interviews will be conducted at different time points, ensuring maximum diversity among participants in terms of age, previous births, and educational qualifications. The method of review by participants will be utilized to confirm the accuracy of data and code extraction. The data will be reviewed by experts to ensure alignment between the identified categories and participants’ statements. In this study, the researcher will strive to establish a rapport with the participants to create a conducive environment for open interviews and obtain rich data. Additionally, the researcher’s presence in the research field will facilitate a deeper understanding and foster trust among the participants.

To confirm the transferability of the research, the findings will be presented to individuals who did not participate in the study to assess the similarity between the results and their own experiences. Expert review will also be conducted. To ensure the verifiability of the text, a selection of codes and categories extracted from the interviews will be shared with the researcher’s colleagues and members of the executive committee who are knowledgeable about qualitative research analysis but did not participate in the study. They will be asked to verify the accuracy of the data coding process. In this research, the originality of the study will be enhanced through purposeful sampling of participants, obtaining informed consent, and respecting the participants’ nature. While considering these factors, the researcher will draw on the expertise and supervision of associate professors as well as their own 12 years of experience in the field of labor and delivery. The researcher has also completed theoretical courses on research methods and a workshop on qualitative research methods.

Ethical consideration

This study is conducted in accordance with ethical code IR.MUI.NUREMA.REC.1401.150 and has received approval from the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants, and their confidentiality will be ensured.

Limitations and recommendations

The main limitation of this project is the potential noncooperation of participants during interviews, which may be due to various mental and emotional conditions. This may affect their ability to express their experiences of receiving negative news about their fetus. To mitigate this limitation, informed consent will be obtained from participants, and all ethical standards and confidentiality measures will be respected.

Discussion

An important outcome of this research is gaining insight into the experiences of pregnant mothers when they receive negative news about their fetus. Previous studies by Atienza-Carrasco et al. (2018),[30] Brouwer et al. (2020),[31] Rafiei et al.,[2] and Karkowsky et al.[32] have also explored the topic of delivering bad news in the field of nursing and midwifery. The findings of this research can be used to educate midwives on effective ways to communicate bad news to these mothers and create a foundation for further investigations into the issues faced by pregnant mothers with abnormal fetuses. Given the decline in the quality of healthcare due to increased workload and reliance on technology, it is crucial to prioritize the human and spiritual dimensions of care. This includes emphasizing empathy, authenticity, and nonjudgmental listening. A model of clinical relationship based on shared decision-making and clear delineation of the roles of the multidisciplinary team can help alleviate maternal distress during pregnancy termination. Implementation of protocols to ensure comprehensive care, not only addressing biological concerns but also providing psychosocial support, is essential. Additionally, healthcare professionals should receive training to enhance their social skills and cultural competence.[30] The anticipated outcomes of this research include improving the education and counseling of pregnant mothers with abnormal fetuses; reducing the psychological, physical, and family consequences; and increasing maternal awareness. Furthermore, the results of this study will enhance the knowledge, attitude, and skills of healthcare teams, particularly midwives and gynecologists, in effectively delivering bad news to pregnant mothers and providing coping strategies rooted in Iranian-Islamic culture.
