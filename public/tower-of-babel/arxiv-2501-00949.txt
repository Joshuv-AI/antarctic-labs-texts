Abstract
. We give optimal constants of smoothing estimates for the d d -dimensional free Dirac equation for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Our main abstract theorem shows that the optimal constant C C of the smoothing estimate associated with a spatial weight w w and smoothing function ψ \psi is given by C = sup k ∈ ℕ sup r > 0 λ ~ k ​ ( r ) C=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{r>0}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r) , where { λ ~ k } \{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}\} is a certain sequence of functions defined via integral formulae involving ( w , ψ ) (w,\psi) . This is an analogue of a similar result for Schrödinger equations given by Bez, Saito, and Sugimoto ( 2015 ), and also extends previous results of Ikoma ( 2022 ) and Ikoma and Suzuki ( 2025 ) for d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 to arbitrary dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . In order to prove this, we establish a modified version of the spherical harmonics decomposition of L 2 ​ ( 𝕊 d − 1 ) L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}) , which is well suited to the Dirac operator and allows us to find optimal constants. Furthermore, using our abstract theorem, we give explicit values of optimal constants associated with typical examples of ( w , ψ ) (w,\psi) . As it turns out, optimal constants for Dirac equations can be written explicitly in many cases, even when it is impossible for Schrödinger equations. In particular, the classical result of Simon ( 1992 ) for Schrödinger equations, which holds when d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 but fails when d = 2 d=2 , is true for Dirac equations whenever d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and remains valid for the massless two-dimensional case.

Introduction

The Kato smoothing estimate is one of the fundamental results in the study of dispersive equations such as Schrödinger equations and Dirac equations, which was originally developed by
Kato [15]
in an abstract setting.
First, we consider the free Schrödinger equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=-\Delta u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

In this case, the space-time global smoothing estimate is expressed as

(1.2)

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{it\Delta}u_{0}(x)\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert u_{0}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

or equivalently,

(1.3)

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\left\lvert\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\psi(\lvert\xi\rvert)e^{-it\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)\,d\xi\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert\widehat{u_{0}}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
w,\psi\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
are some given functions, which are called a spatial weight and a smoothing function, respectively.
For example, this inequality holds in the following cases:

Our aim in this paper is to establish analogues of Theorems
1.1

1.2
for the free Dirac equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here we recall the definition of the Dirac equation.
Let
d\geq 2
,
N\coloneqq 2^{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}
,
and
\{\gamma_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq d+1}

be a family of
N\times N
Hermitian matrices satisfying the anti-commutation relation
\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}+\gamma_{k}\gamma_{j}=2\delta_{jk}I_{N}
. Such matrices are usually called the gamma matrices or the Dirac matrices.
The
d
-dimensional Dirac equation with mass
m\geq 0
is given by

(1.21)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=H_{m}u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

Here
u
and
u_{0}
are
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued, and
the Dirac operator
H_{m}
is the Fourier multiplier operator whose symbol is

(1.22)

A_{m}(\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}\gamma_{j}+m\gamma_{d+1}.

Note that
A_{m}
satisfies

(1.23)

A_{m}(\xi)^{2}=(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+m^{2})I_{N},

so that the Dirac operator satisfies

(1.24)

H_{m}^{2}=(-\Delta+m^{2})I_{N}.

For simplicity, hereinafter we write

(1.25)

\phi_{m}(r)\coloneqq(r^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2}.

Since
H_{m}^{2}=(-\Delta+m^{2})I_{N}
, it is natural to expect that the smoothing estimate for the Dirac equation is similar to that for the relativistic Schrödinger equation:

(1.26)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=(-\Delta+m^{2})^{1/2}u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

On the other hand, in the case of the relativistic Schrödinger equation, we can show that the smoothing estimate is actually equivalent to that for the Schrödinger equation thanks to the following
comparison principle
established by
Ben-Artzi, Ruzhansky, and Sugimoto [5]
:

Theorem 1.3

(
[
5

)

Let

be a self-adjoint operator on

satisfying
\sigma(H)=\sigma_{\text{ac}}(H)
, and
assume that

(1.27)

\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\lvert\psi_{0}(H)e^{-itH}u_{0}(x)\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert u_{0}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

holds for some
C>0
, where
w\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
and
\psi_{0}\colon\sigma(H)\to\mathbb{R}

[

f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})
when
m=0
, and there are no extremisers when
m>0
.
Here we recall that
A_{0}
denotes the symbol of the massless Dirac operator
H_{0}
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\setminus\{0\}
, and assume that
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(d)}
is non-negative.
Then we have

(1.52)

\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}(w,r^{1/2})=2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})},

and there are no extremisers.
In particular, in the case (
C
), we have

(1.53)

\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}((1+r^{2})^{-s/2},r^{1/2})=\pi^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma((s-1)/2)}{\Gamma(s/2)}

for any
d\geq 3
.

Furthermore, actually Theorem
1.9
is still valid for the massless two-dimensional Dirac equation unlike for the Schrödinger equations, which is a little surprising.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
d=2
,
m=0
,
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\setminus\{0\}
, and assume that
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}
is non-negative.
Then we have

(1.54)

\bm{C}_{\textup{D},0}^{(2)}(w,r^{1/2})=2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})},

and there are no extremisers.

As a consequence of (
1.44
) and Theorem

(1.55)

\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}\leq\bm{C}_{\textup{S}}^{(2)}(w,r^{1/2})\leq 2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}.

Moreover, using Theorem

(1.56)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\downarrow 2}(s-2)\bm{C}_{\textup{S}}^{(2)}((1+r^{2})^{-s/2},(1+r^{2})^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\pi,

(1.57)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\downarrow 2}(s-2)\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(2)}((1+r^{2})^{-s/2},(1+r^{2})^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\pi,&m=0,\\
2\pi,&m>0.\end{cases}

See Section
6

Remark 1.C
.

Comparing Theorems

On the other hand, in the case (

2.
Preliminaries

In order to study the optimal constant of the smoothing estimate for the Dirac equation,
we introduce a linear operator
\widetilde{S}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
defined by

(2.1)

(\widetilde{S}f)(x,t)\coloneqq(2\pi)^{-d/2}w(\lvert x\rvert)^{1/2}\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\phi_{m}(\lvert\xi\rvert)^{-1/2}\psi(\lvert\xi\rvert)e^{-itA_{m}(\xi)}f(\xi)\,d\xi,

so that

(2.2)

\lVert\widetilde{S}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}=\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}(w,\psi).

In order to compute
\lVert\widetilde{S}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\mathbb{C}^{N})}
, we will use spherical harmonic polynomials.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\{p_{k}^{n}\}_{n}
be the space of spherical harmonic polynomials of degree
k
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and its orthonormal basis with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, respectively.
The spherical harmonics decomposition and the Funk–Hecke theorem are as follows:

Theorem 2.1

(spherical harmonics decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there uniquely exists
\{f_{k}^{n}\}_{k,n}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfying

(2.3)

\displaystyle f(\xi)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{-(d-1)/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}p_{k}^{n}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}^{n}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\lVert f_{k}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}^{2}.

Conversely, for any
\{f_{k}^{n}\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfying

(2.5)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\lVert f_{k}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}^{2}<\infty,

the function
f
given by (
2.3
) is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and satisfies (
2.4
).

Theorem 2.2

(Funk–Hecke theorem)
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
F\in L^{1}([-1,1],(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,dt)
and write

(2.6)

\lambda^{(d)}[k;F]\coloneqq\frac{2\pi^{(d-1)/2}}{\Gamma((d-1)/2)}\int_{-1}^{1}F(t)P_{k}^{(d)}(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,dt.

Then, for any
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we have

(2.7)

\int_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F(\xi\cdot\eta)Y(\eta)\,d\sigma(\eta)=\lambda^{(d)}[k;F]Y(\xi).

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
are classical and well known.
For example, see
Atkinson and Han [1, Theorems 2.22, 2.38]
.
Note that the function
\lambda_{k}
can be written as

(2.8)

(2\pi)^{d/2-1}\lambda_{k}(r)=\frac{1}{2}r^{d-2}(\psi(r))^{2}\lambda^{(d)}[k;\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(d)}(r\sqrt{2(1-\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}})})],

in other words, it satisfies

(2.9)

\frac{1}{2}r^{d-2}(\psi(r))^{2}\int_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(d)}(r\sqrt{2(1-\xi\cdot\eta)})Y(\eta)\,d\sigma(\eta)=(2\pi)^{d/2-1}\lambda_{k}(r)Y(\xi)

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For readers’ convenience, we list some other basic properties of
\lambda_{k}
here.

Proposition 2.3
.

We write
\lambda_{k}^{(d)}
for
\lambda_{k}
in dimension
d
.
Then the following hold:

(2.9)

We have
\lambda_{k}^{(d)}(r)\geq 0
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
.

(2.9)

If
2k_{1}+d_{1}=2k_{2}+d_{2}
, then we have
\lambda_{k_{1}}^{(d_{1})}=\lambda_{k_{2}}^{(d_{2})}
.

(2.9)

If
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(d)}
is non-negative, then we have
\lambda_{k+1}^{(d)}(r)\leq\lambda_{0}^{(d)}(r)
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
.
Furthermore, if
\lambda_{k+1}^{(d)}(r)=\lambda_{0}^{(d)}(r)
, then we have
\lambda_{0}^{(d)}(r)=0
or
\lambda_{k+1}^{(d)}(r)=\infty
.

(2.9)

If
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(d+2j)}
is non-negative for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then we have
\lambda_{k+1}^{(d)}(r)\leq\lambda_{k}^{(d)}(r)
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

(
2.9
) and (
2.9
) immediately follow from (
1.9
).
To see (
2.9
), use (
1.10
) and the fact that the Legendre polynomial
P_{k}^{(d)}
satisfies

(2.10)

\lvert P_{k}^{(d)}(t)\rvert<P_{0}^{(d)}(t)=1

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
d\geq 2
, and almost every
t\in[-1,1]
(see
Atkinson and Han [1, (2.39)]
).
(
2.9
) follows from (
2.9
) and (
2.9
):

\lambda_{k+1}^{(d)}\underset{\eqref{item:properties of lambdak 2}}{=}\lambda_{1}^{(d+2k)}\underset{\eqref{item:properties of lambdak 3}}{\leq}\lambda_{0}^{(d+2k)}\underset{\eqref{item:properties of lambdak 2}}{=}\lambda_{k}^{(d)}.\qed

Note that (
2.9
) is useful to find explicit values of
\bm{C}_{\textup{S}}^{(d)}
, since
\lambda_{k+1}(r)\leq\lambda_{0}(r)
implies

(2.11)

\bm{C}_{\textup{S}}^{(d)}=\sup_{r>0}\lambda_{0}(r).

However, it does not work well for
\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}
, since
\lambda_{k+1}(r)\leq\lambda_{0}(r)
does not guarantee
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k+1}(r)\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}(r)
.
On the other hand, if we have
\lambda_{k+1}(r)\leq\lambda_{k}(r)
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then we also have
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k+1}(r)\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r)
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

(2.12)

\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}=\sup_{r>0}\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}(r)

holds. This is the reason why (
2.9
) is more useful than (
2.9
) in order to find
\bm{C}_{\textup{D},m}^{(d)}
.

3.
Spherical harmonics and invariant subspaces

Throughout this section, we consider
A_{m}
as a linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
via

(3.1)

(A_{m}f)(\xi)\coloneqq A_{m}(\xi)f(\xi).

Our aim in this section is to construct a certain orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
satisfying some nice properties, which allows us to prove our Theorem
1.6
by an argument similar to that for Theorem
1.1
in
[
7
]
.

We begin with the following facts:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
V\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
be a linear subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Then

(3.2)

V+A_{0}V\coloneqq\{\,f+A_{0}g:f,g\in V\,\}

is an invariant subspace for
A_{0}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
1\leq j\leq d
,
k\geq 1
, and
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then there exist
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
W\in\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(3.3)

\xi_{j}Y(\xi)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}Z(\xi)+W(\xi).

As a consequence, we have

(3.4)

A_{0}\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})\subset\mathcal{H}_{k-1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})\obot\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
f,g\in V
. Then we have

(3.5)

A_{0}(f+A_{0}g)=A_{0}f+A_{0}^{2}g=A_{0}f+g\in V+A_{0}V,

since
A_{0}(\xi)^{2}=I_{N}
for
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Let

(3.6)

\displaystyle Z=\frac{1}{d+2k-2}\partial_{j}Y,

(3.7)

\displaystyle W=\xi_{j}Y-\frac{1}{d+2k-2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}\partial_{j}Y.

Then it is trivial that

•

\xi_{j}Y=\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}Z+W
,

•

Z\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

•

W
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k+1
.

Hence, it suffices to show that
\Delta W=0
.
To see this, note that
\Delta Y=0
implies

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Delta(\xi_{j}Y)=2\partial_{j}Y,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Delta(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}\partial_{j}Y)=2d\partial_{j}Y+4\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Y.

Furthermore, since
\partial_{j}Y
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k-1
,
we have

(3.10)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Y=(k-1)\partial_{j}Y.

Therefore, we conclude that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\Delta W

\displaystyle=2\partial_{j}Y-\frac{1}{d+2k-2}\left(2d\partial_{j}Y+4\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Y\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{d+2k-2}\left((k-1)\partial_{j}Y-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Y\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=0

holds.
∎

Using Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, we prove the following result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\{V_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of linear subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
defined by

(3.14)

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}),

(3.15)

\displaystyle V_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(A_{0}V_{k})^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\{\,Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}):\forall Z\in V_{k},\langle Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0\,\}.

Then

(3.17)

\displaystyle V_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}),

(3.18)

\displaystyle A_{0}V_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}),

(3.19)

\displaystyle A_{0}V_{k}\obot V_{k+1}=\mathcal{H}_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})

hold for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
As a consequence, we have

(3.20)

L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})=\bigobot_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(V_{k}\obot A_{0}V_{k}).

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

(
3.17
) is trivial from the definition of
V_{k}
.
Moreover, (
3.19
) immediately follows from (
3.18
) and the definition of
V_{k+1}
.
We prove (
3.18
) by induction.

The case
k=0
is trivial since
V_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

To see the case
k=1
, fix
Y\in V_{1}
arbitrarily.
Since we know that
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
holds by (
3.17
),
Proposition
3.2
implies

(3.21)

A_{0}Y\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})\obot\mathcal{H}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}).

On the other hand, using the definition of
V_{1}
and the self-adjointness of
A_{0}
, we get

(3.22)

\langle A_{0}Y,Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\langle Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0

for any
Z\in V_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Therefore, we conclude that (
3.18
) holds for
k=1
.

Now we show that if (
3.18
) holds for
k=k_{0}
,
then it also holds for
k=k_{0}+2
.
Fix
Y\in V_{k_{0}+2}
arbitrarily.
Since we know that
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
holds by (
3.17
),
Proposition
3.2
implies

(3.23)

A_{0}Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})\obot\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+3}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}).

Moreover, since we have

(3.24)

\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})=A_{0}V_{k_{0}}\obot V_{k_{0}+1}

by the induction hypothesis, we obtain

(3.25)

A_{0}Y\in A_{0}V_{k_{0}}\obot V_{k_{0}+1}\obot\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+3}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N}).

On the other hand, using the definition of
V_{k_{0}+2}
and the self-adjointness of
A_{0}
, we get

(3.26)

\langle A_{0}Y,Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\langle Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0

for any
Z\in V_{k_{0}+1}
.
Furthermore, using
V_{k_{0}+2}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
and the unitarity of
A_{0}
, we get

(3.27)

\langle A_{0}Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\langle Y,Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0

for any
Z\in V_{k_{0}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Therefore, we conclude that (
3.18
) holds for
k=k_{0}+2
.
∎

Furthermore, in order to compute
\gamma_{d+1}Y
and
\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Y
for
Y\in V_{k}
, we decompose
V_{k}
as follows:

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu\in\{1,-1\}
, we write

(3.28)

V_{k,\mu}\coloneqq\{\,Y\in V_{k}:\gamma_{d+1}Y=\mu Y\,\}.

Then we have

(3.29)

\displaystyle V_{k}=\bigobot_{\mu\in\{1,-1\}}V_{k,\mu},

(3.30)

\displaystyle V_{k,\mu}=\{\,Y\in V_{k}:\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Y=-\mu A_{0}Y\,\}.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

In order to prove (
3.29
), it is enough to show that

(3.31)

\gamma_{d+1}V_{k}\subset V_{k}

holds, since
\gamma_{d+1}
is a Hermitian matrix whose eigenvalues are
\pm 1
.
We prove (
3.31
) by induction.
In the case
k=0
, it is trivial since
V_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
Suppose that (
3.31
) holds for
k=k_{0}
and
fix
Y\in V_{k_{0}+1}
arbitrarily.
Since
\gamma_{d+1}Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
is immediate from
V_{k_{0}+1}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k_{0}+1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, it suffices to show that

(3.32)

\langle\gamma_{d+1}Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0

for any
Z\in V_{k_{0}}
.
To see this, notice that we have:

•

\langle Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0
for any
Z\in V_{k_{0}}
, since
Y\in V_{k_{0}+1}\subset(A_{0}V_{k_{0}})^{\perp}
.

•

\gamma_{d+1}V_{k_{0}}\subset V_{k_{0}}
by the induction hypothesis.

Combining these, we get

(3.33)

\langle\gamma_{d+1}Y,A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\langle Y,\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=-\langle Y,A_{0}\gamma_{d+1}Z\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=0.

Therefore, (
3.31
) holds for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now we prove (
3.30
).
If
Y\in V_{k,\mu}
, then

(3.34)

\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Y=-A_{0}\gamma_{d+1}Y=-\mu A_{0}Y.

On the other hand, if
Y\in V_{k}
satisfies
\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Y=-\mu A_{0}Y
, then

(3.35)

\gamma_{d+1}Y=\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}^{2}Y=-A_{0}\gamma_{d+1}A_{0}Y=\mu A_{0}^{2}Y=\mu Y,

hence
Y\in V_{k,\mu}
.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 3.5
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu\in\{1,-1\}
, let
\{e_{k,\mu}^{n}\}_{n}
be an orthonormal basis for
V_{k,\mu}
,
and write

(3.36)

E_{k,\mu}^{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}e_{k,\mu}^{n}&A_{0}e_{k,\mu}^{n}\end{pmatrix},&\mu=1,\\
\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}e_{k,\mu}^{n}&e_{k,\mu}^{n}\end{pmatrix},&\mu=-1.\end{cases}

Then the following hold:

(3.36)

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, there uniquely exists
\{f_{k,\mu}^{n}\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{C}^{2})
satisfying

(
3.36
.i)

\displaystyle f(\xi)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{-(d-1)/2}\sum_{\mu\in\{1,-1\}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}E_{k,\mu}^{n}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k,\mu}^{n}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

(
3.36
.ii)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})}^{2}=\sum_{\mu\in\{1,-1\}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\lVert f_{k,\mu}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}.

Conversely, for any
\{f_{k,\mu}^{n}\}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{C}^{2})
satisfying

(3.37)

\sum_{\mu\in\{1,-1\}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n}\lVert f_{k,\mu}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}<\infty,

the function
f
given by (

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{2^{d/2-2}\Gamma((d-1)/2)\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\rvert}\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[w(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)](\xi)\cdot\lvert\xi\rvert^{-1}\,d\xi

(5.82)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{2^{d/2-2}\Gamma((d-1)/2)\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\rvert}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\lvert x\rvert)\cdot\frac{2^{d/2-1}\Gamma((d-1)/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\lvert x\rvert^{-(d-1)}\,dx

(5.83)

\displaystyle=2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})},

which completes the proof.

Remark 5.B
.

When
d=3
,

(5.84)

\lambda_{0}(r)=(\pi/2)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{2r}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(3)}(t)t\,dt

is non-decreasing, but not strictly increasing in general.
For example, consider the case
w(r)=(1-\cos(r))/r^{2}
.
In this case, we have

(5.85)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}w(r)\,dr=\pi/2,

(5.86)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(3)}(r)=\begin{cases}(\pi/2)^{1/2}/r,&0<r<1,\\
0,&r>1,\\
\end{cases}

(5.87)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(r)<\lambda_{0}(r)=\begin{cases}\pi r,&0<r<1/2,\\
\pi/2,&r\geq 1/2.\end{cases}

See
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik [10, 12.33.5]
for
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(3)}
.
As a consequence,
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\setminus\{0\}
is an extremiser of the smoothing estimate for the Schrödinger equation if and only if it is radial and satisfies
\supp\widehat{u_{0}}\subset\{\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\lvert\xi\rvert\geq 1/2\,\}
.
Nevertheless, there are no extremisers for the Dirac equation due to the strict inequality
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r)<2\lambda_{0}(r)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.10
, recall that
\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}
is non-decreasing even in the case
d=2
, as we saw in the proof of Theorem
1.9
. Therefore, if
m=0
, then we have

(5.88)

\sup_{r>0}\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}(r)=\sup_{r>0}(\lambda_{0}(r)+\lambda_{1}(r))=\lim_{r\to\infty}(\lambda_{0}(r)+\lambda_{1}(r))=2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}.

Hence, it suffices to show that

(5.89)

\lambda_{k}(r)+\lambda_{k+1}(r)<2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}

holds for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
.
To see this, notice that
P_{k}^{(2)}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree
k
, in other words, it satisfies

(5.90)

P_{k}^{(2)}(\cos{\theta})=\cos{k\theta}.

Thus, by changing variable of integration and using trigonometric addition formulae, we get

(5.91)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(r)+\lambda_{k+1}(r)

\displaystyle=r\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(r\sqrt{2(1-t)})(P_{k}^{(2)}(t)+P_{k+1}^{(2)}(t))(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}\,dt

(5.92)

\displaystyle=2r\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(r\sqrt{2(1-\cos{2\theta})})(\cos{2k\theta}+\cos{2(k+1)\theta})\,d\theta

(5.93)

\displaystyle=4r\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(2r\sin{\theta})\cos{(2k+1)\theta}\cos{\theta}\,d\theta.

Hence, if
\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(2r\sin{\theta})=0
for almost every
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, then obviously

(5.94)

\lambda_{k}(r)+\lambda_{k+1}(r)=0<2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}

holds. If not, then we have

(5.95)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(r)+\lambda_{k+1}(r)

\displaystyle=4r\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(2r\sin{\theta})\cos{(2k+1)\theta}\cos{\theta}\,d\theta

(5.96)

\displaystyle<4r\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(2r\sin{\theta})\cos{\theta}\,d\theta

(5.97)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{2r}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(t)\,dt

(5.98)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{w}^{(2)}(t)\,dt

(5.99)

\displaystyle=2\lVert w\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}.

This completes the proof.
∎
