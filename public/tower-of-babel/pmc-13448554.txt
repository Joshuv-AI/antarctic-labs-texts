Benchmarking explainable offensive language detection in Somali with human-annotated rationales
Authors: Abdisalam Mahamed Badel, Ting Zhong, Xovee Xu, Wenxin Tai, Fan Zhou, Abdulahi Abdirashid Dahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Offensive language presents significant challenges on the internet and requires robust moderation. However, the efficacy of such moderation often depends on providing clear and interpretable justifications for each classification. Unfortunately, many existing datasets lack annotated rationales, and most detection models offer limited interpretability and transparency. These limitations hinder the development of trustworthy systems and the implementation of effective content moderation strategies. In this paper, we introduce SomOffXplain, an interpretable framework for detecting offensive language in Somali, which generates human-understandable explanations for its predictions. SomOffXplain performs span-level rationale extraction at both the word and phrase levels, enabling it to highlight text segments that support its predictions. Given that Somali is a low-resource language, we first construct a new benchmark dataset of 10,175 samples, each annotated with human-provided rationales. We evaluate our method against five fine-tuned pre-trained models using Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME). Additionally, we adapt four large language models (LLMs) through few-shot and zero-shot prompting to assess their ability to understand and produce rationales in Somali. Our proposed model demonstrates superiority in terms of explainability and predictive accuracy, exhibiting higher plausibility and faithfulness compared to the baselines. Furthermore, our results reveal that half of the state-of-the-art LLMs evaluated fail to generate high-quality rationales that align with human-annotated ground truth rationales, whereas LIME-based methods also prove to be weak explainers for Somali text. We believe our contributions support online safety, help prevent harassment in under-resourced language communities, enhance the trustworthiness of language models, and promote transparency in artificial intelligence systems.

Introduction

Nowadays, internet users can anonymously post content, including targeted offensive remarks, with their identities often unknown to victims or readers. Large language models (LLMs) can also generate offensive content1. A recent incident involving the Grok AI chatbot exemplifies this issue2. Such content may negatively affect those who encounter it3, especially targeted users. Offensive language, including hate speech, is widely recognized as a significant source of harm4. It can result in human rights violations, personal harm, and, in some cases, criminal behavior5. It may also pose serious risks, such as suicide attempts6. According to Ref7, offensive language generally refers to any expression that is hurtful, insulting, morally repugnant, or degrading. Hate speech, which is a subset of offensive language, targets people based on characteristics such as sexual orientation, gender, or race8. In this study, following9, we define offensive language as any comment containing a threat, insult, harmful or discriminatory action, swear words, profane language, or improper speech that violates accepted standards of normal discourse, whether targeted or not. Numerous studies, including10, confirm that hate speech significantly contributes to online abuse and harassment. In contrast11, notes that some countries, including Canada and France, have implemented laws prohibiting hate speech. A range of resources and datasets has been developed to support research in this field12–14, along with proposed models15 to address this issue. While some approaches rely on Transformer-based architectures3,16, others investigate LLMs’ ability for hate speech detection17,18. However, many existing datasets do not provide rationales, and most models lack interpretability. Moreover, most existing models fail to offer transparent and trustworthy justifications for their classifications19,20. This limitation is significant because platform moderators require explainable tools to understand why specific content violates policy guidelines21. A new study on an understudied language22argues that a large disparity exists between low-resource and high-resource languages from a performance perspective. On the other hand23, states that a significant research gap exists in detecting hate speech in low-resource languages, mainly due to linguistic diversity and the limited availability of annotated datasets. Somali, a low-resourced language, lacks many natural language processing (NLP) resources.

Consequently, a language rich in literature and poetry24,25 remains underrepresented in modern computational linguistics research. To address this gap and contribute to the creation of offensive-free social media and LLMs, we introduce XOLDS, a novel dataset with human-annotated rationales for offensive language detection in Somali. This dataset is not only the largest to date but also the first in the Somali language to include human-annotated rationales. Our dataset comprises 10,175 samples manually collected from TikTok and YouTube, with additional samples gathered from X. Three annotators labeled each sample as offensive or not offensive. Additionally, two of the three annotators selected relevant text segments and provided rationales for their annotations. Using these annotations, we then developed SomOffXplain (Benchmarking Explainable Offensive Language Detection in Somali with Human-Annotated Rationales), an interpretable framework for offensive language detection in Somali. To improve interpretability of SomOffXplain, we adopted class-conditioned contrastive learning and benchmarked the framework’s performance against five fine-tuned models using Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME)26, evaluating both explainability and classification accuracy. SomOffXplain implements explainability via extractive span-level rationales, where the model identifies the word or phrase in an input text that is most predictive of the offensive language class. Specifically, it performs span-level rationale extraction and highlights the exact text segments that support its predictions.

We further studied the rationale generation capability of four LLMs using zero-shot and few-shot (2-shot) settings. We evaluated the effectiveness of LLMs in explainability at both the word and phrase levels for rationale generation. Finally, this work proposes the first rationale-based benchmark dataset for the Somali language. We develop a computationally less expensive explainable framework for Somali offensive language detection. By comprehensively evaluating various methods, we create a new benchmark for explainable offensive language detection in Somali that can also be extended to other low-resource languages. Thus, we believe our work makes a significant contribution to the NLP community, particularly by addressing barriers faced by low-resource languages. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: We introduce the first rationale-based and largest dataset for offensive language detection in Somali, consisting of 10,175 samples with human-annotated spans.We propose a simple yet effective interpretable framework, SomOffXplain, for detecting offensive language in Somali, which can also be adapted for other low-resource languages.We investigate the capabilities of four LLMs in rationale generation and offensive language detection for Somali text. We also conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate and benchmark the performance of SomOffXplain in terms of both explainability and classification accuracy, comparing it against fine-tuned models that use LIME-based explanations and state-of-the-art LLMs.

Related work

In this section, we discuss the Somali language, prior work related to offensive language, and explainability in offensive language detection. Specifically, we first briefly introduce the Somali language, then summarize studies on offensive language, and finally review existing rationale-based work on offensive language detection.

Somali

Somali uses the Latin script and adopts rules from the Roman orthography25. It is a member of the Cushitic family and is mostly spoken in the Horn of Africa. The Somali language is the official language of Somalia25 and one of the five languages designated as future official languages in Ethiopia27. It is also widely spoken in some parts of Kenya and is one of the predominant languages in Djibouti25. Somali has a system of twenty-two consonants and five vowels25, and it is morphologically classified as an agglutinative language24. In summary, based on our investigation, Somali is a very low-resource language in computational linguistics. The language has limited electronic resources available in this field, which is a strong justification for conducting this project.

Offensive language

The study of offensive language is an ongoing and prominent research field, with numerous studies conducted. We discuss some of these studies in the literature, such as28, who studied offensive language in Greek and Arabic, while29categorized offensive language into distinct subcategories, such as offensive identification, offensive targets, and offensive types. Nevertheless11, emphasizes the persistent challenge of effectively categorizing offensive instances. Moreover30, investigated the impact of annotators on hate speech classification, emphasizing the necessity for clear definitions of hate speech. The fact that offensive language detection is an ongoing challenge leads to numerous studies employing diverse methodologies across multiple languages. For example31, proposed a multi-modal framework for hate speech, while32 used dual contrastive loss to address semantic information and distributional imbalance in hate speech classification. In addition, the authors in33explored implicit hate speech detection using contrastive learning34, focused on cross-lingual challenges. Furthermore, offensive language detection has been examined from multiple perspectives, including general offensive language6, sexism35, hate speech36,37, abusive language38, and cyberbullying39. Researchers argue that effective moderation requires clearly defined reasoning behind classification decisions20,21. This highlights the importance of explainability, which our work seeks to address.

Existing rationale-based works on offensive language

Most prior work in explainable text classification has relied on human-annotated rationales, first introduced by40, or on automatically generated rationales, later explored by41. In recent years, interpretability in offensive language detection has attracted considerable attention and has been studied across multiple languages. For example42, employed rationales for English hate speech classification, while43 introduced datasets for code-switched contexts in English-Tamil and English-Kannada.

Similarly44, examined toxic language detection using span annotations for English. Research on offensive language detection with human-annotated rationales has expanded to other languages, including Vietnamese45, Persian46, and Portuguese20. Nevertheless, offensive language detection in low-resource languages remains a significant challenge23. Moreover, model interpretability often depends on the availability of high-quality, human-annotated rationales20. To address this gap in the literature, we introduce a novel rationale-based dataset featuring human-annotated text spans in Somali. Furthermore, we present SomOffXplain, a simple, interpretable model designed for offensive language detection in Somali.

Dataset

In the dataset construction section, we provide a brief overview of the dataset collection process, label annotation methodology, target group identification, rationale generation, and the establishment of ground truth rationales.

Dataset collection

We collected this dataset primarily between April 2022 and August 2023 from the comments sections of YouTube and X/Twitter, with additional data collected from TikTok in 2024. To ensure the integrity of the dataset, we collected the data manually.

The dataset exclusively comprises text data, and we removed all personally identifiable information. We omitted names, links, and other identifying details, or anonymized them using the @user token, following prior work by Ref42. We took these measures to protect privacy, prevent potential rights violations, and comply with platform guidelines on ethics.Fig. 1Screenshot of the custom website designed for annotating the dataset. Dataset annotation

We recruited five data annotators, all native speakers of Somali. All annotators received extensive training and well-defined annotation guidelines. After training, following42 each annotator completed a pilot annotation on a sample of 100 comments. Annotation was conducted in two stages: Stage 1: Three annotators labeled 8,117 samples with labels and targets. Stage 2: Three annotators worked together, one from the previous team (who annotated 2,058 samples with labels and targets such as group, women, person, or other) and two new annotators (who annotated the full dataset with labels, targets, and rationales/spans explaining why a sample text was offensive or not). In this stage, labels, targets, and rationales were annotated, but rationales were identified only by the two new annotators. In the first phase, our annotation platforms were KoboCollect47 and Google spreadsheets48, and in the second phase we used a custom website developed for this task (see Fig. 1), which makes rationale selection easy. On this website, the rationale is selected with a mouse click. Table 1 presents the statistics of our dataset. Please note that we have tried our best to remove non-Somali texts from the dataset. For quality control, we used three annotators per item and applied majority voting. However, to assess voting reliability, we calculated Fleiss kappa (k)49 for binary label identification among the three annotators, which was 76%. Regarding annotator roles, we assigned one annotator who was more experienced than the others to serve as the disagreement resolver each time. Target group

In addition to labeling each comment as offensive or non-offensive and providing rationales, we instructed annotators to identify the target of each offensive comment, such as a group, woman, person, or other. The other category was introduced for instances in which the offensive comment had no explicit target, the target was ambiguous, or the target was difficult to determine. Furthermore, we identified 248 instances in which the annotators failed to reach a consensus. Following42, we excluded these disputed samples from the dataset. The distribution of targets within the offensive class was 4402 persons, 1140 groups, 124 women, and 457 others. As in prior work by50, we used majority voting and selected labels based on the majority decision of the three annotators. In cases of disagreement, the third annotator, who had the most experience, served as the adjudicator throughout the annotation process. We resolved all disagreements to ensure that all annotators agreed on a single label. Nevertheless, identifying the target category was challenging. For example, distinguishing between a person and a group, or between a woman and a person, often led to disagreement and required several rounds of resolution.

Annotator demographics

We worked with two teams, as the dataset was annotated in two rounds. In the first round, the annotation team consisted of three people: one woman and two men. In the second round, the team also consisted of three annotators: one woman and two men. One annotator from the first round also participated in the second round, so we had five annotators in total. Their ages ranged from 24 to 31, and their educational backgrounds included two with master’s degrees, two with bachelor’s degrees, and one with a high school diploma. All annotators were native Somali speakers.

Proposed framework

In the proposed framework section, we first provide an overview of the entire framework. We then describe the main components of the framework in detail. Specifically, we discuss (1) the backbone, (2) multi-detector learning, which consists of two subcomponents: (a) the offensive classifier and (b) the span extractor, and (3) class-conditioned contrastive learning.

Overview

We utilize a BERT encoder51 as the backbone for processing Somali text. It extracts features and generates embeddings, which are then passed into a multi-detector module that jointly performs (a) offensive language classification and (b) rationale span extraction. The offensive language classifier determines whether the text is offensive or non-offensive, while the span extractor identifies and extracts the relevant spans. The extracted spans and predicted labels are subsequently fed into a class-conditioned contrastive learning module, which uses averaged span embeddings as a semantic explanation and labels as additional information. Finally, at inference time an explainer generates explanations that include the predicted label, the rationale, and a justification for why the text belongs to that label. We apply simple preprocessing steps to the rationale and text, which normalizes them by converting the text to lowercase, removing whitespace, and handling NaN values. Class-conditioned contrastive learning

Empirically, we observed that most fine-tuned models achieve higher classification performance but fail to deliver good explainability. Based on this observation, we came up with the idea that span embeddings should be separated based on their class, with spans of the same class closer together and spans of dissimilar classes farther apart. This approach allows the contrastive loss to become more discriminative. Thus, we propose a contrastive rationale loss applied to span representations.

Unlike most existing rationale-based approaches like20,46, our method introduces a class-conditioned contrastive learning loss that uses not only generated span embeddings but also predicted label information. This architecture enables the model to cluster spans more effectively, and enhances explainability. Data ethics

To annotate this dataset, we manually collected publicly available textual data. We explained the purpose of the dataset to our annotators, assured them that their privacy would be protected, and obtained their full consent to participate, and we also paid them properly. In addition, we followed responsible NLP guidelines to preserve data privacy and strictly applied data anonymization. Our aim is to make the Somali language more resourced and to assist future researchers in creating offensive-free social media. We do not intend to harm anyone; therefore, we have properly anonymized the dataset and used only comments written in text, ensuring that identifying information has been removed or replaced with the @user token to the best of our ability. Overall, our methods were performed in accordance with relevant regulations and guidelines. Experiment

In this section, we provide a comprehensive overview of the dataset used, training details, baselines, and evaluation metrics.

Dataset

We used a dataset containing 10,175 samples and adopted a splitting strategy by allocating 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing. We selected a dataset that fit the addressed problem well, followed a standard random splitting approach, and ensured splits were representative of the whole dataset. Inspired by many offensive language detection studies, including42, we report only the results obtained from the test dataset.

Training details

We trained SomOffXplain using the BERT-base-uncased encoder with a hidden size of 768. We used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 3e-5, a batch size of 8, and a maximum sequence length of 128 for 3 epochs. We set random seeds to 42 for reproducibility. All experiments were conducted on a Windows 11 machine configured with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti Laptop GPU.

Baselines

To evaluate the effectiveness of SomOffXplain, we use two types of baselines: (1) fine-tuned models with LIME as the explainability baseline, and (2) four LLMs and the fine-tuned models as classification baselines. In addition to these two baselines, we further compare the rationale generation capabilities of the four LLMs with human-generated ground-truth rationales. Finally, the baselines we used are: LIME-based baselines. Explainability Baseline Using Fine-Tuned Models: We apply LIME, which is proposed by Ref26, to five fine-tuned pretrained models: RoBERTa52, mBERT51, DistilBERT53, XLNet54, and ALBERT55. We also use these fine-tuned models themselves as classification baselines. During fine-tuning, we tested two well-known explainability frameworks, LIME and SHAP, and selected LIME over SHAP for memory efficiency. For example, when experimenting with SHAP, we faced challenges during model inference and training with medium batch sizes greater than 4. Both frameworks have their advantages and disadvantages, but in this specific experiment, our main justification for selecting LIME was memory efficiency, along with lower complexity and reduced latency.LLM baselines. Evaluation metrics

We briefly discuss two types of evaluation metrics in this section: explainability metrics and classification metrics. For explainability, we employed IOU F1, Token F1, AUPRC, comprehensiveness, and sufficiency to evaluate our model and the LIME-based models. For the LLMs, we use F1 score, precision, recall, and exact match. For classification, we used metrics such as macro F1, macro precision, macro recall, F1 score, precision, accuracy, AUROC, and recall. We discuss the explainability metrics and the classification metrics as follows:

Explainability metrics.To measure explainability, we follow the ERASER framework of Ref58 and prior works such as20,42, which provide definitions and methods for evaluating model explainability. We evaluate explainability using two key measures: plausibility and faithfulness. Faithfulness assesses the correctness of the model’s reasoning59, while plausibility evaluates how persuasive the model’s interpretations are to humans. To measure plausibility, we apply both soft selection and discrete metrics. For discrete metrics, we use Intersection-over-Union (IOU) F1 score, Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), and token F1-score. IOU F1 score measures the overlap between two rationale spans divided by their combined token length, while token F1 is calculated based on token-level precision and recall58. We assess faithfulness using sufficiency and comprehensiveness58. Sufficiency evaluates whether the model can make accurate predictions using only the extracted rationales.

We calculate sufficiency as Sufficiency = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y_l \mid x) - P(y_l \mid r)$$\end{document}, where x denotes the original input text, r is the generated rationale, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_l$$\end{document} is the class which is predicted, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y_l \mid x)$$\end{document} is the model’s probability using the full text, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y_l \mid r)$$\end{document} is the probability using only the rationale. Comprehensiveness examines whether the extracted tokens include all important information for accurate predictions. We calculate it as: Comprehensiveness = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y_l \mid x) - P(y_l \mid x \setminus r)$$\end{document}, where x ∖ r is the text with the rationale removed, measuring how important the rationale is to the prediction. For LLM interpretability evaluation, we use both phrase-level and word-level evaluation. Phrase-level evaluation captures the overlap of full rationale phrases or semantic units, while word-level evaluation measures the overlap between individual words in the human-annotated ground truth rationales and those generated by the LLMs. For this, we use standard metrics such as F1 score, precision, and recall. Additionally, we evaluate LLMs using exact match rate, which focuses on rigorous equality between the predicted and ground-truth rationales.

Classification metrics. Our evaluation metrics follow established practices in offensive language detection evaluation and prior works such as20,42,46. We assess the model’s binary classification performance using macro F1, macro precision, macro recall, F1 score, precision, recall, accuracy, and AUROC.

Rationale generation using LLMs

To assess the effectiveness of LLMs in providing explainability for Somali text, we evaluate how well they generate rationales that align with human-provided ground-truth rationales. First, we use the entire dataset to evaluate the LLMs under zero-shot and few-shot prompting settings. In this evaluation, we perform both word-level and phrase-level analyses. The word-level evaluation measures the semantic overlap between human-generated and LLM-generated rationales, while the phrase-level evaluation measures how many of the rationale phrases produced by the LLMs truly match the human rationales. To this end, we summarize the findings in Table 4. In the few-shot setting, GPT-4o-mini achieves the highest phrase-level F1-score of 51%, although GPT-4o achieves comparable performance, indicating that their reasoning process at the phrase level is better than other models and aligns with human-generated rationales. In contrast, DeepSeek-v3 shows the lowest phrase-level performance of 33% in F1-score, suggesting that its rationale generation diverges more from human reasoning. In the word-level evaluation, all LLMs achieve higher F1-scores: 52%, 49%, 55%, and 56% for DeepSeek-v3, GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o-mini, respectively, indicating a higher degree of overlap between LLM-generated and human-generated words compared to phrase-level evaluations. Our findings reveal that most LLMs can detect explicit offensive words more effectively than implicit ones. This is evident in Table 5, where most LLMs select explicit offensive words. This suggests that LLMs rely more on dictionary-based words and direct translations but struggle to understand out-of-dictionary Somali offensive terms. Figure 4b presents the exact match rate between LLM-generated and human ground-truth rationales. DeepSeek-v3 achieves the highest exact match rate, scoring 12.9% in the few-shot setting. Table 6 shows the evaluation of the LLMs on 10% of the dataset. In these results, we compare human inter-annotator agreement with the LLMs’ few-shot performance on the ground-truth rationales. The two human annotators achieved a 65% F1-score in word-level agreement, while GPT-4o-mini performed closest to humans, with an F1-score of 48%. As presented in Table 4, in the zero-shot setting, GPT-3.5 exhibits greater conceptual disparity from humans, scoring an F1-score of 19% in the phrase-level evaluation, whereas DeepSeek-v3 achieves 52% in the word-level evaluation, indicating that its rationale generation process is more closely aligned with humans. Moreover, other models score moderately: GPT-3.5 scores 45%, GPT-4o scores 51%, and GPT-4o-mini scores 44% in the zero-shot setting. In the 10% subset of the test dataset, GPT-4o shows the highest exact match rate of 7.8% among the LLMs, as shown in Table 6, while GPT-4o-mini achieves the highest recall of 74%, indicating that it selects more rationales from the human-selected rationales compared to the other LLMs. We conclude that existing state-of-the-art LLMs can recognize and select well-known offensive or non-offensive words as rationales, but they mostly fail to detect words containing implicit offensive language. Classification results

We report the binary classification results of SomOffXplain, compared with the LLMs, in Table 7. SomOffXplain achieves a Macro-F1 of 87% and an accuracy of 87%, making it the highest-scoring model. This strong performance is likely due to the combination of span extraction with the offensive classifier, which may indirectly aid in classification. In the few-shot setting, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini achieve better classification performance than the other LLMs, both reaching an accuracy of 79% and a Macro-F1 of 78%. Similarly, in the zero-shot setting, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini again outperform the other LLMs in binary classification, with scores close to those obtained in the few-shot scenario: accuracy of 79% and 78%, respectively.

In Fig. 4a, we show the performance of our model on the individual labels offensive and non-offensive. SomOffXplain achieves the highest F1-score on the offensive label at 89%, which may be because the offensive label has a larger number of samples in the dataset. In Fig. 4c, we present the confusion matrix of SomOffXplain for binary classification performance. Figure 5a–c also visualizes the t-SNE of the training, development, and test sets for SomOffXplain, showing that the model separates classes reasonably well. We also present and analyze the performance of the LLMs on the individual offensive and non-offensive classes. Through these evaluations, the zero-shot results in Fig. 6a–d show that among all four LLMs, DeepSeek-V3 achieves the lowest F1-score of 58% in the non-offensive class. However, DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o-mini display similar performance in the offensive class, each obtaining an F1-score of 81%. GPT-3.5 performs moderately, while DeepSeek-V3 attains the highest recall score of 94% in the offensive class. In the few-shot setting, GPT-4o-mini achieves an F1-score of 81% in the offensive class, outperforming the other models, with DeepSeek-V3 ranked second, as shown in Fig. 7a–d. However, in the non-offensive class, DeepSeek-V3 shows the lowest score at 57% F1, whereas GPT-4o is the best-performing model with an F1-score of 76% in the non-offensive class. In summary, although the LLMs show some consistency in their individual class performances, they still lag significantly behind our proposed model. This suggests that there is still room for improvement in LLMs for low-resource languages. Human-understandable explanations

We measure the overlap between model predicted rationale spans and human-annotated gold rationale spans. This allows us to quantify how closely the extracted rationales correspond to explanations selected by human annotators. Specifically, we report Token-F1, IOU-F1, and AUPRC. As shown in Table 2, SomOffXplain achieves 70.77% Token-F1, 70.32% IOU-F1, and 79.01% AUPRC. It also achieves a token-level precision of 70.41% and recall of 73.00%. These results indicate that the rationales generated by SomOffXplain substantially overlap with human-annotated rationales, supporting their plausibility and interpretability. In addition to the quantitative evaluation, we provide qualitative examples to further illustrate the human interpretability of the generated rationales. For example, in the offensive sentence sharmutoyinka dhan isku dooq waye, meaning “all whores have the same hobby,” the ground-truth rationale is sharmutoyinka, meaning “whore.” The proposed framework correctly selects sharmutoyinka, the offensive term responsible for the classification. Similarly, for the non-offensive sentence horay u soco walalkey qaali, meaning “keep going my dear brother,” the ground-truth rationale is walalkey qaali, meaning “my dear brother,” and the proposed framework selects the same rationale while correctly classifying the sentence as non-offensive.

Real-world applicability and generalizability of SomOffXplain

In this subsection we discuss the generalizability and possible real-world applicability of the proposed framework.

Real-world applicability. The contributions of this work can be applied to areas such as political discourse monitoring, social media moderation, online news comment filtering, and moderation assistance for human content moderators. The proposed framework has several practical advantages, including fast extractive explanations and the ability for a single model to predict both the label and the evidence span. Compared to LIME-based approaches, SomOffXplain has a simpler architecture, which suggests it may be more suitable for low-resource environments. It is computationally less expensive and can be integrated into production systems with minimal latency. Furthermore, the benchmark dataset may support applications such as fine-tuning models for Somali language offensive and hate speech content detection, and could contribute to broader efforts in social media platforms or chatbots to mitigate offensive and toxic content.

Generalizability. To test the generalizability and robustness of SomOffXplain under low-resource conditions, we conducted a reduced-data experiment using 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the available labeled training data. The proposed framework achieved accuracies of 72%, 78%, 84%, and 87% on the respective data subsets in the binary classification task. Error analysis

To identify the weaknesses of the proposed framework and the factors affecting its performance, we conducted an error analysis.

The analysis revealed that the model’s reasoning is generally correct; however, word-mismatch errors still arise during inference. In particular, rationale misalignment is mainly caused by lexical variation. For instance, dilay “killed” may not match the lexical form of dilaan “to kill” in the sentence garxajis waa inay noocan ay iska dilaan intanay somaliland madaxtinimo ka doonin, translated as “garxajis should kill this type before they go to find leadership in Somaliland.” In this case, the model classified the sentence as non-offensive while simultaneously producing a mixed rationale that highlighted both offensive and non-offensive spans. Furthermore, words such as naagahan “these women”, ninkan “this man”, and hooyadiis “his mother” appear to affect both rationale generation and classification, possibly because they occur frequently in offensive sentences (see Fig. 13). These findings suggest that lexical variation and the prominence of certain words may bias the model’s predictions. Overall, the error analysis indicates that the proposed framework is sensitive to specific lexical forms: although it can correctly identify most offensive expressions, it may fail to recognize morphologically different variants, thereby limiting its generalization across syntactic constructions. We also found that the LLMs sometimes generate incorrect rationales and misclassify sentences that do not contain explicit offensive words. For example, the sentence walal ha u gafin waa qalad gafku e was labeled as offensive by at least two LLMs, which also highlighted incorrect spans, even though the sentence is non-offensive. Therefore, we believe that addressing these issues remains an important direction for future work.

Case study

We examined the presence of bias in both binary and target-wise classifications, with the objective of identifying which categories were frequently incorrectly classified. To accomplish this objective, we visualized the dataset using saliency maps.

Our findings show that the model misunderstands some contextual meanings, classifying words such as hooyadiis/hooyadaa “his mother/your mother” and aabahaa/aabe “your father/father” as offensive, despite their positive use in context. This misclassification likely results from the high frequency of these words in offensive sentences, where they are commonly used in insults. For instance, as shown in Fig. 3b, the sentence hooyadiis baa ka mida oo Afrika nala joogta, which translates to “his mother is a member and stays in Africa with us,” was classified as offensive, although it is not. The model’s decision seems to have been influenced by the presence of hooyadiis. When we removed this word, the model correctly classified the sentence as non offensive (see Fig. 13). In addition, understanding context remains a significant challenge for most models. In Somali, the lack of essential NLP tools, such as a part-of-speech tagger (POS-tagger), makes it difficult to handle morphological variations. Words with multiple forms can easily mislead the model. These findings suggest that the development of fundamental NLP tools for Somali considerably helps to improve text preprocessing and overall model performance in this under-resourced language.

Limitations and future work

In this section of the proposed framework, we briefly discuss the limitations of SomOffXplain and future research directions.

Limitations of SomOffXplain. First, we manually collected the dataset over a long period, which made the manual collection process challenging, time-consuming, and costly. Moreover, Somali is a low-resource language, and LLMs may overlook some contextual information, which can sometimes render them unable to detect implicit offensive content. Additionally, our model sometimes incorrectly classifies text when certain words, often used in conjunction with offensive terms, are present, even if these words have positive meanings. Finally, the proposed model faces challenges from different lexical forms and syntactic constructions of the same words.

Future work. for future work, we recommend linguistically informed resource development, such as a well-curated Somali POS-tagged corpus, to improve contextual understanding and enhance LLM-based offensive language detection in Somali. We also recommend using stemmers to resolve lexical ambiguity. Finally, we recommend new studies on targeted sexism in Somali.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an interpretable framework for offensive language detection in the Somali language. To this end, we created a dataset of 10,175 samples, each annotated with human-provided rationales. We then proposed a framework that performs offensive language classification while concurrently extracting relevant rationales. These rationales provide clear explanations for the model’s predictions, thereby enhancing its interpretability and trustworthiness. To establish a benchmark, we evaluated our framework against several baseline models, including LIME-based models and LLMs. Our model demonstrated superior performance in explainability, achieving higher plausibility and faithfulness compared to the baselines.

It also outperformed them in binary classification, attaining a Macro-F1 score and an accuracy of 87%. Our findings show that most LLMs evaluated were unable to detect some implicit offensive text in Somali. Furthermore, LIME-based models achieved lower scores than our model in terms of explainability for Somali text. These results underscore the importance of our work and the dataset we created, in advancing computational linguistics, particularly for Somali. Given that Somali is a low-resource language, we consider both our dataset and framework to be significant contributions to Somali NLP and the broader field of low-resource language research. Moreover, our framework is adaptable to other low-resource languages, and our dataset provides a generalizable approach to data collection and annotation. In summary, our work advances the field of NLP, especially in offensive language detection, where interpretability and transparency are essential for responsible content moderation. This contribution is particularly relevant in today’s digital era, where the widespread use of LLMs and social media necessitates robust methods for identifying and mitigating offensive content.
