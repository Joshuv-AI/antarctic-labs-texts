Food allergy in early life
Authors: Aikaterini Anagnostou
Published: 2024

This issue of the Journal of Food Allergy brings the focus to early life interventions, management approaches, and infant and/or toddler anaphylaxis. It is well-known that both milk and egg allergies are common in early life, with the majority self-resolving in later years. Tolerance to the baked versions of these allergens often suggests the potential for patients to outgrow them. Currently, various options (dietary advancement therapies) exist for individuals who are milk and egg allergic, in addition to strict avoidance, including early static baked allergen introduction, milk and/or egg ladders, and oral immunotherapy.

Dupuis et al.1 present an overview of dietary advancement therapies (DAT) that aim to accelerate the development of tolerance in children who are milk and egg allergic. Age <3 or >3 years old as well as other factors, such as medical history (asthma, anaphylaxis, respiratory and/or cardiovascular comorbidities), results of allergy testing, caretaker level of comfort, and previous adherence to medications, may all help determine whether a child is a good candidate for such interventions. The authors propose the development of universal guidelines for screening for ideal candidates for DATs as well as for dosing protocol standardization.

A review of the use of DATs for managing non-IgE-mediated and IgE-mediated food allergies, with emphasis on recent guidelines, and available literature is provided by Mack et al.2 The authors highlight that the effectiveness of ladders in hastening allergy resolution is still debated, with inconsistent evidence to suggest that they expedite tolerance development. They also discuss the uncertainty surrounding long-term effectiveness and safety of DATs as well as the potential risk of adverse reactions. In addition, the authors note that safe clinical practice and implementation rely heavily on patient education and counseling about the process, risk reduction, and management of comorbid health conditions.

Potential benefits to less strict avoidance in children who are milk allergic are described by Groetch et al.3 These may include hastening of resolution of a food allergy, increased diet diversity, less health-care utilization, decreased overall cost, decreased patient burden, and decreased nutrition risk. When considering that no biomarkers exist currently that can reliably predict tolerance to baked milk and baked egg, oral food challenges remain the criterion standard diagnostic tool. Khan et al.4 report on how to identify optimal candidates for baked food challenges and how to engage families in shared decision-making with regard to the timing and location of introduction as well as the benefits and risks associated with the food challenge process.

This issue wraps up with the diagnostic and management challenges in early age (infant and toddler) anaphylaxis.5 Anaphylaxis can occur at any age, but specific challenges are present for preschool children. Infants are nonverbal and cannot clearly express subjective symptoms. Certain symptoms and signs may be difficult to interpret because they can be common in healthy infants and are nonspecific for anaphylaxis. Early life anaphylaxis remains an area of intense interest especially after recent changes in prevention strategies as well as novel therapeutic approaches that target this age. The authors highlight that improved education and consistent training of families and health-care professionals have the potential to mitigate the multiple challenges in identifying and treating preschool anaphylaxis.
