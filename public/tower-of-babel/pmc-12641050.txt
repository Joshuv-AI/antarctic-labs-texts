White Blood Cells in Neurodegenerative Diseases in the Context of Neuroinflammation
Authors: Agnieszka Wełnicka-Wesołowska, Monika Gołąb-Janowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurodegenerative diseases are a multifactorial problem for modern neurology, due to their unknown etiology, difficult diagnostics, and, consequently, the lack of causal methods of treatment. Rising life expectancy exacerbates the problem by increasing the number of patients, as most of them fall ill in older age. Recent research shows that inflammation, both peripheral and neuroinflammation, may take part in the pathogenetic pathways of disease development. Some studies suggest that the initiation of the process occurs outside of the nervous system and is causally related to the immune system. This review focuses on the role of basic peripheral immune cells, including lymphocytes and neutrophils, in the course of selected central nervous system pathologies and their potential use as a biomarker. Both types of leukocytes not only change their quantity, but, more importantly, their function. Furthermore, they interact with microglial cells that constantly surveil the central nervous system and respond to pathological factors. Their joint action, however, can be either neuroprotective or neurotoxic. This review highlights that the development of neurodegeneration is associated with a dysregulation of the balance between immune cell phenotypes and their interactions. The disturbed immune response does not allow for the organism to correctly control the disease.Keywords: Neuroinflammation, Neurodegenerative diseases, White blood cells, Lymphocytes, Neutrophils

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases are a group with a wide spectrum of clinical symptoms (motor, sensory, but also cognitive), diverse etiology, and various anatomical distributions of degeneration. They are characterized by the progressive loss of specific populations of neurons—their deficits are eventually replaced by glial cells. In neuropathological examinations, the neurodegenerative process is accompanied by inflammation of the central nervous system (CNS). Certain diagnosis of many of those disorders is only possible after neuropathological assessment, and the diagnostic criteria available in vivo are complicated and initially difficult to meet. In some, particularly amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), there are no clear diagnostic laboratory biomarkers apart from rare gene mutations [1]. Although demyelinating diseases, such as multiple sclerosis (MS), belong to a different group, the pathology occurring in them can also be described as CNS degeneration [2].

The immune system within the CNS is treated as separate and privileged due to the limited permeability of the blood–brain barrier (BBB). The cells responsible for constant immune surveillance in the CNS are microglial cells—they respond specifically to continuous changes in their local environment by secreting appropriate cytokines, and their functions adapt according to the location and mutual interactions with nearby cells and structures [2, 3]. In some types of inflammation, e.g., in neurodegenerative diseases, a loss of control over the inflammatory response occurs, probably due to the inability to remove the triggering factor [4, 5]. Microglial activity is then significantly increased, and in addition, immune cells from the blood also infiltrate. Their joint action, depending on the situation, can be either neuroprotective or neurotoxic—what regulates this balance is not fully understood [2].

However, the activation of T cells, particularly known in the pathology of MS, takes place outside the brain [6]. Also, the features of inflammation in mice with the SOD1 mutation associated with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis are present even before the onset of degeneration [7, 8]. These reports may suggest that the onset of the disease process in neurodegenerative disorders may occur outside the CNS and be associated with inflammation and activation of the innate immune system [8, 9]. Additionally, numerous recent discoveries in the field of CNS immunology, including those concerning the lymphatic system of the CNS [10] or the functional diversity of microglia [3], suggest the need to revisit the basic immune cells of the blood, which are white blood cells.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Of all neurological diseases, the number of lymphocytes is particularly important in the pathogenesis of MS and T cells play a crucial role here, reacting with myelin autoantigens and causing their damage, as well as the destruction of the axons lying beneath them. In animal models, it has been proven that CD4 + lymphocytes become sensitized to myelin antigens before reaching the CNS (in the lymphatic organs). Then, they move there, migrate through the BBB through alpha-4 integrin receptors, and settle in both white and gray matter, especially in active demyelinating plaques. Microglial cells additionally promote their proliferation and activation [2, 3, 11]. Administration of fingolimod, a popular MS drug, which causes sequestration of T lymphocytes in lymphoid organs, reduces the number of circulating T lymphocytes and alleviates the course of the disease [12]. As it turns out, T cells infiltrating the CNS belong mainly to the Th1 and Th17 subtypes, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ and IL-17. In research studies, neutralization of IL-17 by anti-IL-17 antibody, or the selective deletion of the previously mentioned alpha-4 integrin genes in T cells, delays the progression of the experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE), although not its complete elimination [11, 13]. CD8 + lymphocytes have received less attention so far, but they also accumulate and activate in the periphery, penetrate the CNS, and infiltrate active demyelinating plaques, intensifying changes initiated by CD4 +, but also inducing CNS autoimmunity on their own [14, 15]. The quantity of CD8 + T lymphocytes in the active, inflammatory plaques of patients is even higher than that of CD4 +, as clonal expansions of CD8 + dominate the T cell infiltrate there. Also, in MS patients, an increased frequency of oligoclonal CD8 + cells expressing particular gene segments is found, when compared to healthy controls [16]. CD8 +, stimulated during MS relapses, show greater adhesion to cerebral vessels [17] and tend to penetrate the BBB to a greater extent [18]. The results of research on animal models turned out to be conflicting. In some studies, the pathogenic nature of these lymphocytes was proven, while in others, their deficiency resulted in a more severe course of the disease [19]. Initially, it was believed that the number of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells decreased in MS, but over time, it became clear that it is more functional impairment (represented by changes in cellular differentiation markers) than quantity that plays a role here and it is their activity, not their number, that is dysregulated in pathological conditions [11]. It seems significant that potentially autoreactive T lymphocytes directed against myelin antigens are also present in healthy people, which could imply that impaired regulatory mechanisms contribute to the development of the disease. Indeed, the activity of regulatory T cells is reduced in patients with MS, which causes, among others, reduced levels of protective IL-10 and poorer ability to suppress Th1, IFN-γ, and IL-17 [20, 21]. In experimental studies, the loss of regulatory cells increased the inflammatory infiltration and disease severity [22] and their transfer from the outside had a protective effect [23], possibly dependent on IL-10 and TGF-β [24]. Moreover, one older study [25] showed that neurons themselves can induce the proliferation of activated CD4 +, resulting in the conversion of encephalitogenic T cells to T regulatory cells that inhibit EAE. It seems that the disturbed balance between the inflammatory and regulatory responses is crucial in the pathogenesis of MS.

B lymphocytes have so far attracted much less attention in this illness, which has been changing in recent years. Both memory B cells and plasma cells are also present in demyelinating plaques and in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients [26]. Their infiltration is even greater than in other inflammatory diseases of the CNS, particularly in the active form of MS, and their number correlates with the progression of neurodegeneration. B lymphocytes secrete antibodies (intrathecal production of the so-called oligoclonal bands is particularly noteworthy), but they also have the ability to present antigen, intensify the proliferation of T lymphocytes, and produce pro-inflammatory cytokines (in MS, particularly TNF-alpha, IL-6, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)) and toxic factors contributing to the damage to oligodendrocytes and neurons. In addition, they provide a reservoir for the Epstein-Barr virus, which is also suspected of being associated with MS [27]. These conclusions led to the discovery that reducing the number of B cells in peripheral blood may be an effective therapeutic strategy. In recent clinical trials, new drugs that affect B cells have not had much success, unlike the already widely used anti-C20 antibodies (also used in MS, such as ofatumumab and ocrelizumab)—their action results in a significantly reduced pro-inflammatory response of CD4 + and CD8 + T lymphocytes, as well as myeloid cells. When looking for the reason for the difference in effectiveness, the authors paid particular attention to memory B lymphocytes, which were not affected by the new drugs [28, 29]. On the other hand, in mice with EAE, B cells attenuated CNS inflammation by producing anti-inflammatory cytokines, mainly TGF-β and IL-10. In MS patients, these lymphocytes show a deficiency in the production of IL-10 compared to the healthy control group and therefore a lower ability to weaken immune responses. Therefore, abnormalities in the functioning of both of the above-mentioned types of lymphocytes may play a role in the pathogenesis of MS, or abnormalities in one cell type may cause an exaggerated response of the other [27]. It is noteworthy that almost all current immunotherapies used in MS are based on depleting T lymphocytes through different mechanisms.

Neutrophil infiltration into the CNS and subsequent secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines triggers an inflammatory cascade in patients with MS, causing damage to the BBB [30]. It is not the lymphocytes, typical of this disease, but neutrophils that are the blood cells most abundantly represented just before or at the appearance of clinical symptoms, which could suggest their role in the initiation of the pathology is crucial for the early phase of the development of the demyelinating plaque. Their number decreases afterward, but they are still observed in large quantities in the areas of vascular leaks, demyelination, and axonal damage. In EAE, after entering the CNS, they extravasate and in close contact with T and B cells they can acquire immunostimulatory and antigen presenting properties [31]. These cells can also damage neurons directly—by CXCR2 signaling (a key regulator of neutrophil neurotoxicity) and producing oxidative stress. Blocking this pathway in mice with EAE had a protective effect [32]. Depletion of neutrophil levels also alleviated disease symptoms [33]. Their increased number in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) compared to healthy controls is primarily attributed to a reduction in spontaneous apoptosis in a pro-inflammatory environment [34]. Such neutrophils are also more active in terms of degranulation, oxidative bursts, and extracellular neutrophil traps (NET) release [35].

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)

In Alzheimer’s disease, amyloid β (Aβ) proteins seem to have the ability to migrate to cervical lymph nodes, where APCs present them to T lymphocytes, which activate an immune response [36, 37]. Immune deficiency is also not beneficial, as Marsh et al. discovered that in an AD mouse model lacking T, B, and NK cells, there was a significant increase in Aβ pathology as well as exacerbation of neuroinflammation. The authors suggested that adaptive immunity inhibits the development of AD [38]. Different types of T lymphocytes have been studied in AD.

Infiltration of CD4 + and CD8 + cells into the CNS in association with tau protein and amyloid deposits has been discovered (in both white and gray matter, especially the hippocampus), including through α4 integrins, which show stronger expression than in healthy people [39]. The quantity of serum CD4 + helper cells seems to also be slightly elevated compared to controls [40]. Th17 lymphocytes, a subtype of these cells, in particular, appear in increased numbers, producing pro-inflammatory IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22, which accumulate in much higher concentrations in the serum of patients than in controls [13, 41]. While an increase in Th17 itself is particularly noticeable in the initial phases of the disease and in mild cognitive disorders [42], a decrease in the total CD4 + count was found in the advanced phase [39, 43, 44]. Murine models show that the accumulation of IL-17-producing cells in the brain and meninges exacerbates cognitive and synaptic dysfunction [45] and that its neutralization delays clinical progression at early stages of the disease [45, 46]. Aβ1–42 injected into rats’ hippocampi causes an impairment of BBB and Th17 cell infiltration into brain parenchyma [46]. Furthermore, Aβ-specific Th1 combined with Th17 clones exacerbate memory deficits in mice [47]. A greater number of CD4 + lymphocytes also exacerbated the risk of developing AD in the future [48]. On the other hand, CD4 + may also have a protective effect by enhancing amyloid β phagocytosis and promoting neuronal repair [49]. It seems that the interaction of Th17 cells with other T cell subpopulations may result in a different cytokine profile and different outcomes—this subtle interplay changes throughout the course of the disease.

Another lymphocyte T type, CD8 + cytotoxic, plays a major role in the direct killing of abnormal cells. In the early stages of AD, researchers observed their increased levels [50], but considering all patients, a large meta-analysis showed a decrease in CD8 + lymphocytes in serum [51]. In the CNS, their infiltration has been detected both in humans and in murine models, in cerebrospinal fluid and in perivascular spaces in the hippocampi [52]. Brain CD8 + cells may lead to dysfunction in neurons, especially those regulating synaptic plasticity [53], but also to damage in all surrounding structures by releasing greater amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, in AD, these lymphocytes bind to neurons expressing MHC-I and produce IFN-γ, thus triggering neuronal damage and boosting neuroinflammation and may, therefore, promote brain changes associated with disease progression [13]. It was determined that the CD8 + population decreases corresponding with the higher severity of dementia in women, but not in men [54], while reduced CD4 + levels correlated with more severe dementia in patients of both sexes [43]. One study found a correlation between clonally amplified CD8 + lymphocyte levels in the CSF and cognitive decline in patients [52]. Moreover, reduced recruitment of CD8 + T lymphocytes into the hippocampus, caused by purinergic receptor P2X7 deficiency, leads to reduced Aβ plaque formation [55].

Regulatory T lymphocytes play an important role in regulating immune response. As in MS, in AD, the number of resting regulatory T cells decreases, and the number of their effector subtype increases [39] as they respond to misfolded Aβ proteins [56]. Moreover, their immunosuppressive activity fluctuates—from initially raised to significantly impaired in the advanced phase of dementia [54, 57], which makes them unable to control the inflammatory response. In the early stages of the disease, their level is inversely proportional to the depth of cognitive deficits and the deposition of amyloid plaques [58]. Studies indicate that Aβ-specific T regulatory cells can reduce reactive microglia and amyloid deposition, and also may inhibit immune cell infiltration into the CNS [37, 59]

In dementias of various causes, including AD, a reduction in the number of B lymphocytes is observed [44, 60], especially considering naive IgD + CD27- cells [61]. Also, low IgG antibody levels are detected in AD patients’ hippocampi [62]. However, when compared to healthy elders, AD patients have an elevated level of double-negative (IgD-CD27-) B lymphocytes [63]. This subpopulation is still enigmatic, as the absence of IgD indicates previous isotype switching, but the absence of CD27 specifies fewer differentiated cells. Double-negative cells are anticipated to originate after defective reactions in germinal centers or to derive from memory B cells after immune senescent or immune exhaustion processes [64]. Also, an increase in IgG synthesis and upregulation of the chemokine receptor-5 is observed, suggesting activation of these lymphocytes in AD, possibly by Aβ interaction [65]. Furthermore, murine models showed that B lymphocyte count (measured by CD19 expression) differs between AD and healthy mice, with lower counts in the hippocampi, but also peripherally, in the blood and the spleen [62]. Apart from Ig production, B cells can secrete a substantial amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially in response to Aβ contact. Aβ antibodies can be found in the serum of both healthy people and AD patients, although their levels are higher in the latter [66]. Physiologically, as seen in transgenic mice, these antibodies prevent plaque formation and can improve cognitive functions. In AD, however, they are thought to interfere with Aβ aggregation and toxicity [67]. Moreover, not only Aβ, but also tau proteins, neurofilaments, and microtubule-associated proteins, when released into the serum due to AD cell damage, can stimulate specific antibody production [68]. Also, because of a compromised BBB, more antibodies can infiltrate into the CNS. In the context of Aβ antibodies, immunotherapy, as a promising approach to AD treatment, is worth mentioning. Immunotherapies in AD focus mainly on using monoclonal antibodies to decrease the Aβ protein (sometimes tau protein) levels by inducing the immune system to remove them from the CNS and, ultimately, to slow the progression of the disease. Another option, currently in clinical trials, is active vaccines against AD, mostly against the Aβ protein deposition [69].

Inflammatory agents, such as Aβ, activate NK cells, which lead to increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, creating a feedback loop that causes neuroinflammation progress and cognitive decline. In comparison to healthy individuals, NK cells in AD show elevated responsiveness to inflammatory stimuli [70]. Moreover, researchers demonstrated active NK cell infiltration in the brains of mice with AD [71]. Treatment with anti-NK antibodies resulted in enhanced neurogenesis [72].

Studies on neutrophils in AD have revealed a greater activity and number of these cells circulating in the peripheral blood of patients compared to healthy controls [37, 73] and greater amounts of reactive oxygen species produced by them [74], which were correlated with disease severity and the rate of progression of mental decline [75]. Neutrophils may differ in phenotype—the ratio between the hyperreactive, senescent phenotype and the immunosuppressive neutrophil subsets rises with disease progression [76]. A systemic inflammatory response, which can cause peripheral neutrophil hyperactivation, coupled with a compromised BBB [77], makes it easier for them to infiltrate into the brain parenchyma. Those stimulated cells migrate across the BBB in significant numbers even before the onset of clinical symptoms due to the increased presence of the CD11b receptor on their surface [31, 75]. After entering the CNS, they accumulate [78] and enhance the pro-inflammatory effect and neurodegeneration (including the formation of NETs and reactive oxygen species). Moreover, when inside the CNS, in the mouse model at least, migrated neutrophils show target Aβ plaques specifically [79]. On the other hand, their depletion or migration disorders alleviate the symptoms of AD at every stage of the disease. Amyloid β accumulating in brain tissue plays a key role in neutrophil recruitment by changing the affinity of their receptors [80, 81]. Peripherally, pro-inflammatory proteins produced by neutrophils (e.g., lipocalin-2 (NGAL)) also have a negative impact on the memory and executive functions of patients, and although we do not fully know that mechanism, they certainly damage the BBB [82, 83]. Clinical data suggest that AD patients have increased plasma NGAL levels [83], but there was no difference in CSF NGAL concentrations compared to healthy individuals [84]. Both in the AD mouse model and in patients, there is also a reduction in cerebral blood flow due to the blockage of capillaries by tightly adherent neutrophils (due to increased ICAM-1-LFA-1 interaction), which is additionally intensified by inflammatory cytokines. Vascular dysfunction precedes cognitive dysfunction here [85–87]. Decreased cerebral blood flow has been identified as a prominent sign of AD onset [88] and can also amplify brain inflammation [89] and change energy metabolism in the brain [90]. Additionally, transcriptional analyses conducted in several studies reveal a significant increase in the number of differentially expressed genes related to neutrophil signaling pathways in AD patients compared to the control group [87]. Interestingly, some of the neutrophil granule enzymes also have a positive effect, playing a role in the removal of accumulated amyloid [91], and furthermore, these cells also have the ability to secrete neurotrophic factors (specifically neuromodulating ciliary neurotrophic factor) [92]. Such different roles may be a result of the high heterogeneity of neutrophils.

Furthermore, the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes (NLR) allowed many researchers to differentiate between patients with AD and healthy controls and between people with mild cognitive impairment and healthy controls. In patient groups, it was higher (the optimal cut-off value was considered 2.48 in the older study and 1.91 later). The indicator correlated with the progression of cognitive dysfunctions, including in people without a certain diagnosis. NLR may reflect peripheral inflammatory processes related to the pathogenesis of the disease [73, 93–96]. It was also determined that NLR can be used as a prognostic factor for the occurrence of dementia of various causes in the future [97, 98], which may be explained by its relationship with atherosclerosis of large cerebral vessels, but it is uncertain in the case of small vessel disease [99, 100]. A higher index was significantly associated with poorer visual memory and visuospatial awareness, as well as with a larger volume of white matter hyperintensity and brain tissue atrophy in magnetic resonance imaging [101, 102].

Using a Mendelian randomization study to determine the risk of AD coming from circulating immune cells, researchers discovered specific immune signatures associated with higher (three out of seven related to T cells [103]) and lower disease risk (four out of seven related to T cells [104]). Considering this fact, further studies could investigate whether the changes in immune cells are causal to AD or whether they occur in correlation with other irregularities to ultimately induce AD together.

Parkinson’s Disease (PD)

Among other neurological diseases associated with a pathological lymphocytic reaction, Parkinson’s disease (PD) should be mentioned. From an epidemiological point of view, individuals with autoimmune disorders have a higher PD risk, which suggests a role of peripheral immunity in the disease [105]. PD is characterized by microglial activation, but also an increased level of pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by autoreactive T lymphocytes, and moreover, autoantibodies directed against α-synuclein peptide antigens, produced by autoreactive B lymphocytes [106–108]. The profiles of these cells are more pro-inflammatory [109].

Infiltration of CD4 + and CD8 + cells into the CNS in PD has been widely confirmed, as in AD [110]. The total population of circulating CD4 +, CD8 +, and total lymphocytes in serum is reduced in PD, particularly in its late stages [111, 112]. Studies based on murine models show higher levels of Th1 and Th17 cells and lower levels of T regulatory cells [113, 114]. In concordance with that, it is believed that CD4 + is the main mediator of dopaminergic damage, and the main phenotypes leading to neurodegeneration are the pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 [115]. In mice, pharmacological blocking of Th17 cells ameliorated neurodegeneration and motor decline [116], and their adoptive transfer from mice immunized with alpha-synuclein (α-syn) worsened dopaminergic neurodegeneration [117]. One study linked cognitive decline in patients to greater Th1 levels and dysregulation of regulatory T cells [118]. Also, a Th1/Th17 pro-inflammatory bias has been detected in some studies [119, 120]. On the other hand, T regulatory cells seem to play a positive role in PD. The reduction of Tregs in a mouse model exacerbated neurodegeneration [121], while increasing their response ameliorated it and improved motor defects [122]. Additionally, in animal models, activated Tregs have the ability to interact with local glial cells in the CNS and alter the reactive microglial phenotype from a toxic to a trophic state and, furthermore, induce astrocyte expression of growth factors to promote neuron survival [123]. Other studies on PD have reported Treg cells with a lower regulatory and suppressive capacity combined with an increase in pro-inflammatory effector T cells [110]. Also, active rodent immunization with a low dose of α-syn achieved neuroprotection associated with, among others, a high number of Treg cells [124]. Both CD4 + and CD8 + seem to be significant in PD, as their depletion in a mouse model reduced the neurodegeneration and motor deficits [125] and their infiltration promoted pathological changes [126]. Furthermore, lymphocyte counts correlate negatively with the PD stage assessed with the Hoehn and Yahr scale [127]. On the other hand, genetic lymphocyte deficiency also has detrimental effects [128], and a lower serum lymphocyte count correlates with lower CSF total α-syn levels [127]. The number of lymphocytes and their function change during the course of the disease, as in AD. Immunotherapy agents in PD are currently in trial, but most of them influence T regulatory cells. Granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), an inflammatory modulator, was tested both in animal models and in clinical trials in PD as a therapeutic factor. Its role is associated with, among others, inducing T regulatory lymphocytes and preventing T cell activation. The use of anti-CD3 antibodies also leads to immunological tolerance by induction of Tregs and apoptosis or anergy in activated T cells (besides Tregs). Lastly, vasoactive intestinal peptide also increases Treg number and function, simultaneously suppressing microglial activation and reducing neuronal degeneration [115]. Altogether, evidence indicates that T lymphocytes play a role in PD development and that the balance between the cells seems crucial. Moreover, future therapeutic prospects related to immune cells should be considered.

In PD, B lymphocyte infiltration into the CNS is not confirmed [110], but their number in the peripheral blood is reduced [111], particularly considering naïve cells [129]. Similarly to T lymphocytes, regulatory B cells seem to have a protective role, as their higher number correlates with better motor functioning [130]. Moreover, the same pro-inflammatory bias is detected in PD B cells as in T cells [120, 129]. When it comes to α-syn antibodies in serum, we know they are elevated in early PD, but it remains unclear whether they take part in the clearance of pathological proteins and so have a positive effect or are deleterious [131, 132].

Studies on NK cells show their higher percentage in PD patients’ serum and their possible protective role. Those cells have been shown to degrade α-syn aggregates, and their depletion exacerbates synuclein pathology and motor dysfunction in a PD mouse model [133].

The influence of neutrophils on the pathogenetic pathways in PD has also been postulated [85], but so far, these are single, ambiguous reports. However, NLR was assessed in PD patients, where it is significantly higher than in healthy controls [134, 135] and correlates with the concentration of C-reactive protein [136]. That may be especially visible in patients with late-stage PD, where, as in AD, the number of lymphocytes is reduced. NLR also appears to be related to symptom severity [137] and even to degeneration of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic system as assessed by striatal dopamine transporter density (DAT-SCAN) [138]; however, no differences were found between subtypes of the disease [139]. Moreover, a greater NLR also increases the risk of developing PD in the future [140, 141], probably in the same mechanism as in AD, when a greater number of CD4 + cells exacerbate the risk of the disease. Research on NLR in AD could suggest that NLR could also be used in PD to predict future cognitive deficits or even disease progression and mortality. However, interestingly, in patients with additionally diagnosed cognitive disorders, NLR turned out to be lower [142]. Studies on atypical parkinsonism found abnormalities as well. In multiple system atrophy, higher index was associated with shorter patient survival [143]; it was also higher in patients with progressive supranuclear palsy [144].

As concluded from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), there is a common genetic pathway between PD and inflammatory bowel disease [145], which supports the gut-brain immune interaction hypothesis. According to it, changes in the gut microbiome can increase infiltration through the gut barrier, leading to the penetration of bacterial toxins into the systemic circulation [146]. Local bowel inflammation promotes synucleinopathy in enteric neurons, which can propagate via the vagus nerve to the CNS [147]. Also, a systemic inflammatory response can translate into CNS pathology. Accordingly, PD patients indeed show persisting bowel inflammation when compared to healthy individuals [148]. In murine models, overexpression of human a-syn leads to aggregations of the protein both in the gut and the brain and also to the development of PD symptoms. On the contrary, when these mice are bred in a germ-free environment or treated with antibiotics, they do not show signs of irregularities in their brains or signs of PD symptoms [149]. The number of lymphocytes (in particular elevated levels of CD4 +) and NLR in PD also suggest systemic inflammation as an important factor, possibly linking the intensification of immune response to disease severity.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) and Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD)

The role of most T cells in ALS appears to be rather positive, but it depends largely on their subpopulation. In 1992, autopsies revealed the accumulation of T lymphocytes and reactive forms of microglia in the anterior horns of ALS patients [150]. In 2008, it was proven that, in mice with the SOD1 mutation, CD4 + lymphocytes have a neuroprotective function—they increase the preservative potential and weaken the toxic reactions of microglia, promoting its transition to the anti-inflammatory state [151]. In subsequent works by the same authors [152, 153], the issue was broadened. It was found that the number of endogenous regulatory T cells in the mouse spinal cord increases in the early, slowly progressing stages, when the level of interleukin-4 expression is high and the protective function of microglia is stimulated, but when the disease accelerates rapidly and the pro-inflammatory phenotype dominates, it decreases in favor of Th1 cells. Also in humans, the level of regulatory T cells was inversely correlated with the rate of disease progression. The count of these cells in the blood is also lower than in healthy controls [154]. Moreover, the transfer of wild-type CD4 + cells to ALS mice lacking functional T lymphocytes prolonged the disease duration and survival, but only in the initial phase of the illness. The genetic removal of CD4 + T cells had the opposite, harmful effect of accelerating the progression of the disease [151, 153, 155]. Unfortunately, studies regarding the number of CD4 + cells remain inconclusive. In some of them, an increase in their number is found in the blood [156, 157] and the spinal cord of patients [158], and in others, above all, there is no difference between the groups [159]. Mostly, however, there is a downward trend, which is additionally associated with a faster progression of symptoms and a greater risk of cognitive disorders [160–162]. In another paper, an adverse effect of low lymphocyte counts on prognosis was found in women, but not in men. Moreover, their impact on cognitive disorders was revealed only in the group of patients over 75 years of age [163]. CD4 + cell-related neuroprotection in ALS is likely due to their interaction with microglia and astrocytes. The risk of developing ALS in correspondence with CD4 + levels has never been assessed, which could be a prospect for future research. CD8 + cell counts are more often reported to be higher in ALS than in healthy controls [157, 158, 164], but have also sometimes been found to be lower [156] or the same [159]. This probably depends largely on the stage of the disease. In more detailed studies examining the impact of different lymphocyte subpopulations, CD4 + cells had different effects depending on the subtype—Th1 and Th17 were unfavorable, Th2 and T regulatory were protective, while CD8 + was harmful and positively associated with the risk of death [165–167]. Furthermore, as proven by Beers et al. [107], the lymphocytes themselves may be dysfunctional. Regulatory T cells of both, slowly and rapidly progressing, ALS patients showed reduced ability to suppress the proliferation of responding T cells compared to healthy controls. Interestingly, these cells regained their normal function after being removed from the diseased organism and multiplied in vitro [168, 169]. Attempts have been made to administer regulatory T cells to mice and humans with ALS (sometimes together with interleukin 2), obtaining significantly longer survival, but above all proving the safety of such an action, which allows further research on larger groups [170, 171].

The number of NK cells also increases in the peripheral blood of ALS patients [172, 173]. In the study by Zhao et al. [153], the same thing happened with their activity in the spinal cord of SOD1 mutant mice. After artificially reducing this activity, the rodents survived longer and lost fewer motor neurons, which researchers associated with a possible suppressive effect on regulatory lymphocytes. Murdock et al. observed this effect only in female mice [174]. On the contrary, a higher NK cell count was associated with a lower risk of death [166]. Furthermore, it seems that the risk of developing ALS in the future depends on their subtype—with a higher expression level of CD16- CD56 + cells (immunoregulating type), the risk is lower [175]. B lymphocytes, on the other hand, do not seem to play a major role in ALS [176]. The lack of mature B cells in SOD1 mice did not alter ALS progression [177]. Studies on lymphocytes are difficult to compare—some cover the entire group, others cover B, T, or NK cells, while only a few divide them according to antigens (markers) of cellular differentiation (CD). Therefore, attempts were made to profile patients according to lymphocytic CD classes, where it turned out that the distinguished profiles with clearly recognizable immunological differences also differ in clinical features, including survival [157, 166, 178].

In an interesting study linking ALS to leukocytes, mice with induced overexpression of TDP-43 (one of the genes associated with the pathogenesis of the disease) were observed, and a significant increase in the infiltration of CD3 + and CD4 + T cells into the CNS was found. When rodents were additionally exposed to lipopolysaccharide, designed to simulate low-grade systemic inflammation, neutrophil infiltration also increased significantly. This could suggest that the action of TDP-43, which leads to the pathogenetic pathways of ALS, is related to the permeability of the BBB [179]. However, this protein dysfunction occurs in several different CNS pathologies, e.g., AD, so there must be an additional factor determining the development of a given sickness. Moreover, one study stated that patients with the C9orf72C9 mutation demonstrate an altered immunophenotype, which does not have the ability to suppress inflammation [180].

In a paper by Trias et al. [181] considering the results of autopsy samples of the quadriceps muscles in ALS patients, it turned out that intramuscular neutrophils were numerous in study group, but completely absent in the control group, and their number correlated with the number of mast cells. These granulocytes made direct contact with muscle fibers to form multicellular aggregates—interacted with mast cells and generated large NETs, leading to the denervation of neuromuscular junctions and muscle atrophy. In the same study, rats with the SOD1 mutation were also examined. The researchers detected aggregates of both types of cells around their motor axons in the extensor digitorum longus muscle, sciatic nerve, and ventral roots. This may indicate that immune cell infiltration extends along the entire peripheral motor pathway. Moreover, the number of neutrophils increased several fold between the onset of motor disorder and advanced paralysis. Administration of a tyrosine kinase inhibitor to such rats reduced the infiltration of mast cells and neutrophils into the sciatic nerve and prevented muscle atrophy [181]. Another paper suggested the possibility of neutrophils participating in repair processes [182]. In several different studies, increased levels of these cells and, less frequently, their ratio to agranulocytes were found in the peripheral blood of ALS patients, which correlated with the speed of disease progression, but also with worse respiratory efficiency or hypermetabolism [160, 163, 183–187]. Moreover, a higher neutrophil count at diagnosis was associated with shorter survival, which was especially significant in women [163, 188, 189]. Gender discrepancy can be explained by various mechanisms, e.g., the influence of sex hormones on the activity of neutrophils and cytokines (immunomodulatory effect) or the variability of the immune system [85]. Only one study, quite unusual because it is based on Mendel’s randomization method, found that a genetically determined increased number of neutrophils were associated with a reduced risk of ALS [190]. As it turned out, various markers demonstrated on the surface of immune cells themselves are associated with different features of the disease. For example, higher CD16 expression on murine neutrophils, indicating high activity of these cells, is related to greater disease severity and faster rate of progression [191]. In the search for ALS biomarkers, genetic profiling of the blood was also performed, which pointed to low-grade neutrophilia as a possible useful indicator, absent in mimetic diseases [184]. Additionally, higher levels of neutrophils and NLR were associated with a higher risk of developing ALS in the future [192]. Other works focused specifically on the NLR rate in ALS. In a prospective cohort study from 2008, low-grade systemic inflammation, defined as an increased number of leukocytes and NLR, was discovered [193]. In the paper by Leone et al. [194], higher NLR values were correlated with a faster rate of disease progression and higher mortality—the cut-off point was 2.315, beyond which the mortality rate was twice as high. Similarly, Wei et al. [195], Grassano et al. [163], and Choi et al. [196] found an association of higher NLR with respiratory failure, faster progression, and shorter survival of patients, although they also noted the older age of onset in these groups. As the NLR is higher in most of mentioned neurodegenerative disorders, it could also be implied that it can be used to predict other factors influencing ALS, e.g., cognitive functions or imaging parameters describing the brain (atrophy in particular). However, it must be taken into account that NLR itself may increase with age, for example in the course of atherosclerotic plaque accumulation or vessel disease and many other disorders more common in older individuals.

ALS is connected to another neurodegenerative disease, frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Researchers nowadays treat them as one pathology (ubiquitinated neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions positive for TDP-43), but with symptoms on different ends of a spectrum. Ultimately, 50% of ALS patients develop frontal lobe dysfunction [197]. When it comes to white blood cells, studies also show similar results. Peripheral immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes, also infiltrate into the brain of FTD patients [198]. In a murine model, CD8 + cytotoxic cells, in particular, were found in the affected hippocampi, with much lower levels of CD4 + [199]. The serum of FTD patients shows a decrease in B and T cell populations, which, as Busse et al. [40] conclude, may lead to increased activation of the remaining cell populations. Moreover, a decrease in the number of CD4 + lymphocytes expressing CTLA-4 (an inhibitory receptor) specifically was found [200]. On the other hand, a significantly increased expression in genes associated with adaptive immune cells, including CD4 + and CD8 +, was found in FTD, while the expression of genes associated with neutrophils was decreased [201]. We found only one human study on neutrophils in FTD, where their median count was lower than healthy controls and also patients with other dementia types, but the difference was not statistically significant [202]. In a murine model, however, TDP-43 overexpression promoted neutrophil infiltration in the brain parenchyma [203]. A limited number of studies regarding the role of adaptive and innate immunity in tauopathies remain to be further investigated [204].

Other Diseases

In Huntington’s disease (HD), researchers also observe neuroinflammation, particularly in a form of a microglial activation, but, as huntingtin protein is expressed in peripheral immune cells, possible inflammatory changes in plasma can be significant [205]. Among others, B cells, T cells, and monocytes show detectable levels of the huntingtin protein, which are positively correlated with disease status [206]. Differently than in previously mentioned disorders, leukocyte influx into CNS (striatum, cortex, and cerebellum) is not increased in HD in postmortem studies. As a result, the major culprits seem to be microglia and neurons themselves [207, 208]. On the other hand, one study shows shifts in CD4 + and CD8 + cell populations in the CNS [209]. However, what is currently under more consideration is not the count of leukocytes, but their properties—DNA damage, which is greater in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells in patients than in healthy controls; leukocyte telomere length, which is shorter in HD [210]; and micro-RNA-9, which is downregulated in HD leukocytes [211]. The function of T lymphocytes themselves does not appear to be affected [212]. Studies consider monocytes as a bigger contribution to the immune dysregulation in HD. The depletion of monocytes and macrophages revealed their major role in the chronic inflammatory environment observed during the course of the disease. This could lead to immunomodulatory strategies targeting monocytes and macrophages being more relevant [206]. Neutrophils are not widely studied in HD; however in mouse models, sick individuals had 60% fewer infiltrating neutrophils in the peritoneum than wild-type mice, accompanied with impaired leukocyte recruitment and migration [213].

Although autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is not a degenerative (as we know of), but more a neurodevelopmental disorder, interesting dysregulations in innate and adaptive immunity were also implicated in its pathogenesis [214]. A decreased number of lymphocytes with an imbalanced ratio of helper/regulatory cells and lowered response to mitogens were found in children with ASD. Moreover, a significant increase in CD4 + memory and decrease in CD4 + naïve T cells was observed [215], as well as an imbalance of cytokines with skewing toward anti-inflammatory response. Other studies showed a lower number of Tregs in the ASD patients’ serum. Furthermore, NK cell activity was reduced, although their count was higher [216]. In ASD, neutrophils are activated and may cause increased oxidative stress in a response to environmental factors [85].

Leukocytes seem to have important pathological significance in other neurological (but not primary neurodegenerative) illnesses including also: traumatic brain injury and ischemic stroke (infiltration of peripheral immune cells after both [217, 218]), encephalopathies, myasthenia gravis and autoimmune neuropathies (particularly B cell involvement associated with antibody production) [85, 219].

Conclusions

Most studies postulate that the path to the development of neurodegeneration starts with a dysregulation of the delicate balance between cell phenotypes and their interactions. It seems that the regulation of immune response is disturbed, not allowing for the normal removal of pathological factors (such as amyloid beta) and, therefore, not controlling the inflammation. The common factor for neurodegenerative diseases seems to be leukocyte infiltration into the CNS (through a damaged BBB) and an increased number of pro-inflammatory Th1, Th17 cells, and neutrophils with a decrease (quantitative or functional) of T regulatory cells. Levels of the latter cells, in particular, which are increased only in the early phases and then decrease during the course of disease, suggest exhaustion of immune mechanisms. That is also associated with age, which has a negative effect on immune responses and increases disease susceptibility. Furthermore, aging itself is connected to low-grade inflammatory status, microglial activation, deteriorated remyelination, and decreased number of neurons and cortical volume; additionally impaired phagocytosis causes excessive accumulation of neuron deposits [220]. The visible onset of neuroinflammation before neurodegeneration in neurons might indicate a primary dysfunction on the part of the immune system. This hypothesis is also supported by the fact that every organism forms neurodegenerative deposits and autoreactive lymphocytes in a small amount, but in healthy individuals, they are easily removed. Since age is a risk factor in most of the disorders discussed, and the concept of immunosenescence is proven, we might suspect a correlation here too. The increased detection of autoimmune diseases today is a consequence of many factors, e.g., better access to medicine and better treatments. However, it may also be a result of a greater number of changing environmental risk factors, which would also suggest a greater role of acquired (which can adapt to changing conditions), more than innate immunity. As mentioned before, lymphocytes appear before the degeneration occurs, while neutrophils appear before the clinical symptoms appear, i.e., much later. Stimulation of innate immunity seems to worsen the pathology (non-specifically), not cause it.

Therefore, in the context of lymphocytes, this could imply either excessive activity of T helpers or reduced activity of regulatory T cells. As the latter are only ones having mostly positive influence on degenerative neurons (Fig. 1), it may seem that their role is primarily disturbed (although not necessarily their number). In studies on ALS, as mentioned, the impaired function of regulatory lymphocytes improved after they were multiplicated in vitro, which could also suggest that the extracellular environment in the disease might contain some kind of an additional factor inhibiting their activity or might lack a stimulating substance. As for B cells, NK cells, and neutrophils, we currently have too little data to determine whether they have a causal role; however, their depletion could also have a beneficial symptomatic effect.

The immune cells interact with microglia and promote local inflammation in the CNS. However, we do not know if the immune irregularities themselves are the cause of neurodegeneration. Further research is needed to conclude whether they might be just one of many factors involved in pathogenic pathways or maybe only function incorrectly in a toxic environment. It is noteworthy that it is not the quantity of leukocytes that should be assessed, but their changing function, especially considering total CD4 +, which subtypes play a very different role in neurodegeneration. White blood cells are definitely impaired in neurodegenerative diseases. However, their changes (as we know them now) are not specific and cannot be used as a much-needed diagnostic marker. On the other hand, as we know that the infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the CNS is mostly detrimental to neurons, we may continue to develop solutions to benefit the patients by modifying their actions, even though we do not know the exact mechanism of pathology. Immunotherapy is common in MS, but still in a very early phase when it comes to other neurodegenerative disorders. Further studies are needed to establish changes in peripheral immunity throughout the course of the diseases to achieve improvement without side effects.
