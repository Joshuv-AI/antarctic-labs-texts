Reframing Value in Low-Risk Aortic Stenosis
Authors: Jae-Sik Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Over the past decade, the management of severe aortic stenosis has changed remarkably. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), once reserved for patients who were inoperable or at high surgical risk, is now routinely considered even in low-risk populations. Randomized trials have shown that TAVI achieves clinical outcomes comparable to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) in these patients.1, 2, 3, 4 As a result, the discussion has naturally moved beyond whether TAVI works, toward a more nuanced question of whether it truly represents good value, particularly in younger patients with longer life expectancy.

Against this background, the study by Kawakami et al5 is both timely and important. What sets this analysis apart is the choice of comparator. Rather than comparing TAVI with conventional full-sternotomy SAVR, the authors focus on minimally invasive cardiac surgery–aortic valve replacement (MICS-AVR), which arguably better reflects current surgical practice. This is not a trivial distinction. Minimally invasive approaches have steadily improved over time, with less surgical trauma, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery. As these techniques mature, the clinical gap between surgery and TAVI has become smaller than it once was.6 Evaluating TAVI against this evolving standard therefore provides a more realistic picture of its value in contemporary practice.

Using a decision-analytic model with a 10-year time horizon, in this issue of JACC: Asia, Kawakami et al5 show that TAVI is associated with higher overall costs and only modest gains in quality-adjusted life years. The resulting incremental cost-effectiveness ratio sits close to the commonly accepted willingness-to-pay threshold in Japan.5 In practical terms, TAVI appears to be borderline cost-effective under base-case assumptions. Perhaps more importantly, when surgical outcomes are modeled to reflect high-volume centers, MICS-AVR becomes both less costly and slightly more effective.5 This shift in results is quite revealing. It reminds us that cost-effectiveness is not something inherent to TAVI itself but rather something that depends strongly on how well the alternative strategy performs.

One of the strengths of this study is precisely this reframing of the comparator. For many years, the economic case for TAVI was built on comparisons with conventional surgery. However, surgical practice has not stood still. Minimally invasive techniques are now widely used and continue to improve.6 When the comparator changes, the conclusions about value may also change. This is particularly relevant in low-risk patients, where absolute differences in clinical outcomes are relatively small and economic considerations inevitably carry more weight.

The study also offers useful insight into what actually drives cost-effectiveness. Not surprisingly, procedural costs play a major role. However, the analysis highlights that long-term quality of life after the procedure is even more influential.5 Small differences in utility can translate into meaningful changes in the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio, a well-recognized feature of economic modeling.7,8 This is an important point, and one that is often underappreciated. Ultimately, the value of a therapy depends not only on how patients do early after the procedure but on how they live in the years that follow.

At the same time, there are several points that deserve careful interpretation. As with any decision-analytic model, the results depend on a number of assumptions. For example, the model treats long-term outcomes as largely independent of early perioperative events. In clinical practice, this is unlikely to be entirely true. Complications such as stroke or heart failure can have lasting consequences, and these downstream effects are difficult to fully capture within simplified modeling structures.

Another area of uncertainty relates to quality-of-life estimates. The model assumes relatively stable utility beyond the first year, reflecting the limited availability of long-term data in contemporary low-risk cohorts. In reality, differences in functional recovery, symptom burden, or late complications may become more apparent over time. These nuances are not easily incorporated into current models, but they may still influence long-term value.

Perhaps the most important unresolved issue is valve durability. Although current data on TAVI are reassuring in the mid-term, long-term durability beyond 10 years remains less certain. Even small differences in structural valve deterioration could have a meaningful impact on lifetime outcomes and, by extension, cost-effectiveness, particularly in younger patients.9,10 The 10-year horizon used in this analysis is reasonable given current evidence, but it inevitably leaves some of these longer-term questions unanswered.

The findings also draw attention to the role of institutional experience. The observation that MICS-AVR performs particularly well in high-volume centers is consistent with what has been seen across many areas of cardiovascular care.5 Outcomes are closely linked to operator experience and system-level factors. From this perspective, the comparison between TAVI and surgery is not purely about devices or techniques but also about how care is delivered in different settings.

Taken together, these considerations have important implications for clinical practice and policy. In low-risk patients, both TAVI and MICS-AVR are reasonable options, and the choice between them should remain individualized. At the same time, economic considerations are becoming increasingly relevant. The variability in cost-effectiveness seen in this study suggests that a single, uniform approach to reimbursement may not fully capture differences in real-world value. A more flexible framework, one that takes into account institutional performance and patient characteristics, may ultimately be needed.8

Looking ahead, several factors are likely to influence how this balance evolves. Advances in TAVI technology may improve durability and reduce complications. Device costs may also decrease over time, which would favor TAVI from an economic standpoint. Meanwhile, continued refinement of minimally invasive surgical techniques will likely maintain, and perhaps even strengthen, the role of surgery.

Equally important will be the availability of longer-term data. As TAVI is increasingly used in younger patients, extended follow-up from trials and real-world registries will be essential. Better data on durability, repeat interventions, and patient-reported outcomes will help refine future economic evaluations.

Future research should focus on defining patient-specific, lifetime value through long-term data, improved durability assessment, and integration of patient-centered outcomes. Cost-effectiveness is only one part of this decision, but it is an increasingly important one.

In summary, the study from Kawakami et al5 provides a thoughtful and clinically relevant assessment of TAVI in low-risk aortic stenosis using a more contemporary comparator. Their findings suggest that when evaluated against minimally invasive surgical approaches, the economic advantage of TAVI is less clear and highly dependent on context. This work highlights the need to move beyond simple comparisons of efficacy and toward a broader understanding of value in modern cardiovascular care.
