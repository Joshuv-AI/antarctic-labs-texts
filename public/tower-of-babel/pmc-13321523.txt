How social influence and innovativeness shape technology perceptions and usage intentions: a TAM extension
Authors: Muhammad Asif, Zhen Shao, Muhammad Naeem Sharif, Tianhui Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the factors influencing the adoption of mobile banking applications (MBAs) in Pakistan’s banking sector, using an extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). Extending TAM is important here because it helps account for the unique socio-cultural and personality factors that greatly impact technology adoption in developing countries like Pakistan. We specifically investigate the mediating roles of perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) in the relationships between consumer susceptibility to interpersonal influence (CSII) and MBAs usage. Additionally, we explore the moderating effect of Personal Innovativeness (PI) within our model. Data was collected through an online survey of 268 Pakistani banking customers with prior mobile banking experience. Our analysis was performed using Smart PLS 4. The findings reveal that CSII significantly influences both PU and PEU. Furthermore, PU and PEU are key predictors of behavioural intention to use (BIU) mobile banking apps. The study also confirms that PU and PEU play mediating roles between CSII and MBAs adoption. Notably, PI moderates the relationship between CSII and PEU, indicating that individuals with higher innovativeness perceive MBAs as easier to use when influenced by their social circles. However, PI does not impact the relationships between CSII-PU and CSII-MBAs. These insights are particularly valuable for banks and financial institutions in Pakistan, enabling them to develop targeted strategies that promote mobile banking adoption by focusing on social impact, consumer perceptions, and personal innovation. The observation also extends the present TAM literature by confirming its applicability in a developing country context and highlighting the importance of individuals’ innovativeness.Keywords: Mobile banking applications, Technology acceptance model, Consumer susceptibility to interpersonal influence, Perceived usefulness, Perceived ease of use, Personal innovativeness

Introduction

The rapid growth of mobile technologies has deeply transformed many sectors, especially the banking industry, which exemplifies this significant change [24, 88]. Mobile banking applications (MBAs) have become vital interfaces between banks and customers, providing unmatched convenience and accessibility for a wide range of financial transactions [82]. This digital shift is increasingly critical, as MBAs are now the main channel for customer interaction globally [80]. By 2025, it is projected that over 2.8 billion people worldwide will use MBAs. This trend is driven by consumer demands for quick, secure, and seamless financial services that eliminate the need for physical bank visits. In Pakistan, where mobile phone penetration is high, mobile banking holds great promise, indicating a favourable outlook for the industry.

This global shift is primarily relevant in emerging economies like Pakistan, where a high mobile phone penetration rate presents an extensive opportunity for financial inclusion and the digitization of financial services [40]. Pakistan’s fintech sector has shown considerable growth, marked by a significant rise in mobile wallets, Raast, and asaan mobile account (AMA) platforms [67]. Nevertheless, despite widespread access to smartphones and mobile internet, the usage of MBAs among Pakistanis has not yet reached its full potential. Recent statistics indicate that while a large segment of the population uses mobile phones, the number of registered mobile banking users remains relatively low [12, 92]. Challenges include a significant portion of the population still relying on cash, as well as a persistent digital divide, with nearly half the population lacking internet access [67]. This contradiction highlights the need to investigate the factors that influence consumers’ decisions to use MBAs in Pakistan. These factors help banks and information systems (IS) developers to evolve their strategies that enhance adoption and encourage user engagement.

The study addresses the slow adoption and usage of MBAs in Pakistan, where mobile phone penetration is high, but yet not many consumers switch to this alternative channel for their digital financial services [40]. Although some other studies have been conducted to study technology adoption, each society has its own social, economic, and cultural values, which means that the factors that influence for MBAs in Pakistani societies would be different from those in other countries [44, 65]. The setting of this environment is very intricate because the social and economic aspects play a major role in technological acceptance. The existing literature has mainly focused on constructs such as PU and PEU while downplaying the social influences and personality traits that may shape adoption in collectivist societies [11, 35]. Thus, this study investigates in what way CSII impacts the adoption of MBAs, with PU and PEU as mediators and PI as a moderator. However, despite a growing body of TAM-based studies, few have systematically combined social influence and personality factors to explain mobile-banking adoption, particularly in developing countries. This conceptual gap underscores the need to understand how interpersonal influence and PI jointly shape behavioural intention in contexts such as Pakistan, where collectivist values and technological inequalities coexist. By addressing this gap, the present research contributes theoretically by extending TAM with social-psychological constructs and empirically by offering evidence from an under-explored emerging economy.

The research propositions of this study are fourfold: first, to investigate how CSII directly and indirectly influences MBAs adoption intention. Secondly, we explore the roles of PU and PEU as potential mediating variables in the relationships between CSII and MBAs. Third, this study will examine the moderation effect of PI on the relationships between the CSII and TAM computing constructs (PU and PEU) as well as their influence on MBAs. Ultimately, this study will reveal major determinants behind the use of MBAs in Pakistan, which may be used as a foundation for more research regarding the use of MBAs within the banking industry.

Theoretical framework: extension of the technology acceptance model

This study draws on the technology acceptance model (TAM) [27], which is probably the most commonly used theoretical framework to explain factors influencing the adoption and implementation of information technology. According to the original TAM, factors such as PEU and PU determine an individual’s intention to use a certain technology, which will then predict the actual behaviours of usage [9, 28]. Its reliability has been established across various technologies and settings [61]. This study additionally argues that it extends the scope of the TAM framework by including those constructs which help to understand mobile banking acceptance in Pakistan. Hence, there is a necessity to enhance basic models (TAM) by accounting for digital technologies and cultural context to increase the accuracy of predictions and ensure the relevance of outcomes [61]. This holds specifically true for emerging markets, like Pakistan, where social bonds and group opinions are the foremost influencers in personal choices [72], and peer effect and social networks play a dominant role in technology adoption [57]. Therefore, the CSII is integrated into TAMs because it helps people acquire new technology [30].

We also include PI as a moderator, since it may capture personal variance in the propensity to experiment with new technologies. [5] state that PI is predicated upon an individual’s readiness to experience new information technologies and their intentions to be early adopters of innovations. By introducing PI, it helps us in understanding how individual characteristics may affect the interaction with external and perceived traits in adopting new technologies [5]. The main outcome that we are interested in is the use of MBAs, and this is operationalized using the behavioural intention to use (BIU) such applications.

This expanded TAM framework provides a more comprehensive view of the factors affecting mobile banking adoption. It expands upon the core TAM components to include social influence and individual innovativeness. This is in line with some recent studies arguing to tailor and broaden the existing theories, such as TAM and UTAUT2, to new technologies and contexts since they were evolving appreciably, for instance, various studies on mobile banking adoption in Pakistan and other emerging economies [53, 66]. Various studies showed that extended TAM models hold valuable insights for the adoption of IT into developing economies, ranging from mobile banking in Jordan [18], digital banking in Cyprus [29], to artificial intelligence in e-commerce [84] and smart transportation [16]. The model incorporates several psychological and social factors into the traditional technology acceptance constructs to more comprehensively predict how users will interact with technology.

The prior literature and discussion indicate that TAM expansion represents an evolving perspective: technology acceptance is no longer seen solely as a mental process but is also influenced by social and personal contexts. This more extensive sight points out that researchers should not restrict their examination to the absolute values of technology features such as PU and PEU, but also consider the social context (CSII) and personal traits of users (PI).

Literature review and hypothesis development

Consumer susceptibility to interpersonal influence (CSII) and technology adoption

CSII is a type of social influence that describes the extent to which an individual’s attitudes and opinions are influenced by perceived expectations and motivation from significant others such as family, friends, and peers [19]. CSII is critical to technology adoption, especially in cultures that strongly depend on collective decision-making and social conformity [64]. More recently, work has confirmed the role of social influence in technology adoption, indicating that users are more likely to learn from successful peers and from others sharing positive experiences [57]. This indicates that social influence is a significant antecedent of the adoption intentions toward new technologies [81]. Likewise, studies by [10] and [40] have shown that digital trust, usable security, effort expectancy and peer recommendations jointly shape intention to adopt mobile and meta-banking services.

For instance, research on the factors that determine the mobile banking adoption in Pakistan using the UTAUT2 model presented social influence as an important predictor of behavioral intention [1, 41]. Likewise, [50] and [55] had also found social influence as a significant sign for the adoption of mobile banking in Pakistan. This suggests that the more people feel normative social pressure, the higher the probability that they adopt mobile banking [57]. However, recent evidence from emerging fintech contexts explained by [87], found social influence to be insignificant in enhancing perceived value in their meta-banking study. These findings reveal that the predictive power of social factors can vary across digital-banking environments, suggesting that cultural and contextual elements moderate their impact. This ambiguity underscores the necessity of the present study, which focuses specifically on the advanced role of the CSII, a construct that captures whether influences are informational (forming views based on others) or normative (pressure to conform). This distinction may be particularly pronounced in Pakistan, where collective perceptions and social context are a bigger part of the decision-making process. As a result, it is expected that higher levels of CSII are associated with higher behavioural intentions to use MBAs.

In addition, the present study posits that CSII, as an external variable, will influence an individual’s perceptions regarding the usefulness of MBAs. It is worth noting that CSII and PU are associated with each other in a variety of contexts as well, where numerous studies emphasized the direct effect of CSII on PU. For example, [83] found that subjective norms significantly influence PU of technology in voluntary settings. Likewise, other studies have found that PU is significantly influenced by social influence on mobile banking decisions [70]. When individuals receive positive recommendations or observe others benefiting from mobile banking, their perceptions of usefulness are likely to be strengthened [48]. This claim can also be explained with respect to social learning theory, postulating that people adopt beliefs and attitudes by learning from experience or from the opinions of others [39]. Recently, [68] demonstrated that social norms have a positive influence on PU with regard to technology adoption. This pathway suggests that social influence operates by shaping individuals’ cognitive assessments of the utility of the technology.

Similarly, CSII and PEU are also key research areas, with CSII likely influencing individuals’ perceptions of MBA usability. PEU is naturally an intrinsic factor related to technology use, and social influence can play an important role in shaping this perception. However, Studies on technology adoption have shown that social influence can be a more significant determinant of PEU [20, 31]. For example, in the study of [60] on mobile commerce, where social influence was shown to have a significant direct and positive relationship with usage, whilst PEU had an insignificant direct and positive impact. Alternatively, if people see others using mobile banking effortlessly or hear positive feedback on its simplicity, their perception of PEU will be positive [48]. This supports the idea that social influence, or subjective norms, can positively impact PEU. Therefore, we suggested that social influence can reduce perceived obstacles to adoption by shaping beliefs about the ease of using the technology.

Perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) as antecedents of behavioral intention

Technology adoption is consistently influenced by PU and PEU [8, 74]. PU is the intention of a person who may think that the new system allows them to work better or faster on their work [10]. From a mobile banking perspective, this interprets the convenience of conducting transactions anytime and anywhere to improve financial management [23, 89]. Several studies have reported the predictive power of PU in the adoption of new technology [10] and that higher levels of usage are associated with a favorable attitude toward acceptance [23]. However, PEU refers to the perceived belief that minimum effort will be required of a user in using the system [10]. In the context of MBAs, this relates to the user friendliness, ease of navigation, and clear processes laid out in the app [3]. Recent indications still emphasize the importance of PEU, as it has been found to increase significantly the level of user confidence about using a technology, which is gradually transformed from a source of competitive advantage into a basic user expectation [23, 47].

The impact of PU and PEU on the adoption of mobile banking services is well evidenced by a number of recent studies [10, 21, 77]. For example, research conducted in Kathmandu metropolitan city has revealed that both PU and PEU have a significant impact on mobile banking adoption: in particular is the case among lower-income segments [4]. This consistent evidence from various TAM literature for different technologies and contexts [6, 71] would support the notion that users are more likely to adopt a technology that they prefer if it is perceived as useful and easy to use. Moreover, recent studies on AI-driven financial tools confirm that PU and PEU remain the strongest cognitive predictors of adoption. [90] demonstrated that PEU enhances continuous usage intention of intelligent services, while [68] verified PU as the most powerful determinant of engagement in smart-payment platforms. These results extend TAM validity to newer fintech environments. Based on these results, it is hypothesized that PU and PEU will have a positive direct impact on MBAs adoption.

Perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) as mediators

The direct effects call for the mediating role of PU and PEU in the relationship of CSII with MBAs, as suggested by this study. It is the critical mediation pathway that helps to better understand psychological construction in technology acceptance because external social factors frequently function in combination with an individual’s cognitive beliefs to indirectly influence behavior [85]. The ultimate premise of the TAM is that the impacts of numerous external variables on behavioral intention are largely, if not entirely, mediated by the key cognitive constructs of PU and PEU [7, 28]. This underscores a chain of interconnection where social influence serves as an initial trigger that shapes a user’s core perceptions of a technology.

The information received via our social circle, such as peers or family, is processed cognitively by people who are susceptible to interpersonal influence. This cognitive processing changes our PU and PEU [22, 90], playing a vital role in the adoption process. For example, hearing friends discuss the convenience of paying bills through an app increases its PU, while observing a family member navigate the app effortlessly boosts its PEU. Once the individual understands and internalizes these improved concepts, those insights become direct actions that motivate them to own those same technological innovations.

This indirect mechanism is supported by recent research results. For instance, studies in the fintech domain show that social norms and influence do not directly drive adoption, but shape their PU and PEU [9, 52, 81]. This process of internalization, where external social proof is converted into personal belief about the technology’s attributes, is the core of the mediation effect [13, 62]. Supporting this mechanism, [52] and [22] found that social cues indirectly influence technology-use intention through changes in perceived usefulness and ease of use, confirming PU and PEU as critical cognitive mediators linking external influence to behavioural outcomes in fintech adoption. For example, a research that is based on AI product adoption also found social influence to increase the purchase intention of users by changing their beliefs and perceptions towards the technologies, e.g., PU and PEU [90]. This indicates that social influence is not a direct “behavior driver,” but more as a “belief shaper” in the case of voluntary adoption.

The analysis of indirect effects in this study demonstrates that PU and PEU are the underlying mechanisms through which CSII affects the adoption of MBAs. Understanding these mediation effects is essential for creating effective marketing strategies. Such strategies should focus on using social networks not just to generate awareness but also to actively foster PU and PEU, as these are the main elements that directly affect adoption intention. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Personal innovativeness (PI) as a moderator

The current research in technology adoption emphasizes the significance of moderating variables in predicting user behaviour [91], making it crucial to identify key moderators that can impact information system adoption. PI is also an important determinant of individual behaviour that indicates a person’s willingness to explore new information technologies and their tendency to be an early adopter of innovations [5, 90]. Individuals with higher PI are more willing to accept new technologies and want to explore them, while at the same time, are less concerned about potential challenges [14]. They are also more likely to actively seek out information and accept higher risk levels associated with adopting new products [90]. PI can significantly influence how consumers perceive and respond to new banking applications

Prior studies confirmed innovativeness as a crucial predictor of intention to adopt MBAs. For example, a study conducted in Pakistan identified innovativeness as the most important factor in mobile banking usage intentions, indicating that innovative individuals tend to adopt such technologies [51]. Existing literature provides evidence of PI as an antecedent to whether individuals will use new technologies in multiple contexts, such as AI products and smart transportation [14, 17, 75]. For example, prior research has revealed that PI tends to strengthen the effect of social influence on PEU in AI product adoption [90]. Highly innovative individuals are more receptive to information about new technologies from their social circles and more motivated to act on such information, especially regarding practical use [14]. Individuals low in PI, however, might not be as susceptible to social pressure or suggestions, as their natural reluctance to try new things may overshadow external influences [58, 90]. While PI is a valued indicator to adopt new technologies, its moderating effect can be more or less pronounced depending on the specific affective links also identified in certain studies [14, 45].

These specific findings indicate that people are more likely to trust information about new technologies coming from their social networks and to act on it [2]. Recent cross-cultural research further demonstrates that PI moderates technology-adoption dynamics in emerging digital sectors. [14] highlighted that highly innovative users show stronger relationships between social influence and PEU in AI-product adoption, while [45] observed similar amplification effects in smart-service acceptance models. These findings substantiate the moderating role of innovativeness proposed in the present framework. Therefore, we hypothesize that the increases in MBAs use behavioral intention, PU, and PEU associated with the adoption of CSII will be higher among individuals who are more PI.

Hypothesized research model

This study’s hypothesized research model (Figure. 1) combines the core elements of the TAM with the additional constructs of CSII and PI. In this framework, CSII functions as the independent variable, directly impacting the adoption of MBAs. PU and PEU, derived from TAM, act as key mediators through which CSII influences BIU. It is hypothesized that CSII positively affects both PU and PEU, and that these, in turn, positively influence BIU. Additionally, PI is included as a moderating variable, expected to affect the strength of the relationships between CSII and BIU, CSII and PU, and CSII and PEU. The model implies that the effect of interpersonal influence on adoption intentions and its cognitive antecedents (PU and PEU) will depend on an individual’s inherent innovativeness. The primary dependent variable is the behavioural intention to use MBAs, serving as a proxy for actual adoption in TAM-based studies.

Methodology

Research design

Data is collected through a cross-sectional survey from Pakistani mobile banking users in this quantitative study. The research is descriptive and correlational, explaining the relationship between the independent variable (CSII), mediators (PU and PEU), moderator (PI), and dependent variable (behavioral intention to use MBAs). A survey method suits the hypotheses based on the extended TAM, enabling large sample data collection for statistical analysis. The structured questionnaire ensures data consistency and supports the use of Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). This technique is consistent with earlier findings of technological acceptance and effective for other factors for mobile banking among the customers in Pakistan [46, 54].

Participants and sampling

The study targeted active users of mobile-banking apps from major commercial banks in Pakistan, including both public and private institutions. Respondents were approached through online banking communities and university networks. We employed a non-probability convenience sampling technique to collect data from 327 respondents through an online Google Form. This method is commonly used in technology-adoption research conducted in developing-country contexts [7, 10]. Although convenience sampling limits generalizability, it enables efficient access to a heterogeneous population of real mobile-banking users.

Out of the total responses, 59 questionnaires were excluded because of missing data or wrong information. A total of 268 questionnaires were valid for further data analysis. An a priori G*Power test confirmed that this sample size was statistically adequate, achieving a power level of 0.80 at a medium effect size (f² = 0.15, p < 0.05) [32, 73]. This number comfortably exceeds the minimum threshold recommended for PLS-SEM models with six or more predictors [38, 56], ensuring robust estimation. Data collection spanned approximately 2.5 months. The demographic profile of the respondents indicated that 63% were males, 52% were in the age group of 18–24 years, 38% held a bachelor’s degree, and 48% had more than 1 year of experience in MBAs in Pakistan. Internal consistency, reliability, and convergent validity

Table 2 exhibits detailed information on the internal variance inflation factor (VIF), item loadings, Cronbach’s alpha (CA), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct and its respective items. All values of CA were above the recommended criterion of 0.70 [38], which indicates a good internal consistency for all constructs. Specifically, CSII had a CA of 0.867, PI scored 0.819, PU achieved 0.803, PEU recorded 0.834, and behavioral intention to use showed a CA of 0.746. The CR values, which are considered a more robust measure of internal consistency, also surpassed the 0.70 benchmark for all constructs [38]: CSII (0.904), PI (0.881), PU (0.871), PEU (0.883), and BIU (0.886).

Convergent validity was assessed using factor loadings and average variance extracted (AVE). All indicator loadings were above the recommended value of 0.708, indicating that the items adequately measured their respective constructs [38]. The AVE values for all constructs were also above the 0.50 threshold, further supporting convergent validity: CSII (0.653), PI (0.649), PU (0.629), PEU (0.601), and BIU (0.796). In addition, the VIF values for all items were well below the common cut-off of 3 (ranging from 1.479 to 2.326), suggesting no significant multicollinearity issues among the indicators [73].

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity assesses the extent to which a construct is distinct from other constructs in the model. This was evaluated using the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) and the Fornell-Larcker criterion.

Heterotrait-Monotrait ratios (HTMT)

Table 3 presents the HTMT ratios. For adequate discriminant validity, HTMT values should be below 0.85 (or 0.90 for conceptually similar constructs) [43]. All HTMT values are well below 0.85. For example, the HTMT between BIU and CSII is 0.452, between BIU and PEU is 0.657, and between PU and PEU is 0.654. The highest HTMT value observed is 0.807 (between BIU and PU), which is still below the more conservative threshold of 0.85. This suggests that the constructs are empirically distinct from each other.

Fornell and Larcker’s criterion

Table 4 presents the results for the Fornell-Larcker criterion. For adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE for each construct (diagonal values in bold) should be greater than its highest correlation with any other construct [33], off-diagonal values in the corresponding row and column. The square roots of AVEs are: BIU (0.892), CSII (0.808), PEU (0.776), PI (0.806), and PU (0.793). Examining the off-diagonal values, for instance, the correlation between BIU and CSII is 0.366, which is less than the square root of AVE for BIU (0.892) and CSII (0.808). This pattern holds for all constructs, satisfying the Fornell-Larcker criterion.

For the direct effects, Hypothesis 1 (H1), which proposed a direct positive effect of CSII on MBAs, was not supported (β = 0.068, t = 1.111, p = 0.133). However, CSII was found to have a significant positive influence on PU (H2: β = 0.387, t = 5.709, p < 0.001) and PEU (H3: β = 0.257, t = 4.266, p < 0.001), thus supporting H2 and H3. As expected, both PU (H4: β = 0.476, t = 7.118, p < 0.001) and PEU (H5: β = 0.220, t = 3.371, p < 0.001) demonstrated significant positive effects on MBAs, supporting H4 and H5. These findings align with the core tenets of the TAM, where PU and PEU are critical drivers of technology adoption.

The study also investigated indirect effects. Hypothesis H6, positing that PU mediates the relationship between CSII and MBAs, was supported (β = 0.184, t = 4.433, p < 0.001). Similarly, H7, which proposed that PEU mediates the CSII-MBAs link, was also supported (β = 0.057, t = 2.639, p = 0.004). These results suggest that while CSII does not directly influence MBAs, its impact is channeled through the mediating variables of PU and PEU.

Regarding the moderating effects of PI, H8, which proposed that PI moderates the CSII-MBAs relationship, was not supported (β = 0.018, t = 0.398, p = 0.345). Similarly, H9, suggesting PI moderates the CSII-PU relationship, was also not supported (β = 0.035, t = 0.885, p = 0.188). However, a significant moderating effect of PI was found for the relationship between CSII and PEU (H10: β = 0.075, t = 2.964, p = 0.002), indicating that PI strengthens the positive influence of CSII on PEU. This implies that for individuals high in PI, social influence has a more pronounced effect on their perception of how easy MBAs are to use.

For qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the extended TAM, we further examined the explained variance (R2), predictive relevance (Q2), as well as effect size (F2). R² values for the two endogenous constructs reached 0.457 (BIU) and 0.278–0.174 for the mediators (PU and PEU, respectively). It is evident from these results that the model explains a significant portion of the variance in Pakistani consumers’ mobile banking intentions and perceptions beyond the 0.26 threshold [26, 38]. The f² analysis demonstrates that PU is the most potent predictor of BIU, exhibiting a large effect (f² = 0.276). Likewise, CSII is a strong antecedent that has medium-to-large effects on PEU (f2 = 0.063) and PU (f2 = 0.125). Nonetheless, the model was moderately but significantly impacted by the route from PEU to BIU of MBAs (f2 = 0.051). These classifications follow the conventional cut-offs suggested by [26] and reiterated by [38]. All remaining paths, including the non-significant moderator effects, fall below the 0.02 “small” bound, also documenting their incremental contribution.

Q² (Stone-Geisser) is positive across every endogenous variable (0.349 for BIU, 0.103 for PU, 0.161 for PEU), demonstrating that the model possesses predictive relevance: it outperforms the naïve mean-prediction benchmark when confronted with omitted data during blindfolding [25, 34, 78]. Following the rule of thumb that Q² >0 signifies predictive usefulness [25, 76], the extended TAM can reliably forecast mobile banking adoption intentions in new samples. Collectively, these diagnostics reinforce that the hypothesized relationships are not only statistically significant but also substantively and predictively meaningful in the Pakistani mobile banking context.

Discussion

Interpretation of direct effects

The direct effects observed in this study provide significant insights into the adoption of MBAs in Pakistan, particularly within the framework of the extended TAM. The finding that CSII does not directly influence behavioral intention to use MBAs (H1a not supported) is noteworthy. This suggests that, in the context of mobile banking in Pakistan, simply being influenced by peers, family, or other social networks may not be sufficient on its own to drive an individual’s decision to adopt mobile banking. This result resembles the findings of [87], who also observed that social influence did not directly predict meta-banking adoption. However, it tends to shape perceptions through a process of technology internalization rather than direct compulsion [57]. It contrasts with studies conducted in mandatory settings where social influence can have a direct impact [81], but supports recent work in fintech and mobile services, which found that the effect of social norms is often fully mediated by cognitive perceptions like PU and PEU [42, 90]. This may suggest that other factors are more salient for potential users when forming their intentions of using mobile banking, or that the influence of others is more nuanced and operates through different psychological mechanisms rather than a direct causal link to intention. This finding contrasts with some traditional views on social influence [36]. However, it aligns with studies signifying that the direct impact of social influence on technology adoption can be moderated or mediated by other factors [57].

For instance, previous research suggested that social influence mainly affects adoption indirectly by shaping PU and PEU [49]. This is an important theoretical contribution and suggests a more nuanced causal pathway. Additionally, it is important to note that the present study has a voluntary nature of mobile banking adoption, compared to non-voluntary settings where social influence might have a more direct impact [36].

Conversely, the significant positive effects of CSII on both PU (H1b supported) and PEU (H1c supported) are important. These results indicate that interpersonal influence shapes users’ perceptions of MBAs’ utility and user-friendliness [6]. Positive social influence about mobile banking benefits and ease of use leads to improved perceptions among individuals. This is consistent with social learning theory in which beliefs are informed by the experience of others [63, 68]. The strong impact of CSII on PU (β = 0.387) and PEU (β = 0.257) highlights the importance of word-of-mouth and social proof in forming beliefs about new technology. Banks should focus on creating positive experiences that can be shared within social networks, which can significantly influence potential adopters.

Additionally, the study confirms the fundamental principles of the TAM, with both PU (H2 supported) and PEU (H3 supported) identified as significant direct predictors of the behavioral intention to use mobile banking applications. The notable positive impact of PU and PEU on BIU suggests that when users perceive mobile banking as a helpful tool that enhances their banking experience, saves time, or provides valuable services, their likelihood of using it increases considerably. These results are consistent with a vast body of TAM literature across various technologies and contexts, reinforcing the idea that users are more likely to adopt technologies they find both beneficial and easy to interact with [10, 79]. The practical implication is that banks must not only ensure their mobile apps are feature-rich and beneficial (high PU) but also invest in user-friendly design and intuitive interfaces (high PEU) to encourage adoption [23].

Interpretation of indirect effects

The analysis of indirect effects in this study reveals the underlying mechanisms through which CSII ultimately impacts the behavioral intention to use MBAs. The significant indirect effect of CSII on BIU via PU (H4 supported, β = 0.184) highlights a crucial mediating pathway. This finding indicates that while CSII may not directly lead to adoption intention, it significantly influences an individual’s perception of mobile banking’s usefulness. When people are influenced by others, they are more likely to internalize positive information from their social circles about the benefits of using mobile banking. This, in turn, boosts their PU of the technology, which then increases their intention to adopt it. This pathway highlights the cognitive process through which social influence works: by changing a person’s belief about the utility of the technology, interpersonal cues can indirectly promote adoption intentions [59]. For example, friends talking about how simple mobile check deposits or quick fund transfers are may influence a user’s belief in the utility, which in turn influences usage.

Similarly, the significant indirect effect of CSII on BIU through PEU (H5 supported, β = 0.057) shows another important mediating pathway. This pathway indicates that susceptibility to interpersonal influence also affects how users perceive the ease of interacting with mobile banking apps. If peers or family members share positive experiences about the app’s intuitive design, simple navigation, or straightforward processes, individuals susceptible to such influences are likely to develop a more positive perception of the app’s ease of use [63]. This enhanced PEU then positively influences their intention to use the mobile banking service. Although the magnitude of this indirect effect is smaller than that through PU, it is still statistically significant and substantively important. It underscores that social influence can lower perceived barriers to adoption by shaping beliefs about the effortlessness of using the technology.

For example, word of mouth from friends who are comfortable with the technology, telling you how easy it is to initiate and then become familiar with MBAs, can encourage you to try it out. Together, these indirect effects provide a clear understanding of how interpersonal influence impacts the adoption of mobile banking. Instead of a direct leap from social influence to adoption, the process is more nuanced, with CSII first shaping the core TAM beliefs of PU and PEU. This enhanced perceptions of usefulness and ease of use subsequently led to the adoption intention. This kind of mediated relationship is very important to know and understand how the psychological process of technology acceptance works. It suggests that marketing strategies aimed at promoting mobile banking should not only focus on the features but also on facilitating positive social discourse around the app’s utility and user-friendliness. Encouraging satisfied users to share their experiences can create a ripple effect, influencing the perceptions and, ultimately, the adoption decisions of others within their social circles. The combined indirect effects (0.184 + 0.057 = 0.241) further emphasize the substantial total indirect impact of CSII on BIU, mediated through PU and PEU.

Interpretation of moderating effects

The moderating effects of PI in this extended TAM model provide refined insights into how individual differences shape the adoption process for mobile banking apps. The finding that PI does not significantly moderate the direct relationship between CSII and behavioral intention to use (H6a not supported) suggests that an individual’s inherent tendency to innovate or try new technologies does not alter the strength of the (already non-significant) direct link between social influence and adoption intention. This implies that regardless of whether a person is high or low in personal innovativeness, the direct impact of CSII on their decision to adopt mobile banking remains negligible. This could be because the decision to adopt a financial technology like mobile banking involves considerations beyond just novelty or social pressure, such as trust, security, and perceived need, which might overshadow the moderating role of PI on this specific direct path [14]. Comparable results were reported by [45], indicating that innovativeness often strengthens ease-related perceptions rather than usefulness-oriented ones, consistent with our pattern of supported and unsupported moderations.

Similarly, CSII and PU (H6b not supported) indicate that PI does not change how interpersonal influences shape an individual’s perception of the usefulness of mobile banking. Whether an individual is highly innovative or not, the extent to which they perceive mobile banking as useful based on social cues appears to be consistent. This finding aligns with studies such as [45], which reported a nonsignificant moderating role of innovativeness in smart-service adoption. It also partially supports [14], who found that PI tends to strengthen other (perceived trust) perceptions rather than usefulness. Taken together, these results imply that innovativeness may enhance a user’s readiness to try new systems but does not necessarily amplify how they judge the usefulness of those systems. This suggests that the perception of usefulness derived from social influence might be more universally accepted or that PI influences PU through other mechanisms not captured in this moderation [90].

However, the most significant finding in the moderation analysis is the support for H6c, which posited that PI moderates the relationship between CSII and PEU (β = 0.075, p = 0.002). This indicates that the level of an individual’s PI influences how susceptible they are to interpersonal cues when forming their perception of how easy MBAs are to use. Specifically, the high values of the positive beta coefficient are only for those high in PI, indicating that the effect on PEU from CSII is strengthened.

In other words, innovative individuals who are also susceptible to interpersonal influence are more likely to perceive mobile banking as easy to use when they receive positive social cues about its ease. This could occur because innovative people are more open to new experiences and so will be more likely than non-innovative people to get positive information from their network about how easy it is to use [37, 86]. They may be more inclined to trust others’ assurances about ease of use, or they might interpret such information more positively because of their natural curiosity and confidence in trying new technologies. This finding supports the idea that PI can strengthen certain types of social influence, especially those related to practical aspects like how easy a technology is to use [90]. That means that for marketing mobile banking to highly innovative groups, user testimonials and social proof about the ease of use may be very effective. Taken together, these comparisons reveal that while perceived usefulness and ease of use remain robust TAM predictors, social and personality variables show context-specific variability. This confirms that the extended TAM must be flexibly interpreted across different fintech settings, such as Pakistan’s mobile-banking environment.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to technology adoption knowledge, especially in mobile banking, by extending TAM with CSII as an antecedent and PI as a moderator. While PU and PEU remain key predictors of behavioral intention, the research highlights the importance of social and individual differences for a complete understanding of adoption, particularly in collectivist cultures like Pakistan. It finds CSII influences BIU indirectly through PU and PEU, not directly, adding nuance to social influence within TAM. Social cues are processed cognitively, shaping beliefs about utility and usability, which drive adoption [90]. This enhances understanding of how external factors influence internal beliefs. It demonstrates that the pathway from social pressure to adoption intention is not a simple stimulus-response mechanism but a complex cognitive process. This aligns closely with principles from social learning theory [15] and the elaboration likelihood model [69], where external social information is actively processed, internalized, and transformed into personal cognitive beliefs (about usefulness and ease of use) before guiding behavioral intentions. Our model, therefore, moves beyond establishing that social influence matters and begins to delineate how it matters by shaping the very cognitive appraisals that are central to individual decision-making.

Secondly, the study contributes to the understanding of the role of PI in technology adoption. While PI did not moderate all hypothesized relationships, its significant moderating effect on the CSII-PEU link highlights that individual innovativeness can amplify the impact of social influence on perceptions of ease of use. This suggests that highly innovative individuals are not necessarily more convinced by others about a technology’s utility; rather, their innate curiosity and confidence make them more receptive to assurances about its learnability and effortlessness. These positions PI not as a general amplifier of all social effects, but as a targeted catalyst that primarily lowers perceived behavioral controls by enhancing the credibility of social proof regarding ease of use.

Thirdly, by validating this extended TAM model in the specific context of Pakistan’s banking sector, the study addresses the call for more research in developing countries and culturally distinct environments. The findings provide empirical evidence that while core TAM relationships hold, the influence of social factors and individual traits can manifest differently, underscoring the need for context-specific adaptations of established theories. This research, therefore, enriches the TAM literature by providing a more granular understanding of the adoption process for mobile banking.

Practical implications for the banking sector

The findings of this study offer several actionable practical implications for banks in Pakistan, aiming to enhance the adoption and usage of their MBAs. Firstly, since CSII significantly impacts PU and PEU, banks should actively foster positive word-of-mouth and social proof. For example, banks could launch referral-reward or peer-demonstration campaigns that leverage positive word-of-mouth within customers’ social circles. This can be achieved by leveraging social media platforms to highlight the benefits and ease of use of their mobile apps [80]. Encouraging existing satisfied customers to share their experiences within their social networks can be a powerful and cost-effective marketing strategy.

Secondly, the strong direct influence of PU and PEU on behavioral intention to use reinforces the need for banks to continuously improve the functionality and user experience of their mobile apps. This includes offering a comprehensive suite of services that customers find genuinely valuable, such as using push notifications to alert users about bill payments or subscription renewals, proactively demonstrating the app’s value (enhancing PU). Also, ensuring the app interface is intuitive, user-friendly, and requires minimal effort to learn and operate, develop in-app tutorials or a ‘sandbox’ mode for first-time users to build confidence without fear of errors (enhancing PEU). Regular user testing and feedback mechanisms can help identify areas for improvement.

Thirdly, the moderating role of PI suggests that banks should consider segmented marketing and communication strategies. For customers identified as highly innovative (e.g., early adopters, tech-savvy individuals), marketing messages can emphasize the novel features, such as offering early access to new features like AI-powered spending insights and the ease with which they can master the app, potentially leveraging social influence from similar innovative peers [14]. For customers lower in innovativeness, who may be more cautious or resistant to change, communication should focus on the reliability, security, and practical benefits of mobile banking, providing clear instructions and support to build their confidence and PEU. Specially, for this segment, banks should offer security guarantees and organize digital literacy workshops or offer in-branch one-on-one setup sessions to overcome initial adoption barriers.

Finally, understanding that social influence primarily shapes cognitive beliefs, banks should ensure that their marketing communications are credible and provide clear information that helps potential users form positive perceptions of usefulness and ease of use, which are the ultimate drivers of adoption intention. Beyond direct implications, banks and policymakers should address systemic challenges by investing in digital literacy to bridge the digital divide and improve skills [67]. Robust cybersecurity and continuous monitoring are essential to maintain trust, particularly in the wake of recent incidents. Partnering with government initiatives like Raast and AMA can also expand financial inclusion for women and underserved groups [67]. By addressing these factors, financial institutions in Pakistan can more effectively bridge the gap between the availability of mobile technology and its widespread adoption.

Limitations and future research directions

While this study offers valuable insights into the mobile banking industry, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations to inform future research. First, the use of a convenience sampling approach, which, while practical for exploratory purposes, may limit the generalizability of results to Pakistan’s entire banking population. Future research should replicate these findings using probability-based or stratified samples, incorporate cross-cultural comparisons, and adopt longitudinal or mixed-method designs to examine changes in adoption behavior over time and across demographic segments. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences and the ability to observe changes in adoption behavior over time; a longitudinal approach would better establish causality and show how user perceptions evolve. Third, the study focused on an extension of the TAM framework (CSII, PU, PEU, PI, BIU), omitting other important factors such as trust, security concerns, perceived risk, facilitating conditions, and cultural variables, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of adoption. Fourth, behavioral intent was used as a stand-in for actual usage, but intention does not always result in behavior; future research should include actual usage data for validation.

Conclusion

This study investigated the factors influencing the usage of MBAs in Pakistan by extending the TAM. The research model incorporated CSII as an independent variable, PU and PEU as mediating variables, and PI as a moderator, to predict the behavioral intention to use of MBAs in the banking sector of Pakistan. Results revealed that CSII does not directly affect MBAs but significantly boosts PU and PEU. These, in turn, strongly predict MBAs, supporting TAM. Further, PI moderates the CSII-PEU link, with more innovative individuals perceiving mobile banking as easier when socially influenced, but this does not affect CSII-PU and CSII-BIU. These findings guide banks in Pakistan to boost mobile banking by emphasizing utility, ease of use, social influence, and user innovativeness. The study supports TAM’s extension in a developing country, revealing social, cognitive, and personal factors’ roles in tech adoption.
