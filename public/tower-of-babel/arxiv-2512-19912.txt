Solving strategies for data-driven one-dimensional elasticity exhibiting nonlinear strains Journal: a peer-reviewed journal
Thi-Hoa Nguyen, Viljar H. Gjerde, Bruno A. Roccia, Cristian G. Gebhardt

Abstract
In this work, we extend and generalize our solving strategy, first introduced in [ 1 ] , based on a greedy optimization algorithm and the alternating direction method (ADM) for nonlinear systems computed with multiple load steps. In particular, we combine the greedy optimization algorithm with the direct data-driven solver based on ADM which is firstly introduced in [ 2 ] and combined with the Newton-Raphson method for nonlinear elasticity in [ 3 ] . We numerically illustrate via one- and two-dimensional bar and truss structures exhibiting nonlinear strain measures and different constitutive datasets that our solving strategy generally achieves a better approximation of the globally optimal solution. This, however, comes at the expense of higher computational cost which is scaled by the number of “greedy” searches. Using this solving strategy, we reproduce the first cycle of the cyclic testing for a nylon rope that was performed at industrial testing facilities for mooring lines manufacturers. We also numerically illustrate for a truss structure that our solving strategy generally improves the accuracy and robustness in cases of an unsymmetrical data distribution and noisy data.

Introduction

Data-driven computational mechanics (DDCM) emerged nearly a decade ago as a novel numerical approach in the field of computational mechanics.
Firstly introduced in
[
2
]
, the direct DDCM approach enables the
use of constitutive data (i.e. stress-strain pairs) obtained from experiments, bypassing the need for ad-hoc material models and avoiding information loss.
The main idea is to formulate the boundary-value or initial-boundary-value problem as an optimization problem, seeking the stress-strain pairs from a given dataset that are closest to those pairs satisfying the equilibrium, compatibility, boundary, and initial conditions
[
2
,
4
,
5
]
.
Minimizing the distance between these two pairs is obtained by finding the global minima of an objective function that is the weighted sum of the square
L^{2}
-norm of this distance.
Hence, the resulting optimization problem is often considered as a distance-minimizing problem, or
a discrete–continuous quadratic optimization problem due to the discrete and continuous nature of the dataset and the variable fields, respectively.
In
[
2
]
, the authors proposed a solving strategy that begins with randomly chosen but fixed initial stress-strain pairs and solves for solution that satisfies the constraint set and are simultaneously closest to these initial pairs.
It then searches for new stress-strain pairs from the dataset that are closest to those from the obtained variable fields, i.e. the obtained structural solution, and repeats these steps until the stress-strain data converges to the same value.
This idea is the same as the alternating direction method (ADM) without initialization
[
6
,
7
]
.
There exist alternatives to the direct DDCM approach that include
the inverse DDCM approach
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, which reconstructs the material model based on the given dataset as a traditional energy functional for the computations,
and the hybrid DDCM approach
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, which combines both the direct and inverse approaches.
The direct DDCM approach has been
extended by numerous other works to
noisy data
[

,
15
]
,
dynamics
[
4
]
,
large deformations

,

,

,

[

]
,

[

,
21
]
,
and multiscale mechanics
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
,
and also coupled with game theory
[
25
]
.
Another field of application is material modeling where the direct DDCM approach is reformulated to identify the stress field based on the given strain data
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
In other fields than mechanics, the direct DDCM approach also finds its applications, for instance, in
magnetism
[
30
]
,

[
31
]
, or

Different properties of the direct DDCM approach have been investigated and various approaches developed for their improvement.
One of the first aspects addressed, e.g., in
[
33
,
34

35

This

To tackle this, Kanno

Such a solver, however, requires high computational cost that rapidly increases with increasing number of data points.
Recently, another approach that guarantees global optima for linear systems in

Other attempts to achieve a better approximation of the global optima, improving the accuracy, include

In particular, our solving strategy reduces the value of the global objective function by searching for alternatives to the initial stress-strain pairs in a given dataset.
Another important aspect of the direct DDCM approach that has been addressed and investigated is the computational cost, mainly due to the search of the optimal stress-strain pairs from a given dataset.
In
[

, the authors proposed efficient data structures to reduce the effort of this search through the dataset.
In

We also briefly discuss and numerically show via a truss structure that our solving strategy generally improves the accuracy and robustness in cases of an unsymmetrical data distribution and noisy data.

2
Nonlinear data-driven one-dimensional elasticity

In this section, we state the formulation of the boundary-value problem for one-dimensional elasticity with nonlinear strain measures studied in this work.
We start with the formulation
in the spatially continuous setting, which is the formulation of a discrete-continuous nonlinear optimization problem (DCNLP).
We then employ the finite element method and obtain the associated spatially discrete formulation.
We also derive the linearized system of equations in a matrix form that is the backbone of the solving strategy discussed in the next section.
We close this section with the discussion and reformulation of the DCNLP as a mixed-integer quadratic programming problem which yields global optima when solved with several existing software packages.

2.1
Continuous setting

Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}

and a physical body that occupies the closure
\bar{\Omega}
, which is represented as a one-parameter curve
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\xi\in[0,L_{0}]
is the arc-length coordinate.
Assume that the physical body
is subjected to body forces
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and exhibits only axial deformations.
Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
e\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
s\in L^{2}(\Omega)
denote the displacement, axial strain, and stress fields, respectively,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\mu\in L^{2}(\Omega)
the dual fields of Lagrange multipliers.
While the indicated function spaces fit perfectly for linear analysis, the nonlinear strain measure employed in this work, defined in the following Equation (
3
), might require spaces with increased regularity. This study does not pursue a dedicated analysis of these regularity requirements, leaving such considerations for future work.
Furthermore, for the sake of notation, we define:

\displaystyle x:=(\boldsymbol{u},\,e,\,s)\in X:=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,\times\,L^{2}(\Omega)\,\times\,L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

\displaystyle y:=(\,e,\,s)\in Y:=L^{2}(\Omega)\,\times\,L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

\displaystyle z:=(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,\mu)\in Z:=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,\times\,L^{2}(\Omega)\,.

Let
{\Theta}
define the enforcement of the equilibrium and the compatibility conditions using Lagrange multipliers:

{\Theta}\left(z,\,x;\,\boldsymbol{f}\right)=0\quad\forall\,z\in Z\,,

(1)

with

{\Theta}\left(z,\,x;\,\boldsymbol{f}\right):=\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,\mathcal{B}^{T}s-\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\mu,\,\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})-e\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(2)

Here,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\mu
correspond to the enforcement of the equilibrium and compatibility conditions, respectively,

\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})
is the displacement-based strain, and

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
L^{2}
scalar product.
In this work, we consider the following displacement-based strain and corresponding strain-displacement operator:

\displaystyle\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\cdot)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime}\cdot(\cdot)^{\prime}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\cdot(\cdot)^{\prime}\,,\quad\alpha\in\{0,1\}\,,

respectively, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\xi)
denotes the reference configuration of the physical body, which relates to the current configuration as
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\boldsymbol{u}
, and

(\cdot)^{\prime}
the weak differentiation with respect to the arc-length coordinate
\xi
,
(\cdot)^{\prime}:=\partial(\cdot)/\partial\,\xi
.
Here,
\alpha
is a factor that has either a value of 0 or 1 to neglect or consider the nonlinear term in the definition of
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})
, respectively.

Consider a given closed data set of stress-strain pairs
\mathfrak{D}
. One can construct the corresponding strain and stress fields from
\mathfrak{D}
as follows:

\mathcal{D}:=\{\tilde{y}:=(\tilde{e},\,\tilde{s})\in L^{2}\crossproduct L^{2}:\left(\tilde{e}(\xi),\,\tilde{s}(\xi)\right)\in\mathfrak{D}\;\forall\,\xi\in\Omega\}\,.

(4)

We note that in general, the dataset consists of available experimental measurements, i.e. discrete data points.
The case when a constitutive manifold exists is a special case (see also
[
43
]
).
The static structural analysis of the physical body can be formulated as a discrete-continuous quadratic optimization problem
[
2
,
36
]
as follows:

Find
x\in X
such that:

\displaystyle\inf_{x,\,\tilde{y}}\;\sup_{z\in Z}\;\text{dist}_{G}\left(y,\,\tilde{y}\right)\,+\,{\Theta}\left(z,\,x;\,\boldsymbol{f}\right)\text{ s.t. }\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\text{with}

\displaystyle\text{dist}_{G}\left(y,\,\tilde{y}\right):=\frac{c}{2}||e-\tilde{e}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2c}||s-\tilde{s}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

where
c
is a constant weighting scalar to ensure unit consistency.
Here,
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the global objective function.
We note that (
5
) is a
discrete-continuous nonlinear optimization problem

since the objective function
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a quadratic function
and (
5
) combines the data field
\mathcal{D}
of a discrete nature and continuous primal and dual fields,
x
and
z
, respectively, that subject to the equilibrium and compatibility conditions in
{\Theta}
.

The first-order necessary optimality conditions (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions) state
the resulting stationary problem as follows:

Given a fixed
\tilde{y}=(\tilde{e},\,\tilde{s})\in\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle 0=\,\delta\left(\text{dist}_{G}\left(y,\,\tilde{y}\right)+{\Theta}\left(z,\,x;\,\boldsymbol{f}\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{u},\,\mathcal{B}^{T}\mu+\mathcal{B}_{1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,s\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(6)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta e,\,c(e-\tilde{e})-\mu\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta s,\,\frac{1}{c}(s-\tilde{s})+\mathcal{B}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mu,\,\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})-e\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,\mathcal{B}^{T}s-\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\,\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})\,,

where
\boldsymbol{q}^{T}=\left[\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\;e\;s\;\mu\;\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\right]^{T}
and

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\alpha\,(\cdot)^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\prime}\mathbf{I}
, with
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix.
For the derivation of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, we refer to
A
.
Linearizing Equation (
6
), using e.g. the Taylor expansion, leads to:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})\,\approx\,\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})\,\Delta\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})\,+

(7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{u},\,\mu\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\mathcal{B}_{1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,\Delta s+\mathcal{B}^{T}\Delta\mu+s\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta e,\,c\Delta e-\Delta\mu\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta s,\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{c}\Delta s+\mathcal{B}\Delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mu,\,\mathcal{B}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}-\Delta e\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,s\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\mathcal{B}^{T}\Delta s\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

For more detail on the intermediate steps of the linearization of
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q})=0
, we refer to
A
.
The solution of (
6
) (or (
7
)) consists of the structural solution,
\boldsymbol{q}
, and stress-strain pairs in
\tilde{y}
which is then the minimizer of (
5
).
In this work, we denote the obtained minimizer with the superscript
(\cdot)^{*}
, i.e.
\tilde{y}^{*}:=(\tilde{e}^{*},\,\tilde{s}^{*})
.

2.2
Spatial discretization

In this work, we deal with Equations (
7
) derived in the previous subsection using the finite element method.
In particular,
we spatially discretize the primal and dual fields with
N
linear Lagrange polynomials,
N_{i}(\xi)
,
i=1,\ldots,N
,
and
M
constant basis function,
R_{j}(\xi)
,
j=1,\ldots,M
,
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\approx\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,N_{i}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}=\mathbf{N}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{1}_{h}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

(8)

\displaystyle e\approx e_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\,R_{i}\,\hat{e}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}\,,

\displaystyle e_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{h}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

(9)

\displaystyle s\approx s_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\,R_{i}\,\hat{s}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}\,,

\displaystyle s_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{h}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\mu\approx\mu_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\,R_{i}\,\hat{\mu}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{h}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}\approx\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,N_{i}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}=\mathbf{N}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{1}_{h}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,,

(12)

where
\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{R}
denote the matrix of the basis functions
N_{i}
and
R_{i}
, respectively,

\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
the vector of the unknown coefficients of the corresponding variable fields.
Here, the spaces
\mathcal{V}^{0}_{h}
and
\mathcal{V}^{1}_{h}
are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{0}_{h}:=\left\{v_{h}\in L^{2}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{\Omega_{ei}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Omega_{ei})\,\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{1}_{h}:=\left\{v_{h}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{\Omega_{ei}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Omega_{ei})\,\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}\right\}\,,

respectively, where
\Omega_{ei}
denotes the domain of the
i
-th element and
M
also the number of elements.
For the sake of notation employed later on, we define:

\displaystyle\hat{y}:=\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{i}:=\left(\hat{e}_{i},\,\hat{s}_{i}\right)\,,\;i=1,\ldots,M\,,

and the element objective function
\text{dist}_{E}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
corresponding to the global one in (
5
) as follows:

\text{dist}_{E}\left(\hat{y}_{i},\,\tilde{y}_{j}\right):=\frac{c}{2}||\hat{e}_{i}-\tilde{e}_{j}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{ei})}+\frac{1}{2c}||\hat{s}_{i}-\tilde{s}_{j}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{ei})}

(13)

where
\tilde{y}_{j}:=(\tilde{e}_{j},\,\tilde{s}_{j})\in\mathfrak{D}
denotes the arbitrary
j
-th stress-strain pair in
\mathfrak{D}
,
j=1,\ldots,n_{D}
with
n_{D}
is the number of available stress-strain pairs in
\mathfrak{D}
.
We also spatially discretize the reference configuration with
N
functions
N_{i}(\xi)
as follows:

\boldsymbol{\Phi}\approx\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}=\mathbf{N}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\,,

(14)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
is the vector of nodal positions in the reference configuration.
The discrete current configuration is then:

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}+\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\,.

(15)

Inserting the discretization above into the
the first-order necessary optimality conditions (
6
), we obtain the following spatially discrete formulation of these conditions:

\displaystyle 0\,=\,\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q}_{h})\,=

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\mathcal{B}^{T}\mu_{h}+\mathcal{B}_{1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\,s_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(16)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta e_{h},\,c(e_{h}-\tilde{e}_{h})-\mu_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta s_{h},\,\frac{1}{c}(s_{h}-\tilde{s}_{h})+\mathcal{B}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mu_{h},\,\epsilon_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-e_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},\,\mathcal{B}^{T}s_{h}-\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{q}_{h}^{T}=\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{T}\;e_{h}\;s_{h}\;\mu_{h}\;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{T}\right]^{T}
and

\epsilon_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
is the discrete displacement-based axial strain:

\epsilon_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}\,.

(17)

Here,
\tilde{e}_{h}
and
\tilde{s}_{h}
are:

\tilde{e}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\,R_{i}\,\tilde{e}_{i}\,,\qquad\tilde{s}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\,R_{i}\,\tilde{s}_{i}\,,

(18)

respectively.
For the sake of notation, we define
\tilde{y}_{h}:=\left(\tilde{e}_{h},\,\tilde{s}_{h}\right)
,
and

\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\left[\tilde{e}_{1}\,\ldots\,\tilde{e}_{M}\right]^{T}
and

\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}=\left[\tilde{s}_{1}\,\ldots\,\tilde{s}_{M}\right]^{T}

the vector of the strain and stress data in

, respectively.

The linearization of the conditions (
16
) can be obtained by inserting the spatial discretization into (
7

), that is:

\displaystyle 0=\,\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{q}_{h})\,+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\,\mu_{h}\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\mathcal{B}_{1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\,\Delta s_{h}+\mathcal{B}^{T}\Delta\mu_{h}+s_{h}\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(19)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta e_{h},\,c\Delta e_{h}-\Delta\mu_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta s_{h},\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{T}\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\frac{1}{c}\Delta s_{h}+\mathcal{B}\Delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mu_{h},\,\mathcal{B}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\Delta e_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h},\,s_{h}\,\mathcal{B}_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\mathcal{B}^{T}\Delta s_{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

which can be expressed in the following matrix form (KKT system):

\mathbf{A}\,\Delta\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}\,=\,\boldsymbol{b}\,,

(20)

where

\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{11}&\mathbf{H}^{T}\\
\mathbf{H}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{b}=-\int_{\Omega}\,\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\left(\mu_{h}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}+\alpha\,\mu_{h}\,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}+\alpha\,s_{h}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{\prime}\right)\\

\mathbf{R}^{T}\left(\epsilon_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-e_{h}\right)\\
\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}\right)\,s_{h}-\mathbf{N}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

(21)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{11}=\int_{\Omega}\,\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\,\mu_{h}\,\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\mathbf{N}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}&\alpha\,\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}\\
\mathbf{0}&c\,\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{0}\\
\alpha\,\mathbf{R}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{\prime\,T}\mathbf{N}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}&\frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,\vskip 8.5359pt

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\int_{\Omega}\,\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}\right)^{T}\mathbf{N}^{\prime}&-\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{0}\\
\alpha\,s_{h}\,\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\mathbf{N}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\prime}\right)\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,\quad\text{and}\vskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}=\left[\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}^{T}\;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{T}\;\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{T}\;\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}^{T}\;\boldsymbol{\hat{\lambda}}^{T}\right]^{T}\,.

We note that the system matrix

Remark 2.1
.

For our computations in Section

2.3
Mixed-integer nonlinear optimization

An alternative to the data-driven solver introduced in
[
2
]
is the mixed-integer programming solver, as Kanno introduced in
[
33
]
.
The main idea is to formulate the data selection behind the methodology of the data-driven solver introduced in
[
2
]
, i.e. the selection of
stress-strain pairs in
\mathfrak{D}
that are closest to the stress-strain fields satisfying the equilibrium and compatibility conditions,
in terms of binary constraints.
Since the objective function is a quadratic function, the resulting problem is then a mixed-integer quadratic programming (MIQP) problem which can be solved globally with a standard mixed-integer programming solver provided by several software packages such as Gurobi
1
1

1

https://www.gurobi.com/
or CPLEX
2
2

2

https://www.ibm.com/products/ilog-cplex-optimization-studio

Such solvers guarantee optimal solution and hence can be employed to obtain reference solutions for benchmarking of other algorithms and solvers.
However, the required computational cost increases rapidly when the size of the problem increases
[
1

33
]
.
In this work, to investigate the global optimality of our solving strategy discussed in the next section,
we reformulate the optimization problem (

For our computations in Section

To tackle the data-driven structural analysis formulated as a
discrete-continuous nonlinear optimization problem,
the authors of
[

introduced the direct data-driven solver that seeks the stress-strain pairs in a given dataset,

This solver is an iterative scheme where
\tilde{y}
are firstly fixed with initial selected data in
\mathfrak{D}
to solve for the variable fields that satisfy the equilibrium and compatibility conditions. The solver then seeks new values of
\tilde{y}
that are closest to the obtained solution of the variable fields by minimizing the objective function at the element level and repeats until
\tilde{y}
converge to a minimizer
\tilde{y}^{*}

This necessarily means that the direct data-driven solver introduced in
[

\tilde{y}\in\mathfrak{D}
to yield optimal continuous variables

x
to obtain optimal discrete variables
\tilde{y}\in\mathfrak{D}
.
This solving algorithm is the main idea behind the alternating direction method (ADM)
[
6
,
7
]
(see also
[
44
]
for a historical overview of ADM), which
also iteratively alternates between minimizing separate blocks of variables while holding the others fixed in solving each of the subproblems.
This approach is applicable to both convex and certain non-convex formulations, although its basic form often suffers from slow or unreliable convergence because coupling constraints are enforced only implicitly.
To tackle this issue, particularly for convex problems, an extension of ADM is the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) which employs augmented Lagrangian method and hence dual variables to speed up convergence
[
45
,
46
]
.
These enhancement stabilizes the iteration and provides convergence guarantee for convex problems.
For non-convex problems, ADM remains the chosen method in practice, although it generally does not guarantee local or global optimal solution despite globally optimal solutions of each subproblem (see e.g. discussions in
[
36
]
).

3.2
Greedy-optimization based alternating direction method

As discussed in the previous subsection, the direct data-driven solver based on the alternating direction method (ADM-solver) does not guarantee globally optimal solution.
To generally achieve a better approximation of the global optimal,
we extend this solving strategy with the greedy optimization algorithm
[
39
]
.
First introduced in
[
39
]
, greedy optimization algorithm has been applied in various fields, for instance in computational mechanics for model order reduction
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
,
data and signal compression
[
50
]
, and approximation problems
[
40
]
.
For an overview of its applications, we refer to
[
51
]
.
Greedy algorithms belong to a class of approximation methods that
iteratively improves the local optima with the aim of approximating the global optima.
Although they do not always guarantee the absolute best outcome, these algorithms are highly practical in situations where computing an exact optimal solution would be prohibitively expensive or even not possible.

We can see in
Algorithm
3
that the ADM-solver seeks the stress-strain pairs from the given data set,
\tilde{y}\in\mathfrak{D}
, at the element level and hence yields at best local optima.
Employing the greedy optimization, we iteratively search for better alternatives to
\tilde{y}
in
\mathfrak{D}
for each element that possibly reduce the value of the global objective function.
We describe the details and each step of this solving strategy in Algorithms
4
and
5
.
The resulting solver is a direct data-driven solver based on alternating direction method (ADM) combined with the greedy optimization algorithm, and hence we refer to this solver as GO-ADM solver.
Algorithm
4
shows the solving strategy for each load step, including data initialization and solving with the GO-ADM solver described in Algorithm
5
.
We note that the Algorithm
4
is the same as the solving strategy based on ADM described in Algorithm
1
except for the employed solver that is now the GO-ADM- instead of the ADM-solver.
We compute first results using the ADM-solver (see also Algorithm
2
) and evaluate both the global and local objective function using these results,
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\text{dist}_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively (see lines 1-3 and 8 in Algorithm
5
).
We then sort the elements in descending order of the corresponding
\text{dist}_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
(see line 9 in Algorithm
5
) and start the “greedy” search with the first one in this sorted array, i.e. the element that has the largest value of
\text{dist}_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
and hence contributes the most to
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
The “greedy” search finds two closest pairs
\tilde{y}\in\mathfrak{D}
by minimizing
\text{dist}_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
, as two best alternatives to
\tilde{y}
, for each element (see lines 12-13 in Algorithm
5
).
For the
i
-th element,
if the current
\tilde{y}_{i}
is not the same as the best alternative, we continue with this alternative as new
\tilde{y}_{i}
.
If the current
\tilde{y}_{i}
is the best choice, i.e.
\tilde{y}_{i}
is the same as the first alternative, we continue with the second-best alternative to test whether this one reduces the value of
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
later on (see lines 15-19 in Algorithm
5
).
Using
\tilde{y}
with new choice of
\tilde{y}_{i}
for the current element as initial selected values,
we employ the ADM-solver to solve for new solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}
and update
\tilde{y}
and reevaluate
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
with these new results (see lines 21-23 in Algorithm
5
).
If
\text{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
is reduced, we overwrite
\tilde{y}_{i}
with the new choice and start the “greedy” search from the beginning (line 7 in Algorithm
5
).
If this is not the case, we continue with the next element with the next-largest value of
\text{dist}_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
(i.e. line 10 in Algorithm
5
), and again search for two best alternatives to
\tilde{y}_{i}
.

We note that an ideal converging criterion of the “greedy” searching loop is when the global objective function reaches a threshold value.
However, meeting this criterion is not guaranteed, leading to non-converging computations, and hence is not practical.
To this end, we define a maximum number of “greedy” searches as the convergence criterion due to computational stability.
We count the searches for each element, including those that do not improve the global objective function.
Similarly to the ADM-solver, the number of searches is unknown and hence also the total required computational effort.
Compared to the ADM-solver, the GO-ADM solver scales the computational cost by the number of searches since it recomputes the discrete solution and updates the stress-strain data pairs using the ADM-solver with better initial data pairs for each search.
We note that the new initial data pairs, however, might not associate with any structural solution and hence lead to a higher number of iterations within the ADM-solver at each search.
The number of iterations when solving with the ADM-solver is unpredictable (see also Remark
3.1
).
Furthermore, to reduce the computational cost of the GO-ADM solver, particularly
the number of “greedy” searches for alternative initial data pairs, one can define an active searching region for each element, instead of searching over the complete dataset.
Another approach is to employ GO-ADM at or up to selective load steps instead of all steps, which, however, does not guarantee improvement of the solution and global objective function for sequential steps.

Data initialization scheme.

We now discuss and give an overview of three common approaches for the initialization of the stress-strain data pairs
\tilde{y}
in the solving strategies discussed in the previous subsections (see also Algorithms
2
and
5
) considered in this work:
i) a random initialization
[
2
]
,
ii) a stress-free initialization
[
11
]
,
iii) and a structure-specific initialization
[
36
]
.
We describe the initialization using these approaches in details in Algorithm
6
.
The first approach is to randomly select
\tilde{y}
as firstly introduced together with the direct data-driven solver in
[
2
]
.
In general, this random selection is not related to any physical state of the underlying structure and loading scenario, hence, can lead to diverging Newton-Raphson scheme.
For geometrically linear structural problems, we can expect convergence, however, a larger number of iterations for both the Newton-Raphson scheme and the ADM-solver.
Although the initialized data
\tilde{y}
is employed only for the first load step, the inaccurate solution might affect the accuracy and convergence at following load steps.
Moreover, noises and pollutions in the dataset may amplify this effect over the load steps.

Alternatively, one can select zeros values for the data pairs
\tilde{y}
, which necessary correspond to the stress-free state of the underlying structure.
This initialization approach reduces the chance but theoretically does not eliminate the case of diverging Newton-Raphson scheme for geometrically nonlinear structural problems.
The third approach considered in this work is a structure-specific initialization first introduced in
[
36
]
.
The main idea is to select the initial data pairs for
\tilde{y}
that consist of stresses being closest to the
solution of the equilibrium condition of the underdetermined linear system.
This initialization contains structure-specific information and guarantees globally optimal solution for geometrically linear structures in certain symmetric cases
[
36
]
.
For geometrically nonlinear cases, this is no longer guaranteed. Nevertheless, the structure-specific initialization avoids the divergence of the Newton-Raphson scheme since the resulting values of
\tilde{y}
relate to the solution of the underlying linear system, i.e. the solution obtained in the first Newton-Raphson iteration at the first load step.
Hence, this initialization approach has the most favorable properties among the three considered approaches in this work. Therefore, we choose this as the default choice in Algorithm
6
.

For completeness, we state here the equations of the equilibrium conditions of the linear system (i.e. linear strains or
\alpha=0
) corresponding to the optimization problem (
16
) studied in this work:

\int_{\Omega}\,\mathbf{N}^{\prime\,T}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}^{\prime}\,\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}=\int_{\Omega}\,\mathbf{N}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}=0\,.

(25)

Solving this system of equations for
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}
, one can then seek the stress-strain pairs in the given dataset
\mathfrak{D}
with stresses being closest to
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}
(see also line 8 in Algorithm
6
).
For more theoretical details and discussion on solving this system of equations, we refer to
[
36
]
.
We note that one can also employ
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}
as
initial stresses in the solution guess of
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}
for the first load step (see also Algorithms
2
and
5
).
Given an approximated constitutive manifold, one can further obtain the corresponding strains
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}
to improve the solution guess of
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}
.
Another possible extension is to solve the underlying linear structural problem with an approximated constitutive manifold to obtain the complete solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}
for the first load step.
This approach is out of scope of this work and is considered in future work.

4
Numerical studies

In this section, we numerically illustrate the favorable properties of the introduced solving strategy based on the greedy optimization algorithm and the alternating direction method (ADM), discussed in the previous section (see also Algorithm
4
).
We start with the benchmarking of our implementation for a one-dimensional bar and a truss structure in two-dimensional space.
To illustrate the application of the introduced solving strategy to a practical scenario with a real experimental dataset, we consider a cyclic test of a mooring line provided by an anonymous industry partner.
We then numerically illustrate via a truss structure that our solving strategy generally leads to better approximation of the global optima.
This, however, requires higher computational cost compared to the standard direct data-driven solving strategy.
Lastly, we briefly discuss the effect of the quality of the dataset on the solution.

4.3
Global optimality and robustness

We now numerically illustrate favorable properties with respect to the global optimality and robustness of our solving strategy in the case of nonlinear constitutive relation.
In particular, we show via two-dimensional truss structures that our solving strategy achieves a better approximation of the globally optimal solution, compared to the standard solving strategy based on ADM.
This, however, comes at the expense of increased computational cost, reflecting a trade-off between solution quality and efficiency.
We also briefly investigate and discuss the effect of the data initialization and of unsymmetrically distributed and noisy datasets.
We numerically illustrate that for the studied truss structure, our solving strategy generally improves the robustness.
Particularly, it reduces the effect of the data initialization and of the dataset on the value of the global objective function,
and reduces the effect of an unsymmetrically distributed dataset on the accuracy of the discrete solution.
We also show that, however, an unsymmetrical data distribution and noisy data increase
the computational cost of our solving strategy more significantly than the ADM solving strategy, i.e. its computational cost is less robust with respect to the data distribution.

5
Summary and conclusions

In this work, we extended and generalized our solving strategy, introduced in
[
1
]
, based on the greedy optimization algorithm and the alternating direction method, for nonlinear systems.
The introduced solving algorithm combines the direct data-driven solver based on the alternating direction method (ADM), introduced in
[
2
]
, and the Newton-Raphson scheme (see also
[
3
]
) at each load step, while “greedy” searching for better alternatives of the stress-strain data pairs to reduce the global objective function.
We briefly discussed the computational cost of our solving strategy that is scaled with the number of searches, compared to the standard ADM-solver.
We integrated three common initialization approaches of the data pairs: a random, a stress-free, and a structure-specific initialization, in our solving strategy.
Particularly, we initialize the data pairs with one of these approaches for the computations at the first load step, and employ the minimizer obtained from the preceding load step as initial data pairs at the next load step.
For nonlinear systems, the three studied initialization approaches do not guarantee globally optimal solution.
A random initialization might lead to a diverging Newton-Raphson scheme and hence does not guarantee numerical stability.
Using either a structure-specific or stress-free initialization improves this stability.
We numerically illustrated via one-dimensional bar and two-dimensional truss structures exhibiting nonlinear strains with different constitutive datasets
that our solving strategy achieves a better approximation of the global optima.
This, however, comes at the expense of higher computational cost in terms of the number of iterations and computing time, which is scaled by the number of “greedy” searches.
Using our solving strategy, we also computed the hysteresis loop in the load-deflection curve of a nylon rope during an industrial cyclic testing.
We also numerically show for truss structures that our solving strategy generally improves the robustness.
Particularly, it reduces the effect of the initialization approaches, an unsymmetrical data distribution, and a noisy data on the global objective function.
It also reduces the effect of an unsymmetrical data distribution on the accuracy of the discrete structural solution.
The computational cost of our solving strategy, however, strongly depends on the data distribution and is increased more significantly by the asymmetry or noises in the data distribution, compared to the standard solving strategy based on ADM.

Our results open various potential directions for future research.
One aspect is to employ accelerating approaches for the direct data-driven solver and/or the greedy optimization algorithm, such as the
approximate nearest-neighbor algorithms
[
41
]
,
using adaptive hyperparameters for the distance-minimizing method
[
35
]
,
momentum-based acceleration techniques
[
52
,
53
]
,
randomization and multi-armed bandit approaches
[
54
]
.
These would accelerate the material data searches within both solving strategy, without affecting the accuracy of the solution.
Another potential approach is the Dijkstra’s algorithm and its extended version, the A
∗
algorithms, for pathfinding, which would improve the “greedy” searching step.
A second aspect is to improve and/or develop new initialization approaches for nonlinear systems.
One can employ the solution and minimizer of the corresponding linear system using the standard direct data-driven solver as initial stress-strain data pairs and solution guess.
An alternative is to solve an approximate nonlinear optimization problem based on an approximated constitutive relation for the nonlinear system.
This would improve the accuracy,
aiming at achieving global optima, however, at the expense of higher computational cost for the initialization step.
Another aspect is to extend the application of the introduced solving strategy
and corroborate its potential for finite element formulations of more complex structural models, in particular, geometrically exact beams, shells, and solid elements.
