Unicuspid Aortic Valve Presenting With Severe Aortic Regurgitation and Successfully Repaired by Bicuspidization
Authors: Naoko Soejima, Hidekazu Tanaka, Tatsuya Miyoshi, Ayano Yoshida, Hiroki Matsuzoe, Genichi Sakaguchi, Gaku Nakazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnicuspid aortic valve (UAV) is a rare congenital anomaly and is often misdiagnosed as a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV). It typically presents with aortic stenosis rather than regurgitation.Case SummaryA 21-year-old man presented with exertional dyspnea and was diagnosed with severe aortic regurgitation (AR) and aortic root dilation. He had been followed as having BAV since childhood. However, transesophageal echocardiography revealed a unicommissural UAV. The patient underwent valve-sparing aortic root replacement using the reimplantation technique combined with bicuspidization. Postoperative echocardiography showed mild residual AR with good valve function.DiscussionUAV is frequently misdiagnosed on transthoracic echocardiography, and its presentation with severe AR is uncommon. This case highlights the diagnostic value of transesophageal echocardiography and the feasibility of valve-sparing surgery in selected young patients.Take-Home MessagesUAV may be overlooked as BAV and can present with severe AR. Accurate diagnosis and valve-sparing surgery are key in young patients.Key words: aortic regurgitation, bicuspid aortic valve, echocardiography, unicuspid aortic valve, valve-sparing aortic root replacement

History of Presentation

A 21-year-old man was referred to our cardiovascular department for further evaluation of aortic valve disease after transitioning from pediatric care. He reported exertional dyspnea. Physical examination showed blood pressure of 90/52 mm Hg and a regular pulse of 81 beats/min. A Levine grade II/VI diastolic murmur was auscultated along the left sternal border. Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio of 59%. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed high voltage consistent with left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy. Laboratory data revealed no significant abnormalities, and brain natriuretic peptide levels were not elevated.

Past Medical History

The patient had been noted to have a cardiac murmur during a routine school health examination at the age of 6 years. At that time, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed moderate aortic regurgitation (AR) associated with a presumed bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), and he had been followed annually in a pediatric clinic without intervention.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of severe AR in this young patient included BAV with cusp prolapse, which was initially suspected based on TTE findings. However, other congenital valve anomalies, including unicuspid aortic valve (UAV), were also considered. In addition, infective endocarditis and connective tissue disorders associated with aortic root dilation were considered potential causes, although the clinical presentation and imaging findings were not suggestive of these conditions.

Investigations

TTE demonstrated LV enlargement, with an LV end-diastolic diameter of 61 mm and an end-systolic diameter of 40 mm. The LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes were 228 mL and 86 mL, respectively, with a preserved LV ejection fraction of 62% (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Color Doppler imaging revealed severe AR (Figure 2, Videos 3 and 4). Quantitative assessment using the proximal isovelocity surface area method demonstrated a radius of 9.7 mm, an effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.38 cm2, and a regurgitant volume of 78 mL. Holodiastolic flow reversal was observed in the abdominal aorta. The regurgitant jet was eccentric and directed toward the anterior mitral leaflet. Based on these findings, the aortic valve was initially diagnosed as a BAV with right and left coronary cusp fusion (Figure 3, Video 5). The aortic root was also dilated. To further evaluate valve morphology, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed. TEE confirmed severe AR with an eccentric regurgitant jet (Figure 4, Video 6). Short-axis imaging revealed a UAV with a single commissure between the left coronary cusp and the noncoronary cusp, and 2 raphes between the right and left coronary cusps and between the right coronary cusp and the noncoronary cusp (Figure 4, Video 7). These findings were consistent with a unicommissural UAV. Three-dimensional TEE further confirmed the unicuspid morphology (Figure 5, Video 8). Subsequently, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed to provide a comprehensive preoperative assessment of the aortic valve, aortic root, and ascending aorta. Three-dimensional CT reconstruction also demonstrated a UAV morphology, consistent with the TEE findings (Figure 6). CT showed dilation of the sinus of Valsalva, sinotubular junction, and ascending aorta, with maximum diameters of 45.0, 41.0, and 43.0 mm, respectively.

Management

Given symptomatic severe AR and aortic root dilation, surgical intervention was indicated. After multidisciplinary heart team discussion, a valve-sparing strategy was preferred because of the patient’s young age. Intraoperative inspection confirmed a UAV, and the valve morphology was fully consistent with the preoperative TEE findings (Figure 7). The patient then underwent valve-sparing aortic root replacement using the reimplantation technique combined with bicuspidization of the aortic valve (Figure 7).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful. During approximately 6 months of follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic. Follow-up TTE demonstrated mild residual AR without evidence of recurrent valve dysfunction. LV dimensions showed favorable reverse remodeling, with a decrease in LV end-diastolic diameter from 61 to 58 mm and end-systolic diameter from 40 to 35 mm. LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes also decreased from 228 to 170 mL and from 86 to 56 mL, respectively, whereas LV ejection fraction improved from 62% to 67%. Although longer-term follow-up is still required, these findings suggest satisfactory early ventricular remodeling after valve-sparing repair.

Discussion

UAV is a rare congenital anomaly, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.02% in the general population.1,2 It is frequently misdiagnosed as BAV, particularly when evaluated by TTE alone. In the present case, the patient had been followed as having BAV since childhood; however, detailed evaluation with TEE revealed a unicommissural UAV. This highlights an important diagnostic pitfall and underscores the limitations of TTE in accurately defining aortic valve morphology. Differentiating UAV from BAV has important clinical implications beyond establishing the correct diagnosis. Precise characterization of valve morphology is essential for surgical planning because commissural configuration, raphe distribution, and leaflet morphology determine the feasibility and technical strategy of valve repair. In particular, bicuspidization requires accurate identification of the native commissure and raphes to reconstruct a symmetric bicuspid valve with adequate leaflet coaptation. Misclassification of UAV as BAV may influence preoperative planning and potentially limit appropriate consideration of valve-sparing repair. Accordingly, TEE should not only be considered a complementary imaging modality but should be regarded as an essential component of the preoperative assessment when congenital aortic valve disease is suspected and valve repair is contemplated. TEE and CT provide complementary information in the preoperative evaluation of congenital aortic valve disease. Although TEE is particularly useful for dynamic assessment of leaflet motion, commissural configuration, and the mechanism of AR, CT provides more comprehensive and reproducible measurements of the aortic root and ascending aorta and delineates their 3-dimensional relationships with the coronary arteries and surrounding structures. These benefits should be balanced against radiation exposure and the use of iodinated contrast material, particularly in young patients. UAV typically presents with aortic stenosis, especially in younger patients, whereas pure AR is uncommon.1, 2, 3 In a pathologic series, pure AR accounted for only approximately 1% of congenitally malformed aortic valves.4 Therefore, the present case represents an unusual clinical presentation of UAV requiring intervention for severe AR. In addition, UAV is often associated with dilation of the aortic root and ascending aorta, as observed in this case, further contributing to the indication for surgical intervention. Traditionally, aortic valve replacement has been the standard treatment for UAV with significant valve dysfunction. However, published series have demonstrated that bicuspidization can provide acceptable intermediate-term outcomes in selected patients with UAV. In an adult series of 25 patients with predominantly AR who underwent bicuspidization combined with aortic root remodeling, freedom from reoperation and aortic valve replacement at 5 years was 81% and 91%, respectively.1 Three patients required reoperation during follow-up, including 1 who underwent valve replacement. In a pediatric series of 60 patients undergoing bicuspidization, freedom from aortic valve reoperation was 73% at 5 years and 50% at 10 years, and 15 patients (25%) ultimately required valve replacement.5 These findings suggest that bicuspidization may defer or avoid valve replacement, particularly in young patients, although recurrent valve dysfunction and late reintervention remain important concerns. In the present case, a valve-sparing aortic root replacement using the reimplantation technique combined with bicuspidization resulted in satisfactory early postoperative outcomes. Although the follow-up period is currently limited to approximately 6 months, the patient remained asymptomatic with stable mild residual AR and demonstrated favorable reverse LV remodeling, including reductions in LV dimensions and volumes together with preserved systolic function. Nevertheless, longer-term follow-up remains essential to evaluate the durability of repair and continued ventricular remodeling in this young patient.

In addition to these considerations, several important educational aspects of this case merit further discussion. First, this case highlights the critical role of TEE not only in diagnosis but also in surgical decision-making. Although TTE suggested BAV, TEE provided precise anatomical information, including commissural configuration and raphe distribution, allowing accurate diagnosis of UAV. Such detailed morphologic assessment is essential when considering valve repair, and TEE should be regarded as indispensable in patients with suspected congenital aortic valve anomalies undergoing surgical evaluation. Second, the rationale for selecting a valve-sparing approach extends beyond the patient’s young age. Surgical decision-making should be guided by valve morphology and tissue quality. In this case, AR was likely related to cusp malcoaptation with relatively preserved leaflet tissue, suggesting that the valve was suitable for reconstruction. Furthermore, the eccentric regurgitant jet directed toward the anterior mitral leaflet indicated a localized mechanism rather than diffuse leaflet degeneration, supporting the feasibility of repair. Finally, bicuspidization of a UAV is based on restoration of a more physiological valve geometry. By converting a unicommissural valve into a bicuspid configuration with 2 symmetrically positioned commissures, leaflet coaptation can be improved and stress distribution across the valve optimized. This symmetric commissural alignment is considered a key factor for achieving durable valve function. When combined with valve-sparing aortic root replacement using the reimplantation technique, stabilization of the annulus and sinotubular junction further enhances coaptation and functional durability. Taken together, this case not only illustrates the diagnostic pitfalls associated with UAV but also emphasizes the importance of multimodality imaging and anatomical understanding in guiding surgical strategy. These considerations are essential for optimizing outcomes in patients with congenital aortic valve disease. Finally, surgical strategy should also be individualized according to institutional expertise. Although referral to an experienced valve repair center should be considered whenever feasible, conventional aortic valve replacement remains an established and appropriate treatment when durable valve repair cannot be confidently achieved. Therefore, careful preoperative anatomical assessment together with multidisciplinary heart team discussion is essential to determine the optimal treatment strategy for each patient.

Conclusions

This case highlights that UAV may be misdiagnosed as BAV and can present with severe AR. TEE is essential not only for accurate diagnosis but also for guiding surgical decision-making. In selected young patients, valve-sparing aortic root replacement combined with bicuspidization may represent a feasible and anatomically rational alternative to valve replacement.

Take-Home Messages

•Unicuspid aortic valve can be misidentified as a bicuspid valve on transthoracic echocardiography, and transesophageal echocardiography is essential for accurate anatomical diagnosis when clinical suspicion remains.
