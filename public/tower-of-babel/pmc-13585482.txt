Relationship Between Alcohol Consumption and Sleep Quality in a Community‐Based Adult Sample From Central‐Western Spain
Authors: Jesús Gonzalez‐Sanchez, Cesar Jimenez‐Vaquero, Rosario Alonso‐Dominguez, Irene A Garcia‐Yu, Natalia Sanchez‐Aguadero, Andrea Crespo‐Sedano, María I Rihuete‐Galve, Arthur Eumann Mesas, Jose I Recio‐Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAlcohol is one of the most widely consumed psychoactive substances worldwide. Frequent and excessive consumption can lead to negative health and quality‐of‐life effects, including impaired sleep quality. This study aimed to analyse the relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep quality indices in a stratified random sample of adults aged 25–65 years residing in Salamanca and Ávila, central‐western Spain.MethodsA cross‐sectional descriptive study was conducted in the cities of Salamanca and Ávila, central‐western Spain. The study population included a sample of 500 individuals aged 25–65, selected via stratified random sampling by age and sex from the health card database of the regional health service. Sleep quality was assessed via actigraphy and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).ResultsOur results showed an association between alcohol consumption (measured in grams/week) and PSQI score, as well as with certain sleep parameters measured by actigraphy. Participants in the high‐risk alcohol consumption group (> 180 g/week) had higher PSQI scores, more awakenings, and a higher sleep fragmentation index compared with those reporting no alcohol consumption. Significant differences were also found between all alcohol consumption categories in sleep efficiency and Wake After Sleep Onset.Discussion and ConclusionsHigh‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) was associated with poorer self‐reported sleep quality in a community‐based adult sample from central‐western Spain, as well as with worse values in actigraphy‐derived sleep parameters, including sleep efficiency, Wake After Sleep Onset, number of awakenings, and sleep fragmentation index.Keywords: age groups, alcohol consumption, epidemiology, maintenance disorders, sleep initiation

Introduction

According to the 2024 World Health Organization report on alcohol and health, approximately 400 million individuals worldwide suffer from alcohol use disorders, and 209 million are alcohol‐dependent [1]. Alcohol is the most consumed psychoactive substance in Spain. In 2017, 91% of Spaniards aged 15–64 reported having consumed alcoholic beverages at least once (94% men, 89% women), and 63% had consumed alcohol in the previous 30 days [2]. Given that both frequent and excessive alcohol intake can significantly affect health, this issue has become a public health priority [3]. About 30% of adults have some form of sleep disorder. These problems are increasingly concerning worldwide as they are associated with higher morbidity and mortality, reduced quality of life, impaired cognitive function and elevated accident risk [4, 5]. Sleep disorders are linked to various factors, including chronic and excessive alcohol use [6].

However, the extent to which different categories of alcohol consumption influence sleep quality in the general population remains unclear. An inverse relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep quality has been reported [7]. Other studies suggest that how alcohol is consumed [8] and even sex may moderate this association [9]. Previous studies have typically evaluated only one or two sleep parameters (e.g., insomnia, daytime sleepiness, or obstructive sleep apnea), and few have assessed sleep quality as a whole in representative samples [10, 11]. Experimental and observational studies have shown that alcohol consumption before sleep may reduce sleep latency but disrupt sleep architecture, delay rapid eye movement sleep, and increase sleep fragmentation and nocturnal awakenings, particularly with higher doses or repeated consumption [12, 13]. In addition, epidemiological studies have reported associations between drinking behaviours, sleep duration and mental health outcomes, suggesting that alcohol consumption patterns may influence sleep quality and overall well‐being in adult populations [14, 15]. Despite recent advances, the available evidence remains heterogeneous due to differences in populations, alcohol consumption patterns and sleep assessment methods, particularly regarding objective sleep parameters measured by actigraphy. Consequently, further research integrating both subjective and objective sleep measures in representative community‐based samples is needed to better understand how different categories of alcohol consumption affect sleep quality and sleep–wake characteristics in real‐world settings. Thus, this study aimed to examine the association between alcohol consumption and sleep quality indices in a stratified random sample of adults aged 25–65 years from central‐western Spain.

Methods

Study Design

This observational cross‐sectional study was conducted in Ávila and Salamanca, from central‐western Spain, between May 2022 and April 2024. It was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05324267), and the study protocol has been previously published [16].

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the expected score in the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), using the values reported by Gómez‐García et al. [17], who, after analysing a sample of Spanish nurses, reported a mean and standard deviation (SD) of 6.8 (3.38) points. Based on these data, the sample size was calculated for the estimation of a population mean using a two‐sided 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an expected standard deviation of 3.38 points, and a precision (margin of error) of 0.32 PSQI points. Under these assumptions, a minimum sample size of 428 participants was required. After accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 15%, the final target sample size was set at 500 participants. Although the study by Gómez‐García et al. [17] was conducted in a sample of nursing personnel, it was used as a reference to estimate variability because the assessed sleep parameters reflect general physiological sleep processes rather than occupation‐specific characteristics, making it an appropriate methodological reference in the absence of population‐based studies using similar approaches. Additionally, this is the only study published within our geographical setting.

Study Population and Recruitment

The sample consisted of 500 individuals aged between 25 and 65 years, residing in the provinces of Salamanca and Ávila, located in the Castilla y León region, central‐western Spain. Participants were selected using stratified random sampling by age and sex, based on the health card database of the Castilla y León Health Service. One hundred individuals who were exactly 25, 35, 45, 55, or 65 years of age at the time of recruitment were included, with 50 men and 50 women in each age group. Participants were contacted by telephone. Individuals who declined to participate were replaced to reach the target sample size. Data stratified by age and sex are presented in Figure 1.

Flowchart of participant recruitment and study procedures, including sleep assessments (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and actigraphy) and alcohol consumption classification according to the criteria established by the Spanish Ministry of Health.

Inclusion Criteria

Sampling included individuals registered in the Castilla y León Health Service health card database, residing in Salamanca or Ávila (Spain), who gave free and informed consent to participate in the research and who did not meet any of the exclusion criteria.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants were excluded if they had a terminal illness, were unable to attend the research centre for the required assessments, or were pregnant.

Visit Structure and Planning

Once the sample was selected, researchers contacted participants by phone to invite them to take part in the study, ensuring they met the inclusion criteria. Those who accepted were scheduled for a face‐to‐face interview with the investigator responsible for the evaluations. During this first visit, lasting approximately 60–90 min, participants received detailed information about the study's objectives and procedures, signed the informed consent, and completed the relevant questionnaires. At the end, an accelerometer was placed on each participant's non‐dominant wrist to be worn for five consecutive days, along with self‐administered questionnaires to be completed during this period. The accelerometer was used to obtain objective measures of sleep and sleep–wake characteristics, including sleep efficiency, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset (WASO), number of awakenings and sleep fragmentation, thereby complementing the subjective assessment of sleep quality obtained through the PSQI. Afterward, participants returned to the research centre to return the accelerometer and completed questionnaires.

Study Variables

Sociodemographic Variables

Sociodemographic information was collected through a structured questionnaire and included age, sex, marital status, educational level and current employment status.

Alcohol Consumption

Participants were first classified as alcohol consumers or non‐consumers, regardless of whether consumption was regular or occasional. Individuals reporting any alcohol consumption during the previous 7 days were considered alcohol consumers, whereas those reporting no alcohol intake were considered non‐consumers. For participants who consumed alcohol, information on the type and quantity of alcoholic beverages consumed during the previous 7 days was collected. Weekly alcohol consumption was estimated in grams according to the recommendations of the Spanish Ministry of Health [18], whereby 10 g of alcohol correspond to half a glass of wine (125 mL), one glass of beer (250 mL), or one shot of spirits (30 mL), and 20 g correspond to one glass of wine (250 mL), one beer mug (500 mL), or two shots of spirits (60 mL). Based on these estimates, participants were categorised according to the weekly alcohol consumption classification established by the Spanish Ministry of Health as follows: no alcohol consumption (0 g/week), low‐risk alcohol consumption (< 110 g/week), hazardous alcohol consumption (110–180 g/week) and high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week). These thresholds represent increasing levels of alcohol‐related health risk associated with sustained alcohol consumption and are intended to reflect the likelihood of long‐term adverse physical and mental health outcomes rather than harms associated with a single drinking episode [18]. For descriptive analyses, these four categories were retained. For comparative analyses, however, the hazardous and high‐risk categories were combined (≥ 110 g/week) to increase statistical power and facilitate the interpretation of the results.

Sleep Assessment

Sleep Evaluation by Actigraphy

Objective sleep assessment was performed using the ActiGraph GT3X+ device, a validated and reliable method for sleep research [19]. The actimetry monitoring period spanned five consecutive days; however, devices were placed on participants in such a way that at least one weekend day was always included. Including both weekday and weekend measurements was intended to provide a more representative assessment of participants' habitual behaviours and improve the ecological validity of the measurements. Actigraphy data were downloaded and analysed using Actilife 6 software (Version 6.13.15, ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL, USA). The Cole‐Kripke scoring algorithm [20], was used for sleep data analysis, while sleep periods were automatically identified using the Tudor‐Locke method [21], which defines sleep onset when 5 consecutive epochs are identified as sleep. This method provides continuous, accurate and reliable data on sleep–wake cycles, generating detailed reports and calculating several sleep parameters. These included Total Sleep Time, defined as the actual sleep duration excluding wake periods and expressed in minutes; Time in Bed, defined as the total time spent in bed from bedtime to final awakening; Sleep Efficiency, calculated as the percentage of Total Sleep Time divided by Time in Bed, with values ≥ 85% indicating optimal sleep quality [22]; WASO, defined as the total time spent awake after sleep onset; the number of Awakenings during the sleep period; Average Awakening length, calculated as the mean duration of all detected awakenings; the Movement Index, calculated as the percentage of time spent moving while in bed; the Fragmentation Index, reflecting sleep continuity through the proportion of one‐minute immobility sequences; and the Sleep Fragmentation Index, calculated as the sum of Movement Index and Fragmentation Index, with higher values indicating greater sleep fragmentation.

Sleep Evaluation via the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

The PSQI [22] is a validated instrument for assessing sleep quality. It includes 19 items that generate 7 core components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 (no problems) to 3 (severe problems), yielding a global score from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer self‐reported sleep quality. Validation in the Spanish population showed high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.81), with a sensitivity of 88.63% and specificity of 74.99% [23].

Other Variables

Lifestyle‐related variables were collected through a structured questionnaire. Smoking status was classified according to current tobacco use (yes/no). Participants were also asked about cannabis use and cocaine use during the previous 7 days, and these variables were recorded as dichotomous variables (yes/no). Consumption of coffee, energy drinks and other stimulant beverages during the previous 7 days was also assessed. Energy drinks were defined as commercially available stimulant beverages containing caffeine and other stimulating ingredients (e.g., taurine) and were recorded as a dichotomous variable (yes/no). The Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) [24], validated in Spanish, was used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms as a screening measure. Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the validated Generalised Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) questionnaire [25], a screening scale designed to assess the presence and severity of anxiety symptoms rather than to establish a clinical diagnosis. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed because both conditions are known to be associated with alcohol consumption and sleep disturbances. Therefore, PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 scores were included as covariates in the multivariable regression models to control for their potential confounding effects on the association between alcohol consumption and sleep outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out in accordance with the study protocol [16]. The distribution of the variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The numerical variables analysed showed a normal distribution and are expressed as means and standard deviation, while the categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Sleep outcomes included the global Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score and the actigraphy‐derived parameters of total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, WASO, number of awakenings, movement index, fragmentation index and sleep fragmentation index. Finally, to analyse the differences in these sleep quality indices between the different categories of alcohol consumption, analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post hoc comparisons was performed. The analyses were adjusted for age group, sex, level of education, and scores on screening scales for depressive and anxiety symptoms (PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7). These variables were included as covariates because they are known to be associated with both alcohol consumption patterns and sleep‐related outcomes and therefore could act as potential confounding factors. The estimated marginal means after the adjustments were made are presented. In all cases, a risk α of 0.05 was set as the limit of statistical significance for hypothesis testing. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Sample Characteristics

Of the 500 participants included in the study, 282 (56.4%) reported alcohol consumption, whereas 218 (43.6%) reported no alcohol consumption. According to the alcohol consumption categories established by the Spanish Ministry of Health, 200 participants (40.0%) were classified as having low‐risk alcohol consumption (< 110 g/week), 43 (8.6%) as having hazardous alcohol consumption (110–180 g/week), and 39 (7.8%) as having high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week). Women showed a higher prevalence of no alcohol consumption and low‐risk alcohol consumption (< 110 g/week), whereas men showed higher prevalences of hazardous (110–180 g/week) and high‐risk (> 180 g/week) alcohol consumption (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Figure 2 shows the marginal means of the PSQI score index according to the alcohol consumption category, adjusted for age group, sex, educational level, and scores on the anxiety and depression scales. The group of participants with high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) presented higher PSQI scores than the group reporting no alcohol consumption (p < 0.05). Significant differences were observed among all alcohol consumption categories for sleep efficiency and WASO. In contrast, differences in the number of awakenings and the sleep fragmentation index were significant only between participants reporting no alcohol consumption and those in the high‐risk alcohol consumption group (> 180 g/week) (both p < 0.05), with higher values observed in the high‐risk group (Figure 3).

Discussion

This study provides relevant evidence on the relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep quality in a community‐based adult sample from central‐western Spain (Castilla y León), aged 25–65 years. Overall, the findings suggest that high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) is associated with poorer subjective sleep quality and alterations in objective sleep parameters measured by actigraphy, highlighting alcohol intake as an important and potentially modifiable factor in sleep health. By combining self‐reported and objective sleep measurements, this study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how different categories of alcohol consumption may affect sleep continuity and nocturnal recovery in the general population.

Alcohol Consumption and PSQI Score

The results related to PSQI scores are consistent with previous studies conducted in general population samples showing that higher alcohol consumption is associated with poorer subjective sleep quality [7, 9]. These findings suggest that individuals with high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) tend to perceive their sleep as less restorative and more disturbed, which may reflect a greater degree of nocturnal sleep disruption and impaired overnight recovery. Similar results have been reported in community‐based and epidemiological studies, supporting the hypothesis that alcohol consumption negatively affects perceived sleep quality and daytime functioning [14, 15]. The relationship between alcohol consumption and PSQI scores may also be influenced by behavioural and physiological mechanisms. Alcohol intake, particularly in the evening, can alter sleep architecture, suppress rapid eye movement sleep during the first half of the night, and promote sleep instability, increased wake after sleep onset, and greater sleep fragmentation during the second half of the night [12, 13]. In addition, alcohol consumption may increase sympathetic nervous system activity and impair physiological recovery processes during sleep, contributing to poorer subjective sleep quality and next‐day functioning [26]. Differences across studies, such as those reported by Sirtoli et al. [8], may be explained by variations in age groups, cultural context, drinking patterns or measurement tools. In this regard, the present study adds relevant evidence by analysing a representative adult sample and controlling for key confounding variables, including anxiety and depressive symptom screening scores.

Alcohol Consumption and Sleep Parameters Measured by Actigraphy (Efficiency, Wake After Sleep Onset (WASO), Number of Awakenings, and Sleep Fragmentation Index).

The findings related to actigraphy‐derived sleep parameters provide additional insight into the mechanisms linking alcohol consumption and sleep quality. Previous research has shown that alcohol consumption, particularly in the evening, disrupts normal sleep architecture and is associated with poorer sleep maintenance throughout the night [27]. Consistent with these findings, participants who reported alcohol consumption, particularly those in the high‐risk alcohol consumption category (> 180 g/week), exhibited more disturbed nocturnal sleep patterns, reflected by higher WASO values and greater sleep fragmentation than non‐consumers. These alterations reflect disruptions in nocturnal stability and recovery, which may not always be detected through subjective measures alone. The present study integrates actigraphy‐derived indicators such as sleep efficiency, WASO, number of awakenings, and sleep fragmentation index, allowing for a multidimensional evaluation of sleep in natural environments. This approach increases ecological validity and reinforces the idea that sleep should be understood as part of the broader sleep–wake and circadian rhythm framework. Alcohol consumption may disrupt normal sleep regulation and architecture, leading to increased nocturnal wakefulness and impaired overnight recovery [12, 13].

Although some studies have reported higher sleep efficiency in heavy drinkers [28, 29], these findings may be explained by short‐term sedative effects of alcohol or differences in study design and sample characteristics. In contrast, research conducted in younger or community‐based populations has shown increased awakenings and sleep fragmentation associated with alcohol use [30, 31]. Overall, the present findings align more closely with studies suggesting that high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) is associated with poorer sleep maintenance and a greater degree of nocturnal sleep disruption in adult populations.

Implications

It is also important to consider the potentially bidirectional relationship between alcohol consumption and sleep disturbances. Some individuals experiencing sleep difficulties may use alcohol as a self‐management strategy because of its initial sedative effects and its ability to facilitate sleep onset [32]. However, although alcohol may provide short‐term subjective benefits, repeated consumption can disrupt sleep architecture, increase nocturnal awakenings, and impair overall sleep quality [12, 13]. Consequently, alcohol use may contribute to a self‐perpetuating cycle in which sleep problems promote alcohol consumption, while alcohol consumption further exacerbates sleep disturbances [32]. From a clinical perspective, these results highlight the importance of routinely assessing alcohol consumption in individuals presenting with sleep complaints. Alcohol intake is often underestimated in clinical settings, despite being a modifiable factor that may contribute to sleep disturbances and impaired recovery [33]. Screening for alcohol consumption patterns in primary care and sleep medicine could help clinicians better understand sleep problems and provide tailored recommendations aimed at reducing harmful drinking patterns. Furthermore, the use of objective sleep measures such as actigraphy may improve clinical assessment by identifying sleep fragmentation and nocturnal wakefulness that are not always detected through self‐report. Addressing alcohol consumption as part of sleep hygiene interventions may therefore enhance treatment effectiveness and improve patient outcomes.

From a public health perspective, the findings are particularly relevant in the Spanish context, where alcohol consumption is highly prevalent and culturally normalised [2]. Strategies aimed at reducing high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) could have broader benefits beyond preventing chronic diseases, including improvements in sleep quality, daytime functioning, and overall quality of life. Public health campaigns should therefore emphasise not only the long‐term health risks of alcohol consumption but also its short‐term impact on sleep and daily performance. Reducing high‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) and increasing awareness of sleep health may contribute to healthier lifestyles and improved population well‐being.

In addition, this study supports the importance of integrating subjective and objective sleep assessments in epidemiological and clinical research. Understanding how alcohol consumption affects different dimensions of sleep may help develop more targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies, particularly in community‐based adult populations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of a stratified random sample, the combination of subjective and objective sleep measurements, and the adjustment for several important covariates, including age, sex, educational level, and anxiety and depressive symptom screening scores. The use of validated instruments such as PSQI, PHQ‐9, GAD‐7 and actigraphy‐derived parameters further supports the reliability and robustness of the findings.

However, some limitations should be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design does not allow causal relationships to be established between alcohol consumption and sleep quality, and bidirectional associations cannot be ruled out. Although information on tobacco, cannabis, and cocaine use, as well as coffee and energy drink consumption, was collected, these variables were not included as covariates in the adjusted analyses. As some of these factors may independently influence sleep quality and sleep–wake patterns, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Second, alcohol consumption was self‐reported, which may introduce recall bias or underestimation, particularly among heavy drinkers. Third, although actigraphy provides objective sleep measurements in natural environments, it does not capture sleep architecture or physiological parameters in the same way as polysomnography. Alcohol consumption was assessed based on intake over the previous 7 days. Participants were categorised according to predefined weekly alcohol consumption thresholds, with high‐risk consumption defined as > 180 g/week. This time frame may not adequately capture patterns of episodic binge drinking, which do not always occur on a weekly basis and are relatively common in Spain. These patterns are associated with acute consequences, such as hangovers, and may influence sleep quality, particularly in younger people [34]. Therefore, future studies should consider longer assessment periods (e.g.,1 month) to better capture the variability in alcohol consumption patterns. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using screening scales (PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7), which evaluate symptom severity but do not provide clinical diagnoses. Finally, although a stratified random sampling strategy was used, the study population was drawn exclusively from two provinces (Salamanca and Ávila) in central‐western Spain. Therefore, the sample cannot be considered representative of the entire Spanish population. External validity is thus limited to similar adult community populations within this geographical context. Future research should use longitudinal designs, longer alcohol assessment periods, and more detailed measures of drinking patterns, including timing of intake and binge drinking episodes, to better understand the mechanisms linking alcohol consumption and sleep disturbances in the general population.

Conclusions

High‐risk alcohol consumption (> 180 g/week) was associated with poorer self‐reported sleep quality in a community‐based adult sample from central‐western Spain, as well as with worse values in actigraphy‐derived sleep parameters, including sleep efficiency, WASO, number of awakenings, and sleep fragmentation index.
