Dual-Difficulty Curriculum Learning for Direct Preference Optimization
Mengyang Li, Haozhan Geng, Zhong Zhang, Shuang Liu

Abstract
. Curriculum learning enhances Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs), yet existing methods rely on a one-dimensional view of difficulty. In this work, we reframe alignment difficulty as a two-dimensional space spanned by Prompt Complexity (PC) and Pairwise Distinguishability (PD), providing a more principled foundation for alignment. We first demonstrate the efficacy of this space by developing DM-Curri-DPO, a framework of static curricula that already achieves significant gains over baseline methods. Moving beyond these handcrafted paths, we introduce our primary contribution: GSP-Curri-DPO, a novel Group-wise Self-Paced Learning framework. This advanced method empowers the model to navigate the difficulty grid, discovering an optimal learning trajectory based on its own evolving capabilities. Extensive experiments show our self-paced approach not only sets a new state-of-the-art on key benchmarks but, more importantly, demonstrates superior data efficiency and robustness to preference noise. Our work establishes a new paradigm for LLM alignment, offering both a structured difficulty space and an intelligent, model-driven methodology for navigating it.

1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide spectrum of tasks, yet aligning their outputs with human preferences remains a fundamental challenge
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
Bai et al., 2022
)
. Misaligned models may generate responses that are factually incorrect, unsafe, or inconsistent with user intent, which poses significant risks in real-world deployments. Among preference-based alignment techniques, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
has gained significant traction as a stable and computationally efficient alternative to Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Christiano et al., 2017
)
, directly optimizing policies on preference pairs without requiring an explicit reward model.

Figure 1
.
Standard DPO win rate (%) against SFT baseline across the PC-PD difficulty grid on UltraFeedback. Win rates decrease monotonically along both the Prompt Complexity (PC) and Pairwise Distinguishability (PD) axes, with a 22.2% gap between the easiest and hardest cells. This two-dimensional performance variation cannot be captured by any single difficulty metric.
A 3 by 3 grid showing DPO win rates. Win rates are highest in the bottom-left corner and decrease towards the top-right corner, indicating that both prompt complexity and pairwise distinguishability affect performance.

A promising direction for improving DPO is curriculum learning
(
Bengio et al., 2009
)
, which organizes training data in a structured progression from easy to hard examples. Recent work has demonstrated that curriculum-based approaches can improve convergence and final performance in preference optimization
(
Croitoru et al., 2025
)
. However, existing methods operate under a limited view of difficulty. They primarily focus on a single metric, namely the Pairwise Distinguishability (PD) between chosen and rejected responses, which captures only the output-level ambiguity of preference samples.

This one-dimensional perspective overlooks a critical second axis: the semantic complexity of the input prompt itself. We begin with an empirical observation that motivates our approach. As shown in Figure
1
, we partitioned the UltraFeedback training set into a grid defined by two axes and measured the win rate of a standard DPO model on each cell. The results reveal a striking 22.2% performance gap between the easiest region (simple prompts, clear preferences) and the hardest region (complex prompts, ambiguous preferences). Importantly, this gap cannot be explained by either axis alone: cells with identical PD but different Prompt Complexity show up to 15% performance difference, and vice versa. A curriculum that treats samples with the same PD as equally difficult conflates samples that impose very different learning demands on the model.

To understand why a single difficulty axis is insufficient, we analyze the gradient of the DPO loss. The DPO gradient can be decomposed into two multiplicative components: a weighting term determined by the implicit reward margin, and a direction term given by the log-probability difference between chosen and rejected responses. We show in Section 3 that the variance of this gradient receives contributions from two distinct sources. The first source is prompt understanding uncertainty, which reflects the model’s difficulty in assigning consistent probabilities to responses for complex prompts. The second source is preference discrimination uncertainty, which reflects ambiguity in the preference signal when chosen and rejected responses are of similar quality. A single-metric curriculum controls only one of these variance sources, leaving the other unaddressed and causing residual gradient noise.

Motivated by this analysis, we propose to characterize alignment difficulty as a two-dimensional construct. We introduce a difficulty space spanned by two complementary axes: Prompt Complexity (PC), which quantifies the inherent challenge of interpreting the input, and Pairwise Distinguishability (PD), which measures the clarity of preference between outputs. Our empirical analysis confirms that these two dimensions exhibit low correlation (Pearson
r=0.12
on UltraFeedback and
r=0.08
on OpenAssistant), validating that they capture distinct aspects of sample difficulty.

Building upon this framework, we develop DM-Curri-DPO, a family of static curriculum strategies that systematically traverse the PC-PD difficulty grid. We investigate four distinct traversal strategies encoding different philosophies of difficulty progression. To ensure stable training across curriculum stages, we further introduce a KL-divergence based adaptive reference mechanism that dynamically updates the reference model when policy drift exceeds a threshold. Moving beyond these handcrafted strategies, we further propose GSP-Curri-DPO, a Group-wise Self-Paced Learning framework that empowers the model to dynamically navigate the difficulty grid based on its own evolving learning state, discovering a near-optimal traversal path without manual design.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

We begin by analyzing the gradient structure of the DPO loss to provide a theoretical foundation for our dual-metric framework. Prior work has shown that training dynamics carry rich information about sample difficulty in supervised learning
(
Li et al., 2025a
)
. We extend this perspective to preference optimization, revealing that gradient variance in DPO arises from two distinct and separable sources, motivating the need for a two-dimensional characterization of sample difficulty.

(2) We develop DM-Curri-DPO, a family of static curriculum strategies with four distinct traversal paths through the difficulty grid, complemented by a KL-adaptive reference mechanism and curriculum smoothing for enhanced training stability.

(3) Moving beyond static curricula, we introduce GSP-Curri-DPO, a Group-wise Self-Paced Learning framework that empowers the model to dynamically navigate the difficulty grid based on its own evolving learning state, removing the need for manually designed traversal strategies.

(4) Through extensive experiments on UltraFeedback and OpenAssistant with models from 7B to 70B parameters, evaluations on MT-Bench, Arena-Hard, and AlpacaEval 2.0 using both LLM judges and human evaluation, and comparisons against curriculum, data selection, and preference optimization baselines, we demonstrate that our approach yields statistically significant improvements, with GSP-Curri-DPO achieving the best overall performance.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Preference-Based LLM Alignment

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Christiano et al., 2017
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
Stiennon et al., 2020
)
trains a reward model on human preference data and fine-tunes a policy model using PPO
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
to maximize predicted rewards under KL regularization. Despite its empirical success, RLHF suffers from a complex multi-stage pipeline, training instability, and high computational cost. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
emerged as an elegant alternative that directly optimizes policies on preference pairs by reparameterizing the reward function in terms of the policy itself, eliminating the need for a separate reward model. The simplicity and strong performance of DPO have led to widespread adoption
(
Ivison et al., 2023
;
Samo et al., 2024
;
Tunstall et al., 2024
)
.

Subsequent work has addressed various limitations of DPO. KTO
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
)
handles scenarios with only binary feedback rather than pairwise comparisons. IPO
(
Azar et al., 2024
)
introduces regularization to prevent overfitting to preference data. SimPO
(
Meng et al., 2024
)
removes the dependency on a reference model by using average log probability as an implicit reward. ORPO
(
Hong et al., 2024
)
combines supervised fine-tuning and preference alignment into a single stage. CPO
(
Xu et al., 2024
)
incorporates contrastive learning principles. Recent work has also explored leveraging intrinsic model feedback to improve alignment quality
(
Li et al., 2026
)
. Our work operates within the DPO framework, focusing on enhancing its effectiveness through principled data scheduling rather than modifying the optimization objective.

2.2.
Curriculum Learning and Data Selection

Curriculum learning
(
Bengio et al., 2009
)
presents data in a meaningful order, typically progressing from easy to hard examples. This approach has demonstrated benefits in computer vision
(
Guo et al., 2018
;
Hacohen and Weinshall, 2019
)
, natural language processing
(
Platanios et al., 2019
;
Xu et al., 2020
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
Narvekar et al., 2020
;
Portelas et al., 2021
)
. Self-paced learning
(
Kumar et al., 2010
;
Jiang et al., 2015
)
allows the model itself to determine the curriculum based on its current learning state.

In the context of LLM alignment, Curriculum-DPO
(
Croitoru et al., 2025
)
introduced curriculum learning to preference optimization by ordering training samples based on Pairwise Distinguishability. While demonstrating the value of structured data presentation, this approach relies on a single difficulty metric capturing only output-level characteristics. Beyond curriculum approaches, data selection strategies have been explored for preference optimization. Reward-weighted methods
(
Liu et al., 2024a
)
assign importance weights to preference pairs based on estimated reward differentials. Active preference selection methods
(
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Chen et al., 2024
)
identify samples that provide the most learning signal for the current model state. Our work addresses a complementary dimension by providing a principled multi-metric difficulty characterization that can be combined with these data selection strategies.

3.
Gradient Analysis of DPO Difficulty

We begin by analyzing the gradient structure of the DPO loss to provide a theoretical foundation for our dual-metric framework. This analysis reveals that gradient variance in DPO arises from two distinct and separable sources, motivating the need for a two-dimensional characterization of sample difficulty.

3.1.
Gradient Variance Decomposition

The DPO loss for a single preference sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})
is:

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\left(\beta\hat{r}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})\right),

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function,
\beta
controls preference strength, and the implicit reward margin is defined as:

(2)

\hat{r}_{\theta}=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})}.

The gradient with respect to model parameters
\theta
can be written as:

(3)

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta\cdot\underbrace{\sigma(-\beta\hat{r}_{\theta})}_{w(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})}\cdot\underbrace{\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})\right]}_{g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})}.

This gradient is the product of a scalar weighting term
w(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})\in(0,1)
and a directional term
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})
that points toward increasing the probability of the chosen response relative to the rejected one.

To analyze the variance of this gradient over training samples, we consider the mini-batch gradient estimator
\hat{G}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}w_{i}\cdot g_{i}
for batch size
B
. Under the assumption that the weighting term
w
and directional term
g
have bounded covariance, the variance of the gradient estimator can be approximately decomposed as:

(4)

\text{Var}[\hat{G}]\approx\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Tr}(\text{Var}[g])}_{\text{directional variance}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]\cdot\text{Var}[w]}_{\text{weighting variance}}.

This decomposition separates the gradient variance into two components with distinct origins. Here
\text{Tr}(\text{Var}[g])
denotes the trace of the covariance matrix of the vector-valued directional term
g
, reducing it to a scalar that measures total directional variability, while
\text{Var}[w]
is the scalar variance of the weighting term. The directional variance term is governed by the variability of the log-probability gradient across samples. When the model encounters a complex prompt for which it lacks consistent internal representations, the log-probabilities
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})
and
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})
become unreliable, producing high variance in
g
even if the preference direction is clear. This component is therefore primarily driven by Prompt Complexity. The weighting variance term
\text{Var}[w]
is governed by the variability of the sigmoid weight
\sigma(-\beta\hat{r}_{\theta})
. When PD is low (chosen and rejected responses are of similar quality), the implicit reward margin
\hat{r}_{\theta}
fluctuates near zero across samples, causing
w
to vary widely between values near 0 and 1. This component is therefore primarily driven by Pairwise Distinguishability. We formalize this observation below.

Proposition 0.

Consider a batch of DPO training samples drawn from a distribution where Prompt Complexity and Pairwise Distinguishability are independent. Let
\text{Tr}(\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g])
denote the trace of the conditional covariance of the directional gradient given fixed PD, and let
\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
denote the conditional variance of the weighting term given fixed PC. Then the total gradient variance satisfies:

(5)

\text{Var}[\hat{G}]\geq\frac{1}{B}\left(\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Tr}(\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g])+\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]\cdot\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]\right).

A curriculum that controls only PD (by presenting high-PD samples first) reduces the second term while leaving the first term uncontrolled. Similarly, a curriculum controlling only PC reduces the first term while leaving the second uncontrolled. A dual-metric curriculum that jointly controls both PC and PD can simultaneously reduce both variance components.

The proof follows from the law of total variance applied to the product
w\cdot g
under the independence assumption, combined with the observation that conditioning on low-PC samples reduces
\text{Tr}(\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g])
and conditioning on high-PD samples reduces
\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
. We provide the complete proof in Appendix A.

4.
Formalizing the Difficulty Space

Building on the gradient analysis, we now define the two metrics that span our difficulty space and describe the construction of the difficulty grid.

4.1.
Prompt Complexity (PC)

Prompt Complexity quantifies the inherent semantic and reasoning challenge posed by an input prompt
\mathbf{x}
, independent of any specific response pair. Our analysis in Section 3 shows that complex prompts produce high directional gradient variance because the model assigns inconsistent log-probabilities to responses. We operationalize this by measuring the variability of response quality under sampling, which reflects the breadth of the plausible response space.

Given a prompt
\mathbf{x}
and a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, we generate a set of
N
candidate responses via temperature sampling:

(6)

\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\quad i=1,\dots,N.

We then compute the perplexity of each response using a fixed external language model
p_{\text{LM}}
:

(7)

\text{PPL}(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|\mathbf{x})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{L_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{L_{i}}\log p_{\text{LM}}(y_{i,t}|\mathbf{x},y_{i,<t})\right),

where
L_{i}
is the length of response
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}
. The Prompt Complexity is defined as the standard deviation of these perplexity scores:

(8)

\text{PC}(\mathbf{x})=\underset{i=1,\dots,N}{\text{StdDev}}\left(\text{PPL}(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|\mathbf{x})\right).

A higher PC value indicates greater variability in plausible responses, reflecting a more challenging prompt for the model to interpret consistently.

The rationale for this definition connects to our gradient analysis and aligns with the broader insight that difficulty-aware characterization improves learning efficiency
(
Li and Zhao, 2026
)
. When a prompt is well-constrained, the space of reasonable responses is narrow, leading to similar perplexity scores across samples and low directional gradient variance.
Conversely, ambiguous or complex prompts admit a wider range of valid interpretations, producing high perplexity variance and correspondingly high gradient variance in the directional component.

To validate that PC captures meaningful prompt difficulty, we conducted two studies. First, we randomly sampled 500 prompts from UltraFeedback and recruited three annotators to rate each prompt on a 1 to 5 complexity scale based on reasoning depth, domain knowledge, and inherent ambiguity. The Spearman correlation between PC and average human ratings was 0.68 (
p<0.001
). Second, we evaluated the robustness of PC under different computation settings (temperature, external model, sample count), which we report in detail in Section 6.5. The PC ranking remains stable across these variations, with Kendall
\tau>0.85
in all tested configurations.

4.2.
Pairwise Distinguishability (PD)

Pairwise Distinguishability measures the clarity of preference between a chosen response
\mathbf{y}_{w}
and a rejected response
\mathbf{y}_{l}
. Our gradient analysis shows that low PD produces high variance in the sigmoid weighting term, contributing to unstable gradient magnitudes. We define PD using a quality scoring model.

Let
S_{\text{judge}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
denote the quality score assigned to response
\mathbf{y}
for prompt
\mathbf{x}
. The Pairwise Distinguishability is defined as:

(9)

\text{PD}(\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})=\left|S_{\text{judge}}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})-S_{\text{judge}}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})\right|.

A key concern is whether the choice of judge model introduces systematic bias. To address this, we compute PD using two independent scoring systems: GPT-4 scoring on a 1 to 10 scale, and the open-source ArmoRM reward model
(
Hong et al., 2025
)
. We report results using both scoring systems in our experiments and verify that the conclusions are consistent across judges (Section 6.4).

The Pearson correlation between PC and PD is 0.12 on UltraFeedback and 0.08 on OpenAssistant. These low correlations confirm that the two metrics capture distinct difficulty dimensions rather than redundant information.

4.3.
The Dual-Metric Difficulty Grid

For each preference sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})
in dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we compute its PC and PD values. We then discretize each metric into
K
and
M
ordered bins using quantile-based binning, ensuring balanced sample distribution across bins.

Each sample is assigned a PC rank
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and a PD rank
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, mapping it to grid cell
\mathcal{C}_{k,m}
. By convention, lower ranks indicate easier characteristics: lower
k
means simpler prompts, and lower
m
means clearer preferences (higher raw PD scores). This
K\times M
difficulty grid serves as the organizational structure for our curriculum strategies. We empirically find that a
3\times 3
grid offers a robust balance between granularity and data sufficiency for datasets in the 30K to 100K sample range (ablation in Section 6.6).

Building upon the dual-metric difficulty framework, we now present DM-Curri-DPO, our family of static curriculum strategies that systematically traverse the difficulty grid.

5.1.
Curriculum Traversal Strategies

The core design choice in a static curriculum is the order in which grid cells are traversed. Each strategy defines a sequence
\tau=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{KM})
that determines the progression from easiest to hardest samples. We investigate four strategies, visualized in Figure
2
.

The PC-first strategy prioritizes mastering prompts of increasing complexity before tackling more ambiguous preferences. The curriculum proceeds column by column through the grid, exhausting all PD levels within a given PC level before advancing to more complex prompts. Cells are ordered primarily by PC rank
k
and secondarily by PD rank
m
. According to our gradient analysis, this strategy first reduces the directional variance
\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
before addressing the weighting variance.

The PD-first strategy prioritizes mastering preference discrimination. The curriculum proceeds row by row, exhausting all PC levels within a given PD level before moving to more ambiguous preferences. Cells are ordered primarily by PD rank
m
and secondarily by PC rank
k
. This strategy first reduces the weighting variance
\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
.

The Sum-then-PC (S-PC) strategy aims for a balanced, diagonal progression. Cells are prioritized by the sum of their ranks
s=k+m
. For cells with equal sums, ties are broken by favoring lower PC rank
k
:

(10)

\text{order}(\mathcal{C}_{k,m})=K\cdot(k+m)+k.

This strategy simultaneously reduces both variance components at a comparable rate.

The Sum-then-PD (S-PD) strategy similarly prioritizes based on rank sum
s=k+m
, but breaks ties by favoring lower PD rank
m
:

(11)

\text{order}(\mathcal{C}_{k,m})=K\cdot(k+m)+m.

The sum-based strategies are motivated by the principle that neither variance component should increase too rapidly relative to the other, preventing scenarios where the model faces a sudden jump in one type of difficulty while still struggling with the other.

5.2.
KL-Adaptive Reference Mechanism

In multi-stage curriculum learning, the choice of reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
significantly impacts training stability. The DPO loss relies on log-probability ratios between the policy
\pi_{\theta}
and reference
\pi_{\text{ref}}
:

(12)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})}\right).

A fixed reference model can become stale as the policy evolves through curriculum stages, leading to large log-ratio values and noisy gradients. However, updating the reference too frequently may hinder convergence by constantly shifting the optimization landscape. We introduce a KL-adaptive update mechanism that refreshes the reference when policy drift exceeds a threshold:

(13)

\text{Update }\pi_{\text{ref}}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta}\quad\text{if}\quad\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})>\delta,

where
\delta
is a predefined threshold. The KL divergence is estimated using a rolling window of the most recent training batches:

(14)

\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\right].

We note that similar adaptive reference ideas have appeared in iterative DPO
(
Xu et al., 2024
)
and online DPO settings. Our contribution is integrating this mechanism with curriculum learning, where the non-stationary data distribution across stages makes reference staleness particularly problematic. We maintain a running estimate over the most recent 100 batches and check every 100 training steps.

5.3.
Curriculum Smoothing

To further enhance stability at stage transitions, we employ curriculum smoothing. When training on stage
t
, the training pool combines all samples from the current cell
\mathcal{C}_{t}
with a random subsample drawn from the previous cell
\mathcal{C}_{t-1}
:

(15)

\mathcal{D}_{\text{pool}}^{(t)}=\mathcal{C}_{t}\cup\text{Sample}(\mathcal{C}_{t-1},f_{s}\cdot|\mathcal{C}_{t}|)

where
f_{s}
controls the fraction of the current cell size drawn from the previous stage. Mini-batches are then uniformly sampled from this combined pool. This gradual transition prevents abrupt distribution shifts between curriculum stages.

6.
GSP-Curri-DPO: Self-Paced Curriculum

While the static traversal strategies in DM-Curri-DPO provide consistent improvements, they require manual selection and cannot adapt to the model’s evolving learning state. To address this, we propose GSP-Curri-DPO, a Group-wise Self-Paced Learning framework that allows the model to dynamically discover its own traversal path through the difficulty grid.

6.1.
Self-Paced Cell Selection

At each curriculum stage
t
, the model evaluates its readiness for each unvisited grid cell by computing a validation loss reduction score. Specifically, for each candidate cell
\mathcal{C}_{k,m}
not yet trained on, we compute:

(16)

\Delta\mathcal{L}_{k,m}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}^{(t-1)}(\mathcal{C}_{k,m})-\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}(\mathcal{C}_{k,m}),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}(\mathcal{C}_{k,m})
is the DPO validation loss on a held-out subset of cell
\mathcal{C}_{k,m}
after training stage
t
. The model selects the cell with the highest expected learning gain:

(17)

\mathcal{C}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{C}_{k,m}\in\mathcal{U}}\Delta\mathcal{L}_{k,m},

where
\mathcal{U}
denotes the set of unvisited cells. This selection mechanism naturally implements a self-paced curriculum: the model gravitates toward cells where it can currently learn the most, which typically correspond to cells of moderate difficulty relative to its current capability.

6.2.
Connection to Static Strategies

A notable finding is that the self-paced trajectory discovered by GSP-Curri-DPO closely resembles the sum-based diagonal paths of the static strategies. On UltraFeedback, the learned traversal order has a Kendall
\tau
of 0.82 with the S-PD ordering, suggesting that the manually designed strategies are near-optimal. However, GSP-Curri-DPO provides additional flexibility at transition points, occasionally revisiting cells where residual learning potential remains.

7.

We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate DM-Curri-DPO across multiple dimensions including main performance comparisons, analysis by difficulty region, training dynamics, robustness of the PC metric, sensitivity to the PD judge, and ablation studies.

Several consistent patterns emerge across both settings. All four DM-Curri-DPO strategies outperform the strongest single-metric baseline SM-Curri-PD across all metrics on both datasets. On UltraFeedback, DM-Curri with S-PD achieves 41.2% on Arena-Hard compared to 38.5% for SM-Curri-PD, a relative improvement of 7.0%. On AlpacaEval 2.0, S-PD reaches 35.1% compared to 32.1% for SM-Curri-PD. These improvements are statistically significant under paired bootstrap tests (
p<0.05
) across five random seeds.

The data selection baselines provide important controls. The Random Curriculum, which uses the same multi-stage training framework but assigns samples randomly to stages, achieves 8.08 on MT-Bench, substantially below both SM-Curri-PD (8.28) and DM-Curri S-PD (8.48). This result confirms that the performance gains of DM-Curri-DPO come from the principled difficulty ordering rather than from the multi-stage training structure itself. Reward-Weighted DPO performs comparably to standard preference optimization methods but below curriculum approaches, suggesting that importance weighting alone is less effective than structured data scheduling. The Pooled DPO baseline, which increases data quantity without structure, performs worse than Standard DPO on several metrics, underscoring that data scheduling quality, not merely data quantity, is the critical factor.

Among the four traversal strategies, the sum-based approaches consistently outperform the single-axis strategies. On UltraFeedback, S-PD achieves 8.48 on MT-Bench while PC-first and PD-first achieve 8.35 and 8.32 respectively. This is consistent with our gradient analysis: the diagonal traversal reduces both variance components simultaneously, while single-axis traversals leave one component uncontrolled. The optimal choice between S-PC and S-PD appears dataset-dependent. S-PD performs best on UltraFeedback with its GPT-4 derived preferences, while S-PC excels on OpenAssistant with potentially noisier human annotations. We hypothesize that when preference labels are less reliable, prioritizing prompt complexity provides a more stable curriculum anchor since PC is computed from the model’s own behavior rather than external annotations.

GSP-Curri-DPO further improves upon the best static strategies on both datasets, achieving 8.52 on MT-Bench and 41.8% on Arena-Hard for UltraFeedback, and 8.05 on MT-Bench and 36.5% on Arena-Hard for OpenAssistant. These gains confirm that allowing the model to adaptively select its traversal path yields a more effective curriculum than any fixed strategy, while also removing the need for manual strategy selection.

8.
Conclusion

We proposed a curriculum learning framework that decomposes alignment difficulty into Prompt Complexity and Pairwise Distinguishability, motivated by a gradient variance analysis of DPO. Building on this dual-metric space, we developed static traversal strategies (DM-Curri-DPO) and a self-paced variant (GSP-Curri-DPO) that dynamically discovers near-optimal traversal paths. Experiments across model scales from 7B to 70B, two datasets, and multiple evaluation protocols demonstrate consistent improvements, with the largest gains on reasoning-intensive tasks. The framework further generalizes to GRPO, confirming its broad applicability to preference optimization. Our approach has limitations: the PC metric introduces preprocessing overhead that may become significant at million-prompt scale, the benefits beyond the 70B parameter range remain untested, and the current two-dimensional characterization may not capture all relevant difficulty factors such as response length or domain specificity. We leave exploration of richer difficulty spaces and scaling validation to future work.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1

We provide a complete proof of the gradient variance decomposition stated in Proposition
3.1
.

Consider the DPO gradient for a single sample as the product
h=w\cdot g
, where
w=\sigma(-\beta\hat{r}_{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}
is the scalar weighting term and
g=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{w}|\mathbf{x})-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the directional term, with
d
denoting the parameter dimension.

The mini-batch gradient estimator is
\hat{G}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}w_{i}g_{i}
. For independent samples, we have:

(18)

\text{Var}[\hat{G}]=\frac{1}{B}\text{Var}[wg].

We analyze
\text{Var}[wg]
using the law of total variance. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the PC level and
\mathcal{Q}
denote the PD level. Under the assumption that PC and PD are independent:

\displaystyle\text{Var}[wg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|wg-\mathbb{E}[wg]\|^{2}]

(19)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[w^{2}\|g\|^{2}]-\|\mathbb{E}[wg]\|^{2}.

Using the identity
\text{Var}[wg]=\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]-\|\mathbb{E}[w]\mathbb{E}[g]\|^{2}
(which holds when
w
and
g
are independent, a condition approximately satisfied when PC and PD are independent), we can write:

\displaystyle\text{Var}[wg]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}[w]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Var}[\|g\|]

(20)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Var}[w]\cdot\text{Var}[\|g\|].

Dropping the non-negative cross term
\text{Var}[w]\cdot\text{Var}[\|g\|]
, we obtain the lower bound:

(21)

\text{Var}[wg]\geq\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Var}[g]+\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]\cdot\text{Var}[w].

Now we relate each term to its controlling difficulty metric. By the independence of PC and PD:

The term
\text{Var}[g]
can be decomposed via the law of total variance with respect to
\mathcal{P}
:

(22)

\text{Var}[g]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[\text{Var}[g|\mathcal{P}]]+\text{Var}_{\mathcal{P}}[\mathbb{E}[g|\mathcal{P}]].

The first term
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[\text{Var}[g|\mathcal{P}]]
represents the average within-PC-level variance, which is precisely
\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
in our notation. Since
\text{Var}[g]\geq\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
, the first component of the lower bound is at least
\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
.

Similarly,
\text{Var}[w]\geq\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
by an analogous decomposition with respect to
\mathcal{Q}
.

Combining these bounds yields the stated result:

(23)

\text{Var}[\hat{G}]\geq\frac{1}{B}\left(\mathbb{E}[w]^{2}\cdot\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]+\mathbb{E}[\|g\|^{2}]\cdot\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]\right).

The final claim follows directly: a curriculum that conditions on high-PD samples reduces
\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
but does not affect
\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
. Conversely, conditioning on low-PC samples reduces
\text{Var}_{\text{PC}}[g]
but not
\text{Var}_{\text{PD}}[w]
. A dual-metric curriculum that jointly conditions on both reduces both terms.
\square

Discussion of assumptions.

The proof relies on approximate independence of
w
and
g
, which holds when PC and PD are independent (empirical correlation:
r=0.12
on UltraFeedback,
r=0.08
on OpenAssistant). When PC and PD are weakly correlated, additional covariance terms arise but are second-order corrections (less than 8% of total variance in our experiments).

p=0.95
(maximum generation length 512 tokens). Perplexity is computed using Llama-2-7B as a fixed external language model, chosen from a different model family than the training models (Llama-3, Qwen2.5) to reduce systematic bias. The PC score is the standard deviation of these perplexities. The total preprocessing cost is approximately 18 GPU-hours for UltraFeedback (64K prompts) on a single A100 GPU, and is embarrassingly parallel across GPUs. We use a generation batch size of 8 and a perplexity evaluation batch size of 32.

B.2.
Grid Construction

The difficulty grid is constructed using quantile-based binning. For a
3\times 3
grid, we compute the 33rd and 67th percentiles of both PC and PD distributions, then assign each sample to a cell based on its quantile membership. This ensures roughly balanced sample counts per cell, which is important for providing sufficient training data at each curriculum stage.

B.4.
Human Evaluation Protocol

Three annotators with graduate-level education in computer science were recruited. Each received 300 evaluation instances (prompt + two blind, randomly ordered responses) and selected the preferred response based on helpfulness, accuracy, completeness, and clarity; a tie option was available. The 300 prompts were stratified across the difficulty grid: 100 from easy cells (
\mathcal{C}_{1,1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{1,2}

\mathcal{C}_{2,3}

). Ties (14% to 16% of judgments) were excluded from win rate computation.

The Random Curriculum uses the same multi-stage framework (9 groups, KL-adaptive reference, curriculum smoothing) but assigns samples to stages randomly. Its average performance (8.08) falls below Standard DPO (8.15), confirming that the multi-stage structure alone does not explain the gains. The slight decrease relative to Standard DPO is likely because random stage boundaries create artificial distribution shifts that the model must adapt to, without the compensating benefit of structured difficulty progression. The gap from Random Curriculum to SM-Curri-PD (0.20) and from SM-Curri-PD to DM-Curri S-PD (0.20) shows approximately equal contributions from difficulty-based ordering and the second difficulty dimension. The consistency across the five random seeds indicates that this finding is stable and not an artifact of any particular random partition. Taken together, these results establish that the performance gains of our framework arise specifically from the principled dual-metric difficulty ordering rather than from incidental aspects of the training pipeline.
