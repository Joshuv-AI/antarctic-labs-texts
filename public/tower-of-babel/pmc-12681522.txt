Periodontitis exacerbates metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease via the gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolism-AHR axis in obesity
Authors: Rixin Chen, Jun Qian, Qunyi Wang, Yue Li, Zhonghan Xu, Miaomiao Zhang, Min Wang, Hua Nie, Wenrong Yang, Xin Tong, Fuhua Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPeriodontitis is linked to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD); however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsPeriodontitis was investigated in male mice with high-fat diet (HFD)-induced MASLD. Gut microbiome and metabolomic profiling were conducted using16S rRNA gene sequencing, along with both untargeted and targeted metabolomic profiling via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Intestinal barrier integrity was evaluated by histopathological analysis. Faecal microbiota transplantation was conducted and the vital role of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) was confirmed using Ahr gene knockout (Ahr−/−) mice. The protective roles of tryptophan derivative indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) and the tryptophan-metabolising probiotic Limosilactobacillus reuteri were assessed following their administration via oral gavage. The impact of endotoxin-mediated hyperinflammation on hepatic mitochondrial dynamics was examined in vitro.FindingsPeriodontitis promoted MASLD, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and tryptophan metabolism depletion, leading to intestinal barrier dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and endotoxin overexpression in HFD-fed mice. Periodontitis-accelerated MASLD was attenuated in HFD-fed Ahr−/− mice. In an AHR-dependent manner, IPA or L. reuteri alleviated the detrimental effects of periodontitis on MASLD progression, intestinal barrier impairment, systemic inflammation, and endotoxin translocation to the liver. Conditioned medium from endotoxin-stimulated THP-1 cells promoted mitochondrial fission in HepG2 cells by upregulating Drp1 expression.InterpretationPeriodontitis exacerbates MASLD by disrupting the gut microbiota–tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis, leading to intestinal barrier dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and endotoxin translocation. Endotoxin plays a pivotal role in promoting hepatic mitochondrial fission during the exacerbation of MASLD by periodontitis. AHR agonists offer a novel intervention strategy for patients with comorbid MASLD and periodontitis.FundingThis work was supported by the Jiangsu Province Key Research and Development Program [No. BE2022670]; National Natural Science Foundation of China [No. 82270979]; Jiangsu Provincial Medical Key Discipline Cultivation Unit [No. JSDW202246]; and High-Level Hospital Construction Project of Nanjing Stomatological Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Institute of Stomatology, Nanjing University [No. 0224C001].Keywords: Periodontitis, Gut microbiota, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Tryptophan metabolism, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, Probiotics

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Periodontal disease—a prevalent oral disease that affects up to 90% of individuals globally—has been associated with an increased risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). MASLD is the most common liver disease worldwide, affecting >25% of the global population. However, the mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. The gut microbiota plays a well-established role in MASLD progression and undergoes compositional shifts in periodontitis, contributing to the pathogenesis of extraoral conditions. However, the effect of periodontitis-induced gut metabolic dysregulation on MASLD development and its underlying mechanisms remain elusive.

Added value of this study

Periodontitis promoted MASLD and hepatic mitochondrial fission in mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD). Mechanistically, it altered the gut microbiome and reduced the production of gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites, resulting in impaired intestinal barrier function, intestinal inflammation, and increased endotoxin production in HFD-fed mice via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signalling pathway. The effects of periodontitis on the liver and colon were abrogated in HFD-fed Ahr gene knockout (Ahr−/−) mice. Faecal microbiota transplantation directly demonstrated that periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD was mediated by the gut microbiota. Supplementation with either the tryptophan-derived metabolite indole-3-propionic acid or the tryptophan-metabolising probiotic Limosilactobacillus reuteri via oral gavage diminished the impact of periodontitis on the liver and colon in HFD-fed mice. Conversely, these protective effects were abolished in HFD-fed Ahr−/− mice with periodontitis. Finally, we identified endotoxins as potent hepatotoxic factors that disrupted mitochondrial dynamics in HepG2 cells by increasing Drp1 expression under MASLD conditions in vitro.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study combines multi-omics approaches with integrated analyses to identify the gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis as the specific key metabolic pathway underlying periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD. Targeted modulation of this axis with metabolite-based or probiotic-based strategies provides a foundation for developing novel therapies for patients with comorbid MASLD and periodontitis.

Introduction

Periodontitis, a highly prevalent polymicrobial oral disease, is closely associated with multiple systemic conditions, including the metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).1,2 Characterised by intrahepatocyte lipid accumulation (>5%), MASLD represents the most common liver disease worldwide, affecting >25% of the global population.3 The high global prevalence of MASLD parallels increasing rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome.4 A Japan-based cohort indicated that moderate-to-severe periodontitis elevated liver fibrosis risk (OR 1.82, 95% CI 0.94–3.49).5 Consistently, elevated serum antibody titres against periodontal pathogens are significantly associated with fibrosis progression in patients with biopsy-proven MASLD.6 Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms underlying these conditions remain unexplored.

Growing evidence links the pathogenesis and progression of MASLD to intestinal homoeostatic disruption.7 Periodontitis, an infectious disease driven by oral dysbiosis, facilitates the translocation of oral microorganisms to the gut, thereby perturbing intestinal homoeostasis.8,9 Periodontitis-related salivary microbiota induces gut dysbiosis and compromises intestinal barrier function in mice.10 The liver is anatomically and physiologically connected to the intestine and is highly vulnerable to such pathogenic disturbances.11 Oral administration of periodontal pathogens induces hepatic steatosis through gut flora disequilibrium.12 Notably, the salivary microbiota of patients with periodontitis was found to exacerbate MASLD in obese mice.13 Furthermore, gut microbiota-derived metabolites—including secondary bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, and tryptophan catabolites—modulate hepatic metabolic functions through local colonic signalling and systemic effects.14 Despite these connections, whether periodontitis-induced gut dysbiosis drives MASLD progression through metabolic derangements remains unclear. Elucidating microbiome-metabolome crosstalk is therefore essential for identifying targeted therapeutic interventions.

Microbial tryptophan metabolites are implicated in the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases, including obesity and MASLD.15 Both preclinical and clinical studies have shown that MASLD is associated with a reduced tryptophan metabolism by the gut flora.16 The tryptophan derivative indole propionic acid attenuates MASLD symptoms and related conditions in a dose-dependent manner by strengthening intestinal epithelial barrier function and promoting intestinal homoeostasis.17 Tryptophan metabolism involves three major pathways—the 5-hydroxytryptamine, kynurenine, and gut microbiota indole pathways.18 Metabolites from the indole pathway, including indole and its derivatives such as indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), indole-3-acrylic acid, and indole-3-lactic acid,19 are ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR).19,20 These compounds contribute critically to the maintenance of the intestinal barrier, regulation of inflammatory responses, and modulation of immune cell development and function.19,20 The AHR is a ligand-dependent transcriptional nuclear receptor that is expressed in enterocytes and immune cells.19,20 Additionally, plasma tryptophan metabolites and the AHR signalling pathway are significantly suppressed in periodontitis.21 Infection with periodontal pathogen exacerbates inflammation by inhibiting the AHR signalling pathway in macrophages.22 Based on these observations, we hypothesised that periodontitis may aggravate MASLD via the microbial tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis.

In this study, we identified that tryptophan metabolism was critically involved in periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD using microbiome and metabolomic profiling. Using Ahr knockout (Ahr−/−) mice, we established the essential function of the AHR in mediating this pathological interaction. Furthermore, we investigated the therapeutic potential of IPA and the tryptophan-metabolising probiotic L. reuteri in mice with MASLD and periodontitis. We aim to provide mechanistic insights into how periodontitis influences MASLD within the gut–liver axis framework and offer promising perspectives for the aetiology and treatment of both conditions.

Methods

Ethics

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Nanjing Agricultural University (Nos. PZW2022040 and PZW2022058, China) and Aniphe Biolab (No. JSAB24002B, China), in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines.

Mice

This study involved 6–8-week-old, wild-type, male C57BL/6 mice and Ahr gene knockout (Ahr−/−) mice on a C57BL/6J background. Mice were assigned to receive either a standard chow diet or a high-fat diet (HFD; for detailed composition, see Supplementary Table S3). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility with a 12-h light–dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 40%–70% humidity. The mice were acclimatised for 1 week before the experiment and were anaesthetised with isoflurane and euthanised with a sodium pentobarbital overdose to minimise suffering and pain. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Genotyping strategy

Ahr−/− mice generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and housed at Aniphe Biolab (Jiangsu, China). The Ahr gene has three transcripts, and the knockout of exon 2 of the Ahr-202 (ENSMUST00000116436.8) transcript, which contains a 188 bp coding sequence, disrupts Ahr function (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Homozygous knockout mice were verified using PCR and gel electrophoresis with the following primers: F1, CTTATCAGTGGGTGGAGCAATAAG; R1, CCTTGCTATTTCAGCATGGTG (knockout: 362 bp); F2, CAATGGAGCTGTTTGGATGGTAC; and R2, TGCCTTATTCCCACTGAACCTG (knockout: 0 bp, Supplementary Fig. S1b).

Preparation of bacteria

L. reuteri was cultured in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe media at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Bacterial purity was confirmed through Gram staining and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Bacteria in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The bacterial cell pellet was washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in PBS (109 colony-forming units [CFU]/mL) for further use.

Experimental periodontitis

At week 10, experimental periodontitis was induced by bilateral ligation around the maxillary second molars with 4–0 nonabsorbable sutures, whereas the control mice were subjected to sham ligation. Once the ligatures fell off, the tooth would be re-ligated. After 6 weeks, mice were euthanised and the periodontitis was confirmed through haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections and micro–computed tomography (μCT) scans.

Oral administration of IPA or L. reuteri

IPA (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction; catalogue #HY-W015229) was prepared in sterile PBS and administered to mice by oral gavage at a dosage of 50 mg/kg, three times per week for 6 weeks. To evaluate the effects of L. reuteri, mice received 200 μL of bacterial suspension via oral gavage on the same schedule. Control animals were given an equal volume of PBS as a vehicle control.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

At week 10, the recipient mice received an antibiotic cocktail (ABX: vancomycin, 1.5 mg/mouse; neomycin sulphate, 3 mg/mouse; metronidazole, 3 mg/mouse; and ampicillin, 3 mg/mouse) via oral gavage daily for 2 weeks to eliminate endogenous gut flora. Then one pellet of fresh faeces per mouse from donor mice in the same group (n = 6) was collected every other day, and pooled with 1.2 mL of sterile PBS to form a faecal homogenate. The supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (200×g for 10 min) and resuspended in 1.2 mL of sterile PBS. Each recipient mouse received 200 μL of bacterial suspension.

16S rRNA sequencing

Total genomic DNA from the gut bacteria was extracted from the caecal contents (∼100 mg) using kits (MagPure Stool/Soil DNA KF Kit, Guangzhou, China). The V3–V4 variable regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified with the universal primers 343 F: 5ʹ-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3ʹ and 798 R: 5ʹ-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3ʹ. The amplicon quality was visualised via agarose gel electrophoresis, and the amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (Agencourt, Beverly, MA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 250 bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data were in FASTQ format, and the paired-end reads were preprocessed, trimmed, and filtered to remove chimeric reads using the DADA2 method with the QIIME2 package (2020.11), as described previously.23,24 Taxon assignments were determined using non-singleton amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), and a representative read for each ASV was selected using QIIME2. All representative reads were annotated and BLAST-searched against the Silva database (version 138).

Liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry

An untargeted metabolome analysis of the liver and caecal samples was performed using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system coupled to a Q Exactive quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer to measure metabolites in positive and negative modes. The binary gradient elution system consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min and a column temperature of 45 °C. Metabolomic data were obtained using an MS detector in full-scan mode, and quality control samples were injected at regular intervals for monitoring. Data were matched against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) online database. Analyses of tryptophan-derived metabolites and the kynurenic acid-to-kynurenine ratio were performed.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Tryptophan derivatives were quantified using external purified standards. Approximately 50 mg of caecal contents and 50 μL of serum samples were extracted with methanol, and 10 μL of an internal standard mixed solution (250 ng/mL) was added for normalisation. Serial dilutions ranging from 0.01 to 5000 ng/mL were prepared in 50% methanol. To ensure instrument stability, a quality control sample was inserted for every 10 test samples. Data quality was further verified by both Pearson (r = 0.99, p < 0.01) and Spearman (r = 0.999, p < 0.01) correlation analyses. Standard curves were constructed by plotting the analyte-to-internal standard peak area ratio versus its concentration ratio (or the analyte response versus its concentration), with a satisfactory residual plot and acceptable accuracy of back-calculated standard concentrations. Integrated peak areas for all the detected samples were applied to the linear standard curve equation to calculate metabolite concentrations in tissue samples.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error from at least three independent experiments. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene's test. For two-group comparisons, the two-tailed Student's t-test was used for normally distributed data; otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD post hoc test was applied to normally distributed data, while the Kruskal–Wallis H test with Dunn's post hoc test was employed for non-normally distributed data. Alternatively, for pre-planned specific comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied following ANOVA. For longitudinal analyses within the same subjects, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. For 16S rRNA sequencing data, β-diversity analysis was conducted in QIIME2 software based on an unweighted UniFrac distance matrix and visualised via principal coordinate analysis. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was performed using the LEfSe package (version 1.0.0) within the R software (version 3.6.1). Associations between variables were evaluated using Pearson's correlation (for normally distributed data) or Spearman's correlation (for non-normally distributed data or ordinal data). All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Role of funders

The funders had no role in the study design, data analysis or manuscript preparation.

Results

Periodontitis exacerbates MASLD in HFD-fed mice

HFD-fed mice were subjected to periodontitis for 6 weeks to assess its impact on MASLD (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. S2a and b). A 20% increase in body weight was observed in the obese mice compared with the normal-chow diet-fed mice by week 6 (Fig. 1b). Food intake remained unaffected by periodontitis (Fig. 1c). However, the weights of liver and inguinal, epididymal, and perirenal white adipose tissues were significantly greater in the HPD group compared with the HFD group, although the liver-to-total weight ratio did not differ significantly between these groups (Fig. 1d). Biochemically, HPD mice exhibited a significant increase in plasma triglycerides (TG), and a non-significant upward trend in aspartate aminotransferase and hepatic TG, compared with HFD mice, despite comparable levels of plasma total cholesterol and alanine aminotransferase (Fig. 1e and f). H&E and Oil Red O staining revealed higher NAFLD activity score (NAS) scores and lipid area fractions in HPD mice compared with HFD mice (Fig. 1g and h). Additionally, immunofluorescence detection of the macrophage marker F4/80 confirmed greater cell infiltration in HPD mice than in HFD mice (Fig. 1i). Periodontitis promoted mitochondrial fission in hepatocytes, as evidenced by elevated expression of Drp1 (encoded by Dnm1l) and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 1j–l). However, the alterations in these parameters between the Ctrl and PD groups were marginal.

To characterise the underlying molecular and metabolic changes, we conducted RNA sequencing and metabolome profiling on liver tissues from HFD and HPD mice. In the HPD group, 29 genes were significantly downregulated and 28 were upregulated compared with the HFD group (|Log2FC| ≥ 1; FDR-adjusted p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S2c). Gene set enrichment analysis highlighted upregulation of pathways related to reactive oxygen species metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and mitochondrial outer membrane activity in HPD mice (Supplementary Fig. S2d). Fatty acid biosynthesis was the most prominent pathway, with enrichment of saturated fatty acids (including stearic acid and palmitic acid) and decreased levels of unsaturated fatty acids (including palmitoleic acid, linolenic acid, and eicosapentaenoic acid) in the HPD group compared with the HFD group (Supplementary Fig. S2e and f). Collectively, these results suggested that periodontitis aggravated MASLD, mitochondrial fission, and fatty acid metabolic dysregulation in obesity.

Periodontitis inhibits the gut microbial tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis in HFD-fed mice

To investigate the role of the gut microbiome in the progression of MASLD, we conducted 16S rRNA gene sequencing of caecal contents. β-Diversity analysis revealed that periodontitis markedly altered the microbial communities in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 2a). At the phylum level, the abundance of Bacteroidota decreased by 38.35%, whereas the abundance of Desulfobacterota increased by 1.88-fold, resulting in a significantly lower Bacteroidetes-to-Firmicutes ratio in the HPD group than in the HFD group (p < 0.05; Fig. 2b). At the genus level, Erysipelatoclostridium, Mucispirillum, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, were significantly more abundant, whereas Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella were less abundant in HPD mice compared with HFD mice (Fig. 2b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified tryptophan metabolism as one of the most significantly altered pathways (Fig. 2c), with a broad reduction in multiple tryptophan derivatives and a lower kynurenic acid/kynurenine ratio in HPD mice than in HFD mice (Fig. 2d). Spearman's correlation coefficients indicated that Muribaculaceae and Lactobacillus were positively correlated with tryptophan derivatives, whereas Erysipelatoclostridium was negatively correlated (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, targeted metabolomics revealed that plasma levels of IPA and nicotinic acid were significantly decreased in HPD mice (Fig. 2f). Given that tryptophan-derived metabolites act as bioactive AHR ligands, we next assessed AHR signalling activity in the colon.19,20 Notably, periodontitis suppressed the expression of Ahr and its downstream effector cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily A member 1 (Cyp1a1), along with Ahr protein levels, in the colon of HPD mice (Fig. 2g and h). However, no marked differences were observed between the Ctrl and PD groups. These findings indicated that periodontitis aggravated gut microbiota dysbiosis and inhibited the microbial tryptophan metabolism–AHR pathway in HFD-fed mice.

Periodontitis promotes gut barrier damage and intestinal and systemic inflammation in HFD-fed mice

Since the tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis is essential for gut barrier function, we evaluated the impact of periodontitis on colon histology. H&E and Alcian blue staining showed HPD group had significantly reduced crypt depth, a thinned mucus layer, and a trend toward lower villus height compared with the HFD group (Fig. 3a and b). The expression of tight junction-associated proteins ZO-1 (encoded by Tjp1) was significantly downregulated in HPD mice relative to HFD-fed mice, albeit the Occludin (encoded by Ocln) was not significantly altered (Fig. 3c and d). The HPD group showed a greater infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages in the intestinal submucosa than the HFD group (Fig. 3e). The mRNA expressions of Tjp1 and Cldn2 in the colon were markedly lower in HPD mice than in HFD mice (Fig. 3f). Conversely, plasma concentrations of IL-1β, IL-17A, and endotoxin, which reflect the degree of systemic inflammation, were significantly elevated in the HPD group (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, Spearman's correlation analysis indicated that MASLD pathological indicators were positively associated with circulating endotoxin and IL-1β levels, and negatively correlated with AHR-dependent intestinal barrier integrity and IPA levels (Fig. 3h). These results suggested that periodontitis-induced disruption of the tryptophan metabolism–AHR pathway damaged the intestinal barrier and promoted systemic endotoxin release, which may exacerbate MASLD via the gut–liver axis in obesity.

Periodontitis aggravates MASLD in HFD-fed mice via the gut microbiota

To determine whether the periodontitis-associated gut microbiota contributed to MASLD progression, we conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in HFD-fed recipients (Supplementary Fig. S3a). 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed high similarity in the gut microbial profiles of recipient and donor mice, indicating successful microbial colonisation after FMT (Supplementary Fig. S3b and c). Compared with the FMT-H group, the FMT-P group exhibited more severe MASLD, as evidenced by higher NAS scores, increased hepatic lipid accumulation, upregulation of Drp1 expression, and enhanced inflammation (Supplementary Fig. S3d–i). Similarly, intestinal barrier impairment and serum endotoxin levels were markedly elevated in FMT-P recipients, whereas AHR activity was downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S3j–l). Pearson correlation analysis revealed that plasma endotoxin levels were negatively correlated with intestinal Ahr expression (r = −0.85, p = 0.0053) and positively correlated with hepatic Dnm1l expression (r = 0.7570, p = 0.0112), suggesting that endotoxins may contribute to the aggravation of MASLD underlying the periodontitis-associated gut microbiota (Supplementary Fig. S3m).

Ahr knockdown diminishes the effects of periodontitis on the liver and colon in HFD-fed mice

To further investigate whether periodontitis mediated MASLD progression in an AHR-dependent manner, we induced periodontitis in HFD-fed Ahr−/− mice (Fig. 4a and b). Histological analyses indicated that periodontitis had a mild effect on liver pathology in Ahr−/− mice, with comparable hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, biochemical indices, and mitochondrial dynamics between the knockout groups (Fig. 4c–h). Furthermore, the detrimental effects of periodontitis on mucosal barrier integrity, intestinal inflammation, and systemic cytokine and endotoxin levels were abolished (Fig. 4i–n). These findings indicated that the AHR pathway was essential for periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD in obesity.

IPA mitigates MASLD, gut barrier damage, and endotoxaemia in HPD mice

IPA has been reported to ameliorate metabolic conditions and enhance intestinal barrier function.25,26 To determine whether rescuing the periodontitis-induced deficiency of IPA in the caecum and serum could confer therapeutic benefits, we administered exogenous IPA to HPD mice via oral gavage (Supplementary Fig. S4a). While IPA showed negligible effects on the severity of periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. S4b and c), it significantly attenuated body weight gain, hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and dyslipidaemia in HPD mice (Fig. 5a–c, e–g). Additionally, IPA prevented mitochondrial fragmentation by significantly reducing Drp1 expression; however, no significant changes were observed in the expression of Fis1, Mfn2, and Opa1 (Fig. 5d and h). Metabolic profiling of liver samples indicated notably decreased levels of arachidonic acid metabolites and increased levels of linoleic acid metabolites in HIPA mice than in HPD mice (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, IPA supplementation restored intestinal barrier function, as evidenced by increased protein expression of ZO-1 and Ahr, and upregulation of the genes Tjp1, Ocln, Cldn2, Ahr, and Cyp1a1 in the colon (Fig. 5j and k). IPA levels in the caecum and serum were restored following IPA therapy (Fig. 5l). In contrast, serum IL-1β, TNF-α, and endotoxin levels were decreased in IPA-treated mice (Fig. 5m). IPA exerted negligible effects on the gut microbiota (Supplementary Fig. S4d). Together, our findings demonstrated that IPA ameliorated periodontitis-aggravated MASLD via the intestinal AHR pathway, thereby restoring gut barrier integrity and attenuating endotoxemia.

L. reuteri alleviates MASLD, intestinal homoeostasis imbalance, and endotoxaemia in HPD mice

The probiotic L. reuteri, which can metabolise tryptophan into bioactive derivatives through specific microbial pathways, is considered a potential therapeutic intervention for MASLD.27,28 To evaluate the efficacy of L. reuteri against periodontitis-associated MASLD and to elucidate its underlying mechanisms, we administered the probiotic to HPD mice via oral gavage (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Despite the persistence of periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. S5b, c), L. reuteri administration ameliorated liver histology and reduced body weight gain in HPD mice (Fig. 6a, b, e). Additionally, HLR mice exhibited improved hepatic biochemical profiles and reduced inflammation relative to HPD mice (Fig. 6c, f, g), with decreased expression of Drp1 and Fis1 (Fig. 6d and h). Liver metabolome analysis revealed that L. reuteri notably replenished linoleic acid and tryptophan metabolism in HPD mice (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, HLR mice demonstrated a better colonic mucosal barrier than HPD mice, with higher Ahr and Cyp1a1 expression in the colon (Fig. 6j–l). Metagenomic sequencing and LEfSe analysis identified Ligilactobacillus and Ileibacterium as differentially abundant genera between HPD and HLR mice (Supplementary Fig. S5d). Specifically, Ligilactobacillus murinus, Ileibacterium valens, and Allobaculum sp910579655 were more prominent in HLR mice compared with HPD mice (Fig. 6m). KEGG enrichment analysis suggested these genera were primarily involved in tryptophan metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S5e). Additionally, plasma IPA concentration increased, whereas TNF-α and endotoxin concentrations significantly decreased in the HLR group compared with the HPD group (Fig. 6n and o). Therefore, we concluded that L. reuteri alleviated periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD by restoring intestinal homoeostasis, enhancing AHR activity through tryptophan metabolism, and reducing systemic endotoxin levels.

Ahr knockdown diminishes the beneficial effects of L. reuteri and IPA on the liver and colon in HPD mice

To determine whether the hepatoprotective and colon-protective effects of L. reuteri and IPA were mediated through an AHR-dependent manner, we administered them to obese Ahr−/− mice with periodontitis (Fig. 7a and b). As expected, neither L. reuteri nor IPA ameliorated MASLD in Ahr−/− mice, as evidenced by persistent lipid droplet accumulation, hepatic inflammation, and aberrant mitochondrial dynamics (Fig. 7c–h). The protective effects of L. reuteri or IPA on the colon, systemic inflammation, and endotoxaemia were eliminated in Ahr−/− mice (Fig. 7i–n), indicating that the AHR pathway was essential for the attenuation of periodontitis-aggravated MASLD by both L. reuteri and IPA.

Endotoxin-mediated inflammation impairs the mitochondrial dynamics of hepatocytes under MASLD conditions

Endotoxin acts as a potent driver of MASLD, and excessive translocation of endotoxin to the liver can activate immune cells and initiate an inflammatory cascade.29 To identify the specific mechanism underlying periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD in vitro, we established a classical model using palmitic acid (PA) to induce hepatocyte steatosis. Cell viability assays and Oil Red O staining confirmed that 0.06 mM PA exhibited favourable cytocompatibility and pro-MASLD effects, and was thus selected for subsequent in vitro modelling of MASLD (Supplementary Fig. S6a and b). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that combined LPS and PA treatment for 48 h promoted M1 macrophage polarisation, as evidenced by elevated CD86 and diminished CD206 expression (Fig. 8a). This pro-inflammatory shift was further confirmed at the transcriptional level, with increased iNOS and decreased CD206 mRNA levels (Fig. 8b). Furthermore, the secretion of macrophage-derived inflammatory factors, notably IL-1β and TNF-α, was significantly enhanced by the co-treatment in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 8c, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Accordingly, HepG2 cells were treated with the 48 h-conditioned medium (Fig. 8d). This treatment significantly impaired mitochondrial membrane potential, as shown by the reduced TMRM-labelled hepatocyte population (Fig. 8e and f), and concurrently elevated Drp1 expression (Fig. 8g and Supplementary Fig. S6c). These findings collectively suggested that endotoxin-driven hyperinflammation disrupted hepatic mitochondrial dynamics by promoting fission, thereby aggravating MASLD.

Discussion

Despite the close association between periodontitis and MASLD progression via factors circulating in the blood,30 little is known about the role of the periodontitis-associated gut microbiota in MASLD. In this study, comprehensive in vivo investigations indicated that periodontitis exacerbated MASLD by impairing the gut microbiome-derived tryptophan metabolism–AHR signalling pathway and that IPA or L. reuteri could counterbalance these effects in the context of obesity. Mechanistically, periodontitis-induced endotoxin overexpression stimulated the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, in hepatic macrophages, compromising hepatic mitochondrial membrane dynamics and metabolic processes in MASLD (Supplementary Fig. S8).

MASLD is strongly associated with the disruption of intestinal homoeostasis, including the gut microbiota, metabolites, and the mucosal barrier.7 Periodontitis can influence the gut microbiome through the oral–gut axis, contributing to the onset and progression of MASLD.31 However, changes in the gut flora are inconsistent and context-specific. Kuraji et al. reported that the relative abundance of Firmicutes decreased, whereas Bacteroidetes abundance increased in mice with periodontitis and MASLD.32 In contrast, the salivary microbiota associated with periodontitis promoted MASLD through increases in Firmicutes in the gut.13 Our results showed that the Bacteroidetes-to-Firmicutes ratio was significantly lower in HPD mice than in HFD mice due to a decreased proportion of Bacteroidetes, which aligned with previous findings in patients with MASLD.33 Disparities in methods of periodontitis induction and oral microbiomes between humans and mice may contribute to interstudy discrepancies. Notably, Erysipelatoclostridium, a metagenomic signature associated with MASLD, predominated in the HPD group.34,35 This finding aligns with the translocation of oral-origin Erysipelotrichaceae to the distal gut observed in patients with severe periodontitis.10 Gut microbiota dysbiosis was mild in mice fed a standard chow diet, suggesting that obesity was a prerequisite for periodontitis to worsen gut microbiota disorders. To evaluate this hypothesis, the periodontitis-associated gut microbiota was transplanted into obese mice. Pathogenic phenotypes in the liver and colon were recapitulated in recipient mice, confirming that the gut microbiota was a crucial factor in periodontitis-exacerbated MASLD.

Numerous studies have demonstrated bidirectional crosstalk between the liver and microbiome in MASLD36; however, few have investigated metabolites and related signalling pathways.37 Tryptophan metabolism, in particular, has been studied extensively.23 The gut microbiota is essential for the metabolism of tryptophan into indole compounds, which can then be converted into various active products.38 These metabolites act as ligands for AHR and impaired AHR ligands are considered key indicators of metabolic syndrome.16 Both preclinical and clinical studies suggest that certain tryptophan metabolites may have beneficial effects on MASLD.39 Significantly lower IPA levels were observed in the suspected MASLD group than in healthy individuals.39 IPA supplementation dramatically reduces body weight gain, enhances glucose metabolism, and lessens insulin resistance.40 Infection with oral pathogen can disrupt gut flora homoeostasis and impair microbial tryptophan metabolism.41 Similarly, our results revealed significantly decreased the levels of tryptophan derivatives, specifically IPA, in the gut and serum of HPD mice, along with a notable decrease in AHR expression in the colon. However, these effects were abrogated in the Ctrl and PD groups, consistent with the results of the microbial analyses, indicating a close interplay between gut bacteria and tryptophan metabolites. Spearman's correlation analysis further showed that Muribaculaceae, Alloprevotella, and Lactobacillus were positively correlated and that Erysipelatoclostridium was negatively correlated with intestinal tryptophan derivatives and AHR levels, consistent with previous findings.42,43 To further validate the role of AHR, we used Ahr−/− mice and confirmed the critical involvement of this receptor in periodontitis-induced MASLD exacerbation.

The tryptophan metabolism–AHR axis plays critical roles in colonic barrier function, inflammatory responses, and immune activity.44,45 Impaired tryptophan metabolism (particularly indole derivatives) is closely associated with inflammatory bowel disease in humans and mice.46 AHR deficiency results in the prolonged differentiation of intestinal epithelial cells and increased lethality during infection.47 Conversely, IPA intervention significantly enhances the secretory function of goblet cells and mucin activity.26 IPA can increase the expression of tight junction proteins and inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines via sirtuin 3.26,48 Despite the established association between AHR and intestinal homoeostasis, the role of AHR-mediated signalling in periodontitis-associated MASLD remains elusive. A recent study implicated imbalanced tryptophan metabolism and reduced AHR activity in hyperactive colonic inflammation and the pathogenesis of periodontitis-induced insulin resistance.49 In our study, periodontitis disrupted the AHR pathway, leading to a compromised gut barrier, increased colonic inflammation, and systemic endotoxins, ultimately promoting MASLD. IPA alleviated MASLD in HPD mice by restoring the AHR-mediated intestinal barrier and reducing systemic endotoxin levels. However, IPA had a minor effect on the gut microbiota, which was inconsistent with previous findings.48 One plausible explanation is that a high-fat-diet could suppress the growth of IPA-producing bacteria. In addition to AHR, IPA has been identified as an endogenous ligand for the intestinal pregnane X receptor and may partly mediate inhibition of the intestinal inflammatory response and steatosis.50,51 Notably, AHR has a higher affinity for tryptophan-derived ligands in humans than in mice,52 and ACSM2A influences circulating IPA levels.53 Consequently, further investigations are warranted to determine the optimal dosage for human use before clinical trials.

Excessive levels of circulating endotoxins, as observed in the presence of an impaired gut barrier, are a hallmark of MASLD.29,54 The increased translocation of endotoxin into the bloodstream acts as a potent trigger for the recruitment of monocyte-like macrophages to the liver and the activation of hepatic immune cells through toll-like receptor 4, resulting in an inflammatory cascade via the NF-κB/MAPK signalling pathways.29 Alternatively, endotoxins promote oxidative stress and reduce mitochondrial DNA copy number and membrane potential in hepatocytes.55,56 Our transcriptome and metabolome analyses revealed that periodontitis-worsened MASLD was more complex, involving not only lipid accumulation but also dysregulated mitochondrial fitness and fatty acid metabolism, with endotoxin likely serving as a key mediator. Endotoxin levels were positively associated with the pathogenetic features of MASLD, including Drp1 levels, NAS scores, and hepatic TG levels. IPA decreased the expression of Drp1 and improved metabolic profiles in the liver by repairing intestinal barrier integrity, suppressing endotoxin production, and inactivating macrophages. Mitochondria are essential organelles for liver metabolism, and mitochondrial fission plays a mechanistic role.57,58 The periodontal pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis promotes Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission in endothelial cells,59 indicating that endotoxins derived from periodontal bacteria may also influence the liver through the bloodstream and damage mitochondria. To validate this, we evaluated the effects of IPA supplementation. IPA attenuated MASLD but had little effect on periodontitis, suggesting that gut microbes played a primary role in this process. Notably, the levels of arachidonic acid metabolites—implicated in the development and progression of metabolic diseases—were reduced in HIPA mice, possibly due to the direct anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of IPA.60

Microbiota-targeted therapies using probiotics, which are considered safe, well-tolerated, and cost-effective for long-term application, have shown promise for improving MASLD.61,62 Multi-omics analysis in our study indicated that Lactobacillus was positively correlated with multiple tryptophan metabolites. L. reuteri, a subgroup of the Lactobacillus genus, is a recognised probiotic with the potential to modulate the gut ecosystem and metabolic disorders.63,64 The depletion or absence of L. reuteri has been linked to elevated cholesterol anabolism and liver tumourigenesis.65 Additionally, L. reuteri catabolises tryptophan into various compounds via the aromatic amino acid aminotransferase.28 Furthermore, L. reuteri has been proposed as a potent yet safe probiotic with antibacterial properties against oral pathogens.66 However, whether and how L. reuteri alleviates periodontitis-related MASLD remains unclear. In our study, L. reuteri reshaped the gut microbial composition of HPD mice, characterised by enrichment of L. murinus, I. valens, and Allobaculum sp910579655. Interactions between Lactobacillus spp. and I. valens has been shown to improve hepatic lipid metabolism and regulate chronic inflammation in atherosclerosis.67 Similarly, Lactobacillus may interact with the genus Allobaculum to exert a protective effect against liver lipid accumulation during metabolic stress.68 Elevated IPA levels after L. reuteri administration substantiated the mechanistic link between this probiotic and IPA-mediated protection against exacerbated MASLD. Notably, L. reuteri treatment significantly restored tryptophan metabolism in the liver, potentially through the recovery of mitochondrial function. These findings supported L. reuteri as a promising therapeutic candidate for periodontitis-associated MASLD. However, the highly heterogeneous nature of the gut microbiome across individuals may underlie the variable efficacy of L. reuteri, which remains to be validated in future well-designed clinical trials.69

This study had several limitations. First, a considerable disparity exists between human and murine oral/gut microbiota and metabolic profiles. Therefore, further collection and analyses of human oral and gut microbiomes are warranted to track oral-gut metagenomic dynamics in patients with comorbid MASLD and periodontitis. Additionally, intestinal epithelial cell-specific Ahr knockout (VillinCreAhrfl/fl) models with larger sample sizes are necessary to define cell-type-specific mechanisms underlying periodontitis-associated MASLD and the protective effects of specific interventions. Furthermore, our study employed an HFD-induced MASLD model to reflect the clinical context of obesity-related MASLD. To evaluate the broader relevance of our findings, future studies utilising alternative dietary models—such as methionine-choline deficient, high-fat high-cholesterol, or Western diets—are warranted. While the exclusive use of male mice minimised confounding effects of the female oestrous cycle, subsequent research should validate the applicability of these findings in females. Despite these limitations, our findings provide experimental evidence that periodontitis promotes MASLD via the gut microbiota-tryptophan-AHR axis, highlighting the therapeutic significance of metabolite/probiotic-mediated feedback regulation of AHR activity in preventing periodontitis-associated metabolic disorders.
