​​Dielectric and simulated radiation shielding properties of cross-linked PMMA/ZrO2 and PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites
Authors: Rasha R. Alharbi, Jeenat Aslam, Ali H. Bashal, Mohammed Muzibur Rahman, Nazeeha S. Alkayal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanocomposites of modified poly (methyl methacrylate) (C-PMMA) reinforced with zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) and cerium dioxide (CeO2) nanoparticles were prepared through in situ polymerization. Their dielectric and radiation shielding properties were also evaluated. We used p-phenylenediamine (p-PDA) to modify the PMMA matrix to make it more rigid and stable at high temperatures. Ceramic nanoparticles were incorporated into the composite at various loadings ranging from 5 to 40 wt%. Structural, morphological, and thermal analyses using FTIR, XRD, SEM–EDX, and TGA all showed strong interfacial adhesion and even distribution of nanoparticles. The composites showed enhanced thermal stability, with residual mass rising to 25–30% and onset degradation temperature increasing to ~ 305 °C compared to 280 °C for pristine C-PMMA. Dielectric analysis at 110 °C shows remarkable improvement in electrical properties. The dielectric constant (ε′) increased from ~ 3.0 for pristine C-PMMA to ~ 4.6 for the 5 wt% ZrO2 composite at 500 kHz, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 53%. Moreover, the AC conductivity improved from ~ 6–7 µS (C-PMMA at 1000 kHz) to ~ 25–26 µS for the 5 wt% ZrO2 composite, which is almost 3–4 times better. Composites of CeO2 showed mild yet systematic enhancement, with a conductivity of approximately 8 µS at 40% filler loadings. Radiation attenuation simulated experiments conducted with assistance from the Phy-X/PSD software revealed that both CeO2- and ZrO2-filled nanocomposites. The linear attenuation coefficient confirmed the great improvement in low-energy photon-shielding capability at high ceramic loadings at 0.015 MeV, which increased by approximately 37.5 cm−1 for the C-PMMA/ 40% wt. CeO2 and 9.95 cm−1 for C-PMMA/ 40% wt. ZrO2 than pristine C-PMMA, while the corresponding half-value layer at 8 MeV decreased by approximately 39%. The fabricated composites could be used as promising materials for components such as housings, encapsulation layers, and support structures in electronic modules that operate close to radiation sources, where moderate radiation shielding, electrical insulation, and decreased weight are all needed at the same time. However, further verification of mechanical dependability and experimentally determined shielding efficacy will determine their ultimate usefulness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50960-x.

Introduction

​​​​​​​​​​Poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) is a clear thermoplastic polymer widely applied in optics, electronics, and biomedical applications due to its high optical transparency, low weight, and environmental stability1​​. The material demonstrates poor performance when it comes to blocking electromagnetic and ionizing radiation, and PMMA exists as a material with weak mechanical characteristics in its unmodified form2,3. Yu et al. demonstrated that PMMA properties improve through cross-linking and nanoparticle addition, which results in enhanced stiffness and thermal stability, dielectric stability, and superior energy absorption characteristics4​. The process of modification creates covalent bonds between polymer chains, which results in improved dimensional stability and thermal resistance. Inorganic nanoparticle addition of high-permittivity metal oxides produces interfacial polarization sites that improve dielectric performance and radiation shielding capabilities efficiency5,6. These hybrid polymer nanocomposite systems are very interesting for practical applications where lightweighting, functional performance, and thermal resistance are paramount, like in electronic packaging, medical radiation shields, and aerospace structural components6,7.

When aromatic diamines like p-phenylenediamine (p-PDA) are used to modify PMMA, covalent bonds are created that improve rigidity, glass-transition temperature, and dimension stability8,9.​​ The chemical modification of the polymer backbone leads to improved mechanical properties and creates new functional groups which enable better bonding of inorganic nanoparticles for enhanced filler distribution and adhesion10,11​. Such cross-linked systems are particularly desirable for multifunctional nanocomposites in which mechanical strength and functional sensitivity must be achieved simultaneously. Ceramic nanoparticles, specifically zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) and cerium dioxide (CeO2), have been extremely keen on application as fillers due to their atomic numbers, high dielectric values, and superior chemical inertness12,13​​.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​ ZrO2 possesses high refractoriness, mechanical strength, and stability whereas CeO2 exhibits typical redox behavior regarding the Ce3+/Ce4+ couple and high oxygen vacancy density14–16​. When integrated in an organic matrix, oxides are interfacial polarization centers that enhance dielectric and charge storage properties through Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars effects12. Their high-Z content also makes it more likely that photons will be absorbed through photoelectric and Compton interactions. This makes them advantageous for targeted radiation shielding parts in both medical devices (like protective panels in radiology rooms and X-ray shields) and aerospace instruments (like satellite electronics and lightweight shielding layers)17​. Polymer–ceramic nanocomposites represent a promising group of materials that integrate the processability and mechanical flexibility inherent to polymers with the functional advantages of the inorganic fillers18,19​. Several works have determined the usefulness of integrating high-Z oxides into polymeric matrices to improve dielectric properties and electromagnetic shielding properties. Few studies have been aimed at relating mechanisms of dielectric enhancement to exposure to radiation attenuation effects in modified PMMA matrices with ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles.

Earlier, PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites were shown to contain 29 nm crystalline CeO2 with modified optical and dielectric behavior and significant antibacterial activity, demonstrating potential in optoelectronic and biomedical applications7. Joseph and Ryu20,21 reported that PVDF/PMMA/ZrO2 nanocomposite films (5–25 wt% ZrO2) made via solution casting exhibited enhanced mechanical, thermal, dielectric, and EMI shielding properties, and that 25 wt% ZrO2 showed the highest performance, as well as absorption-dominated EMI shielding. Alabsy et al. reported that PMMA composites enhanced with 15–45 wt% micro- and nano-ZrO2 had improved radiation shielding, and that nano-ZrO2 had greater mass attenuation values, lower HVL/TVL/MFP values, and improved shielding of γ-rays, which could be applied to radiation shielding in both medical and non-medical applications22. Bani-Salameh showed that PMMA-PS thin films doped with 1–7% CeO2 nanoparticles had high transmittance, a red-shifted absorption edge, a decrease in the optical band gap from 4.03 eV, characteristic vibrational modes for Ce–O, and thermal stability up to 110 °C, demonstrating promise for optical and optoelectronic applications23. According to Fadil and Hashim, PMMA/SiO2–CeO2 nanostructure films made by casting showed increased dielectric parameters with a higher nanoparticle content, frequency-dependent ε′ and ε″ reduction, and rising σAC, suggesting that they could be used in electrical and electronic nanodevice applications24.

Furthermore, it is still an open research topic to explore how filler concentration and type impact charge transport, interfacial polarization, and photon attenuation efficiency. By synthesizing PMMA/ZrO2 and PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites modified with p‑phenylenediamine and thoroughly examining their structural, thermal, dielectric, and theoretical radiation shielding characteristics, the current study closes these gaps. To determine filler dispersal and thermal stability, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were used. Polarization and charge transport behavior were taken into account by dielectric and AC conductivity measurements. Critical shielding parameters, including the linear attenuation coefficient (LAC), mass attenuation coefficient (MAC), half-value layer (HVL), tenth-value layer (TVL), and effective atomic number (Zeff), were also determined using Phy-X/PSD-based simulations for a broad photon energy (15 keV–15 MeV) range. These analyses together demonstrate how effective modified polymer-based nanocomposites of PMMA with ZrO2 and CeO2 have the potential to be used as lightweight radiation shields for medical and aerospace applications, dielectric materials in electronic packaging, and as multifunctional structural materials exposed to ionising radiation.

Experimental

Materials and methods

Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA, Mn = 300 K) was acquired from Alfa Aesar (Germany). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98%, solid) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Zirconium(IV) nitrate [Zr(NO3)4, ≥ 99%] was obtained from Merck (Germany), while cerium(IV) nitrate [Ce(NO3)4, ≥ 99%] was procured from Fluka (Switzerland). Tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99.5%) and p-phenylenediamine (p-PDA, (C6H4NH2)2, ≥ 99%) were supplied by Fisher Scientific (UK). All reagents were utilized as received without further purification. Deionized water was employed in all synthesis and processing steps.

Synthesis of nanocomposites

Ceramic nanoparticles, specifically ZrO2 and CeO2, were produced using a traditional sol–gel technique as documented in previous studies25,26. The cross-linked PMMA-based nanocomposites, labelled as C-PMMA/x wt% ZrO2 and C-PMMA/x wt% CeO2 (where x represents 5, 10, 20, and 40 wt%), were then created through a polycondensation process. The lower contents (5–10 wt%) were chosen as representative low–to–moderate nanoparticle values that are frequently utilized in polymer nanocomposites, where significant increases in dielectric characteristics may be attained and particle dispersion remains reasonably controlled. While retaining acceptable processability and film continuity, the larger contents (20–40 weight%) were selected to investigate the impact of an increasing filler fraction on composite density and the computed photon attenuation parameters. Due to severe particle agglomeration and poor film quality, preliminary trials with loadings above 40 weight% were excluded from the final sample set. Filler loading (x wt%) is defined in relation to the composite’s total mass in all formulations. The polymer phase (PMMA + p‑phenylenediamine) is defined as 100 wt% of the polymer mixture, while the oxide filler (ZrO2 or CeO2) content is given in wt% of the total composite mass. Accordingly, the filler fraction is added on top of the 100 wt% polymer phase, and all compositions are calculated on this basis. The polymer percentages shown in Tables 1 and 2 relate to the polymer fraction before the addition of the ceramic fillers, and the PMMA: p-PDA ratio was kept at 80:20 inside the organic phase. In a standard procedure, 1 g of PMMA was dissolved in 35 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) with continuous stirring until a uniform solution was achieved. Separately, the necessary amount of pre-synthesized ZrO2 or CeO2 nanoparticles, corresponding to the desired weight%, was dispersed into the PMMA solution using ultrasonic agitation for 10 min to ensure even distribution of the nanoparticles. Following this, p-phenylenediamine (p-PDA) was added as a modifying agent, and the reaction mixture was refluxed at 70 °C for 6 h with constant stirring to promote interaction between the polymer and filler. Once the reaction was complete, the resulting viscous mixture was poured into clean petri dishes and allowed to dry at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h to form thin, flexible composite films. The dried films were carefully removed and stored in desiccators before characterization and testing27,28. The entire synthesis process is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1.

Instrumentation

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted utilizing a TGA4724 analyzer in an air atmosphere, maintaining a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1. The analysis spanned a duration of 0–77.5 min, with calibration performed using indium standards (E Indium = 191.5376 J g−1, dE ₍relative₎ = − 23.3291 + 0.0000T + 0.0000T2). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired using a Nicolet Magna 6700 spectrometer in transmittance mode (100%) across a wavenumber range of 4000–600 cm−1, with a scan rate of 32 scans per minute. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å), operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Data collection was performed using a LynxEye one-dimensional detector over a 2θ range of 10°–80°, with a step size of 0.02° and a count time of 1 s per step. Surface morphology was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, FEI TENEO VS) equipped with an EDAX energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector for elemental analysis. Keysight E4990A-02 was used for dielectric characteristics and electrical conductivity of C-PMMA, C-PMMA/ZrO2, and C-PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites at 110 °C.

Radiation shielding

The radiation shielding numerical calculations of the synthesized samples, specifically C-PMMA, C-PMMA/ZrO2, and C-PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites, were assessed by utilizing the Phy-X/PSD software package. This computational tool facilitates the calculation of photon attenuation parameters across an extensive energy spectrum, based on the elemental composition and density of the material. In this investigation, photon energies ranging from 0.015 to 15 MeV were considered to encompass low-, medium-, and high-energy regions pertinent to medical, industrial, and nuclear applications. The input parameters required by the software included the chemical formula and measured density of each composite sample, as these factors directly affect photon–matter interaction probabilities. Dielectric properties

The dielectric properties of C-PMMA, C-PMMA/ZrO2, and C-PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites were comprehensively analyzed to elucidate the effects of nanofillers on charge transport and polarization behavior. Previous studies7,32 have shown that ceramic nanoparticles have a significant impact on interfacial polarization in polymer matrices.

The dielectric measurements were investigated using a Keysight impedance analyzer. The samples were produced as circular films having an average thickness of ~ 1 mm and a radius of ~ 0.5 cm respectively. In order to obtain electrical measurements, electrodes with copper tape were attached on the two sides of the films by means of a thin layer of adhesive. This arrangement was chosen to guarantee good electrical contact, without the use of conductive pastes (e.g. silver paste) which may diffuse in the polymer matrix and create artifacts in the impedance response. The spectra are recorded over the frequency range of 5–1000 kHz with an AC voltage amplitude of 0.5 V and zero DC bias. All measurements were done at 110 °C on a temperature-controlled hot plate. Dielectric measurements were taken at this temperature to investigate the material near glass transition (Tg) region of PMMA. A mercury bulb thermometer was used to keep an eye on the sample temperature. It was placed very close to the sample surface to make sure the temperature readings were correct. To make sure the data was reliable and could be repeated, each measurement was done on at least three samples that were prepared separately. The results were consistent within the experimental error.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization (FTIR and XRD)

​​FTIR spectra, as shown in Fig. 2a, b, verified the successful interaction between PMMA and p-phenylenediamine (p-PDA) and the successful inclusion of ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles within the polymer matrix. Spectra of the original C-PMMA showed clear absorption peaks at 2990–2940 cm−1 due to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic systems, and the strong band at 1720 cm−1 corresponding to the carbonyl (C=O) stretch of the ester functionality. The band appearing close to 1490 cm−1 accounts for the aromatic C=C stretch, and the broad band appearing between 3450 and 3370 cm−1 accounts for the N–H stretch of the amide linkage formed by the modification reaction between PMMA and p-PDA32,33. N–H bending vibration appeared close to 1615 cm−1, supporting the chemical reaction of the carbonyl oxygen of PMMA and amine of p-PDA34.

​​​​After the addition of nanoparticles, slight shifts and intensity alterations of the C=O and N–H peaks appeared, suggesting strong interfacial interactions between the polymer and the ceramic fillers32,33,35​. Similar FTIR behaviour was observed by others, who reported that the incorporation of ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles into PMMA caused comparable shifts in the C=O and N–H bands, confirming the formation of interfacial hydrogen bonding and coordination interactions35–37​. This consistency suggests that the interaction mechanism in the present study aligns well with previously reported polymer–nanoparticle systems.​.

These results confirm the successful chemical inclusion and uniform distribution of the ZrO2 and CeO2 within the p-PDA polymeric network33. Furthermore, the selected nanoparticle loading range (5–40 wt%) was adopted to maintain consistency with previous polymer nanocomposite research, where such concentrations provided optimal dispersion and interaction without significant particle agglomeration33,35. Beyond this range, aggregation often reduces functional performance, hence our concentration range enables direct comparability and structural integrity similar to earlier findings.

XRD patterns, Fig. 3a, b, provide supporting evidence of structural changes upon the addition of nanoparticles. For the pristine C-PMMA, there was a broad amorphous halo between 15° and 30° (2θ), typical of non-crystalline polymers. With the addition of ZrO2 nanoparticles, well-defined diffraction peaks appeared roughly at 30°, 35°, 50.5°, and 60°, which match the positions of the tetragonal ZrO2 planes, namely, the planes of (111), (200), (220), and (311)32,35 (Fig. 3a). Similar ZrO2 diffraction peaks have been observed in PMMA and epoxy matrices by Zhang Chęcińska, indicating that these peaks correlate to the preservation of ZrO2’s tetragonal crystalline structure even after incorporation into a polymer network33,35.

In contrast, CeO2-filled composites displayed typical reflections at 28.5°, 33.1°, 47.4°, and 56.3°. These reflections are indexed to the planes of the CeO2 cubic fluorite structure, specifically the planes of the (111), (200), (220), and (311). CeO2-filled composites, on the other hand, showed typical reflections at 28.5°, 33.1°, 47.4°, and 56.3°, and they are indexed to the planes of the cubic fluorite structure of CeO2, namely, the planes of the (111), (200), (220), and the planes of the (311) Fig. 3b. This observation is consistent with findings reported by32,33, where CeO2 maintained its cubic fluorite structure when embedded in polymeric matrices, suggesting excellent phase stability and interfacial compatibility.

As the nanoparticle concentration rises from 5 to 40 wt%, the intensities of the crystalline ZrO2/CeO2 peaks augment, while the C-PMMA halo diminishes, indicating an increased contribution from the crystalline filler phase. Nonetheless, this does not inherently signify an enhancement in the crystallinity of the PMMA phase. Maji and Checińska discovered a comparable pattern, noting that well-dispersed nanoparticles can affect the organization of polymer chains and the overall microstructure. In this study, we refrain from ascribing quantitative alterations in polymer crystallinity to the observed XRD patterns without additional independent research33,35. Lack of any extra peaks gives evidence of the absence of any formation of the second phases upon synthesis, and this means the polymer-bound ceramic nanoparticles retained their structural integrity. This result agrees with previous reports, confirming that well-dispersed nanoparticles do not generate secondary phases, ensuring that the composite maintains the amorphous characteristics of the PMMA matrix and the crystalline configuration of the fillers within the presently attainable resolution. The lack of further peaks further corroborates the phase stability of the nanoparticles and the consistency of the production process32,33.

Morphological and elemental analysis (SEM–EDX)

The filler-free C-PMMA and the filler-reinforced C-PMMA show significant morphological differences, as shown in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs in Fig. 4. The surface of pure C-PMMA is relatively uniform and smooth, which is a clear indication of the polymer’s amorphous nature. This smooth morphology is in agreement with the findings of33,35,38,39, who reported similarly homogeneous surfaces for pristine PMMA films before nanoparticle incorporation, confirming the amorphous and defect-free nature of the base polymer.

However, upon the addition of ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles, the surface morphology becomes increasingly coarse and heterogeneous, exhibiting obvious nano- and micro-scale phases. Similar roughening and phase contrast evolution have been previously observed in polymer nanocomposites by Gad and Zidan38,39, where nanoparticle inclusion introduced interfacial microdomains that enhanced charge trapping and interfacial polarization effects. These morphological transformations indicate improved filler–matrix interactions and provide pathways for enhanced dielectric and mechanical performance.

At lower filler concentrations of 5–10 wt%, the particles exhibit a relatively well-dispersed and uniform distribution inside the polymeric matrix, with minimal agglomeration noted, yet higher filler content loads of 20–40 wt% depict partial clustering and agglomeration. This behaviour corresponds closely with observations by Gad and Zidan et al., who found that below ~ 10 wt% loading, nanoparticles remain well distributed, while higher concentrations promote localized aggregation due to van der Waals forces38,39. Thus, our micrographs validate the essential function of filler loading in regulating dispersion, aligning with established trends in composite morphology. This morphological evolution augments the interfacial area and may enhance interfacial polarization, thereby affecting the dielectric and shielding properties. Comparable enhancements in interfacial polarization have been reported by scientists linking the roughened morphology and increased surface heterogeneity to improved dielectric constants and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding capability in C-PMMA–metal oxide systems35,39.

Elemental confirmation of the addition of the nanoparticles occurs through the energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectra, Figures S1 and S2. Pure C-PMMA depicts peaks corresponding to carbon (C), oxygen (O), and nitrogen (N), which are attributed to the polymer backbone and the cross-linker. Such elemental compositions have also been noted in earlier PMMA/p-PDA-based polymer studies, validating the chemical consistency of the synthesized base material35,39. For the C-PMMA/zirconia composites, peaks corresponding to zirconium (Zr) become evident and increase in intensity for higher filler content. This progressive Zr signal enhancement has similarly been documented earlier38,39, where incremental nanoparticle loading led to proportionally stronger Zr counts in EDX spectra, confirming successful incorporation and homogeneity of the ZrO2 phase Figure S1a–e. Characteristically, C-PMMA/ceria composites depict cerium (Ce) peaks, the intensity of which correlated positively with increasing filler content. Comparable trends were also observed by zhang and zidan33,39, who reported a linear rise in Ce signal intensity with increasing CeO2 addition, demonstrating stable dispersion without elemental contamination Figure S2a–d. The uniformity seen in many investigations corroborates the effective integration of nanoparticles within the composite matrix; nonetheless, the occurrence of some agglomerates at elevated loadings suggests that the nanoscale distribution is not entirely homogeneous. Absence of any extra elements or impurities serves as proof of the successful encapsulation of the ceramic nanoparticles within the C-PMMA polymeric matrix and supports the morphological results aptly. This observation is further supported by researchers, who emphasized that the lack of extraneous signals in EDX spectra indicates clean surface chemistry and strong chemical affinity between the polymer and filler phases, validating both the synthesis route and purity of the final composites.

Thermal stability (TGA)

To evaluate the thermal behaviour of the nanocomposites, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was utilized Fig. 5. A single significant weight-loss event occurred in the pristine C-PMMA between 300 and 450 degrees Celsius, which corresponded to the breakdown of the polymer backbone. Similar decomposition behaviour of pure PMMA has been reported earlier, confirming the typical one-step degradation associated with polymer chain scission33,39. As demonstrated by a shift in the onset decomposition temperature to higher values and an increase in residual mass at 600 °C, the addition of ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles appeared to improve thermal stability. This enhancement aligns with earlier observations, noted the ceramic fillers act as effective thermal shields and enhance polymer degradation resistance. The ceramic fillers’ natural thermal resistance and barrier effect, which prevent heat transfer and the diffusion of volatile degradation products, are responsible for this improvement. Comparable barrier effects have been attributed to restricted polymer chain mobility at the filler interface. The residual mass at 500 °C increased from below 3% to about 25–30%, indicating improved thermal stability due to the presence of thermally robust ceramic fillers. The onset degradation temperature increased from roughly 280 °C for neat PMMA/p–PDA to about 305 °C for the composites containing 40% ZrO2 or CeO233,35.

Additionally, the nanoparticles and polymer chains have a strong interfacial bond that limits molecular motion and postpones degradation. This interfacial stabilization is consistent with earlier PMMA–oxide systems already reported38,39. The composites containing 20–40 wt% filler produced the highest char yields, indicating that higher nanoparticle concentrations provide superior thermal protection. Zhang discovered a comparable concentration-dependent increase in char residue, confirming the contribution of ceramic content to improving thermal endurance33. All things considered, these findings demonstrate that the structural stability and thermal endurance of C-PMMA are significantly enhanced by the addition of ceramic nanoparticles. Overall, the present findings are in strong agreement with previous polymer–ceramic nanocomposite studies, validating the thermal reinforcement effect of ZrO2 and CeO2 inclusion.

Electrical and dielectric properties

To assess the effect of ceramic nanoparticle incorporation on charge transport and polarization mechanisms, the study examined the electrical and dielectric characteristics of cross-linked PMMA, C-PMMA/ZrO2, and C-PMMA/CeO2 nanocomposites. Previous studies have shown that metal oxide fillers significantly influence interfacial polarization and charge transport in PMMA-based systems7,32.

The measurements of dielectric properties, AC conductivity (σAC), dielectric constant (ε′), dielectric loss (tan δ) and electric modulus (M′), were carried out at a temperature of 110 °C (Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9). This value was purposely chosen near the glass transition (Tg ≈ 105 °C) of PMMA. At this temperature, the C-PMMA matrix becomes sufficiently mobile to cause Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars (MWS) interfacial polarization that otherwise is muted in the glassy state. When the temperature is high, the free volume also increases. Therefore, it is easier to distinguish charge transport and relaxation mechanisms between pure C-PMMA and nanocomposites. Despite the hindrance of long-range chain mobility in C-PMMA due to modifying comparison to PMMA-associated one, hopping conduction and dipolar reorientation predominate at 110 °C8. This is the best window period to study the effect of ZrO2 and CeO2 fillers on the dielectric characteristics such that the enhanced values of ε′, tan δ, and M′ are due to the incorporation of nanoparticles and not due to thermal effects40,41. The dielectric response of PMMA-based systems strongly depends on temperature. At room temperature, the polymer is in a glassy state exhibiting restricted segmental mobility, thereby resulting in the limited dipolar orientation and suppressed interfacial polarization, ultimately resulting in reduced dielectric permittivity and dielectric loss. However, as PMMA approaches its glass-transition temperature (Tg), both chain mobility and the presence of free volume promote the relaxation of dipoles and transport of charge carriers, which significantly improves the dielectric characteristics of PMMA. Similar to PMMA systems, SnO2/PMMA composites have also been reported to exhibit an increase in both dielectric permittivity and dielectric loss with increasing temperature because of improved dipolar and interfacial polarization42,43. The frequency range of 5–1000 kHz was selected for this experiment to provide an adequate resolution of dipolar relaxation and interfacial polarization in polymer nanocomposites, as well as to minimize the effect of electrode polarization at the lowest frequencies and instrumental limitations at the highest frequencies.

Dielectric constant behaviour

The dielectric constant (ε′) is a measure of the ability to polarize when a material experiences an electric field (real part of dielectric permittivity). The two systems: C-PMMA/ZrO2 and C-PMMA/CeO2 have been shown to have an increasing dielectric constant with increasing frequency at an elevated temperature (110 °C). The dielectric constant (ε′) of pristine C-PMMA (ε′ ≈ 3.0 at 500 kHz) was substantially increased with the addition of oxide nanoparticles because of the high permittivity of the nanoparticles and the establishment of interfacial polarization (Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars effect) between the polymer and the filler40.

Quantitatively, the dielectric constant of the C-PMMA/ZrO2 system is shown in Fig. 7a where the 5 wt% composite exhibited the highest dielectric constant (ε′ ≈ 4.6 at 500 kHz) with an approximate 53% increase when compared to pristine C-PMMA (ε′ ≈ 3.0 at 500 kHz) and ~ 4.3 at 1000 kHz). All of the composites with the exception of the 5 wt% composite exhibited a moderate increase in dielectric constant presumably due to a difference in dispersion of the filler and/or differences in adhesion at the interface of the polymer and filler. Additionally, both systems showed that the dielectric response of each system varied with the weight% of the filler used. In contrast, in the C-PMMA/CeO2 system, the dielectric response of the dielectric constant dropped in a linear manner with increasing frequency as shown in Fig. 7b for the 40 wt% composite (ε′ ≈ 2.8 at 500 kHz) while a more pronounced dielectric dispersion was exhibited by the 10 wt% composite (ε′ > 4.5 at low frequency; ε′< 1.5 at 400 kHz). This indicates that at the intermediate filler loading the ability to polarize is non-uniform across the size of the polymer and possibly reflects an interface of heterogeneous characteristics. The dielectric enhancement detected is due to the charge accumulation and interfacial (Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars polarisation) polarisation at the filler-matrix interfaces; for the composites containing CeO2, the oxygen vacancies from the transition Ce3+/Ce4+ can provide further sources of dipolar polarisation. For ZrO2 based composites, the dielectric enhancement is primarily from interfacial polarisation/electric field contrast, since zirconium predominantly exists as Zr4+. As a result, redox mechanisms due to Ce are not valid for ZrO2 composites and will provide a measure of chemical integrity. All samples have a gradual decrease in ε’ at higher frequency due to the inability of dipoles to keep pace with the rapidly fluctuating electric field, which results in an overall decrease in the amount of polarisation45,52.

Dielectric loss (tan δ) analysis

Figure 8a, b illustrates the dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) changes with frequency at 110 °C. In contrast to the C-PMMA composites, the tan δ values decreased with frequency, indicating reduced dipolar and interfacial polarization at higher frequencies. The tan δ of the C-PMMA/ceramic filler composites was higher than that of pristine C-PMMA, indicating that the fillers contributed to interfacial polarization within the polymer matrix. In the C-PMMA/ZrO2 system shown in Fig. 8a, the quantitative results show that the 5 wt% composite exhibits the highest tan δ ~ 0.15 at 800 kHz, whereas pristine C-PMMA has a tan δ of approximately 0.05. The 5 wt% composite also shows an abrupt decrease in loss tan δ from approximately 0.51 at 1 kHz to almost zero at 1000 kHz, which is typical for dielectric dispersion. The composites containing C-PMMA/CeO2, however, demonstrate much lower loss (shown in Fig. 8b). Among the C-PMMA/CeO2 composites, the 40 wt% composite exhibits a moderate loss (tan δ ≈ 0.075 at 800 kHz), while the 10 wt% composite has the lowest loss (≈ 0.045) at high frequencies. The moderate tan δ values indicate that while the filler provides the potential for interfacial polarization to increase energy loss, the fabrication of the composite in this manner means that these composites have retained excellent dielectric properties and insulating characteristics53.

The higher dielectric losses observed in the ZrO2 system are attributed to increased interfacial polarization caused by the higher permittivity of ZrO2 particles, which leads to more pronounced space-charge accumulation at the polymer–filler interface. Conversely, CeO2-filled composites exhibit lower energy dissipation and improved dielectric stability, with tan δ values generally remaining below 0.10. Overall, these results indicate that both filler type and loading critically influence the balance between polarization-induced energy dissipation and the insulating behavior of the composites7,46.

Radiation shielding simulation studies

The Linear Attenuation Coefficient (LAC, μ)

It is the fraction of a narrow beam of X-ray or gamma-ray photons that are attenuated per unit thickness of a material. It has units of inverse length (cm−1) and describes the exponential reduction in intensity as radiation passes through a substance. The LAC of C-PMMA, C-PMMA/ZrO2, and C-PMMA/CeO2 vary with photon energy, shown in Fig. 10a–d (in the energy range 0.015–15 MeV). Higher LAC values are the consequence of the photoelectric effect, which is the dominating interaction at low photon energy. Because cerium has a greater effective atomic number (Zeff) than zirconium, the CeO2-filled composites showed the greatest LAC values at low photon energies. The photoelectric effect transfers to Compton scattering at high energies (> 1 MeV), which resulted in the LAC values decreasing. CeO2 was better in the low-energy region, while ZrO2-based composites showed more consistent attenuation over the whole energy range30. The LAC of C-PMMA/ 40% wt. CeO2 nanocomposite ≈ 37.5 at 0.015 MeV with an approximate increase when compared to pure C-PMMA (LAC ≈ 1.25 at 0.015 MeV) and C-PMMA/ 40% wt. ZrO2 (LAC ≈ 9.95 at 0.015 MeV). This significant enhancement shows the impressive attenuation capacity of the C‑PMMA/40 wt% CeO2 nanocomposite at low photon energy.

The Mass Attenuation Coefficient (MAC)

Figures S4(a, b) show that the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) is the ratio of the LAC to the density of the material expressed in (cm2/g). Increasing the ratio of nanoceramic particles on C-PMMA/CeO2 and C-PMMA/ZrO2 refers to increasing the MAC. However, CeO2 as a filler shows a greater increase in MAC values than ZrO2 at all loading levels, especially at low photon energy30,31. At low photon energy, the photoelectric effect favors a higher atomic number and density of materials, resulting in CeO2 composites having shielding efficacy more than ZrO2. The MAC of C-PMMA/CeO2 is 22 at 0.015 MeV, markedly surpassing that of pure C-PMMA (1 at 0.015 MeV) and C-PMMA/ZrO2 (6 at 0.015 MeV), signifying a considerably improved attenuation capacity. The significantly elevated MAC value of C-PMMA/CeO2 indicates its enhanced suitability for radiation shielding applications at 0.015 MeV. The MAC values at photon energy above 1 MeV of all samples are nearly constant, due to Compton scattering which depends primarily on electron density rather than atomic number.

The effective atomic number (Zeff)

Zeff is the atomic number of a composite that detects the photon interaction properties of a compound. The effective electron density (Neff)

Neff in Figures S6a, b, which represents the number of electrons per unit volume interacting with photons, was higher in both ZrO2 and CeO2 composites relative to pure C-PMMA. CeO2 composites yielded larger Neff at low photon energies due to their higher electron concentration. This increase is due to stronger photon–electron interactions, which are related to the higher LAC and MAC in the same region29,30. At higher energies, the Neff of two fillers is slightly different, reflecting the decreasing influence of high filler on Compton scattering.

The effective conductivity (Ceff)

It describes the overall electrical or thermal conductivity of a composite material, taking into account the conductivities and volumetric contributions of all constituent phases. The Ceff in Figures S7a, b, which reflects the interaction between radiation and free electrons, increased with nanoceramic loading. The CeO2 composites have higher electron density and polarizability, showing higher Ceff values at low energies. ZrO2 composites displayed Ceff the same behaviour of CeO2 composites at high energy. The correlation between Ceff and AC conductivity trends suggests that CeO2 contributes more strongly to localized conduction and scattering events30.

Fast Neutron Removal Cross Section (FNRCS)

FNRCS is a parameter that quantifies the probability per unit path length for a fast neutron to be attenuated or removed from a beam due to both absorption and scattering interactions in each material, shown in Fig. 13. The FNRCS of the C-PMMA is 0.11, increasing marginally to 0.115 for C-PMMA/ 40% ZrO2 and decreasing to 0.106 for C-PMMA/ 40% CeO2, signifying minor fluctuations in their neutron-related interaction cross-sections. The minimal variation in FNRCS values indicates that the compositional alterations in nanocomposites do not substantially affect their overall neutron interaction properties relative to the PMMA29,30.

The Energy Absorption Buildup Factor (EABF) and the Exposure Buildup Factor (EBF)

The Figures S8–S11 display EABF and EBF. The modified response to un-collided photons is measured by integrating the effects of scattered photons in relation to photon energy, as produced at various MFP values. Both EBF and EABF varied with photon energy, exhibiting a peak in the intermediate energy region where Compton scattering is dominant. CeO2 composites showed lower accumulation factors at low energies because of their enhanced photon absorption, which minimized the contributions of scattered photons. ZrO2 composites performed more steadily over the whole energy range, although exhibiting more accumulation at low energies29,30. All samples show an increase in accumulation factors with penetration depth (MFP). However, CeO2 composites showed a slower rate of rise, indicating their effectiveness in reducing the contributions of scattered radiation.

Conclusion

The dielectric characteristics of PMMA-based composites embedded ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles show that the number of fillers is an important parameter that decides the AC conductivity, dielectric constant, and dielectric loss. The increased concentration of the ceramic fillers leads to an increase in the dielectric constant due to the effective interaction of the polymer matrix and the ceramic nanoparticles, which increases interfacial polarization. Consequently, the AC conductivity also increases for higher filler content for CeO2 nanoparticles due to the dielectric properties of CeO2 nanoparticles, which is correlated with the increased space charge polarization and improved mobility of the charge carriers. Although the dielectric loss tan δ is at a moderate level, showing its good insulating character. It improves with the increase in frequency, indicating that dipolar relaxation takes place at higher frequencies. The composites containing CeO2 nanoparticles appear to be more advantageous. The C-PMMA/5% ZrO2 and C-PMMA/40% CeO2 nanocomposites exhibited the highest AC conductivity among all the prepared composites. Higher radiation shielding properties make the PMMA with a higher wt% (40%) of nanoceramic particles perfect for photon attenuation applications. The inclusion of ZrO2 and CeO2 increases the MAC value at a specific energy. In the low-energy spectrum, 40% CeO2 composites showed a higher increase in the LAC and MAC than 40% ZrO2 composites. However, in the same energy range, increasing the ceramic content caused a decrease in the MFP, with CeO2 filler being the best at this reduction. As the percentage of ceramic fillers increased, so did the Zeff, Neff, and Ceff of PMMA. Compared to undoped C-PMMA, doped C-polymers with CeO2 are more suited for ionizing radiation shielding applications at lower energy. In terms of neutron shielding, PMMA/ZrO2 composites outperformed other PMMA/CeO2 composites. The present work did not address the mechanical properties of the nanocomposites. Given the relatively high ceramic loadings up to 40 wt%, future studies must include tensile, flexural, and impact testing to evaluate whether the structural integrity remains acceptable for practical deployment.
