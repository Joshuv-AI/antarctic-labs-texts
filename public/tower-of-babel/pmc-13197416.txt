Heart rate optimizer: a novel bio-inspired metaheuristic algorithm
Authors: Mosa E Hosney, Marwa M Emam, Mohammed R Saad, Nagwan Abdel Samee, Essam H Houssein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing complexity of real-world optimization problems demands robust and adaptive metaheuristic approaches. Despite notable progress, many existing algorithms suffer from premature convergence, imbalanced exploration-exploitation trade-offs, and sensitivity to high-dimensional search spaces. Motivated by the adaptive regulation mechanisms of the human cardiovascular system, this paper proposes a novel bio-inspired metaheuristic termed the Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO). The algorithm models heart rate variability and autonomic nervous system dynamics to enhance optimization performance, incorporating tachycardia for accelerated global exploration, bradycardia for intensified local exploitation, and Lévy flight-based arrhythmic behavior to escape local optima. In addition, an Orthogonal Learning strategy is integrated to regulate the interaction between exploration and exploitation while preserving population diversity. Extensive experiments conducted on the IEEE CEC2017 and CEC2022 benchmark suites demonstrate that HRO achieves superior solution accuracy, faster convergence, and improved stability compared to nine state-of-the-art algorithms. Moreover, its effectiveness is validated on challenging engineering design problems, including the welded beam, pressure vessel, and tension-compression spring designs, confirming the robustness and practical relevance of the proposed approach.Keywords: Heart rate optimizer, Metaheuristic algorithms, Bio-inspired optimization, Engineering design problemsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Optimization is a cornerstone of numerous disciplines, including artificial intelligence, engineering, operations research, and data science, where the objective is to identify optimal or near-optimal solutions within complex, often constrained decision spaces. Optimization involves identifying the best solution from a set of feasible alternatives to achieve predefined objectives while satisfying specific constraints1. This process entails translating real-world challenges into mathematical optimization problems and developing efficient algorithms to solve them. Such problems are ubiquitous across domains like engineering design2, economic management3, artificial intelligence4, and data science, where they often involve high-dimensional, nonlinear, and constrained decision spaces5.

Single-objective minimization problem, these problems, prevalent in applications such as signal processing, production scheduling, machine learning, and mechanical design, are characterized by high dimensionality, nonlinearity, nonconvexity, and multimodal landscapes. An optimization problem typically comprises decision variables, constraints, and one or more objective functions to be minimized or maximized, aiming to achieve goals like minimizing cost, time, or risk, or maximizing efficiency, quality, or profitability6. These problems vary widely, classified by decision variables (continuous or discrete), constraints (constrained or unconstrained), optima (unimodal or multimodal), objectives (single-, multi-, or many-objective), computational cost (inexpensive or expensive), or temporal behavior (static or dynamic)7. The growing complexity of real-world optimization tasks, particularly NP-hard problems like the traveling salesman or knapsack problems, renders traditional mathematical methods, such as linear programming, nonlinear programming, or gradient-based techniques, ineffective due to their reliance on derivative information and exponential computational complexity8.

Deterministic algorithms follow structured strategies to locate global optima, offering high computational efficiency and strong global search capabilities for well-behaved problems. However, their limitations, including restricted applicability to black-box problems and sensitivity to non-differentiable or nonconvex objective functions, make them impractical for many real-world scenarios9. In response, non-deterministic algorithms, particularly metaheuristic algorithms (MAs), have gained prominence over the past two decades as robust alternatives10. MAs are classified as non-deterministic due to their inherent use of randomness and uncertainty in their search processes. Because of this stochastic nature, repeated runs of the same MA may produce varying results, and convergence to the optimal solution within a fixed number of iterations is not guaranteed. However, unlike deterministic methods, MAs do not impose strict requirements on the properties of the objective function, making them highly adaptable for black-box optimization problems. Moreover, MAs can efficiently generate near-optimal solutions for large-scale problems within practical timeframes, which underscores their widespread applicability in real-world scenarios.

MAs have emerged as robust alternatives, leveraging gradient-free, stochastic, and problem-independent search strategies to tackle complex, high-dimensional, and non-differentiable optimization problems11. Derived from the Greek terms “meta” (beyond) and “heuristic” (to discover), MAs serve as high-level frameworks that guide heuristic search processes, relying solely on objective function evaluations without requiring structural or derivative information12. This black-box approach enables MAs to address diverse challenges, including multimodal, non-differentiable, and dynamic optimization problems, across scientific, technical, and industrial domains13. MAs are inspired by natural phenomena, such as biological evolution, swarm behavior, or physical processes, and are broadly categorized into single-solution-based (S-metaheuristics) and population-based (P-metaheuristics) approaches14.

S-metaheuristics, such as Simulated Annealing15, Hill Climbing16, and Variable Neighborhood Search17, refine a single solution iteratively, offering computational efficiency and fewer function evaluations, ideal for time-sensitive problems. However, their reliance on a single search trajectory increases susceptibility to premature convergence in complex or multimodal landscapes. In contrast, P-metaheuristics, such as Genetic Algorithms (GA)18, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)19, and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)20, evolve a population of candidate solutions through operators like crossover, mutation, or velocity updates, using selection mechanisms to generate subsequent generations until a stopping criterion is met. P-metaheuristics excel at escaping local optima through parallel exploration but require higher computational costs. P-metaheuristics solutions are mostly inspired by nature21.

A critical issue is balancing exploration (searching diverse regions) and exploitation (refining promising solutions), as excessive exploration slows convergence, while overemphasizing exploitation risks premature convergence22. Strategies like dynamic parameter tuning or hybridization often lack generalizability across diverse problem domains23. Additionally, the stochastic nature of MAs obscures their decision-making processes, reducing interpretability and limiting adoption in safety-critical applications. Theoretical aspects, such as convergence analysis and computational complexity, also remain underdeveloped, creating a gap between empirical performance and formal guarantees23. The No Free Lunch (NFL) theorem further underscores the need for innovation, stating that no single algorithm performs optimally across all problem types, necessitating tailored solutions24. Moreover, incremental improvements to existing MAs yield diminishing returns, particularly for complex, large-scale, or dynamic problems, highlighting the demand for novel frameworks.

In recent years, optimization algorithms have played a pivotal role in addressing a wide range of real-world practical applications across diverse domains. In engineering design, optimization techniques are extensively used to minimize material cost, energy consumption, and structural weight while satisfying complex safety and performance constraints, such as in welded beam, pressure vessel, truss, and mechanical component design problems. In industrial and manufacturing systems, optimization algorithms support production scheduling, resource allocation, supply chain management, and logistics planning, where decision spaces are often large-scale, nonlinear, and NP-hard. Moreover, in emerging fields such as healthcare and biomedical engineering, optimization algorithms have become indispensable for tasks including feature selection, parameter tuning of machine learning models, medical image analysis, and treatment planning, where robustness and adaptability are critical. These application areas demand optimization algorithms that are not only computationally efficient but also capable of handling uncertainty, high dimensionality, and complex constraint structures, thereby motivating the development of advanced and adaptive metaheuristic frameworks.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes the Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO) is a novel bio-inspired metaheuristic algorithm that mimics the dynamic fluctuations of human heart rate (HR) to achieve an adaptive balance between exploration and exploitation in optimization tasks. Inspired by cardiovascular physiology, HRO models key aspects of heart rate variability (HRV), including tachycardia (accelerated HR), bradycardia (decelerated HR), and autonomic nervous system regulation, to intelligently guide search agents. The algorithm mathematically represents the natural rhythmic behavior of HR through periodic functions to dynamically adjust search intensity and diversity. Core innovations of HRO include: (1) bio-inspired HR dynamics where agents emulate heart rate responses to physiological stimuli, with tachycardia-like behaviors promoting aggressive exploration and bradycardia-like states focusing on local refinement; (2) Lévy flight-integrated pulsatility that models short-term HR fluctuations to enable non-local jumps for escaping local optima while maintaining steady convergence through long-term trends; (3) Orthogonal Learning (OL) that mimics the sympathetic-parasympathetic interplay in HRV to systematically direct agents toward promising regions while preserving population diversity; and (4) dynamic boundary control that analogizes the normal HR range (60–100 bpm) as a search space boundary with adaptive penalty mechanisms for constraint violations mirroring pathological HR conditions. The algorithm’s performance is rigorously validated through numerical optimization comparisons with nine state-of-the-art algorithms on IEEE CEC’2017 (20D) and CEC’2022 (100D) benchmarks, practical engineering applications including welded beam, pressure vessel, three-bar truss and speed reducer design problems, and biomedical relevance tests through feature selection and classification tasks on medical datasets, demonstrating HRO’s superior robustness in high-dimensional spaces and suitability for healthcare-related optimization.

Literature review

Metaheuristic algorithms, representing a prominent class of bio-inspired computational methods, have found extensive applications across diverse problem domains. Current taxonomies categorize these algorithms into four primary groups according to their foundational inspiration: (1) evolutionary algorithms (EA), (2) physics-inspired algorithms (PhA), (3) human behavior-based algorithms, and (4) swarm intelligence (SI) approaches6.

EA are biologically-inspired optimization techniques grounded in Darwinian evolutionary principles, with Genetic Algorithms (GA)18 and Differential Evolution (DE)25 representing two prominent implementations. These population-based methods simulate natural selection through iterative processes of selection, crossover, and mutation operations to progressively refine candidate solutions. Demonstrating exceptional exploration capabilities, EAs have found successful applications across diverse domains ranging from hyperparameter optimization in machine learning to complex engineering design problems. The strength of evolutionary approaches lies in their sophisticated balance between exploration and exploitation, achieved through carefully designed selection pressures and genetic operators. By maintaining population diversity while simultaneously intensifying search efforts in promising regions, these algorithms effectively mitigate premature convergence to suboptimal solutions. This dual capability enables EAs to navigate complex solution spaces while preserving the potential for discovering global optima.

On the other hand, PhA derive their optimization mechanisms from fundamental physical phenomena and natural laws. The canonical example is Simulated Annealing15, which adapts the thermodynamic annealing process of metals to optimization problems. Modern physics-based algorithms extend this concept by modeling diverse physical systems, including: (1) Equilibrium Optimizer (EO)26, (2) celestial mechanics through Gravitational Search Algorithm (GSA)27 and Kepler Optimization Algorithm (KOA)5, (3) fluid dynamics in28, and (4) mathematical physics principles29–31. These algorithms demonstrate particular effectiveness in high-dimensional search spaces by maintaining natural balances between exploration and exploitation through their physical analogies. For instance, the Archimedes Optimization Algorithm32 implements buoyancy principles to prevent premature convergence, while the Water Cycle Algorithm28 mimics evaporation and precipitation processes to maintain population diversity. Another algorithm is the Bighorn Sheep Optimization Algorithm, an efficient approach for Wireless Sensor Network31, in addition to the Cape lynx optimizer33.

Human-inspired optimization algorithms model various aspects of human intelligence and social behavior to enhance computational problem-solving. These metaheuristics incorporate cognitive processes, social interactions, cultural evolution, and decision-making mechanisms to improve search efficiency. Notable implementations simulate learning behaviors, knowledge transfer, and adaptive reasoning patterns observed in human societies. The algorithms’ strength lies in their ability to dynamically balance solution refinement (through cultural learning or experience accumulation) with innovative exploration (via simulated creativity or social diversity). This bio-inspired approach provides particular advantages in complex optimization scenarios by: (1) maintaining solution diversity through simulated social structures, (2) accelerating convergence via knowledge-based learning mechanisms, and (3) adapting search strategies dynamically based on problem characteristics. Such human-like optimization paradigms demonstrate superior flexibility when handling non-linear, high-dimensional problems compared to conventional mathematical programming methods. The development of human-inspired optimization algorithms has evolved through several generations of methodological innovations. Early approaches include the Harmony Search (HS) algorithm34, which formalizes the musical improvisation process as an optimization framework. Subsequent advances produced the Teaching-Learning-Based Optimization (TLBO) algorithm35, modeling pedagogical interactions between teachers and students. Contemporary developments feature specialized algorithms including the Hiking Optimization Algorithm (HOA)36, Deep Sleep Optimizer (DSO)37, and Dream Optimization Algorithm (DOA)1. These methods systematically encode diverse human activities into optimization paradigms from collective problem-solving behaviors and economic decision-making to perceptual processes and physiological patterns. By mathematically representing these human experiences, the algorithms achieve robust performance across complex search spaces while maintaining intuitive operational principles.

Swarm intelligence algorithms emulate the emergent problem-solving capabilities observed in animal collectives, where decentralized agents, such as bird flocks, ant colonies, or wolf packs, achieve global optimization through local interactions and simple behavioral rules. Recent advances have expanded these biological metaphors through sophisticated models including Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO)38 for cooperative hunting, Slime Mold Algorithm (SMA)39 for adaptive pathfinding, and novel paradigms like Arctic Puffin Optimization (APO)40 and Horned Lizard Optimizer (HLOA)41 that incorporate niche ecological behaviors, while cross-disciplinary approaches such as Tianji’s Horse Racing Optimization (THRO)42, Starfish optimization algorithm (SFOA)43 and Stellar Oscillation Optimizer (SOO)44 demonstrate the versatility of swarm-inspired design. By maintaining an adaptive balance between exploration (via agent diversity) and exploitation (through local convergence), these algorithms excel in dynamic, high-dimensional search spaces, with applications ranging from engineering design to artificial intelligence

Beyond classical optimization tasks, recent studies have increasingly integrated metaheuristic algorithms with advanced instrumentation, measurement, and data-driven modeling techniques to address complex real-world systems. In particular, metaheuristics have been combined with self-supervised and deep learning frameworks for anomaly detection and condition monitoring in large-scale infrastructure systems, such as electrified railway networks. For example, invariant feature learning and prototype-based measurement strategies have been employed to enhance anomaly detection under varying operational conditions, while data-driven modeling approaches based on Koopman operators have enabled accurate representation of nonlinear pantograph catenary dynamics45. Moreover, under sample-constrained or limited-data scenarios, multimodal measurement techniques supported by optimization-driven feature selection and parameter tuning have demonstrated improved robustness and generalization performance46.

Despite their diverse inspirations and mechanisms, all metaheuristic algorithms fundamentally operate through two complementary search phases: exploration and exploitation. The exploration phase enables global investigation of the search space to identify regions of potential optimality, while exploitation facilitates intensive local search to refine solutions and achieve convergence. Effective algorithm design necessitates careful equilibrium between these competing phases, as their optimal balance directly determines the method’s problem-solving efficacy47. Building upon these foundational principles, the proposed Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO) algorithm introduces a novel biologically-inspired mechanism that dynamically maintains this critical exploration-exploitation balance. Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO)

This section thoroughly explores the core concepts and theoretical background of the Fast Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO). It also delves into the fundamental ideas that inspired the development of this algorithm, presenting a detailed account of its mathematical structure and the logic behind the population-based metaheuristic approach introduced as the Heart Rate Optimizer.

Source of inspiration

Heart rate (HR) refers to the number of times a human heart beats per minute (bpm). It is a key physiological indicator that reflects the cardiovascular condition of the body and the activity of the autonomic nervous system. HR is not static; it fluctuates naturally in response to various physical, emotional, and environmental stimuli. The key aspects of heart rate include its normal range, typically between 60 and 100 beats per minute (bpm) for a healthy adult at rest. A heart rate below 60 bpm is known as bradycardia, while a rate above 100 bpm is known as tachycardia70.

Another important measure is heart rate variability (HRV), which represents the variation in time intervals between consecutive heartbeats. HRV reflects the balance between the sympathetic (activating) and parasympathetic (calming) branches of the autonomic nervous system. Generally, a higher HRV indicates better cardiovascular adaptability and resilience, whereas a lower HRV may suggest stress, fatigue, or potential health problems71.

Several factors influence HR. Physical activity increases HR to meet the body’s demand for more oxygenated blood during exercise. Stress and emotions, such as anxiety or excitement, can also increase HR due to the activation of the sympathetic nervous system. In contrast, sleep and relaxation typically lead to a decrease in HR as the body enters a state of rest. Health status plays a significant role; conditions such as fever, illness, or cardiovascular disease can alter HR. In addition, age and fitness levels are important; for example, well-trained athletes often exhibit lower resting HR due to more efficient heart function72.

Heart rate can be represented through mathematical modeling, often using sinusoidal or periodic functions to simulate its natural rhythmic behavior. These models are particularly useful in fields such as biomedical engineering, sports science, and computational optimization (e.g., the Heart Rate Optimizer Algorithm, HROA), where they help mimic and analyze biological dynamics for improved system performance and decision-making73.

Experimental analysis of HRO for combinatorial optimization

Combinatorial optimization problems involve finding an optimal solution from a finite but extremely large set of possibilities where the objective is typically to minimize or maximize a given function subject to certain constraints. These problems, such as the Bin Packing Problem (BPP), Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP), and Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSSP), are often NP hard, meaning exact algorithms become computationally infeasible as problem size grows. To address this challenge, metaheuristic algorithms such as Simulated Annealing, GA, PSO, and ACO are widely used. Metaheuristics provide approximate solutions within reasonable time by intelligently exploring and exploiting the search space while balancing diversification through exploration and intensification through exploitation. Although they do not guarantee the global optimum, their adaptability, efficiency, and ability to escape local optima make them powerful tools for tackling complex combinatorial optimization problems across engineering, logistics, and data science applications80.

The following experimental series explores combinatorial optimization challenges, focusing on the BPP (Subsection "Bin packing problem (BPP)"), QAP (Subsection "Quadratic assignment problem (QAP)"), and JSSP (Subsection "Job shop scheduling problem (JSSP)"). Each problem is analyzed separately to demonstrate the adaptability of the proposed HRO across diverse combinatorial settings.

The ultimate goal is to construct an optimal schedule that ensures all jobs are completed within their deadlines while minimizing the overall scheduling cost.

Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents the Heart Rate Optimizer (HRO), a novel bio-inspired metaheuristic algorithm that models the dynamic behavior of human heart rate variability (HRV) to achieve a balance between exploration and exploitation. HRO translates key cardiovascular phenomena, including tachycardia (accelerated search phases), bradycardia (focused exploitation), and autonomic nervous system regulation into efficient optimization strategies. The algorithm incorporates Lévy flight patterns to simulate natural heart rate fluctuations, enabling effective escape from local optima, while Orthogonal Learning (OL) mimics the sympathetic-parasympathetic balance to guide structured convergence. These biologically inspired mechanisms allow HRO to dynamically adapt to complex search landscapes. The optimization performance of HRO has been rigorously evaluated against nine state-of-the-art metaheuristics using the IEEE CEC’2017 and CEC’2022 benchmark suites, demonstrating superior performance in terms of solution accuracy, convergence speed, and stability.

Furthermore, HRO has been successfully applied to a range of challenging combinatorial optimization problems, including the Bin Packing Problem (BPP), Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP), and Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSSP), as well as real world engineering design problems such as welded beam design, pressure vessel design, and tension compression spring design. These practical applications validate the effectiveness of HRO in addressing complex and constrained optimization scenarios. In addition, comparative studies with well established algorithms including PSO, AGDE, GWO, WOA, HHO, AOA, RUN, RIME, and SBOA confirm the competitive performance and robustness of HRO across diverse test cases.

Despite the promising results, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The performance of HRO is influenced by a set of control parameters, including population size and learning rate coefficients, which were empirically determined and may not be optimal for all problem classes. Consequently, suboptimal parameter configurations could adversely affect performance in certain scenarios. Moreover, although HRO has been evaluated on standard benchmark suites, representative combinatorial optimization problems, and selected real world engineering design tasks, its effectiveness on large scale, highly constrained, and dynamic optimization problems remains insufficiently investigated. In addition, the computational complexity of HRO increases with problem dimensionality due to the integration of OL and Lévy flight mechanisms, which may pose scalability challenges in very high dimensional search spaces.

Future research will focus on enhancing and extending HRO’s capabilities. Potential directions include developing an adaptive version with self-tuning parameters to improve its applicability across different problem types. A multi-objective extension of HRO could be explored to address complex problems with competing objectives. The algorithm shows particular promise for biomedical applications, such as medical image segmentation and physiological signal analysis, where its bio-inspired nature may offer unique advantages. Additional investigations into engineering applications, particularly in biomechanical design and healthcare systems, could further demonstrate HRO’s practical value. Continued refinement and application of HRO are expected to contribute significantly to the advancement of biologically-inspired optimization methods and provide effective solutions to complex real-world challenges across multiple disciplines.
