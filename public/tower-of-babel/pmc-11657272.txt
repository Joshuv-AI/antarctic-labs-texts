Blood lipid metabolic biomarkers are emerging as significant prognostic indicators for survival in cancer patients
Authors: Qiliang Peng, Changli Zhan, Yi Shen, Yao Xu, Bixin Ren, Zhengyang Feng, Yong Wang, Yaqun Zhu, Yuntian Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDyslipidemia is a common comorbidity in patients with cancer, yet the impact of abnormal lipid levels on tumor prognosis remains contentious. This study was conducted to synthesize the current evidence regarding the prognostic utility of blood lipid levels, including high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), and apolipoprotein B (ApoB), in predicting overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in cancer patients.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed across electronic databases to assess the associations between blood lipid levels and OS or DFS in cancer patients. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to analyze the data. The research protocol was previously submitted to the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42023458597.ResultsOur study represents the largest and most extensive evaluation of the prognostic significance of blood lipid levels in cancer to date. It includes a meta-analysis of 156 eligible studies involving 85,173 cancer patients. The findings revealed a significant association between elevated levels of HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1 and improved OS and DFS in cancer patients. In contrast, no significant relationships were identified between LDL-C, TG, and ApoB levels and the OS or DFS of cancer patients.ConclusionBlood lipids, particularly HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1, emerge as accessible and cost-effective biomarkers that may aid in assessing survival outcomes in cancer patients and potentially inform clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13265-8.Keywords: Blood lipids, Cancer biomarkers, Overall survival, Disease-free survival

Background

Malignant tumors have emerged as a significant public health concern, posing a substantial threat to the well-being of the population [1]. Notwithstanding substantial progress in the diagnostic and therapeutic arenas for malignant tumors, a dearth of efficacious treatments remains for numerous tumor types. There is a keen interest among researchers in identifying cancer prognostic biomarkers due to their substantial utility in forecasting clinical outcomes and directing therapeutic strategies. It has been observed that an elevated body mass index is linked to a heightened risk of developing malignant tumors [2]. Potential biological factors considered to contribute to this outcome include dysregulations in lipid metabolism, the secretion of adipokines (such as lipocalin, leptin, and endostatin), and factors associated with a chronic inflammatory response (including tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-6), as well as altered hormone levels [3]. Among these factors, disrupted lipid metabolism is increasingly acknowledged as a pivotal mechanism underlying the development of cancer [4]. Earlier research has established a correlation between lipid levels and both the incidence of cancer and mortality associated with the disease, suggesting that lipids can serve as biomarkers for assessing the prognosis in patients with malignant tumors [5].

The principal lipid assessments frequently employed in clinical settings include high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), and apolipoprotein B (ApoB). Serving as the primary cholesterol transporters, HDL-C and LDL-C significantly influence tumor cells, predominantly via receptor-mediated pathways. Moreover, they contribute to the modulation of cancer cell signaling processes [6]. Corroborative experimental research has yielded evidence that HDL-C can facilitate cancer progression, particularly by harnessing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [7]. Certain prospective studies have revealed a significant correlation between pre-diagnosis serum levels of HDL-C and the incidence of various malignancies, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and liver cancer [8]. These findings suggest that HDL-C might be a significant player in the onset and progression of tumors. Nevertheless, some prospective studies have indicated that HDL-C levels are not linked to the risk of certain cancers [9]. Reports suggest that LDL-C can enhance tumor metastasis by modulating integrin expression. Clinical research has demonstrated that patients with high levels of LDL-C undergoing treatment for nasopharyngeal carcinoma exhibit poorer local control and survival rates, with a higher incidence of distant metastases compared to those with normal LDL-C levels [10]. Some studies have suggested a correlation between elevated levels of LDL-C and the risk of cancer, while others have reported no such association [11]. Investigations into the nexus between HDL-C and LDL-C levels and the prognosis of patients with malignant tumors have yielded mixed results, with some studies finding a correlation while others have not. Additionally, a subset of prospective studies has assessed pretreatment HDL-C and LDL-C levels in the context of prognosis for various cancers, including breast, gastric, and prostate cancers, leading to further controversy regarding the association between these lipid profiles and patient outcomes.

TC represents the aggregate cholesterol content within all lipoproteins present in the bloodstream. The serum TC level is influenced by a spectrum of factors, including age, genetics, gender, heredity, dietary habits, and psychological state. Males typically exhibit higher levels than females, while manual laborers often present with lower levels compared to those engaged in mental labor. Given the significant association between serum TC levels and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions such as atherosclerosis, coronary heart disease, and stroke, measurement of serum TC has been institutionalized as a standard component of lipid profile analysis [12]. Research also indicates a positive correlation between elevated serum TC levels and an increased risk for certain types of cancer [13]. TG serves as the primary energy reserve in the body, being metabolized to meet the body’s demands. The breakdown products of TG are utilized by the majority of tissues to provide energy. Moreover, certain tissues, such as the liver and adipose tissue, have the capacity to synthesize TG and store them within adipose deposits [14]. While TG perform various physiological roles, excessive levels of TG can result in alterations to fat cell function and elevated blood viscosity. Such conditions may heighten the risk of developing coronary heart disease and various malignancies [15]. Certain investigations have further suggested that elevated levels of TG are associated with the incidence and mortality rates of diverse cancers [16].

Apolipoproteins play a crucial role in the metabolism of lipoproteins, exercising key physiological functions [17]. Apolipoproteins are designated by the ABC nomenclature system, and numerous variants have been identified, typically classified into 5 to 7 distinct categories. Chief among these are the two primary types: ApoA1 and ApoB. ApoA1 is a predominant protein constituent of HDL. Recent research has revealed that ApoA1 possesses the capacity to inhibit the formation of tumor blood vessels, to foster an anti-tumor immune microenvironment, and to forestall tumor progression [18]. Certain studies have indicated that elevated serum levels of ApoA-1 prior to diagnosis are associated with a decreased risk of various malignancies, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, and colon cancer [19]. Multiple retrospective investigations have revealed that serum levels of ApoA-1 are correlated with the prognosis in a range of cancer types [20]. ApoB is a critical structural protein component of LDL-C, comprising over 90% of the total LDL protein mass. Similarly, there exists a strong correlation between the serum concentration of ApoB and the level of LDL-C. Studies have reported that ApoB levels are associated with the prognosis of malignant tumors, suggesting that they could serve as potential biomarkers [21]. Nonetheless, there has been less research conducted on the relationship between ApoB and the prognosis of malignant tumors, and inconsistencies remain in the findings reported across different studies.

In essence, blood lipid metabolism markers are intricately linked to the onset and progression of cancer, with various lipid components carrying distinct implications for malignant tumors. The current state of research into the connections between blood lipids and cancer prognosis is marked by discrepancies. The results from any single study are deemed inadequate to establish whether blood lipids serve as reliable predictors of cancer survival. Consequently, we initiated this investigation to delve into the correlations between levels of blood lipid parameters, including HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB, and the prognosis of various cancers. Our objective was to obtain a quantitatively precise assessment of the influence of blood lipid metabolic biomarkers on the survival rates of patients with malignant tumors. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents the most extensive and comprehensive examination to date regarding the relationship between blood lipids and survival outcomes in the context of cancer.

Materials and methods

Design

The study was meticulously designed and conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. The PRISMA_2020_checklist was presented in Table S1. Previously, our research protocol was submitted to the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42023458597.

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted up to October 1, 2023, utilizing databases such as PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. The search strategy incorporated the following terms: “Lipoprotein,” “HDL Lipoproteins,” “LDL Lipoproteins,” “Total Cholesterol (TC),” “Triglycerides (TG),” “Apolipoprotein A-1 (ApoA1),” “Apolipoproteins B (ApoB),” “Cancer,” “Tumor,” “Neoplasm,” “Carcinoma,” “Survival,” “Mortality,” “Prognosis,” and “Outcome.” The search query was formulated using a combination of subject-specific terms, free-text words, logical operators, and wildcard characters to ensure comprehensive retrieval. Subsequently, we meticulously reviewed the references of all pertinent studies, as well as the abstracts and full texts, and manually scanned for additional records that had not been identified through automated database searches.

Eligibility criteria

The data were independently extracted by two reviewers in accordance with the predefined criteria. Studies were included in our analysis if they met the following criteria: (1) they were published as original articles in peer-reviewed journals; (2) they investigated the association between blood lipid markers (HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB) and prognosis in various types of cancer; (3) they provided patient survival data, including risk ratio (HR) estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI), or survival data could be derived indirectly from Kaplan–Meier curves; and (4) the literature was restricted to English-language publications.

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) letters, reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, and expert opinions; (2) when multiple publications described the same or overlapping populations, the study with the largest sample size or the most recent publication was chosen for analysis; (3) survival data that could not be obtained directly or indirectly for quantitative analysis; (4) studies that did not provide sufficient information to assess the risk of methodological bias or were unable to establish research eligibility were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent data extractors systematically reviewed all eligible studies and gathered the necessary information for our analysis. In instances of disagreement, a third extractor was brought in to participate in the discussion and facilitate consensus when required. The following data were extracted in a standardized format: Author, year of publication, country of study, sample size, tumor type, median age, median follow-up time, type of outcome, HR, and corresponding 95% CI. For instances where the required data were not available in the published articles, the entry was recorded as “not available (NA)”. When studies provided HR and 95% CI, these values were directly extracted from the original research. In the absence of HR and 95% CI in the literature, survival data were extracted from Kaplan–Meier survival curves using Engauge Digitizer 4.1 software. Additionally, Tierney’s method was employed to calculate HR and 95% CI indirectly [23]. The quality of the included studies was assessed by both researchers using the New Castle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort analysis, evaluating aspects such as selection, comparability, and outcome measures [24]. Studies with a NOS score of 6 or higher were deemed to be of high quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis for this study was conducted using Stata 14.0 software. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for a two-tailed test. A pooled HR greater than 1 indicated a poorer prognosis, while a pooled HR less than 1 suggested a better prognosis, provided the P-value was less than 0.05. The impact of different levels of HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB on prognosis across various tumors was assessed by combining HRs with their corresponding 95% CI values. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic [25]. If the I2 was less than 50% and the P-value was greater than 0.05, indicating no significant heterogeneity, a fixed effect model was employed to estimate the combined HR value. Conversely, a random effects model was utilized [26]. Subgroup analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between lipid marker levels (HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB) and the prognosis of different tumors, based on baseline characteristics such as tumor type, median follow-up time, age, sample size, analysis method, and the ethnicity of the population, and to explore potential sources of heterogeneity [27]. Sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the robustness of the results. The removal of individual studies had little impact on the conclusion, indicating that the findings of this study were relatively stable. Finally, the potential for publication bias was assessed through the use of Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test [28].

Results

Characteristics of the studies

The flow diagram illustrating the selection process of the study is presented in Fig. 1. Initially, our database search yielded 5,398 studies based on the defined selection criteria. Upon reviewing the titles and abstracts, 4,701 articles were identified as either irrelevant or duplicates and were consequently excluded, leading to a preliminary compilation of 697 studies. A meticulous examination of the full-text articles ensued, narrowing down the selection to 144 papers, which included a total of 156 studies that met our literature inclusion and exclusion standards. This resulted in the inclusion of 85,173 patients’ data, examining the correlation between pretreatment serum lipid levels and cancer survival outcomes [10, 29–171].

PRISMA flowchart of the selection process

A total of 93 studies focused on HDL-C, 72 on LDL-C, 101 on TC, 76 on TG, 44 on ApoA1, and 33 on ApoB. Predominantly, cohort studies originated from China, with a substantial contribution of 120 studies. Additional studies were conducted in Japan (7), South Korea (7), the United States (4), Italy (3), Canada (2), Portugal (2), Australia (1), the Czech Republic (1), Denmark (1), Finland (1), Germany (1), Norway (1), Slovenia (1), Sweden (1), Switzerland (1), Turkey (1), and the United Kingdom (1). The cancer types with sample sizes exceeding 10 were colorectal cancer (CRC; n = 24), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; n = 20), lung cancer (LC; n = 19), renal cell carcinoma (RCC; n = 16), gastric cancer (GC; n = 14), breast cancer (BC; n = 14), esophageal cancer (EC; n = 11), gynecological cancer (GYC; n = 11), and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC; n = 11). The distribution of studies encompassed 69 that investigated both OS and DFS, 72 that focused solely on OS, and 15 that were concerned with DFS alone. Studies were deemed eligible based on a NOS score of 6 or higher. Details of the included studies are provided in Table 1.

Pretreatment blood HDL-C level and cancer outcomes

Collectively, 83 studies with 41,386 patients assessed the link between HDL-C levels and OS. Significant statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 76.9%, P < 0.001), necessitating the use of a random-effects model to compute the HRs and their 95% CIs. The findings revealed that patients with elevated HDL-C levels prior to treatment exhibited a significant association with prolonged OS (pooled HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.72–0.81, P < 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 2. Subgroup analysis was subsequently conducted to dissect potential sources of heterogeneity, focusing on OS in relevant studies (Table 2). When stratified by cancer type, the aggregate results suggested that higher blood HDL-C levels were strongly associated with improved OS in patients with breast cancer (BC; pooled HR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.33–0.83, P = 0.006), bladder cancer (BLCA; pooled HR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.38–0.82, P = 0.003), colorectal cancer (CRC; pooled HR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.57–0.74, P < 0.001), gastric cancer (GC; pooled HR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.78–0.93, P < 0.001), gynecological cancer (GYC; pooled HR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.44–0.82, P = 0.001), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; pooled HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.67–0.92, P = 0.003), and lung cancer (LC; pooled HR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.49–0.82, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the relationship between higher blood HDL-C levels and better OS remained statistically significant across various age groups, sample sizes, follow-up durations, and analytical methodologies. When ethnicity was considered, higher blood HDL-C levels were significantly associated with improved OS in Asian patients (pooled HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.72–0.81, P < 0.001), whereas this association was not observed in non-Asians patients (pooled HR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.46–1.03, P = 0.072).

A total of 52 studies, incorporating 26,817 patients, investigated the correlation between HDL-C levels and DFS. Pretreatment blood TG level and cancer outcomes

A comprehensive analysis of 76 studies encompassing 39,035 patients examined the relationship between serum TG levels and OS. Seventy-two of these investigations, involving 35,555 patients, investigated the impact of TG levels on OS. Collectively, these studies yielded no significant association between serum TG levels and OS in cancer patients (pooled HR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.99–1.03, P = 0.469; Fig. 8). In GYC patients, eight studies reported on the correlation between TG levels and OS, revealing that elevated serum TG levels were a significant predictor of unfavorable OS (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.07–1.30, P = 0.001). However, no such prognostic significance was observed for TG levels in other malignancies. With regards to ethnicity, high blood TG levels were found to be significantly associated with reduced OS rates in non-Asians patients (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.02–1.62, P = 0.036). Publication bias

To assess the presence of publication bias, Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were employed. The analyses revealed that there was no discernible evidence of publication bias in the aggregate results for the association between blood LDL-C (Fig. S1, Fig. S2), TG (Fig. S3, Fig. S4), and ApoB (Fig. S5, Fig. S6) levels and the OS and DFS of cancer patients. However, suggestive evidence of publication bias was identified for blood HDL-C (Fig. S7, Fig. S8) and TC (Fig. S9, Fig. S10) in their associations with OS and DFS. In the case of blood ApoA-1, there was a hint of publication bias for OS (Fig. S11); conversely, no indication of bias was found for DFS (Fig. S12).

Discussion

The link between obesity and disease risk could be mediated by blood lipid profiles and the metabolism of lipids within tissues [172]. Elevated levels of blood lipids, particularly HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB, are known risk factors for cardiovascular disease. It is not uncommon for patients battling malignant tumors to concurrently experience dyslipidemia, a condition that is intricately linked to the presence of cancer and its subsequent treatment. Despite this, the relationship between altered lipid profiles and the prognosis of tumors remains speculative, with the current scientific literature demonstrating conflicting conclusions. The purpose of this study was to ascertain whether the concentrations of these six lipid fractions (HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoA1, and ApoB) can serve as prognostic indicators for individuals suffering from cancer. To the best of our understanding, this research constitutes the largest and most thorough investigation into the prognostic relevance of these lipid markers among cancer patients.

In our comprehensive analysis, we tabulated a substantial dataset derived from 144 pertinent articles, encompassing 156 individual studies, and a collective total of 85,173 cancer patients. Our findings revealed that pre-treatment levels of HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1 emerged as prognostic factors for OS and DFS across various malignancies. Conversely, LDL-C, TG, and ApoB levels did not exhibit a consistent association with overall prognosis. Notably, patients with elevated HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1 levels prior to treatment experienced prolonged OS and DFS. Among these, ApoA1 stood out as a significant predictor (OS: HR = 0.65, 95%CI = 0.58–0.74, P < 0.001; DFS: HR = 0.69, 95%CI = 0.62–0.77, P < 0.001), surpassing the influence of HDL-C (OS: HR = 0.76, 95%CI = 0.72–0.81, P < 0.001; DFS: HR = 0.72, 95%CI = 0.66–0.79, P < 0.001) and TC (OS: HR = 0.96, 95%CI = 0.95–0.98, P < 0.001; DFS: HR = 0.96, 95%CI = 0.95–0.98, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses further elucidated that high pre-treatment HDL-C levels were beneficial for OS in BC, CRC, GC, GYC, HCC, and LC, and for DFS in BC, CRC, GC, GYC, LC, NPC, and RCC. Additionally, favorable OS was associated with elevated TC levels in BLCA, GC, LC, and RCC, while DFS was linked to higher TC levels in CRC, GC, NPC, and RCC. Furthermore, high ApoA1 levels were found to be a strong predictor of improved OS in CRC, EC, HCC, LC, NPC, and RCC patients, and a potential prognostic biomarker for DFS in CRC, HCC, NPC, and RCC patients.

Research has firmly established that reduced levels of HDL-C and ApoA1 serve as potent, independent indicators of atherosclerosis and inflammatory conditions. In the absence of these diseases, pre-treatment lipid profiles assume critical prognostic value for patients with cancer. To date, the intricate biological mechanisms underpinning the strong association between ApoA1, HDL-C, and cancer patient survival remain somewhat elusive. Nonetheless, the rationale behind the extended OS and DFS observed in cancer patients with elevated ApoA1 and HDL-C levels is multifaceted and potentially encompasses a combination of factors [173–175]. First, low levels of HDL and ApoA1 may play a role in the inflammatory processes that are intricately linked to tumor development. Second, within the tumor microenvironment, ApoA1 and HDL can facilitate cholesterol uptake, which in turn promotes the recruitment and accumulation of M1 phenotype macrophages, known for their anti-tumor activities. Third, the regulation of cholesterol content by ApoA1 and HDL can influence immune function or signaling pathways within the tumor cell membrane lipid rafts. Additionally, HDL possesses antioxidant properties that can mitigate oxidative stress by inhibiting the cascade of LDL oxidation. Furthermore, ApoA1 may contribute to tumor regression by restraining angiogenesis.

Several factors may explain why serum TC levels influence the unfavorable prognosis in patients. Firstly, individuals with disrupted TC regulation frequently exhibit compromised immune system function. Notably, studies have documented a notable decline in total T cells and CD8 + T cells among patients with lower TC levels compared to those with higher TC levels. Secondly, the antioxidant defense mechanisms in serum are linked to TC levels; thus, reduced TC levels may potentiate oxidative stress vulnerability. Furthermore, decreased TC levels have been associated with elevated serum interleukin-6 (IL-6), a pro-inflammatory cytokine that is recognized for its role in the onset and progression of cancer [176, 177].

Although the relationship between blood LDL-C levels and survival outcomes in cancer patients yielded negative statistical significance overall, intriguing positive associations were identified through subgroup analyses. Our findings indicate that pre-treatment LDL-C levels correlate with a significant impact on DFS in BC, EC, and RCC. The rationale behind the association between higher LDL-C levels before treatment and a poorer prognosis in certain cancer types may lie in the mechanisms by which tumor cells adhere to the vascular endothelium. This adhesion primarily involves molecules on the tumor cell surface, and LDL has been shown to stimulate the expression of various adhesion-related molecules, thereby enhancing tumor cell adhesion. Increased expression of markers such as integrins, endothelial cell adhesion molecules, and vascular cell adhesion molecules contributes to tumor cell migration and invasion, ultimately influencing the prognosis of cancer patients [178].

Our investigation revealed that elevated levels of TG are potent predictors of poor OS and DFS in GC patients. The precise biological underpinnings of the potential connection between TG levels and cancer survival outcomes remain elusive. TG serve as a key energy reserve and provide a substratum for fatty acid oxidation, which is critical for cellular proliferation and migration. Excessive TG accumulation can result in enlarged adipocytes that alter the secretion of adipokines, steroid hormones, and inflammatory mediators, thereby perturbing the cellular milieu. Such alterations may contribute to the initiation and progression of tumorigenesis and metastasis [179].

Our investigation further suggests that serum ApoB levels may serve as a prognostic biomarker for OS in GC patients and for DFS in LC patients. ApoB, predominantly synthesized in the liver, encompasses both ApoB100 and ApoB40. It plays a crucial role as a structural constituent of the LDL receptor, facilitating the elimination of LDL from the bloodstream and participating in the production and secretion of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL). VLDL, upon attachment to vessel walls, undergoes degradation, with its triglycerides being broken down into free fatty acids and glycerol, thereby delivering energy to peripheral tissues. In this degradation process, VLDL is converted into intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL), and subsequently, IDL is transformed into LDL. LDL, bound to liver cells via its ApoB100 component, is then taken up by the LDL receptor. Subsequently, ApoB100 is broken down into amino acids within the liver during the next metabolic cycle [180]. We postulated that elevated circulating levels of ApoB indicate diminished LDL receptor (LDLR) expression, and that the unfavorable postoperative outcomes might be associated with compromised liver function.

Cancer is a disease marked by substantial heterogeneity, and the interplay of lipid metabolism with cancer is complex. On one hand, dyslipidemia may offer an enhanced cholesterol supply to cancer cells, prompting them to generate greater quantities of pro-cancerous oxidized cholesterol, thereby elevating the risk of tumor development [181]. Conversely, lipid levels are a mirror of the body’s nutritional status. Fatigue is a prevalent symptom among patients with advanced cancer, and the clinical course of many tumor patients is typically marked by a progressive, irreversible decline in appetite, weight loss, and a worsening of nutritional health, ultimately leading to the patient’s demise. Research has unequivocally demonstrated that malnutrition significantly impairs the course of cancer treatment and prognosis, principally exhibited through: abbreviated survival rates, reduced tolerance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, diminished responsiveness to cancer-fighting therapies, compromised immune function due to biochemical alterations such as anemia and hypoproteinemia during treatment interruptions, heightened susceptibility to infections, and extended hospital stays [182]. Hence, it is not difficult to comprehend why elevated lipid levels can elevate the risk of cancer while potentially offering a more favorable prognosis for certain tumor types. Nonetheless, additional research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of specific lipids that participate in the initiation and progression of cancer.

To further enhance the management of blood lipid levels for improved prognosis in cancer patients, the following strategies can be implemented: First, develop and disseminate clear clinical guidelines and protocols that highlight the importance of managing blood lipid levels in cancer patients. These should be evidence-based and tailored to different types of cancer. Second, educate patients about the relationship between blood lipid levels and cancer prognosis. Provide information on lifestyle modifications and the importance of adhering to lipid-lowering therapies. In addition, encourage and support patients in adopting a healthy lifestyle, including a balanced diet, regular physical activity, and smoking cessation, which can significantly impact blood lipid profiles. Moreover, implement a system for regular monitoring of blood lipid levels in cancer patients, allowing for timely interventions when necessary. Last but not least, foster a multidisciplinary approach to patient care, involving oncologists, cardiologists, dietitians, and other healthcare professionals to address the complex needs of cancer patients with dyslipidemia.

Statins have been reported to have anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant effects, as well as a potential effect on cancer. Moreover, statins and blood lipid levels can potentially impact outcomes independently and they are also related. Current evidence has supported that blood lipid levels are associated with cancer incidence and overall statins use is significantly associated with a reduction in risk of multiple cancers. However, whether statin use is causally associated with survival outcome of cancers should be further studied. In clinical care, we usually do not go for encouraging low-fat diets or using statins in patients with tumors due to malaise combined with malnutrition. On the contrary, we will encourage patients to eat more meat so that they will have enough nutrition to cooperate with the treatment and fight against the tumor. In this study, we did not analyze the direct effect of statins on the results of this study.

This analysis features several noteworthy aspects. First, the substantial sample size of 156 cohort studies, encompassing over 80,000 cancer patients, enhanced the statistical power and ensured the reliability of the merged findings. Second, our research has yielded the most exhaustive insights into the prognostic significance of blood lipid levels across diverse malignancies through rigorous statistical examination. Third, our findings suggest that the levels of blood lipid metabolic biomarkers, particularly HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1, could serve as potent and non-invasive indicators for forecasting the prognosis in cancer patients.

Beyond the intriguing findings, our analysis is not without its limitations. First, the majority of the study designs included in this paper are retrospective, and there is a paucity of prospective research necessary to corroborate the association between blood lipid levels and cancer prognosis. Second, the sample sizes for certain cancer type subgroups are small, leading to statistically fragile conclusions; accordingly, larger-scale studies are imperative to validate our findings. Third, the heterogeneity observed across studies is associated with factors such as sample size, ethnicity, and tumor type, yet this heterogeneity has not been parsed through subgroup analyses based on other pertinent clinical features (e.g., tumor stage, smoking history, gender). The influence of this heterogeneity on the relationship between HDL-C, ApoA1, and TC levels and cancer prognosis is unclear and may potentially exaggerate the associations observed. Furthermore, the variability in the cut-off values for blood lipids and the methods used to determine these values introduces inconsistency into our analysis. Additionally, the predominantly Chinese population studied may introduce a degree of bias. To confirm the relevance of blood lipid levels in predicting patient survival, replication studies in diverse geographical regions are warranted. Lastly, we did not elucidate the effect of statins on outcomes and their impact with lipid levels in disease management.

Conclusions and clinical perspective

In summary, blood lipid metabolic biomarkers, particularly HDL-C, TC, and ApoA1, emerge as promising predictors of survival outcomes in patients with diverse forms of cancer. Our investigation may assist clinicians in distinguishing patients at elevated risk for unfavorable outcomes and in tailoring individualized treatment approaches. Moving forward, large-scale, well-designed, prospective, multicenter studies are essential to validate and enhance our understanding of the prognostic value of blood lipids in cancer patients.
