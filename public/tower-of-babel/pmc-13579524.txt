Porous organic polymers for sustainable pollutant capture and environmental remediation: recent advances and future perspectives
Authors: Poornachandra Shamanna Prabhakar, Shivakumar, Asha Danthi, Parveen Shaik, Amgoth Ramesh, T. N. Varshitha, Udaya Kumar Dalimba, B. C. Revanasiddappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Environmental pollution, climate change, and increasing demands for sustainable remediation require adsorbents that combine high efficiency with selectivity, stability, and regenerability. Porous organic polymers (POPs) have emerged as versatile platforms because their covalently linked networks, permanent porosity, and tunable surface chemistry enable molecular-level control of pollutant–framework interactions. This review critically examines major POP families, including hypercrosslinked polymers, conjugated microporous polymers, porous aromatic frameworks, covalent organic frameworks, and covalent triazine frameworks, with emphasis on the relationships among pore architecture, surface functionality, charge distribution, and environmental performance. Recent applications in CO2 capture, volatile organic compounds, heavy metals, radionuclides, dyes, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and other organic pollutants are evaluated across water, air, and soil remediation. Particular attention is given to adsorption mechanisms, selectivity, regeneration, moisture tolerance, long-term stability, and practical deployment through membranes, fibers, aerogels, and hybrid systems. Emerging green synthesis strategies, including aqueous, solvent-free, mechanochemical, microwave-assisted, catalyst-free, and biomass-derived approaches, are assessed in terms of sustainability and scalability. The review further considers barriers to commercialization, including precursor and processing costs, regeneration energy, performance in complex environmental matrices, continuous-flow operation, and competition with established low-cost adsorbents such as activated carbon. Finally, opportunities involving stimuli-responsive and multifunctional POPs, hybrid technologies, and machine-learning-assisted materials design are discussed as pathways toward sustainable, selective, and application-oriented environmental remediation.

Introduction

The ever-increasing world population is leading to the increasing growth of industries and agriculture that are causing an increased need for the consumption of natural resources.1–5 Although these changes have contributed to the improvement in the quality of lives of the people, at the same time, they have created environmental problems related to climate change, soil degradation, and pollution1,4,6,7 For instance, in the atmosphere, the high need for energy has resulted in an increased use of fossil fuels and consequently in anthropogenic greenhouse gases, including CO2 and CH4, causing global warming and extreme weather conditions.3,6,8 Additionally, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) air pollution has become a serious environmental problem in the world, and its concentration is strongly related to the population density and energy consumption.3

Beyond atmospheric concerns, the discharge of untreated industrial waste has resulted in the co-contamination of both soil and water.1,4 The natural environment has been degraded by toxic substances such as artificial dyes, for example, Methylene Blue (MB) and Rhodamine B (RhB), pharmaceutical residues, and heavy metals.1,4,9,10 Heavy metals are highly dangerous since they do not degrade and bioaccumulate along the food chain, hence resulting in health problems such as life-threatening organ failure and cancers.1,4 Globally, there is an estimate that up to 80% of wastewater is dumped untreated, hence worsening the problem of water scarcity and environmental contamination.4,11

Pollution can be categorized into point sources, which have known points of pollution release such as industrial pipes, and non-point sources like agricultural runoff and urban rainfall-runoff that are more challenging to regulate.2,12 Agriculture is one of the largest contributors to pollution through diffusion, causing water pollution due to nitrates and phosphates leaching.2,12 One of the main environmental pressures is the deposition of mercury, characterized by spatially legacy effects where contaminants are deposited and transported downstream using digital river networks.11 These problems of climate change and pollution have led to a situation where close to 80% of the population on earth is exposed to high risk of water insecurity, while the freshwater biodiversity undergoes extinction at similar rates as major changes in geological transitions.4,11 The solution to these problems involves utilizing sustainable and cost-effective technologies such as nanomaterials and adsorption with porous organic polymers (POPs).1,4,5

In order to solve the increasing problems arising from environmental pollution, sustainable strategies employing the concepts of green chemistry that focus on the reduction of waste generation, energy efficiency, and the development of environment-friendly technology are necessary.13–15 A number of methods have been investigated for environmental remediation, such as precipitation, coagulation, electrodialysis, catalytic destruction, membrane filtration, and adsorption.13,16–19 Among these approaches, adsorption has gained great importance owing to its ease of operation, economically feasibility, effectiveness, and eco-friendliness.13,18,20–22

An effective process of adsorption mainly requires the development of high-performance adsorbent materials with high selectivity and affinity towards the pollutants like pharmaceuticals, heavy metal ions, and radioactive elements.13,18,21–23 Adsorbents, like alum, clay, activated carbon, silica, and zeolite have been extensively used in the removal of pollutants.13,18,20,24 These conventional adsorbents suffer from certain disadvantages, including low selectivity, instability at extremes of pH values, and loss of performance on multiple usage.13,15,18–20 Hence, advanced adsorbent materials are increasingly sought after, which include attributes of high adsorption, high selectivity, chemical stability, and facile regeneration.13,15,19 Besides water purification, the advanced adsorbent materials are also studied for their ability in capturing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere as part of the efforts towards minimizing global CO2 emissions using methods like post combustion capture, pre-combustion capture, oxy-fuel combustion, and direct air capture.19,25–27 Methods involved in capturing carbon are already known; however, the main challenge still remains to develop advanced materials that would allow efficient CO2 capture with minimal regeneration temperature.25

The recent development in materials science has resulted in the synthesis of different types of porous materials such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs),28,29 covalent organic frameworks (COFs),30 and POPs.31,32 All these materials exhibit high porosity along with extensive surface area which renders them highly effective materials for various uses such as gas storage, catalysis, adsorption, and environmental remediation.13,15,19,31 In addition to this, POPs have become increasingly popular because of their outstanding diversity in structure and chemical stability along with the tunable porosity.13,15,19,26,32,33

POPs comprise covalently linked organic networks constructed predominantly from carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O), while their frameworks may also incorporate heteroatoms such as boron (B), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), and silicon (Si), as well as, less commonly, other elements such as germanium (Ge).15,25,26,31,33,34 The highly cross-linked structure of POPs provides them with enormous surface area, porosity, and pores that are easily customizable to introduce different functional groups.14,21,25–27,33 Due to their characteristics, they are able to adsorb multiple types of pollutants like CO2, heavy metals (such as Pb and Hg), and organic pollutants (like dyes, pharmaceuticals) from the environment.13,18,25,30–33 Specialized POPs have also been used for the detection of oxic substances, e.g., polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, within soil samples.35

POPs exhibit several advantages over other porous substances. For instance, MOFs feature crystalline structures and tunable porosities but are characterized by low stability in tough environments as well as high costs of production.13,15,21,22,25–27,31 Activated carbon is favored for its low price, although its selectivity tends to be low.19,25–27 On the other hand, zeolites are known for their chemical stability but may not always display good adsorption capacity in complex settings.13,15,27 While COFs feature well-ordered crystallinity and tailored functionalities, the process of their synthesis is relatively complicated and difficult to conduct on an industrial scale.14,15,27,31

On the contrary, the POPs possess high thermal and chemical stability due to the covalent bonding with modular design flexibility and scalability in synthesis14,25–27,33,34 Since POPs are usually isolated as kinetic materials, they provide ease of scalability in comparison with their crystalline analogues with fewer variables.14,27 The structural modification of POPs is possible through the judicious choice of monomers and appropriate synthetic routes such as Friedel–Crafts arylation, Sonogashira coupling, and Schiff base reaction.19,21,27,33,34 Due to these reasons, POPs have gained attention as novel adsorbents for pollution prevention and control.31,33,36,37

Despite the existence of numerous studies and review articles about POPs used for various applications including gas storage, catalysis, and environmental remediation, most of the existing works have mainly concentrated on either a few types of POPs (COFs, CMPs, or HCPs), pollutant classes, or individual adsorption mechanisms.15,19,27,33 Moreover, while many review papers concentrate on synthesis and structure properties of materials, no systematic effort has been made to relate framework properties, including hierarchical porosity, surface functionalities, charge distribution, and physical stability of POPs, to their practical pollutant capturing efficiencies under atmospheric, aquatic, and terrestrial conditions. Additionally, recent trends regarding smart POP materials, multimodal hybrid systems, machine learning assisted-materials development, and sustainable synthetic routes have remained unexplored in the context of pollutant removal. Hence, in this review, we try to bridge this gap and analyze the progress in developing environmentally functional POPs from a variety of perspectives including synthetic methods, structures, adsorption properties, selectivity, regeneration process, and practical pollutant removal applications.

Fundamentals of porous organic polymers (POPs)

POPs are the newly emerged family of 2D/3D macro-molecular network materials synthesized by the connection of small organic molecules through stable covalent bonding leading to permanently porous structures.19,25–27,33 The framework of POPs is mainly made up of light elements such as carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O); besides, POPs also contain boron (B) and/or silicon (Si).19,27,31,33,34 As compared to the coordination-based materials, MOFs, for example, the existence of stable covalent linkages (e.g., C–C, C–N, and C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N) in POPs makes these materials highly chemically and physically stable, i.e., POPs withstand harsh thermal and chemical environments.18,19,25,26,33,34

POPs can be broadly classified into amorphous and crystalline subclasses depending upon their level of long range periodicity.18,19,21,33,38 The amorphous POPs are characterized by lack of periodic regularity and presence of highly advanced microporous structures as well as large surface area, and include various classes such as hyper-crosslinked polymers (HCPs) like Davankov resins, HCP-1, KFUPM-2; conjugated microporous polymers (CMPs) including CMP-1, LKK-CMP-1, CMP-LS2; porous aromatic frameworks (PAFs) such as PAF-1, PAF-3, JUC-Z1; and polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) like PIM-1, hPIM-1.15,19,25,26,33 However, crystalline POPs, especially the one exemplified by COFs (such as COF-1, COF-5, TpPa-1, [EtNH2]75-H2P-COF), show a high degree of order with uniform channels in the pores that are obtained due to reversible dynamic covalent chemistry.19,21,25,26 Although several covalent triazine frameworks (CTFs) (e.g., CTF-1, CTF-hex1–6) are amorphous, some methods of synthesis will yield crystalline varieties of this material.15,19,25,33

A defining characteristic of POPs is the high flexibility with which their structural and chemical features can be engineered.14,25,33,34 They offer a great deal of design flexibility; the pore environments and skeletal structures can be tailored with the use of multifunctional monomers or through the use of post-synthetic modification (PSM) to incorporate functional groups like –COOH, –SO3H, or –NH2 (e.g., PPN-6-SO3H, PAF-1-NH2).19,25,27,33 Moreover, most of the POP systems are based on polymers and are free from metals which eliminate the potential for recontamination during the environmental cleanup process.21,23,33 Because POPs are macromolecular network materials, they can be processed into a wide range of macroscopic forms, including thin-film membranes, fibrous monoliths, and beads, providing useful shape and processability for practical applications.23,26,33,38 In addition, POPs tend to be produced as kinetic products, which allows for easy scalability to the industrial level with much fewer optimization criteria than those associated with thermodynamic crystalline structures.14,27,33 This combination of permanent porosity, structural robustness, synthetic versatility, and adaptable processability provides a strong foundation for designing application-specific POPs capable of addressing a broad range of environmental challenges, which are discussed in the following sections.

Numerous traditional adsorbent materials, such as activated carbon, silica, zeolites and clay minerals have been utilized extensively for pollutant removal and gas adsorption yet are often subjected to many limitations, such as a lack of tunability in terms of pore size, a lack of selectivity for specific pollutants, and a lack of stability in harsh chemical or humid conditions.13,18,19,22,25,26 While zeolites and silica have good chemical stability, they often have limited capacity for adsorption or greatly reduced performance when exposed to water; activated carbon typically is a non-specific adsorbent and has to be regenerated at elevated temperatures and won't become fully restorable.13,18,19,25,30 POPs are seen as novel alternatives due to their ability to combine the characteristics of polymers with porous materials, providing them with outstanding structural versatility, tunable pore structure & chemically stable frameworks constructed from purely organic monomers.15,19,27,33 In contrast to inorganic adsorbent materials, POPs consist of strong covalent bonds (e.g. C–C, C–N, CN) as the means of linking one polymer chain to another and therefore provide POPs with significantly higher levels of thermal stability, chemical durability, and mechanical strength than do inorganic materials; thus POPs can be maintained structurally within chemically demanding conditions (e.g. strong acids or base solutions).18,19,25,26,33,34

POPs have emerged as an attractive alternative because they combine the structural robustness of covalently linked polymeric frameworks with the versatility of molecular-level design.25,33 The flexibility in the molecular design of POPs permits their well-defined engineering on a molecular level, and thus enables incorporation of specific functional groups such as –COOH, –SO3H, and –NH2, into them for creating materials such as PPN-6-SO3H or PAF-1-CH2NH2, which then enhance selective adsorption via dipole–quadrupole interactions and hydrogen bonding.19,25–27,33 Likewise, surface framework engineering with the phenolic-OH groups has enabled materials such as PAF-24 for efficient iodine capture and TrzPOP-3 for highly selective CO2/N2 separation (167.4 at 273 K), illustrating the ability of POPs to address separation challenges beyond the capability of conventional non-functionalized adsorbents.19,33 Additionally, POPs are typically composed of low-density elements (i.e. C, H, N, O) resulting in a low skeletal density relative to heavier, inorganic sorbents, thus further contributing to the high gravimetric adsorption capacity of POPs than heavier inorganic sorbents.19,21,27,31,33

The recyclable and regenerative capabilities of POPs are further enhanced by their stable covalent bond networks preventing both degradation and leaching of active sites during multiple cycles.13,14,18,19,26 An example is porphyrin-based frameworks such as PPOP which maintains over 98% of its original capacity after five cycles, while a cationic composite of 120-TMA@Fe demonstrates excellent performance after 10 cycles in harsh environments.18,31 Due to the absence of metal nodes in many POP structures, such as the metal-free iVOFm or iPOP-3, there is virtually no potential for secondary contamination of metal ions during the water purification process.13,23,31,33 POPs have the desirable characteristics of high capacity and shapeability, they can be manufactured as membranes and beads, and their scalability through kinetic pathways make POPs an advanced solution to the limitations inherent to traditional adsorbents for environmental remediation, gas capture, and heterogeneous catalysis.14,19,21,23,27,33

Synthesis of POPs

The synthesis of POPs directly influences their network connectivity, porosity, surface functionality, and, consequently, their pollutant-capture behavior. Diverse polymerization and post-synthetic strategies have therefore been developed to tailor framework architecture and chemical functionality, including condensation, metal-catalyzed coupling, Friedel–Crafts knitting, ionothermal, radical, microwave-assisted, mechanochemical, and post-synthetic approaches.19,25,27,33,34,39–42 Among these, the selection and geometry of shape-persistent building blocks such as tetraphenylmethane, triptycene, porphyrin, triazine, pyrene, and related rigid units are particularly important because they promote permanent free volume while providing sites that can be further exploited for selective pollutant interactions.19,27 Accordingly, this section focuses on how synthetic strategies and building-block design govern the structural and functional features of POPs, with particular emphasis on approaches relevant to environmental separation and the emerging need for more efficient, sustainable, and scalable synthesis.

Condensation polymerization

Condensation polymerisation is among the most versatile routes for constructing POPs, particularly because complementary functional groups can generate robust covalent linkages under relatively mild conditions while allowing systematic control of pore chemistry and framework functionality. Schiff-base reactions between aldehydes and amines are especially important, although hydroxyl, azo, aminal, hydrazone, and related condensation/coupling chemistries have also expanded the accessible structures. The resulting frameworks combine permanent porosity with chemically stable and functionalised backbones, as demonstrated by TPE-COF-II (2168 m2 g−1), TrzPOP-3 (8.54 mmol g−1 CO2 at 273 K), and HAT-CTF-450/600, which reaches a CO2/N2 selectivity of 160 through enhanced quadrupole and hydrogen-bonding interactions.43 Nitrogen-rich COP-19 (Scheme 1), prepared by one-pot polycondensation of melamine and terephthalaldehyde in DMSO at 180 °C, provides another example; subsequent PEI incorporation affords COP-97/COP-98, in which deliberate reduction of surface area increases CO2-philic functionality. COP-97B reaches 102.5 mg g−1 CO2 uptake at 273 K, while COP-97C achieves a CO2/N2 selectivity of 2511.4 at 298 K and maintains performance over 10 cycles; carbamate formation under dry conditions and bicarbonate formation under humid conditions further explain its moisture tolerance.44

Ionothermal cyclotrimerisation also produces CTFs through ZnCl2-mediated conversion of nitrile-bearing monomers (TAT, TOT and TST), where molten ZnCl2 acts simultaneously as Lewis's-acid catalyst and reaction medium. Sequential heating is required to accommodate differences in monomer reactivity, with final polymerisation at 400 °C requiring 75, 15 and 5 h for PhNH-, PhS- and PhO-linked CTFs, respectively45 (Scheme 2). These frameworks retain high surface areas, reaching 1748 m2 g−1 even after partial carbonisation, while CO2 affinity follows PhNH > PhO > PhS, with a calculated binding energy of −20.7 kJ mol−1 for the PhNH-linked framework; the PhNH-CTF prepared at 600 °C reaches 5.0 mmol g−1 CO2 at 273 K and 1 bar.45 COF-609 is another example in which imine condensation of TFPT and DABA initially affords COF-609-Im (724 m2 g−1), followed by Fe3+-catalysed aza-Diels–Alder conversion with CVE and subsequent TRPN installation through nucleophilic substitution46 (Scheme 3). Although the resulting disorder and flexible amines decrease porosity, functionalisation increases CO2 uptake 1360-fold to 6.8 cm3 g−1 at 0.4 mbar and 25 °C, with a further 29% enhancement at 50% relative humidity because water promotes carbamate formation.46

The TrzPOP series further demonstrates that chemical functionality can outweigh surface area in adsorption. One-step Schiff-base polycondensation of triazine-containing tetraamine SL-1 with dialdehydes in anhydrous DMSO at 180 °C produces TrzPOP-1, TrzPOP-2 and TrzPOP-3 with BET areas of 772–995 m2 g−1; incorporation of phenolic OH groups in TrzPOP-2 and TrzPOP-3 enhances CO2 interactions, giving 7.51 and 8.54 mmol g−1 uptake at 273 K, respectively, despite their lower surface areas than TrzPOP-1 47 (Scheme 4). Acid-catalysed ternary polycondensation provides KFUPM-1 from 1,4-benzenediamine, pyrrole and paraformaldehyde in DMF at 90 °C, yielding an 88% product with a BET area of 305 m2 g−1. Its polar amine-rich network affords 23.4 cm3 g−1 CO2 uptake at 298 K, CO2/N2 selectivity of 141, and 15.1 cm3 g−1 uptake even at 91% relative humidity48 (Scheme 5). Aromatic nucleophilic polycondensation provides CoPIMs from 2,3,5,6-TFTPN with TTSBI and THDN in DMAc/toluene at 160 °C for 120 min. The resulting rigid, contorted microporous structures improve thermal stability to 430 °C; DNPIM-33 reaches CO2 permeability of 4646 Barrer, O2/N2 selectivity of 3.75 beyond the Robeson upper bound, and CO2/N2 selectivity of 19.2.49

Aminal-containing systems broaden condensation chemistry beyond conventional imine linkages. NAPOP-1–4 are obtained through one-pot Schiff-base/aminal-linked polycondensation of BATB with N-heterocyclic aldehydes in DMSO at 180 °C 50 (Scheme 6). Functional variation using piperazine, imidazole, tetrazole and pyridine tunes pore environments and gives BET areas of 458–702 m2 g−1. NAPOP-3 adsorbs 2.52 mmol g−1 CO2, whereas NAPOP-4 captures 265 wt% iodine within 25 h; the former also shows a CO2/N2 selectivity of 77 at 273 K and 100 kPa, supported by a 31.2 kJ mol−1 adsorption heat. Their thermal stability up to 400 °C, resistance to organic solvents and stability toward concentrated acids and bases support their practical robustness.50 Similarly, solvothermal condensation between TAPA and PTA produces the three-dimensional, eightfold-interwoven diamond network COF-DL229 with ordered one-dimensional channels and a BET area of 1762 m2 g−1 51 (Scheme 7). Its diphenylimine-rich pores facilitate charge-transfer interactions with iodine, producing a greater iodine-vapour uptake of 4.7 g g−1 at 75 °C; the framework remains stable to 500 °C, permits rapid iodine removal in methanol and retains up to 80% of its initial capacity during cycling.51 BFTB-PyTA, prepared through one-pot solvothermal condensation of BFTB-4CHO and PyTA-4NH2, forms stable imine-linked hollow microtubules with 1133 m2 g−1 surface area52 (Scheme 8). Its pyrene-rich architecture enables RhB removal of 2127 mg g−1 within 5 min at >99% efficiency, while maintaining performance for five cycles and stability in 1 M KOH and up to 433 °C.52

Recent developments increasingly address the environmental burden of conventional solvothermal condensation through aqueous, catalyst-free and energy-efficient routes. Schiff-base [2 + 2] polycondensation of m-phenylenediamine and terephthalaldehyde affords MPA-TPA POP in ethanol or water/ethanol with aqueous acetic acid at room temperature – 120 °C, reaching yields up to 98% and a BET area of 1015 m2 g−1; its imine/aminal-rich hierarchical structure provides 3.51 mmol g−1 CO2 uptake at 273 K and 1 bar 14 (Scheme 10). Azo coupling provides another aqueous and sustainable pathway. GA-azo-DADPM POP, obtained from gallic acid and DADPM using NaNO2/HCl, reaches 87% yield and 402 m2 g−1 surface area, while its phenol/azo-rich network adsorbs 141.94 mg g−1 MB and exhibits antibacterial activity against E. coli66 (Scheme 11). Similarly, CTT-TPC, prepared by diazo coupling of CTT and triptycene-NH2 in water, produces a 90% yield, 600 m2 g−1 microporous framework with phenolic-OH/azo functionality for antibiotic extraction; its performance remains stable for up to 100 cycles.67 Tannin-derived TA-POP, prepared by aqueous diazotisation coupling with DADPM, retains phenolic OH groups, gives 208.266 m2 g−1 surface area and reaches 125 mg g−1 methylene-blue uptake with good cycling stability.68 Ionic iPOP-6, obtained by aqueous coupling of phloroglucinol and Vio-NH2 at 0–5 °C, contains quaternised nitrogen and azo groups; despite its modest 35 m2 g−1 surface area, it rapidly removes permanganate and triiodide ions at nearly 99% efficiency69 (Scheme 12).

Microwave assistance provides a further route to accelerate condensation. Imine-linked POF-1 is produced from tris(4-aminophenyl)methane and 2,2′-bithiophene-5,5′-dicarboxaldehyde in ethanol at 125 °C in only 45 min 72 (Scheme 15). NaBH4-mediated reductive homocoupling produces azo-linked AZO-E-M in approximately 5 min at 140 °C, while MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs can be obtained under 600 W microwave irradiation at 393 K for 3 h instead of conventional three-day solvothermal processing; rapid volumetric heating arises from dipole polarisation and ionic conduction and enables porous networks for CO2 and pesticide capture.73,74 The guanidinium-based ionic POP gn-iPOP-Cl similarly illustrates the benefit of mild condensation that TGDM and TMB form the cationic framework in 45 min at room temperature with acetate catalysis instead of 120 °C/72 h, producing a crystalline material with 34 m2 g−1 surface area and highly rapid permanganate removal, reaching 9.4 g g−1 capacity and complete removal within 20 min 75 (Scheme 16).

Finally, solid-phase cyclotrimerisation extends condensation chemistry to solvent-free POP fabrication. PTSA-catalysed self-condensation of polyacetyl-containing aromatic monomers, including 1,1′-diacetylferrocene, affords WFMC-1 at 130–148 °C within 10–12 h and yields above 98%. The resulting conjugated benzene-linked mesoporous network contains ultrafine Fe2O3 nanoparticles, exhibits high thermal stability and captures iodine up to 3.56 g g−1 while retaining performance over five cycles.76 Collectively, these studies demonstrate that condensation polymerisation has evolved from conventional imine-forming solvothermal synthesis toward multifunctional, aqueous, catalyst-free, microwave-assisted and solvent-free strategies. Importantly, adsorption performance is not governed solely by BET surface area: framework polarity, heteroatom content, hydrogen bonding, charge-transfer interactions, pore confinement and specific host–guest interactions can substantially outweigh surface-area effects, as demonstrated by TrzPOP-2/3, COP-97/98, NAPOPs and several azo-linked POPs. Nevertheless, many conventional condensation routes still require high temperatures, prolonged reaction times and organic solvents, while post-functionalisation can compromise crystallinity or porosity, indicating that future development should prioritise low-energy synthesis, precise linkage control, scalable processing and simultaneous optimisation of pore architecture and chemical functionality.

Cross-coupling polymerization

Cross-coupling polymerization is a versatile strategy for constructing POPs through transition-metal-mediated formation of C–C or C–N bonds, particularly for CMPs, PAFs, and nitrogen-containing POPs. Sonogashira–Hagihara coupling generates rigid poly(arylene ethynylene) networks with extended π-conjugation, whereas Suzuki–Miyaura coupling offers mild conditions and broad functional-group tolerance for C–C-linked frameworks. Yamamoto coupling enables highly connected aromatic networks from a single halogenated monomer, while Buchwald–Hartwig coupling provides C–N-linked, nitrogen-rich frameworks with enhanced host–guest interactions. Despite their structural versatility, catalyst dependence, oxygen/moisture sensitivity, and competing homocoupling or deboronation reactions remain limitations.77–79

The carbonylative Sonogashira–Hagihara synthesis of ynone-linked y-POP (Scheme 17) provides a representative example in which α,β-alkynic ketones serve as sites for subsequent tren functionalization. The resulting y-POP-A1–A3 frameworks exhibit substantially improved CO2 adsorption and selectivity, demonstrating that post-synthetic introduction of amine functionalities can be more influential than the parent pore structure alone.80 Sulfur-rich SCMP-II, prepared by Sonogashira–Hagihara coupling (Scheme 18), combines an extended π-conjugated network with thiophene sulfur sites, enabling high iodine-vapor capture and solution-phase iodine removal while retaining recyclability.81 The CMP-LS1–3 series further demonstrates the influence of coupling chemistry and topology: CMP-LS1 and CMP-LS2 are Suzuki-derived, whereas CMP-LS3 is obtained through Sonogashira–Hagihara coupling (Scheme 19). Their gas adsorption and picric-acid sensing performance confirms that framework connectivity and rigidity, rather than surface area alone, govern functional behavior.82

Cross-coupling also enables the construction of diverse nitrogen-containing POPs, including TTF-1, POP-Py, LKK-CMP-1, 6FA-PE-CL, and PyPOP@G. Complementary ionothermal, microwave, and mechanochemical routes have produced F-CTF-1, while nitrogen-rich systems such as MPC-550, POP-Py(0), POP-Cu@MWCNT, and P4VP-modified frameworks demonstrate the importance of nitrogen sites for CO2 interactions, electrocatalytic intermediate stabilization, conductivity enhancement, gas separation, and selective HCOOH formation.83 The NT-POP-1–6 series, synthesized through Suzuki–Miyaura and Sonogashira–Hagihara polycondensation (Scheme 20) and subsequently pyrolyzed to NT-POP@800-1–6, further establishes the strong dependence of porosity and adsorption on monomer geometry, particularly meta-versus para-linking.84

Por-Py-CMP extends Sonogashira–Hagihara coupling to porphyrin–pyrene building blocks (Scheme 21), integrating CO2 and iodine adsorption with photosensitization and demonstrating the contribution of bulky aromatic units to preventing framework interpenetration.85 Similarly, Buchwald–Hartwig coupling affords PTPM-1–4/mPTPM-1–4 from tetrakis(4-bromophenyl)methane and aryl diamines, followed by diiodomethane-mediated alkylation to generate methylene-bridged cages. The resulting mPTPM-2 and mPTPM-3 exhibit CO2 and iodine adsorption, respectively, while structural modification through ortho-substitution and amine alkylation enhances electron density and guest binding.86

Beyond these systems, modular C–C coupling encompasses Sonogashira–Hagihara-derived CMP-1, Yamamoto-type Ullmann-derived PAF-1 (PPN-6), Suzuki-derived PAF-70-NH2, Eglinton/Glaser-derived PPN-1, Friedel–Crafts-derived SHCP-3, and C(sp3)–C(sp3)-linked porous polyadamantane (PPA). Functionalized frameworks including CMP-1-COOH, PAF-80-SO3H, PAF-33, iPAF-28, and COP-150 demonstrate that pore polarity, functional groups, and framework flexibility can outweigh surface area in determining adsorption, separation, and decontamination performance. The durability of such architectures is further exemplified by PAF-111 and organocatalytic PAF70-thiourea, which maintains catalytic activity over repeated cycles.87

The evolution of cross-coupling POPs began with Yamamoto–Ullmann synthesis of PAF-1 (PPN-6) and PPN-4 using Ni(COD)2, alongside the emergence of Buchwald–Hartwig coupling for amine-containing frameworks and condensation-derived BILP-1, BILP-4, BILP-101, TpPa-1, and TpPa-2. The new frontier (2025–2026) focuses on materials that can have their chemistry modified from their solid state after undergoing the cross-coupling reaction process. According to recent studies by Mohamed et al.,89,90 the Pd(PPh3)4/CuI complexation is utilized in constructing BBT-BZTB-Py POP and Pyr-BZ-Pyr POP. These molecules are later transformed through ROP to give high cross-linked polymers known as poly(BBT-BZTB-Py) POP and poly(Pyr-BZ-Pyr POP), respectively, containing dense phenolic hydroxyl groups that capture gases effectively (Scheme 24).

Overall, cross-coupling polymerization has progressed from the construction of rigid, high-surface-area CMPs and PAFs toward chemically programmable and post-synthetically transformable frameworks. Across CMP-1, PAF-1, PPN-1, SHCP-3, PPA, y-POP, SCMP-II, CMP-LS, NT-POP, Por-Py-CMP, mPTPM, and the emerging hybrid and post-functionalized systems, performance is increasingly governed by the deliberate combination of linker geometry, coupling chemistry, heteroatom content, pore polarity, framework rigidity/flexibility, and secondary functionalization rather than surface area alone. Thus, although catalyst dependence and reaction sensitivity remain challenges, cross-coupling provides a powerful platform for establishing precise structure–property relationships across adsorption, separation, sensing, catalysis, and energy-related applications.

Ionothermal synthesis

Ionothermal synthesis is an effective route for preparing stable porous polymer networks, particularly CTFs, using molten metal salts such as ZnCl2 or FeCl3 as both the reaction medium and Lewis's-acid catalyst. The high-temperature environment, generally around 310–450 °C, promotes extensive condensation and can simultaneously induce partial carbonization, allowing framework formation and pore development in a single solvent-free step. The synthesis temperature therefore strongly influences the resulting structure and properties. For example, CPOP1-I1 synthesized at 310 °C is predominantly microporous and exhibits a CO2 uptake of 18.5 wt% at 273 K, whereas CPOP1-I4 prepared at 450 °C develops interconnected micro–mesopores, demonstrating the strong influence of ionothermal temperature on pore development and gas-adsorption behaviour. These observations highlight the importance of controlling synthesis temperature to tailor the textural properties of porous polycarbazoles.92

The ionothermal approach has been particularly important for CTF formation, where molten ZnCl2 or related metal halides promote nitrile cyclotrimerization at elevated temperatures (Scheme 25). Conventional preparation using ZnCl2 or ZnBr2 at approximately 350–500 °C can generate porous networks, but excessive dehalogenation and graphitization may reduce nitrogen and halide contents. In contrast, the I-CTF series (I-CTF-1 to I-CTF-5) demonstrates an alternative “benign by design” strategy based on nucleophilic aromatic substitution under aqueous, room-temperature conditions. Although these materials possess relatively low surface areas (11–27 m2 g−1), they achieve CO2 uptake of 2.2 mmol g−1 at 1 bar and 98% efficiency in epichlorohydrin conversion. Their catalytic selectivity toward terminal over internal epoxides is influenced by the nucleophilicity of the halide counterion (I-CTF-1 > Br-CTF > Cl-CTF), while dense C–O bond formation produces a nonporous morphology. Their thermal stability up to 350 °C and four-cycle reusability further demonstrate the importance of framework composition and ionic functionality.93

The synthetic route for the preparation of charged CTFs.

For conventional ionothermal CTF synthesis, ZnCl2 can act simultaneously as solvent and catalyst for the direct cyclotrimerization of nitrile-containing monomers, typically around 400 °C. This approach enables the direct assembly of robust, insoluble frameworks such as CTF-Cl and CTF-PF, including permanently charged systems containing imidazolium tectons. Such ionic environments can strengthen interactions with guest molecules and gases. For example, CTF-PF-4 exhibits CO2 adsorption of 44.7 cm3 g−1 (approximately 2.0 mmol g−1) at 273 K and iodine-vapor uptake of up to 312 wt%, while its high concentration of ionic and nitrogen-containing sites contributes to CO2 interactions with (Qst > 33) kJ mol−1. Structural modification also has a direct effect on porosity: increasing ZnCl2 concentration promotes porous amorphous structures, whereas replacing chloride with bulkier PF6− ions increases the BET surface area to 889 m2 g−1. These frameworks remain thermally stable up to 900 °C and retain approximately 84–86% of their iodine uptake after five adsorption regeneration cycles.94

A related development is represented by the bipyridinium-derived ionic CTFs (ICTFs) (Scheme 26). Here, molten ZnCl2 provides both the reaction medium and Lewis's-acid catalytic environment for nitrile cyclotrimerization at 400 °C. The resulting frameworks possess interconnected micropores and remain stable in boiling water, concentrated HCl (4 M), and NaOH (0.5 M). ICTF-SCN exhibits CO2, SO2, and NO adsorption capacities of 2.48, 9.22, and 4.05 mmol g−1, respectively, while ICTF-Cl reaches a CO2/N2 selectivity of 119.1 at 273 K. Importantly, counter-anion exchange provides a direct structure–performance relationship that replacing Cl− with SCN− increases the BET surface area from 751 to 1010 m2 g−1 and increases CO2 uptake from 2.36 to 2.48 mmol g−1 at 273 K. The materials also retain their structure and gas-capture performance over five SO2 adsorption–desorption cycles and remain thermally stable up to 500 °C.95

Synthetic methodologies for functionalized CTFs involve the preparation of ionic bipyridinium-based frameworks (ICTF series) via ZnCl2-mediated cyclotrimerization at 400 °C.

The influence of molecular rigidity and synthesis conditions is further illustrated by the anthracene-derived CTFs (An-CTFs) (Scheme 27). These frameworks were obtained by ionothermal polymerization of 9,10-dicyanoanthracene through nitrile cyclotrimerization in molten ZnCl2 at 400 or 500 °C, using ZnCl2/monomer ratios of 1 : 10 and 1 : 20. The resulting nitrogen-containing microporous materials possess BET surface areas of 406–751 m2 g−1. Among the resulting frameworks, An-CTF-10-500 exhibits the highest CO2 adsorption capacity in this series, reaching 5.65 mmol g−1 at 273 K. The enhanced adsorption performance is attributed to the rigid anthracene-derived framework, nitrogen-rich microporosity, and controlled pore architecture. These results demonstrate that ionothermal synthesis can provide effective control over framework rigidity and porosity for gas-capture applications.96

Multi-step synthesis of anthracene-based frameworks (An-CTF).

Despite these advantages, conventional ionothermal CTF synthesis has important practical limitations. CTF-1 and related materials can provide stable, microporous structures suitable for carbon capture, but their preparation generally requires temperatures above 400 °C and involves substantial energy consumption and subsequent removal of residual metal salt. These requirements also create challenges for scalability. Consequently, condensation-based approaches have attracted increasing attention for nitrogen-containing frameworks such as TMTA-DHa and TMTA-TP, providing alternative routes to triazine-containing porous materials.27,33,97,98

Overall, ionothermal synthesis demonstrates a strong relationship between reaction conditions, framework architecture and resulting performance. Temperature can shift the balance between microporosity and interconnected micro–mesoporosity, while salt concentration, counter-anion identity and monomer structure can further regulate surface area, ionic environments and adsorption behavior. Thus, although the high-temperature and salt-removal requirements limit the broader scalability of the conventional approach, ionothermal synthesis remains an important strategy for constructing robust CTFs and related POPs with tunable porosity, ionic functionality, gas-capture capability and electrochemical performance.

Knitting methods

Knitting is a simple, cost-effective, and efficient bottom-up strategy for preparing hypercrosslinked polymers (HCPs), mainly through acid-catalyzed Friedel–Crafts alkylation of readily available aromatic monomers using cross-linking agents such as formaldehyde dimethyl acetal or chloromethyl ether. The approach produces rigid three-dimensional frameworks with hierarchical porosity and surface areas approaching 2000 m2 g−1, while its one-pot nature is favorable for large-scale preparation. The resulting networks can support efficient mass transfer and stabilization of active species, contributing to high selectivity and recyclability. Functionalized HCPs containing ionic or imidazolium groups can also be prepared using Lewis's acids such as FeCl3, AlCl3, SnCl4, or ZnBr2, enabling the incorporation of phosphorus, NHC, and porphyrin functionalities for applications such as CO2 conversion. Their performance can be retained for up to 15 cycles without significant metal leaching, highlighting the practical potential of this approach.99

Azo-bonding provides a greener alternative to conventional knitting and illustrates how the linkage chemistry can influence pollutant capture. man-Azo-P1, prepared through an aqueous two-step process from aromatic diamines such as 4,4′-diaminobiphenyl and 1,3,5-trihydroxybenzene, forms permanent azo-linked porosity with surface areas up to 290 m2 g−1. The material captures CO2 (32 cm3 g−1 at 273 K) as well as Pb, Cr, As, Ni, Cu, and Hg, with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 80 at 273 K. Its stability in water over different pH conditions and up to 300 °C, together with regeneration using 0.5 mol L−1 HCl over five adsorption/desorption cycles, demonstrates that the approach combines functionality with chemical stability.100 A related system, APP-BP-OH, represents the porphyrinic extension of azo-linking and reaches a surface area of 673 m2 g−1. Its CO2 affinity is controlled by the local chemical environment: hydroxyl groups favor gas binding, whereas bulky methyl groups can hinder adsorption. The azo-linked porphyrin-based porous organic polymer (APP) materials also show thermal stability up to 200 °C and regeneration within 5 min, demonstrating the importance of functional-group engineering rather than surface area alone.101

The conventional Friedel–Crafts knitting strategy can similarly be directed toward nitrogen-rich frameworks using 2,4,6-tris[4-(bromomethyl)phenyl]-1,3,5-triazine with carbazole or triphenylamine. The initial use of anhydrous FeCl3 resulted in iron nanoparticle impurities, whereas AlCl3 enabled the preparation of SB-TRZ-CRZ and SB-TRZ-TPA (Scheme 28). The dense triazine sites provide a strong basis for CO2 interaction, reflected in CO2 uptakes of 25.5 wt% for SB-TRZ-CRZ at 273 K and 1 bar and 44.5 wt% at 3 bar, compared with 16 wt% for SB-TRZ-TPA at 1 bar. Their CO2/N2 selectivities of 377 and 97, respectively, further demonstrate the influence of framework chemistry on separation performance. SB-TRZ-CRZ has a BET surface area of 642 m2 g−1, while the frameworks remain thermally stable to approximately 300 °C and 400 °C, respectively.102

Synthetic pathway for the preparation of the HCPs SB-TRZ-CRZ and SB-TRZ-TPA.

A related bottom-up strategy is illustrated by the Co(ii)@TA-TF COF, although this material is not produced by the knitting process itself. Its inclusion demonstrates how selection of molecular building blocks can introduce additional functionality into porous frameworks. The imine-linked layered framework combines Co(ii) porphyrin and pyrene motifs and exhibits 16.9 wt% CO2 uptake at 298 K while catalyzing CO2 conversion with epoxides, reaching 99% conversion for propylene oxide at 313 K and 0.1 MPa. The markedly lower conversion of styrene oxide (4%) highlights substrate-size-dependent behavior associated with the slipped TA-TF AA stacking. The framework remains stable up to 400 °C, retains crystallinity after 7 days in different aqueous and organic media, and maintains its activity and structure for at least five cycles without metal leaching.103

Overall, the examples demonstrate that knitting and related bottom-up approaches offer more than a route to high porosity such as linkage chemistry, functional-group environment, framework architecture, and active-site accessibility collectively determine performance. The contrast between man-Azo-P1, APP-BP-OH, and the triazine-rich SB-TRZ systems shows that chemical functionality can be as important as surface area for controlling adsorption and selectivity, while Co(ii)@TA-TF further illustrates how structural accessibility can govern catalytic selectivity. Thus, the key advantage of these approaches is the ability to combine relatively straightforward synthesis with tunable porous and chemical environments, stability, and regeneration.

Other emerging methods

Beyond the established synthesis routes, radical polymerization, multicomponent reactions, Diels–Alder chemistry, and post-functionalization strategies provide alternative routes for constructing POPs with tailored architectures and functionalities. Radical copolymerization of amino-functionalized monomers with divinylbenzene using initiators such as AIBN affords hierarchical porous materials such as POP-3 in yields exceeding 90%. Related nitrogen-containing frameworks, including POP-Phe, POP-Pyr, POP-Bce, DAT-1, DAT-2, and AO-POPs-FN-3, further demonstrate the versatility of radical copolymerization for incorporating heterocyclic functionality into porous networks.104–107 In contrast, multicomponent polymerization offers a one-pot route to functional ionic frameworks; for example, the Radziszewski reaction between primary amines, glyoxal, and formaldehyde in water produces iPOP-1, iPOP-2, and IB-iPOP, illustrating the potential of aqueous and simplified synthesis strategies.108,109 Similarly, the Diels–Alder reaction provides a catalyst-free alternative, with DA-POP-1 and DA-POP-2 prepared from terminal alkynes and furans in diphenyl ether at 220 °C to generate porous networks.110

A particularly important emerging direction is the development of ionic and quaternized POPs, where permanent or exchangeable charges provide additional control over ion exchange, mass transport, and selective capture. Frameworks such as HB-IPOP, HD-IPOP, OH-iPOP, PTPA-PIP, NPOP-TIPA, TPE-C1, TPE-C4, CTN-POP-1, CTN-POP-2, and POP-SO3Na have been prepared through quaternization, nucleophilic substitution, or Zincke reactions involving imidazole, pyridine, bipyridinium, and viologen building blocks. Their charged structures broaden the functionality of POPs toward radioisotope separation, toxic oxo-anion uptake, and ion capture.111–118 These examples indicate that introducing ionic sites can provide a functional advantage beyond porosity alone by enabling selective interactions with charged species.

Further structural functionality can be introduced through post-functional groups and molecular receptors. Phosphate-containing frameworks such as AZO-POP-P and phosphate-based organic polymer network (OPN) are synthesized through nucleophilic substitution of aromatic alcohols with POCl3 under ice-cold conditions, providing another route for incorporating chemically active groups into porous networks.119 Likewise, the incorporation of crown ether units into Py-B18C6-COF introduces macrocyclic recognition sites within the framework, with the design intended to minimize pore blocking.120 Collectively, these emerging approaches demonstrate a shift from simply generating porous networks toward integrating specific chemical functionalities during framework construction, thereby improving the ability of POPs to participate in selective ion capture, separation, and molecular recognition.

Green and sustainable synthetic strategies

The increasing environmental burden associated with organic solvents, prolonged heating, hazardous reagents, and energy-intensive purification has stimulated the development of greener routes for POP synthesis. Among these approaches, mechanochemistry has emerged as a particularly promising strategy because mechanical energy generated by grinding or ball milling can drive polymerization under solvent-free or solvent-minimized and often low-temperature conditions. Mechanochemical processing has been extended to diverse POP families and bond-forming reactions, including Schiff-base condensation, β-ketoenamine and amide formation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation, dibenzodioxane formation, and Menshutkin coupling. Representative materials include TpBpy COF, COF-TpMA, Thdz@COF-F, TMCPD(MC), TMCBD(MC), COF-TP, COF-TE, MHP-P5Q, CTFs, PAFs, CPPs, PIPs, PIM-1, PIM-4, TpPa-1, TpPa-2, TpBD, LZU-1, DhaTph, HCPs, MTPs, OCP-NPN1–3, TPP1, CzPP, and NDPC-71-F-700, demonstrating that mechanical activation is applicable to both crystalline and amorphous porous architectures. These materials have been investigated for solid-state electrolytes, hydroxyl-radical sensing, Pb(ii) and CH3I capture, gas separation, CO2 capture, catalysis, lubrication, and related functions. The broader significance of mechanochemistry therefore lies not simply in replacing solvent-based synthesis, but in its potential to shorten reaction times, overcome monomer-solubility limitations, reduce energy requirements, and facilitate process intensification. Nevertheless, the final framework properties remain sensitive to milling frequency, milling time, ball-to-powder ratio, catalyst loading, and liquid-assisted grinding conditions, indicating that green processing does not eliminate the need for careful control of synthesis–structure relationships.121–124

Mechanochemistry is complemented by other emerging low-impact strategies, including microwave-assisted, sonochemical, photochemical, ionothermal, room-temperature, solvent-free, liquid-assisted grinding (LAG), ion- and liquid-assisted grinding (ILAG), twin-screw extrusion (TSE), and resonant acoustic mixing (RAM). These approaches provide different routes for reducing reaction time and energy demand while improving reaction homogeneity or enabling process intensification. Microwave-assisted synthesis, for example, has enabled the preparation of functional imine-linked COFs in short reaction periods, including iodine-capturing COFs with rapid adsorption and reusable performance, while microwave-assisted preparation of CD-COF-Li has demonstrated the potential of rapid synthesis for ion-transport applications. Similarly, LAG has facilitated the preparation of LZU-1, DhaTph, TpPa-1, TpPa-2, and TpBD, whereas scalable mechanical-processing concepts such as TSE and RAM provide opportunities to move beyond conventional batch milling. Other green routes, including solvent-free and room-temperature polymerization, microwave, sonochemical, ionothermal, and photochemical processes, have been applied across COFs, CTFs, PAFs, CMPs, and HCPs, with reported applications spanning CO2 capture, VOC adsorption, dye and metal-ion removal, radioactive iodine sequestration, catalysis, sensing, and energy-related processes. Importantly, these methods should not be considered intrinsically sustainable merely because they reduce solvent or reaction time; catalyst toxicity, precursor origin, energy input, purification requirements, waste generation, and scalability must also be considered when assessing their overall environmental benefit.121–123

The transition from laboratory-scale green synthesis to practically sustainable POP production is best illustrated by materials that combine reduced synthetic burden with demonstrated performance under realistic conditions. Solvent-free mechanochemical preparation of MSHMP-1 through Friedel–Crafts crosslinking produced a triptycene-based microporous polymer with a BET surface area of 1780 m2 g−1 and strong VOC adsorption under humid conditions; notably, its benzene uptake decreased by less than 10% at 90% relative humidity and the material retained performance over at least five adsorption–desorption cycles.125 More directly relevant to cost and resource utilization, PP-BM-1, PP-BM-2, and PP-BM-3 were obtained from inexpensive petroleum pitch through rapid ball-milling-assisted Friedel–Crafts crosslinking, producing BET surface areas of 580–867 m2 g−1. The optimized PP-BM-1 reached an NH3 uptake of 14.97 mmol g−1 at 273 K, exceeded the reported capacities of PIM-1 and sPANs and those of the compared commercial zeolites and activated carbons, and maintained its performance over five adsorption–desorption cycles.126 Such examples demonstrate that the principal value of green synthesis is not merely a reduction in solvent consumption but the possibility of simultaneously lowering production time, utilizing inexpensive feedstocks, improving process efficiency, and retaining functional performance. However, significant gaps remain in reproducibility, continuous and large-scale processing, control of crystallinity and pore architecture, catalyst/additive recovery, life-cycle assessment, and techno-economic validation. Consequently, future green POP synthesis should be evaluated through an integrated synthesis–performance–regeneration–cost–environmental framework rather than by adsorption capacity or solvent reduction alone.122,123

Post-synthetic methods

PSM provides an effective route for tailoring the chemical environment of preformed POPs without reconstructing the entire porous framework. Common modifications include sulfonation, amination, protonation, chloromethylation, and quaternization, as demonstrated for TP-POP-SO3NH4, PTO-1S, PTT-1S, PTPA-PIP, TP-iPOP-PPh3, PAF-5-CN-EDA, Azo-POP, and amine-modified POPs for CO2 capture.98,111,127–131 These transformations mainly improve hydrophilicity, ion-exchange ability, adsorption selectivity, or provide new binding sites. For example, protonation of PTPA-PIP improves water dispersibility and pollutant uptake, whereas chloromethylation followed by triphenylphosphine grafting produces TP-iPOP-PPh3 for gold nanoparticle capture.111,128 Similarly, sulfonation enhances the hydrophilicity and ion-exchange characteristics of the parent frameworks.130 Thus, PSM offers a practical means of tuning surface chemistry while retaining the pre-existing porous architecture.

Recent developments have extended this concept toward more practical material formats and scalable processing. Electrochemical polymerization enables the direct formation of porous TPETCz dendrimer films on electrodes through redox processes, while monolithic adsorbents such as TD-POP@CS/PEI-2 integrate a phthalocyanine-containing TD-POP into chitosan/polyethyleneimine aerogels through epichlorohydrin cross-linking, addressing some limitations associated with powdered adsorbents.132,133 At the processing level, large-scale intensification has enabled the preparation of NpTOC with yields up to 99.8% and quantities of 5 g.134 These developments indicate that the importance of post-synthetic strategies extends beyond functional-group introduction toward improving handling, processability, and practical deployment of POP-based adsorbents.

A related emerging strategy involves thermoresponsive polymer functionalization, exemplified by PDMAPS-modified polyester fabrics prepared by single-sided spray coating and in situ polymerization. Below the upper critical solution temperature (UCST; ≈28–30 °C), the polymer adopts a collapsed hydrophobic state that contracts the fabric pores and enhances thermal insulation, whereas above the transition temperature it becomes hydrophilic and promotes unidirectional moisture transport. The resulting material achieved a water-vapor transmission rate of 8925.69 g m−2 d−1 at 40 °C while maintaining a skin-facing temperature up to 4.5 °C higher than the unmodified fabric.135 Although demonstrated on polyester rather than a POP framework, this approach illustrates the broader potential of post-fabrication functionalization to introduce dynamic, environment-responsive properties into porous polymeric materials. Overall, PSM and related integration strategies provide a complementary pathway to conventional bottom-up synthesis by allowing surface chemistry, functionality, morphology, and material format to be adjusted after framework formation, thereby facilitating the development of task-specific POP-based materials.15,33,39,41

Key design parameters of POPs for environmental applications

The performance of POPs in remediating the environment depends upon several of the most critical design aspects of POPs, such as porosity and surface area, which ultimately dictate the active site density available for gas capture or pollutant adsorption.18,25,33 Therefore, the multifunctional monomers need to be designed with features such as shape persistent, three-dimensional geometries (e.g., tetraphenylmethane (TPM), adamantane, or triptycene), so that they provide the structural support necessary to ensure that the framework remains intact during solvent removal from the pore structure.19,27,33 Shape-persistent three-dimensional building blocks, such as tetraphenylmethane, adamantane, and triptycene, help preserve free volume and structural integrity during solvent removal. The very high surface area reported for tetrakis(phenyl)-linked PPN-4 (6461 m2 g−1) illustrates the influence of monomer geometry on framework porosity. However, adsorption performance cannot be determined by surface area alone; the selection of monomers, polymerization strategy, pore architecture, and post-synthetic modification collectively governs pore accessibility, surface functionality, and pollutant–framework interactions.25,33 Additionally, the kinetic diameters of the target molecules (e.g., CO2) or contaminants in water will dictate the pore size distributions in order for the molecular sieving and transport process to work efficiently.25,33

The thermal and chemical stability of the framework is another vital parameter, determined by the nature of the strong covalent linkages.18,33,34 The means by which the POP material is manufactured (i.e. selection of method used for polymerization such as Friedel–Crafts arylation, Sonogashira coupling or nitrile trimerization) and what solvent is used during reactions will have a significant impact on the properties of the resulting POP material including textural characteristics and gas uptake properties.19,25,33 For POP's that have more complex hierarchical configurations (such as MPA-TPA POP or CDPI derived from macrocyclic materials), which interlink micropores and mesopores, often done in order to facilitate faster movement of mass or ions during catalysis or separation processes.14,21,33

Through functional group engineering with respect to monomer selection and PSMs, polar heteroatoms (N, O, S) and specific functional groups such as –COOH, –SO3H, or –NH2 (e.g., PPN-6-SO3H or PAF-1-CH2NH2) may be introduced into ionic PPCs to create dipole–quadrupole interactions or hydrogen bond interactions, enhancing their affinity toward certain pollutants based on molecular structure.19,25,26,33 In regards to remediation of water, ionic POPs (iPOPs) are specifically designed for the selective removal of toxic oxo-anionic species (e.g. chromate; pertechnetate) by means of ion exchange.13,22,31 The incorporation of macrocyclic receptors (e.g. crown ethers; calixarenes; cyclodextrins) will also enhance POP performance due to unique properties of molecular recognition interactions and host–guest chemistries, thereby enhancing affinity toward diverse pollutants, including heavy metals, organic contaminants, radionuclides, and greenhouse gases.21,33,37 The combined optimization of these structural and chemical attributes ultimately determines the practical applicability of POPs in environmental remediation. The individual contribution of each design parameter is discussed in the following subsections.

Porosity and high surface area

Porosity is a fundamental determinant of POP adsorption because it governs accessible void volume, diffusion pathways and host–guest interactions. Interconnected micropores below 2 nm, particularly those approaching the kinetic diameter of CO2, favour molecular sieving and stronger gas–framework interactions, whereas meso- and macropores facilitate mass transport and accessibility to internal active sites. Thus, effective adsorption depends not simply on total pore volume but on the combination of pore size, connectivity and accessible surface area.19,27,32–34,98,110,136–139 The BET surface area remains an important descriptor because increasing accessible surface generally increases the number of adsorption sites; however, rigid multifunctional monomers such as TPM, triptycene, adamantane and tetraphenyladamantane, combined with Friedel–Crafts arylation, Sonogashira–Hagihara coupling, nitrile trimerization, Schiff-base polycondensation, Diels–Alder polymerization and microwave-assisted polymerization, demonstrate that permanent porosity also depends strongly on framework rigidity and crosslinking.14,15,33,39,40,55,56,110,140 Strong covalent networks further provide the chemical and thermal stability required for repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.39,136,137

PAFs, PPNs, CMPs and PIMs provide representative high-porosity platforms. PAF-1 and PPN-4 illustrate the contribution of rigid three-dimensional architectures and high surface areas, while 3D-Py-COF and 3D-Py-COF-TANM combine highly accessible three-dimensional voids with large void fractions (∼0.93–0.94), favouring rapid molecular transport and iodine uptake.21,25,134,141 However, these examples also indicate that the high surface area should not be treated as the sole determinant of adsorption performance. CMP-1–4 generate permanent microporosity through acetylene-linked aromatic networks, whereas the contorted structure of PIM-1 prevents efficient chain packing and preserves free volume for gas separation and storage.19,26,38 Hypercrosslinked PAF-DBrM further demonstrates the importance of matching pore dimensions to molecular size, with appropriately sized ultramicropores enhancing SF6 adsorption and separation.15,142

Nitrogen-rich and Schiff-base POPs further establish the relationship between framework geometry, crosslink density and accessible porosity. DAT-1 combines bimodal micropores with interparticle mesopores, providing both adsorption sites and mass-transport pathways, while NRPOP-1 and NRPOP-2 demonstrate the contribution of rigid crosslinked structures to permanent porosity.60,104 ITN-POP shows that effective iodine capture can be achieved through permanent microporosity even without a greater BET surface area (502.1 m2 g−1).143 Conversely, triphenylamine-derived TTPPA, TTDAB and Tm-MTDAB demonstrate that excessive crosslinking can reduce accessible porosity through pore blocking and restricted N2 diffusion.136 Similarly, CBPOP, CNPOP and CAPOP exhibit low surface areas because of strong π–π interactions and chain aggregation.144 CTT-TAPB and CTT-TPC retain substantially greater microporosity for antibiotic enrichment, whereas TpPa-1 illustrates how poor crystallinity can restrict pore accessibility.67,145 These comparisons show that optimal rather than maximum crosslink density is critical for maintaining accessible porosity.

Azo- and porphyrin-based frameworks reinforce the importance of combining rigid architecture with appropriate chemical functionality. TP-PPOPs employ triptycene and porphyrin units to generate stable three-dimensional porous networks, while azo-linked PBPOP3 provides a highly porous, nitrogen-rich environment favourable for acidic guest capture and repeated cycling.70,146 APP-Ph-Me further demonstrates that local chemical environment can influence CO2 uptake beyond the contribution of surface area alone.101 Co(ii)@TA-TF COF uses controlled interlayer spacing and slipped stacking to prevent aggregation while exposing catalytic metal sites.103 Bioinspired porphyrin-rich PPOP provides an important counterexample in which effective heavy-metal removal is achieved despite only moderate surface area through the combined effects of porosity, electrostatic attraction and coordination interactions.64

Rigid triptycene-, adamantane- and aromatic-based building blocks are particularly effective in preserving permanent free volume and preventing framework collapse. The NOP series, including NOP-55, NOP-53 and NOP-54, demonstrates how micropores can be integrated with meso- and macropores to balance molecular confinement with mass transport, particularly for larger ions.76,98,146 TP-POP and TP-iPOP-PPh3 illustrate the trade-off between accessible surface area and functional-group incorporation, since post-functionalisation can partially block existing pores while introducing stronger binding sites.127,128 The triptycene-based Tp(PPh3) framework similarly benefits from a rigid twisted geometry that prevents efficient chain packing and preserves internal free volume, while NS-TMP and ultramicroporous Keto-POP demonstrate the value of rigid architectures and subnanometre pores for small-molecule confinement.147–149 PAF-5 further shows that robust porous skeletons can tolerate extensive PSM, allowing additional functional groups to be introduced without complete structural collapse.129

Hierarchical pore structures provide a means of reconciling strong host–guest interactions with rapid diffusion. Ph-POP and Tt-POP combine narrow micropores with larger mesopores, allowing micropores to provide confinement and molecular sieving while mesopores facilitate CO2 transport.138 WFMC polymers provide broader mesoporous channels suitable for larger adsorbates, whereas PEMA demonstrates the usefulness of appropriately developed mesopores for heavy-metal and iodine recognition.56,76 Ac_POP-5 combines micropores and mesopores to enhance iodine uptake beyond what its BET surface area alone would suggest.55 Tf-DHzOBn-BCMBP10 similarly integrates micropores with larger transport channels, reducing mass-transfer resistance and improving micropollutant recognition.139,150,151 These examples collectively demonstrate that pore hierarchy can be more important than total surface area when diffusion and accessibility limit adsorption.

The influence of linker architecture is further evident in melamine-, aminal-, imine- and polyaminal-based frameworks. MPOP-6C and MPOP-6N possess greater accessible porosity than their denser heterocyclic analogues MPOP-5N and MPOP-5O, highlighting the effect of framework packing on pore accessibility.54 MPA-TPA POP and microwave-assisted MPA-TPA/BDA POPs provide porous platforms for pesticide removal, although adsorption of pesticide can progressively occupy available pores.14,74,152 TTPEPOP-O and TTPEPOP-S demonstrate the contribution of tetraphenylethylene-based aminal networks, while PrPOA-BP and PrPSN-BP employ hierarchical channels to reduce diffusion barriers.39,59 PPOP-UOZ-1 further shows that permanent porosity can remain useful for heavy-metal extraction even when the overall surface area is relatively modest.57

Supramolecular and macrocyclic architectures provide another route to selective adsorption by combining network porosity with molecular recognition. POP-TCE-15 and CalP4 integrate crown-ether and calixarene units with porous networks, while Py-B18C6-COF uses crown-ether functionality to preserve accessible binding environments.21,120 In contrast, Cr-TPA-4BZ-Py-POP illustrates how flexible crown-ether-containing structures can undergo densification and suffer severe loss of accessible porosity.88 Mac-DMP and Mac-TMP provide endo- and exo-receptor environments for selective gold recovery despite modest BET areas, whereas NpTOC demonstrates that preorganized macrocyclic cavities can generate greater ion-capture behaviour that cannot be predicted from surface area alone.134,153

PSM and thermal transformation can substantially reorganize pore structures. OVS-DHBZ POIP undergoes an increase in accessible porosity during thermal ring-opening polymerization, whereas Pyr-BZ-Pyr POIP experiences surface-area loss after curing because of framework densification.89,91 More pronounced pore development occurs when BBT-BZTB-Py POP is converted into poly(BBT-BZTB-Py) POP-600, producing hierarchical micro/mesoporosity suitable for energy-storage applications.90 Carbonization and KOH activation of poly(Cr-TPA-4BZ-Py-POP)-800 likewise generate nitrogen-doped microporous carbon with improved ion transport and electrochemical behaviour.88 These examples demonstrate that a lower BET surface area after modification is not necessarily detrimental when functional-site accessibility, pore connectivity or transport properties are simultaneously improved.

Biomass-derived and magnetic POPs further illustrate the need to balance porosity with practical functionality. QPOP combines high surface area and pore volume with effective dye adsorption, while GA-azo-DADPM, PAPOP-A and PAPOP-B use mesoporosity to facilitate diffusion of relatively large pollutants.66,71,154 TA-POP derived from tannin combines moderate porosity with phenolic hydroxyl groups favourable for dye adsorption.68 Ph-MPOP sacrifices surface area because of its magnetic component but gains convenient magnetic separation, while M-Qct-B and M-Qct-T retain useful porous structures for phenolic-contaminant removal.61,155 Thus, practical adsorbent design may require accepting reduced surface area when an additional property, such as magnetic recovery, substantially improves usability.

Importantly, functionalisation can improve adsorption despite reducing BET surface area. Sulfonation of TP-PPOPs and PTO-1S substantially decreases accessible porosity, but introduces acidic and hydrophilic sites that strengthen electrostatic and other interactions with target pollutants.130,146 Azo-functionalisation of Azo-POP similarly reduces surface area while introducing chemically active sites, whereas amidoximation of POP-FN-0.75 partially blocks internal channels but provides nitrogen-rich sites for uranyl-ion capture.105,131 Ionic-liquid modification in A6 frameworks likewise reduces physical porosity because the ionic liquid occupies pre-existing pores, while simultaneously introducing additional chemically selective interaction sites.156 These examples establish that chemical affinity can compensate for, and sometimes outweigh, loss of physical surface area.

This principle is particularly pronounced in ionic POPs, where counterions, flexible linkers and strong intermolecular interactions can obstruct pore channels. iPOP-6 and iPOP-4 therefore possess relatively low accessible surface areas, while Pyd-PPOPs-Br, HB-IPOP and HD-IPOP demonstrate how flexible frameworks and pore occupation can further suppress measurable porosity.69,111–113,157 FCOP-222 exhibits restricted porosity because of dense hydrogen-bonded networks, while IB-iPOP@I− combines low surface area with limited pore volume.108,158 CTN-POP-1 and CTN-POP-2 illustrate the contribution of framework rigidity and charge density to accessible adsorption environments.114 In IPM-403, Br− counterions obstruct the channels sufficiently that CO2 adsorption must be evaluated under conditions different from conventional N2 measurements.159 Nevertheless, TPE-C1, TPE-C4, POP-1, POP-SO3Na, gn-iPOP-Cl, NPOP-TIPA, OH-iPOP, H-iPOP and viologen-based TET frameworks demonstrate that low BET surface areas do not necessarily preclude rapid kinetics or high pollutant uptake when strong electrostatic interactions, ion-exchange sites, functional groups and mesoporous transport pathways are present.75,115–118,160,161

Overall, the examples demonstrate that BET surface area is an important but incomplete descriptor of POP adsorption. Micropores provide confinement and molecular sieving, meso- and macropores facilitate diffusion, and rigid frameworks preserve accessible free volume; however, excessive crosslinking, aggregation, interpenetration, functionalisation or counterion occupation can restrict these advantages. Conversely, polar, ionic, macrocyclic and other functional groups can generate strong and selective host–guest interactions even when they reduce the measured surface area. Therefore, rational POP design should balance surface area, pore-size hierarchy, framework rigidity, pore accessibility, functional-site density and chemical affinity, rather than maximizing surface area alone.

Chemical and thermal stability

Chemical and thermal stability are among the most important design considerations governing the long-term performance of POPs in environmental remediation. During practical applications, adsorbents are frequently exposed to chemically aggressive media, including acidic and alkaline wastewaters, organic solvents, humid environments, and repeated adsorption–desorption cycles. Therefore, maintaining structural integrity under such conditions is essential for preserving pore accessibility, adsorption capacity, and regeneration efficiency. In POPs, these characteristics primarily originate from the presence of strong covalent bonds (e.g., C–C, C–N, CN, and triazine linkages), together with highly crosslinked architectures constructed from rigid aromatic building blocks. These structural features minimize framework collapse and provide excellent resistance against chemical degradation, thermal stress, and mechanical deformation during prolonged operation.18,19,25–27,33,34,162–166

Different classes of POPs employ distinct structural strategies to achieve high framework stability while preserving permanent porosity. PAFs generally utilize rigid tetrahedral building units that generate three-dimensional covalently connected networks with greater structural robustness. In contrast, CMPs are constructed through rigid monomer-to-monomer coupling reactions, such as Sonogashira–Hagihara cross-coupling, producing extended π-conjugated networks that simultaneously provide mechanical stability and efficient charge delocalization. Likewise, triazine-containing PAF derivatives and zeolite-inspired porous frameworks combine rigid covalent architectures with permanent porosity, enabling selective molecular adsorption while maintaining excellent structural stability under demanding operating conditions.162–166

The practical durability of these frameworks has been demonstrated in numerous studies. Representative PAF materials remain insoluble in most common organic solvents and retain their structural integrity under strongly acidic, alkaline, and humid environments because their highly crosslinked covalent networks effectively resist chemical attack. Hydrothermal stability has also been demonstrated for PAF-1, which preserves its framework even after prolonged exposure to boiling water, illustrating the remarkable resistance of covalently bonded porous networks toward hydrolytic degradation. Similarly, PAF-2, PAF-3, and PAF-4 exhibit excellent resistance to solvent exposure and elevated temperatures, while zeolite-inspired JUC-Z frameworks maintain high structural stability under most operating conditions, although partial degradation may occur in highly concentrated acidic media owing to the susceptibility of Si–O–Si linkages.162–166

Thermal stability is equally important because industrial adsorption and regeneration frequently require elevated operating temperatures. Thermogravimetric studies consistently demonstrate that many POPs retain their framework over a broad temperature range before significant decomposition occurs, reflecting the inherent stability imparted by extensive covalent crosslinking. Among the various POP families, CMPs provide an additional advantage because their continuous π-conjugated backbones contribute not only to adsorption performance but also to excellent mechanical integrity and film-forming capability. For example, CMPN-3 exhibits outstanding chemical resistance, remains insoluble in common organic solvents, and possesses high thermal stability, making it well suited for repeated adsorption and separation processes under harsh operating environments.162–167

The importance of framework stability becomes even more apparent when POPs are compared with crystalline porous materials such as COFs. Although COFs possess highly ordered pore channels that are advantageous for molecular transport, their long-range crystallinity may deteriorate before the framework undergoes complete chemical decomposition. Recent studies have shown that several two-dimensional COFs experience structural amorphization at temperatures substantially lower than their decomposition temperatures, indicating that crystallinity and chemical stability do not necessarily coincide. Factors such as linkage chemistry, lattice dimensions, pore functionalization, and interlayer interactions strongly influence the thermal stability of COFs, while strategies including interlayer hydrogen bonding have been developed to reinforce framework rigidity and improve resistance toward thermal deformation.167,168

Overall, the greater durability of POPs originates from the synergistic combination of rigid molecular building blocks, permanent covalent crosslinking, and highly stable porous architectures. These characteristics enable POPs to preserve their pore structure, adsorption sites, and mechanical integrity during repeated regeneration cycles and prolonged exposure to chemically and thermally demanding environments. Consequently, chemical and thermal stability constitute fundamental design parameters that underpin the successful application of POPs in pollutant adsorption, gas capture, and other environmentally relevant separation processes.18,19,25–27,33,34,162–168

Tunable functional groups and surface charge

Among the distinguishing characteristics of POPs, the ability to precisely tailor their chemical functionality and surface charge is one of the most significant factors governing adsorption selectivity and pollutant-specific interactions. Unlike conventional porous adsorbents, the chemical environment within POPs can be engineered either through rational monomer selection during synthesis or via PSM, enabling the incorporation of a wide range of functional groups without compromising the structural integrity of the porous framework. Such molecular-level control allows the adsorption properties of POPs to be optimized for specific environmental applications by regulating host–guest interactions, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, coordination bonding, π–π interactions, dipole–quadrupole interactions, and charge-transfer processes with target contaminants including heavy metals, dyes, pharmaceuticals, and gaseous pollutants. The final functionality of POPs is further influenced by synthetic variables such as monomer composition, monomer ratio, solvent system, catalyst selection, and reaction temperature, all of which determine the distribution of functional groups, pore accessibility, and surface charge characteristics of the resulting framework.169,170

The incorporation of polar functional groups provides an effective strategy for enhancing adsorption affinity toward gases with significant quadrupole moments, particularly CO2. A representative example is the carbonyl-functionalized HCP synthesized through solution polymerization of divinylbenzene (DVB) and 4-vinylbenzyl chloride (VBC), followed by FeCl3-catalyzed Friedel–Crafts post-crosslinking to produce the methylene-bridged polymer (PDV-pc-1). Subsequent introduction of carbon tetrachloride during the one-pot post-crosslinking process generated the carbonyl-bridged framework (PDV-pc-2), in which rigid carbonyl bridges were incorporated into the polymer backbone (Scheme 29). Although PDV-pc-2 possessed a relatively lower BET surface area than its methylene-linked analogue, it exhibited substantially higher CO2 uptake (86 mg g−1), demonstrating that adsorption performance depends not only on surface area but also on the chemical nature of accessible adsorption sites. The enhanced performance was attributed to the combined influence of abundant micropores and polar carbonyl groups, which strengthen dipole–quadrupole interactions and hydrogen bonding with CO2 molecules.171

Similarly, the incorporation of electron-rich functional groups such as alkynes, amines, triazines, azo linkages, imine bonds, and porphyrinic units markedly improves the affinity of POPs toward heavy-metal ions and organic contaminants. Nitrogen-containing functionalities serve as efficient coordination sites for soft metal ions such as Hg(ii), whereas alkyne groups provide electron-rich environments that facilitate metal binding. For instance, palladium nanoparticles immobilized within conjugated porous carbon frameworks (Pd@CNPC) combine a highly conjugated porous network with uniformly dispersed active sites, allowing efficient Hg(ii) adsorption while preventing nanoparticle aggregation and metal leaching. The chemically robust framework, together with its high surface area and tunable pore architecture, provides abundant accessible binding sites and rapid adsorption kinetics for wastewater remediation.172

Precise regulation of surface functionality can also be achieved through controlled polymerization strategies. Functional nanonetwork structured polymers (FNSPs), synthesized via atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP), provide high flexibility in controlling polymer architecture by adjusting molecular weight, side-chain composition, and crosslink density. Side chains such as poly(tert-butyl acrylate) can subsequently undergo in situ hydrolysis to generate poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), introducing abundant negatively charged carboxyl groups throughout the porous network. These highly accessible functional sites strongly attract positively charged contaminants through coulombic and electrostatic interactions, thereby significantly improving both adsorption capacity and adsorption kinetics compared with unmodified polymeric networks.173

The remarkable versatility of HCPs further illustrates the importance of functional group engineering in POP design. Typically synthesized through Friedel–Crafts reactions, HCPs combine hierarchical pore structures with excellent chemical stability and broad synthetic flexibility. Their adsorption behaviour can be systematically tailored by introducing hydroxyl, amino, nitro, carboxyl, or ionic functionalities through either bottom-up synthesis or PSM. Such functionalization enables the optimization of pore polarity, surface charge, and adsorption affinity toward target molecules including NH3, CO2, dyes, and ionic pollutants. In addition, surface charge can be deliberately manipulated by incorporating ionic moieties or exchanging counterions, thereby enabling applications in molecular separation, ion sieving, and water purification. Negatively charged PAA-modified nanonetwork polymers exhibit enhanced dye adsorption through electrostatic attraction, whereas positively charged quaternary pyridinium functionalities facilitate selective ion transport and size-dependent molecular separation.174

Surface charge engineering has proven particularly effective for achieving highly selective adsorption of ionic pollutants. NH2-functionalized HCPs prepared through one-pot Friedel–Crafts alkylation using m-phenylenediamine (MPD) provide an excellent example of this strategy. Direct incorporation of amino groups during polymerization generates a high nitrogen content (12.65%) and produces a positively charged framework, as confirmed by zeta potential measurements. Consequently, the material exhibits outstanding selectivity toward anionic dyes through strong electrostatic attraction while showing negligible affinity toward cationic dyes such as RhB and MB. Accordingly, adsorption of MB remains limited (7.0 mg g−1), whereas the adsorption capacity for reactive brilliant red (X-3B) reaches 3290 mg g−1. These results clearly demonstrate that rational surface charge engineering can dominate adsorption selectivity even when the porous structure remains largely unchanged.175

Although electrostatic interactions frequently govern pollutant adsorption in functionalized POPs, they are not always the predominant adsorption mechanism. This is illustrated by amino-functionalized TPP-NH2 polymers synthesized through a Friedel–Crafts knitting strategy, where multiple amino functionalities are incorporated into a rigid three-dimensional porous framework. Despite the presence of amino groups, adsorption performance shows minimal dependence on solution pH, indicating that electrostatic attraction contributes only marginally to dye removal. Instead, the high adsorption capacities for MB (560.6 mg g−1) and methyl orange (803.2 mg g−1) arise primarily from extensive π–π stacking interactions between the aromatic polymer backbone and dye molecules, assisted by the large accessible surface area and permanent porosity of the framework. Furthermore, the material exhibits rapid adsorption kinetics, removing more than 95% of both cationic and anionic dyes within 60 min, highlighting that the synergistic interplay between functional group chemistry and pore architecture ultimately determines adsorption performance rather than any single structural parameter.176

Selectivity toward specific contaminants

Selective adsorption is one of the defining advantages of POPs, particularly for the efficient removal or separation of target contaminants from complex environmental matrices. Unlike conventional adsorbents that primarily rely on nonspecific physical adsorption, POPs can be rationally engineered to preferentially recognize specific molecules or ions through precise control over pore architecture, functional group distribution, and surface charge. Such molecular-level tailoring enables selective interactions based on size exclusion, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, coordination chemistry, and host–guest recognition, thereby improving adsorption efficiency while minimizing interference from competing species.25,177

Surface charge engineering provides one of the most effective approaches for achieving selective adsorption in aqueous systems. A representative example is the magnetic porous polymer FC-POP-EDA@Fe3O4, synthesized through the Friedel–Crafts reaction, which possesses abundant amino functionalities together with aromatic and hydroxyl groups distributed throughout a mesoporous framework (BET surface area: 402.11 m2 g−1). Zeta-potential measurements demonstrate that the material maintains a negative surface charge over a broad pH range (2–9), resulting in strong electrostatic attraction toward cationic dyes while simultaneously repelling anionic dyes. In addition to electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking between aromatic frameworks and dye molecules, together with hydrogen bonding, further enhances adsorption efficiency. Consequently, the material exhibits excellent adsorption capacities for basic red (379.75 mg g−1) and basic blue (240.9 mg g−1), while showing comparatively lower uptake of anionic dyes such as Direct Scarlet 4BS and Acid Naphthol 6B. Importantly, the adsorbent retains nearly 90% of its adsorption efficiency after four adsorption–desorption cycles, highlighting its potential for practical treatment of dye-containing textile wastewater.177

For gas separation applications, adsorption selectivity depends on both molecular dimensions and framework chemistry. Efficient carbon capture requires preferential adsorption of CO2 over competing gases such as N2, H2, and CH4, particularly from flue gases containing approximately 15% CO2. The selectivity of POPs is commonly evaluated using Henry's law constants, Ideal Adsorbed Solution Theory (IAST), or breakthrough experiments. Separation generally occurs through two complementary mechanisms: (i) kinetic selectivity arising from differences in molecular size, where the kinetic diameter of CO2 (3.30 Å) is smaller than that of N2 (3.64 Å), and (ii) thermodynamic selectivity resulting from the higher quadrupole moment and polarizability of CO2. Incorporation of nitrogen- and oxygen-containing functional groups, including amines, triazines, imidazoles, hydroxyls, and carboxyl groups, strengthens specific interactions with CO2, while appropriately designed microporous networks further improve adsorption selectivity. Although physisorption alone provides limited selectivity at elevated temperatures, stronger chemisorptive interactions, such as carbamate formation, can significantly enhance CO2 capture performance.25

Selective molecular recognition is equally important in biomolecular separation. Zinc porphyrin-functionalized porous polymers (PPs-Zn) illustrate how pore dimensions can be matched with molecular size to achieve size-selective protein adsorption. PPs-Zn-1, possessing a pore size of approximately 18 nm (BET surface area: 18 m2 g−1), preferentially adsorbs relatively small proteins such as lysozyme, lipase, and bovine serum albumin (BSA), whereas the larger-pore analogue PPs-Zn-2 (approximately 90 nm) exhibits superior affinity toward larger biomolecules including myosin and human fibrinogen. Beyond pore-size matching, the porphyrin framework contributes additional selectivity through nitrogen-containing heterocyclic units that promote π–π stacking and aromatic interactions with protein molecules, demonstrating the synergistic influence of pore architecture and molecular recognition in selective adsorption.178

Surface functionalization can also impart a greater selectivity toward ionic pollutants. Cationic HCPs such as C-NSANaph-HCP@Br selectively remove anionic contaminants through ion-exchange mechanisms rather than simple size exclusion. Owing to its permanently cationic framework, this material exhibits strong affinity toward anionic dyes such as methyl orange (MO), while showing negligible adsorption of similarly sized cationic dyes such as RhB. The presence of multiple naphthyl groups further enhances adsorption through π–π interactions with aromatic pollutants. Moreover, the material demonstrates remarkable selectivity toward dichromate ions (Cr2O72−), maintaining nearly complete removal even in the presence of a fivefold molar excess of competing monovalent anions including Cl−, Br−, and NO3−. This behaviour is attributed to the higher charge density and stronger electrostatic interactions of dichromate ions with the positively charged framework, combined with the material's relatively high BET surface area (788 m2 g−1).179

Another representative example highlighting the relationship between framework chemistry and gas selectivity is provided by triptycene-based 1,2,3-triazole-linked network polymers (TNPs), synthesized through Cu(i)-catalysed click chemistry. These rigid microporous polymers combine triptycene building blocks with CO2-philic triazole linkages, producing frameworks with BET surface areas as high as 1348 m2 g−1. By introducing additional nitrogen-rich heteroaromatic units such as pyrazine, the pore environment can be tuned to strengthen Lewis's’s acid–base interactions with CO2. Among this family, TNP3 exhibits the highest CO2/N2 selectivity (up to 48 at 273 K) while maintaining high adsorption capacity despite the typical trade-off between surface area and selectivity. The frameworks also display moderate CO2/CH4 selectivity (approximately 8–9 at 273 K), reflecting the relatively greater polarizability of CH4 compared with N2. These results demonstrate that rational control of pore architecture together with heteroatom incorporation enables POPs to serve as efficient adsorbents for carbon capture from flue gases and natural gas purification.180

Overall, contaminant-specific selectivity in POPs arises from the synergistic combination of pore-size engineering, functional group incorporation, and surface charge modulation rather than any single structural feature. Rational integration of these design parameters enables POPs to discriminate efficiently between molecules with similar sizes or chemical compositions, making them highly promising adsorbents for the selective removal of dyes, heavy metals, oxyanions, proteins, and greenhouse gases in advanced environmental remediation technologies.

Regeneration and reusability

Regeneration and reusability are among the most critical design parameters of POPs, as they determine the long-term economic feasibility, environmental sustainability, and practical applicability of these materials in pollutant remediation. An ideal POP should not only exhibit high adsorption capacity but also preserve its structural integrity and adsorption performance over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles. Such durability minimizes material consumption, reduces secondary waste generation, and lowers operational costs, thereby facilitating the large-scale implementation of POP-based technologies for water purification, gas separation, and heterogeneous catalysis.181,182

The outstanding regeneration capability of POPs primarily originates from their permanently cross-linked covalent frameworks, which provide a high chemical, thermal, and mechanical stability under repeated operating conditions. Strong covalent linkages prevent framework collapse during adsorption and desorption, while permanent porosity and interconnected pore channels ensure efficient diffusion of both adsorbates and regenerating agents. Consequently, active adsorption sites remain accessible throughout repeated cycles without significant pore blockage or structural degradation. In addition, resistance to acidic, alkaline, and organic solvent environments enables POPs to maintain their performance under harsh operating conditions, making them suitable for continuous environmental remediation processes.181,182

The regeneration efficiency of POPs can be further improved through rational framework design and appropriate chemical functionalization. Electron-rich aromatic backbones, nitrogen-containing building units, and hydrophobic structural domains enhance framework rigidity while stabilizing host–guest interactions through π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and other non-covalent forces.181 The selection of suitable regeneration solvents, including methanol, ethanol, and tetrahydrofuran (THF), also facilitates efficient desorption of adsorbed species without damaging the framework, thereby preserving adsorption performance over repeated cycles.181

Several representative POPs clearly demonstrate the importance of these design principles. TPB-DMTP-COF exhibits nearly unchanged adsorption efficiency after repeated adsorption–desorption cycles following regeneration with common organic solvents, owing to its chemically robust crystalline framework, highly accessible pore channels, and excellent structural stability.181 Similarly, the CMP CPOP-19 maintains stable formaldehyde adsorption during repeated use because of its rigid π-conjugated backbone and remarkable resistance to thermal, acidic, and alkaline environments.182 Beyond adsorption, AuNPs@CPOP serves as a highly recyclable catalyst support that retains excellent catalytic activity over successive reaction cycles, while CPOP-12 functions as an efficient reusable heterogeneous oxidation catalyst with sustained catalytic performance.182 These representative examples collectively demonstrate that the long-term operational stability of POPs depends primarily on robust framework architecture rather than adsorption capacity alone.

Overall, regeneration and reusability are indispensable requirements for translating POPs from laboratory-scale materials to practical environmental technologies. The ability to retain adsorption capacity over repeated adsorption–desorption cycles is one of the key requirements for translating POPs from laboratory research to industrial environmental applications, as it ensures long-term operational stability, minimizes material replacement, and reduces the overall cost of pollutant remediation. Therefore, the rational design of chemically stable covalent frameworks that preserve pore accessibility, structural integrity, and active adsorption sites throughout repeated operating cycles remains essential for developing durable, cost-effective, and sustainable POP-based adsorbents and catalysts for future environmental applications.181,182

Structural and functional tuning of POPs

The performance of POPs in adsorption, separation, and heterogeneous catalysis is closely governed by their pore architecture and chemical functionality. Accordingly, structural and functional tuning provides a means of adjusting surface area, pore volume, surface chemistry, and molecular recognition sites according to the target application. Incorporation of heteroatoms such as nitrogen, sulfur, and fluorine, together with macrocyclic units such as porphyrins and phthalocyanines, can introduce charge-transfer and anion–π interactions. These structural features can substantially influence pollutant uptake; for example, triazine-based and hyper-cross-linked frameworks have demonstrated iodine uptake as high as 15.0 g g−1. Similarly, controlling pore geometry and size distribution, including pores in the 6–8 Å range and cavitand-to-framework architectures, can improve molecular selectivity. The high thermal, chemical, and radiation stability reported for several POPs further supports their use under demanding conditions, including nuclear-waste-related environments. Reversibility is another important advantage, as captured species can often be removed and the framework reused; iodine-loaded frameworks, for example, can be regenerated by washing or filtration with ethanol.183

Among the synthetic strategies relevant to structural tuning, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and knitting polymerization provide economically attractive routes to hyper-cross-linked polymers (HCPs). These methods employ readily available external linkers such as dichloromethane, formaldehyde dimethyl acetal, dimethoxymethane, paraformaldehyde, dibromomethane, and p-xylene dichloride with Lewis's acids such as AlCl3 or FeCl3.33,140,146,184 Representative materials include CBAP-1,34 TP-POP, TP-POP-SO3NH4,127 Keto-POP,149 IISERP-POF6–8,185 HPCOP-1, HPCOP-2,186 NS-TMP,148 POP-W,187 ITN-POP, TN-POP,143 I3C-HCP,65 NOP series, Mac-DMP, and Mac-TMP.153 These materials generally form highly cross-linked amorphous networks with stable microporosity and thermal stability. Microwave-assisted Friedel–Crafts polymerization further demonstrates how reaction conditions can be modified to shorten synthesis times, producing heteroaromatic frameworks such as HAF, HAT, and HAP within minutes while retaining CO2-capture capability.40 Similarly, slow-knitting approaches have enabled fluorinated frameworks such as the PAF-DBrM series, in which dibromomethane acts as both solvent and crosslinking electrophile at temperatures up to 100 °C.142

Functional group incorporation

Functional-group incorporation provides a direct means of tailoring POP chemistry for adsorption, separation, catalysis, and environmental applications. Functionalities can be introduced either through bottom-up monomer selection, in which functional building blocks are incorporated during polymerization, or through PSM of an existing framework. The resulting performance depends not only on the identity of the functional group but also on its accessibility within the porous network and its interaction with the target species.

Nitrogen-containing groups, including triazine, imine, amine, and azo functionalities, provide electron-rich sites capable of participating in charge-transfer interactions and metal coordination. Triazine-based CMPs such as TTPPA, TTDAB, and Tm-MTDAB exhibit iodine uptake values of 4.90, 3.13, and 3.04 g g−1, respectively, attributed to polyiodide formation.188 Melamine-based MPOP-4 similarly reaches an iodine vapor-phase equilibrium capacity of 6.30 g g−1 at 80 °C.71 Nitrogen functionality can also improve pollutant-specific interactions: M-NH2-THCP uses triazine and amine groups for nitroimidazole removal through synergistic hydrogen bonding,189 while nitrogen-doped pp-tpta enhances CO2 adsorption through interaction with the gas quadrupole.190

Sulfur- and thiol-containing functionalities provide additional high-affinity sites, particularly for soft metal ions and iodine. COF-S-SH enables co-extraction of Hg(ii) and BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes) contaminants and exhibits a mercury-binding capacity of 588.2 mg g−1.191,192 Likewise, POF-1 and P-TzTz, containing bithiophene/imine and thiazolothiazole functionalities, respectively, show iodine uptake of 4.25 g g−1 and 326 wt%.72,193 Thiophene-containing Th-2 further demonstrates the potential of sulfur-rich frameworks for mercury removal from water to safe drinking-water levels.194

Other functionalities provide complementary interactions. Oxygen-containing POP-O, with carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, exhibits a RhB adsorption capacity of 1012 mg g−1.195 Phenolic frameworks such as TrzPOP-3 and TAPs enhance CO2 adsorption and CO2/N2 selectivity through dipole–quadrupole and hydrogen-bonding interactions.47,196 Charged PAF-23, PAF-24, and PAF-25 exhibit iodine uptake ranging from 2.60 to 2.76 g g−1.197 Brønsted-acid functionalization, exemplified by sulfonylation of PTO-1S, increases NH3 uptake to 11.52 mmol g−1.130 Fluorination of PIM-15 provides narrow slit pores useful for gas separation,198 whereas phosphorus-containing aromatic POPs provide suitable electronic environments for metal nanoparticles in catalytic reactions.199

Overall, these examples demonstrate that functional-group selection should be viewed as a structure–interaction–performance relationship rather than simply as a method for increasing surface functionality. Different chemical groups provide distinct binding environments, and their effectiveness therefore depends on the nature of the target molecule and the accessibility of the functional sites within the framework.

Pore size distribution and surface area tuning

Pore size and surface area are important structural parameters because they influence the accessibility of functional sites, pore filling, diffusion, and mass transport. The supplied examples also show, however, that surface area alone does not necessarily determine adsorption performance. In amine-functionalized silica systems, for example, pore volume and the residual porosity available after amine loading are important for maintaining accessible transport pathways. SBA-15 exceeds MCM-41 in residual surface area at 50 wt% PEI loading (80 versus 11 m2 g−1) and exhibits approximately 140 mg g−1 CO2 uptake, while KIT-6 and mesocellular foams provide capacities of 135 mg g−1 and 2.3 mmol g−1, respectively. Macroporous silica with approximately 56 nm pores and a pore volume of 1.80 cm3 g−1 further illustrates the importance of pore volume for avoiding pore blockage and maintaining adsorption kinetics. The supplied discussion therefore identifies a minimum support porosity of 1.8 cm3 g−1 as important for CO2 adsorption.200

Solvent control provides another route for modifying pore structure. TPE-COF-II, synthesized through a [2 + 4] condensation pathway, possesses a BET surface area of 2168 m2 g−1 and a pore size of 1.19 nm, compared with 1535 m2 g−1 for TPE-COF-I prepared through [4 + 4] condensation. The resulting TPE-COF-II exhibits CO2 uptake of 5.3 mmol g−1 (118.8 cm3 g−1) at 273 K and 1 atm.201 Similarly, Co(ii)@TA-TF COF combines a BET surface area of 1076 m2 g−1 with uniform 1.6 nm micropores, enabling CO2 capture and catalytic conversion of epoxides to cyclic carbonates with yields up to 99% under 313 K and 0.1 MPa. Its pore architecture also produces size-dependent catalytic behaviour, with propylene oxide converted efficiently while styrene oxide gives only 4% yield. The framework remains stable up to 400 °C and retains activity for at least five cycles without metal leaching.103

Anion exchange provides another means of tuning ionic frameworks. ICTFs synthesized through ZnCl2-catalyzed ionothermal polymerization show an increase in specific surface area from 751 to 1010 m2 g−1 following anion exchange, accompanied by CO2 capacities of 2.36 mmol g−1 for ICTF-Cl and 2.48 mmol g−1 for ICTF-SCN at 273 K and 1 bar. ICTF-Cl also exhibits a CO2/N2 selectivity of 119.1 and maintains performance over five cycles.95 KFUPM-1, a mesoporous/microporous polymer, has a surface area of 305 m2 g−1 and CO2 uptake of 1.04 mmol g−1 (23.4 cm3 g−1) at 298 K, with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 141 and stable performance over 45 cycles, including under 91% relative humidity.48

Composition and linker ratios can likewise alter pore accessibility. In POP-T, optimization of the m-terphenyl to biphenyl dichlorobenzyl ratio produces POP-T (1 : 1.5), with a surface area of 1416 m2 g−1 and a pore size of 1.73 nm. Its porous structure enables RhB uptake of 1414.4 mg g−1 while maintaining more than 90% removal efficiency after four cycles.202 The JUC-Z2 framework, prepared by Yamamoto-type Ullmann cross-coupling, contains uniform 1.2 nm micropores and a surface area of 2034 m2 g−1, giving a CO2 uptake of 3.17 mmol g−1 (71 cm3 g−1) at 273 K and thermal stability up to 430 °C.203

Beyond POPs, advances in precision membrane formation further demonstrate the importance of controlling pore dimensions and transport pathways at the sub-nanometre scale. Anhydrous interfacial polymerization (AIP) of polyamide at a solid–liquid interface eliminates water-induced side reactions and enables a more ordered condensation process, producing a selective layer with an ionic sieving accuracy of approximately 0.5 Å and a thickness of about 35 nm. The resulting membrane achieved Mg2+/Li+ and Cl−/SO42− separation selectivities of 78.3 and 29.2, respectively, while maintaining a water flux of up to 13.6 L m−2 h−1 bar−1. Although this membrane is not a POP, the study provides a useful comparative perspective: precise control of pore-size distribution and interfacial polymerization can reduce transport resistance while enhancing molecular and ionic discrimination, reinforcing the broader principle that structural precision can be more important than surface area alone for selective separation.204

Taken together, these examples indicate that pore volume, pore accessibility, size distribution, and framework composition must be considered alongside BET surface area. Optimizing these parameters can improve not only adsorption capacity but also diffusion, selectivity, and recyclability, demonstrating the importance of balancing textural properties with the intended application.

Modulating hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity

The balance between hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity is an important aspect of POP design because surface–guest interactions influence water uptake, target-molecule accessibility, selectivity, and stability under humid conditions. Hydrophobic modification can reduce competitive water adsorption and preserve accessible micropores, whereas hydrophilic functionality can increase interactions with water and polar species. Thus, the appropriate surface character depends on the intended application rather than on maximizing either hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity alone.

Hydrophobicity can be introduced through non-polar functionalities such as fluorine and nitro groups. In F-DCBP-CTF-1, fluorine-containing groups reduce water adsorption within the micropores.205 Similarly, trifluoromethyl groups in the polyimide MPI-6FA improve moisture stability despite its high micropore volume. Mechanochemical preparation has also produced the superhydrophobic MSHMP-1, in which the conjugated aromatic structure limits water interaction, resulting in a water-vapor uptake of only 16.5 mg g−1 at 90% relative humidity.125 These examples demonstrate that reducing water affinity can be advantageous when moisture competes with the target adsorbate.

Conversely, hydrophilic surfaces can be generated by incorporating polar heteroatoms such as oxygen, boron, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur or by introducing ionic functionalities. The positively charged COP-120,205 containing quaternary ammonium groups, exhibits a water uptake of 44.9 wt% at P/P0 = 0.9 while maintaining reversibility over repeated humidification cycles. Sulfonation provides another route to increasing water affinity: PPOPs-SO3H206 exhibits enhanced polarity and hydrophilicity and achieves a methylene blue adsorption capacity of 980.4 mg g−1 from aqueous solution. These results illustrate that surface polarity can be deliberately modified to improve dispersion and interaction with polar contaminants.

The importance of hydrophilicity under realistic conditions is further demonstrated by KFUPM-1, which contains abundant amine sites and selectively captures CO2 over N2 even at 91% relative humidity while maintaining structural stability and performance for at least 20 cycles. Epoxy-substituted POPs (ep-POPs), with surface areas reaching 852 m2 g−1, facilitate water diffusion through hydrogen bonding with epoxy groups and retain their performance over 40 cycles. Within the MOPI family, hydrophilicity also varies with the molecular connectivity of the bridging units, with bicyclooct-7-en units showing greater water absorption than diphenyl sulfone and benzophenone units. More broadly, these observations demonstrate that careful selection of molecular connectivity and surface functionality can tune porous frameworks for applications ranging from gas adsorption to desiccant materials.31

A recent study on nanoparticle-assisted CO2 hydrate formation further demonstrates the importance of surface chemistry and interfacial properties in controlling capture kinetics. The study showed that both hydrophilic and hydrophobic nanoparticle surfaces can influence hydrate formation, while their effects depend strongly on nanoparticle characteristics and concentration. Hydrophilic surfaces promote rapid hydrate formation through favourable interfacial interactions, whereas hydrophobic particles can influence the early-stage formation process through gas–liquid–solid interfacial effects. However, excessive nanoparticle concentrations can cause aggregation and hinder mass transfer, demonstrating that surface functionality must be balanced with dispersion and transport. Although these nanoparticle media are not POPs, this finding provides a useful broader perspective for POP design: surface chemistry should be optimized not only for equilibrium uptake but also for interfacial mass transfer and capture kinetics.207

Overall, hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity should be regarded as application-dependent design parameters. Excessive water affinity can increase competitive adsorption, whereas insufficient polarity may limit interaction with polar targets. Consequently, controlling the chemical environment of the pore surface provides a means of balancing moisture stability, adsorption selectivity, transport, and recyclability.

Surface charge engineering

Surface-charge engineering provides another route for modifying POP performance by controlling electrostatic, charge-transfer, and host–guest interactions. Introducing charged or electronically active groups can alter the affinity of a framework toward ions, gases, and other target species, while the accessibility and stability of these charge sites determine their practical effectiveness.

Cz-TPM materials prepared through FeCl3 oxidative coupling polymerization contain abundant carbazole groups at the pore surface and enable fluorescence-based detection of I− and Hg2+ ions, with a reported detection limit of 7.9 µM. Their stability toward common solvents and regeneration by centrifugation also support their reuse.208 Charge-transfer interactions provide another mechanism for pollutant capture. TTF-CMPs capture iodine through electron donation from tetrathiafulvalene (TTF) units, resulting in partial oxidation of the TTF moieties and formation of charge-transfer species involving oxidized TTF radical cations and iodide/polyiodide anions (TTFCMPC+-In−).209

Positively charged frameworks can also improve CO2 capture and conversion. Porous cationic polymers (PCPs) containing viologen functionalities, exemplified by PCP-Cl, exhibit a CO2 uptake of 101.7 mg g−1 and enable conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates with efficiencies of up to 99%. Their catalytic behaviour is also influenced by substrate size, and the materials remain reusable for at least four cycles.210 In another approach, post-metalation of PAF-26-COOH with Li, Na, K, and Mg modifies the surface charge and polarity of the framework, thereby influencing charge–quadrupole and induced-dipole interactions with CO2. Among these materials, PAF-26-COOMg exhibits a CO2/N2 selectivity of 80 and maintains stable CO2-capture performance for more than four months under air exposure.211

These examples demonstrate that surface charge provides a complementary design variable to pore size and functional-group incorporation. Rather than relying solely on surface area, charge engineering can strengthen specific electrostatic or charge-transfer interactions and thereby improve selectivity toward targeted species. However, its effectiveness depends on the nature and accessibility of the charged sites, as well as the stability of the resulting framework under operating conditions. Thus, rational control of surface charge, together with pore architecture and chemical functionality, provides a more comprehensive strategy for developing application-specific POPs.

Mechanisms of pollutant adsorption

The removal of pollutants by POPs is governed by the chemical affinity between the pollutant and the framework, together with pore architecture and accessibility. Depending on the pollutant, the dominant mechanisms include electrostatic attraction and ion exchange, hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions, π–π interactions, coordination or chelation, hydrogen bonding, and pore filling. Their relative contributions depend strongly on pollutant charge, molecular structure, and the functional groups and pore environment of the POP.

For charged pollutants such as oxoanions and ionic dyes, electrostatic attraction and ion exchange are particularly important. Cationic frameworks containing exchangeable counterions can replace these ions with oppositely charged species from solution. For example, QUST-iPOP-1 efficiently removes MnO4− and anionic dyes, whereas the pyridinium-based anion trap PQA-pN(Me)2Py-Cl enables selective TcO4− extraction.212,213 For non-polar or weakly polar organic pollutants, hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions can be reinforced by extended π-conjugated surfaces, as demonstrated by COF TPAPC and the porphyrin-based PCPF-1.213,214

Coordination provides a more specific mechanism for metal-ion capture because donor atoms within the framework form coordinate interactions with target metal ions. POP-oNH2-AO, for example, uses amidoxime functionality for uranium extraction, while thiol-functionalized COF-S-SH provides strong binding sites for Hg(ii).213 Hydrogen bonding can similarly enhance the uptake of polar contaminants; the nitrogen-rich EPS-CC-3, containing triazine functionalities, promotes the capture of 2,4-dichlorophenol.215 In contrast, pore filling is primarily governed by the compatibility between pollutant dimensions and the pore environment, as illustrated by tetracycline uptake in EPS-FDA-2.215

Thus, high adsorption performance does not arise from a single structural parameter. Rather, pore accessibility, surface chemistry, molecular recognition, and pollutant–framework interactions act together, with the dominant mechanism changing according to the chemical nature of the contaminant. This distinction is important when comparing POPs because a framework with a lower surface area can outperform a more porous material when it provides stronger and more selective binding sites.

Heavy metal adsorption mechanisms

Heavy-metal removal by POPs is governed primarily by coordination/chelation, soft–soft interactions, ion exchange, ligand exchange, and, in some cases, reduction, with performance strongly dependent on the nature and accessibility of the functional groups. Sulfur-containing frameworks are particularly effective for soft metal ions. For example, COF-S-SH combines thiol sites with high porosity to achieve 588.2 mg g−1 Hg(ii) and 88.28% removal even in the presence of competing Pb, Cu, Mg, Zn, and Fe ions, while maintaining performance over five regeneration cycles.192 Thiol-functionalized calixresorcinarene HCPs provide rapid Hg(ii) uptake, reaching saturation within 2 min, whereas thiophene-containing Th-2 captures Hg(ii) through S–Hg–S interactions with a capacity of 145 mg g−1 and can be regenerated for six cycles.194,216 Nitrogen- and oxygen-containing sites can provide complementary coordination environments, as demonstrated by HCMP-1, which achieves 604 mg g−1 Hg(ii) and approximately 95.6% removal through interactions involving triazine and dibenzofuran functionalities.217

Ion- and ligand-exchange mechanisms broaden POP applications to inorganic and radioactive contaminants. Sulfonated HCPs capture Sr2+ (95.6 mg g−1) and Cs+ (273 mg g−1) through ion exchange, whereas Ca-functionalized hydrophilic polymers HHCP1-Ca and HHCP2-Ca remove fluoride through ligand exchange and CaCO3-to-CaF2 conversion, with HHCP1-Ca reaching 267 mg g−1.218 Their performance is maintained between pH 3–7 but decreases under strongly acidic conditions because of Ca leaching. These examples illustrate that functional-group chemistry and ion-specific affinity can be more decisive than surface area alone, particularly for selective heavy-metal and oxyanion removal.

Reductive remediation provides a complementary pathway when contaminants can be converted to less hazardous forms. Sulfide-modified zero-valent iron (S-nZVI) reduces Cr(vi), Se(vi), nitrate, and chlorinated compounds, with sulfidation increasing the reduction efficiency of Cr(vi) by approximately eightfold and TCE by elevenfold relative to bare nZVI.219 Sulfur modification also suppresses undesired H2 evolution, enhances contaminant accessibility, and extends Fe0 longevity in water from approximately 45 to >160 days. Overall, the comparison indicates that coordination strength, ion exchange, and controlled redox activity should be matched to the chemical form of the target contaminant, rather than relying solely on high porosity.

Organic dye removal processes

Organic-dye adsorption in POPs arises mainly from electrostatic attraction, π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and pore filling, with the dominant contribution determined by both dye structure and framework chemistry. Aromatic HCPs demonstrate the contribution of π-rich and hydrophobic environments: Cz-pyr-P adsorbs MB at 175.44 mg g−1, while pentiptycene-derived PHCP reaches 877 mg g−1 for crystal violet and 289 mg g−1 for MB, with >99% removal within 5 min.220,221 Triptycene-based THPS achieves still higher capacities of 1538 mg g−1 for Congo red and 787 mg g−1 for MB, supported by its hierarchical porosity and 1426 m2 g−1 surface area; it also retains performance over five cycles.222 These results demonstrate that rapid uptake can arise from the combined effects of accessible pores and aromatic/hydrophobic interactions rather than surface area alone.

Electrostatic matching becomes particularly important when framework charge is deliberately controlled. Positively charged NH2-HCP shows a high 3290 mg g−1 capacity for the anionic dye X-3B, although its performance decreases with increasing pH as the positive surface charge weakens.175 Conversely, POP-TCE-15 preferentially captures cationic MB and RhB, with capacities of 787.4 and 421.9 mg g−1, respectively, but becomes sensitive to alkaline conditions because of hydroxide competition.223 Noria-POP-1 similarly exploits its negatively charged phenolic surface to achieve 2434 mg g−1 MB, retaining >2068 mg g−1 after five cycles.224 These contrasting examples show that surface charge can provide strong selectivity, but also introduces pH dependence.

Other systems demonstrate that high adsorption does not necessarily require dominant electrostatic interactions. TPP-NH2 adsorbs MB (560.6 mg g−1) and MO (803.2 mg g−1) mainly through π–π stacking, giving relatively low pH sensitivity and five-cycle reusability.176 In APEADA, hydrogen bonding dominates CR removal (92.03%), whereas electrostatic interactions control RhB adsorption; CR removal falls to approximately 10% at pH 2–3, highlighting the importance of pollutant-surface charge compatibility.225 EBPAR combines electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions to remove >99% MO and >97% EBT, although regeneration is less effective, with capacities retained at approximately 63% for MO and 29% for EBT after cycling.226

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that dye-removal performance is controlled by the interplay of surface charge, aromaticity, hydrogen-bonding functionality, hydrophobicity, and pore accessibility. Importantly, the highest adsorption capacity does not necessarily correspond to the most practical material; pH tolerance, adsorption kinetics, selectivity, regeneration efficiency, and long-term stability must also be considered when translating POP-based dye adsorption from laboratory studies to wastewater treatment.

Volatile organic compounds (VOC) adsorption mechanisms and applications

Volatile organic compound (VOC) adsorption by POPs is governed by accessible porosity, surface hydrophobicity, aromaticity, pore-size distribution, and specific host–guest interactions. HCPs, CMPs, PIMs, PAFs, and COFs consequently provide different routes for tailoring adsorption toward aromatic hydrocarbons, alkanes, and other volatile pollutants. Highly porous PPN-4, with a BET surface area of 6461 m2 g−1, provides abundant adsorption sites for aromatic hydrocarbons and saturated alkanes. PAF-1 exhibits particularly high uptake of benzene (1306 mg g−1) and toluene (1357 mg g−1), associated with its hydrophobic aromatic framework and structural stability. PIM-1 is additionally attractive for gas separation because its solubility and processability enable fabrication into flexible thin films.227

Among POPs, HCPs are particularly suitable for non-polar VOC capture because their permanently cross-linked aromatic networks combine hydrophobicity with π–π and dispersive interactions. Friedel–Crafts alkylation generates rigid networks that resist structural collapse during adsorption. F-HCP exhibits a toluene uptake of 2487 mg g−1, whereas HC-polydivinylbenzene reaches 2343 mg g−1 for benzene. Aromatic precursors such as benzene, truxene, and carbazole favor interactions with aromatic VOCs, whereas adsorption of aliphatic compounds such as n-hexane is comparatively less favorable. HCP-based resins can also maintain their performance at 80% relative humidity, highlighting their potential for humid industrial streams where competitive water adsorption can compromise conventional adsorbents.228

POP functionality can further extend VOC treatment from adsorption to selective sensing. The luminescent carbazole-derived TCB-CMP enables turn-on fluorescence detection of arene vapors including benzene and toluene. Ionic POPs (iPOPs) provide an alternative mechanism through electrostatic interactions with charged pollutants and toxic gases. Furthermore, processing POPs into macroscopic forms such as beads, fibers, and membranes can improve handling and facilitate real-time environmental remediation of VOCs and other pollutants.33

POP-based sensing is also relevant to food-quality monitoring, where volatile compounds indicate spoilage and ripeness. Ethylene (C2H4) serves as a ripeness indicator, whereas trimethylamine (TMA) represents a foul-smelling volatile. Amine-functionalized polymers can selectively interact with biogenic amines and CO2 through acid–base interactions and chemical bonding. CMPs and HCPs are also promising for portable and disposable sensors because their low density and film-forming properties facilitate detection of volatile nitrosamines, formaldehyde, acetone, and other oxygenated compounds.229

The advantages of POPs should nevertheless be evaluated against established adsorbents such as activated carbon (AC). AC remains widely used for aromatic VOCs including xylene and ethylbenzene because of its availability and low cost. POPs offer a complementary advantage through molecular control over pore dimensions and surface chemistry, which can improve selectivity toward specific VOCs, including chlorinated compounds such as trichloroethylene. COFs further demonstrate the potential of controlled pore architectures for selective separation of light gases such as CH4 and H2, highlighting the importance of pore-size engineering in porous organic materials.230

A major practical limitation of VOC adsorption is energy-intensive regeneration. Incorporating 4 wt% carbon black into HCPs produced a 90-fold increase in dielectric loss, enabling rapid microwave heating. The resulting CB/HCP composite was applied to n-hexane and toluene capture; microwave treatment reached 166 °C within 10 min and achieved approximately 99% desorption efficiency for hexane. This strategy demonstrates how conductive additives can improve regenerability and facilitate cyclic VOC capture while reducing regeneration time.231

The hydrophobic, carbon-rich frameworks of PAF-1 and F-HCP can limit competitive water adsorption while maintaining affinity toward benzene and toluene under humid conditions. Their strong covalent C–C and C–N linkages contribute to physicochemical stability under demanding conditions, supporting repeated adsorption–desorption operation.228

Overall, the reported examples demonstrate that effective VOC removal depends on more than surface area alone. Hydrophobicity, aromaticity, pore accessibility, molecular recognition, humidity tolerance, processable morphology, and regeneration efficiency must be considered collectively. PAF-1 and F-HCP illustrate the importance of hydrophobicity and stability for aromatic VOC capture; TCB-CMP extends POPs toward VOC sensing; beads, fibers, and membranes provide routes toward practical deployment; and CB/HCP composites address regeneration limitations. Future VOC-oriented POPs should therefore prioritize selective adsorption under realistic humid conditions, scalable processing, low-energy regeneration, and cost competitiveness with activated carbon, rather than relying solely on exceptionally high surface area.33,227,228,230,231

Applications of POPs in environmental remediation

POPs have emerged as versatile materials for environmental remediation because their permanent porosity, tunable surface chemistry, and chemical stability enable selective capture and transformation of diverse contaminants. Their practical value is most evident in water, air, and soil remediation, where pore architecture and functional groups can be tailored to the physicochemical properties of the target pollutant.

Water treatment

POPs are particularly promising for water purification because functionalized pores can combine high capacity with contaminant selectivity. Thiol-functionalized PAF-1-SH and POP-SH provide a notable example, achieving Hg(ii) uptake of up to 1216 mg g−1 and reducing Hg concentration from 5 ppm to 1 ppb within 10 min, demonstrating their potential for meeting stringent drinking-water requirements.31,232,233 Cationic iPOPs similarly enable rapid removal of toxic chromate and dichromate through ion exchange.4,31,234

For organic contaminants, hydrophobic and π-rich frameworks provide broad adsorption capability. POP-T removes RhB, MB, and diclofenac at capacities of 1414.4, 1256.6, and 1028.9 mg g−1, respectively, while β-cyclodextrin-based POPs extend POP applications to persistent perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA).31,33,202 A gallic-acid-derived azo POP further demonstrates multifunctionality by simultaneously removing MB (141.94 mg g−1) and E. coli.66 POPs are also relevant to radioactive-waste treatment: amidoxime frameworks target uranium, whereas N-rich and multifunctional POPs enable reversible capture of iodine and I3−.31,33,76,160 Beyond adsorption, porphyrin-linked and triazine-bridged POPs can act as visible-light photocatalysts for degrading persistent organic pollutants, thereby extending their role from contaminant capture to destruction.31,34,235

Air purification

For air purification, POPs offer a combination of high gas-accessible surface area, chemical stability, and tunable gas–framework interactions. PAF-1 illustrates the potential of highly porous frameworks, with a Langmuir surface area of 7100 m2 g−1 and CO2 uptake of approximately 1300 mg g−1, while remaining stable at 520 °C and after seven days in water.236 However, these values also highlight an important distinction between intrinsic porosity and application-specific selectivity. For example, amino-functionalized PAF-33-NH2 achieves a CO2/N2 selectivity of 250.5 while retaining stability to approximately 360 °C.237 Click-chemistry surface modification provides further control over functional-group density, with amine-functionalized surfaces showing enhanced CO2/N2 discrimination through acid–base interactions and stability from pH 2 to 11.238

More application-oriented systems demonstrate the importance of regeneration and energy requirements. PPN-125-DETA captures CO2 from humid flue gas while retaining >90% capacity after 50 adsorption–desorption cycles and requiring approximately one-third of the energy associated with aqueous amine technology.239 Functionalized POPs can also combine capture with conversion: POM-Ims provides 14.5 wt% CO2 uptake together with metal-free conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates and maintains performance after prolonged exposure to hot water.240 8BIPLP-1 functionalized with Cu(BF4)2 enhances CO2 uptake by approximately 200% at 0.15 bar, with CO2/N2 and CO2/CH4 selectivities of 101 and 20, respectively.241 Phosphonium-functionalized POPs similarly combine adsorption and catalysis, reaching 12.9 wt% CO2 uptake and a selectivity of 56, while maintaining performance over six cycles and thermal stability to 300 °C.242 Collectively, these examples indicate that practical air-purification materials should be assessed not only by adsorption capacity but also by selectivity under humid conditions, regeneration energy, cycling stability, and integration of capture with conversion.

Soil remediation

Soil remediation presents a different challenge because contaminants such as PAHs, PCBs, and organochlorine pesticides are persistent, hydrophobic, and strongly associated with soil organic matter, increasing their long-term mobility and bioaccumulation risks.243,244 The tunable π-rich surfaces and hierarchical pores of PAFs, CMPs, HCPs, and COFs can immobilize such pollutants through pore filling and π–π interactions while providing opportunities for targeted surface reactions.33 Their principal advantage over conventional amendments is the possibility of designing specific binding and reactive sites, rather than relying only on nonspecific sorption.

The potential for selective heavy-metal immobilization is illustrated by AMTD-TCT, whose hierarchical N/S-coordinated architecture achieves 1257.7 mg g−1 Hg(ii) and promotes rapid transport and access to active sites in soil–water systems.245 Its robust framework remains functional across approximately pH 3–8 and withstands highly acidic and alkaline treatment, while pollutant speciation can further immobilize mercury as insoluble HgS for at least 356 days.245,246 This combination of adsorption and chemical stabilization is particularly relevant because it can reduce contaminant mobility rather than merely transferring pollutants between phases.

Long-term reuse and environmental stability remain essential for field implementation. Co-TpBpy COF reportedly retains >94% of its performance after 1000 consecutive uses, while regenerated COFs can operate for approximately 60 h without measurable loss of crystallinity or porosity.247,248 Such durability may reduce secondary contamination and help prevent heavy-metal accumulation in crops. Nevertheless, the soil environment is substantially more complex than controlled aqueous systems; therefore, competition from natural organic matter, pollutant ageing, regeneration requirements, and the fate of the POP itself should be incorporated into future assessments of field-scale remediation.

Overall, the application studies demonstrate that POPs are moving beyond the concept of high-capacity adsorbents toward multifunctional remediation platforms capable of selective capture, contaminant immobilization, and photocatalytic or catalytic transformation. However, comparisons across water, air, and soil applications should not rely on adsorption capacity alone. Selectivity in realistic matrices, regeneration energy, material recovery, long-term stability, secondary contamination, scalability, and cost are ultimately more decisive for translating laboratory-scale POP performance into practical environmental technologies.

Challenges in the applications of POPs

Scalability of synthesis methods

The transition of POPs from laboratory synthesis to industrial application remains constrained by harsh reaction conditions, long synthesis times, expensive catalysts, hazardous solvents, and limited batch scalability. Conventional ionothermal synthesis of CTFs, for example, commonly requires 400–700 °C, excess ZnCl2, and prolonged reaction times, which can promote partial carbonization and complicate scale-up. Solvothermal approaches may similarly require expensive metal catalysts and toxic solvents. These limitations have motivated milder and more energy-efficient routes. Solid-phase cyclotrimerization of benzene-linked POPs using catalytic p-toluenesulfonic acid at 130–148 °C affords yields above 98% and is more amenable to scale-up than conventional high-temperature CTF synthesis.76 Low-temperature one-pot polycondensation of aldehydes with amidines provides another mild alternative to conventional CTF synthesis,15 while Knoevenagel condensation offers highly stable frameworks with potential for larger-scale production.97 Microwave-assisted Friedel–Crafts alkylation can improve reaction uniformity through more efficient energy transfer,156 and microwave-assisted Schiff-base polycondensation reduces the synthesis time of amino/imino-linked MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs from 72 h to 3 h.40,74

An equally important route to scalability is the replacement of precious-metal catalysts with inexpensive Lewis's acids or catalyst-free processes. Friedel–Crafts polymerization employing industrially available AlCl3 or FeCl3 enables multi-gram synthesis of keto-functionalized triptycene HCPs, imidazole-based frameworks, and carbon-based CBPOP/CNPOP under relatively mild conditions.140,144,149 Metal-free condensation has also been demonstrated for triazine-based aminal-linked POPs (ALPOPs) at 180 °C 39 and thiophene-containing NRPOP-1 and NRPOP-2.60 Melamine-based MPOP-4 can be synthesized hydrothermally at 180 °C with an 82% yield and 51.2% nitrogen content,150 while the Radziszewski multicomponent reaction (RMC) enables the preparation of imidazolium-based frameworks from inexpensive precursors under ambient conditions.109

Green and rapid synthesis further improves the prospects for scale-up by reducing solvent, energy, and processing requirements. Mechanochemical ball milling produces PAF-210 within 15 min at >92% yield,249 while solvent-free synthesis of guanidinium-based gn-iPOP-Cl requires only 45 min.75 NpTOC reaches a nearly quantitative yield of 99.8%,134 whereas the solvent-free slow-knitting synthesis of fluorinated PAF-DBrM frameworks provides a 99.25% yield without metal catalysts.142 Aqueous routes have also been demonstrated for hydroxyl-rich POPs (∼90% recovery),67 tannin-derived POPs,68 and azo-linked frameworks (97% yield).73 Simple quaternization and nucleophilic substitution afford ionic frameworks such as TPE-C1 in 99% yield, while HB-iPOP/HD-iPOP provide rapid pollutant removal through comparatively straightforward synthetic routes.112,113,115

Importantly, scale-up should not be evaluated solely from reaction yield. Industrial implementation also requires retention of performance after increasing batch size, reproducibility, processability, and compatibility with continuous operation. Triptycene-based frameworks illustrate this potential, achieving gold recovery of 3.79 g g−1 and iodine uptake of 2.87 g g−1, with 99.98% gold selectivity even at 14 ppb in seawater; these materials can also reduce chromate concentrations below 40 ppb in continuous-flow reactors.128 Other high-performance examples include keto-HCPs with CO2 uptake of 207.1 mg g−1,149 nitrogen-doped carbons with CO2 uptake of 4.4 mmol g−1,88 and CTF-DB-MA with iodine uptake of 2255.81 mg g−1.62 Sonogashira- and Heck-derived networks remain stable up to 665 °C,89 while Diels–Alder and azo–porphyrin polymers (APPs) provide surface areas above 1000 m2 g−1.250 Acridine-, pyrene-, and cyano-rich POPs further demonstrate that robust architectures can be combined with high adsorption performance for large-scale remediation.55,105

The strong covalent bonding of POPs contributes to scale-up indirectly. It does not itself make synthesis easier, but it provides process tolerance. Chemically and thermally robust networks are less susceptible to framework collapse during drying, solvent exchange, washing, regeneration, and repeated operation, thereby reducing the risk that larger-scale processing will compromise the final material. Thus, the principal scale-up advantage of POPs arises from the combination of robust covalent structures with simplified reactions, inexpensive catalysts, shorter reaction times, solvent reduction, and reproducible processing not from covalent stability alone.

Cost of production and commercialization barriers

The commercialization of POPs is governed by the transition from laboratory synthesis to economically viable manufacturing. Major barriers include expensive noble-metal catalysts such as Ni and Pd, toxic organic solvents, high reaction temperatures, long processing times, and costly specialty monomers that can reach hundreds or thousands of USD per gram. Therefore, although the strong covalent networks of POPs provide excellent durability, they do not automatically make POPs cheaper than activated carbon. Their competitiveness must instead arise from lower synthesis and regeneration costs combined with substantially higher selectivity, capacity, lifetime, and functionality for specific contaminants. This distinction is particularly important when comparing POPs with activated carbons produced from inexpensive biomass feedstocks.

Activated carbon remains difficult to outperform on a simple cost-per-mass basis because biomass-derived precursors are abundant and carbonization/activation processes are already industrially established. POPs can therefore be competitive mainly where their molecularly tunable pores and functional groups provide advantages that nonspecific activated carbon cannot easily deliver, such as selective CO2 capture, radionuclide sequestration, precious-metal recovery, highly selective ion removal, or simultaneous adsorption and catalysis. Consequently, future commercialization should focus on applications where the value of selectivity and regeneration outweighs the higher initial material cost, while continued development of low-cost monomers, biomass-derived building blocks, catalyst-free synthesis, solvent-free processing, and rapid reactions is needed to narrow the cost gap.

Progress toward this objective is evident in sub-kilogram production. POPs have been produced at scales of approximately 108 g per batch using ethanol and water as green solvents, with estimated costs approaching $10 USD per kg, comparable to commercial activated carbon.14 Nitrogen-rich and aniline-based POPs further reduce precursor costs by using inexpensive industrial amines. PTPA-PIP, for example, can be synthesized under ambient, catalyst-free conditions with high yield, reducing the estimated laboratory-scale cost to approximately $1.65 per g.111 Microwave-assisted synthesis of MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs reduces reaction time from 72 h to 3 h.74 Biomass-derived systems such as QPOP and M-Qct-B replace petroleum-based monomers with renewable feedstocks, although long-term recyclability remains an issue requiring further optimization.154,155 Triazine- and melamine-based materials, including MPOP and CTF-DB-MA, use relatively inexpensive precursors and mild hydrothermal conditions while maintaining thermal stability up to 350 °C for carbon-capture applications.54,62

Further cost reduction is achieved through catalyst- and solvent-free synthesis. Thiophene-based NRPOP-1 and NRPOP-2 can be prepared by metal-free Schiff-base polycondensation, avoiding expensive inorganic benchmarks such as silver-based zeolites.60 Fluorinated PAF-DBrM frameworks employ a slow-knitting strategy in which dibromomethane serves as both solvent and cross-linking reagent, eliminating metal catalysts.142 Mechanochemical synthesis of guanidinium-based gn-iPOP-Cl reduces reaction time from 72 h to 45 min without toxic solvents.75 TPE-C1 reaches 99% yield through one-step nucleophilic substitution, while imidazole-based BILPs offer low-energy regeneration at room temperature, potentially reducing operating costs relative to ionic liquids.42,115

Other economical strategies include solid-phase cyclotrimerization of benzene-linked WFMC-1–5 using catalytic p-toluenesulfonic acid, giving >98% yield without conventional toxic solvents such as toluene.76 Triptycene-based TP-POP-SO3NH4 replaces toxic NaCN-containing chemistry with a simpler route for radionuclide sequestration.127 Sulfur-rich TpTHU provides an economical platform for mercury removal,53 while porphyrin-based azo-POPs enable one-step synthesis without catalyst waste.60 Acridine-based Ac_POP-5 and keto-functionalized POPs produced by inexpensive AlCl3-catalyzed Friedel–Crafts reactions further demonstrate the shift toward simpler and more economical synthesis.55,149

Nevertheless, commercialization cannot be judged solely from synthesis cost. Regeneration energy, material lifetime, performance in complex matrices, and the cost of separation and purification must also be considered. Systems such as NPOP-TIPA117 and NpTOC134 demonstrate that performance under realistic conditions remains an important consideration. Overall, the economic pathway for POPs lies not in universally replacing activated carbon, but in developing application-specific materials in which selective adsorption, higher capacity, long service life, low regeneration energy, and multifunctionality compensate for higher initial synthesis costs. The combination of green solvents, rapid synthesis, low-cost monomers, catalyst-free routes, renewable feedstocks, and scalable processing is therefore central to achieving commercial viability.

Long-term stability in real-world conditions

Long-term stability under realistic operating conditions is essential for POPs used in environmental remediation, gas separation, and related applications. Their highly crosslinked covalent architectures generally provide strong resistance to moisture, aggressive chemicals, thermal treatment, and repeated regeneration. However, long-term stability should be distinguished from stability over only a few laboratory cycles; industrial deployment may require hundreds or thousands of cycles with consistent capacity, low regeneration energy, and minimal functional-group degradation.

Nitrogen-rich and aniline-based POPs, including MPA-derived imine- and aminal-linked frameworks, retain molecular integrity and porosity after heating to 400 °C.14 Triazine-based nanoarchitectures (NPOPs) and azo-linked frameworks exploit the N2-phobic character of azo groups to maintain CO2 capture and thermal stability over repeated cycles.251 CBPOP, CNPOP, and CAPOP possess rigid conjugated aromatic frameworks and remain permanently porous under post-combustion CO2 capture conditions, with thermal stability up to 600 °C and negligible performance loss over ten adsorption–desorption cycles.144 Ionic and imidazole-based iPOP-ANT remains intact after seven days in 2 N HCl and 2 N KOH,13 while Friedel–Crafts-derived MCTP-1 and MCTP-2 show strong resistance to acidic and basic media.34 Viologen-containing PHOF-1 retains structural integrity and solution processability in various organic solvents and during successive ammonia-capture cycles,23 and guanidine-based and Ni-containing COFs maintain high catalytic CO2 conversion over repeated cycles.15

For water treatment, porphyrin-based Py-POPs retain 98% adsorption capacity after five regeneration cycles.18 Macrocyclic systems such as POP-TCE-15 remain effective across pH 2–10,21 while hydroxyl-functionalized TrzPOP-3 retains structural integrity and adsorption reversibility under humid conditions.19 These examples demonstrate that covalent stability is particularly valuable when adsorbents must withstand repeated wetting, regeneration, and exposure to chemically complex streams.

Recent nitrogen-rich frameworks further reinforce this stability profile. TMTA-DHa and TMTA-TP remain thermally stable up to 400 °C and retain mercury-removal performance over repeated cycles, while MPOP-6C, MPOP-6N, MPOP-5N, and MPOP-5O remain structurally stable up to 350 °C under post-combustion conditions.54,97 Cyano-rich AO-POPs-FN-3 and acridine-based Ac_POP-5 tolerate strong acidic/basic media and organic solvents while maintaining removal performance over repeated cycles.55,105 DAT-1 and ITN-POP exhibit thermal stability up to approximately 406 °C and good catalytic recyclability. β-Ketoenamine-linked TAPPy-TFP and ethylenediamine-modified POP-3 show cycling stability for CO2 capture under humid flue-gas conditions.58,104,106 Porphyrin-based TP-PPOPs-SO3H and PBPOP materials remain stable in air and solvents and at temperatures above 300–488 °C while retaining adsorption performance over repeated cycles.70,146 AZO-POP-T and APP frameworks exhibit thermal stability of approximately 351 °C and maintain CO2 or iodine capture during repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.101,119 Bio-inspired PPOP shows rapid kinetics with only ∼3.1% efficiency loss during repeated heavy-metal extraction.64

Aniline-based PTPA-PIP and MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs tolerate broad pH conditions (pH 0–13) and maintain removal performance through reuse cycles.74,111 Tröger's-base TAPM-TB shows minimal degradation up to 400 °C and supports long-term NOX stabilization.252 Ph-MPOP loses only ∼10% mass at 387 °C while maintaining CO2 capture over repeated cycles.61 Hydroxyl-rich CTT-TPC retains performance over 100 adsorption–desorption cycles.67 Imidazole- and benzimidazole-linked BILP-3 and BILP-4 resist 2 M HCl/NaOH and remain stable up to 400 °C, with regeneration possible at room temperature.42 DAB-POP and HD-iPOP tolerate highly corrosive conditions, including 12 M HCl, while maintaining adsorption performance.113,253 Stilbene-crosslinked PSB (a neutral polymer) and PSBIm (an imidazolium-cationic polymer) and azobenzene-functionalized POPs maintain structural integrity and >99% removal efficiency in the presence of competing ions.131,140 Pyridine-based POP-IB, POP-AB, and Pyd-PPOPs-Br remain stable across broad pH ranges and preserve dye/contaminant removal during repeated cycles.137,157 Perylene- and anthracene-based PT-POP and POP-W are insoluble and stable across diverse solvents and pH conditions.187,254

Ionic POPs, including viologen-, guanidinium-, pyridinium-, and imidazolium-based systems, also show high stability in extreme aqueous environments. iPOP-6, TP-iPOP-PPh3, and NPOP-TIPA retain structural integrity in strong acids/bases and exhibit good recyclability in real-water matrices.69,117,128 Guanidinium-based gn-iPOP-Cl remains stable for seven days in 2 N acid/base media while maintaining 100% removal efficiency.75 Viologen-based HB-iPOP and CTN-POP-1 retain adsorption performance and structural integrity through repeated cycling and harsh conditions.112,114 TP-POP-SO3NH4 additionally withstands radiation doses up to 300 kGy and strongly acidic/basic environments, making it particularly relevant to nuclear-waste remediation.127 Hydroxy-rich Ph-POP and Tt-POP tolerate 6 M acid/base and boiling water while maintaining gas-capture performance.138 Benzoxazine-linked Cr-TPA-4BZ-Py-POP, Pyr-BZ-Pyr POP, and OVS-DHBZ POIP exhibit thermal stability up to approximately 665 °C and long-term cycling stability in energy-storage applications.88,91

Carbon-rich POPs provide another stability advantage because of their robust C–C and CC-linked architectures. IISERP-POF6, IISERP-POF7, and IISERP-POF8 retain structural integrity after exposure to 6 N HCl, 6 N NaOH, and 80% relative humidity, while HPCOP-2 remains stable up to 320 °C and resists interference from toluene during repeated denitrogenation cycles.185,186 Pillared Tf-DHzOBn-BCMBP10 retains porosity and adsorption capacity after six months of air exposure and treatment at pH 2–12 and 120 °C.139 Thiophene/benzoxazine BBT-BZTB-Py remains stable near 400 °C and retains 97.7% capacitance after 5000 electrochemical cycles.90 Ferrocene-based FcLR-P1 combines thermal stability up to 700 °C with catalytic recyclability.255 Cyclophosphazene POP-SO3Na retains 78% adsorption capacity over repeated cycles between pH 3–8, while microwave-synthesized ionic A6 maintains 99% radioactive-species removal through four cycles.116,156 Diels–Alder DA-POP-1 and DA-POP-2 retain approximately 74% mass even at 800 °C and preserve Cr(vi)-removal performance during repeated cycles. Biomass-derived QPOP and M-Qct-B and macrocyclic Mac-DMP remain effective in complex natural-water systems, although some performance loss may occur after extended cycling.153,154 Crown-ether-based NpTOC also maintains lithium-ion capture over multiple aqueous cycles.134

Thiophene-, sulfur-, and porphyrin-containing POPs provide further evidence of operational durability. POF-1 remains stable in water, acetone, and 0.1 N NaOH, although its acid stability is comparatively lower, and releases only 0.14% of its captured iodine over seven days under natural conditions.72 PBPOP1, PBPOP2, and PBPOP3 possess chemically stable azo-bridged networks, remain thermally stable above 300 °C, and maintain adsorption under humid conditions.70 PPOP shows only a 3.1% decline in heavy-metal removal after repeated cycles.64 Phenothiazine-based PTZ-TPC-MA remains a rigid, insoluble network up to 350 °C.151 TTDP-1 provides durable capture of volatile iodine species.256 POF-2 remains stable in DMF, THF, and AcCN and in 0.1 M HCl/NaOH, retaining approximately 66% adsorption capacity after five cycles.257 Thiophene-based POP-1 maintains triiodide adsorption over pH 2–10 and retains 92% of its initial capacity after five regeneration cycles, including application in column-based water purification.160 Fluorinated PAF-DBrM frameworks show minimal weight loss below 300 °C and retain >90% SF6 adsorption capacity after six cycles.142 NRPOP-1 and NRPOP-2 remain thermally stable up to 350 °C with consistent adsorption, while NS-TMP and CTF-DB-MA provide strong recyclability and thermal robustness for CO2 and iodine capture.60,62,72,148

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that POP stability originates from the combination of strong covalent linkages, high crosslinking, rigid aromatic or heteroatom-rich architectures, and application-specific functionalization. Nevertheless, laboratory demonstrations over several cycles should not be equated with proven industrial durability. For carbon capture, wastewater treatment, radioactive-waste management, and continuous-flow remediation, future studies should evaluate hundreds to thousands of cycles, regeneration energy, functional-group retention, mechanical stability, leaching, performance in complex matrices, and life-cycle environmental impacts.

Overall, commercialization of POPs requires more than demonstrating higher adsorption capacity than existing adsorbents. Successful deployment will depend on scalable synthesis, precursor and catalyst cost, energy and solvent consumption, regeneration requirements, long-term stability, and performance under realistic conditions. POPs are unlikely to universally replace inexpensive biomass-derived activated carbon; rather, their strongest opportunities lie in applications where molecular-level control, high selectivity, multifunctionality, and long service life provide sufficient performance and economic value to justify their higher manufacturing costs.

Future directions

The future development of POPs is expected to move beyond maximizing adsorption capacity toward materials that combine environmental responsiveness, selective recognition, catalytic transformation, processability, recyclability, and long-term stability. Equally important will be the development of sustainable-by-design synthesis routes, including mechanochemical, solvent-free, aqueous, microwave-assisted, low-temperature, and metal-free approaches already demonstrated for PAF-210, gn-iPOP-Cl, PAF-DBrM, hydroxyl-rich and tannin-derived POPs, azo-linked frameworks, MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs, and related systems.40,67,68,73–76,142,249 Integration of renewable or inexpensive precursors, reduced solvent and catalyst consumption, shorter reaction times, and scalable processing should increasingly be considered alongside adsorption performance when evaluating next-generation POPs.

Emerging applications in advanced remediation

The next generation of POPs is expected to move beyond passive adsorption toward environmentally adaptive and stimuli-responsive remediation. Photoresponsive systems such as Azo-COP-1-11, AzoPPOP, and AzoPPN exploit reversible cis/trans isomerization of azo groups to regulate pore accessibility and adsorption behavior, including CO2 uptake.18,27 Similar azobenzene- and phosphonium-containing Azo-POPs undergo conformational changes that increase hydrophilicity and expose adsorption sites, thereby facilitating rapid removal of anionic dyes.97,131 Thermal responsiveness has also been demonstrated in Pyr-BZ-Pyr POP and OVS-DHBZ POIP, where thermally activated ring-opening processes influence CO2 adsorption, with an uptake of 1.89 mmol g−1 at 273 K.89,91 Temperature-dependent adsorption has additionally been reported for sulfonated PTO-1S.130,258 pH and chemically responsive systems, including POP-SO3Na, POP-1, OH-iPOP, FCOP-2, and MPA-TPA/BDA-POPs, further demonstrate how environmental conditions can be used to regulate pollutant capture and release.116,160

A related direction is molecularly selective host–guest recognition. Crown ether-containing POP-TCE-15, cyclodextrin-based βBnCD-HCPP, and pillar[5]arene-containing P5-CMP-1 provide recognition sites capable of selectively interacting with target contaminants.21 The cavitand/cage-to-framework strategy extends this concept to triazine cage polymers, Noria-based POPs, calixarene-derived CalP and CalP4, and resorcinarene-based RN4-Az-OH, allowing pore dimensions and binding environments to be tailored for toxic gases and ions.21,27 Such structural precision is reflected in the highly selective lithium-ion capture of NpTOC,134 while Keto-POP, DAB-POP, and TET-series materials have demonstrated applications in CO2 capture, iodine sequestration, and ion exchange.149,161,253 Triptycene-based TP-PPOPs-SO3H and cage-type InPOP-2′ and TAPM-TB further illustrate the potential of rigid, size-selective architectures for pharmaceutical and volatile-pollutant removal.65,146,252 Phosphite-based COPs have likewise been investigated for rapid sequestration of sulfur-containing antibiotics.184

Magnetic and composite POPs provide another important route toward practical remediation because they facilitate post-treatment recovery. Incorporation of Fe3O4 into MP-POP, Fe3O4@COFs, Fe3O4@QP-CTP, and magnetic triazine-based NCTP has enabled efficient removal of strontium ions and selective uptake of cationic dyes, while FC-POP-EDA@Fe3O4 demonstrates the potential of magnetic recovery combined with selective adsorption.18,34 Hybridization with graphene, carbon nanotubes, zeolites, and metal oxides can further improve mechanical properties, charge transport, and catalytic or antimicrobial activity; TPE-SC4A and WFMC-1 are representative examples of such multifunctional architectures.21,70,76 Likewise, iFP-POP and thiophene-containing NRPOP-1 combine antimicrobial activity against E. coli with contaminant removal, including Cr(vi).60,66 Composite aerogels such as TD-POP@CS/PEI-2 and porphyrin-derived PPOP-UOZ-1 provide additional opportunities for converting high-performing powders into mechanically useful remediation materials.57,133

The catalytic dimension of advanced POPs is particularly promising for capture-and-conversion processes. Incorporation of ionic liquids, amine groups, and basic nitrogen sites in [AeImBr]83%-TAPT-COF, [Emim]BF4@Zn-S-COF, and iCMP systems increases local CO2 concentration and facilitates its conversion into value-added products.15,19,27 Metal-containing systems such as Ir/AP-POP, POMP-NHC-Ir, and Pd/MPA-TPA POP have been explored for catalytic transformations leading to products such as formic acid and DMF, while porphyrinic systems provide additional catalytic platforms including HUST-1-Co and Co-CMP.15,27 Pd@Tp_PPh3 further demonstrates the possibility of performing Suzuki–Miyaura coupling within porous frameworks.147 At the same time, functional-group engineering in PPN-6, PP1-2-TAEA, hydroxy-rich Ph/Tt-POP, and nitrogen-rich PYTP, DAT-1, and TAPT-Tr-POP can improve both adsorption selectivity and catalytic functionality.98,104,138,259

Future POPs are also likely to employ hierarchical and multi-stimuli-responsive architectures. pH-responsive GA-azo-DADPM and ion-framed Pyd-PPOPs-Br, the latter reaching a gold uptake of 1208.50 mg g−1, demonstrate how environmental responsiveness can be coupled with selective contaminant recovery.120,157 AO-POPs-FN-3, IPM-403, and HD-IPOP illustrate multi-stimuli-responsive systems for radionuclide sequestration and sensing.56,105,113,159 Hierarchical structures such as imine-linked MPA-TPA POP, aminal-linked DAP-BDA POP, macrocyclic T-E-CDP, and fluorinated PAF-DBrM can improve mass transfer and therefore accelerate gas-separation processes.14,21,142 Other specialized systems include TMTA-TP for mercury capture, MPOP-4 and PTZ-TPC-MA for iodine binding, TP-iPOP-PPh3 and Mac-DMP for gold recovery, and CTF-DB-MA for sensing applications.62,128,150,151,153 The stability and selectivity required for such advanced applications are further demonstrated by OH-iPOP, CTT-TPC, TpPa-1, In-Mel-HCP, 2PhIn-POP, CBPOP, CNPOP, CAPOP, and CTN-POPs.67,114,144,260–262

Importantly, these advanced functions should be developed alongside green and scalable synthesis. Solvent-free, aqueous, mechanochemical, microwave-assisted, and low-temperature routes discussed in Section 8 provide promising foundations for producing responsive, hierarchical, and hybrid POPs without simply increasing synthesis complexity. Thus, future design should not focus solely on maximizing adsorption capacity; it should simultaneously consider synthesis energy, solvent consumption, precursor availability, regeneration, recyclability, and end-of-life management.

Integration of POPs with hybrid technologies

Integration of POPs with complementary materials represents an important strategy for overcoming limitations associated with powder handling, mechanical strength, mass transfer, and recovery.255 Combining POPs with MOFs, POMs, polymer matrices, fibrous substrates, and stimuli-responsive components can introduce properties that are difficult to obtain from a single framework, including improved processability, mechanical strength, selectivity, and photocatalytic activity.263,264

One particularly promising direction is the development of POP/MOF-based membranes and mixed-matrix materials. Although MOFs provide ordered pores and high separation performance, their incorporation into processable separation layers can be challenging. Hybrid systems such as PI-UiO/GO-1 (hyper-crosslinked polyimide with UiO together with graphene oxide) have therefore been investigated, with CO2/N2 selectivity reaching 64.71.265,266 Similarly, polymer-supported porous layers such as ZIF-8/PSS demonstrate improved mechanical integrity while maintaining high water flux and methylene-blue rejection.266 These examples highlight an important future objective: combining the molecular selectivity of porous crystalline materials with the processability of polymeric matrices.

Electrospinning offers another route for translating POP powders into practically deployable forms. POP-functionalized fibrous materials (POP-FMs), including hydrolyzed PIM-1 fibers, can provide high accessible surface area together with improved mechanical handling and have demonstrated adsorption capacities of 157 mg g−1 for MB as well as efficient VOC capture.263 Hybrid functional porous materials (HFPMs) provide a complementary approach for selective contaminant removal. For example, IPcomp-3 incorporates nanoscale metal–organic polyhedra within an imine COF to form highly selective “nanotraps” capable of reducing arsenate concentrations in groundwater below WHO permissible limits.267,268

Stimuli-responsive hybrids are particularly attractive for capture–release and regeneration cycles. Diarylethene-loaded PAF (DArE@PAF-1) enables light-controlled CO2 capture and release, whereas P(Azo-N-EO2MA) responds simultaneously to light, temperature, and pH, providing control over solubility and cyclic gas adsorption.269 Interpenetrated polymer networks and gel-based materials provide another means of converting porous powders into practical treatment media. CMP-1 aerogels have been investigated for PM2.5 filtration, while PVA/alginate/bentonite hydrogel beads have been applied to organic dye removal.270

The integration of POPs into mixed-matrix membranes and interpenetrating networks can further address the mechanical limitations of conventional porous powders.266 Self-cleaning membranes incorporating Cu-POM nanoclusters within MIL-101 and PVDF/polydopamine matrices have achieved approximately 99% Congo red rejection while enabling photocatalytic degradation of the dye under UV irradiation, thereby helping to mitigate membrane fouling.271 Similarly, electrospun POP-FMs combine the porosity of CMPs and HCPs with the mechanical stability of polymeric fibers, creating promising formats for air purification and aqueous micropollutant removal.263 HFPM systems such as IPcomp-3 additionally demonstrate that incorporation of nanoscale amino-functionalized metal–organic polyhedra into imine COFs can produce selective traps for oxoanions including AsO43− and radioactive 99TcO4−.267

Another emerging concept is adsorption-assisted photocatalysis, in which adsorption concentrates pollutants close to catalytic sites before their degradation. Ferrocenyl Lawesson's reagent metal–organic porous polymers (FcLR-MOPPs) can degrade both cationic MB and anionic MO under visible light.255 Their conjugated porous structures facilitate charge transport and reactive oxygen species generation, thereby coupling pollutant concentration with degradation. POM-CMPs similarly exploit the synergistic combination of polyoxometalates and CMPs for photochemical mineralization of dyes such as RhB.

Finally, hybridization is increasingly being linked with circular-economy principles. Bio-hybrid adsorbents based on renewable matrices combined with MOFs or metal nanoparticles can be fabricated through approaches such as directional freezing, producing porous structures with improved mechanical integrity and affinity toward toxic metals such as Pb and Cd.272 Such systems are particularly relevant to future remediation because they offer a route to combine the performance of synthetic POP-type materials with renewable feedstocks. This direction also complements the green synthesis strategies discussed previously and may help reduce the environmental footprint associated with POP production.

Development of multifunctional POPs

A major future direction is the development of multifunctional POPs capable of simultaneously capturing, sensing, converting, or degrading pollutants rather than functioning solely as passive adsorbents. Incorporation of amine, thiol, imide, ionic, and catalytic functionalities can create materials with multiple operating modes, including selective gas separation, heterogeneous catalysis, optical sensing, and pollutant capture. Bio-POPs derived from lignin and cellulose, for example, combine heavy-metal adsorption with heterogeneous catalytic functionality.273 Porphyrin-containing POPs prepared through the Alder–Longo reaction can simultaneously promote Knoevenagel condensation and selective oxidation reactions, illustrating how catalytic functions can be integrated into a single porous platform.274

The conversion of POPs into fibrous structures through electrospinning provides another level of multifunctionality by adding mechanical strength and hierarchical porosity to the intrinsic adsorption properties of POPs.263 Similarly, imidazolium ionic-liquid-containing frameworks can combine CO2 capture with catalytic conversion of CO2 and epoxides into value-added products.275 These approaches shift the focus from simple pollutant sequestration toward capture-and-conversion, which may reduce the need for energy-intensive regeneration.

Multifunctionality is also increasingly associated with simultaneous remediation and sensing. Nitrogen-containing POP-En combines radioactive iodine capture with fluorescence-based Fe-ion sensing.276 Chiral Salen-type SS/RR-POP1 integrates chiroptical behavior with multistimuli-responsive luminescence, allowing changes in metal concentration, mechanical stress, and acidic conditions to be detected through optical responses.277 GA-azo-DADPM combines organic-dye adsorption with antimicrobial activity,66 while Py-POP couples selective pollutant adsorption with visible-light photocatalytic degradation.278

Other systems demonstrate how molecular recognition can be directly coupled with catalytic activity. KI@CE-POPs use crown-ether sites for selective CO2 capture while simultaneously promoting heterogeneous catalytic reactions without an additional catalyst.279 Phosphate-containing SBF-POP combines iodine-vapor capture with selective uranium extraction.280 BeTz-COP extends multifunctionality beyond environmental remediation by combining gas and pollutant capture with pH-responsive drug-delivery behavior.281 These examples suggest that future POPs may evolve toward platform materials with several coordinated functions, although such complexity should be balanced against synthesis cost, regeneration requirements, stability, and environmental safety.

Machine learning and computational design of POPs

Machine learning (ML) and computational modelling are emerging as important tools for reducing the trial-and-error nature of POP discovery. The structural diversity, tunable porosity, and large chemical space of POPs, COFs, and CTFs make purely experimental screening inefficient; computational approaches can therefore identify promising structures before synthesis.19,282 ML-based materials genomics can establish relationships between structural descriptors and performance for applications ranging from gas separation to energy storage.283 The emerging “digital-intellectual” approach combines large datasets, computational prediction, and human expertise to shorten the time required to identify promising materials and optimize their properties.284

Early studies demonstrate the scale of this opportunity. Artificial neural networks and response-surface methodologies have been used to correlate parameters such as BET surface area and total pore volume with CO2 adsorption, obtaining correlation coefficients of approximately 0.80.285 More extensive automated ML screening predicted methane-storage performance across 403 959 COF structures with approximately 99% prediction accuracy and a testing R2 of 0.992, substantially reducing the experimental design space.286 ML-assisted prediction has also been applied to PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) adsorption. Ensemble models trained using generated adsorption-energy datasets achieved R2 values approaching 0.999 with RMSE below 1 kJ mol−1 for adsorption energies spanning approximately −705.81 to −405.76 kJ mol−1.287 Likewise, Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Networks (CGCNNs) screened 64 959 potential structures for PFBA adsorption selectivity, achieving a testing R2 of 0.905 while reducing computational demand from approximately 3.2 million CPU hours to less than 20 GPU hours.288

Computational screening is also beginning to guide target-specific POP design. ML-guided screening for CO2 capture from humid flue gas identified tetraphenylporphyrin as a promising building block, leading to functionalized COFs with CO2 uptake of 4.4 mmol g−1 and CO2/N2 selectivity of 277.6.289 For biogas purification, computational screening indicated that COFs with a large cavity diameter of approximately 12.5 Å and a void fraction near 0.65 could provide favorable simultaneous sulfur- and carbon-containing gas removal.290 High-throughput screening for indoor formaldehyde removal identified COF-LZU8 as a promising candidate, with a reported selectivity of 39 000 and an adsorbent performance factor of 29 947.291

ML can further be combined with optimization algorithms to identify balanced combinations of structural and adsorption properties. Integrated gradient-boosting and genetic-algorithm approaches have identified structures with a surface area of approximately 1615.8 m2 g−1 and porosity of 0.957, corresponding to a predicted CO2 uptake of 4.5 mmol g−1 at 273 K.292 Experimental work on amorphous POPs has similarly demonstrated that operating variables such as temperature and pressure can account for approximately 53% of uptake variation, while optimized models achieved a predictive R2 of 0.995.293,294

Despite these advances, data quality remains a major limitation. ML predictions are only as reliable as the datasets used for training, and differences in synthesis conditions, activation procedures, adsorption protocols, humidity, particle size, and reporting practices can introduce substantial uncertainty. Therefore, future computational studies should prioritize standardized datasets, experimentally validated predictions, uncertainty quantification, and closed-loop integration between prediction and synthesis. Combining ML with the green and scalable synthesis approaches discussed in above sections could ultimately enable a more rational workflow in which promising POPs are computationally screened, synthesized using low-energy and low-solvent methods, experimentally validated under realistic conditions, and subsequently optimized through iterative data-driven design.19,284–288

Overall, the future of POP-based environmental remediation is unlikely to depend on adsorption capacity alone. The most promising materials will need to integrate selectivity, responsiveness, catalytic activity, processability, regeneration, long-term stability, low-cost synthesis, and environmental compatibility. The combination of advanced molecular design, hybrid architectures, multifunctional operation, green synthesis, and computational screening therefore provides a more realistic pathway toward translating POPs from high-performing laboratory materials into practical remediation technologies.

Conclusion

POPs have emerged as versatile platforms for environmental remediation owing to their permanent porosity, chemical robustness, tunable pore structures, and readily functionalizable surfaces, enabling applications ranging from heavy-metal, dye, pharmaceutical, volatile organic compound, and radioactive-ion removal to gas purification and pollutant degradation through catalytic and photocatalytic pathways. Nevertheless, their transition from laboratory demonstrations to practical technologies remains constrained by the scalability and sustainability of synthesis, production cost, regeneration requirements, and uncertain performance in complex environmental matrices. Although recent solvent-free, aqueous, mechanochemical, microwave-assisted, catalyst-free, and biomass-derived approaches offer promising routes toward greener and more scalable production, POPs should not be considered inherently superior to low-cost activated carbon; rather, their practical advantage must be demonstrated through application-specific benefits such as higher selectivity, faster kinetics, improved regeneration, multifunctionality, or effective treatment of contaminants that are difficult to remove using conventional sorbents. Furthermore, the frequently reported high adsorption capacities under single-component laboratory conditions need to be complemented by systematic evaluation in real wastewater, natural waters, humid gas streams, and contaminated soils, together with continuous-flow experiments, long-term cycling, regeneration efficiency, fouling resistance, and assessment of possible secondary contamination. Future development should therefore shift from simply maximizing surface area and equilibrium capacity toward application-oriented designs incorporating hierarchical porosity, precisely controlled functional groups, stimulus responsiveness, magnetic recovery, membrane integration, and adsorption–catalysis coupling. Hybrid POP systems and multifunctional architectures can further improve processability, pollutant degradation, sensing, and regeneration, while machine learning and computational screening can accelerate the identification of promising structures and strengthen structure–property relationships when supported by reliable experimental datasets. Ultimately, the successful implementation of POPs will depend on simultaneously addressing selectivity, kinetics, stability, processability, regeneration, environmental sustainability, and economic competitiveness. Integrating green and scalable synthesis with realistic performance testing, techno-economic and life-cycle assessment, continuous-flow processing, and data-driven molecular design could enable POPs to progress from high-performance laboratory materials toward robust, multifunctional, and economically viable platforms for next-generation environmental remediation.
