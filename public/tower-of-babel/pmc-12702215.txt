Interactive effects of folate and methylmalonic acid on cardiovascular disease mortality: Epidemiological and experimental evidence
Authors: Xinyu Zhang, Chunping Wang, Jinfeng Wang, Xin Liu, Shengnan Zhao, Tuanwei Wang, Peng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryFolate shows paradoxical associations with cardiovascular disease (CVD), and its interaction with methylmalonic acid (MMA) may contribute to this risk. We examined the independent and interactive effects of MMA on CVD mortality using data from 22,487 NHANES participants (1999–2014). Over 12.1 years of follow-up, 1,510 CVD deaths occurred. Restricted cubic splines showed a U-shaped association between folate and CVD mortality and a linear association for MMA. Both low and high folate were linked to was linked to increased mortality only when MMA exceeded 182 nmol/L, with significant additive interaction. In vitro, MMA amplified folate-induced cytotoxicity in H9c2 cardiomyocytes, reducing viability to 49%, while transcriptomic analysis identified 53 inflammation-related genes under combined exposure. These findings provide both epidemiological and mechanistic evidence that elevated folate and MMA independently and synergistically worsen cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting the importance of considering functional B-vitamin status in risk assessment and public health strategies.

Introduction

Folate has been consistently implicated in cardiovascular health due to its essential role in one-carbon metabolism.1,2 Epidemiological studies suggest that higher folate status may reduce the risk of CVD by lowering homocysteine (HCY) levels.3,4 However, this protective effect has not been consistently confirmed in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Several large RCTs using B-vitamin supplementation to reduce HCY levels have failed to show significant reductions in CVD incidence or mortality.5,6,7,8,9 In addition, increasing evidence indicated a potential J- or U-shaped association between folate levels and CVD, where both low and excessive folate may be detrimental.1,10,11,12,13 These findings highlight the importance of maintaining optimal folate levels, while raising concerns that excessive folate intake may constitute a metabolic risk factor. However, the biological mechanisms underlying these paradoxical associations remain unclear.

Notably, folate does not act in isolation but functions within a complex network of metabolic interactions to maintain one-carbon homeostasis.14 Most existing studies have evaluated folate as an independent factor in relation to CVD, without considering its interactions with other metabolic components.13,15,16 Disruption of this metabolic balance, particularly in the presence of high folate, may contribute to increased CVD risk.14 Methylmalonic acid (MMA) is a sensitive biomarker of functional vitamin B12 deficiency.17 Elevated MMA is an independent risk factor for CVD incidence and mortality.18 Excessive folate intake may mask B12 deficiency by correcting clinical features such as anemia, while leaving the underlying metabolic dysfunction unresolved, allowing MMA to accumulate undetected.19 This mismatch between normal clinical features and underlying metabolic dysfunction may give rise to an overlooked high CVD risk metabolic phenotype with high folate, normal vitamin B12, and HCY levels but elevated MMA. This metabolic phenotype raises the possibility that an interaction between folate and MMA may contribute to the increased CVD risk. However, the potential interaction and contribution to CVD risk remain unexplored.

In this study, a nationally representative sample from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was used to explore the independent and interactive associations of circulating folate and MMA levels with CVD mortality risk. We further used cell-based models to preliminarily verify the interaction and potential mechanism.

Independent associations of red blood cell folate and methylmalonic acid on cardiovascular disease mortality risk

CVD survival was significantly lower in both the higher RBC folate and higher MMA groups compared to their respective lower groups (p < 0.001 for both; Figure S2). In multivariable-adjusted models, each unit increase in RBC folate was associated with a 20% higher risk of CVD mortality (aHRs 1.20, 95% CI: 1.06–1.36, p = 0.003). Compared to individuals with RBC folate ≤1.71 μmol/L, those in the higher group had consistently elevated risk across models, with a fully aHRs of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.15–1.54, p < 0.001). Similarly, each unit increase in MMA was associated with a 39% higher risk of CVD mortality (aHRs 1.39, 95% CI: 1.30–1.49, p < 0.001), with robust associations across all adjustment models (final model aHRs = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.36–1.93, p < 0.001). Interaction effect of red blood cell folate and methylmalonic acid on cardiovascular disease mortality risk

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that participants with elevated levels of both RBC folate and MMA had a significantly lowest survival rate compared to those with lower levels of both RBC folate and MMA (p < 0.001) (Figure S2C). The combined influences of the RBC folate and MMA on CVD mortality were further verified by Cox regression analysis. In the lower MMA group, participants with higher RBC folate levels exhibited a 2.28-fold increased risk of CVD mortality in model 1 (95% CI: 1.92–2.70; p < 0.001). However, this association was non-significant in models 2 and 3 (Model 2: aHRs 1.11 [95% CI 0.94–1.30], p = 0.221; Model 3: aHRs 1.11 [95% CI 0.94–1.31], p = 0.210). In higher MMA group, participants with lower RBC folate was associated with increased risk of CVD mortality (Model 1: aHRs 3.41 [95% CI 2.89–4.02], p < 0.001; Model 2: aHRs 1.46 [95% CI 1.21–1.75], p < 0.001; Model 3: aHRs 1.43 [95% CI 1.19–1.71], p < 0.001); participants with higher RBC folate exhibited the highest risk of CVD mortality (Model 1: aHRs 10.6 [95% CI 8.18–13.7], p < 0.001; Model 2: aHRs 2.37 [95% CI 1.84–3.04], p < 0.001; Model 3: aHRs 2.36 [95% CI 1.85–3.00], p < 0.001) (Figure S2C). An additive interaction between RBC folate and MMA was observed for CVD mortality (RERI = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.06–1.58), indicating 82% excess risk when both exposures were present compared to no interaction, with 35% of the increased risk attributed to their joint effect (Figure 2A). Sensitivity analyses

Weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed that the survival probability was significantly higher in the lower RBC folate or MMA group compared to the higher RBC folate or MMA group, irrespective of whether the follow-up duration was within 10 years or extended beyond 10 years (Figures S2D and S2E). Multiplicative interaction analysis also confirmed a significant interaction between RBC folate and MMA (Figure S3). Among individuals with higher MMA, the aHRs for CVD mortality associated with high RBC folate were 1.61 (95% CI: 1.29–1.99), compared to 1.12 (95% CI: 0.94–1.33) in those with lower MMA levels (Figure S3). After excluding individuals who passed away during the first year of follow-up, a robust association between RBC folate, MMA, and CVD mortality was consistently identified (Figure S4). A significant interaction between RBC folate and MMA was consistently observed in both male and female populations (Figure S5). Spline models showed minimal overlap in the interaction lines, highlighting a significant effect on CVD mortality in different gender populations (all P for interaction <0.001) (Figure S6).

In vitro assays

Effects of folic acid and methylmalonic acid on the viability of H9c2 cells

We assessed the viability of H9c2 cells to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of FA and MMA. The results demonstrated that both FA and MMA led to reduced cell proliferation. FA treatment at 0.1 mM exhibited no cytotoxicity in H9c2 cells. Concentrations of 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 mM showed no significant cytotoxic effects, maintaining cell viability at 98.16%, 93.77%, and 88.60%, respectively (p > 0.05). In contrast, higher FA doses (1.6, 3.2, and 6.4 mM) significantly reduced viability to 79.29%, 64.48%, and 36.61% (p < 0.01) (Figure 3A). MMA treatment at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mM showed no significant cytotoxicity in H9c2 cells, with survival rates maintained at 98.10%, 96.17%, 92.44%, and 90.46% (p > 0.05). In contrast, higher concentrations (8, 16, and 32 mM) significantly reduced viability to 79.78%, 40.88%, and 16.20% (p < 0.001), demonstrating significant cytotoxic effects (Figure 3B). The FA&MMA combination (FM) is defined as FM. FM2 treatment showed no significant effect (92.00% viability, p > 0.05), whereas higher combinations (FM3-FM6) exhibited progressively severe cytotoxicity, reducing cell viability to 83.64% (FM3), 49.26% (FM4), 29.51% (FM5), and 9.31% (FM6) (p < 0.001) (Figures 3C and 3D). Accordingly, the synergistic FM4 combination (1.6 mM FA+8 mM MMA) was selected for subsequent mechanistic studies. Furthermore, the CI values were derived from CCK-8 assay data of assessing FA, MMA, and their combination treatments on H9c2 cells. As shown in Figures 3E–3H, when FA and MMA were used in combination, the CI values varied with different dose combinations. The CI value of FM6 (6.4 mM FA+32 mM MMA) was the lowest, followed by that of FM4 (1.6 mM FA+8 mM MMA). CompuSyn analysis confirmed significant synergy in the FM4 combination (CI < 1), establishing it as the optimal candidate for subsequent experiments. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of the interacting effects of folate and MMA on CVD mortality by combined epidemiological and experimental evidence. Notably, a significant additive synergistic effect was observed between folate and MMA on CVD mortality. Sensitivity analyses consistently underscored the robustness of our finding. Experimental data further confirmed the toxic effects of the interaction between folate and MMA on cardiomyocytes and provided preliminary mechanistic insights.

Our study found that both elevated folate and elevated MMA were associated with increased CVD mortality. The effect of folate on CVD remains controversial. Two meta-analyses of RCTs reported either a significant inverse association or no beneficial effect between dietary folate intake and CVD mortality risk.15,21 However, these studies are typically conducted in populations with inherently low baseline folate levels, such as diabetic or elderly individuals, where folate supplementation tends to show more protective effects.14,22 In the general population with adequate folate levels, excessive supplementation may cause circulating folate accumulation.13,23 This can disrupt the metabolic balance between folate and vitamin B12, impairing their normal utilization and potentially leading to inhibited DNA synthesis and aberrant DNA methylation.24,25 In our study, RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped association between RBC folate levels and CVD mortality, demonstrating increased risk at both low and high RBC folate levels, which corroborates previous epidemiological findings.1,11,26 Our cell-based experiments demonstrated that folate concentrations exceeding 1.6 mM significantly increased cellular apoptosis rates. These observations demonstrate that maintaining erythrocyte folate concentrations within optimal ranges, as opposed to unmonitored supplementation, may reduce CVD mortality in populations. In addition, we observed a significant linear association between MMA and CVD mortality, consistent with previous studies.10 The underlying mechanism may involve MMA-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis via oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytotoxicity.27,28 These findings indicated that both higher RBC folate and MMA may have a distinct and pivotal function in the development and progression of CVD.

This study also identified a significant interaction between folate and MMA on CVD mortality risk at both additive and multiplicative scales. Previous research has confirmed that MMA levels tend to rise more significantly in individuals with high folate status and concurrent vitamin B12 deficiency.14 Our findings further reveal a differential association based on MMA status: at MMA levels ≤182 nmol/L, elevated folate concentrations show no correlation with CVD mortality risk. Conversely, when MMA >182 nmol/L, both low and high folate levels demonstrate significant associations with increased CVD mortality risk. Similar results were observed in vitro experiments. CCK-8 assays and CompuSyn analysis revealed that at MMA ≥8 mM, the synergistic interaction between folate and MMA markedly suppressed cell viability. These findings demonstrate that MMA may play a pivotal role in folate-induced CVD risk, partially elucidating the mechanism by which elevated folate levels contribute to cardiovascular pathogenesis. Notably, individuals with elevated folate levels frequently present with clinically normal vitamin B12 and HCY levels—a deceptive metabolic profile resulting from folate’s masking effect on underlying dysfunction.19 This concealed metabolic disturbance drives the accumulation of MMA, defining a distinct subgroup with high CVD risk: high folate, high MMA, and ostensibly normal clinical indices. Therefore, the metabolic abnormalities, pathogenic mechanisms, and potential regulatory pathways in this population require systematic investigation.

Regarding mechanism research, previous studies have reported that a high folate state can induce cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells,29 and the concomitant increase in IFN-γ secretion could contribute to elevated CVD mortality risk. Moreover, high MMA exposure compromised cell survival and exacerbated inflammatory signaling, concomitant with suppressed nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) activity and sustained nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation.30 Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that combined FA and MMA treatment significantly regulated 53 differentially expressed genes, which were notably enriched in multiple inflammation-related signaling pathways, including the IL-17, HIF-1, and TNF pathway. Our findings align partially with those of prior studies. These results suggest that the FA and MMA may promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis by activating inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain to be fully elucidated.

Our study has four significant strengths. First, the study benefits from the utilization of nationally representative data, where the large sample size allowed robust analyses with sufficient statistical power. Second, both epidemiological and experimental analyses revealed that the combined effect of high folate and high MMA significantly increased CVD mortality. Third, transcriptome analysis uncovered the activation of key inflammatory pathways, highlighting potential targets for future research. Fourth, we suggest that individuals exhibiting elevated folate and MMA levels alongside normal clinical markers represent a high-value target population for precision prevention.

In conclusion, findings from both population-based and experimental studies demonstrate that elevated folate and MMA levels independently increase the risk of CVD mortality. Their interaction synergistically aggravates adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Therefore, focusing solely on the safety of folate supplementation is far from sufficient; it is also essential to assess individuals' vitamin B12 status and MMA levels. In countries where folate fortification is implemented, MMA levels should be closely monitored, and vitamin B12 deficiency risks rigorously assessed. Additionally, the underlying mechanisms of the combined effects of folate and MMA warrant further in-depth investigation.

Limitations of the study

The study also has several limitations. First, both folate and MMA levels were measured cross-sectionally; no dynamic changes or long-term effects were obtained. Second, the conclusions are based on U.S. population data, requiring careful consideration regarding generalizability to other racial backgrounds and communities. Third, despite adjustment for numerous confounders, residual confounding remains possible. Fourth, the specific mechanisms behind the interaction between folate and MMA need to be explored. Fifth, the experimental validation employs the H9c2 cell line, which is of rat embryonic cardiac origin. While this model is widely used for preliminary cardiotoxicity and metabolic studies, it does not fully replicate the phenotype of human cardiomyocytes. In the future, it will be necessary to use human cell models for verification.

Experimental model and study participant details

Observational analyses

This study analyzed data from five cycles (1999–2014) of the NHANES, the National Death Index (NDI) database, and the Food Patterns Equivalents Database (FPED) of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA). Conducted biennially by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), NHANES is approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved with an exemption from the Ethics Committee of Shandong Second Medical University. We confirm that this study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

In vitro assay

The H9c2 cardiomyocyte cell line, originally derived from embryonic BD1X rat heart tissue, was obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Catalog No. GNR 5). This cell line is widely used as a model for studying CVD.31,32 The utility of the H9c2 cell model lies in its preservation of key pathways—including energy metabolism, oxidative stress response, and apoptosis—which are crucial for investigating cardiovascular disease mechanisms.33,34,35,36

Method details

Observational analyses

Study population

The study excluded participants under 20 years of age (n = 24,276), those with incomplete survival data (n = 44), pregnant women (n = 855), and individuals lacking complete red blood cell (RBC) folate (n = 2,976) and MMA measurements (n = 419). The final analysis comprised 22,487 participants, with a median follow-up of 12.1 years (Figure S1). Table S1 summarizes the number and percentage of missing values for covariates prior to imputation.

Exposure measurement

RBC folate levels were measured using the Bio-Rad Laboratories Quantaphase II radioimmunoassay from 1999 to 2006 and with a microbiological assay (MA) from 2007 to 2014.37 To ensure comparability between the two periods, the RBC folate values obtained from 1999 to 2006 were converted to their equivalents for the 2007–2014 period using fractional polynomial regression.38 For MMA analysis, plasma or serum samples were collected via venipuncture. MMA levels were quantified using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS) in the 1999–2004 surveys, whereas liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was employed for the 2011–2014 surveys.39

The threshold values of RBC folate and MMA with the strongest correlation to survival outcomes were identified using the 'maxstat' package for maximally selected rank statistics.40 The optimal cutoffs for predicting survival were determined to be 1.71 μmol/L for RBC folate and 182 nmol/L for MMA.

Ascertainment of mortality

The NCHS linked participant records with death certificate data from the NDI.41 Mortality follow-up data was utilized up to 31 December 2019.42 The underlying causes of CVD-related deaths were classified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51 and I60-I69).43

Definition of covariates

Potential confounding variables analyzed in this research encompass, age (years), gender (male/female), race (Mexican American/other Hispanic/non-Hispanic White/non-Hispanic Black/other), education (less than high school graduate/high school graduate or GED/some college/college graduate or above), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), smoking status (never smoker/former smoker/current smoker) [35], alcohol intake (never drinker/light drinker/moderate drinking/heavy drinking) [36], physical activity (metabolic equivalent (MET) scores, METs-min/week), glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) (%), fasting glucose (mg/dL), healthy eating index 2015 (HEI-2015) score (The HEI-2015 was calculated using a two-day average of total nutrient intakes from the FPED files), systolic blood pressure (SBP) (mm Hg), fasting time (hours), serum vitamin B12 (pmol/L), dietary intake of vitamin B12 per day (μg).

In vitro assays

Cell culture

Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator maintained at 37°C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and counted using trypan blue and hemocytometer (Count star, #C0010101, Shanghai, China) for use in the study.

Cell viability

The experimental groups comprised a blank group (serum-free DMEM), a control group (treated with cell medium only), folic acid (FA) groups (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, and 6.4 mM/L), MMA groups (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 mM/L), and FA&MMA combination groups (0.4 mM & 2 mM, 0.8 mM & 4 mM, 1.6 mM & 8 mM, 3.2 mM & 16 mM, and 6.4 mM & 32 mM, as determined by preliminary experiments). Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were plated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 5×103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Following 24-h treatment with FA or MMA at various concentrations, blank and control groups received no additional treatment. 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland).

Transcriptome analysis of FA and MMA treated H9c2 cells

Based on cytotoxicity assessment of FA, MMA, and their combination, we selected concentrations of FA (1.6 mM), MMA (8 mM), and their combination (1.6 mM FA + 8 mM MMA) that elicited 20–50% cell viability reduction for subsequent experiments. Triplicate biological replicates were established for each treatment group. Total RNA was isolated from H9c2 cells across four experimental groups (control, FA, MMA, and FA&MMA) using TRIzol reagent, generating twelve total samples for analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Observational analyses

All analyses in this study considered sample weights, stratification, and clustering. Baseline characteristics were described in terms of the survey-weighted means for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables.44 To address the potential impact of covariates with missing values on survival outcomes, the 'mice' package was utilized to impute missing values using values from chained equation analysis (multiple imputation).

Weighted Kaplan-Meier curves for survival status were plotted across RBC folate and MMA strata, with the aid of the log rank test. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were assessed by survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards (CPH) models for the associations between RBC folate (both continuous and by cutoff value), MMA (both continuous and by cutoff value), their interaction and CVD mortality. Three models adjusted for covariates were assessed. Model 1 was the unadjusted model without covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, PIR, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake and physical activity. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for HbA1c, fasting glucose, HEI-2015 score, SBP, fasting time, serum vitamin B12, and dietary intake of vitamin B12. Model for MMA was further adjusted for RBC folate (continuous, only for MMA in model 3). RCS based on multivariable-adjusted weighted Cox regression were employed to explore potential non-linear relationship between RBC folate or MMA and CVD mortality. Due to the skewed distribution and wide ranges, log2-transformed RBC folate and MMA were used in the correlation analyses.

Additive interaction method was conducted to explore the biological interaction between RBC folate and MMA with fully adjusted weighted Cox models. Three interaction measures were calculated: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI).45,46 The RERI value is defined as the relative excess risk when two exposure factors exist simultaneously, compared to when there is no interaction. The AP value is attributable proportion used to evaluate the interaction between two exposure factors. The SI value is a synergy index employed for the evaluation of the interaction between two exposure factors.46 In this study, biological interactions were defined by statistically significant thresholds: RERI >0, AP > 0, or SI > 1.46 Spline models were used to visualize the interaction, with the RBC folate treated as a continuous variable.

To validate the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses. First, we conducted an analysis of the associations of RBC folate and MMA with CVD mortality during the two included durations (within 10 years and >10 years) to determine whether the relationship varied over time. Second, multiplicative interaction analysis was used to verify the interaction between RBC folate and MMA. Third, we excluded patients who died within 1 year to mitigate potential reverse causation bias. Fourth, the interaction effect of RBC folate and MMA on CVD mortality was analyzed in different gender populations. Fifth, interaction analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of RBC folate (as a continuous variable) and MMA on CVD mortality across different gender populations, with spline models used to visualize the interaction.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0), with a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 being considered statistically significant.

In vitro assays

Synergistic effects of the combination of FA and MMA were explored using a CCK-8 assay, the Chou-Talalay method, and CompuSyn software (version 1.0).47 Combination index (CI) values were calculated using CompuSyn based on the concentrations and inhibitory rates of FA, MMA, and their combination (FA&MMA).47 CI is a rigorous quantitative metric for assessing drug interaction effects, derived from the median-effect principle of mass-action kinetics. According to the CI, CI < 1, CI = 1, and CI > 1 indicates synergism, additive effect, and antagonism, respectively.48

Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times and the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was employed to analyze the data across multiple groups. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001).

RNA sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Shanghai, China). A likelihood ratio test expressed cell percentage changes in mean expression levels and cellular detection rates revealed cluster-specific differentially expressed genes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the find markers function of the R package “Seurat”. Kyoto encyclo-pedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed in R software (version 4.5.0) to identify enriched pathways. Adjusted p value <0.05 and |log2foldchange| > 1.0 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression.
