Graphene-Inspired Wafer-Scale Ultrathin Gold Films
Authors: Mikhail
S Mironov, Dmitry I Yakubovsky, Georgy A Ermolaev, Igor A Khramtsov, Roman V Kirtaev, Aleksandr S Slavich, Gleb I Tselikov, Andrey A Vyshnevyy, Aleksey V Arsenin, Valentyn S Volkov, Kostya S Novoselov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the trajectory toward the graphene era continues, there is a compelling need to harness 2D technology further for the transformation of three-dimensional (3D) materials production and applications. Here, we resolve this challenge for one of the most widely utilized 3D materials in modern electronics—gold—using graphene-inspired fabrication technology that allows us to develop a multistep production method of ultrathin gold films. Such films demonstrate continuous morphology, low sheet resistance (10 Ω/sq), and high transparency (80%), offering opportunities in a variety of technological and scientific sectors. To this end, we demonstrate smart contact lenses and thermal camouflage based on ultrathin gold. Technologically, the record-breaking characteristics of ultrathin gold films open new horizons for flexible and transparent electrodes for photonics and optoelectronics. Most importantly, the demonstration of transferable wafer-scale ultrathin gold changes the paradigm of the field of 2D crystals and dramatically expands the range of available quasi-2D materials.Keywords: graphene, ultrathin gold films, ellipsometry, smart contact lens, flexible electrodes

The rise of graphene (Gr) and other two-dimensional (2D) materials has led to countless novel, exciting physical phenomena, applications, and technologies.1−3 Indeed, the fabrication of high-quality 2D samples and cutting-edge devices requires only standard laboratory facilities.4−7 It inspired tremendous progress in both science and industry, with a huge commercial potential in transparent electrodes, biomedical sensors, and low-cost electronic and photonic devices.8−10 Hence, the next step toward the graphene era is leveraging 2D technology to revolutionize the production and applications of three-dimensional (3D) materials.

In this regard, the creation of ultrathin metals via adopted 2D methods looks to be a promising direction. Ultrathin metal films with thicknesses below 10 nm are considered a very practical and promising platform for transparent electrodes in wearable devices and transparent flexible displays.11−15 Moreover, it is the dream of atomically thin metallic electronics that led to the discovery of graphene.16 To date, the only problem for a successful implementation of ultrathin metal films is a lack of reliable, cost-effective, easy-to-use and wafer-scale techniques for their fabrication because metals usually follow 3D (island or Volmer–Weber) growth mode;17,18 that is, islands grow on the substrate and then connect with each other. As a result, up to the high thickness, the metal film contains numerous cracks and voids or is even incapable of conducting electric current. To obtain smooth and continuous highly conductive films, numerous efforts have been devoted toward converting a metallic 3D growth mode into 2D (layer-by-layer or Frank–van der Merwe) one via substrate functionalization that reduces the surface free energy of the metal while improving adhesion of metal atoms to the substrate.19,20 This 2D growth mode is similar to the epitaxial growth and favors the formation of layers without voids. However, this functionalization approach complicates the fabrication technology and is limited in the choice of substrates since the obtained film cannot be transferred from the functionalized substrate to another surface.21−24 Besides, a functionalization layer may introduce undesired optical losses and adversely affect the metal film electrical conductivity.21,25 Although recent work26 elegantly resolves some of these problems by chemical approach, its technology of 2D gold fabrication requires substantial improvement to meet industrial standards since obtained flakes are below 1 μm scale. By contrast, graphene technologies do not face these challenges: graphene can be grown on copper without an auxiliary layer27 and delivered onto the target substrate via electrochemical or chemical polymer-assisted transfer.28,29

By analogy with graphene, we develop a method of fabrication of atomically thin gold on copper and subsequent electrochemical transfer to any substrate. Using this technology, we synthesized gold films with thicknesses down to 3.5 nm and demonstrated their continuous structure, wafer-scale dimensions, low sheet resistance of around 10 Ω/sq, and high transparency of around 80%. These characteristics of our films are close to the “golden standard” of transparent electrodes—indium tin oxide (ITO),13 whereas our films also preserve conductivity under extreme bending. Moreover, our gold films demonstrate one of the best trade-offs between transmittance and conductivity among other synthesis approaches.

The best route to achieve metals’ 2D growth (Frank–van der Merwe growth) is to use a substrate with high bond energy with the metal of interest.19,23,30 In the case of gold, copper provides a high bond energy with Au adatoms (HAu–Cu = 228.0 kJ/mol) that exceeds even the bond energy among Au adatoms with each other (HAu–Au = 226.2 kJ/mol).19 Still, this approach suffers numerous limitations because, to date, it has been unclear how to detach ultrathin gold from the copper substrate. A similar situation was observed for graphene growth on copper, where numerous methods were developed to transfer graphene onto the desired surface (Figure 1a).31 Hence, combining metallic growth techniques with graphene technologies provides a solution for high-quality ultrathin gold film synthesis.

Ultrathin gold of graphene-inspired fabrication process. (a) Schematic view of graphene transfer technology. (b) Schematic view of the fabrication method and transfer of ultrathin gold films.

Given this framework, we designed a multistep fabrication method for ultrathin gold films. The process consists of three main stages as shown in Figure 1b: (i) physical vapor deposition of multilayer structure; (ii) film delamination from the substrate; (iii) film transfer on the target substrate. The first step begins with the electron beam evaporation deposition of a thick copper-wetting layer (Figure 1b-1) in a high vacuum chamber on several substrates (Gr/SiO2/Si, SiO2/Si, and Cu foil). The substrates were heated in a high vacuum at 250 °C, and copper was deposited immediately after the heating of a substrate. Smooth cooling in combination with high temperature at high vacuum ensures an increase in the crystallinity of copper.32 After the substrates were cooled to room temperature, a thin film of gold was deposited (Figure 1b-2). As a result, we avoid copper oxidation and achieve epitaxial growth of gold.30,33 Next, we covered our structure with poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) polymer as a protective and transfer-assisting layer (Figure 1b-3). This coating is used when transferring CVD graphene to preserve the transferred material since 2D materials always require a supporting layer. Now, our structure is ready for electrochemical delamination from the substrate (Figure 1b-4). The smooth immersion of the structure into the electrolyte solution and the applied voltage make it possible to peel off the Cu/Au/PMMA structure due to the surface tension and gas release. Undesired copper is easily dissolved in ammonium persulfate (Figure 1b-5). After copper removal, the structure is transferred to the surface of deionized water to remove residues of the reagents and reaction products. As a result, we obtain PMMA/Au film (Figure 1b-6), which is ready for transfer on any substrate (Figure 1b-7). Finally, acetone removed the PMMA protection layer (Figure 1b-8). As a result, we obtained ultrathin gold film on any substrate (Figure 1b-9). Notably, we adopted all steps (Figure 1) from graphene transfer technologies28 to achieve high-quality ultrathin gold films (see Supplementary Note 1 for further technical details). Moreover, our technology allows the wafer-scale production of transferable ultrathin gold films (Figure 2a). For example, Figure 2b demonstrates a 4 in. wafer with our synthesized ultrathin gold film. Hence, among available technologies only our graphene-inspired method meets industrial standards for implementation of ultrathin gold (Figure 2) with high-quality morphology replicating the surface of copper (see Supplementary Note 2).

For optical characterization of our ultrathin gold films, we transferred them on glass and performed transmission and ellipsometry spectroscopies. Figure 3a shows the resulting total transmittance in the visible spectral range of 3.5, 5.5, and 8.0 nm thick gold ultrathin films transferred on a glass substrate. We can also synthesize films with smaller thicknesses; for example, our current record is 2 nm film, but the reproducibility is still an issue below 3.5 nm, while above this value we get reproducible results in terms of transmittance and conductivity (Supplementary Note 3). As expected, transmittance greatly increases with the decrease in gold film thickness from 70% for 8.0 nm film to 76% for 5.5 nm film and 80% for 3.5 nm film at 550 nm. Further, to determine the optical properties of quasi-2D gold films, we performed spectroscopic ellipsometry (Supplementary Note 1). Ellipsometric parameters Ψ and Δ were analyzed using the point-by-point fitting approach37 to extract the real ε1 and imaginary ε2 parts of the gold dielectric function. Then, we fitted the extracted dielectric function with the Drude–Lorentz dispersion model38 with results plotted in Figure 3b. From Figure 3b, we conclude that all our gold films exhibit pronounced metallic response: ε1 < 0 and a large plasmonic figure of merit |ε1|/ε2 for the infrared wavelengths.

The high transparency and metallic response of our ultrathin gold films make them promising for numerous photonic applications, including thermal management.39,40 Here, we demonstrate two examples of possible applications: thermal camouflage (Figure 4) and smart contact lenses (Figure 5).

The optical characteristics of our ultrathin gold make it suitable for thermal camouflage applications (Figure 4). Indeed, ideal thermal camouflage should be transparent in the visible range (400–800 nm) and exhibit low emissivity in the mid-infrared range (3–30 μm). Our ultrathin gold films satisfy both criteria. First, their high visible transparency is achieved by their atomic thickness. Second, their low infrared emissivity follows from Kirchhoff’s law40 since their high metallic reflectivity reduces their infrared absorption. Figure 4a proves this idea: the visual temperature of gold film recorded by a thermal camera (see Methods) is much smaller than the actual (background) temperature. Moreover, the visual temperature demonstrates a linear dependence on the actual (background) temperature (Figure 4b), implying a broad operation temperature range of our gold thermal camouflage. Besides, we can use it on any surface, including the human body (Figure 4c), to hide it from thermal camera imaging. This application example unambiguously shows the great usage potential of ultrathin gold for wearable optics.

Apart from high transparency, optoelectronic applications, especially transparent electrodes, require high electrical conductivity, which is a challenge because these properties affect each other—an increase in conductivity is accompanied by a decrease in transmittance. Our four-point probe measurements also reveal this trend for synthesized ultrathin gold films (Figure 5a). As seen from Figure 5a, this behavior is universal for all ultrathin films,14,23 whereas our gold films demonstrate one of the best trade-offs between transparency and conductivity. Furthermore, their atomic thicknesses lead to their flexible mechanical properties. In order to investigate the flexibility of our gold films, we perform repeated bending cycles with an ultrasmall radius of curvature r ≈ 1.6 mm, reproducing the harshest conditions of flexible transparent electrodes. Despite this strong bending, quasi-2D gold films preserve conductivity with only two to four times increment in resistivity after a thousand bending cycles, as seen in Figure 5b. This is a result of small cracks (Figure 5c) originating from the bending cycles. Therefore, our ultrathin gold is now available for a large variety of technological applications where high conductivity and transparency are required:41−44 flexible displays, tattoo medicine, ultracompact antennas, switchable metasurfaces, extended reality, and smart contact lenses. In particular, transparent electrodes are an indispensable element for the realization of smart contact lenses (Figure 5d) since large conductivity helps energy-efficient operation, while high transparency delivers a seamless wearer experience. For demonstration, we used our gold films to successfully operate a light-emitting diode (LED) on a contact lens at a standard voltage of 2.5 V (Figure 5d). Hence, our technology (more details in the Methods) in Figure 1b already enables practical use of our ultrathin gold films for flexible and transparent electrodes in cutting-edge optoelectronic devices with curved surfaces.

In summary, we adopted graphene technologies for the synthesis of transferable wafer-scale ultrathin gold films with a continuous flexible structure and one of the best optoelectronic performances in terms of electrical conductivity (10 Ω/sq) and transparency (80%). Such unique properties make our ultrathin gold films up-and-coming candidates for a variety of photonic and optoelectronic applications where atomic thickness, flexibility, and high conductivity/transparency play a vital role, including topological darkness,48,49 flexible electrodes,50 metamaterials,51−53 integrated biosensing,54,55 thermal management,56 and tattoo medicine.57 Furthermore, in contrast to conventional methods of ultrathin metal film fabrication,37 the proposed graphene-inspired method provides transferable and percolation-free gold films. It opens up new opportunities for investigation of novel physical effects in atomically thin metals, such as tunable,23 nonlocal,58 and anisotropic59 plasmonics. Finally, we believe our synthesis technology could be easily modified for the creation of quasi-2D metals beyond gold, including aluminum, silver, and platinum.60 Beyond metals, our work marks an important milestone, where 2D fabrication techniques start making their way into the domain of conventional 3D materials, bringing simplicity and flexibility and enabling unprecedented fabrication quality.
