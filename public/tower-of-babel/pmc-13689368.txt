Classification of gestational diabetes mellitus placental histopathological images based on MONAI and improved GAMatrix-YOLOv8
Authors: Zhifa Jiang, Weirui Wu, Jingwen Liu, Xiekun Chen, Ruoping Lin, Xiangyun Ye, Donghui Huang, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) poses a significant threat to maternal and fetal health, and placental pathology is crucial for assessing disease severity. Traditional diagnosis relies on clinicians’ visual assessment, which is often inefficient and subjective.MethodsTo address these issues, we developed a MONAI-based image augmentation strategy and integrated the GAMatrix attention module within the YOLOv8 framework. Our study used a dataset of 5,065 placental pathology images (80% for training and 20% for validation) covering five pathology categories, supplemented by an external validation set of 500 images. A MONAI-based image augmentation pipeline was used to increase training-image diversity.ResultsFour model configurations were compared: YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, YOLOv8 trained with MONAI-based augmentation, GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, and GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained with MONAI-based augmentation. In the original comparative analysis, YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset achieved a macro-F1 score of 95.56% (95% CI, 94.86%-96.26%) in internal validation. The GAMatrix-YOLOv8 model trained with MONAI-based augmentation showed the strongest overall performance among the evaluated configurations, while external image-level evaluation indicated persistent class-specific errors, particularly for thrombotic vascular lesion and chorangiosis.ConclusionThese findings suggest that attention-enhanced YOLOv8 combined with MONAI-based augmentation may support placental histopathological image classification, but the results should be interpreted as image-level findings and require patient-level, multicenter, and prospective validation.Keywords: GAMatrix-YOLOv8, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Pathological images, Classification

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a significant pregnancy complication that poses risks to both maternal and fetal health, including increased rates of preeclampsia, macrosomia, and preterm birth [1–3]. Pathological changes in the placenta, such as delayed villous maturation, villous edema, and maternal vascular malperfusion, play a central role in mediating these adverse outcomes [4–6]. Accurate and timely identification of these pathological features is critical for understanding the mechanisms of GDM and improving clinical care. However, traditional placental pathology analysis is time-consuming and heavily reliant on the expertise of trained pathologists, which can lead to variability in diagnostic accuracy and increased workload [7, 8].

In the field of medical image research, YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once version 8) has emerged as a powerful deep-learning model for object detection and image classification [9]. As the latest iteration of the YOLO family, YOLOv8 is designed for high accuracy and speed, making it particularly suitable for analyzing complex medical imaging datasets. Its advanced architecture incorporates features such as dynamic anchor optimization, an improved backbone network, and a lightweight structure that supports deployment in resource-constrained environments [10]. Additionally, YOLOv8’s ability to handle multi-class classification tasks and its adaptability to small datasets through transfer learning makes it an ideal candidate for automating pathology image analysis [11]. These advantages position YOLOv8 as a valuable tool for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in medical imaging research.

In our preliminary research, we developed an enhanced YOLOv8 model incorporating an attention mechanism, achieving a recognition accuracy of 0.997 for the classification of "delayed villous maturation" in GDM placental pathology images, along with strong performance metrics (precision: 0.975, recall: 0.97, F1-score: 0.972). These promising results highlight the model's potential for automating the diagnosis of placental pathologies with high accuracy and reliability [12]. Building on this foundation, this study extends the application of YOLOv8 to multi-class classification and recognition of placental pathology images, aiming to classify diverse pathological features, including chorioamnionitis, villous edema, and maternal vascular malperfusion.

Data augmentation is widely used in medical image analysis to improve training-image diversity when annotated datasets are limited [13, 14]. In histopathological image classification, variations in tissue orientation, staining intensity, image acquisition, and field selection may affect model performance and generalizability [15, 16]. Augmentation strategies that introduce plausible spatial and intensity variations can help reduce overfitting and improve model robustness. In this study, a MONAI-based image augmentation pipeline was used to apply stochastic transformations to the training images, including affine transformation, flipping, Gaussian noise, contrast adjustment, and zooming. This approach was intended to increase training-image diversity for model training while leaving validation and external evaluation images unaugmented.

No additional internal test partition was created from the internal dataset; instead, a separately collected external image-level evaluation set was used to assess model performance outside the internal dataset.

Model training performance

Throughout 100 training epochs, the model demonstrated a clear convergence trend: the training loss fell sharply from 0.609 initially to 0.056 by epoch 50 and then stabilized, while the validation loss decreased gradually from 0.942 to 0.906. Despite some overfitting (with training loss markedly lower than validation loss), the absence of a significant rebound in validation loss indicates that the data augmentation effectively suppressed overfitting. The Top-1 accuracy surged from 97.4% to over 99.8%, reaching 99.6% by the 5th epoch, suggesting that the model had begun capturing essential pathological features early on.

MONAI-based augmentation markedly improved both the training accuracy and feature extraction capability. By supplementing the morphological diversity of small sample classes through MONAI-augmented images of conditions such as Villitis of Unknown Etiology and thrombotic vascular disease, the model accelerated its early learning of key pathological features. Experimental results demonstrated a 73.8% loss reduction within the first 5 epochs and rapid convergence by epoch 50, with a training/validation loss ratio (0.055) significantly superior to that of the non-augmented model (> 0.2) (see Fig. 2), underscoring the efficacy of the augmentation strategy in enhancing model generalization.

Training results

Furthermore, MONAI-augmented images of thrombotic vascular disease exhibited high consistency in critical diagnostic regions (such as vascular lumens and fibrin stratification). Because the MONAI augmentation transforms perturb the original images geometrically and in intensity without altering their content, the diagnostic regions of the original slides (e.g., vascular lumens and fibrin stratification) were preserved while plausible appearance variation was introduced, which supported feature learning for the small-sample categories.

Internal validation results

In internal validation, we compared the performance of four models—YOLOv8 and GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, and their counterparts trained on a dataset augmented with MONAI-based augmentation—in the recognition and classification of 5 types of pathological images, evaluating precision, F1-score, and recall. The results revealed that YOLOv8 achieved high predictive accuracy, with a macro-F1 score of 95.56% (95% CI, 94.86–96.26%), although errors of 3.46% and 4.50% were noted in the “Villitis of Unknown Etiology” and “Chorangiosis” categories, yielding predictive accuracies of 96.54% and 95.50%, respectively. The incorporation of MONAI-augmented data enhanced the model’s precision and recall by approximately 2–3 percentage points on average, and the integration of the GAM module further improved these metrics. The combined use of MONAI-augmented data and the GAM module resulted in peak performance, with precision and recall values in several categories approaching or exceeding 99%, accompanied by similarly Macro-F1 and Micro-F1 scores (refer to the revised Table 4). The proximity of these two metrics indicates a balanced and robust performance across all pathological categories. These outcomes demonstrate that the augmented data effectively broadened the morphological diversity of the training set, facilitating faster convergence and improved generalization.

External validation results

Using the same methodology as internal validation, we conducted classification of five types of images on an external validation dataset to assess the model’s portability and generalizability. The results revealed that the baseline YOLOv8 model's performance on the external dataset was moderate, achieving a macro-F1 score of 81.80% (95% CI, 76.73–86.87%). In particular, the “Thrombotic Vascular Disease” category showed a marked decline, with accuracy dropping to 78%, indicating deficiencies in recognizing complex pathological features (Table 5). In contrast, the augmented model exhibited stronger adaptability during external validation, achieving 88% accuracy in the “delayed villous maturation” category and 94% and 90% accuracy in the “Villitis of Unknown Etiology” and “Normal” categories, respectively. Overall, its performance was superior to that of YOLOv8, with comparable enhancements observed in both Macro-F1 and Micro-F1 scores as well as recall (Tables 6, 7).

GAMatrix-YOLOv8 demonstrated robust stability in external validation, with predictive accuracies in “Villitis of Unknown Etiology” and “delayed villous maturation” rising to 97%, representing clear improvements over YOLOv8. However, its accuracy in the “Thrombotic Vascular Disease” category remained at 90%, suggesting that isolated model optimization still faces limitations in complex tasks. Ultimately, the combination of GAMatrix-YOLOv8 with the augmented model produced the most remarkable results, achieving high accuracies across all categories—100% for “Villitis of Unknown Etiology”, 96% for “delayed villous maturation”, and a significant increase to 94% for “Thrombotic Vascular Disease”. This outcome confirms the potent generalization ability of the GAMatrix-YOLOv8 combined with the augmented model on external datasets.

Additionally, the performance improvements after MONAI-augmented were clearly mirrored in the F1-scores, with both the macro and micro averages showing significant increases. With the application of both MONAI-augmented and the attention mechanism, the F1-scores increased significantly (Table 7). Although the baseline YOLOv8 model delivered moderate performance, when trained on the augmented dataset it exhibited a balanced synergy between precision and recall, which translated into substantially higher F1-scores. This improvement highlights the enhanced generalizability of the augmented model on external datasets, even when contending with additional challenges such as varying staining protocols, imaging resolutions, and a broader range of gestational ages. The enhanced performance across several categories following MONAI-augmented validates that our data augmentation strategy and attention-based methodology can effectively alleviate issues related to data distribution differences and imaging artifacts, thereby bolstering the overall diagnostic reliability of the classification system.

Statistical and confusion-matrix error analysis

One-way ANOVA of the internal validation metrics showed significant differences in accuracy, recall, and F1-score among the four model configurations (Fig. 3A). YOLOv8 trained with MONAI-based augmentation showed higher overall performance than YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, suggesting that increased training-image diversity may improve internal validation performance. GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset also outperformed the baseline model, indicating that the attention mechanism may help emphasize relevant pathological image features. The strongest internal validation performance was observed when GAMatrix-YOLOv8 was trained with MONAI-based augmentation, suggesting a combined benefit of image augmentation and attention-based feature enhancement. In the external validation analysis, one-way ANOVA again showed significant differences among the four model configurations (Fig. 3B). The combined model showed the strongest overall external performance, particularly for precision and F1-score, although external-domain variability remained evident. While these aggregate metrics support the value of combining MONAI-based augmentation with GAMatrix-YOLOv8, they do not fully describe the direction and category distribution of classification errors. Therefore, confusion-matrix analysis was further used to characterize class-specific misclassification patterns, particularly for thrombotic vascular lesion and chorangiosis.

Discussion

Performance metrics

The internal validation demonstrated that while the YOLOv8 model trained on the original dataset already achieved commendable performance metrics, its capabilities were markedly enhanced when augmented data generated via a MONAI-based augmentation strategy were further refined with a GAM attention module. Overall model performance, as measured by both Macro-F1 and Micro-F1 scores (Villitis of Unknown Etiology, delayed villous maturation, normal, thrombotic vascular disease, and chorangiosis) was significantly improved with these enhancements.

In external validation, despite overall lower absolute values, models utilizing augmented data continued to outperform the baseline YOLOv8. The external dataset, obtained from the Pathology Department of the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangdong Province, included images captured with a different microscope (Guangzhou mshot’s MD50) at a resolution of 2048 × 1536. Compared to the internal dataset, the external data exhibited greater variability in imaging protocols and staining techniques (H&E, immunohistochemistry, Gram staining), challenging the baseline YOLOv8 with complex pathological features and resulting in noticeable drops in precision, recall, and overall F1-scores. Conversely, the MONAI-augmented models integrated with the GAMatrix module showed clear improvements over the baseline on the external set, with higher Macro-F1 scores (indicating balanced performance across classes) and Micro-F1 scores (reflecting overall accuracy) (Tables 6, 7). On the retained external subset, the final selected model reached 91.44% accuracy, with residual class-specific errors as detailed in the confusion-matrix analysis (Fig. 7). One-way ANOVA (p < 0.001) confirmed that these improvements are statistically significant, even when external data differ in imaging equipment, resolution, and staining protocols. External validation not only reinforced internal findings but also highlighted the performance under external image-level evaluation of the enhanced models, demonstrating that MONAI-based augmentation can effectively bridge the gap between controlled internal conditions and the more heterogeneous real-world data [17, 18]. This enhancement is primarily due to the increased morphological diversity provided by augmented data and the GAM module’s effectiveness in directing model attention to clinically relevant features. The attention mechanism appears especially beneficial in emphasizing fine-grained details within complex tissue architectures, thereby enabling more precise feature extraction. These findings corroborate previous studies that have shown data augmentation and attention-based architectures can substantially improve medical image analysis performance [19, 20].

The differences between internal and external validation likely stem from multiple factors warranting further investigation [21, 22]. Internally, the dataset is homogeneous, collected under standardized protocols with a narrow gestational age range (28–40 weeks) and consistent H&E staining, which contributes to higher performance metrics. In contrast, the external dataset spans a broader gestational range (26–42 weeks) and incorporates multiple staining methods, resulting in greater visual variability and potential imaging artifacts. Additionally, differences in imaging devices lead to variations in resolution and image quality, which in turn affect feature extraction and classification. Models trained with the augmented dataset benefit from augmented data that enrich the representation of underrepresented pathological features, thus demonstrating adaptability to such variations.

Comparisons between the baseline and MONAI-augmented models reveal that the integration of augmented data and attention mechanisms significantly contributes to performance improvements in both internal and external validations, although the degree of enhancement varies. Internally, the enhanced model achieved near-perfect classification—especially in categories like thrombotic vascular disease, which are challenging for the baseline due to morphological complexity. The rapid decline in training loss and swift increase in Top-1 accuracy indicate that a more diverse dataset enables the model to quickly learn key pathological features, as supported by our statistical analysis. Externally, despite lower overall scores due to variability in imaging protocols and data distribution, the augmented model exhibited greater resilience, with significant improvements across all performance metrics. These results underscore that while data augmentation and attention mechanisms considerably boost model performance and generalizability, rigorous calibration of evaluation metrics and continuous external validation are essential for potential applicability as an auxiliary image-level tool [23]. The use of image augmentation in this study demonstrates significant potential for overcoming the inherent challenges in medical image analysis and lays the groundwork for real-world clinical applications.

MONAI-based image augmentation and Gamatrix attention

In pathological image analysis, the use of image augmentation enables the model to learn pathological features from various angles and scales, thereby enhancing its ability to accurately identify lesion regions [24–26]. By diversifying the augmented images, the model can adapt to different input types and avoid reliance on specific positions or orientations. It is crucial during the augmentation process to maintain image quality and avoid unnecessary deformations or noise, so that the generated images retain the key diagnostic information of lesion areas [27, 28]. In our project, we utilized MONAI to process and augment GDM pathological images. MONAI’s efficient data loading and preprocessing capabilities allowed us to automatically manage medical images of different formats. Moreover, MONAI offers a variety of augmentation techniques that generate images similar to the originals with slight variations, thereby increasing dataset diversity and enhancing the model’s generalization ability. Our external validation indicated that models trained on the augmented dataset significantly outperformed the baseline YOLOv8. In addition, MONAI’s support for pixel-level adjustments helps the model capture subtle diabetes-related lesions more effectively. By avoiding the introduction of excessive noise during augmentation, we ensured that the augmented images retained the essential features of the original images, thus improving performance under external image-level evaluation and reducing overfitting. External validation also confirmed that MONAI-augmented training images can alleviate discrepancies caused by differences in imaging devices, staining protocols (H&E, immunohistochemistry, Gram staining), and data distribution. Such consistent performance across heterogeneous datasets is crucial for clinical applications, as it evidences the model's potential for deployment in inherently variable real-world conditions [29, 30].

Furthermore, the GAMatrix module utilizes an attention mechanism to steer the model’s focus toward diagnostically critical regions—such as the fine structural details in Villitis of Unknown Etiology and the intricate vascular architectures in thrombotic vascular disease. This targeted focus minimizes misclassifications and enhances model sensitivity; research indicates that attention mechanisms assist deep networks in concentrating on prominent features within medical images [20, 31]. Additional studies have found that certain pathological features, particularly those in thrombotic vascular disease and Villitis of Unknown Etiology, derive greater benefit from MONAI-based augmentation, which increased the variety of plausible appearances of these structures (orientation, scale, and intensity), which contributed to improved model performance. Their integration enables the model to learn a wider spectrum of morphological variations, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting to the limited examples present in the original dataset.

Additionally, the GAMatrix module’s attention mechanism further optimizes the process by concentrating computational resources on the most diagnostically relevant regions, thereby enhancing the accuracy of feature extraction and classification. This combined approach of dataset expansion and refined feature extraction is consistent with prior studies in medical image analysis [32, 33], which have demonstrated that data augmentation and attention mechanisms can effectively address challenges related to class imbalance and limited sample sizes [34, 35]. In the context of GDM pathological image recognition, the GAM attention mechanism aids the model in honing in on diabetes-associated pathological features. By applying weighted emphasis to key image regions, GAM improves the model’s discriminatory power, enabling it to more accurately identify and classify lesion areas even within complex backgrounds. The MONAI-based augmentation strategy increased the diversity of training images for complex categories such as thrombotic vascular lesion and Villitis of Unknown Etiology, thereby mitigating the limitations imposed by class imbalance and limited sample diversity. Furthermore, the attention mechanism of the GAMatrix module further sharpens the model’s focus on critical diagnostic regions, offsetting the challenges posed by increased noise and heterogeneity in external datasets [36].

Error analysis

An investigation into misclassified images during the model’s classification and recognition tasks revealed that, in the model trained on the original dataset, errors were primarily concentrated in the “Thrombotic Vascular Disease” category—especially in the external validation set, where the predictive accuracy for this category dropped to only 74%. Typical misclassifications involved labeling thrombotic vascular lesions as either “Normal” or “Chorangiosis”, possibly due to insufficient representation of the subtle variations in thrombus stratification in the training data. Images often contained mixed signals (for instance, areas showing both normal and pathological features), complicating the model’s ability to assign a consistent label.

Variations in slice orientation, staining intensity, and imaging artifacts also contribute to ambiguities that the model may misinterpret. Even after extensive data augmentation and attention-based enhancements, the intrinsic heterogeneity and multidimensional nature of placental pathological images can lead to errors, as the model is compelled to learn patterns that might overlap or contradict one another.

Moreover, error analysis suggests that the inherent complexity of pathological images and the challenge of multi-class classification limit the model’s robustness. Although the MONAI-based augmented dataset enhances the diversity of the training images, it might not fully encompass the subtle variations present in real-world samples. While the GAMatrix module aids by directing focus to key diagnostic regions, the model can still struggle when fine-grained features are exceedingly subtle or when multiple pathological features coexist in a single image. Additionally, variations in imaging protocols, such as differences in staining methods and resolution, further exacerbate these challenges. In summary, despite high overall performance metrics, the model exhibits certain weaknesses in distinguishing complex and overlapping pathological features.

Limitations and future prospects

Although our study yielded promising results, it also underscores persistent challenges in medical image analysis. Because patient-level identifiers were unavailable in the de-identified image dataset, the current findings should be interpreted as image-level validation rather than patient-level validation; accordingly, the internal validation metrics may overestimate patient-level performance, and confirmation through patient-level, multicenter and prospective studies is required before any clinical application. A notable gap between training and validation losses suggests that, despite conventional augmentation, some overfitting remains; models may not have learned fully generalizable features. Future work should integrate additional regularization (e.g., dropout, weight decay, advanced domain adaptation) to mitigate overfitting [37, 38]. Furthermore, the consistently high (> 99%) Top-1 accuracy across models raises concerns that current evaluation metrics might overlook subtle differences among similar pathological features, potentially concealing misclassifications in challenging categories like thrombotic vascular lesion and chorangiosis. Enhanced evaluation using metrics such as per-class specificity or confusion matrices is recommended [39]. Finally, although enhanced models showed improved performance on external data, lower overall performance, due to variations in data distribution, imaging protocols, and staining techniques, indicates that more diverse datasets and improved domain adaptation methods are needed to fully capture real-world variability [40].

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that integrating a MONAI-based image augmentation strategy with the GAMatrix attention module improved YOLOv8 performance in the classification of GDM placental pathological images. Models trained with augmented images achieved improved accuracy, recall, and both Macro- and Micro-F1 scores in internal and external validation, with notable gains in challenging categories such as thrombotic vascular lesion and villitis of unknown etiology. These improvements may be related to the increased training-image diversity provided by augmentation and the ability of the attention mechanism to emphasize diagnostically relevant regions. These findings suggest that attention-enhanced deep learning may be useful for placental histopathological image classification. Further patient-level, multicenter, and prospective studies are needed to confirm its generalizability and clinical applicability.

Methods and materials

Dataset description

Data source: placental pathological images were obtained from GDM patients who received antenatal care and delivered at the Huizhou First Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangdong Province, China. Placental tissue samples were prepared using a standard protocol by pathologists, stained with H&E, and the original images were supplied by the pathology department.

Ethics approval: This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Huizhou First First Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Huizhou City (approval number: 20240328 (A14)). All data were collected following the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024), with informed consent obtained from all participants.

Dataset characteristics: the dataset comprises 5065 placental pathological images at a resolution of 4000 × 3000, depicting various GDM-related pathological features. Moreover, an independent external validation set of 500 images from the pathology department of the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangdong Province—representing different groups or imaging settings—was included to evaluate the model’s generalizability and robustness.

Model architecture

YOLOv8

YOLOv8 is an efficient deep-learning object detection algorithm extensively used in medical image analysis, particularly for target recognition and classification in pathological images. Employing end-to-end training, YOLOv8 achieves high-speed detection while maintaining precise target localization. It utilizes convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for feature extraction and incorporates fully connected layers for target classification and localization. Compared with earlier YOLO models, YOLOv8 offers an improved balance between accuracy and speed.

In our study, YOLOv8 effectively classifies various pathological features in images. Its efficient real-time detection capability renders it well-suited for clinical image screening and decision support. Moreover, its robust multi-scale feature extraction enables the processing of pathological images of different sizes and resolutions, ensuring accurate detection even in complex scenarios.

GAM attention mechanism

The Global Attention Mechanism (GAM) strengthens feature representation, making it particularly suited for complex medical imaging data [41]. In pathological image analysis, subtle yet critical details can easily blend into intricate backgrounds and escape traditional feature extraction methods. By modeling global context and weighting the input feature map (Fig. 5), GAM guides the model to automatically focus on the most informative regions, thereby improving the detection of subtle lesions in pathological images.

GAM attention mechanism

In our preliminary research, the GAM attention mechanism was incorporated into a YOLOv8 framework for placental pathological image classification, and comparative performance against YOLOv5, YOLOv6, YOLOv7, and GoogleNet was reported previously [12]. The current revised analysis focuses on the available final selected five-class checkpoint rather than reclaiming a new multi-model comparison.

MONAI

In this study, MONAI was utilized for pathological image data augmentation. MONAI is an open-source deep learning framework tailored for medical image analysis, integrating a suite of advanced techniques including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and convolutional autoencoders (CAEs). It supports a range of tasks such as classification, object detection, segmentation, and image augmentation [42], with its network architecture depicted in Fig. 6. The framework accommodates various medical imaging formats—CT, MRI, X-ray, and pathological images, thereby enabling efficient handling of diverse data and proving especially effective for pathological image analysis.

MONAI architecture diagram

To address the limited morphological diversity of the training images, we used MONAI as a medical-imaging framework to construct a standardized image-augmentation workflow. MONAI offers composable spatial and intensity transforms tailored for medical image preprocessing. In placental histopathological images, variations in tissue orientation, sectioning angles, staining intensities, and local acquisition conditions can significantly confound model learning. Therefore, our pipeline was designed to expose the classifier to a broader spectrum of plausible image-level appearances during training, while preserving the critical histopathological structures required for classification.

Specifically, the training images underwent a series of stochastic transformations: intensity scaling to [0, 1] (ScaleIntensity); random affine transformations (RandAffine with a probability of 0.8, translation within ± 10 pixels per axis, rotation within ±(π)/6, scale variation within ± 0.1, and border padding); random flipping (RandFlip along spatial axis 1 with a probability of 0.5); random Gaussian noise insertion (RandGaussianNoise with a standard deviation of 0.02 and a probability of 0.5); random contrast adjustment (RandAdjustContrast with a gamma range of 0.8–1.2 and a probability of 0.5); and random zooming (RandZoom between 0.9 and 1.1 with a probability of 0.5). For each original training image, three augmented variants were generated (scale factor = 3), expanding the training set fourfold. Crucially, these data augmentation operations were applied exclusively to the training set; the internal validation and external evaluation images remained unaugmented to ensure unbiased performance assessment.

Training procedure

Training equipment

The training was executed on a LAPTOP-J0EJ18SQ device featuring an AMD Ryzen 5 5600H processor (3.30 GHz), 16.0 GB RAM, and an integrated Radeon Graphics GPU. Despite the modest hardware setup, optimized training procedures (e.g., reduced batch size and mixed-precision training) along with efficient resource allocation met the fundamental requirements for YOLOv8, GAMatrix-YOLOv8, and the MONAI framework. Throughout the training, multi-core CPU capabilities accelerated data processing and model inference, while memory optimization techniques (such as data chunk loading) minimized memory usage to ensure stable execution.

Preprocessing

Prior to training, medical images were standardized to ensure data consistency and model stability. Initially, input images were uniformly resized to 4000 × 3000 pixels to meet the model’s requirements and reduce computational load. Then, pixel values were normalized to a [0, 1] range to expedite convergence and enhance stability. Furthermore, data augmentation techniques from the MONAI framework, such as affine transformation, flipping, Gaussian noise, contrast adjustment, and zooming, were employed to increase training-image diversity.

Hyperparameters

Extensive experimentation refined the training hyperparameters to ensure rapid convergence and mitigate overfitting. An initial learning rate of 1e-4 was employed, with a cosine annealing schedule dynamically balancing speed and accuracy. A batch size of 16 was selected to optimize GPU memory usage given the hardware constraints, and training was scheduled for 100 epochs. An early stopping criterion—halting training after 5 consecutive epochs without improvement in validation loss—further safeguarded against overfitting.

Validation strategy

Model comparison

This study will compare the internal and external validation accuracies of YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, YOLOv8 trained with MONAI-based augmentation, GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained on the original dataset, and GAMatrix-YOLOv8 trained with MONAI-based augmentation. Testing on these datasets will provide a comprehensive assessment of the models’ accuracy, robustness, and generalization capabilities.

Internal validation

A k-fold cross-validation was implemented within the training set to assess the model’s performance across different subsets. Metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and both Macro-averaged and Micro-averaged F1-scores were averaged to derive a robust estimate of performance.

External validation

An independent external validation dataset was employed to assess model performance on unseen data. The external images were preprocessed identically to the internal dataset to ensure compatibility with the trained model. Metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, both Macro-averaged and Micro-averaged F1-scores were calculated to evaluate the models’ robustness and applicability in practical scenarios.

Performance metrics

The evaluation will employ metrics including accuracy, recall, both Macro-averaged and Micro-averaged F1-scores to comprehensively assess the performance of YOLOv8 and GAMatrix-YOLOv8 across various datasets, thus furnishing a scientific basis for model selection and optimization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using MATLAB R2018b (The MathWorks Inc., USA). The results were expressed as the means ± SEM or boxplots. The homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. After confirming that the tested data satisfy homogeneity of variance, one-way ANOVA test was used for comparisons among groups. Statistically significant differences were inferred at p < 0.05 with stronger ones at p < 0.01 or p < 0.001.
