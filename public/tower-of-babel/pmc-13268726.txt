Prioritizing the patient through a continuum of personalized care: insights from the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines
Authors: Gyungah Kim, Won-Young Kim
Published: 2026

Sepsis and septic shock remain the leading causes of mortality worldwide, despite decades of international efforts by initiatives such as the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) and the Global Sepsis Alliance [1,2]. The recently published 2026 SSC guidelines mark an evolution from the 2021 guidelines and present a highly refined and personalized approach to sepsis management that spans the complete clinical trajectory from pre-hospitalization to post-discharge (Table 1) [3]. The new 2026 guidelines provide valuable and practical resources for clinicians treating patients with sepsis and septic shock, in both resource-abundant and resource-limited settings.

Recognizing the importance of early intervention [4,5], the new guidelines suggest using standardized sepsis screening tools during ambulance or aeromedical transport (e.g., National Early Warning Score 2) [6]. The guidelines further suggest administering antimicrobial therapy before hospital arrival for patients with septic shock with an anticipated delay of over 60 minutes, as pre-hospital antibiotic administration has been associated with a possible reduction in mortality [7]. Building on these pre-hospital strategies, the guidelines also introduce “code sepsis,” a coordinated, in-hospital protocol dedicated to the rapid recognition and management of sepsis [8]. Upon sepsis recognition, early administration of antibiotics should be considered. Antimicrobial therapy should be delivered within 1 hour for patients with septic shock or those with a high likelihood of sepsis and within 3 hours for patients with possible infection who do not meet these criteria. In a recent observational study of 273,255 sepsis patients, the greatest absolute mortality reduction was observed among those with septic shock who received antibiotics sooner [9]. The 2026 guidelines extend the scope beyond discharge and focus on providing post-critical illness follow-up services, including physical rehabilitation and mental health support, to address the profound long-term sequelae faced by sepsis survivors. Health systems are advised, in a good practice statement, to implement strategies to ensure that clinicians can support sepsis survivors and their families during the posthospital recovery.

The 2026 SSC guidelines also emphasize a personalized management approach and encourage clinicians to continuously monitor and adjust treatment according to individual patient profiles. For instance, while the initial mean arterial pressure (MAP) target remains 65 mm Hg for most adults, guidelines suggest a lower initial MAP target of 60–65 mm Hg for patients aged ≥65 years, aiming to reduce the risks associated with excessive vasopressor exposure [10]. Fluid resuscitation is similarly personalized for patients with obesity; the initial 30 ml/kg bolus should be calculated using adjusted or ideal body weight rather than actual body weight [11]. To complement a personalized fluid approach, concurrent vasopressor administration along with initial fluid resuscitation is suggested on a case-by-case basis, particularly in patients with hemodynamically unstable shock [12]. Vasopressors may be started peripherally with frequent monitoring of extravasation [13]. Early initiation of vasopressors is partly intended to limit intravenous fluid volume, although the Crystalloid Liberal or Vasopressors Early Resuscitation in Sepsis (CLOVERS) trial did not confirm better outcomes associated with the restrictive fluid strategy [14]. Refining when and how to initiate vasopressin as an adjunctive agent is another area of personalization that is currently under rigorous investigation, as evidenced by a recent Optimal Vasopressin Initiation in Septic Shock (OVISS) reinforcement-learning study [15]. Similarly, the 2026 update broadens the recommendation for corticosteroid use to all septic shock patients, rather than limiting initiation to the vasopressor thresholds suggested in the 2021 guidelines, and allows decisions to be made based on clinical judgment [16]. The precision also extends to antimicrobial stewardship, suggesting targeted antimicrobial therapy for patients with identified risk factors, rather than routine empiric coverage against multidrug-resistant pathogens, fungi, or anaerobes [17-19]. This represents a step forward from the 2021 recommendations, as the 2026 update more explicitly discourages routine empiric antifungal therapy and newly addresses empiric anaerobic treatment based on the risk of anaerobic infection. Additionally, selective decontamination of the digestive tract was newly introduced for mechanically ventilated adults with sepsis or septic shock in units with low prevalence of antimicrobial resistance [20]. In contrast to a rapid practice guideline that recommends against uniformly applying high oxygen targets to all intensive care unit patients [21], the 2026 SSC guidelines suggest individualized oxygen targets, either higher (≥96% SpO2) or lower (90%–93% SpO2), based on patient characteristics and resource availability [22]. As part of the de-escalation phase, guidelines suggest initiating active fluid removal with diuretics, and if these prove insufficient, escalating to ultrafiltration or extracorporeal fluid removal based on individual assessment [23].

The 2026 guidelines also highlight emerging strategies that prioritize patient comfort through noninvasive and low-burden management. For instance, for patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, the guidelines suggest using a high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) as the initial therapy over noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), noting that HFNC provides better comfort and ease of use without interfering with the patient’s ability to eat and speak. However, a definitive mortality benefit of HFNC over NIPPV has not been established [24,25]. Peripheral oxygen saturation monitoring can be used in conjunction with clinical evaluation to assess oxygenation, but it cannot replace the gold standard of arterial blood gas analysis because of its lower accuracy in patients with darker skin tones [26]. In terms of hemodynamic and perfusion monitoring, the 2026 guidelines support the use of capillary refill time (CRT), a simple, noninvasive, and rapid bedside assessment, as a valuable tool to guide resuscitation in conjunction with existing indicators, such as serum lactate [27]. The ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 trial demonstrated that a personalized CRT-guided resuscitation protocol was superior to usual care for a hierarchical composite outcome in early septic shock, primarily by reducing the duration of organ support [28]. Furthermore, the guidelines revisited blood pressure monitoring recommendations and shifted from a preference for invasive measurement to the recognition of noninvasive blood pressure monitoring as an alternative to arterial catheter insertion [29]. The Early versus Deferred Arterial Catheterization in Critically Ill Patients with Acute Circulatory Failure (EVERDAC) trial, published after the evidence review for the 2026 SSC guidelines was finalized, showed that a noninvasive blood pressure monitoring strategy was noninferior to early arterial catheter insertion for 28-day all-cause mortality [30]. Nevertheless, there are concerns regarding the consistency between these two methods. Given that discrepancies between noninvasive and invasive measurements have been associated with worse outcomes [31], noninvasive blood pressure monitoring should be considered a reliable alternative, primarily when repeated measurements remain consistent with the clinical picture.

Overall, the 2026 SSC guidelines underscore the multidimensional shift in sepsis care. This evolution is based on three key components: continuous care from pre-hospital diagnosis to post-hospital recovery, a transition to tailored and individualized treatment, and efforts toward noninvasive and patient-centered management. Clinicians should initiate early screening and treatment in the pre-hospital stage, administer antimicrobials early when sepsis is suspected but tailor their use according to disease severity and risk of infection, individualize hemodynamic and respiratory support according to patient profiles, and provide continuous care even after discharge. Although personalized treatment is often informed by evidence from smaller or more narrowly defined patient populations, it represents a meaningful advancement over uniform protocols that do not account for individual clinical variations. The 2026 guidelines provide a comprehensive roadmap for every clinician by integrating personalized interventions with a holistic view of the patient's entire clinical trajectory.
