Abstract
. In this article, we study the ccs-Daugavet, ccs- Δ \Delta , super-Daugavet, super- Δ \Delta , Daugavet, Δ \Delta , and ∇ \nabla points in the unit balls of vector-valued function spaces C 0 ​ ( L , X ) C_{0}(L,X) , A ⁡ ( K , X ) A(K,X) , L ∞ ​ ( μ , X ) L_{\infty}(\mu,X) , and L 1 ​ ( μ , X ) L_{1}(\mu,X) . To partially or fully characterize these diametral points, we first provide improvements of several stability results under ⊕ ∞ \oplus_{\infty} and ⊕ 1 \oplus_{1} -sums shown in the literature. For complex Banach spaces, ∇ \nabla points are identical to Daugavet points, and so the study of ∇ \nabla points only makes sense when a Banach space is real. Consequently, we obtain that the seven notions of diametral points are equivalent for L ∞ ​ ( μ ) L_{\infty}(\mu) and uniform algebra when K K is infinite.

In 1963, I.K. Daugavet
[

In this article, we study classes of points on the unit sphere

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The spaces
\boldsymbol{C_{0}(L,X)}
,
\boldsymbol{A(K,X)}
, and
\boldsymbol{L_{p}(\mu,X)}

Let
L
be a locally compact Hausdorff topological space. The space
C_{0}(L,X)
is the set of
X
-valued continuous functions on
L
that vanish at infinity, endowed with the supremum norm. That is, for each
f\in C_{0}(L,X)
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact
K\subset L
such that
\|f(t)\|_{X}<\varepsilon
for all
t\in L\setminus K
. If
X=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, then we obtain the usual
C_{0}(L)
space. Also, if
L=K
is compact, we have the usual
C(K,X)
and
C(K)
spaces, respectively.

For a compact Hausdorff topological space
K
, a
uniform algebra

A(K)
is a closed subspace of
C(K)
that contains all constant functions and separates points. Here “separating points” means that for every
s\neq t\in K
, there exists
f\in A(K)
such that
f(s)\neq f(t)
. Examples of uniform algebras include the disk algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
and the space
A_{w^{*}u}(B_{X^{*}})
of weak* uniformly continuous functions that are holomorphic on the interior of
B_{X^{*}}
. In the case of uniform algebras, we only need to consider the range space to be
\mathbb{C}
because the real uniform algebra is
C(K)
by the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem (see
[
27
, pg 122]
).

To study the geometrical properties of uniform algebras and their related spaces, certain points in
K
play an important role. A point
t_{0}\in K
is said to be a
strong boundary point
if for every open neighborhood
U
of
t_{0}
, there exists
f_{U}\in S_{A(K)}
such that
f_{U}(t_{0})=1
and
\sup_{t\in K\setminus U}|f_{U}(t)|<1
. A boundary
S
for
A(K)
is the set of elements in
K
such that for every
f\in A(K)
, there exists an element
t\in S
such that
|f(t)|=\|f\|_{\infty}
. The smallest closed boundary for
A(K)
is called the
Shilov boundary
, denoted by
\Gamma
. It is known
[
10
,
24
]
that the set of strong boundary points coincides with the Choquet boundary
\Gamma_{0}
for
A(K)
, that is, the set of all extreme points on
K_{A(K)}=\{\lambda\in A(K)^{*}:\|\lambda\|=\lambda(1_{A(K)})=1\}
, where
1_{A(K)}
is the unit of
A(K)
, and that
\Gamma_{0}
is dense in
\Gamma
.

Now, we recall the definition of a class of function spaces associated with uniform algebras. The space
A(K,X)
is a closed subspace of
C(K,X)
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The base algebra
A:=\{x^{*}\circ f:x^{*}\in X^{*}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,f\in A(K,X)\}
is a uniform algebra over
K
.

(2)

A\otimes X\subset A(K,X)
.

(3)

For every
f\in A
and
g\in A(K,X)
,
f\cdot g\in A(K,X)
.

This space is endowed with the supremum norm
\|f\|=\sup_{t\in K}\|f(t)\|_{X}
. If
X=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, then we have a uniform algebra
A(K)
. For more information on this vector-valued function space, we refer to
[
22
,
23
]
. To prove certain results in
A(K,X)
, we will only consider the space of functions in
A(K)
restricted to its Shilov boundary
\Gamma
, and we denote this by
A(\Gamma)
. We adopt the analogous notation for the space
A(K,X)
. It is well-known that
A(\Gamma)
is isometrically isomorphic to
A(K)

[
24
, Theorem 4.1.6]
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space, and let
X
be a Banach space. Given a measurable set
A
in
\Sigma
,
\chi_{A}
is the
characteristic function
on
A
which has the value 1 on
A
and 0 on
\Omega\setminus A
. A function

is called
\mu
-simple
if it is of the form

f(\omega)=\sum_{1}^{n}x_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}(\omega)\ \ \ (\omega\in\Omega),

where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
.
Recall that a function
f:\Omega\to X
is said to be a strongly
\mu
-measurable if it is a pointwise limit of a sequence of
\mu
-simple functions. Two strongly measurable functions
f,g
are said to be
equivalent
if they are equal
\mu
-a.e.

For
1\leq p<\infty
,
L_{p}(\mu,X)
is the Banach space of equivalent classes of
p
-Bochner integrable functions, that is, strongly
\mu
-measurable functions satisfying

\|f\|_{p}=\left(\int_{\Omega}\|f(t)\|^{p}_{X}d\mu\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

In the case of
p=\infty
, the space
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
is defined to be the Banach space of all equivalent classes of essentially bounded strongly
\mu
-measurable functions

, that is,
f
satisfies the condition

\mu(\{\omega:\|f(\omega)\|_{X}>r\})=0

for some
r>0
.
In this case, the essential supremum norm
\|f\|_{\infty}
is defined to be the infimum of all

satisfying
\mu(\{\omega:\|f(\omega)\|_{X}>r\})=0
.

If
X=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, then we get the usual

spaces. To characterize the notions in

with full generality, we do not assume the
\sigma
-finiteness and nonatomicity of

here. Also, we only need to consider atoms of finite measure, since every strongly
\mu
-measurable function has value

a.e. on atoms of infinite measure. We say that two atoms

and

are identified if their characteristic functions

and

are equivalent.

As a final remark, there is an alternative space

in the literature, where the functions are assumed to be strongly measurable instead of strongly

4.3
are also true for that space without any changes.

i\in\{1,2\}
let
x_{i}\in S_{X_{i}}
and
a_{i}\in(0,1]
be such that
a_{1}+a_{2}=1
. The following holds.

(1)

(x_{1},0)\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}X_{2}}
is
\nabla
if and only if so is
x_{1}
.

(2)

(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2})\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}X_{2}}
is
\nabla
if and only if
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2})
are all Daugavet.

Note that these characterizations can be naturally extended to any finite sum of spaces.

Remark 3.3
.

Recall that in the complex case
\nabla
and Daugavet points coincide (see Proposition
2.2
). It is worth noting that in this scenario, the characterizations above coincide with the respective stability results for Daugavet points from the literature, as expected.

3.2.
Daugavet and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
points

The stability of Daugavet points under absolute sums has been characterized in
[
17
]
. In the case of
\oplus_{\infty}
-sums, the following has been shown for the sum of two spaces and can be easily generalized to any finite sum.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
17
, Proposition 2.4 and Theorem 3.2]
)
.

Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
be a finite collection of Banach spaces. For each
1\leq k\leq n
, let
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
and
a_{k}\geq 0
be such that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in S_{\ell_{\infty}^{n}}
. Then
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\ldots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}}
is Daugavet if and only if there is some
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
a_{k}=1
and
x_{k}
is Daugavet.

For
\Delta
points the situation is more complicated, as there are Banach spaces
X,Y
and points
x\in S_{X}
and
y\in S_{Y}
such that
(x,y)
is
\Delta
in
S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
, but none of
x
or
y
are
\Delta
(see
[
17
, Section 4]
). Nevertheless, a characterization of the
\Delta
points in
X\oplus_{\infty}Y
can be achieved by combining
[
17
, Propositions 4.4 and 4.5]
with
[
3
, Claim inside the proof of Theorem 5.8]
and studying the final missing case (where only one of the coordinates is
\Delta
, which can be tackled analogously to the last cited result). To extend the result to finite sums, we make a refinement of the computations done in those results. We include the details.

Recall (
[
17
, Definition 4.2]
) that
x\in S_{X}
is a
\Delta_{p}
point
with
p>1
if for every slice
S(B_{X},x^{*},\alpha)
containing
x
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
u\in S(B_{X},x^{*},p\alpha)
such that
\|x-u\|\geq 2-\varepsilon
. Note that every
\Delta
point is
\Delta_{p}
for all
p>1
, and that every
\Delta_{p}
point is
\Delta_{r}
for all
1<p\leq r
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n>1
be an integer, let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be Banach spaces, and for each
1\leq k\leq n
, let
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
. Let
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\ell_{\infty}^{n}
be such that
a_{k}\geq 0
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Then
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\ldots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}}
is
\Delta
if and only if either of the following conditions holds:

(1)

There is
1\leq k\leq n
with
a_{k}=1
such that
x_{k}
is
\Delta
.

(2)

If
A:=\{k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\,a_{k}=1\}
, then for every
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in A}
with
p_{k}>1
for each
k\in A
and
\sum_{k\in A}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=1
, there is some
k\in A
such that
x_{k}
is
\Delta_{p_{k}}
.

In particular, if
A
only has one element, say
k
, then
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})
is
\Delta
if and only if
x_{k}
is
\Delta
.

Proof.

Write for simpliticy
A=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\}
,
C:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
D:=C\setminus A=\{k_{m+1},\ldots,k_{n}\}
if
m<n
. Note that if
D\neq\emptyset
, then we can split
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})
as

((a_{k_{1}}x_{k_{1}},\ldots,a_{k_{m}}x_{k_{m}}),(a_{k_{m+1}}x_{k_{m+1}},\ldots,a_{k_{n}}x_{k_{n}}))\in\left[\bigoplus_{r=1}^{m}X_{k_{r}}\right]_{\infty}\oplus_{\infty}\left[\bigoplus_{r=m+1}^{n}X_{k_{r}}\right]_{\infty}.

Thus, if
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})
is
\Delta
, then so is
(a_{k_{1}}x_{k_{1}},\ldots,a_{k_{m}}x_{k_{m}})\in\left[\bigoplus_{r=1}^{m}X_{k_{r}}\right]_{\infty}
by
[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
.

The converse is also true, and in fact we show more by arguing analogously to the Claim inside
[
3
, Theorem 5.8]
. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
be given, and without loss of generality assume that
1\in A
, and suppose that
x_{1}
is
\Delta
(the same argument can be applied to any other coordinate). Then by
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
we can find some
z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\in S_{X_{1}}
such that

\left\|x_{1}-\frac{1}{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}z_{j}\right\|<\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\|x_{1}-z_{j}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq l.

Now, on the one hand, we have

\left\|(x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})-\frac{1}{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}(z_{j},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})\right\|=\left\|x_{1}-\frac{1}{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}z_{j}\right\|<\varepsilon,

and on the other hand, for each
1\leq j\leq l
, we have

\|(x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})-(z_{j},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})\|=\|x_{1}-z_{j}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon.

This shows that
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})
is a
\Delta
point in this case.

Therefore, to prove the rest of the result, it suffices only to consider (for the sake of notational simplicity) the case where
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{n}=1
, that is,
A=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, since any other case can be reduced to this one by the previous argument, we will assume this from now on.

We have seen that (1) implies that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
. Suppose now that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
but (1) does not hold, and we will see by contradiction that then (2) holds. Indeed, assume that there are
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in A}
with
p_{k}>1
for all
k\in A
and
\sum_{k\in A}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=1
such that for all
k\in A
,
x_{k}
is not
\Delta_{p_{k}}
. Then there is
\varepsilon>0
, and for each
k\in A
there are
x_{k}^{*}\in S_{X_{k}^{*}}
and
\alpha_{k}>0
such that
x_{k}\in S(B_{X_{k}},x_{k}^{*},\alpha_{k})
, but for all
u_{k}\in S(B_{X_{k}},x_{k}^{*},p_{k}\alpha_{k})
, we have
\|x_{k}-u_{k}\|<2-\varepsilon
. For each
k\in A
, define

\lambda_{k}:=\frac{\prod\limits_{j\in A\setminus\{k\}}p_{j}\alpha_{j}}{\sum\limits_{i\in A}\prod\limits_{j\in A\setminus\{i\}}p_{j}\alpha_{j}}.

Let
\alpha=\sum_{k\in A}\lambda_{k}\alpha_{k}
and
f^{*}:=(\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{*},\ldots,\lambda_{n}x_{n}^{*})\in S_{X_{1}^{*}\oplus_{1}\ldots\oplus_{1}X_{n}^{*}}
, and note that

\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})))=\lambda_{1}\operatorname{Re}(x_{1}^{*}(x_{1}))+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\operatorname{Re}(x_{n}^{*}(x_{n}))>\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(1-\alpha_{k})=1-\alpha.

Choose any
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in S(B_{X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\ldots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}},f^{*},\alpha)
. We have

\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})))=\lambda_{1}\operatorname{Re}(x_{1}^{*}(u_{1}))+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\operatorname{Re}(x_{n}^{*}(u_{n}))>1-\alpha.

Fix any
1\leq k\leq n
. Since

1-\alpha<\lambda_{k}\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(u_{k}))+\sum_{j\in A\setminus\{k\}}\lambda_{j}\operatorname{Re}(x_{j}^{*}(u_{j}))\leq\lambda_{k}\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(u_{k}))+\sum_{j\in A\setminus\{k\}}\lambda_{j},

we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(u_{k}))

\displaystyle>\frac{\lambda_{k}-\alpha}{\lambda_{k}}=1-\left(\alpha_{k}+\frac{\sum_{j\in A\setminus\{k\}}\alpha_{j}\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)=1-\left(\frac{p_{k}\alpha_{k}}{p_{k}}+\sum_{j\in A\setminus\{k\}}\alpha_{j}\frac{p_{k}\alpha_{k}}{p_{j}\alpha_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-p_{k}\alpha_{k}.

Thus, by the assumption,
\|x_{k}-u_{k}\|<2-\varepsilon
for all
k\in A
, but this contradicts the fact that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
and proves the claim.

Finally, it remains to show that if (2) holds but (1) does not, then
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
. We will show this in 2 steps.

Step 1:
First we will see that, under these assumptions, there is some
\emptyset\neq B\subset A
and some
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in B}
with
p_{k}>1
for all
k\in B
and
\sum_{k\in B}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=1
such that for all
k\in B

x_{k}
is a
\Delta_{p_{k}}
point. For each
k\in A
, let
B_{k}:=\{j\in[1,+\infty):\,x_{k}\text{ is }\Delta_{j}\text{ in }X_{k}\}
, and write
b_{k}:=\inf\{j:\,j\in B_{k}\}
.

First, we show that if for some
k\in A
we have
B_{k}\neq\emptyset
, then
b_{k}\in B_{k}
. Indeed, fix
\varepsilon>0
,
x_{k}^{*}\in S_{X_{k}^{*}}
, and
\alpha_{k}>0
such that
x_{k}\in S(B_{X_{k}},x_{k}^{*},\alpha_{k})
. Let
\gamma_{k}>0
be such that
\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(x_{k}))>1-(\alpha_{k}-\gamma_{k})
, and let
j_{k}=\frac{b_{k}\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{k}-\gamma_{k}}>b_{k}
, so
j_{k}\in B_{k}
. Thus, there is
u_{k}\in S(B_{X_{k}},x_{k}^{*},j_{k}(\alpha_{k}-\gamma_{k}))=S(B_{X_{k}},x_{k}^{*},b_{k}\alpha_{k})
such that
\|u_{k}-x_{k}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon
. But this means that
x
is
\Delta_{b_{k}}
, that is,
b_{k}\in B_{k}
.

Now, notice that in order to satisfy (2), at least one of the sets
B_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq n
) must be non-empty, and if (1) does not hold, in particular, at least 2 of such sets must be nonempty. Let
B:=\{k\in A:\,B_{k}\neq\emptyset\}
. We will see that
\sum_{k\in B}\frac{1}{b_{k}}\geq 1
. Indeed, otherwise there would exist
\delta>0
, and if
B\neq A
there would also exist some
\{c_{r}\}_{r\in A\setminus B}
with
c_{r}>1
for each
r\in A\setminus B
, such that

\sum_{r\in B}\frac{1}{b_{r}-\delta}+\sum_{s\in A\setminus B}\frac{1}{c_{s}}=1.

But by assumption, this would imply that there is some
k\in B
such that
x_{k}
is
\Delta_{b_{k}-\delta}
. This contradicts the definition of
b_{k}
and finishes this claim.

Step 2:
Finally, we will show that if such
B
and
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in B}
exist, then
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
. Once more, to simplify notation, by the first part of the proof, it suffices to show this for the case where
B=A
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
,
f^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},.\ldots,x_{n}^{*})\in S_{X_{1}^{*}\oplus_{1}\ldots\oplus_{1}X_{n}^{*}}
, and
\alpha>0
such that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in S(B_{X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\ldots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}},f^{*},\alpha)
. Then,

(\operatorname{Re}(x_{1}^{*}(x_{1}))-\|x_{1}^{*}\|)+\ldots+(\operatorname{Re}(x_{n}^{*}(x_{n}))-\|x_{n}^{*}\|)>-\frac{\alpha}{p_{1}}-\ldots-\frac{\alpha}{p_{n}}.

Note that there is some
k\in A
such that
\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(x_{k}))>\|x_{k}^{*}\|-\frac{\alpha}{p_{k}}
, since otherwise we would have that
\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})))\leq 1-\alpha
, which is a contradiction. Let such
k
be given. Then there is some
u_{k}\in S\left(B_{X_{k}},\frac{x_{k}^{*}}{\|x_{k}^{*}\|_{X_{k}^{*}}},\frac{\alpha}{\|x_{k}^{*}\|_{X_{k}^{*}}}\right)
such that
\|x_{k}-u_{k}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon
. Fix
\eta>0
small enough so that
\operatorname{Re}(x_{k}^{*}(u_{k}))>\|x_{k}^{*}\|-\alpha+\eta
, and for
j\in A\setminus\{k\}
, find any
u_{j}\in S_{X_{j}}
such that
\operatorname{Re}(x_{j}^{*}(u_{j}))>\|x_{j}^{*}\|-\frac{\eta}{n}
. Notice that

\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})))>1-\alpha,

and clearly

\|(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})-(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|\geq\|u_{k}-x_{k}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon.

This shows that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is
\Delta
, and the proof is finished.
∎

In the case of
\oplus_{1}
-sums,
\Delta
and Daugavet points were also characterized in
[
3
,
17
]
for the sum of two spaces, and the result can be easily extended once more to any finite sum of spaces.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
3
, Claim inside Theorem 5.8]
and
[
17
, Theorems 2.2, 3.1, and 4.1]
)
.

Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
be a finite collection of Banach spaces. For each
1\leq k\leq n
, let
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
and
a_{k}\geq 0
be such that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in S_{\ell_{1}^{n}}
. Then
(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n})\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\ldots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}}
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet) if and only if for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
a_{k}\neq 0
, the point
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet).

In fact, we show now that this result can be extended to countable
\ell_{1}
-sums of Banach spaces, as this fact will be needed in a later section.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a countable collection of Banach spaces, and let
Z:=\left[\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k}\right]_{\ell_{1}}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
and
a_{k}\geq 0
be such that
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)\in S_{\ell_{1}}
. Then
(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots)\in S_{Z}
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet) if and only if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{k}\neq 0
, the point
x_{k}\in S_{X_{k}}
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet).

Note that, as usual, by the properties of absolute sums, it suffices to consider the case where
a_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots)
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet), and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
a_{n}>0
. For simplicity, assume that
n=1
(the same argument can be used for any other value of
n
). Then
(a_{1}x_{1},(a_{2}x_{2},a_{3}x_{3},\ldots))\in X_{1}\oplus_{1}\left[\bigoplus_{k=2}^{\infty}X_{k}\right]_{\ell_{1}}
is also
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet), and by Lemma
3.6
, so is
x_{1}\in X_{1}
.

For the converse, note that the case where
S=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\,a_{n}\neq 0\}
is a finite set is a consequence of applying Lemma
3.6
, so we assume now that
S
is infinite. Moreover, the case where
\mathbb{N}\setminus S\neq\emptyset
can be reduced to the case where
S=\mathbb{N}
(as
x\in S_{X}
is
\Delta
or Daugavet if and only if so is
(x,0)\in X\oplus_{1}Y
for Banach spaces
X
and
Y
). So assume without loss of generality that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}>0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left\|(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots)-\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{k}|a_{n}|}(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{k}x_{k},0,0,\ldots)\right\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

For simplicity, we write

x:=(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2},\ldots)\quad\text{and}\quad y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots):=\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{k}|a_{n}|}(a_{1}x_{1},\ldots,a_{k}x_{k},0,0,\ldots).

We distinguish 2 cases now.

Case 1:
Suppose that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}\in S_{X_{n}}
is
\Delta
. Then
y\in S_{Z}
is also
\Delta
. By
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
, there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in S_{Z}
such that

\left\|y-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}\right\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\|y-y_{n}\|\geq 2-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ for }n=1,\ldots,k.

But then triangle inequality yields that

\left\|x-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}\right\|<\varepsilon,\quad\text{and}\quad\|x-y_{n}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon\text{ for }n=1,\ldots,k.

This shows that
x
is
\Delta
.

Case 2:
Suppose that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}\in S_{X_{n}}
is Daugavet. Then
y\in S_{Z}
is also Daugavet. Fix
z\in S_{Z}
. By
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
, there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in S_{Z}
such that

\left\|z-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}\right\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\|y-y_{n}\|\geq 2-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ for }n=1,\ldots,k.

But then the triangle inequality yields that

\|x-y_{n}\|\geq 2-\varepsilon\text{ for }n=1,\ldots,k.

Since this can be done for any arbitrary
z\in S_{Z}
, this shows that
x
is Daugavet.
∎

3.3.
Super-Daugavet and super-
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
points

The stability of super-Daugavet and super-
\Delta
points was first studied in
[
25
]
. In the case of
\oplus_{\infty}
-sums, we have the following partial characterization.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces, let
x\in S_{X}
,
y\in S_{Y}
, and
b\in[0,1]
. The following statements hold:

(1)

If
x
is super-
\Delta
(respectively super-Daugavet), then so is
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
.

(2)

If
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
is super-
\Delta
(respectively super-Daugavet) with
b<1
, then so is
x
.

Proof.

(1) was shown in
[
25
, Propositions 3.26 and 3.28 and Remark 3.29]
, so let us see (2).

Let
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
be a super-Daugavet point, and let
\delta>0
be such that
b<1-\delta
. Fix any
(z,t)\in B_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
. Then there is some net
\{(x_{\lambda},y_{\lambda})\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\subset B_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
such that
(x_{\lambda},y_{\lambda})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\rightarrow}}(z,t)
and
\|(x,by)-(x_{\lambda},y_{\lambda})\|\rightarrow 2
. But note that then
\{x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\rightarrow}}z
, and since
\|by-y_{\lambda}\|\leq 2-\delta
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we must have
\|x-x_{\lambda}\|\rightarrow 2
. This shows that
x\in S_{X}
is a super-Daugavet point. For super-
\Delta
points, it suffices to repeat the same argument, just taking
(z,t)=(x,by)
.
∎

In the case of
\oplus_{1}
-sums, the following upwards stability result was shown in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
25
, Propositions 3.26 and 3.28]
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be Banach spaces, and for each
i\in\{1,2\}
let
x_{i}\in S_{X_{i}}
and
a_{i}\in(0,1]
be such that
a_{1}+a_{2}=1
. The following holds.

(1)

If
x_{1}
is super-
\Delta
(respectively, super-Daugavet), then so is
(x_{1},0)\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}X_{2}}
.

(2)

If
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are both super-
\Delta
(respectively super-Daugavet), then so is
(a_{1}x_{1},a_{2}x_{2})\in S_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}X_{2}}
.

3.4.
ccs-Daugavet and ccs-
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
points

The stability of ccs-Daugavet points was first studied in
[
25
]
. In the case of
\oplus_{\infty}
-sums, the following partial characterization can be achieved.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces, let
x\in S_{X}
,
y\in S_{Y}
, and
b\in[0,1]
. The following holds:

(1)

If
x
is ccs-Daugavet, then so is
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
.

(2)

If
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
is ccs-Daugavet for some
b<1
, then so is
x
.

Proof.

(1) was shown in
[
25
, Theorem 3.32]
, so let us see (2).

Let
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
be a ccs-Daugavet point with
\|x\|=1>|b|
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
C=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}S_{i}
be a convex combination of slices of
B_{X}
where for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
S_{i}:=S(B_{x},x_{i}^{*},\delta_{i})
for some
\delta_{i}>0
and some
x_{i}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
. Fix
0<\delta<\varepsilon
such that
|b|<1-\delta
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, set
f_{i}^{*}:=(x_{i}^{*},0^{*})\in S_{X^{*}\oplus_{1}Y^{*}}
,
\widetilde{S_{i}}:=S(B_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y},f_{i}^{*},\delta_{i})
, and
\widetilde{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\widetilde{S_{i}}
is a convex combination of slices of
B_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
. Thus, there is
(z,t)\in\widetilde{C}
with
\|(x,by)-(z,t)\|=\|x-z\|>2-\delta
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, there is
(z_{i},t_{i})\in\widetilde{S_{i}}
such that

z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}z_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad t=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}t_{i}.

Note that
1-\delta_{i}<\operatorname{Re}(f_{i}^{*}(z_{i},t_{i}))=\operatorname{Re}(x_{i}^{*}(z_{i}))
, so
z_{i}\in S_{i}
, and thus,
z\in C
.
∎

As for ccs-
\Delta
points, an easy adaptation of the previous proof provides the following downwards stability result.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces, let
x\in S_{X}
,
y\in S_{Y}
, and
b\in[0,1]
. If
(x,by)\in S_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
is ccs-
\Delta
for some
b<1
, then so is
x
.

Proof.

It suffices to repeat the proof of Lemma
3.10
, just noting that if
x\in C
, then
(x,by)\in\tilde{C}
(this is because for every
(z,by)
and every
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
f_{i}^{*}(z,by)=x_{i}^{*}(z)
). This concludes the proof.
∎

The case of
\oplus_{1}
-sums is more delicate, however. In
[
25
, Proposition 3.30]
it was shown that if either
X
or
Y
has a strongly exposed point, then
X\oplus_{1}Y
fails to have ccs-Daugavet points. In fact, it is enough to assume that one of the spaces has a denting point.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two Banach spaces such that
X
contains a denting point
x\in S_{X}
. Then
X\oplus_{1}Y
has convex combinations of slices around
0
of arbitraryly small diameter. In particular,
X\oplus_{1}Y
fails to contain ccs-Daugavet points.

Proof.

Let
x\in S_{X}
be denting. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a functional
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
and some
0<\delta<\varepsilon
such that
x\in S_{1}:=S(B_{X},x^{*},\delta)
and
\diam(S_{1})<\varepsilon
. Consider
f^{*}:=(x^{*},0^{*})\in S_{X^{*}\oplus_{\infty}Y^{*}}
, and let
C:=\frac{1}{2}S_{1}+\frac{1}{2}S_{2}
, where
S_{1}:=S(B_{X\oplus_{1}Y},f^{*},\delta)
and
S_{2}:=S(B_{X\oplus_{1}Y},-f^{*},\delta)
. Note that
(0,0)\in C
.

Let
(z,t)\in C
. There exist
(z_{1},t_{1})\in S_{1}
and
(z_{2},t_{2})\in S_{2}
such that
(z,t)=\frac{1}{2}(z_{1},t_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(z_{2},t_{2})
. In particular,

\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(z_{1}))=\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((z_{1},t_{1}))>1-\delta\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(z_{2}))=\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((z_{2},t_{2}))<-1+\delta.

This implies in particular that
\min\{\|z_{1}\|,\|z_{2}\|\}\geq 1-\delta
, so
\max\{\|y_{1}\|,\|y_{2}\|\}\leq\delta<\varepsilon
. Finally, note that since
z_{1}\in S(B_{X},x^{*},\delta)
, we have
\|x-z_{1}\|\leq\varepsilon
, and similarly
\|-x-z_{2}\|\leq\varepsilon
by symmetry, so

\|(z,t)\|=\frac{1}{2}(\|z_{1}+z_{2}\|+\|t_{1}+t_{2}\|)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\|z_{1}-x\|+\|x+z_{2}\|+\|t_{1}\|+\|t_{2}\|)<2\varepsilon.

This shows that
\diam(C)\leq 2\varepsilon
.
∎

However, the situation is different for ccs-
\Delta
points, where the following upwards stability result is true regardless of the involved spaces.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
X,Y
be two Banach spaces and
x\in S_{X}
. If
x
is a ccs-
\Delta
point, then
(x,0)\in S_{X\oplus_{1}Y}
is also a ccs-
\Delta
point.

Proof.

Suppose that
x
is a ccs-
\Delta
point and fix
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\widetilde{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\widetilde{S_{i}}
be a convex combination of slices of
B_{X\oplus_{\infty}Y}
containing
(x,0)
where for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
\widetilde{S_{i}}:=S(B_{X\oplus_{1}Y},f_{i}^{*},\delta_{i})
for some
f_{i}^{*}=(x_{i}^{*},y_{i}^{*})\in S_{X^{*}\oplus_{\infty}Y^{*}}
and
\delta_{i}>0
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, there exist
(z_{i},t_{i})\in\widetilde{S_{i}}
such that
(x,0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(z_{i},t_{i})
. But since
x
has norm
1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
, we must have
\|z_{i}\|=1
, and so,
t_{i}=0
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Now, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, set
S_{i}:=S(B_{X},x_{i}^{*},\delta_{i})
. Notice that

\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((z_{i},t_{i})))=\operatorname{Re}(x_{i}^{*}(z_{i}))>1-\delta_{i}.

This means that
x
belongs to the convex combination of slices
C=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}S_{i}
of
B_{X}
, so there must be some
z\in C
such that
\|x-z\|\geq 2-\varepsilon
. Note now that

\operatorname{Re}(x_{i}^{*}(z))=\operatorname{Re}(f^{*}((z,0)))>1-\delta_{i},

so
(z,0)\in\widetilde{C}
, and clearly we have that

\|(z,0)-(x,0)\|=\|z-x\|\geq 2-\varepsilon.

This shows that
(x,0)
is a ccs-
\Delta
point.
∎

We will use this result in a later section to extend some conclusions from
[
25
, Section 4]
. Also, both of the previous results can be adapted to other
\oplus_{p}
-sums if
1\leq p<\infty
.

3.5.
Final remarks on stability results

Stability results can be used to study the points in spaces that can be split as a direct sum
Z=X\oplus Y
. In particular, upwards stability results provide sufficient conditions to get such points
(x,y)\in S_{X\oplus Y}
, whereas downwards stability results provide necessary conditions for such points to exist. Also, note the following.

Remark 3.14
.

The stability results associating
x\in S_{X}
with
(x,0)\in S_{X\oplus Y}
can be used as in
[
25
, Remark 4.4]
to extend the conclusions stated there. More specifically, as a consequence of all these stability lemmas, we have the following:

(1)

If two notions among Daugavet,
\Delta
, super-Daugavet, super-
\Delta
, and ccs-Daugavet can be separated in a Banach space
X
, then they are also distinct in any space that can be expressed as
Z=X\oplus_{\infty}Y
for some Banach space
Y
. This is the case for
C_{0}(L,X)
when
L
has isolated points, for
A(K,X)
when
\Gamma
has isolated points, and for
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
when
\mu
has atoms. This provides a big contrast between the scalar-valued and the vector-valued versions of these spaces.

(2)

If
\Delta
and Daugavet points can be separated in a Banach space
X
, then they are also distinct in any spaces that can be expressed as
Z=X\oplus_{1}Y
for some Banach space
Y
. This is the case for
L_{1}(\mu,X)
spaces when
\mu
has atoms.

4.
Main results

In this section we will study the point notions in several classes of Banach spaces. Given a set
A
in some topological space, we will always denote by
A^{\prime}
the the set of its limit points.

4.1.
Results on
\boldsymbol{C_{0}(L,X)}
spaces

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space and let
X
be a Banach space. As mentioned in the introduction, it is known (see
[
25
, Corollary 4.3]
) that for
f\in S_{C(K)}
, the first six diametral point notions from Definition
1.2
are equivalent, and in fact
f
is any of those types of points if and only if it attains its maximum (norm) at a limit point, that is, if there exists
t\in K^{\prime}
such that
|f(t)|=\|f\|_{\infty}
. If
C(K)
is a complex space or an infinite-dimensional space, then those notions also coincide with
\nabla
points by Proposition
2.2
(note however that this is no longer true in the real case, as for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the real space
\ell_{\infty}^{n}
has
\nabla
points, see
[
16
]
). All these results can also be extended to
C_{0}(L)
spaces where
L
is a locally compact Hausdorff space, as we will see (see also
[
16
, Proposition 3.9 and Remark 3.10]
).

However, the situation is different in the vector-valued case. For
f\in S_{C(K,X)}
, attaining the maximum norm at a limit point implies being a ccs-Daugavet point (see
[
25
, Theorem 4.2]
), but being
\Delta
does not imply this condition in general, as pointed out in
[
25
, Remark 4.4]
(also see Remark
3.14
). In this section we will study the point notions in the more general setting of
C_{0}(L,X)
spaces for a locally compact Hausdorff
L
.

In the next result, we fully characterize Daugavet,
\Delta
, and
\nabla
points of
C_{0}(L,X)
in terms of
L
and
X
. We also characterize super-Daugavet, super-
\Delta
, and ccs-Daugavet points in terms of
L
and finite
\oplus_{\infty}
-sums of
X
, and provide some conditions about ccs-
\Delta
points.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
L
be a locally compact Hausdorff topological space, let
X
a Banach space, and let
f\in S_{C_{0}(L,X)}
.

(1)

If
f
attains its maximum norm at some non-isolated point
t_{0}\in L^{\prime}
(that is, if
\|f(t_{0})\|=\|f\|_{\infty}
), then
f
is a ccs-Daugavet point.

(2)

Otherwise, let
T:=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subset L
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) be the collection of isolated points
t_{k}
where
f
attains its maximum norm, which is finite and non-empty. We have the following.

(a)

f
is super-Daugavet (resp. super-
\Delta
, or ccs-Daugavet) if and only if
f|_{T}
is super-Daugavet (resp. super-
\Delta
, or ccs-Daugavet) in
C_{0}(T,X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n)}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X
.

(b)

f
is Daugavet if and only if there is some
t_{0}\in T
such that
f|_{\{t_{0}\}}
is Daugavet in
C_{0}(\{t_{0}\},X)=X
.

(c)

If
f
is ccs-
\Delta
, then
f|_{T}
is ccs-
\Delta
in
C_{0}(T,X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n)}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X
.

(d)

f
is
\Delta
if and only if either there is some
t_{k}\in T
such that
f|_{\{t_{k}\}}
is
\Delta
in
C_{0}(\{t_{k}\},X)=X
, or for each
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset(1,+\infty)
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=1
, there exists some
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
f|_{\{t_{k}\}}
is
\Delta_{p_{k}}
in
C_{0}(\{t_{k}\},X)=X
.

(e)

If
L\neq T
, or if
C_{0}(L,X)
is a complex Banach space,
f
is
\nabla
if and only if
f
is Daugavet.

(f)

If
L=T
, then
f
is
\nabla
if and only if either
f|_{\{t_{i}\}}
is
\nabla
in
C_{0}(\{t_{i}\},X)=X
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, or there is some
t_{0}\in T
such that
f|_{\{t_{0}\}}
is Daugavet in
C_{0}(\{t_{0}\},X)=X
(and in this case,
f
is Daugavet as well).

Proof.

We show all the cases.

(1)

We adapt the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 4.2]
. Let
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(f(t_{0}))=\|f(t_{0})\|_{X}=1
and
C:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}S_{i}
be a convex combination of slices of
B_{C_{0}(L,X)}
. Pick
g_{i}\in S_{i}
for every
i=1,\cdots m
. Let
K
be a compact subset of
L
that contains
t_{0}
such that
\|f(t)\|_{X}<\varepsilon
for all
t\in L\setminus K
. Since
t_{0}
is a limit point in
L
, we claim that there exists a sequence
(U_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of open subsets containing
t_{0}
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

U_{0}=L

(ii)

\overline{U_{n+1}}
is a compact, proper subset of
U_{n}
for every
n\geq 0

(iii)

\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(f(t))>1-\frac{1}{n+1}
and
\|g_{i}(t)-g_{i}(t_{0})\|_{X}<\frac{1}{n+1}
for every
t\in\overline{U_{n}}
,
i\in\{1,\cdots m\}
, and
n\geq 1
.

We construct this by an inductive process. Let
L=U_{0}
. Since
K\subset U_{0}
is normal and has non-empty interior, there exists an open subset
V_{1}
containing
t_{0}
such that
V_{1}\subset\overline{V_{1}}\subset K\subset U_{0}
. Then by normality, there exists an open subset
W_{1}
containing
t_{0}
such that
\overline{W_{1}}
is a proper subset of
\overline{V_{1}}
. By the continuity of
x^{*}\circ f
and
g_{i}
’s, there exists an open subset
U_{1}
of
W_{1}
containing
t_{0}
such that
\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(f(t))>\frac{1}{2}
and
\|g_{i}(t)-g_{i}(t_{0})\|_{X}<\frac{1}{2}
for every
t\in\overline{U_{1}}
and
i\in\{1,\cdots m\}
. Next, notice that
U_{1}
also contains a compact neighborhood
K_{1}
of
t_{0}
, since
L
is locally compact. Then there exists an open subset
V_{2}
containing
t_{0}
such that
V_{2}\subset\overline{V_{2}}\subset K_{1}\subset U_{1}
. Hence we can also find an open subset
W_{2}
containing
t_{0}
such that
\overline{W_{2}}
is a proper subset of
\overline{V_{2}}
, and so there exists an open subset
U_{2}
containing
t_{0}
such that
\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(f(t))>\frac{2}{3}
and
\|g_{i}(t)-g_{i}(t_{0})\|_{X}<\frac{1}{3}
for every
t\in\overline{U_{2}}
and
i\in\{1,\cdots m\}
. By inductively continuing this procedure, we obtain the desired sequence
(U_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of open subsets containing
t_{0}
that satisfy the conditions (i), (ii), and (iii).

Now, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
F_{n}=\overline{U_{n+1}}\setminus U_{n+2}
. Each
F_{n}
is not only closed but also compact. Moreover,
F_{n}\subset U_{n}\setminus\overline{U_{n+3}}\subset L
. Hence, there exists
\phi_{n}
such that
0\leq\phi_{n}(t)\leq 1
where
\phi_{n}(t)=1
on
t\in F_{n}
and
\phi_{n}(t)=0
on
t\in(L\setminus U_{n})\cup\overline{U_{n+3}}
. Notice that the sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset S_{C_{0}(L)}
converges pointwise to
0
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, define

g_{i,n}(t):=\frac{n}{n+1}(g_{i}(t)-(f(t_{0})+g_{i}(t_{0}))\phi_{n}(t)).

For every
t\in(L\setminus U_{n})\cup\overline{U_{n+3}}
,
\|g_{i,n}(t)\|_{X}=\frac{n}{n+1}\|g_{i}(t)\|_{X}<1
. On the other hand, for
t\in U_{n}\setminus\overline{U_{n+3}}
we have

\|g_{i,n}(t)\|_{X}=\frac{n}{n+1}\|g_{i}(t)-(f(t_{0}))+g_{i}(t_{0}))\phi_{n}(t)\|_{X}\leq\frac{n}{n+1}(1+\|g_{i}(t)-g_{i}(t_{0})\|_{X})\leq 1.

Thus, each
g_{i,n}\in B_{C_{0}(L,X)}
and so
g_{i,n}
converges weakly to
g_{i}
with
n
for each
i=1,\cdots,m
. From the fact that slices are weakly open subsets, there exists
N
such that for every
n\geq N
,
g_{i,n}\in S_{i}
. Furthermore, the function
h_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i,n}
where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
belongs to
C
for every
n\geq N
.

Let
t\in F_{n}\subset\overline{U_{n+1}}\subset\overline{U_{n}}
. From the condition (iii) we have
\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(f(t))\geq 1-\frac{1}{n+1}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i,n}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n+1}(\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t))-1-\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n+1}(\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t))-\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0}))+\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0}))-1-\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n+1}\left(\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0}))-1-\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{0}))\right)+\frac{1}{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+\frac{2}{n+1}.

This implies that

\|f-h_{n}\|\geq\operatorname{Re}\,x^{*}\left(f(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i,n}(t)\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{n+1}+1-\frac{2}{n+1}=2-\frac{3}{n+1},

and so
\|f-h_{n}\|\rightarrow 2
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The proof is finished.

(2)

Let
T
be given, and note that
T
is necessarily finite and non-empty. If
T=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subset L
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, note that
C_{0}(T,X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n)}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X
, and in fact, for each
t\in L
,
C_{0}(\{t\},X)=X
isometrically. Note also that if
L\neq T
, then
f=(f|_{L\setminus T},f|_{T})
in
C_{0}(L,X)=C_{0}(L\setminus T,X)\oplus_{\infty}C_{0}(T,X)
, and
\|f|_{L\setminus T}\|<1
by assumption. We can now finish the proof in view of the stability lemmas.

For (a), it suffices to apply Lemmas
3.8
and
3.10
. (b) follows from Lemma
3.4
. (c) follows from Lemma
3.11
. (d) follows from Lemma
3.5
. Finally, for
\nabla
points, the complex case is shown in Proposition
2.2
, and in the real case, items (e) and (f) follow from Lemma
3.1
. ∎

An immediate consequence of the theorem is the following well-known fact:
C_{0}(L,X)
has Daugavet property if and only if either
L=L^{\prime}
or
X
has Daugavet property. For the backward implication, just use the previous theorem. For the forward implication, it suffices to apply the following well-known lemma, which can be derived from Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given two Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, if
Z=X\oplus_{\infty}Y
has Daugavet property, then so does
X
.

Recall that a Banach space
X
is said to be
locally uniformly rotund
(or just LUR) if

\lim_{k}\|x_{k}-x\|=0\quad\text{whenever}\quad\lim_{k}\left\|\frac{1}{2}(x_{k}+x)\right\|=\lim_{k}\|x_{k}\|=\|x\|.

Equivalently, a Banach space is LUR if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
x\in S_{X}
, the
modulus of local rotundity at
x

\delta_{X}(x,\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{1-\left\|\frac{x+y}{2}\right\|:\,y\in B_{X},\,\|x-y\|\geq\varepsilon\right\}

is positive.

Theorem
4.1
reinforces once more the claim derived from Remark
3.14
: unlike in the scalar-valued case, in
C_{0}(L,X)
spaces the diametral point notions do not coincide in general. However, the situation changes if
X
is LUR. In fact, we have the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
L
be a locally compact Hausdorff space, and let
X
be a Banach space. Given
f\in S_{C_{0}(L,X)}
, consider the following diagram of implications

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{array}[]{c}\|f(t)\|=\|f\|_{\infty}\\
\text{ for some }t\in L^{\prime}\end{array}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ ccs-Daugavet}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ super-Daugavet}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ Daugavet}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ }\nabla}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ ccs-}\Delta}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ super-}\Delta}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f\text{ }\Delta}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{array}[]{c}\|f(t)\|=\|f\|_{\infty}\\
\text{ for some }t\in L^{\prime}\end{array}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(1)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(4)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(3)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(2)}

Then:

(i)

The unnumbered implications are always true in every Banach space.

(ii)

The implication
(1)
always holds in
C_{0}(L,X)
.

(iii)

While the implication
(2)
is not always true in general, it is true if
X
is LUR.

(iv)

The implication
(3)
holds for every
f\in S_{C_{0}(L,X)}
if and only if either
L=L^{\prime}
or
X
has no
\nabla
points.

(v)

The implication
(4)
holds for every
f\in S_{C_{0}(L,X)}
if and only if either
L=L^{\prime}
or
X
has no Daugavet points.

Proof.

Note that (i) is well known, and (ii) has been shown already in Theorem
4.1
. For (iii), given that
X
is LUR, assume to the contrary that
f\in S_{C_{0}(L,X)}
is a
\Delta
-point that does not attain its maximum norm at any limit point of
L
. Denote
F=\{t\in L:\|f(t)\|_{X}=1\}
. The set
F
contains only isolated points of
L
and is nonempty. Fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Since
L
is locally compact, we can find a compact subset
K:=K_{f}\subset L
that contains
F
and such that
\|f(t)\|_{X}<\varepsilon_{0}
for all
t\in L\setminus K
. Because of the compactness of
K
, the set
F
must be finite (otherwise, there exists a limit point in
F
where
f
attains its norm). Also, for each
t\in F
, we can find
x_{t}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
x_{t}^{*}(f(t))=1
by the Hahn-Banach theorem. Since
X
is LUR, take
\delta:=\inf\left\{\delta_{X}\left(f(t),\frac{1}{2|F|}\right):t\in F\right\}>0
.

If
\operatorname{Re}\,x_{t}^{*}(x)\geq 1-\delta
for
x\in B_{X}
, then

\left\|\frac{x+f(t)}{2}\right\|_{X}\geq\operatorname{Re}\,x_{t}^{*}\left(\frac{x+f(t)}{2}\right)\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}<1-\delta_{X}\left(f(t),\frac{1}{2|F|}\right).

This implies that
\|x-f(t)\|_{X}<\frac{1}{2|F|}
.

Let
\varepsilon=1-\max_{t\in L\setminus F}\{\|f(t)\|_{X},\varepsilon_{0}\}>0
and define a bounded linear functional
\psi\in C_{0}(L,X)^{*}
by

\psi(g)=\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{t\in F}x_{t}^{*}(g(t)).

Since
\psi(f)=1
, we have
\|\psi\|=1
. With this bounded linear functional, we define a norm-one, rank-one projection
P:C_{0}(L,X)\rightarrow C_{0}(L,X)
by
P(g):=(\psi\otimes f)(g)=\psi(g)\cdot f
.

To finish the proof, we show that the projection
P
satisfies
\|I-P\|<2
. For a given
g\in B_{C_{0}(L,X)}
, if
t\in L\setminus K
we have

\|g(t)-Pg(t)\|_{X}=\|g(t)-\psi(g)(t)f(t)\|_{X}\leq 1+\|f(t)\|_{X}<2-\varepsilon.

For
t\in K
we consider two cases. First, if
|x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(g(t^{\prime}))|\geq 1-\delta
for
t^{\prime}\in F
there exists
\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\in S_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
|x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(g(t^{\prime}))|=x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime}))\geq 1-\delta
. Hence,
\|\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime})-f(t^{\prime})\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{2|F|}
. So we obtain

\displaystyle\|g(t^{\prime})-Pg(t^{\prime})\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{t\in F}x_{t}^{*}\psi(g)(t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}g(t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime}))f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime})-f(t^{\prime})\|_{X}+\left\|f(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime}))f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|F|}+1-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime}))+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2-\frac{1}{2|F|}.

If
|x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}g(t^{\prime})|<1-\delta
for
t^{\prime}\in F
, then we have

\displaystyle\|g(t^{\prime})-Pg(t^{\prime})\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{t\in F}x_{t}^{*}\psi(g)(t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}g(t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{|F|}x_{t^{\prime}}^{*}(\alpha_{t^{\prime}}g(t^{\prime}))f(t^{\prime})\right\|_{X}+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{|F|}(1-\delta)+\frac{|F|-1}{|F|}=2-\frac{\delta}{|F|}.

Hence, we obtain
\|g-Pg\|\leq\max\{2-\frac{1}{2|F|},2-\frac{\delta}{|F|},2-\varepsilon\}<2
for every
g\in B_{C_{0}(L,X)}
, and so
\|I-P\|<2
. However, since
f
is a
\Delta
-point, this cannot happen by
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
(for complex Banach spaces, see
[
23
, Theorem 2.2]
). Therefore,
f
must attain the norm at a limit point of
L
.

For (iv) and (v) note that if
L=L^{\prime}
, then
C_{0}(L,X)
has Daugavet property, so every point is Daugavet. Otherwise, you can write
C_{0}(L,X)=X\oplus_{\infty}Y
for some Banach space
Y
. Now the conclusion follows from Lemmas
3.1
and
3.4
.
∎

Recall that a Banach space
X
is said to have the
polynomial Daugavet property
if every weakly compact polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}(X;X)
satisfies the Daugavet equation
\|\operatorname{Id}+P\|=1+\|P\|
. If
X
has the polynomial Daugavet property, then the space also has the Daugavet property. It is known that
C_{0}(L,X)
has the polynomial Daugavet property if and only if either
X
does or
L=L^{\prime}
(see
[
8
, Proposition 6.10]
). Thus, as a consequence of Theorem
4.3
, we get the following.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
L
be a locally compact Hausdorff space and
X
be a LUR Banach space. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the polynomial Daugavet property.

(2)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the Daugavet property

(3)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the restricted DSD2P.

(4)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the DD2P.

(5)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the DLD2P.

(6)

C_{0}(L,X)
has the property (
D
).

(7)

L
does not contain isolated points.

Proof.

Note that
\textup{(7)}\Rightarrow\textup{(1)}\Rightarrow\textup{(2)}\Rightarrow\textup{(3)}\Rightarrow\textup{(4)}\Rightarrow\textup{(5)}\Rightarrow\textup{(6)}
were already known. Finally, that
\textup{(6)}\Rightarrow\textup{(7)}
is implicitly shown in the proof of item (iii) from Theorem
4.3
, as the operator
P
that leads to the contradiction is a norm-one rank-one projection.
∎

4.2.
Results on
\boldsymbol{A(K,X)}
spaces

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff topological space, and let
X
be a Banach space. It was shown in
[
22
, Corollary 5.5]
that a point in a uniform algebra
f\in S_{A(K)}
being Daugavet and
\Delta
is equivalent to attaining the maximum norm at a limit point of the Shilov boundary. In the case of
A(K,X)
spaces, a point on
S_{A(K,X)}
attaining the maximum norm at a limit point of the Shilov boundary of the base algebra

is Daugavet, but the point being
\Delta
does not imply this condition in general (since it does not for
C(K,X)
). However, if
X
is uniformly convex (see
[
22
, Theorem 5.4]
), this implication holds. Also, note that uniform algebras over an infinite compact
K
do not have strongly exposed points (see
[

, Main Theorem]
), so in this case
\nabla
and Daugavet notions coincide (see
[
16
, Theorem 2.3]
and also Proposition
2.2
).

In this section we will see that all the results from Subsection
4.1
hold analogously for
A(K,X)
spaces. To do so, we need some preliminary results. First, note that if the Shilov boundary of the base algebra has an isolated point, then we can write
A(K,X)=Y\oplus_{\infty}X
for some Banach space
Y
. This is a consequence of the fact that characteristic functions of isolated points always belong to a uniform algebra.

Lemma 4.5

(
[
22
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
A(K)
be a uniform algebra on a compact Hausdorff space
K
and let
t_{0}
be an
isolated point of the Shilov boundary
\Gamma
of A. Then there exists a function
\phi\in A(K)
such that

\phi(t_{0})=\|\phi\|=1\text{ and }\phi(t)=0\text{ for }t\in\Gamma\setminus\{t_{0}\}.

We need one more tool. Note that the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 4.2]
, like many other geometrical properties of

spaces from the literature, heavily relies on using the Urysohn’s lemma. However, in the context of uniform algebras

, this lemma does not hold in general, since the functions generated by the lemma may not be in
A(K)
(see
[
7
]
). To adapt Theorem
4.1
to
A(K,X)
spaces we will use the following Urysohn-type lemma from
[
7
]
.

Lemma 4.6

(
[
7
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff topological space,
A_{u}(K)
be a (real or complex) uniform algebra over
K
, and let
\Gamma_{0}
be its Choquet boundary. Then, for every open set
U\subset K
with
U\cap\Gamma_{0}\neq\emptyset
and
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
f\in A_{u}(K)
and
t_{0}\in U\cap\Gamma_{0}
such that
f(t_{0})=\|f\|_{\infty}=1
,
|f(t)|<\varepsilon
for every
t\in K\setminus U
, and for all
t\in U
, we have
|f(t)|+(1-\varepsilon)|1-f(t)|\leq 1
.

In order to apply that lemma for this setting, we need a geometric result for which we provide an elementary proof.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then for every complex number
x
in the Stolz region

\operatorname{St}_{\varepsilon}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|+(1-\varepsilon)|1-z|\leq 1\},

there is some
y\in[0,1]\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

|x-y|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{4(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let
x\in\operatorname{St}_{\varepsilon}
. First note that, trivially, the region
\operatorname{St}_{\varepsilon}
is entirely contained in the ellipse given by

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,(1-\varepsilon)|z|+(1-\varepsilon)|1-z|\leq 1\right\},

that is,

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-0|+|z-1|\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right\},

with foci on the points
0
and
1
. Since the major semiaxis measures

M_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{2\left(1-\varepsilon\right)},

the minor semiaxis measures

m_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2\left(1-\varepsilon\right)}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}.

Note that if
a,b,a-b,a+b>0
, then clearly

\sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}=\sqrt{a-b}\sqrt{a+b}>a-b,

so in particular,

m_{\varepsilon}>M_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, this ellipse is entirely contained in the convex hull
E:=\overline{\operatorname{conv}}(B_{1}\cup B_{2})
, where
B_{1}=B(0,m_{\varepsilon})
and
B_{2}=B(1,m_{\varepsilon})
are balls with radius
m_{\varepsilon}
centered on the foci (see Figure
1
). This shows that there exists
y\in[0,1]
such that
|x-y|\leq m_{\varepsilon}
, and in fact this point can be taken explicitly as follows

\begin{cases}y=0,\quad&\text{if }\operatorname{Re}(x)<0,\\
y=\operatorname{Re}(x),\quad&\text{if }\operatorname{Re}(x)\in[0,1],\\
y=1,\quad&\text{if }\operatorname{Re}(x)>1.\end{cases}

Figure 1.
Stolz region (blue) is contained in the ellipse (orange), which is contained in the convex hull (gray) of the small balls centred at the foci (green and red).

We are ready to state and prove the analogous result of Theorem

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

(2)

Otherwise, let

of

(see for instance the argument used in the proof of

Indeed, on the one hand, note that for all

and all

, if

On the other hand, note that for all

\displaystyle\|g_{i}(t)-(f(t_{0})+g_{i}(t_{0}))p_{k}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{k}}+\|g_{i}(t_{0})-(f(t_{0})+g_{i}(t_{0}))p_{k}(t)\|.

\displaystyle\|g_{i}(t_{0})-(f(t_{0})

\displaystyle\leq 1+2R_{k},

since

. Since the finitely many slices

(

belongs to

for all

. Fix

, and let us compute a lower bound for

. Consider the point

\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(f(t_{k})))\geq 1-\frac{1}{n_{k}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(g_{i,k}(t_{k})))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\frac{2}{n_{k}}+2R_{k}}\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{k})-(f(t_{0})+g_{i}(t_{0}))p_{k}(t_{k})))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\frac{2}{n_{k}}+R_{i,k}}\left(-1+\operatorname{Re}(x^{*}(g_{i}(t_{k})-g_{i}(t_{0})))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{2}{n_{k}}+2R_{k}}\left(-1+\frac{1}{n_{k}}\right)=-1+\frac{2R_{k}+\frac{3}{n_{k}}}{1+\frac{2}{n_{k}}+2R_{k}}.

\|f-h_{k}\|_{\infty}\geq\operatorname{Re}\left(x^{*}\left(f(t_{k})-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}g_{i,k}(t_{k})\right)\right)\geq 2-\frac{1}{n_{k}}-\frac{2R_{k}+\frac{3}{n_{k}}}{1+\frac{2}{n_{k}}+2R_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}2.

Since this can be done for each

Once more, it is not true in general that these notions coincide in

is LUR. In fact, we have the following.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff topological space and let
X
be a Banach space. Let
\Gamma
be the Shilov boundary of the base algebra
A
of

. Given
f\in S_{A(K,X)}
, consider the following diagram of implications

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{array}[]{c}\|f(t)\|=\|f\|_{\infty}\\

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 4)}

Then:

(i)

The unnumbered implications are always true in every Banach space.

(ii)

The implication

.

(iii)

While the implication
(2)

(iv)

The implication
(3)

if and only if either

or

Proof.

(i) is well known, and (ii) has been shown in the proof of Theorem
4.8
. To show (iii) when

is LUR, it suffices to follow verbatim the proof of
[

, Theorem 5.4]
, but instead of taking
\delta=\delta_{X}(1/|F|)
, we take

\delta=\inf_{t\in F}\delta_{X}\left(f(t),\frac{1}{|F|}\right),

where

\delta_{X}(x,\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{1-\left\|\frac{x+y}{2}\right\|:\,y\in B_{X},\,\|x-y\|\geq\varepsilon\right\}

is the modulus of local rotundity at

4.3.
Results on
\boldsymbol{L_{\infty}(\mu,X)}
spaces

In this section, we will study the notions in
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
spaces, where
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is a measure space. Recall that if
\mu
is a measure, then
L_{\infty}(\mu)
is isometric to
C(K)
for some compact
K
, and thus in these spaces all diametral point notions (including
\nabla
except in the real and finite-dimensional case) are equivalent. However, to the authors’ knowledge, there are no known conditions that characterize these points in
L_{\infty}(\mu)
spaces. Furthermore, much less is known about the vector-valued
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
spaces, where the situation is often different from the scalar-valued case.

In
[
26
, Theorem 2.5]
, it was shown that if
\mu
is
\sigma
-finite, then the space
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
has Daugavet property if and only if either
\mu
is atomless or
X
has Daugavet property. Here we will provide a characterization of all the
\Delta
, Daugavet, and
\nabla
points in
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
spaces, as well as some conditions regarding the other notions. One of the consequences of our results is that
[
26
, Theorem 2.5]
still holds even when
\mu
is an arbitrary measure, where the forward implication comes from Lemma
4.2
.

First we characterize Daugavet points in
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
spaces.

Theorem 4.12
.

Given
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
a measure space and
X
a Banach space, let
f\in S_{L_{\infty}(\mu,X)}
.

(1)

If for every
\varepsilon>0
there is an infinite collection of disjoint sets
\{B_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of positive measure such that

\|f(t)\|_{X}>1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ for each }t\in B_{n},\text{ and each }n\in\mathbb{N},

then
f
is a Daugavet point.

(2)

Otherwise, the collection of atoms
\{A_{j}\}_{j\in J}
such that
\|f|_{A_{j}}\|=1
for all
j\in J
is finite and non-empty, and
f
is Daugavet if and only if there is
j_{0}\in J
such that
f|_{A_{j_{0}}}
is Daugavet in
L_{\infty}(\mu|_{A_{j_{0}}},X)=X
.

Proof.

(1): We follow the steps of the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 2.5]
. Recall that
f
is a Daugavet point if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
,
B_{L_{\infty}(\mu,X)}=\overline{\co}(\Delta_{\varepsilon}(f))
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
\{B_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a family of pairwise disjoint measurable subsets of
\Omega
. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and choose
k
distinct elements
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
of
\{B_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Fix any
h\in B_{L_{\infty}(\mu,X)}
. For each
1\leq i\leq k
, define
g_{i}:=h\cdot\chi_{\Omega\setminus C_{i}}-f\cdot\chi_{C_{i}}
. If
t\in C_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
, then

\left\|h(t)-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}g_{j}(t)\right\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{k}\|h(t)+f(t)\|_{X}\leq\frac{2}{k},

and if
t\notin\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}C_{i}
, then
h(t)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}g_{j}(t)
. Finally, note that for all
1\leq i\leq k
,
\|f-g_{i}\|>2-\varepsilon
, so
g_{i}\in\Delta_{\varepsilon}(f)
. This shows that
f
is a Daugavet point.

(2): Note that if the condition of (1) is not satisfied, there is no atomless set
S\subset\Omega
such that
\|f|_{S}\|=1
, and the collection
\{A_{j}\}_{j\in J}
cannot be infinite or empty, so
0<|J|<\infty
. Let us denote
B=\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}
. Then we can split
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)=L_{\infty}(\mu|_{\Omega\setminus B},X)\oplus_{\infty}L_{\infty}(\mu|_{B},X)
and
L_{\infty}(\mu|_{B},X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|J|}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X
. Hence, by Lemma
3.4
we obtain the result.
∎

As a consequence, we have the following result.

Corollary 4.13

(see
[
26
, Theorem 2.5]
for
\sigma
-finite measures)
.

Given a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and
X
a Banach space, the space
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
has Daugavet property if and only if either
\mu
is atomless or
X
has Daugavet property.

Proof.

If
\mu
is atomless, then every point
f\in S_{L_{\infty}(\mu,X)}
satisfies the condition of Theorem
4.12
.(1). Hence
L_{\infty}(\mu,X)
has Daugavet property. For the remaining implications it suffices to apply Theorem
4.12
.(2) together with Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Example 4.14
.

Theorem
4.12
.(1) provides an easy characterization of Daugavet points in
L_{\infty}(\mu)
spaces (and thus, of every other notion as well). In particular, a point
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\in S_{\ell_{\infty}}
is a ccs-Daugavet point (equivalently, a

\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{n}|=1
.

Now, we can study all the notions as we did in
C_{0}(L,X)
and

spaces.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space, let
X
be a Banach space, and let

. Suppose that
\Omega
contains an atom
A_{0}
such that

, and denote the distinct atoms by

for some index set

.

(1)

If there is an atomless set

such that

, then

is a ccs-Daugavet point.

(2)

If for every
\varepsilon>0
there is an infinite collection of distinct atoms
\{A_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
such that for every
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have
\|f|_{A_{\lambda}}\|>1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

(3)

Otherwise, let

. We have the following.

(a)

f
is super-Daugavet (resp. super-
\Delta
, or ccs-Daugavet) if and only if
f|_{\mathcal{B}}
is super-Daugavet (resp. super-
\Delta
, or ccs-Daugavet) in
L_{\infty}(\mu|_{\mathcal{B}},X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n)}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X
.

(b)

f

such that
f|_{A_{0}}
is Daugavet in

.

(c)

If
f
is ccs-
\Delta

f|_{\mathcal{B}}
is ccs-
\Delta
in
L_{\infty}(\mu|_{\mathcal{B}},X)=X\oplus_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n)}}{{\cdots}}\oplus_{\infty}X

(d)

f

such that

is
\Delta

such that

is
\Delta_{p_{k}}
in

(e)

If

, or if

is
\nabla
if and only if

is Daugavet.

(f)

If
\Omega=\mathcal{B}
, then

is
\nabla
if and only if either
f|_{A_{i}}
is
\nabla

, or there is some atom

Proof.

To show (1), just note that we can split

Note that (2) is already shown in Theorem

4.4.
Results on
\boldsymbol{L_{1}(\mu,X)}
spaces

In this section, we will study
L_{1}(\mu,X)
spaces, where
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
will always denote a measure space. It is well-known that in
L_{1}(\mu)
, the notions of
\Delta
, Daugavet, super-
\Delta
, and super-Daugavet (and of course
\nabla
in the complex case) points are equivalent. Moreover, these notions can be characterized by the support being atomless (see
[
25
, Proposition 4.7 and Theorem 4.8]
). In the real case, it is also shown that ccs-
\Delta
points also coincide with those notions (
[
25
, Proposition 4.9]
), and that
f\in S_{L_{1}(\mu)}
is
\nabla
if and only if its support is either atomless or a single atom (
[
16
, Proposition 3.7]
). However, these notions do not coincide with ccs-Daugavet because
L_{1}(\mu)
contains ccs-Daugavet points if and only if it has Daugavet property (
[
25
, Proposition 4.12]
).

The situation is different in the vector-valued case: for
f\in S_{L_{1}(\mu,X)}
, having atomless support implies being super-Daugavet (
[
25
, Theorem 4.8]
), but being
\Delta
does not imply this condition or being Daugavet in general (see Remark
3.14
). In this section we will characterize
\Delta
, Daugavet, and
\nabla
points in
L_{1}(\mu,X)
spaces and study the other notions. Let us start with an improvement of the result in the scalar-valued case.

As mentioned, the
real

L_{1}(\mu)
spaces, atomless support implies being ccs-
\Delta

[
25
, Proposition 4.9]
. This proof relies on a result from
[
2
, Theorem]
whose proof only works in the real case. However, we can show further by Lemma
3.13
.

Theorem 4.17

(Extension of
[
25
, Proposition 4.9]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be an arbitrary measure space, and let
X
be a Banach space. If
f\in S_{L_{1}(\mu,X)}
has atomless support, then it is a ccs-
\Delta
point.

Proof.

Write
S:=\supp(f)
. Since
S
is atomless,
L_{1}(\mu|_{S},X)
has Daugavet property, so
f|_{S}
is a ccs-
\Delta
point in this space. Then, by Lemma
3.13
,
f=(f|_{S},0)
is a ccs-
\Delta
point in
L_{1}(\mu,X)=L_{1}(\mu|_{S},X)\oplus_{1}L_{1}(\mu|_{\Omega\setminus S},X)
.
∎

In particular, we can characterize ccs-
\Delta
points in
L_{1}(\mu)
spaces even in the complex case.

Corollary 4.18

(
[
25
, Corollary 4.11]
for the real case)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be an arbitrary measure space and let
f\in S_{L_{1}(\mu)}
. Then the following claims are equivalent:
\supp(f)
is atomless,
f
is a ccs-
\Delta
point,
f
is a super-Daugavet point,
f
is a super-
\Delta
point,
f
is a Daugavet point,
f
is a
\Delta
point.

Now we will characterize
\Delta
, Daugavet, and
\nabla
points in
L_{1}(\mu,X)
spaces.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be any measure space, let
X
be a Banach space, and let
f\in S_{L_{1}(\mu,X)}
.

(1)

If
\supp(f)
is atomless, then
f
is a super-Daugavet and ccs-
\Delta
point.

(2)

Otherwise, let
\mathcal{A}:=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
be the union of all distinct atoms
A_{i}
in the support of
f
. Then the following holds.

(a)

f
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet) if and only if for every
i\in I
, the function
\frac{f|_{A_{i}}}{\|f|_{A_{i}}\|}
is
\Delta
(respectively Daugavet) in
L_{1}(\mu|_{A_{i}},X)=X
.

(b)

In the real case,
f
is
\nabla
if and only if either
f
is Daugavet or there is an atom
A
such that
f=(0,f|_{A})\in L_{1}(\mu|_{\Omega\setminus A},X)\oplus L_{1}(\mu|_{A},X)
and
f|_{A}
is a
\nabla
point in
L_{1}(\mu|_{A},X)=X
.

Proof.

Note that (1) is shown in
[
25
, Theorem 4.8]
and Theorem
4.17
. Also, (2).(b) is deduced from Lemma
3.2
.

Finally, we show (2).(a). Let
\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
be given. Since the image of
f
is essentially separable,
I
is at most countable. Let
S=\supp(f)\setminus\mathcal{A}
, and let
\mathcal{B}:=\Omega\setminus\supp(f)
. Note that we can split
f=\left(f|_{S},0,\|f|_{\mathcal{A}}\|\frac{f|_{\mathcal{A}}}{\|f|_{\mathcal{A}}\|}\right)
in

L_{1}(\mu,X)=L_{1}(\mu|_{S},X)\oplus_{1}L_{1}(\mu|_{\mathcal{B}},X)\oplus_{1}L_{1}(\mu|_{\mathcal{A}},X),

where
L_{1}(\mu|_{\mathcal{A}},X)
is
\ell_{1}(X):=\left[\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}X\right]_{\ell_{1}}
if
I
is infinite and
\left[\bigoplus_{n=1}^{|I|}X\right]_{\ell_{1}}
otherwise.

Finally, if
S\neq\emptyset
, then
f|_{S}=\|f|_{S}\|\frac{f|_{S}}{\|f|_{S}\|}
, and
\frac{f|_{S}}{\|f|_{S}\|}
is a super-Daugavet point in
L_{1}(\mu|_{S},X)
(in particular a Daugavet and a
\Delta
point), so the rest of claims follow from applying Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

In
real

L_{1}(\mu)
spaces, the
\nabla
points are characterized by their supports being either atomless or a single atom. Theorem
4.19
shows that the situation is quite different for the vector-valued case. Indeed, on the one hand, if
X
contains no Daugavet points, then a point being
\nabla
implies the condition on its support, but the converse statement does not necessarily hold, because there may be functions supported on an atom that are not
\nabla

On the other hand, the condition on the support is not necessary in some cases. Indeed, if

contains a Daugavet point and
\Omega
contains at least
2
disjoint sets of positive measure, then there are always

points whose support is neither atomless nor a single atom.

Note that we can also characterize when being

or Daugavet implies that the support is atomless, or when

implies Daugavet.

.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space, and let

be a Banach space. Let

(1)

The support of

contains no atoms.

(2)

f
is a ccs-
\Delta

(3)

f
is a super Daugavet point.

(4)

is a super
\Delta

(5)

(6)

is a
\Delta

Then
(1)
implies every other item, and every item implies

. Moreover,

(a)

If
\mu
is atomless, then

implies
(1)
.

(b)

If
\mu

(i)

(1)
. Otherwise:

(ii)

(iii)

(iv)

Remark 4.22
.

There are classes of Banach spaces with no
\Delta
points. Some examples of such spaces are asymptotically uniformly smooth Banach spaces, asymptotically uniformly convex Banach spaces with reflexivity
[
4
]
, and locally uniformly nonsquare Banach spaces
[
19
,
23
]
(which includes LUR Banach spaces). On the other hand, there exists a reflexive Banach space with super-
\Delta
points
[

, Theorem 3.1]
.

We make a comment on ccs-Daugavet points now. Recall that
S_{L_{1}(\mu)}
contains a ccs-Daugavet point if and only if
L_{1}(\mu)
has Daugavet property, and in that case every point is ccs-Daugavet. We have the following.

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be any measure space, and let
X
be a Banach space containing a denting point. Then the following claims are equivalent:

(1)

L_{1}(\mu)
contains a ccs-Daugavet point.

(2)

L_{1}(\mu,X)
contains a ccs-Daugavet point.

(3)

\mu
is atomless.

Proof.

The equivalence between (1) and (3) was established in
[
25
, Proposition 4.12]
.

(3) implies (2): If
\mu
is atomless, then
L_{1}(\mu,X)
has Daugavet property (see for instance
[
25
, Theorem 4.8]
), so every point is ccs-Daugavet.

(2) implies (3): When
\Omega
consists of a single atom,
L_{1}(\mu,X)=X
cannot contain ccs-Daugavet points (because if a space has a ccs-Daugavet point, in particular all slices have diameter 2, so there cannot be denting points, which is a contradiction to our assumption). So we only need to prove this for the case where
\Omega
contains at least two disjoint subsets of positive measure. If
\mu
contains an atom, then we can split
L_{1}(\mu,X)=X\oplus_{1}Y
for some Banach space
Y
. Since
X
has a denting point,
L_{1}(\mu,X)
cannot have a ccs-Daugavet point in view of Proposition
3.12
.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

The condition on
X
containing a denting point cannot be dropped in general. Indeed, consider a measure space

).

Remark 4.25
.

Let

, let
