Combined Effects of Air Pollution and Changes in Physical Activity With Cardiovascular Disease in Patients With Dyslipidemia
Authors: Hye Jun Kim, Yun Hwan Oh, Sun Jae Park, Jihun Song, Kyuwoong Kim, Daein Choi, Seogsong Jeong, Sang Min Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSedentary behavior elevates cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in patients with dyslipidemia. Increasing physical activity (PA) is recommended alongside pharmacological therapy to prevent CVD, though benefits across environmental conditions are unclear.Methods and ResultsWe analyzed data from 113 918 newly diagnosed patients with dyslipidemia (2009–2012) without prior CVD, sourced from the Korea National Health Insurance Service. Ambient particulate matter (PM) 2.5 and PM10 levels were collected from the National Ambient Air Monitoring System in South Korea. Changes in PA, measured in metabolic equivalents of task–min/wk before and after dyslipidemia diagnosis, were evaluated for associations with air pollution levels and CVD risk using Cox proportional hazards regression. Patients were followed from January 1, 2013, until CVD onset, death, or December 31, 2021. Among patients exposed to low to moderate PM2.5 levels (≤25 μg/m3), increasing PA from inactive to ≥1000 metabolic equivalents of tasks–min/wk was associated with a lower risk of CVD (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.70–0.97]; P for trend=0.022). In high PM2.5 (>25 μg/m3) conditions, increasing PA from inactive and decreasing PA from ≥1000 metabolic equivalents of task–min/wk was associated with reduced (P for trend=0.010) and elevated (P for trend=0.028) CVD risks, respectively. For PM10, increased PA was linked to reduced CVD risk (P for trend=0.002) and decreased PA to elevated risk (P for trend=0.042) in low to moderate PM10 (≤50 μg/m3) conditions, though benefits diminished at high PM10 (>50 μg/m3) exposures.ConclusionsPromoting PA, while considering the high potential cardiovascular risk associated with air pollution, may be an effective intervention against CVD in patients with dyslipidemia.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, cohort study, dyslipidemia, lifestyle behavior, particulate matter, physical activitySubject Categories: Exercise, Epidemiology, Cardiovascular Disease

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the primary cause of global death, resulting in the loss of nearly 17.9 million lives annually. 1 With its rising prevalence, identifying individuals at higher risk of CVD and ensuring that they receive appropriate intervention strategies are important to mitigate the health care burden. 1 , 2 Dyslipidemia stands out as a firmly established risk factor for CVD. The World Health Organization reported that dyslipidemia is associated with >50% of all global instances of ischemic heart disease, contributing to >4 million deaths each year. Substantial evidence suggests that low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) and its oxidized state are pivotal in initiating cardiovascular dysfunctions by promoting atherosclerosis. 3 Elevated levels of LDL‐C and diminished levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) are indeed associated with heart attacks and stroke. 4

A few measures, including pharmacological therapy and lifestyle modifications such as regular physical activity (PA), are advised for patients with dyslipidemia to better manage the disease and prevent cardiovascular complications. 5 , 6 PA is widely recognized for its role in reducing CVD risk by improving the plasma lipoprotein profile, maintaining arterial wall balance, and enhancing insulin sensitivity. 7 Additionally, due to the low rate of statin prescriptions among patients with dyslipidemia, with >50% not receiving this therapy, lifestyle modifications such as PA are recommended as the primary approach for those not on lipid‐lowering medication. 8 Even among patients with dyslipidemia undergoing pharmacological therapy, adherence to lipid‐lowering medications often falls short of achieving an optimal lipid profile level. 9 Moreover, concerns about the potential for statins to increase the risk of diabetes and uncertainties about their effectiveness in reducing CVD risk have been raised by the US Food and Drug Administration. 9

Despite the cardiovascular benefits of PA, minimal differences in health behavior have been reported between those aware and those unaware of a diagnosis among Korean adults. 10 Furthermore, the cardiovascular benefits of PA can vary depending on the atmospheric conditions in which PA occurs. Air pollution, including particulate matter (PM) 2.5, which is known to penetrate deep into the lungs, causes pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction and is considered to be associated with the risk of cardiovascular diseases. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, the cardiovascular outcome after exposure to PM while engaging in PA remains inconclusive. For example, a Korean study involving 1 469 972 young adults in their 20s and 30s found that both increasing PA in a highly polluted area with high levels of PM2.5 or PM10 and reducing PA in an area with low to moderate PM levels can adversely affect cardiovascular health. 15 On the other hand, other studies have shown consistent cardiovascular health benefits of PA across all levels of PM. 16 , 17 Notably, no study has yet explored the association between PA, PM, and CVD risk among patients with dyslipidemia within the Korean population.

Hence, this study sought to examine how PA and exposure to ambient air pollutants collectively affect newly diagnosed patients with dyslipidemia in the Korean population, aiming to fill the existing gap in evidence.

Methods

In accordance with the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) policy, the data used in this study cannot be shared with other researchers or third parties.

Study Population

The NHIS offers comprehensive health insurance to Korean citizens, with a 97% enrollment rate. 17 This coverage extends to a wide range of health care services, including hospital admissions, outpatient visits, and medication prescriptions. 18 The NHIS also offers health screening examinations every 2 years for individuals aged ≥20 years who are employed or self‐employed, as well as for dependents aged ≥40 years. 19 These examinations encompass laboratory tests, anthropometric measurements, and gathering information on lifestyle behaviors through self‐reported questionnaires. Several large‐scale population‐based epidemiological studies have used the NHIS database, and its validity has been elucidated in previous studies. 18 , 20 , 21 , 22

A total of 122 844 adult patients, newly diagnosed with dyslipidemia between 2009 and 2012, underwent 2 consecutive biennial health screening examinations during the same period. Newly diagnosed dyslipidemia was defined as those who had an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code of E78 and prescription records of lipid‐lowering medications, such as statins. Among them, exclusions were made for 183 subjects who passed away before the first follow‐up investigation, 8333 subjects with a history of CVD, and 410 subjects with missing covariate values. The final analytical cohort comprised 113 918 patients with newly diagnosed dyslipidemia who were followed up from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2021 (Figure 1).

The Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital approved this study (IRB number: E‐2204‐038‐1314). Informed consents were waived, as the NHIS cohort database adheres to strict confidentiality protocols and anonymizes patient information.

Assessment of Individual Exposure to the Air Pollutants

The National Ambient Air Monitoring System in South Korea, accessible at https://www.airkorea.or.kr, supplies data regarding ambient concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10. Daily air pollution data are gathered from up to 280 atmospheric monitoring sites across the country. The distribution of these monitoring sites is determined by factors such as the population size, residential area location, emission sources, and the specific area targeted for monitoring.

Typically, each administrative residential district within metropolitan cities has at least 1 designated atmospheric monitoring station. However, PM2.5 monitoring was restricted to 3 major metropolitan cities (Seoul, Incheon, and Busan) between 2009 and 2012. As a result, a cohort of 113 918 patients residing in 50 administrative residential districts within these cities was considered, covering those monitored by atmospheric stations.

Air pollution information for administrative residential districts obtained from the National Ambient Air Monitoring System was linked to individual patients using residential district codes of South Korea. This process involved gathering yearly information regarding patients' residences and aligning them with the annual average PM2.5 and PM10 levels specific to their residential districts for each year. This detailed method ensured precise evaluation of air pollution exposure among migrant populations. Employing a methodology established in previous studies, we computed the 4‐year average cumulative levels of PM2.5 and PM10 for each individual. 23 , 24 This entailed averaging the yearly levels of PM2.5 and PM10 from 2009 to 2012, corresponding to the residential district of each patient, thus estimating the individual's air pollution exposure level. The degree of exposure to the air pollutants was categorized into 2 groups (low to moderate and high) using the average daily air pollutant concentrations, in accordance with the interim targets set by the World Health Organization's global air quality guidelines for PM10 and PM2.5. 25

Assessment of Changes in PA

PA was assessed through self‐administered questionnaires distributed in 2 consecutive health examinations, using a recall method covering the past 7 days known for its reliability in monitoring population PA levels. 26 By extracting responses from the NHIS questionnaires during 2 consecutive biennial health screening examinations (2009–2010 and 2011–2012), we obtained weekly frequencies of light‐intensity (such as leisure walking for ≥30 min/d), moderate‐intensity (such as slow cycling for ≥30 min/d), and vigorous‐intensity (such as running for ≥20 min/d) PA.

Assigning metabolic equivalent of task (MET) ratings of 2.9, 4.0, and 7.0 to light, moderate, and vigorous PA, respectively, as established in previous research, we computed the total energy expenditure of PA in MET minutes per week (MET‐min/wk). 15 This calculation involved summing up the MET values across the 3 types of PA, taking into account their frequency, duration, and intensity.

Individuals were grouped into 4 categories: 0, 1 to 499, 500 to 999, and ≥1000 MET‐min/wk for each health screening examination. Changes in PA levels were assessed by subtraction of MET‐min/wk values obtained at the first health screening examination from the values recorded at the subsequent health screening examination.

Cardiovascular Disease Events

The ICD‐10 codes of the NHIS database were used to detect occurrences of CVD, encompassing coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. Adhering to the guidelines by the American Heart Association, we used specific ICD‐10 codes (I20–I25 and I60–I69) to identify instances of CVD, with a focus on pinpointing cases of CHD (I20–I25) and stroke (I60–I69). 27 To ensure the inclusion of genuine CVD cases, we defined CVD outcomes in individuals who had been hospitalized for a minimum of 2 days, during which relevant ICD‐10 codes associated with CVD were documented.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the time to CVD, and the secondary outcomes were time to CHD and stroke. Individuals with incomplete information on follow‐up and covariate data were excluded from the final analytic cohort. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages, while continuous variables, which follow a normal distribution, are presented as mean values along with their standard deviations. To assess the association between combined exposure to air pollutants and changes in PA with the risk of CVD, Cox proportional hazards regression model stratified by PM2.5 and PM10 levels was employed. Adjustments were made for various factors including age (years, continuous), sex (male and female), district (Seoul, Busan, and Incheon), household income (upper half and lower half), waist circumference (cm, continuous), cigarette smoking (never, former, and current), alcohol intake (yes or no), systolic blood pressure (mm Hg, continuous), fasting serum glucose (mg/dL, continuous), total cholesterol (mg/dL, continuous), and Charlson comorbidity index (0, 1, and ≥2). The proportional hazards assumptions were not violated from the Schoenfeld residual tests.

Restricted cubic splines were used to examine the risk of CVD associated with continuous changes in MET‐min/wk, with knots placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles. Subsequent restricted cubic splines were generated for newly diagnosed patients with dyslipidemia with specific lipid profile criteria (HDL‐C <40 mg/dL and LDL‐C ≥160 mg/dL). For the sensitivity analyses, we assessed the risk of CVD according to the changes in PA, after considering the 1‐year average air pollutant levels for 2009 and 2012, respectively. In addition, we also evaluated the association of changes in PA (MET‐min/wk) with the risk of CVD according to changes in air pollution levels from 2009 to 2012.

All participants were stratified according to the level of PM2.5 or PM10 exposure. Further stratified analyses of the Cox proportional hazards regression model were conducted on the basis of sex, obesity (body mass index ≥25 kg/m2), cigarette smoking, Charlson comorbidity index, hypertension, and diabetes among physically inactive individuals at the health screening examination period I (2009–2010) to evaluate the effects of PA increase. Decrease of PA from ≥1000 MET‐min/wk was also assessed. P values for interaction were evaluated for the PA strata (0, 1–499, 500–999, and ≥1000 MET‐min/wk) and variables used for the stratification against the risk of CVD. A significance level of 2‐sided P values <0.05 was adopted. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used for all analyses, data collection, and data‐mining procedures.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the patients with newly diagnosed dyslipidemia are presented in Table 1. There were 60 654 male and 53 264 female patients with a mean±SD age of 53.0 (±12.0) years. Patients were slightly more physically inactive during the health screening examination in period I (2009–2010) compared with period II (2011–2012). The mean±SD levels of total cholesterol, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C were 208.7 (±40.5) mg/dL, 52.9 (±14.7) mg/dL, and 124.8 (±39.1) mg/dL, respectively. Hypertension was present in 46 441 (40.8%) patients, and diabetes was present in 29 294 (25.7%) patients. Additionally, more than one‐fourth of the patients (n=32 001 [28.1%]) had a Charlson comorbidity index ≥2.

Table 2 illustrates the association of changes in PA categories with the risk of CVD, stratified on the basis of the level of air pollution. For those who were exposed to low or moderate PM2.5 (≤25 μg/m3), those who increased PA from physically inactive to ≥1000 MET‐min/wk had a lower risk of CVD (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.70–0.97]) as compared with physically inactive patients in both health screening examination periods (P for trend=0.022). Among patients exposed to high PM2.5 (>25 μg/m3), increasing PA from physically inactive was associated with a reduced risk of CVD (P for trend=0.010). In addition, decreasing PA from ≥1000 MET‐min/wk was associated with a higher risk of CVD (P for trend=0.028).

Figure 2 shows restricted cubic spline curves of the relationship between changes in PA and CVD across different levels of air pollution. The reduced risks of CVD, CHD, and stroke were more noticeable among patients exposed to low to moderate levels of PM2.5 and PM10 compared with those exposed to high levels of these pollutants. Analyzing the association of changes in PA with CVD risk in newly diagnosed patients with dyslipidemia with specific lipid profiles (HDL‐C<40 mg/dL, Figure S1; LDL‐C≥160 mg/dL; Figure S2) revealed that increasing PA is associated with a lower risk of CVD, while decreasing PA is associated with a higher risk of CVD, although these findings were not statistically significant except for a notable increase in CVD risk among patients with LDL‐C ≥160 mg/dL exposed to high PM10 (>50 μg/m3) and decreased PA. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess 1‐year average air pollution levels in 2009 (Figure S3) and 2012 (Figure S4) among patients with newly diagnosed dyslipidemia in relation to the risk of CVD. These analyses supported the primary findings, which were evaluated using a 4‐year average exposure to the air pollutants. Additionally, the association between changes in PA and the risk of CVD according to changes in air pollution levels from 2009 to 2012 are shown in Figure S5. Generally, an increase in PA was associated with a lower risk of CVD, while a decrease in PA was associated with a higher risk of CVD regardless of the changes in air pollution levels.

Stratified analyses regarding the combined effects of air pollution and increased PA from physical inactivity state on incident CVD are presented in Table S3. Among patients exposed to low to moderate levels of PM2.5, individuals with obesity exhibited a lower risk of CVD when PA increased to ≥1000 MET‐min/wk. In contrast, patients without obesity had a lower risk of CVD when PA increased to 500 to 999 MET‐min/wk (P for interaction=0.042). Significant interactions were observed for patients exposed to low to moderate PM10, indicating that those with hypertension (P for trend<0.001) and diabetes (P for trend<0.001) significantly benefited from an increase in PA. However, the risk of CVD was not significantly reduced for those without hypertension (P for trend=0.077, P for interaction=0.036) and diabetes (P for trend=0.357, P for interaction=0.049). As for the subgroup analyses examining the combined effects of air pollution and changes in PA on subsequent CVD among newly diagnosed patients with dyslipidemia who engaged in at least 1000 MET‐min/wk during health examination period I (2009–2010), there were no variables with a significant interaction, indicating that decreasing PA from ≥1000 MET‐min/wk was associated with either a higher risk of CVD or had no significant effect on CVD risk (Table S4).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study reaffirmed sedentary behavior as a CVD risk factor among patients with dyslipidemia in the Korean population. Notably, it revealed that a reduction in PA is associated with an elevated risk of CVD, while an increase in PA is associated with a decreased risk of CVD in both low to intermediate and high PM10 and PM2.5 levels. However, the protective effect of increased PA against CVD risk was attenuated in areas with high PM10 and PM2.5 levels.

Previous studies examining the combined effect of PA and PM on CVD risk have yielded conflicting results. Kim et al 15 found a U‐shaped relationship between PA and CVD risk in young Korean adults after exposure to PM, indicating that a significant increase in PA in high PM2.5 and PM10 environments could negatively affect cardiovascular health. Another Korean study involving 5‐year cancer survivors with a mean age in their 50s and 60s showed that engaging in moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA) at least 5 times per week in high PM2.5 areas did not show a reduction in CVD risk, whereas engaging in MVPA at least 3 times per week in high PM2.5 areas, MVPA at a maximum of 2 times per week in low PM2.5 areas, and MVPA at a maximum of 2 times per week in high PM2.5 areas were associated with a higher risk of CVD. 28 Conversely, Kim et al 16 reported MVPA of at least 5 times per week to be associated with a reduced CVD risk across all levels of PM10 and PM2.5 among healthy individuals aged in their 50s, suggesting that the advantages of PA may surpass potential adverse effects even with increased exposure to PM. Elliott et al 17 also suggested a robust association between higher PA and a lower CVD risk across all levels of PM2.5 exposure among US women with an average age in their 60s, aligning with the findings in our study. These discrepancies of results are likely attributed to differences in characteristics within the study population, such as age group and their health conditions. To date, no study has investigated the combined association of PA and PM exposure on CVD in patients with dyslipidemia within the Korean population.

Generally, inhaling PM2.5 and PM10 is known to cause inflammation in the respiratory system and systemic inflammation throughout the body. 11 Also, increased oxidative stress, endothelium dysfunction, and disruption of the autonomic nervous system may be induced by exposure to PM. 12 Consequently, damaged cells due to oxidative stress, increased risk of blood clotting, and greater heart rate variability may lead to the development of atherosclerosis, heart attacks, stroke, arrhythmias, or other cardiovascular health issues. 12

Nevertheless, the beneficial effect of PA on CVD risk should be acknowledged. While high plasma concentrations of LDL‐C and triglyceride, along with low HDL‐C levels, are recognized as established risk factors for CVD, 29 PA can mitigate CVD risk by improving these lipid profiles. It is known that engaging in regular PA leads to HDL‐C increase, triglyceride decrease, or beneficial effects on total cholesterol and its LDL and very‐low‐density lipoprotein fractions independent of changes in body weight and BMI. 30 These effects may be driven by improved lipid metabolism. For example, greater PA is associated with heightened activity of lipoprotein lipase activity, a key enzyme in triglyceride breakdown. 31 Increased lipoprotein lipase activity promotes the hydrolysis of triglycerides in very‐low‐density lipoprotein and chylomicrons, releasing fatty acids for energy use and reducing circulating triglyceride levels. 32 Also, PA stimulates the activity of hormone‐sensitive lipases, predominantly found in adipose tissue. 33 Hormone‐sensitive lipase holds a pivotal role in decomposing triglycerides stored in adipocytes, by enhancing the efficient conversion of triglycerides into glycerides and free fatty acids, thereby facilitating their mobilization from the tissues. 34 In addition, PA can enhance insulin sensitivity, as increased muscle contractions activate adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase. 35 Adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase activation improves glucose uptake by stimulating glucose transporter proteins, translocating them to the cell membrane and enhancing metabolism in skeletal muscles. 35 These can improve insulin sensitivity, contributing to healthier lipid profiles. 36

Besides impacting lipid profiles, PA has anti‐inflammatory effects that can enhance both metabolic and cardiovascular health. 37 Chronic inflammation is associated with the development of atherosclerosis and unfavorable lipid profiles. 38 PA has been demonstrated to decrease the levels of proinflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor‐α and interleukin‐6, while elevating the presence of anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐10. 39 For example, the increased interleukin‐6 levels after muscle contraction during PA stimulate the generation of immune regulatory substances like the interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist and interleukin‐10, alongside the suppression of tumor necrosis factor‐α by monocytes and macrophages. 33 In addition, the release of myokines such as interleukin‐15 and interleukin‐7 from skeletal muscles during PA may enhance immune cell activity, such as naïve T‐cell function and lymphocyte proliferation. 39 These effects may help better manage inflammation and improve overall cardiovascular health. 40 Moreover, PA improves cardiorespiratory fitness through increasing mitochondrial content and myoglobin desaturation in skeletal muscles. 41 These can enhance the muscle's oxidative capacity for aerobic metabolism, reducing the strain on the heart, improving endurance and cardiovascular fitness. 41

Thus, despite the adverse effects of PM on CVD, the health benefits of PA may alleviate or surpass adverse effects of PM on cardiopulmonary function, such as deposition of PM2.5 and PM10 on heart or pulmonary oxidative stress and systematic inflammation induced following inhalation of PM. Consequently, engaging in more PA, even in areas with high levels of PM, may outweigh the detrimental effects of air pollutants on the heart. This suggests a protective role of PA against the risk of CVD across all levels of PM, as indicated by the findings of our study.

This study has several limitations. First, PM concentrations were determined using data from air quality stations, which limits the ability to precisely assess individual‐level exposure, taking into account factors such as specific locations, time spent indoors versus outdoors, and the use of personal protective equipment. Second, we used older data for the evaluation of PM levels to capture long‐term effects of air pollution against the risk of cardiovascular disease. PM exposure may have changed significantly over the past decade due to stricter regulations. In addition, other air pollution components were also not considered in this study. Third, PA levels were assessed through questionnaires, introducing the potential for recall bias. Furthermore, information on changes in MET‐min/wk relied on data from biennial health screening examinations, which hindered precise consideration of the specific intensity and alterations in PA during the follow‐up period. Fourth, seasonal trends, ambient temperature, and relative humidity could not be adjusted due to data limitations. Future studies considering other meteorological factors as well as PM are warranted. Fifth, being a retrospective study, it is susceptible to issues of reverse causality. Nonetheless, we performed sensitivity analyses by evaluating 1‐year average air pollution levels instead of 4‐year average exposures and considering the changes in air pollution levels from 2009 to 2012, reinforcing our primary findings. Finally, the study's reliance on the Korean population may restrict the generalizability of the results. More studies encompassing diverse populations from various countries and ethnic backgrounds are needed to enhance the applicability of the findings.

In conclusion, higher PA levels were associated with a decreased risk of incident CVD, while a decrease in PA was associated with an increased risk of developing CVD among individuals with dyslipidemia. This relationship exhibited a consistent trend in both low to intermediate and high PM10 or PM2.5 levels; however, the beneficial impact of PA against CVD risk was lowered in highly polluted areas. Implementing proactive measures to promote PA, with a consideration of the high potential cardiovascular risk associated with air pollution, could potentially serve as an effective intervention strategy against CVD for individuals with dyslipidemia.
