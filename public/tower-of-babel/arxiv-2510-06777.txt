Strong Dinatural Transformations and Generalised Codensity Monads Volume: NN Journal: Electronic Notes in Theoretical Informatics and Computer Science Email: mpirog@cs.uni.wroc.pl Email: f.sieczkowski@hw.ac.uk
Maciej Piróg a, Filip Sieczkowski b

Abstract
We introduce dicodensity monads : a generalisation of pointwise codensity monads generated by functors to monads generated by mixed-variant bifunctors. Our construction is based on the notion of strong dinaturality (also known as Barr dinaturality), and is inspired by denotational models of certain types in polymorphic lambda calculi – in particular, a form of continuation monads with universally quantified variables, such as the Church encoding of the list monad in System F. Extending some previous results on Cayley-style representations, we provide a set of sufficient conditions to establish an isomorphism between a monad and the dicodensity monad for a given bifunctor. Then, we focus on the class of monads obtained by instantiating our construction with hom-functors and, more generally, bifunctors given by objects of homomorphisms (that is, internalised hom-sets between Eilenberg–Moore algebras). This gives us, for example, novel presentations of monads generated by different kinds of semirings and other theories used to model ordered nondeterministic computations.



keywords

Dinatural transformations, Cayley representations, monads, list monad

1
Introduction

Consider the continuation monad for some fixed answer
O
, given at
A
as:

(A\to O)\to O

(1)

This can be read as a type in a type theory (in which case both
A
and
O
are types) or categorically, as an object in a category
\mathscr{A}
, where it denotes the
\mathscr{A}[A,O]
-fold product of
O
(a power). Continuation monads occupy a special place in the space of all monads, which can be seen through their possible generalisations in different settings.

One such generalisation, on the categorical side, is the codensity monad
[
7
]
on a category
\mathscr{A}
induced by a functor
G:\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
, which we denote
\mathfrak{T}^{G}
. We discuss this monad in general in Section
4
, but to see its connection with the continuation monad, it suffices to say that if
\mathscr{A}=\mathbf{Set}
, then
\mathfrak{T}^{G}A
is given by the set of transformations natural in
X
of the following type:

{(GX)}^{A}\to GX

(2)

Broadly speaking, the codensity monad
\mathfrak{T}^{G}
approximates the monad
GF
generated by a pair of adjoint functors, even if
G
’s left adjoint
F
does not exist (see
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
20
]
for examples). Moreover, if
G
does have a left adjoint
F
, the monad
\mathfrak{T}^{G}
is isomorphic to
GF
. Thus, codensity monads give alternative presentations of monads, which is used, for example, in functional programming to boost performance of monadic computations
[
5
,
23
,
24
]
.

On the side of type theory, say in System F, one can always give a monadic structure
(with laws satisfied up to
\beta\eta
-equivalence) to the following family of types varying in
A
, where
T
is any type that can contain the variable
X
:

\forall X.(A\to T)\to T

(3)

In particular,
X
can have both positive and negative occurrences in
T
, which makes
T
– at least when only the basic type formers are involved – a mixed-variant bifunctor. One example of such a type is the Church encoding of the list monad, given by the type
\forall X.(A\to X\to X)\to X\to X
.

This motivates the notion of
dicodensity monad
introduced in Section
3
: a monad
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
that generalises codensity monad to a monad generated by a mixed-variant bifunctor
R:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
. The definition of
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
uses the concept of
strong dinaturality

[
4
]
(also known as
Barr dinaturality
, detailed in Section
2
), which is one of a few possible generalisations of the usual notion of natural transformation between functors to the case of mixed-variant bifunctors. On
\mathbf{Set}
, given a bifunctor
R:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbf{Set}
, we define
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
to be the set of transformations strongly dinatural in
X
of the following type:

(RXX)^{A}\to RXX

(4)

On an arbitrary category,
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
is given by particular
objects
of strong dinatural transformations defined via a universal property.

Our construction generalises the codensity monad of a functor
G:\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
in two aspects. Not only
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
instantiates to
\mathfrak{T}^{G}
with
RXY=GY
(Section
4
), but it also enojys a similar construction as a limit over an appropriately generalised diagram (Section
5
).
This construction also generalises our previous results
[
18
]
, where we studied computational effects captured by
\mathbf{Set}
-monads given by the formula (
4
) for bifunctors
R:\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
of the shape
RXY=PX\to Y
for a polynomial functor
P
.

For a particular bifunctor
R:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
, two questions are of interest. First, we consider whether
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
exists, which may not be obvious — for instance, for
\mathscr{A}=\mathbf{Set}
, the collection of transformations (
4
) needs to be small enough to form a set for every
A
. Second, we may ask if
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
has a more direct characterisation, specifically whether it is isomorphic to a given particular monad. Just as in the case of codensity monads, answers to these questions are not always trivial.

In Section
6
, we give a set of sufficient conditions for
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
to exist and be isomorphic to the monad
UF
given by a pair of adjoint functors. Roughly speaking, these conditions state that the bifunctor
R
carries a structure that is a form of representation of objects from the codomain of
F
in the base catgory
\mathscr{A}
. For example, the monad
\mathfrak{C}^{\mathbf{Set}[{-},{=}]}
generated on
\mathbf{Set}
by the hom-functor
\mathbf{Set}[{-},{=}]
is isomorphic to the list monad (the free monad of the theory of monoids), while
\mathbf{Set}[A,A]
(the set of all functions
A\to A
) together with composition and identity is the usual Cayley representation of monoids with carrier
A
. The sufficient conditions additionally require the abstraction and representation morphisms to be strongly dinatural, and to appropriately commute with strong dinaturals.

Finally, in Section
7
, we consider a particular class of bifunctors that encompasses hom-functors (as in the example above), for which we simplify the construction of the previous section. This is achieved by generalising hom-functors to bifunctors given by objects of homomorphisms
[
9
]
, which are internalised hom-sets of Eilenberg–Moore categories. All these bifunctors are examples of “arrow” types on different levels of generality, for which the sufficient conditions given in Section
6
are a form of generalised Cayley representation of monoids.

1.2
Notation

When the types are known, we often omit subscripts in natural transformations. The letter
R
stands for a bifunctor, and we omit parentheses, i.e., we write
RXY
for what we would usually write as
R(X,Y)
, and
R(FX)(GY)
for
R(FX,GY)
. We use
\mathscr{A}[A,B]
for the hom-functor of a category
\mathscr{A}
, while
\theta
always stands for a strong dinatural transformation. We use

to define functions. We write

for exponential objects and internal homs, but

is sometimes used to stress that the exponential lives in

. Similarly,
\cdot
is used for composition of morphisms, but we sometimes use

2
Dinaturality and Strong Dinaturality

In this section, we give the necessary background on (strong) dinatural transformations between mixed variant bifunctors, which appear in models of (restricted) parametric polymorphism. The classic generalisation of naturality to mixed-variant bifunctors is
dinaturality
, introduced by Dubuc and Street
[
3
]
. The general definition is as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Given two bifunctors
G,H:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
, a
dinatural transformation

\xi:G\to H
is a collection of
\mathscr{A}
-morphisms
\xi_{X}
indexed by objects of
\mathscr{D}
, such that for all morphisms
f:X\to Y
, the following diagram commutes:

GYX
GXX
HXX
HXY
GYY
HYY
GfX
GYf
\xi_{X}
HXf
\xi_{Y}
HfY

Note that in the categorical definition the domain of bifunctors
G
and
H
can be different than their range — this is more general than the case in System F, where we only have a single universe of types.

While this definition is often useful, it is also rather weak. Dinatural transformations do not compose in the general case, which is a property necessary for our constructions. Moreover the dinaturality and parametricity do not necessarily correspond directly. For instance, the type of Church numerals in System F,
\forall X.(X\to X)\to(X\to X)
, is inhabited exactly by natural numbers, while the collection of dinatural transformations
\xi_{X}:X^{X}\to X^{X}
in
\mathbf{Set}
is a proper class (see Paré and Román
[
16
]
for a thorough discussion). One way to strengthen dinaturality is the following definition:

Definition 2.2
.

Given two bifunctors
G,H:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
, a
strong dinatural transformation
(also called
Barr
-dinatural transformation
[
16
]
)
\theta:G\to H
is a collection of
\mathscr{A}
-morphisms
\theta_{X}
indexed by objects of
\mathscr{D}
, such that for all morphisms
g_{1}:Z\to GXX
,
g_{2}:Z\to GYY
, and
f:X\to Y
, if the inner square in the following diagram commutes, the entire diagram commutes:

Z
GXX
HXX
HXY
GYY
HYY
GXY
g_{1}
g_{2}
\theta_{X}
HXf
\theta_{Y}
HfY
GXf
GfY

We denote the collection of strong dinatural transformations from
G
to
H
as
\mathbf{SDin}[G,H]
.

Not all System F types of the shape
\forall X.F\to G
have this property – Vene
et al.

[
22
]
give a set of syntactic sufficient conditions for this to be the case (broadly speaking: when the functors have no variables in double-negative positions). However, strongly dinatural transformations are well-behaved: they compose, and the collection of strongly dinatural transformations
\xi_{X}:X^{X}\to X^{X}
in
\mathbf{Set}
is isomorphic to the set of natural numbers (a direct proof was given by Paré and Román
[
16
]
, but it is also a simple corollary of our Example
6.5
). So, while famously there are no set-theoretic models of System F
[
19
]
, we can interpret a class of sufficiently simple types even in
\mathbf{Set}
, and, of course, generalise this to other categories.

3
Dicodensity Monads

Assume locally small categories
\mathscr{A}
(‘ambient’) and
\mathscr{D}
(‘domain’). In this section, we construct the dicodensity monad
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
on
\mathscr{A}
induced by a functor
R:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
. The loose intuition is that it is the monad given type-theoretically by
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A=\forall X\in\mathscr{D}.(A\to RXX)\to RXX
. In
\mathbf{Set}
, it is given by the set of transformations
(RXX)^{A}\to RXX
strongly dinatural in
X
.

We define this monad in the form of a Kleisli triple (see Manes
[
12
]
under the name ‘clone forms’, or Moggi
[
14
, Definition 1.2]
), which consists of an assignment of objects
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
, the unit of the monad
\eta_{A}:A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
, and lifting of morphisms
f:A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}B
to
f^{*}:\mathfrak{C}^{R}A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}B
subject to appropriate conditions. We prefer this format, because the usual ‘monoid’ form of
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
is quite cumbersome to work with in this case, as the argument
A
appears in a double-negative position.

First, we define the object assignment. For this, we note that for a morphism
f:A\to B
in
\mathscr{A}
, the collection of morphisms
\mathscr{A}[f,RXX]
indexed by
X
is a strong dinatural transformation
\mathscr{A}[B,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]
. Since strong dinatural transformations compose, this means that for all objects
C
in
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathbf{SDin}[\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}],\mathscr{A}[B,R{-}{=}]]
is contravariantly functorial in
B
with the action on a morphism
f:A\to B
given as follows:

\theta\mapsto\mathscr{A}[f,R{-}{=}]\cdot\theta:\mathbf{SDin}[\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}],\mathscr{A}[B,R{-}{=}]]\to\mathbf{SDin}[\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}],\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]]

We use this functor in the definition of the object assignment
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{D}
, and
R
be as above. For an object
A
in
\mathscr{A}
, we call an object
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
in
\mathscr{A}
an
A
-continuation
if there exists the following isomorphism natural in
C
:

\mathscr{A}[C,\mathfrak{C}^{R}A]\cong\mathbf{SDin}[\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}],\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}]]

(5)

Of course, such an object may or may not exist for a given
A
, and if it does, it is defined up to isomorphism. In the remainder, we assume a particular choice of an
A
-continuation for an object
A
(provided at least one exists), denoted
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
.

We now spell out some details of Definition
3.1
. We write
\lceil\text{-}\rceil
for the isomorphism from left to right, that is, for an
\mathscr{A}
-morphism
f:C\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
, we obtain a strong dinatural transformation
\lceil f\rceil:\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}]
. In the other direction, for a strong dinatural transformation
\theta:\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}]
, we get
\lfloor\theta\rfloor:C\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
. Using this notation, the naturality conditions can be stated as follows:

•

For morphisms
g:B\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
and
f:C\to B
in
A
, it is the case that
\mathscr{A}[f,R{-}{=}]\cdot\lceil g\rceil=\lceil g\cdot f\rceil
,

•

For a strong dinatural transformation
\theta:\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[C,R{-}{=}]
and an
\mathscr{A}
-morphism
f:B\to C
, it is the case that
\lfloor\theta\rfloor\cdot f=\lfloor\mathscr{A}[f,R{-}{=}]\cdot\theta\rfloor
.

Remark 3.2
.

Given a strong dinatural transformation
\theta:\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[B,R{-}{=}]
, if we unfold the definition of the hom-functor, the condition for strong dinaturality of
\theta
can be stated as follows: For all
f:X\to Y
in
\mathscr{D}
,
g_{1}:A\to RXX
and
g_{2}:A\to RYY
in
\mathscr{A}
, if
RXf\cdot g_{1}=RfY\cdot g_{2}
, then
RXf\cdot\theta_{X}(g_{1})=RfY\cdot\theta_{Y}(g_{2})
.

Using the Yoneda lemma and the fact that currying and uncurrying with a constant preserves strong dinaturality, it is not difficult to show that if
\mathscr{A}=\mathbf{Set}
and
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
exists for a set
A
, then
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
is (up to isomorphism) the set of transformations of the type
(RXX)^{A}\to RXX
strongly dinatural in
X
.

We can now define the rest of the monad structure on
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
:

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
exists for all objects
A
in
\mathscr{A}
, the assignment
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
can be given a monad structure defined by the unit
\eta
and the Kleisli extension
(\text{-})^{*}
as follows:

•

\eta_{A}=\lfloor\mathsf{id}_{\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]}\rfloor:A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
,

•

For
f:A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}B
, we define
f^{*}=\lfloor\Phi^{A}\cdot\lceil f\rceil\rfloor:\mathfrak{C}^{R}A\to\mathfrak{C}^{R}B
, where
\Phi^{A}=\lceil\mathsf{id}_{\mathfrak{C}^{R}A}\rceil:\mathscr{A}[A,R{-}{=}]\to\mathscr{A}[\mathfrak{C}^{R}A,R{-}{=}]
.

As the first example to illustrate this theorem, we consider the simplest setting: when
R
is a constant
\mathbf{Set}
-bifunctor. In such a case, our construction instantiates to the usual continuation monad:

Example 3.4
.

We consider
\mathscr{A}=\mathbf{Set}
and write
A\Rightarrow B
for the exponential object. Assume
RXY=O
for a chosen set
O
. Since
R
is constant,
\mathbf{SDin}[\mathbf{Set}[A,R{-}{=}],\mathbf{Set}[B,R{-}{=}]]
is equal to
\mathbf{Set}[\mathbf{Set}[A,O],\mathbf{Set}[B,O]]=(A\Rightarrow O)\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow O)
. Thus,
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
is defined as
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A=(A\Rightarrow O)\Rightarrow O
together with the isomorphism
\lceil\text{-}\rceil:B\Rightarrow((A\Rightarrow O)\Rightarrow O)\cong(A\Rightarrow O)\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow O):\lfloor\text{-}\rfloor
given by
\lceil f\rceil(k)(x)=f(x)(k)
and
\lfloor g\rfloor(x)(k)=g(k)(x)
. We instantiate the construction from Theorem
3.3
with this data and obtain:

•

\eta(a)=k\mapsto k(a)
,

•

For
f:A\to(B\Rightarrow O)\Rightarrow O
, the extension
f^{*}(x)=k\mapsto x(y\mapsto f(y)(k))
.

One can compare this with the usual definition of the continuation monad via a Kleisli triple, e.g., in Moggi
[
14
, Example 1.4]
.

4
Codensity Monads

Assume the categories
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{D}
, and the bifunctor
R:\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
as in the previous section. We say that
R
is
dummy
in its contravariant argument if it is of the shape
RXY=GY
for some
G:\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
.
In this section, we show that if
R
is such a functor, the monad
\mathfrak{C}^{R}A
instantiates to the pointwise codensity monad of
G
.

In general, a
codensity monad
of a functor
G
(see, e.g., Mac Lane
[
11
]
) is the right Kan extension of
G
along itself. Here, we are interested in
pointwise
codensity monads, which can given by the following end formula:

\mathfrak{T}^{G}A=\int_{X\in\mathscr{D}}\mathscr{A}[A,GX]\pitchfork GX

(6)

The notation
\int_{X}FXX
denotes the
end
of a mixed-variant functor
F
. The symbol
\mathscr{A}[A,RX]\pitchfork RX
denotes the
\mathscr{A}[A,RX]
-
power
of
RX
, which can be thought of as an
\mathscr{A}[A,RX]
-ary product of
RX
with itself, and can be concisely defined as an object
P
that satisfies the following isomorphism natural in
C
:

\mathscr{A}[C,P]\cong\mathbf{Set}[\mathscr{A}[A,RX],\mathscr{A}[C,RX]]

(7)

(The similarity of (
7
) and (
5
) is of course not coincidental.) We use the simple fact that strong dinaturality collapses to the usual naturality if the involved bifunctors are simply functors:

Lemma 1
.

Given two functors
G,H:\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
, we can treat them as
bifunctors
\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{A}
dummy in their first
argument. Then, strong dinatural transformations
G\to H
are exactly
natural transformations
G\to H
.

We use this lemma and some known facts about ends to show that the codensity monad of
G
satisfies the isomorphism from Definition
3.1
:

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{SDin}[\mathscr{A}[A,G{-}],\mathscr{A}[C,G{-}]]

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{Nat}[\mathscr{A}[A,G{-}],\mathscr{A}[C,G{-}]]

(Lemma
1
)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\ \int_{X}\mathbf{Set}[\mathscr{A}[A,GX],\mathscr{A}[C,GX]]

(sets of nat. trans. as ends)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\ \int_{X}\mathscr{A}[C,\mathscr{A}[A,GX]\pitchfork GX]

(def. of power)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\ \mathscr{A}[C,\int_{X}\mathscr{A}[A,GX]\pitchfork GX]

(ends commute with hom-functors)

Applying the Yoneda lemma and comparing the monad structures, we get:

Theorem 4.1
.

For a functor
RXY=GY
dummy in its first argument, assuming the mentioned ends exist, the monad
\mathfrak{C}^{R}
exists and is isomorphic to the codensity monad of
G
.

We also remark on other candidates for generalised codensity monads:

Remark 4.2
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
relies on the naturality condition in the equation (
5
), but not explicitly on strong dinaturality. This suggests that we could define more monads by replacing
\mathbf{SDin}
with different kinds of transformations. However, the other obvious choices do not lead to anything interesting. Transformations natural in both arguments yield the usual codensity monad of the bifuctor treated as a functor from the product category
\mathscr{D}^{\mathsf{op}}\times\mathscr{D}
, while dinatural transformations do not compose in general, so in such a case the construction in Definition
3.1
does not yield a monad.

5
Dicodensity Monads from Limits

Codensity monads can be alternatively constructed as a limit over a diagram indexed by a comma category. In this section, we show a similar result for our dicodensity monads, however, the diagram needs to be slightly more involved.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper continues the exploration of continuation monads and Cayley representations in semantics, stemming from the work of Hinze
[
5
]
, and building on the formal footing provided by Paré and Román
[
16
]
. In this line, we build on our prior work
[
18
]
, where we only considered polynomial Cayley representations on
\mathbf{Set}
and introduce generalised codensity monads over arbitrary bifunctors. We also relate them to generalised notion of representation, in particular where the bifunctors in question represent sets (or objects) of
T
-algebra homomorphisms, which is a common source of concrete examples.

One direction of future work would be to use the machinery developed in this paper to extend the polynomial representations over one variable to the multi-variable case, and explore the effects that they denote. Another possible question would be whether codensity liftings
[
6
]
or similar constructions can be naturally generalised to mixed-variant bifunctors and whether such constructions have practical use in programming or semantics.
