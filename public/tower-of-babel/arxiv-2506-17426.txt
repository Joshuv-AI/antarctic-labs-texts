Spectral asymptotics of pseudodifferential operators with discontinuous symbols
A. Derkach, A. V. Sobolev

Abstract
. It was known that in general, the singular values s k s_{k} of such an operator satisfy the bound s k = O ( k − 3 / 4 ) s_{k}=O(k^{-3/4}) , k = 1 , 2 , … k=1,2,\dots . 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

One of the challenging and important questions of spectral theory
of pseudodifferential operators is the study of operators with discontinuous symbols.
In this paper we study pseudodifferential operators on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined on
functions
u
from the Schwartz class by the formula

\displaystyle({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi]u)(x)=

\displaystyle\ \op^{\rm w}({\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}\phi)

(1.1)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\,e^{i(x-y)\xi}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\bigg)\,\phi\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\bigg)\,u(y)\,dyd\xi.

Here
{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}
is the indicator of
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and

\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. If
\Omega
is bounded then an arbitrary

\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

is allowed. In any case, the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}={\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi]
is compact,
even Hilbert-Schmidt, see Lemma
2.1
.
We assume that
\phi
is real-valued, and hence
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi]
is self-adjoint. Due
to the dependence on
(x+y)/2
(and not, e.g. on
x
or
y
alone), the function

\phi_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\phi(\boldsymbol{\tau})
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi)
, is called
the
Weyl symbol
of the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
.
Denote by
\lambda_{k}^{(+)}
and
-\lambda_{k}^{(-)}
,
k=1,2,\dots,

the positive and negative eigenvalues of
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
.
Each sequence
\lambda^{(+)}_{k}
,
\lambda_{k}^{(-)}

is enumerated in non-increasing order counting multiplicity. Sometimes we write

\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})
or
\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi])
to indicate the dependence on the operator.
We are interested in the decay rate of the eigenvalues as
k\to\infty
.
This spectral problem naturally embeds into the
context of
time-frequency analysis
, see
[
12
]
.
The phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is then interpreted as the time-frequency plane, and the primary
problem of the analysis is
to find a function (signal) that
is concentrated in both time and frequency within a fixed domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
More precisely, one is looking for functions
u\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}}=1
,
that deliver consecutive maxima
of the quadratic form
\big({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi]u,u\big)
, see
[
10
]
. In spectral terms,
this means that one seeks eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi]
.
For piece-wise smooth bounded
domains
\Omega
and
\phi=1
, it was shown in
[
15
]
that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\,\lambda_{k}^{(+)}+\sum_{k}\,\lambda_{k}^{(-)}=\infty,

so that the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[1]
is not trace class, and in general,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}=O(k^{-\frac{3}{4}}).

An alternative proof of (
1.3
) was given in
[
8
, Corollary 3.2.4]
.
A calculation for annular domains in
[
15
]
demonstrates that
this bound is sharp.
In the case where
\Omega
is an ellipse,
an explicit formula for the eigenvalues was found in

[
10
]
,
see also
[
14
, Ch. 3]
.

1.2.
Main result

In the present paper we find that for polygonal domains (not necessarily convex)
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}
decay faster than in (
1.3
).
Our main result gives an asymptotic formula for
\lambda_{k}^{\pm)}
in the case where

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a sector.
We always assume that
\Omega
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\theta}=\{(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<\arg(x+i\xi)<\theta\},\quad\textup{with some opening angle}\quad\theta\in(0,2\pi),

see Remark
1.2
(
3
) for justification.
The next theorem is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a sector with the opening angle

\theta\in(0,\pi)
or
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
, and let
\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be real-valued.
Then

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\,\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi])\,\frac{k}{\log k}=\frac{|\phi(0,0)|}{4\pi^{2}}.

If
\phi(0,0)=0
, then
\lambda^{(\pm)}\big({\sf{A}}_{\Omega_{\theta}}[\phi]\big)=O(k^{-1})
.

Remark 1.2
.

(1)

The asymptotics (
1.4
) are in agreement with (
1.2
).

(2)

The angle
\theta=\pi
is excluded from the statement, since
the sector
\Omega_{\pi}
is a half-plane, and hence by the second part of the theorem,

\lambda^{(\pm)}\big({\sf{A}}_{\Omega_{\pi}}[\phi]\big)=O(k^{-1})
.

(3)

The asymptotic formula (
1.4
) easily generalizes to an arbitrary sector

\Lambda_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the opening angle
\theta
. Indeed,
\Lambda_{\theta}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\theta}=\mathbf{O}(\Omega_{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\omega})

with some vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and some rotation
\mathbf{O}
.
The associated metaplectic transformation
provides unitary equivalence of
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega_{\theta}}[\phi]
and
{\sf{A}}_{\Lambda_{\theta}}[\tilde{\phi}]

where
\tilde{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\phi(\mathbf{O}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\omega}),\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi)
,
see
[
11
, Theorem 4.51]
and
[
14
, Theorem 1.2.20 and Lemma A.2.4]
.

(4)

The formula (
1.4
) does not depend on the angle
\theta
. This is not surprising
at least for

\theta\in(0,\pi)
since in this case
the sector
\Omega_{\theta}
is symplecticaly equivalent to a quarter-plane,
see

[
14
, P. 114]
. Precisely, the symplectic matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\cot\theta\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

acts as follows:
\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\Omega_{\theta}=\Omega_{\pi/2}
.
As in the previous remark, by an appropriate
metaplectic transformation the operators

{\sf{A}}_{\Omega_{\theta}}[\phi]
and
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega_{\pi/2}}[\tilde{\phi}]
,

\tilde{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\phi(\mathbf{M}_{\theta}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau})
, are unitarily equivalent. Thus it suffices to
prove (
1.4
) for
\theta=\pi/2
.

Discussion of the case
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
is deferred until Section
7.1
.

The asymptotics (
1.4
) lead to the following bound.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a polygon (not necessarily convex), and let
\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Then

(1.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(\pm)}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}[\phi])=O(k^{-1}\log k),\quad k\to\infty.

Indeed, introducing a suitable partition of unity, using the Ky Fan inequalities
from Sect.
3
and Remark

1.2
(
3
), Theorem
1.1
implies (
1.5
).

Theorem
1.1
will be proved in Sect.
7
.

Although it is not directly relevant to the main results,
it is instructive to compare spectral problem for the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
with
another model adopted in time-frequency analysis. Instead of
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
consider
the operator

\displaystyle(T_{\Lambda,\Gamma}u)(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Lambda}(x)\,\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\,e^{i(x-y)\xi}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Gamma}(\xi){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Lambda}(y)u(y)\,dyd\xi,

where
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
are bounded intervals of the real line, and

{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Lambda}
and
{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Gamma}
are their
indicator functions. A detailed study of the
eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of
T_{\Lambda,\Gamma}
(all of which are positive)
was conducted by Landau, Pollack and Slepian in a series of papers
starting with
[
17
]
. Further analysis of the problem
in
[
3
, Corollary 3]
showed that
\lambda_{k}^{(+)}

decay superexponentially as
k\to\infty
, cf. (
1.4
).
We also point out another difference between
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
and
T_{\Lambda,\Gamma}
. Since
T_{\Lambda,\Gamma}

is a product of three orthogonal projections, we always have
T_{\Lambda,\Gamma}\leq 1
.
As shown by Flandrin in
[
10
]
, if
\Omega
is an ellipse, then

A_{\Omega}[1]\leq 1
as well. In the same paper Flandrin conjectured that
this bound should hold for
any convex domain
\Omega
. This conjecture was disproved in
[
7
]
and

[
14
, Ch. 5]
, presenting
several examples of convex domains, bounded and unbounded,
for which supremum of the spectrum of
A_{\Omega}[1]
is strictly greater than
1
.
One such example
is given by the quarter-plane

\Omega_{\pi/2}
.

1.3.
The method

Our proof has three ingredients. The first two concern pseudodifferential
operators on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with arbitrary
d\geq 1
, and they can be of independent interest.

At the heart of our approach is the notion of a

dual
symbol that we introduce in (
2.5
). For any square-integrable
symbol
a(x,\xi)
its dual
a^{*}(x,\xi)
is defined (modulo some scaling)
as its symplectic Fourier transform, see e.g.
[
11
, Prologue]

for the definition of the simplectic Fourier transform.
One useful feature of the transformation
\op^{\rm w}(a)\mapsto\op^{\rm w}(a^{*})

is that it preserves singular values.
Furthermore, the utility of the dual symbol is especially evident when the original symbol
a

is discontinuous but decays rapidly at infinity, so that
a^{*}
is smooth, which
puts at our disposal
a vast arsenal of standards methods. This observation
plays a key role in our analysis of the operator (
1.1
).

Another critical ingredient of our method is a new bound for
singular values of pseudodifferential operators
on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with smooth symbols. Very loosely speaking
we show that under some decay conditions on the symbol
a=a(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi),

the spectral counting function
of the compact operator

A=\op^{\rm w}(a)

is controlled by the phase space volume of the “classically allowed” region,
see Theorem
4.3
.
For operators whose symbols
have product structure, i.e.
a(x,\xi)=f(x)g(\xi)
, such estimates
were first derived by Cwikel
[
4
]
, and then complemented by a result of Simon,
see
[
16
, Theorem 4.6]
.
We stress that we
do not assume any separation of variables in the symbol. The proofs rely on the
bounds in the Schatten-von Neumann classes, obtained by the second author
in
[
19
]
, and on the ideas from
[
16
, Ch. 4]
.

The final ingredient is the Birman-Schwinger principle
which links spectral counting function of the operator

{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
with that of a suitable Schrödinger operator on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Schrödinger operators
have been very well studied in the literature, with a large number
of results on their
discrete spectrum. We are relying on the asymptotic formula found in
[
20
]

which allows us to obtain (
1.4
).

.

1.5.
Notational conventions

By

\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
we denote the pair of standard canonical
d
-dimensional
variables.

Fourier transforms.

For a function
f(x,\xi)
on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
introduce the notation

\displaystyle({\mathcal{F}}_{1}f)(\eta,\xi)=

\displaystyle\ \,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\eta\cdot x}\,f(x,\xi)\,dx,

\displaystyle({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(x,y)=

\displaystyle\ \,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-iy\cdot\xi}\,f(x,\xi)\,d\xi,

and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}f)(\eta,y):=({\mathcal{F}}_{1}\,{\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(\eta,y)=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}e^{-iy\cdot\xi-i\eta\cdot x}\,f(x,\xi)\,d\xi\,dx.

Integrals without indication of the domain are assumed to be taken over the entire Euclidean
space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
depending on the context.

Derivatives and function spaces.

We use the generally accepted notation for partial derivatives: for

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
m=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we write

\partial_{x}^{m}f=\partial_{x_{1}}^{m_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{m_{d}}f
, and

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}^{k}f|=\sum_{|m|=k}\,|\partial_{x}^{m}f|,\ k=0,1,\dots.

The symbols
\textup{{{W}}}^{l,p}
,
l=0,1,\dots
,
p\geq 1
, stand for the standard
Sobolev spaces, see e.g.
[
9
, Ch. 5]
.

Bounds.

For two non-negative numbers (or functions)

X
and
Y
depending on some parameters,
we write
X\lesssim Y
(or
Y\gtrsim X
) if
X\leq CY
with
some positive constant
C
independent of those parameters.
If
X\lesssim Y\lesssim X
, then we write
X\asymp Y
.
To avoid confusion we may comment on the nature of
(implicit) constants in the bounds.

2.
Pseudodifferential operators: preliminaries

In this section we consider pseudodifferential operators on

\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for arbitrary dimension
d\geq 1
.

2.1.
Generalities

We shall encounter several types of pseudodifferential operators.

For a function (
symbol
)
a=a(x,\xi)
,
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
arbitrary
t\in[0,1]
, we define
the
t
-pseudodifferential operator in the standard way:

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\op^{(t)}(a)u)(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\iint e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}a((1-t)x+ty,\xi)u(y)\,dyd\xi,

where
u
is in the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We shall be working mostly with
t=0
and
t=1/2
,
in which case we use the standard notation

\displaystyle\op^{\rm l}(a):=\op^{(0)}(a),\quad\op^{\rm w}(a):=\op^{(1/2)}(a).

Recall that the symbol
a(x,\xi)
of

\op^{\rm l}(a)
is usually called
the Kohn-Nirenberg symbol
,
and the symbol
a((x+y)/2,\xi)
of
\op^{\rm w}(a)
is called
the Weyl symbol
.
We also need a more general type of a pseudodifferential operator.
Let
p=p(x,y,\xi),x,y,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, be a smooth function.
Recall the standard notation for the pseudodifferential operator
with
amplitude

p
:

\displaystyle(\op^{\rm a}(p)u)(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\iint e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}p(x,y,\xi)u(y)\,dyd\xi.

Our assumptions on
symbols
a
and amplitudes
p
will ensure that all the integrals above
are convergent. In particular, we always assume that

a\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, in which case it is well known that
the operators
\op^{(t)}(a)
are Hilbert-Schmidt. We give the proof for completeness.
Below the class of Hilbert-Schmidt operators is denoted by
\mathbf{S}_{2}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
a\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, then for all
t\in[0,1]
we have

\op^{(t)}(a)\in\mathbf{S}_{2}
and

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|\op^{(t)}(a)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|a(\boldsymbol{\tau})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{\tau},\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi).

Let

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\sf{A}}_{\tiny{\rm int}}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\op^{(t)}(a)\,dt,

with some
s>0
.
Then
A_{\tiny{\rm int}}(s)\in\mathbf{S}_{2}
and

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}_{\tiny{\rm int}}(s)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{s^{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|a(\boldsymbol{\tau})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{\tau}.

Proof.

The kernel of
{\sf{A}}:=\op^{(t)}(a)
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}(\mathcal{F}_{2}a)((1-t)x+ty,y-x).

Therefore

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint|(\mathcal{F}_{2}a)((1-t)x+ty,y-x)|^{2}\,dxdy.

Changing the variables to

\displaystyle(1-t)x+ty=w,\quad y-x=z,

we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint|(\mathcal{F}_{2}a)(w,z)|^{2}\,dwdz.

By Plancherel’s identity, the last double integral coincides with the norm

\|a\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}}^{2}
, as claimed.

The bound (
2.4
) immediately follows from the estimates

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}_{\tiny{\rm int}}(s)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\leq\bigg[\int_{0}^{s}\,\|\op^{(t)}(a)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}\,dt\bigg]^{2}\leq s\int_{0}^{s}\,\|\op^{(t)}(a)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\,dt,

and from (
2.2
).
∎

2.2.
Reduction to a ”dual” symbol

Let
a\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
be an arbitrary complex-valued function.
Instead of the operator
\op^{\rm w}(a)
one can study
a pseudodifferential operator with a
dual symbol
, which is defined as

(2.5)

\displaystyle a^{*}(x,\xi)=2^{d}\,(\mathcal{F}a)(2\xi,-2x).

The next theorem links
\op^{\rm w}(a)
and
\op^{\rm w}(a^{*})
with one another.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
a\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, and let

U:\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mapsto\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the unitary operator
of reflection, i.e.
(Uf)(x)=f(-x)
. Then

\op^{\rm w}(a)U=\op^{\rm w}(a^{*})
.

Proof.

Due to Lemma
2.1
we may assume that

a\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Introduce the new variables

\displaystyle z=\frac{x+y}{2},\quad v=\frac{x-y}{2}.

Thus, rewriting
{\sf{A}}=\op^{\rm w}(a)
as an integral operator we get

\displaystyle({\sf{A}}f)(x)=

\displaystyle\ \int\mathcal{A}(x,y)\,f(y)\,dy,\quad\mathcal{A}(x,y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\int\,e^{2iv\cdot\xi}\,a(z,\xi)\,d\xi,

Rewrite the kernel:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}(\mathcal{F}_{2}a)(z,-2v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int e^{i\eta\cdot z}(\mathcal{F}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{2}a)(\eta,-2v)\,d\eta.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int e^{i(x+y)\cdot\frac{\eta}{2}}(\mathcal{F}a)(\eta,-(x-y))\,d\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{2^{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int e^{i(x+y)\cdot\eta}(\mathcal{F}a)(2\eta,-(x-y))\,d\eta.

Consequently,

\displaystyle({\sf{A}}U\,f)(x)=\frac{2^{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint e^{i(x-y)\cdot\eta}(\mathcal{F}a)(2\eta,-(x+y))f(y)\,dyd\eta=\big(\op^{\rm w}(a^{*})f\big)(x),

as claimed.
∎

Note that the
dual symbol can be rewritten as

a^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=2^{d}\big({\mathcal{F}}_{\tiny{\rm symp}}a\big)(2\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi)
,
with
{\mathcal{F}}_{\tiny{\rm symp}}
being the so-called
symplectic Fourier transform
,
see e.g.
[
11
, Prologue]
. We do not use this fact in what follows.

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.2
can be slighly generalized in the following way.
Let
a
be as in the theorem, and let
g=g(x)
be a bounded function. Denote

\displaystyle p(x,y,\xi)=a\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\bigg)\,g(x-y).

Then
\op^{\rm a}(p)U=\op^{\rm w}(b)
with

\displaystyle b(x,\xi)=2^{d}\big({\mathcal{F}}a\big)(2\xi,-2x)\,g(-2x).

The proof requires only minor modifications.

Theorem
2.2
will be our main tool when proving Theorem
1.1
.
It will allow us to replace the discontinuous symbol
\phi_{\Omega}={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}\phi

by its smooth dual symbol.
In the next proposition we calculate this dual symbol for the case of the quarter-plane

\Omega=\Omega_{\pi/2}
.
Below we denote by
\zeta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
an arbitrary function such that
\zeta(x)=1

for
|x|\geq 2
and
\zeta(x)=0
for
|x|<1
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{\pi/2}
. The symbol
\phi_{\Omega}^{*}(x,\xi)
dual to
\phi_{\Omega}(x,\xi)

satisfies
\phi_{\Omega}^{*}\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
the following bounds hold:

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\,\phi_{\Omega}^{*}(x,\xi)|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-1-m}\,\langle\xi\rangle^{-1-n}.

Furthermore,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\phi_{\Omega}^{*}(x,\xi)=b_{0}(x,\xi)+b_{1}(x,\xi),

where

(2.7)

\displaystyle b_{0}(x,\xi)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4\pi}\phi(0,0)\,\frac{\zeta(x)}{x}\,\frac{\zeta(\xi)}{\xi},

(2.8)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\,b_{1}(x,\xi)|\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \langle x\rangle^{-1-m}\,\langle\xi\rangle^{-2-n}+\langle x\rangle^{-2-m}\,\langle\xi\rangle^{-1-n}.

Proof.

For brevity denote
b=\phi_{\Omega}^{*}
.
By definition,

\displaystyle b(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2i\xi y}e^{2ix\eta}\phi(y,\eta)\,dyd\eta,

and hence

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\,b(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2i\xi y}e^{2ix\eta}(2i\eta)^{m}(-2iy)^{n}\phi(y,\eta)\,dyd\eta.

We begin with examining the case where
|x|\geq 1
and
|\xi|\geq 1
.

Performing several tedious (but elementary) integrations by parts we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\,b(x,\xi)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{n!m!}{4\pi}\frac{(-1)^{n+m}}{\xi^{n+1}x^{m+1}}\,\phi(0,0)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{i\,n!}{2\pi}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\xi^{n+1}}\bigg(\frac{i}{2x}\bigg)^{m+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2ix\eta}\,\partial_{\eta}^{m+1}\big((2i\eta)^{m}\phi(0,\eta)\big)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\ +\frac{im!}{2\pi}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{x^{m+1}}\bigg(-\frac{i}{2\xi}\bigg)^{n+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,e^{-2i\xi y}\partial_{y}^{n+1}\big((-2iy)^{n}\phi(y,0)\big)\,dy

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg(\frac{i}{2x}\bigg)^{m+1}

\displaystyle\bigg(-\frac{i}{2\xi}\bigg)^{n+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2ix\eta}e^{-2i\xi y}\partial_{y}^{n+1}\partial_{\eta}^{m+1}\big((2i\eta)^{m}(-2iy)^{n}\phi(y,\eta)\big)\,dyd\eta.

Without writing it out explicitly, observe that another integration by parts ensures that
each of the last three integrals exhibits an extra decay in
x
or
\xi
:
precisely, the first integral
is bounded by
|x|^{-1}
, the second and the third one – by
|\xi|^{-1}
. Therefore,

(2.9)

\displaystyle b(x,\xi)=\frac{\phi(0,0)}{4\pi x\xi}+\tilde{b}_{1}(x,\xi),

where

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\tilde{b}_{1}(x,\xi)|\lesssim|x|^{-m-1}|\xi|^{-n-2}+|x|^{-m-2}|\xi|^{-n-1},\quad|x|\geq 1,|\xi|\geq 1.

Since
1-\zeta(x)
is compactly supported,
the functions
x^{-1}
and
\xi^{-1}
in (
2.9
) can be replaced
with
\zeta(x)x^{-1}
and
\zeta(\xi)\xi^{-1}
respectively and the difference can be
incorporated in
\tilde{b}_{1}(x,\xi)
to form a new symbol that we call
b_{1}(x,\xi)
.
This gives the representation (
2.6
).

In the case where
|x|<1
and
|\xi|>1

the derivatives of
b
are estimated by integrating by parts in the variable
\xi

only. Similarly, for the case
|x|>1
and
|\xi|<1
. If
|x|<1
and
|\xi|<1
,
then the derivatives are trivially bounded. Thus the proof of (
2.6
) is complete.
∎

3.
Compact operators

We need some information about classes of compact operators on a Hilbert space.
For a compact self-adjoint operator
A
we denote by
\lambda^{(+)}_{k}(A)
and

-\lambda^{(-)}_{k}(A)
,
k=1,2,\dots
, its positive and negative eigenvalues respectively,
enumerated in non-increasing order. For an arbitrary compact
A
we define
its singular values (s-values) as
s_{k}(A)=\sqrt{\lambda^{(+)}_{k}(A^{*}A)}=\sqrt{\lambda^{(+)}_{k}(AA^{*})}
.
Clearly, for any unitary
U
the operators
A
and
AU
have the same singular values.
Recall the standard notation for the counting functions:

\displaystyle n(s;A)=\#\{k=1,2,\dots:s_{k}(A)>s\},\quad n_{\pm}(\lambda;A)=\#\{k=1,2,\dots:\lambda^{(\pm)}_{k}(A)>\lambda\},

where
s>0,\lambda>0
.
For a pair
A_{1},A_{2}
of compact operators, the introduced quantities satisfy the following inequalities for all
k,m=1,2,\dots
, and all
s_{1},s_{2}>0
, known as the
Ky Fan inequalities:

(3.1)

\displaystyle s_{k+m-1}(A_{1}+A_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\ s_{k}(A)+s_{m}(A),

(3.2)

\displaystyle n(s_{1}+s_{2};A_{1}+A_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\ n(s_{1};A_{1})+n(s_{2};A_{2}).

If
A_{1},A_{2}
are self-adjoint, then

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\pm)}_{k+m-1}(A_{1}+A_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\ \lambda^{(\pm)}_{k}(A)+\lambda^{(\pm)}_{m}(A),

(3.3)

\displaystyle n_{\pm}(s_{1}+s_{2};A_{1}+A_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\ n_{\pm}(s_{1};A_{1})+n_{\pm}(s_{2};A_{2}).

The notation
\mathbf{S}_{p},0<p<\infty
, is standard for the Schatten-von Neumann class of
compact operators
A
on a Hilbert space, for whose singular values
s_{k}(A),k=1,2,\dots
,
the functional

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\mathbf{S}_{p}}:=\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\,s_{k}^{p}(A)\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}

is finite. In particular,
\mathbf{S}_{2}
is the usual Hilbert-Schmidt class, that has been mentioned previously. Observe that

(3.4)

\displaystyle s_{k}(A)\leq k^{-1/p}\,\|A\|_{\mathbf{S}_{p}}.

The class of operators that satisfy the individual bounds
s_{k}(T)=O(k^{-1/p})

with some
p>0
, is denoted by
\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}\supset\mathbf{S}_{p}
. The functional

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}}=\sup_{k}\,s_{k}(A)k^{\frac{1}{p}}

defines a quasi-norm on
\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}
. We refer for details on these classes
to
[
2
, Chapter 11.6]
and
[
16
, Ch. 4]
.

In this paper, however, we work with more general classes of compact operators that
were studied in
[
21
]
.
Let, for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle f(t)=f_{\sigma}(t)=\frac{t}{\log^{\sigma}(t+e^{\sigma+1})},\ \quad\sigma\geq 0.

The function
f_{\sigma}
is non-negative, and an elementary calculation shows that
f_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(t)<0

for all
t>0
, so that it is (strictly) concave.
Therefore, it is subadditive:

\displaystyle f_{\sigma}(t_{1}+t_{2})\leq f_{\sigma}(t_{1})+f_{\sigma}(t_{2}),\quad t_{1},t_{2}\geq 0.

Define
\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma},p>0,\sigma\geq 0
, as the class of compact operators
A

satisfying the bound
s_{k}(A)=O(f(k)^{-1/p})
. Let

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\,s_{k}(A)\,f(k)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\big(\sup_{s>0}\,s^{p}\,f\big(n(s;A)\big)\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Note that in
[
21
]
more general concave functions
f
are allowed.
Along with (
3.5
) introduce the asymptotic quantities

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A)=\limsup\limits_{k\to\infty}s_{k}(A)\big(f_{\sigma}(k)\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\limsup\limits_{s\to 0}\,s\,\big(f_{\sigma}\big(n(s;A)\big)\big)^{\frac{1}{p}},\\[8.5359pt]
{\sf{g}}_{p,\sigma}(A)=\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}s_{k}(A)\big(f_{\sigma}(k)\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\liminf\limits_{s\to 0}\,s\,\big(f_{\sigma}\big(n(s;A)\big)\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.\end{cases}

If
n(s;A)
is replaced by
n_{\pm}(\lambda;A)
, then the notation for these functionals
is
{\sf{G}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
and
{\sf{g}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
.
Note that the values of these asymptotic functionals
do not change if the function
f_{\sigma}
is replaced
by
t\log^{-\sigma}(t+a)
with an arbitrary
a\geq 0
.

The next proposition was proved in
[
21
]
, but we provide the proof
for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.1
.

The functional (
3.5
) defines a metric on
\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}
. More prescisely,
for any
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}
the inequality holds:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|A_{1}+A_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\leq\|A_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}+\|A_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{\frac{p}{p+1}}.

Furthermore,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\big({\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1}+A_{2})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\leq\big({\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}+\big({\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{2})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}},\\[8.5359pt]
\big({\sf{g}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1}+A_{2})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\leq\big({\sf{g}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}+\big({\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{2})\big)^{\frac{p}{p+1}},\end{cases}

and the same inequalities hold for
{\sf{G}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
and
{\sf{g}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
.

Proof.

Due to (
3.2
),
by subadditivity and monotonicity of

f
we obtain for any
s>0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle f\big(n(s;A_{1}+A_{2})\big)\leq f\big(n(\varepsilon s;A_{1})\big)+f\big(n((1-\varepsilon)s;A_{2})\big),

and hence

\displaystyle s^{p}\,f\big(n(s;A_{1}+A_{2})\big)\leq\frac{(\varepsilon s)^{p}}{\varepsilon^{p}}\,f\big(n(\varepsilon s;A_{1})\big)+\frac{((1-\varepsilon)s)^{p}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{p}}\,f\big(n((1-\varepsilon)s;A_{2})\big),

Taking the supremum over
s>0
, we get

\displaystyle\|A_{1}+A_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{p}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}}\,\|A_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{p}+\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)^{p}}\,\|A_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}}^{p},

Minimizing in
\varepsilon
we obtain (
3.7
).
In the same way, taking
\limsup
or
\liminf
we arrive at (
3.8
).

The inequalities for
{\sf{G}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
and
{\sf{g}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}

are derived from (
3.3
)
in the same way.
∎

The following corollary will be of central importance for us.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1}-A_{2})=0
, then
{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1})={\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{2})
,

{\sf{g}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{1})={\sf{g}}_{p,\sigma}(A_{2})
,
and the same equalities hold for
{\sf{G}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
and
{\sf{g}}^{(\pm)}_{p,\sigma}
.

This is an immediate consequence of the bounds (
3.8
).

4.1.
Estimates in terms of lattice norms

We rely on the estimates for the operator (

Let

For a function

denote

\begin{cases}{\,\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}\,h{\,\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}{\vrule depth=3.0pt,height=9.0pt}\,_{r,\delta}=\biggr[\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\biggl(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}|h(x)|^{r}dx\biggr)^{\frac{\delta}{r}}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{\delta}},\ \ 0<\delta<\infty,\\[8.5359pt]

For a symbol

denote

(4.2)

The next proposition
is a part of

It is stated in the form convenient for our purposes.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\displaystyle n=n(q)=[dq^{-1}]+1.

If

with a constant independent of

.

4.2.
Phase space bounds

Our objective is to obtain bounds in classes
\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}
with suitable
p\in(0,2)

and
\sigma>0
. To this end instead of tiling
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with the
cubes
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
, as in Proposition
4.1
,
we consider a cover of
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
by cubes

\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\mathbf{n}}=(-1,1)^{2d}+\mathbf{n}
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
.
Recall the notation
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x,\xi)
and let

\zeta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\mathbb{0}})
be a non-negative function such
that the translations

\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\zeta(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\mathbf{k}),\,\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
,
form a partition of unity subordinate to the above cover:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=1,\ \quad\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Then the operator
{\sf{A}}={\sf{A}}^{(t)}=\op^{(t)}(a)
can be represented as

{\sf{A}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,\op^{(t)}(\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}a)
.
Since the parameter
t
is fixed, we often omit it from the notation.
For a subset
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
, introduce a ”trimmed” variant of the operator

{\sf{A}}
:

\displaystyle{\sf A}_{K}={\sf{A}}_{K}^{(t)}:=\op^{(t)}(a_{K}),\quad a_{K}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=a(\boldsymbol{\tau})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in K}\,\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\,.

In order to estimate the
\mathbf{S}_{q}
-quasi-norm of
{\sf A}_{K}
introduce the sequence

(4.5)

\displaystyle v_{\mathbf{k}}=\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\mathbf{k}}}\,|F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\tau};a)|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{\tau}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad{\sf v}=\{v_{\mathbf{k}}\}.

To avoid cumbersome notation, we do not indicate
the dependence of the sequence
{\sf{v}}
on the symbol
a

and number
n
, but one should always keep this dependence in mind.

The next fact follows directly from Proposition

4.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
q\in(0,1]
, and let
the sequence
{\sf v}
be as defined in (
4.5
) with the number

defined in (

).
Suppose that
{\sf v}
belongs to

\textup{{{l}}}^{q}(K)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}_{K}^{(t)}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{q}}^{q}\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in K}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{q},

{\sf{A}}_{K}^{(t)}
and the integral

\displaystyle\|{\sf{A}}_{K}^{(t)}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in K}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2},\quad\|({\sf{A}}_{K})_{\tiny{\rm int}}(s)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\lesssim s^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in K}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}.

, and of

and

Apply Proposition

to the operator

.

The bounds (

∎

Now we can estimate the singular values of

.
To this end assume that for the sequence

defined in (

),

\displaystyle M_{p,\sigma}[{\sf v}]:=\sup_{E>0}\,E\log^{-\sigma}\big(E^{-1}+2\big)\,\big(\#\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}:v_{\mathbf{k}}>E\}\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty,

with some

and

. For such a sequence

The proof of the next theorem is inspired by the proof of

[
16
, Theorem 4.6]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
p\in(0,2)
, and let the sequence
{\sf v}=\{v_{\mathbf{k}}\}
be defined by (

)
with

where

.
Suppose that for some
\sigma\geq 0

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}(\op^{(t)}(a))\lesssim M_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[{\sf v}].

.
The constant in the above inequality is independent of the symbol
a
and the number

.

Proof.

Note that
M_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[\mu{\sf v}]=\mu M_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[{\sf v}]

for any

.Thus, if

, then without loss of generality

. As a result,

M_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[{\sf v}]=0
or
M_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[{\sf v}]=1
.
Denote

(4.11)

\displaystyle S_{n}=S_{n}[{\sf v}]:=\sup_{0<E\leq 2^{n}}\,E\log^{-\sigma}\big(E^{-1}+2\big)\,\big(\#\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}:v_{\mathbf{k}}>E\}\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

and set

.
Thus

or

as

.

Fix an integer

satisfying the condition

\displaystyle m\leq-1-S[{\sf v}],

and split

into two operators:

\displaystyle{\sf{A}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}:v_{\mathbf{k}}>2^{2m}}\op(a\,\zeta_{\mathbf{k}})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}:v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{2m}}\op(a\,\zeta_{\mathbf{k}})=:{\sf A}^{(1)}_{m}+{\sf A}^{(2)}_{m}.

with

. The set
K
is finite, and
hence the restriction of the sequence

to

.
Therefore, by Proposition

, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|{\sf A}^{(1)}_{m}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{q}}^{q}\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}:v_{\mathbf{k}}>2^{2m}}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{q}=\sum_{l=2m}^{\infty}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}:2^{l}<v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{l+1}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{q}.

\displaystyle\#\{\mathbf{k}:v_{\mathbf{k}}>2^{l}\}\leq 2^{-pl}\log^{p\sigma}(2^{-pl}+2)\,S_{l}^{p}\lesssim(l_{-})^{p\sigma}2^{-pl}\,S_{l}^{p},

where

,
and hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}:2^{l}<v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{l+1}}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{q}\lesssim S_{l}^{p}\,2^{(l+1)q}(l_{-})^{p\sigma}2^{-pl}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{l=2m}^{\infty}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}:2^{l}<v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{l+1}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{q}\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \sum_{l=2m}^{\infty}\,S_{l}^{p}2^{(l+1)q}(l_{-})^{p\sigma}2^{-pl}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ S_{m}^{p}|2m|^{p\sigma}\sum_{l=2m}^{m}\,2^{(q-p)l}+S^{p}|m|^{p\sigma}\sum_{l=m}^{\infty}\,2^{(q-p)l}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ S_{m}^{p}|m|^{p\sigma}2^{2(q-p)m}+S^{p}|m|^{p\sigma}2^{(q-p)m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ |m|^{p\sigma}2^{2(q-p)m}\big(S_{m}^{p}+S^{p}2^{-(q-p)m}\big).

Because of the condition

4.12
), the term in the brackets does not exceed

(S_{m}+2^{-\varepsilon|m|})^{p}

with some
\varepsilon>0
, which leads to

\displaystyle\|{\sf A}^{(1)}_{m}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{q}}^{q}\lesssim\,|m|^{p\sigma}\,2^{2(q-p)m}\,T_{m}^{p},\quad T_{m}:=S_{m}+2^{-\varepsilon|m|}.

For the operator
{\sf A}^{(2)}_{m}
we use

) with

to conclude that

\displaystyle\|{\sf A}^{(2)}_{m}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{k}:v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{2m}}\,v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}=\sum_{l\leq 2m-1}\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}:2^{l}<v_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{l+1}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}.

Therefore, from (
4.13
) with

we obtain that

\displaystyle\|{\sf A}^{(2)}_{m}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{l\leq 2m-1}\,S_{l}^{p}\,|l|^{p\sigma}\,2^{2(l+1)}2^{-pl}\lesssim S_{m}^{p}|m|^{p\sigma}\,2^{2(2-p)m},

where we have used that
p<2
. Using (
3.1
) and (
3.4
),
we arrive at the bounds

\displaystyle s_{2k}({\sf{A}})\leq

\displaystyle\ s_{2k-1}({\sf{A}})\leq s_{k}({\sf{A}}^{(1)}_{m})+s_{k}({\sf{A}}^{(2)}_{m})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ k^{-\frac{1}{q}}|m|^{\frac{p\sigma}{q}}\,2^{2(1-\frac{p}{q})m}\,T_{m}^{\frac{p}{q}}+k^{-\frac{1}{2}}|m|^{\frac{p\sigma}{2}}\,2^{(2-p)m}\,S_{m}^{\frac{p}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ k^{-\frac{1}{p}}|m|^{\sigma}\bigg[k^{\frac{1}{p}(1-\frac{p}{q})}|m|^{-\sigma(1-\frac{p}{q})}2^{2(1-\frac{p}{q})m}\,T_{m}^{\frac{p}{q}}+k^{\frac{1}{p}(1-\frac{p}{2})}|m|^{-\sigma(1-\frac{p}{2})}2^{(2-p)m}S_{m}^{\frac{p}{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ k^{-\frac{1}{p}}|m|^{\sigma}\bigg[\big(k^{\frac{1}{p}}|m|^{-\sigma}2^{2m}\big)^{1-\frac{p}{q}}\,T_{m}^{\frac{p}{q}}+\big(k^{\frac{1}{p}}|m|^{-\sigma}2^{2m}\big)^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\,S_{m}^{\frac{p}{2}}\bigg].

For each

take

such that

. This implies that

as

Since

Taking

∎

5.
Examples of bounds for singular values

In this section we illustrate the utility of Theorem
4.3
by two examples.

5.1.
A variant of Theorem
4.3

First we prove a variant of Theorem
4.3

that could be more convenient for applications. Let
\rho=\rho(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
be some positive function.
For
p>0
and
\sigma\geq 0
define

(5.1)

\displaystyle N_{p,\sigma}[\rho]:=\sup_{E>0}E\log^{-\sigma}(E^{-1}+2)|\{\boldsymbol{\tau}:\rho(\boldsymbol{\tau})>E\}|^{\frac{1}{p}}.

This quantity is finite if and only if the following functional is finite:

Similarly to

(see (

is homogeneous in

for any

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\rho=\rho(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, be a positive function such that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{\tau})\asymp\rho(\boldsymbol{\nu}),\quad\textup{if}\quad|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\nu}|\leq 1.

Suppose also that
N_{p,\sigma}[\rho]<\infty
for some

and some

.
Assume that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{m}a(\boldsymbol{\tau})|\lesssim\rho(\boldsymbol{\tau})

for all

, with a constant potentially depending on

{\sf{A}}^{(t)}=\op^{(t)}(a)\in\mathfrak{S}_{p,\sigma}

and
{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}({\sf{A}}^{(t)})\lesssim N_{p,\sigma}^{\circ}[\rho]
.

Proof.

Let us pick a number
q\in(0,1]
,

, and define the sequence
v_{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
,
as in (
4.5

. Due to the condition (
5.3
),
we have

. Now the claim follows from Theorem

4.3
.
∎

We apply the above theorem to symbols with a power-like decay.

5.2.
Example 1

6.
From the Weyl to Kohn-Nirenberg symbol

In this section we consider smooth
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}
-symbols
a
and demonstrate that,
modulo some mild assumptions, switching
from the Weyl to Kohn-Nirenberg symbol does not affect the spectral asymptotics,

Under the assumption
a\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})

the operator
\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)
is Hilbert-Schmidt, see
Lemma
2.1
.
Let us estimate its Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Below
\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
denotes the standard
Sobolev space on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that
a\in\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Then the operator

\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)
is Hilbert-Schmidt and

\displaystyle\|\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)\|_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4(2\pi)^{d}}\iint\,\big|((\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\xi})\,a)(x,\xi)\big|^{2}\,dxd\xi.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, it suffices to conduct the proof for
a\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.
Denote

(6.1)

\displaystyle p(x,y;\xi)=a\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\bigg)-a(x,\xi),

so that
\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)=\op^{\rm a}(p)
.
Rewrite the amplitude:

\displaystyle p(x,y,\xi)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\,(y-x)\cdot\nabla_{x}a\bigg(x+s\frac{y-x}{2},\xi\bigg)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{0}^{1/2}\,(y-x)\cdot\nabla_{x}a\big(x+s(y-x),\xi\big)\,ds.

Thus, integrating by parts, we can represent the kernel
\mathcal{P}(x,y)
of the operator

\op^{\rm a}(p)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{i}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\int e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}\,\int_{0}^{1/2}(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\xi})a\big(x+s(y-x),\xi\big)\,ds\,d\xi.

This implies that

\displaystyle\op^{\rm a}(p)=\int_{0}^{1/2}\ \op^{(s)}(w)ds,\quad w(x,\xi)=-i(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\xi})a(x,\xi).

The required bound follows from (
2.4
).
This completes the proof.
∎

Now we can estimate singular values of the difference

\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)
. To this end
we have to impose stronger conditions on

than in Lemma
6.1
.

Let
q\in(0,1]
, and let
the sequence
{\sf v}=\{v_{\mathbf{k}}\}
be defined by (
4.5
)
with
n=n(q)
, see (
4.3
) for definition.
Apart from

we also use

(6.2)

\displaystyle w_{\mathbf{k}}=\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\mathbf{k}}}\,|(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\xi})a(\boldsymbol{\tau})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{\tau}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}.

Recall that
M_{\gamma,\sigma}[{\sf v}]
and
M_{\gamma,\sigma}^{\circ}[{\sf v}]
are defined in
(
4.8
) and (
4.9
)
respectively.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that for some

, some

, such that

, and some
\sigma\geq 0
, we have

and

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{p,\sigma}\big(\op^{\rm w}(a)-\op^{\rm l}(a)\big)=0.

Proof.

If

, then by Theorem
4.3
, the functional

equals zero for each of the operators
\op^{\rm w}(a)

4.3

To a large extent, our proof follows that of Theorem
4.3
. In particular, we use
the notation (
4.11
) and note that
S_{n}[{\sf v}]\to 1
and
S_{n}[{\sf w}]\to 0

as

.

Fix an integer
m
satisfying (
4.12
)
and split
{\sf{P}}:=\op^{\rm a}(p)
with the amplitude
(
6.1
) into two operators:

\displaystyle{\sf{P}}=

\displaystyle\ {\sf{P}}^{(1)}_{m}+{\sf{P}}^{(2)}_{m},

\displaystyle\ \sum_{\mathbf{k}:w_{\mathbf{k}}>2^{2m}}\big(\op^{\rm w}(a_{\mathbf{k}})-\op^{\rm l}(a_{\mathbf{k}})\big),

\displaystyle\ \sum_{\mathbf{k}:w_{\mathbf{k}}\leq 2^{2m}}\big(\op^{\rm w}(a_{\mathbf{k}})-\op^{\rm l}(a_{\mathbf{k}})\big),

\Omega=\Omega_{\pi/2}
.

Proof of

In view of

2.2
,
s_{k}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=s_{k}(\op^{\rm w}(\phi_{\Omega}^{*})),k=1,2,\dots
,
where
\phi_{\Omega}^{*}
is the dual of
\phi_{\Omega}
.

, by Theorem
2.4
,

,

)
with

.

7.3.
Spectral symmetry

We begin the proof of (
7.1
) with demonstrating that
the spectrum of
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
is asymptotically symmetric about
\lambda=0
.
Denote
{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\pm}={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\mathbb{R}_{\pm}}
. The same symbol will be used for the orthogonal projections
in
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\pm})
.

Lemma 7.2
.

{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\pm}\op^{{\rm w}}(\phi_{\Omega}){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\pm}\in\mathbf{S}_{1,\infty}
.

Proof.

For definiteness consider the projection
{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}
only.
Since
\phi
is compactly supported, we have

\phi(x,\xi)={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(-R,R)}(x)\,\phi(x,\xi)
for some
R>0
, so we can rewrite the symbol of

{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}\op^{{\rm w}}({\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{\Omega}\phi){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}
as

\displaystyle{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}(x)\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(-R,R)}\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2}\bigg)\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}(y)\,\phi_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\bigg).

This symbol equals zero outside the square

\displaystyle\{(x,y):0<x<2R,\,0<y<2R\}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}\op^{{\rm w}}(\phi_{\Omega}){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}\,\op^{{\rm w}}(\phi_{\Omega})\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}.

Let
\eta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
be a function such that
\eta(t)=1
for
t\in[-4R,4R]
. Then

{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}(x)\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}(y)={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}(x)\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{(0,2R)}(y)\eta(x-y)
.
This leads to the representation

\displaystyle{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}\op^{{\rm w}}(\phi_{\Omega}){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}={\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}\op^{{\rm a}}(p)\,{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+},\quad p(x,y,\xi)=\phi_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{x+y}{2}\bigg)\,\eta(x-y).

Estimate singular values of
\op^{{\rm a}}(p)
.
Let the unitary operator
U
be as in Theorem
2.2
, i.e.
(Uf)(x)=f(-x)
.
Since
the multiplication by a unitary operator does not change the
s
-values,
we consider
\op^{{\rm a}}(p)U
instead.
By Remark
2.3
, this operator coincides with
\op^{\rm w}(b)
, where

\displaystyle b(x,\xi):=\phi_{\Omega}^{*}(x,\xi)\,\eta(-2x),

and
\phi_{\Omega}^{*}
is the dual symbol of
\phi_{\Omega}
, as defined in (
2.5
).
Since
\eta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}
, Theorem
2.4
ensures that
the symbol
b
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{\xi}^{n}\,b(x,\xi)\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-\beta}\,\langle\xi\rangle^{-1},\quad m,n=0,1,\dots,

with an arbitrary
\beta>0
. According to Corollary
5.4
,

\op^{\rm w}(b)
belongs to
\mathbf{S}_{1,\infty}
, and hence so does

{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}\op^{{\rm w}}(\phi_{\Omega}){\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}
,
as claimed.
∎

The above lemma entails the following asymptotic symmetry. Let
{\sf{G}},{\sf{G}}^{\pm}
, and

{\sf{g}},{\sf{g}}^{\pm}
be as defined in Sect.
3
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{\pi/2}
. Then

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1,1}^{+}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})={\sf{G}}_{1,1}^{-}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=\frac{1}{2}{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}),\quad{\sf{g}}_{1,1}^{+}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})={\sf{g}}_{1,1}^{-}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=\frac{1}{2}{\sf{g}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}).

Proof.

Represent
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
in the block-matrix form:

\displaystyle{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}={\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}+{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(2)},

\displaystyle{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{-}\\
{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{-}{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}&0\\
0&{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{-}{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{-}\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
7.2
,
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(2)}\in\mathbf{S}_{1,\infty}
, so that

{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(2)})=0
.

As for the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}
, its spectrum is symmetric, i.e.

n_{+}(\lambda;{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})=n_{-}(\lambda;{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle W^{*}{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}W=-{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)},\ \quad\textup{with the unitary}\quad W=\begin{pmatrix}{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{+}&0\\
0&-{\rm{\mathbf{1}}}_{-}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus

n(s;{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})=2n_{\pm}(s;,{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})
for all
s>0
, and therefore,

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}^{\pm}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})=\frac{1}{2}{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}),\ {\sf{g}}^{\pm}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)})=\frac{1}{2}{\sf{g}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}).

Since
{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(2)})=0
, by Corollary
3.2
, in the equalities above
the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}^{(1)}
can be replaced with
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
which completes the proof.
∎

7.4.
The Schrödinger operator

At the last stage of the proof we link the counting function
for the operator
{\sf{A}}_{\Omega}
with that of some Schrödinger operator on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In the subsection
we provide the necessary facts about discrete spectrum of the appropriate Schrödinger operator.

Let
V=V(x),x\in\mathbb{R}
, be a bounded real-valued function such that

V(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
.
Let
H
be the self-adjoint differential operator associated with the closed quadratic form

(7.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\big(|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}-V(x)|u(x)|^{2}\big)\,dx,\quad u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}).

On its domain, the operator
H
is given by the differential expression

\displaystyle Hu=-u^{\prime\prime}-Vu,

i.e. it is the Schrödinger operator with a potential
-V
. We write
H_{0}
if
V=0
.
The operator
H
can have only discrete spectrum below
\lambda=0
.
Let
\#(\lambda,V),\lambda>0,
be the number of eigenvalues below
-\lambda<0
.
Along with the operator
V
consider the
compact operator

\displaystyle T(\lambda)=(H_{0}+\lambda)^{-1/2}\,V\,(H_{0}+\lambda)^{-1/2},\ \lambda>0.

The next identity, known in the literatrure as the Birman-Schwinger principle,
relates the counting functions for
H
and for
T(\lambda)
:

(7.3)

\displaystyle\#(\lambda,V)=n_{+}(1,T(\lambda)).

If
V\geq 0
, then the operator
T(\lambda)
can be factorized in a natural way:

(7.4)

\displaystyle T(\lambda)=\big(\op^{\rm l}(P_{0})\big)^{*}\,\op^{\rm l}(P_{0}),\quad\textup{where}\quad P_{0}(x,\xi)=\sqrt{V(x)}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\lambda}}.

Thus the identity (
7.3
) can be recast as follows

(7.5)

\displaystyle\#(\lambda,V)=n(1,\op^{\rm l}(P_{0})).

We shall use this equality to obtain spectral information on
\op^{\rm l}(P_{0})
from
spectral properties of the Schrödinger operator. To this end we
define Schrödinger operators on
sub-intervals of the real line.

For
R>0
let
H^{(<)}_{R}
,
H^{(\circ)}_{R}
,
H^{(>)}_{R}
be the self-adjoint operators
associated with the
quadratic form (
7.2
) on the domains
\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{0}(-\infty,-R)
,

\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{0}(-R,R)

and
\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{0}(R,\infty)
respectively. In other words,
the above operators are Schrödinger operators on
(-\infty,-R)
,
(-R,R)
and
(R,\infty)

with the Dirichlet condition at
x=-R
and
x=R
.
Denote by
\#^{(<)}_{R}(\lambda,V)
,
\#^{(\circ)}_{R}(\lambda,V)
and
\#^{(>)}_{R}(\lambda,V)
the number of eigenvalues of each operator below
-\lambda<0
.
Recall the well-known
decoupling principle
:

(7.6)

\displaystyle\big|\#(\lambda,V)-

\displaystyle\ \big[\#^{(<)}_{R}(\lambda,V)+\#^{(\circ)}_{R}(\lambda,V)+\#^{(>)}_{R}(\lambda,V)\big]\big|\leq 2.

This inequality is based on standard facts of theory of extensions for symmetric operators,
see e.g.
[
1
, Ch. 8]
and
[
2
, Ch. 9.3]
.
We need
the following formula for
\#^{(\circ)}_{R}(\lambda;V)
,
see
[
20
, Theorem 6.4]
and also
[
18
, Lemma 5.2]
:

Proposition 7.4
.

Suppose
V\in\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is a non-negative
function, and that
0<R<\infty
. Then for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\#^{(\circ)}_{R}(\lambda,V)-

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-R}^{R}\,\sqrt{V(t)}\,dt\bigg|

(7.7)

\displaystyle\ \qquad\qquad\leq\int_{-R}^{R}\,\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{|V^{\prime}(t)|}{(V(t)+|\lambda|)}\,dt+\frac{6R\sqrt{|\lambda|+1}}{\pi}+1.

We shall apply the formula (
7.4
) with a very specific choice of the potential
V
:

Corollary 7.5
.

Let

\displaystyle V(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}.

Then for all
g\geq 1
, we have

(7.8)

\displaystyle|\#(1,gV)-\frac{\sqrt{g}}{\pi}\log g|\lesssim\sqrt{g},

with a constant independent of
g
.

Proof.

We use the decoupling principle (
7.6
) with
R=\sqrt{g}
. Then
gV(x)<1
for

|x|>R
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-R}\big(|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}+(1-gV(x))|u(x)|^{2}\big)dx>0,\quad\textup{for all}\quad u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{0}(-\infty,-R),

and the same holds for the interval
(R,\infty)
. Therefore,

(7.9)

\displaystyle\#^{(<)}_{R}(1,gV)=\#^{(>)}_{R}(1,gV)=0.

To estimate
\#^{(\circ)}_{R}(1,gV)
we use (
7.4
).
Find the integral on the left-hand side of (
7.4
):

\displaystyle\int_{-\sqrt{g}}^{\sqrt{g}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\,dx=\log g+O(1).

The right-hand side of (
7.4
) does not exceed

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<\sqrt{g}}\,\frac{|x|}{1+x^{2}}\,dx+\sqrt{g}+1\lesssim\log g+\sqrt{g}+1.

Together with (
7.9
), the formulas (
7.6
) and (
7.4
) entail (
7.8
).
∎

7.5.
Proof of formula (
7.1
)

Recall that
\theta=\pi/2
. Without loss of generality we assume that
\phi(0,0)=1
.
By virtue of Proposition
7.3
, the equalities (
7.1
) will follow if we prove that

(7.10)

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})={\sf{g}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}.

To avoid writing too many formulas, we discuss only
{\sf{G}}_{1,1}
.
The functional
{\sf{g}}_{1,1}
can be
considered in the same way. Theorem
2.4
implies that

{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})={\sf{G}}_{1,1}(\op^{\rm w}(b))
for the dual symbol
b=\phi_{\Omega}^{*}
defined
in (
2.5
).
In the representation (
2.6
) the symbol
b_{1}
satisfies
(
2.8
), and hence, by virtue of
Corollary
5.4
,
{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm w}(b_{1})\big)=0
. Thus it follows from
Corollary
3.2
that

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm w}(b)\big)={\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm w}(b_{0})\big),\quad b_{0}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{\zeta(x)}{x}\,\frac{\zeta(\xi)}{\xi}.

Since
\partial_{x}\partial_{\xi}b_{0}(x,\xi)=0(\langle x\rangle^{-2}\langle\xi\rangle^{-2})
and

N_{1,1}^{\circ}[\langle x\rangle^{-2}\langle\xi\rangle^{-2}]=0
, we conclude that the sequence

\sf w
defined by (
6.2
) with
a=b_{0}
, satisfies
M_{1,1}^{\circ}[{\sf w}]=0
.
As a result, Theorem
6.2
and Corollary

3.2

guarantee that

{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm w}(b_{0})\big)={\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(b_{0})\big)
.
The singular values remain the same if we mutliply

\op^{\rm l}(b_{0})

from the right by
{\rm sgn}\,\xi
, and from the left by
{\rm sgn}\,x
,
which leads to the equality

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(b_{0})\big)={\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(\tilde{b}_{0})\big)\quad\textup{with}\quad\tilde{b}_{0}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\zeta(x)\zeta(\xi)}{|x|\,|\xi|}.

By Corollaries
5.4
and
3.2
again,

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(\tilde{b}_{0})\big)={\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(p_{0})\big),\quad p_{0}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\langle x\rangle\,\langle\xi\rangle}.

To find the functional
{\sf{G}}_{1,1}\big(\op^{\rm l}(p_{0})\big)
write

\displaystyle n(s;\op^{\rm l}(p_{0}))=n(1;\op^{\rm l}(P_{0})),

where
P_{0}(x,\xi)
is the symbol defined by (
7.4
) with
\lambda=1
and
the potential
s^{-2}V(x)>0
,
where

\displaystyle V(x)=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}.

By (
7.5
) and (
7.8
),

\displaystyle n(s;\op^{\rm l}(p_{0}))=\#(1;s^{-2}V)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}s}\log\frac{1}{s}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{s}\bigg).

Substituting this formula in definition (
3.8
), we obtain that

{\sf{G}}_{1,1}(\op^{\rm l}(p_{0}))=(2\pi^{2})^{-1}
. Consequently,

{\sf{G}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=(2\pi^{2})^{-1}
as well. The same argument shows that

{\sf{g}}_{1,1}({\sf{A}}_{\Omega})=(2\pi^{2})^{-1}
. This proves (
7.10
) and hence completes the proof
of the formula (
7.1
).
∎

One should remark that the spectral asymptotics of

Given the specific form of the symbol

Sect.
