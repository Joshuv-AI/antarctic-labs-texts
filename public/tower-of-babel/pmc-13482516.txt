Seroprevalence and associated risk factors of Toxoplasma gondii in goats in Choma District, Zambia
Authors: Chiluba Zimba, Musso Munyeme, Simegnew Adugna Kallu, Nicholus Chintu Sande, John Bwalya Muma, Victor Chisha Zulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToxoplasmosis, caused by Toxoplasma gondii, is a widespread zoonotic disease associated with reproductive losses in livestock and important public health consequences. Despite the recognized veterinary and public health importance of toxoplasmosis, data on T. gondii infection in goats in Zambia are currently lacking. Therefore, a cross-sectional seroepidemiological study was conducted among goats in eight veterinary camps in Choma District to determine the seroprevalence and associated risk factors of T. gondii infection. A total of 324 goat serum samples were examined for IgG antibodies against T. gondii using a commercial ELISA kit. Potential risk factors associated with T. gondii seropositivity in goats were evaluated using chi-square and logistic regression analyses. The overall seroprevalence of T. gondii was 3.1% (95% CI: 1.68–5.59). Female goats showed a higher seroprevalence (4.13%), whereas all male goats tested negative. The significant risk factors identified were the presence of stray cats within farm yards (cOR = 5.54; 95% CI: 1.40–21.90; p = 0.015) and cat access to feed and water troughs (cOR = 5.69; 95% CI: 1.37–23.54; p = 0.016). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of T. gondii in goats in Zambia, providing baseline seroprevalence data and evidence of zoonotic exposure risk. These findings have implications for veterinary and public health, as well as food safety, and highlight the need for integrated One Health surveillance strategies.Keywords: Elisa, Goats, Seroprevalence, Toxoplasma gondii, Zambia

Introduction

Toxoplasmosis is a globally distributed zoonotic disease caused by the obligate intracellular protozoan parasite Toxoplasma gondii (Dubey, 2010; Tenter et al., 2000). The parasite infects all warm-blooded animals, including humans, livestock, wildlife, and companion animals, making it one of the most important foodborne and environmentally transmitted pathogens worldwide (Hill & Dubey, 2002; Robert-Gangneux & Dardé, 2012). Members of the family Felidae, particularly domestic cats, are the definitive hosts and shed environmentally resistant oocysts in their faeces, contaminating soil, water, feed, and pasture (Dubey, 1998; Elmore et al., 2010). Due to its wide host range, complex transmission dynamics, and public health significance, T. gondii remains an important concern in both veterinary and human health (Montoya & Liesenfeld, 2004).

Goats are considered one of the most susceptible livestock species to toxoplasmosis and play an important epidemiological role in the transmission of T. gondii (Stelzer et al., 2019). Infection of T. gondii is associated with reproductive disorders including abortion, stillbirth, neonatal mortality, fetal mummification, and weak offspring, leading to substantial economic losses in both smallholder and commercial production systems (Dubey, 2010; Ortega-Mora et al., 2007). In addition to its veterinary importance, caprine toxoplasmosis also has public health significance because humans may acquire infection through consumption of raw or undercooked goat meat and unpasteurized goat milk (Cook et al., 2000; Dubey et al., 2014; Muhammad et al., 2026). The presence of viable tissue cysts in edible goat tissues highlights the importance of goats in the epidemiology of foodborne toxoplasmosis (Khairullah et al., 2024).

The seroprevalence of T. gondii infection in goats varies considerably worldwide depending on geographical location, climatic conditions, management systems, diagnostic methods, and the level of environmental contamination with oocysts (Ahaduzzaman & Hasan, 2022; Hajimohammadi et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2022). Warm and humid environmental conditions favour long-term survival of oocysts and increase transmission risk (Lélu et al., 2012).

In Africa, toxoplasmosis in goats has gained increasing attention because of its impact on livestock productivity and its zoonotic importance (Tonouhewa et al., 2017). Several serological studies, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses conducted across Africa, have demonstrated widespread exposure of goats to T. gondii, highlighting the extensive distribution and epidemiological importance of the parasite in caprine populations across the continent (Atif et al., 2024; Karshima et al., 2020; Gebremedhin & Tadesse, 2015; Odeniran et al., 2020; Ouchetati et al., 2021). Despite increasing evidence from many African countries, information regarding caprine toxoplasmosis remains limited in Zambia.

Reproductive loss in goats in Zambia remains poorly understood. Muma et al. (2006) reported that goats with a history of abortions presented with arthritis during the final trimester but tested negative for Brucella species, suggesting that other pathogens may be implicated. Although serological evidence of T. gondii infection has been reported in humans and cats in Zambia (Daka et al., 2024; Frimpong et al., 2017; Phiri et al., 2023), information on its occurrence in goats remains scarce. The detection of infection in these hosts suggests possible environmental contamination and active circulation of the parasite within the country, highlighting the potential risk to livestock and public health. This represents a critical knowledge gap that hinders the development of effective prevention and control strategies. Baseline seroprevalence data are essential for quantifying the burden of infection, identifying associated risk factors, and assessing public health risks linked to the consumption of goat meat and other products. Within the One Health framework, understanding T. gondii epidemiology in goats is further warranted by the close interface between humans, animals, and the environment. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of T. gondii infection in goats in Choma District of Zambia and identify associated risk factors. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to the understanding of caprine toxoplasmosis in Zambia and support future surveillance and control strategies under a One Health approach.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Choma District, located in the Southern Province of Zambia. Choma is one of the largest districts in the Province (Fig. 1). It has a latitude of 16.771,111, a longitude of 26.992,222 and is 1337 m above mean sea level. Samples were collected from veterinary camps within Choma District namely; Mapanza (26° 52′E and 16° 8′S), Simaubi (26° 49′E and 16° 20′S), Namoonza (27° 5′E and 16° 51′S), Batoka (27° 14′E and 16° 46′S), Choma central (26° 47′E and 16° 49′S), Nakeempa (26° 54′E and 17° 0′S), Simaluba (27° 2′E and 17° 4′S), and Masuku (27° 5′E and 17° 15′S). Farming is the primary source of income in the eight veterinary camps with major activities including crop production and rearing of animals under an extensive farming system for both meat and milk production. According to the 2023 livestock survey report, the total goat population in the country as of April 2023 was 5568,730 and the Southern province had the highest population of goats which accounts about 38.1% of the total national goat herd. Most goats are kept extensively in homesteads. In Zambia, goats are an important component of smallholder mixed crop–livestock systems and contribute substantially to household income, food security and asset buffering. Nationally, goat numbers have increased over recent decades, and the species is widely distributed across all provinces, with high concentrations in rural areas where they are primarily kept under traditional, low‑input management. Goats are mainly reared for meat, which is consumed both at household level and through informal and formal markets, and to a lesser extent for milk and as a source of cash during times of need. Herds are typically small to medium in size, dominated by indigenous breeds, and managed under communal grazing or free‑range systems, with limited use of intensive housing or concentrate feeding.

Study design and sample size determination

A cross-sectional seroepidemiological study was conducted from June to September 2021 to determine the seroprevalence and risk factors of T. gondii infections in goats. The study population was selected purposely and divided into eight veterinary camps. The study population consisted goats in the study area. A simple random sampling method was used to select the household to participate. The number of goats sampled from each camp was determined proportionally to the total goat population in the study area.

The sample size was calculated using the formula provided by Thrusfield (2018) as n = Z2 Pexp (1-Pexp)/d2 where, n is desired sample size, Z is the standard normal value corresponding to 95% confidence level (1.96), Pexp is expected prevalence, which was assumed to be 50% since the expected prevalence was not known, and d is margin of error (0.05), which yield a total of 384 goats. Although the calculated sample size was 384, a total of 324 samples were ultimately collected and analyzed. For each sampled goat, a structured data sheet was used to record individual-level epidemiological variables, including age, sex, presence of stray cats in the farm, and presence and access of cats to feed and water.

Blood sampling and serological examination using ELISA

A total of 324 goat serum samples were collected during the study period. Approximately 3 ml of blood was collected from the external jugular vein of each goat using plain Vacutainer tubes. The blood was held at room temperature to allow clotting, after which serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Aliquots of serum were transported in a cooler box with ice to the University of Zambia, School of Veterinary Medicine. The serum samples were stored at −20 °C until the time for analysis. The sera were tested for IgG antibodies against T. gondii using an indirect ELISA diagnostic kit (IDVET Innovative Diagnostic kit, ID Screen, Montpellier, France) with 100% sensitivity and 96% specificity following the manufacturer's instructions.

About 90 µl of Diluted Buffer 2 was added to each micro-well. Then, 10 µl of negative control was added to wells A1 and B1, 10 µl of positive control was added to wells C1 and D1, and 10 µl of each sample was added to the remaining wells. The plate was then incubated for 45 min at 21 °C. Each well was emptied and washed three times with 300 µl of wash solution. The 1x conjugate was prepared by diluting the 10x concentrated conjugate 1:10 in dilution buffer 3. Then, 100 µl of 1x conjugate was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 30 min at 21 °C. After the wells were emptied and washed three times with 300 µl of wash solution, 100 µl of substrate solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 15 min at 21 °C in the dark. Finally, 100 µl of stop solution was added to each well to halt the reaction.

Positive and negative controls, provided by the manufacturer, were used to validate each test. The Optimal Density (OD) was read at 450 nm using an ELISA plate reader. The percentage values were calculated as %OD = (OD sample – OD negative control)/ (OD positive control – OD negative control) * 100. Samples were classified as positive when the OD value was ≥ 50%, doubtful when it was between 40% and 50%, and negative when it was ≤ 40%.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2013, where they were checked and cleaned for completeness and consistency before being exported to SPSS (SPSS Inc., IBM Corporation, version 21, Chicago, IL, USA) for analysis. Age of goats was estimated by examining the lower incisors and classifying animals according to the number and pattern of erupted permanent teeth, following standard dentition-based guidelines for small ruminants (Wilson & Durkin, 1984). For analysis, we grouped goats into two age categories: young (< 2 years) and adult (≥ 2 years). Initially, associations between each independent variable and T. gondii seropositivity were assessed using chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate due to small cell counts). Given the small number of seropositives, variables with a p-value <0.10 in the univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Seroprevalence of T. gondii in goats

A total of 324 goat serum samples were tested for T. gondii antibodies; 10 were seropositive, yielding an overall seroprevalence of 3.1% (95% CI: 1.68–5.59). Seroprevalence was higher in females at 4.13% (95% CI: 2.26–7.44) than in males, where no positives were detected, however, this proportion of seropositive did not differ significantly (p = 0.062). By age, adult showed a 4.00% (95% CI: 2.12–7.43) seroprevalence and 1.01% (95% CI: 0.18–5.50) in young goats, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.152). Among veterinary camps, Simaubi had the highest seroprevalence at 6.38% (95% CI: 2.19–17.16), followed by Masuku at 5.97% (95% CI: 2.35–14.37), Batoka at 2.33% (95% CI: 0.41–12.06), Namoonza at 2.22% (95% CI: 0.39–11.57), and Siamaluba at 1.92% (95% CI: 0.34–10.12); no seropositive samples were found in Mapanza, Nakeempa, or Choma Central.

The overall seroprevalence of T. gondii in goats differed by cat presence in the farm vicinity and by cat access to feed and water troughs (Table 1). Goats on farms with stray cats in the vicinity had a seroprevalence of 7.00% compared with 1.34% on farms without stray cats (p = 0.006). Similarly, seroprevalence was 12.00% among goats on farms where cats had access to feed and water troughs versus 2.99% on farms without such access (p = 0.007). These differences indicate a significantly higher prevalence of T. gondii infection in goats exposed to cats or to cat‑accessible feed and water sources.

In the univariable analysis, the presence of stray cats in the farm vicinity was associated with more than a fivefold increase in the odds of T. gondii seropositivity in goats (crude OR = 5.54; 95% CI: 1.40–21.90; p = 0.015). Goats on farms where cats accessed water and feed troughs were 5.69 times more likely to be T. gondii positive than goats on farms without such access (crude OR = 5.69; 95% CI: 1.37–23.54; p = 0.016). Although adult (crude OR = 4.08; 95% CI: 0.51–32.68) goats and females (crude OR = 7.45; 95% CI: 0.42–128.59)) had higher odds of seropositivity, these associations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

Toxoplasma gondii is an important zoonotic protozoan parasite with considerable veterinary and public health significance because of its association with reproductive disorders in small ruminants and its potential transmission to humans through contaminated meat, milk and environmental exposure. Goats are regarded as highly susceptible intermediate hosts and may play an important role in the epidemiology of toxoplasmosis in endemic areas. In the present study, an indirect ELISA detected an overall seroprevalence of 3.1% among goats in Choma District, Zambia, confirming exposure of goats to T. gondii in the study area. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented report of T. gondii seroprevalence in goats in Zambia, thus providing important baseline epidemiological information for the country. The detection of seropositive goats is consistent with previous reports of T. gondii infection in women attending antenatal care clinics in Namwala (Daka et al., 2024), which borders Choma District, as well as in Lusaka (Frimpong et al., 2017), and reports of infection in cats from Lusaka District (Phiri et al., 2023). Together, these findings suggest that the parasite is already circulating within both definitive and intermediate hosts in Zambia. Although the seroprevalence observed was relatively low compared with findings from several other African countries, the detection of seropositive goats indicates exposure to T. gondii and suggests the parasite is present in the study area.

The current study finding is in agreement with the 3.2% seroprevalence in Botswana (Sharma et al., 2003), 3.2% in Algeria (Dechicha et al., 2015), 4.3% in South Africa (Samra et al., 2007), and 4.57% in Nigeria (Kamani et al., 2010). However, higher seroprevalence in goats were reported in other regions of Africa and elsewhere, including the 8.5% in Morocco (Benkirane et al., 2015), 19.7% (Zewdu et al., 2013) and 27.6% (Tilahun et al., 2018) in Ethiopia, 38.28% in Egypt (Selim et al., 2023), 53.91% in South Africa (Tagwireyi et al., 2019), 18% in Pakistan (Khattak et al., 2024), 41.2% in Argentina (Novoa et al., 2023). Despite the low seroprevalence observed, this study highlights a potential risk of T. gondii to both human and animal health in Zambia. Variations in reported seroprevalence are largely driven by differences in exposure and study factors, including water and feed sources, regional climate, sample size, animal age, husbandry system (intensive versus extensive), and diagnostic methods used (Dubey, 2004; Innes et al., 2009).

Seroprevalence was higher in female goats (4.13%) than in males (0%), aligning with findings from Clementino et al. (2007) and Zewdu et al. (2013). However, Bahreh et al. (2021) and Zhao et al. (2011) reported the opposite trend, with higher rates in males. Other studies found no significant sex-based differences (Chettih et al., 2024). Female goats often show higher seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis than males, likely due to management and physiological factors. Females are usually kept longer for breeding and milk production, increasing their chance of repeated exposure to T. gondii oocysts in feed, water, soil, and pasture (Rodrigues et al., 2022). The increased seropositivity of females may also be associated with reduced immunological resistance during certain life stages, such as pregnancy and lactation, which can cause immunosuppression and make them more susceptible to T. gondii infection (Guimaraes et al., 2013). In addition, females are often kept closer to homesteads and cat-contaminated areas, which can further raise exposure risk (Rodrigues et al., 2022). Nevertheless, this pattern is not consistent across all studies, suggesting that differences in husbandry, environmental contamination, and local epidemiology also play important roles (Gebremedhin & Tadesse, 2015; Rodrigues et al., 2022). Although seropositivity appeared higher in females, this finding should be interpreted with caution because only 10 animals tested positive, limiting the strength of inference regarding sex-related differences.

In the present study, adult goats showed a higher seroprevalence of T. gondii (4.00%) compared with younger goats (1.01%); however, the difference was not statistically significant. The relatively higher seropositivity observed in adult animals may be attributed to increased cumulative exposure to T. gondii oocysts over time, as older goats have a greater likelihood of encountering contaminated feed, water, pasture, or contaminated environments during their lifespan. Similar age-related trends have been reported in several studies in small ruminants, where seroprevalence increased with age because of prolonged exposure opportunities (Bachan et al., 2018; Fortes et al., 2018; Tegegne et al., 2016; Zewdu et al., 2013). Nevertheless, the absence of a statistically significant association in the current study suggests that age may not have been a major determinant of infection in the study population, possibly because of the overall low prevalence detected, the limited number of seropositive animals, or relatively similar management and environmental exposure conditions among the different age groups.

This study identified key cat-related factors associated with T. gondii seropositivty in goats, including presence of stray cats and cat access to goat feed and water troughs. These associations are biologically compelling, as felids are the only definitive hosts of T. gondii and are responsible for shedding vast number of environmentally resistant oocysts that contribute substantially to environmental contamination and transmission of the parasite worldwide (Dubey et al., 2020; Elmore et al., 2010; Shapiro et al., 2019). Goats from households where stray cats were present had approximately fivefold higher odds of T. gondii seropositivity. Stray cats roam freely across grazing pastures, animal shelters, feed storage areas, and water points, thereby facilitating widespread environmental contamination with T. gondii oocysts. Their predatory behaviour further amplifies transmission risk because hunting rodents and birds, which commonly serve as intermediate hosts, increases the likelihood of acquiring and subsequently shedding infective oocysts into the environment (Zhu et al., 2022). Cat access to goats’ feed and water troughs tripled infection odds, underscoring contaminated feed and water as critical transmission pathways in our study area. Cats often defecate directly into or near these sites, enabling goats to ingest oocysts while feeding or drinking. A single infected cat can excrete millions of oocysts daily for up to two weeks, rendering soil, pasture, hay, and troughs highly infectious for grazing goats over extended periods (Dubey et al., 2020).

Similar associations have been reported both in African and worldwide, including from Algeria (Chettih et al., 2024), Brazil (Fortes et al., 2018), in Myanmar (Bawm et al., 2016), in Pakistan (Ahmad et al., 2015), and Romania (Hotea et al., 2021), In these studies the presence of cats on farms was identified as a significant risk factor for T. gondii infection in small ruminants, reflecting increased environmental exposure to oocysts shed in cat faeces. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis has also confirmed that cat presence is one of the most consistently reported risk factors for caprine toxoplasmosis globally, strengthening the biological plausibility of the associations observed in the present study (Rodrigues et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings align with the well-established role of felids as definitive hosts of toxoplasmosis, in which they shed large numbers of environmentally resistant oocysts that contaminate soil, pasture, feed, and water. The consistency between the current results and previous studies across different production systems and regions highlights the importance of implementing targeted control measures, such as restricting cat access to goat housing, feed storage areas, and watering points, as a practical strategy to reduce environmental contamination and interrupt transmission in endemic settings. Nevertheless, not all studies have reported significant associations between cat-related factors and T. gondii seroprevalence in goats, and similar inconsistencies in the significance of cat-associated risk factors have been documented in some epidemiological investigations conducted elsewhere (Djokic et al., 2014; Gazzonis et al., 2015; Tilahun et al., 2018; Tonouhewa et al., 2019; Zewdu et al., 2013). The absence of statistically significant associations in some investigations may be attributed to differences in study design, ecological conditions, sample size, management systems, and the widespread environmental persistence of oocysts, which can complicate accurate assessment of direct cat exposure. In addition, indirect transmission through contaminated pasture, feed, soil, or water may occur even in the apparent absence of cats at the time of sampling, thereby masking measurable associations between cat-related variables and seropositivity.

The demonstration of T. gondii infection in goats in this study carries important public health implications, as infected animals can serve as a source of infection for humans through the consumption of raw or undercooked meat, unpasteurized milk, and milk products containing tachyzoites or tissue cysts. Given the confirmed link between consumption of unpasteurized milk and large‑scale toxoplasmosis outbreaks in humans (Shapiro et al., 2019), the practice of drinking raw milk, common in many pastoral and rural communities, may represent a significant yet underappreciated route of transmission. Furthermore, the presence of T. gondii in goats reinforces concerns about the broader role of small ruminants in the parasite’s epidemiology, particularly in settings where husbandry practices favour close contact with cats, contaminated soil, untreated water, and poorly washed vegetables. These findings underscore the need for targeted public health interventions, including health education on safe food and milk handling, promotion of pasteurization, proper cooking of meat, and improved hygiene practices in both livestock management and household food preparation to reduce the risk of zoonotic transmission.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design precludes inference of causality between identified risk factors and T. gondii seropositivity. Serological testing reflects prior exposure only and does not distinguish between recent and chronic infection. The relatively small number of seropositive animals may have reduced the statistical power of the risk factor analysis and increased uncertainty around some estimates. In addition, important confounders such as detailed cat population density, grazing behaviour, and environmental contamination levels were not fully measured, which may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, the study was conducted in a limited geographic area of Zambia, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other ecological and production systems. Despite these limitations, the study provides important baseline evidence on the epidemiology of T. gondii in goats and identifies key environmental risk factors that warrant further investigation using longitudinal designs and molecular approaches.

Conclusion

This study provides the first documented evidence of T. gondii exposure in goats in Zambia, detecting a 3.1% seroprevalence in Choma District and identifying cat-related factors as significant predictors of infection, which points to environmental contamination with oocysts as a key transmission route. Although prevalence was relatively low compared with many settings, the finding indicates ongoing transmission and a potential zoonotic threat via meat and milk. We recommend expanding serological and molecular surveillance across districts and production systems to define the national burden, implementing targeted control measures, restricting cat access to housing, feed and water, improving hygiene, managing stray-cat populations, and controlling rodents, and prioritizing longitudinal and genotyping studies to confirm active infections and assess public‑health risk.
