Unseen or neglected? school educators’ perspectives on identifying and supporting adolescent young carers in Pakistan: a qualitative study
Authors: Muhammad Ateeb, Shahbaz Ahmad Zakki, Ijaz Ul Haq, Najia Zulfiqar, Kiran Rizwan, Sabahat Bibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSchool professionals are key to student well-being, yet their role in identifying and supporting young carers (YCs) in Pakistan remains unexplored. Existing literature highlights limited awareness among educators but evidence from low-resource settings is lacking. This study aims to address this gap by exploring educators’ awareness, perceptions and support strategies regarding YCs to inform context-sensitive school mental health interventions.DesignA qualitative study employing in-depth, semi-structured interviews was conducted between March and June 2025, following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist. Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis.SettingThe study was conducted in public higher secondary schools located in the district of Haripur, Pakistan.ParticipantsThirty-three teachers, administrators and school staff were recruited through purposive sampling to ensure a diversity of roles, gender and professional experience.ResultsMost participants (80%) were unfamiliar with the term ‘young carer’, though many had encountered caregiving students. Five core themes emerged: limited awareness shaped by cultural perceptions, informal identification processes, the academic and emotional burden experienced by YCs, insufficient institutional support and concrete recommendations for policy and practice. Educators expressed a strong willingness to help but cited a pervasive absence of training, policies and dedicated services as primary barriers.ConclusionLimited awareness and structural barriers hinder schools’ ability to support YCs. Findings reveal an urgent need to equip school professionals with targeted training and pragmatic strategies to identify and support YCs, enabling them to transform their willingness into effective action.

Introduction

Children and adolescents who assume responsibility for supporting an ill, disabled or dependent family member are commonly referred to as young carers (YCs).1 Their responsibilities may include emotional support, household chores, practical assistance or even medical tasks, typically for parents, siblings or grandparents, but sometimes for extended relatives in multi-generational households.2 3 Caregiving can foster empathy, resilience and maturity,4 5 but outcomes depend heavily on recognition and support from families, peers and institutions.6 7

Caregiving at school age

Adolescence is a critical period for education, identity formation and social development.8 For YCs, these formative years are often disrupted by balancing school with caregiving.9 The majority of YCs are aged 11–17,10 although some may begin their responsibilities at a young age of five. The number of YCs is expected to rise due to ageing populations and shifting family structures.11 12 While their contributions are vital, studies report heightened psychological distress, anxiety and emotional fatigue compared with non-carers.13 Social isolation and disrupted identity formation are also common,14 affecting educational attainment, employment and long-term well-being.15 16

School as a site of recognition and support

Schools can provide YCs with stability, opportunities for socialisation and respite from caregiving.17 Teachers, counsellors and school nurses are often the first to notice absenteeism, distress or declining performance.18 19 Yet, awareness in educational settings remains inconsistent. Many YCs conceal their roles due to stigma or fear of intervention.20 21 Even when disclosed, support is often minimal, with educators citing a lack of training or clear guidelines.22

While studies from high-income countries have improved understanding of how schools identify and support YCs, important questions remain unanswered in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where educational resources, formal caregiving services and student support systems are often limited. Qualitative studies in Switzerland23 and France24 have primarily documented educators’ awareness of YCs and the challenges associated with recognition. However, little is known about how school professionals in LMIC settings interpret caregiving responsibilities, respond to caregiving-related challenges and perceive their role in supporting affected students within distinct cultural and institutional contexts.25

In Pakistan, extended family systems and scarce formal healthcare services suggest many adolescents, especially girls, shoulder hidden caregiving roles.26 27 With 40% of the population under 18, schools could be critical for early recognition and support.28 Yet, no studies have examined how school professionals perceive or respond to these students in Pakistan, particularly in rural and semi-urban regions, which raises critical questions: do schools have supportive environments for YCs? Can educators recognise the subtle signs of caregiving stress? Why has the role of educators in supporting YCs been largely ignored in policy?

The present study

The present study aims to explore school professionals’ knowledge, perceptions and proposed support strategies regarding YCs in Pakistan. By extending previous work into an under-researched LMIC context, this study sought to generate practical evidence for teachers, school counsellors, school leaders and policymakers on improving the identification and support of YCs within educational settings.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was conducted between March and June 2025 in public higher secondary schools located in Haripur District, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. The district includes a mix of rural and semi-urban schools, offering insights into diverse institutional contexts. Schools were selected based on findings from a prior unpublished caregiver screening survey carried out in August 2024 because they were considered information-rich settings where caregiving responsibilities among students were more likely to be identified. This approach enabled the research team to explore educators’ experiences and perceptions in schools where potential YCs had already been observed.

Screening survey and selection of schools

The screening involved 41 public schools and used brief caregiver-related questions adapted from validated tools in the literature.29 30 These questions aimed to identify students potentially involved in caregiving roles at home, thereby serving as an indicator of the likely presence of YCs in the student population. Across these 41 schools, the screening identified a total of 1814 YCs. Most were girls (70.4%) and the majority were caring for a parent or grandparent. From these 41 schools, 10 were selected for in-depth qualitative interviews based on maximum variation criteria: geographical location, gender composition, school size and number of YCs identified. Among these 10 selected schools, the number of YCs per school ranged from 35 to 92, reflecting our prioritisation of schools with higher concentrations of the target population to ensure rich data.

Participant recruitment and sampling

A purposive sampling approach was used to ensure diverse perspectives across roles, disciplines and experience levels. The inclusion criteria for participants comprised current employment in a public school in Haripur, direct engagement with students through teaching, administration or student support, and a minimum of 1 year of professional experience in the education sector. Recruitment continued until sufficient depth, diversity and richness of data had been generated to address the study objectives. In practice, the emergence of new ideas was monitored across consecutive interviews by reviewing interview summaries after each set of five interviews, noting when recurring patterns appeared without contradictory or novel findings. After approximately 30 interviews, no substantially new insights arose.31 However, consistent with Braun and Clarke’s32 reflexive thematic analysis approach, the goal was meaningful interpretation rather than achieving thematic saturation as traditionally defined.32 A total of 33 school professionals were interviewed. The final sample ensured representation of both teaching and non-teaching voices, contributing to a holistic understanding of school-level identification and support practices regarding YCs. This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for transparency in reporting (see online supplemental file 1). Anonymised participant interview details and summary statistics of participant characteristics are provided in online supplemental table S1 and online supplemental table S2, respectively (see online supplemental file 2).

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on a previous study by Justin et al.24 Their study of 33 school staff in France found that almost all had encountered a YC, yet few took action to support them, with healthcare professionals and counsellors more aware than teachers. Key domains from their guide included recognition of caregiving roles, perceived impacts on students, barriers to identification and potential support strategies. Building on this foundation, our guide included a deeper exploration to examine not only whether educators in Pakistan were aware of YCs but also how they interpreted the concept once introduced, as shown in table 1. This approach moves beyond simple identification to provide richer insight into the ways school professionals in Pakistan perceive and respond to the hidden caregiving experiences of students. The interview guide was culturally adapted through discussions among the research team to ensure relevance to the Pakistani educational and sociocultural context. Adaptations included the use of locally familiar terminology, exploration of family caregiving norms, gender-related expectations and culturally appropriate examples to facilitate participants’ understanding of caregiving responsibilities among adolescents. The interview guide was pre-tested with two teachers not included in the final sample, and minor revisions were made for clarity and relevance. Content validity was ensured through expert review by experts in the domain.

Data collection procedure

The semi-structured interviews were conducted in a private space within each school to ensure comfort and privacy. The face-to-face interview guide protocol is provided in online supplemental file 3. Informed written consent was secured from all participants before data collection, and a copy of the consent form is included in online supplemental file 4. With permission, the interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Thematic analysis

Interviews were analysed using thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke.33 The process involved six key steps: familiarisation with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the final report. The coding process was primarily inductive, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data. However, we also applied deductive elements by cross-referencing emerging codes with established constructs from the literature, such as ‘hidden caregiving’, ‘academic burden’ and ‘young carer identification and support’. This hybrid approach ensured that our analysis was both grounded in participant voices and connected to existing theoretical frameworks.34 All transcripts were first read multiple times to develop familiarity with the contents, followed by organisation and theme development using NVivo software (V.15). The themes were then refined through comparison across interviews to ensure consistency and richness.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The research team comprised individuals with backgrounds in public health (n=2), psychology (n=1) and qualitative research (n=3). Several members had prior experience working with adolescents and school-based mental health initiatives in Pakistan. Recognising that researchers’ professional backgrounds and assumptions may influence data interpretation, reflexive discussions were conducted throughout the study. Coding decisions, theme development and interpretations were regularly reviewed among team members to enhance transparency, minimise individual bias and strengthen analytic rigour. Regular memo-writing and reflective journaling were maintained throughout the process to enhance the credibility and transparency of the analysis.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 33 school professionals participated in the study, including subject teachers (n=28), administrators (n=2) and non-teaching support staff (n=3). The mean age was 37.6 years, ranging from 24 to 55 years. A majority were female (n=19) and most were secondary school teachers (57.6%), followed by teaching trainers, administrators and support staff. Participants had an average of 10.5 years (SD=±8.2) of teaching or professional experience, with a range between 1 and 25 years (table 2).

Thematic findings

The thematic analysis of the interviews led to the development of five major themes, further divided into fourteen interrelated sub-themes. These themes provide a comprehensive understanding of school educators’ awareness, identification strategies, perceptions of impact, current support systems and proposed strategies. Limited awareness and culturally-shaped perceptions

Educators demonstrated limited prior awareness of the concept of YCs, with their understanding often influenced by cultural norms that normalise caregiving roles among children, especially girls. Care responsibilities tended to remain culturally invisible, frequently perceived as natural familial duties rather than exceptional burdens.

Unfamiliar terminology

Most respondents (80%) were unfamiliar with the term “young carer”, despite having encountered such students in practice.

Young carer? That’s a new term for me. (P14)

However, after the definition was shared, many recalled having taught students who fit the description.

Honestly, I had never heard the term before your visit. But now that I think about it, there are definitely children here who take care of their families. (P30)

This indicated that terminological gaps were a barrier to early identification and structured support, not necessarily the absence of caregiving experiences in schools. The concept of “young carers” was not only underrepresented in policy but also linguistically absent in educational discourse.

Hidden caregiving within cultural and family norms

Participants reported that many students engaged in substantial caregiving at home. These tasks exceed typical expectations for adolescents. More than half of participants (57%) described such responsibilities in their students’ lives.

One male student often skipped assignments. When I asked, he said his father was paralysed and needed help moving around the house. That was when I realised some students don’t just go home; they step into a full-time job. (P4)

Deep-rooted cultural norms further obscure recognition. Most participants (96.9%) viewed caregiving by children, especially girls, as part of a good upbringing or moral duty rather than problematic. Gender roles shaped expectations, with female students disproportionately taking on caregiving responsibilities.

We are taught that caring for parents is a virtue. So, if a student is doing it, it’s good. (P18)

In many families, girls are expected to cook, clean, look after younger siblings and even take care of their ill grandparents. (P9)

About 68% felt these responsibilities were simply part of growing up rather than a burden. Many could not name such roles in society. Adolescent caregiving remains invisible, not because it is hidden but because it is assumed to be normal.

Informal identification within school settings

School professionals highlighted a lack of systematic identification methods for YCs. Recognition of these students was often based on incidental disclosures, behavioural cues and informal teacher-student interactions rather than formal protocols.

Family and peer disclosures

Some teachers reported that caregiving roles came to light through accidental disclosures, either during conversations with parents or via classmates who unintentionally revealed a peer’s situation. About 39% of participants mentioned ever being directly informed about a student’s caregiving duties by the student or their family

Sometimes friends tell us, ‘Sir, he didn’t do his homework because he had to go to the hospital with his father’. It’s not the student himself, but his friends who become the bridge. (P29)

Awareness often comes through students confiding in a trusted teacher or peers.

Classroom red flags

Many educators identified caregiving responsibilities based on behavioural and academic signs such as frequent absences, low participation, emotional withdrawal or sudden drops in performance. Approximately 36% of participants acknowledged noticing such patterns but rarely linked them explicitly to caregiving.

One of my students kept falling asleep during class. Later, I found out she wakes up at five am to take care of her mother before school. (P28)

The findings revealed how behavioural red flags could have signalled hidden caregiving responsibilities but were often misinterpreted due to a lack of awareness or formal training. In many cases, teachers responded with disciplinary measures rather than providing appropriate support.

Not identified or overlooked

Some participants admitted to overlooking or misinterpreting signs, especially in large classes. About 24% (n=8) stated they had not been able to identify such students at the time, though they now recognise them in hindsight.

There are so many students in one class—it’s not always possible to notice who is suffering quietly. (P3)

We might label them as lazy or inattentive….not knowing they’re shouldering adult responsibilities at home. (P19)

These reflections highlight those systemic constraints, like teacher workload and lack of psychosocial training, that contribute to the invisibility of YCs, even when warning signs are present.

Profound impacts on young carers’ lives

Educators described how caregiving responsibilities adversely affected YCs’ academic performance, emotional health and social engagement. At the same time, some educators acknowledged unintended positive outcomes, such as increased empathy and maturity in these students.

Absenteeism and academic disruption

Several participants (94%) noted that academic underperformance was often the first visible sign of underlying caregiving responsibilities.

One teacher shared, ‘There was this… girl who was always behind on assignments and was frequently absent from class. When I asked, she quietly said she is an only child, and her mother is on dialysis, and she has to do all the housework” (P20).

Such disclosures were rare, as students typically hesitated to share personal struggles, leading educators to misinterpret caregiving-related challenges such as academic disinterest or irresponsibility.

Silent burnout

Participants described signs of psychological exhaustion that several referred to as ‘emotional fatigue’. About 52% (n=17) of the participants said they had encountered students who appeared emotionally numb, withdrawn or irritable but without understanding the underlying caregiving burden.

One girl had anxiety attacks during exams. We thought it was exam stress. Later, I came to know she was taking care of her younger sibling with autism and doing night shifts. (P12)

This quote illustrates the invisible toll of caregiving, where children experience chronic stress and mental fatigue but internalise it to avoid burdening others or being labelled as different.

Maturity and empathy development

While their experiences are challenging, some teachers (21%) observed enhanced maturity in these students.

One boy would help other students constantly—carrying books, solving problems. Later, I found he was doing the same at home for his disabled brother. His kindness came from experience. (P19)

Such reflections suggested that while caregiving may have fostered personal growth in some students, it could not compensate for their unmet needs or the systemic neglect of their vulnerabilities.

Current school support systems and gaps

Educators described various informal support mechanisms currently offered within schools, while also pointing to major gaps in institutional training, resources and policy. Support efforts were generally individualised and inconsistent, leading to inconsistencies and unmet needs.

Ad-hoc educators’ support

Participants (58%) shared that after realising their caregiving responsibilities, they personally tried to accommodate or emotionally support students they suspected to be under caregiving stress. These acts included extending deadlines, offering extra tuition or simply listening.

I let one student submit her assignments late because I knew he had to take his relative to dialysis every week. But this is just my personal decision; there’s no official guideline to follow. (P20)

We help where we can, but we’re not psychologists. I feel helpless sometimes. (P22)

Teachers’ compassionate responses underscore the goodwill within the education system, but their limitations reveal the lack of institutionalised procedures and professional support.

Systemic resource and policy gaps

Most participants (84%) reported that even when they recognise caregiving burdens, the absence of essential support services limits meaningful intervention. Trained counsellors, psychologists and referral protocols are absent from schools in Pakistan.

We don’t have any psychologist or counsellor in our schools. If a child is facing stress or is mentally disturbed, we can’t offer any proper help except moral support. (P3)

Almost 90% (n=29) were unaware of any policy or checklist related to caregiving burdens among students.

There’s no form or column where you can mark a student as a carer. We just write ‘poor performance’ or ‘irregular’. That’s it. (P1)

The lack of policy hampers consistent support and invalidates the identity of YCs, leaving them invisible within the education system.

Proposed strategies for supporting young carers

Forward-looking perspectives were offered by educators on what institutional and policy changes were necessary to support YCs more effectively.

Building educator capacity

Most participants (76%, n=25) emphasised the lack of awareness among school staff about YCs, with only three participants having received any prior training related to psychosocial issues or caregiving burdens, revealing a major gap in current practice. Consequently, they called for structured sensitisation training.

Experts can provide training and resources to school staff to better understand and support young carers and create a supportive environment. (P9)

We’re not trained to deal with these emotional complexities. We need capacity-building workshops—like Self-Help Plus or training-of-trainers models—where we’re first equipped ourselves and then able to deliver the same support to students, so they can help themselves. (P33)

These reflections signal a readiness among educators to learn but also expose systemic gaps in pre-service and in-service teacher education.

Academic flexibility

Some participants (27%) proposed flexible academic accommodations for known carers, including relaxed attendance policies, modified workloads and personalised support. Rigid academic systems inadvertently punish caregiving students.

One boy failed twice just because he couldn’t attend school regularly. His father is paralysed and he does everything for him. Instead of helping, the school pushed him out. (P4)

Educators noted that such accommodations could prevent dropout and improve retention among YCs without compromising academic integrity.

School-based support services

Access to mental health and counselling services emerged as a critical unmet need. Some respondents (33%) emphasised the importance of in-school counselling or referral pathways to mental health professionals.

Even a monthly visit by a psychologist would be better than nothing. We can’t fix everything as teachers. (P23)

Additionally, some participants supported financial support measures for YCs from low-income families.

Many of them are also financially responsible. Some kind of financial support would go a long way. (P31)

Equity-based scholarships could reduce school discontinuation and keep YCs in education.

Formal recognition and policy inclusion

Nearly all participants (97%) called for formal recognition of YCs in national education policy. They suggested inclusion in admission forms, school record systems and child protection frameworks.

If the child is poor, we have a column. If the child is an orphan, we note it. But if a child is caring for a sick mother or disabled sibling, it’s invisible. (P4)

Make it part of the education policy. Train headteachers. Add it to admission forms. Give it a name so we can give them help. (P18)

Formalised identification systems are foundational for channelling academic, emotional and financial support.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to highlight the institutional invisibility of YCs in Pakistan and explore school educators’ awareness, perceptions and proposed responses within the public education system. Although many educators described encountering students with caregiving responsibilities, awareness of the ‘young carer’ concept was limited and formal mechanisms for identification and support were largely absent. The findings suggest that caregiving responsibilities are frequently normalised within family and cultural contexts, reducing opportunities for recognition and early intervention within educational settings. The clear, actionable recommendations for training and policy offered by educators reveal a systemic gap that, if addressed, could transform schools into effective safety nets for this vulnerable population.

Global evidence shows that YCs remain largely hidden across both high- and low-income countries.35–37 In Pakistan, this invisibility is intensified by the absence of terminology, cultural taboos and institutional gaps. Whereas the United Kingdom and Australia have enacted formal policies and school-based identification protocols,38 39 most educators in this study had never encountered the term YC. This mirrors findings from other LMICs where the lack of formal classification frameworks hampers recognition.24 40 In contrast to the findings of Dearden and Becker in the UK,41 Pakistani teachers struggled to label caregiving as a problem because family duty is viewed as a normal rite of passage. Such cultural “blindfolds” obscure psychological distress and academic disruption among caregiving students,42 offering South Asian, Muslim-majority insights to an under-represented evidence base. This invisibility was particularly gendered. Educators described caregiving among girls as a socially expected family responsibility rather than a potential burden, aligning with international literature showing that in collectivist and patriarchal societies, girls are disproportionately assigned caregiving roles.43 44 Unlike high-income countries, where gender disaggregated data on YCs are available,21 Pakistan has no such data, meaning interventions must address the specific cultural mechanisms that render girl carers invisible.

Once provided with a definition of ‘young carers’, many educators recalled having interacted with students who appeared to take on such roles. However, these identifications were largely unstructured, relying on personal intuition, behavioural observation or occasional peer disclosures. Similar to prior international studies,24 our findings indicate that educators serve as first-line observers but often lack the training to respond systematically. The absence of screening tools, referral protocols or school-based awareness programmes has resulted in inconsistent recognition practices. While India has piloted regional school-based awareness programmes for educators,45 Pakistan lacks even basic training, leaving YCs invisible due to cultural norms, institutional neglect and limited teacher awareness.17 Unlike high-income countries, where identification procedures and support pathways are well established,40 schools in LMIC settings face a more fundamental challenge: recognition itself. Before formal referral systems can be implemented, educators must first understand caregiving as a distinct student experience rather than an expected family obligation, an important contribution of the present study to the emerging LMIC evidence base.

The impacts of caregiving on students’ emotional, academic and social lives were strongly articulated by teachers. Consistent with global findings,46 47 YCs in Pakistan often face absenteeism, emotional fatigue and disrupted academic progress. Yet, in parallel, some teachers observed positive traits such as resilience, empathy and maturity, echoing earlier research suggesting that caregiving can foster socio-emotional competencies under supportive conditions. These positive outcomes are more likely to be realised when YCs are identified early. Evidence from other countries shows that earlier identification leads to improved school attendance, reduced emotional distress, lower school dropout rates and decreased anxiety and depression compared with those identified later or not at all.4 48

The existing school support systems in Pakistan are largely fragmented and informal. While some educators reported offering personal support to affected students, they consistently noted a lack of resources, appropriate referral options and access to psychological counselling within public schools. These gaps align with national studies,49 50 which reveal limited psychosocial support integration within existing healthcare systems. The lack of formal policies or training on YCs further undermines institutional response capacity.40 Teachers were often unsure of how to escalate concerns or whom to contact for professional help. As noted by one participant, “Experts can provide training and resources to school staff, enabling them to better understand and support YCs”. Evidence from high-income countries shows that uptake of YCs’strategies is supported by designated school leads, staff training programmes, awareness campaigns and collaboration between educational and health services. It was found that tailored support produced meaningful well-being improvements for YCs and their families.6

Encouragingly, teachers proposed actionable strategies, including awareness campaigns, policy inclusion, formal recognition, academic flexibility, scholarships and integration of psychosocial services into school settings. These recommendations align with international guidelines, such as the UK’s Young Carers Support Act (2014) and the WHO School Health Guidelines (2021). Notably, teachers stressed that awareness-raising must begin at the school level.51 A teacher remarked, ‘We are not trained to recognise these burdens. If you give us tools and connect us with health services, we can do more’. This bottom-up engagement, led by trained educators, is crucial for embedding inclusive support structures, and although the UK programmes651 may not transfer directly to resource-constrained Pakistan, international evidence confirms that low-cost initiatives can substantially improve recognition and support in settings where no infrastructure currently exists.52

This study offers a valuable contribution by engaging a diverse group of school professionals to uncover hidden caregiving roles among students, an area previously unexamined in Pakistan. The findings carry important implications for teacher education, school mental health initiatives and broader educational policies. Among the most feasible initial steps within the Pakistani education system are incorporating YCs’awareness into existing teacher professional development programmes, developing simple school-based guidance for recognising caregiving-related challenges and strengthening referral pathways between schools and available health and social support services. Such measures require relatively limited resources and may provide an important foundation for more comprehensive support systems.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted in public schools within a single district, which may limit the transferability of findings to other educational settings. Because participants were recruited from schools identified through a preliminary screening process, there is also the possibility of selection bias, as educators with greater exposure to caregiving-related situations may have been more likely to participate. In addition, the findings are based on educators’ perceptions and experiences rather than direct observations or reports from YCs themselves. Consequently, some caregiving experiences may have remained unidentified or been interpreted differently by students. Fieldwork constraints, including scheduling difficulties, limited privacy during interviews and participants’ unfamiliarity with the topic, may also have influenced the depth of discussion. Future research should incorporate the perspectives of YCs, families and policymakers across diverse educational contexts to develop culturally appropriate and sustainable support strategies. Recognising and responding to the needs of YCs has the potential not only to improve their educational and psychosocial outcomes but also to contribute to a more inclusive and equitable school system in Pakistan.

Conclusion

This study revealed substantial gaps in the recognition and support of YCs within Pakistani schools. Although educators expressed a strong willingness to help, limited awareness, culturally shaped perceptions and the absence of formal policies and support mechanisms hindered effective identification and assistance. These findings highlight the need to integrate YC awareness into teacher training, school policies and existing student support services. Future research should incorporate the perspectives of YCs themselves and evaluate scalable school-based interventions that can be adapted across diverse educational settings in Pakistan and other LMICs.
