Poster 263. Revision Posterior Labral Repair: Outcomes and Risk Factors for Failure
Authors: Aidan M McQuade, Conor F McCarthy, Hannah P Gibbs, Scott M Feeley, Valentina Ramirez, Benjamin W Hoyt, Jonathan F Dickens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Arthroscopic posterior labral repair is the most common surgical treatment for posterior instability. However, for patients with recurrent instability, little is known about outcomes after revision arthroscopic stabilization. The purpose of this study was to evaluate outcomes after revision posterior labral repair in a young, active population, and to evaluate risk factors for subsequent clinical failure. We hypothesized that there would be a high failure rate and that failure would be associated with osseous factors associated with posterior instability.Methods:We identified revision posterior labral repairs performed in the Military Health System between 2006 and 2022 for instability and/or pain. Preoperative imaging was evaluated for glenoid version, posterior glenoid bone loss, acromial tilt, acromial height, and humeral head subluxation. Mechanism of injury and operative characteristics were recorded to include labral tear location using the clock face technique, as well as anchor number and location. Patients were excluded if either the index or revision stabilization procedures did not include a posterior labral repair with anchor fixation or if a bone block augmentation was performed. The primary outcome was the rate of failure, defined as any of the following: reoperation, activity-limiting pain, separation from the military related to the shoulder, or recurrent instability (dislocation, subluxation, or pain with instability on exam).Results:Thirty-two patients met inclusion criteria. At median available follow up of 4.0 years (IQR 2.5-8.4) after revision surgery, 13/32 patients (40.6%) experienced failure of their revision procedure, with recurrent instability as the most common cause of failure (9/13, 69.2%). Factors associated with revision failure included older age at index surgery (25.2 years versus 22.5 years, p=0.0449), less time from index surgery to revision surgery (2.46 years versus 3.55 years, p=0.046), and earlier (more anterior extension of tear) start time on the clock face (4:30 versus 5:30.0151). There was also an association between less posterior humeral head subluxation prior to revision surgery and revision failure (60.1% v 52.1%, p=0.0398). There was no association with traumatic versus atraumatic mechanism (p=0.863), reason for failure of index surgery (pain versus instability, p=0.671), or with osseous morphology including glenoid version (p=0.246), glenoid bone loss at time of revision (p=0.651), acromial tilt (p=0.6744), or acromial height (p=0.550).Conclusions:Failure after revision posterior labral repair occurred in more than 40% of this young, active population, most commonly due to recurrent instability. In contrast to prior evidence in primary posterior labral repair, osseous morphology was not significantly associated with failure in this cohort, while older age was associated with failure. Notably, posterior humeral head subluxation appeared to be a protective factor for revision failure.
