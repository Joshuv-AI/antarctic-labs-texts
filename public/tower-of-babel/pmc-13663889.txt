Dating, disclosure, and romantic relationships pose challenges for adults with congenital heart disease: A mixed-methods study
Authors: Floor C. van der Meijs, Philippine Kiès, Loes A.M. van Winden, Marloes Duijkers, Veronica R. Janssen, Henk W. Elzevier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs survival into adulthood has improved, adults with congenital heart disease (CHD) increasingly face psychosocial challenges related to long-term living with their condition. Romantic relationships are a key source of social support, yet the impact of the condition on dating and romantic experiences remains poorly understood.ObjectivesTo explore dating and romantic relationship experiences in adults with congenital heart disease (CHD), focusing on disclosure and communication, while describing patterns by sex and disease complexity and incorporating partner perspectives.MethodsThis national cross-sectional online survey included individuals aged ≥16 years with CHD. Dating- and relationship-related questionnaire items were analyzed using descriptive quantitative and inductive thematic qualitative analyses, including descriptive subgroup analyses by sex and disease complexity. Partner perspectives were included to provide relational context.ResultsAmong 109 CHD patients (69% women, 54% aged 26-40 years, 87% with moderate to severe disease), 21% reported dating difficulties, 40% difficulties disclosing their condition to dates, and 14% relationship dissolution attributed to CHD. Relationship challenges mainly concerned family planning (27%) and limitations in shared activities (25%). Qualitative findings, supplemented by five partner perspectives (n = 5), suggested fear of rejection during early disclosure and the importance of open communication. Descriptively, women and patients with moderate to severe disease reported more difficulties.ConclusionsThis mixed-methods study provides novel exploratory insight into the relational impact of CHD. Disclosure and communication appear particularly challenging during relationship establishment, whereas physical limitations contribute to challenges within established relationships. These findings support integrating relational support into routine CHD care.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital anomaly, affecting approximately one percent of live births [1]. Over recent decades, advances in medical and surgical care have led to substantial improvements in survival, with over 85% of individuals with CHD now reaching adulthood [2]. As a result, the population of adults with congenital heart disease (CHD) is steadily growing, highlighting the importance of understanding the long-term psychosocial impact of living with CHD.

Social support is a key protective factor for psychosocial well-being, and romantic relationships represent one of the most important sources of such support in adulthood [3]. In the general population, being in a romantic relationship is associated with better psychosocial functioning, including higher quality of life, lower psychological distress, healthier behaviors, and stronger social integration [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Within the CHD population, romantic relationships have similarly been identified as a major protective factor against depression [8], suggesting that intimate relationships may also play a broader role in psychosocial well-being in this population.

At the same time, living with a chronic physical condition may pose challenges within romantic and intimate contexts. Individuals with chronic illness and their potential partners have reported concerns related to social stigma, anxiety, and anticipated caregiving burden [9,10]. For CHD patients, additional factors such as visible surgical scars, repeated medical interventions, and uncertainty regarding health, fertility, and life expectancy may negatively influence body image, self-confidence, and comfort with emotional and physical intimacy [11,12]. These challenges may contribute to findings that CHD patients are less often involved in romantic relationships compared to healthy peers [13].

Disclosure and communication constitute another important aspect of dating and romantic relationships in the context of chronic illness. Self-disclosure is essential for the development and maintenance of intimate relationships and is associated with greater perceived support and well-being [14]. However, many young adults with chronic conditions report concealing their illness due to fear of stigma or being perceived as weak or burdensome [15]. When disclosure does occur, couples who engage in open communication and shared coping report higher relationship satisfaction and improved well-being [16], underscoring the relevance of disclosure processes in romantic relationships among CHD patients.

Existing research on romantic relationships in CHD patients has primarily focused on broad psychosocial outcomes such as health-related quality of life and psychological distress [17,18]. This literature is largely quantitative and outcome-oriented, often examining relationship status or distress scores rather than relational experiences [8,13,19]. Moreover, qualitative studies remain limited and tend to address broader life experiences or adjacent themes, rather than examining dating and romantic relationships specifically [20,21]. Consequently, the lived experience of dating, intimacy, and disclosure in this population remains insufficiently explored.

Therefore, the aim of this mixed-methods study is to explore how CHD patients experience dating and romantic relationships in relation to their condition. Specifically, we examine the perceived influence of CHD on romantic relationships and experiences of disclosing CHD within dating and intimate contexts. Additionally, variation by sex and disease complexity is explored to identify potentially more vulnerable subgroups. To provide further relational context, partner perspectives are included on an exploratory basis.

Methods

Study design and participants

This national cross-sectional online survey included Dutch-speaking individuals aged ≥16 years with self-reported CHD living in the Netherlands and partners of individuals with CHD. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. The study was conducted in collaboration with the Dutch patient association for congenital heart disease (Patiëntenvereniging Aangeboren Hartafwijkingen) and Leiden University Medical Center.

Questionnaire development

The present study formed part of a larger questionnaire-based research project examining the psychosocial impact of living with CHD [22]. As no validated instruments covered the psychosocial domains of interest, a purpose-designed questionnaire was developed by a multidisciplinary team in collaboration with the patient association. Questionnaire content was informed by the study aims, literature, and prior institutional research. The questionnaire was pilot tested for clarity and feasibility by 20 clinicians and patient association representatives, and refined accordingly.

The patient questionnaire comprised 67 items across five domains. The present analyses included 16 items: five demographic and eleven closed-ended items on dating and romantic relationships. Seven closed-ended items used a three-point Likert scale. Included questionnaire items are provided in the Appendix. Items were developed and administered in Dutch and translated into English for publication. Participants could skip any item.

Partners completed a separate questionnaire comprising 20 closed-ended items and one open-ended question about their relationship with an individual with CHD. Responses were not linked to patient questionnaires. Due to the low response rate (n = 11), only the open-ended question, asking partners to provide advice for current or prospective partners of individuals with CHD, was analyzed qualitatively.

Data collection

The questionnaire was disseminated through the patient association via its website, member newsletter, and social media between October and December 2024. Healthcare professionals affiliated with the association were also asked to inform eligible patients about the study. All responses were collected anonymously in accordance with applicable privacy and data protection regulations.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize response patterns. For consistency across items, positively framed disclosure-related items were reverse-coded before analysis and visualization, with higher values indicating greater perceived difficulty.

CHD type was self-reported in an open-ended demographic item. CHD complexity was subsequently classified by a cardiologist (PK) as mild, moderate, or severe based on anatomical characteristics according to the 2020 ESC Guidelines [23]. The morphological distribution of defects within each complexity category was described using the Botto classification system [24].

Qualitative analysis

The open-ended item was examined using inductive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [25]. Themes were derived directly from the data without a pre-existing coding framework. One researcher (FvdM) performed the initial coding of all responses, assigning individual statements to one or more themes where applicable. Responses that were irrelevant to the research question were excluded, and the number of excluded responses was documented.

To enhance the coherence of the developing thematic structure, a second researcher (PK) independently coded a randomly selected 16% subset of responses, in line with recommendations [26]. Differences were discussed to refine codes and themes, which were iteratively reviewed against the full dataset. All analytic decisions were documented. Themes were summarized in a conceptually clustered matrix, with descriptive frequency counts indicating occurrence rather than importance. Illustrative quotations were selected, and all responses were translated from Dutch into English for publication.

Handling of missing data

Analyses focused on a single domain of a broader questionnaire, for which item completion was optional. Participants who completed at least one relationship item were included. Analyses were performed using available data without imputation, and the number of valid responses is reported for each item. Baseline characteristics of responders and non-responders were compared using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses defined responders as participants completing ≥50% (6/11) of relationship items.

Results

Response rate

Between October and December 2024, 259 individuals with self-reported CHD initiated the questionnaire. Of these, 109 (42.1%) completed at least one relationship item and were included in the analyses. Eleven partners initiated the partner questionnaire, of whom five completed the open-ended question included in the qualitative analysis. As the number of eligible individuals exposed to the survey was unknown, a response rate could not be calculated.

Missing data

No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between responders (participants completing ≥1 relationship item) and non-responders, and findings were robust in sensitivity analyses. Among the 109 responders included in the analyses, 95 completed all relationship items. Three of 98 patient responses were excluded from the qualitative analyses because they were not relevant to the research question. Item-level missing data were not assessed for partners, as only a single open-ended question was analyzed.

A separate multiple-choice item assessed specific relationship difficulties, with participants able to select multiple responses. Approximately half reported no relationship difficulties (53.2%, n = 58). Among those reporting difficulties, concerns related to family planning and having children (26.6%, n = 29) and limitations in shared activities (24.8%, n = 27) were most common, whereas sexuality-related (10.1%, n = 11) and other difficulties (12.8%, n = 14) were less frequently reported.

Communication and disclosure

A separate multiple-choice item assessed the timing of disclosure. Disclosure most often occurred on a first date (30.3%, n = 33) or later in the dating process (27.5%, n = 30), whereas disclosure after a relationship was established was uncommon (5.5%, n = 6). A substantial proportion selected other response options (36.7%, n = 40), including prior partner awareness, situational disclosure, or non-applicability. The most prominent theme was open and honest communication. Patients described disclosing their condition openly, often early in the relationship, viewing it as an integral part of their identity and expressing little interest in relationships in which it was not accepted. Some reported that visible surgical scars facilitated early disclosure, whereas others emphasized openness about both their condition and associated emotions.

Neutral communication also emerged frequently and was characterized by factual, matter-of-fact disclosure focused on practical implications. In contrast, emotionally charged communication involved fear, vulnerability, or tension, particularly during early dating. Fear of rejection was another common theme, often related to disclosure itself and its perceived consequences, including family planning.

Acceptance and understanding were commonly described, with patients reporting supportive and reassuring responses from partners, including during periods of more severe symptoms. Perceived lack of understanding was less common and reflected experiences of rejection or negative reactions from partners, dates, or their families.

Discussion

In this mixed-methods study, we explored how patients with CHD and their partners experience dating and romantic relationships in relation to the condition. Building on previous research, which has predominantly focused on relationship status or associations with coping and mental health outcomes [8,13,19,27], we combined quantitative and qualitative data to contextualize disclosure and communication, describe patterns by sex and disease complexity, and explore partner perspectives.

The psychosocial impact of CHD appeared to evolve across relationship stages, with women and patients with more complex disease reporting challenges more frequently. Early dating was characterized by disclosure concerns and fear of rejection, whereas established relationships were more often characterized by open communication and acceptance. However, disease-related challenges persisted despite this openness, suggesting that relationship challenges evolve alongside relationship development.

With regard to dating, approximately one-fifth of patients in the present study reported condition-related difficulties. Although relationship status was not assessed, this finding is consistent with previous research showing that CHD patients are less likely to be in romantic relationships than healthy peers and may avoid dating situations or experience dating-related distress [13,19]. These findings align with the scoping review by Jordan et al. [10], which identified symptom-, opportunity-, and confidence-related factors as key contributors to relationship challenges in young people with chronic conditions. Our qualitative findings suggested that these confidence-related difficulties were primarily driven by disclosure concerns and fear of rejection, although symptom-related (e.g., fatigue and physical limitations) and opportunity-related factors (e.g., reduced social participation and ongoing medical care) also contributed [11,28,29].

Although few patients wished to keep their condition private, many reported difficulties with disclosure and uncertainty regarding its timing. Nearly all patients disclosed their condition before entering a serious relationship, underscoring its relational significance, while qualitative findings showed that disclosure was often experienced as a vulnerable and emotionally charged process despite patients generally favoring openness. These findings may be understood through a normative study by Derlega et al. [30], which demonstrated that disclosure in romantic relationships involves weighing relational closeness and a perceived sense of duty to inform one's partner against fears of rejection or burdening the partner. In the context of chronic illness, this process may be further complicated by anticipated stigma and fears of being perceived as a burden or a poor long-term “investment”, often linked to concerns about prognosis, fertility, and life expectancy, as also reflected in prior qualitative CHD studies and the present data [20,31].

Qualitative findings indicated that, once the condition had been disclosed, communication with partners often became open and honest, and experiences of acceptance and understanding were described as meaningful. Although based on only five qualitative responses, partner responses similarly emphasized openness following disclosure, including communication about sensitive topics such as sexuality. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that disclosure and subsequent communication may function not only as sources of vulnerability during dating, but also as relational resources once a partnership is established [10,16].

Nevertheless, relational openness did not appear to fully eliminate disease-related challenges. Quantitative findings indicated that CHD-related difficulties persisted within specific relational domains, particularly limitations in shared activities and concerns regarding family planning. Notably, these domains are consistent with the most frequently reported insecurities identified in our previous study based on the same questionnaire [22]. In addition, patients reported that partners could experience the condition as burdensome, and that CHD could contribute to relationship dissolution. Together, these findings suggest that acceptance and open communication may coexist with, but do not necessarily offset, the ongoing physical and practical consequences of CHD.

Across relationship stages, women and individuals with more complex CHD generally reported difficulties related to dating, disclosure, and intimate relationships more frequently, consistent with previously reported psychosocial patterns in CHD patients [22]. Although these findings are descriptive and do not allow causal inference, they may reflect a greater disease-related burden in patients with complex CHD [17], and sex-related differences in psychosocial outcomes, including anxiety, self-esteem, and emotional responses [[32], [33], [34]].

Notably, while patients with mild CHD generally reported fewer relationship-related difficulties, disclosure of the condition nevertheless emerged as a relevant challenge even within this group. This suggests that disclosure represents a particularly sensitive aspect of romantic relationships in CHD patients, independent of overall disease severity. These descriptive patterns may suggest a role for both sex and disease complexity and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its nationwide recruitment through the national patient association. The mixed-methods design strengthened interpretation of the quantitative findings by contextualizing them with participants' lived experiences.

Several limitations should be considered. Recruitment through the patient association may have introduced selection bias, resulting in an overrepresentation of women, individuals aged 26–40 years, and patients with more severe CHD, thereby limiting generalizability of the findings. This recruitment strategy likely also contributed to the very small partner sample. As only five partners provided qualitative responses, partner-related findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution. In addition, subgroup analyses by sex and disease complexity were descriptive and exploratory and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and was not formally validated, and item non-response was substantial; however, responders and non-responders were comparable on measured baseline characteristics, suggesting limited non-response bias.

Reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability bias, and clinical characteristics could not be externally verified. Moreover, functional capacity and illness perceptions were not assessed, despite evidence that these factors may be more strongly associated with perceived disease burden than anatomical complexity in this population [18,35]. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality.

Implications for further research

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore partner perspectives in the context of dating and romantic relationships in CHD patients. Despite active recruitment efforts, partner participation was limited. The low response rate among partners may reflect broader patterns of lower participation among men in survey-based research [36,37], which was also observed in the predominantly female patient sample. Future studies should therefore explore targeted recruitment strategies to enhance partner participation.

Future research should include both patients and partners using comparable measures, preferably within longitudinal designs, to enable direct assessment of partner perspectives on burden, communication, and relationship challenges in CHD patients. In addition, the observed pattern of greater difficulties reported by women and individuals with more complex CHD warrants investigation in larger, adequately powered longitudinal studies, to test subgroup differences, explore underlying mechanisms, and assess potential age-related variation in experiences of dating and romantic relationships.

Conclusion

Dating, disclosure, and romantic relationships emerged as important concerns for CHD patients that warrant greater attention in routine care. Disclosure emerged as a particularly vulnerable stage of relationship development, while CHD-related relationship challenges persisted across relationship stages despite open communication and acceptance. Limited partner perspectives suggested that some relationship challenges may also be experienced by partners. Integrating relational and psychosocial support into CHD care may improve patient well-being and relationship functioning.
