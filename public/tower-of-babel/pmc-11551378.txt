Characterization of RNA editing and gene therapy with a compact CRISPR-Cas13 in the retina
Authors: Satheesh Kumar, Yi-Wen Hsiao, Vickie H. Y. Wong, Deborah Aubin, Jiang-Hui Wang, Leszek Lisowski, Elizabeth P. Rakoczy, Fan Li, Luis Alarcon-Martinez, Anai Gonzalez-Cordero, Bang V. Bui, Guei-Sheung Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceRNA editing with CRISPR-Cas13 has emerged as a powerful tool for transient control of gene expression, although recurrent reports of collateral activity are a hurdle for clinical translation. This study probes CRISPR-Cas13-mediated RNA editing and demonstrates gene therapy from a compact Cas13 enzyme known as Cas13bt3 to establish its potential as an anti-VEGF therapeutic against retinal neovascularization. Using AAV-mediated delivery into human embryonic stem cell-derived retinal organoids and humanized VEGF transgenic mice, the study elucidates overall transduction and transcriptomic impact from active Cas13bt3 in retinal cells and tissues. Furthermore, efficient and specific control of human VEGFA mRNA expression is demonstrated in a mouse model of proliferative retinopathy. Overall, the study reveals critical considerations and capabilities for Cas13-mediated retinal gene therapy.

The emergence of CRISPR and CRISPR-associated (Cas) enzymes, known as CRISPR-Cas, has fueled several gene editing efforts for novel therapeutics in the past decade (1). Developed by repurposing the bacterial adaptive immune defense against phage viruses, CRISPR-Cas harnesses the ability to induce targeted changes to DNA or RNA sequences. Among CRISPR-Cas proteins, CRISPR-Cas13 is of particular interest for its exclusive specificity for RNA. Targeting RNA is especially attractive as it enables control of gene expression without perturbing the genome and introducing permanent changes and is therefore an emerging avenue for therapeutic development (2).

Eleven variants of CRISPR-Cas13 have been described to date (3). Among them, Cas13d (commonly referred to as CasRx) described in 2018, garnered special attention for its compact size of 930 amino acids, making it the first Cas13 enzyme amenable to adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery. Studies investigating the development of RNA-targeting therapeutics have widely used CasRx to demonstrate preclinical efficacy against disorders of vision, hearing, and brain function (4–7); however, significant unintended editing has been reported hampering its clinical progress (8, 9), and the exact specificity profile of CasRx remains contested (10). More recently, hypercompact Cas13bt3 (also reported as Cas13X) enzymes were described, ranging even smaller at 775 amino acids (11, 12). The newfound enzyme has been reported to possess high specificity and its small size enables further applications such as RNA base editing using a single AAV (13–15). The therapeutic potential of Cas13bt3 RNA silencing is yet to be demonstrated, however initial in vitro studies show promising efficiency and safety (13, 16).

As a leading cause of blindness in the elderly population affecting 200 million people globally, ocular neovascularization refers to a group of conditions characterized by the abnormal growth of leaky blood vessels in the eye. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has been described as a key mediator of this process, and the overexpression of VEGFA underlies pathological angiogenesis in common ocular diseases like diabetic retinopathy (DR) and neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Intravitreal administration of anti-VEGF drugs is the approved front-line treatment modality for these patients (17). Frequent, ongoing (every 1 to 4 mo) intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents for long periods, often a lifetime, are required for the long-term stability of vision. However, repeated intravitreal injections are invasive and risk potentially blinding complications (18).

We have previously described a potential alternative therapy to current anti-VEGF injections using CRISPR-CasRx RNA editing system for the treatment of ocular neovascularization. This approach was validated in vitro in HEK293FT and MIO-M1 cells, demonstrating more than 90% and 50% VEGFA mRNA knockdown, respectively (19). Here, we used Cas13bt3 to demonstrate potent VEGFA knockdown in vitro in human cell lines, 3D human embryonic stem cells (hESC)-derived retinal organoids, and in vivo in humanized VEGF transgenic mouse retinae. Promisingly, our results show a significant knockdown of VEGFA mRNA in human cell lines without impact on predicted off-target genes. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was used to characterize Cas13bt3-mediated VEGFA mRNA knockdown at single cell resolution in human retinal organoids, identifying the overall transcriptomic impact in retinal cells. Last, in vivo gene therapy showed robust VEGFA mRNA silencing in mouse models of proliferative retinopathy along with control of neovascularization symptoms. Overall, these findings identify key considerations for developing CRISPR-Cas13 retinal gene therapies and demonstrate a potential clinical application as an anti-VEGF agent for an alternative to current treatment options.

Results

CRISPR-Cas13bt3 Can Knockdown VEGFA mRNA in Mammalian Cells with Minimal Unintended Targeting Activity.

We used our previously described single guide RNA (sgRNA) (19), based on the targeting sequence of bevasiranib (20), against Exon 4 of VEGFA mRNA to produce the Cas13bt3 plasmids and assess RNA cutting efficiency, followed by collateral and off-target cleavage (Fig. 1 A and B). Collateral activity is the trans-cleavage of nearby mRNA transcripts by Cas13 enzymes after nuclease domain activation (Fig. 1C). This is random and will differ with cell type and environment. Off-target activity, on the other hand, is the targeting of nontarget mRNA transcripts due to unspecific (or partially specific) binding of the sgRNA (Fig. 1D). These genes are expected to be targeted across cell type and environment should they be expressed.

We sought to compare and validate the efficiency of the Cas13bt3 enzyme by testing RNA cutting against two previously established RNA-targeting methods, RNA interference (short hairpin RNA, shRNA), and CasRx (a variant of Cas13d). The final all-in-one AAV plasmids carrying either VEGFA-targeting shRNA or sgRNA with either CasRx or Cas13bt3 (Fig. 1E) were transfected into HEK293FT cells. Here, to further enhance the expression of Cas13bt3, we incorporated the stronger cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter instead of the previously used elongation factor 1α short (EFS) promoter (19).

Significant VEGFA mRNA knockdown was observed from all three RNA-targeting methods, with up to 70% shRNA VEGFA mRNA knockdown (P < 0.001), while CasRx and Cas13bt3 achieved up to 90% knockdown (P < 0.0001) compared to vector controls (AAV plasmid carrying a fluorescent marker) (Fig. 1F; n = 4 independent experiments). Next, we sought to determine if the reduction in mRNA would also lead to a decrease in protein levels. Interestingly, ELISA for VEGFA revealed that only the Cas13bt3 treatment led to a significant reduction of VEGFA protein in the cell supernatant (Fig. 1G, P < 0.05; n = 4 independent experiments). While it is unclear why only the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA group showed a significant reduction in protein levels, it is well established that mRNA transcripts are not predictive of the corresponding protein levels (21). We further ruled out the influence of the Cas13bt3 enzyme and sgRNAs in mRNA silencing by demonstrating VEGFA mRNA knockdown only when Cas13bt3 was delivered with a targeting sgRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1; n = 3 independent experiments). Surprisingly, delivery of Cas13bt3 alone resulted in a significant upregulation of VEGFA mRNA (P < 0.001), while this was not the case when Cas13bt3 carried a nontargeting (NT) sgRNA, indicating crRNA loading may influence cellular response to Cas13bt3.

To identify the collateral activity of Cas13bt3 on the transcriptome, bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed after HEK293FT cells were treated with either vector control, Cas13bt3 only, Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA or Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA plasmids. DEG analysis of Cas13bt3 samples against control confirmed significant downregulation of VEGFA mRNA only in cells that received the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA treatment (Fig. 1H). Interestingly, we noted the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group compared to Cas13bt3 only or Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA (81 vs 27 and 23, respectively; Fig. 1I), a finding that corresponds with recent studies (16, 22). Moreover, we also explored the collateral activity of Cas13bt3 compared to shRNA and CasRx. A similar level of DEGs was found in all 3 groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), indicating that collateral activity from Cas13bt3 is comparable to the previously established RNA editing methods.

To understand off-target activity from Cas13, genes with high complementarity to the targeting sgRNA must be identified. We therefore identified potential off-target genes using NCBI BLAST for the VEGFA sgRNA sequence and studied the expression levels of the top 10 candidate off-target genes (VEGF sgRNA1; SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Promisingly, none of the off-target genes were significantly downregulated, indicating a lack of off-target activity from Cas13bt3, as was the case for shRNA and CasRx (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

To further examine the DEG levels between the nontargeting and targeting sgRNAs, we identified the top significantly enriched gene ontology (GO) terms for the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA and Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA groups. This allows identification of biological processes and molecular functions that may be affected by differential gene expression. Top 5 significant GO terms for the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group were all positive enrichments and specifically showed upregulation of RNA binding processes, such as “pre-mRNA binding” and “mRNA 3’ UTR binding”, possibly explaining the elevated levels of DEGs in this group. On the other hand, with the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA group, the top 5 GO terms were all negative enrichments and particularly showed downregulation of “platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) binding” and “PDGF receptor binding”, both angiogenesis promoting factors, indicating suppression of the angiogenic process (Fig. 1K).

The small size of Cas13bt3 and the significant downregulation of VEGFA mRNA with a single sgRNA further prompted us to investigate if multiple sgRNAs can be packaged with Cas13bt3 and whether this will influence overall RNA silencing. We obtained two additional sgRNAs targeting VEGFA mRNA at Exon 2 (sgRNA2) and Exon 4 (sgRNA3) based on RNA structure prediction to avoid stem-loops as previously described (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) (19). To develop a multiplexed Cas13bt3 system, we flanked each sgRNA with Cas13bt3 direct repeats on both ends to form a VEGFA sgRNA array driven by the U6 promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). We then compared the individual sgRNAs with the VEGFA sgRNA array to determine if the VEGFA sgRNA array affected overall silencing. Compared to the original (referred to here as sgRNA1), both sgRNA2 and sgRNA3 had poorer efficiency, with sgRNA2 yielding little to no VEGFA knockdown while sgRNA3 exhibited ~30% knockdown. However, with the VEGFA sgRNA array, a significant knockdown comparable to the original design (sgRNA1) group was observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C, n = 3 independent experiments). This showed that the VEGFA sgRNA array could still be processed by Cas13bt3 and competition for crRNA loading did not affect RNA silencing.

We then performed RNA-seq to similarly determine collateral and off-target cleavage from the VEGFA sgRNA array. To account for perturbations from the NT sgRNA, we compared DEGs to the NT sgRNA group and found DEGs for both single and sgRNA array groups were comparable, suggesting minimal collateral activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). Again, we identified top candidate off-target genes for the other VEGFA sgRNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C) and did not detect any significant off-target activity in these genes by the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA array system (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). As multiplexing Cas13bt3 did not achieve improved silencing activity, we investigated Cas13bt3 with a single sgRNA further.

AAV2.7m8 Delivery Efficiently Expresses Cas13bt3 in Various Retinal Cells.

Human organoids are excellent models for gaining clinically relevant information on new therapeutic agents, as they are derived from human pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) and reproduce morphological, functional, and transcriptomic features in vitro (23). Recently, hESC-derived retinal organoids have been used to investigate eye development and model inherited retinal diseases, as well as to test novel gene therapies (24, 25). While CRISPR-Cas9 has entered the clinic, CRISPR-Cas13-based therapies are still being extensively explored at the preclinical stage and demonstrating the utility of Cas13 in retinal organoids will be a major advance for ocular gene therapy.

Here, we used our well-established 2D/3D differentiation protocol to generate hESC-derived retinal organoids containing all the major cell types in the retina (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) (26). First, to ensure maximum expression of the Cas13bt3 in the retinal cells, we codon optimized the Cas13bt3 protein sequence to obtain two other variants of Cas13bt3 (Cas13bt3COP1 [GenScript] and Cas13bt3COP2 [Benchling]) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). We compared the expression levels of the original Cas13bt3 [IDT] with Cas13bt3COP1 and Cas13bt3COP2 by transfection in HEK293FT cells and found both variants to exhibit increased expression and knockdown efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B–D). Among them, Cas13bt3COP1 (Cas13bt3 hereafter) showed the highest expression and knockdown and therefore was used for subsequent investigation. To elucidate the activity of Cas13bt3 and efficacy for AAV gene therapy in bona fide human retinal cells, we transduced 19-week-old organoids with AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 and AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 vectors. Retinal organoids were then harvested for single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) 6 wk after treatment (~25 wk; Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S1). First, we confirmed that the major retinal cell types were clustered similarly across experimental conditions (S1: Untreated; S2: AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3; and S3: AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3) (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8; n = 3 pooled organoids per condition). We used our recently described retinal atlas dataset to determine the fidelity of retinal organoids and identified the expression of the different retinal cell markers (27), confirming exclusive cell-specific expression of the markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

Next, to examine Cas13bt3 expression in the retinal organoids, we evaluated read numbers and classified cells containing 10 or more reads of Cas13bt3 to be positive for viral infection. Using this threshold, we determined an average transduction efficiency of 18.78% (1,403 out of 7,467 cells) for the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA group and 25.95% (2,260 out of 8,706 cells) for the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group. Next, we analyzed the transduction and the presence of Cas13bt3 in specific cell types. While all cell types showed noticeable levels of Cas13bt3, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells showed high levels of expression with over 50% of cells containing Cas13bt3 in the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA (S2) treatment group (153 out of 264 cells). This is likely due to the reported preferential transduction of RPE cells by the AAV2.7m8 serotype (28). Interestingly, in the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group, only ~15% of RPE cells expressed Cas13bt3, which could owe to varying culture conditions. Bipolar cells showed the least expression of Cas13bt3 (≤ 10%), while all other cell types showed moderate expression (≤25%) (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Overall, our results indicated AAV2.7m8 to be a useful delivery vector for cells across retinal cell types, confirming several previous studies (29–32).

To investigate the impact of the Cas13bt3 expression in retinal cells, we compared DEGs between cells positive and negative for Cas13bt3 in the retinal organoid that received the AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 or AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFsgRNA.Cas13bt3. Here, we observed a significant number of DEGs across all cell types in the retinal organoid that received the AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3, where most DEGs were upregulated genes (Fig. 2D). The same pattern was observed in the retinal organoid transduced with AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 (Fig. 2E), albeit at a lower extent, suggesting that the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA exhibited a greater impact on the transcriptome. To determine the specific cell types that were more sensitive to Cas13bt3 expression, we identified unique DEGs from each cell type (to remove common effects from AAV delivery and culturing conditions) across the retinal cells. We found macroglial (indicating Müller cells in this population) and bipolar cells exhibited the highest DEGs, which were especially higher in the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group (Macroglial–NT sgRNA: 5571 vs VEGFA sgRNA: 2928, Bipolar–NT sgRNA: 3725 vs VEGFA sgRNA: 1633) (Fig. 2F). These results emphasized the need to consider the effect of nontargeting sgRNAs when assessing Cas13 specificity.

Cas13bt3 can Significantly Knockdown VEGFA mRNA in Retinal Organoids.

Next, we examined Cas13bt3 targeting efficiency and its effect on VEGFA expression across the retinal cell types. First, we determined VEGFA expression in control untreated organoids. While Cas13bt3-positive cells have been identified, the abundance of Cas13bt3 reads within cells may still vary (e.g. 10 reads vs 100 reads in a cell) and affect the determination of targeting efficiency. We hypothesized that increased intracellular Cas13bt3 transcripts allow for higher targeting efficiency and thereby increased silencing of VEGFA mRNA (Fig. 2G). We first examined the number of Cas13bt3 reads in cells that received AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 and AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 treatments (Fig. 2H and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To accurately determine the Cas13bt3 effect and to understand the abundance of the targeting complex within each cell, we examined AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 treated cells. We selected a threshold to classify high (≥50 reads) and low (<50 reads) expression of Cas13bt3 that enables evaluation of targeting efficiency (Table S2). Here, we found that RPE and macroglial cells expressed high levels of Cas13bt3 expression while most cones, rods, and horizontal and bipolar cells only showed low levels of Cas13bt3 expression (Fig. 2H).

We then quantified VEGFA mRNA expression in cells expressing high and low levels of Cas13bt3. To this end, we pooled cells within the high and low Cas13bt3 thresholds and compared average VEGFA expression against Cas13bt3 negative cells to determine if loss of VEGFA is dependent on high levels of Cas13bt3. A significant decrease of VEGFA in high Cas13bt3-expressing cells compared to low Cas13bt3-expressing cells would indicate VEGFA silencing from increased targeting activity, while a significant reduction compared to negative cells would indicate VEGFA loss from viral delivery. A prominent observation was that viral delivery appeared to upregulate VEGFA expression in macroglial cells with both low and high Cas13bt3-expressing cells exhibiting a significant increase in VEGFA expression (Fig. 2I). Moreover, we observed that only RPE cells showed a significant reduction of VEGFA expression in high Cas13bt3 expressing cells compared to low Cas13bt3 expressing cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2J, red square, and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). When we correlated Cas13bt3 reads to VEGFA expression, again, only RPE cells that received the AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 treatment showed a significant reduction (p = 0.043) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

VEGFA Knockdown Impedes Disease Progression in a Mouse Model of Proliferative Retinopathy.

Last, we sought to investigate if VEGFA knockdown can control retinal neovascularization in vivo in mouse models of proliferative retinopathy. This offers a potential tool against neovascular eye diseases. To this end, we used the trVEGF029 (VEGFA+/-, Kimba) mouse, a humanized transgenic mouse model where the neuroretina overexpresses hVEGFA, leading to neovascularization like human proliferative DR (33). To ensure that optimal transduction of the retina is achieved, we delivered mCherry reporter driven by a CMV promoter using the AAV2.7m8 serotype, known to have excellent transduction of the retina (34), intravitreally into C57BL/6 mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B). Immunohistochemistry analysis of AAV2.7m8.CMV.mCherry-treated retinal flatmounts showed high levels of fluorescence through all retinal layers (RGCs, bipolar and photoreceptor cells) 4 wk following injections, confirming quick onset and deep penetration of the retina from intravitreal delivery (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 C and D; n = 6 eyes from 3 animals).

Next we treated Kimba mice with AAV2.7m8 viruses driving our codon optimized Cas13bt3 carrying NT sgRNA (AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3) or VEGFA sgRNA (AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3) (Fig. 3A). Six-week-old Kimba mice received bilateral, intravitreal injections of AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 or AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 mixed with AAV2.7m8.CMV.mCherry. For control, mice were only injected with the AAV2.7m8.CMV.mCherry virus. Eight weeks following treatment, the eyes were analyzed for VEGFA mRNA expression, neovascular growth, and retinal degeneration (Fig. 3B). Similar to the control (mCherry reporter) treatments, high transduction of photoreceptor cells was observed as demonstrated by expression of mCherry along with Rhodopsin positive cells (indicating photoreceptor outer segment) and expression of HA-tag (indicating Cas13bt3) (Fig. 3 C and D). In addition, RT-qPCR showed high expression of Cas13bt3 in Kimba retinas injected with AAV.2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 and AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 (Fig. 3E; n = 3 to 12 retinas). This corresponded with ~50% reduction (P < 0.001) of human VEGFA (hVEGFA) mRNA (Fig. 3F) but not mouse Vegfa (mVegfa) (Fig. 3G) in the AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 group. No reduction of hVEGFA or mVegfa was observed with AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 or control groups, indicating guide-dependent silencing of hVEGFA mRNA (Fig. 3 F and G; n = 3 to 12 retinas). Despite the high complementarity of human and mouse VEGFA sequences (29 out of 30 nucleotides) in the targeting region (Fig. 3H), Cas13bt3 could achieve a specific knockdown of hVEGFA. RNA sequencing further confirmed that mVegfa expression and predicted off-target genes were not significantly altered, indicating low off-target activity from Cas13bt3-mediated RNA silencing (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

To confirm whether VEGFA mRNA knockdown influenced disease progression, we analyzed fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) images from Kimba eyes for vessel leakage and quantified vessel density and number of branch points, signs that indicate the neovascular process (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). We used fluorescein to label and image vessels of the AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3, AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3-treated, and control eyes. Here, we observed large spots of vessel leakage, indicating neovascularization had progressed in both control- and Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA-treated eyes (Fig. 3I). However, in the AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 group, lesser vessel leakage was observed (Fig. 3I; Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA). We quantified the area of leakage and found a significantly reduced leakage area in the AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 group compared to control eyes and AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3-treated eyes (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 B and C, P < 0.05; n = 4 to 6 retinas). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry was performed to stain laminin-2 across the retinal vascular layers (superficial, intermediate, and deep vascular plexuses) (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and B). Here, vessels appeared diminished in the intermediate and deep vascular layers for the Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA group. In the Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA group, the neovascular process could be observed through leaky vessel structures in the same layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S17C; n = 4 retinas). As disease progression in each retina is variable, we performed paired analyses of recordings from each retina at the endpoint to its baseline. The AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 treated eyes showed significantly lower leaky vessel density and branch points compared to baseline at 8 wk posttreatment, unlike the control and the AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 eyes (Fig. 3 J and K and SI Appendix, Fig. S18, P < 0.05; n = 4 to 13 retinas). Next, to determine whether VEGFA knockdown leads to rescue of overall retinal structure. We performed optical coherence tomography (OCT) to analyze total retinal thickness (TRT) before and after treatment. However, we observed no significant changes between the untreated and treated groups and a general decline in TRT across all animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). We postulate that the overexpression of hVEGFA promotes rapid retinal degeneration that could not be controlled or reversed through VEGFA silencing.

Overall, our findings showed that Cas13bt3-mediated RNA silencing significantly reduces VEGFA mRNA expression, specifically targeting hVEGFA and controlling hallmark signs of neovascularization in a humanized mouse model. This approach promises a useful alternative to current anti-VEGF drugs for proliferative retinopathy.

Discussion

Our comprehensive in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo characterization of Cas13bt3 activity and performance in the retina provides insights for therapeutic use of the enzyme. Cas13bt3 was first described three years ago but has garnered significant attention for its compact size and reported specificity (11). Structural characterization and activation mechanisms have recently been described spurring immense interest in employing the molecule for therapeutic development (16, 35). Due to ongoing discussions over the specificity of Cas13 nucleases (10), we aimed to characterize Cas13bt3 efficiency and specificity in mouse and human cells. We chose to target the VEGFA gene as this growth factor is essential for both physiological and pathological angiogenesis. In the retina, physiological functions include its role as a survival factor for retinal ganglion cells (36), photoreceptors, Müller cells (37), and RPE (38). In disease, overexpression of VEGFA leads to neovascularization and vision loss. Therefore, the clinical applicability of VEGFA downregulation in specific cell types of the retina is of great importance.

Previous studies have demonstrated transcript knockdown using CasRx nucleases (11, 12). Notably, CasRx-mediated VEGFA mRNA knockdown has also been shown to reduce choroidal neovascularization after silencing VEGFA mRNA in RPE cells in vivo in a laser-induced choroidal neovascularization model (4). Our results confirmed that Cas13bt3 mediates significant knockdown of VEGFA in human cell lines, hESC-derived retinal cells, and the mouse retina.

In vitro, and specifically in HEK293FT cells, several studies have shown Cas13 nucleases to be highly precise (10). Most of these studies, however, compared differentially expressed genes to a NT sgRNA control, not accounting for transcriptomic perturbations from the Cas13-NT sgRNA complex. A recent study showed high collateral activity from CasRx when compared to an empty vector (39). Here, we compared targeting Cas13bt3 to vector controls, which showed minimal but positive collateral activity. More importantly, we show that Cas13bt3 with a NT sgRNA has a significant transcriptomic impact resulting in high levels of DEGs. While Cas13bt3 may initiate target search after maturation of a NT sgRNA and crRNA loading; without adequate homologous pairing, the catalytic pocket cannot be formed for nuclease domain activation and efficient cleavage (40). On the other hand, with a targeting gRNA, Cas13bt3 would be activated to form the Cas13bt3-gRNA-target complex for targeted knockdown. Our results suggest that with a NT sgRNA, Cas13bt3 initiates an unspecific target-search process involving significant RNA binding that impacts the expression levels of several genes, while a targeting sgRNA narrows the target-search process to reduce overall transcriptomic impact. NT sgRNAs are therefore not appropriate controls for determining the specificity of Cas13 nucleases, raising awareness against such evaluation.

A recent study showed CasRx to have intrinsic targets that affected essential processes like RNA metabolism and mitotic cell cycle (22) and opined that this could underlie the lethality seen upon CasRx expression in a mouse model (41). Intrinsic targeting refers to the specific targeting of genes by Cas13 even in the absence of crRNA loading and nuclease domain activation. Here, we found that laminin-related processes (e.g., “laminin binding”, “laminin complex”) were strongly downregulated across all groups (Cas13bt3 only, Cas13bt3-NT sgRNA, and Cas13bt3-VEGFA sgRNA) suggesting intrinsic targets could exist for Cas13bt3 as well (Dataset S1). While we did not observe significant toxicity in our cell cultures, whether this is detrimental to cellular health and overall host organism needs to be investigated.

The small size of Cas13bt3 also allows the incorporation of multiple sgRNAs within AAV capacity for multiplexed targeting. Packaging three VEGFA targeting sgRNAs, we found RNA silencing was not affected despite varying targeting efficiencies of the individual sgRNAs. Interestingly, DEG levels were comparable in both single and multiple sgRNA vectors, suggesting the additional sgRNAs do not increase overall collateral activity. Future work could apply multiplexed Cas13bt3 to ocular neovascularization by simultaneously targeting the VEGFA and PDGF genes for enhanced treatment (42).

We also investigated AAV-mediated Cas13 RNA editing in retinal organoids at single cell resolution. While several studies have demonstrated VEGFA silencing for control of neovascularization, previous studies have focused on specific cell types that are believed to be major contributors to VEGFA expression, like RPE and Müller cells (4, 43). While this proves useful to demonstrate proof-of-concept, it does not provide a comprehensive understanding relevant to therapeutic development as VEGFA silencing in one cell type may have collateral effects on other retinal cells. Retinal organoid models enable the testing of therapies in an environment that mimics the human retina containing lamination and major retinal cell types of the developing retina. Here, we sought to understand the retina-wide effect following the delivery of Cas13bt3 as well as the targeted silencing of VEGFA. Our findings not only demonstrate the use of Cas13bt3 for ocular gene therapy but also bring to light several considerations for its use. First, we showed transduction across all retinal cell types studied with the clinically relevant AAV2.7m8 vector, confirming its effectiveness as a vector for retinal transduction and supporting its clinical utility (28, 31). Among the retinal cell types studied, bipolar cells showed the least transduction by AAV2.7m8 while RPE cells seemed more susceptible. These differences are likely due to cell-specific transduction as RPE cells are readily transduced by a variety of AAV capsids, particularly AAV2.7m8 (29). It should also be noted that overall transduction was determined using an arbitrary cut-off (Cas13bt3 read counts ≥ 10) based on our previous experience with the retinal organoids (26).

Like our in vitro findings, NT sgRNA activity following gene therapy led to numerous DEGs relative to VEGFA targeting sgRNA. This emphasizes the need to account for RNA perturbations from a Cas13-NT sgRNA complex when determining Cas13 specificity. Retinal organoids showed significantly more DEGs than HEK293FT cells, which is expected partly due to cellular response to virus delivery. Notwithstanding response to AAV, the higher DEG levels indicate retinal cells, especially macroglial and bipolar cells, to be more sensitive to Cas13 demonstrating the importance of studying nuclease activity and efficiency in physiologically relevant models, an important consideration for CRISPR-Cas-mediated gene therapies. To further understand safety, the impact of these DEGs would be best evaluated in live models to determine their influence on cellular and overall visual function.

As preclinical studies with Cas13 nucleases are commonly performed in HEK293FT cells, its specificity has often been exaggerated in the literature. While our study used viruses to deliver Cas13 into retinal cells unlike lipid transfection in HEK293FT cells, the different transcriptomic profile observed is noteworthy. Our results suggest that Cas13 specificity must be further evaluated in relation to untreated or delivery vector controls, with different delivery systems and in organ-specific cells to obtain a comprehensive understanding of Cas13 specificity relevant for therapeutic development.

On the front of therapeutic potential, we could only confirm significant targeted VEGFA mRNA knockdown in RPE cells ex vivo, likely due to preferential transduction of AAV2.7m8 and the strong expression of Cas13bt3 in these cells. Lower copies of Cas13bt3 in the other cells may have resulted in the lack of significant VEGFA knockdown, and further studies are required to understand the levels of Cas13 expression necessary in other retinal cells for effective activity. Testing of cell type-specific promoters could also be beneficial as the CMV promoter is known for its susceptibility to silencing in certain cell types, such as stage-specific PSCs-derived photoreceptor cells (44). Importantly, with RPE cells being the main producers of VEGFA in nAMD, our results show that Cas13bt3 gene therapy could be a potential anti-VEGF strategy for other types of neovascular eye diseases.

Last, we tested the anti-VEGF potential of Cas13bt3 in the retina of a transgenic mouse model of proliferative retinopathy, the Kimba mouse. We opted to examine the anti-VEGF potential of Cas13bt3 in Kimba mice as it shows chronic disease and all major signs of proliferative retinopathy (33). Importantly, as Kimba mice express human VEGFA, the same vectors tested in retinal organoids could also be used in vivo. Following RNA editing gene therapy in Kimba retinae, we demonstrated the specific knockdown of human VEGFA, without impact on endogenous mouse Vegfa. Silencing of VEGFA mRNA also rescued vascular phenotypes showing an improvement in controlling vessel growth and leakiness. The major concern with current anti-VEGF treatment is the need for frequent intravitreal injections. Recent efforts to address this problem have employed gene editing strategies like targeting VEGFA using CRISPR-Cas9 (43, 45). However, the efficacy of such treatments is limited by the delivery vehicles, the poor efficiency of compact Cas9 nucleases, and the risk of genomic alterations from the long-term expression of Cas9 (46). Our results indicate that intravitreal injection with all-in-one AAV Cas13bt3 can achieve potent silencing of VEGFA, addressing drawbacks with current gene editing strategies. While RNA editing is generally considered safer than DNA-targeted options as unwanted editing is not permanently retained in the host (47), it would be prudent to consider the effects of long-term Cas13 expression, and Cas13 systems amenable to regulation are of consideration for therapeutic development.

There are a few limitations to our study. First, we did not extensively investigate the stress responses we observed from the retinal cells. While HEK293FT appears resistant to high levels of collateral activity, retinal cells exhibited significantly higher levels of transcriptomic effects. This is supported by the high levels of DEGs we observed in various cells from the retinal organoids suggesting significant inflammatory responses as well as cell-specific collateral activity. We also found the upregulation of ubiquitin-like protein (UBL) processes across the cell types in all groups regardless of VEGFA silencing (Dataset S2), strongly indicating increased cellular stress from delivery of Cas13bt3. As RNA-targeting molecules like Cas13 require persistent expression for therapeutic effect, this could pose a significant safety concern and must be further studied. This could be done through studying the expression of essential growth factors and developmental and functional genes after treatment. Also, visual responses in wild-type mice can be assessed before and after virus injection to confirm the safety of the therapeutic. Second, while vessel density and branching were noticeably reduced, we could not extensively study the recovery of phenotype in our mouse model due to the severity of the disease. OCT readings showed significant retinal degeneration at the treatment timepoint, making rescue of phenotype challenging to assess. Last, we were also unable to confirm the reduction of VEGFA protein from the mice retinas as not enough protein could be obtained for quantitative analysis.

CRISPR-Cas technology has advanced at breakneck speed in the last decade, revolutionizing medicine and offering potential treatment opportunities for numerous previously incurable diseases. The recent FDA approval of an ex vivo CRISPR therapy for sickle-cell disease (branded Casgevy) is evidence of this progress. However, a comprehensive understanding of the CRISPR nucleases is crucial in furthering safe and efficacious CRISPR therapies from the bench to bedside. In this study, we offer insights into the Cas13 targeting mechanism and its utility in targeting retinal and RPE cells, providing evidence for the potential of Cas13-based therapies for ocular disease. This study demonstrated that the hypercompact Cas13bt3 can be efficiently delivered and expressed in various retinal cell types for effectively controlling VEGFA expression and neovascularization. An in-depth evaluation of collateral activity in retinal cells would facilitate the development of a safe and effective Cas13-mediated anti-VEGF gene therapy. Alternative delivery methods that can achieve high RNA silencing from low copy expression and protein engineering to modulate immunogenic epitopes can also further enable development of Cas13 as a safe therapeutic against retinal disease.

Materials and Methods

Plasmid Construction.

The protein sequences of CasRx and Cas13bt3 were gifts from Hsu (48) and Yang (11), respectively. Cas13bt3 was codon optimized using online tools from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), GenScript, and Benchling. The all-in-one AAV plasmids for shRNA, CasRx, and Cas13bt3 were designed as previously described (19), and synthesized by GenScript. All plasmids used in this study are available from Addgene.

Cell Culture and Transfections.

HEK293FT cells (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Bovogen Biologicals) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

RT-qPCR.

At 48 h after transfection, RNA was extracted from cells using the Monarch® Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs) and 200 ng of RNA was converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. cDNA was diluted with water at the ratio of 1:10. qPCR was performed with 2 µL of cDNA using the TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturers’ instructions with the following TaqMan probes human VEGFA (Hs00900055_m1; Thermo Fisher Scientific), mouse VEGFA (Mm00437306_m1; Thermo Fisher Scientific), human GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1; Thermo Fisher Scientific), mouse GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mouse eyeballs were enucleated and stored in 4 °C in RNALater® until dissection. The retina was dissected from the eyeballs and placed in lysis buffer and sonicated for 2 s prior to RNA extraction and qPCR as previously described.

Western Blot.

At 48 h after transfection, cells were harvested and collected in Pierce™ RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing Pierce™ Phosphatase Inhibitor Mini Tablets (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysate was sonicated for 5 s and centrifuged for 15 min at 13,300 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein levels were quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per manufacturers’ instructions. For western blot, 10 µg of protein was mixed with NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (4X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and loaded in NuPAGE™ 4 to 12% Mini Protein Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for gel electrophoresis at 150 V for 30 min. Transfer to Amersham™ Hybond™ PVDF membrane (Cytiva) was performed at 30 V for 1 h. Protein blot was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by incubation with HA-tag (C29F4) Rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology®) or Anti-Actin Antibody, clone C4 (Merck) overnight at 4 °C. Blots were then stained with Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, HRP (Invitrogen™), or Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, HRP (Invitrogen™) for 2 h at room temperature. Blot was incubated with Pierce™ ECL Western Blotting Substrate for 1 min before imaging using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc. All washes were performed with 1 × PBST for 5 min, repeated 3 times.

ELISA.

At 48 h after transfection, 100 µL of supernatant was collected and ELISA was performed using the Human VEGF DuoSet ELISA (R & D Systems) as per manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, 96-well plates were incubated with Capture Antibody in ELISA Plate coating buffer overnight at room temperature. Plates were then blocked with Reagent Diluent for 1 h at room temperature, and 100 µL of supernatant was incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Then, detection antibody was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation with Streptavidin-HRP and Substrate reagent for 20 min each, the provided stop solution was added, and plate was read using the Tecan Spark® Micro plate reader at 450 nm with reference wavelength 570 nm.

Immunohistochemistry.

Retinal flatmounts were washed with 1 × PBS containing 0.5% Triton-X-100 (PBST). After washing with PBST, flatmounts were incubated in anti-rhodopsin antibody (1:500; Abcam) with 2% donkey serum and 2% PBST in 4 °C for 72 h. This was followed by incubation in Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch) at room temperature for 2 h. After washing, the flatmounts were stained with Anti-Laminin-2 (α-2 Chain) antibody, Rat monoclonal (1:250; Merck), and incubated in 4 °C for 5 nights. This was followed by incubation in Alexa Fluor® 647 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rat IgG (H + L) (1:250; Jackson ImmunoResearch) at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the retinae were washed and incubated in mCherry Monoclonal Antibody (16D7), Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 72 h. Retinal cryosections were blocked using serum-free protein block (Agilent Dako) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight with primary antibody as previously described. Secondary antibody incubation was performed at room temperature for 1 h as previously described. Slides were stained with NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ Reagent (Hoechst 33342; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min before mounting as previously described and imaged on the Stellaris 5 confocal microscope.

hESC Maintenance and Retinal Differentiation Culture.

hESCs WA09 (H9 ESCs) were purchased from WiCell and maintained in feeder-free conditions on E8 (Thermo Fisher) and Geltrex-coated six-well plates. In brief, for retinal differentiation hESCs were maintained until 90 to 95% confluent, then FGF-free medium was added to the cultures for 2 d followed by a neural induction period where RPE and retinal vesicles appeared. NRVs were excised and kept in low-binding 96-well plates for maturation.

AAV Transduction of Retinal Organoids.

Retinal organoids were maintained in ALT90 medium as previously described (26) and AAV transduction was performed during 15 to 19 wk of differentiation. On the day of transduction, AAV vectors were diluted in 300 µL of fresh ALT90 medium at titer of 1 × 1011 vg/organoids. The following day, treated wells were supplemented with 700 µL of fresh ALT90 medium. Transduced organoids were incubated for 72 h in the AAV vector-containing medium. After the 72 h incubation, the vector-containing medium was aspirated, and 1 mL of fresh ALT90 medium was added to each well. Transduced organoids underwent 6 wk culture until harvesting for experimental procedures.

Immunohistochemistry of Retinal Organoids.

Retinal organoids were fixed for 45 min in 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated overnight in 20% sucrose, prior to embedding in OCT. Cryosections (14 µm thick) were collected for analysis and preserved at −20 °C. Cryosections were blocked in 5% goat serum,1% bovine serum albumin and 0.1X triton in PBS for 2 h. Primary antibodies (ARR3: 1:200, Rhodopsin: 1:1,000, VSX2: 1:200, CRALBP: 1:200, PROX1:1:200, and HuCHuD:1:500) were incubated overnight at 4 °C in blocking solution After washing of primary antibody with PBS, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody in a blocking solution for 1 h at RT, washed, and counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich). Alexa fluor 488 and 546 secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used at a 1:500 dilution.

Intravitreal Injections.

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committees of University of Melbourne and conducted in accordance with The Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animal in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Ethics approval was obtained from The Florey Institute of Neuroscience and Mental Health Animal Ethics Committee (22-036-UM). The Akimba mice developed by Rakoczy et al. (49) and the heterozygous Kimba mice (hVEGFA+/−) were used for this study. Mice were bred in the Melbourne Brain Centre animal facility (Kenneth Myer Building, Parkville, VIC, Australia), and were housed in a well-ventilated environment that was kept at a constant room temperature of 21 °C, with a 12-h diurnal light–dark cycle (lights on: 7 am, off: 7 pm; room illumination <50 lx). Mouse chow (Barastoc, Melbourne, VIC, Australia) and water were provided ad libitum. 6-week-old Kimba mice were anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) solution mix (Troy Laboratory, Smithfield, NSW, Australia; i.p. with 30G needle) and given a drop of 0.5% proxymethacaine hydrochloride (Alcaine™, Alcon Laboratories, Frenchs Forest, NSW, Australia) and 0.5% tropicamide (Mydriacyl™, Alcon Laboratories). Baseline fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) was taken (as described below), and animals were intravitreally injected with 1 µL of a AAV2.7m8 viral mixture (1 × 1010 vg/µL), containing AAV2.7m8.U6.VEGFAsgRNA.Cas13bt3 or AAV2.7m8.U6.NTsgRNA.Cas13bt3 virus (8 × 109 vg) and AAV2.7m8.mCherry (2 × 109 vg) (4:1 ratio) using a Hamilton syringe attached to a 33-gauge dental needle. Post injection, mice were subcutaneously administered 0.3 mL of atipamezole hydrochloride (0.25 mg/mL) and allowed to recover on heating pads with food and water ad libitum.

Fundus Fluorescein Angiography and Optical Coherence Tomography.

Retinal vasculature was assayed at baseline prior to intravitreal injection of AAVs and 8 wk after AAV-treatment using fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) immediately after intraperitoneal injection of sodium fluorescein (1%, 100 µL/kg, fluorescite 10%; Alcon Laboratories). Five images (central, nasal superior, temporal superior, nasal inferior, and temporal inferior) were obtained for each eye and analyzed using AngioTool (50) for vessel density and branch points using default settings.

In vivo retinal structure was assayed with spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) (Spectralis®, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) with general anesthesia, mydriasis, and corneal hydration (Systane, Alcon Laboratories) maintained. During image acquisition, retinal volumes (8.1 × 8.1 × 1.9 mm) centered on the optic nerve head were acquired each consisting of 121 evenly spaced horizontal B-scans. Each B-scan comprised of 768 A-scans with 3.87 µm axial depth resolution, 9.8 µm lateral resolution. OCT scans were captured with an automated real-time tracking average of 6 frames, at an average speed of 85,000 A-scans per second. The Heidelberg Eye Explorer 2 OCT reader plugin (Heidelberg Engineering) was used for analysis. All retinal layers (retinal nerve fiber layer, RNFL; ganglion cell inner plexiform layer, GCIPL; inner nuclear layer, INL; outer plexiform layer, OPL; outer nuclear layer, ONL; photoreceptor segments, PR segment) as well as total retinal thickness (TRT) were automatically segmented (Heidelberg Eye Explorer 2 software). A circular ring around the optic nerve head (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS] outer ring) was employed for analysis.

Determination of Candidate Off-target Genes.

Off-targets of the gRNAs were predicted using a sequence-based approach. gRNAs in length of 30 bps were first aligned to the human transcriptome (Grch38 cdna from emsembl release 100) using blastn. Flexible options were set to capture the potential offtargets which could tolerate up to 7 mismatches in this step, i.e., max_target_seqs = 10,000, evalue = 10000, word_size = 5, perc_identity = 0.6. Second, the candidates were further filtered with the threshold that at least 13 bp perfect match should exist in each alignment. For mouse off-target genes, the same parameters were used with gRNAs aligned to the mouse transcriptome (Reference RNA sequences: mus musculus (taxid: 10090).

Bulk RNA-seq Data Analysis.

Raw data processing was performed using FASTQC (51) to check the reads quality and Trimmomatic (52) tool to further remove the bases and reads with low quality, adaptors, and barcodes. The clean reads were aligned to the reference human genome (version GRCh38), utilizing STAR v2.7 (53) as the aligner. For RNA quantification, RSEM (54) was employed to normalize raw read counts, transforming them into TPM (transcripts per million) values, providing a relative expression level that is theoretically comparable across samples. The magnitude (log2-transformed fold change) and significance (P-value) of differential expression between groups were then calculated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected based on the criteria of a P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (55), focused on Gene Ontology (56), was conducted using the R 4.3.1 Bioconductor package clusterProfiler (57) to elucidate whole transcriptomic patterns between groups. To account for multiple testing, P-values underwent adjustment through the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR). Raw data files from bulk RNA-seq for experiments in mammalian cells and mouse retina are available from GEO accession: GSE259220. Fig. 1: batch 5. SI Appendix, Fig. S15: batch 4. SI Appendix, Fig. S2: batch 2. SI Appendix, Fig. S5: batch 1.

Single-cell RNA-seq Data Analysis.

After checking the reads quality, the sequencing files were aligned to reference human genome (version GRCh38) using 10X Genomics CellRanger software (v7.1.0) count pipeline to remove noncellular barcodes and keep the cellular barcodes and gene matrix. To further distinguish the Cas13bt3-valid and -invalid cells, FASTX-toolkit (58) was used to keep the reads with cellular barcodes and convert FASTAQ file into FASTA file. Then, Blastn (59) was used to compute the similarity between cellular reads and Cas13bt3 reference sequence.

Based on the quality of the sequencing reads, appropriate cutoff values for both the percentage of identical positions and the alignment length were employed to delineate cells as either Cas13bt3-valid or Cas13bt3-invalid. For either Cas13bt3-valid or -invalid cells, we further used Seurat v4.3 for data preprocessing, data normalization, feature selection, data scaling, dimensional reduction, and clustering. To refine the characterization of cell types within each cluster, ScType (60) was employed, leveraging genesets specific to eight retinal cell types—amacrine, bipolar, cones, horizontal, macroglial, microglial, rods, and RPE—validated by Kim et al. (27). The magnitude (log2-transformed fold change) and significance (P-value) of differential expression between cell types were then calculated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected based on the criteria of a P-value < 0.05 and log2 fold change ≥ 1. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (55), focused on Gene Ontology (56), was conducted using the R 4.3.1 Bioconductor package clusterProfiler (57) to elucidate whole transcriptomic patterns between groups. To account for multiple testing, P-values underwent adjustment through the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR). Raw data from scRNA-seq is available from GEO accession: GSE259219.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Analytical methods are described in respective figure legends with P-value indicators. For ex vivo studies, to assess the strength of the relationship between gene expression values and the number of Cas13bt3-valid read counts following log2 transformation in each cell type, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed. The associated P-value serves as an indicator of the likelihood or probability that any observed correlation is merely a result of chance.
