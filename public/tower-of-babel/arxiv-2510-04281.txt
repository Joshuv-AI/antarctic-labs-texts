GROK: From Quantitative Biomarkers to Qualitative Diagnosis via a Grounded MLLM with Knowledge-Guided Instruction
Zhuangzhi Gao, Hongyi Qin, He Zhao, Qinkai Yu, Feixiang Zhou, Eduard Shantsila, Uazman Alam, Alena Shantsila, Wahbi El-Bouri, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Yalin Zheng, [0.6ex] These authors contributed equally

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) hold promise for integrating diverse data modalities, but current medical adaptations—such as LLaVA-Med—often fail to fully exploit the synergy between color fundus photography (CFP) and optical coherence tomography (OCT), and offer limited interpretability of quantitative biomarkers. We introduce GROK , the first MLLM that jointly processes CFP, OCT, and text to deliver clinician-grade diagnoses of ocular and systemic disease. GROK comprises three core modules—Knowledge-Guided Instruction Generation, CLIP-Style OCT–Biomarker Alignment, and Supervised Instruction Fine-Tuning—that together instill a quantitative-to-qualitative diagnostic chain-of-thought, mirroring real clinical reasoning when producing detailed lesion annotations. To evaluate our approach, we introduce the Grounded Ophthalmic Understanding benchmark, which covers six disease categories and three tasks—macro-level diagnostic classification, report generation quality, and fine-grained clinical quality assessment of the generated chain-of-thought. Experiments show that, with only LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) fine-tuning of a 7B-parameter Qwen2 backbone, GROK outperforms comparable 7B and 32B baselines on both report quality and fine-grained clinical metrics—and even exceeds OpenAI-o3. Our code and data are released at GROK’s repository .

To realize these capabilities, GROK’s modular architecture comprises three key components:
Knowledge-Guided Instruction.
As shown in
Figure 3
(1), we construct the Ophthalmic Interpretation Benchmark,
which contains 15 663 UKBiobank CFP–OCT pairs, each annotated with 31 OCT and 6 CFP biomarkers.
These biomarkers are inserted into an doctor-verified
Eye-Guideline
prompt template;
the completed prompts are then fed to OpenAI-o3, which returns a fine-grained
chain-of-thought that traces how each quantitative abnormality supports the final diagnosis.
The benchmark thus supplies supervision for GROK to learn quantitative-to-qualitative
reasoning.
Semantic Alignment.

Off-the-shelf CLIP-style encoders do not exist for volumetric OCT data.
We therefore pre-train an OCT backbone by contrastively aligning
the embeddings of 2-D central-foveal OCT B-scan images with their
corresponding 3-D biomarker vectors, grounding each slice in
clinically meaningful features and supplying rich representations
for downstream multimodal reasoning.
Cross-Modal Fusion.
A shared projection layer maps CFP and OCT embeddings into a unified, language-compatible space; concatenated with prompt tokens and fed into the LLM’s next-token head, this enables the model to seamlessly fuse and explain OCT and CFP findings.

The main contributions of this work are:

Grounded Retinal MLLMs.
We present GROK, the first knowledge-guided multimodal retinal MLLM. GROK couples dual CLIP-style encoders for CFP and OCT with a cross-modal fusion projector that maps both visual streams into the language-model embedding space, and it is instruction-tuned on our fine-granularity Ophthalmic Interpretation Benchmark —enabling the LLMs to emulate clinician reasoning and to perform quantitative-to-qualitative chain-of-thought inference.

Ophthalmic Interpretation Benchmark.
We release a fully reproducible pipeline—including biomarker-extraction
code and the Eye-Guideline prompt templates that can be used to
generate the 37 biomarker-based instruction–response pairs from
UKBiobank CFP/OCT scans. Building on these resources, we establish an
evaluation suite that assesses grounded diagnosis, feature grounding, and
decision reliability.

Multimodal Alignment and Cross-Modal Fusion Mechanisms.
To compensate for the lack of CLIP-style encoders for volumetric OCT,
we contrastively align 2-D OCT-slice embeddings with their 3-D biomarker
vectors and project both OCT and CFP features into a shared space,
yielding clinically grounded representations that power downstream
LLMs training and multimodal diagnosis.

Multimodal Large Language Models

[

[

More recent systems such as BLIP-2
[
19
]
and

[
20

inject visual features directly into a
frozen LLM backbone, enabling free-form image–text dialogue far beyond
the short answers produced by classic VQA networks. In healthcare,
existing MLLMs typically focus on either imaging (e.g. radiology
X-rays, CT scans
[
9
]
) or physiological signals (e.g. ECG
[
21
]
) in isolation. Yet no study attempts to ground
CFP–OCT representations in biomarker measurements and to generate an
interpretable, chain-of-thought diagnosis—a gap that our GROK framework
fills.

II-B

MLLMs-based Ophthalmic Data Analysis

OCT and CFP are fundamental ophthalmic modalities, and integrating these
images with clinical text via MLLMs has recently gained traction.

OphGLM
[
11
]
fine-tunes a lightweight fusion module on
the expert-annotated FundusTuning-CN dataset, outperforming general
vision–language baselines in fundus-disease classification and clinical
dialogue. RetinaVLM
[
12
]
aligns
self-supervised OCT features with LLaMA-3 by curriculum training and a

biomarker identification and staging. Lingshu
[
10
]

extends Qwen2.5-VL
[
22
]
through
multi-stage training and reinforcement learning on a large-scale
medical multimodal corpus, thereby improving performance on general
clinical tasks. However, these models remain largely black-box
input-to-output systems and do not provide an explicit
quantitative-to-qualitative reasoning chain, rendering their diagnostic
processes opaque.

Most existing ophthalmic MLLMs either process a single imaging modality
or output a diagnosis without a transparent sub-inference chain, which
limits interpretability and clinical trust.
GROK
addresses
this gap by jointly modelling CFP and OCT with explicit,
biomarker-anchored reasoning, yielding an interpretable
quantitative-to-qualitative chain of thought and thereby enhancing
trustworthiness.

III
METHODS

Overview:
Figure
2
depicts the GROK architecture and training
workflow. The design follows the LLaVA paradigm but extends it with

two
CLIP-style encoders—one for CFP, one for OCT. Features from each modality are first encoded and projected into the
LLM’s token space; the resulting visual tokens are concatenated with the
textual query and then decoded by the language model (Qwen2) to produce a
fine-grained diagnostic report.

This capability is enabled by a carefully designed three-stage training pipeline:
Stage I: Knowledge-guided instruction generation.

We design a domain-specific prompting strategy, named
Eye-guideline
, to guide the powerful OpenAI-o3 model in generating structured diagnostic responses based on patients’ data, which are subsequently used as instruction (training) data.
Stage II: CLIP-style OCT–biomarker alignment.

We train a CLIP-style visual encoder to align OCT with their corresponding fine-grained biomarkers recorded in the UKBiobank, thereby grounding OCT representations in quantitative clinical attributes.
Stage III: Supervised instruction fine-tuning.
In this stage, the LLM is jointly fine-tuned with the Cross-Modal Fusion modules using the instruction data generated in Stage I. The Cross-Modal Fusion modules are responsible for mapping CFP and OCT features into the text embedding space.

III-B

Knowledge Guided Instruction
Generation

We carefully designed the instructions (training data) generation process for training the GROK model, as shown in
Figure 3
(1). The knowledge-guided aspect of our method lies chiefly in the instruction-generation stage: we design a doctor-verified Eye-Guideline prompt that embeds clinical expertise and steers OpenAI-o3 to produce a fine-grained chain of thought that links every quantitative abnormality to the final diagnosis.

This template enables OpenAI-o3 to leverage its vast built-in medical knowledge and robust chain-of-thought reasoning capability, fostering reliable, evidence-based links between quantitative information and qualitative interpretations (sub-inference) while explicitly aligning each sub-inference with the final diagnosis—thereby reducing hallucinations and strengthening clinical plausibility.
Another key aspect of this process lies in the query components. Beyond ophthalmic images, we incorporate clinically interpretable OCT and CFP biomarkers, which drive a
“Quantitative Biomarkers to Qualitative Diagnosis”
reasoning pipeline. These biomarkers are fused with visual information to construct intermediate inferences that iteratively lead to final diagnostic conclusions, embodying a structured chain-of-thought based on clinical evidence.

The OCT biomarkers are derived from validated structural metrics in UKBiobank Category ID 100079, including macular thickness, volume, area, and nerve layer thickness across multiple ETDRS subfields. After filtering out biomarkers with more than 20% missing values, 31 high-quality OCT biomarkers were retained. In addition, 6 CFP biomarkers, including vertical cup-to-disc ratio, arteriovenous ratio, fractal dimension of arteries and veins, and vessel tortuosity, were extracted using a custom pipeline (this will be introduced in Section 4).

III-C

CLIP‑Style OCT–Biomarker Alignment

Although CLIP-style encoders are well established for CFP—here we adopt
RetiZero
[
8
]
as the CFP eencoder—off-the-shelf
CLIP-style encoders do not yet exist for volumetric OCT data.Moreover, our instruction data contain rich quantitative biomarkers;
vanilla CLIP encoders, trained only on qualitative image–text pairs,
are unlikely to infer such numeric measurements. To alleviate this, we use the provided 2-D
central-foveal OCT B-scan images to contrastively align iht heir corresponding
3-D biomarkers. The resulting unified encoder fuses OCT image and biomarkers’ numeric embeddings, improving representation quality and downstream generalization for MLLMs.

As shown in
Figure 3
(2), we apply a CLIP-style contrastive loss in the shared embedding space to align projections across different modalities, rather than within the same modality. Specifically, we maximize the cosine similarity between matched OCT and biomarker pairs while minimizing the cosine similarity of all the other pairs. Thus the image to biomarkers loss can be express as:

\ell_{i\to t}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\cos(z^{o}_{j},z_{j}^{t})/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{k=1,k\neq j}^{N}\exp\!\bigl(\cos(z^{o}_{j},z_{k}^{t})/\tau\bigr)},

(2)

where
\tau
is a temperature hyperparameter that scales the cosine similarity scores.
\ell_{i\to t}
and
\ell_{t\to i}
represent image-to-text and text-to-image loss, respectively.

\ell_{t\to i}
is calculated analogously, and the total loss is

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\ell_{i\to t}+\ell_{t\to i}\bigr).

(3)

III-D

Supervised Instruction Finetuning

Independent training of OCT and CFP encoders produces embeddings in disparate latent subspaces that are incompatible with the LLM’s text embedding space. To ensure the LLM can interpret OCT and CFP inputs as effectively as text, we project both modalities into the same dimensionality as the textual embedding space, aligning them within a unified semantic representation.

As illustrated in
Figure 3
(3), we freeze the pre‑trained encoders
f^{o}(\cdot)
and
f^{c}(\cdot)
, yielding their respective output embeddings
z^{\mathrm{o}}
and
z^{\mathrm{c}}
. We then project the OCT embeddings into the same dimensionality as the CFP embeddings using an MLP projector with parameter
\theta_{M_{o}}
:

\hat{z^{\mathrm{o}}}=\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{o}}\bigl(z^{\mathrm{o}};\theta_{M_{\mathrm{o}}}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{Bs\times d_{m}},

(4)

Subsequently, we employ an additional projector called Feature-to-Text Mapper with parameter
\theta_{M}
to map both OCT and CFP image representations into a consistent textual space:

h^{\mathrm{o}}=\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{text}}\bigl(\hat{z^{\mathrm{o}}};\,\theta_{M}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{Bs\times d_{t}},

(5)

h^{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{text}}\bigl(z^{\mathrm{c}};\,\theta_{M}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{Bs\times d_{t}}.

(6)

Here,
d_{t}
denotes the dimensionality of the text embedding space. The projector transforms both
\hat{z}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
z^{\mathrm{c}}
into
\mathbb{R}^{Bs\times d_{t}}
, aligning them with the LLM’s text embedding space and enabling their seamless interpretation as text‑equivalent inputs.
Subsequently, these projected embeddings
h^{\mathrm{c}},h^{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Bs\times d_{t}}
are concatenated with the textual query
x_{q}
, which is a simply prompt to require the model to interpret the retinal images, to form the joint multimodal representation:

x=\mathrm{Concat}\bigl(h^{\mathrm{c}},\,h^{\mathrm{o}},\,\mathrm{Embed}(x_{q})\bigr)

(7)

During the supervised finetuning using the knowledge guided generated instructions data, both CFP and OCT encoders are frozen while the projectors and Large Language Model are jointly optimized. The CFP encoder we used is Retizero
[
8
]
. And the OCT encoder is our biomarker-aligned RETFound backbone
[
7
]
. For the language model, we utilize Qwen2-7B-Instruct, since this is the latest LLM support provided by the LLaVA framework that we adopted to build MLLMs.

Let
\theta_{\mathrm{LLM}}
denote the parameters of LLM, the model is trained with a standard auto-regressive objective, which minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the target sequence
y
given the multimodal inputs
x
. Formally,

\mathcal{L}\bigl(y;\,\theta_{\mathrm{LLM}}(x)\bigr)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P\bigl(y_{i}\mid x;\,\theta_{\mathrm{LLM}}\bigr)\,.

(8)

Here,
N
represents the length of the target sequence
y
, and
y_{i}
its
i
-th token.

IV-C

Evaluation

To evaluate GROK’s performance comprehensively, we frame the task as generating an evidence-based diagnostic report from an OCT–CFP pair (
Figure 4
). Our multi-faceted evaluation covers three aspects:

•

Macro-level Diagnostic Classification:
At a high level, this task can be framed as a multi-class classification problem and evaluated using Macro F1-Score, which accounts for class imbalance.

•

Report Generation Quality:
To evaluate the fidelity of the generated reports, We employ the BERT-Score, a standard metric that that quantifies the semantic overlap between model outputs and reference reports, ensuring the analytical narrative aligns with clinical grounded report.

•

Fine-Grained Clinical Quality Assessment:
To perform a deeper fine-grained assessment of the clinical utility of the generated reports, we design a
Rubric-based Scoring Framework
, implemented with OpenAI-o3 as LLM-judge
[
27
]
to score reports against templates, ground truth, and biomarkers. It comprises six metrics:
Quantitative Accuracy
, which checks if numeric statements are correct;
Qualitative Accuracy
, which evaluates if descriptive findings are accurate;
Evidence Grounding
, which verifies if claims are grounded in evidence;
Reasoning Consistency
, which ensures the logic is coherent and non-contradictory;
Coverage Completeness
, which checks if the interpretation addresses all key anatomical domains; and
Error Severity Penalty
, which penalizes dangerous or management-changing errors.

We benchmarked GROK against representative baseline models, including: (1) closed-source models, OpenAI-o3 and GPT-4o; (2) the 32B-parameter open-source vision-language model, Qwen2.5-VL
[
22
]
, and its medically tuned version, Lingshu
[
10
]
; (3) their corresponding 7B-parameter versions, matching GROK’s scale. To ensure a fair comparison and mitigate potential style biases from our training data, we standardized prompts across all baseline models, explicitly requiring grounded reasoning and quantitative estimates to align their output styles. The detailed evaluation rubric and implementation are available in our open-source repository.

Experimental Results

The comprehensive performance of all evaluated models is summarized in
Table II
.

From a macro-level diagnostic classification perspective
, all models exhibit low Macro F1-scores (
\leq 0.25
), far below clinically deployable thresholds, highlighting the inherent difficulty of multi-class diagnosis on real-world, imbalanced datasets, as shown in
Table I

Despite the general difficulty of multi-class diagnosis under long-tailed label distributions, our 7B model GROK achieves a competitive Macro F1-score of 0.23, matching the performance of the much larger, medically-specialized Lingshu-32B (0.23) and approaching the proprietary OpenAI-o3 (0.25). Notably, GROK significantly outperforms other open-source 7B models such as Qwen2.5-VL (0.19) and Lingshu-7B (0.18), while being trained via lightweight
LoRA
fine-tuning with only minimal parameter updates.

From a report generation quality perspective

IV-E

Ablation Study

Although certain metrics—such as macro F1 and fine-grained clinical quality scores—remain suboptimal, our ablation experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of GROK’s architectural design and training methodology.

Domain-specific encoders significantly outperform generic counterparts.

As shown in Table
II
, replacing our OCT–CFP–biomarker–aligned encoders with vanilla CLIP-ViT (GROK-CLIP-ViT) causes Quantitative Accuracy to fall from 78.23 to 61.52, Qualitative Accuracy from 45.98 to 40.81, and Macro F1 from 0.23 to 0.19. This underscores the importance of domain alignment for capturing fine-grained retinal features.

V
Limitations Analysis

This study has four main limitations. First, the absence of publicly available paired CFP–OCT datasets forced us to rely solely on UKBiobank dataset, resulting in pronounced class imbalance and a long-tail disease distribution. Second, our CLIP-style pretraining aligns only central-foveal 2D B-scans with 3D biomarkers, omitting volumetric context and thus limiting spatial feature capture. Third, limited computational resources prevented use of larger backbones or full-model fine-tuning, capping potential performance gains. Fourth, obtaining detailed clinical feedback requires additional coordination; we will conduct extensive clinician evaluations in future work to address these gaps.

VI
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduce GROK, a multimodal retinal MLLM that jointly processes CFP, OCT, and text for clinician-grade diagnosis of ocular and systemic disease. GROK uses a CLIP-style dual-encoder, cross-modal alignment, and LoRA-based knowledge-guided fine-tuning to implement a quantitative-to-qualitative chain-of-thought, greatly enhancing interpretability. Compared to traditional eyes-analysis pipelines, GROK reduces expert analysis; versus other medical MLLMs such as Lingshu, it offers superior readability and clinical explainability. On our Grounded Ophthalmic Understanding benchmark, GROK surpasses 7B- and 32B-parameter baselines in diagnostic classification, report quality, and fine-grained clinical assessment, and outperforms OpenAI-o3 on key metrics. These results confirm that domain-aligned dual encoders plus knowledge-guided instruction substantially improve diagnostic robustness and fidelity. Future work will integrate additional imaging modalities and explore full-model fine-tuning on larger backbones to further boost performance and explainability.
