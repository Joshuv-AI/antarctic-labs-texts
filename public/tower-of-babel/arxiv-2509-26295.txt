P P -adic Gamma classes and overconvergent Frobenius structures for quantum connections
Shaoyun Bai, Dan Pomerleano, Paul Seidel

Abstract
. Consider the small quantum connection on a monotone symplectic manifold, with p p -adic coefficients. We conjecture that this always admits an overconvergent Frobenius structure, whose constant term is given by a characteristic class associated to Morita’s p p -adic Gamma function. We prove this conjecture for toric Fano varieties and Grassmannians, and also supply additional experimental evidence.



1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a closed monotone (
c_{1}(TM)=[\omega_{M}]
) symplectic manifold; the main source of such manifolds are smooth complex projective Fano varieties. Take the (single-variable small) quantum product on
H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})[q]
,

(1.1)

x\ast_{q}y=x\smile y+q(x\ast^{(1)}y)+q^{2}(x\ast^{(2)}y)+\cdots

Here,
\ast^{(k)}
counts rational curves in
M
with first Chern number
k
, hence is an operation of degree
-2k
. The corresponding version of the quantum connection is

(1.2)

\displaystyle\nabla_{q\partial_{q}}:H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})[q]\longrightarrow H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})[q],

\displaystyle\nabla_{q\partial_{q}}x=q\partial_{q}x+c_{1}(TM)\ast_{q}x=q\partial_{q}x+c_{1}(TM)\smile x+c_{1}(TM)\ast^{(1)}x+\cdots

We will be interested in arithmetic aspects of this connection, specifically making contact with the theory of
p
-adic differential equations. This means switching from integer coefficients to ones in a
p
-adic field.

Let’s give a minimal amount of background. Take an odd prime
p
. We use the field

(1.3)

K={\mathbb{Q}}_{p}(\mu),

where
\mu
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity. The
p
-adic valuation on
K
, normalized so that
\mathrm{val}(p)=1
, takes values in
\frac{1}{p-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
. The
p
-adic norm is

(1.4)

|x|=p^{-\mathrm{val}(x)}.

Our field
K
contains a unique element
\pi
with
[
11
, Proposition 4.4.36]

(1.5)

\displaystyle\pi^{p-1}=-p,

\displaystyle|(1+\pi)-\mu|\leq p^{-2/(p-1)}.

Let
O\subset K
be the ring of integers. The maximal ideal is
\pi O\subset O
, and the residue field is
O/\pi O={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

Suppose we have a connection (a system of first order linear differential equations) in one variable
t
, defined over
K
. In informal terms, a Frobenius structure is an isomorphism between the original equation and its pullback under the coordinate change
F(t)=t^{p}
. Even if the original equation is algebraic in
t
, the Frobenius structure generally won’t be, hence it makes sense to ask about the region in which it is defined. We will be focused on the behaviour near
t=0
, so the main question is the radius of convergence around that point. Number theory and algebraic geometry both underline the importance of Frobenius structures which are overconvergent, meaning with convergence radius
>1
(see Section
3
for rigorous definitions).

Example 1.1
.

Consider the equation

(1.6)

df/dt=cf,

for some constant
c
. The Frobenius pullback is
df^{(p)}/dt=c\,pt^{p-1}f^{(p)}
. The relation between the two equations is that

(1.7)

f(t)=\Phi(t)f^{(p)}(t),\;\;\Phi(t)=e^{c(t-t^{p})},

so
\Phi(t)
is a Frobenius structure (and the unique one, up to multiplying by a nonzero constant). In the simplest case
c=1
, the function
\Phi(t)
, defined by the usual power series, has
p
-adic convergence radius
<1
; so that equation doesn’t admit an overconvergent Frobenius structure. However, when
c=\pi
or an integer multiple of that,
\Phi(t)
is overconvergent (see Section
2
); which is why the constant
\pi
is important in this theory.

Returning to the quantum connection, now with
K
-coefficients, we rescale the variable to

(1.8)

t=q/\pi

for
\pi
as in (
1.5
). For simplicity, we also restrict to the even degree part of cohomology (one could carry out a parallel discussion for the odd degree part). The outcome is

(1.9)

\displaystyle\nabla_{t\partial_{t}}:H^{\mathrm{even}}(M;K)[t]\longrightarrow H^{\mathrm{even}}(M;K)[t],

\displaystyle\nabla_{t\partial_{t}}x=t\partial_{t}x+c_{1}(M)\ast_{\pi t}x=t\partial_{t}x+c_{1}(M)\smile x+\pi tc_{1}(M)\ast^{(1)}x+\cdots

For the following elementary facts, see Section
4
. Given any class
b\in H^{\mathrm{even}}(M;K)
which is invertible for the cup product (meaning, its degree
0
part is nonzero), there is a unique Frobenius structure
\Phi=\Phi_{0}+t\Phi_{1}+\cdots
for the quantum connection, whose constant term is the endomorphism

(1.10)

\Phi_{0}(x)=p^{-\mathrm{deg}(x)/2}b\smile x.

For general
b
, this structure is not overconvergent. We now bring in an additional ingredient, Morita’s
p
-adic Gamma function
\Gamma_{p}(z)
(see Section
2
). This has a Taylor expansion with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. Hence, it gives rise to a multiplicative characteristic class for any complex vector bundle
E\rightarrow M
,

(1.11)

\Gamma_{p}(E)\in H^{\mathrm{even}}(M;{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}).

Conjecture 1.2
.

For the quantum connection on any monotone symplectic manifold, the Frobenius structure with constant term (
1.10
), where
b=\Gamma_{p}(TM)
, is overconvergent.

Example 1.3
.

Take
M={\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
. In the standard basis
(1,\,[point])
of
H^{*}(M)
, the quantum connection is

(1.12)

q\partial_{q}+2\begin{pmatrix}0&q^{2}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix};

or after the change of variables (
1.8
),

(1.13)

t\partial_{t}+2\begin{pmatrix}0&\pi^{2}t^{2}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The constant term from Conjecture
1.2
is

(1.14)

\Phi_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)&1/p\end{pmatrix}.

Continuing the general discussion of
p
-adic differential equations, an overconvergent Frobenius structure can be evaluated at
t=\theta
for some
(p-1)
-st root of unity
\theta
(there is one such
\theta\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
in each nonzero residue class). Since
\theta=\theta^{p}
, this is an endomorphism of the vector space on which our differential equation lives. In applications, its eigenvalues often have number-theoretic or algebro-geometric meaning.

Example 1.4
.

Take the situation from Example
1.1
, with
c=k\pi
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
\Phi(\theta)\in K
is a
p
-th root of unity, for instance
\Phi(1)=\mu^{k}
by
[
41
, p. 396]
. In particular,
\mathrm{val}(\Phi(\theta))=0
.

Conjecture 1.5
.

Take the Frobenius structure from Conjecture
1.2
, and consider
\Phi(\theta)
for some
(p-1)
-st root of unity
\theta
. Then, the valuations of the eigenvalues of
\Phi(\theta)
are nonpositive integers; and among them (listed with multiplicities), the number of times that each
k
appears equals the Betti number
b_{-2k}(M)
.

We point out that for elementary reasons (see the discussion preceding Lemma
3.9
), the valuations under consideration in Conjecture
1.5
are independent of
\theta
.

Some cases of these conjectures reduce to classical results on
p
-adic differential equations.

•

The pioneering paper
[
17
]
constructed an overconvergent Frobenius structure, or more precisely its inverse, for the Bessel equation; in our terms, this is the quantum connection for
M={\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
. The computation there proves that case of Conjecture
1.2
. Specifically, the inverse
\Phi(0)^{-1}
of(
1.14
) appears in
[
17
, top of p. 723]
; the result is not stated in terms of
\Gamma_{p}
, but it suffices to compare the formula there with (
2.12
).

•

Dwork’s construction was generalized to
M={\mathbb{C}}P^{n}
in
[
45
]
; explicit formulae for
\Phi(0)^{-1}
are given in
[
45
, Equation 3.3.3, Theorem 4.2.11]
. Again, the outcome is not stated in terms of the
p
-adic Gamma function, as would be required by Conjecture
1.2
. In principle, the compatibility of the two formulae should be verifiable by an elementary argument, since both use Taylor coefficients of the Dwork exponential; we have not checked that, but one can close the gap by using the computation from Section

instead. Note that in this case, the overconvergent Frobenius is unique up to scalars, by
[

. The paper
[

]

in that case.

Remark 1.6
.

An entirely different approach to Frobenius structures for the quantum connection on
{\mathbb{C}}P^{n}
was given in
[
44
, Theorem 7.2]
. There, the Frobenius acts by
q\mapsto q^{p}
rather than
t\mapsto t^{p}
, and overconvergence is replaced by a weaker condition
[
44
, Definition 5.1]
. Hence, it is not immediately clear how those results relate to ours.

The constructions in
[
17
,
45
]
are explicit (and so is
[
1
]
, which we will mention below). In contrast, more recent work tends to use the abstract formalism of overconvergent
F
-isocrystals and
p
-adic cohomology: see e.g.
[
32
, Appendix B]
for a bite-sized introduction;
[
8
]
for the relation between the two approaches; and
[
48
]
for a representation-theoretic application, which in geometric terms includes the quantum connection for minuscule flag varieties
[
35
]
(and in particular, once more, projective spaces). While the abstract approach is a more natural and powerful way of constructing overconvergent Frobenius structures, it does not seem to lend itself naturally to the computation of the
t=0
term, which is a problem for the purposes of Conjecture
1.2
.

Remark 1.7
.

There are visible similarities between Conjecture
1.2
and a series of previous works: the lattice from
[
27
]
and
[
30
, Section 3]
, and the “Gamma conjecture I” in
[
23
]
. These also concern the quantum connection, and use the characteristic class derived from a modified version of the classical Gamma function. However, while the coincidence is striking, there seems to be no a priori mathematical connection between the two contexts. This unexplained parallel is a puzzling but well-known phenomenon in differential equations (a basic example is
[
9
, Equations 6.1 and 6.7]
; see also
[
2
, p. 47–48]
for a more succinct explanation).

Remark 1.8
.

There is also a significant body of work concerning the
p
-adic quantum connection for Calabi-Yau manifolds, much of which deals with specific examples; e.g.
[
43
,
10
,
5
]
. We do not discuss it further here, since the aims appear to be different from the monotone case.

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.9
.

Conjecture
1.2
holds when
M
is a smooth projective toric Fano variety.

The proof of Theorem
1.9
combines mirror symmetry with an explicit construction of the Frobenius structure. The (closed string, toric, equivariant) mirror symmetry statement we need is a simplified version of that in
[
28
]
; we derive it in Sections
5b
–
5c
. Sections
7
–
10
concern the Frobenius structure. The construction has a precedent in
[
1
]
, but that paper was focused on defining it for one value of
t
at a time, and applications to exponential sums (hence does not mention differential equations at all). We set up the framework based on convergent power series in

, generalizing the approach in
[
45
]
. There are actually two versions of that formalism in the paper. The first one (Section

) is weaker but easier to do computations in; we determine the constant term in that setup, using an argument that’s elementary but inspired by mirror symmetry (Section

). The second, more sophisticated, version is then used to prove overconvergence (Section

).

There is a relation between the quantum connection for projective spaces and Grassmannians. This was used in
[

, Section 6]
for the ordinary Gamma conjecture. Using the same idea, we obtain (Section

Theorem 1.10
.

Conjecture
1.2
holds for Grassmannians
M=\mathit{Gr}_{k,N}
; and if

, so does Conjecture
1.5
.

Finally, in addition to the theoretical work, an Appendix to this paper provides experimental evidence supporting Conjectures
1.2
and
1.5
. Part of that (Examples
A.1
and

) may seem redundant, since it overlaps with the previous literature or with Theorem

; however, even in those cases, the computation provides some new indications concerning the actual radius of convergence of the Frobenius. The remaining examples are not covered by existing results: among them is one class of non-algebraic monotone symplectic manifolds (

2.
Background on
p
-adic functions

We recall
p
-adic estimates for the coefficients in some Taylor series. Legendre’s formula implies

(2.1)

\frac{m}{p-1}-\log_{p}(m)-1\leq\mathrm{val}(m!)\leq\frac{m-1}{p-1}.

As a consequence,
\exp(z)
has radius of convergence
p^{-1/(p-1)}
. Next, take the Dwork exponential

(2.2)

D(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\exp(z+z^{p}/p)=\sum_{m\geq 0}d_{m}z^{m}.

By definition
D^{\prime}(z)=(1+z^{p-1})D(z)
, which translates into the relation

(2.3)

d_{m}=(m+1)d_{m+1}-d_{m-p+1}

between Taylor coefficients (with the negative index ones set to zero). The radius of convergence of (
2.2
) is
[
41
, p. 389]

p^{(1-2p)/(p^{3}-p^{2})}
, which is more than that of the exponential, but less than
1
(one can think of (
2.2
) as including the first term in the Artin-Hasse exponential, which has radius of convergence
1
). More precisely, we have
[
11
, Theorem 4.2.22]

(2.4)

\mathrm{val}(d_{m})\geq\frac{1-2p}{p^{3}-p^{2}}m.

These statements become more memorable after rescaling by (
1.5
):

(2.5)

\displaystyle\exp(\pi z)\text{ has radius of convergence $1$},

(2.6)

\displaystyle D(\pi z)=\exp(\pi(z-z^{p}))\text{ has radius of convergence $p^{(p-1)/p^{2}}>1$.}

The Morita
p
-adic Gamma function
\Gamma_{p}(z)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
is a continuous function of
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, which is determined by

(2.7)

\Gamma_{p}(z+1)=\begin{cases}-z\cdot\Gamma_{p}(z)&\text{if }z\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times},\\
-\Gamma_{p}(z)&\text{if }z\in p{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}.\end{cases}

For the purpose of this paper, we follow the discussion of
\Gamma_{p}(z)
from
[
12
, Chapter 11]
, which uses the collection of Mahler expansions
[
12
, Proposition 11.6.15(3)]

(2.8)

\Gamma_{p}(-j-pz)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-p)^{m}d_{mp+j}z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1),\quad j\in\{0,\dots,p-1\},\;z\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

where the coefficients come from (
2.2
). Note that the formulae (
2.8
) for different
j
compute
\Gamma_{p}
on disjoint subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. Nevertheless, they are related by (
2.3
), which implies

[
12
, Corollary 11.6.8(3)]

(2.9)

\Gamma_{p}(-j-pz)=\frac{\Gamma_{p}(-pz)}{(pz+1)\cdots(pz+j)},\quad\text{$j,z$ as before.}

Our first task is to convert the
j=0
case of (
2.8
) into a Taylor expansion for
\Gamma_{p}(z)
around
z=0
. From (
2.4
) we have

(2.10)

\mathrm{val}((-p)^{m}d_{pm})\geq m\frac{p^{2}-3p+1}{p^{2}-p}

which goes to infinity as
m
grows. Hence, we can extract the
z^{k}
term from each summand in the Mahler expansion, and add those up over all
m\geq k
, which yields a Taylor series

(2.11)

\Gamma_{p}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}z^{k}

with
\mathrm{val}(g_{k})\geq k(1-2p)/(p^{2}-p)
(this is by no means an optimal bound, compare the proof of Lemma
2.3
below). For instance,

(2.12)

\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)=g_{1}=\sum_{k>0}p^{k-1}d_{kp}\,(k-1)!

Remark 2.1
.

There is a plethora of related formulae for
\Gamma_{p}(z)
. For instance
[
12
, Proposition 11.6.15(1)]

(2.13)

\Gamma_{p}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}d_{m}z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1),\quad z\in p{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

from which one gets another formula
[
43
, Corollary 3.4]
for (
2.12
),

(2.14)

\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)=-\sum_{k\geq 0}d_{k}\,(k-1)!

One has
[
12
, Corollary 11.6.8(2) and Proposition 11.6.12]

(2.15)

\Gamma_{p}(-z)\Gamma_{p}(z)=-\Gamma_{p}(1-z)\Gamma_{p}(z)=1,\quad z\in p{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}.

For the Taylor expansion, this implies
[
12
, Theorem 11.6.18(2)]

(2.16)

g_{k}=(-1)^{k/2-1}(g_{k/2}^{2})/2+\sum_{0<j<k/2}(-1)^{j-1}g_{j}g_{k-j}\quad\text{for even $k>0$,}

which reduces the computation of derivatives
\Gamma^{(k)}_{p}(0)
to the case of odd
m
:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)^{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p}^{(4)}(0)=4\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)\Gamma^{(3)}_{p}(0)-12\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)^{4},

\displaystyle\dots

Alternatively, one can work with the logarithm of the
p
-adic Gamma function,

(2.18)

\log\Gamma_{p}(z)=\sum_{m>0}\frac{l_{m}}{m!}z^{m}.

Because of (
2.15
) this is an odd function, meaning
l_{m}=0
for even
m
. The first nontrivial coefficients are

(2.19)

\displaystyle l_{1}=\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0),

\displaystyle l_{3}=\Gamma_{p}^{(3)}(0)-\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)^{3},

\displaystyle l_{5}=\Gamma_{p}^{(5)}(0)+10\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)^{2}\Gamma_{p}^{(3)}(0)+9\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)^{5},

\displaystyle\dots

Remark 2.2
.

The
l_{m}
have a meaning in terms of
p
-adic
L
-functions
[
12
, Prop. 11.5.19]
. Concretely,
-\Gamma_{p}^{\prime}(0)
is the
p
-adic Euler constant, and

(2.20)

(-1)^{\frac{1}{(m-1)!}}l_{m}=L_{p}(\omega^{1-m},m),\quad m\geq 2.

The number on the right hand side of (
2.20
) is often denoted by
\zeta_{p}(m)
, since it can be obtained as a
p
-adic limit of classical zeta values; see e.g.
[
34
, Equation (1.1)]
.

For computational purposes, it is useful to make parts of the discussion above more precise:

Lemma 2.3
.

To compute the
k
-th derivative
\Gamma_{p}^{(k)}(0)
up to an error in
p^{G}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, it is sufficient to consider the finite sum over
m<M
in the
j=0
case of (
2.8
), where
M
is chosen such that

(2.21)

M\geq\frac{kp}{(p-1)\log(p)}+1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{k}{p-1}+M\frac{p-1}{p}-\log_{p}(k)-k\log_{p}(M-1)-\frac{2p-1}{p-1}>G.

Proof.

The
z^{k}
coefficient of
z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1)
is a sum of terms, each of which is obtained from
\pm(m-1)!
by removing
(k-1)
factors. Hence, from (
2.1
) we get

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}\big(\text{$z^{k}$-coefficient of }z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{val}((m-1)!)-(k-1)\log_{p}(m-1)\geq\frac{m-1}{p-1}-k\log_{p}(m-1)-1.

Combining that with (
2.10
) yields

(2.23)

\mathrm{val}\big(\text{$z^{k}$-coefficient of }(-p)^{m}d_{mp}z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1)\big)\\
\geq m\frac{p-1}{p}-k\log_{p}(m-1)-\frac{p}{p-1}.

Hence, the contribution of the
m
-th term in (
2.8
) to
\Gamma_{p}^{(k)}(0)
has valuation

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(k!)+\mathrm{val}\big(\text{$z^{k}$ coefficient of }(-p)^{m}d_{mp}z(z-1)\cdots(z-m+1)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{p-1}+m\frac{p-1}{p}-\log_{p}(k)-k\log_{p}(m-1)-\frac{2p-1}{p-1}.

If we write
f(m)
for the right hand side, fixing
k
, then
f^{\prime}(m)=(p-1)/p-k/((m-1)\,\log(p))\geq 0
for
m\geq\frac{kp}{(p-1)\log(p)}+1
. Hence, if
f(M)>G
for some
M\geq kp/{(p-1)\log(p)}+1
, then
f(m)>G
for all
m>M
as well. These are the conditions (
2.21
).
∎

3.
Background on differential equations

Consider formal power series connections in one variable
t
, of rank
r
, with a nilpotent simple pole, and having coefficients in the field
K
from (
1.3
). This means

(3.1)

\nabla_{t\partial_{t}}=t\partial_{t}+A(t),\quad A(t)=A_{0}+tA_{1}+t^{2}A_{2}+\cdots,\quad\text{$A_{0}$ nilpotent,}

where the
A_{m}
are
r\times r
matrices over
K
. The first part of the following Lemma gives the formal gauge classification of such connections. For the second part, see
[
32
, Proposition 18.1.1]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(i) Given (
3.1
), there is a unique

(3.2)

\Pi(t)=\Pi_{0}+t\Pi_{1}+\cdots,\quad\Pi_{0}=I,

which intertwines

(3.3)

\nabla_{t\partial_{t}}^{\mathrm{pole}}=t\partial_{t}+A_{0}

and the original connection. Concretely, this means that

(3.4)

t\partial_{t}\Pi+A(t)\Pi(t)-\Pi(t)A_{0}=0.

(ii) Suppose that
A(t)
converges on the open disc with radius
\rho>0
. Then:
\Pi(t)
converges on that disc iff the same holds for its (formal power series) inverse
\Pi(t)^{-1}
.

(iii) Suppose that there is some
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(3.5)

\mathrm{val}(A_{m})\geq\sigma m\;\;\text{ for all $m\geq 0$.}

Then
\Pi
converges, and is bounded, on the open disc with radius
p^{\sigma-2N/(p-1)}
, where
(N+1)
is the size of the largest Jordan block for
A_{0}
.

Proof.

(i) Write out (
3.4
) order by order:

(3.6)

m\Pi_{m}+[A_{0},\Pi_{m}]=-\!\!\sum_{0\leq i<m}A_{m-i}\Pi_{i},\quad m\geq 1.

Let
R_{m}
be the right hand side of (
3.6
). Since
A_{0}
is nilpotent, we can solve for
\Pi_{m}
by the formula

(3.7)

\Pi_{m}=R_{m}/m-[A_{0},R_{m}]/m^{2}+[A_{0},[A_{0},R_{m}]]/m^{3}+\cdots,

where the last term is divided by
m^{2N}
.

(ii) Suppose that
\Pi(t)
converges on the open disc with radius
\rho
. Writing
\wp(t)=\mathrm{det}\,\Pi(t)
,
a(t)=\mathrm{tr}\,A(t)
,
a_{0}=\mathrm{tr}(A_{0})
, one has
\wp(0)=1
and

(3.8)

t\partial_{t}\wp(t)+(a(t)-a_{0})\wp(t)=0.

This implies (using the Weierstrass preparation theorem, e.g.
[
33
, p. 105]
) that
\wp(t)\neq 0
for all
|t|<\rho
. As a consequence,
1/\wp(t)
, as defined by its formal power series, is also convergent for
|t|<\rho

[
19
, Appendix to Ch. 2]
. Note that all minors of
A(t)
also converge for
|t|<\rho
. Therefore, Cramer’s rule gives a formula for
\Pi(t)^{-1}
with the same convergence behaviour. The argument in converse direction is parallel.

(iii) Taking (
3.5

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(\Pi_{m})

∎

Example 3.2
.

Take the connection

For context concerning the following notions, see e.g.

(compared to that reference, we have drastically reduced the scope). The Frobenius pullback of a connection (

Definition 3.3

A Frobenius structure for

Lemma 3.4

(i) The constant term
\Phi_{0}

(iv) Suppose that the map

(i) The necessity of (

\displaystyle m\Phi_{m}+A_{0}\Phi_{m}-p\Phi_{m}A_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i<m}-A_{m-i}\Phi_{i}+p\Phi_{i}A_{\frac{m-i}{p}};

Here, the convention is that any terms

(ii) There is an analogue of (

(iii) Set

(iv) The constant endomorphism

(v) Consider

(i) The equation (

Definition 3.6

Take a Frobenius structure, and consider its characteristic polynomial

from (

Then
[

:

.

The valuations of the roots of

We conclude our exposition with a minor reformulation, which brings one back to more familiar territory insofar as the quantum connection is concerned. Namely, let’s use the rescaled variable

Example 3.8

Take

The

5.
Mirror symmetry for Fano toric varieties

The aim of this section, which follows (a simplified version of) arguments from
[
28
,
29
]
, is to prove a form of closed string mirror symmetry for compact Fano toric varieties. The proof passes through computations in equivariant quantum cohomology, but that is in a sense a technical tool; the ultimately desired statements drop the equivariance.

Convention 5.1
.

Our setup will involve several quantum variables
(q_{1},\dots,q_{r})
, one for each toric divisor. Nevertheless, the collection of such variables will often be denoted by a single
q
, as in the quantum product (
5.20
) or the superpotential (
5.41
). In particular, we use notation

(5.1)

\displaystyle\vec{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r},

\displaystyle q^{\vec{v}}=q_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots q_{r}^{v_{r}}.

The reason for this multivariable setup is not to have a richer theory: rather, we need it in order to carry out a specific argument (Section
8
). Following that, we will specialize back to a single variable (Section
9
).

5a.
Fano toric varieties

Let
T
be a rank
n
torus, and
N=\mathit{Hom}({\mathbb{C}}^{*},T)
its cocharacter lattice (of one-parameter subgroups). We fix an identification
T=({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
, and correspondingly
N={\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Take a fan in
N\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
giving rise to a smooth projective Fano toric variety
M
, on which
T
acts. Let
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{r}\in N
be the primitive generators of the rays of the fan. The following statement expresses combinatorially the smoothness of
M
:

Lemma 5.2
.

Each
k
-dimensional cone of the fan is of the form
{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}+\cdots+{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}\mathbf{e}_{i_{k}}
, where
(\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{i_{k}})
form part of a basis of the abelian group
N
.

Given
\mathbf{n}\in N
, write

(5.2)

\mathbf{n}=\psi_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\psi_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r},\quad\psi_{i}=\psi_{i}(\mathbf{n})\geq 0,

with the property that all
\mathbf{e}_{i}
with
\psi_{i}>0
belong to a single cone of the fan. This expression is unique, by Lemma
5.2
, hence defines a function

(5.3)

\vec{\psi}=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{r}):N\longrightarrow({\mathbb{Z}}^{\geq 0})^{r}.

Then,

(5.4)

w=\psi_{1}+\cdots+\psi_{r}:N\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{\geq 0}

is the unique function which is linear on each cone of the fan, and such that
w(\mathbf{e}_{i})=1
for all
i
. We extend
w
to
N\otimes{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
, without changing notation. The following statement expresses combinatorially the fact that
M
is Fano
[
13
, Lemma 6.1.13 and Theorem 6.1.14]
:

Lemma 5.3
.

(i) The function
w
is convex, meaning that

(5.5)

w(r_{1}\mathbf{n}_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}\mathbf{n}_{k})\leq r_{1}w(\mathbf{n}_{1})+\cdots+r_{k}w(\mathbf{n}_{k})\quad\text{for $\mathbf{n}_{j}\in N\otimes{\mathbb{R}}$, $r_{j}>0$.}

(ii)
w
is strictly convex in the following sense: if in (
5.5
), not all
\mathbf{n}_{j}
are contained in a single cone of the fan, then the inequality is strict.

Geometrically, the
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
correspond to the toric divisors
\{D_{i}\subset M\}
, hence to equivariant cohomology classes
[D_{i}]_{T}\in H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
. These form a basis of that (free) cohomology group,
H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})={\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
. Taking the
k
-th components of the vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, we get classes

(5.6)

\lambda_{k}=e_{1,k}[D_{1}]_{T}+\cdots+e_{r,k}[D_{r}]_{T}\in H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}}),\quad k=1,\dots,n.

The
\lambda_{k}
are the generators of
H^{2}_{T}(\mathit{point};{\mathbb{Z}})\subset H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
; and

(5.7)

H^{2}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})=H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})/({\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{n}).

The cohomology
H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
is concentrated in even degrees, and is a free abelian group. Hence, the Leray–Serre spectral sequence from
H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
to
H^{*}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
degenerates, and the forgetful map

(5.8)

H^{*}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})

is split surjective. From a choice of splitting, we get an isomorphism of
H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};{\mathbb{Z}})
-modules,

(5.9)

H^{*}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};{\mathbb{Z}}),

which reduces to (
5.8
) when composed with
H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};{\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
. This shows that (
5.8
) induces an isomorphism

(5.10)

H^{*}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})/(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})H^{*}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}}),

generalizing (
5.7
). For any
\mathbf{n}\in N
, define

(5.11)

\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}[D_{i}]^{\psi_{i}(\mathbf{n})}\in H_{T}^{2w(\mathbf{n})}(M;\mathbb{Z}).

These elements form a basis for
H^{*}_{T}(M;\mathbb{Z})
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. For future purposes, we introduce the following:

Definition 5.4
.

Let
F
be any field. An
F
-basis set is a finite set
\Delta\subset N
such that the non-equivariant images of the classes
(\alpha_{\mathbf{n}})_{\mathbf{n}\in\Delta}
form a basis of
H^{*}(M;F)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

(i) For any field
F
, there is an
F
-basis set.

(ii) If
\Delta
is an
F
-basis set, it has the same property for any other field with the same characteristic.

(iii) If
\Delta
is an basis set for a characteristic
p>0

0
.

Proof.

(i) By applying the universal coefficient theorem, one sees that the
(\alpha_{\mathbf{n}})_{\mathbf{n}\in N}
form a basis of
H^{*}_{T}(M;F)
, and that the forgetful map (
5.8
) is onto with
F
-coefficients.

(ii), (iii) Take the map
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\Delta}\rightarrow H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
which takes the standard generators to the non-equivariant images of the
\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}
. We can think of it as a matrix with integer coefficients. Then,
\Delta
is a generating set for fields of characteristic
0
if the matrix (is square and) has nonzero determinant; and in characteristic

5b.
Equivariant quantum cohomology

Identify
H_{2}^{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
via the basis dual to
[D_{i}]_{T}\in H^{2}_{T}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
, so that
{\mathbb{Q}}[H_{2}^{T}(M)]={\mathbb{Q}}[q_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,q_{r}^{\pm 1}]
. Recall the notation (
5.1
). We also write

(5.17)

Consider the ring

(5.18)

Occasionally we find it useful to have a grading, given by setting
\mathrm{deg}(q_{i})=2
; which means that
\Lambda
is nonnegatively graded, and

is the part with positive gradings.

The equivariant quantum product

(5.20)

\ast_{q}^{T}:H^{*}_{T}(M;\Lambda)\otimes H^{*}_{T}(M;\Lambda)\longrightarrow H^{*}_{T}(M;\Lambda)

counts curves in any class

with

. Note that
(A\cdot D_{1})+\cdots(A\cdot D_{r})=A\cdot c_{1}(TM)\geq 0
, since our variety is Fano. Equivalently, the weight can be written as
q^{\vec{j}(A)}
, where
\vec{j}:H_{2}(M)\rightarrow H_{2}^{T}(M)={\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
is the canonical map. We add a loop rotation parameter
u
(corresponding to the trivial action of
S^{1}
on
M
), and define the equivariant quantum connection as the collection of mutually commuting operators

(5.21)

\displaystyle\nabla_{uq_{i}\partial_{q_{i}}}^{T}:H^{*}_{T}(M;\Lambda[u])\longrightarrow H^{*}_{T}(M;\Lambda[u]),

\displaystyle\nabla_{uq_{i}\partial_{q_{i}}}^{T}(x)=uq_{i}\partial_{q_{i}}x+[D_{i}]_{T}\ast_{q}^{T}x.

For the grading, we set
\mathrm{deg}(u)=2
as well, so that

(5.22)

\displaystyle\deg(\alpha u^{k}q^{\vec{v}})=\deg(\alpha)+2k+2|\vec{v}|.

Then, (
5.20
) preserves the grading, and (
5.21
) has degree
2
.

-equivariant quantum cohomology of

The construction of shift operators involves a fibration

with fibre

. One considers classes

is the pullback of

along (

5.3
(i), and also with the de Rham differential. We denote the associated graded spaces by
GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
and
G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}
; they are still modules over
\bar{\Lambda}
. Explicitly, because of Lemma
5.3
(ii), multiplication in
GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
is given by

(6.3)

q^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{m}}\cdot q^{\vec{w}}z^{\mathbf{n}}=\begin{cases}q^{\vec{v}+\vec{w}}z^{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n}}&\text{if $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{n}$ lie in the same cone of the fan,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By comparing (
6.3
) with (
5.12
), we see that the map

(6.4)

\displaystyle GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}\longrightarrow H^{*}_{T}(M;\bar{\Lambda}),

\displaystyle q^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}\longmapsto\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}

is an isomorphism of rings. The superpotential is the mod
p
version of (
5.41
), for which we use the same notation. Write

(6.5)

\bar{\lambda}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i,k}q_{i}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\in R_{\bar{\Lambda}},\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

where
e_{i,k}
is the
k
-th component of
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, as in (
5.6
). Then,

(6.6)

dW_{q}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{k}\frac{dz_{k}}{z_{k}}.

From now on, we will consider the
\bar{\lambda}_{k}
as elements of
GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
. Under the isomorphism (
6.4
), they map to the generators of
H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};\bar{\Lambda})
, as described in (
5.6
). The following is essentially
[
1
, Theorem 2.14, Theorem 2.17]
(the complex in question is isomorphic to the Koszul complex studied there, tensored with
\bar{\Lambda}
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
); we give a more geometric argument.

Lemma 6.1
.

(i) We have

(6.7)

H^{i}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q})=0\quad\text{for }i<n.

(ii) The map (
6.4
) descends to an isomorphism

(6.8)

H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q})\cong H^{*}(M;\bar{\Lambda}).

Proof.

Our complex can be written as

(6.9)

0\rightarrow GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}\rightarrow GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\oplus n}\rightarrow GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\oplus\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n\\
2\end{smallmatrix}\right)}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}\rightarrow 0.

From (
6.6
), one sees that the differentials are built from multiplication with the
\bar{\lambda}_{k}
. This is in fact the Koszul complex associated to
(\bar{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\bar{\lambda}_{n})\in GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
. We know from (
5.9
) that
H^{*}_{T}(M;\bar{\Lambda})
is a free module over
H^{*}_{T}(\mathit{point};\bar{\Lambda})
. Transferring this via (
6.4
) shows that
GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
is a free module over
\bar{\Lambda}[\bar{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\bar{\lambda}_{n}]
. Hence, the
\bar{\lambda}_{k}
form a regular sequence. This implies that the only nonzero cohomology group in (
6.9
) is the last one, which is statement (i); and that nonzero group is equal to
GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}/(\bar{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\bar{\lambda}_{n})GR_{\bar{\Lambda}}
, which by comparison with (
5.10
) yields (ii).
∎

Next, following Section
5c
, we introduce an auxiliary parameter
u
and the de Rham differential:

Lemma 6.2
.

(i) We have

(6.10)

H^{i}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}[u],ud+dW_{q})=0\quad\text{for }i<n.

(ii) The map

(6.11)

H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}[u],ud+dW_{q})\longrightarrow H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q})

obtained by setting
u=0
is onto; and after choosing a
\bar{\Lambda}
-linear splitting of it, one gets a (non-canonical)
\bar{\Lambda}[u]
-linear isomorphism

(6.12)

H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q})[u]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}[u],ud+dW_{q}).

Proof.

Let’s temporarily change the grading on
G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}[u]
by setting

(6.13)

\deg(u^{l}q^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}\frac{dz_{j_{1}}}{z_{j_{1}}}\wedge\frac{dz_{j_{2}}}{z_{j_{2}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dz_{j_{k}}}{z_{j_{k}}})=2|\vec{v}|+2l-k,

which is compatible with our differential. For this alternative grading, the
u
-adic filtration is bounded in a given degree (only finitely many
u
-powers can arise); hence, the associated spectral sequence is convergent. The starting page is nonzero only in degrees congruent to
n
mod
2
, by Lemma
6.1
, and hence the spectral sequence must degenerate. The desired statements follow from this by a standard argument.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

H^{i}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q})=0
for
i<n
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
6.2
by setting
u=1
.
∎

The next result is our version of
[
1
, Theorem 2.18]
. Formally, it is analogous to Corollary
5.13
:

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\Delta\subset N
be an
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-basis set (Definition
5.4
). Then:

(i) The classes

(6.14)

[q^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}\,\mathit{vol}]\in H^{n}(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},dW_{q}),\quad\mathbf{n}\in\Delta

freely generate that cohomology group as a
\bar{\Lambda}
-module.

(ii) The same remains true if we replace the differential in (
6.14
) with
d+dW_{q}
.

Proof.

(i) By definition, the isomorphism (
6.8
) maps
[q^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}\,\mathit{vol}]\in H^{n}(G\Omega^{*},dW_{q})
to the non-equivariant image of
\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}
. Hence, our assumption implies the statement in question.

(ii) By Lemma
6.2
, the same monomials freely generate
H^{*}(G\Omega_{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*}[u],ud+dW_{q})
over
\bar{\Lambda}[u]
. Finally,

(6.15)

H^{*}(G\Omega_{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*},d+dW_{q})\cong H^{*}(G\Omega_{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*}[u],ud+dW_{q})/(u-1),

and so the desired result follows.
∎

7.
Dwork’s inverse Frobenius

We will construct a version of Dwork’s inverse Frobenius from
[
17
]
for the quantum connection on Fano toric varieties. The analytic framework in the present section is not sufficient to prove overconvergence, but it easy to define and to do computations in.

Convention 7.1
.

This section and the following one use parameters
(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
. These will ultimately be related to the previous
(q_{1},\dots,q_{r})
by rescaling as in (
1.8
), see (
7.30
) below. We write
t^{\vec{v}}=t_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{v_{r}}
as in (
5.1
).

7a.
Basic definitions

The first step is to introduce appropriate spaces of
p
-adic functions and differential forms, motivated by ones in
[
1
, §1]
. Recall that
K={\mathbb{Q}}_{p}(\mu)
;
O\subset K
is the ring of integers; and
\pi
is the constant (
1.5
).

Definition 7.2
.

(i) Let
B
be the ring of series

(7.1)

\sum_{|\vec{v}|\geq 0}a_{\vec{v}}t^{\vec{v}},\quad a_{\vec{v}}\in K,

such that:

(7.2)

for any
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there are only finitely many
\vec{v}
with
\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}})\leq C
.

(One could write it as
B=K\langle t_{1},(t_{1}^{-1}t_{2})^{\pm 1},\dots\rangle
; in the language of nonarchimedean analytic geometry, it is an affinoid algebra.) Write
IB\subset B
for the subspace where we only allow
|\vec{v}|>0
. Write
OB\subset B
for the subspace where
a_{\vec{v}}\in O
. Because the valuations appearing in any element of
B
are bounded below, we have
B=OB\otimes_{O}K
.

(ii) Let
R_{B}
be the space of series

(7.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v},\,\mathbf{n}\\
|\vec{v}|\geq w(\mathbf{n})\end{subarray}}a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\,\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}},\quad a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\in K,

with the same kind of condition as before:

(7.4)

for any
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there are only finitely many
(\vec{v},\mathbf{n})
with

\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})\leq C
.

Let
R_{OB}\subset R_{B}
be the subspace where
a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\in O
. We again have
R_{B}=R_{OB}\otimes_{O}K
.

(iii) Let
\Omega^{k}_{B}
be the space of
k
-forms in the variables
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
with coefficients in
B
, which is the free
R_{B}
-module with basis (
5.42
). As before, we can write
\Omega^{*}_{B}=\Omega^{*}_{OB}\otimes_{O}K
.

R_{B}
is a ring (and a
B
-algebra). To check that, take two elements written as in (
7.3
), with coefficients
a^{k}_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}
,
k=1,2
. The formal product, written in the same way, has coefficients

(7.5)

a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}_{1}+\vec{v}_{2}=\vec{v}\\
\mathbf{n}_{1}+\mathbf{n}_{2}=\mathbf{n}\end{subarray}}a_{\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{1}a_{\vec{v}_{2},\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{2}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n}_{1})+w(\mathbf{n}_{2})-w(\mathbf{n})}.

In order for this sum to be nonempty, we need
|\vec{v}_{k}|\geq w(\mathbf{n}_{k})
for both
k
, which implies
|\vec{v}|\geq w(\mathbf{n})
by Lemma
5.3
(i). For the same reason, the
\pi
-powers in (
7.5
) are all nonnegative. Let
C_{\operatorname{min}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
be the minimum of the valuations of
a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}^{k}
over all
(\vec{v},\mathbf{n},k)
. Choose some
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In any sum (
7.5
), there are only finitely many
(\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1})
where
\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{1})\leq C-C_{\mathrm{min}}
. For all others, we have

(7.6)

\mathrm{val}\big(a_{\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{1}a_{\vec{v}_{2},\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{2}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n}_{1})+w(\mathbf{n}_{2})-w(\mathbf{n})}\big)\geq\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{1})+\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}_{2},\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{2})\geq C;

this implies that the series (
7.5
) is
p
-adically convergent. One can slightly refine the argument to show that the
a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}
satisfy (
7.4
). Namely, when looking at an expression (
7.5
), there are two possibilities: either there are
(\vec{v}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{1},\vec{v}_{2},\mathbf{n}_{2})
such that
\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}_{k},\mathbf{n}_{k}}^{k})\leq C-C_{\operatorname{min}}
for both
k
, which is the case for only finitely many
(\vec{v},\mathbf{n})
; or else (
7.6
) applies to all terms in the series, so that
\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})\geq C
.

The superpotential is now

(7.7)

W_{t}=\pi\sum_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\in R_{B}.

As before, we consider the differential and connection

(7.8)

\displaystyle d+dW_{t}:\Omega^{*}_{B}\longrightarrow\Omega^{*+1}_{B},

(7.9)

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}=t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}+(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}W_{t}):\Omega^{*}_{B}\longrightarrow\Omega^{*}_{B}.

We will also need Frobenius-twisted versions of the previous notions.

Definition 7.3
.

(i) Denote by
R_{B}^{(p)}
the space of series

(7.10)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v},\,\mathbf{n}\\
|\vec{v}|\geq p\,w(\mathbf{n})\end{subarray}}a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\,\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}

such that (
7.4
) holds.

(ii) The spaces
\Omega_{B}^{(p),*}
of differential forms are defined as before, but using
R_{B}^{(p)}
as coefficients.

Consider the Frobenius map on the base
B
,

(7.11)

F(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})=(t_{1}^{p},\dots,t_{r}^{p}),

and the associated map (for which we use the same notation)

(7.12)

F:R_{B}\longrightarrow R_{B}^{(p)}.

If we introduce formal roots
t_{i}^{1/p}
, with
(t_{i}^{1/p})^{p}=t_{i}
, then (
7.12
) induces an isomorphism

(7.13)

R_{B}[t_{1}^{1/p},\dots,t_{r}^{1/p}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}R_{B}^{(p)};

the same holds for differential forms. In that sense, the spaces in Definition
7.3
are Frobenius pullbacks of those in Definition
7.2
. Correspondingly, we use the pullback superpotential, differential, and connection:

(7.14)

\displaystyle W_{t^{p}}=\pi\sum_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}^{p}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\in R_{B}^{(p)},

(7.15)

\displaystyle d+dW_{t^{p}}:\Omega^{(p),*}_{B}\longrightarrow\Omega^{(p),*+1}_{B},

(7.16)

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}^{(p)}=t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}+(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}W_{t^{p}}):\Omega^{(p),*}_{B}\longrightarrow\Omega^{(p),*}_{B}.

The differential form version of (
7.12
) then satisfies

(7.17)

\displaystyle(d+dW_{t^{p}})\circ F=F\circ(d+dW_{t}),

(7.18)

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}^{(p)}\circ F=F\circ(p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}),

where the extra
p
in (
7.18
) comes from the effect of
t\mapsto t^{p}
on the rotational vector field
t\partial_{t}
.

7b.
Functional analysis setup

The standard Gauss norm on
B
is

(7.19)

\|\sum_{\vec{v}}a_{\vec{v}}t^{\vec{v}}\|=\mathrm{max}_{\vec{v}}(|a_{\vec{v}}|)=p^{-\mathrm{min}_{\vec{v}}(\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v}}))}.

We similarly equip
R_{B}
with the norm

(7.20)

\|\sum_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\,\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}\|=\mathrm{max}_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}(|a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}|)=p^{-\mathrm{min}_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}(\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}))}.

Lemma 7.4
.

The norm (
7.20
) makes
R_{B}
into a Banach algebra.

Proof.

Completeness is standard. To show that this is a Banach algebra, it suffices to look at the coefficients of the product, see (
7.5
), and the first inequality in (
7.6
).
∎

One can introduce the same norm on
\Omega^{*}_{B}
, using the basis (
5.42
). The same works for the Frobenius-twisted versions
R_{B}^{(p)}
, using the norms of the coefficients in (
7.10
); and then (
7.12
) is a map of Banach algebras. Again, the same applies to
\Omega^{(p),*}_{B}
.

Lemma 7.5
.

The connection (
7.9
) is a bounded operator, of norm
\leq 1
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the assertion for
\Omega_{B}^{0}=R_{B}
. We consider the two terms in (
7.9
) separately; the desired statement follows because the norm on
R_{B}
is non-archimedean. We have

(7.21)

t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}\big(\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}\big)=v_{i}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}.

Since the
v_{i}
are integral,
|v_{i}|\leq 1
. Hence, if we apply the same to series (
7.3
), the norm of each coefficient cannot increase. For the second term, let
\vec{f}_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
be the
i
-th unit vector, so that
t_{i}=t^{\vec{f}_{i}}
and
(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}W_{t})=\pi t^{\vec{f}_{i}}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}
. Since
w(\mathbf{e}_{i})=1
by definition, we have

(7.22)

(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}W_{t})\cdot\big(\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}\big)=\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})+1}t^{\vec{v}+\vec{f}_{i}}z^{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_{i}}=\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})+w(\mathbf{e}_{i})-w(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}\,\big(\pi^{w(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}t^{\vec{v}+\vec{f}_{i}}z^{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_{i}}\big),

where the coefficient
\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})+w(\mathbf{e}_{i})-w(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}
has norm
\leq 1
by (
5.5
).
∎

7c.
Computing Dwork cohomology

Our strategy is by reduction to the mod
p
computations from Section
6
. We have

(7.23)

\displaystyle OB\twoheadrightarrow OB/\pi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bar{\Lambda},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{v}}a_{\vec{v}}t^{\vec{v}}\longmapsto\sum_{\vec{v}}\bar{a}_{\vec{v}}q^{\vec{v}},

where
\bar{a}_{\vec{v}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is the mod
\pi
image of
a_{\vec{v}}\in O
; the condition (
7.2
) ensures that the sum on the target side is finite. Similarly,

(7.24)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle R_{OB}\twoheadrightarrow R_{OB}/\pi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}GR_{\bar{\Lambda}},

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n},\vec{v}}a_{\mathbf{n},\vec{v}}\,\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}}\longmapsto\sum_{\mathbf{n},\vec{v}}\overline{a_{\mathbf{n},\vec{v}}}\,q^{\vec{v}}z^{\mathbf{n}};

the fact that the mod
\pi
reduction of the ring structure on
R_{OB}
satisfies the relations (
6.3
) follows from Lemma
5.3
. The same applies to
\Omega^{*}_{OB}\rightarrow G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}}
, using the basis (
5.42
). Comparing (
5.41
) and (
7.7
) shows that (
7.24
) maps
W_{t}
to
W_{q}
. As a consequence, we have an isomorphism of complexes

(7.25)

(\Omega^{*}_{OB},d+dW_{t})/\pi\cong(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},d+dW_{q})

The following technical result is a variant of
[
1
, Prop. A.1]
, with the same proof.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let

(7.26)

C^{*}=\{\cdots\to C^{n-2}\to C^{n-1}\to C^{n}\to 0\}

be a bounded above complex of flat
O
-modules, such that the
\pi
-adic filtration on each
C^{i}
is separated and complete. Let
\bar{C}^{*}=C^{*}/\pi
be the associated complex of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-vector spaces.

(i) The natural map
H^{n}(C^{*})/\pi\rightarrow H^{n}(\bar{C}^{*})
(
n
being the degree above which the complex is trivial) is an isomorphism.

(ii) For any
i
,
H^{i}(\bar{C}^{*})=0
implies
H^{i}(C^{*})=0
.

(iii) Suppose that
H^{i-1}(\bar{C}^{*})=0
for some
i
. Then:
H^{i}(C^{*})
is a flat
O
-module; the module
\operatorname{im}(d:C^{i-1}\to C^{i})
is complete with respect to the
\pi
-adic filtration; and
H^{i}(\bar{C}^{*})
is separated with respect to the
\pi
-adic filtration.

The following is an analogue of
[
1
, Prop. 3.1]
.

Proposition 7.7
.

(i)
H^{i}(\Omega^{*}_{OB},d+dW_{t})=0
for
i<n
.

(ii) Let
\Delta\subset N
be an
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-basis set (Definition
5.4
). Then, the classes

(7.27)

[t^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}]_{\mathbf{n}\in\Delta}\in H^{n}(\Omega^{*}_{OB},d+dW_{t}),\;\;\mathbf{n}\in\Delta,

freely generate that cohomology as an
OB
-module.

(iii) Analogues of (i) and (ii) apply to
(\Omega^{*}_{B},d+dW_{t})
, after replacing
OB
by
B
everywhere.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
7.6
with
C^{*}
being
(\Omega^{*}_{OB},d+dW_{t})
, and
\bar{C}^{*}
being
(G\Omega^{*}_{\bar{\Lambda}},d+dW_{q})
, using the isomorphism (
7.25
). The necessary cohomological vanishing for
\bar{C}^{*}
comes from Corollary
6.3
. Part (i) then follows immediately.

Let’s turn to (ii). Lemma
7.6
implies that
H^{n}(C^{*})
is is
O
-flat and
\pi
-separated. Consider the natural map

(7.28)

\Big(\bigoplus_{\mathbf{n}\in\Delta}t^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}\,\mathit{vol}\cdot\mathit{OB}\Big)\longrightarrow H^{n}(C^{*})\longrightarrow\widehat{H^{n}(C^{*})},

where the last term is
\pi
-adic completion. After reduction mod
\pi
, this map is an isomorphism; that follows from Lemma
7.6
(i) and Corollary
6.4
. Nakayama’s lemma (in the form
[
47
, Lemma 10.96.1 (2)]
) then shows that (
7.28
) is surjective. We already know that the second map in (
7.28
) is injective, that being separability. Therefore, the first map must also be surjective (and the second map an isomorphism). We next show that

(7.29)

\Big(\bigoplus_{\mathbf{n}\in\Delta}t^{\vec{\psi}(\mathbf{n})}\pi^{w(\mathbf{n})}z^{\mathbf{n}}\,\mathit{vol}\cdot\mathit{OB}\Big)\cap(d+dW_{t})(\Omega^{n-1}_{OB})=0.

From Lemma
7.6
we know that
(d+dW_{t})(\Omega^{n-1}_{OB})
is complete, hence so is the intersection (
7.29
). From Corollary
6.4
it follows that if we reduce that intersection modulo
\pi
, it is zero. Therefore, it vanishes by Nakayama’s Lemma.

(iii) Follows by taking the tensor product with
K
, over
O
(this is an exact functor).
∎

Our next concern is to compare the analytic theory constructed here with the algebraic one from Section
5c
. For that, consider the change of coordinates

(7.30)

\displaystyle\Lambda\longrightarrow B,

\displaystyle q_{i}\longmapsto\pi t_{i};

similarly
R_{\Lambda}\rightarrow R_{B}
, and also for differential forms. This maps
W_{q}
to
W_{t}
, hence we get a homomorphism of twisted de Rham complexes

(7.31)

(\Omega^{*}_{\Lambda},d+dW_{q})\longrightarrow(\Omega^{*}_{B},d+dW_{t}).

Moreover, this map is compatible with the connections (
5.61
), (
7.9
).

Corollary 7.8
.

The map (
7.31
) induces an isomorphism

(7.32)

H^{n}(\Omega^{*}_{\Lambda},d+dW_{q})\otimes_{\Lambda}B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{*}(\Omega^{*}_{B},d+dW_{t}),

where the tensor product is taken with respect to (
7.30
).

Proof.

Let
\Delta\subset N
be an
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-basis set. By Lemma
5.5
(iii), this is also a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis set, hence gives a basis (
6.14
) for the free
\Lambda
-module

. Under (

), the elements of this basis map to those in (

), so appealing to Proposition
7.7
(iii) completes the argument.
∎

7d.
The inverse Frobenius

Dwork’s construction for the (inverse) Frobenius comes in two steps. Start with a differential form in
\Omega^{*}_{B}
. The first step is multiplication with an invertible power series constructed from the Dwork exponential (
2.2
), namely

(7.33)

\displaystyle G_{t}

\displaystyle=D(\pi t_{1}z^{\mathbf{e}_{1}})\cdots D(\pi t_{r}z^{\mathbf{e}_{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0}d_{m_{1}}\cdots d_{m_{r}}\pi^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}\,t^{\vec{m}}z^{m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}}.

Remark 7.9
.

A word of caution: recall (see Section
2
) that the Dwork exponential has
p
-adic radius of convergence
<1
. As a consequence,
G_{t}\notin R_{B}
, so multiplication with that function does not preserve the spaces
\Omega^{*}_{B}
.

The second step is to make the substitution
z_{i}\mapsto z_{i}^{1/p}
and throw away non-integer powers of
z_{i}
, which is denoted by
\mathit{Loc}
. This is extended to differential forms so as to be compatible with the de Rham differential. Explicitly,

(7.34)

\mathit{Loc}\Big(z_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{i_{n}}\frac{dz_{j_{1}}}{z_{j_{1}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dz_{j_{l}}}{z_{j_{l}}}\Big)=\begin{cases}z_{1}^{i_{1}/p}\cdots z_{n}^{i_{n}/p}\cdot p^{-l}\frac{dz_{j_{1}}}{z_{j_{1}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dz_{j_{l}}}{z_{j_{l}}}&\text{$i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in p{\mathbb{Z}}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 7.10
.

If
\theta\in\Omega^{*}_{B}
, then
\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\cdot\theta)\in\Omega^{(p),*}_{B}
.

Proof.

We explain the proof for degree
0
differential forms, meaning
R_{B}=\Omega^{0}_{B}
; the general case works in the same way. If the original element is written as in (
7.3
), the image under
\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\cdot)
is

(7.35)

\sum a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}d_{m_{1}}\cdots d_{m_{r}}\pi^{|\vec{m}|+w(\mathbf{n})}t^{\vec{m}+\vec{v}}z^{(\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p},

where the summation is over
(\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n})
satisfying

(7.36)

\displaystyle m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0,

\displaystyle|\vec{v}|\geq w(\mathbf{n}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}\in p{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

By Lemma
5.3
(i), each collection (
7.36
) satisfies

(7.37)

\displaystyle p\cdot w((\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p)

\displaystyle=w(\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})\leq w(\mathbf{n})+|\vec{m}|\leq|\vec{v}+\vec{m}|,

which meets the condition placed on monomials for elements in
B^{(p)}
. To match (
7.10
) more closely, write (
7.35
) as

(7.38)

\displaystyle\sum b_{\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}\,\pi^{w((\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p)}t^{\vec{m}+\vec{v}}z^{(\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p},

\displaystyle b_{\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}=a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}}d_{m_{1}}\cdots d_{m_{r}}\pi^{|\vec{m}|+w(\mathbf{n})-w((\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p)}.

Then

(7.39)

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(b_{\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{val}(d_{m_{i}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{p-1}\big(|\vec{m}|+w(\mathbf{n})-w((\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})+|\vec{m}|\big(\frac{1-2p}{p^{3}-p^{2}}\big)+\frac{1}{p}\big(|\vec{m}|+w(\mathbf{n})\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{p^{2}-p}\big(|\vec{m}|+w(\mathbf{n})-w(\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{val}(a_{\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})+\frac{1}{p}w(\mathbf{n})+\frac{p^{2}-3p+1}{p^{3}-p^{2}}|\vec{m}|.

In the third line, we have used the estimate (
2.4
); the last inequality comes from Lemma
5.3
(i). The last line, seen as a function of
(\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n})
, is proper and bounded below: it is the sum of a proper bounded below function in the variables
(\vec{v},{\mathbf{n}})
(the first term), another such function in
\vec{m}
(the last term), and a nonnegative middle term. Hence, for any
C
there are only finitely many
(\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n})
with
\mathrm{val}(b_{\vec{m},\vec{v},\mathbf{n}})<C
.

The first consequence is that for each fixed value of
(\vec{m}+\vec{v},\mathbf{n}+m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})
, the relevant terms in (
7.38
) form a
p
-adically convergent series. Secondly, once one adds up those terms, the outcome is a series in the
(t,z)
variables which satisfies the finiteness condition defining
B^{(p)}
.
∎

Having checked that this makes sense, we now state the outcome formally:

Definition 7.11
.

The Dwork inverse Frobenius is the
B
-linear map

(7.40)

\displaystyle\Psi:\Omega^{*}_{B}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\Omega_{B}^{(p),*},

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\cdot\theta).

Remark 7.12
.

The computation in Lemma
7.10
shows that
\Psi
is a bounded operator between Banach spaces, of norm
\leq 1
.

Lemma 7.13
.

(i)
\Psi
is a chain map.

(ii)
\Psi
intertwines the connections (
7.9
) and (
7.16
):

(7.41)

\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}^{(p)}\circ\Psi=\Psi\circ\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}.

Proof.

This is a formal computation, which can be carried out without considering the convergence conditions involved in our Banach spaces. The formula

(7.42)

G_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\exp(\pi(t_{i}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}-t_{i}^{p}z^{p\mathbf{e}_{i}})),

indicates that multiplication with
G_{t}
conjugates the twisted de Rham differentials associated to
W_{t}
and
\pi\sum_{i}t_{i}^{p}z^{p\mathbf{e}_{i}}
; and
\mathit{Loc}
relates the latter function to
W_{t^{p}}
. Compatibility of connections can be derived from the same conjugation argument, but we prefer a direct computation:

(7.43)

\displaystyle\nabla^{(p)}_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\,\theta)=\big(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}+(\pi pt_{i}^{p}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}})\big)\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathit{Loc}\big(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}(G_{t}\,\theta)+G_{t}\,\pi pt_{i}^{p}z^{p\mathbf{e}_{i}}\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\mathit{Loc}\big(G_{t}\,(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}\theta)+G_{t}\,\pi(t_{i}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}-pt_{i}^{p}z^{p\mathbf{e}_{i}})\,\theta+G_{t}\,\pi pt_{i}^{p}z^{p\mathbf{e}_{i}}\,\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\mathit{Loc}\big(G_{t}\cdot(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}\theta+\pi t_{i}z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}})\theta\big)=\mathit{Loc}(G_{t}\cdot\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}\theta).

∎

8.
The initial term

The aim of this section is to compute the previously constructed inverse Frobenius modulo
IB
, relating it to the
p
-adic Gamma function. The key idea in the computation is to insert one of the operations (
7.9
) into the Mahler expansion (
2.8
) with

:

(8.1)

\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-p)^{m}d_{mp}\,\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-1)\cdots(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-m+1):\Omega_{B}^{*}\longrightarrow\Omega_{B}^{*}.

This makes sense since:
\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}
is an endomorphism of
\Omega_{B}^{*}
of norm
\leq 1
(Lemma
7.5
), hence so is
(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-k)
for any

; and

goes to zero

-adically as

, by (

). This means that the series (
8.1
) converges absolutely in the operator norm of the space of endomorphisms of
\Omega^{*}_{B}
.
The operators
\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}
commute with each other, hence so do the endomorphisms (

); and we can write their composition as

(8.2)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sum_{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0}\!\!\!(-p)^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}d_{m_{1}p}\cdots d_{m_{r}p}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-1)\cdots(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-m_{i}+1).

We now apply this to the element (
5.43
).

Lemma 8.1
.

In
\Omega^{n}_{B}
, we have

(8.3)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})\mathit{vol}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\!\!\!\!m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0}d_{m_{1}p}\cdots d_{m_{r}p}\pi^{(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r})p}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}z^{m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}}\mathit{vol}

Proof.

Consider

(8.4)

\nabla_{\partial_{t_{i}}}=t_{i}^{-1}\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}=\partial_{t_{i}}+\partial_{t_{i}}W_{t}=\partial_{t_{i}}+\pi z^{\mathbf{e}_{i}}.

This makes no sense as an endomorphism of
\Omega_{B}^{*}
, since it does not preserve the inequality on monomials from (
7.3
). However,
t_{i}^{m}\nabla_{\partial_{t_{i}}}^{m}
does make sense for any

, and (as a general property of connections)

(8.5)

t_{i}^{m}\nabla_{\partial_{t_{i}}}^{m}=\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-1)\cdots(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-m+1).

Hence, one can rewrite (

) as

(8.6)

\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-p)^{m}d_{mp}\,t_{i}^{m}\nabla_{\partial_{t_{i}}}^{m}.

The same works for (

), yielding

(8.7)

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})=\sum_{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0}(-p)^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}d_{m_{1}p}\cdots d_{m_{r}p}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}\nabla_{\partial_{t_{1}}}^{m_{1}}\cdots\nabla_{\partial_{t_{r}}}^{m_{r}}.

By applying (

) repeatedly, one gets

(8.8)

\displaystyle(-p)^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}\nabla_{\partial_{t_{1}}}^{m_{1}}\cdots\nabla_{\partial_{t_{r}}}^{m_{r}}\mathit{vol}

\displaystyle=(-p)^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}\pi^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}z^{m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{e}}\mathit{vol}

\displaystyle=\pi^{(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r})p}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}z^{m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}}\mathit{vol}.

Inserting that into (
8.7
) yields (
8.3
).
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

In
\Omega^{(p),n}_{B}
, we have

(8.9)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-\nabla^{(p)}_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})\,\mathit{vol}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0\\
m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}\in pN\end{subarray}}\!\!\!d_{m_{1}}\cdots d_{m_{r}}\pi^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}z^{(m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p}\mathit{vol}\;\;\mathrm{mod}\;IB.

Proof.

Take the result from Lemma
8.1
and map it to the Frobenius-twisted version by (
7.12
) to the Frobenius-twisted version. The outcome, bearing (
7.18
) in mind, is

(8.10)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}^{(p)})\,\mathit{vol}=F\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{p}(-p\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}})\,\mathit{vol}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0\\
m_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\end{subarray}}d_{m_{1}p}\cdots d_{m_{r}p}\pi^{(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r})p}t_{1}^{pm_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{pm_{r}}z^{m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\geq 0\\
m_{i}\in p{\mathbb{Z}}\end{subarray}}d_{m_{1}}\cdots d_{m_{r}}\pi^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}}t_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{m_{r}}z^{(m_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}\mathbf{e}_{r})/p}\mathit{vol};

in the last line, we have simply changed the indices

to

From Lemma

(8.11)

For all

In

We will use

\alpha\sim\beta\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\alpha-\beta=(d+dW_{t})(\gamma)+\delta,\text{ for some }\gamma\in\Omega^{n-1}_{B},\;\delta\in IB\cdot\Omega^{n}_{B}.

.

For any

Let’s introduce some temporary notation:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-1)\cdots(\nabla_{t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}}-m_{i}+1)\,\mathit{vol}

Let’s switch to thinking of the Frobenius as acting on

, as in Corollary

.

Theorem 8.7
.

, the Frobenius (

10b.
Banach space setup

Fix some

The space of functions is correspondingly

(10.9)

R_{L(b)}=\Big\{\sum_{k\geq w(\mathbf{n})}a_{k,\mathbf{n}}t^{k}z^{\mathbf{n}},\;\mathrm{val}(a_{k,\mathbf{n}})-bk\text{ is bounded below}\Big\}.

\displaystyle R_{L(b,c)}=\prod_{k\geq 0}t^{k}R_{L(b,c),k},\qquad R_{L(b,c),k}=\Big\{\sum_{k\geq w(\mathbf{n})}a_{k,\mathbf{n}}z^{\mathbf{n}},\;\mathrm{val}(a_{k,\mathbf{n}})\geq bk+c\Big\}.

\mathrm{val}(\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\bar{\Phi}^{1}_{m}+\cdots+\tilde{\gamma}^{r}\bar{\Phi}^{r}_{m})<K+H\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\mathrm{val}(\bar{\Phi}_{m})=\mathrm{val}(\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\bar{\Phi}^{1}_{m}+\cdots+\tilde{\gamma}^{r}\bar{\Phi}^{r}_{m}).

It suffices to choose

A.3

.

In the examples below, we have plotted

, so overconvergence means that our graph has a smaller linear growth rate than that. Obviously, computations up to a finite order in
