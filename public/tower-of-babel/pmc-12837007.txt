Family views on health resources and services in the intermountain west: a qualitative study
Authors: Gladys Lopez Castaneda, Mariah P. Hoy, Kato K. Haupeakui, Halle M. Jarman, Robert A. Chaney, Brianna M. Magnusson, AliceAnn Crandall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesThe aims of this study were to (1) Examine how families access health information; (2) Explore community perceptions of the role, purpose, and expectations of health agencies; and (3) Assess the perceptions of how well local public and private agencies make services accessible to diverse families.MethodsWe evaluated families’ perceptions of public health programs and services through 25 semi-structured interviews that were conducted in October 2022. Inductive thematic coding was used to analyze the interviews.ResultsWe identified the following themes: Objective 1— Families wanted more personalized sources of information, less fragmentation of information, and increased promotion of health information in family friendly spaces (e.g. libraries, schools, and churches); Objective 2— families were unsure of the different roles and responsibilities of the health department and nonprofits; Objective 3— participants felt that both sectors could increase their awareness of different family structures as well as cultural values of minorities by increasing staff cultural competency and creating culturally sensitive environments.ConclusionsThis study emphasizes the importance of tailoring health information to families, promoting in family friendly spaces, alleviating the impact of fragmented information, improving resource accessibility, and developing culturally sensitive environments for all families to feel seen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13891-3.

Background

People gather information about health issues from a variety of living (e.g., a trusted relative or physician) and nonliving sources (e.g., the internet) [1]. Sometimes, the number of sources results in people feeling overwhelmed [2, 3]. This was particularly pronounced during the COVID-19 pandemic as individuals and families were bombarded with information from a variety of sources, evoking an “infodemic” [4]. Many struggled to make informed decisions for their families because of the difficulty in deciphering information accuracy [5].

The consequences of this information overload were increased by systemic issues in the U.S. healthcare system. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the American population experienced a disproportionally high mortality rate with up to 400,000 more deaths per capita than populations of other wealthy nations, prior to the availability of vaccines [6]. One contributing factor was the fragmented nature of the U.S. healthcare system, which often lacks coordination across providers and organizations, leading to many services being provided through a disconnected supply chain [6]. Research has shown that a close coordination and unification of healthcare delivery systems brings higher health outcomes and efficiency. However, competition between entities in the healthcare system often leads to miscommunication and gaps in care. Ultimately, this disconnection leaves individuals and families without clear guidance and adequate support, even as they attempt to navigate through overwhelming levels of health information [6, 7].

As healthcare needs in U.S. communities grow in complexity, due to more individuals experiencing a lack of healthcare coverage, many families are choosing to seek care outside of traditional medical settings [8, 9]. Alternative resources such as nonprofit organizations and local health departments, play a vital role in addressing community needs, and promoting health and well-being. These organizations, often embedded within the communities they serve, offering essential services that private providers might not [10]. Although the services vary by entity, common examples of services include immunizations, community health education, communicable disease control, epidemiology and surveillance efforts, food safety regulations, and other environmental health controls. However, despite their critical role, these organizations often target their health information and services at individuals rather than families [9, 10].

Families are central to individual and community health and development [11, 12], and despite the awareness of the importance of families to health, public health programming often fails to adequately engage and utilize family-centered thinking for health promotion [13, 14]. Part of the challenge may be that U.S. culture strongly advocates for protecting the privatization of families, making public and private providers hesitant to engage families [15]. However, family impact principles support the privacy of families while emphasizing the importance of involving families to better sustain health promotion efforts [16]. To better support health, it is necessary to look beyond individuals and include families, while also recognizing that families are shaped by the communities and environments around them.

When considering the effects of public engagement and health promotion efforts in communities, it is beneficial to address family health because families are the foundational unit that make up communities [17]. Because behaviors and health patterns are best monitored and practiced within household environments, the family unit becomes crucial to focus interventions and activities on. However, as families do become a focus of effort, it does not mean that they should be observed alone. Households are inextricably affected by their social and economic environments [18]. This means that family-centered programs and services must be dynamic to meet the diverse needs of families within a variety of environments [19].

One challenge for including families more in public health programs is that public health agencies may be unsure of how to best interact with families [20]. To our knowledge, no research has been conducted that examines how families want agencies to interact with them. To address this gap, the purpose of this study was threefold: (1) To examine the methods and processes families use to access health information; (2) To explore community perceptions of the role, purpose, and expectations of local public and private agencies; and (3) To assess family perceptions of how well local public and private agencies make services accessible to diverse families. To evaluate the effectiveness of public health agencies’ interactions with the family, we conducted semi-structured interviews with parents to examine their preferences for how agencies communicate with them, their understanding of public health agencies, and barriers families face engaging with public health agencies.

Methods

Sample and procedures

We sampled 25 families who had at least one child under the age of 18 years, and lived in Utah County in the United States. Utah County is the second most populous county in the state of Utah, with approximately 700,000 residents. Its median household income is $96,877, has an employment rate of 69.3%, and a 9% poverty level. It is largely occupied by White residents: 77% White, 16% Hispanic/Latino, 2% Asian, 1% Black, 1% Native American and Alaska Native, 1% Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and 2% identifying as multiracial [21].

The local health system is composed of a state health department with eight subdivisions (environmental health, health promotion and prevention, nursing, mosquito abatement, senior volunteers, vital records, WIC nutrition, and communication and quality improvement). In addition to the health department, there are several nonprofit organizations outside of the government sector that provide health promotion and health services to the local community. The state has a culture of active community engagement, and many adults are familiar with public and private nonprofit organizations, as Utah consistently ranks among the highest in the nation for volunteerism. For example, AmeriCorp data from 2023 shows that within the past year 46.6% of Utah residents participated in formal volunteer efforts (e.g. work with food banks, health departments, schools and libraries and 68.2% had engaged in informal volunteer efforts, such as helping their neighbor, placing both rates as some of the highest in the nation [22].

Interviews were conducted by twenty-five first year Master of Public Health (MPH) students, including the first three authors, who had taken a class on qualitative data collection and analysis. Interviewers were enrolled in a graduate-level families and public health course at the time data collection took place. Students were enrolled in a one semester class on qualitative data collection and analysis where they learned to develop interview guides, practiced conducting interviews. Each student was required to complete an interview as part of a class assignment. Students signed up to conduct interviews of people from different family types, and participants were selected using a convenience sample approach. To ensure that a variety of family structures were included in our study, participants were required to fit within different family types (e.g., single parent families, two parent families, families with children in a variety of age groups, different family sizes, multiracial families, and families where both parents were non-White). There were both male and female interviewers, but the interviews were not gender matched. Some students knew their interviewees beforehand while others met participants for the first time at the time of the interview. Participants were approached via e-mail, text, phone, and face-to-face. Students were trained by the senior authors (all with PhDs) on the interview guide and specific human subjects procedures for the study, including cultural considerations for Spanish speaking participants. Students had practiced conducting interviews and reflectively calibrating between student–interviewers.

A total of 25 interviews were conducted, but three had both parents present leading to a total of 28 participants. Information was not collected about potential participants who refused to cooperate, and thus a response rate cannot be calculated. Interviews were conducted in October 2022 via Zoom and lasted approximately 30–60 min. Each interview was recorded and transcribed using Zoom software. Transcriptions were verified by study research assistants but were not reviewed by participants. Interviews were conducted in English or Spanish. The study was approved by the Brigham Young University Human Research Protection Program.

Description of instrument

All interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide consisting of 12 questions. Before asking the questions, the interviewers read a description of the study and gained participant verbal consent to proceed. In the description, the interviewer explained that they were conducting the interview for a class assignment and also explained that it was a research study that had been approved by a human ethics committee. Questions included: participant knowledge about public and private health agencies and services, their preferred method of receiving health information, barriers families experienced interacting with health agencies, and the degree to which health services recognized a diversity of family needs. The instrument was developed for this study and is available in supplementary materials 1.

Data analytic methods

Inductive thematic coding was used to analyze the data. We developed a codebook by open coding randomly selecting interviews to develop an initial coding scheme. This was repeated to include more interviews and discussing with the authors after each step until the codebook was saturated (n = 13 interviews). Each interview was coded with this final codebook by two coders.

Interviews were then axially coded using the codebook Atlas.ti software (version 23) to identify major themes. Data were coded for each of our three objectives and include major themes and sub-themes, which can be found in Tables 1, 2 and 3. After each phase of coding interviews, the three coders would meet to reconcile differences by discussing the selected coding themes and reaching a consensus on which codes were most appropriate for the selected interview. Each coded interview had a Krippendorf’s alpha (α) intercoder agreement ranging from 0.72 to 0.90.

Results

The majority of participants were female (82%). Nearly half (48%) of participants were 30 to 39 years old, with participant age ranging from 24 to 50 years. Participants self-reported their race as White (65%), Latino (15%), multiracial (12%), and Black (8%). On average, participants had three children (range: 1–6 children). All but one participant were married.

Objective 1: The method and process of families accessing local health information

As a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, many families described an increased desire to seek local health information. This shift revealed how families navigated health information and their concerns regarding trust, accuracy, and access to local sources of information. Three primary themes emerged from the discussion on how families made sense of local health information within their everyday contexts (See Table 1).

Personal connection

Families consistently turned to sources that were viewed as trustworthy, reliable, and personally relevant to them such as relatives, friends, or medical providers. These choices suggest that health information was not only valued because of its factual content, but also because of how it aligned with their family circumstances and trusted relationships. For example: “…I ask my sister. She’s an epidemiologist, studying public health too” (26 years old, Married Mother, 2 children, Caucasian). In addition to family or friends, many participants sought out their medical providers in combination with their family, for example:… my doctor. Number one. …and number one and a half would probably just be from my mom or grandmother when she was alive. I suppose older women that I feel are credible enough for me personally. (46 years old, Married Mother, 4 children, Chinese/Hawaiian/Portuguese)

… my doctor. Number one. …and number one and a half would probably just be from my mom or grandmother when she was alive. I suppose older women that I feel are credible enough for me personally. (46 years old, Married Mother, 4 children, Chinese/Hawaiian/Portuguese)

Other participants sought out institutional sources, such as government agencies, insurance companies, or doctor’s offices as secure and reliable providers of health information. For example:…you know the Health Department or city references, or Insurance company, you know. If they’re going to give some information or an article, a doctor’s office, they’re going to give a link to an article or whatever. So, the source, I think, is the most important. (46years old, Married Mother, 4 children, Caucasian)

…you know the Health Department or city references, or Insurance company, you know. If they’re going to give some information or an article, a doctor’s office, they’re going to give a link to an article or whatever. So, the source, I think, is the most important. (46years old, Married Mother, 4 children, Caucasian)

Scattered health information

Families also described encountering health information across fragmented sources. While many recognized government sources such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention or health department websites, many participants also relied on other sources such as pharmacies, private clinics, and nonprofits. This abundance of sources often created confusion and left families feeling overwhelmed by the amount of information they were receiving. For example, “I feel like we’re inundated with so much information all the time that picking out what’s important is a little tricky.” (44 years old, Married Mother, 6 children, Caucasian).

In addition to the number of information sources, participants were hesitant towards particular sources of information (e.g. news). They also expressed a general sense of unease regarding consuming health information and their confidence in its accuracy.I think one of the issues we have is having too much knowledge or too much access to information constantly. I don’t know, go on the web when you have a flu, and you’re wondering like what my illness is, and everyone thinks you’re going to die because WebMD and everything is a side effect of death. You know what I mean, they think they can figure it out themselves. So, I don’t know. I still like that’s the hard part of either getting the right information out there, because there is so much information that you don’t know what’s right and what’s wrong… (33 years old, Widowed Mother, 2 children, Caucasian)

Many participants were uncertain of where they went to seek information. This included refraining from using certain low-trust channels such as specific news outlets or social media, and described being unconfident in the knowledge shared on “trusted” sources of information (such as government websites).

Family-friendly social spaces

Family-friendly spaces are locations or environments where individuals felt that they could receive family-centered health information. They were also places that promoted social interactions such as schools, libraries, recreation centers, community events and churches. For example, one participant mentioned:Assemblies at kids’ schools are effective. Some things could maybe be said over the pulpit at church, which is a big part of the community here…, but it depends on what that is. (44 years old, Married Mother, 6 children, Caucasian)

Assemblies at kids’ schools are effective. Some things could maybe be said over the pulpit at church, which is a big part of the community here…, but it depends on what that is. (44 years old, Married Mother, 6 children, Caucasian)

Objective 2: The perception of the role, purpose, and expectation of local nonprofit agencies and the local county health department

Participants described their view on what local nonprofit agencies and health departments do and the role they play in the health of their family (See Table 2). Most participants focused on the health department’s role addressing mental health, immunizations, and enforcing health codes. However, many participants misunderstand the roles of nonprofits and local health departments. Another common perception was that public and private health services were targeted toward lower socioeconomic groups; many participants felt that their services did not apply to their families.

They do what?

More than half of the participants were unsure of what the different responsibilities of the health department and local nonprofits are. This confusion was especially evident in services like food banks, WIC, or immunizations, which participants struggled to associate with one organization. For example:I didn’t realize that it covered so much. Like when you mentioned food bank, I wasn’t surprised with WIC cuz I see that on the sign at the health department. But I didn’t realize they have any correlation with food banks (52 years old, Married Mother, 5 children, Caucasian)

I didn’t realize that it covered so much. Like when you mentioned food bank, I wasn’t surprised with WIC cuz I see that on the sign at the health department. But I didn’t realize they have any correlation with food banks (52 years old, Married Mother, 5 children, Caucasian)

Others misunderstood the health department’s role in vaccination, assuming that it was only available for families without insurance.I have a couple of friends who get all their children vaccinated there, and I thought that they were using that because they didn’t have good insurance, and so I would always go to my doctor first for vaccines. And then there is a second place. These friends even go to the same doctor… pediatrician but still always go to the Health Department for vaccines. So, I always thought it was for people that didn’t have insurance to cover. (52 years old, Married Mother, 5 children, Caucasian)

These accounts demonstrate how blurred the division between the two organizations can be, making it more difficult for families to clearly distinguish the roles of the two.

Perceptions of the health department

Participants primarily associated the local health department with vaccinations, but many also mentioned its role in mental health, preventative health services (e.g. mammograms, physicals), social services, health promotion and prevention (e.g. immunizations, health education), and policy enforcement. However, many expressed only partial or uncertain knowledge about its services, for example, “I guess, help me in the health field if I need say a mammogram or clinics, or anything of that sorts. I’m guessing they do immunizations too, maybe?” (46 years old, Married Mother, 4 children, Hawaiian/Portuguese) Some recalled outreach experiences, like newborn screenings, through referrals to outside agencies, but they often questioned why those services fell under the role of the health department.I know that they do some public outreach. Maybe, I think when my second child was born, I think the public or the [redacted] Health Department reached out and did like a newborn well screening thing. I don’t know why, but they did. (28 years old, Married Mother, 2 children, Caucasian)

I know that they do some public outreach. Maybe, I think when my second child was born, I think the public or the [redacted] Health Department reached out and did like a newborn well screening thing. I don’t know why, but they did. (28 years old, Married Mother, 2 children, Caucasian)

Many participants mentioned the importance of policy enforcement in ensuring that restaurants, daycares, and other locations are following guidelines such as food safety regulations. One participant said, “I think they’re in charge of making public health so when I think of things like making sure restaurants are up to snuff with their health codes and stuff.” (31 years old, Married Father, 2 children, Caucasian) Overall, participants were able to recognize certain roles, but they still held a limited view of the reach and broader responsibilities local health departments have.

Perceived target audiences

Many participants perceived that services offered by local nonprofits were not targeted towards people in their own socioeconomic bracket and thus were not applicable to them. Most participants in our study self-identified as “middle class” and often distanced themselves from these programs, for example, “But I yeah, I don’t know I’m not too diverse in having enough money or insurance or health problems” (24 years old, Married Mother, 1 child, Caucasian/Asian/ Pacific Islander), and “I don’t necessarily fit the demographic of a refugee. I have the resources in my life to take care of a lot of those things” (44 years old, Married Mother, 6 children, Caucasian). Generally, participants felt health department services were for lower socio-economic families, and those who lacked resources to be independent of outside help.

Robust communication from health departments

Families frequently expressed the desire of wanting better communication from health departments so that their messages were engaging, up-to-date, and easy to access. This included alerting the community of important events such as a disease outbreak, vaccines, and having hotlines available to call. Discussion

This was an exploratory study to examine how families want to receive health information, their perceptions of public and private agencies, and how well families feel these agencies reach a diversity of families. Participants reported that their communication preferences were dependent on their personal and family circumstances and that they would actively pursue health information through sources like family, friends, social media, medical providers, and insurance providers. Participants also reported that accessing health information in family friendly spaces such as schools and churches, were highly favored. Barriers to accessing health information included fragmentation of health information sources, confusion of public and private health agencies, and cultural competency in health services and environment.

Methods and processes families use to access health information

Consistent with prior studies, families in this study relied on both social networks and professional sources when seeking health information. Parents often turned to trusted relatives or friends first, followed by medical providers when making health decisions, supporting prior research that health knowledge is co-constructed within social networks as opposed to individually [23–26]. The current study adds to the extant literature indicating that it is important for professionals to help build trust and personal connections rather than just focusing on professional authority.

Families also described the difficulty of navigating fragmented sources of information. This aligns with research on the COVID-19 infodemic, where overwhelming and contradictory health messages made it more difficult for families to determine which source was most accurate [27]. This study advances the literature by showing how contradictory information can lead families to feel less trusting and more skeptical of information provided. This demonstrates a critical gap in accessing health information: when institutions do not coordinate, families have to carry the burden of making health decisions that lead to more stress and confusion.

Finally, participants expressed a preference for receiving health information in family-centered community spaces, such as schools or churches. Prior research has emphasized the role of schools and libraries in advancing health promotion [28], however, our findings add that these spaces are primarily viewed by families as being trusted settings where health information can be naturally embedded into daily life.

Community perceptions of local and private agencies

Families frequently expressed uncertainty about the roles of the health department and nonprofits. This is in direct accordance with research showing that fragmented U.S. health systems make the roles of health sectors unclear and confusing [7, 29]. While prior studies have mainly focused on systemic inefficiencies [30, 31] our findings extend beyond this and demonstrate how uncertainty about the roles of each sector directly impacts family trust and decision-making. Families often assumed that most services were for those of low-income, which stigmatized their use and excluded middle-income families from accessing beneficial services. This perception aligns with previous work on the stigma in health services and demonstrates how programs are generally viewed as being only for others with fewer resources and contributes to community disengagement [32].

At the same time, families also wanted nonprofits and health departments to be more visible in community spaces, aligning with prior studies on proactive community outreach that emphasize the need for being accessible and present in everyday life [14]. Our study contributes by demonstrating how being present is not enough, as agencies also need to be able to communicate their relevance across various socioeconomic groups in order to counteract the assumptions of class exclusivity.

Family perceptions on how well agencies reach diverse families

Participants valued the acknowledgement of diverse family structures, cultural competence of staff, and inclusive environments. Prior studies’ have recommended creating culturally tailored health promotion and build upon them by showing how inclusivity is central to whether families feel safe engaging with health services [33–35]. For example, language assistance and availability can shape whether families feel comfortable asking questions [36–38].

Our findings also contribute to prior studies on equity and representation in healthcare. Families reported that visual cues (e.g. diverse images, multilingual materials) and diverse staffing practices (e.g. hiring staff from the same racial/ethnic backgrounds, hiring bilingual/multilingual staff) helped them to feel recognized and respected. This further exemplifies how trust in health services is not centered on factual recommendations alone, but also on the established social relationships between families and health sectors [39].

Recommendations for practice

Based on the study findings, we provide three primary recommendations to public and private agencies to provide more family-centered services. First, deliver information tailored for families in the settings they most frequently engage such as schools and churches, were deemed suitable for accessing family-centered health information [40]. Information should be communicated via quick, easy, and engaging methods.

Our second recommendation is centered on increasing public and private community engagement. Families frequently felt they did not qualify or need services being offered by the health department or nonprofit organizations because they believed services were for those of low socioeconomic status. We recommend that agencies partner with their local school districts to disseminate information through Parent-Teacher Associations, school nurses, newsletters, or emails to student guardians.

Our final recommendation is to provide training to health officials and employees centered on diverse family structures, backgrounds, ethnicities, and cultures, to create a more culturally sensitive environment [33]. By making health workers more aware of the needs of different family structures, agencies can create an environment in which families are more satisfied with their care and receive better coordination of information [41]. Developing and implementing cultural competence training allows community health workers and other staff to be more sensitive to cultural differences, religious beliefs, languages, and family contexts and ensures that minority groups feel safe and comfortable when seeking services [42].

Limitations

There are limitations to be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted in a county in Utah and may not be generalizable to other parts of the United States and globally. Second, while the participating families displayed diversity in their demographics, it is essential to note that participants did not encompass all possible family structures and contexts. Third, in all but three interviews, only one family member was present. Interviewees sometimes emphasized their individual perspectives. To have more family perspectives, future research should focus on interviewing multiple members of the same family.

Conclusion

This qualitative research uncovered insights into the challenges faced by families in accessing health information and services in the United States. Through this research, we clarified what information sources families typically use to collect health information, learned about family perceptions of local health agencies, and identified mechanisms through which health agencies can better provide services that reach all families. This study provides information for public and private health agencies to be better equipped to reach families and support them in the health challenges they face.
