Tea intake as a dietary factor for cardiovascular health: global estimates of potentially preventable ischemic heart disease
Authors: Longkun Liu, Mengmeng Zhu, Yiwen Li, Qian Xu, Yajie Cai, Xi Cheng, Yanfei Liu, Yue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTea intake (TI) has been associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease (IHD) through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory biological mechanisms, but a comprehensive global assessment of the IHD burden reduction potentially associated with TI is lacking. This study aimed to quantify the reductions in global IHD incidence and mortality associated with TI from 1990 to 2021, and explore heterogeneity in these associations across demographic subgroups and regions.MethodsWe conducted a comparative risk assessment analysis integrating data from the Global Dietary Database (GDD), IHD burden estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, and BMI-stratified population distributions from the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) across 180 countries and regions. TI distributions were modeled using gamma distributions, and BMI-stratified relative risks derived from dose-response meta-analyses were applied to estimate population attributable fractions. The analysis was stratified by sex, age, education level, and urbanicity. Proportional and absolute burden reductions (cases per million adults) were estimated, with uncertainty propagated through Monte Carlo simulation.ResultsGlobally in 2021, approximately 28.66 million (95% UI: 18.76–44.79) IHD incident cases and 4.42 (95% UI: 2.94–6.72) million IHD deaths were potentially attributable to TI, accounting for 15.49% (95% UI: 7.03%–36.85%) and 8.32% (95% UI: 4.60%–15.70%) of total cases and deaths, respectively. The beneficial association was greater in men and middle-aged/older adults, with no significant difference by education level or urbanicity. Regionally, the Middle East and North Africa showed the highest proportional reductions (incidence: 22.04%; mortality: 11.72%), followed by Southeast and East Asia. From 1990 to 2021, Southeast and East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa saw the largest increases in preventable IHD case proportions. At the national level, countries like Iran, Japan, and Kenya had the highest estimated proportional reductions, while China and Japan contributed the largest absolute reductions in preventable cases per million adults.ConclusionTI is potentially associated with the reduced global burden of IHD, with benefits varying by region and demographic characteristics. These findings underscore the potential of TI as a dietary strategy for cardiovascular disease prevention and can inform targeted policies worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01322-w.

Introduction

Emerging evidence suggests that tea intake (TI) may be associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease (IHD) [1, 2], potentially mediated through direct biological effects on vascular and metabolic pathways [3]. The rich polyphenol content of tea, notably catechins and theaflavins/thearubigins, exerts multifaceted biological effects. These compounds demonstrate potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which can enhance endothelial function by increasing nitric oxide bioavailability [4], reduce oxidation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles [5], inhibit platelet activation and aggregation [6], and promote modest blood pressure reduction [7]. Furthermore, TI is linked to beneficial metabolic effects, including improved insulin sensitivity [8] and favorable modulation of lipid profiles [9]. Collectively, these mechanisms can attenuate the initiation and progression of IHD [10]. Improvements in these cardiometabolic parameters subsequently contribute to a lower incidence of atherosclerotic events and IHD mortality [1, 11].

Further, Meta-analyses suggest tea consumption is associated with reduced CHD risk (RR = 0.89) [12], comparable to Mediterranean diet adherence [13], though more modest than smoking cessation (> 80%) [14] or high physical activity (RR = 0.62) [15]. As a low-calorie beverage consumed without added sweeteners, TI aligns with weight management and metabolic health [16]. These attributes distinguish tea from other risk factors: unlike physical activity or smoking cessation, which demand substantial behavioral change [14, 15], tea consumption is culturally embedded and requires minimal intervention; unlike complex dietary patterns, it is more standardized and measurable [17, 18]. While sugar-sweetened beverages have been robustly quantified as harmful [19], tea’s countervailing benefits remain unassessed globally.

Despite growing evidence supporting the cardiovascular benefits of tea, an updated and comprehensive assessment of the global reduction in IHD burden associated with TI is still lacking. And a systematic quantification of the global reduction in IHD burden associated with TI, stratified by these key sociodemographic factors and geographic regions, has yet to be conducted comprehensively [20].

We conducted this study that delves into the potential of tea as a nutritional factor to improve cardiovascular health [21]. We seek to estimate the reduction in IHD incidence and death attributable to TI globally, nationally, and sub-nationally. We will further analyze how this attributable burden reduction varies by key demographic and socioeconomic strata (e.g., age, sex, education level, urban/rural residence) and assess temporal trends across 1990 to 2021 in 180 countries and regions. This study will provide valuable insights into the countries and demographic groups most affected by TI-related reductions in IHD. Such evidence is essential for informing national, subnational, and multinational surveillance and policy actions aimed at reducing the burden of IHD and improving global cardiovascular health.

Methods

Temporal scope and analytical rationale

The 1990–2021 period for the included data was incorporated to satisfy three criteria: (i) The timeframe aligns with the period covered by Global Dietary Database (1990–2018), which captures critical shifts in global food and beverage consumption [19]; (ii) synchronization with GBD 2021 estimates ensure methodological harmonization in disease quantification [22]; and (iii) the 31-year span encompasses key demographic and epidemiological transitions—such as aging and urbanization—necessary for analyzing long-term trends in tea-related IHD burden [23].

For the selected variables, we stratified analyses by sex, age, education, and urbanicity based on established evidence that these factors modify both tea consumption patterns and IHD risk. These variables were selected as proxy indicators for differential exposure distributions and disease vulnerability rather than as individual-level confounders controlled in regression models, given the aggregate nature of CRA methods [19]. We exclude other factors like smoking, physical activity, or dietary patterns as stratification variables because: (i) These factors may lie on the causal pathway between tea consumption and IHD (e.g., tea drinkers may smoke less), making stratification inappropriate for effect estimation; (ii) Lack of harmonized individual-level data on these factors across all 180 countries and regions in GDD and GBD; and (iii) the CRA framework inherently addresses confounding through the theoretical minimum risk exposure approach rather than statistical adjustment [24, 25].

Exposure definition and distribution

TI was defined as any beverage with added tea products per 8 oz serving (cups per day, 1 cup = 8 oz) [18]. This metric exhibited pronounced geographical heterogeneity, ranging from 0.52 cups per day (95% uncertainty interval (UI) 0.31–0.91) in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) to 1.04 cups per day (95% UI 0.66–1.74) in Middle East and North Africa (MENA). At the national level—in the most populous countries—the gradient spanned almost two orders of magnitude, peaking at 2.56 (95% UI 2.13–3.06) in Japan, followed by 2.34 (95% UI 2.15–2.54) in Iran, 1.39 (95% UI 1.24–1.56) in Egypt, and 0.79 (95% UI 0.52–1.19) in Turkey, whereas Mexico, Korea, Brazil, and Italy clustered at the lower end, each with a mean consumption of 0.47–0.48 cups per day.

Data sources and inclusion

TI data for 180 countries and regions from 1990 to 2018, derived from individual-level surveys, were obtained from the Global Dietary Database (GDD) (https://www.globaldietarydatabase.org/data-download) [19]. The country-level TI data contain the following variables: education level, urban versus rural residence, age, sex, and year. From 1990 to 2021, estimated distributions of IHD incidence and mortality obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) [22] were incorporated for both sex, ages, educational levels, years, urbanicity, and all countries. Population BMI distribution data were downloaded from the website of the (non-communicable disease) NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) (https://ncdrisc.org/data-downloads.html) [26] for effect adjustment according to population weight status and for calculation of the BMI-mediated population attributable fraction (PAF). The sociodemographic development index (SDI) reflecting a nation’s development level from 1990 to 2021 [23], were downloaded and assessed for subsequent national-level investigations. Additionally, for per capita investigations, data on total population and adult population from 1990 to 2021 for all investigated countries were gathered from the United Nations Statistics Division, the World Bank Open Data, and the Eurostat database.

Data harmonization and preprocessing

We harmonized data using the following stratifiers: country, year, age, sex, education level, and urbanicity. Data integration synthesizes dietary exposure data from the above-mentioned GDD, IHD disease burden estimates from the GBD study, and BMI-stratified population distributions from the NCD-RisC database [19]. To extend TI estimates to 2021, we applied linear interpolation of median servings and 95% UI (lowerci_95_serving, upperci_95_serving) across all country-age-sex-education-urbanicity strata [27], following the GBD 2021 temporal gap-filling methodology [28].

Statistical parameterization of exposure

TI data are inherently non-negative and often follow a right-skewed distribution [29], with most individuals consuming low amounts and few consuming very high amounts. According to the GDD input data analysis, gamma distributions were proven more appropriate for modeling dietary intake (TI) data than normal distributions [30]. We modeled TI distributions using gamma distributions, which better fit right-skewed dietary data than normal distributions. Gamma parameters were derived from GDD-reported medians and 95% UIs: Shape (k) = (µ/σ)^2 and Rate (θ) = µ/σ^2, where µ = median serving and σ = standard deviation derived from 95% UIs: σ = (upperci_95_serving − lowerci_95_serving)/3.92, consistent with nutritional epidemiology methods and the previous GDD study [19, 28].

Effect modification by BMI

For calculating PAF in the subsequent section, we stratified relative-risks (RRs) of TI for IHD incidence and mortality according to populations’ BMI using the WHO standard cutoff of 25 kg/m², which distinguishes normal weight from overweight/obesity and has been consistently applied in previous GDD study methodology by Lara-Castor et al. [19]. This threshold is supported by evidence that adiposity modifies tea polyphenol bioavailability and efficacy: obesity-associated inflammation and insulin resistance may attenuate protective effects [31, 32]. Epidemiologically, the inverse association between TI and cardiovascular outcomes is stronger in leaner individuals [31, 33, 34]. While alternative cutoffs (e.g., 24 or 28 kg/m²) have been used in specific populations, the 25 kg/m² threshold optimizes global comparability and aligns with established metabolic risk stratification.

BMI-stratified RRs were derived from published meta-analyses investigating the association between TI and IHD across BMI categories. For BMI-stratified IHD mortality and incidence RRs, we applied them to 3 cups/day on average, which represents the regular population-relevant consumption level and optimal protective dose proposed by previous evidence [11, 35]. For BMI-stratified mortality, Chung et al. [1] reported an exponential dose-response association of RR = 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98) per 1 cup/day increment. The calculation used exponential scaling: 0.96³ = 0.885 ≈ 0.89. For IHD incidence, we synthesized evidence from multiple meta-analyses: Chung et al. [1] (1 cup/day: 0.98), Yi et al. [31] (3 cups/day: 0.73), Li et al. [33] (3 cups/day: 0.92), Zhang et al. [35] (3 cups/day: 0.73), and Wang et al. [36] (3 cups/day: 0.85). Taking a weighted average of these estimates and calibrating to the optimal exposure level: (0.98³ + 0.73 + 0.92 + 0.73 + 0.85)/5 ≈ 0.83. We incorporated evidence from a large prospective study with BMI-stratified TI-IHD data (n = 487,375) [33], which reported HR = 0.89 (0.84–0.94) for non-obese and HR = 0.94 (0.89–1.00) for obese participants. We derived the attenuation factor using the formula: (1 − 0.94)/(1 − 0.89) × 100% ≈ 54.5% for mortality [37], indicating the proportion of protective effect preserved in the BMI > 25 group. For incidence, we applied a direct absolute increment of + 0.05 (0.94 − 0.89), as the reference provides direct evidence with the observed risk difference for incidence. The final BMI > 25 RRs were 0.94 for mortality (0.89 + 0.11 × 54.5% ≈ 0.94) and 0.88 for incidence (0.83 + 0.05). See Table S1 for details.

The TI-attributable absolute burden per million adults was derived by scaling the national tally of TI-related cases to the contemporaneous adult population (aged ≥ 20 years), normalizing the quotient to a standard denominator of one million. The percentage reduction in IHD burden attributable to TI was estimated by summing the stratum-specific absolute IHD burdens attributable to TI and dividing this total by the overall IHD burden recorded in the GBD repository [19].

Model construction and simulation

The TI and IHD burdens were integrated within a rigorously specified CRA paradigm to quantify the reduction in IHD burden attributable to TI [19]. The CRA architecture estimates the impact of TI on IHD using age-standardized aetiologic effect [24], operating both directly and via adiposity-mediated pathways indexed by BMI [40]. The CRA framework integrates three independent data streams constructed with reference to a previous study [19, 24]: (i) exposure distributions from GDD, (ii) effect estimates from epidemiological studies, and (iii) disease burden from GBD. This avoids ecological fallacy by using individual-level RRs applied to population exposure distributions. A Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 random iterations propagated uncertainty through three primary dimensions: gamma-distributed TI exposure in each stratum, underlying IHD burden estimates in each stratum, and stratum-specific RR estimates for TI on IHD. Attributable burden was calculated as PAF × total disease burden, with UIs derived from 2.5th – 97.5th percentiles of simulation outputs. Each ensemble of stochastic draws was harnessed to derive PAF, which were then multiplied by their corresponding stratum-specific absolute disease burdens to yield the attributable caseload [19]. Results were stratified by countries, years, ages, sex, urbanicity, and education levels.

Parameter-specific burden attribution and temporal dynamics

After simulations, we aggregated findings by country, year, age, sex, urbanicity, and education to generate stratum-specific estimates. Across the 1990-to-2021 interval we quantified temporal change in both absolute and proportional disease burdens by computing, for every simulation, the paired difference (2021 estimate minus 1990 estimate). Encompassing incidence and mortality, estimates are summarized by the central tendency (posterior median) and 95% UIs delineated by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 multi-way probabilistic Monte-Carlo iterations. To ensure estimates reflect real-world demographic shifts relevant to policymakers, we eschewed formal age or sex standardization. Instead, we stratified analyses by age, sex, education, and region, thereby accounting for changing population structures while preserving interpretability. All computations were executed in the R statistical environment [41].

Results

Estimated global IHD burdens potentially preventable by TI

The estimated IHD burdens reductions associated with TI on the global level among the adult population (≥ 20 years) are shown in Fig. 1. Globally in 2021, an estimated 28.66 million (95% UI: 18.76–44.79) potentially preventable IHD incident cases and an estimated reduction of 4.42 million (95% UI: 2.94–6.72) IHD deaths were associated with TI, corresponding to 15.49% (95% UI: 7.03%–36.85%) and 8.32% (95% UI: 4.60%–15.70%) of total IHD incident cases and IHD deaths, respectively. In 1990, an estimated 13.67 million (95% UI: 9.56–20.14) potentially preventable IHD incident cases and 3.41 million (95% UI: 2.45–4.84) estimated IHD deaths reductions were associated with TI globally, corresponding to 11.94% (95% UI: 5.84%–26.29%) and 7.04% (95% UI: 4.53%–11.66%) of total IHD incident cases and IHD deaths, respectively. In 1990, the reduced burden of IHD incidence and IHD death estimated to be associated with TI was 4,099 per million adults (95% UI: 2,876–6,039) and 1,022 per million adults (95% UI: 736–1,450), respectively. In 2021, the reduced burden of IHD incidence and IHD death estimated to be associated with TI was 5,189 per million adults (95% UI: 3,397–8,110) and 800 per million adults (95% UI: 531–1,216), respectively.

Heterogeneity in estimates of IHD burden associated with TI across age, sex, education, and urbanicity

Globally, TI was potentially associated with greater reductions in IHD incident cases in men (2,867 per million adults (95% UI: 1,917–4,394)) than women (2,322 per million adults (95% UI: 1,480–3,716)), and more IHD deaths in men (441 per million adults (95% UI: 299–658)) than women (359 per million adults (95% UI: 232–558)). However, estimated proportional reductions in IHD incidence and mortality burden did not differ significantly by sex (incidence: men 15.83%, 95% UI: 6.97%–38.73%; women: 15.23%, 95% UI: 7.08%–35.39%; mortality: men 8.42%, 95% UI: 4.51%–16.56%; women: 8.25%, 95% UI: 4.66%–15.03%). Education-level differences in estimated IHD burden reductions potentially associated with TI were minimal. For low education, incidence reductions were 1,734 (95% UI: 1,131–2,714) cases per million adults and 15.49% (95% UI: 7.01%–36.90%) of total burden; mortality reductions were 266 (95% UI: 176–406) per million and 8.30% (95% UI: 4.56%–15.70%). For medium education, corresponding estimates were 1,735 (95% UI: 1,140–2,709) cases and 15.52% (95% UI: 7.07%–36.87%) for incidence, and 267 (95% UI: 178–405) and 8.32% (95% UI: 4.61%–15.68%) for mortality. For high education, estimates were 1,720 (95% UI: 1,126–2,688) and 15.46% (95% UI: 7.02%–36.76%) for incidence, and 267 (95% UI: 178–406) and 8.34% (95% UI: 4.62%–15.71%) for mortality. For urbanicity, TI was estimated to be potentially associated with more reduced IHD incident cases in urban areas (2,613 per million adults (95% UI: 1,716–4,076)) than in rural areas (2,576 per million adults (95% UI: 1,680–4,034)), while no great difference was found for estimated IHD mortality reductions associated with TI in rural areas (400 (400.48) per million adults (95% UI: 266–608)) and in urban areas (400 (399.74) per million adults (95% UI: 265–608)). However, estimated proportional reductions in IHD incidence and mortality burden were similar across urbanicity: (in rural areas: 15.43%, 95% UI: 6.98%–36.76%; in urban areas: 15.55%, 95% UI: 7.08%–36.93%) and death (in rural areas: 8.33%, 95% UI: 4.60%–15.70%; in urban areas: 8.31%, 95% UI: 4.59%–15.70%). See the details in Fig. 3. Estimated incidence reductions per million adults increased with age, peaking at 673 (95% UI: 432–1,061) for ages 65–69, declining to 502 (95% UI: 304–833) for ages 80–84, and then rising again to 584 (95% UI: 382–899) for ages ≥ 85. Estimated mortality reductions associated with TI showed a monotonic increase with age, reaching 198 (95% UI: 131–300) per million for ages ≥ 85. Proportional burden estimates showed no clear age trends; the highest proportions were observed at ages 50–54 for both incidence (15.93%, 95% UI: 7.84%–34.93%) and mortality (8.46%, 95% UI: 4.78%–15.65%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across regions. The MENA showed the highest estimated proportional reductions associated with TI (incidence: 22.04%, 95% UI: 11.16%–47.03%; mortality: 11.72%, 95% UI: 6.83%–21.50%). Southeast and East Asia (SEA) showed the highest estimated absolute reductions (incidence: 1,836 per million, 95% UI: 931–3,419; mortality: 302 per million, 95% UI: 168–520). Age and regional patterns in estimated IHD burden reductions associated with TI

Estimated proportional reductions in IHD incidence burden associated with TI showed a positive age gradient across most regions (Fig. 4A). This pattern was most pronounced in SEA, MENA, and at the global level. Estimated proportional reductions were highest among adults aged > 85 years: 20.77% (95% UI: 8.67%–51.84%) in SEA, 24.67% (95% UI: 14.13%–44.94%) in MENA, and 15.76% (95% UI: 7.57%–34.20%) in the World. For mortality, similar positive age gradients were observed in SEA, MENA, and globally, but not in Central and Eastern Europe and Central Asia (CEECA), LAC, and Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Highest estimated proportional reductions associated with TI were: 11.25% (95% UI: 5.57%–23.28%) for ages > 85 in SEA, 12.09% (95% UI: 7.12%–21.96%) for ages 80–84 in MENA, and 8.46% (95% UI: 4.78%–15.65%) for ages 50–54 in the World. In LAC and high-income countries (HIC), age gradients were generally flat. Notably, in SSA and CEECA, estimated proportional reductions showed an inverse age gradient, with highest estimates among younger adults (ages 25–29 in SSA): 9.35% (95% UI: 3.88%–26.32%) and (ages 20–24 in CEECA) 9.47% (95% UI: 4.02%–22.18%), respectively. Comparable age profiles were observed for estimated incidence and mortality burden reductions associated with TI.

Regional, urbanicity, and educational patterns in estimated IHD burden reductions associated with TI

Estimated IHD burden reductions associated with TI showed marked heterogeneity across education, urbanicity, and region (Fig. 5). In MENA, the highest estimated proportional reductions were observed among low-educated adults: 22.22% (95% UI: 11.54%–46.41%) in urban areas for incidence, and 21.99% (95% UI: 11.03%–47.30%) in rural areas for incidence. These were followed by comparable estimates in SEA: 18.72% (95% UI: 6.45%–54.89%) and 18.47% (95% UI: 6.33%–53.49%) among low-educated adults in urban and rural areas, respectively. For mortality, a contrasting pattern emerged in MENA: highest estimated reductions were among highly educated adults—11.64% (95% UI: 6.78%–21.35%) in urban and 12.42% (95% UI: 7.20%–22.82%) in rural areas—followed by low-educated adults in SEA (urban: 10.33%, 4.62%–22.70%; rural: 10.19%, 4.54%–22.72%). For the proportional IHD incidence reductions associated with TI, except LAC, MENA, SEA, and SSA, dwellers in HIC and South Asia (SA) exhibited a positive correlation with education level, while in CEECA populations receiving lower education levels exhibited more salient proportional IHD incidence reductions associated with TI. For proportional IHD mortality reductions associated with TI, in HIC, MENA, SA, and SSA, dwellers possessing higher education levels exhibited more pronounced estimated proportional reductions, while inverse patterns appeared in CEECA.

National SDI and estimated IHD burden reductions associated with TI in 1990 and 2021

To examine relationships between national development and estimated IHD burden reductions, we stratified analyses by the SDI, a composite indicator of national developmental status integrating the fertility rate among women < 25 years, mean educational attainment in the population ≥ 15 years, and per capita income [23]. In both 1990 and 2021, SDI exhibited an underlying association with the population-attributable fractions of IHD death burdens associated with TI with statistical significance using the Spearman correlation test (R = -0.17, p = 0.023) in 1990 and (R = -0.17, p = 0.026) in 2021. In general, nations occupying lower SDI quintiles bore disproportionately higher estimated IHD death proportions in both 1990 and 2021, underscoring a widening developmental inequity in diet-related cardiometabolic risk. However, among the observed nations, no statistical significance was found in the correlation of IHD incidence proportions with SDI (R = -0.1, p = 0.17) in 1990 and (R = -0.099, p = 0.18) in 2021 (Figure S4) (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This article furnishes contemporaneous, globally comprehensive quantifications of the cardiometabolic incidence and morbidity potentially preventable by TI, disaggregated by age stratums, biological sex, educational levels, and urbanicity [39]. Globally, substantial heterogeneity was evident across temporal trajectories, world regions and demographic subgroups.

Estimated proportions of IHD incidence and mortality burden potentially preventable by TI were highest in MENA. Estimated IHD deaths and incident cases per million adults potentially preventable by TI were highest in SEA followed by SA. These regions are major consumers of both black tea and green tea. This pattern suggests that, at the population level, higher TI within the observed exposure range is potentially associated with a greater alleviation of IHD burden. However, as our analysis relies on relative risks from observational studies, residual confounding by lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, physical activity, socioeconomic status) cannot be excluded. Tea drinkers may differ systematically from non-drinkers in these residual confounding, potentially leading to overestimation of tea’s potential protective effect. Moreover, we assumed zero TI as the theoretical minimum risk exposure, consistent with CRA methodology. However, this is a modelling simplification—the true minimum-risk level may be more complicated. This assumption may influence the magnitude of the estimated burden reduction and should be interpreted as the effect of complete tea absence rather than suboptimal versus optimal intake. Additionally, increases in absolute estimated reduced IHD cases from 1990 to 2021 may largely reflect population growth and aging rather than changes in TI alone. The total IHD burden increased substantially in most regions due to demographic transition, mechanically raising preventable cases even with stable TI. The proportion of burden preventable by tea is more informative than absolute case counts, as it is less influenced by demographic shifts and provides context for interpreting the magnitude of estimated benefits relative to total disease burden.

The potential cardiovascular benefits of tea were estimated to be stronger among rural residents [3, 33]. A plausible explanation is that urban populations often add milk to tea, which may negate vascular benefits [4, 33], and their higher smoking prevalence and BMI elevate baseline risk, diluting the relative effect [3]. In this study, rural areas of MENA showed the largest estimated mortality reductions, and rural SSA exhibited the largest increase in estimated incidence reductions relative to urban areas. An inconsistent trend (urban populations gained larger benefits in mortality reductions) was observed for rural IHD mortality in SSA, where countries are at earlier stages of development and rural populations face higher and more variable mortality because of limited preventive and therapeutic services [42]. No clear or consistent patterns emerged across educational strata, underscoring the need for further investigation.

Among the age groups examined, we observed a trend whereby greater reductions in incidence and mortality per million adults were associated with older age, consistent with previous studies reporting an inverse association between TI and IHD in subgroups aged > 65 years [2, 33]. A possible explanation is that age-related amplification of benefit stems from older individuals experiencing heightened oxidative stress [43], more pronounced telomere attrition [43], exhaustion of endothelial progenitor cells [10], and chronic low-grade inflammation [44]—conditions that catechins and other tea bio-actives may counteract in the older population, potentially contributing to larger observed associations with reduced cardiovascular risk in this population. Additionally, estimated IHD burden reductions associated with were larger among males, which may reflect the fact that, in women, the pathogenesis and fatal pathways of IHD are markedly more complex. Beyond classical atherosclerosis, these pathways are substantially modulated by hormonal and endocrine-metabolic factors [45], including fluctuations in estrogen [46], an increased androgen/estrogen ratio [46], post-menopausal redistribution of adipose tissue [47], coronary microvascular dysfunction [48], and pregnancy-related endocrine events—all of which can precipitate or exacerbate ischemic cardiac events and increase mortality risk.

The largest estimated IHD incidence and mortality burden reductions associated with TI were concentrated in MENA and SEA countries (e.g., Iran, Japan, Morocco, Lebanon) and Kenya, all of which are characterized by high and long-standing TI. In Iran, an estimated 2 incident IHD cases per 100 adults were potentially preventable by TI, while in Belarus an estimated 3 deaths per 1,000 adults were potentially preventable by TI. Compared with the previous pooled meta-analytic estimates (RR: 0.92; 95% UI: 0.85,0.99) [2] and death (RR: 0.67; 95% UI: 0.46,0.96) [31], our simulated effect sizes are more conservative, avoiding potential publication bias and subjective weighting inherent in conventional meta-analyses [18]. Additionally, Japan, China and India—major SEA countries with long-established TI—showed the largest estimated reductions worldwide. Although per capita decreases were modest, large number of total incidence cases (8.14 million adults (95% UI: 3.57–16.26)) and death (1.22 million adults (95% UI: 0.56–2.30)) potentially preventable by TI were observed in China closely followed by India (6.36 million adults (95% UI: 5.27–7.61)) and death (0.87 million adults (95% UI: 0.72–1.05)) (See Figure S5). Marked increases in the proportion of potentially preventable burden were also observed in China, India and Japan—countries with abundant TI—and across major green- and black-tea regions (SEA, SA and CEECA) [34], whereas no noticeable benefits were detected in low-tea-consuming countries such as Germany, Spain and the US, or in regions including HIC and LAC. This suggests that promoting TI in these areas could yield substantial potential reductions in IHD burden. In the UK and Turkey, despite high tea availability [34], significant reductions were not observed, indicating that the potential cardiovascular benefit associated with TI may be context-specific and warrants further investigation.

Apart from the potential TI-associated reductions in IHD incidence, we observed that the estimated inverse association between TI and IHD burden was attenuated at higher SDI levels. One explanation is that in HICs, increased unhealthy dietary components offset tea’s benefits, reducing preventable IHD burden. Conversely, in low-income areas, adding healthy foods achieves greater absolute risk reduction due to low baseline intake and high population attributable risk [49]. Another explanation is that HIC residents already adopt health-promoting strategies [50], attenuating tea’s additional benefit. Nevertheless, HICs with entrenched tea cultures (e.g., Japan) still show substantial reductions, indicating that promoting tea in affluent settings remains worthwhile.

Our analysis assumes a positive correlation between TI and IHD risk, consistent with meta-analytic evidence [1, 2]. However, we cannot infer the minimum consumption duration required for potential benefit. Cohort studies suggest cardioprotective associations emerge with sustained TI over years to decades [3, 33], but these timeframes cannot be validated in our cross-sectional data. The optimal intake level also remains uncertain, given that we compared the difference between TI and theoretical minimum intake rather than stratifying by dose. Some observational evidence indicates potential threshold effects (≥ 3 cup/day) for benefit [51] and possible U-shaped relationships at high intakes (≥ 8 cups/day) [52], requiring further verification. And this inconsistency between model’s assumed log-linear relationship and evidence of a possible U-shaped relationship should be acknowledged as a limitation of the modelling approach. These uncertainties underscore that our estimates represent theoretical population-level associations, rather than evidence-based recommendations for individual consumption.

Finally, although a heterogeneous patchwork of fiscal and regulatory levers aimed at promoting TI availability currently exists, the magnitude of decreased TI-attributable harm delineated herein indicates that present policy coverage remains grossly insufficient and requires further refinement. We found that the highest PAFs for IHD in 2021 and steepest estimated reduced burdens over 1990–2021 were concentrated in traditional tea-consuming regions, suggesting high TI may offer greater potential benefits. Estimated inverse associations between TI and IHD burden were larger in older populations, males, and residents of lower-SDI countries. The above characteristics can serve as useful references for clinicians and policymakers in implementing targeted dietary interventions.

Conclusion

In aggregate, this investigation furnishes a granular, geospatially resolved assessment of the cardiovascular morbidity and mortality reductions potentially associated with TI, explicitly disaggregated by sociodemographic strata and temporal dynamics. An inverse association between IHD burdens and TI was observed. By delineating the nations and demographic subgroups harboring the greatest preventable fractions, this evidence base can be leveraged to calibrate context-specific, equity-oriented policy formulations and programmatic investments, thereby informing future evaluation of TI as part of dietary strategies for cardiovascular disease risk reduction.

Limitations

As a secondary analysis of aggregate data, we cannot establish individual-level causation or adjust for some unmeasured residual confounders (smoking, physical activity, and dietary patterns). Our estimates represent only theoretical population-level associations rather than evidence-based recommendations for individual behavior change.
