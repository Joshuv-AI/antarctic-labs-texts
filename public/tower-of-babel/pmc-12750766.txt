Plastome diversity and phylogenetic insights among modern Egyptian wheat cultivars: Genome-Wide and Gene-Level perspectives
Authors: Abeer Al-Andal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents a genome-wide and gene-level characterization of plastome diversity and phylogenetic relationships among ten modern Egyptian wheat cultivars, representing both hexaploid and tetraploid lineages. Raw sequencing data for all cultivars were retrieved from NCBI BioProject PRJNA1290394, and plastome assemblies were generated using the published wheat chloroplast genome (GenBank accession MW889057.1) as the seed reference under default settings. Comprehensive plastome sequencing revealed an exceptionally conserved genome structure and gene content across cultivars, yet identified distinct mutation hotspots, with the rps2 gene exhibiting the highest single-gene SNP burden (up to 21 SNPs in cultivar Sahel 1). SNP profiling demonstrated clear differentiation between genetically divergent cultivars such as Sahel 1 and GMZ9, and documented variation in overall mutational load—ranging from 7 to 74 SNPs per cultivar—mirroring phylogenetic clade structure. Chloroplast heteroplasmy analysis uncovered cultivar-specific genome stability patterns, informing breeding decisions and highlighting cytoplasmic diversity. SSR motif analysis confirmed predominant AT-rich homopolymers and subtle motif-length differences distinguishing tetraploid and hexaploid genotypes. Codon usage analysis showed a highly conserved AT-bias and effective translational optimization across ploidy levels. Phylogenetic reconstruction using complete plastome sequences resolved finer relationships than gene-based approaches, while matK emerged as a superior biomarker for distinguishing Poaceae lineages. mVISTA comparison revealed conserved plastome structure with divergence hotspots at atpH–atpI and atpA regions, underscoring their value as potential molecular markers for phylogenetic and barcoding applications. Taken together, these findings provide deep insights into plastome-driven genetic diversity, robust cultivar identification, and the evolutionary context for Egyptian wheat breeding and conservation programs. Background

Triticum aestivum, or bread wheat (AABBDD genome, 2n = 6x = 42 chromosomes), is the world’s most important wheat species, accounting for 95% of global wheat production [1]. This hexaploid species evolved through two successive hybridization events: first between Triticum urartu (AA genome, 2n = 2x = 14 chromosomes) and a species related to Aegilops speltoides (ancestral BB genome species, 2n = 2x = 14 chromosomes) to form tetraploid wheat around 800,000 years ago [1], followed by hybridization with Aegilops tauschii (DD genome, 2n = 2x = 14 chromosomes) approximately 8,000–9,000 years ago to create modern bread wheat [1]. Its unique ability to form viscoelastic dough makes hexaploid wheat ideal for bread production, and it represents about 30% of all cereal grain consumed worldwide [1, 2]. Triticum turgidum (durum wheat) is a tetraploid species (AABB genome, 2n = 4x = 28 chromosomes) that serves as the evolutionary intermediate between diploid ancestors and hexaploid bread wheat [2, 3]. This species, particularly the durum subspecies, is the second most important wheat commercially and is primarily used for pasta production due to its hard grain characteristics [3]. The evolutionary relationships involve Triticum urartu as the AA genome donor [4, 5], wild relatives like Aegilops speltoides - contributing to the BB genome lineage [6, 7].

Other relative species include Triticum monococcum (einkorn) [8–10] - contributing to the AA genome lineage and the first domesticated wheat around 10,000 years ago [10], Triticum timopheevii [11] - representing an independent tetraploid lineage with AAGG genome, and Triticum zhukovskyi [12] - a hexaploid (GGAuAuAmAm) arising from hybridization between T. timopheevii (GGAuAu) and T. monococcum (AmAm). Secale cereale (rye), contributing to the RR genome lineage [13], diverged from wheat approximately seven million years ago [14] within the Triticeae tribe, while, Triticum macha represents a traditional hexaploid wheat variety historically grown in the Caucasus region [15]. These wild relatives and ancient species continue to serve as crucial genetic resources for modern wheat improvement programs [1, 16]. Molecular marker studies provide quantitative measures showing genetic similarity among wheat genotypes typically ranges from 0.27 to 0.92, with average genetic distance among wheat accessions approximately 0.623 [17]. Specific relationships show that Triticum aestivum and T. turgidum have a genetic similarity of 0.86, while among tetraploid species, T. durum and T. turgidum show 0.86 genetic similarity [18]. The three subgenomes of bread wheat demonstrate differential genetic distances, with the A genome showing 8.2% non-shared sequence between diploid and hexaploid forms, the B genome showing 0% non-shared sequence between tetraploid and hexaploid forms, and the D genome showing 18.1% non-shared sequence between Aegilops tauschii and hexaploid wheat [17].

Plastomes, or chloroplast genomes, have emerged as indispensable molecular tools in modern plant genomics and molecular profiling due to their unique structural characteristics and evolutionary properties across diverse plant taxa [19–21]. The significance of plastomes extends far beyond their primary role in photosynthesis, establishing them as powerful resources for plant breeding applications [22, 23]. The multi-copy nature of plastomes, with hundreds to thousands of copies per cell, enhances the reliability of molecular biomarkers by reducing the impact of PCR bias and providing sufficient template for analysis even from degraded samples [24, 25]. Additionally, the conserved gene content and organization of plastomes across related species facilitates comparative studies and the development of universal primer sets that can be applied across broad taxonomic groups [24, 26, 27]. The power of plastome biomarkers lies in their ability to detect subtle genetic differences that may be overlooked by traditional nuclear markers, particularly in populations with recent divergence or ongoing gene flow [28, 29]. Recent studies have demonstrated that plastome-derived biomarkers can reveal population structure and genetic differentiation patterns that are often more informative than nuclear markers, especially in cases where nuclear gene flow has homogenized population structure (28, 29). Furthermore, the high density of polymorphic sites in specific plastome regions, such as the intergenic spacer trnL_UAG-ndhF, likely provides exceptional resolution for a number of population genetic studies [28]. The moderate evolutionary rates of plastomes strike an optimal balance for population genetic studies, evolving slowly enough to provide stable markers for phylogenetic reconstruction while accumulating sufficient variation to detect recent evolutionary events [30, 31]. This evolutionary rate, typically 1–3 times slower than nuclear genes, makes plastome biomarkers particularly suitable for studies spanning intermediate time scales, from intraspecific phylogeography to genus-level systematics [24, 30, 31].

The current investigation focuses on characterizing the plastome architectures of contemporary hexaploid and tetraploid wheat cultivars cultivated in Egypt, and leveraging this information to assess genetic relationships among these domesticated wheat varieties and their closely allied genera and species. Additionally, the study seeks to quantify genetic divergence among various Egyptian wheat cultivars at both the whole-genome and single gene scales, identify their unique DNA barcodes, and appraise the effectiveness of the three principal plastid gene biomarkers—matK, rbcL, and trnL—for resolving phylogenetic relationships within the Poaceae family.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and quality control

Raw sequencing data for ten Egyptian wheat cultivars—including nine bread wheat (Triticum aestivum, 2n = 6× = 42, AABBDD) and one durum wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum, 2n = 4× = 28, AABB) cultivar as detailed in Table S1—were obtained from the NCBI BioProject PRJNA1290394 database. The numerical codes [1–10] correspond to cultivars Giza 168 (GZ168), Misr 2 (MSR2), Sakha 95 (SKH95), Gemiza 10 (GMZ10), Sahel 1, Shandweel 1 (SWL1), Sakha 94 (SKH94), Gemiza 9 (GMZ9), BeniSweif 4 (BSF4), and Sids 13 (SDS13), respectively. The accession numbers assigned to cultivars coded 1 through 10 in the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) are as follows: SAMN49918895, SAMN49919007, SAMN49918931, SAMN49918924, SAMN49919079, SAMN49918905, SAMN49918933, SAMN49919009, SAMN49919012, and SAMN49919008, respectively. These raw sequencing reads underwent quality assessment using FastQC (v0.11.9) [32].

Plastome assembly and annotation

The plastomes of these cultivars were assembled using NOVOPlasty (v4.3.5) [33], employing the published wheat chloroplast genome (GenBank accession no. MW889057.1) as the seed reference with default parameters and a k-mer size of 39. Assembly completeness and circularity were verified through visual inspection and coverage analysis. Annotation of the assembled plastomes was conducted with the GeSeq web server [34] (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html), enabling comprehensive identification of gene features including 79–80 protein-coding genes, 37 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes [35]. The annotation process utilized the Embryophyta chloroplast reference database with manual curation to correct potential annotation errors and circular genome maps were generated using OGDRAW (v1.3.1) [36].

Sequence alignment and submission

The annotated plastome sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.489) [37] with the L-INS-i algorithm optimized for accurate alignment of closely related sequences, employing the parameters --localpair --maxiterate 1000. The finalized plastome assemblies for the ten cultivars were submitted to NCBI GenBank under accession numbers PX367300, PX367302 through PX367309, and PX367301, corresponding to cultivar codes 1–10 as listed in Table S1.

Variant detection and functional annotation

To assess genomic variation among cultivars, quality-filtered sequencing reads were mapped to the reference plastome (accession no. SRR1170670, hexaploid wheat) using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) [38] with standard parameters, yielding mean genome-wide coverage depths ranging from 95× to 140× per cultivar. The raw dataset for this reference plastome was assembled de novo using the same NOVOPlasty pipeline to ensure consistency and address potential inconsistencies in previously published wheat plastome sequences. Additionally, de novo assembly of the plastome from ERR11450770 (tetraploid wheat) was performed to represent the modern Triticum turgidum lineage in comparative analyses.

Variant identification was carried out using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.2.6) [39] HaplotypeCaller module, allowing for the detection of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels). Variants were filtered using GATK’s VariantFiltration with the following criteria: quality by depth (QD) < 2.0, Fisher strand bias (FS) >60.0, mapping quality (MQ) < 40.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0, and depth of coverage (DP) ≥ 10× per site and ≤ mean + 3 × SD. The identified variants were classified according to their genomic context (coding versus non-coding regions, synonymous versus non-synonymous substitutions), and their potential functional impacts were annotated and predicted using SnpEff (v5.1d) [40]. SnpEff was invoked with default annotation stringency, and all output annotations were cross-validated against reference gene predictions from GeSeq (34).

Phylogenetic reconstruction

Comprehensive whole-plastome phylogenetic analysis was conducted to evaluate genetic divergence among the ten Egyptian wheat cultivars and the two de novo assembled reference plastomes (SRR1170670 and ERR11450770). The identification of both coding and noncoding chloroplast sequences exhibiting interspecific variation within the family Burseraceae was accomplished through the Limited Area Global Alignment of Nucleotides (LAGAN) algorithm, implemented within the Multiple Visual Interactive Sequence Alignment (mVISTA) platform. This analytical framework is particularly advantageous for large-scale comparative genomic assessments, owing to its capacity to align extensive plastid sequences while preserving high-resolution detection of divergent loci [41, 42]. LAGAN employs a progressive alignment framework wherein sequence validation is inherently achieved through a multistage process. Initially, local alignments between pairs of sequences are generated to identify conserved regions or anchors. These local alignments are subsequently organized into an ordered chain, forming a preliminary global map that delineates the relative arrangement of homologous blocks. Finally, a refined global alignment is computed within confined regions surrounding this map, thereby optimizing accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency [43, 44]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method [45] implemented in IQ-TREE with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates for statistical support assessment [46].

Codon usage analysis

Codon usage frequencies within the coding sequences (CDS) of the ten plastomes were calculated using the Sequence Manipulation Suite (SMS v2.0) [47] accessible at https://www.bioinformatics.org/sms2/. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were computed for all protein-coding genes, and codon usage bias was assessed using the effective number of codons (ENC) calculated with CodonW (v1.4.2) [48]. Correspondence analysis of RSCU values was performed using R software (v4.3.1) [49] with the FactoMineR package [50].

Microsatellite and repeat analysis

MegaSSR (v1.0) [51] was utilized to detect simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the plastome sequences with the following parameters: minimum repeat unit length of 1–6 nucleotides, minimum repeat number of 10 for mononucleotides, 6 for dinucleotides, and 5 for tri- to hexanucleotides. This analysis supported the identification of potential molecular markers for population genetic studies and cultivar discrimination.

Heteroplasmy analysis

Structural heteroplasmy was evaluated [52] to enable the detection of multiple chloroplast genome configurations within individual plants across all ten cultivars [53]. Sequencing reads were mapped to various predicted structural haplotypes using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [54, 55], and variant allele frequencies (VAFs) were calculated using custom Python scripts to quantify heteroplasmy levels at both population and individual scales [56]. Heteroplasmy was quantified using Cp-hap logic [52] and computed VAF at each site.

Gene-Level phylogenetic and barcode analysis

For gene-level phylogenetic analysis, three widely-used chloroplast marker genes (matK, rbcL, and trnL) were extracted from the assembled plastomes. DNA sequences of these three genes were trimmed and filtered to generate 795, 637, and 589 bp, respectively, aligned using MAFFT with the L-INS-i algorithm) [37], and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using both maximum likelihood (IQ-TREE) and neighbor-joining (MEGA X v10.2.4) [57] methods with appropriate bootstrap replicates. The analysis included related species within the Poaceae family obtained from GenBank to assess phylogenetic resolution at different taxonomic levels. DNA barcode diagrams for the ten wheat cultivars were generated using manually generated in PowerPoint, providing graphical representations of the unique molecular signatures for each cultivar based on the individual and possible concatenated matK-rbcL-trnL gene sequences.

Geographic Distribution, ploidy Levels, and pedigree complexity

The dataset in Table S1 comprised ten Egyptian wheat cultivars (codes 1–10) that are distributed across three geographic regions, with six cultivars being found in the Delta region (GZ168, SKH95, GMZ10, SKH94, GMZ9, SDS13), two being located in Sinai (MSR2, Sahel 1), and two being situated in Upper Egypt (SWL1, BSF4). Nine cultivars are classified as hexaploid bread wheat (Triticum aestivum), while BSF4 is identified as tetraploid (T. turgidum), with complete pedigree information being available for all except SKH95. The pedigrees were shown to possess common parent lines including KAUZ (which is found in MSR2, SKH94, and SDS13), MRL and BUC (both of which are present in GZ168 and SWL1), with breeding complexity being observed to range from simple crosses like MSR2 (KAUZ"S”//BAV92) to highly complex multi-generational crosses such as GMZ10 (MAYA74"S”/ON//1160 − 147/3/BB/GLL/4/CHAT"S”/5/CROW"S”).

Cultivar-Specific patterns of Chloroplast heteroplasmy and genome stability

Based on the analysis of chloroplast heteroplasmy via the calculation of variant allele frequencies (VAF) in the ten wheat cultivars, the data revealed significant variation in heteroplasmy rates across cultivars, indicating differences in chloroplast genome stability (Figure S9). Cultivars SKH95, SKH94, and BSF4 showed high heteroplasmy with VAF peaks 30–40% across multiple genomic positions (e.g., ~ 17,000 bp; 32,000–35,000 bp), which indicates substantial intra-cultivar variation in chloroplast structure, while cultivars GZ168, GMZ10, and SDS13 exhibited low heteroplasmy with VAF peaks < 10%, suggesting stable, homogeneous chloroplast genomes. Cultivars MSR2, SWL1, and GMZ9 showed moderate heteroplasmy and displayed sporadic VAF (10–20%) at isolated positions (e.g., ~ 48,000 bp; 70,000 bp).

Interestingly, cultivar with Sahel 1 (code 5) showed no detectable heteroplasmy. These results are indicative of cultivar-specific evolutionary trajectories at the level of chloroplast genome stability. Such stratification underscored the influence of genetic background on chloroplast heteroplasmy dynamics in durum and bread wheats.

Evaluating plastome biomarkers for taxonomic resolution: MatK as the key biomarker in Poaceae

Discussion

Plastome-Derived molecular biomarkers for phylogeographic studies

The foundational significance of plastomes in molecular profiling stems from their highly conserved quadripartite structure, which consists of a large single-copy region (LSC), a small single-copy region (SSC), and two inverted repeat (IR) regions [21, 58]. This structural conservation, exemplified in the Egyptian wheat cultivars examined in this study, provides a stable genomic framework that facilitates comparative analyses across different taxonomic levels. In the present study, plastomes of the ten Egyptian wheat cultivars demonstrated remarkable conservation in gene content and organization, ranging from 135,895 bp (Sahel 1) to 135,909 bp (SWL1, BSF4), with almost identical gene complements across all cultivars (Figs. 4 and 5, S1-S8). This conservation enables researchers to conduct meaningful comparative studies while detecting subtle but phylogenetically informative variations. The architectural organization of the wheat chloroplast genome is comprehensively detailed in Table 1, which reveals the presence of the pbf1 (photosystem biogenesis factor 1) gene—formerly designated psbN—a plastome-encoded locus responsible for producing a diminutive thylakoid membrane protein critical for the assembly and stabilization of Photosystem II and, to a lesser extent, Photosystem I [59]. Additionally, contemporary chloroplast genomic nomenclature recognizes the reclassification of the ycf3 gene as pafI, an intron-bearing locus universally conserved throughout angiosperm plastomes [60]. Notably, the structural inventory also underscores the conspicuous absence of the accD gene, which encodes the β-carboxyltransferase subunit of the plastidial acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase) heterotetrameric complex and has undergone endosymbiotic gene transfer to the nuclear genome during monocotyledonous evolution [61, 62]. The functional essentiality of accD is exemplified by its indispensability in species such as tobacco and Arabidopsis, where plastome accD knockout confers lethality unless functionally compensated by nuclear-encoded orthologs, thereby demonstrating the phylogenetic divergence in genomic compartmentalization of this metabolic enzyme across plant lineages [63].

Plastomes also serve as sensitive indicators of genetic variation through multiple types of molecular markers, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertion-deletion events (indels), and simple sequence repeats (SSRs) [19, 64]. These markers offer advantages of uniparental inheritance, multi-copy nature, and moderate evolutionary rates that make them particularly suitable for maternal lineage tracing and population structure analyses [65]. The comprehensive SNP analysis in the Egyptian wheat cultivars revealed some gene-specific mutation hotspots, with rps2 emerging as the predominant mutational target harboring 33 total SNPs across all cultivars (Figs. 2). This finding aligns with broader patterns observed in plant plastomes, where certain genes consistently serve as mutation hotspots across diverse taxa, with rps2 being particularly notable for its susceptibility to mutations in various species [66, 67]. This fine-scale resolution of genetic variation enables precise characterization of genetic diversity within and between populations, providing crucial information for conservation genetics and breeding programs.

The comprehensive suite of molecular markers available from plastomes extends beyond these primary categories to include SSRs, dispersed repeats, and hypervariable regions, each offering unique advantages for different analytical purposes [27, 64, 68]. These types of molecular markers serve as powerful tools for population genetics and phylogeographic studies [25, 64]. The distribution of SSRs in plastomes follows consistent patterns across plant families, with mononucleotide repeats being the most abundant type, followed by dinucleotide and higher-order repeats [25, 64, 68]. These markers are particularly valuable because they combine the advantages of high polymorphism with the stability conferred by the non-recombining nature of chloroplast genomes [24, 25]. The Egyptian wheat study identified 14 different SSR motifs, predominantly A/T homopolymers, with specific length variations that differentiate cultivars and ploidy levels (Fig. 6, Tables S12-S21). The prevalence of A/T homopolymers in plastome SSRs appears to be a universal feature across plant taxa, likely reflecting the AT-rich nature of chloroplast genomes and the propensity for slippage events during replication in these regions [25, 64, 68].

Evolutionary conservation of codon usage patterns across wheat ploidy levels

Figures 7 and 8 present comprehensive relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), normalized per 1000 codons, analyses for hexaploid bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) and tetraploid durum wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum), respectively, revealing fundamental characteristics of chloroplast translation optimization. The comparison between the two species reveals extraordinary conservation of codon usage patterns despite different nuclear genome compositions (AABBDD vs. AABB). The similar RSCU patterns across ploidy levels indicate that chloroplast codon optimization strategies have remained stable throughout wheat polyploidization events. This conservation confirms that chloroplast translational machinery operates under fundamental constraints that override nuclear genome influences [69, 70]. The conservation of codon usage patterns across hexaploid and tetraploid wheat also provides molecular markers for phylogenetic analysis and breeding applications. The subtle differences in codon usage frequencies between ploidy levels, though minimal, can contribute to understanding evolutionary relationships and developing cytoplasmic male sterility systems in wheat breeding programs [70]. The maintenance of similar codon preferences across different wheat species reflects the critical importance of optimized translation for photosynthetic function and the evolutionary stability of chloroplast genetic systems [69, 71].

The RSCU values demonstrate pronounced bias patterns that reflect both evolutionary constraints and functional requirements of chloroplast protein synthesis [72–74]. The two figures reveal a striking preference for codons terminating with adenine (A) or thymine (T) at the third codon position across all amino acids exhibiting synonymous codon degeneracy (see Figs. 7 and 8). This AT-bias is consistent with the general compositional characteristics of chloroplast genomes, where AT content typically ranges from 62 to 68% [71, 73]. The preference for AT-ending codons reflects optimization for the limited tRNA repertoire available in chloroplasts, facilitating efficient translation of essential photosynthetic proteins [71, 72]. Research demonstrates that this bias results from both mutational pressures favoring A↔T transitions and selective constraints that optimize codon usage to match the available tRNA pool [72, 74]. The chloroplast’s streamlined translational machinery, containing only 30–40 tRNA genes compared to ~ 400 in the nuclear genome, creates strong selective pressure for codon optimization to maximize translation efficiency [73, 75]. The codon usage patterns in wheat also reflect optimization for high-level expression of essential chloroplast genes. Photosynthetic proteins such as RuBisCO large subunit (rbcL), photosystem I and II components, and ATP synthase subunits require rapid synthesis rates to support photosynthetic function [72, 76]. The preference for AT-ending codons facilitates this high-level expression by matching codon usage to abundant tRNA species. The strong codon bias evident in both figures suggests low effective number of codons (ENC) values (typically < 35), indicating significant deviation from random codon usage [73, 77]. This low ENC reflects the optimization of chloroplast genes for translational efficiency, with highly expressed genes showing stronger bias toward optimal codons than housekeeping genes [77, 78]. The codon usage patterns demonstrated in Figs. 7 and 8 primarily reflect natural selection rather than neutral mutational drift. Prior research indicates that natural selection accounts for approximately 80–90% of codon usage bias in chloroplast genomes, with mutational pressure contributing only 10–20% [71, 72, 74]. This strong selective component reflects the critical importance of efficient protein synthesis for chloroplast function.

Results of RSCU in wheat also highlight that the two non-degenerate codons encoding methionine (ATG) and tryptophan (TGG) uniquely utilize guanine (G) at the third position, representing notable exceptions to the general AT-preference pattern aligning with recent studies [79–82]. These codons cannot be optimized for AT-bias due to their non-degenerate nature, creating functional constraints that override compositional preferences. The preservation of these G-ending codons reflects the essential roles of methionine in translation initiation and tryptophan in photosystem protein complexes [79–82]. The methionine codon (ATG) serves dual functions as both the universal start codon and the sole methionine-encoding triplet, creating additional selective pressure for its conservation. This functional constraint explains why ATG maintains its G-ending despite the overall AT-bias, demonstrating how functional requirements can override compositional optimization [71, 83].

The codon usage patterns observed in wheat chloroplasts (Figs. 7 and 8) are consistent with broader patterns observed across diverse plant lineages. Studies of Aroideae, Rutaceae, Juglandaceae, and other plant families reveal similar AT-bias patterns, suggesting universal optimization strategies for chloroplast translation [74, 76, 84]. This universality indicates that the constraints shaping chloroplast codon usage are fundamental to chloroplast biology rather than species-specific adaptations. The most frequently used codons highlighted in both figures (marked with red and blue circular markers in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively) represent optimal codons that have been selected for translational efficiency. These optimal codons typically show RSCU values >1.6, indicating over-representation relative to neutral expectations, while under-utilized codons mostly show RSCU values < 0.6 [85, 86].

Heteroplasmy in chloroplasts as a genetic diversity indicator

Other emerging area of plastome significance involve the detection and characterization of structural patterns [87, 88]. The heteroplasmy analysis in Egyptian wheat cultivars revealed cultivar-specific patterns that reflect genetic background influences on chloroplast genome stability, with implications for understanding cytoplasmic inheritance and breeding strategies (Figure S9). This application demonstrates the plastome’s utility in uncovering previously unrecognized levels of organellar genetic complexity. Analysis of chloroplast heteroplasmy in wheat underscores its role as a genetic diversity indicator, with direct implications for selective breeding and yield optimization [89]. High heteroplasmy rates were recently reported to reflect enhanced adaptability to environmental stresses due to chloroplast diversity but could face reduced grain yield stability, while low heteroplasmy rates might be ideal for breeding programs prioritizing uniformity in cytoplasmic traits [89].

The Kruskal-Wallis test was selected for statistical analysis because it is a non-parametric method that remains reliable when data distributions deviate from normality and when variances are heterogeneous, making it particularly suitable for VAF datasets that may not meet the strict assumptions required by parametric procedures such as ANOVA [90]. The analysis in the present study revealed that cultivars with high structural chloroplast heteroplasmy—like SKH95, SKH94 (both from the Delta region), and BSF4 (Upper Egypt; the sole tetraploid)—share not only particular maternal lineages but also pedigrees that reflect marked introgression or the involvement of diverse parental germplasm (see Table S1). These lines, as detailed in the pedigree information, trace ancestry through multiple cross-breeding cycles that incorporate international founder lines and, in several cases, involve germplasm from CIMMYT or distinct adaptation to stress-prone local environments. Such pedigree complexity, particularly the frequent utilization of multifaceted, multi-parental crosses, may increase the likelihood of retaining or generating structural variants in the chloroplast genome, which are manifested as elevated heteroplasmy.

Heteroplasmy assessments conducted on Egyptian wheat varieties revealed varying chloroplast genomic stability patterns, suggesting significant implications for optimizing breeding methodologies [91–93], where cultivars exhibiting high heteroplasmy rates may offer enhanced adaptability to environmental stresses due to chloroplast diversity [92, 94, 95], while low heteroplasmy cultivars might be preferred for breeding programs prioritizing uniformity in cytoplasmic traits [23, 96, 97]. The data in the present study shows that cultivars with high chloroplast heteroplasmy include the hexaploids SKH95, and SKH94, and the tetraploid BSF4, while cultivars with low heteroplasmy include GZ168, GMZ10, and SDS13, with SWL1’s CIMMYT-derived germplasm showing moderate heteroplasmy levels (Figure S9). This comprehensive dataset demonstrates the genetic diversity within Egyptian wheat breeding programs and provides crucial context for understanding chloroplast genome stability patterns across different cultivars, geographic origins, and breeding histories.

Comparative mVISTA analysis of Egyptian wheat plastomes

The mVISTA-based comparative analysis of Egyptian wheat plastomes revealed an exceptionally high degree of sequence conservation, consistent with the characteristic structural stability observed within Poaceae chloroplast genomes. Alignment of the complete chloroplast genome against reference Triticum aestivum sequences demonstrated near-identical gene synteny across all Egyptian cultivars, with minimal nucleotide divergence across both coding and noncoding regions —most notably demonstrating exceptional sequence homogeneity in the cultivar Sahel 1 (Figure S14). Such uniformity underscores the conservative evolution of monocot plastomes, where functional constraints maintain stable gene order and operon integrity. Minor sequence variations were largely confined to intragenic and intergenic loci within the wheat chloroplast genome, including genes such as atpA, rps18, and ndhB, as well as spacer regions like atpH-atpI, rps4-ndhJ, petA-psbJ, psbE-petL, and rpl16-rps3. Comparable patterns of divergence have been widely reported across angiosperm chloroplast genomes, where noncoding intergenic spacers often display higher levels of nucleotide polymorphism relative to coding regions due to reduced selective constraints [98, 99]. Studies employing mVISTA alignments of multiple species, such as in Atractylodes or Illicium verum, demonstrated similar divergence peaks localized in the same intervals of atpH–atpI, highlighting their potential as molecular markers in phylogenetic and barcoding analyses. Previous studies have shown that the atpA locus, which encodes the α-subunit of chloroplast ATP synthase, exhibits modest mutational tolerance within non-critical domains, while maintaining functional conservation across angiosperm plastomes [100]. These divergence hotspots likely represent lineage-specific mutational dynamics shaped by replication slippage, recombination, or differential repair efficiency, thereby offering valuable insights into plastome evolution and species differentiation [98, 99].

The highly conserved pattern observed in the Egyptian wheat cultivars further confirms the absence of structural rearrangements, insertions, or large deletions, in line with evolutionary patterns reported for other temperate grasses such as Lolium, Festuca, and Hordeum. The loss of the plastid-encoded accD gene, corroborated by the mVISTA alignment gap in this region, is a hallmark of Poaceae evolution and supports earlier findings that this gene has been transferred to the nuclear genome, where it continues to encode the β-subunit of acetyl-CoA carboxylase [63].

Phylogenetic resolution and molecular dating: complete plastome vs. Single-Gene approaches

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have revolutionized the field of plastome genomics, enabling the rapid generation of complete plastome sequences and the identification of species-specific molecular markers [26, 68, 101]. The integration of plastome data with nuclear markers is increasingly recognized as essential for comprehensive understanding of plant evolution, population structure, and conservation genetics [28, 29, 102]. Such a “super-barcode” approach affords enhanced phylogenetic resolution relative to conventional single-gene approach, particularly among closely related taxa where nuclear markers frequently exhibit insufficient variation [58, 103–106]. Unlike conventional DNA barcodes that rely on single-gene regions, complete plastome sequences provide comprehensive genomic signatures that enable discrimination of closely related species and even cultivar-level identification [104, 107]. This principle is effectively demonstrated in the Egyptian wheat investigation, wherein complete plastome analyses successfully discriminated phylogenetic relationships across the ten wheat cultivars—albeit without genetically distinguishing the tetraploid accession—whereas single-gene assessments utilizing three established biomarker genes failed to resolve genetic differentiation among these cultivars (Figs. 3 and 9, S10, S11). The capacity of complete plastome sequences to elucidate evolutionary relationships is recently evidenced by their recent deployment in constructing robust phylogenetic frameworks that clarify intergeneric relationships and illuminate diversification histories [106]. Nevertheless, while comprehensive plastome sequences provide unparalleled discriminatory capacity for fine-scale evolutionary inquiries and phylogenetically proximate taxa, the judicious implementation of rbcL and trnL biomarkers continues to fulfill indispensable functions in large-scale phylogeographic investigations, community ecological research, and applications necessitating high-throughput taxonomic identification across disparate systematic assemblages [67, 108–111].

Nevertheless, at higher taxonomic tiers encompassing diverse Triticum species and allied genera (Aegilops, Secale), the single-gene approach employing matK in this study achieved successful generic-level discrimination within Poaceae, although the alternative biomarkers rbcL and trnL proved inadequate for taxonomic resolution. Phylogenetic tree of Poaceae plastomes in Fig. 9, inferred from the chloroplast matK gene using a Maximum Likelihood approach, shows two well-supported clades whose branch lengths quantitatively reflect both recent and ancient divergence events: modern hexaploid and tetraploid wheat cultivars (including Triticum aestivum, T. turgidum, and T. macha) cluster tightly in Clade I, exhibiting minimal matK sequence variation due to their shared maternal A and B genome lineages and the genetic bottlenecks of modern breeding [112–114], whereas Clade II comprises independent lineages—T. timopheevii and T. zhukovskyi (distinct AAGG and AAuAuAmAmGG polyploids), Aegilops species (genome donors diverging at different times), and Secale cereale (diverged ~ 7 Ma)—whose longer branches and higher matK nucleotide substitution rates correspond to millions of years of separate evolution, with high bootstrap values (>95%) validating these intergeneric and intrageneric genetic distances [114, 115]. These temporal frameworks provide essential insights for elucidating speciation mechanisms, biogeographical distribution patterns, and the evolutionary trajectory of economically significant cereal crops [1, 116–119].

The preponderance of polymorphisms in the chloroplast-encoded ribosomal protein gene rps2—particularly its 33 SNPs across Egyptian wheat cultivars and an exceptional 21-SNP burden in Sahel 1—carries clear functional and breeding implications, as rps2 is integral to 30 S ribosome assembly and plastid translation, with allelic variation potentially influencing photosynthetic efficiency, stress resilience, and cytoplasmic–nuclear genome compatibility [108–110]. Elevated polymorphism and the prominent mutation hotspot detected in rps2 may indicate functional diversification at the heart of the plastid translation apparatus, reflecting adaptive evolution to optimize protein synthesis under varied physiological or environmental conditions. Such rps2 diversity can serve as a robust cytoplasmic lineage tracer and, pending correlation with agronomic traits, a marker for selection of superior maternal backgrounds. Concurrently, matK emerges as a discriminative plastid barcode in our dataset—consistent with its established utility in several angiosperm phylogenetic analyses—enabling precise cultivar authentication, informed parental selection to maximize heterosis, and monitoring of introgressed cytoplasmic haplotypes in breeding programs [108–110]. Together, rps2 and matK constitute a dual toolkit that links plastome structural variation to functional outcomes, thereby enhancing marker-assisted selection strategies for wheat improvement [108–111].

Breeding applications and cultivar development

Based on comprehensive research analysis, Egyptian wheat cultivars demonstrate distinct tolerance patterns across abiotic and biotic stress conditions, with clear hierarchical differences in performance. GZ168, MSR2 and GMZ10 emerge as the most balanced performers, demonstrating good tolerance to both drought and heat stress while maintaining reasonable disease resistance [120–123]. Sahel 1, SKH95 and SKH94 excel specifically in drought tolerance, ranking among the superior genotypes for water-limited conditions [122, 124, 125]. SKH94 excels in disease resistance, particularly against rust diseases [126]. GMZ9 demonstrates superior heat tolerance with less than 50% membrane injury in thermostability tests [127]. However, SWL1 consistently ranks as highly sensitive to drought stress across multiple studies, showing the lowest mean productivity values and reduced proline accumulation compared to drought-tolerant varieties [122, 123]. SDS13 demonstrates exceptional drought tolerance, consistently ranking as one of the drought-tolerant cultivar among Egyptian wheat varieties, while possesses limited heat stress tolerance capabilities and high susceptibility to leaf rust disease [128, 129]. BSF4 shows promising heat tolerance but is severely compromised by its high susceptibility to leaf rust disease [130–132]. Based on comprehensive research analysis, Egyptian wheat cultivars demonstrate varying degrees of tolerance to both abiotic and biotic stress conditions, with significant differences observed across different stress types and cultivar performance. Understanding these differential performance enables strategic cultivar deployment based on anticipated stress conditions, optimizing wheat production under Egypt’s diverse agro-ecological conditions [103, 120, 133–135].

While this study primarily investigates plastome diversity among Egyptian wheat cultivars of Triticum aestivum and one T. turgidum cultivar, our analyses go beyond basic cataloguing by uncovering intra-specific diversity linked to breeding history and pedigree structure, as well as cultivar-specific patterns of chloroplast heteroplasmy with potential implications for cytoplasmic inheritance and breeding strategies [87, 88, 136, 137]. Furthermore, by comparing the three principal chloroplast DNA barcodes (matK, rbcL, and trnL) across our cultivars and a broad panel of related Triticum, Aegilops, Secale, and other Poaceae taxa, we extend our findings to robust inter-specific and inter-generic phylogenetic contexts, thereby enhancing the evolutionary relevance and interpretive power of our plastome data [138, 139].

A significant limitation of this study is the exclusive reliance on publicly available raw sequencing data from NCBI, with no new biological samples or sequencing experiments conducted. While the utilization of open-access genomic resources enables comprehensive comparative analyses and accelerates scientific discovery [140, 141], our findings must be interpreted within the constraints of this approach. The dataset represents a specific, previously collected cohort of Egyptian wheat cultivars, which limits the broader generalizability of our conclusions and restricts our ability to assess potential sampling biases or environmental effects that may have influenced the original data collection [142, 143]. Furthermore, technical variability introduced by the original sequencing protocols, library preparation methods, and platform-specific artifacts cannot be experimentally controlled or validated through independent replication [144, 145]. These considerations underscore the importance of integrating future studies with expanded sampling frameworks and direct experimental validation to strengthen the evolutionary and breeding implications derived from public genomic archives.

Conclusion

Plastome sequencing delivers valuable information on genetic diversity, evolutionary dynamics, and phylogenetic relationships among plant cultivars, as highlighted in the study of Egyptian wheat; however, the interpretive reach of such analyses is limited by narrow regional sampling and the absence of nuclear genome data, resulting in reduced resolution for population-level and trait-specific studies. While plastomic approaches offer accessible markers and conserved gene content well-suited for comparative genomics, they provide only a partial view of genetic variation relevant for breeding and adaptation, making their findings most reliable as supplementary insights alongside nuclear genomic information. As sequencing technologies advance, integrating plastome and nuclear datasets will be essential to address present shortcomings and realize the full potential of molecular profiling for plant genomics and breeding applications.
