Few procedures epitomize modern medicine’s triumphs more than cataract surgery — a highly standardized and successful operation performed millions of times each year. Routine cataract surgeries are now performed under topical anesthesia with or without procedural sedation, and the role of anesthesiologists in low-risk cataract surgery is controversial. In this issue of CMAJ, Aucoin and colleagues used population-based linked administrative data in Ontario to examine models of anesthesia care in cataract surgery, including fee-for-service (FFS) anesthesiologist care, anesthesiology care teams (ACTs) supervised by a physician anesthesiologist, or anesthesia care without a physician anesthesiologist. 1 They found that patients who underwent cataract surgery had a slightly lower risk of postoperative emergency department (ED) visits and a lower risk of adverse surgical events when an anesthesiologist was involved in the surgery than when sedation was managed by the treating ophthalmologist with nonphysician support. Canada has a well-documented crisis in anesthesiology human resources, which has resulted in widespread surgical back-logs; 2 thus, the study’s findings raise an important question about how to deploy that workforce effectively to ensure optimal patient safety. We discuss patient risk stratification to identify those most likely to benefit from anesthesiologist care and the generalizability of innovative models of anesthesia care.

The authors of the related study found that, across 1 271 251 cataract surgeries, the adjusted odds ratio (OR) for an adverse systemic event (95% were postoperative ED visits) was 0.93 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.88 to 0.99) for FFS anesthesiologist care and 0.83 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.89) for ACTs, compared with no physician anesthesiologist involvement.3 The marginal benefit observed must be weighed against the findings of previous studies, including an analysis of 36 652 Medicare beneficiaries who underwent cataract surgery in the United States and a prospective cohort study of 651 procedures conducted in France, both of which found no meaningful increase in the frequency of death or hospitalization when anesthesia personnel were absent, provided patients were appropriately selected.4,5

Systematic risk stratification sounds straightforward in principle but proves elusive in practice for cataract surgery. Existing surgical risk indices were not designed for cataract surgery and, although anesthesia intervention is required only in a substantial minority of cases, the need cannot be reliably predicted.3,6 A Canadian study of 15 440 consecutive cataract surgeries with the ACT model of care found that 2.6% of procedures required anesthesiologist intervention.3 Although American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification and age were correlated with the need for anesthesiologist intervention, no combination of variables reliably predicted the need for intervention. A practical consequence of removing anesthesiologists from the picture in cataract surgery, however, is that some surgeons may be reluctant to operate on patients with substantial comorbidities — the same patients who may have the most to gain from restored vision.4,7

The availability of anesthesiology care during cataract surgery may also decrease risks of adverse surgical events. For example, cataract surgery demands absolute patient stillness during the procedure; this can be compromised and risk of surgical error increased by patient cognitive impairment, anxiety, or movement disorders, or simply their discomfort with the unfamiliar experience of being awake during intraocular surgery.4 Having an anesthesiologist available to deepen sedation or administer anesthesia can mitigate this. Indeed, Aucoin and colleagues found that involvement of a physician anesthesiologist during surgery was associated with significantly lower odds of surgical complications, with an adjusted OR of 0.42 (95% CI 0.33 to 0.53) for FFS care and 0.61 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.72) for ACT care — the largest effect sizes in the study.1 A risk stratification framework that accounts for patient ocular factors (e.g., use of tamsulosin, a commonly prescribed drug that can lead to floppy iris syndrome; pseudoexfoliation syndrome, which may predispose a patient to developing both cataracts and complications during surgery) and degree of surgical complexity — alongside general patient-level variables — is needed to better match the level of anesthesia support to the demands of each case.8,9

Aucoin and colleagues’ findings underscore an important point: even the most carefully designed model of anesthesia care is only as effective as the setting in which it operates. That is, models that demonstrate effectiveness in a well-resourced teaching hospital centre may not be safely replicated in settings with different personnel and capabilities. Aucoin and colleagues found that the benefit of involving a physician anesthesiologist was greater in hospitals than in out-of-hospital settings, and in nonteaching than teaching hospitals.1 These findings might suggest that the protective signal attributed to anesthesiologist involvement in cataract surgery may be partly a function of setting and patient selection rather than direct intervention by the anesthesiologist. Surgeon experience adds a further dimension. Less experienced surgeons face a genuine challenge of managing their patient’s need for sedation while ensuring their cooperation with awake surgery and simultaneously performing a technically demanding intraocular procedure. For these surgeons and their patients, ready access to anesthesiologist support is a logical safeguard.10

The fundamental principle guiding the approach to cataract care must remain clear: every patient, regardless of health status, should have timely access to safe cataract surgery. Aucoin and colleagues’ findings should not be used to argue for wholesale removal of anesthesiologists from cataract care but for a more sophisticated conversation about where, for whom, and in what form anesthesiology expertise adds the most value in the context of cataract surgery in Canada. Innovations that multiply the reach of anesthesiologists through team-based models, thoughtful risk stratification, and care pathways adapted to local capacity offer the clearest path to a cataract surgery system that is both safe for patients and sustainable for the system that serves them.
