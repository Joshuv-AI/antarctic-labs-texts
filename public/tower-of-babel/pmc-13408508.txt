Evaluation of Screen Time, Oral Health Status, and Sleep Disturbances in Children between 6 and 13 years: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Vaishnavi Padmanabhan, Sushmita Shan, K Vivek, Kavitha Swaminathan, H Selvakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep is a critical component required for the growth and development of a child. Deviation from the normal sleep pattern, quality, and quantity directly affects the baseline functioning of the individual. Introduction

Sleep is a crucial component required for the normal growth and development of a child.1 It aids in optimal development and growth of the brain, enhances attention, learning, and memory of the child, and strengthens the immune function.2 Deviation from the physiological sleep pattern, quality, and quantity directly affects the overall baseline functioning of the individual.3 The International Classification of Sleep Disorders-3 provides seven major categories of sleep disorders, which include—sleep-related breathing disorders, parasomnias, sleep-related movement disorders, insomnia, central disorders of hypersomnolence, circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, and other sleep disorders.4 The global prevalence of these disorders in children is notably high, estimated to range between 25 and 40%.5 Evidence suggests a negative association between screen time and sleep quality in children. Increased screen time in children leads to a decrease in sleep duration and causes sleep problems.6

Late bedtime has been identified as a potential risk factor for dental caries in the deciduous dentition.7 It is recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) that children and adolescents should limit their screen time to no more than 2 hours per day.8 Extended screen time and screen time 1 hour before bedtime decreases the child's quality and quantity of sleep and increases the incidence of dental caries in these children. Sleep deprivation affects the salivary flow and increases the prevalence of Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) and proinflammatory cytokines, thus contributing to a higher risk of occurrence of dental caries in children.9 Increased exposure to screens is associated with consumption of food which are higher in fats and added sugars. This dietary pattern not only increases the risk of obesity, but it also increases the risk of dental caries due to its cariogenic nature. The number of decayed teeth present and the DMFT index score were reported to be higher in the children who were exposed to screens for a longer duration.10 Children with screen exposure higher than 2 hours per day showed a higher prevalence of dental caries than those who had lesser screen time exposure.11 Overall reduction in oral health status and oral health-related quality of life was evident in children with sleep-disordered breathing. The reduced salivary flow in these children show association with increased occurrence of dental caries and gingival inflammation.12

Post the COVID-19 pandemic, there is a significantly higher increase in the time spent on using screen-based devices.13 The literature establishing the impact of extended screen time usage on the occurrence of sleep disorders in children, and its influence on oral health status in children, is scarce and limited. The relationship between screen time usage, occurrence of sleep disorders, and disruption of the oral health status in children has not been extensively studied. Hence, the aim of this present cross-sectional study will be to assess the average screen time exposure, oral health status, and prevalence of sleep disorders in children between 6 and 13 years of age.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This prospective observational cross-sectional study was conducted between July 2024 and January 2025. Children reporting to the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry were included in the study. The study participants were included using the convenience sampling method according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (CSP/23/SEP/136/829). Written informed consent and assent were obtained from the parents/guardians and the children, respectively. No financial assistance was provided to the participants.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Children above 6 years and less than 13 years of age reporting to the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry for routine dental treatment were screened. Children with physical disabilities, neurological disorders, developmental disabilities, psychological conditions, and any other known systemic illness were excluded from the study. Those who did not provide consent for the study were not included in the study.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size was estimated by applying the formula n = 4pq/L2. The minimum required sample size of 341 participants was estimated based on the study by Yilmaz et al.11 A total of 370 participants were screened and included in the study.

Method

The study was conducted by a Primary Investigator (VP) who initially recorded the participants' demographic details, including age, gender, class of study, parents' highest level of education, and social status (using the Modified Kuppuswamy Scale). A validated, close-ended Screen Time Assessment Questionnaire was then administered to the parents/guardians of the participants, followed by an assessment of sleep disorders using the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children.14

The Content Validity Index (CVI) for the developed Screen Time Assessment Questionnaire was calculated using Lawshe's content validity formula,15 yielding an overall CVI of 0.83, which indicates high content validity. Clinical assessment was then performed using the ICDAS II index to assess the dental caries status of the child. The primary investigator was calibrated to record the ICDAS II index on 10 randomly selected patients. To examine the intra-examiner reliability, the measurements were repeated after a 2-week interval, yielding a Cohen's Kappa coefficient of 0.84, indicating strong agreement.

To minimize inter-examiner variance, all assessments were conducted by a single examiner (VP).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 26.0 software (Chicago, IL, USA). The descriptive statistical was reported in the form of mean and standard deviation. The ANOVA test was employed to compare the continuous data between two or more than two groups. Significance level was fixed at 5% (α = 0.05). Bivariate and multivariate linear regression analysis will be employed to study the strength of association between the explanatory variables and the continuous outcome variable. Statistical significance was obtained when the p-value was ≤0.05.

Results

Gender Distribution

The gender distribution of the study population indicates a slightly higher number of females (54.8%) compared to males (45.1%), which did not show any statistical significance. This suggests a balanced sample representation, with no extreme gender bias.

Socioeconomic Status

Most of the participants belonged to the upper-middle socioeconomic status (74.6%), while the lower class had no representation. The presence of 10.5% from the upper class and 14.3% from the lower-middle class suggests some diversity, though the sample leans toward higher socioeconomic groups.

Device Usage

The study reveals that most participants use mobile phones (45.2%) or a combination of mobile and TV (45.2%). A small percentage (1.6%) uses multiple devices, such as mobile phones, TVs, and laptops.

Ownership and Usage Patterns

Most children (94.3%) did not own a personal device, but among those who did (5.7%), mobile phones were the predominant device owned. Additionally, 92.7% reported using screens an hour before bedtime.

Screen Habits

Nearly all children (98.6%) used screens during mealtimes, though 91.9% have imposed screen limits; only 27% adhered to these restrictions. The majority of the participants (74.6%) use screens almost every day, with 60.3% reporting 1–2 hours of screen time on weekdays and 56.5% reporting 2–3 hours on weekends.

Gaming and First Exposure to Screens

Most children (59.2%) engage in gaming or recreational screen activities for 1–2 hours daily. The first exposure to screens predominantly occurred between 18 and 23 months (42.2%), followed by those who were exposed after 24 months (38.6%), as shown in Figure 1.

Socioeconomic Classes and Sleep Disturbance

When comparing the total sleep score among different socioeconomic classes, the highest mean was observed in the lower-middle class (31.02) and the lowest in the upper-lower class (26.50). A significant p-value (0.035) indicates that socioeconomic factors may influence overall sleep quality due to easier access to screen-based devices among the higher classes.

Comparison of Cavitated Carious Lesions between Categories of Screen Time among Different Levels of Socioeconomic Status

The Spearman correlation test was employed to assess the relationship between screen time and the occurrence of cavitated carious lesions across different socioeconomic strata (SES 1–SES 4). A weak correlation was noted for cavitated teeth, with SES 2 showing a positive association (r = 0.101, p = 0.032), implying a higher likelihood of cavitated lesions with increased screen time in this group, as shown in Table 1.

Relation between Total Sleep Score and Caries Status

When the number of cavitated and noncavitated lesions was compared with the Total Sleep Score in the children, a weak, non-significant positive correlation was observed in the case of cavitated lesions, and no statistically significant association between total sleep score and the number of noncavitated lesions was observed.

Comparison of Caries Status among Children with and without the Use of a Screen While Eating

The analysis of screen usage during mealtime revealed a significant association with the prevalence of both cavitated and non-cavitated carious lesions. The mean number of cavitated teeth was notably higher among children who engaged in screen use while eating (7.63) compared to those who did not (6.45), with the difference being statistically significant (p = 0.02). Moreover, the mean number of noncavitated lesions was substantially greater among screen users (1.75) relative to nonusers (0.65), further underscoring a significant disparity (p = 0.01) as presented in Table 3.

Overall Prevalence of Children at Risk for Sleep Disorders

The prevalence of children at risk for sleep disorders was categorized into mild, moderate, and severe. Mild sleep disorders were observed in 7.0% of participants, moderate sleep disorders were present in 0.5%, and severe sleep disorders were found in only 0.3%. A vast majority (92.2%) of the study population exhibited no signs of sleep disorders, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Overall prevalence of children at risk for sleep disorders

Sleep Disturbances Parameters

Among the sleep-related symptoms assessed, teeth grinding (bruxism) had the highest mean score (1.91 ± 1.55), indicating it was the most common and variable disturbance in the study group, as shown in Table 3 and Figure 3. Snoring (1.22 ± 0.64) and repetitive movements (1.09 ± 0.49) were also noted, but less frequently. Most other symptoms, such as sleepwalking, vivid dreams, gasping, and waking up screaming, had mean values of 1.00, indicating a lesser frequency of occurrence.

Discussion

Electronic media plays a paramount role in the lives of children in this current generation.16 Increase in screen exposure, especially among the pediatric population, leads to poor sleep patterns and dietary choices, which, in turn, increase the risk of dental caries and other oral health problems.17 Prolonged screen exposure has been consistently linked to sleep disturbances such as reduction in duration of sleep, a delay in sleep onset, and increased sleep fragmentation.18 The results from this study show that nearly 92.7% of children were exposed to screens, 1 hour before their bedtime which led to an increase in their total sleep score. This could be attributed to the reason that evening and nighttime exposure to screens leads to suppression of melatonin, which in turn disrupts the circadian rhythm, thus affecting overall sleep quality.19 In a study conducted by Ogawa et al., sleep deprivation was shown to be strongly associated with a reduction in salivary flow and an upsurge in levels of S. mutans. It also reduced the overall salivary flow and production and led to an increase in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, which increased the caries risk in these children. The results thus reinforced the association between sleep disorders and their impact on the oral health of children.20 However, the results from our study did not show a significant correlation between sleep disorders and caries status.

The majority of the participants of the study (77.84%) had an average screen time exposure of over 2 hours, especially during the weekends, which is more than the recommendation given by the American Academy of Pediatrics. This finding aligns with previous studies that have also documented excessive screen time beyond recommended limits. Notably, unlike prior research,21–23 which did not distinguish between weekday and weekend screen usage, our study highlights this critical difference, underscoring the heightened exposure during weekends.

Screen time also influences the dietary behavior of children and increases the likelihood of unhealthy eating habits, which negatively impacts the overall oral health. The findings from this study showed that children who used screen-based devices, especially during meal times, had a higher prevalence of both cavitated and incipient dental carious lesions, which was statistically significant. In the study by Garg et al., a substantial correlation was noted between increased screen time and food pouching, which was evident from the higher DMFT score and poor periodontal health in children with excessive screen exposure. Food pouching over an extended period of time can lead to adherence of debris over the tooth surfaces, leading to increased plaque accumulation, dysbiosis, and thus causing dental caries.23 The systematic review performed by Shqair Q et al. reported a positive association between excessive screen exposure and higher consumption of cariogenic foods, particularly sugary snacks and beverages.24 In the study by Doitchinova et al., television advertising was found to be associated with greater consumption of unhealthy snacks, which in turn heightened the risk of dental caries among children.25

The impact of screen time on children's overall health and well-being has been extensively investigated in the existing literature. Prolonged use of screen devices reduces opportunities for physical activity, further exacerbating the risk for metabolic disorders, which indirectly impact oral health. The WHO European Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative reported that children with excessive screen exposure were more likely to have inadequate sleep and insufficient physical activity.26 Additionally, excessive screen exposure has been linked to sleep bruxism, which can lead to long-term dental complications and pain-related sleep disruptions.27 Our findings revealed a statistically significant increase in reports of nocturnal teeth grinding among participants, highlighting a potential link between excessive screen exposure and the risk of sleep bruxism in children.

Despite providing significant insights into the interrelation between excessive screen time, sleep disorders, and oral health, this study has some limitations. As this study was a questionnaire-based study with data collected from the parents, there exists a high potential for reporting and recall bias. Also, confounding factors such as dietary intake, oral hygiene practices, and parental screen time exposure were not assessed in this study. Future research should evaluate these factors and utilize a longitudinal study design to establish the causal relationship and evaluate the long-term effects of prolonged screen exposure on the overall health and oral health outcomes in children.

Conclusion

The majority of the participants had an average screen exposure of 1–2 hours during weekdays (60.3%) and 2–3 hours during weekends (56.5%). Nearly 98.6% of children experienced screen exposure during mealtimes, and 92.7% used screen devices within 1 hour before bedtime. This study shows a substantial correlation between increased screen time and prevalence of dental caries in children, especially when exposure occurs during mealtime and 1 hour before bedtime. Despite the presence of excessive screen exposure, the incidence of clinically significant sleep disturbances remained relatively low. The findings of this study emphasize the need to monitor and moderate screen usage in children to improve oral and overall health outcomes in children. Educational interventions targeting parents and caregivers are crucial to promote healthier screen habits.

Clinical Significance

This study highlights the clinical relevance of screen time habits as modifiable risk factors impacting children's oral and sleep health. A significant association was found between screen use during meals and increased dental caries, emphasizing the need for early behavioral interventions. These findings support the importance of integrating screen-time counseling into routine pediatric and dental care to improve overall health outcomes.
