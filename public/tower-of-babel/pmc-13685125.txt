Preoperative prediction of positive margins in breast-conserving surgery using an ensemble model based on DCE-MRI: a multi-center retrospective and prospective study
Authors: Xue Zhao, Jing-Wen Bai, Li-Ang Dong, Zhen-Hui Li, Jie-Zhou He, Yi-Xin Chen, Yong Liu, Wen-Tai Hou, Ze-Yan Xu, Zhi-Cheng Du, Sen Jiang, Shao-Zi Li, Guo-Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast-conserving surgery (BCS) is the standard care for early breast cancer. However, positive surgical margins lead to high recurrence and require reoperation, remaining a clinical challenge. This study aims to develop an ensemble model to preoperatively predict positive margins using dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI).MethodsA total of 887 patients from three Chinese medical centers were included. Patients from the Yunnan (581 patients) and Fujian (51 patients) provinces were used for model development, with external testing on 188 patients from Guangdong and prospective testing on 67 patients from Yunnan. Three base models were developed: the clinical model based on 32 features, the deep learning (DL) model using ResNet-34, and the radiomics model utilizing radiomics features. The ensemble model combined predictions from these three base models using weighted voting. Performance metrics (accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity) were computed, and prognostic analyses were conducted with a public dataset.ResultsThe ensemble model outperformed each of the three base models, with the AUC of 0.931, accuracy of 0.872, sensitivity of 0.871, and specificity of 0.880 in the validation set. Robustness was further confirmed in independent test cohorts, yielding AUCs of 0.762 and 0.861. Furthermore, patients identified at high-risk by the model exhibited poor recurrence-free survival (RFS, HR = 8.117, p < 0.0001) and overall survival (OS, HR = 5.748, p < 0.0001), underscoring its prognostic value.ConclusionsThe ensemble model based on DCE-MRI effectively predicts positive margins and prognosis for BCS patients, facilitating the clinical decision-making on the surgery. Trial registration This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Yunnan Cancer Hospital (KYLX2023-134), Xiang’an Hospital of Xiamen University (XDYX202305K26), and Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (2024059). The study was also registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400083298, Registered 19 April 2024, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?%20proj=207948).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02354-0.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1, 2]. Breast-conserving surgery (BCS), followed by radiotherapy, has long been the standard therapeutic approach for early-stage breast cancer. Extensive clinical trials have demonstrated that BCS provides outcomes comparable to or better than mastectomy [3, 4], with the added benefit of preserving cosmetic appearance and reducing postoperative complications in the upper extremities [5, 6]. However, positive surgical margins remain a significant risk factor for local recurrence and increased reoperation rates [7–9].

Currently, the status of surgical margins is primarily determined through intraoperative or post-surgery pathological examination, a time-consuming method that cannot be performed preoperatively. A negative margin is defined as “no ink on tumor” for invasive carcinoma or “with a minimum of 2 mm from the margin” for ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) [10]. The rate of positive margins ranges from 6.8% to 23.9% [8, 11, 12], influenced by factors such as tumor size, grade, multifocality, and HER2 status as reported [13–17]. Despite developing several prediction models based on clinicopathological features, their accuracy remains limited, with predictive area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.508 to 0.733 [18–20]. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with its high-resolution and multi-parametric capabilities, can help better locate tumors and identify multifocality, making it a promising tool for predicting positive margins in BCS [21–23]. Previous studies have utilized MRI-derived radiomics features to predict the risk of positive margins in BCS [24–26]. However, these studies predominantly relied on nomograms [26] or traditional machine learning (ML) algorithms [24, 25], resulting in relatively simplistic model architectures. Consequently, the predictive performance of these models remains suboptimal, and none of the above studies incorporated prospective validation.

Ensemble methods are particularly effective when the models exhibit complementary strengths, as they enhance robustness, accuracy, and generalizability by reducing overfitting and bias [27]. In breast cancer research, ensemble models have been used to predict the possibility of axillary lymph node metastasis [28], pathological complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [29], and molecular subtypes [30]. The ensemble model demonstrated more stable and superior classification and prediction performance in breast MRI studies than other Deep Learning (DL) models [31]. Although these models have shown promising performance, no study has explored ensemble learning for predicting margin positivity in breast cancer patients undergoing BCS [31].

This study aimed to develop an ensemble model that integrates clinicopathological data, DL, and radiomics features from dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) to provide a more comprehensive and accurate preoperative prediction of positive margin risk in BCS patients. The model was further validated across multiple cohorts and clinical centers, and its clinical utility was thoroughly assessed. Additionally, recurrence and survival analyses were conducted to explore the prognostic value of the ensemble model.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This multicenter study enrolled 887 breast cancer patients who underwent preoperative MRI and BCS at three institutions between January 2015 and November 2024. The study was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating center (XDYX202305K26, KYLX2023-134, 2024059). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female participants with histologically confirmed breast cancer; (2) aged 18 – 75 years; (3) underwent BCS as their primary surgical treatment; (4) received preoperative DCE-MRI within 1 month; and (5) with complete clinical and pathological data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior history of breast cancer; (2) bilateral breast cancer; (3) receipt of neoadjuvant therapy; and (4) poor quality of breast DCE-MRI images.

All patients were divided into four datasets. Patients from Yunnan Cancer Hospital (YNCH, n = 581) and Xiang’an Hospital of Xiamen University (XAH, n = 51) were used for model development and randomly split into training and validation sets (7:3) using stratified sampling to preserve the distribution of margin status. The test set included patients from the Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (SUMCCH, n = 188), and a prospective test set was recruited from YNCH (YNCH-Pro, n = 67). Additionally, 427 BCS patients from the Duke-Breast-Cancer-MRI dataset [32] were included to validate the prognostic significance of the ensemble model, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those applied to our institutional datasets. MRI procedure

DCE-MRI was conducted using 1.5-T or a 3.0-T scanner (Siemens Healthineers or GE Healthcare). T1-weighted pre-contrast images were acquired, followed by gadolinium contrast (Gd-DTPA) administration, with five to nine post-contrast phases acquired. Detailed parameters were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Surgical and clinicopathological data

All BCS were conducted by experienced surgeons following the principles of “no ink on tumor” for invasive carcinoma and “with a minimum 2 mm from the margin” for DCIS. At SUMCCH, a circumferential cavity margin assessment was performed. It used radial perpendicular sectioning, with inking of six anatomical surfaces (anterior, posterior, superior, inferior, medial, and lateral) to ensure accurate margin orientation and evaluation. At YNCH and XAH, margin evaluation was routinely performed using a random cavity margin sampling technique, in which multiple small tissue samples were randomly obtained from different directions of the lumpectomy cavity immediately after specimen removal to assess residual disease. Both intraoperative frozen section and final paraffin pathology reports were collected for all enrolled patients. When discrepancies occurred between the two assessments, the final paraffin diagnosis was considered the reference standard for analysis.

Information regarding the primary surgeon, including surgeon’s ID, professional title, and 3D margin measurements (length, width, height) were recorded. Margin-related features were derived from pathological margin widths measured along the three orthogonal specimen dimensions (length, width, and height), denoted as x, y, and z for standardization. For each surgeon, the mean margin width along each dimension (Margin_avg_x/y/z/avg) and the mean absolute deviation (MAD) (Margin_mad_x/y/z/avg) were calculated to represent the central tendency and variability of surgical margin practices.

Clinicopathological features obtained before BCS, including age, menopausal status, tumor size, cTNM stage, biomarkers (CA153, CEA), and surgeon metrics, were included for model construction. Key clinicopathological parameters, including estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, and clinical stage, were recorded for subgroup analysis of the model. ER and PR expression were assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and defined as positive when ≥ 1% of tumor nuclei were stained, following the ASCO/CAP guidelines [33]. The Ki-67 index was evaluated by IHC and dichotomized into low (< 14%) and high (≥ 14%) expression groups, consistent with commonly used thresholds for molecular subtyping [34, 35]. Notably, for patients with DCIS, immunohistochemical staining was routinely performed to assess estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression for postoperative endocrine therapy consideration. Molecular subtyping was not performed for DCIS, as this is not standard clinical practice for in situ lesions [10].

Data preprocessing

The DCE-MRI images were processed using Python (version 3.9.12). The pre-processing steps included: (1) N4ITK bias correction [36] implemented via the SimpleITK library (version 2.2.1) using default parameters (50 iterations per resolution level) to correct low-frequency intensity inhomogeneity; (2) contrast enhancement extraction, in which pre-contrast images were subtracted from the first post-contrast phase, followed by maximum intensity projection (MIP) along the Z-axis to highlight enhancing tumor regions; (3) isotropic resampling to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 voxel spacing using third-order B-spline interpolation to standardize spatial resolution across scanners; and (4) intensity normalization using histogram equalization, with intensity values clipped to the 1st–99th percentile to reduce the influence of outliers.

Automated tumor segmentation was performed using a model developed in our previous study [37]. Radiologists reviewed and modified the segmentations. Additionally, volume of interests (VOIs) for DL model were defined by expanding the outermost boundary of the segmented tumor region by 1 cm. Missing clinicopathological data were handled using mean (continuous) or mode (categorical) imputation, and all features were standardized with z-score.

Radiomic feature extraction

1132 radiomic features were extracted from the tumor regions defined in the DCE-MIP images using PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) [38]. These features included: (1) Shape features describing tumor morphology and dimension. (2) First-order features: reflecting intensity distribution within the tumor. (3) Second-order features: Capturing spatial relationships between intensity pairs. (4) Higher-order features: extracting after applying various filters to enhance specific characteristics of the tumor. The filters used in this study include wavelet transform and Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG). The PyRadiomics documentation (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) provided detailed definitions and formulas for all radiomic features, which are consistent with the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) [39]. For patients with unilateral multifocal disease, each tumor focus was manually segmented individually, and all ipsilateral tumor voxels were combined into a single union mask for feature extraction. This composite ROI contained only tumor voxels, without inclusion of intervening normal tissue. Accordingly, shape features such as Maximum3DDiameter and Sphericity characterize the overall spatial extent and geometric complexity of the combined lesion mask, rather than the morphology of any individual focus. To improve methodological transparency, we additionally provided a supplementary table summarizing PyRadiomics features potentially affected by composite-ROI analysis in multifocal disease and clarifying their interpretation in single-lesion versus multifocal settings (Supplementary Table S2).

Model development

The clinical model was developed using clinicopathological features. Features were selected using T-tests (p < 0.1) and LASSO regression. Various ML classifiers, including Support Vector Machines (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Trees (DT), Random Forests (RF), Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), and XGBoost (XGB), were tested. The classifier with the highest AUC was selected for further use.

The DL model utilized a 3D ResNet-34 architecture, incorporating eight surgeon tendency features (e.g., Margin_avg_x/y/z, Margin_mad_x/y/z). The Cross-Entropy Loss function was employed, with class weights set to 1 for negative samples and 3 for positive samples. Optimization was performed using the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.001. Furthermore, a gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) [40] was employed for model interpretability.

The radiomics model utilized the same ML pipeline as the clinical model based on radiomics features. The model with the best AUC was selected as the final model for predicting positive margin risk.

The ensemble model combined predictions from the clinical, DL, and radiomics models using a weighted voting mechanism. Ensemble weights were optimized by grid search on the validation set (step = 0.05, weights summing to 1). The combination achieving the highest AUC was selected as final (0.1 for clinical, 0.4 for DL, and 0.5 for radiomics).

Model evaluation

The training set was used exclusively for model fitting (updating parameters and weights). The validation set was used for hyperparameter optimization and model selection, including adjustment of learning rate schedules, regularization parameters, and early stopping criteria [41, 42]. In the ensemble model, this validation set was also used to determine the optimal weighting scheme among the three base models (clinical, radiomics, and deep learning). The test set and prospective test set were completely withheld from all stages of model training and tuning. They were used solely for final, unbiased performance evaluation to reflect real-world generalizability.

The base and ensemble models were trained on the training set, with hyperparameters optimized through the validation set. The performance of the ensemble model was then evaluated using both the test set and prospective test set. Evaluation metrics, including the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and standardized confusion matrix, were calculated for each model. Moreover, subgroup performance was evaluated across ER, PR, HER2 status, clinical stage, background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) and non-mass enhancement (NME).

To assess potential inter-center heterogeneity, we performed additional center-stratified analyses. Baseline distributions of key clinical variables and model-selected imaging features were compared across institutions (YNCH/YNCH-Pro, XAH, and SUMCCH). Model performance was also evaluated separately within each center, reporting AUC with 95% confidence intervals, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. To further evaluate cross-center robustness, a leave-one-center-out (LOCO) sensitivity analysis was conducted. In each iteration, one center was completely excluded from training, while the model was trained using data from the remaining centers and subsequently tested on the held-out center. Model training procedures and hyperparameters were identical to those used in the main analysis. AUC confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations).

Prospective cohort procedures

In the YNCH-Pro prospective cohort, surgeons were blinded to the preoperative model predictions during the initial breast-conserving surgery. Model inference was performed offline after MRI preprocessing, and prediction results were stored separately and not accessible to the surgical team prior to or during the operation. Following final pathological assessment, model predictions were retrospectively compared with intraoperative and postoperative outcomes to evaluate predictive accuracy under real-world conditions.

Prognostic analysis

Patients were categorized into low- and high-risk groups based on the ensemble model’s predictions. The low- and high-risk stratification was based on the ensemble model’s predicted probability using a fixed cutoff (Youden index from the internal validation cohort). Precisely, Kaplan-Meier curves for recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were plotted, with group differences analyzed using log-rank tests. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated via Cox regression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9.12) and R (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as counts or percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Chi-square test for categorical data and either the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data, depending on the distribution of the variables. The Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied during multiple comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

The study incorporated four patient cohorts: three retrospective cohorts (YNCH, XAH, SUMCCH) from Yunnan, Fujian, and Guangdong Provinces of China, and one prospective cohort (YNCH-Pro) from YNCH (Fig. 2). These comprehensive datasets included key clinical factors such as patient age, ER, PR, HER2 status, molecular subtype, and surgical features. The baseline characteristics of patients stratified by cohort and center were summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S3. The positive margin rates were 11.7, 33.3, 28.7, and 9.0% for YNCH, XAH, SUMCCH, and YNCH-Pro, respectively. No significant differences were found between patients with negative or positive margins in each cohort, except for age and menopause status in SUMCCH and family history of cancer in YNCH-Pro.

In addition, we further reviewed the distribution characteristics of patients with multiple ipsilateral lesions across cohorts (Supplementary Table S4). Multiple lesions accounted for 10.0, 15.7, 10.6, and 9.0% of patients in the YNCH, XAH, SUMCCH, and YNCH-Pro cohorts, respectively. Most multifocal/multicentric cases contained only two lesions, and lesions separated by > 3 cm were uncommon across all cohorts. In contrast, the majority of cases demonstrated closely clustered lesions with a maximum inter-lesion distance ≤ 1 cm. These findings indicate that widely dispersed multifocal disease represented only a small minority of the overall study population.

Performance of clinical model

We first developed three base models—clinical, DL, and radiomics—using single modalities. For the clinical model, 32 preoperative clinicopathological features were collected, and five features were identified as most predictive for margin status, including cT stage, CA153 value, Margin_avg_z, Margin_avg_x and Margin_mad_z (Fig. 3A–B). The importance of these features is visually represented in Fig. 3B. Notably, cT stage was a positive and Margin_mad_z was a negative predictor strongly correlated with margin positivity risk. BPE and NME data were recorded but excluded from model training, being used solely for subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table S5). Using logistic regression, the clinical model achieved an AUC of 0.642 in training and 0.696 in validation set (Fig. 3C). In the validation set, the model demonstrated an accuracy of 0.819, a sensitivity of 0.876 and a specificity of 0.480 (Fig. 3D–E). Despite suboptimal performance, the clinical model provides valuable insights into the key features influencing positive margin prediction and serves as an essential baseline for further model refinement.

Performance of DL model

The DL model was constructed using a ResNet-34 architecture, a robust convolutional neural network renowned for its capability to learn intricate patterns from medical images. We incorporated eight additional features representing the surgeons’ operative tendencies to improve the model’s spatial understanding. The optimized DL model achieved AUCs of 0.859 and 0.800 for the training and validation sets, respectively (Fig. 3F). The validation set demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.742 and specificity of 0.760 (Fig. 3G), indicating the model’s proficiency in identifying positive margin cases, although there is room for improvement in specificity. The Grad-CAM heatmap highlighted key image regions contributing to predictions, which closely aligned with high-risk areas for positive margins, especially in positive samples (Fig. 3H). These findings offer valuable insights into the model’s prediction rationale and assist clinicians in understanding the model’s decision-making process.

Performance of the radiomics model

We next developed a radiomics model based on features extracted from DCE-MIP MRI images. A total of 1132 radiomic features were initially extracted, then reduced via t-test and LASSO regression to eight key features, including three shape-related features and five high-order features derived from wavelet transformations (Fig. 3I–J). These findings highlighted the critical role of tumor morphology and texture in predicting margin positivity. The list of selected radiomics features is provided in Supplementary Table S6. The radiomics model utilized an MLP classifier and demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving AUCs of 0.913 and 0.870 in the training and validation sets (Fig. 3K). In the validation set, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.867, a sensitivity of 0.871, and a specificity of 0.800 (Fig. 3L–M), indicating good predictive capability. These results suggested that radiomics-based features, which capture underlying tumor morphology and texture patterns, strongly predict margin positivity risk.

Ensemble model for improved prediction of positive margin risk

To improve prediction further, we constructed an ensemble model integrating the three base models. The ensemble model aims to leverage the strengths of each model while mitigating their respective weaknesses, resulting in a more robust and accurate prediction of margin positivity risk. Using a grid search strategy, the optimal configuration (0.1, 0.4, 0.5) achieved the highest validation AUC (Figure S1). As a result, the ensemble model significantly outperformed the individual models, achieving an AUC of 0.931, accuracy of 0.872, sensitivity of 0.871, and specificity of 0.880 in the validation set (Fig. 4A–B and Table S7 in Supplementary Data). The Venn diagram (Fig. 4C) illustrated the complementary nature of the models (ensemble and base models), with each base model contributing unique error patterns. For instance, 19 samples misclassified by the clinical model were correctly predicted by others. This variability highlighted the ensemble model’s ability to reduce misclassifications, thus improving prediction accuracy. Besides, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the models (Supplementary Fig. S2 A–C). Across the internal validation, external test, and pooled datasets, the ensemble model consistently demonstrated higher net benefit than the individual models and conventional strategies, particularly within the clinically relevant threshold range of 15–50%.

To further validate the generalizability of the ensemble model, we conducted additional testing on two independent cohorts: the SUMCCH test set and the YNCH-Pro prospective test set. On SUMCCH, the ensemble model achieved an accuracy of 0.739, AUC of 0.762, sensitivity of 0.776, and specificity of 0.648 (Fig. 5A–B and Table S9 in Supplementary Data). On YNCH-Pro, the model demonstrated superior performance with an accuracy of 0.821, AUC of 0.861, sensitivity of 0.820, and specificity of 0.833 (Fig. 5C–D and Table S9 in Supplementary Data). Grad-CAM visualizations were also generated for the test and prospective test sets. In both settings, the attention maps consistently highlighted regions corresponding to the final positive margins or adjacent high-risk edges, suggesting that the model captured morphologic patterns relevant to incomplete resection (Supplementary Fig. S4). To further evaluate cross-center robustness, a leave-one-center-out (LOCO) sensitivity analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. S5). When each center was excluded from training and used exclusively for testing, the ensemble model achieved AUC values ranging from 0.732 to 0.896, indicating stable performance under varying training-testing center combinations.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis of the test set indicated that the ensemble model performed consistently well across most subgroups, except for patients in clinical stage I (Fig. 5E–H). Additional analyses suggested that this reduction in performance may reflect heterogeneity within Stage I tumors as well as limited separability of imaging-derived features between positive- and negative-margin cases in early-stage disease (Supplementary Fig. S6). Recurrence and survival validation of the ensemble model

The clinical relevance of the ensemble model was further evaluated by assessing its predictive capability for patient recurrence and survival outcomes. A positive margin in BCS is a well-established risk factor for recurrence. To investigate the prognostic value of the ensemble model, we tested it using the Duke-Breast-Cancer-MRI cohort (n = 427). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on predicted margin status. Among them, 47 (11.0%) were classified as high-risk. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that high-risk patients had significantly worse RFS than low-risk patients, with a p-value < 0.0001 and an HR of 8.117 (Fig. 6A). Additionally, OS analysis also revealed significant differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups, with a p-value < 0.0001 and an HR of 5.748 (Fig. 6B). To further explore the relationship between the model-derived prediction score and survival outcomes, univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed. After adjustment for clinicopathologic variables, the prediction score remained independently associated with both recurrence-free survival (RFS) (HR = 30.1, 95% CI 9.20–98.6, p < 0.001) and overall survival (OS) (HR = 7.55, 95% CI 1.79–31.8, p = 0.006) (Fig. 7). Significantly, in the Duke-Breast-Cancer-MRI cohort, the positive margin status was not available, as the dataset includes only imaging and outcome variables. Therefore, recurrence and survival analyses were performed based on the model-predicted risk groups, not on actual margin pathology .

To further explore potential biological correlates, clinicopathologic characteristics were compared between the two risk groups (Supplementary Table S10 and Fig. S7). High-risk patients exhibited lower proportions of ER-positive (59.6% vs. 79.6%) and PR-positive (51.1% vs. 71.5%) tumors, but higher frequencies of HER2-positive (12.8% vs. 8.6%), HER2-overexpressing (6.4% vs. 3.1%), and triple-negative (34.0% vs. 14.9%) subtypes. In addition, they more frequently presented with multifocal or multicentric lesions, pectoralis / chest wall involvement, skin or nipple invasion, and more advanced T and N stages. These results indicate that the model-predicted high-risk group captures tumor phenotypes associated with biologically aggressive behavior and poorer long-term outcomes.

In conclusion, these findings underscored the clinical utility of the ensemble model in identifying patients at high risk for recurrence and poor survival outcomes.

To facilitate potential clinical translation, we also proposed a workflow integrating the ensemble model into the preoperative evaluation process (Fig. 8). In this framework, preoperative DCE-MRI and routine clinical variables are used as inputs to generate a predicted margin-risk score, which can stratify patients into risk groups and support surgical planning. The model is intended to serve as an adjunctive decision-support tool to assist surgeons in identifying patients who may benefit from enhanced intraoperative margin assessment or wider resection strategies.

Discussion

This study developed a novel ensemble model that integrates DCE-MRI radiomics, deep-learning signatures, and clinicopathological variables to predict the risk of positive surgical margins in breast cancer patients undergoing BCS. By combining complementary information from multiple data sources, the ensemble framework achieved superior performance compared with individual base models, supporting the rationale that tumor morphology, enhancement kinetics, and patient-level factors collectively contribute to margin status. The model’s robust performance across retrospective, external, and prospective cohorts underscores its clinical applicability and generalizability.

The weighted voting ensemble strategy effectively mitigated the instability inherent to single-modality predictions [27]. Each base model captured different dimensions of tumor phenotype—radiomics reflected spatial heterogeneity and interface complexity, the deep-learning model learned high-level enhancement patterns, and the clinical model quantified established risk factors. Integrating these heterogeneous representations provided a more holistic assessment of the tumor–normal tissue interface, leading to improved stability across cohorts.

Compared with previous MRI-based margin prediction models, which relied on single-modality features or lacked external validation, our model achieved superior and more consistent performance. The study by Zhou et al. on HER2-overexpressing breast cancer achieved 74.4% specificity and 71.4% sensitivity using a single SVM model without external validation [24], while Li et al. developed an XGBoost radiomics model with AUCs of 0.76–0.88 in multicenter cohorts [25]. Another nomogram-based approach reported an AUC of 0.76 but was limited to single-center data [26]. In contrast, our ensemble model—uniquely integrating clinical, radiomics, and deep-learning features—achieved AUCs of 0.931 (validation), 0.762 (external test), and 0.861 (prospective test), consistently outperforming prior methods. Although a slight performance decrease was observed in the external cohort, likely reflecting inter-center differences in MRI scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient composition, the model maintained clinically meaningful accuracy and demonstrated transportability across independent and prospective cohorts despite residual heterogeneity. Notably, to our knowledge, this is the first multicenter, prospectively validated MRI-based ensemble model for preoperative prediction of positive margins in breast-conserving surgery.

In our cohort, ER, PR, and HER2 status were not significantly associated with margin status, consistent with previous studies and clinical guidelines suggesting that surgical margin adequacy in breast-conserving surgery is largely independent of molecular subtype. However, consistent with prior studies, DCIS cases in our cohort exhibited substantially higher positive margin rates compared with invasive cancers (32.0% vs. 12.7% in the training–validation cohort; 33.3% vs. 28.3% in the test cohort; 25.0% vs. 7.9% in the prospective cohort), likely attributable to their propensity for diffuse and multifocal ductal spread [43, 44]. Notably, our ensemble model maintained high discriminative performance in DCIS patients (AUC 0.952–0.960), suggesting that deep learning and radiomics features derived from DCE-MRI may capture microstructural complexity and subtle enhancement patterns characteristic of DCIS lesions. These findings underscore the potential role of MRI-based modeling in optimizing surgical planning for DCIS, a population where underestimation of disease extent remains a major clinical challenge. Furthermore, imaging characteristics such as BPE and NME—known to correlate with difficulty in achieving clear margins [45–47]—also followed expected trends in our dataset. The model’s stable performance across these subgroups suggested that deep-learning and radiomics representations may implicitly encode features related to tissue vascularity and background enhancement, thereby enhancing model generalizability.

The proposed workflow highlights the potential role of the model as an adjunctive decision-support tool for individualized surgical planning. The inclusion of a prospective cohort further supports its clinical applicability. Future studies will expand prospective data collection and evaluate the model in multicenter interventional trials providing real-time predictions to surgeons before or during BCS to determine whether model-guided strategies can reduce re-excision rates and improve local control.

Notably, the reduced predictive performance observed in Stage I patients in the external cohort highlights an important limitation of the current model. Early-stage tumors are typically small and show limited enhancement on DCE-MRI, which may reduce the discriminative power of imaging-derived features. Moreover, differences in data distribution across centers—including variations in positive-margin prevalence and surgical practice patterns—may affect model calibration and increase overlap between positive and negative cases. In this context, the model may be best applied as an adjunctive decision-support tool rather than a standalone determinant of surgical strategy for Stage I tumors. For clinical deployment, center-specific recalibration, such as threshold adjustment or fine-tuning using local data, may help improve performance and adapt the model to institution-specific characteristics.

Beyond surgical margin prediction, the model-derived margin-risk score was also associated with recurrence and overall survival. Patients classified as high-risk experienced more frequent recurrence and poorer survival despite achieving negative postoperative margins, consistent with previous evidence that margin difficulty may reflect biologically aggressive disease [7–9]. The high-risk group was also enriched for unfavorable molecular subtypes and locally advanced features, including multifocality, chest wall or skin invasion, and higher T/N stage, which are established predictors of recurrence and mortality [48–50]. Although the model was developed to predict positive margin risk rather than survival outcomes, the derived score may capture imaging phenotypes related to tumor extent and infiltrative growth patterns that influence both surgical margin difficulty and disease progression. Therefore, the score likely reflects a combination of factors related to surgical complexity and underlying tumor biology rather than representing a purely biology-driven prognostic model.

An important methodological aspect of this study is the handling of multifocal disease. For patients with multiple ipsilateral tumor foci, we used a composite ROI by merging all tumor voxels into a single union mask to preserve the overall spatial extent of disease relevant to surgical margin control in breast-conserving surgery. Under the PyRadiomics framework and standardized IBSI definitions, shape features are computed from the surface mesh of the ROI, and the composite ROI is treated as a single geometric entity. Accordingly, in multifocal cases, features such as Maximum3DDiameter reflect overall disease extent, while shape descriptors such as Sphericity and Compactness characterize the geometric compactness and spatial dispersion of the combined tumor distribution rather than the morphology of any individual focus. This distinction is important, as these features capture the overall disease footprint—including spatial extent and geometric complexity—which may influence the difficulty of achieving negative margins beyond the size of the dominant lesion. The use of unified ROIs for anatomically non-contiguous regions has also been described in prior radiomics studies [51], supporting the feasibility of this approach. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis excluding all patients with multiple ipsilateral lesions demonstrated comparable performance of the Clinical, Radiomics, Deep Learning, and Ensemble Models across the validation, external test, and prospective cohorts, suggesting that inclusion of multifocal cases did not materially influence the overall conclusions of this study (Supplementary Figs. S8 and S9). However, we acknowledge that this strategy may obscure inter-lesion heterogeneity, and future studies could explore lesion-wise modeling or multi-instance learning to better capture both lesion-level variability and spatial relationships.

There are several limitations to be acknowledged. First, positive margin rates and model performance varied across centers, likely reflecting differences in surgeon expertise, institutional surgical practices, and pathology assessment. Broader multicenter inclusion and standardized surgical workflows will be essential to further enhance model generalizability. Second, although the ensemble model significantly improved predictive accuracy, specificity remains an area for further enhancement, particularly in heterogenous real-world cohorts. Third, for patients with multifocal disease, all ipsilateral tumor foci were merged into a single union mask for radiomics extraction to represent the overall disease footprint relevant to margin risk. While clinically practical, this strategy may not fully capture heterogeneity between individual foci, and future studies with larger multifocal cohorts could explore focus-wise feature extraction and aggregation approaches (e.g., multi-instance learning). Finally, although preliminary prognostic analysis indicated potential prognostic utility, causal interpretation remains limited. Additional clinical trials are needed to determine whether model-informed surgical strategies can meaningfully improve patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study highlights the potential of ensemble learning to integrate multimodal data for precise risk prediction and decision support in BCS. By leveraging the strengths of clinical, radiomics, and DL features, the proposed model improves predictive accuracy for positive surgical margins and provides valuable prognostic insights. These findings pave the way for incorporating advanced predictive tools into routine clinical practice, fostering better outcomes for breast cancer patients.
