Effect of Yoga on Biomarkers and Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia in Participants with Hypertension from India’s Central Industrial Security Force
Authors: Shankar Lal Jat, Padmini Tekur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The central industrial security force (CISF) in India works in high-stress conditions, contributing to hypertension among its personnel. This study examined the impact of a yoga intervention on cardiovascular biomarkers among CISF personnel with hypertension.Methods:The randomized controlled trial consisted of 88 male CISF personnel aged 25–60 posted in the Indian Space Research Organization in Bengaluru. The yoga program included loosening exercises, pranayama, asanas, relaxation, and meditation. The intervention was conducted for 1 h daily, 5 days a week for 3 months. Cardiovascular biomarkers, blood pressure (BP), weight, height, and waist circumference were assessed at baseline and postintervention.Results:The yoga group demonstrated significant reductions in systolic BP (W = 1802.00, P < 0.001), diastolic BP (W = 1824.50, P < 0.001), and heart rate (W = 1685.00, P < 0.001) at postintervention. However, nonsignificant trends were observed in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), homocysteine levels, respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) per inspiration, and waist circumference. Furthermore, significant improvements were noted in RSA per expiration (W = 688.00, P = 0.002) and weight (W = 709.00, P = 0.03) in the yoga group compared to the control group.Conclusion:The study has shown the effectiveness of yoga in improving cardiovascular health among CISF personnel with hypertension. Significant reductions were observed in BP, heart rate, and weight. However, the study found nonsignificant changes in inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP and homocysteine. Yoga shows promise as a complementary therapy for managing hypertension alongside conventional treatment.

Introduction

The central industrial security force (CISF) is India’s leading multi-skilled security agency, operating under the Union Ministry of Home Affairs. Tasked with protecting vital infrastructure, its personnel routinely face intense work-related pressures that can contribute to the onset of hypertension. The demanding nature of their roles, characterized by long and irregular working hours, is a significant contributor to chronic stress – a key factor in the onset of hypertension.[1] In addition to this occupational strain, prolonged periods of standing and continuous alertness add to the physical burden of the job. Further, there are lifestyle factors such as smoking[2] and excessive alcohol consumption[3] that add to the risk for these security professionals.

Yoga is an ancient Indian mind-body practice that consists of physical postures, breathing exercises, meditation, and philosophical understandings and has shown enhancement in overall well-being. Previous findings confirm yoga’s cardiovascular effectiveness through parasympathetic activation via pranayama techniques and modulation of stress-inflammatory pathways. Studies by Cramer,[4] Tyagi and Cohen,[5] and Khandekar et al.[6] observed significant blood pressure (BP) reduction through improved vagal tone, diminished sympathetic dominance, and lowered cortisol/pro-inflammatory markers. Previous studies mostly focus on general populations, resulting in significant methodological gaps concerning occupation-specific groups. Security personnel have working patterns that include the demanding nature of work, prolonged hypervigilance, and chronic occupational hazards. This has created typical physiological stressor profiles and circadian imbalances that may lead to specific patterns of cardiovascular dysfunction requiring targeted treatment exploration.

This study explored biomarkers in line with yoga’s recognized cardiovascular processes. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was opted as the principal inflammatory marker due to its predictive validity for cardiovascular events and the previous finding highlighted as anti-inflammatory effects of yoga via parasympathetic modulation.[7] The evaluation of homocysteine was included as an independent cardiovascular risk factor and its possible sensitivity to stress-reduction techniques due to yogic practices.[8] Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) functioned as a cardiac autonomic evaluation, offering a quantitative assessment of parasympathetic nervous system activity, which has been observed as a physiological mechanism by which yoga confers cardiovascular advantages.[9]

Methods

This randomized controlled study utilized a pre-post design, including CISF posted at Indian Space Research Organization (ISRO), Bengaluru. Ethical approval has been obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, accompanied by registration with the Clinical Trials Registry-India. The informed consent was obtained after a thorough explanation of the procedure. Participants were recruited through collaboration between the ISRO medical department and CISF administration via (1) screening of medical records from routine examinations, (2) assessment of physician eligibility, and (3) formal enrolment procedures. The inclusion criteria were male CISF personnel aged 25–60 years diagnosed with hypertension (defined as a systolic BP of ≥140 mmHg, a diastolic BP of ≥90 mmHg, or the administration of antihypertensive medication) and possessing medical clearance for physical activity. Exclusion criteria: mental illnesses, recent surgical procedures, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes mellitus. Eighty-eight individuals were randomly assigned by computer-generated software to yoga and control groups (n = 44 each).

Intervention

The yoga intervention was based on a structured integrated yoga program consisting of asanas (physical postures), pranayama (breathing) techniques, and meditation practices. The implementation was over a 3-month period with 1-h sessions, conducted 5 days per week. The protocol included four main components. The loosening exercises prepare the body. Second were pranayama techniques, specifically Anuloma Viloma, Ujjayi, Sukha Pranayama, Bhramari, and Chandra Bhedana. Third were position-specific asanas: standing postures (Ardha Matsyendrasana, Ardha Chakrasana, Trikonasana, and Vrikshasana), seated postures (Vakrasana, Ardha Sirshasana, Ushtrasana, and Vajrasana), supine postures (Setu Bandhasana, Pavanamuktasana, Shavasana, and Uttanpada Pashchimottanasana), and prone postures (Bhujangasana, Shalabhasana, Dhanurasana, and Makarasana). Finally, contemplative practices comprised OM meditation and Nada-meditation techniques.

Yoga sessions were conducted in a facility within the ISRO residential complex, accommodating 8–10 participants per session. There were morning and evening schedules to match the shift work patterns of the personnel. The protocol was delivered by qualified yoga therapists holding master’s degrees in yoga therapy from an accredited university. To follow independent practice, participants received illustrated posture guidelines and instructional handouts. The residential setting enabled most favorable adherence monitoring. There were reported compliance issues or practice-related adverse events. The control group received no structured intervention and continued with their usual medical care and routine physical activity.

Assessments

Blood samples were collected at baseline and postintervention from fasting participants at the ISRO Government Hospital, Bengaluru. For each participant, a 5-mm venous blood sample was drawn using standard phlebotomy techniques. The primary focus was on hs-CRP and homocysteine levels, which were analyzed using standard kits on a semi-automatic analyzer. In addition, electrocardiograms were recorded using a 12-lead electrocardiograph (BPL India Ltd.). BP readings were obtained using a calibrated sphygmomanometer, while weight was measured with a digital scale, height using a stadiometer, and waist circumference with a nonstretchable tape. These assessments were conducted for each participant to provide a comprehensive evaluation.

Results

Several outcome variables (BP, hs-CRP, and homocysteine) demonstrated nonnormal distributions upon exploratory analysis. Parametric tests such as t-tests or analysis of variance assumptions were not met. Statistical analyses were performed using the Mann–Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, which are nonparametric methods.

The yoga group has a mean age of 51.82 ± 7.56 years, with 47.73% (n = 21) of participants on antihypertensive drugs. Control group: mean age 44.91 ± 10.52 years, with 40.91% (n = 18) on antihypertensive pharmacotherapy. Participants were instructed to continue their prescribed medications throughout the study period. Medication adherence and changes in medication frequency or dosage were not systematically monitored during the 3-month intervention period.

The data compilation and subsequent analyses were conducted utilizing the JASP (Version 0.11.1, JASP Team, Amsterdam, Netherlands) statistical software.[10]

Yoga group

Within-group analysis using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed significant reductions in weight (W = 703.00, z = 5.30, P < 0.001), body mass index (BMI) (W = 861.00, z = 4.06, P < 0.001), systolic BP (W = 928.50, z = 5.50, P < 0.001), diastolic BP (W = 853.50, z = 5.48, P < 0.001), and heart rate (W = 824.50, z = 4.24, P < 0.001) after the intervention. In addition, hs-CRP levels significantly decreased (W = 780.00, z = 5.44, P < 0.001). However, changes in homocysteine (W = 510.50, z = 0.74, P = 0.46), RSA per inspiration (W = 24.00, z = 1.69, P = 0.07), and waist circumference (W = 45.00, z = 1.78, P = 0.07) were not statistically significant.

Control group

In contrast, significant increases were observed in weight (W = 5.50, z = −4.99, P < 0.001), systolic BP (W = 57.00, z = −4.74, P < 0.001), and diastolic BP (W = 73.50, z = −3.70, P < 0.001). Significant reductions were found in hs-CRP (W = 152.00, z = −3.74, P < 0.001) and homocysteine (W = 176.50, z = −3.29, P = 0.001). However, BMI (W = 405.00, z = 1.59, P = 0.11) and waist circumference (W = 0.00, z = 3.06, P = 0.0016) showed different patterns: BMI did not significantly change, but waist circumference showed a small yet statistically significant increase. Changes in RSA per inspiration (W = 7.50, z = 0.91, P = 0.42) and RSA per expiration (W = 4.00, z = −1.69, P = 0.07) were also not statistically significant.

Between-group comparison

To assess the variations within the specified domains before and after the intervention, we applied the Mann–Whitney U-test, enabling a comparative analysis between the Yoga and control groups [Table 1]. Preintervention: no significant differences were observed in weight. BMI, hs-CRP levels, homocysteine levels, RSA, or waist circumference between the groups. Postintervention: the Yoga group showed significantly lower BMI compared to the Control group (W = 1224.00, P = 0.033), a greater reduction in weight (W = 709.00, P = 0.03), nonsignificant trends toward reduced hs-CRP levels (W = 786.00, P = 0.13), homocysteine levels (W = 1021.50, P = 0.66), RSA beats per inspiration (W = 843.50, P = 0.20), waist circumference (W = 959.50, P = 0.95), and a significant difference in RSA beats per expiration (W = 688.00, P = 0.002). Discussion

The yoga intervention in this study showed a range of cardiovascular benefits among CISF personnel with hypertension. Significant reductions were observed in RSA (measured as beats per expiration), body weight, systolic BP, diastolic BP, and heart rate in the yoga group compared to the control group. These improvements suggest enhanced cardiovascular regulation and better stress adaptation resulting from regular yoga practice. However, no significant changes were seen in hs-CRP levels, homocysteine levels, or waist circumference postintervention. Our study has shown the effectiveness of yoga-based programs in specific cardiovascular functions and supporting weight loss. However, the impact on the metabolic and inflammatory markers, such as hs-CRP and homocysteine, highlighted the need for further research.

A structured 3-month yoga program conducted through daily 1-h sessions of yoga has shown effectiveness in cardiovascular health and supporting weight management. Our findings are consistent with Park and Han and Lauche et al., finding reductions in body weight and both systolic and diastolic BP following yoga practice.[1112] However, hs-CRP level results contrast with the findings of Balaji et al., who observed significant reductions in hs-CRP levels among hypertensive patients following a 6-month yoga intervention.[13] This contrast underscores the importance of intervention duration in producing measurable changes in hs-CRP, a biomarker sensitive to chronic stress and inflammation. Maybe the shorter duration of our program was insufficient to elicit notable reductions in hs-CRP. The duration of the yoga program may be considered an important factor in impacting the changes on hs-CRP levels. These insights highlighted the need for optimal duration of yoga interventions to understand the impact of physiological and cardiovascular health outcomes.

The findings of homocysteine levels maintained no significant change following the yoga intervention. This trend was aligned with the previous findings in homocysteine among women with gestational diabetes mellitus.[14] However, in contrast to our study and that of Khandani et al., there was a reduction in homocysteine levels in adolescent females following yoga practice in Chien et al.’s research.[15] The conflicting results highlight that the impact of yoga intervention on homocysteine may have factors such as demographic and physiological factors. The results showed a need to understand different factors such as individual metabolic states, intervention duration, and the role of lifestyle variables in the creation of the yoga protocol. Well-designed studies with proper longitudinal follow-up studies taking care of interaction between individual metabolic states, intervention duration, and lifestyle variables may clarify how yoga modulates homocysteine levels across diverse populations.

The differential impact of a yoga intervention on RSA, measured as beats per inspiration and expiration, provides valuable insights into yoga’s specific effects on autonomic function. Postintervention comparisons for RSA measured as beats per inspiration showed no significant differences, suggesting that the yoga intervention may not have a substantial influence on the sympathetic nervous system,[16] which is more active during the inspiration phase of the respiratory cycle. However, the significant reduction in RSA beats per expiration in the yoga group can be associated with the parasympathetic pathways. The expiration phase of breathing is linked to the parasympathetic nervous system. The voluntary breathing and relaxation techniques have shown impact on vagal tone[16] and promote parasympathetic dominance.

Recent research observed regular yoga sessions helped relieve symptoms of chronic venous insufficiency among industrial workers. The findings included the signs of reduced vascular inflammation, which were supported by lower plasma homocysteine measurements. These findings demonstrate yoga’s potential for improving cardiovascular health and for addressing vascular conditions commonly seen in physically demanding occupations.[17] Yoga also reported impact on musculoskeletal pain and discomfort, alleviating perceived stress, and improving sleep quality among industry workers.[18] These emphasize yoga as an add-on to regular health promotion in the high-stress work environments.

The yoga protocol should also adopt a focused approach while targeting a high-stress occupational group. This training module should consider demographic constraints of the participant groups and the dose response of the intervention period to track long-term outcomes. It is also valuable to investigate different yoga components to understand their effects in high-stress-profile groups. Studies incorporating active comparisons with a control group, such as regular exercise or mindfulness practices, would enhance comparative analyses and help to understand yoga’s unique mechanism.

Conclusion

Yoga intervention significantly improved cardiovascular health in RSA beats per expiration, BP, heart rate, and body weight among CISF personnel with hypertension. The intervention has shown no impact on hs-CRP or homocysteine levels. These findings highlighted yoga’s potential as a complementary therapy for enhancing cardiovascular health. There is need for further in-depth studies to explore yoga’s effects on inflammatory markers and broader metabolic profiles to achieve the therapeutic benefits in stress profile working conditions.
