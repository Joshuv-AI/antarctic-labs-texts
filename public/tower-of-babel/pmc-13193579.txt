Automated full-text screening and accelerated reviews using large language models with context-aware agents: an exploratory analysis in biomarker research
Authors: Yongxin Ye, Martina Colombo, Jennifer Meessen, Negin Hajizadeh, Lauren De Vore, Hajar Ghaem Sigarchian, Christof Peters, Supraja Krovvidi, Shloke Meresh, Laurent Hasson, SaiRamesh Jakka, Benoit Tyl, Arantxa Barandiaran, Arantxa Barandiaran, Bjarke Folin, Deborah Novelli, Dirk Mueller-Wieland, Ece Buldurluoglu, Katarina Pardali, Maria Chiara Bassi, Michela Cinquini, Carolina Lavazza, Noemi Pavo, Paul Balfanz, Roberto Latini, Sara Klas, Silvia Maria Balosso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsArtificial intelligence (AI) tools utilizing large language models (LLMs) can accelerate scientific literature reviews by automating title, abstract, and full-text-based screenings of relevant patient populations and biomarkers. We developed an AI-based tool to automate and improve full-text screening performance using LLMs to accurately identify relevant publications that meet complex criteria.Methods and resultsWe conducted a literature review utilizing the Population, Intervention—biomarkers, Comparison, Outcome framework to define our inclusion and exclusion criteria, focusing on biomarkers in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). An AI-based full-text screening tool was created to process 5405 selected publications, combining multi-level and task-oriented retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and agent-based methods, establishing ground truth standards to evaluate performance metrics both for the tool and human reviewers. Intra-LLM reliability was assessed by rerunning screenings on a batch of publications. Among the public and private domain models, LLaMA 3.3 70B was selected for its superior accuracy (82%), precision (71%), and recall (100%) in screening 49 manuscripts by LLMs. During the training phase, based on several hundred manuscripts, performance metrics significantly improved. Validation results showed a sensitivity of 91.4%, specificity of 53.2%, a false positive rate of 46.8%, and a false negative rate of 8.6%. The LLM outperformed human reviewers in F1 score and interrater reliability, achieving 100% consistency across multiple runs, with each run consisting of multiple LLMs on 1000 documents.ConclusionOur study demonstrated that AI tool can reduce labour-intensive efforts while maintaining accuracy in literature reviews, with greater inter-rater agreement compared to human reviewers.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied in healthcare to extract and analyse information from complex scientific literature and documents.1,2 By automating tasks such as literature summarization and information extraction,3 LLMs can capture linguistic nuance and accelerate evidence synthesis and help researchers and clinicians answer targeted questions.4–6 However, reviewing biomarker studies is challenging. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) identifies multiple biomarker types and uses, each with different evidentiary requirements and implications for clinical practice.7 Moreover, primary studies often report biomarker results in inconsistent ways: Using non-standard terminology, varied assays, or unclear intended use, which hinders aggregation and comparability of different studies and complicates both automated and manual curation even for experienced researchers.

Literature screening faces additional challenges related to study design. Randomized intervention trials are relatively straightforward to extract information reliably due to their standardized structure and clear inclusion and exclusion criteria. By contrast, observational studies are more complex because of heterogeneity in design and reporting, nonstandard terminology, and the lack of uniform structure, which complicates automated parsing and extraction.8

Human screening is also imperfect and time-consuming. Preliminary evidence shows that inter- and intra-reader variability is common in human reading and interpretation of medical literature, especially when readers are not experienced.9,10 Consequently, LLMs may offer greater consistency in screening a variety of scientific documents (in various written language styles)—extracting relevant nuggets of information such as the presence and numerical values of biomarkers, and study outcomes.11

To date, most studies that explored the performance of LLMs for paper selection in systematic review mainly focused on title/abstract screening.12,13 Whether LLM performance is consistent and deterministic in such a challenging environment with high document variability, when assessing these studies, remains unknown.14 To overcome these limitations, reduce time consumption, and alleviate the burden of human reviewers, AI tools—such as LLMs—can assist or partially automate certain review steps of the review process.5,12 Besides, the rapid evolution of models requires evaluation frameworks that emphasize reproducibility, benchmarking procedures, and the ability to update assessments as models evolve. We therefore propose a reproducible framework for LLM-based full-text screening that defines standardized metrics, benchmarking procedures for new models, and recommendations for deployment and validation in domain-specific reviews. We implemented this AI-tool framework to assist in selecting manuscripts for reviewing circulating biomarkers in HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) as part of the iCARE4CVD project.15

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria of publications

Our review primarily includes clinical trials, real-world evidence studies, and various types of reviews, such as narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. We used the Population, Intervention—biomarkers, Comparison, Outcome framework16 to define the inclusion and exclusion criteria for publications (Supplementary material online, Table S1). We aimed to identify studies on the use of biomarkers in HFrEF, defined either as patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% or those receiving continuous flow left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), or described as having HF with systolic dysfunction. Studies were also included if they provided a subgroup analysis of HFrEF as previously defined. The main exclusion criteria were studies on HF with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (HFpEF or LVEF > 50%), new onset HF, heart transplant, and congenital heart disease. A list of 21 biomarkers of interest was generated by cardiologists and biomarker experts of the iCARE4CVD consortium (Supplementary material online, Table S2). To identify relevant literature for the review, a comprehensive search was conducted in the MEDLINE and EMBASE databases, covering all records from database inception through July 2024 using a specific search string (Supplementary material S1).

AI-based full-text screening and publication selection

To classify scientific papers retrieved from the literature according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, we developed an AI-based tool that integrates retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and agent-based processing methods.17 We began with general prompts, observed unreliable and non-auditable outputs, demonstrating that a standalone prompt was insufficient for reliable screening. We therefore implemented a RAG + agent architecture to overcome the nondeterministic nature of LLM. The overall architecture and workflow of the AI-based document analysis tool are depicted in Figure 1 and are subsequently detailed below, organized according to the four sections presented therein.

Illustration of the AI-based approach for selecting the publication. 10 ‘criteria’ agents and 6 ‘biomarker’ agents that we templatized to apply to the 21 biomarkers.

PDF document handling (Figure 1, step 1)

The PDF documents of all full text manuscripts have first been downloaded manually and stored into a private data lake to preserve the privacy and security of the content, and this serves as a centralized repository. Once stored, the whole documents undergo a pre-processing phase where their content is parsed and segmented into smaller units called chunks. We selected ∼1000-character chunks (with overlap) after iterative development to balance context for task-specific prompts with traceability of evidence. This chunk size, combined with top-10 retrieval per task, improved retrieval precision and reduced hallucination risk for our structured extraction tasks. Further details are provided in Supplementary material S2. Each chunk is then vectorized by converting it into a high-dimensional numerical representation using language embedding models and finally stored in a vector database.18 Some PDF documents retrieved through the search strategy were not text-based and were therefore excluded. They consisted of scanned pages that would have required optical character recognition or other image-based models, which were beyond the scope of this work.

Text search (Figure 1, step 2)

At this stage, two categories of engineered prompts are developed: 10 static prompts addressing inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 6 templatized prompts applied across the 21 biomarkers of interest. The latter combination generates 126 task-specific instances, which, together with the static set, yield a total of 136 tuned and validated prompts addressing 136 tasks. Each task is a collection of prompts consisting of words and phrases (Supplementary material S3). This focused configuration enables the LLM to achieve higher accuracy while mitigating the scope of hallucinations. All prompts are subsequently vectorized, meaning that they are converted into floating-point numerical values that represent the words and their relationships in a mathematical form or a vector of numbers. This is done to enable similarity-based search with the massively large vector database of tens of thousands of scientific documents.

To support the text search process, the AI tool uses a previously constructed medical dictionary (Supplementary material S4), which includes relevant terminology from UMLS, rxNorm, SNOMED and MeSH, abbreviations, and domain-specific expressions. To ensure proper interpretation of the abbreviations, synonyms, or shorthand form by the LLM, we developed the dictionary iteratively: cardiology experts and reviewers first provided core terms and known variants, we then extracted candidate synonyms and abbreviations from a sample of the corpus, and finally curated and validated entries to ensure clinical correctness. This hybrid semi-supervised process (expert seed + corpus driven extraction + manual curation) ensured the dictionary captured both canonical terminology and real-world variants while keeping the list focused and relevant. This allows the AI tool to identify related content, even when medical terms are presented as abbreviations or alternative scientific notations, addressing a common limitation of standard LLMs, which often struggle with the inconsistent language of cardiovascular literature (for example, ‘ejection fraction’ can appear as ‘EF,’ ‘LVEF,’ or ‘ejection fraction ratio’).

At this point, the tool uses a similarity-based search to link each task to relevant parts of the document. This innovative approach compares the vectorized prompt against the contents of the vector database, identifying the ten best chunks from each full text. These chunks represent the most relevant sections of the PDF that have the closest match (i.e. the lowest cosine distance) to the terms and word combinations in a prompt. This ensures that the identified sections are most likely to contain the necessary information for addressing the corresponding task.19

LLM task execution (Figure 1, step 3)

After retrieval, the 10 most relevant chunks for each prompt are passed to an LLM agent (an encapsulated software component designed to carry out a specific task) along with a set of guidelines and system instructions explaining how to complete it.20 Each LLM agent is responsible for generating the best possible response to its assigned task, based specifically on the relevant document chunks retrieved by the RAG method.21

Document classification (Figure 1, step 4)

The resulting responses from each LLM agent are collected in tabular format (a table in a relational database and/or an Excel file), where each row represents a document with a unique identifier (document ID), and each column presents the output of a specific task (see Supplementary material online, Table S3). For each publication, the set of task responses is then logically combined into a final column, which summarizes the assessment of each inclusion and exclusion criteria and provides an overall decision on whether the paper should be included or excluded.

The processes described above are orchestrated by a central component, the LLM manager, which serves as the core engine of the AI system. It coordinates all steps, from managing documents to extracting relevant information, running the RAG process, activating the LLMs for each task, and collecting and verifying their responses. The LLM Manager also handles document classification and manages any processing errors. Beyond these core functions, it ensures the secure handling of private documents and controls access permissions. For a full description, see Supplementary material S5.

LLM model selection

For each publication, the AI tool first evaluates two independent domains: HFrEF-related criteria and biomarker-related criteria. Each domain is assessed through a predefined set of task-specific prompts, with each prompt addressing a single focused question. The outputs of these prompts are then combined using a deterministic, Boolean rule-based aggregation logic to generate two intermediate decisions: HFrEF_DECISION and Biomarker_DECISION (Supplementary materials S3). The final eligibility decision is derived from these two intermediate outputs. A publication is included only if both HFrEF_DECISION and Biomarker_DECISION are true; otherwise, it is excluded. This structure ensures that inclusion requires satisfaction of both the HFrEF-related and biomarker-related criteria according to predefined logical rules. To screen candidate LLMs quickly for our AI tool, we evaluated several models on a subset of 49 open-access publications randomly selected from our set of papers.22 Their outputs were compared to human-generated references to assess classification performance using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and error rates (Table 1). This pilot informed initial choices but was not intended as a definitive statistical comparison. In selecting the final model, criteria such as LLM privacy and openness were prioritized, favouring models that can be run locally in a controlled environment, where data access permissions can be managed, and documents remain fully private to protect copyright (Supplementary material S6). We then validated the full AI-based tool pipeline on larger training (n = 200) and validation (n = 97) sets to assess end-to-end performance.

Training and validation of an AI tool

The overall development process of the AI tool, including both the training and validation phases, is illustrated in Figure 2.

Workflow of training and validation process.

Training process

The training process includes two components: the human-based and the AI-based selection process.

Human-based selection

A human-based double-blind review process was implemented to obtain the ground truth standard of paper exclusion and inclusion. The training process involved 200 publications randomly selected from the 5405 identified studies (i.e. 3.6% of the total dataset), divided into two batches: batch 1 and batch 2, with 100 publications each. Five pairs of reviewers, one senior and one junior, independently screened title, abstract and full text of the two batches of publications and applied inclusion/exclusion criteria to determine whether they should be included or excluded, with each pair of reviewers evaluating the same 20 publications in each batch. In case of discrepancy between the two reviewers, the undecided publications were adjudicated by a third reviewer, an expert of HF. The ground-truth standard obtained by this double-blind review, together with the expert adjudication, was used to evaluate the performance of the AI tool.

AI-based selection

During the training phase, the AI tool has been run multiple times on batches 1 and 2. The objective of these repeated runs was to progressively improve performance using a test-and-learn approach. After each iteration, the output produced by the LLM agents across the 136 tasks was assessed by comparing each result to the human-generated reference standard. Whenever an LLM output was found to be incorrect, the corresponding prompt for that specific task was fine-tuned to obtain the appropriate response. This training process continued until the AI tool consistently achieved at least 80% accuracy in identifying the required information. While running the first iterations, the LLM model struggled to correctly recognize certain domain-specific terms, abbreviations, and short forms used in the text, defining the need to create the medical dictionary previously discussed (Supplementary material S4). Furthermore, insufficient inter-reader agreement on batch 1 led us to analyse the most frequent discrepancies between human reviewers and refine the criteria used by human and the AI tool, while also providing detailed explanations to guide the review of the next batches. We specified, for instance, that studies with mixed HF populations (e.g. combining HFpEF and HFrEF) could be included if a subgroup analysis of HFrEF patients was available. Following the evaluation of batch 2, the false negative rate remained above 10%. To address this, we further fine-tuned the prompts with a specific focus on reducing false negatives and further increasing sensitivity to minimize the risk of excluding relevant publications. An example of the update in LLM screening output is described in detail in Supplementary material online, Table S3.

Validation process

Once trained, the AI tool was applied to the remaining 5205 publications. From this set, 100 publications were randomly selected for model performance evaluation (validation batch). These publications were assessed by human reviewers following the same process as previous batches. The AI tool’s results were then compared to those of the human reviewers to calculate performance metrics.

Intra-LLM reliability

To evaluate the reliability of the selected LLM across multiple runs, we applied the same full-text screening process on batch 1 publications three times and compared the results for each run.

Statistical analysis

The full-text screening performances of both the AI-tool and human reviewers were determined by calculating the sensitivity, specificity, false positive rate, false negative rate, and positive predictive value (PPV). PPV measures the proportion of correctly included publications, indicating the precision of LLM full-text screening and selection. We also calculated the comprehensive indicator F1 Score with ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect precision (the proportion of true positive results in all positive predictions) and perfect recall (the proportion of true positive results in all actual positive cases). Two Cohen’s kappa coefficients (κ) were computed. The first assessed inter-rater agreement between two independent reviewers (a junior and a senior reviewer). The second assessed the agreement between the LLM-generated results and the human ground truth, defined by two reviewer concordance and, when discrepancies occurred, by adjudication from a heart failure expert. This statistic accounts for agreement occurring by chance and ranges from −1 to +1. The interpretation of Cohen’s κ is as follows: values 0.0–0.20 indicate no agreement, 0.21–0.40 indicate minimal, 0.41–0.59 indicate weak, 0.60–0.79 indicate moderate, and 0.80–0.90 indicate almost perfect agreement.23 Additionally, prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) was calculated with prevalence and bias effect correction, which tends to provide a more reliable assessment in imbalanced datasets than Cohen’s κ.

Results

Identification of papers

We identified 6363 publications through the search string. After the removal of duplicates, the dataset was reduced to 5523 unique records. Of these, 118 were excluded either due to language issues (51 papers non-English) or because the full text was not freely accessible (67 papers), resulting in 5405 publications (Figure 3).

LLM model selection

As shown in Table 1, by applying the same approach, the best performing LLMs were GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.3 70B, both achieving the highest accuracy (82%), precision (71%), and recall (100%), with no false negatives. Among the smaller language models, the performance dropped notably for LLaMA 3.2 3B (3B implies 3 Billion parameters used for modelling), and GEMINI 2 Flash showed lower accuracy and struggled to maintain balanced precision and recall; LLaMA 3.1 8B and LLaMA 3.2 11B had relatively high false positive rates, leading to modest precision. Within the available LLMs at the time of the study, we selected LLaMA 3.3 70B for integration into the AI tool as it offered the best balance of precision, recall, and overall accuracy, while also being an open-source model and privacy-compliant when deployed and executed locally in a controlled and secure environment.

In the training phase, AI-based screening showed significant improvement in performance metrics from batch 1 to batch 2. Sensitivity increased from 77.8% to 81.8%, and specificity rose from 62.5% to 78.2%. The false positive rate decreased from 37.5% to 21.4%, and the false negative rate decreased from 22.2% to 18.2%, while still exceeding the predefined 10% threshold. As specified in the methods section, the model was adjusted to focus on reducing false negatives and further increasing sensitivity. Therefore, in the validation phase, the model demonstrated a false positive rate of 46.8%, a sensitivity of 91.4%, a specificity of 53.2%, and a false negative rate of 8.6%. Overall, the highest training F1 score was recorded in batch 2 at 0.78, indicating better precision and recall, while the validation phase scored an F1 of 0.67. Regarding interrater reliability, Cohen’s κ in the validation phase was 0.38 (PABAK: 0.39), slightly higher than batch 1 (Cohen’s κ: 0.34, PABAK: 0.43) but lower than batch 2 (Cohen’s κ: 0.59, PABAK: 0.60) (Table 2).

In the performance comparison between the LLM and human reviewers, the LLM’s full-text screening outperformed human reviewers. After considering both precision and recall, LLM showed a higher F1 score (batch 1: 0.58 vs. human reviewers 0.48, batch 2: 0.78 vs. human reviewers 0.74, validation: 0.67 vs. human reviewers 0.56) and a high Cohen’s kappa value (LLM vs. human reviewers: batch 1: 0.34:0.21, batch 2: 0.59:0.57, validation: 0.38:0.23). Discussion

Our study demonstrated the potential of an LLM-driven, agent-based AI tool to reduce labour-intensive efforts while maintaining accuracy in literature reviews. Its reliability is further reinforced by greater inter-rater agreement of the AI tool between the same batch compared to human reviewers. The AI tool is both accurate in its positive selection (inclusion of papers) and effective at capturing relevant information for the assessment of inclusion/exclusion criteria, making it a valuable instrument for the time-consuming task of paper selection. In fact, our results demonstrated that the AI tool can perform the same publication screening task in 2 min, making it five times faster than a human reviewer, who requires at least 10 min.

Comparison with other AI tools

Prior to 2024, the landscape of literature focused on LLM full-text screening was limited, as most studies concentrated on title and abstract screening,13,24,25 only four studies25–27 have implemented full-text screening with different LLMs and in different disease domains, reporting sensitivities between 32.7% and 87.0%. In contrast, our study achieved a commendable sensitivity of 91.4%, highlighting our model’s ability to minimize the risk of overlooking relevant studies. While the specificity of our training batches falls within the range reported in previous studies (69.0% to 96.0%), the validation specificity is slightly lower at 53.2%, reflecting a sensitivity trade-off associated with the willingness to minimize the false negative rate. Furthermore, our study screened over 5000 publications, significantly exceeding the volume in other full-text screening studies, which ranged from 14625 to 150.28

We developed a comprehensive automated AI-tool system for full-text screening, by using a graph-based task structure for LLMs to process complex clinical criteria, and not just a few prompts for an LLM to process at once. This approach that integrates RAG and task-based LLM searches enables the retriever to encode the full text of the publications (PDF form) and ensure that our tool is adaptable and can leverage any LLM effectively, but simply by implementing any new LLM without specific adaptation. Several measures were taken to help the LLM to stay focused on the relevant evidence and reduce errors. First, narrow task-specific prompts so each agent answers one precise question, improving relevance and accuracy. Second, targeted retrieval, in which only the 10 most relevant text chunks are retrieved and provided, reduces noise and ensures grounded, source-based answers. Finally, a curated medical dictionary clinician-refined prompts standardize terminology and account for variations in reporting across publications. This AI-tool based approach scales by allowing quick evaluation of newer models against established baselines in the fast pace of development in the field of open-source LLMs. The enhanced performance is a result of the overall architecture rather than solely that of the LLM used. This sets our approach apart from previous studies that frequently relied only on the capabilities of specific individual LLMs.

Enhanced reliability

To ensure a stable and robust reference standard, we implemented a double-blind human review process with adjudication by an expert. Conducting this alongside LLM development strengthened the workflow and more closely reflected real-world paper selection, helping us to establish a reliable ground truth. Human reviewers generally demonstrate better consistency than LLMs as measured by κ values that indicate varying levels of agreement (higher values reflect a stronger consensus beyond chance, with values above 0.6 generally considered good). For example, Khraisha et al. reported markedly higher inter-rater agreements compared to LLM after full-text review (κ of 0.77 vs. 0.07, respectively).28 In contrast, our AI-tool approach showcased a higher inter-rater agreement (κ=0.58, 0.78, 0.38 in different batches) compared to human reviewers (κ = 0.34, 0.59, 0.23 in different batches), indicating that our approach provides more consistent and reliable results in identifying relevant publications based on predefined criteria. Furthermore, the high intra-LLM reliability of our AI tool underscores the approach’s robustness and its ability to replicate results accurately, ensuring minimal risk of hallucinations across multiple trials. We also observed that human reviewers in our study showed lower inter-rater agreement compared to other publications, where values ranged from 0.46 to 0.98.28,29 This reduced consistency may be attributed to the complexity of the inclusion/exclusion criteria, which required further clarification after initial attempts, as well as the variability among reviewers, often composed of a mix of senior and junior researchers, not all familiar with HF. A recent paper showed that the more experienced the reviewer, the highest the chance to outperform LLM.30 While we do not claim that LLMs outperform experienced reviewers, in practice, such expertise is rarely available to evaluate thousands of papers at scale. Other challenges, such as time constraints and potential biases, may also impact the reliability of human evaluation. Consequently, our AI tool can serve as a valuable complement to human reviewers, especially in large-scale reviews, when highly trained reviewers familiar with the topics are not available.

Challenges in LLM screening

After careful review of the discrepancies between the human and the AI tool in the training phase, two issues have been identified triggering the tool to exclude manuscripts, which should have been included. First, papers presenting the population of interest (HFrEF) as a subgroup analysis were initially excluded, leading to a number of false negatives. Even if a publication had around 5% HFrEF population in the study protocol, then that publication could be eligible for review, provided data are presented separately from HFpEF data. Similarly, scientific documents investigating general populations and HF biomarkers with corresponding LVEF-specific values (that met criteria) should have been considered for inclusion. Second, changes in definitions over time might prove difficult for LLMs to judge papers, while a specialist in HF should be aware of the evolution defining the need for a specific programming algorithm. For instance, publications written during the 1980s and 1990s used the term ‘Acute systolic,’ and the AI tool excluded them as they did not meet any of the LVEF criteria; prior to the 1990s, LVEF was rarely used as a term. This challenge arises from the historical evolution of heart failure classification, as earlier literature frequently used descriptive terminology prior to the standardization of LVEF-based definitions. The issue was addressed during the training phase through targeted prompt refinement conducted in collaboration with cardiologists. This experience highlights that, in clinical domains where definitions evolve over time, AI-assisted screening benefits from structured expert oversight to ensure accurate interpretation and reduce the risk of incorrectly excluding relevant studies.

Sensitivity-specificity trade-off

Different search strategies for systematic literature reviews yield varying levels of sensitivity and specificity, often requiring a trade-off based on expected outcomes. Strategies prioritizing higher sensitivity typically involve a more labour-intensive analysis due to the increased number of selected papers. Our AI tool exhibited strong performance after initial training, as evidenced by batch 2 results. However, a false discovery rate of approximately 20% was considered too high, risking the exclusion of significant manuscripts. For example, the LLM initially excluded publications from the 1980s and 1990s that included ‘acute systolic heart failure’ descriptions because they did not meet contemporary HFrEF criteria; prior to the 1990s, specific LVEF parameters were often not included in studies. We revised our guidelines to include these papers, lowering the false discovery rate to under 10%, although this increased the false positive rate. These findings underscore that the trade-offs seen in previous studies are also relevant to LLMs and must be considered when balancing the risks of falsely excluding papers against the potential benefits of LLMs in reducing the manual workload by including more manuscripts for analysis. In short, the objective of the LLM-assisted manuscript review for inclusion/exclusion was not to achieve the highest precision. Rather, it aimed to exclude a significant proportion of manuscripts with the highest level of certainty, thereby facilitating manual review while ensuring that the risk of falsely excluding relevant manuscripts was minimized.

Limitations

While our study illustrates significant advancements in the AI-tools based approach for full-text screening, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the Cohen’s kappa values in human reviewers were relatively lower than the LLM, indicating a moderate level of agreement between reviewers. This moderate agreement can be attributed to several factors, including the subjective nature of evaluation, diversity in the reviewer expertise, different understanding of the criteria, and the potential cognitive biases impacting individual judgements. The moderate level of agreement between human reviewers may make it challenging to determine a clear benchmark for performance, making it harder to assess how well the LLM performs in comparison. Besides, the imbalanced data distributions, characterized by unequal inclusion and exclusion rates within a dataset (e.g. an imbalance rate of 0.42 in batch 1 during training), could lead to overfitting due to the underrepresentation of one class, also resulting in higher false positive and false negative rates in the screening process. However, the overall accuracy of the sensitivity from the entire list of documents did improve. A recent study using LLMs for extracting structured data from cardiology reports also faced similar methodological challenges, including using LLMs to classify complex domain-specific text, benchmarking against human reviewers, and dealing with ambiguity and class imbalance.31 Finally, the exclusion of publications due to language barriers, inaccessible formats, and lack of accessibility may also limit the comprehensiveness of our literature review, potentially leaving out relevant studies that could further inform our findings.

Broader implications

These findings underscore that LLM-based AI tools could accelerate the systematic review process by full-text screening in medical research, enhance efficiency, and improve the quality of manuscript selection for reviews. Our study demonstrates how such tools can effectively capture and summarize real-world evidence, serving as a proof of concept for an integrated AI-tool approach in medical research, including analysing clinical pathways from de-identified medical records in text formats. Furthermore, a nuanced understanding of the balance between sensitivity and specificity in screening practices can inform the development of future AI applications. This integrated approach has the potential to comprehensively assess medical data and publications, facilitating the exploration of complex research questions and ultimately enhancing decision-making in clinical practice.

Future development

Our current work focused on extraction and eligibility assessment rather than analysing biomarker–outcome relationships. The current pipeline uses narrow, task-specific prompts, targeted retrieval of relevant text chunks, and a curated medical dictionary to improve reliability for screening tasks. Interpreting biomarker–outcome links, such as for prognostic modelling, meta-analytic effect synthesis, or biomarker-guided trial design, typically requires causal or logic-based reasoning and integration of additional data. Incorporating knowledge-graph enrichment, symbolic validation, or causal-inference components into the RAG + agent architecture would be a logical direction to support such deeper interpretation.
