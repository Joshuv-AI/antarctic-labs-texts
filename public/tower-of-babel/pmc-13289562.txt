Oral alginate microspheres deliver Rg3/aspirin liposomes to modulate foam cells and gut microbiota in atherosclerosis
Authors: Yong-bo Liu, Jing-tian Zhang, Jun-nan Hu, Shen Ren, De-yang Huo, Ling-jie Song, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a complex pathology driven by chronic inflammation, lipid accumulation, and thrombosis, necessitating multi-pathway therapeutic strategies. Herein, we engineered an oral multi-stage platform (RALM) comprising aspirin (Asp) and ginsenoside Rg3 co-loaded phosphatidylserine (PS) liposomes (RA-Lipo) encapsulated within sodium alginate microspheres. Physicochemical and biological evaluations revealed that the incorporation of Rg3 into the lipid bilayer as a cholesterol substitute yielded uniform RA-Lipo, which exhibited colloidal stability, intestinal epithelial transport, and prolonged systemic circulation. The electrosprayed alginate matrix functioned as a pH-responsive barrier, preventing premature degradation in the gastric environment and enabling the sustained release of RA-Lipo within the intestinal tract. Following oral administration in HFD-induced ApoE−/− mice, integrated 16 S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics revealed that RALM reshaped HFD-induced dysbiosis and modulated the metabolic profile, characterized by an increased Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes (B/F) ratio along with decreased prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and elevated arginine levels. Fluorescence tracking experiment further demonstrated that the liberated RA-Lipo accumulated within aortic plaques. At the cellular level, RA-Lipo activated the PPARγ pathway, thereby enhancing cholesterol efflux and inhibiting foam cell formation. Ultimately, this synergistic regulation translated into remarkable therapeutic efficacy, effectively reducing plaque burden, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and demonstrating superior anticoagulant activity. Supported by a favorable safety evaluation, this RALM platform offers a comprehensive approach for alleviating atherosclerosis progression.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04478-z.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic and progressive vascular disease that serves as the pathological basis of most cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [1, 2], making it a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. The pathological mechanisms of AS are largely driven by endothelial dysfunction, which increases vascular permeability and facilitates the infiltration of monocytes and lipids into the subendothelial space [3, 4]. This process promotes the formation of foam cells and plaque growth, accompanied by the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [5, 6]. Ultimately, plaque rupture triggers thrombosis, leading to acute cardiovascular events. While current therapeutic strategies focus on alleviating inflammation, improving lipid metabolism, or reducing thrombogenesis [7, 8], these pharmacological agents typically possess a single mechanism of action and lack lesion-specific targeting. Furthermore, although guideline-directed therapies such as statins and aspirin (Asp) reduce risk, residual events, long-term adverse effects, and incomplete control of the multifactorial pathobiology remain major challenges [9, 10]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for integrated therapeutic approaches that can simultaneously orchestrate multiple biological pathways for AS management.

Natural products, particularly ginsenosides, have emerged as promising candidates for multi-target AS management due to their excellent biosafety and pleiotropic effects. For example, ginsenoside Rg3 has been reported to ameliorate plaque burden in high-fat diet-fed ApoE−/− mice and in diabetic AS models, potentially via PPARγ activation, while also suppressing the expression of cell adhesion molecules [11, 12]. In addition, ginsenoside Re has been shown to alleviate AS by inhibiting endothelial cell migration through modulation of PFKFB3-mediated glycolysis [13]. Similarly, several studies have reported that co-administration of certain ginsenosides with Asp can enhance aspirin bioavailability and attenuate Asp-induced gastric ulceration and mucosal injury [14, 15]. However, the therapeutic potential of these compounds is often hampered by their poor systemic bioavailability and lack of lesion-specific accumulation.

To address these delivery barriers, liposomes provide a biocompatible and highly tunable framework for therapeutic intervention. Phosphatidylserine (PS)-modified liposomes have demonstrated the capacity to selectively target macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques by engaging scavenger receptors (SRs) [16, 17]. Nevertheless, PS-modified liposomes frequently encounter limitations, including compromised colloidal stability and rapid systemic clearance within complex physiological environments [18]. Recent studies suggest that substituting cholesterol in the liposomal bilayer with ginsenoside Rg3 introduces abundant glycosyl ligands, which significantly heighten the surface polarity of the vesicles. This increased polarity facilitates the formation of a robust hydration layer on the nanoparticle surface, thereby enhancing colloidal stability [19, 20].

Furthermore, for chronic conditions such as AS that necessitate long-term management, conventional intravenous administration is often limited by poor patient compliance due to the requirement for frequent dosing. In recent years, modulating the intestinal microenvironment has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for AS intervention [21, 22]. However, our findings indicated that RA-Lipo (Asp and ginsenoside Rg3 co-loaded PS liposomes) was susceptible to phospholipid hydrolysis and structural disintegration within the acidic gastric environment, leading to premature drug leakage. As shown in Scheme 1, to enhance stability within the gastrointestinal tract, we encapsulated these engineered liposomes into acid-resistant sodium alginate (SA) microspheres, termed RALM. By leveraging the pH-responsive properties of the SA hydrogel, this architecture serves as a robust physical barrier that shields the liposomes from gastric acid erosion, thereby preserving their structural integrity [23]. Additionally, compared with other intestinal-targeted formulations, the inherent mucoadhesive characteristics of SA prolonged the residence time of the carrier within the intestinal mucosa [24, 25], ensuring the site-specific intestinal release of RA-Lipo and facilitating its subsequent accumulation within atherosclerotic lesions. Finally, the integrated therapeutic efficacy of RALM, including anti-platelet activity, lipid regulation, and remodeling of the intestinal microenvironment, was systematically evaluated in vivo. Particular attention was devoted to assessing the system’s protective capacity against Asp-induced gastric mucosal injury and its long-term biosafety profile.

Materials and methods

Materials

Soybean lecithin (PC) was purchased from Thermo Scientific. PS was obtained from Shanghai Yingxin Laboratory, and cholesterol was provided by Beijing Biotopped Technology Co., Ltd. Asp was acquired from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Oil Red O staining solution and commercial kits for TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and Rhodamine B were purchased from MCE (Beijing, China). Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) was obtained from Yiyuan Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). mPEG2000-DSPE was obtained from Macklin. All other chemical solvents were supplied by the Beijing Chemical Reagent Research Institute (Beijing, China). The primary rabbit monoclonal antibody against Lamin B was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Primary rabbit monoclonal antibodies against ABCA1, ABCG1, PPARγ, and GAPDH were purchased from Proteintech (USA).

Preparation of RA-Lipo and fluorescently labeled liposomes

The lipid components, including PC, PS, Rg3 (or cholesterol), and Asp, were weighed at a mass ratio of 25:15:5:10 and dissolved in 1.5 mL of anhydrous ethanol to form the lipid phase. Nanoliposomes were fabricated using a microfluidic platform at a total flow rate of 16 mL/min and a lipid-to-aqueous phase (ultrapure water) volume ratio of 1:5. The collected liposomal suspension was diluted with an equal volume of ultrapure water, dialyzed, and subsequently lyophilized to obtain RA-Lipo and CA-Lipo. For the preparation of PEGylated liposomes, mPEG2000-DSPE was incorporated into the lipid phase at a mass ratio of 1:40 relative to the total phospholipids, while all other parameters remained unchanged.

To prepare Rhodamine B-labeled liposomes, Rhodamine B was dissolved in the aqueous phase at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL prior to the microfluidic process described above. The lipid phase was prepared following the aforementioned procedure. To ensure stable fluorescent labeling and minimize disruption to the liposomal surface structure, Rhodamine B was encapsulated within the aqueous core and anchored to the inner leaflet of the lipid bilayer via electrostatic interaction between the positively charged Rhodamine B and the anionic phospholipids. The collected suspension was then dialyzed against double-distilled water using a dialysis membrane (MWCO 12 kDa) for 12 h at 4 °C in the dark to remove unencapsulated Rhodamine B.

Characterization and stability evaluation

The composition and chemical structure of RA/CA-Lipo were analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (PerkinElmer). The particle size and zeta potential of the liposomes were measured via DLS (Brookhaven Instruments, USA).

To evaluate the stability of RA-Lipo and CA-Lipo, the formulations were incubated in PBS and 10% FBS. The physical stability was monitored by measuring the average particle size over a 12 h period. Additionally, the drug leakage of Asp was quantified over 24 h using HPLC (Waters 2489 UV/Vis, USA).

Preparation and characterization of Rg3&Asp-liposomes microspheres (RALM)

RALM were prepared using electrospray (ES) technology. 100 mg of RA-Lipo and 300 mg of SA were dissolved in 10 mL of double-distilled water, followed by stirring to achieve a homogeneous dispersion. The dispersed phase was loaded into a syringe equipped with a 17-gauge needle, which was connected to a high-voltage electrospray apparatus. The voltage was set to 22,000 V, and the flow rate was maintained at 0.65 mm/min. The RALM particles were collected in a bath containing 3% (w/v) CaCl₂ in double-distilled water under continuous stirring. Subsequently, the RALM were washed three times with double-distilled water and lyophilized for storage. Additionally, microspheres containing Rhodamine B-loaded liposomes (RB&RALM) were prepared as the samples. The surface morphology and elemental composition of the RALM particles were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

Stability and drug release of RA-Lipo and RALM in simulated gastrointestinal conditions

The stability of RALM under simulated gastrointestinal conditions was evaluated by monitoring their morphological changes. Freshly prepared RALM were first incubated in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, 37 °C), and samples were withdrawn at predetermined time points (0, 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 h). Following SGF incubation, the microspheres were separated from the medium using a mesh filter and subsequently transferred into simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, 37 °C) for continued incubation. Morphological observations were performed at 6.0, 7.5, 9.0, and 10.5 h using an optical microscope. In a parallel experiment, RALM suspensions were divided into two independent batches and incubated separately in SGF or SIF, with morphological assessments performed at 0, 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 h to compare structural stability in each medium.

To evaluate the drug release profiles of RA-Lipo, samples were placed in SGF and SIF at 37 °C. The contents of Asp and Rg3 were quantified at 0, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 min using HPLC.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 and IEC-6 cells (Fuheng Biotechnology) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (all from Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the culture medium was replaced every 48 h.

To establish the in vitro foam cell model, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and allowed to adhere. The cells were then stimulated with oxLDL (5–160 µg/mL, diluted in DMEM from a 1 mg/mL stock) for 24 h. Based on preliminary experiments, 80 µg/mL oxLDL was determined as the optimal concentration for model induction. Subsequently, the foam cells were treated with various concentrations of Rg3 (5, 10, and 20 µM) for 24 h. Quantitative analysis of the images was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Cellular uptake of liposomes by RAW264.7 cells

To evaluate the targeting capability of the liposomes, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well, after 12 h of incubation, the cells were divided into oxLDL(-) and oxLDL(+) groups, the latter was stimulated with oxLDL (80 µg/mL) for 24 h. Both groups were then incubated with Rhodamine B-labeled CA-Lipo, PEG-CA-Lipo, or RA-Lipo (all at a final concentration of 200 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, stained with DAPI, and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Leica) and a confocal microscope. For the uptake mechanism studies, oxLDL-treated RAW264.7 cells were pre-incubated with 30 mM glucose for 60 min prior to the addition of liposomes.

In vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution analysis

The body circulation time of liposomes was assessed as described previously [26]. The Rhodamine B labeled CA-Lipo (RB&CA-Lipo), PEG-CA-Lipo (RB&PEG-CA-Lipo), and RA-Lipo (RB&RA-Lipo) were then administered to C57BL/6 mice via tail vein injection at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected from the submandibular vein at 0.25, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection, The fluorescence intensity of the serum was measured at an emission wavelength of 554 nm. Pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed using PKSolver software according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the biodistribution study, mice received the same intravenous dose as mentioned above. At 4 h post-injection (near the estimated half-life), mice were euthanized, and major organs were collected and imaged using the GS-8000 small animal imaging system (GingYee Technology, China) to assess the distribution of RA-Lipo.

Ex vivo gastrointestinal fluorescence imaging

To evaluate gastrointestinal distribution, C57BL/6J mice were orally administered Rhodamine B (5 mg/kg), RB&RAlipo (200 mg/kg), or RB&RALM (800 mg/kg). At 0.5, 3.0, 6.0, and 12.0 h post-treatment, mice were euthanized to harvest the intact gastrointestinal tracts. Fluorescence signals from the ex vivo organs were captured using a Tanon ABL-X5 imaging system and analyzed quantitatively with the accompanying software.

Single-cell transcriptomic data analysis of aortic immune cells

To characterize foam cell characteristics in AS, we re-analyzed a public single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE97310 (published in 2018), which profiles CD45+ immune cells from aortas of control and Ldlr−/− mice on a high-fat diet (11–20 months). Although the original study described macrophage phenotypes, foam cells were not explicitly delineated. After quality control and data filtering, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and annotation were performed to define cell populations. The proportions of various cell types across groups were compared using bar plots. Monocyte-derived macrophages, general macrophages, and foamy macrophages were subsequently subsetted and re-clustered at a higher resolution (2.0) to specifically isolate and stratify foamy versus non-foamy macrophages for a comparative analysis of Slc2a1 (GLUT1) expression Subsequently, normalized Slc2a1 expression levels and metadata were extracted using the FetchData function in R, and statistical significance between the foam cell and non-foam cell groups was determined. Finally, violin plots were generated using the ggplot2 package to quantitatively evaluate the differential expression of GLUT1 during the transition to the foam cell phenotype.

Animals experiment

Fifty male ApoE−/− mice (8 weeks old) were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China) under license number SCXK (Su) 2022-0016. The mice were housed in groups (10 mice per cage) with access to water and maintained on a 12-h light / dark cycle at a temperature of 25 ℃ and 50 ± 5% humidity. Following a 7-day acclimation period, the mice were randomized into five groups (n = 10): Control (standard diet), HFD (high-fat diet), Asp (HFD + 20 mg/kg Asp), Rg3 + Asp (HFD + 20 mg/kg Rg3 + 20 mg/kg Asp), and RALM (HFD + RALM containing an equivalent dose of 20 mg/kg Asp). After an initial 8-week HFD induction period, the respective treatments were administered orally once daily for an additional 12 weeks. One hour after the final administration, tail bleeding assays were performed to evaluate anticoagulant effects [27]. Subsequently, samples of the aorta, aortic valve, and blood were collected from mice in the various experimental groups for further validation. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee, with review acceptance number 20,230,813,001, and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

In vivo aortic plaque targeting

To evaluate the in vivo targeting of RALM, four mice each from the Control, HFD (Model), and RALM groups were selected and intragastrically administered Rhodamine B (5 mg/kg for the Control and HFD groups) or RB&RALM (at a dose equivalent to 5 mg/kg Rhodamine B). Mice were euthanized 5 h after administration, and their aortas were subsequently imaged. Imaging was conducted using a multifunctional in vivo imager (Tanon 5200 Multi imaging system), with fluorescence intensity in each region quantified using the in vivo imaging system software. Thereafter, the aortas were stained with DAPI and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed on both cultured cells and aortic tissue sections following a previously established protocol [28]. Samples were pre-treated (antigen retrieval for tissues; fixation/permeabilization for cells), blocked with serum, and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against PPARγ and ABCA1. The samples were then incubated with a Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody at 37 ℃. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate protein expression and localization.

Western blot analysis

Total, nuclear, and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted from cells or tissues using commercial extraction kits and quantified via the BCA method [29]. Samples were lysed in RIPA buffer containing PMSF, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against ABCA1, ABCG1, PPARγ, Lamin B, and GAPDH. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence and analyzed with a Tanon 5200 Multi system.

Oil red O staining for intracellular lipid accumulation

Oil Red O staining was performed to evaluate intracellular lipid accumulation. Cells were divided into three groups: Control, Model, and Treatment groups. The Control group cells were cultured under normal conditions, while the Model group cells were exposed to oxLDL (80 µg/mL) for 24 h to induce lipid deposition. In the Treatment groups, cells were established as the model and then incubated with Asp, Asp+Rg3, or RA-Lipo for 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After an additional PBS wash, cells were stained with 0.5% Oil Red O solution for 30 min, followed by a 60% isopropanol rinse to remove excess dye. Lipid accumulation was visualized and imaged under a light microscope to compare the degree of lipid loading across groups.

Permeability assay

An in vitro Transwell model using IEC-6 cells was established to evaluate the permeability of RB&RA-Lipo and RB&CA-Lipo across the intestinal epithelial monolayer. IEC-6 cells were seeded onto the apical (AP) side of 12-well Transwell inserts and cultured until a confluent and dense monolayer was formed. The integrity of the monolayer was verified via a fluorescein-dextran permeability assay. For the transport studies, free Rhodamine B, RB&RA-Lipo, and RB&CA-Lipo were added to the AP side, with the dosage for each formulation normalized to an equivalent Rhodamine B concentration of 10 µg/mL. Serum-free medium was added to the basolateral (BL) side. Samples were collected from the BL side at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 h. The fluorescence intensity of Rhodamine B was measured using a microplate reader, and the transport efficiency of each group was quantified and compared.

Analysis of gut microbiota composition and metabolites

Fecal samples were collected one week prior to euthanasia (n = 6 per group), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 ℃. For microbial analysis, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (V3-V4 region) was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Novogene, China). Sequences were processed via QIIME2, with operational taxonomic units (OTUs) clustered at 97% similarity against the SILVA database. After excluding outliers (n = 5 remaining per group), alpha and beta diversities were characterized using Chao1/Shannon indices and Bray–Curtis-based PCoA, respectively. Inter-group taxonomic differences were assessed by the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Untargeted metabolomics was conducted using UHPLC-Q-TOF MS in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. Following data normalization, multivariate statistics, including PCA and PLS-DA were employed to delineate metabolic shifts. Differential metabolites were identified based on VIP > 1 and P < 0.05, with functional annotation and pathway enrichment performed using HMDB and KEGG databases. Specific focus was placed on the relative quantification of arginine and PGE2.

Safety assessment

Twenty-four healthy male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): Control, Asp, Rg3 + Asp, and RALM groups. The dosages administered were consistent with those described in Sect. "Animals experiment". Body weight was monitored and recorded daily throughout the experimental period. On day 90, blood was collected from the retro-orbital plexus under anesthesia. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃ and stored for subsequent biochemical analysis. Following blood collection, the mice were euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach, and colon were rapidly excised. A portion of each tissue was stored at -80 ℃ for biochemical assays, while the remaining tissues were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 21.0. A two-tailed test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess statistical significance, with a threshold of P < 0.05. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Characterization of RA-Lipo

RA-Lipo was engineered by employing ginsenoside Rg3 as a functional cholesterol surrogate. DLS analysis revealed that RA-Lipo exhibited a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 73.90 ± 1.81 nm, representing a marginal increase relative to the cholesterol-based control (CA-Lipo, 65.47 ± 1.04 nm) (Fig. 1A). TEM imaging confirmed the formation of well-defined, uniform spherical vesicles.

The structural integrity and successful integration of components were validated via FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 1B, S1B). Specifically, RA-Lipo displayed characteristic peaks of Rg3 at 1075 cm⁻¹ and 1044 cm⁻¹ (asymmetric glycosidic bond stretching) and Asp-specific signals at 1749 cm⁻¹ (ester C = O) and 751 cm⁻¹ (aromatic C–H stretching), thereby confirming dual-drug encapsulation. As determined by HPLC analysis, the drug loading (DL) efficiencies for Asp and Rg3 in RA-Lipo were 6.87% ± 0.30% and 7.24% ± 0.21%, respectively (Table S1, Fig. S1A). Furthermore, the substitution of cholesterol with Rg3 led to a significant modulation in surface charge, with the zeta potential shifting from − 46.46 ± 5.55 mV (CA-Lipo) to − 34.99 ± 2.49 mV (RA-Lipo) (Fig. 1C, P < 0.05).

Regarding physiological stability, both formulations showed minimal size variations in PBS and 10% FBS over 12 h, exhibiting sustained Asp release kinetics (< 25% at 24 h) (Fig. 1D–E). However, RA-Lipo demonstrated distinct sensitivity in SGF, where Asp liberation reached 85.72% within 160 min, contrasting with its relative stability in SIF (< 40% release) (Fig. S1C). The transepithelial transport capacity of RA-Lipo was further substantiated by a Transwell-based IEC-6 cell monolayer transport assay. RA-Lipo treatment yielded significantly higher basolateral fluorescence signals at 1 h and 2 h compared to the control group (P < 0.01), indicating favorable intestinal epithelial permeability (Fig. S1D). In vivo pharmacokinetic profiles further corroborated the extended systemic circulation of RA-Lipo, which achieved a prolonged plasma half-life (t 1/2 = 4.49 h) and a significantly elevated AUC relative to CA-Lipo (P < 0.01), performing comparably to PEGylated formulations (Table S2; Fig. 1F-G). Ex vivo biodistribution analysis at 4 h post-injection revealed that while RA-Lipo underwent typical hepatic clearance, it exhibited accumulation within the heart and aortic tissues (Fig. 1H-I). Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of RA-Lipo as a platform for targeted cardiovascular therapy.

Characterization and gut targeting capacity of RALM

RALM were synthesized via electrospray technique, which yielding spherical microspheres with a mean diameter of 87.9 ± 4.85 μm (Fig. 2A). Fluorescence microscopy images displayed discrete red fluorescent puncta distributed within the RALM matrix, which is consistent with the loading of RA-Lipo (Fig. 2B). SEM images further verified a uniform spherical morphology (Fig. 2C). Elemental analysis via EDS identified C, O, and Ca as the primary constituents, aligning with the expected chemical composition of the calcium alginate-based framework, Au presence in the spectra was attributed to the sputter-coating process during sample preparation (Fig. 2D).

In vitro stability assays demonstrated that RALM microspheres maintained their structural integrity in SGF for up to 4.5 h. Conversely, in SIF, RALM swelled to 2.5-fold to their initial size within 1.5 h, structural disintegration at 3.0 h, and completely degraded by 4.5 h (Fig. 2E–G, Fig. S2, P < 0.01). In vivo imaging revealed that the RALM group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the RA-Lipo and free Rhodamine B groups at 3.0 h post-administration. By 6.0 h, while fluorescence in the control groups had nearly vanished, RALM maintained a signal concentrated in the jejunum, ileum, and cecum (Fig. 2H-J), indicating prolonged intestinal residence and targeted drug release in the distal gastrointestinal tract. Collectively, these results suggest that RALM effectively withstands gastric degradation, enabling pH-responsive release and extended retention in the lower gastrointestinal tract.

RALM restructures gut microbiota in ApoE−/− mice

To assess whether RALM modulates gut microbiota, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on fecal samples from each group. Shannon rarefaction curves plateaued in all groups, confirming adequate sequencing depth (Fig. S3A). PCoA revealed a marked shift in microbiota structure along Pcoa1 in the Model group compared to the Control group, indicating substantial compositional divergence. RALM treatment attenuated this AS-induced shift, modulating the microbial composition toward the profile observed in the Control group (Fig. 3A).

At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes were the dominant taxa (Fig. 3B–C). Compared to the Control group, the Model group exhibited a decrease in Bacteroidetes and an increase in Firmicutes, leading to a lower Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes (B/F) ratio (P < 0.01). RALM treatment modulated these abundances, resulting in a significant increase in the B/F ratio (P < 0.01; Fig. S3B). At the genus level, RALM significantly increased the abundance of Ligilactobacillus and Prevotellaceae (P < 0.01; Fig. 3D, S3C–D). The RALM and Control groups shared 435 OTUs, which was markedly higher than the 230 OTUs shared between the Model and Control groups. This suggests that RALM partially mitigated the HFD-induced loss of microbial richness, shifting the composition closer to the Control (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that RALM ameliorates AS-associated dysbiosis by reshaping gut microbiota composition at both phylum and genus levels, enriching beneficial taxa, and increasing microbial diversity.

Regulation of gut microbial metabolites in ApoE−/− mice

To investigate the metabolic alterations associated with RALM-mediated gut microbiota modulation, untargeted metabolomics was performed using UPLC-QTOF-MS. In total, 1,808 metabolites were detected, and 250 anionic fragment ions were confirmed, identifying 47 metabolites with significant differences among Control, Model, and RALM groups (Fig. 4A-B, S4A–B). PCA revealed pronounced alterations in lipid/lipid-like molecules and amino acid metabolites (Fig. S4C–E). Similarly, analysis of 384 cationic fragments identified 57 significantly altered metabolites (Fig. 4A–B, S4F–G), which were also enriched in lipid/lipid-like and amino acid categories (Fig. S4H–J).

Heatmap clustering (|Log₂FC| > 1.5, P < 0.05) demonstrated distinct metabolite profiles across the groups (Fig. 4C–D). Functional enrichment analyses of the differential anionic and cationic metabolites between Control and Model groups, and between Model and RALM groups, are presented in Figures S5–S6, with their respective correlation networks shown in Fig. S7. Pathway analysis indicated that RALM may attenuate AS by modulating arachidonic acid metabolism and arginine biosynthesis through the regulation of gut microbiota (Fig. 4E–F). Quantification of key microbial metabolites showed that RALM significantly increased arginine levels(P < 0.05) and suppressed PGE2 (P < 0.05; Fig. 4G). In summary, these results indicate that RALM may alleviate AS by improving gut microbiota homeostasis and ameliorating dysregulated arachidonic acid and arginine metabolism.

Targeting efficiency and cellular uptake of RA-Lipo and RALM

Fluorescence imaging revealed significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity in the aortas of RALM‑treated mice compared with both Control and Model groups (P < 0.01), exhibiting a heterogeneous distribution (Fig. 5A). Oil Red O staining confirmed a substantial reduction in plaque area in the RALM group (3.99%) relative to the Model group (11.1%, P < 0.01, Fig. 5B). Furthermore, fluorescence in frozen aortic arch sections was found to be confined primarily to the plaques of RALM-treated mice (Fig. 5C), indicating effective in situ targeting.

Cellular uptake assays corroborated cell‑level specificity of the formulation. PEG‑CA‑Lipo showed reduced macrophage uptake compared with CA‑Lipo (P < 0.05) with no difference between unmodified macrophages and oxLDL-induced foam cells. In contrast, RA‑Lipo displayed greater uptake by foam cells than by unmodified macrophages (P < 0.01, Fig. 5D–E). Notably, hyperglycemic pre‑incubation markedly reduced RA‑Lipo uptake (P < 0.01), suggesting a competitive uptake mechanism likely mediated by glucose transporters.

To investigate the mechanism underlying this plaque‑targeting strategy, we assessed whether foam cell formation is accompanied by GLUT1 upregulation. Single‑cell RNA sequencing of CD45⁺ immune cells from aortic tissues of control and high‑fat diet–fed mice with advanced atherosclerosis (11–20 months) identified eleven immune cell subpopulations after quality control, clustering, and annotation (Fig. S8, Fig. 5F). The proportion of foam cell was markedly higher in the Model group than in the Control group (Fig. 5G). Notably, Slc2a1 (the gene encoding GLUT1) expression in foam cells was significantly higher than in other macrophage subsets (P < 0.01, Fig. 5H), indicating pronounced GLUT1 upregulation during AS progression. Collectively, these findings suggest that the elevated expression of GLUT1 in foam cells provides a molecular basis for the preferential accumulation of RALM within atherosclerotic lesions.

RALM potentiates aspirin’s antithrombotic and anti‑atherosclerotic effects

The pathogenesis of AS involves complex interactions among lipid deposition, inflammation, and platelet-mediated thrombosis. Tail bleeding assays showed significantly prolonged bleeding time in the Asp, Asp+Rg3 groups compared with Model (P < 0.01, Fig. 6A). RALM treatment elicited a further extension in bleeding time (P < 0.05, vs. Asp). In terms of systemic inflammation, serum IL-6 levels, which were markedly elevated in the Model group (P < 0.01; Fig. 6B), were significantly suppressed following treatment with Asp, Asp+Rg3, RALM (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Furthermore, lipidomic analysis revealed that RALM and Asp+Rg3 effectively mitigated HFD-induced elevations in TG, LDL-C, and TC (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01; Fig. 6C), while simultaneously restoring HDL levels (P < 0.01). These results indicate that RALM exerts an anti-atherosclerotic effect.

Oil Red O staining revealed reduced plaque progression in RALM and Rg3 + Asp treated groups. Foam cell numbers were markedly decreased in Asp + Rg3 and RALM groups compared with the Model group (P < 0.01; Fig. 6D–E), with RALM achieving the most pronounced reduction (P < 0.05, vs. Asp + Rg3). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the expression of PPARγ (a stress-protective factor) was elevated in lesions of the Model group (P < 0.05), further increased by Asp + Rg3 and RALM (P < 0.01), and higher in RALM-treated mice (P < 0.05, vs. Asp + Rg3).

For in vitro confirmation, oxLDL-induced RAW264.7 macrophages developed abundant lipid droplets and increased TC and MDA levels (P < 0.01, Fig. S9A–B). RA‑Lipo treatment significantly reduced TC and inflammatory cytokines (P < 0.01, Fig. 6F). Additionally, Oil Red O staining confirmed reduced lipid accumulation in Rg3, Asp + Rg3 and RA‑Lipo groups (P < 0.01, Fig. 6F, S9C–D).

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, the expression of cholesterol efflux-related proteins ABCA1 and ABCG1 was examined. Both proteins were markedly upregulated by Asp + Rg3 and RA‑Lipo (P < 0.01; Fig. 6G–H). oxLDL stimulation elevated the nuclear levels of PPARγ, while Asp + Rg3 and RA-Lipo further increased its nuclear accumulation (P < 0.01, Fig. 6I–J). Collectively, RALM retains the bioactivities of both Rg3 and Asp, inhibiting inflammatory cytokines and suppressing foam cell formation by activating the PPARγ/ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway, which contributes to the observed synergistic anti-atherosclerotic effect.

Toxicity of RALM In vivo

H&E staining of the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, colon, and lung revealed no histopathological abnormalities in the RALM group compared with saline-treated controls, indicating its safety (Fig. 7A). Consistently, serum biochemistry further showed no significant changes in AST, ALT, ALP, BUN, UA, or CREA (Fig. 7B). In contrast, Asp treatment induced significant gastric mucosal injury compared with the control group (P < 0.01; Fig. 7C–D). Co-administration of Rg3 with Asp significantly reduced ulcer area (P < 0.05) and enhanced SOD1 expression (P < 0.01), suggesting antioxidant-mediated protection. The RALM group exhibited minimal gastric ulceration (P < 0.01), which supports the targeted intestinal release and safety profile of the formulation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that RALM provides therapeutic efficacy with low toxicity, effectively attenuating Asp-induced gastrointestinal injury.

Discussion

AS is a multifactorial pathological process involving lipid accumulation, immune dysregulation, and vascular wall remodeling [30, 31]. Plaque stability is jointly regulated by lipid composition, immune cell activity, and the integrity of the vascular structure [32, 33]. Given the complexity of its pathogenesis, monotherapeutic approaches often fail to achieve satisfactory outcomes, making multi-pathway strategies an attractive therapeutic direction [34, 35].

In this study, RALM encapsulating RA-Lipo was developed to enhance AS therapy through targeted delivery to atherosclerotic plaques, modulation of lipid profiles, and regulation of the gut composition and metabolic profiles. RA-Lipo was encapsulated within calcium-crosslinked SA microspheres to facilitate intestinal release. These microspheres not only protect the liposomes from premature degradation in the acidic gastric environment but also exhibit sustained-release properties that improve delivery efficiency [36, 37]. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that RALM significantly reshaped gut microbiota composition and associated metabolic profile. 16 S rRNA and metabolomics analyses revealed that RALM increased the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio—an alteration recognized as a potential strategy for alleviating AS, representing a microbial signature previously linked to AS attenuation [38, 39]. At the metabolite level, RALM treatment elevated arginine levels and suppressed PGE2 production. Arginine has been documented to promote nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation and immune regulation, thereby attenuating the progression of AS [40, 41]. Consistent with our results, both SA and Rg3 have been documented to exert therapeutic effects by modulating the microbiota, thereby ameliorating dyslipidemia and systemic inflammation [42–44].

In addition to these microbiota-mediated effects, RALM exhibited in vivo anti-atherosclerotic efficacy. The RA-Lipo liberated from RALM demonstrated enhanced accumulation within atherosclerotic plaques. Previous studies have documented that liposomes functionalized with ginsenosides (e.g., Rg3 and Rh2) can achieve targeted delivery to tumor tissues via GLUT1 receptors [20, 45, 46]. Integrating our results from glucose inhibition assays and single-cell transcriptomic analysis with existing literature [47], we hypothesize that the high expression of GLUT1 in foam cells facilitates the preferential enrichment of RA-Lipo within the plaques. Mechanistic investigations revealed that Rg3 inhibited foam cell formation via the PPARγ/ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway. Consistent with our findings, Geng et al.. and Guo et al. reported that Rg3 acts as PPARγ agonist to ameliorate AS [11, 12]. Furthermore, RALM preserved the anti-inflammatory and anti-platelet activities of Asp while exhibiting enhanced efficacy, which is likely attributable to the optimized pharmacokinetic profile of Asp afforded by the sustained release and prolonged systemic circulation of the RALM platform. In conclusion, this study validates the potential of RALM as an oral controlled-release delivery strategy for AS therapy, providing a promising paradigm for the synergistic integration of bioactive natural products with conventional clinical pharmaceuticals in the management of cardiovascular diseases.

Despite these promising findings, certain limitations remain: RALM potentiated the anti-platelet activity of Asp; although our evaluations confirmed its safety without evidence of gastric hemorrhage or ulceration, the potential for bleeding complications during chronic administration warrants further investigation. Furthermore, given the profound modulatory effects of RALM on the gut microbiota, future studies should focus on elucidating the functional interplay between the delivery system, the microbiome, and the host, thereby delineating the specific molecular pathways involved in AS attenuation.

Conclusion

To address the multifaceted pathological complexity of AS and the side effects of conventional pharmacotherapies, this study engineered an oral controlled-release delivery platform (RALM). Our findings revealed that the replacement of cholesterol with the bioactive ginsenoside Rg3 yielded stable, uniform RA-Lipo with transepithelial transport capacity and significantly extended systemic circulation. The electrospray-fabricated RALM encapsulation provided pH-responsive protection, effectively shielding the liposomes from gastric degradation while facilitating sustained release within the intestinal milieu. In vivo evaluations in HFD-fed ApoE⁻/⁻ mice demonstrated that RALM treatment markedly attenuated atherosclerotic plaque progression, inhibited platelet activation, and ameliorated dyslipidemia. Furthermore, RALM reshaped the microbiota composition and regulated key metabolites—specifically upregulating arginine while suppressing PGE2 levels, which may represent another mechanism by which RALM modulates AS via the gut-heart axis. Safety assessments indicated that the RALM platform exhibited a favorable safety profile without inducing significant systemic toxicity, while simultaneously providing substantial protection against aspirin-induced gastric mucosal injury. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of RALM microspheres as a therapeutic strategy for the effective management of AS.
