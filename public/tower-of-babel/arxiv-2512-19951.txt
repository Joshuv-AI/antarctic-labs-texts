Efficient Mod Approximation and Its Applications to CKKS Ciphertexts
Yufei Zhou

Abstract
The mod function plays a critical role in numerous data encoding and cryptographic primitives. However, the widely used CKKS homomorphic encryption (HE) scheme supports only arithmetic operations, making it difficult to perform mod computations on encrypted data. Approximating the mod function with polynomials has therefore become an important yet challenging problem. The discontinuous and periodic characteristics of the mod function make it particularly difficult to approximate accurately under HE. Existing homomorphic mod constructions provide accurate results only within limited subranges of the input range, leaving the problem of achieving accurate approximation across the full input range unresolved. In this work, we propose a novel method based on polynomial interpolation and Chebyshev series to accurately approximate the mod function. Building upon this, we design two efficient data packing schemes, BitStack and CRTStack , tailored for small-integer inputs in CKKS. These schemes significantly improve the utilization of the CKKS plaintext space and enable efficient ciphertext uploads. Furthermore, we apply the proposed HE mod function to implement a homomorphic rounding operation and a general transformation from additive secret sharing to CKKS ciphertexts, achieving accurate ciphertext rounding and complete secret-share-to-CKKS conversion. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves high approximation accuracy (up to 10 − 8 10^{-8} ). Overall, our work provides a practical and general solution for performing mod operations under CKKS, extending its applicability to a broader range of privacy-preserving computations.



1
Introduction

Homomorphic encryption (HE) enables computation directly on encrypted data, providing strong privacy guarantees by allowing operations to be performed without decryption
[
12
,
41
]
. Despite its advantages in privacy-preserving computation, HE still faces significant practical challenges, particularly regarding computational overhead, memory usage, and communication costs
[
19
,
35
]
. These limitations are especially critical in resource-constrained environments such as smart home devices
[
46
]
, health monitoring systems
[
47
]
, and IoT platforms, where both computational and bandwidth resources are limited.

Among various HE schemes, those based on the Ring Learning with Errors (RLWE) assumption—such as BFV
[
12
]
and CKKS
[
19
]
—enable efficient encrypted vector processing through Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD) operations. These schemes can encrypt multiple plaintext values into a single ciphertext, greatly improving throughput. However, ensuring strong security guarantees requires large ciphertext sizes and a substantial number of encrypted slots (up to
2^{15}
in some cases)
[
35
]
. While SIMD operations reduce computation time per plaintext, the communication cost associated with transmitting large ciphertexts remains a major bottleneck, limiting the scalability and practicality of HE in real-world deployments.

The CKKS scheme, in particular, has become widely adopted for approximate real-number arithmetic due to its efficiency in handling complex computations. However, CKKS natively supports only addition and multiplication, lacking direct support for non-linear or discontinuous operations such as the mod function, which are essential in many cryptographic protocols and data processing tasks. The discontinuous and periodic nature of the mod function makes it particularly challenging to approximate accurately in HE. Existing works typically provide approximations that are only valid within restricted subranges of the input, leaving the problem of accurate global approximation unresolved.

To address this challenge, we propose a novel method for accurately approximating the mod function over the entire input range using polynomial interpolation and Chebyshev series expansion. Our approach enables precise mod operations on small integers directly within CKKS ciphertexts, effectively extending the functional expressiveness of CKKS. Building on this, we further propose two efficient data packing techniques,
BitStack
and
CRTStack
, which leverage the proposed homomorphic mod operation to significantly improve the utilization of the CKKS plaintext space. These techniques enable more efficient ciphertext uploading for small inputs.

Beyond these improvements, we demonstrate additional applications of the proposed homomorphic mod function. In particular, we implement a homomorphic rounding function for CKKS that enables accurate rounding on ciphertexts of small integers, achieving an approximation error as low as
10^{-10}
. Moreover, we present the first complete HE-based transformation scheme that converts additive secret shares into CKKS ciphertexts without any modification to the underlying secret sharing schemes, thereby bridging the gap between secret sharing and HE.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose an accurate polynomial approximation for the mod function using polynomial interpolation and Chebyshev series, overcoming the challenges posed by its discontinuous and periodic nature.

2.

We design two efficient data packing methods,
BitStack
and
CRTStack
, tailored for small-integer inputs in CKKS, significantly reducing the computational and communication overhead of ciphertext uploading.

3.

We implement a homomorphic rounding function based on the proposed mod approximation, enabling accurate rounding for CKKS ciphertexts of small integers.

4.

We realize a general transformation from additive secret sharing to CKKS ciphertexts using the proposed homomorphic mod function. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first complete conversion scheme based only on CKKS operations.

Notations and Preliminaries

3.1
Notations

We denote the message set by
\mathbb{M}
. Vectors are represented by bold lowercase letters. Polynomials are represented by their coefficient vectors. Let
N
be a power of 2, and
\Phi(x)=x^{N}+1
be the
2N
-th cyclotomic polynomial. Define the polynomial ring
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}[x]/\Phi(x)
. For an integer
q
, let
\mathcal{R}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}_{q}[x]/\Phi(x)
. Any polynomial
f(x)\in\mathcal{R}_{q}
has its polynomial reduced modulo
\Phi(x)
and its coefficients reduced modulo
q
. The plaintext in HE schemes based on RLWE is typically a polynomial in
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, so we use
\mathcal{R}_{t}
to denote the plaintext space.
When
t\equiv 1\pmod{2N}
, the plaintext space can be mapped to an
N
-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{t}^{N}
via a ring isomorphism. Thus, using SIMD,
N
integers can be packed into a single plaintext. Note that CKKS can only pack
\frac{N}{2}
complex numbers. Let
n=\frac{N}{2}
for CKKS. That is,
n
denotes the number of slots. Ciphertext rotation enables the exchange of encrypted messages in different slots.

Ciphertexts generally consist of two polynomials in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
, and we denote the ciphertext space by
\mathcal{R}_{q}^{2}
. The ciphertext of a vector
\mathbf{x}
is denoted as
[\![\mathbf{x}]\!]
. We use
\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}
to represent the concatenation of vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, creating a new vector. To distinguish from variables, we denote the imaginary unit by
1_{i}
.
Additionally, we define the mod function
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
as the remainder of
x
modulo
p
. This maps
x
to
y
such that
y\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,p)
and satisfies
x=p\times z+y
, where
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

3.2
Homomorphic Encryption

We primarily focuses on approximate HE scheme CKKS
[
19
]
, and does not discuss bit-wise HE schemes like TFHE
[
20
]
or integer HE schemes like BGV
[
11
]
.
HE supports basic arithmetic operations such as addition and multiplication on encrypted data. It enables SIMD computation by packing multiple plaintext values into a single ciphertext. Ciphertext rotation operations are supported to facilitate data movement across slots in the packed ciphertext.

Homomorphic addition and multiplication are performed in a slot-wise manner, where messages in different slots do not interfere with each other. Specifically, homomorphic addition is performed as vector addition:

[\![\mathbf{x}]\!]+[\![\mathbf{y}]\!]=[\![(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}+\mathbf{y}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathbf{y}_{n})]\!].

Homomorphic multiplication corresponds to the Hadamard product of vectors:

[\![\mathbf{x}]\!]\times[\![\mathbf{y}]\!]=[\![(\mathbf{x}_{1}\times\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\times\mathbf{y}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\times\mathbf{y}_{n})]\!].

We denote
\operatorname{rot}([\![\mathbf{x}]\!],i)
as the rotation of ciphertext
[\![\mathbf{x}]\!]
by
i
positions; a positive
i
indicates a left rotation, while a negative
i
indicates a right rotation. This rotation is performed in a cyclic manner with a period of
n
:

\operatorname{rot}([\![\mathbf{x}]\!],i)=[\![(\mathbf{x}_{i+1},\mathbf{x}_{i+2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{i})]\!].

In addition, CKKS supports homomorphic complex conjugation. We denote
\operatorname{conjugate}([\![a+b\times 1_{i}]\!])
, which returns the ciphertext of conjugate denoted as
[\![a-b\times 1_{i}]\!]
.

Fresh ciphertexts are directly encrypted using the public key
pk
and contain minimal initial noise. Each homomorphic operation increases the noise, with ciphertext-ciphertext multiplication contributing the most to noise growth. CKKS employ modulus switching to reduce the ciphertext modulus and control noise expansion during multiplications. Each multiplication consumes one level of multiplicative depth. After a certain number of multiplications, the accumulated multiplicative depth exhausts the available levels, and the ciphertext modulus becomes too small to support further operations.
Bootstrapping can be used to increase the ciphertext modulus and suppress noise, enabling arbitrary homomorphic computations.

4
Accurate Mod Function for CKKS

Existing works have been on how to approximate
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
within a small interval.
For example, Lee et al.
[
37
]
employed the composite function
\frac{p}{2\pi}\times\arcsin\left(\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{p}\right)\right)
to approximate
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
, which is the method that provides the largest approximation range among the existing works, as far as we know.
As shown by the green line in
Figure

1
, a good approximation is achieved within a certain input range. However, in other intervals, particularly at the middle of adjacent multiples of
p
, the approximation error becomes significantly larger,.
Therefore, a new method is needed to accurately approximate the mod function over the entire input range.

Figure 1:
Example of approximated
\operatorname{ModP}(x,9)
over
[-18,18]
. The polynomial degree of Ours is
80
.

The function
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
is discontinuous and exhibits jump discontinuities. As shown by the blue points in
Figure

1
,
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
demonstrates significant jumps, making approximation across the entire interval very challenging.
Unlike existing methods, we take the fixed interval
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
as discrete sample point pairs
(x,x\bmod p)
. Our approximation focuses on the accuracy at integer points and does not care about the values at non-integer positions.

However, directly employing interpolation methods to fit these points leads to large coefficients and numerical instability. For instance, when interpolating
\operatorname{ModP}(x,9)
over the interval
[0,80]
using Lagrange interpolation, the largest coefficient of the resulting polynomial reaches about
2.64\times 10^{22}
, which is evidently impractical for CKKS computation due to the substantial amplification of computational error.

To address this issue, we adopt the Chebyshev series of the first kind to approximate
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
. The Chebyshev series is defined as follows
[
43
]
:

T_{n}(x)=\cos{(n\times\arccos{(x)})},\quad n=0,1,2,\cdots,

(1)

where
x\in[-1,1]
.
T_{n}(x)
is in fact a polynomial of degree
n
, and it can be defined recursively as follows:

T_{0}(x)=1,T_{1}(x)=x,T_{n}(x)=2xT_{n-1}(x)-T_{n-2}(x).

(2)

Suppose we aim to approximate
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
over the interval
[0,B]
using a polynomial of degree
D
, where
D>B
. We obtain
B+1
sample points
\mathbf{x}=(i,i\bmod p)
, where
i=0,1,2,\cdots,B
. First, we need to map the input range of the Chebyshev series to the interval
[-1,1]
using the following function:

\operatorname{f}(x)=\frac{2x}{B}-1.

(3)

Let the coefficients of the Chebyshev series be denoted as
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{D}
. We construct the following system of equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=0}^{D}\alpha_{i}\times&T_{i}(\frac{2\times 0}{B}-1)=(0\bmod p)\\
\sum_{i=0}^{D}\alpha_{i}\times&T_{i}(\frac{2\times 1}{B}-1)=(1\bmod p)\\
&\vdots\\
\sum_{i=0}^{D}\alpha_{i}\times&T_{i}(\frac{2\times B}{B}-1)=(B\bmod p)\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

We can solve
Eq.

4
using the least squares method and select the solution with the minimal
\ell_{2}
-norm as the coefficients of the Chebyshev series.
Since the degree of our polynomial is relatively high, we adopt the Paterson–Stockmeyer method
[
42
]
to efficiently compute the approximation. More specifically, we use the variant based on the Chebyshev basis proposed in
[
14
]
.

The Paterson–Stockmeyer method employs polynomial long division to decompose the target polynomial into several sub-polynomials. These sub-polynomials are evaluated individually and then combined to produce the final result.

Suppose we want to evaluate
f(x)=\sum_{i}^{D}\alpha_{i}T_{i}(x)
at a point
u
. We choose positive integers
k
and
m
such that
k\approx\sqrt{D/2}
and
k(2^{m}-1)>D
. Compute the powers

\displaystyle\mathbf{bs}

\displaystyle=(T_{1}(u),T_{2}(u),\cdots,T_{k}(u)),

\displaystyle\mathbf{gs}

\displaystyle=(T_{k}(u),T_{2k}(u),\cdots,T_{2^{m-1}k}(u)).

Let
\tilde{f}(x)=f(x)+T_{k(2^{m-1}-1)}
.
We apply polynomial long division to decompose
\tilde{f}(x)
as
\tilde{f}(x)=T_{k2^{m-1}}q(x)+r(x)
. Let
\tilde{r}(x)=r(x)-T_{k(2^{m-1}-1)}
. Using long division again, we decompose
\tilde{r}(x)=c(x)q(x)+s(x)
. With the precomputed powers, we evaluate
c(x)
at
u
. Then, we define
\tilde{s}(x)=s(x)+T_{k(2^{m-1}-1)}
. We recursively apply long division and evaluate
q(x)
and
\tilde{s}(x)
at
u
.
When
q(u)
and
\tilde{s}(u)
are computed, we can compute
\tilde{f}(u)=(T_{k(2^{m-1}-1)}(u)+c(u))q(u)+\tilde{s}(u)-T_{k(2^{m}-1)}
.

When the coefficients are greater than 1, using CKKS for computation may cause the outputs of the sub-polynomials to exceed 1, leading to a rapid amplification of errors during the combination phase.

To mitigate this, after obtaining the Chebyshev series coefficients from
Eq.

4
, we divide them by a scaling factor
\delta
to ensure that all coefficients remain below 1. The scaled coefficients are given by
\beta_{i}=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\delta}
.
The final approximation is

\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)\simeq\delta\times\sum_{i=0}^{D}\beta_{i}T_{i}(\frac{2x}{B}-1).

(5)

The choice of
\delta
depends on the solution of
Eq.

4
; it only needs to ensure that the resulting coefficients are significantly less than 1.
We observe that our approximation method essentially involves selecting hyperparameters for a mod function over a fixed interval, i.e., calculating the coefficients of the Chebyshev polynomials. We can iterate over all the inputs (which are finite integers) to obtain all the coefficients in the calculation process, thereby allowing us to choose an appropriate value for
\delta
. Since this calculation does not involve private inputs, all computations are performed on plaintext, not on homomorphic ciphertext, so the overhead of selecting
\delta
is minimal.

As for the selection of
D
, it must be sufficiently large to ensure that the coefficient matrix of
Eq.

4
has full rank, i.e., rank
B+1
. The orthogonality of the Chebyshev polynomials guarantees the existence of such a
D
.

Limitations of Our Approximation.

Our approximation method, along with the required degree of the approximation polynomial, is dependent on the input range. The larger the range, the higher the degree of the polynomial required, meaning that the input range supported by our method is not very large—typically, on the order of tens or a few hundreds. However, we believe that, despite this limitation, our method has a suitable range of applications, such as for dealing with small integers. Small integers are common in privacy-preserving computations, such as genomic data, which only require 2 bits (with possible values 0, 1, and 2) as noted in
[
27
]
. In the following sections, we discuss how to leverage our homomorphic modular approximation function for data packing and unpacking.

5
Data Packing for Small Inputs

5.1
System Model and Assumptions

We assume that the user’s input is a small input. The term "small input" has two meanings here: one refers to the small length of the data, and the other refers to the small range of values for each element in the input. The user performs plaintext data packing, combining multiple data items into a single one, which is then sent to the server. The server then unpacks the data on ciphertext, obtaining the ciphertext of each individual data item. The server performs privacy-preserving processing tasks on each input data and returns the resulting ciphertext to the user, who decrypts it to obtain the final result.
Similar to Transcipher, our focus is on how the server obtains the CKKS ciphertext for each input, rather than the specifics of the privacy-preserving processing tasks.

We adopt the semi-honest threat model, common in existing HE-based privacy-preserving data processing systems
[
48
,
35
,
5
]
. In this model, the adversary may control the server, but follows the protocol honestly while attempting to infer private information from observed messages.
If the server is compromised, the adversary can only know the data length, range, and encrypted values. The values of data length and range are public parameters known in advance and are independent of the actual input values. The encrypted values leak no information about inputs based on the security of used HE.
Therefore, the adversary learns nothing about the private inputs.

We first introduce how to concatenate multiple short vectors,
VecConcat
and
ImgConcat
, to utilize the slots, and then present our data packing methods for small integers,
BitStack
and
CRTStack
, and how they can be combined with
VecConcat
and
ImgConcat
. Each data packing method consists of two parts: one is the encoding of the data into plaintext by the user, and the other is the unpacking on ciphertext by the server, which involves splitting the ciphertext of different data items. The computation on the user side is simple and can be performed on plaintext, while the unpacking on the server side requires homomorphic operations on ciphertext, which is often the greatest challenge in the design.

5.2
VecConcat

For clarity, suppose the sizes of the message vectors packed into the same ciphertext are
n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{d}
, with
n\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
, meaning we assume a ciphertext can pack
d
messages.
The packing process is straightforward for the user, who possesses the private key, as it only requires vector concatenation and encrypted the concatenated vector.
The user can directly reorganize the messages in plaintext with negligible overhead.
In contrast, the server, which does not have the private key, must perform unpacking on the ciphertext.

Unpack Ciphertext.

In addition to rotation, the unpacking of ciphertexts requires multiplication by a mask vector to prevent interference from other data during subsequent computations.
The
i
-th mask vector,
\mathbf{k}^{(i)}
, is a vector with the first
d_{i}
elements set to 1 and the remaining elements set to 0. We rotate the input ciphertext to the left by
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}n_{j}
steps, then multiply it by
\mathbf{k}^{(i)}
to obtain the unpacked
i
-th ciphertext.

[\![\mathbf{a}^{(i)}]\!]=\mathbf{k}^{(i)}\times\operatorname{rot}([\![\mathbf{x}]\!],\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}n_{j}).

(6)

Note that we need to perform
d
rotations on the same input ciphertext. Therefore, we can optimize this process using the hoisting rotation technique proposed in
[
29
]
.
Generally, the ciphertext rotations are performed in three steps: (1) the automorphism is applied to the ciphertext, (2) the automorphed values are decomposed into digits, and (3) key switching is applied to make it possible to further compute on the ciphertext. Hoisting rotation is a technique that first performs the digit decomposition on the original ciphertext, and then applies the automorphism and key switching on the decomposed digits. Since the automorphism preserves the distributive properties of addition and multiplication and does not significantly amplify the coefficients of the ciphertext polynomial, the noise increase is similar to that of the standard rotation. The benefit of this approach is that the digit decomposition is independent of the automorphism rotation step, so it can be reused for multiple different rotation steps.

We can also use multiple threads to parallelize the unpacking process, as the unpacking of different ciphertexts is independent.

Repack.

Different algorithms may require distinct input packing formats, and the ciphertext after unpacking may not be directly applicable for subsequent operations.
For example, the input in
[
35
,
5
]
is repetitively packed to enable parallel convolution.
We take this repetitive packing as an example to illustrate how to implement repacking on ciphertexts.

Assume we have a ciphertext
[\![\mathbf{x}|(0,0,\cdots,0)]\!]
, where the length of
\mathbf{x}
is
d_{x}
, and the desired number of repetitions is
r
. That is, we aim to repack the ciphertext into the form

[\![\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}|\cdots|\mathbf{x}|(0,0,\cdots,0)]\!],

where the plaintext
\mathbf{x}
is repeated
r
times.

Let the binary representation of
r
from the least significant bit to the most significant bit be
(r_{0},r_{1},\cdots,r_{\ell})
.
We denote by
[\![\mathbf{x}_{i}]\!]
the ciphertext in which
\mathbf{x}
is repeated
i
times. Then:

[\![\mathbf{x}_{2i}]\!]=[\![\mathbf{x}_{i}]\!]+\operatorname{rot}([\![\mathbf{x}_{i}]\!],-i\times d_{x}).

(7)

We can obtain
[\![\mathbf{x}_{2^{1}}]\!],[\![\mathbf{x}_{2^{2}}]\!],\cdots,[\![\mathbf{x}_{2^{\ell}}]\!]
through
\ell
rotations.

The result can be obtained by

[\![\mathbf{x}_{r}]\!]=[\![\mathbf{x}_{2^{\ell}}]\!]+\sum_{i=0,r_{i}=1}^{\ell-1}\operatorname{rot}([\![\mathbf{x}_{2^{i}}]\!],-d_{x}\times\sum_{j=i+1,r_{j}=1}^{\ell}2^{j}).

(8)

Therefore, the repacking procedure requires at most
2\ell
rotations.

5.3
ImgConcat
for CKKS

The plaintext space of CKKS is a complex vector space, while the data typically processed, such as images or text, is usually represented in real numbers. CKKS allocates half of the slots to represent the imaginary part, which leads to at least half of the slots being redundant when encrypting real numbers.
Our basic idea is to encrypt a message as the imaginary part of another message, using a complex number representation.

Suppose we have two message vectors,
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, with sizes
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
, and both less than or equal to
n
. The corresponding ciphertexts are
[\![\mathbf{a}]\!]
and
[\![\mathbf{b}]\!]
. The plaintext packing simply encrypts
\mathbf{b}
as the imaginary part of
\mathbf{a}
. Below, we describe how to unpack the imaginary part on ciphertexts.

Unpack Ciphertext.

We achieve the separation of the real and imaginary parts on ciphertext by using the homomorphic conjugate and mask vectors. The first mask vector

\mathbf{r}^{(1)}=(0.5,0.5,\cdots,0.5,0,0,\cdots,0),

where the first
n_{1}
elements are
0.5
and the rest are
0
. The second mask vector

\mathbf{r}^{(2)}=(-0.5_{i},-0.5_{i},\cdots,-0.5_{i},0,0,\cdots,0),

where the first
n_{2}
elements are
-0.5_{i}
. By multiplying the mask vector
\mathbf{r}^{(1)}
with the sum of the ciphertext
[\![\mathbf{x}]\!]
and its conjugate, we obtain the ciphertext
[\![\mathbf{a}]\!]
.

[\![\mathbf{a}]\!]=\mathbf{r}^{(1)}\times\left([\![\mathbf{x}]\!]+\operatorname{conjugate}([\![\mathbf{x}]\!])\right).

(9)

Similarly, we obtain the ciphertext
[\![\mathbf{b}]\!]
by the following formula:

[\![\mathbf{b}]\!]=\mathbf{r}^{(2)}\times\left([\![\mathbf{x}]\!]-\operatorname{conjugate}([\![\mathbf{x}]\!])\right).

(10)

For the scalar example, if the packed ciphertext is
[\![a+b\times 1_{i}]\!]
, then the conjugate ciphertext is
[\![a-b\times 1_{i}]\!]
. We have

\displaystyle[\![a]\!]

\displaystyle=0.5\times\left([\![a+b\times 1_{i}]\!]+[\![a-b\times 1_{i}]\!]\right)

\displaystyle[\![b]\!]

\displaystyle=-0.5_{i}\times\left([\![a+b\times 1_{i}]\!]-[\![a-b\times 1_{i}]\!]\right).

Repack.

Since the imaginary part is typically not required for computation, the data are first packed into
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
according to the format required by the processing algorithm before encryption. Therefore, after unpacking, repacking is no longer necessary. For example, in repetitive packing,
[\![\mathbf{a}]\!]
and
[\![\mathbf{b}]\!]
are in the format of repetitive packing.

5.4
BitStack
Packing

An intuitive packing method is to represent the data to be packed using binary strings and then concatenate them into a single binary string, thereby better utilizing the entire plaintext space.
Suppose we need to pack
d
pieces of data,
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{a}_{d}
, along each dimension. We divide each dimension into
d
layers, with
\mathbf{a}_{i}
placed in the
i
-th layer. Let the bit length of each layer be
l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{d}
, and define
L=\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}
. Each
l_{i}
should be greater than or equal to the bit length of the maximum value in
\mathbf{a}_{i}
, so as to avoid data overlapping between different layers. Since the processing for each dimension is the same, we take a single-dimensional input
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{d}
as an example for illustration.
The packed result is as follows:

x=a_{1}+a_{2}\times 2^{l_{1}}+a_{3}\times 2^{l_{1}+l_{2}}+\cdots+a_{d}\times 2^{l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{d-1}}.

(11)

We use binary as an example here; however, the space size of the
l
-th layer does not necessarily have to be
2^{l}
. It can be any sufficiently large integer, provided that the range of each layer can be separated from the previous ones in terms of their representation range.

In addition to using homomorphic mod operations, other existing techniques can also be employed for unpacking. Below, we first introduce two possible unpacking methods, followed by a description of our own unpacking approach:

Extract (Extract bits with comparison).
In BLEACH
[
25
]
, authors used comparison function to extract bits. Below, we describe how to use their method to separate the numbers packed together using
BitStack
.

In
[
31
]
, a fast comparison method on BGV and BFV is proposed, while
[
36
]
introduces an accurate comparison function for CKKS. Therefore, unpacking can be performed using a lookup table with homomorphic comparison. However, the computational overhead of using a lookup table is significant, with the complexity of comparison operations being
O(2^{L})
. So we adopt a recursive approach, iteratively extracting the highest bits, which reduces the complexity of the comparison operation to
O(L)
.

In
[
36
]
, the comparison function returns 0 if
x<y
, 0.5 if
x=y
and
1
if
x>y
. But we require a binary return: 1 if the highest bit is 1, and 0 otherwise. To extract the
L
-th bit from the ciphertext
[\![x]\!]
, we compare it with
2^{L-1}-0.5
where
0.5
is used to avoid error when
x=2^{L-1}
. The comparison result is returned as
[\![c_{L}]\!]
, and we update
[\![x]\!]
as follows:

[\![x]\!]\leftarrow[\![x]\!]-2^{L-1}\times[\![c_{L}]\!].

(12)

After this step, the plaintext in
[\![x]\!]
will have
L-1
bits remaining. This process is repeated to extract all the bits. The extracted bits are then used to unpack the data from different layers, resulting in the ciphertexts
[\![a_{1}]\!],[\![a_{2}]\!],\cdots,[\![a_{d}]\!]
. But is is inefficient to extract one bit at a time. In addition, the accumulation of errors in comparison functions limits the number of bits that can be packed together. According to our experiments, when using the comparison function proposed in
[
36
]
, the extraction error reaches the order of
10^{-2}
when two layers of 4-bit data are packed. Moreover, multiple times of bootstrapping are required, which is very expensive.

Switch (Floor with scheme switching).

We can extract multiple bits at once using the floor function. Suppose we need to pack two values
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, where the bit-length of
a_{1}
is
\ell
. Let the packed ciphertext be
[\![x]\!]
. Then,
[\![a_{2}]\!]=\operatorname{Floor}([\![x]\!],2^{\ell})
, which corresponds to dividing
[\![x]\!]
by
2^{\ell}
and taking the integer part.
[\![a_{1}]\!]
can then be unpacked as
[\![a_{1}]\!]=[\![x]\!]-2^{\ell}\times[\![a_{2}]\!]
. The unpacking process can be applied recursively when multiple values are packed together.
However, applying the floor function directly on CKKS or BGV ciphertexts is challenging, while it is simpler on TFHE or FHEW ciphertexts. Several studies, such as
[
39
,
10
]
, have explored schemes for converting between CKKS and FHEW. Suppose the original ciphertext is in CKKS format. We first convert it to FHEW ciphertext, apply the floor function, and then convert the result back to CKKS ciphertext. It’s important to note that the conversion process depends on the used slots in the ciphertext: the more slots a ciphertext contains, the slower the conversion becomes.

Unpack Ciphertext.

In contrast, we can elegantly solve the unpacking of
BitStack
using mod functions.
First, we compute the homomorphic mod of
[\![x]\!]
with respect to
2^{l_{1}}
to extract
[\![a_{1}]\!]
.

[\![a_{1}]\!]=\operatorname{ModP}([\![x]\!],2^{l_{1}}).

(13)

Then, subtract
[\![a_{1}]\!]
from the packed ciphertext
[\![x]\!]
:

[\![x^{(1)}]\!]=\frac{1}{2^{l_{1}}}\times([\![x]\!]-[\![a_{1}]\!]).

(14)

Now,
[\![x^{(1)}]\!]
contains only the ciphertexts of
a_{2},a_{3},\cdots,a_{d}
.
Repeat this process recursively, we can extract all ciphertexts of data packed in each layer.

Multiplicative depth optimization for
BitStack
.

Since the unpacking process of
BitStack
is performed serially, it requires a large multiplicative depths. To mitigate this issue, we employ constant folding to reduce the overall multiplicative depth of the unpacking operation.
When computing
Eq.

13
, we fuse the constants
\frac{1}{2^{l_{1}}}
and
\delta
from
Eq.

14
. Compute

[\![\frac{a_{1}}{2^{l_{1}}}]\!]=\frac{\delta}{2^{l_{1}}}\times\sum_{i=0}^{D}\beta_{i}T_{i}(\frac{2x}{B}-1),

(15)

where
\sum_{i=0}^{D}\beta_{i}T_{i}(\frac{2x}{B}-1)
is computed using Paterson–Stockmeyer method. As for
[\![a_{1}]\!]=2^{l_{1}}\times[\![\frac{a_{1}}{2^{l_{1}}}]\!]
, it can be computed using additions.
For example, if we want to compute
[\![x]\!]\times 2^{l_{i}}
, we can compute it using

[\![x]\!]\times 2^{l_{i}}=[\![x]\!]\times 2^{l_{i}-1}+[\![x]\!]\times 2^{l_{i}-1}.

(16)

The expression
[\![x]\!]\times 2^{l_{i}-1}
can be recursively computed using the same formula.
Since only addition is required, no extra multiplicative depth is needed, while keeping the scale of the ciphertext unchanged. However, it is important to note that the value of
l_{i}
should not be too large, as this could lead to increased computation time. In our implementation, we typically do not package too much data, so
l_{i}
is generally smaller than 10.

Since
[\![\frac{a_{1}}{2^{l_{1}}}]\!]
has a higher multiplicative depth than
[\![x]\!]
, we can thus obtain
[\![x^{(1)}]\!]
with more remaining multiplicative depth with the following computation:

[\![x^{(1)}]\!]=\frac{1}{2^{l_{1}}}\times[\![x]\!]-[\![\frac{a_{1}}{2^{l_{1}}}]\!].

(17)

5.5
CRTStack
Packing

Inspired by the CRT, we propose a parallelizable packing method. We construct a system of congruences using the data to be packed, and then solve the system to achieve the packing. During unpacking, we retrieve the packed data using mod function with different moduli. This allows the unpacking of data in different layers to be performed in parallel. Below, we describe the packing and unpacking process in detail.

Suppose we need to pack
d
vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{a}_{d}
into one vector for encryption into a single ciphertext.
We select
d
integers
P_{1},P_{2},\cdots,P_{d}
as the basic moduli, which are pairwise coprime.
For each
i
, the modulus
P_{i}
is chosen to be greater than the largest element in the corresponding vector
\mathbf{a}_{i}
.
Similar to
BitStack
, we describe only a single-dimensional input, denoted as
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{d}
, since the packing algorithm operates in an element-wise manner with identical operations across all dimensions. Below, we describe how to pack the
d
integers
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{d}
into a single integer using
CRTStack
packing method.

We construct the following system of congruences based on the numbers to be packed:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x&\equiv a_{1}\bmod P_{1}\\
x&\equiv a_{2}\bmod P_{2}\\
&\vdots\\
x&\equiv a_{d}\bmod P_{d}\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

The solution to the system of congruences is exactly the packed result we aim to obtain.
Let
P=P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\cdots\times P_{d}
. First, we compute
P_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{P}{P_{i}}
. Then, we compute the modular inverse of
P_{i}^{\prime}
modulo
P_{i}
, denoted as
m_{i}
. That is
P_{i}^{\prime}\times m_{i}\equiv 1\bmod P_{i}
. This inverse can be computed using the extended Euclidean algorithm, with a complexity of
O(\log P_{i})
. We can obtain the packed result as follows:

x=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\times P_{i}^{\prime}\times m_{i}\right)\bmod P.

(19)

The basic moduli are chosen as public parameters prior to packing.
Therefore, the values of
P^{\prime}_{i}
and
m_{i}
can be precomputed.
In fact, we can compute
b_{i}=P^{\prime}_{i}\times m_{i}\bmod P
in advance, before packing any data. Then, during the packing phase, we just need to compute

x=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\times b_{i}\right)\bmod P.

(20)

This simplifies the packing process to a inner product computation, which can be accomplished in a single loop.

Unpack Ciphertext.

For plaintext, unpacking is simple:
a_{i}=x\bmod P_{i}
. However, unpacking a ciphertext requires performing homomorphic mod operations on the ciphertext. According to the CRT, the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{P}
is isomorphic to the product ring
\mathbb{Z}_{P_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{P_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{P_{d}}
, since the basic moduli
P_{1},P_{2},\cdots,P_{d}
are pairwise coprime. Therefore,
\mathbb{Z}_{P_{i}}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{P_{j}}
are independent. The mappings from
\mathbb{Z}_{P}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{P_{i}}
and from
\mathbb{Z}_{P}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{P_{j}}
do not interfere with each other. As a result, during homomorphic unpacking, we can unpack each layer of data in parallel.

[\![a_{i}]\!]=\operatorname{ModP}([\![x]\!],P_{i}).

(21)

5.6
Discussion of Different Packing Methods

Application Scenarios.

VecConcat
is broadly applicable to BGV, BFV, and CKKS, and integrates easily into existing frameworks since unpacking requires only one multiplicative level and packing fresh ciphertexts incurs no depth. It is particularly suited when sufficient slot redundancy exists, such as for short vectors.

ImgConcat
leverages the imaginary part of CKKS plaintexts to further utilize slot redundancy, but cannot be applied under the conjugation-invariant CKKS variant
[
33
]
.

The advantage of our packing method lies in its ability to reduce the user’s overhead and increase the throughput of privacy-preserving data processing. On the server side, to ensure that the packed data does not interfere with subsequent privacy-preserving tasks, we additionally introduce homomorphic unpacking. Therefore, our method is suitable for outsourcing privacy-preserving processing for the user, where the user has multiple data items, each of which is a small input.

BitStack
vs.
CRTStack
.

CRTStack
requires selecting mutually coprime basic moduli, which may introduce additional redundancy at each level of the range dimension. In contrast,
BitStack
performs packing by directly stacking binary representations into a large integer, resulting in a more compact structure than
CRTStack
.
When approximating
\operatorname{ModP}
,
CRTStack
typically requires a wider approximation range, potentially demanding higher-degree polynomials. Moreover, the final layer of
BitStack
does not require the evaluation of
\operatorname{ModP}
, making it potentially faster for serial unpacking.

However,
CRTStack
supports parallel unpacking, as each layer requires only a single
\operatorname{ModP}
function evaluation. In contrast,
BitStack
must be unpacked sequentially, layer by layer, which consumes more multiplicative depth. Moreover, in serial decoding, the error from one layer in
BitStack
accumulates and propagates to the next, whereas in
CRTStack
, the accuracy of each layer’s unpacking depends solely on the approximation of
\operatorname{ModP}
and is independent of other layers. As a result, the intermediate-layer precision of
BitStack
is generally lower than that of
CRTStack
.

Combination.

It is worth noting that
BitStack
and
CRTStack
do not need to be used simultaneously, while other packing methods can be combined with greater flexibility.
This is because
BitStack
and
CRTStack
address the issue of range redundancy, while
VecConcat
and
ImgConcat
solve the problem of mismatched input data length and the number of slots in HE.
An example of such a combination is shown in
Fig.

2
. We first apply
VecConcat
to group the data into several vectors of length
n
, then use either
CRTStack
or
BitStack
to further stack the data, and finally apply
ImgConcat
to pack the result into complex vectors. During unpacking, the reverse order should be followed: we first use the unpacking of
ImgConcat
, then apply the unpacking of
CRTStack
(or
BitStack
), and finally use the unpacking of
VecConcat
to separate the individual data components. The packing order can be adjusted, but the unpacking process should follow the reverse sequence of packing.

Limitations.

Our scheme,
BitStack
and
CRTStack
, currently only supports inputs with a small value range, such as image data, genetic data, etc.
VecConcat
and
ImgConcat
, although not restricted by the input range, perform better with shorter input vectors.

6
Other Possible Applications of Mod

In this section, we explore additional potential applications that can benefit from our approximation of the mod function under outsourced processing based on HE.

6.1
Rounding of HE Ciphertexts

The rounding function typically takes three forms: the floor function
\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)
, which returns the largest integer less than or equal to
\frac{x}{p}
; the ceiling function
\operatorname{Ceil}(x,p)
, which returns the smallest integer greater than or equal to
\frac{x}{p}
; and the rounding function
\operatorname{Round}(x,p)
, which returns the integer closest to
\frac{x}{p}
. These functions are typical step functions, which makes directly simulating them using polynomials quite challenging. However, we can perform the computation using the mod function.

To review,
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
returns
r
such that
x=pz+r
where
z\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Therefore:

\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)=\frac{1}{p}\times(x-\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)).

(22)

Since CKKS supports real-number multiplication, the above formula can be directly implemented using CKKS. It is important to note that this can also be applied to unpacking of
BitStack
, which follows the same principle as the unpacking method described for Switch in
Section

5.4
.

The
\operatorname{Ceil}(x,p)
function is very similar to the
\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)
function, and we can implement it with the help of the
\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)
function and a comparison function, which can be precisely implemented using CKKS
[
36
]
. The comparison function
\operatorname{comp}(x,y)
returns 0 if
x<y
, 0.5 if
x=y
, and 1 if
x>y
. Thus, we have

\operatorname{Ceil}(x,p)=\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)+\operatorname{comp}(\operatorname{ModP}(x,p),0.5).

(23)

Similarly, we have

\operatorname{Round}(x,p)=\operatorname{Floor}(x,p)+\operatorname{comp}(\operatorname{ModP}(x,p),\frac{p}{2}-0.25).

(24)

Here, we use
\frac{p}{2}-0.25
to avoid the case where
\operatorname{comp}(x,y)
returns 0.5.

6.2
Secret Shares to HE Ciphertexts

Secret sharing is widely used in secure multi-party computation. Compared to HE, it has a lower storage cost. On the other hand, HE computation does not require interaction between parties, and its protocol design is simpler. In
[
1
]
, Akavia et al. proposes storing data using secret sharing and then having the server reconstruct the HE ciphertext to process the data privately using HE. However, they introduced a specific two-party secret sharing scheme and did not truly address the problem of converting secret shares to HE ciphertexts.
In this subsection, we describe how to reconstruct HE ciphertexts using secret shares, taking the
n
-party additive secret sharing as an example.

Suppose there are
n
participants
P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{n}
, where each participant
P_{i}
holds a secret share
s_{i}
. The secret shares satisfy the equation
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\mod p
, where
s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Each participant encrypts their secret share
s_{i}
, producing the ciphertext
[\![s_{i}]\!]
, and sends the ciphertext to the reconstructor. The reconstructor computes:

[\![x]\!]=\operatorname{ModP}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\![s_{i}]\!],p\right).

(25)

When the number of participants is large, the range of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
will be large. In this case, the degree of the polynomial used to approximate
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
must be high. However, we can reduce the input range by using a tree-based reconstruction approach. For example, we can compute:

[\![x]\!]=\operatorname{ModP}\left(\operatorname{ModP}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}[\![s_{i}]\!],p\right)+\operatorname{ModP}\left(\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}[\![s_{i}]\!],p\right),p\right).

(26)

This is actually a multi-way tree summation. Each leaf node corresponds to
[\![s]\!]_{i}
. From bottom to top, the internal nodes sum the values of their child nodes and perform
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
. Finally, the root node returns the HE ciphertext of
x
.
However, this approach increases the number of calls to
\operatorname{ModP}(x,p)
.

Maximum Chebyshev coefficients

To select an appropriate value for

8
Conclusion

In this work, we propose a method for approximating the mod function over its full input range, enabling mod operations on small integers within CKKS ciphertexts. Based on the proposed homomorphic mod function, we design two data packing schemes,
BitStack
and
CRTStack
, which significantly improve the utilization of the CKKS plaintext space. Furthermore, we demonstrate additional applications of the proposed HE mod function, including the conversion from secret shares to CKKS ciphertexts. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first complete scheme that enables the transformation from additive secret sharing to CKKS encryption. Although our current method is limited to small-integer inputs, it still covers a wide range of practical applications and offers new insights for future research on mod function approximation in HE.
