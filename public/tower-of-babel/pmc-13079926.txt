Reciprocal regulation of TNF receptor 1–mediated signaling and inflammatory damages by MARCH2 and USP22
Authors: Hui-Fang Li, Rui Liu, Peng Ren, Hong-Bing Shu, Shu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTNF is a secreted protein that binds to TNF-R1 to trigger systemic or local inflammatory responses and diseases. TNF inhibitors have been clinically proven effectively for inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. Here, we reveal that the level of TNF-R1 is regulated by a specific posttranslational modification called K27-linked polyubiquitination. We find that MARCH2 catalyzes the polyubiquitination of TNF-R1, leading to its degradation; whereas USP22 removes the polyubiquitin modification of TNF-R1, leading to its stability. In mouse models, USP22 deficiency alleviates psoriasis-like dermatitis and acute liver inflammation, whereas MARCH2 deficiency increases acute liver injury. Our study suggests that modulation of MARCH2 and USP22, such as by small molecule drugs, can regulate TNF-R1 level, offering a potential strategy for treating TNF-induced inflammatory diseases.

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a pleiotropic cytokine with important functions in homeostasis and disease pathogenesis, such as inflammation, proliferation, differentiation, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis (1–3). It has been shown that excessive TNF triggers a range of severe local inflammatory damages or autoimmune diseases, such as acute liver injury and psoriasis (1, 4). TNF promotes inflammation by activating the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathways, which collectively lead to the transcriptional induction of various proinflammatory genes. TNF indirectly potentiates and exacerbates inflammatory responses by triggering cell death such as apoptosis and necroptosis (5–7).

TNF binds to two distinct receptors, TNF-R1 and TNF-R2. It has been demonstrated that TNF-R1 serves as the primary signaling receptor, whereas TNF-R2 is expressed in limited cell types (8). Binding of TNF to TNF-R1 induces its oligomerization, triggering the recruitment of the death domain-containing adaptor protein TRADD to the receptor complex. TRADD then recruits additional adaptors such as TRAF2, TRAF5, and the death domain kinase RIP1 into the complex. Subsequently, RIP1 promotes the aggregation and activation of TAK1 by recruiting TAK1-TAB2/3 complex. TAK1 phosphorylates downstream kinases IKKα/IKKβ, which in turn phosphorylate the NF-κB inhibitor IκBα. This phosphorylation marks IκBα for ubiquitin-dependent degradation, leading to activation of the transcription factor NF-κB (p50/p65) and induction of downstream inflammatory genes (9–12).

Various studies have demonstrated that posttranslational modifications are critically involved in the regulation of TNF-R1-mediated intracellular signaling networks (13–16). Whether and how TNF-R1 is post-translationally regulated remains enigmatic. Recently, one study has reported that the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF8 mediates K63-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1, and deficiency of RNF8 impairs assembly of the TNF-R1 death-inducing signaling complex and subsequent apoptosis. Unexpectedly, RNF8-mediated polyubiquitination does not affect TNF-R1-mediated NF-κB activation (17). Whether and how TNF-R1 is post-translationally regulated by other mechanisms is unclear.

As a member of ubiquitin-specific proteases, USP22 deconjugates polyubiquitin moieties from the histone and nonhistone substrates and has been associated with cancer progression, neurological disorder, and telomere homeostasis (18–20). It has been reported that USP22 interacts with SPT-ADA-GCN5 acetyltransferase (SAGA) complex and removes polyubiquitin moieties from histone H2B (21, 22), thereby assuring chromatin compaction and maintaining accessible regions in an open conformation (23). USP22 also modulates the stability of membrane-associated proteins including EGFR, ER-α, and PD-L1 to regulate their downstream signaling cascades (24–26).

The membrane-associated RING-CH-type finger (MARCH) ubiquitin ligases constitute a large family of E3 ubiquitin ligases. The MARCH E3 ligases exhibit a conserved structure, typically containing an N-terminal RING finger domain and one or more C-terminal transmembrane domains (27, 28). Multiple MARCH family members have been demonstrated to ubiquitinate and promote the degradation of various immune receptors, resulting in negative regulation of these receptor-mediated immune responses. For example, MARCH3 mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of IL-1R1 and IL-3Rα (29–31), PD-1 signaling induces BATF-dependent MARCH5 expression, thereby promoting K27-linked polyubiquitination and subsequent degradation of the γc chain (32, 33). It has been demonstrated that MARCH2 and MARCH3 target IL-5 receptor alpha in a redundant manner to negatively regulate eosinophilic airway inflammation (34). MARCH2 and MARCH3 also redundantly target the Zika virus entry receptor TIM-1 to limit its infection (35). Additionally, MARCH2 catalyzes K48-linked ubiquitination of NEMO at K326, leading to NEMO degradation and negative regulation of NF-κB activation (36).

In this study, we identified MARCH2 and USP22 as reciprocal regulators of TNF-R1 stability and functions. MARCH2 is constitutively associated with TNF-R1, catalyzing K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 and its proteasomal degradation. Conversely, TNF stimulation increases the association of TNF-R1 and USP22, resulting in deubiquitination and increased stability of TNF-R1, and subsequent augmentation of TNF-triggered induction of proinflammatory genes. USP22 deficiency alleviates IMQ-induced psoriasis-like dermatitis. USP22 deficiency also reduces TNF/D-gal-induced acute liver injury, whereas MARCH2 deficiency has the opposite effects. These findings reveal a dynamic regulation of TNF-R1 stability by MARCH2 and USP22, providing a layer of regulatory mechanisms on TNF-R1-mediated inflammatory response.

Results

USP22 Positively Regulates TNF-Triggered Signaling by Up-Regulating TNF-R1.

In an attempt to identify additional TNF-R1-associated proteins, we expressed Flag-tagged TNF-R1 in HEK293 cells, and TNF-R1-bound proteins were immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag antibody and analyzed by mass spectrometry. Among the 327 proteins identified, 6 are deubiquitinases (Dataset S1). Cotransfection experiments indicated that overexpression of USP22 but not the other 5 deubiquitinases caused up-regulation of TNF-R1 (Fig. 1A). Overexpression of USP22 increased the levels of TNF-R1 but not the IL-1 receptor accessory protein (IL-1RAP) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Overexpression of USP22 also potentiated TNF-induced NF-κB activation (Fig. 1C), phosphorylation of IκBαS32/36 and p65S536 (Fig. 1D), and transcription of downstream inflammatory genes CXCL10, IL-8, and CCL20 (Fig. 1E).

We next generated USP22-deficient THP-1 cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 method. Flow cytometry analysis indicated that USP22 deficiency down-regulated the protein level of plasma membrane-associated TNF-R1 (Fig. 2A). TNF stimulation transiently increased the levels of TNF-R1, p-IκBαS32/36, and p-p65S536, while USP22 deficiency reduced TNF-induced increase of TNF-R1, p-IκBαS32/36, and p-p65S536 levels (Fig. 2B). USP22 deficiency also inhibited TNF-induced transcription of CXCL10, IL-8, and CCL20 genes in THP-1 cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, we generated macrophage-specific Usp22 knockout mice (Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre). TNF-induced increase of TNF-R1, p-IκBαS32/36, and p-p65S536 levels (Fig. 2D) and transcription of Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Ccl20 genes (Fig. 2E) were down-regulated in USP22-deficient mouse bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) and dendritic cells (BMDCs) compared to their wild-type counterparts. These results suggest that USP22 positively regulates TNF-R1 level and TNF-triggered signaling.

USP22 Removes K27-Linked Polyubiquitin Moieties from TNF-R1 and Impairs Its Proteasomal Degradation.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms responsible for USP22-mediated promotion of TNF-triggered signaling, we examined whether USP22 is associated with components in TNF-triggered signaling pathways. Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments indicated that USP22 was associated with TNF-R1 and its downstream components including TRAF2, TRAF5, and RIP1 but not TRADD in mammalian overexpression system (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). However, overexpression of USP22 only caused up-regulation of TNF-R1, but not the examined downstream components including TRADD, TRAF2, TRAF5, and RIP1 (Fig. 3A). Endogenous co-IP experiments indicated that USP22 was minimally associated with TNF-R1 in THP-1 cells in the absence of TNF stimulation (Fig. 3B). Notably, the association was increased at 2.5 min, peaked at 5 min and returned to basal level at 30 min after TNF stimulation, suggesting a dynamic regulation of TNF-R1 by USP22.

Since USP22 is a deubiquitinating enzyme and mediates up-regulation of TNF-R1, we next investigated whether it deubiquitinates TNF-R1. Overexpression of USP22 but not its enzymatic inactive mutant USP22C185S removed polyubiquitin moieties from TNF-R1 and up-regulated the levels of TNF-R1 in HEK293 cells (Fig. 3C). Utilizing ubiquitin mutants in which one or six lysine residues are replaced with arginine (R), we found that USP22 removed K27- but not other lysine residue-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Endogenous ubiquitination assays indicated that TNF stimulation slightly increased K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1, and these effects were enhanced in USP22-deficient THP-1 cells (Fig. 3D) and Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) compared to their controls respectively. These results suggest that USP22 removes K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 before and after TNF stimulation. To further explore the mechanisms responsible for polyubiquitination-primed degradation of TNF-R1, we treated HEK293 cells with inhibitors for various protein degradation pathways. Immunoblotting analysis indicated that MG132 markedly inhibited TNF-R1 degradation after termination of protein synthesis by cycloheximide (CHX) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Up-regulation of TNF-R1 by USP22 was inhibited by treatment of cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 but not the lysosome inhibitor NH4Cl or autophagosome inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Notably, TNF-R1 levels in MG132-treated cells were higher than mock-treated cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E), probably due to the inhibition of basal proteasomal degradation of TNF-R1 by MG132. These results suggest that USP22 removes K27-linked polyubiquitin moieties of TNF-R1 and reduces its proteasomal degradation. We next investigated the residues in TNF-R1 that are conjugated with K27-linked polyubiquitination. We individually mutated each of the 3 lysine residues (K340, K347, and K372) within the intracellular region (aa274–455) of TNF-R1 to arginine and examined whether these mutants could be modified by K27-linked polyubiquitination. The results indicated that mutation of K340 but not the other 2 lysine residues to arginine dramatically impaired USP22-removed K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 (Fig. 3E). Under these experimental conditions, TNF-R1K340R retained residual basal K27-linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 3E). It is possible that the basal polyubiquitination of TNF-R1K340R is originated from its nonspecific binding to other ubiquitinated proteins under the experimental conditions or additional minor ubiquitination on residues other than K340 exists on TNF-R1. Consistently, overexpression of USP22 failed to up-regulate the level of TNF-R1K340R (Fig. 3F). Sequence analysis indicated that K340 of human TNF-R1 was conserved in various species (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). These results suggest that USP22 deconjugates K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 at K340.

MARCH2 Mediates K27-Linked Polyubiquitination and Degradation of TNF-R1 at K340.

We next attempted to identify potential E3 ubiquitin ligases that mediate K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1. Previous studies have shown that the MARCH E3 ligase family members target certain cytokine receptors for polyubiquitination and degradation. We wondered whether TNF-R1 is also targeted by members of the MARCH family. We cotransfected each of the 11 MARCH family proteins with TNF-R1 and examined their regulation on TNF-R1 levels. These screens indicated that overexpression of MARCH2 most dramatically down-regulated TNF-R1 level (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Overexpression of MARCH2 but not MARCH3 caused the down-regulation of TNF-R1 in a dose-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Endogenous coimmunoprecipitation experiments showed that MARCH2 was constitutively associated with TNF-R1 in unstimulated cells, and their association was slightly reduced after TNF stimulation for 2.5 min (Fig. 4A).

We next explored the molecular mechanisms of MARCH2-mediated polyubiquitination of TNF-R1. Overexpression of MARCH2 but not MARCH3 or MARCH2 inactive mutants (MARCH2C64S, MARCH2C67S, or MARCH2C80S) promoted polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). In vitro ubiquitination assays indicated that MARCH2 catalyzed polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 with UbcH5 as an E2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D), suggesting that MARCH2 serves as a direct E3 ligase for TNF-R1. Further experiments showed that overexpression of MARCH2 increased K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1, which was removed by USP22 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Coimmunoprecipitation experiments showed that USP22 has no marked effects on the interaction between TNF-R1 and MARCH2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F), suggesting that the interaction of TNF-R1 and MARCH2 precedes the K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1. Overexpressed MARCH2 catalyzed K27-linked polyubiquitination of wild-type TNF-R1 but not TNF-R1K340R (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Endogenous ubiquitination assays indicated that MARCH2 deficiency inhibited basal and TNF-induced K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 (Fig. 4C). Reconstitution experiments indicated that both the basal and TNF-induced polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 were higher than that of TNF-R1K340R, and the level of TNF-R1 but not TNF-R1K340R was down-regulated following TNF stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H). Additionally, confocal microscopy experiments indicated that when overexpressed in cells, wild-type TNF-R1 colocalized with MARCH2 and proteasomes at the plasma membrane, whereas TNF-R1K340R had much reduced colocalization with MARCH2 and proteasomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). These results suggest that MARCH2 mediates K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 at K340 and promotes its subsequent proteasomal degradation, whereas USP22 has opposite effects. Consistently, reconstitution of TNF-R1K340R in TNF-R1-deficient HEK293 cells increased TNF-induced transcription of the downstream genes CXCL10, IL-8, and CCL20 compared with cells reconstituted with wild-type TNF-R1 (Fig. 4D).

To further investigate the functions of MARCH2 in primary immune cells, we generated MARCH2- and MARCH3-deficient mice utilizing the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy (37). Flow cytometry indicated the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of TNF-R1 on March2−/− BMDMs and BMDCs were higher than that on wildtype BMDMs and BMDCs respectively (Fig. 5A). In addition, MARCH2 deficiency increased TNF-induced phosphorylation of IκBα and p65 (Fig. 5B), as well as transcription of Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Ccl20 genes in BMDMs and BMDCs (Fig. 5C). These results further confirm that MARCH2 down-regulates TNF-R1 as well as TNF-induced signaling in primary mouse immune cells.

USP22 Deficiency Alleviates Imiquimod-Induced Psoriasis-Like Dermatitis.

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder that is characterized by thickened, scaly plaques and most frequently begins in young adults. It has been demonstrated that TNF plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis and anti-TNF antibodies have been approved for the treatment of psoriasis (38–40). Therefore, we attempt to explore whether USP22 is involved in psoriasis-like dermatitis. A simple and useful method to induce psoriasiform dermatitis in mice is the topical administration of the TLR7/8 agonist imiquimod (IMQ), which acts by activating plasmacytoid and myeloid dendritic cells (41). To determine the effects of USP22 deficiency on IMQ-induced psoriasis-like dermatitis, age- and sex-matched Usp22f/f and Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice were treated with IMQ on the shaved back for 7 consecutive days. Psoriasis area severity index (PASI) score was used to assess the degree of erythema, scale, and thickness of skin lesions in mice (42). As shown in Fig. 6 A and B, USP22 deficiency alleviated the IMQ-induced psoriatic phenotypes with correspondingly lower PASI scores. The levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-17A, IL-22, and TNF) were significantly lower in the sera of Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared with those in Usp22f/f mice at 7 d post IMQ administration (Fig. 6C). Consistently, the mRNA levels of keratinocyte differentiation markers (Krt6, Krt16, Krt17, and Involucrin) in skin were markedly down-regulated in Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared to Usp22f/f mice at 7 d post IMQ administration (Fig. 6D). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showed that the Vaseline-treated Usp22f/f mice displayed typical epidermal morphology without significant thickening and preserved intact dermal architecture. The epidermal thickness of Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice was thinner than that of Usp22f/f mice induced by IMQ (Fig. 6E). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining indicated that the proliferative marker Ki67 was significantly decreased in Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared with Usp22f/f mice treated with IMQ (Fig. 6F). Splenomegaly also serves as an indicator of worsening pathology (43). Our results indicated that the spleens of Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice were enlarged to lower degrees compared to Usp22f/f mice following IMQ treatment. Both spleen weight and splenic mass index were significantly reduced in Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared with Usp22f/f mice following IMQ treatment (Fig. 6G).

USP22 Deficiency Alleviates TNF/D-Gal-Induced Acute Liver Injury.

We next investigated the functional significance of USP22- and MARCH2-mediated regulation of TNF-R1 polyubiquitination in vivo. Coadministration of TNF/D-gal (D-galactosamine) is a well-established model to induce acute liver injury (ALI) in mice, which has been widely used to study the pathophysiology of ALI and identify novel therapeutic strategies (44). D-gal mediates the sensitivity of hepatocytes to TNF-induced cytotoxicity (45). To assess the effects of USP22 deficiency on ALI, age- and sex-matched Usp22f/f and Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice were intraperitoneally injected with TNF plus D-gal for 6 h. ELISA experiments indicated that levels of the inflammatory cytokines (CXCL1, CXCL10, and IL-6) were significantly lower in the sera of Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared with those in Usp22f/f mice (Fig. 7A). Consistently, hepatic mRNA levels of Cxcl10, Mcp1, Il-1β, and Tnf-α were down-regulated in Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice relative to Usp22f/f mice (Fig. 7B). Gross examination showed that livers from mock-injected Usp22f/f mice were bright red, smooth, and soft, whereas livers from TNF/D-gal-induced Usp22f/f mice appeared dark red with numerous bleeding spots, and livers from TNF/D-gal-induced Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice exhibited reduced hemorrhaging compared to those from TNF/D-gal-induced Usp22f/f mice (Fig. 7C). HE staining showed that the liver from Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice displayed less hemorrhaging, congestion, inflammatory cell infiltrate, and structural disorders compared with Usp22f/f mice (Fig. 7D). IHC staining further demonstrated the attenuated hepatic myeloperoxidase (MPO) expression in the livers of TNF/D-gal-induced Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice compared to those of Usp22f/f mice (Fig. 7E). Survival analysis revealed that Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice were less susceptible to death compared to Usp22f/f mice following challenge with TNF plus D-gal (Fig. 7F). The levels of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in Usp22f/f:Lyz2-Cre mice were markedly reduced compared to those in Usp22f/f mice after TNF/D-gal treatment (Fig. 7G). These results collectively suggest that USP22 deficiency reduces TNF/D-gal-induced hepatic inflammatory response and ALI.

MARCH2 Deficiency Exacerbates TNF/D-Gal Induced Acute Liver Injury.

To assess the functions of MARCH2 in regulating TNF-mediated inflammatory responses in vivo, age- and sex-matched March2+/+and March2−/−mice were intraperitoneally injected with TNF plus D-gal for 6 h. We found that the levels of CXCL1, CXCL10, and IL-6 were significantly higher in the sera of March2−/−mice compared with those in March2+/+mice (Fig. 8A). Consistently, the mRNA levels of Cxcl10, Mcp1, Il-1β, and Tnf-α in livers were up-regulated in March2−/−mice compared to March2+/+ mice (Fig. 8B). The livers of the TNF/D-gal-induced March2−/−mice appeared dark red with numerous bleeding spots, whereas March2+/+ livers exhibited markedly reduced damage (Fig. 8C). HE staining indicated that March2−/−mice hepatocytes exhibited more severe destruction and necrosis, along with increased nuclear swelling, fragmentation, and inflammatory infiltration compared with March2+/+mice induced by TNF/D-gal (Fig. 8D). IHC staining further demonstrated the exacerbated hepatic MPO expression in the livers of March2−/−mice treated with TNF/D-gal (Fig. 8E). Survival analysis revealed that March2−/−mice were more susceptible to TNF/D-gal-induced lethality (Fig. 8F). Consistently, March2−/− mice exhibited markedly higher serum levels of ALT and AST post TNF/D-gal-treatment compared with March2+/+ mice (Fig. 8G). These results suggest that MARCH2 deficiency exacerbates TNF/D-gal-induced hepatic inflammatory response and ALI in mice.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that the TNF-R1/NF-κB axis plays crucial roles in various biological processes, as well as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, Crohn’s disease, and ulcerative colitis (46–48). The level and activity of TNF-R1 are precisely regulated through posttranslational modification to balance protective immunity while preventing excessive inflammatory damage following external stimulation (17). In this study, we demonstrate that the level of TNF-R1 is negatively regulated by MARCH2-mediated K27-linked polyubiquitination and positively regulated by USP22-mediated deubiquitination, revealing a reciprocal regulatory mechanism of TNF-R1 level and TNF-R1-mediated inflammatory responses.

Our experiments demonstrate that the E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH2 is constitutively associated with TNF-R1 under physiological conditions, catalyzes its K27-linked polyubiquitination at K340 and promotes its subsequent proteasomal degradation. MARCH2 inactive mutants fail to mediate K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 at K340, demonstrating an essential role of its E3 ligase activity in mediating polyubiquitination of TNF-R1K340. MARCH2 deficiency reduces basal and TNF-induced K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 and increases its expression at the protein level. MARCH2 deficiency potentiates TNF-induced phosphorylation cascades and transcription of downstream proinflammatory cytokines in BMDMs and BMDCs. Consistently, reconstitution of TNF-R1-deficient cells with TNF-R1K340R potentiates TNF-triggered transcription of downstream proinflammatory genes compared to reconstitution with wild-type TNF-R1. These results suggest that MARCH2 mediates K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 at K340 and its subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby negatively regulating TNF-triggered inflammatory response in various cell types. Our results are consistent with a previous study showing that after TNF stimulation, TNF-R1 is translocated into lipid rafts, where it is ubiquitinated and degraded (49, 50). It has also been demonstrated that upon TNF stimulation, TNF-R1 is endocytosized to endosomes and multivesicular bodies, which is essential for its ability to mediate apoptosis (51). In light of these and our studies, it is possible that TNF-R1 can be regulated by multiple mechanisms with distinct outcomes on TNF-triggered biological and pathological effects.

Our experiments show that the deubiquitinase USP22 is minimally associated with TNF-R1 under physiological conditions, and their association is increased following TNF stimulation. Overexpression of USP22 but not its inactive mutants deconjugates K27-linked polyubiquitin moieties at K340 of TNF-R1, leading to up-regulation of TNF-R1 level. USP22 deficiency enhances K27-linked polyubiquitination of TNF-R1 at K340 and down-regulates its level before and after TNF stimulation. USP22 deficiency also suppresses TNF-triggered phosphorylation of IκBα and p65 as well as transcription of downstream proinflammatory genes. These results suggest that USP22 deubiquitinates TNF-R1 and up-regulates its level, thereby promoting TNF-triggered inflammatory response. Collectively, our results suggest that MARCH2 and USP22 reciprocally regulate K27-linked polyubiquitination and stability of TNF-R1. Based on our data, the simplest model on the reciprocal regulation of TNF-R1 is that TNF-R1 is constitutively associated with MARCH2 in unstimulated cells, and TNF stimulation induces the disassociation of TNF-R1 with MARCH2, which enables it to recruit USP22 to remove the K27-linked polyubiquitin moieties conjugated to TNF-R1, leading to suppression of its proteasomal degradation.

We further evaluated the functional significance of reciprocal regulation of TNF-R1 polyubiquitination by USP22 and MARCH2 in mouse models. In the model of IMQ-induced psoriasis, USP22-deficient mice display alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration and thinner epidermal thickness compared to wild-type mice. In an acute liver injury model, USP22-deficient mice exhibit markedly alleviated liver damage response, such as lower transcriptional levels of inflammatory cytokines following TNF/D-gal treatment. USP22-deficient mice are also less susceptible to TNF/D-gal-induced death and have lower activity of MPO compared to wild-type mice. These results suggest that USP22-mediated deubiquitination and up-regulation of TNF-R1 acts as a mechanism of augmenting TNF-triggered inflammatory response. Conversely, MARCH2-deficient mice display aggravated liver damage response, such as increased inflammatory cytokine levels in the sera following TNF/D-gal treatment. MARCH2-deficient mice are also more susceptible to TNF/D-gal-induced inflammatory death and have greatly increased activity of ALT and AST compared to wild-type mice. These results suggest that MARCH2 constitutively targets TNF-R1 for K27-linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby maintaining TNF-R1 at a proper level to avoid excessive TNF signaling activity and inflammatory response. Although the phenotypes observed in the above animal models support our conclusions that TNF-R1 is reciprocally regulated by USP22 and MARCH2, our experiments cannot exclude that the other substrates of USP22 and MARCH2 also contribute to the phenotypes. Additional studies, such as knockin of TNF-R1K340R, conditional or cell-type-specific knockout animal models are needed to address this outstanding question.

In conclusion, our study reveals a reciprocal regulatory mechanism of TNF-R1 by MARCH2-mediated polyubiquitination and USP22-mediated deubiquitination, which contributes to proper regulation of TNF-R1-mediated inflammatory response. The deregulation of MARCH2 or USP22 promotes TNF-induced inflammatory response and tissue damage in animal models. Therefore, MARCH2 and USP22 may represent potential targets for development of drugs for inflammatory diseases.
