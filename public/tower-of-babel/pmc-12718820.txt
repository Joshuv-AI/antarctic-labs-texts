Psoas Syndrome Following a Generalized Seizure
Authors: Yoonjeong Na, Jung-Ju Lee, Byung-Kun Kim, Kyusik Kang, Woong-Woo Lee, Ilhan Yoo, Namoh Kim, Michelle Sojung Youn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoas syndrome is a rare form of muscle irritation that causes pain and weakness in the lower extremities. A 52-year-old male presented to the emergency department with complaints of having had a seizure and weakness in the left lower extremity. A computerized tomography scan of the abdomen and pelvis revealed reversible thickening of the left psoas muscle, consistent with psoas syndrome. We report a case of psoas syndrome following a generalized seizure and discuss its clinical characteristics and differential diagnoses.Keywords: Psoas, Computerized tomography, Seizure

Introduction

Psoas syndrome (PS) is a rare form of muscle irritation that causes pain and weakness in the lower extremities. The most common causes of PS are overuse of the hips and sports injuries. Mechanical strain may lead to spasm, inflammation, or fibrosis.1,2 Diagnosis can be made through clinical findings such as the resisted straight-leg raise and it can be confirmed with diagnostic imaging.1 We experienced a patient compatible with PS following a generalized seizure, and herein we discuss the clinical characteristics and differential diagnoses.

Case Report

A 52-year-old male visited the emergency department (ED), complaining of having had a seizure the previous day. He suddenly experienced blurry vision at 9 pm and tried to talk to his family about his symptoms but was unable to speak at that time. A few minutes after these symptoms appeared, he lost consciousness. His family noticed a generalized convulsion with left-side eyeball deviation and a tongue bite lasting about 2–3 minutes. On that day, he visited a local hospital, where routine blood tests and a brain computerized tomography (CT) scan were performed, revealing no specific abnormalities. The next morning, after waking up, he noticed that he was unable to walk due to weakness in the left leg and pain in the left groin and anterior hip area. Because the symptoms were not relieved after self-massage and stretching, he visited our ED.

He had been consuming two or more bottles of Korean sake almost every day since he was in his twenties. He was diagnosed with alcoholic liver cirrhosis 10 years prior to his visit and had been taking medications for his liver. He last consumed Korean sake about 24 hours before the seizure.

He was unable to flex his hip while lying in bed. Hip adduction was moderately limited (medical research council [MRC] grade 3), but hip abduction was relatively unimpaired (MRC grade 4). He could not walk unassisted. During the straight leg-raising test, he felt pain in the left groin and hip. Local tenderness was observed in the left groin area. Distal muscle weakness in the left extremity was not observed. Deep tendon reflexes were normal in the patellar and achilles tendons. The Babinski reflex was absent. Other focal neurological abnormalities were unobservable.

On routine blood tests, the levels of liver enzymes (asparatate aminotransferase [AST], 486 IU/L; alanine aminotransferase [ALT], 61 IU/L; normal values: AST, 0–40 IU/L; ALT, 0–40 IU/L), total bilirubin (3.9 mg/dL; normal value: 0.2–1.2 mg/dL), direct bilirubin (1.5 mg/dL; normal value: 0.0–0.5 mg/dL), and creatine phosphokinase (CPK) (CPK, 5,400 IU/L; normal value: 46–171 IU/L) were elevated. On urinalysis, a severe degree of microscopic hematuria was observed. Imaging tests, including brain CT and diffusion-weighted image, revealed no abnormalities. He was diagnosed with alcohol withdrawal seizure and admitted to the Department of Neurology.

On the second day of admission, electroencephalography was performed, which revealed no abnormalities. On a CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis, performed to determine the cause of microscopic hematuria, thickening of the left psoas muscle was observed (Fig. 1). The proximal leg weakness of the patient gradually subsided during admission and resolved on the third day of admission, but the CPK level continued to elevate until the fifth day of admission (up to 31,346 IU/L). The patient was treated with hydration and chlordiazepoxide (5 mg thrice daily) to prevent delirium tremens. On a follow-up CT scan performed on the sixth day of admission, the thickness of the left psoas muscle was significantly reduced (Fig. 2). He was discharged after tenth day of admission without any neurological sequelae.

Discussion

Psoas muscle is a long, ribbon-shaped muscle in the lower lumbar region. The function of the psoas muscle is to connect the upper body to the lower body, linking the appendicular to the axial skeletons.3 The psoas muscle is a major contributor of flexion of the hip joint. It functions as a hip flexor, hip adductor, and hip external rotator.4

Patients with PS typically present with anterior groin pain that worsens with hip flexion. Pain and discomfort may also involve the sacrum, buttocks, pelvis, or lower back. It is seldom observed in athletes, particularly runners and those engaging in plyometric activities.1,2 PS is generally caused by prolonged, repetitive muscle activity; however, acute injury can also lead to PS.5 Vigorous seizure activity may result in acute inflammation of the psoas muscles. The incidence and prevalence of PS in the general population are unknown, probably due to underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis, and spontaneous resolution of the symptoms.1

A generalized tonic-clonic seizure commonly causes excessive muscle activity and muscle cell damage. Elevated CPK levels and general weakness accompanied by whole-body aches are common findings after a seizure. However, focal muscle weakness and localized pain after a seizure is unusual. Todd paresis (TP) is one of the possible explanations for focal weakness following a seizure. It most commonly affects one limb or one side of the body. TP is usually observed in patients with focal seizures but can also occur in those with generalized seizures.6 TP may last from minutes to days. The median time for complete resolution of TP is 15 hours.7 In our patient, the symptom persisted for more than 24 hours, and localized pain and tenderness were observed, which are uncommon in TP. Asymmetrical involvement of the psoas muscle was observed in our patient. The underlying mechanism is not definitive; however, patient positioning or the focal nature of seizure activity may account for this asymmetry.

PS is a rare disorder that usually occurs after excessive exercise. Its rarity may be attributed to being overlooked by physicians. Violent generalized seizures may also cause PS. In patients with prolonged focal weakness in the lower extremities and pain in a specific area, PS should be considered as a differential diagnosis.
