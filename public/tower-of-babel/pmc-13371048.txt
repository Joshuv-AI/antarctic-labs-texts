Inhibition of macrophage migration inhibitory factor attenuates bleomycin-induced murine pulmonary fibrosis via the MAPK pathway
Authors: Jingwei Wang, Wei Zhao, Nafeisa Dilixiati, Yuanying Wang, Yawen Song, Qiao Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic progressive disease marked by excessive extracellular matrix deposition and deteriorating lung function. Although macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) has been implicated in pulmonary fibrosis, the downstream mechanisms—particularly whether MIF drives fibrosis through its receptor CD74—remain insufficiently understood. This study investigated whether MIF/CD74 signaling promotes fibroblast activation through specific downstream pathways that regulate proliferation and matrix production.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing and ELISA were used to characterize MIF–CD74 interactions and circulating MIF levels in IPF patients. Bleomycin-induced fibrosis models were established using C57BL/6J mice, Mif⁻/⁻ mice, and the MIF inhibitor 4-IPP. Macrophage–fibroblast co-culture systems were treated with bleomycin, 4-IPP, or CD74 shRNA. Lung injury, fibrosis, and signaling activation were assessed by histology, qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and functional assays.ResultsscRNA-seq identified MIF-CD74 as a dominant macrophage-fibroblast signaling axis in fibrotic lungs, and serum MIF levels were elevated in IPF patients. In mice, bleomycin markedly increased MIF and CD74 expression and activated MAPK signaling. Genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition of MIF attenuated collagen deposition, improved lung architecture, and reduced profibrotic and inflammatory signaling. In vitro, MIF promoted fibroblast proliferation, migration, and matrix synthesis, whereas CD74 knockdown or MIF inhibition significantly attenuated fibroblast profibrotic responses.ConclusionsMIF promotes pulmonary fibrosis by activating CD74-dependent MAPK signaling in fibroblasts, enhancing their proliferation and extracellular matrix production. Background

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung scarring disease of unknown reason, characterized by exertional dyspnea and impaired lung function [1]. The prognosis for IPF is generally poor, with a median survival time of only 2.5 to 3.5 years following diagnosis without therapy, and survival time decreasing with advancing age [2]. In recent years, the incidence of IPF has been on rise [3]. Recent epidemiological studies report global annual prevalence and incidence rates ranging from 0.33 to 4.51 per 10,000 people and 0.09 to 1.30 per 10,000 people, respectively. In Europe alone, approximately 40,000 new patients of IPF are diagnosed each year [4]. The available pirfenidone and nintedanib, which can slow disease progression but cannot reverse scarring [5]. Lung transplantation is the curative option for IPF patients, but age and comorbidities limit its feasibility to only a minority of patients [6]. These limitations highlight an urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies for IPF.

The pathogenesis of IPF involves complex interactions among epithelial, mesenchymal, immune, and endothelial cells mediated by multiple signaling pathways [7]. The activation and polarization of macrophages play a critical role in inflammation and the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis [8, 9]. Macrophages exhibit two distinct phenotypes in the regulation of inflammatory responses: the classically activated phenotype and the alternatively activated phenotype [10]. The pro-fibrotic factors secreted by M2 macrophages, such as TGF-β, FIZZ1, PDGF, and VEGF, may stimulate the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, thereby exacerbating pulmonary fibrosis [11]. Consequently, innovative therapeutic strategies targeting macrophages hold significant promise as potential clinical approaches for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis [12, 13].

Macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) is an inflammatory and stress-regulating cytokine, playing a critical role in inflammatory diseases and atherosclerosis [14]. MIF is recognized as a pleiotropic inflammatory mediator involved in both innate and adaptive immune responses [14], exerting its effects through the activation of multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, AMPK, JNK, and others [15, 16]. It is present in most immune cells, such as macrophages, monocytes and T cells. In pulmonary inflammation, MIF promotes the accumulation of neutrophils by binding to the CD74 receptor on macrophages [17]. Elevated serum MIF levels have been detected in IPF patients with pulmonary hypertension. Significantly higher MIF levels also reported in the lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of bleomycin (BLM)-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse models [18]. In vitro, the fibroblasts treated with BLM exhibit elevated levels of Mif mRNA [19]. Inhibition of MIF in BLM-induced mouse models has been shown to attenuate lung collagen deposition, decrease pulmonary hypertension and reduce perivascular macrophages [18]. These findings suggest that MIF may represent a key driver of IPF and a highly promising therapeutic target.

The mechanisms underlying IPF, including the role of MIF and its downstream pathways, have not yet been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study was designed to clarify the disease progression and potential pathogenesis of IPF by integrating single-cell sequencing technology. In addition, a BLM-induced mouse model and a cellular model of pulmonary fibrosis were established to investigate possible pathogenic pathways and, importantly, potential therapeutic strategies. The findings demonstrated that MIF contributes to the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis by promoting fibroblast proliferation and migration through binding to the CD74 receptor and activating the MAPK pathway. These results not only deepen the understanding of IPF pathogenesis but also provide a scientific rationale for targeting MIF as a therapeutic strategy for IPF.

Methods

scRNA-seq analysis

The dataset GSE135893 which includes RNA-seq data from lung tissue cells of IPF patients, was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. The Seurat R package (version 4.1.0) [20] was extensively utilized for the scRNA-seq data processing.

Cell type annotation

Cell types were identified by determining cluster-specific markers using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. The identified cell types were annotated based on marker genes, with reference to the CellMarker, PanglaoDB databases, and the original study associated with the dataset.

Cell-cell communication analysis

To systematically analyze cell-cell communication, we used CellPhoneDB [21], a public repository of ligand-receptor interactions. Significant mean values and cell communication significance (P < 0.05) were calculated based on the interactions and the normalized cell matrix obtained through Seurat.

Patients

To evaluate MIF expression in patients, we collected serum samples from 15 randomly selected individuals diagnosed with IPF at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, alongside 15 healthy controls. At enrolment, retrospective clinical data were obtained from patients’ medical records. Patients with missing clinical information, a family history of pulmonary fibrosis, or other interstitial lung diseases were excluded.

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China) and acclimatized for at least one week prior to the experiments. MIF knockout (Mif−/−) C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (China). Pulmonary fibrosis was induced via intravenous injection of BLM (2 mg/kg; Nippon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan), with saline used as a control. On day 21, the mice were sacrificed, and blood and lung tissues were collected.

MIF inhibitor treatment

4-iodo-6-phenylpyrimidine (4-IPP), an irreversible MIF inhibitor, was purchased from MedChem Express (USA) and dissolved in DMSO. In vitro, it was used at concentrations of 5, 10, and 15 µM for cell culture. In vivo, doses of 5 and 25 mg/kg were administered. Based on previous studies, mice received 4-IPP for 21 days. On day 21, the mice were sacrificed, blood and lung tissues were collected for further analysis.

Histological staining and analysis

The left lung tissue was fixed in 10% formalin for at least 24 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared and incubated at 60 °C for 4 h. Hematoxylin and 0.2% eosin (Histolab Products, USA) were used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, while adjacent sections underwent Masson’s trichrome staining.

Co-Immunoprecipitation(CoIP)

Proteins were extracted from lung tissue of mice and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies against MIF (CST, USA), CD74 (Abcam, USA), or IgG control (CST, USA). Prewashed protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, USA) were added to the lysates and rotated for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing the beads four times with PBST, the immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by Western blot.

Cell treatment

RAW264.7 and NIH3T3 cell lines were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd(China). BLM was dissolved in saline at a concentration of 1 mg/ml and stored at 4 °C, protected from light. MIF (purchased from Abcam, USA) was dissolved in 0.9% saline at a concentration of 1 µg/ml and stored in aliquots at -20 °C. Depending on the experiment and cell type, MIF was used at concentrations of 1, 10, or 100 ng/ml. MIF shRNA (LV83091560) was cloned into a lentiviral expression vector and purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China) for transfection into NIH3T3 cells.

Fibroblast transwell migration assay

Fibroblast migration was assessed using 24-well Transwell inserts with 8 μm pore membranes (Nunc, ThermoFisher, USA). NIH3T3 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells/ml in the upper chamber with serum-free medium, while the lower chamber contained complete medium with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. MIF was added at concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 ng/ml. After 24 h of incubation, cells in the lower chamber were stained with crystal violet and imaged under a microscope at 10× magnification.

MIF Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, MIF levels in the serum of mice, IPF patients, and healthy controls were assessed using a quantitative ELISA kit specific for MIF (BlueGene Biotech, China). Optical density was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a multifunctional microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 50 mg of lung tissue or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop ND1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the KR103 reverse transcription kit (Tsingke Biotechnology, China), and qPCR was performed with the FP205 SYBR Green PreMix kit (Tsingke Biotechnology, China) on an ABI 7500 Fast system. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin as a reference gene.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from lung tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China), and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Followed by secondary antibody incubation at room temperature for 1 h.

Protein bands were detected using the LICOR Odyssey Imaging System. The secondary antibodies were anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (both from CST, USA).

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EDU) staining

Cells were cultured on glass coverslips, and after 24 h of incubation, EDU was added for 6 h. The cells were stained using an in vitro DNA proliferation detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and ImageJ software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

MIF/CD74 is involved in pulmonary fibrosis

The single-cell dataset comprised 212,566 cells, and after stringent filtering, 166,215 cells remained, including 119,584 from the IPF group and 46,631 from the non-fibrotic control group. Through unsupervised clustering, 19 distinct cell types were identified and annotated based on specific gene expression profiles reported in previous IPF studies [22]. These cell types included fibroblasts, macrophages, epithelial cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, myofibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, mesothelial cells, monocytes, plasma cells, mast cells, NK cells, B cells, T cells, doublet cells. The distribution of these integrated cell populations was visualized using UMAP (Fig. 1A), showing distinct localization patterns of cell types. The differential genes of each cluster are shown in Fig. S1, demonstrating clear separation among the clusters.

To visualize the distribution of macrophages within the integrated single-cell dataset, macrophages were identified and highlighted on the global UMAP (Fig. 1B). Three distinct macrophage subtypes were identified based on their marker expression: CCL17hi monocyte-derived macrophages, FABP4hi macrophages, and SPP1hi macrophages expressing elevated levels of MERTK. While FABP4hi macrophages predominated in the control group, SPP1hi macrophages were significantly more abundant in IPF lung tissues (Fig. 1D). This finding is consistent with prior studies indicating that highly proliferative SPP1hi expressing macrophages may interact with myofibroblasts in the progression of IPF.

Fibroblast cells were identified within the whole single-cell dataset and highlighted on the UMAP plot (Fig. 1C). Fibroblasts were classified into four distinct groups based on marker expression: fibroblasts, HAS1 fibroblasts, SFTPChi fibroblasts, and CLUhi fibroblasts. HAS1hi fibroblasts were only exhibited in IPF patients (Fig. 1E). We utilized the CellphoneDB package to analyze cell-cell interactions between macrophages and fibroblasts in IPF lung tissues, visualized in a circular plot. The quantification revealed that SPP1hi macrophages exhibited the highest level of interactions with fibroblasts, this result aligns with our expectations (Fig. 1F). Among these interactions, the MIF/CD74 axis emerged as a key interaction target (Fig. 1G).

The serum MIF concentrations were significantly elevated in IPF patients compared to controls (Fig. 1H). Moreover, MIF levels were correlated with patients’ lung diffusion capacity (Fig. 1I), suggesting a potential role of MIF in IPF pathogenesis.

MIF/CD74 activation of MAPK in pulmonary fibrosis

A BLM-induced (2 mg/kg) mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis was established, with functional assessments performed on day 21 post-treatment, followed by lung tissue collection (Fig. 2A). Histopathological analysis with H&E staining revealed severe structural damage in the lung, characterized by the loss of alveolar structures, significant thickening of the alveolar septa, and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Masson’s staining further demonstrated extensive collagen deposition within the alveolar septa in BLM-treated mice models (Fig. 2B). Together, these results indicate that the BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis model triggers lung inflammation and fibrosis. Analysis of protein expression levels in lung tissues from both BLM-treated and control mice revealed a significant upregulation of MIF and CD74 in the BLM group (Figs. 2C, D). Furthermore, ELISA analysis of peripheral blood confirmed a marked increase in serum MIF levels in the BLM-treated mice (Fig. 2E). Additionally, Figs. 2F and G show the corresponding increases in mRNA levels of Mif and Cd74 in the BLM-treated lung tissues. CoIP experiments further demonstrated that MIF directly interacts with CD74 (Fig. 2H). Western blot analysis confirmed that activation of the MIF/CD74 axis resulted in downstream activation of the MAPK pathway, accompanied by suppression of AMPK signaling pathway expression. (Figs. 2I, J and Fig. S3B). We propose that in the BLM-induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis, MIF and its receptor CD74 are upregulated, activating downstream AMPK and MAPK pathways to exert their effects.

In the cell migration assays, NIH3T3 fibroblasts were treated with saline or varying concentrations of MIF for 24 h. The MIF-treated group exhibited a significant enhancement in cell migratory capacity (Fig. 3G). Western blot analysis of fibroblasts also showed a marked increase in LC3II/I levels in the BLM-treated groups, suggesting a significant elevation in autophagy compared to controls (Figs. 3H, I). Based on previous studies, we propose that MIF/CD74 activates MAPK pathway, promoting fibroblast proliferation and migration, while inhibiting AMPK, which in turn promotes autophagy [16, 23].

MIF inhibitor 4-IPP impairs BLM-induced lung fibrosis

To investigate the role of MIF in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, we used the MIF inhibitor 4-IPP. As a specific suicide substrate of MIF, 4-IPP covalently and irreversibly binds to MIF, thereby inhibiting its biological activity [24]. In a BLM induced mice model of pulmonary fibrosis, intraperitoneal injections of the MIF inhibitor 4-IPP were administered at concentrations of 5 mg/kg and 25 mg/kg, starting on the first day post-modeling. The effects of 4-IPP administered were also established at later stages (Fig. S2). Lung tissues were collected on day 21 (Fig. 4A). Although the lung coefficients in the 4-IPP-treated groups were lower than those in the BLM group, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4B). Lung function analysis revealed evaluated in forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 0.05 s (FEV0.05), and dynamic compliance (Cdyn) in the 4-IPP-treated groups compared to the BLM group (Fig. 4C).

Histopathological examination using H&E and Masson staining showed that 4-IPP treatment improved lung architecture by reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and collagen deposition relative to the BLM group (Fig. 4D). Further analysis of RNA extracted from lung tissues demonstrated that the mRNA expression levels of Mif, Cd74, Cd44 and Col1a1 were significantly lower in the 4-IPP-treated groups than in the BLM group (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis further confirmed that the protein levels of MIF, CD74, and COL1A1 were significantly lower in the 4-IPP-treated groups compared with the BLM group. Treatment with 4-IPP also markedly inhibited activation of the SRC and MAPK pathway relative to the BLM group (Fig. S3).

Collectively, these results indicate that the MIF inhibitor 4-IPP effectively attenuates BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

MIF knockout alleviates BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis

We next examined the effects of MIF inhibition in vivo using a MIF knockout murine model of pulmonary fibrosis. We selected male Mif−/− C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks, with no significant differences in initial body weight. A BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis model was established, and lung tissue were collected on day 21 (Fig. 5A). The lung coefficients of BLM-treated Mif−/− mice were significantly lower than those of the wild-type BLM-treated group (Fig. 5B).

The BLM-treated Mif−/− mice had higher FVC%, FEV0.05%, Cdyn%, and CST% values compared to wild-type BLM-treated mice, although only the increase in CST% reached statistical significance (Fig. 5C). Histological analysis of the lung showed that Mif−/− mice exhibited less tissue damage, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and lower collagen deposition after BLM treatment. These results suggest that knocking down MIF can partially reduce the progression of BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 5D).

We also extracted RNA from the lung tissues of mice to assess the expression of fibrosis markers, including COL1A1, as well as proteins involved in the MIF/CD74 pathway. qRT-PCR results indicated that after BLM treatment, Mif−/− mice had significantly lower mRNA expression levels of Mif, Cd74, Cd44, and the fibrosis marker Col1a1, compared to wild-type BLM-treated mice (Fig. 5E). These findings demonstrate that MIF knockout can alleviate BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

The control group received saline (Fig. 6A). Supernatants from the co-culture were collected for ELISA analysis, which revealed that MIF levels in the medium were significantly elevated following BLM treatment compared to the control group. MIF levels remained elevated in the groups treated with both BLM and different concentrations of 4-IPP, there was no significant difference compared to the BLM-treated group (Fig. 6B).

RNA was extracted from fibroblasts to assess the expression of fibrosis markers (COL1A1 and FN) and components of the MIF/CD74 pathway. qPCR results showed that BLM treatment increased the mRNA expression of Col1a1, Fn, Cd74, and Cd44 compared to the control group. However, in fibroblasts treated with both BLM and 4-IPP, the expression of Col1a1 and Fn was significantly reduced compared to the BLM-treated group. Additionally, the mRNA expression of Cd74, and Cd44 was also significantly decreased compared to the BLM-treated group (Fig. 6C).

We further knocked down CD74 expression using shRNA and assessed the mRNA levels of fibrosis markers (COL1A1 and FN) as well as components of the MIF/CD74 pathway through qRT-PCR. Following BLM stimulation, the expression of Col1a1 and Fn in the shRNA CD74 group was significantly lower compared to the control group (Fig. 6D). Cd74 expression was also reduced in the shRNA CD74 group, while the expression levels of Cd44 and Cxcr4 showed no significant difference compared to the control group. Western blot analysis were used to analyze the transfection of CD74 shRNA fibroblast protein expression level (Fig. 6E). In NIH-3T3 cells, CD74-shRNA markedly reduced BLM-stimulated COL1A1 protein expression relative to the control group, while α-SMA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that in the in vitro BLM-stimulated fibroblast model, the MIF/CD74 pathway plays a critical role in fibrosis development.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate the involvement of the MIF/CD74 axis in the pathogenesis of BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis, further supporting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target. The increased expression of MIF and CD74 observed in fibrotic lung tissues suggests a pivotal role in promoting fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. Using both in vivo and in vitro models, the study employed complementary genetic and pharmacologic approaches to perturb the MIF–CD74 axis—including MIF knockdown or 4-IPP treatment in vivo, and CD74 shRNA or 4-IPP treatment in vitro—all of which resulted in significant reductions in fibrosis markers, collagen deposition, and overall fibrotic burden.

Our data showed that MIF functions as a pro-fibrotic cytokine that is upregulated under fibrotic conditions. Elevated MIF levels were observed in both IPF patients and BLM-induced mice, contributing to fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix production [25]. Inhibition of MIF significantly attenuated pulmonary fibrosis in this study. Previous study reported that MIF inhibition reduces lung injury and extracellular matrix deposition, suggesting that MIF may exert its effects through the TGF-β1/Smads signaling pathway [26]. MIF inhibitors may also suppress fibrosis in other organs such as liver, kidney, and heart [27–29]. MIF knockdown may decrease the mRNA expression of various factors involved in vascular remodeling, including FGF23 and SerpinB5 [26].

Despite these findings, the mechanisms by which MIF contributes to pulmonary fibrosis remain incompletely understood. Through an integrated approach involving single-cell sequencing, along with in vivo and in vitro experiments, the present study found that MIF interacts with its membrane receptors CD74 and CD44 [30], thereby mediating key signaling pathways such as the MAPK cascade that promote fibroblast proliferation and migration [31, 32]. The MAPK pathway has been implicated in myofibroblast differentiation and tissue remodeling in earlier studies [33, 34]. Consistent with these findings, our data showed that decreased levels of phosphorylated MAPK following MIF inhibition, further supporting the potential of targeting this pathway to suppress fibrotic progression. MAPK inhibition may further slow pulmonary fibrosis progression [35].

In addition, the MIF/CD74 axis suppresses the AMPK pathway, a critical regulator of cellular energy homeostasis that orchestrates the transition between anabolic and catabolic states [36]. In both IPF patients and mouse models, fibrotic regions characterized by metabolically active, autophagy-resistant myofibroblasts exhibited reduced AMPK activity [37, 38]. However, in the cellular experiments conducted in this study, BLM stimulation was associated with increased autophagy in fibroblasts. Given existing concerns regarding treatment-related toxicity, further investigation into the role of autophagy and related mechanisms is warranted.

Beyond corroborating the pro-fibrotic role of MIF, the study suggests that the MIF/CD74 axis may also modulate the inflammatory microenvironment by contributing to immune cell recruitment and activation. Tanino et al. reported that treatment with an anti-MIF antibody reduced mortality and histopathological lung injury scores while suppressing inflammatory cell accumulation [39]. These findings provide additional evidence that the MIF/CD74 axis plays a role in inflammatory responses and immune regulation [18]. The attenuation of fibrosis observed upon MIF inhibition—particularly in the macrophage–fibroblast co-culture model—indicates that MIF may influence cross-talk between these cell types, potentially amplifying fibrotic responses. This highlights the complex interplay between inflammation and fibrosis, as well as the potential therapeutic value of targeting immune-fibrotic interactions in pulmonary fibrosis [40].

Given that our findings are primarily derived from the bleomycin-induced model, it is important to consider their relevance to the patients with IPF. Although the bleomycin model represents an acute, partially self-resolving inflammatory injury rather than a chronic progressive epithelial–fibrotic disorder, it remains a widely used platform to interrogate core fibrogenic mechanisms [41]. Notably, despite these differences, both settings converge on epithelial injury and aberrant repair as central drivers of fibrosis. Within this context, macrophages play important profibrotic roles, promoting fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition through mediators such as TGF-β and PDGF [42]. Consistently, single-cell studies in human IPF have identified profibrotic macrophage populations that expand within fibrotic lungs and interact with fibroblasts to sustain remodeling, which is in line with our observation of expanded SPP1hi macrophages with enhanced interactions with fibroblasts [43]. Together, these findings suggest that macrophage-associated profibrotic signaling represents a shared component of fibrotic responses, although its role may be context-dependent and not necessarily identical between experimental models and the patients with IPF.

Collectively, these findings support a mechanistic model of MIF/CD74 signaling in pulmonary fibrosis, as summarized in Fig. 7. The present findings suggest that targeting this axis may help mitigate lung fibrosis and improve lung function in patients with IPF.
