Low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation attenuates visceral pain in IBS with diarrhoea via inhibition of the medial prefrontal cortex
Authors: Rui-Xia Weng, Wei Lin, Qian Sun, Chun-Tao Ma, Fu-Chao Zhang, Shu-Man Jia, Shufen Hu, Yong-Chang Li, Chen-Hao Zhang, You-Jia Yu, Wei-Ming Zhao, Guang-Yin Xu, Rui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic visceral pain in IBS with diarrhoea (IBS-D) is a profound therapeutic challenge. While aberrant central processing is implicated, the key brain regions driving this visceral pain and their suitability as neuromodulatory targets remain undefined.ObjectiveTo identify a central hub of visceral pain in IBS-D and elucidate the mechanism by which repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) confers analgesic effects.DesignCombined functional MRI with visceral sensitivity assessments was used to pinpoint hyperactive brain regions of patients with IBS-D. Mechanistic studies were conducted in a well-established IBS mouse model. A clinical trial was performed to validate the therapeutic potential of rTMS in patients with IBS-D.ResultsClinical observations identified hyperexcitability of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) as strongly correlated with visceral pain in patients with IBS-D. In IBS mice, visceral pain was driven by the hyperactivity of mPFC glutamatergic (mPFCGlu) neurons, which received nociceptive inputs from the anterior cingulate cortex via an NR2A-dependent mechanism. Low frequency (lf)-rTMS of the mPFC sustainably alleviated visceral pain in IBS mice by inhibiting mPFCGlu neurons and restoring normal synaptic plasticity. Building on these findings, a clinical trial validated that a 2-week course of mPFC-targeted lf-rTMS in patients with IBS-D effectively alleviated visceral pain and improved bowel habits, effects associated with reduced mPFC activity and sustained for at least 8 weeks.ConclusionsHyperexcitability of the mPFC drives chronic visceral pain in patients with IBS-D and lf-rTMS provides analgesia by suppressing this hyperactivity, offering a novel, mechanism-based neuromodulation strategy for IBS-D treatment.

Introduction

IBS, particularly the diarrhoea-predominant subtype, is a highly prevalent disorder of gut–brain interaction, with chronic visceral pain being its most debilitating and treatment-resistant symptom.1 2 Current first-line management strategies, including dietary modifications, antispasmodics and gut-targeted pharmacotherapies (eg, rifaximin, probiotics), provide only modest and often transient relief for a substantial proportion of patients.3–6 The high recurrence rate of visceral pain, coupled with the potential side effects of long-term medication, underscores the pressing need for novel and sustainable therapeutic avenues.

The pathophysiology of chronic visceral pain in IBS is complex, involving a dynamic interplay between peripheral gut factors and central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction.7–12 A growing body of evidence, including work from our group, positions aberrant central processing or central sensitisation as a pivotal mechanism.13–17 Neuroimaging studies have consistently revealed functional and structural alterations in brain networks responsible for pain perception, amplification and modulation in patients with IBS.18 19 This CNS-centric view reframes IBS not merely as a peripheral gut disorder but as a condition amenable to direct neuromodulation. Among the key cortical hubs implicated, the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) stands out due to its integral role in integrating cognitive-evaluative and affective dimensions of pain.20 21 Clinical observations report altered grey matter volume and cerebral blood flow within the mPFC in patients with IBS.22 23 However, the specific cell types, neural circuits and molecular mechanisms through which the mPFC governs visceral pain remain largely uncharted. This lack of mechanistic insight at the microscopic and mesoscopic levels has hindered the development of precisely targeted neuromodulatory therapies.

As a non-invasive technique capable of modulating cortical excitability and influencing subcortical pain-processing pathways,24 25 repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) presents a theoretically compelling strategy for managing chronic pain disorders rooted in central dysregulation. However, its application specifically for chronic visceral pain in diarrhoea-predominant IBS (IBS-D) remains largely unexplored, with neither robust clinical evidence nor mechanistic insights from preclinical models. The translation of rTMS into a viable therapy for this condition is thus confronted by two fundamental, unresolved questions: first, the optimal cortical target for alleviating IBS-D-related visceral pain has not been systematically identified; second, the neurobiological mechanisms through which rTMS might confer any potential benefit, including the cellular substrates and long-term effects on neural plasticity, are entirely unknown. A systematic identification of a hyperexcitable, pain-processing brain region that can serve as a reliable rTMS target is therefore a prerequisite for advancing this field.

Based on this rationale, we first conducted a systematic clinical investigation to identify a consistently hyperactive brain region in patients with IBS-D with visceral pain. Our findings nominated the mPFC as a key neural substrate. We then employed a multidisciplinary approach, leveraging a neonatal maternal deprivation (NMD) mouse model of chronic visceral pain, to dissect the cellular (mPFC glutamatergic (mPFCGlu) neurons), circuit-level (anterior cingulate cortex-mPFC pathway) and molecular (NR2A-mediated glutamatergic hyperexcitability) mechanisms by which the mPFC regulates visceral pain. These mechanistic insights directly informed the optimisation of our intervention strategy, leading us to hypothesise that inhibiting mPFC hyperactivity via low frequency (lf) rTMS would effectively alleviate visceral pain. Finally, we translated these findings back to the clinic, validating the therapeutic potential of this mPFC-targeted rTMS approach in a well-phenotyped IBS-D cohort. Our study thereby establishes a cohesive translational pipeline from clinical observation to mechanistic elucidation and back to clinical application, positioning the mPFC as a critical neuromodulatory target for chronic visceral pain in patients with IBS-D.

Materials and methods

Systematic assessment of healthy controls and patients with IBS-D, including questionnaire evaluations, anorectal manometry26 and functional MRI (fMRI), identified the mPFC as a key brain region relevant to visceral pain in IBS-D.27 To further investigate the underlying mechanisms of mPFC involvement, we employed a combination of fibre photometry, optogenetics, chemogenetics, viral tracing and electrophysiological recordings in a well-established NMD mouse model to mimic the visceral pain in patients with IBS-D.28 This multifaceted approach revealed that the aberrant activity within the mPFC mediates visceral pain through specific neural circuits and molecular mechanisms. Additionally, we observed that rTMS could reverse these specific neuroplastic changes in the hyperactive mPFC. The therapeutic potential of normalising mPFC excitability was further validated in a clinical cohort of patients with IBS-D who underwent either rTMS or sham rTMS. Detailed experimental details were provided in the online supplemental materials.

Results

mPFC hyperexcitability is associated with visceral pain in patients with IBS-D

To systematically identify a consistently hyperexcitable brain region that could serve as a novel therapeutic target for patients with IBS-D, we ultimately enrolled 21 patients with IBS-D and 21 healthy controls (figure 1A). Patients with IBS-D had significantly higher scores on the Visceral Sensitivity Index (VSI) compared with healthy controls (figure 1B). This hypersensitivity was confirmed by anorectal manometry, which showed markedly lower rectal sensory thresholds in the IBS-D group for first sensation, desire of defecation and maximum tolerable volume (figure 1C–E). These results provided robust evidence for heightened visceral pain in the IBS-D cohort. We next investigated the central neural correlates of this hypersensitivity by analysing the dynamics of the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), a metric reflecting regional spontaneous brain activity.29 30 Whole-brain analysis revealed significantly increased ALFF dynamics in several brain regions of patients with IBS-D, including mPFC, cerebellar lobule 9, orbital superior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), hippocampus, among others (false discovery rate-corrected p<0.05; cluster size >50 voxels; figure 1F and G, online supplemental figure S1A,B, online supplemental table S1). Notably, the mPFC exhibited the most significant increased ALFF (online supplemental figure S1C). Correlation analysis revealed that mPFC ALFF was positively correlated with the VSI score and negatively correlated with anorectal manometry measures in patients with IBS-D (figure 1H–K). Furthermore, we conducted correlation analyses between the VSI score and ALFF in other brain regions, and the statistical significance of these correlations was lower than that of the mPFC (online supplemental figure S2). Taken together, these findings strongly implicate hyperexcitability of mPFC as a central neural substrate of visceral pain in patients with IBS-D, nominating it as a promising therapeutic target for neuromodulation.

mPFCGlu neurons are involved in regulating visceral pain

To establish a causal link between mPFC excitability and visceral pain, we used an NMD mouse model, which mimics IBS-D-related chronic visceral pain.31 32 The NMD mice exhibited significantly enhanced visceral pain responses that persisted through 18 weeks of age (online supplemental figure S3A–D). Notably, these mice showed no changes in somatic pain (online supplemental figure S3E,F), indicating that the pain phenotype was specific to the visceral domain. Consistent with the clinical profile of IBS-D, NMD mice also exhibited increased faecal water content (online supplemental figure S3G) and displayed heightened anxiety-like behaviours (online supplemental figure S3H–L). In contrast, the sucrose preference was not altered (online supplemental figure S3M), suggesting that reward processing remained intact.

To identify the specific brain regions activated, we performed immunostaining for the neuronal activity marker c-Fos following colorectal distension (CRD), which revealed activation in several brain regions (figure 2A,B). Notably, the mPFC exhibited markedly more pronounced c-Fos expression in NMD mice (figure 2C), mirroring the human neuroimaging findings. Furthermore, quantitative analysis revealed that CRD-activated neurons in the mPFC were predominantly glutamatergic, with 82.43% of c-Fos cells colocalising with glutamate, while only 6% were colocalising with γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (figure 2D,E). This indicated the predominant population of mPFCGlu neurons as being activated by visceral pain. To directly visualise the real-time dynamics of these neurons during visceral pain, we performed in vivo fibre photometry. We showed that the graded pressure stimuli applied to the colon evoked calcium activity in mPFCGlu neurons, accompanied by a marked increase in calcium-dependent fluorescence signals of NMD mice (figure 2F–I). Finally, to elucidate the regulatory role of mPFCGlu in visceral pain, we employed optogenetic manipulation of mPFCGlu activity and assessed visceral pain responses via electromyography (EMG). Following viral delivery of glutamatergic neuron-specific opsins into the mPFC (figure 2J), colocalisation analysis revealed that nearly 81.7% of EGFP+ cells were glutamatergic positive (online supplemental figure S4A,B). Inhibition of mPFCGlu neurons with 589 nm yellow light significantly attenuated visceral pain in NMD mice (figure 2K). Conversely, photostimulation of mPFCGlu neurons with 473 nm blue light induced visceral pain-related behaviours in CON mice (figure 2L). Collectively, these data demonstrated that the mPFCGlu neurons are involved in regulating visceral pain.

Excitation of mPFCGlu neurons by ACC inputs underlies visceral pain

Next, we investigated the mechanisms underlying the hyperexcitability of mPFCGlu neurons. Using retrograde tracing with AAV2/R-hSyn-EGFP injected into the mPFC (figure 3A), we identified prominent inputs from several brain regions, including the ACC, insular cortex, paraventricular thalamic nucleus, piriform cortex, nucleus reuniens and basolateral amygdala (figure 3B). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the ACC provided the most abundant inputs (figure 3C), which predominantly colocalised with glutamate antibody (figure 3D). The ACC-mPFC connection was confirmed by anterograde monosynaptic tracing by injecting AAV2/1-hSyn-Cre virus into the ACC and AAV2/9-DIO-EGFP virus into the mPFC (figure 3E). Following 3 weeks after injection, a vast majority of EGFP+ neurons in the mPFC were colocalised with glutamate antibody (figure 3F). Optogenetic stimulation of ChR2-expressing ACCGlu terminals in the mPFC elicited excitatory postsynaptic currents in mPFCGlu neurons, which could be abolished by 2,3-dihydroxy-6-nitro-7-amino-methylfluorene (figure 3G–J). Taken together, these data suggest that ACCGlu neurons send projections to mPFCGlu neurons.

To further investigate the functional contribution of this pathway in visceral pain regulation, we combined optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulations. Specifically, AAV2/9-vglut2-eNpHR-EGFP was injected into the ACC and AAV2/9-vglut2-hM3Dq-mCherry was injected into the mPFC of NMD mice (figure 3K–M, online supplemental figure S4C,D). Optogenetic inhibition of ACCGlu terminals in the mPFC significantly alleviated visceral pain behaviours. Crucially, this analgesic effect was blocked by chemogenetic activation of mPFCGlu neurons with Clozapine N-oxide (CNO) (figure 3N,O). These results demonstrate that activation of the ACC-mPFC neural pathway is necessary for the manifestation of visceral pain behaviours. We further confirmed the behavioural specificity of this pathway. During optogenetic silencing of the ACC→mPFC pathway, NMD mice showed no changes in anxiety-like behaviours, locomotor activity, mechanical or thermal pain thresholds or reward-related behaviour (online supplemental figure S5), confirming that the modulation was selective for visceral nociception. Conversely, in CON mice, optogenetic activation of ACCGlu terminals in the mPFC induced visceral pain behaviours, which was abolished by CNO-mediated inhibition of mPFCGlu neurons (online supplemental figure S6).

Inhibition of NR2A in mPFCGlu neurons alleviates visceral pain in NMD mice

To elucidate the role and mechanisms of mPFCGlu neurons activated by the ACC in chronic visceral pain, we used viral vectors to express the glutamate sensor (iGluSnFR3-V857) in the mPFC (figure 4A). Fibre photometry recordings revealed that CRD stimulation evoked significantly greater glutamate release in the mPFC of NMD mice compared with CON mice (figure 4B–D). To further test if this elevated glutamate release originates from ACC inputs, we employed a chemogenetic approach to inhibit the ACCGlu-mPFC pathway (figure 4E). Chemogenetic inhibition of this circuit resulted in a significant reduction of glutamate release during CRD stimulation compared with the saline control (figure 4F–H), confirming that ACC inputs critically regulate glutamatergic tone in the mPFC.

Given the central role of glutamate, we next examined the expression profiles of its receptors (NMDARs, AMPARs and KARs) in the mPFC.33–36 The quantitative PCR analysis identified a specific upregulation of the NR2A (Grin2A) subunit of NMDARs in NMD mice, with no significant changes in other subunits (figure 4I). This increase was confirmed at the protein level by western blot (figure 4J) and spatially resolved by immunofluorescence, which showed predominant NR2A localisation in mPFCGlu neurons (figure 4K,L).

We then investigated the functional contribution of NR2A upregulation to visceral pain. Intra-mPFC microinjection of the NR2A-selective antagonist NVP-AAM077 (10 µmol/L and 100 µmol/L, but not 1 µmol/L) significantly attenuated visceral pain in NMD mice (online supplemental figure S7A). The effective dose of 10 µmol/L NVP-AAM077 also reduced CRD-induced neuronal activation, as evidenced by decreased c-Fos expression (online supplemental figure S7B,C) and attenuated visceral pain responses in EMG recordings (online supplemental figure S7D–F). To further verify whether NR2A in the mPFCGlu neurons regulates visceral pain, we selectively knocked down NR2A in mPFCGlu neurons (figure 4M). Knockdown of NR2A expression in the mPFCGlu neurons not only reduced calcium activity of these neurons induced by CRD stimulation (figure 4N–Q) but also significantly attenuated visceral pain behaviours in NMD mice (figure 4R,S), indicating that NR2A upregulation underlies the pathological activation in visceral pain. Collectively, these findings suggest that upregulation of NR2A in the mPFCGlu neurons is a key molecular mechanism underlying visceral pain in NMD mice.

Low frequency rTMS alleviates visceral pain by normalising hyperexcitability of mPFCGlu neurons and their associated plasticity in NMD mice

Given the observed hyperactivity of mPFCGlu neurons in chronic visceral pain, we sought to translate this finding by inhibiting the mPFC with a clinically feasible approach. While rTMS applied to the mPFC has shown clinical promise,37 38 the mechanisms underlying its long-term benefits, particularly with lf protocols,39 40 remain poorly defined. We first established that a 1 week course of 1 Hz rTMS delivered to the mPFC of NMD mice elevated the visceral pain threshold for 7 days (online supplemental figure S8A,B). Extending this treatment to 2 consecutive weeks dramatically prolonged the analgesic effect, maintaining an elevated pain threshold for up to 42 days post-treatment (figure 5A,B) and also improved stool consistency in the mice (online supplemental figure S8C). Notably, this lf-rTMS-mediated improvement in visceral pain was abolished by chemogenetic activation of mPFCGlu neurons (figure 5C–E), underscoring the pivotal role of mPFCGlu neuronal activity in lf-rTMS efficacy in alleviating visceral pain. After observing the behavioural improvements, we assessed the neuronal activity following rTMS treatment. Immunostaining results revealed that lf-rTMS significantly reduced c-Fos expression in mPFCGlu neurons compared with the sham group, which was accompanied by a reduction in the expression of the NR2A in the mPFC (online supplemental figure S8D). Besides, chemogenetic activation successfully increased the excitability of these same neurons relative to the saline group (figure 5F,G), suggesting that lf-rTMS alleviates visceral pain by decreasing the excitability of mPFCGlu neurons. We next stimulated the ACC while recording long-term potentiation (LTP) in the mPFC. The pathologically enhanced LTP observed in the mPFC of NMD mice (online supplemental figure S9). This enhanced ACC-evoked LTP in the mPFC was normalised by lf-rTMS treatment (figure 5H–J). Chemogenetic activation of mPFCGlu neurons again blocked this therapeutic effect, preventing the restoration of normal LTP (figure 5H–J). This establishes the reversal of aberrant synaptic strengthening at least in part as a key mechanism for the long-term maintenance of rTMS benefits.

Lf-rTMS induces sustained alleviation of visceral pain in patients with IBS-D by reducing neuronal excitability in mPFC

Building on the mechanistic data from mice, we hypothesised that the mPFC represents a viable therapeutic target for lf-rTMS in human patients. To test this, we conducted a clinical trial in which 42 patients with IBS-D were randomly assigned to receive either sham rTMS or lf-rTMS targeting the mPFC for 2 weeks (figure 6A). Following a 2-week treatment regimen, lf-rTMS demonstrated superior clinical efficacy across several domains compared with the sham group. Specifically, lf-rTMS treatment significantly alleviated visceral pain, as evidenced by a marked reduction in the VSI (figure 6B) and concurrent increases in rectal sensory thresholds (first sensation, desire of defecation and maximum tolerable volume; figure 6C). Furthermore, lf-rTMS treatment led to significant improvements in bowel habits, as reflected by reduction of Bristol Stool Form Scale scores (figure 6D). The overall symptom burden was also substantially reduced, with the lf-rTMS group showing significant decreases in total IBS Severity Scoring System scores and abdominal pain subscores relative to the sham lf-rTMS group (figure 6E). Crucially, fMRI assessments revealed that these clinical benefits were accompanied by a significant reduction in mPFC activity (figure 6F,G). Finally, in a follow-up assessment, this therapeutic effect persisted for at least 8 weeks post-treatment (figure 7). Taken together, these findings provide compelling clinical evidence that inhibiting mPFC hyperactivity is an effective and durable strategy for alleviating chronic visceral pain in patients with IBS-D.

Discussion

Chronic visceral pain, the cardinal symptom of IBS-D, represents a profound therapeutic challenge, with existing peripherally-acting treatments offering limited and often transient benefit for a substantial proportion of patients.41 42 While aberrant central processing is widely implicated in its pathophysiology,43–45 a critical translational gap has persisted: the identification of a definitively hyperactive, causally involved brain hub that can serve as a reliable and effective target for neuromodulation. Previous neuroimaging studies, though valuable, have largely been correlative and lack the resolution to delineate the specific cellular populations and circuit mechanisms governing visceral pain.46–48 This lack of mechanistic insight has, in turn, hampered the development of precisely targeted therapies. The present study directly addresses this gap by establishing a complete translational pipeline that not only nominates the mPFC as a key driver of IBS-D-related visceral pain but also elucidates the underlying microscopic and mesoscopic mechanisms and validates its therapeutic relevance through a targeted intervention. Our principal findings demonstrate that: (1) mPFC hyperexcitability is a key neural correlate of visceral pain in patients with IBS-D; (2) In a preclinical model, this hyperexcitability is driven by glutamatergic projections from the ACC to mPFCGlu neurons, mediated by an NR2A-dependent mechanism; (3) Targeting this hub with lf-rTMS inhibits mPFC activity and induces sustained analgesia in both mice and patients with IBS-D.

The clinical findings, which revealed the correlation between mPFC hyperactivity and visceral pain severity in patients with IBS-D, aligned with and extended previous neuroimaging studies reporting functional and structural alterations in prefrontal regions in IBS.49 50 The novelty of our work lies in translating this macroscopic correlation into a mechanistic understanding at the cellular and circuit levels. We identified mPFCGlu neurons as the primary cellular substrate for visceral pain. Furthermore, we delineated a specific ACC→mPFCGlu circuit as the source of this pathological drive and identified the upregulation of postsynaptic NR2A subunits as a key molecular event sustaining neuronal hyperexcitability. This multilevel insight firmly positions the mPFC not merely as a correlated region but as a causally involved, circuit-defined target for intervention.

A pivotal consideration of the work is its exclusive focus on IBS-D. A growing body of evidence suggests that different IBS subtypes may be underpinned by distinct CNS dysfunctions.9 51 52 For instance, studies have hinted that constipation-predominant IBS may be associated with dysregulation of the neuroendocrine transmitter serotonin.52 Meanwhile, our findings indicate that glutamate neurotransmission plays a central role in the pathophysiology of IBS-D. This pathophysiological heterogeneity necessitates a precision medicine approach to neuromodulation; a ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy is unlikely to be optimal. Given the clear hyperexcitability of the mPFC in IBS-D, we employed an inhibitory lf-rTMS protocol, which proved highly effective. This strongly suggests that future rTMS therapies must be tailored based on both the clinical subtype and the functional state (hyperactive vs hypoactive) of the target region. For IBS-D, targeting the hyperactive mPFC with inhibitory stimulation is a logical strategy, whereas for other subtypes characterised by functional deficits in specific circuits, excitatory (high-frequency) protocols might be required. The present study lays a crucial foundation for such a subtype-specific framework in IBS treatment.

This study focused on the interventional effects of lf-rTMS on chronic visceral pain, revealing that it significantly alleviates visceral pain behaviours in IBS-D by inhibiting the excitability of glutamatergic neurons in the mPFC, reducing pain signal transmission and modulating synaptic plasticity to repair neural circuit dysfunction associated with chronic pain. This normalisation of circuit function was supported at the molecular level by a concomitant downregulation of the NR2A in the mPFC, aligning with the observed reduction in neuronal activity and hyperplasticity. This mechanism distinctly differs from the pathways of high-frequency rTMS, which acts by inhibiting GABA release or modulating brain–gut peptides, highlighting the targeted specificity of our intervention strategy.53–55 Although previous studies by researchers including Melchior et al and Algladi et al have shown that high-frequency rTMS stimulation of the motor cortex (M1) area can improve rectal sensory function,56 57 suggesting its potential for brain–gut axis modulation, the heterogeneity in therapeutic outcomes resulting from different targets and parameters warrants further attention. This dual specificity, in both anatomical target and stimulation frequency, likely underlies the remarkably sustained therapeutic effects we observed, which persisted for at least 8 weeks post-treatment. It also helps explain the heterogeneity in outcomes across previous rTMS trials and underscores that optimising therapeutic efficacy requires careful matching of stimulation parameters to the underlying pathophysiology of the specific patient population.58 59

Since the present study is a single-centre study with a relatively modest sample size, it therefore warrants validation in larger, multicentre, randomised controlled trials. While we demonstrated sustained effects for 8 weeks within our observation period, a longer-term follow-up is also needed to determine the durability of this treatment and its impact on patients with IBS-D. Furthermore, psychiatric comorbidities such as anxiety and depression are highly prevalent in IBS and are known to influence mPFC function. Future studies should systematically evaluate how these comorbidities modulate the response to mPFC-targeted rTMS, which will be crucial for refining patient selection and developing truly personalised neuromodulation algorithms.

In summary, this study not only provides a novel theoretical framework for understanding the central neural mechanisms of chronic visceral pain, but more importantly, lays a crucial foundation for developing precise neuromodulation therapeutic strategies. Particularly noteworthy is the innovative application of lf-rTMS technology in treating chronic visceral pain, demonstrating its therapeutic effects through modulation of mPFC function, thereby offering a new non-pharmacological intervention for clinical practice. These research findings hold significant translational medical value and open new research directions for the treatment of IBS and other functional GI disorders.
