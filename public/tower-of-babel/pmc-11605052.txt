MIL-101(Fe)@ceramic-monolith for arsenic removal in aqueous solutions
Authors: D Villarroel-Rocha, C García-Carvajal, S Amaya-Roncancio, J Villarroel-Rocha, D A Torres-Ceron, E Restrepo-Parra, K Sapag
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we present the preparation of a hybrid material constituted by a Metal-Organic Framework (type MIL-101(Fe)) and a natural clay ceramic monolith (NCCM), the materials were successfully assembled through an in-situ hydrothermal method. The composites were characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K and CO2 adsorption at 273 K; these techniques confirmed the obtaining of the hybrid material and, in addition, through studies before and after adsorption mechanisms involved were identified. Then, the As(V) adsorption capacity of the materials was evaluated using an aqueous arsenic solution; from which high adsorption capacities of up to 268 mg g− 1 and 61.5 mg g− 1, respectively. In addition, kinetic and equilibrium studies of As(V) adsorption were carried out. Finally, the potential capacities of the hybrid material consisting of Al2O3, AlO6-SiO4 and Fe3O4 for the adsorption of H3AsO4, were studied through density functional theory calculations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80400-7.Subject terms: Nanoscale materials, Structural materials, Theory and computation, Materials chemistry, Surface chemistry, Theoretical chemistry, Chemistry, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are organic-inorganic materials that can be synthesized using metallic ions and multifunctional organic ligands. By varying these building blocks, materials with different properties can be designed. For this reason, MOFs with good features have been prepared and applied in several fields, such as gas adsorption, adsorption in solution, controlled drug delivery, catalysis, luminescence, among others1–16. However, issues still need to be solved before they are applied practically. Generally, the synthesis of MOF materials requires expensive ligands, which implies a high cost for its production. In addition, MOFs are typically found in powder form, making them difficult to use in water treatment. Many attempts have been made to deal with these issues, such as using waste materials such as PET to prepare MOFs17. For example, in our reported work18. MOF-5 prepared from PET waste for CO2 capture, the composites had higher structural stability and better CO2 adsorption than pristine MOF-5. On the other hand, Wang et al. obtained the Ag3PO4/UiO-66-NH2@PET composite using waste PET as support for light-responsive desorption toward sulfamethoxazole. Chemical recycling was preferred among these technologies because PET can be converted into high-value chemical raw materials like benzene 1,4-dicarboxylic acid (BDC, terephthalic acid). BDC is also the ligand for many MOFs like MOF-5, UiO-66, MIL-88B, and MIL-5318–21. MOFs in the powder form were reported using waste PET as the ligand precursors22,23.

Interest in the search for new adsorbent materials has increased. It is essential to synthesize a promising adsorbent candidate easily and efficiently, which can be used by everyone13,24–27. An alternative could be to use hybrid materials; these materials are formed by combining the distinctive properties of MOFs and other promising materials. However, when considering the socio-economic aspects, all materials synthesized from waste become optimal options.

It’s important to highlight that a major drawback of these materials is their use in powder form. For example, when clays come into contact with water, they swell in their layered structure, which makes possible extractions of water difficult, similar to the extraction of MOF powder28,29. One way to address this issue is by using conformal adsorbents in different structural forms such as monoliths, extruded pellets, or three-dimensional structures. Another option to maintain these structures stable is to develop them as ceramic structures, known for their high mechanical strength, even porosity, and low-pressure drop. This makes them useful in preventing blockages in packed bed column operation30.

Among various MOF-based materials, MIL-101(Fe) is prominent in the fields of gas adsorption, solution adsorption, advanced oxidation, among others31–36. All these are possibly due to its large specific surface area, pore size, structural stability and low biotoxicity37,38. In this work, MIL-101(Fe)@ceramic-monolith ​​was developed through a fusion between MIL-101(Fe) structure and a ceramic monolith (CM) and using waste PET as a ligand source for As(V) removal from solution. The prepared MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​was characterized by a series of characterization techniques. It was found that MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​has a high As(V) removal capacity. The adsorption behaviors of MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​toward arsenic were systematically studied, in which the possible adsorption mechanisms were further deduced. Finally, DFT calculations were performed, and we used the knowledge acquired in arsenic adsorption studies to fully understand the adsorption mechanisms but considering the base material of the composite, which would be Al2O3, AlO6-SiO4 and Fe3O4. It is important to mention that due to the large number of MIL-101(Fe) atoms, it was not possible to perform DFT calculations directly on that structure.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (99.8%, Anedra), ethanol (99.8%, Dorwil), ethanol (99.8%, Anedra). All these reagents were used as received without further purification. Aqueous stock solutions of As(V) (1000 mg L− 1) were prepared by dissolving sodium arsenate dibasic heptahydrate (Na2HAsO4·7H2O, Merck) in distilled water, and work solutions (0.1–100 mg L− 1) were prepared by diluting the stock solution with distilled water.

Synthesis of MIL-101(fe)

The MIL-101(Fe) was synthesized according to the procedure reported by Nagarjun et al. in 202039 and using the BDC ligand obtained from PET with our procedure reported in 202218. BDC was prepared by alkaline solvolysis as follows: 5 g of PET (slices of 10 mm × 10 mm obtained from plastic bottles) was mixed with ethyl alcohol 96% (50 mL) and NaOH, using a molar ratio of 1:2 (PET: NaOH). The mixture was kept under reflux at 200 °C for 4 h until the complete dissolution of PET. The resulting products were dissolved in water and precipitated with 2 M H2SO4. Finally, the obtained white solids were filtered, dried at room temperature.

The synthesis of MIL-101(Fe) consists in which 1.324 g of FeCl3·6H2O and 0.412 g of BDC were dissolved in 40 mL of DMF. The resultant solution was heated at 110 °C for 20 h in a digestion bomb (60 mL of internal volume). After that, the reactor was cooled to room temperature, the solvent was decanted, and the remaining solid was put in contact with DMF (30 mL) for two days replacing the solvent two times. After this period, the solvent, for ethanol (30 mL), and the contact times were repeated. At the end of this process, the solvent was dried under a vacuum at room temperature.

Synthesis of CM

The synthesis of ceramic monoliths from natural clay was obtained using the synthesis conditions reported by García-Carvajal et al.40 the following:

A ceramic paste is obtained with the powder mixture of 47.5% natural bentonite clay (from Santa Gema, San Juan - Argentina), 47.5% alumina (from Carbo, San Luis - Argentina), and 5% starch as binder, these materials were placed in a horizontal mixer at 60 rpm for 10 min to ensure the homogeneity of the solids and, approximately 30–35% w/w of water was added to obtain a paste with the necessary rheology. The green ceramic paste was initially passed through the feed hopper to a simple geometry nozzle to deaerate and homogenize the paste completely. The nozzle was changed, and 2 cm long × 2.7 cm wide honeycomb monoliths were obtained with 2 mm × 2 mm cells and 0.9 mm thick walls. Once extruded, the materials were taken to a vacuum chamber at room temperature for 48 h, ensuring a slow drying to avoid breakage and deformation in the monolith structure due to the rapid evaporation of water. Finally, they were taken to a furnace where heat treatment was carried out with a heating ramp of 5 °C min− 1 from room temperature to 600 °C for 1 h as the final calcination temperature.

Synthesis of MIL-101(fe)@CM

The MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite was synthesized via in situ hydrothermal methods. In the synthesis, they dissolved 1.987 g of FeCl3·6H2O and 0.618 g of BDC in 40 mL of DMF. Subsequently, 1.324 g of CM was added considering the MIL-101(Fe) and CM precursors mentioned above, corresponding to a molar ratio of 2:1 (FeCl3·6H2O: Al2O3·6(SiO2)·H2O(from bentonite)). The precursor mixtures of MIL-101(Fe) and CM were then kept at 110 °C for 20 h in a digestion bomb to produce the MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​composite, where a MOF: monolith ratio (% w/w) of 25:75 was obtained, respectively. The post-treatment procedure of these materials was the same as that described in the synthesis of MIL-101(Fe). The MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite obtained can be seen in Fig. 1.

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was performed on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer, using Cu-Kα radiation, in a continuous mode, between 2 and 50 ° in 2θ, with a scanning rate of 2 ° min− 1 and a step of 0.02 °. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements were performed on a Bruker IFS 66/S spectrophotometer with a resolution of 5 cm− 1 using anhydrous KBr as the dispersing agent. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out to analyze the surface elements of materials on Specs FlexMod using a monochromatic Al Ka source (1486.6 eV). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained with an electronic scanning microscope LEO 1450 VP, the samples were gold-coated (30 nm) and held to the Al sample holder with carbon tape. The images were taken using a voltage of 15 kV. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed in a simultaneous analyzer, SDT Q600 from TA Instruments under air atmosphere (flow rate = 50 mL min− 1), the samples were heated from room temperature to 900 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C min− 1. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were determined at 77 K in an Autosorb iQ sorption analyzer from Quantachrome Instruments, and CO2 adsorption isotherms at 273 K until a pressure of 10 bar in an ASAP 2050 sorption analyzer from Micromeritics. The samples were previously outgassed at 150 °C in vacuum for 12 h. The apparent specific surface area (SBET) was calculated by the BET equation41 using the N2 adsorption data. The micropore volume (VµP−N2), was obtained applying the Dubinin-Radushkevich and αS-plot methods42,43, using the N2 adsorption data. The total pore volume (VTP) was calculated using the Gurvich rule at p/p0 = 0.98 from N2 adsorption data43. The micropore size distributions of all materials were evaluated by the modified Horvath-Kawazoe method for pores with cylindrical geometries44,45 using CO2 adsorption data, whereas, from N2 adsorption data, their mesopore size distributions were obtained by NLDFT method included in AsiQwin software, v. 4.0 (Quantachrome Instruments) using the Kernel “N2 at 77 K on silica (cylindrical pore, NLDFT, adsorption branch)”.

As(V) adsorption test

The quantity of arsenic adsorbed on the studied materials was determined using batch experiments. For adsorption kinetics, a fixed mass of 100 mg of MIL-101(Fe) and 250 mg of MIL-101(Fe)@CM were taken in a volume of 200 and 100 mL with 100 mg L− 1 initial concentration of As(V) in agitation at 200 rpm and pH 7, and 3 mL of sample was taken from t = 0 to t = 24–36 h for remaining arsenic quantification. In the As(V) adsorption isotherms studies, 2.5–10 mg of the material was taken in a volume of 10 mL with 0.1–100 mg As(V) L− 1 for 24 h, in agitation at 200 rpm, pH 7, and room temperature. After 24 h, the powder-form samples from the experiment were filtered, while the monolith was removed from the solution. The remaining and initial arsenic concentrations in the aqueous solutions were quantified by molybdenum blue spectrophotometric (Numak 721 vis equipment), with which the adsorption capacities for each material were calculated.

Computational methods

The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) approach of generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was utilized for the exchange–correlation energy functional in the Kohn-Sham functions48. Ultrasoft pseudo potential with scalar relativistic correction generated by Rappe − Rabe − Kaxiras − Joannopoulos method (RRKJ) was employed to describe the electron-ion interaction49. The cutoff energies chosen were 60 Ry for all atoms involved. The threshold for self-consistency and geometric relaxation was selected in 1 × 10− 6 eV. Dispersion correction to consider long range interactions was implemented in all the calculations into the framework of Semiempirical Grimme’s DFT-D3 correction50. In the case of the cluster systems studied in the present work, Brillouin zone integration was approximated using the Monkhorst − Pack scheme with gamma point sampling, in the case of surfaces a 2 × 2 × 1 k-point sampling51. The Fe3O4 cluster is a cluster obtained from the Fe3O4 bulk. In the Bulk phase, a Fe was taken as a center atom and a 5.0 Å was determined to include all atoms into that radius. From there, a Fe3O4 cluster of 52 atoms with 10 Å diameter was obtained. The spin polarization of Fe atoms was implemented to considering the magnetic properties of Fe3O4, in this case the value of magnetization of 3.58 µB/Fe-atom was obtained, in good accordance with previously reported magnetization for Fe3O4 of 3.55–3.58 µB/Fe52,53. At the other hand, the structure of the cluster Al2O3 was obtained from the bulk of the Corundum-like structure that crystallizes in the cubic Ia̅3 space group54. from there, and taking as center an Al atom, all the atoms out of a 5 Å radio were excluded. From the remaining final atoms, an Al2O3 amorphous clusters of 37 atoms with a diameter of 10 Å was obtained and fully geometrically relaxed. In the case of the aluminosilicate surface, the surface composed for AlO6-SiO4 octahedrons by sharing top oxygen atoms were geometrically optimized for the H3AsO4 adsorption calculations55, the three structures can be obtained in Supporting information. Results and discussion

Characterization

PXRD patterns were obtained for CM, MIL-101(Fe) and the corresponding composite (see Fig. 2). As it is expected, since CM is a combination of montmorillonite and sodium aluminate raw materials, these are the phases that can be seen in the diffraction patterns of the monolith. The formation of the crystalline, microporous MIL-101(Fe) into the CM support was confirmed by the comparison of composites patterns with those of the isolated MOF compound as well as with the corresponding simulated PXRD profile57. The PXRD pattern of isolated MIL-101(Fe) shows well-defined characteristic reflections and the absence of other crystalline phases as impurities is also verified. By analyzing the patterns of MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite it can be determined that the MOF framework was successfully obtained by solvothermal synthesis in the presence of CM.

PXRD pattern of CM, MIL-101(Fe), and MIL-101(Fe)@CM.

It is evident that the pattern of the MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​composite exhibits that the corresponding peak intensities are decreased compared to the powder pattern of the as-synthesized isolated MIL-101(Fe) composite, probably due to the MOF being embedded within the amorphous monolithic phase. Furthermore, the decrease of intensity can be attributed to the lower proportion of MIL-101(Fe) in the composite.

Figure 3 presents the FTIR spectra of MIL-101(Fe), CM and MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​composite which contain common regions of absorption bands from both pristine materials.

FTIR spectra of MIL-101(Fe), CM, and MIL-101(Fe)@CM.

In CM and MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​composite, an absorption band is observed at 1027 cm− 1 that can be related to Si-O-Si bonds present in the tetrahedrons that form the outer layers of the lamellae of montmorillonite from bentonite clay, while the region from 790 to 496 cm− 1 is due to Al–O–Si and Si–O–Si bending vibrations associated with the deformations of the bonds present in the octahedral sheets58,59.

The absorption bands at 1662, 1600, and 1390 cm− 1 in MIL-101(Fe) and MIL-101(Fe)@CM ​​composite spectra can be assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the organic ligand O-C = O60, and the absorption band at 750 cm− 1 corresponding to the chelating bond formed by Fe3+ and carboxylic acid functional groups, consistent with literature61.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K of MIL-101(Fe) and CM (see Fig. 4a) show that the MIL-101(Fe) presents an isotherm Type II according to IUPAC classification62. This type of isotherm is characteristic of microporous materials having mainly micropores and narrow mesopores. Also, the shape of the isotherm suggests the presence of crystal aggregates of MIL-101(Fe). On the other hand, CM material presents a different Type II isotherm with an H3 hysteresis loop. This isotherm is characteristic of materials with a broad pore size distribution43,63.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K of (a) CM and MIL-101(Fe), and (b) MIL-101(Fe)@CM and physical mixture MIL-101(Fe)/CM.

Regarding the pore size distribution (PSD) of the materials under study, the MIL-101(Fe) material presents pore sizes of 1.2 nm (micropore) and 2.8 nm (narrow mesopore). In contrast, CM presents porosity throughout the range of mesopores (see Figure S1). The isotherms and PSD obtained from MIL-101(Fe) and CM are in full agreement with the results reported by other authors39,64.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm of MIL-101(Fe)@CM (see Fig. 4b) can be described as a combination of the Type I isotherm at low relative pressures, and at higher relative pressure it behaves like a Type IV isotherm. The material has a quick increase of N2 amount adsorbed at low p/p0 values, like that of MIL-101(Fe) due to the presence of micropores. It can also be seen that the materials have a Type H4 hysteresis loop, as already mentioned; this is associated with the presence of aggregated particles7,65–67. The PSD of MIL-101(Fe)@CM material; shows a PSD combination of CM and MIL-101(Fe) materials, where a narrow mesopore size distribution at 2.8 nm (see Fig. 5), and the presence of micropores at 1.2 nm (inset in Fig. 5) were observed, which indicates that these materials have bimodal porosity.

Figure 9b shows the arsenic adsorption kinetics of the MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite, which was a little faster compared to the kinetics reported for the ceramic monolith40 and it exceeded more than 70%of its adsorption capacity after 4 h. The model that best describes the kinetics of this material is the Elovich equation, so the adsorption occurs with heterogeneous active sites and different activation energies. This result was somewhat expected because the composite presents a variety of surface groups with which As(V) interacts, such as edge sites of laminar montmorillonite and Fe3+ and carboxylic acid functional groups61.

These results show that As(V) adsorption on MIL-101(Fe) follows the PFO model, indicating strong physisorption. In contrast, MIL-101(Fe)@CM exhibits faster kinetics, fitting the Elovich equation and suggesting enhanced interactions from heterogeneous active sites. This indicates that the incorporation of MIL-101(Fe) onto the ceramic monolith enhances the interaction with As(V) through a combination of physisorption and chemisorption mechanisms, leading to a more complex adsorption process characterized by varying energy profiles across different sites, thereby enhancing the overall adsorption capacity and kinetics.

Figure 10 shows the results obtained in the arsenic adsorption equilibrium experiments on the materials studied, where it can be evidenced that the MIL-101(Fe) material in powder has adsorptive capacities higher than the synthesized compound, reaching values of 268 mg g− 1 compared to the 61.5 mg g− 1 of the composite material; however, the CM presents values less than 0.015 mg g− 1 as the maximum adsorption capacity of As(V); as reported in our work40 so that the incorporation of MOF within the porous structure of the ceramic monolith significantly increases the capabilities of the structured material, also highlighting that the composite is a monolith in a honeycomb type geometric structure with differential mechanical properties to the powdered materials reported in the literature68,69. However, the decrease in adsorption capacity of As(V) observed in the MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite material, compared to the pure MIL-101(Fe), is primarily due to the incorporation of the ceramic monolith (CM), which has a very low adsorption capacity for As(V). Since the composite is a combination of a highly effective adsorbent (MIL-101(Fe)) and a support material with poor adsorption properties (CM), the resulting material exhibits an intermediate adsorption capacity.

The values found for the different models of adsorption isotherms are presented in Table 3, where it can be evidenced that the Langmuir model exhibited the best fit for the experimental data of the arsenic adsorption, with the highest correlation correlation coefficient (R2). Overall, these findings highlight the dominance of Langmuir isotherm behavior in the adsorption of arsenic onto the tested materials, supporting the conclusion that the interaction is predominantly monolayer and homogeneous.

As(V) adsorption mechanisms

To be able to relate the performance and mechanisms involved for MIL-101(Fe)@CM composite in the removal of As(V) in an aqueous medium with their physicochemical properties, FTIR and XPS from materials before and after adsorption studies are present.

The FT-IR spectrum (Fig. 11) shows that after As(V) adsorption, the absorption bands at 1662, 1600 and 1390 cm−1 disappear, which is attributed to the O-C = O stretching vibration70,71. This event demonstrates that O-C = O stretching is involved in arsenic binding. It is also confirmed by the broad spectrum XPS study (see Fig. 12a) and by analyzing the O 1s and C 1s spectra of the hybrid materials before and after adsorption. In the O 1s spectrum (Fig. 12b) of the pristine material, the peaks at 533.40, 531.69 and 530.05 belonged to C = O, Fe-O-C and Fe-O-Fe, respectively. After the adsorption of As(V), an additional peak at 530.90 eV is formed, indicating the generation of Fe-O-As and the C = O bond peak disappears, which would be in total agreement with the FTIR spectra. Similarly, this occurs in the C 1s spectrum (Fig. 12c), which decreases the intensity of the peak at 288.50 eV, which belongs to the C = O bond. It is important to mention that in the spectrum of O 1s a shift can be seen, this is probably due to a calibration issue or loaded samples.

As(V) elution and adsorbent recycling

The regeneration and recycling of adsorbents are essential for developing sustainable and cost-effective materials. Although this study did not specifically explore the regeneration of MIL-101(Fe)@CM, it is proposed that saline solutions with an affinity for arsenic, such as NaCl or NaOH, could be used for desorption. Alternatively, thermal desorption with hot water washing may be considered to minimize energy use and adsorbent degradation. These methods would enable reuse over multiple adsorption-desorption cycles, enhancing the material’s practical viability. This remains a future research direction to assess the effectiveness of such regeneration methods.

DFT calculation

Given the high number of atoms in the MIL-101(Fe) structure57, the fundamental interactions of H3As2O4 with the key components of the studied system were considered. For this, an Al2O3 cluster, an Fe3O4 cluster, and a SiO2 surface were modeled. To understand the adsorption interactions of arsenate (H3AsO4), the final structures of three adsorbents were utilized (Fig. 13). As part of this approach, H3AsO4 was relaxed in proximity to the Al2O3 cluster, aluminosilicate surface, and the Fe3O4 cluster respectively, all the final geometric optimizations are in the Supporting information in xyz format. Subsequently, the adsorption energies were calculated, and the final states are presented. The final adsorption energy calculated for H3AsO4 on the Al2O3 cluster using Eq. 7. is -1.11 eV. This value is in good agreement with a previous report of -1.72 eV for the adsorption energy of arsenate on α-Al2O3 surfaces using the same level of theory72. Similarly, it aligns with the − 1.12 eV reported for As2O3 on γ-Al2O3 by Hu et al.73 (see Fig. 13a). where the nearest distance from the O atom in the H3AsO4 to the Al2O3 cluster was calculated in 2.20 Å, being an Al atom the nearest atom.

Adsorption of Arsenic acid H3AsO4 on (a) Al2O3 cluster, (b) aluminosilicate surface (AlO6-SiO4) and (c) Fe3O4 cluster. The distance molecule-surface was calculated to the nearest atom of the adsorbent.

The adsorption of H3AsO4 on aluminosilicate was calculated with Eq. 7. in -2.17 eV, in this case, it is shown the high energetic stability of the molecule on the surface. the distance of adsorption on the H3AsO4 molecule to the aluminosilicate surface was calculated in 2.20 Å, in this case, the hydrogen atoms of the H3AsO4 aligns towards the Oxygen atoms of the aluminosilicate surface (see Fig. 13b). The presented calculations are in good agreement with the previously reported by Awuah et al.74. , who calculated the H3AsO4 in -2.00 eV and similar values for several configurations of adsorption in the structure of Al(III)-modified natural zeolite clinoptilolite. For adsorption of H3AsO4 on Fe3O4 cluster, the final state after geometric optimization shows a H3AsO4 at 2.20 Å from the nearest Fe atom (see Fig. 13c). In this case, the adsorption energy of H3AsO4, calculated with Eq. 7 was calculated in -3.05 eV. The adsorption of As species was previously predicted by Alonso-Tobón et al.75, who calculated the H3AsO4 adsorption on Fe clusters in -3.11 eV. In the same line, As2O3 adsorption on Fe3O4 surfaces was previously calculated with values of -3.76 eV76. Considering the values ​​of the adsorption energies of H3AsO4 and the relaxations of the structures, it indicates that arsenic has a greater affinity towards iron, as we also saw in the XPS studies; where the Fe-O-Fe bond gives way to the generation of the Fe-O-As bond (see Fig. 12b).

Conclusions

Novel composite MIL-101(Fe)@CM were synthesised through an in-situ hydrothermal method and tested in the removal of arsenic(V). Through an in-depth characterization by different techniques, obtaining MIL-101(Fe) was confirmed, and formation of the composite MIL-101(Fe)@CM. Adsorption capacities reached levels of 61.5 mg g-1 and 268 mg g-1, respectively. The synergistic effect between MIL-101(Fe) and CM showed an interesting affinity towards the As(V) (as evidenced by FTIR and XPS studies). We also demonstrated that the adsorption energies of H3AsO4 on the three systems studied by DFT calculations follow the order from the more stable to the less stable adsorption energies as Fe3O4 > AlO6-SiO4 > Al2O3. In summary, this work provided us with a new strategy to combine MOF with a ceramic monolith, and such a composite with micro-mesoporous porosity could be considered as promising candidates for the removal of arsenic and probably other molecules.
