Precipitation-driven self-sintering for flexible electronics
Authors: Tao Fu, Tao Sun, Yinfang Jiang, Guoqing Wu, Yanming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryFlexible electronics (FE) require conductive materials that can be processed at low-temperature without compromising mechanical compliance. Here, we report a precipitation-driven self-sintering strategy that the highly conductive InGa3 film is formed on the flexible hydrogel under the ambient condition, and the solution-processable system is designed, where the liquid metal (LM) of InGa3 undergoes the controlled in situ precipitation and spontaneous microscale sintering process. The precipitation-driven self-sintering (PDSS) method uniquely enables the spontaneous and additive-free self-sintering of the metal microparticles (MPs) within the hydrogel matrices under the ambient condition, offering a scalable and substrate-friendly route for the FE. The pathway of InGa3 has the high conductivity, mechanical durability and adhesion to the flexible hydrogel without the high-temperature annealing, which is compatible with the scalable manufacturing method. These findings provide a versatile route toward the energy-efficient fabrication of the next-generation FE.

Introduction

Flexible electronics (FE) are expected to revolutionize the applications ranging from wearable medical devices and soft robots to foldable displays and electronic skins,1,2,3 of which core lies on the development of robust, highly conductive and mechanically compatible interconnects and electrodes that can be seamlessly integrated onto the flexible deformable substrates.4,5 Conventional manufacturing approaches, such as high-temperature sintering or pressure-assisted processing of metallic inks, are often incompatible with polymer-based substrates due to their low thermal tolerance and mechanical fragility,6,7,8 which hinder the scalable, energy-efficient, and substrate-friendly production of FE devices.9

In recent years, there are many reports of liquid metal (LM)-hydrogel composites with the conductivity and stretchability,10,11 where the LMs have emerged as promising candidates for the FE owing to their intrinsic metallic conductivity, fluidic deformability, and biocompatibility.12,13,14 The LM-based manufacturing strategies, including printing, embedding hydrogel and surface patterning, have been explored to fabricate the conductive pathways at room temperature (RT).15 Self-sintering has attracted the significant attention due to its ability to enable the conductive pathways without requiring the high-temperature, and some self-sintering mechanisms have also been reported in the literature. Among them, the thermally induced self-sintering relies on the elevated temperatures to activate the atom diffusion and grain coalescence,16 but the process often exceeds the thermal stability limits of flexible substrates,.17 The capillary-force-assisted self-sintering has also been explored, involving solvent evaporation or surface tension effects to bring the particles into the close contact,18 and the chemical reduction or ligand exchange reactions can trigger sintering at RT, which requires the specific chemical environments and introduce the impurities or instability.19,20 Previous studies have also demonstrated the RT integration of LM with the flexible substrates, including high-resolution deposition technique,21 and the GaIn-based alloys have been widely explored. However, the approaches typically require the complex processing condition, additional reagent and sophisticated equipment, which restrict their practicality and scalability. Moreover, the continuous conductive network with the high electrical performance and mechanical durability at RT22 remains a fundamental challenge.

Here, we introduce a precipitation-driven self-sintering (PDSS) method that enables the spontaneous formation of conductive metal films at RT, which utilizes the controlled precipitation of metal nanoparticles from the solution to induce the interparticle contact, coalescence and sintering.23 This PDSS leverages the gradual supersaturation and precipitation of metal ions to induce the particle consolidation and neck formation.24,25 Compared with the existing methods,18,26 and the PDSS is to combine the LM self-sintering with the hydrogel network, where the 3D structure of the hydrogel effectively facilitates the RT densification of LM. Although the prior work has demonstrated the solution-based and gravity-assisted room-temperature sintering of eutectic gallium alloys,27,28 the PDSS method uniquely enables spontaneous, additive-free self-sintering of metal MPs in hydrogel matrices under ambient conditions, offering a scalable and substrate-friendly route for FE. Despite the precipitation and self-assembly mechanisms were investigated,29 the PDSS strategy integrated the acetic acid–assisted nanoparticle aggregation in the hydrogel matrix, enabling the spontaneous microscale sintering that produced the continuous and mechanically robust conductive networks compatible with the flexible substrate, which offers the improved scalability and substrate-friendliness compared to the prior method. To the best of our knowledge, the PDSS in the application of the FE is rarely reported, marking a significant advancement over the existing methods, which complements and advances the current methods in the FE and wearable biomedical devices.

In this study, a PDSS is introduced to fabricate the FE that enables the spontaneous formation of conductive metal films at RT, and the controlled precipitation of metal microparticles (MPs) is archived from the solution to induce interparticle contact, coalescence and sintering at RT. The resulted metallic networks exhibit the high mechanical, electrochemical and biological properties, which are completely solution-processable, substrate-compatible, and readily integrable with the scalable coating technique. This finding establishes the PDSS as the versatile and energy-free pathway toward the high-performance FE. Results and discussion

Structures of FE

The preparation procedure of FE and final hydrogels are illustrated in Figures 1A–1C. The hydrogel was rapidly crosslinked with the Genipin and Arginine at 30 °C for 2 min, forming the stable network and blue-black pigment (Figure 1D). The resulting FE-based electronic skin exhibited high transparency and strong adhesiveness, allowing the conformal attachment to human skin (Figure 1E). Furthermore, the electronic skin enabled real-time monitoring of body motions, demonstrating its wearable and motion-sensing capability (Figure 1F).

The optical microscope (OM) images of the 1IG–4H, 2IG–8H, and 3IG–12H FE were observed, as shown in Figures 2A–2C. When the InGa3 content reached 1%, the intermittent InGa3–MPs on the 1IG–4H FE was observed on the hydrogel surface, which was discontinuous and did not completely cover the hydrogel surface. The more uniform InGa3-MPs was gradually formed on the hydrogel surface as the InGa3content reached 2%, and the formed film significantly became thicker, showing the relatively continuous distribution. Furthermore, when the InGa3 content reached 3%, the dense and uniform InGa3 film was formed on the hydrogel surface, where the InGa3 film referred to the densified network of In-Ga LM-MPs. The formed film structure became complete as the InGa3 content increased, showing the good coverage effect. Furthermore, the InGa3-MPs were freely settled in the gelatin solution due to their much higher density compared to the gelatin (Figure 2D), and the corresponding microstructure diagram showed that a large number of InGa3-MPs gradually deposited at the bottom of the gelatin solution as the sedimentation continued (Figure 2D). The precipitated InGa3-MPs formed self-sintering junction30 under the action of the acetic acid, which promoted the mutual integration of the InGa3-MPs.27 As a result, the complete and dense metal film was formed on the surface of the hydrogel. The SEM images of the FE before and after the self-sintering test are further illustrated in Figure S1. Most of the LM droplets were observed after self-sintering, supporting the self-sintering mechanism and the formation of continuous conductive pathways. Moreover, the OM image of InGa3 film cross-section (Figure S2) illustrated the continuous microparticle network, and XRD analysis did not show distinct crystalline peaks, which was consistent with the largely amorphous or oxide-rich LM network.

Before the sintering treatment, the particles exhibited the distinct boundaries, without visible neck formation; while after the sintering treatment, the particle boundaries became the fused shape, and the neck regions were clearly formed between the adjacent MPs, indicating that the increase of the InGa3 content facilitated the particle coalescence and the development of the fused interfaces. Furthermore, the control experiment of PDSS process was performed without acetic acid, and the formed films exhibited the markedly poorer continuity and significantly reduced conductivity, as confirmed by SEM image and electrical measurements (Figure S3). The PDSS of InGa3 MPs generated a highly conductive and mechanically robust skeleton while passivating the hydrogel, which produced the flexible electrodes with the higher conductivity and improved mechanical stability.31 This structure resulted in the electrodes with the enhanced corrosion resistance, faster electrochemical response and significantly higher charge storage capacity, which was consistent with the formation of continuous conductive networks within the hydrogel.32 Furthermore, the improved performance can be attributed to the combination of spontaneous microscale sintering and hydrogel network stabilization, which enabled the uniform particle consolidation, robust interparticle contacts and effective ion/electron transport pathways. This PDSS offered the substrate-friendly and scalable strategy for the FE, particularly suited for the long-term and on-body sensing applications.

The structures of the InGa3-MPs in the FE were further analyzed using a SEM and EDS mapping, as shown in Figure 3. The average particle sizes of the 1IG–4H, 2IG–8H FE, and 3IG–12H FE were 3.58, 2.76, and 2.81 μm, respectively, which were first increased and then decreased as the InGa3 content increased (Figures 3A, 3D, and 3G). The InGa3-MPs were evenly distributed on the 1IG–4H FE with the moderate particle density, without obvious sintering (Figure 3B). Moreover, the In, Ga, C and O were uniformly distributed on the InGa3–MPs, without agglomeration (Figure 3C). As the InGa3 content reached 2%, the number of the InGa3-MPs were increased on the 2IG–8H FE compared with the 1IG–4H FE (Figure 3D), and the aggregation was also more pronounced (Figure 3E). There were signs of the bonding between the InGa3-MPs, and some InGa3-MPs began to self-sintering, showing the slightly rough surface.33 The element distributions were closely packed (Figure 3F) compared with the particle structure (Figure 3C), which was because the increase of InGa3 content promoted the connection or fusion phenomenon between the InGa3-MPs. As the InGa3 content reached 3%, the number of the InGa3-MPs were decreased on the 3IG–12H FE compared with the 2IG–8H FE (Figure 3G), and the denser particles were packed and aggregated on the 3IG–12H FE (Figure 3H). A large number of the particle boundaries were blurred, and the obvious sintering bridges or aggregates appeared, indicating that the self–sintering behavior occurred between the InGa3-MPs. Some elements appeared more concentrated, such as the dense yellow areas, which indicated the microscopic reconstruction or local element enrichment (Figure 3I). Mechanical properties of FE

The mechanical properties of the FE were tested, as shown in Figures 4A and 4B. The tensile properties of the FE were significantly improved as the InGa3 content increased, showing that its maximal tensile strain was increased by 380% compared with the hydrogel without InGa3, and the corresponding tensile stress also increased by 300%. The introduction of the InGa3 significantly enhanced the mechanical strength of the hydrogel and endowed the FE with the higher flexibility and deformability. Moreover, the fatigue performance of the FE was tested to further describe their service life, as shown in Figure 4C. The recycle number for all samples was set to 200. After 120 cycles, the FEs had obvious stress relaxation, indicating that their mechanical properties had declined. Notably, the stress relaxation was effectively alleviated as the InGa3 content increased, and the fatigue performance of the 3IG–12H FE was significantly improved. It was indicated that the addition of InGa3 not only improved the instantaneous mechanical properties of the 3IG–12H FE, but also extended its service life to a certain extent, making it more durable and stable under the repeated stress environment. In contrast, the FE containing low concentration of InGa3 showed the increased hysteresis and mechanical change, which were attributed to the network fatigue.36 The differences were further rationalized by the molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figures 4D–4F), showing the dynamical assemble of the InGa3–MPs in the hydrogel. The InGa3-MPs were sparsely distributed on the 1IG–4H FE (Figure 4D), which did not form the interconnected domains; while the short-range bead-chain-like structure began to emerge on the 2IG–8H FE (Figure 4E), and the InGa3 clusters were aligned along the polymer backbone to form the percolated and stretch-aligned network on the 3IG–12H FE (Figure 4F), which provided the load-bearing reinforcement and reversible sliding behavior that dissipated the strain energy.37

The Raman spectra (Figure 4G) revealed the broadened –OH stretching bands and blue-shifted C=O peaks on the 3IG–12H FE, indicating the strengthened hydrogen bonding and metal–ligand coordination.38 The complementary XPS spectra (Figure 4H) showed the positive shifts at the binding energies of In3d and Ga3d as the intensities of O– and N–containing chemical states increased, confirming that the In and Ga atoms formed the coordination bonds with the carboxyl and amide groups in the hydrogel.39 As a result, the self–sintering of InGa3 contributed to the formation of the mechanically robust and dynamically reconfigurable conductive network, and the reversible supramolecular interactions at the polymer-metal interfaces were introduced, which endowed the FE with the exceptional fatigue resistance, mechanical recoverability and long-term operational stability, making it a wearable electronic under the continuous deformation. Compared with the pristine sample, the In3d and Ga3d peaks exhibited the positive chemical shifts, while the O1s and N1s spectra confirmed the increased intensities of the carboxyl- and amide-related components (Figure 4I), confirming that the coordination bonds were formed between the In/Ga atoms and the carboxyl/amide groups in the hydrogel.

Electrochemical performance

Figure 5A presents the electrochemical characterization of the FE, where the FE, platinum wire, and saturated Ag/AgCl electrode were served as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. The setup was immersed in PBS solution to ensure efficient ionic conduction, and all FE were rapidly stabilized to the quasi-steady open-circuit potential (OCP) within 5 min (Figure 5B), indicating the chemically stable metal-electrolyte interfaces.40 The increase of the InGa3 content shifted the equilibrium potential toward more negative values and narrowed the potential, demonstrating that the formation of the dense eutectic network effectively suppressed the spontaneous redox reactions, resulting in the thermodynamically stable electrode surface.41,42 The behavior marked the improvement over previously reported the LM composites, highlighting the benefits of the unique self-sintered microstructure.Figure 5Electrochemical behaviors of FE(A) Schematic diagram of electrochemical test setup.(B) OCP measurements.(C) PPC measurements.(D) Nyquist plots.(E) |Z| vs. frequency.(F) Bode plots.(G) CV curves of 1IG–4H FE.(H) CV curves of 2IG–8H FE.(I) CV curves of 3IG–12H FE.

The potentiodynamic polarization curves (PPCs) showed that the anodic and cathodic branches contracted with the higher InGa3 content, lowering the corrosion current density icorr, as shown in Figure 5C. It was confirmed that the created LM pathways during the PDSS process were served as the robust physical barriers to the electrolyte penetration, thereby enhancing the electrode durability.

The Nyquist plots (Figure 5D) revealed the decreased semicircle diameter, corresponding to the reduced charge-transfer resistance Rct. The Bode magnitude plots (Figure 5E) showed the order-of-magnitude reduction in the impedance (|Z|) at 1 Hz; while the phase-angle plots (Figure 5F) shifted to the higher frequencies, and the indicative of the accelerated ion-electron exchange was facilitated by the highly interconnected LM network,43 where the electrochemical kinetics surpassed those of conventional LM composites.

The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Figures 5G–5I) exhibited the quasi-rectangular curves over scan rates from 10 to 100 mV s−1, indicating that the reversible Faradaic process was coupled with the double-layer capacitance. In this case, the electrolyte was phosphate buffered saline (PBS), PH = 7.4, ionic strength was ∼163 mM, and the current density electrode area was 1 × 1 cm2. Notably, the 3IG–12H FE retained 85% of capacitance, emphasizing the rapid ion transport through the self-sintered microchannels. Overall, the PDSS of InGa3 MPs generated the highly conductive and mechanically robust skeleton while passivating the hydrogel matrix.44 This resulted in the flexible electrodes with the enhanced corrosion resistance, rapid electrochemical response and significant charge storage capacity,45 advancing their suitability for the long-term and on-body sensing applications.

Biodegradability and biocompatibility

The degradability of the flexible e-skins was evaluated in type 2 collagenase solution. After 10 min at 36 C, the InGa3 MPs were effectively separated from the hydrogel and recovered by the treatment with 40% sodium hydroxide solution. The efficient recycling process facilitated the reuse of the InGa3 MPs and supported the sustainable development by minimizing the electronic waste. The flexible e-skins were expected to play the positive role in the development and application of environmentally friendly materials, as shown in Figures 6A and 6B. Figure 6C shows the operational performance envelope prior to the degradation. Compared with the adhesive energy of 69.2 J m−2 for the dry skin and 268.3 J m−2 for the sweaty skin,46 the 90° peel test on the porcine skin yielded the average adhesion energy of 240 ± 15 J m−2, which was attributed to the strong hydrogen bonding between the hydrogel amide group and the epidermal keratin.42 The results indicated that the hydrogel achieved the reliable skin attachment while maintaining the biocompatibility, which was essential for the long-term wearable applications.47

The four-point probe measurements under the 30% biaxial strain demonstrated that the FE with the thickness of ∼70 μm (Figure S2) had the low sheet resistance of 18 mΩ sq−1, enabling the stable illumination of the light emitting diode (LED) at 2 V for 1 h without perceptible decay. Moreover, the average resistivity of the different films indicated the consistent electrical performance (Figure S4), and the normalized resistance R/R0 vs. cycle number (Figure S5) showed the minimal variation under the action of repeated loading, confirming the excellent electrical stability. Due to the excellent mechanical properties and high resilience of the FE, the three formulations of FE had the similar electrical conductivity after the bending (fully folded) and tensile cycle tests, as shown in Figure S6, indicating their reliability and applicability for the FE. To further demonstrate the mechanical durability of the PDSS film, the representative R/R0 curve after 1000 bending/strain cycles is shown in Figure S7. This confirmed that the film maintained the high conductivity and structural integrity under the repeated deformation, highlighting its robustness for the FE applications. Collectively, the data established the closed-loop paradigm where the e-skin was adhered conformally was conducted reliably. Furthermore, the collagenase degradation could achieve the closed-loop recyclability of the LM particles, and the similar recycling protocol was previously explored in the LM-based hydrogels as part of sustainability efforts in the FE.48 Nevertheless, the milder enzymatic conditions and efficient LM recovery was advantageous for the applications requiring biocompatibility and biodegradability. This could be on-demand disassembled in the benign aqueous-alkaline milieu that permitted the near-quantitative reclamation of LM (Figure S8; Video S1), underscoring its promise for the transient biomedical electronics and sustainable soft-robotic platforms.

In summary, the PDSS offers a novel PDSS to generate the InGa3 film with the hydrogel in the FE, and the precipitation accelerates the film surface diffusion, which yields the dense networks that are mechanically interlocked with the hydrogel. This PDSS is a versatile and low-energy pathway for manufacturing the next-generation flexible and wearable electronic systems. In the future, the PDSS is extending to the alloy or core-shell nanoparticles, which can further lower the resistivity and enhance the environmental stability, and unlock the transient and ultra-stretchable device platforms when combining with the biodegradable or elastomeric substrates.

Limitations of the study

Although the PDSS strategy enables additive-free, RT integration of liquid-metal microparticles within hydrogels, several limitations remain. (1) Mechanistic support relies on pH/acid-dosage controls and post-sintering morphology; real-time tracking of neck growth was not performed. (2) Electrical metrics were obtained on laboratory-scale films; long-term drift, batch-to-batch variability, and failure modes under multiaxial loading require larger cohorts and standardized environmental testing. (3) Biological evaluation was limited to in vitro cytocompatibility using L929 fibroblasts and extract-based assays; in vivo tolerance, and immune response were not assessed. (4) While liquid-metal recovery was demonstrated under enzymatic/alkaline conditions, a full mass balance across repeated recycling cycles and a life cycle assessment were beyond the present scope.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell line culture

The L929 mouse fibroblasts were cultured in the DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with the 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillinstreptomycin at 37 °C in the humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Method details

Sample preparations

The 2 g of gelatin (Aladdin, CAS 9000–70–8) was dispersed in 8 mL of deionized water and allowed to swell for 30 min. The swollen gelatin was maintained at 36 °C for 2 h, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 1 wt. % acetic acid solution, and kept at 36 °C for another 1 h until the solution is completely clear to obtain Gelatin solution. The LM of InGa3 in 95% ethanol solution was ultrasonically treated with the ice–water–bath (IWB) method,29 and the spherical InGa3 micro–particles (MPs) were obtained. The nominal atomic ratio of In:Ga on the InGa3 film was determined from the EDS/XPS results, suggested the densified network of LM-derived micro-/nano-particles with the possible amorphous or oxide-rich regions.29 Under these conditions, the ethanol, as a polar solvent, could quickly form the oxide film on the surface of the InGa3–MPs, which stabilized the structure of the InGa3–MPs and prevented them from polymerizing or breaking during the ultrasonic process. Meanwhile, the generated heat was absorbed by the function of the IWB during the ultrasonic process, preventing the heat accumulation from causing the InGa3–MPs break or deform. The InGa3–MPs with the uniform size were stably prepared through the combination of the ultrasound and cooling techniques, as shown in Figure 1A. Furthermore, the prepared suspension containing InGa3–MPs was filtered through the 20 μm filter to obtain the uniform InGa3–MPs, as shown in Figure 1B. Subsequently, the 10% w/v hydrogel was added into the filtered InGa3–MPs at 80 °C, and the mixed InGa3–hydrogels were divided into the 1%InGa3–4%hydrogel, 2%InGa3–8%hydrogel and 3%InGa3–12%hydrogel, respectively, where the other was water. After completely stirring to mix evenly, the 0.5% Genipin and 0.1% acetic acid were used as the crosslinking agents to add into the mixed InGa3–hydrogels, which was poured into the mold and stayed for 2 min. The hydrogel began to crosslink and solidify to form the stable structure, and the preparation of the FE were completed, as shown in Figures 1C and 1D. The hydrogel rapidly turned black after the addition of Genipin and Arginine at 30 °C for 2 min (Figure 1D), indicating the formation of crosslinked network. This was because the Genipin was reacted with the primary amine groups of Arginine via the ring–opening reaction, followed by the Schiff base reaction, leading to the formation of blue–black pigment. It was indicated that the stable crosslinked structure was formed through the covalent bonding, which contributed to the mechanical integrity and color change of the hydrogel. Accordingly, the prepared 1%InGa3–4%hydrogel, 2%InGa3–8%hydrogel and 3%InGa3–12%hydrogel were recorded as 1IG–4H, 2IG–8H and 3IG–12H FE, respectively. Furthermore, the FE–based electronic skin exhibited the high optical transparency and strong adhesiveness, enabling conformal and stable attachment to human skin (Figure 1E). Owing to these features, it could be directly applied for the real–time monitoring of body motions, demonstrating the great potential for the wearable and reliable motion–sensing applications (Figure 1F).

Characterization methods

The morphologies and chemical compositions of FE were analyzed using a JSM–6360LA type scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The chemical state of FE was determined using a Thermo Fisher Scientific type X–ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and the type of functional group was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific NICOLET iS50 type spectroscopy Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR).

Molecular dynamics simulations

The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the LAMMPS to investigate the assembly of the InGa3-MPs within the FE, and the polymer chains were modeled as the bead–spring chains with the parameters from the OPLS–AA force field; while the InGa3-MPs were coarse–grained as the spherical beads interacting with the polymers and each other through the Lennard–Jones (LJ) potentials. To mimic different loadings of InGa3, the simulations were constructed with varied particle numbers corresponding to 1IG–4H, 2IG–8H, and 3IG–12H FE. After energy minimization, the systems were equilibrated in the NVT ensemble run at 300 K for 2 ns using the Langevin thermostat, followed by the NPT production running at 300 K and 1 atm for 100 ns with the 1 fs timestep. The trajectories were analyzed to obtain the radial distribution functions (RDFs), cluster size distributions, and mean–square displacements (MSDs), which were calculated to characterize the particle aggregation, polymer–particle interactions and diffusion behaviors.

Mechanical tests

The mechanical properties of the FE were tested using an electronic universal testing machine and its stretching speed and running distance for the for the fatigue testing were 5 mm/min and 2.5 mm, respectively.

Electrochemical tests

The electrochemical performances of the FE in phosphate–buffered saline (PBS) solution were measured using a CHE67OE type electrochemical workstation with the three–electrode system. The current density electrode area was 1 × 1 cm2, and the electrolyte was PBS solution. The platinum sheets, saturated calomel and FE were selected as the auxiliary electrode, reference electrode and working electrode respectively. The FE was immersed in PBS solution for 24 h to measure the stable open circuit potential (OCP). According to OCP results, the potentiodynamic polarization curves (PPCs) were measured at a scanning rate of 0.01 V/s and a sensitivity of 0.01 A/s, and the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted at different times to monitor the impedance evolution during the degradation process, with a measurement frequency range of 0.1 Hz–1 MHz and an AC amplitude of 10 mV. Furthermore, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were carried out using a three–electrode system, which was used to analyze the redox behavior and electrochemical reversibility of the FE.

Cytocompatibility tests

Preparation of extract media (LM and LM–hydrogel)

Both the pure LM and LM hydrogel were extracted separately in the complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin) at 37 °C for 24 h under the gentle agitation (∼60 rpm) using a mass-to-volume ratio of 0.2 g/mL. The hydrogel mass was calculated on a wet-weight basis, and all procedures were performed aseptically in polypropylene vessels and protected from light. After extraction, the supernatants were sterile-filtered (0.22 μm).

Cell culture and seeding (L929)

The L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the 5 × 103 cells/well (100 μL/well) and allowed to attach for 24 h, and the culture medium was then replaced with the corresponding extract medium and refreshed every 24 h.

Live/dead (calcein-AM/PI) staining

The Live/Dead staining was conducted using a calcein-AM/propidium iodide (CA–PI) kit (Solarbio, China). At each time point, the wells were rinsed with PBS and incubated with calcein-AM (2 μM) at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by propidium iodide (3 μg/mL) at room temperature for 5 min, protected from light. Samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 900, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Live (green) and dead (red) cells were counted in ImageJ (Fiji), and cell viability (%) = live/(live + dead) × 100.

CCK-8 proliferation assay

For the cell proliferation, the cells were incubated with 90 μL extract medium +10 μL CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) at 37 °C for 2 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The data were presented as mean ± SD (n = 3), and the between-group comparisons at each time point were analyzed by the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, where ns denoted p > 0.05.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All values are presented as mean ± SD. Significance was assessed with the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test for comparisons of 2 groups. In addition, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons of ≥3 groups. The statistical analyses used for the different experiments were described in the respective figure legends, and results were statistically significant when P was <0.05. Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times, and the statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism.
