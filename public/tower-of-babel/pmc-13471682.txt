Endovascular treatment of an inaccessible aneurysm in moyamoya‑like vessels using the floating‑coil technique
Authors: Jinlu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In moyamoya disease (MMD), moyamoya‑like vessels develop at the base of the brain. A ruptured aneurysm originating from these vessels is challenging to manage with endovascular treatment (EVT). When direct catheterization is infeasible, parent artery occlusion (PAO) may be the last resort. However, if liquid embolic agents are unsafe due to a short reflux distance, deploying floating coils into the parent artery to occlude or trap the aneurysm offers a viable alternative. This maneuver, termed the “floating‑coil technique,” has not been previously described. A 60‑year‑old man with subarachnoid hemorrhage (Hunt–Hess grade II) had MMD and an aneurysm in moyamoya-like vessels arising from the posterior cerebral artery. During EVT, neither the Marathon nor the Echelon‑10 microcatheter could be navigated into the parent artery because of an acute angulation. The floating-coil technique was used: first, an Axium Prime 1 mm × 2 cm coil was pushed out, detached, and floated into the parent artery. Then, 3 Axium Prime 1 mm × 1 cm coils were similarly deployed to occlude the parent artery of the aneurysm. Post‑EVT angiography showed disappearance of the aneurysm and occlusion of moyamoya‑like vessels. The patient had no new deficits and recovered well, with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 at the 2‑month follow‑up. Thus, the “floating‑coil technique” is a valid choice for inaccessible aneurysms in moyamoya‑like vessels.

Introduction

In moyamoya disease (MMD), hemodynamic stress and the fragility of moyamoya‑like vessels can lead to aneurysm formation. The reported prevalence of associated aneurysms in MMD is 3%-14% [1]. When an aneurysm ruptures in MMD, intervention is required, and endovascular treatment (EVT) is a viable option. However, for aneurysms in moyamoya‑like vessels, microcatheter access is challenging due to the tortuous and irregular caliber of the parent artery [2]. Therefore, parent artery occlusion (PAO) may be the last resort [1]. For PAO, liquid embolic agents are not the optimal choice because of the risks of uncontrolled migration and reflux [3]. Deploying coils that float into the parent artery to occlude or trap the aneurysm represents a promising alternative. This maneuver, termed the “floating‑coil technique,” has not been previously reported. Here, this case is reported in detail.

Case presentation

Patient’s onset and diagnosis

A 60‑year‑old Chinese Han man presented to the emergency department with a 3‑hour history of severe headache and vomiting. On physical examination, he was alert and oriented, with normal limb strength (grade 5) and nuchal rigidity. The Hunt–Hess score was Grade II. Computed tomography (CT) revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) (Fig. 1A). CT angiography showed left internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis and abnormal vessels at the skull base, raising suspicion of MMD (Fig. 1B). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was scheduled; if a definitive cause of the SAH was identified, EVT would be performed. The patient had no history of systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cerebrovascular disease. His family history was negative for similar cerebrovascular conditions. Therapeutic process

Under general anesthesia, DSA revealed anterior circulation MMD (Fig. 1C-E) and an aneurysm within the moyamoya‑like vessels arising from the P1 segment of the left posterior cerebral artery (PCA) (Figs. 1F and 2). This aneurysm was confirmed as the cause of the SAH, and EVT was then performed. Right vertebral artery (VA) access was established using a 6F sheath and a 5F distal access catheter (DAC). The DAC was positioned in the V4 segment of the VA. A Marathon microcatheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was used to navigate into the parent artery of the aneurysm via the basilar artery and the PCA P1 segment. However, the Marathon microcatheter was too soft and did not provide sufficient support; it could not be stabilized in the PCA P1 segment. An Echelon‑10 microcatheter (Medtronic, Irvine, CA) was then used but became lodged at the origin of the parent artery. The “floating‑coil technique” was used. First, an Axium Prime 1 mm × 2 cm coil (Medtronic, Irvine, CA) was pushed out of the microcatheter and detached. Under manual saline flush (via a 1‑mL syringe) and high‑pressure angiography, the coil floated into the parent artery of the aneurysm. However, this coil failed to embolize the aneurysm, instead becoming lodged in the distal parent artery beyond the aneurysm (Fig. 3C). Next, a second Axium Prime 1 mm × 1 cm coil was used with the same technique. However, it floated into a branch of the aneurysm’s parent artery (Fig. 3D). Then, 2 additional Axium Prime 1 mm × 1 cm coils were used to occlude the proximal segment of the parent artery. Contrast retention within the aneurysm was observed (Fig. 3E and F). Postoperative and follow-up outcomes

Following EVT, the patient showed no new neurological deficits. On postoperative day 2, magnetic resonance imaging revealed no acute infarction in the brainstem or thalamus (Fig. 4B). The patient was discharged 3 days after the EVT. At the 2‑month follow‑up, he had made a good recovery, with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0, and resumed normal daily activities without any complications.

Discussion

MMD is characterized by progressive stenosis or occlusion of the intracranial portion of the ICA, with compensatory development of moyamoya collaterals and other collateral pathways to maintain cerebral blood flow [1]. In MMD, moyamoya vessels are found mainly at the base of the brain in the anterior circulation, concentrated around the ICA terminus. The PCA is rarely involved. In cases where it is affected, PCA involvement is defined as proximal stenosis or occlusion of the PCA [4]. In this case, MMD involved the bilateral ICAs and the left PCA, with varying degrees of involvement. According to Suzuki grades, the right ICA was Grade II and the left ICA was Grade IV [5]. PCA involvement was at an early stage, characterized by stenosis alone, with the distal PCA preserved.

In MMD, the preserved arteries of the posterior circulation and the residual arteries of the anterior circulation experience significantly greater hemodynamic stress than they would under normal conditions, thereby promoting the development of major trunk aneurysms. These aneurysms are often saccular or dissecting [6]. Furthermore, as the intracranial ICA terminus progressively becomes stenotic or occluded, blood flow is redistributed through moyamoya‑like vessels or collateral pathways, increasing flow velocity and shear stress within these fragile vessels [7]. Consequently, aneurysms may form. Aneurysms in moyamoya‑like vessels may often present as dissecting aneurysms, or even as pseudoaneurysms when ruptured. For major trunk aneurysms, embolization of the aneurysm and preservation of the parent artery are required. For aneurysms in moyamoya‑like vessels, access and selective embolization are frequently challenging, and PAO is often the last resort [1].

In this case, the aneurysm was ruptured and dissecting, located in moyamoya-like vessels. Selective coiling with preservation of the parent artery was difficult due to the dissecting nature and wide neck. Moreover, for a dissecting aneurysm, selective coiling of the aneurysm sac alone may be associated with recurrence or rerupture [8]. In this case, these moyamoya-like vessels arose from the proximal PCA P1 segment, twisted together in the subarachnoid space, and formed a collateral circulation. Because these moyamoya-like vessels were not within the brain parenchyma and did not give off any visible important perforating artery into the thalamus or brainstem, PAO for the aneurysm was considered feasible. A previous study (2023) on ruptured aneurysms in moyamoya vessels or collaterals suggested that, as a last resort, PAO using coiling or Onyx casting for such aneurysms may be safe with acceptable clinical outcomes [1]. Therefore, PAO for the aneurysm was chosen.

When performing PAO, it is better to access the aneurysm by catheterization in order to avoid excessive vessel injury that could result in disastrous ischemia. Besides the routine technique of microcatheter navigation using a microguidewire, other uncommon techniques are shown in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, including: looping and reversing the direction of the microguidewire or microcatheter to catheterize the target vessel; using a looped microguidewire to explore the route; using a Synchro-10 microguidewire to navigate a microcatheter; and advancing a Marathon microcatheter following a coil [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. In this case, the Marathon microcatheter was the optimal initial choice for catheterization of the moyamoya‑like vessel; however, it proved too soft and could not be stabilized within the PCA P1 segment. Therefore, the stiffer, thicker Echelon‑10 was selected. Nonetheless, neither the Synchro‑14 nor the Synchro‑10 microguidewire could negotiate the acute vessel turn to enter the parent artery of the aneurysm. Forceful manipulation, such as pushing or rotating the microguidewire, risked vessel perforation. Moreover, the narrow lumen of the small vessel precluded the use of looping techniques with either the microguidewire or the microcatheter for route exploration. Consequently, EVT had to be performed in the proximal parent artery (PCA P1 segment) of the aneurysm.

Two EVT options were considered. One was to use a liquid embolic agent. As it is carried by the blood flow, the liquid embolic agent can obliterate the aneurysm and its parent artery; however, it may embolize normal vessels. In addition, in this case, the acceptable reflux distance was too short; once the agent refluxes into the basilar artery, disastrous ischemic complications will occur. The other option was to deploy a floating coil. If the coil is soft and small enough, once detached, it may escape from the microcatheter tip and be pushed by the blood flow into the parent artery, thereby achieving PAO.

In this case, the detached coils successfully advanced into the parent artery, facilitated by manual saline flush through a 1‑mL syringe and high‑pressure angiography. The first coil, however, lodged in the distal segment of the parent artery beyond the aneurysm, while the second strayed into an adjacent branch. Subsequent deployment of the third and fourth coils occluded the parent artery, thereby achieving trapping of the aneurysm. The patient remained neurologically intact postoperatively, attributable to limited compromise of moyamoya-like vessels and robust collateral supply from the PCA.

The floating‑coil technique can be summarized as follows: since the smallest available coil is 1 mm, the suitable vessel size is approximately 1 mm—vessels that are too small impede coil migration, while those that are larger lose this advantage, because the microcatheter can be advanced directly. When deploying this technique, the coil can be pushed out to test its movement with pulsatile flow; if it floats into the distal parent artery, it can be detached. After detachment, if the coil does not move forward, manual saline flushing or high‑pressure angiography can initiate migration; once in the bloodstream, systemic flow sustains distal advancement of the coil. However, risks remain: the coil may become stuck at an undesired site, so the proper size of the parent artery is crucial. In addition, before PAO, the proximal occlusion of the parent artery must be judged acceptable (i.e., not causing disastrous ischemia), and the final landing site should be safe—if the coil enters a functional territory, no salvage is possible.

Conclusion

When PAO was chosen as the last resort for an aneurysm in moyamoya-like vessels, and the use of liquid embolic agents was precluded by a short reflux distance, coils that could float into the parent artery to occlude the aneurysm represented a useful option. This approach, designated as the “floating‑coil technique,” was particularly valuable for managing inaccessible aneurysms in moyamoya‑like vessels.
