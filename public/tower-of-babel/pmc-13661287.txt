A self-assembled peptide forms α-helical nanopores for ultrasensitive biomarker profiling
Authors: Varsha Shaji, Rajeev Jain, Neethu Puthumadathil, Kalyanashis Jana, Vedasmiritha T S, Ulrich Kleinekathöfer, Krishnananda Chattopadhyay, Kozhinjampara R Mahendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractα-Helical nanopores are attractive molecular sensors, yet their rational design and assembly remain challenging. Here we show that the single peptide pPorA, derived from porin PorACj, self-assembles into flexible α-helical nanopores, inserts in lipid membranes and exists in distinct small- and large-conductance states. By strategically incorporating unnatural amino acids, we engineered small- and large-diameter pores exhibiting single-channel conductances of 2.4 nS and 3.5 nS in 1 M KCl, respectively, while retaining a common octameric architecture. These nanopores enabled the detection of sugars, peptide enantiomers and intrinsically disordered disease proteins that form dynamic, heterogeneous assemblies. The large pores detected multiple α-synuclein (α-syn) variants, including a pathogenic Parkinson’s disease-associated C-terminal deletion mutant with nanomolar affinity (KD ≈ 20 nM). Selective electrostatic trapping of the α-syn N-terminus enabled charge-resolved identification of individual α-syn species within heterogeneous mixtures. The nanopores further resolved time-dependent and inhibitor-modulated α-syn aggregation pathways from monomers to toxic oligomers and fibrils. The small pores detected humanin and superoxide dismutase peptides associated with apoptosis and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, demonstrating tunable sensing through pore-size control. These conformationally programmable α-helical nanopores provide a versatile platform for ultrasensitive profiling of disease biomarkers.Subject terms: Nanopores, Nanopores, Biosensors, Nanostructures

Main

Nanopore technology has transformed the sensing and sequencing field by enabling the detection of analytes ranging from small molecules to DNA and proteins1–5. Its recent applications include protein sequencing and the detection of post-translational modifications6–10. Recently, nanopores have garnered attention for their applications in detecting disease proteins11–15. These proteins contain disordered regions and exist in various structural conformations, which can lead to aggregation11–14,16. Several disease proteins exhibit heterogeneity in size, shape and aggregation dynamics, making them challenging to detect with conventional diagnostic tools and nanopores17–21. This emphasizes the need for advanced nanopores with tunable dimensions to accommodate the diverse protein conformations22–26.

α-Helical pores have emerged as a promising target, offering several advantages, including flexibility, precise control over pore diameter and enhanced selectivity owing to their specific helical interactions27–29. Importantly, charge selectivity can be engineered in α-helical pores by exploiting their structural geometry, unlike in biological pores28,30,31. These features make them ideal for detecting intrinsically disordered disease proteins, which exhibit irregular aggregation patterns27,28,30,31. Notably, computational and experimental design have enabled the construction of α-helical pores with limited sensing applications31–35. In this work, we (1) assembled the flexible octameric pPorA pores with large (‘L’) and small (‘S’) diameters for size, charge and stereoselective analyte sensing; (2) detected pathological α-synuclein (α-syn) proteins and their time-dependent aggregation pathway using L-pores; and (3) detected smaller humanin and superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) peptides using S-pores. This ability of pPorA pores for sensing conformationally diverse disease biomarkers highlights their potential for advanced biosensing.

Electrical and structural properties of flexible dual-diameter (S and L) pPorA nanopores

The 40-amino-acid-long peptide pPorA, based on the porin PorACj, forms stable channels in lipid bilayers28. The substitution of a lysine with cysteine at position 24 (K24C) in the peptide revealed that this residue is critical for pore self-assembly28,31. Here, we rationally incorporated an unnatural d-cysteine at key position 24 of LpPorA, to tune pore geometry, generating the LpPorA K24C(D) peptide (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). These α-helical peptides were purified for biophysical characterization (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) showed a ~35 kDa band, indicating pre-oligomerization of the peptide as octamers (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). This is further confirmed by analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC), which revealed that LpPorA K24C(D) forms an octamer, consistent with analytical size exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The single-channel properties of these peptide octamers were characterized in 1,2-diphytanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine lipid bilayers (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2). The LpPorA K24C(D) octamers extracted from the gel (n = 100) formed pores of two distinct conductances in 1 M KCl. The low-conductance S-pores (2.4 ± 0.2 nS) and high-conductance L-pores (3.5 ± 0.2 nS) were observed with equal frequency (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2). Both pores were cation-selective and remained open at low voltages (≤±75 mV), with ~90% of the pores showing slightly higher current at negative voltages, indicating specific pore orientation (Extended Data Fig. 1). Octamers purified by SEC and SDS–PAGE displayed identical S- and L-pore electrical properties, indicating that the same octamer assembles into both pore types (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Next, we engineered protease-stable DpPorA K24C(L) by incorporating natural l-cysteine at position 24 of DpPorA, which exhibited identical properties to those of LpPorA K24C(D) (Fig. 1a,b, Extended Data Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Next, we built molecular models of the LpPorA K24C(D) pores to investigate their structural properties (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Text and Supplementary Figs. 3–6). At first, we examined hexameric pore models, which were extremely narrow and exhibited low conductance (Extended Data Fig. 4). Therefore, we constructed octameric pore models that exhibited stable conformations as revealed by root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). The potassium and chloride ions were observed simultaneously diffusing through the pore during the unbiased molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. When a transmembrane potential was applied using the constant electric-field method, the pores remained stable at +0.5 V and the ionic conductance of LpPorA K24C(D) S-pores was calculated to be ~3.0 nS in 1 M KCl (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Furthermore, we designed L-pores with an expanded radius, approximately 0.7 Å larger than that of S-pores, which remained stable in applied-field MD runs and exhibited a higher conductance of ~4.0 nS (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 3–6 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The MD simulations were performed at high applied voltages of ±0.2 V and ±0.5 V to enhance the statistics of ion conductance; therefore, they should be interpreted with caution17,31. The current ratio I+/I− indicated that the S- and L-pores are cation-selective (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Analysis of the radius profile using the HOLE program confirmed distinctions in S- and L-pore geometry, contributing to the difference in pore conductance (Fig. 1c). Notably, even a small difference in the constriction radius (0.7–1.0 Å) resulted in a substantial change in conductance. Furthermore, electrostatic potential maps of LpPorA K24C(D) pores revealed the spatial distribution of charged residues lining the channel (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). Comparison of the potential surfaces showed no notable differences between the S- and L-pores, suggesting that pore widening has a minimal influence on the overall electrostatic landscape of the channel (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). DpPorA K24C(L) also formed stable S- and L-pores with conductance and electrostatic potential map comparable with LpPorA K24C(D) pores (Fig. 1c,d, Supplementary Figs. 3–7 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Size, charge and stereoselective sensing with LpPorA K24C(D) (S and L) nanopores

Next, we examined the binding of cationic cyclodextrin (am6αCD) and nona-arginine (R9) peptides to LpPorA K24C(D) S- and L-pores to assess their functionality. The voltage-dependent dissociation rates (koff) of am6αCD were consistent with its translocation through the L-pores (τoff at +40 mV, 1.44 ± 0.1 ms), whereas am6αCD binds to the S-pores with high affinity (τoff at +10 mV, ~200 ms) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 8). Next, the addition of R9 peptides to the L-pores did not produce any blockage events, whereas it produced voltage-dependent blockage events with S-pores, confirming size-selective sensing (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Fig. 9). Interestingly, R9 produced dominant blockage events of extended dwell time (τoff) in low salt (0.15 M KCl), establishing electrostatic binding (Supplementary Fig. 10). Also, stronger interactions were observed on the trans side as compared with the cis side, and this binding asymmetry indicated pore orientation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Further, the stereospecificity of the LpPorA K24C(D) S-pores was assessed by analysing their binding with R9 enantiomers, R9(L) and r9(D) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 9). R9(L), but not r9(D), produced voltage-dependent blockage events in S-pores, confirming distinct peptide binding kinetics (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 9). To elucidate this molecular mechanism, R9(L) and r9(D) translocation through the LpPorA K24C(D) S-pore was examined using steered molecular dynamics simulations (SMDs) (Fig. 2d). The average force required to pull R9(L) was 500 pN, whereas the force needed to pull r9(D) was 400 pN (Fig. 2d). Structural analysis revealed that R9(L) formed an α-helical hairpin inside the LpPorA K24C(D) S-pore through electrostatic interactions between negatively charged pore residues and positively charged R9(L) (Fig. 2d). This mechanism, based on simulations, is consistent with the experimental data but not directly demonstrated by the experiments. Nevertheless, simulations provide a possible molecular explanation for how pore geometry regulates transport.

Detection and differentiation of neuropathological α-syn variants using L-nanopores

Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) are challenging to detect using conventional systems owing to their low physiological abundance and their tendency to form heterogeneous assemblies. Therefore, we specifically used the LpPorA K24C(D) large-diameter pores for the detection of the intrinsically disordered 140-amino-acid full-length α-syn and its pathogenic mutants involved in Parkinson’s disease (Figs. 3–5, Extended Data Figs. 5–8 and Supplementary Figs. 11–18 and 20). The addition of 25 nM wild-type α-syn (net charge −9) to the trans side resulted in extended blockages at +50 mV (Ires 1, 38.86%) and increasing the voltage to +75 mV (Ires 1, 40.15%) resulted in more closures, with the pore remaining closed (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 3). Notably, wild-type α-syn produced heterogeneous blockages from 2 to 15 min, indicating the presence of diverse α-syn structures. Consistent blockages emerged after ~15 min, suggesting the presence of monomers and intermediate aggregates36 (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 11). We validated these transitions by atomic force microscopy (AFM), thioflavin-T (ThT) assay and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6, and Supplementary Fig. 12). Next, the L-pores were used for sensing the pathological α-syn A30P mutant, which promotes α-syn oligomerization in patients with early-onset familial Parkinson’s disease37. The addition of 25 nM α-syn A30P (net charge −9) to the trans side of the L-pore resulted in immediate pore closure with no detectable reopening (Ires 1 at +50 mV, 42.70 % and Ires 1 at +75 mV, 47.47 %) (Fig. 3b, Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 3). The pore remained blocked, with a shorter open-state duration at +40 mV. By contrast, almost no open state was observed at +75 mV, indicating voltage-dependent pore blocking (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 6). This strong interaction is specific to α-syn A30P, suggesting its large assemblies of enhanced flexibility, consistent with FTIR analysis (Fig. 3b, Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 12). Furthermore, we designed another α-syn mutant, ‘N terminal deletion mutant’ (α-synΔN) (net charge, −11), in which a part of the N-terminus was deleted (amino acids 2–40). The addition of 25 nM α-synΔN resulted in voltage-dependent rapid pore closures, distinct from those of wild-type α-syn and α-syn A30P (Ires 1 at +75 mV, 44.94 %) (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3). For these three α-syn variants, we observed blockages at positive voltages on trans side addition, indicating that the positively charged N-terminus of α-syn is electrophoretically pulled into the pore, confirming charge-selective binding (Fig. 3a–c, Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6 and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 13). To validate this, we produced the negatively charged α-syn C-terminal segment (amino acids 96–140; net charge −12), which produced no blockages with the L- or S-pores (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 14). By contrast, anion-selective pPorA pores, that we previously characterized, facilitated the binding of the α-syn C-terminus28 (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results establish the α-syn N-terminus as the primary binding determinant, activating charge-selective detection at nanomolar affinity in LpPorA K24C(D) L-pores.

Next, we used L-pores for sensing the cationic 103-amino-acid α-syn C-terminal deletion mutant (α-synΔC, net charge +5) (Fig. 4a,b, Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 15). Importantly, this pathogenic mutant rapidly aggregated in vivo and showed two distinct peaks in SEC38. Notably, addition of 25 nM α-synΔC to the trans side of the L-pore produced heterogeneous blockages from 2 to 15 min at +50 mV, indicating the presence of diverse forms, including monomers and oligomers (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7). The 30 min continuous time-series analysis revealed short monomeric events within 0–2 min and after ~15 min, a consistent blockage pattern emerged, indicating the formation of uniform α-synΔC oligomers, which is further supported by AFM, FTIR and ThT assays (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7). Notably, α-synΔC produced well-defined ion current blockages, fluctuating between open and closed states, accompanied by transient spikes from the blocked step at +40 mV (τoff, 233 ± 20 ms; Ires 1, 26.32% and Ires 2, 2.22%) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 15). Increasing the voltage to +75 mV resulted in frequent events with reduced blockage times (τoff, 43 ± 3 ms, Ires 1, 24.85% and Ires 2, 4.40%) (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Table 3). Analysis of these blockages revealed an increase in koff with voltage consistent with effective voltage-dependent translocation (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 15). Subsequently, we designed a cationic 141-amino-acid charge-reversed α-syn mutant (+3) that produced time-resolved ion current blockages of shorter dwell time (τoff), fluctuating between open and two distinct closed states at +50 mV (Ires 1, 36.55% and Ires 2, 10.34%) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 3). Increasing the voltage to +75 mV increased the frequency of these two distinct blockage events (Ires 1, 33.97% and Ires 2, 1.90%) (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 3). To further establish the high discriminatory capability of the pore, we carried out competitive binding studies by sequentially adding ΔC, charged-reversed and A30P α-syn mutants to the pore (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). First, α-synΔC was added to the trans side of the pore, producing well-defined ion current blockages of longer τoff (τoff at +50 mV, 132 ± 11 ms; Ires 1, 26.38% and Ires 2, 3.06%) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). Subsequent addition of charge-reversed α-syn produced two sets of clearly distinguishable events (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). The events with the evidently longer τoff (τoff at +50 mV, 132 ± 11 ms; Ires 1, 26.38% and Ires 2, 3.06%) produced by α-synΔC and the events with shorter τoff (τoff at +50 mV, 0.348 ± 0.03 ms; Ires 1, 36.55% and Ires 2, 10.34%) produced by the charge-reversed α-syn mutant (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). Further addition of α-syn A30P to the pore resulted in rapid closure of the pore, with the pore remaining in the blocked state (Ires 1, 42.70%) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). These three α-syn variants of distinct conformations produced mutually exclusive events, demonstrating the exceptional ability of the L-pores to distinguish different α-syn mutants (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). Further, the interaction of the α-syn proteins with LpPorA K24C(D) pores in low- and high-salt buffers (0.15 M, 1 M and 3 M KCl) revealed that the pores remained functionally stable across all ionic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Time-dependent and blocker-induced α-syn aggregation pathway

To elucidate the time-dependent aggregation pathway of α-synΔC, we produced monomers, various oligomeric forms and fibrils in vitro and examined their properties in electrical recordings (Methods). Notably, early oligomers are neurotoxic and challenging to characterize experimentally owing to their dynamically unstable structures11,13,14. The addition of 0–15 min incubated α-synΔC to the L-pore produced distinct types of events with varying τoff (τoff at +50 mV, ~4 ms to 1 s) but consistent Ires (Ires 1, 26.38% and Ires 2, 3.06%), probably representing L-pore blocking by monomeric and oligomeric α-synΔC (Fig. 5a). Further, the addition of the in vitro generated 12-h incubated α-synΔC produced well-defined ion current blockages with a consistent τoff of 165 ± 15 ms, revealing disordered oligomers (Fig. 5a). Further short additional events were observed from the blocked state, representing the different conformations of α-synΔC oligomers, as they traversed the pore (Fig. 5a). Similar events were observed with the addition of the 24-h incubated α-synΔC; however, the τoff increased to 255 ± 25 ms, indicating the formation of larger ordered oligomers (Fig. 5b). While the addition of 48-h incubated α-synΔC did not produce any well-resolved events, it did shut the pore into a blocked state (Ires 1 at +50 mV, 40.75%) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 18). This suggests that these oligomers were dense, large and structurally stable, and which bind to the pore with high affinity, resulting in the pore closure (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 18). Next, we obtained α-synΔC fibrils from the pellet after 72-h and 96-h incubations. The addition of the 72-h and 96-h incubated α-synΔC produced two distinct interactions: (1) no events, revealing that the fibrils were too large to enter the pore or (2) single and two-step blockages, which indicates that small protofibrils can partially enter the pore39 (Fig. 5c). Thus, α-synΔC monomers, with their different oligomeric forms and fibrils, exhibit distinct pore-interaction current signatures (Fig. 5). This time-dependent aggregation pathway was confirmed by AFM, which showed transition of α-synΔC from early oligomers to larger spherical and linear oligomers, and finally, fibrils40,41 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 19). These species were further characterized by FTIR and ThT assays (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 19). Finally, we characterized the interaction of L-pores with α-synΔC oligomers in the presence of the blocker, polyphenol(−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)42. First, 12-h incubated α-synΔC was added to the L-pore, which produced time-resolved ion current blockages corresponding to translocation of small α-synΔC oligomers (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 8). Subsequent addition of EGCG resulted in permanent pore closure after 30 min, indicating the formation of structurally diverse α-synΔC oligomers through an alternative non-toxic aggregation pathway (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 8). In addition, we assessed the potential of DpPorA K24C(L) and LpPorA K24C pores for sensing α-synΔC, which exhibited comparatively lower capture rates (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Table 4).

Sensing of small intrinsically disordered disease peptides using S-nanopores

Next, we investigated the potential of the LpPorA K24C(D) S-pores for detecting small IDP biomarkers such as 24-residue humanin (net charge +2) and the 25-residue SOD1 peptides (net charge +1) that are linked to apoptosis and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), respectively43–45 (Fig. 6). The addition of wild-type humanin induced long-lived blockage events with fluctuating current and varying τoff values at positive voltages, indicating electrostatic binding of peptides with the pore, consistent with no blockages at negative voltages (τoff at +40 mV, ~15–800 ms; Ires, ~23–55%) (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 9). Next, we assessed the interaction between the S-pores and toxic humanin C8A mutant, which induces apoptosis in cells43. This mutant produced very short, frequent blockage events that increased dramatically with positive voltages, indicating charge-selective binding (τoff at +40 mV, 0.106 ± 0.01 ms; Ires at +40 mV, ~22–59%) (Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 9). The distinct current signatures of humanin peptides indicate their unstable, disordered conformations with occasional absence of blockage events reflecting their complex conformational dynamics, in agreement with MD simulations (Fig. 6a,b, Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 21). We further demonstrated sensing SOD1 peptides that contain a zinc-binding loop, a critical structural factor involved in ALS44,45. The addition of wild-type SOD1 peptides to the S-pores produced consistent, clear-cut blockage events at different positive voltages specifically in the presence of zinc, which stabilizes its uniform conformation (Fig. 6c and Extended Data Fig. 10). The voltage-dependent kinetics revealed the electrophoretic pulling and translocation of these cationic peptides (τoff at +50 mV, 0.22 ± 0.02 ms; τoff at +75 mV, 0.18 ± 0.01 ms). Next, we examined the interaction of the SOD1 H71G mutant peptide, which produced minimal pore blockages owing to conformational changes resulting from the disruption of the zinc-binding site (Fig. 6d and Extended Data Fig. 10). These findings highlight the potential of pPorA pores to detect unstable dynamic structural transitions in IDPs.

Conclusion

Here, we introduce a distinct class of conformationally flexible α-helical pPorA nanopores in which a single oligomeric state can yield two pore architectures, termed S- and L-pores. These dynamic nanopores, with sophisticated pore geometries and electrostatic charge selectivity, enabled the detection of IDP biomarkers implicated in neuropathology, apoptosis and ALS36–38,43,44. Importantly, L-pores facilitated single-molecule detection of various α-syn proteins, including pathogenic mutants with nanomolar affinity. These data highlight the potential of pPorA nanopores for detecting α-syn in blood, which is present at lower concentrations11–15. The aptamer-modified, truncated and monomeric α-syn and amyloid-β peptides have been previously characterized using solid-state and biological nanopores, but their ability to detect toxic disease mutants has been limited11,21,46–48. Our results suggest that the pPorA nanopores provide an effective approach for detecting full-length toxic α-syn variants across a range of conformations. Notably, we resolved the time-dependent aggregation pathway of α-syn variants from monomers to oligomers to fibrils, complemented by biophysical techniques. Thus, this work establishes a platform for screening therapeutic drugs that selectively block toxic α-syn oligomers15,42. In addition to sensing large α-syn proteins, S-pores can detect smaller pathological IDP biomarkers and the conformational changes they undergo upon mutation43–45. Despite these advances, the lack of these pore structures has limited elucidation of the molecular determinants governing their sensing mechanism. Furthermore, the MD simulations of modelled pores agree with the experimental data; however, they rely on several approximations, including simple pore models and high electric fields. In the future, α-helical pore design rules can be refined through computational design to improve their performance under physiological conditions, thereby enabling the development of programmable nanodevices for sensing complex disease biomarkers. In addition, protease-stable α-helical pores composed of unnatural amino acids can be designed for applications beyond biosensing in cellular nanotechnology, such as intracellular delivery.

Methods

Single-channel electrical recordings

The electrical properties of the pores were characterized using 1,2-diphytanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPhPC; Avanti Polar Lipids) bilayers formed across an aperture (∼70 μm in diameter) in a 25-μm thick polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) film (Goodfellow), which separated the apparatus into two 600-µl compartments, cis and trans49. The aperture was made lipophilic by pre-painting both sides with hexadecane in n-pentane (1 μl, 5 mg ml−1), followed by filling both compartments with buffered electrolytes (1 M KCl and 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4). For low- and high-salt buffer conditions, 0.15 M and 3 M KCl were used, respectively. Both sides were overlayed with lipids (DPhPC in n-pentane; 2 μl, 5 mg ml−1), following which the solvent was allowed to evaporate. Planar lipid bilayers were formed by raising and lowering the electrolyte level to bring the two lipid monolayers together at the aperture. The pPorA pores were formed by adding gel-extracted or SEC-purified octamers in 0.1% DDM to the cis side of the chamber under high voltage. The cis compartment was connected to the grounded electrode and the trans compartment was attached to the working electrode. A potential difference was applied through a pair of Ag/AgCl electrodes in 3% agarose containing 3 M KCl. The ion selectivity of the pores was measured by using a KCl salt gradient applied across the bilayer chambers (0.15 M cis/1 M trans). The current was amplified with an Axopatch 200B amplifier, digitized with a Digidata 1550B and recorded with pClamp 11.0 acquisition software (Molecular Devices), with a low-pass filter at 10 kHz and a sampling frequency of 50 kHz. The current signals were further digitally filtered at 2 kHz or 7 kHz, as needed, to represent the data more accurately. The data were analysed using pClamp (version 11.2, Molecular Devices) and plotted with OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab).

Molecular dynamics simulations

Two sets of pores were investigated in this computational modelling study: S- and L-pore variants consisting of LpPorA K24C(D) helices and their stereochemically inverted counterparts consisting of DpPorA K24C(L) helices. First, the LpPorA K24C(D) pores were constructed from their amino acid sequences using the CCBuilder 2.0 webserver50 and further converted to the corresponding mirror pores using BIOVIA Discovery Studio51. The modelled structures for each pore type were subsequently embedded into a DPhPC bilayer. The bilayer systems were solvated with TIP3P water molecules on both sides of the membrane, maintaining a water thickness of 25 Å using the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder52. Counterions (K+ or Cl−) were added to ensure charge neutrality, followed by adding 1.0 M KCl to mimic the experimental ionic conditions and to facilitate ion conductance measurements. Each system, comprising protein, membrane, solvent and ions, was enclosed in a rectangular box with an approximate size of 9.5 × 9.5 × 12 nm3, containing nearly 100,000 atoms. MD simulations were performed using the CHARMM36-m force field and the GROMACS 2022.3 simulation package53,54. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration followed a six-step protocol: two constant-volume (NVT) runs of 5 ns each and four constant-pressure (NPT) runs totalling 75 ns. During the NVT equilibration, a Berendsen thermostat maintained a temperature of 300 K with a coupling constant of 1 ps (ref. 55). The NPT phase was conducted with semi-isotropic pressure coupling at 1 bar using a Berendsen barostat, with a pressure coupling constant of 2 ps, ensuring a relaxation of the system to experimental-like conditions. Moreover, non-bonded interactions were calculated using the Verlet cutoff scheme, with a 10 Å cutoff applied to both Coulombic and Lennard–Jones interactions. Long-range electrostatics was treated using the Particle-mesh Ewald method56. In addition, bond constraints in the proteins were enforced using the linear constraint solver algorithm57. Following equilibration, production simulations were performed in triplicate for each pore system (LpPorA K24C(D) S-pore, LpPorA K24C(D) L-pore, DpPorA K24C(L) S-pore and DpPorA K24C(L) L-pore), with each trajectory extended to 1.0 µs in the NPT ensemble. These runs employed the Nose–Hoover thermostat to maintain the temperature at 300 K and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat with semi-isotropic coupling to maintain pressure at 1.0 bar (refs. 58,59). The trajectory stability was assessed through an analysis of the RMSD relative to the equilibrated structures, providing insight into conformational stability of the different pores. The equilibrated structures obtained after 500 ns of simulation were subsequently used in applied-field MD simulations to explore ion conductance under an external transmembrane potential.

Expression and purification of α-syn variants

Recombinant α-syn proteins (wild-type, A30P, ΔN, ΔC and charge-reversed) were expressed using the pRK172 plasmid in the Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) strain. Protein production was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG when the culture reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, and the culture was incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C under constant stirring at 200 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000g at 4 °C. α-syn proteins were purified using a non-chromatographic method previously reported60. In brief, the cell pellet was resuspended in cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 10 mM EDTA and 150 mM NaCl) and boiled in a water bath for 10 min. Before boiling, 1 mM PMSF was also added to the cell suspension. Cell debris and supernatant were separated by centrifugation at 15,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. To the supernatant obtained, streptomycin sulfate (136 μl of 10% solution per ml of supernatant) and glacial acetic acid (228 μl per ml of supernatant) were added to precipitate out nucleic acids. The resulting cloudy solution was again centrifuged at 15,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, α-syn proteins were precipitated by adding small amount of ammonium sulfate to attain 50% saturation (calculated from EnCor Biotechnology Inc.) with constant stirring at 4 °C. Precipitated protein was centrifuged at 15,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The α-syn protein pellet obtained was washed first with 50% ammonium sulfate solution followed by washing twice with a 1:1 mixture of ammonium acetate (100 mM) and ethanol. Finally, the protein pellet was dialysed overnight against sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM), pH 7.4, using SnakeSkin dialysis tubing (10 kDa MWCO) at 4 °C. The protein pellet was dissolved in sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM), pH 7.4, before dialysis. The dialysed protein was filtered through a 0.22-µm PES syringe filter (Millex-GP, Merck Millipore) and injected into a HiTRAP DEAE FF anion exchange chromatography (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with Tris buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4). The protein was eluted using a NaCl gradient in a Tris buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4). α-Syn was eluted approximately at a 300 mM NaCl concentration. Fractions containing α-syn protein were further purified using a Sephadex gel filtration column (Enrich SEC 650, 10 × 300 column, Bio-Rad) pre-equilibrated with sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4) with a flow rate of 1 ml min−1 and proteins were eluted in 1 CV. Both chromatographic techniques were performed using the NGC Quest 100 FPLC system (Bio-Rad). The collected fractions containing α-syn (fractions 14 and 15 for wild-type; fractions 14 and 15 for A30P; fractions 14, 15 and 16 for charge-reversed; fractions 15 and 16 for ΔN; and fractions 15 and 16 for ΔC) were run on 15% SDS–PAGE. Pure monomeric proteins thus obtained were dialysed overnight against sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4), concentrated, aliquoted into small fractions in microcentrifuge tubes, flash-frozen, lyophilized and stored at −80 °C. For sample preparation, lyophilized proteins were dissolved in ultrapure water, followed by centrifugation at 15,000g for 1 h at 4 °C to remove any trace amounts of oligomeric forms that may have formed during purification steps and storage61. We added purified α-syn proteins to the trans side of the lipid bilayer for single-channel recordings.

Preparation of α-syn oligomers and fibrils

For the preparation of α-syn oligomers and fibrils, α-syn (200 µM) in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) was incubated at 37 °C for the corresponding incubation times (0–15 min, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h) with constant agitation at 200 rpm. After incubation, the solution was ultracentrifuged at 50,000g for 2 h. The supernatant was collected for oligomers and the pellet was collected for fibrils. For oligomers, the supernatant was separated by multiple filtrations through a 100-kDa cutoff filter (Amicon, Merck Millipore), where the monomers passed through the filter, leaving the oligomeric species behind. For fibrils, the protein pellet was resuspended in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4).

Statistics and reproducibility

The single-channel conductance was estimated from >50 single-channel insertions. Analyte binding events were detected using the ‘Single-Channel Search’ function in pClamp (version 11.2, Molecular Devices). The corresponding interaction kinetics were characterized using the parameters, dwell time (τoff), the mean interevent interval (τon), the dissociation rate constant (koff; koff = 1/τoff), the association rate constant (kon; kon = 1/τon x C, where C is concentration) and the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD; KD = koff/kon). These parameters, including dwell time (mean ± s.d.) and Ires, were reported on the basis of three experimental replicates. All unbiased and applied-field MD simulations were performed in triplicate, and average data and RMSD plots for multiple simulations were shown. For α-syn characterization using biophysical tools, data were generated from independent biological replicates performed thrice, unless stated otherwise. Error bars represent standard deviation or standard error of the mean, as applicable. Sample sizes were indicated in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
