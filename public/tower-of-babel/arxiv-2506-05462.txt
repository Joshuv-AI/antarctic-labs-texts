Probing quantum geometry with two-dimensional nonlinear optical spectroscopy
Paul Froese, Mark R. Hirsbrunner, Yong Baek Kim

Abstract
Recent studies have shown that the nonlinear optical response of crystalline systems is fundamentally a quantum geometric property. In this work, we propose two-dimensional coherent spectroscopy (2DCS), which measures the nonlinear conductivity as a function of two independent frequencies using two time-delayed light pulses, as a probe of quantum geometry. We show how the two-frequency second-order nonlinear conductivity, which is naturally measured by 2DCS, decomposes into distinct quantum geometric contributions. We identify a term arising from the multi-band quantum connection that does not appear in linear response, and show that it can be measured in isolation by considering specific polarizations and enforcing time-reversal symmetry. We explore this finding via model calculations for transition metal dichalcogenides and Sr 2 RuO 4 . Through these examples, we demonstrate how 2DCS enables study of the quantum connection, providing a way to compare the quantum geometry of different materials. We also show that one can gain rough momentum-resolved knowledge of the quantum geometry by varying the chemical potential.



Introduction.—

The quantum geometry of electronic eigenstates plays a vital role in condensed matter systems. The earliest appearance of quantum geometry in condensed matter was in the integer quantum Hall effect
v. Klitzing et al. 1980
, wherein the integral of the Berry curvature gives the Chern number
Thouless et al. 1982
. It was later appreciated that that Berry curvature has physical consequences itself: it determines the anomalous velocity that enters the semiclassical equations of motion for a Bloch wavepacket
Chang and Niu 1995
;
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999
. While the Berry curvature is well-known, recent works have revealed the significance of lesser-known geometric quantities, such as the quantum metric and quantum connection. Identifying further geometry-driven phenomena and finding ways to measure quantum geometry are currently of great interest
Törmä 2023
;
Yu et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025
.

In this work, we propose two-dimensional coherent spectroscopy (2DCS), which measures the nonlinear optical conductivity as a function of two frequencies using two time-delayed pulses of light
Mukamel 1995
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Fresch et al. 2023
, as a probe of multi-band quantum geometry. 2DCS provides richer information than conventional single-frequency measurements and is a natural tool for studying the second-order optical conductivity. Here we demonstrate how the second-order conductivity decomposes into distinct multi-band geometric contributions that can be probed by 2DCS. We further show that selecting specific polarizations and enforcing time-reversal symmetry allows one to isolate the contribution from a particular multi-band quantum geometric quantity, the quantum connection. We explore this fact via model calculations, illustrating how 2DCS can reveal differences in the quantum connection between materials using models of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs). Finally, using a model for Sr
2
RuO
4
, we demonstrate how tuning the chemical potential allows 2DCS to probe different regions of momentum space, providing rough momentum-resolved information about the geometry.

The quantum geometry of optical response.—

Geometric objects characterize the local structure of a manifold. A metric, for example, defines a notion of distance, and a connection describes parallel transport of tangent vectors and provides a definition of curvature
Nakahara 2003
.
Quantum geometry broadly refers to the geometric structure of the manifold of eigenstates that diagonalize a parametric Hamiltonian. In crystalline systems, the parameter is the crystal momentum
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
and the Bloch eigenstates,
|\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle=e^{i{\boldsymbol{{k}}}\cdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{{r}}}}}|u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle
, form the manifold.

The most thoroughly studied quantum geometric quantities are the Berry connection,
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{a}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=i\langle u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\partial_{k_{a}}u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle,
the Berry curvature,
\Omega_{ab}^{n},
which characterizes how the phase of a Bloch state changes as the momentum
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
is varied, and the quantum metric,
g_{ab}^{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}}),
which describes the orthogonality, or “amplitude distance”, between states. The quantum metric and Berry curvature are the real and imaginary parts, respectively, of a quantity known as the quantum geometric tensor (QGT)
Provost and Vallee 1980
, defined as

\displaystyle Q^{n}_{ab}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{k_{a}}u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\left(\mathds{1}-|u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle\langle u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\right)|\partial_{k_{b}}u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle.

(1)

The QGT has recently been shown to play a key role in many seemingly disparate areas of condensed matter, ranging from flat-band superconductivity
Peotta and Törmä 2015
;
Chen and Law 2024
;
Hofmann et al. 2020
, capacitance in insulators
Komissarov et al. 2024
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025a
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025b
, the fractional quantum anomalous Hall effect
Roy 2014
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Mera and Ozawa 2021
;
Ledwith et al. 2023
, and, the focus of this work, optical responses
Ahn et al. 2021
;
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016
;
Avdoshkin et al. 2025
;
Mitscherling et al. 2025
;
Bhalla et al. 2022
;
Kumar et al. 2024
;
Chaudhary et al. 2022
;
Carmichael and Claassen 2025
;
Ghosh et al. 2024
.

The geometric nature of optical responses arises from the fact that the electric dipole Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)=e{\boldsymbol{{r}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{E}}}(t),

(2)

couples the perturbing electric field
{\boldsymbol{{E}}}(t)
to the electronic position operator,
{\boldsymbol{{r}}}
. Because the position operator
r^{a}
can be represented as the momentum derivative
i\partial_{k_{a}}
, it provides a basis of tangent vectors on the manifold of Bloch states
Ahn et al. 2021
. Specifically, the interband matrix elements of the position operator in the basis of Bloch states, equivalent to the non-Abelian Berry connection
\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{a}=i\langle u_{m{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\partial_{k_{a}}u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle
, provide the tangent vectors. Geometric quantities like the quantum metric and quantum connection can be constructed from these tangent vectors.

In addition to
g_{ab}^{n}
, we can construct another metric tensor on the manifold of Bloch states by taking the natural definition of the inner product between the tangent basis vectors. This produces the multi-band generalization of the QGT,

Q_{ab}^{mn}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\mathcal{A}_{nm}^{a}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{b}({\boldsymbol{{k}}}).

(3)

Following Fermi’s golden rule, the multi-band QGT is proportional to the optical transition rate between bands, and so naturally appears in the optical conductivity
Ahn et al. 2021
.

Another desirable geometric property of the Bloch manifold is a concept of parallel transport of the tangent vectors, which is characterized by the quantum connection
Ahn et al. 2021
,

\displaystyle C^{mn}_{a;bc}

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{A}^{b}_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\left(\mathcal{A}_{nn}^{a}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})-\mathcal{A}_{mm}^{a}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\right)\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{c}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

(4)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{A}^{b}_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\partial_{k_{a}}\mathcal{A}^{c}_{mn}({\boldsymbol{{k}}}).

The quantum connection is proportional to the real-space shift of the wavefunction between different bands, and thus contributes to the optical response as well
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016
;
Cook et al. 2017
. In contrast to the QGT, which is second order in the position operator, the quantum connection is third order in the position operator, and therefore contributes only to the nonlinear optical response. In the next section we derive the precise dependence of the second-order optical conductivity on the QGT and quantum connection.

Geometric decomposition of second-order nonlinear conductivity.—

To derive the nonlinear optical conductivity, we consider Hamiltonians of the form
\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a single-particle Bloch Hamiltonian and the perturbation
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)
is the electric dipole Hamiltonian given in (
2
). Working in the interaction picture, the density matrix
\rho
follows the equation of motion
i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho(t)=[\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t),\rho(t)]
with the equilibrium initial condition
[\rho_{0}]_{nm}=\delta_{nm}f_{n}
, where
f_{n}=f(\varepsilon_{n})
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution for band
n
and we have suppressed the
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
label. The current
j^{a}(t)
is given by
j^{a}(t)=-e\tr\left(v(t)\rho(t)\right)
, where the trace taken over the single-particle Hilbert space, i.e.
\tr(\cdot)=\sum_{n}\int_{k}\langle u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\cdot|u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle
, and
\int_{k}
is a shorthand for
\int\frac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
.

The equation of motion is solved order by order by expanding the density matrix in powers of the electric field
Aversa and Sipe 1995
;
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Ventura et al. 2017
;
Parker et al. 2019
;
Passos et al. 2021
;
Holder et al. 2020
. Likewise expanding the induced current in powers of the field, the second-order contribution is

\displaystyle j_{2}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{bc}\int d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\sigma^{a;bc}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

(5)

\displaystyle\times E^{b}(t-\tau_{2})E^{c}(t-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}),

where the second-order conductivity
\sigma^{a;bc}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;bc}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\theta(\tau_{1})\theta(\tau_{2})

(6)

\displaystyle\times\tr\left([[v^{a}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}),r^{b}(\tau_{1})],r^{c}]\rho_{0}\right).

Evaluating the commutators and Fourier transforming in both time variables, we find that second-order conductivity naturally decomposes into seven distinct terms,

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;bc}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathrm{Drude}}^{a;bc}+\sigma_{\mathrm{an}}^{a;bc}+\sigma_{\mathrm{DR}}^{a;bc}

(7)

\displaystyle+\sigma_{\mathrm{HOP}}^{a;bc}+\sigma_{C}^{a;bc}+\sigma_{\mathrm{inj}}^{a;bc}+\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}^{a;bc},

where we have suppressed the frequency dependence for conciseness. The explicit form of each term is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{Drude}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}g_{0}^{\omega_{1}}g_{0}^{\omega_{2}}\int_{k}\sum_{n}(\partial_{k_{c}}f_{n})(\partial_{k_{b}}\partial_{k_{a}}\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{an}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}g_{0}^{\omega_{1}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{nm}^{\omega_{2}}(\partial_{k_{c}}f_{nm})\varepsilon_{nm}Q_{ab}^{mn}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{DR}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}\left(\partial_{k_{b}}f_{nm}\right)\varepsilon_{nm}Q_{ca}^{mn}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{HOP}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}(\partial_{k_{b}}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}})g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}f_{nm}\varepsilon_{nm}Q_{ca}^{mn}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{inj}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}g_{0}^{\omega_{2}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}(\partial_{k_{a}}\varepsilon_{mn})Q^{mn}_{cb}

\displaystyle\sigma_{C}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}f_{nm}\varepsilon_{nm}C_{b;ac}^{nm}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}^{a;bc}

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{c}_{nm}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{l\neq m,n}\left(g_{ml}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{ml}\mathcal{A}_{ml}^{a}\mathcal{A}_{ln}^{b}-g_{ln}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{ln}\mathcal{A}^{b}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ln}\right).

Here
f_{nm}=f_{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})-f_{m}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})
,
\varepsilon_{mn}=\varepsilon_{m}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})-\varepsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})
, and the poles are given by
g_{0}^{\omega}=1/\omega
and
g_{mn}^{\omega}=1/(\omega+\varepsilon_{mn}/\hbar)
. The two frequencies
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
correspond to the input and measurement frequencies, respectively. We incorporate a phenomenological scattering rate by adding a small imaginary part to each frequency:
\omega_{1}\to\omega_{1}+i\eta,\omega_{2}\to\omega_{2}+2i\eta
, which both accounts for disorder and interaction effects and ensures convergence
Kaplan et al. 2023
;
Bradlyn and Abbamonte 2024
. The factor of 2 for
\omega_{2}
arises naturally from our frequency convention.

Each contribution is classified according to its geometric interpretation and pole structure, and the names of these terms are chosen to be consistent with previous studies
Bhalla et al. 2022
;
Kumar et al. 2024
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
. The nonlinear Drude term
\sigma_{\mathrm{Drude}}
is peaked at
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(0,0)
and only occurs if a Fermi surface is present. The “anomalous” (an) term is also a Fermi surface term, but is peaked along the
\omega_{1}=0
axis and depends on the QGT. In the
\omega_{2}\to 0
limit, it corresponds to the current arising from the anomalous velocity induced by the Berry curvature dipole
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Du et al. 2021
. The “doubly resonant” (DR) and “higher order pole” (HOP) contributions also depend on the QGT, but are peaked along the diagonal,
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
, and have more complex pole structures. The DR term is a Fermi surface term, while the HOP contribution is a Fermi sea term that arises from differing group velocities between bands, as
\partial_{k_{b}}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}\propto\partial_{k_{b}}\varepsilon_{mn}/\hbar=v_{mm}-v_{nn}
. The “injection current” term
\sigma_{\mathrm{inj}}
also arises from differing group velocities, but is peaked instead on the
\omega_{2}=0
axis. This term plays an important role in the photovoltaic effect
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Parker et al. 2019
.

The remaining contributions arise from higher-order geometric quantities that only appear in the nonlinear response. The first is the
\sigma_{C}
term, named for its dependence on the quantum connection
C_{b;ac}^{nm}
, which contributes on the diagonal
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
. We dub the remaining contribution the “three-band” (3B) term, as it is present only in systems with at least three bands. This term is proportional to a gauge-invariant sum of triple products of Berry connections, and, while the locations of its peaks depend on the details of the band structure, this term vanishes on the diagonal
Sup
. Although the three-band term does not have a known geometric interpretation in general, it simplifies to the quantum torsion on the
\omega_{1}
axis, defined as
T^{mn}_{a;cb}=C^{mn}_{a;cb}-C^{mn}_{b;ca}

Ahn et al. 2021
;
Jankowski and Slager 2024
. In this limit, the three-band term is is already well studied as part of the shift current
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016
;
Chaudhary et al. 2022
;
Avdoshkin et al. 2025
;
Cook et al. 2017
, so we focus on
\sigma_{C}
for the remainder of this work.

We can isolate
\sigma_{C}
in the 2DCS response by studying the diagonal components of the conductivity tensor,
\sigma^{a;aa},
and enforcing time-reversal symmetry, in which case all contributions vanish except for
\sigma_{C}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
. By further specifying to the diagonal of the frequency plane, along which
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
vanishes
Sup
, we can uniquely identify the
\sigma_{C}
contribution to the second-order optical conductivity. We further note that the real part of
\sigma_{C}
is odd under time-reversal symmetry, so the response actually arises only from the imaginary part of the quantum connection, which we denote as
\tilde{\Gamma}.

In short, the diagonal components of the second-order conductivity reduce under time-reversal symmetry to the form

\sigma^{a;aa}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}f_{nm}\varepsilon_{nm}\tilde{\Gamma}^{nm}_{a;aa},

(8)

thus providing a direct probe of the quantum connection.

Two-dimensional coherent spectroscopy.—

In a 2DCS experiment, a sample is illuminated by two strong electric field pulses separated by a delay time
\tau
, and the induced current in the sample is subsequently measured a further time delay
t
after the second pulse. We refer to the first and second pulses as A and B, respectively. The nonlinear part of the induced current,
j^{a}_{\mathrm{NL}}
, can be isolated by subtracting off the response obtained when only the A or B pulse is applied. Taking the ideal limit where the pulses are delta functions, the incoming electric field is given by
{\boldsymbol{{E}}}(t)={\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{\mathrm{A}}\delta(t+\tau)+{\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{\mathrm{B}}\delta(t)
, and the nonlinear induced current at second-order is given by

j^{a}_{2,\mathrm{NL}}(t)=\sum_{bc}E_{A}^{c}E_{B}^{b}\sigma^{a;bc}(\tau,t).

(9)

A second-order 2DCS experiment is therefore a direct measurement of the second-order conductivity in the time domain. Fourier transforming in both time variables yields the frequency dependent conductivity
\sigma^{a;bc}(\omega_{\tau},\omega_{t})
, and thus 2DCS probes the second-order response with
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(\omega_{\tau},\omega_{t})
.

Discussion.—

In this work, we have addressed two important challenges in the field of quantum geometry: identifying physical phenomena that depend on quantum geometry and devising schemes to directly measure geometric quantities.
To address the first challenge, we clarified how the second-order optical conductivity depends on the quantum geometry, decomposing it into distinct geometric contributions. We utilized this decomposition to address the second challenge by identifying conditions under which the second-order conductivity arises solely from the quantum connection, providing a means to measure it. We illustrated this with model calculations, showing how 2DCS can probe the sign structure and momentum dependence of the quantum connection.

These examples illustrated how
\sigma_{C}
can be isolated in the 2DCS response. However, the generic 2DCS response contains additional geometric information in the remaining terms of (
7
) that we made to vanish by choosing specific polarizations and enforcing time-reversal symmetry. Because these terms lie along different axes in the two-dimensional frequency space, it is possible to distinguish these distinct geometric contributions without forcing the rest to vanish. As such, 2DCS allows one to measure multiple geometric aspects of the conductivity with a single experiment.

Some important future work remains to be done. We considered non-interacting systems, but understanding the role of quantum geometry in the nonlinear conductivity of interacting systems is an important next step. Constructing a geometric decomposition of the third-order conductivity is another natural future step, as the third-order response does not require inversion symmetry breaking, making it relevant to vastly more materials.

Appendix A
Second-order optical conductivity in the length gauge

Following the approach of Aversa and Sipe
Aversa and Sipe 1995
, here we derive the nonlinear optical conductivity in the length gauge. Further details regarding this derivation can be found in several recent clarifying works
Ventura et al. 2017
;
Parker et al. 2019
. As introduced in the main text, the system is described by the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the Hamiltonian of the unperturbed electronic system and the perturbing electric field is included through the electric dipole term
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t)=e{\boldsymbol{{r}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{E}}}(t)
. The state of the system is captured by the density matrix
\rho=\sum_{\nu}p_{\nu}|\phi_{\nu}\rangle\langle\phi_{\nu}|
, where
\nu
labels the eigenstates of
\mathcal{H}
and
p_{\nu}
is the probability that the system is in the state
\nu
. We assume that the system is initially described by the equilibrium density matrix
\rho_{0}
in the limit
t\to-\infty
. In the interaction picture, the density matrix evolves according to

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho(t)=[\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t),\rho(t)].

Other operators in the interaction picture time-evolve according to
S(t)=e^{i\mathcal{H}_{0}t/\hbar}Se^{-i\mathcal{H}_{0}t/\hbar}
. In this work, we consider non-interacting systems, allowing us to work in a single particle picture.

We solve for optical conductivity by writing down the induced current,
j^{a}(t)=-e\tr\left(v^{a}(t)\rho(t)\right)
. To determine the
n
-th order conductivity, we expand both the current and density matrix in powers of the field according to
j^{a}(t)=\sum_{i}j_{i}^{a}(t)
and
\rho(t)=\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(t)
, where
j_{i}^{a}
and
\rho_{i}
are
i
-th order in
{\boldsymbol{{E}}}
. By matching powers of the field, we find
j_{i}^{a}(t)=-e\tr\left(v^{a}(t)\rho_{i}(t)\right)
.

The equation of motion for the density matrix can be solved order by order, yielding:

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-ie}{\hbar}\sum_{a}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}[r^{a}(t_{1}),\rho_{0}]E^{a}(t_{1})

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{ab}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{2}}dt_{1}[r^{a}(t_{2}),[r^{b}(t_{1}),\rho_{0}]]E^{a}(t_{2})E^{b}(t_{1}).

The first- and second-order currents are consequently given by

\displaystyle j_{1}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{b}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\tr\left(v^{a}(t)[r^{b}(t_{1}),\rho_{0}]\right)E^{b}(t_{1})

(10)

\displaystyle j_{2}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{bc}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\tr\left(v^{a}(t)[r^{b}(t_{2}),[r^{c}(t_{1}),\rho_{0}]]\right)E^{b}(t_{2})E^{c}(t_{1})

(11)

We proceed by making a change of variables, introducing new time arguments corresponding to time differences:
\tau_{m}=t_{m+1}-t_{m}
, with
t_{n}:=t
for the
n
-th order expression:

\displaystyle j_{1}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{b}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}\tr\left([v^{a}(t),r^{b}(t-\tau_{1})]\rho_{0}\right)E^{b}(t-\tau_{1})

\displaystyle j_{2}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{bc}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau_{1}\tr\left([v^{a}(t),r^{b}(t-\tau_{2})],r^{c}(t-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})]\rho_{0}\right)E^{b}(t-\tau_{2})E^{c}(t-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}).

The first- and second-order optical conductivities are defined according to

\displaystyle j_{1}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\int d\tau_{1}\sigma^{a;b}(\tau_{1})E^{b}(t-\tau_{1})

\displaystyle j_{2}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{bc}\int d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\sigma^{a;bc}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})E^{b}(t-\tau_{2})E^{c}(t-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})

which allows one to read off

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;b}(\tau_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar}\theta(\tau_{1})\tr\left([v^{a}(\tau_{1}),r^{b}]\rho_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;bc}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\theta(\tau_{1})\theta(\tau_{2})\tr\left([v^{a}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}),r^{b}(\tau_{1})],r^{c}]\rho_{0}\right)

where we have used time-translation invariance to shift the time arguments in the trace.

We now evaluate the commutators appearing in the response functions. First we need the matrix elements of the position operator in the Bloch basis
Ventura et al. 2017
;
Blount 1962

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|r^{a}|\psi_{m{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}}\rangle=(2\pi)^{d}\left[-i\delta_{nm}\partial_{k^{\prime}_{a}}\delta({\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})+\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\delta({\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})\right]

The singular intraband part of the matrix element presents a challenge for performing explicit calculations. However, we can circumvent this issue by instead considering commutators between the position operator and
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
- diagonal operators of the form
\langle\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|S|\psi_{m{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}}\rangle=(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})S_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|[r^{a},S]|\psi_{m{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}}\rangle=(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})[r^{a},S({\boldsymbol{{k}}})]_{nm}

\displaystyle[r^{a},S({\boldsymbol{{k}}})]_{nm}=i\partial_{k_{a}}S_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})+[\mathcal{A}^{a}({\boldsymbol{{k}}}),S({\boldsymbol{{k}}})]_{nm}.

(12)

Time evolving the position operator in the interaction picture yields the related identity
Parker et al. 2019

\displaystyle[r^{a}(\tau),S({\boldsymbol{{k}}})]_{nm}

\displaystyle=(i\partial_{k_{a}}+\tau\partial_{k_{a}}\varepsilon_{nm}/{\hbar})S_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{l}\left[e^{i\varepsilon_{nl}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tau/\hbar}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nl}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})S_{lm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})-S_{nl}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\mathcal{A}^{a}_{lm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})e^{i\varepsilon_{lm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tau/\hbar}\right]

\displaystyle v_{nm}^{a}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\delta_{nm}\partial_{k_{a}}\varepsilon_{n}+i\varepsilon_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}\right)

[\rho_{0}]_{nm}=\delta_{nm}f_{n}

is the Fermi distribution function. Meanwhile, the trace is taken over the single particle Hilbert space, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;b}(\omega_{1})=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{k}\bigg\{\sum_{n}f_{n}\frac{\partial_{k_{b}}\partial_{k_{a}}\varepsilon_{n}}{\omega_{1}}+\sum_{nm}f_{nm}\varepsilon_{mn}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{b}_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{mn}}{\omega_{1}+\omega_{mn}}\bigg\}

Appendix C
Isolating the quantum connection term

In time reversal symmetric systems,
\varepsilon_{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\varepsilon_{n}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
and
\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{a}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
. It follows that
f_{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=f_{n}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}}),Q^{mn}_{ab}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=[Q^{mn}_{ab}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})]^{*}
, and
C_{a;bc}^{mn}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=-[C_{a;bc}^{mn}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})]^{*}
. We next note that
Q^{mn}_{aa}=\mathcal{A}_{nm}^{a}\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{a}=|\mathcal{A}_{mn}^{a}|^{2}
is purely real, and is thus even under
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}\to-{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
. Under these conditions it is clear that
\sigma^{a;aa}_{\mathrm{Drude}},\sigma^{a;aa}_{\mathrm{an}},\sigma^{a;aa}_{\mathrm{DR}},\sigma^{a;aa}_{\mathrm{HOP}},
and
\sigma^{a;aa}_{\mathrm{inj}}
are integrals of functions odd in momentum and therefore vanish.

To distinguish the
\sigma_{C}
term from the
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
term, we note that that
\sigma_{C}
is peaked along the diagonal, while
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
is peaked at frequencies satisfying

(\hbar\omega_{1},\hbar\omega_{2})=(\varepsilon_{mn},\varepsilon_{ml})

(13)

or

(\hbar\omega_{1},\hbar\omega_{2})=(\varepsilon_{mn},\varepsilon_{ln}).

(14)

These conditions coincide with the frequency diagonal only when
\varepsilon_{l}=\varepsilon_{n}
or
\varepsilon_{l}=\varepsilon_{m}
. However, the 3B term vanishes for degenerate bands. Explicitly, given
\varepsilon_{l}=\varepsilon_{n}
, the first part of the 3B term in the
a=b=c
limit is of the form

\displaystyle\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}g_{ml}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ln}

\displaystyle\to\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{mn}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ln}

\displaystyle=-\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{-\omega_{1}}f_{nm}(\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm})^{*}g_{mn}^{-\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{mn}(\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ln})^{*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{nm}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{mn}g_{nm}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{mn}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{lm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nl}

\displaystyle=-\frac{ie^{3}}{\hbar^{3}}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{1}}f_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}g_{mn}^{\omega_{2}}\varepsilon_{mn}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{ln}

where we have used the fact that
\sigma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\sigma^{*}(-\omega_{1},-\omega_{2})
. A similar argument holds for the second term in the case where
\varepsilon_{l}=\varepsilon_{m}
. Thus, we have shown that
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
vanishes along the diagonal. Furthermore, in our examples, the conditions (
13
) and (
14
) ensure the 3B term is peaked only far from the diagonal and at large frequencies, and so will be numerically small in the vicinity of the diagonal. Therefore we can unambiguously identify the 2DCS signal
\sigma^{a;aa}(\omega,\omega)
as arising from the quantum connection.

Appendix D
Projector formalism

The non-Abelian Berry connection
\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}
is not invariant under the gauge transformation
|\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle\to e^{i\theta_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}}|\psi_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle
, making numerical calculations inconvenient. However, geometric quantities like the quantum geometric tensor and quantum connection can be expressed in a gauge-invariant way using the projector formalism
Avdoshkin et al. 2025
;
Mitscherling et al. 2025
. We introduce the projector onto the
n
-th band,

P_{n}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=|u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle\langle u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|,

(15)

in terms of which the multi-band QGT and quantum connection can be written as follows:

Q_{ab}^{mn}=-\tr\left(P_{n}(\partial_{k_{a}}P_{m})(\partial_{k_{b}}P_{n})\right)

(16)

C_{a;bc}^{mn}=-\tr\left(P_{n}(\partial_{k_{b}}P_{m})[(\partial_{k_{a}}\partial_{k_{c}}P_{n})+(\partial_{k_{a}}P_{m})(\partial_{k_{c}}P_{n})]\right),

(17)

where here the trace is over the cell-periodic states at a single
{\boldsymbol{{k}}}
point:
\tr(\cdot)=\sum_{n}\langle u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}|\cdot|u_{n{\boldsymbol{{k}}}}\rangle

The triple products of Berry connections appearing in the
\sigma_{\mathrm{3B}}
term can also be written using projectors via

\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{b}_{ml}\mathcal{A}^{c}_{ln}=-i\tr(P_n (\partial_{k_a} P_m)(\partial_{k_b} P_l)(\partial_{k_c} P_n))

(18)

where
m\neq n\neq l
. We have adopted the sign convention of
Ahn et al. 2021
, which differs by a minus sign when compared with
Avdoshkin et al. 2025
. We employ the projector formalism throughout this work for performing the numerical calculations.

Appendix E
Transition metal dichalcogenide model

Here we provide details on the three band tight-binding TMD models used as examples in our work. We include the Hamiltonians and parameters here for reference, and refer the reader to the original source for further details
Liu et al. 2013
.

These models are constructed by considering the symmetry-allowed hopping terms between the relevant
d_{z^{2}},d_{xy}
, and
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
orbitals of the transition metal ions. If only nearest-neighbour (NN) hoppings are considered, the Hamiltonian takes the form

H_{\mathrm{TMD}}^{\mathrm{NN}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\begin{bmatrix}h_{0}&h_{1}&h_{2}\\
h_{1}^{*}&h_{11}&h_{12}\\
h_{2}^{*}&h_{12}*&h_{22}\end{bmatrix}

with the matrix elements given by

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=2t_{0}(\cos 2\alpha+2\cos\alpha\cos\beta)+\epsilon_{1},

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{3}t_{2}\sin\alpha\sin\beta+2it_{1}(\sin 2\alpha+\sin\alpha\cos\beta),

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle=2t_{2}(\cos 2\alpha-\cos\alpha\cos\beta)+2\sqrt{3}it_{1}\cos\alpha\sin\beta,

\displaystyle h_{11}

\displaystyle=2t_{11}\cos 2\alpha+(t_{11}+3t_{22})\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\epsilon_{2},

\displaystyle h_{22}

\displaystyle=2t_{22}\cos 2\alpha+(3t_{11}+t_{22})\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\epsilon_{2},

\displaystyle h_{12}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}(t_{22}-t_{11})\sin\alpha\sin\beta+4it_{12}\sin\alpha(\cos\alpha-\cos\beta),

where we have defined

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)=\left(\frac{k_{x}}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{2}\right).

The parameters of the model are determined by fitting the bands to first principles calculations. For the first example in our work, we use the parameters given in Table
S1
, which result from fitting to the MoTe
2
generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) band structure.

The accuracy of the three band model is improved by considering up to third-nearest neighbour (TNN) hoppings. This is the case considered in our second example, where we compare MoTe
2
GGA, WTe
2
GGA, and MoTe
2
LDA. The Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\mathrm{TMD}}^{\mathrm{TNN}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\begin{bmatrix}V_{0}&V_{1}&V_{2}\\
V_{1}^{*}&V_{11}&V_{12}\\
V_{2}^{*}&V_{12}*&V_{22}\end{bmatrix},

with matrix elements

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{1}+2t_{0}(2\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\cos 2\alpha)+2r_{0}(2\cos 3\alpha\cos\beta+\cos 2\beta)+2u_{0}(2\cos 2\alpha\cos 2\beta+\cos 4\alpha),

\displaystyle\real V_{1}

Finally, we provide the model and further details regarding the Sr
2
RuO
4
model introduced in the main text. The three band model is derived by considering the
t_{2g}

d
orbitals of Ru ions on a square lattice, considering both kinetic and spin-orbit coupling terms
Puetter and Kee 2012
. In the
(c^{yz\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{{k}}}\sigma},c^{xz\dagger}_{vk\sigma},c^{xy\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{{k}}},-\sigma})
basis, the Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\mathrm{SR}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}},\sigma)=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{yz}&\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\mathrm{1d}}+i\sigma\lambda&-\sigma\lambda\\
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\mathrm{1d}}-i\sigma\lambda&\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{xz}&i\lambda\\
-\sigma\lambda&-i\lambda&\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{xy}\end{bmatrix},

with dispersions

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{yz/xz}=-2t_{1}\cos k_{y/x}-2t_{2}\cos k_{x/y}-\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{xy}=-2t_{3}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})-4t_{4}\cos k_{x}\cos k_{y}-\mu_{2}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{{k}}}^{\mathrm{1d}}=-4t_{5}\sin k_{x}\sin k_{y}.

This Hamiltonian is inversion symmetric, and thus the second-order optical response vanishes. In order to break inversion symmetry in the model, we introduce the following perturbation:

H_{\mathrm{SR}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\begin{bmatrix}0&2it_{5}\delta e^{ik_{x}}\sin k_{y}&0\\
-2it_{5}\delta e^{-ik_{x}}\sin k_{y}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

After adding this perturbation, the bands undergo a small spin splitting, and thus the three band model is really a six band model. As a result, we must add spin labels to all the relevant quantities:
\varepsilon_{n}\to\varepsilon_{n\sigma},\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm}\to\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}:=i\langle u_{n\sigma}|\partial_{k_{a}}u_{m\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle
. However, since the Hamiltonian is block diagonal in
\sigma
, the non-Abelian Berry connection vanishes if
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
. Therefore to correct the expression for the relevant conductivity (c.f. (
8
) in the main text), we simply need to add a single spin label:

\sigma^{a;aa}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=ie^{3}\int_{k}\sum_{nm,\sigma}g_{mn,\sigma}^{\omega_{1}}g_{mn,\sigma}^{\omega_{2}}f_{nm,\sigma}\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\sigma}

where
g_{mn,\sigma}^{\omega}
and
f_{mn,\sigma}
depend on
\varepsilon_{mn,\sigma}=\varepsilon_{m\sigma}-\varepsilon_{n\sigma}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\sigma}=\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{A}^{a}_{mn,\sigma}(\partial_{k_{a}}\mathcal{A}_{nm,\sigma}^{a}-i(\mathcal{A}_{nn,\sigma}^{a}-\mathcal{A}^{a}_{mm,\sigma})\mathcal{A}_{nm,\sigma}^{a})
. Including spin, time-reversal symmetry enforces
\varepsilon_{n,\sigma}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\varepsilon_{n,-\sigma}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
and
\mathcal{A}^{a}_{nm,\sigma}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\mathcal{A}^{a}_{mn,-\sigma}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
, which in turn enforce
f_{n\sigma}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=f_{n,-\sigma}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}}),g_{mn,\sigma}^{\omega}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=g_{mn,-\sigma}^{\omega}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\sigma}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})=\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,-\sigma}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})
. Hence we can rewrite the conductivity as

\displaystyle\sigma^{a;aa}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=ie^{3}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{1}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{2}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})f_{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})+g_{mn,\downarrow}^{\omega_{1}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})g_{mn,\downarrow}^{\omega_{2}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})f_{nm,\downarrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\downarrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

\displaystyle=ie^{3}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{1}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{2}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})f_{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})+g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{1}}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{2}}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})f_{nm,\uparrow}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\uparrow}(-{\boldsymbol{{k}}})

\displaystyle=2ie^{3}\int_{k}\sum_{nm}g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{1}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})g_{mn,\uparrow}^{\omega_{2}}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})f_{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})\tilde{\Gamma}_{a;aa}^{nm,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{{k}}})

Therefore it is clear we can work exclusively with the three
\uparrow
bands, which is what is done in the main text, where the spin label has been suppressed, and the extra factor of 2 has been included in calculations. The parameters used for this model are given below in Table
