At eye level
Authors: Kendra Hebert
Published: 2026

You are finishing the first week of a two-week elective in a hospital you had never seen before you arrived.

The day begins outside. The ramp leading to the main entrance rises too sharply, the incline subtle enough to pass inspection, steep enough to demand effort. You register instantly, the way you always do. The concrete is pale, smooth, and pocked just enough to steal momentum. Halfway up, your grip slips. Not dramatically. Just enough to remind you that gravity has opinions, and none of them are neutral.

“Here, I’ve got you,” someone says behind you.

Hands land on the back of your chair before you can answer. The frame jolts forward. Your balance shifts. For a brief, sharp second, your body is no longer yours.

“Please don’t,” you say, louder than intended.

They apologize, appearing flustered and embarrassed. “I was just trying to help.”

“I know,” you say, because that is easier than explaining consent, or autonomy, or how unsolicited help turns bodies into common property. They step back. You finish the ramp yourself, arms burning now, breath measured and controlled. By the time you reach the doors, you have already rearranged your face into something neutral, something clinically appropriate.

Inside, the hospital exhales white light, blue light, reflected light. The automatic doors hesitate, stall just long enough to make you wonder if you have misjudged your position, as if they are assessing whether you qualify for entry. Your badge works on the second swipe. The security desk rises above you, a smooth wall of laminated authority. You can’t see the guard’s mouth unless they lean forward. They do. They smile.

Everyone here is kind.

That has never been the problem.

You chose this elective carefully. The large academic centre had a strong reputation. When you asked about accessibility, the response came quickly, confidently: “Yes, absolutely. Very accessible.” The phrase landed with the solidity of infrastructure, not the fragility of reassurance.

You arrive early every morning because unfamiliar places demand rehearsal. You need time to learn where the automatic doors falter, which badge readers require a particular angle, which corridors narrow without warning, and which thresholds catch your wheels. Early gives you the privacy to adapt without witnesses.

By Wednesday, you stopped getting lost trying to find unmapped accessible routes.

By today, Friday, you are exhausted from what that requires.

You are a fourth-year medical student, which means you are expected to be competent but invisible, confident but deferential. You remind yourself of this as you enter the workroom. It is smaller than you remember. Chairs crowd the walls, legs splayed outward. Someone has slipped an overnight bag under the table, soft-sided but immovable. You calculate clearances, nudge a chair just enough to pass, then take your place at the far end, least likely to obstruct the flow of able bodies.

No one comments.

This is success.

Rounds begin. The team stands. You position yourself half a step back, where you can see faces without being in anyone’s way. This posture is deeply familiar: present but peripheral, included but never central.

The attending teaches while walking. You were warned about this on your first day.

“We move fast here,” someone said admiringly, as though velocity were aspirational.

You follow, adjusting rhythm and force, learning when to advance and when to let the group drift ahead so you can rejoin later. The hallway narrows. It always does. The team compresses instinctively, shoulders brushing, bodies negotiating space without thought, then expands again on the other side. You slow, then surge, arms burning, the kind of exertion that does not count as fatigue, but as functioning. The conversation has advanced without you when you catch up. You nod anyway.

At the bedside, there is no place for you unless one is made; no one does. You stop near the doorframe, careful not to block egress. The patient looks at you first.

This happens often.

There is a moment of recalibration: eyes flick to your chair, then return to your face. You introduce yourself. You ask your question. The patient answers you completely, expansively, as if relieved to speak to someone who does not loom over them. You write quickly. When the attending asks for your thoughts, you offer them. They nod. The group moves on.

Everything accelerates again between rooms. Teaching happens in transit, layered over motion. You learn to listen to the conversation occurring in front of you, catching fragments as they trail behind: electrolytes, imaging, tentative plans, assembling meaning from echoes.

This is not exclusion. It is called efficiency.

The call room’s bathroom is labelled as accessible. Inside, the walls are bare. No grab bars. Accessibility here means only that the door opens wide. You steady yourself briefly against the sink, porcelain cold beneath your palm. You think about falling. You decide not to.

At noon teaching, the room fills until it hums with bodies. Chairs scrape. Someone drags one into the aisle, reducing it by half. You station yourself near the door. The slides are projected too high. You tilt your head back uncomfortably, then abandon the image and listen instead.

Afterward, someone says it must be nice to sit during rounds.

You smile.

You do not correct them.

In the afternoon, a patient asks if you are the social worker.

You tell them no, gently. You say you are a medical student. The patient apologizes. You say it’s fine.

Later, you are sent to another floor to follow up on a consult. You plan the route carefully. The nearest elevator bears a sign: temporarily unavailable. The paper is curled at the edges, taped over an older notice beneath it. You have learned what this means. Not broken, exactly, just not prioritized. You take the long way, weaving through corridors, passing completely useless stairwells that announce themselves proudly.

When you reach the unit, the nursing station counter rises above you like a barrier reef. You wait. Eventually, someone notices and leans forward to speak to you. You keep your voice even, deliberately unhurried, though you know the team upstairs has already moved on.

When you return, rounds are over.

You slide back into the workroom.

No one comments.

This is also a success.

Late in the day, you see a new admission. The chart flags mobility concerns, vague and nonspecific. They are returning from the washroom as you enter. You introduce yourself. The patient’s eyes move from your chair to your face.

“Do you mind if I sit?” they ask, gesturing to the bed.

You smile. “Of course.”

They sit. You are at eye level now. The conversation unfolds without haste. They speak about stairs that exclude them, bathrooms that require improvisation, and the fatigue of planning every movement. You listen. You do not disclose anything about yourself. You do not need to. Recognition settles quietly between you, unspoken and complete.

At the end of the day, the attending thanks the team. They look at you.

“You’re doing really well,” they say.

You nod. You thank them. You wonder what “well” signifies here. That you kept pace? That you absorbed inconvenience without complaint and asked nothing of a system already certain it had done enough?

You log out. You reverse carefully from the desk.

Near the exit, a sign reads, “This facility is committed to accessibility.”

The font is clean. The message is unambiguous.

Nothing here has prevented you from learning medicine.

Nothing here has made it easier either.

Outside, the ramp waits, steeper than it should be, just compliant enough to avoid scrutiny. You manage it, as you always do. At the bottom, you pause, hands resting on the wheels, before turning toward a place that is not home.

Already rehearsing and adjusting, and preparing to do it all again next week.

Because in medicine, bodies like yours are accommodated by endurance, not design. And endurance, you have learned, is expected to be quiet.
