Rare Secondary Neoplasms Arising in Epidermal Nevus: A Case Series and Literature Review
Authors: Haya A. Homsi, Melissa Piliang, Jennifer Lucas, Jennifer S. Ko, Steven D. Billings, Shira Ronen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalignant transformation is known to occur with many nevi, such as nevus sebaceus. However, cases of secondary tumors developing in an epidermal nevus (EN) are rare, with only a few case reports documented in the literature. We present three unique cases of syringocystadenoma papilliferum (SCAP), syringofibroadenoma, trichilemmoma, and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) arising in EN. The first case is a 40‐year‐old female who presented with a verrucal plaque extending from mons pubis to the left lateral thigh since birth. Histologic examination revealed EN with areas of syringofibroadenoma, SCAP, and BCC. The second case is a 31‐year‐old male who had EN since birth on his left axilla, trunk, and buttock. Over time, several verrucous pink nodules developed on these areas. Microscopic evaluation of these nodules demonstrated characteristic features of SCAP and trichilemmoma. The third case is a 29‐year‐old female who developed a suspicious flesh‐colored papule within a pre‐existent EN on her left knee. Upon excision, the papule was diagnosed as a trichilemmoma. The occurrence of epithelial/adnexal tumors in EN remains elusive as to whether these lesions are clonally related or simply independent tumors that have coincidentally collided. We highlight the importance of considering the development of secondary neoplasms in the setting of EN, underscoring the need for regular patient follow‐up.

Introduction

Epidermal nevi (EN) are developmental abnormalities characterized by excessive proliferation of the epidermis, often accompanied by unusual terminal differentiation [1]. These malformations manifest as an overgrowth of keratinocytes, which in some instances, exhibit abnormal maturation, leading to a visible lesion with various clinical patterns. The incidence rate is approximately 1 in every 1000 live newborns [2]. The lesions are usually present but not invariably at birth as yellowish‐brown papillomatosis hyperkeratotic papules or plaques. They commonly affect the trunk, neck, and extremities, typically follow a Blaschkoid distribution, suggesting genetic mosaicism in the epidermis resulting from mutations in early embryonic stages of development [2]. The three main clinical subtypes include nevus verrucosus (also known as verrucous epidermal nevus [VEN], or linear epidermal nevus), nevus unius lateris, and ichthyosis hystrix. VEN is characterized by solitary or multiple localized lesions, while nevus unius lateris refers to a more severe unilateral linear distribution [1]. Ichthyosis hystrix, on the other hand, is used to describe cases of extensive bilateral or generalized distribution. EN can be associated with a number of syndromes, such as Proteus syndrome, CHILD (congenital hemidysplasia with ichthyosiform nevus and limb defects) syndrome, and CLOVE (congenital lipomatous overgrowth, vascular malformations, and EN) syndrome [3].

The characteristic histological changes include hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis [2]. There is a thickening of the granular layer and often a slight increase in basal melanin pigment. Rarely, secondary neoplasms can occur in the setting of EN. The majority of these secondary neoplasms are typically squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) [4, 5]. Adnexal tumors arising from EN are exceptionally rare, particularly the simultaneous occurrence of more than one type [6, 7]. We present three unique EN diagnosed in our institute, each with a different associated neoplasm, in addition to a literature review. To the best of our knowledge, we report the first case of syringofibroadenoma occurring within an EN in the English literature.

Case Reports

Case 1: EN With Syringofibroadenoma, Syrinocystadenoma Papilliferum (SCAP), and BCC

A 40‐year‐old female presented with a large hyperpigmented and Blaschkoidian verrucal plaque that extended from the mons pubis to the left lateral anterior thigh. This EN had been present since birth, but recently, the patient noticed multiple new lesions within the EN that were previously diagnosed as condyloma acuminatum (Figure 1A). As the lesions grew, reaching 4.0 × 3.0 cm with no response to medical management, the patient elected surgical excision.

Histologic sections demonstrated compact hyperkeratosis overlying an epidermis with acanthosis, papillomatosis, and hypergranulosis, consistent with the clinical impression of EN (Figure 1B,C). The nevus displayed areas with multiple cords of epithelial cells in a fenestrating pattern, intertwining with each other with ductal structures. The stroma exhibited loose fibrillary collagen containing mucin and lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate. These findings were interpreted as syringofibroadenoma developing in EN (Figure 1D). Additionally, an endophytic crateriform lesion was noted to be connected to the epidermis and composed of two or more layers of cells forming dilated papillary and cystic structures, with numerous plasma cells in the stroma. These findings were consistent with SCAP (Figure 1E). Moreover, islands of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading, tumor‐stroma retraction artifact, and scattered mitotic figures were observed, consistent with BCC arising in EN (Figure 1F,G). This tumor was diffusely positive for Ber‐EP4 (Figure 1H).

Case 2: EN With SCAP, Trichilemmoma, and Poroma

A 31‐year‐old male with multiple EN on his left axilla, trunk, and buttock since birth. Over the years, he noticed several hypertrophic/verrucous pink nodules appearing at different locations of the EN (Figure 2A–C). A microscopic examination of one of the lateral lesions from the axilla showed a well‐defined, endophytic proliferation of uniform poroid cells extending from the epidermis to the dermis with a vascularized fibrocollagenous stroma. There were also scattered ductal structures. Significant cytologic atypia, mitotic figures, or necrosis were not identified. These findings are consistent with a poroma arising in an EN (Figure 2D,E). Further microscopic examination of the lesions from the axilla, back, abdomen, and buttocks showed SCAP developing within the EN (Figure 2F–H). A biopsy of the lesion on the elbow showed a lobular downgrowth of squamoid to clear cells with peripheral palisading, surrounded by a thick basement membrane, consistent with a trichilemmoma arising in an EN (Figure 2I).

Case 3: EN With Desmoplastic Trichilemmoma

A 29‐year‐old female with EN on her left knee and right groin area. She reported noticing a small, flesh‐colored papule that had become painful. The excision of this lesion showed a dermal proliferation of squamoid cells originating from the epidermis and extending into the dermis. The lesional cells have eosinophilic to clear cytoplasm with peripheral palisading and a conspicuous eosinophilic basement membrane. At the center of the lesion, the tumor comprises cords of cells embedded within a collagenous to desmoplastic stroma consistent with the diagnosis of desmoplastic trichilemmoma (Figure 3A–C).

Literature Review

A review of relevant articles from 1936 to 2023 identified 53 cases of EN with secondary neoplasms, which are summarized in Table 1. There are 17 cases of BCC and 21 cases of SCC, 6 of which were reported as keratoacanthoma (KA). Two additional cases showed both SCC and BCC and 1 case was diagnosed as KA and BCC. Adnexal tumors within EN included 3 cases of SCAP, 2 cases of porocarcinoma, 1 case of trichilemmal cyst, 1 case of papillary apocrine adenoma, and 1 unclassified adnexal carcinoma. Additional individual cases of various lesions arising from EN were reported as: clear cell acanthoma, hemangioma simplex, and seborrheic keratosis. The trunks and extremities were the most common locations for the secondary tumors, mirroring the most common location of EN. Appearance of a new nodule was the common presenting complaint. Seven patients showed multiple lesions at presentation, six of which were diagnosed as BCC and one as SCC.

Discussion

While the onset of various skin tumors is frequently observed in nevus sebaceus (NS), the occurrence of skin tumors in EN is extremely rare [8]. Our case series identified multiple neoplasms arising from EN, including SCAP, syringofibroadenoma, BCC, trichilemmoma, poroma, and desmoplastic trichilemmoma. Upon examination of our cases, we did not observe any abnormalities in hair follicles, apocrine glands, or sebaceous differentiation that might indicate the presence of non‐keratinocytic nevi. The origin of these secondary tumors remains unclear; they may either originate directly from cells of the epidermal nevi, suggesting a clonal relationship with the nevus, or potentially develop from other structures, such as hair follicles or sebaceous, eccrine, or apocrine glands associated with the EN [6]. In the latter scenario, these observations would merely indicate a collision of two independent lesions. However, some argue that the genetic instability of EN, which includes chromosomal breakage and variegated translocation mosaicism, leads to distorted physiologic relationships with the coexisting adnexal structures, creating an environment that promotes their disorganized proliferation [6]. Two previous reports have documented the simultaneous development of poroma and porocarcinoma in two EN cases, suggesting a possible correlation between these two lesions [6]. A single case of syringocystadenocarcinoma papilliferum, a rare entity with only 37 cases reported in the literature, was reported arising in an EN. Additionally, the recurrent development of SCC/BCC in EN, rather than normal skin, may suggest an increased susceptibility of EN to tumor formation [4, 9, 10]. Interestingly, a patient with EN on her left leg developed 47 BCCs within the nevus over a span of 5 years. Gorlin syndrome was considered, but genetic testing confirmed no pathological mutations in the PTCH1 gene [9]. To avoid future recurrence, particularly when there is an increased number of BCCs in EN, the excision of EN has been recommended [11]. Regular follow‐up after excision is advised to promptly identify any residual tumor, recurrence, or further spread [12].

Several large studies have demonstrated that some cases previously reported as BCC arising in NS may, in fact, represent trichoblastomas (TB) [13]. Given the findings from these studies, one might consider our tumor to represent TB. These two neoplasms can be differentiated primarily based on histologic features, with immunohistochemistry serving as a helpful adjunct in some studies [14, 15]. For example, BCC typically shows greater staining for BCL2, p53, Ber‐EP4, and Ki‐67, along with loss of intratumor Merkel cells detected by CK20, which are usually numerous in TB [15, 16]. Additionally, stromal CD10 expression favors the diagnosis of TB [17]. However, in our case, the presence of key histologic features, including tumor‐stroma retraction artifact, prominent peripheral palisading, absence of fibrotic stroma and papillary mesenchymal bodies, and diffuse positivity for Ber‐EP4, strongly supports a diagnosis of BCC. Although less common, BCC has been definitively identified in some cases of NS. For instance, one study observed loss of heterozygosity of the PTCH gene—known to play a role in the development of BCC‐ in 40% of the NS specimens analyzed [18].

While our literature review aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of reported cases of secondary neoplasms arising in EN, we acknowledge the limitations of prior studies and the potential for misdiagnosis, particularly in distinguishing BCC from TB or EN from NS. Although we cannot independently verify the accuracy of these reports, we hope that the peer‐review processes of the journals mitigate such errors. Our goal was to include all reported cases to provide a broad perspective on the spectrum of secondary neoplasms documented associated with EN.

The genetic bases of multiple types of EN have been studied. Mosaicism, defined as the presence of two or more genetically distinct cell populations within an individual, has been strongly linked to EN. Approximately 40% of ENs harbor mosaic mutations in KRAS or HRAS, with HRAS p.(G13R) substitution representing a new hotspot mutation [19]. Mosaic mutations in FGFR3 or PIK3CA were detected in another 42% of these lesions [20]. The site and extent of the EN can vary depending on the timing and location of the genetic mutation during development [3]. In one study, molecular analysis of a BCC arising within an EN revealed a PIK3CA mutation in a biopsy that included both the BCC and adjacent EN tissue, while two additional biopsies from other EN regions lacked this mutation [21]. To enable a more precise comparison of the genomic profiles of the BCC and EN, the sample was microdissected, as complete separation of the two components was not feasible. The mutation signal was significantly stronger in the BCC‐enriched fraction compared to the EN fraction, suggesting that the BCC harbored the PIK3CA mutation. This study also raised the possibility of a shared but unidentified molecular alteration linking BCC and EN [21]. Another investigation compared the FGFR3 mutations between the histologically similar but with drastically different natural history: EN and seborrheic keratosis (SK) [20]. While both lesions shared the FGFR3 (R248C) substitution, SKs exhibited a broader range of mutations, whereas ENs displayed a more uniform mutational profile [20]. Additionally, next‐generation sequencing of a case with SK arising in an EN identified an identical HRAS (p.G13R) in both lesions, marking the first reported instance of this mutation being shared between an EN and a secondary tumor [22]. The HRAS (p.G13R) mutation was also detected in a case of SCAP arising in an EN of a patient with phacomatosis pigmentokeratotica [23]. The same mutation was further reported in a case of multiple SCCs arising in an EN, alongside another NOTCH1 (p.H688Y) mutation [24]. Both mutations were present in EN and in the associated SCCs, with SCCs further showing tumor suppressor gene mutations, like TP53 and RB1 [24].

To shed light on the molecular changes in these secondary neoplasms, we performed additional immunohistochemical staining in case 2. BRAF‐VE1 staining revealed positivity for BRAF V600E in SCAP, while the background EN tissue was negative (Figure 2H). Furthermore, NUT staining was performed on poroma and background EN, both of which were negative. These findings highlight the heterogeneous molecular profiles of secondary tumors arising in EN, suggesting that distinct molecular alterations may drive tumorigenesis in different neoplasms associated with EN. Future studies investigating these molecular differences may provide further insights into the pathogenesis of secondary tumors in EN.

Very few cases in the literature described the coexistence of more than one type of tumors in an EN [6, 7, 25]. Our first case demonstrated three tumors occurring simultaneously in the EN: SCAP, syringofibroadenoma, and BCC, while our second case involved the diagnosis of different tumors at various EN locations over different incidents. To the best of our knowledge, only 3 SCAP developing in EN have been described before and we present the first case of syringofibroadenoma within EN. Although the occurrence of a verruca vulgaris in EN has been described [25], this diagnosis should be made with caution. Due to its warty appearance, EN may be misdiagnosed, especially if it is present in the pelvic area, as condyloma acuminatum, like our first case. Dermatologists and dermatopathologists must differentiate this clinical and/or histological mimic based on additional information such as the duration of the lesion and/or the HPV status.

It was first thought that SCC or BCC arise in long standing and extensive EN lesions, but cases occurring in a singular lesion and young patients, as young as 17 years old, have been reported [26, 27, 28]. This contrasts with general cutaneous SCC or BCC, which seldom occurs in patients younger than 40 years of age. Factors that predispose EN to focal malignant transformation are not fully understood. Proposed factors include exposure to ultraviolet rays and chemicals [24]. Evidence suggests that persistent stimulation of chronic wounds may lead to malignancy through amplifying growth and repair factors. Similarly, local repetitive trauma, such as excision, electrocautery, nicks while shaving, and burns, can induce malignant changes in EN [12, 29]. For example, a BCC was developed in an abraded EN, and a SCC was diagnosed in a patient who shaved their EN [5]. In terms of prognosis of tumors in association with EN, one study found that metastatic rate was higher in patients with SCC arising in EN, compared to general cutaneous SCC [24]. This may be due to the presence of EN potentially obscuring the detection of the SCC lesion by the patient.

In conclusion, understanding that secondary tumors can sporadically and repeatedly develop in EN should guide and impact clinical decisions regarding surveillance or preventive measures. Patients should be encouraged to promptly report any new nodules or changes for clinical and histopathological assessment.
