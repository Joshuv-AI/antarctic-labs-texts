B. adolescentis alleviates inflammation and suppresses PGE2 through remodling gut microbiota to attenuate pulmonary fibrosis in aging mice
Authors: Jiali Ni, Fengjiao Wang, Yechen Wu, Jian Shen, Ziyuan Zhou, Lvwan Xu, Zhuoqi Lou, Yangfan Shen, Bo Qiu, Qiangqiang Xiang, Pengyu Yin, Yanfei Chen, Lanjuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global burden of pulmonary fibrosis is increasing. Recent studies have shown that some pulmonary fibrotic lesions caused by COVID-19 infection may persist for a long time. Emerging evidence suggested a critical association between gut microbiota and pulmonary fibrosis. In this study, the clinical follow-up data from post-COVID-19 patients indicated that those with higher CT image scores were older, had a significantly lower Blautia and Bifidobacterium to Streptococcus ratio (B/S index). We examined whether Bifidobacterium adolescentis could attenuate bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice, with particular attention in the aging mice. Aging mice exhibited more severe pulmonary fibrosis after BLM induction, while the intervention of B. adolescentis attenuated the degree of pulmonary fibrosis in aging mice to a state similar to that of young mice. B. adolescentis alleviated inflammatory responses by enhancing the gut barrier, and reduced fibrotic marker expression (TGF-β, IL-17, α-SMA, Collagen I/III) by modulating PPAR and Th17 signaling pathways. Furthermore, B. adolescentis stabilized gut microbiota and increased the abundance of Bifidobacterium, Turicibacter, and norank_f_Desulfovibrionaceae, thereby suppressed the prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and affected collagen deposition. B. adolescentis alleviates pulmonary fibrosis through the gut-lung axis by regulating PGE2/PPAR/Th17 signaling, providing a promising therapeutic approach for pulmonary fibrosis management.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

The global burden of pulmonary fibrosis is increasing. Genetic factors, aging, microbes (viruses, mycoplasma), or exposure to harmful substances may contribute to the development or progression of pulmonary fibrosis1. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most common form of pulmonary fibrosis, characterized by progressive scarring that leads to breathing difficulties and more prevalent among elderly2,3. Many interstitial lung diseases (ILD) can also progress to pulmonary fibrosis, such as ILD caused by rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis. The incidence rate varies from 1 to 31.5 cases per 100,000 person-years4,5. After the COVID-19 pandemic, approximately one-third of COVID-19 survivors exhibit long-term lung abnormalities, often accompanied by respiratory symptoms and impaired diffusion capacity6,7. Longitudinal studies reveal that post-COVID pulmonary fibrosis (PCPF) persists in many patients, with older age, male sex, and acute disease severity identified as key risk factors8–10.

The elderly are at high risk of developing pulmonary fibrosis, with IPF prevalence increasing from 4.0–75.2 (ages 18–34) to 227.2 cases/100,000 ( ≥ 75 years)10–12. Age-related airway narrowing, reduced alveolar tension, and diminished epithelial cells impair lung function and immunity13–15. Cellular aging leads to the formation of a pulmonary microenvironment that promotes inflammation and oxidation, and immune aging, such as macrophage aging, causes a decrease in the ability to recognize and eliminate pathogens, it increases the susceptibility to infections16–20. Furthermore, aging alters the composition of the extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to an accumulation of collagen, a decrease in elastin, and dysfunction of fibroblasts, thereby increasing the susceptibility of the lungs to fibrosis21,22.

Treatment options for pulmonary fibrosis remain limited. While nintedanib and pirfenidone are approved to slow disease progression, their therapeutic effects are modest23. Lung transplantation, though definitive, is costly and limited by donor availability and rejection risks24. Emerging evidence implicates the gut-lung axis in respiratory pathophysiology25,26. Patients with pulmonary fibrosis display reduced gut microbial diversity, marked by decreased short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)-producing bacteria and increased Actinobacteria and Clostridia27. Silicosis patients exhibit gut dysbiosis with significant reductions in Actinobacteria and Bacillota28. Systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary fibrosis is linked to microbiota imbalances, including depleted Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii but elevated Lactobacillus29. These findings underscore the gut microbiota’s potential as a therapeutic target for pulmonary fibrosis.

Bifidobacterium as a probiotic genus with immunomodulatory properties, mitigates lung injury by suppressing proinflammatory cytokines30–32. Influenza models demonstrate that Bifidobacterium longum administration improves survival rates while attenuating pathological cytokine responses33,34. Clinical studies of COVID-19 patients reveal an inverse correlation between Bifidobacterium abundance and disease severity, with markedly reduced levels observed in critical cases35–38. The protective effects of Bifidobacterium appear mediated through multiple pathways: enhancement of macrophage antibacterial activity via SCFA production, reinforcement of gut barrier integrity through upregulation of mucin and tight junction proteins, and prevention of pathogenic bacterial translocation and endotoxin leakage39,40.

The predominant Bifidobacterium species in human gut microbiota vary across different age groups. In the guts of both young people and the elderly, Bifidobacterium catenulatum and Bifidobacterium adolescentis are the dominant species41. Notably, the abundance of B. adolescentis demonstrates a significant decrease beyond 60 years of age42. The current study is designed to explore the associations between gut Bifidobacterium and pulmonary fibrosis. Furthermore, the therapeutic potential of B. adolescentis in pulmonary fibrosis mice across age groups were examined as well as the potential mechanism using multi-omics approach.

Results

Pulmonary fibrosis patients tend to be older and exhibit a lower ratio of Bifidobacterium and Blautia to Streptococcus

This prospective study enrolled 33 patients who had recovered from COVID-19 (Fig. 1). Based on the results of lung CT results at 6-months post discharge, the patients were divided into the fibrosis group and the non-fibrosis group. Table 1 shown that patients in the fibrosis group were older and had significantly abnormal lung function pulmonary function indexes like FVC%, FEV1%, IVC, TLC%, and DLCO%. In addition, we found that CT image scores were negatively correlated with the gut Bifidobacterium and Blautia to Streptococcus radio (B/S index), and the gut B/S index was significantly lower in the fibrosis group (P = 0.001).

B. adolescentis reduce the level of inflammation and lung injury in aging mice

BLM exposure resulted in decreased activity and respiratory distress in mice, with aging mice showing greater susceptibility to BLM-induced injury. Both PF and PFY groups exhibited significant weight reduction, while BA and BAY groups demonstrated slower weight loss rates (Fig. 2, B and C, Supplemental Fig. 2, A and E). Initial mortality was observed in the PF group on day 6. The mortality rates in the PF group and the PFY group were higher, while that in the BA and BAY groups decreased (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Fig. 2, B and F). However, there was no statistical significance in the mortality rate (Supplemental Table 3). As shown in Fig. 2, E–G, mice in PF group displayed significantly higher BALF cell counts, protein exudation levels, and lung coefficients compared to PFY gourp (PF vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, respectively). Furthermore, after the B. adolescentis intervention, the levels of the indicators above decreased significantly in both age groups (Fig. 2, E-G, BA vs. PF, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, P < 0.001; BAY vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, respectively). Inflammatory factor levels in BALF and serum were elevated in aging mice relative to young mice (Fig. 2, H–K, PF vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, respectively). While IL-17 levels showed no significant difference between PF and PFY groups, compared with the BAY group, IL-17 showed a decreasing trend in the BA group (Fig. 2L). After B. adolescentis intervention, the levels of inflammatory factors and lung barrier function in BA group and BAY group were significantly improved compared with those in PF group and PFY group.

Histological examination revealed well-preserved alveolar architecture with uniform airspace dimensions and normal epithelial morphology in NC and NCY control groups (Fig. 2M). In contrast, PF and PFY groups showed extensive structural damage characterized by airway occlusion, necrotic foci, peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration, and extensive fibrotic areas with honeycomb lung formation. After B. adolescentis intervention, the injury of lung tissue was attenuated. The alveolar walls thicken, the alveoli in the small areas collapse or fuse, inflammatory cell infiltration can be seen around the bronchi and blood vessels, interstitial congestion, and fibrous bands or fibrous masses can be observed in the small areas. As shown in Fig. 2N, by comparing the HYP levels with those of healthy mice, it was observed that HYP significantly increased after BLM induction, and the increase in HYP in the PF group was higher than that in the PFY group (PF vs. PFY, P < 0.01). Compared with the PFY group, the fibrosis condition in the PF group was more severe. In the Masson staining (Fig. 2, O and P) of the PF group and the PFY group, large areas of blue color were observed, and in the Sirius red staining (Fig. 2, Q and R), bright red or orange-yellow colors were presented, indicating the aggregation of collagen fibers and the disruption of the alveolar structure (PF vs. NC, P < 0.001, P < 0.001. PFY vs. NCY, P < 0.001, P < 0.001). B. adolescentis significantly reduces collagen accumulation. Quantitative analysis confirmed larger fibrotic areas in PF group compared to PFY group (Fig. 2, P and R, PF vs. PFY, P < 0.01, P < 0.001). The fibrous deposition and structural damage in the PF group were more severe.

The pathological results further proved that the pulmonary fibrosis lesions in aging mice were more severe than those in young mice. After the intervention of B. adolescentis, the degree of pulmonary lesions and the degree of collagen fiber deposition in lung of the BA group and the BAY group were comparable.

B. adolescentis reduced collagen fiber accumulation

BLM induction significantly upregulated pulmonary pro-fibrotic factor expression (Fig. 3, A–C, Supplemental Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3, A–C, the PF group demonstrated markedly elevated expression levels of TGF-β, α-SMA, and Collagen I (PF vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.01, P < 0.001). B. adolescentis reduced the expression level of TGF-β, α-SMA, and Collagen I, but there is no significant difference between the BA group and the BAY group. In Fig. 3, D–F, the results of immunohistochemical (IHC) and quantitative analysis revealed that B. adolescentis significantly diminished BLM-induced deposition of α-SMA and Collagen I in the lungs induced by BLM (BA vs. PF, P < 0.001, P < 0.001. BAY vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, respectively). Moreover, the accumulation of collagen fibers in aging mice was more severe than that in young mice (PF vs. PFY, P < 0.001, P < 0.01). TUNEL staining (Fig. 3G) showed that the normal cell nuclei in the NC and NCY groups are blue, while green fluorescence labels appear in the PF group and PFY group, suggesting the cell nuclei of apoptotic cells. B. adolescentis reduced fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition and decreases cell nuclear apoptosis.

B. adolescentis regulated the PPAR signaling pathway and Th17 cell differentiation

Transcriptomic analysis identified 48 significantly upregulated and 75 downregulated DEGs in the PF group compared to the BA group (Fig. 4A, Supplemental Fig. 4). KEGG pathway analysis of these DEGs revealed 5 significantly upregulated pathways and 11 downregulated pathways. Notably, in Fig. 4, B and C, Th17 cell differentiation and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways showed significant upregulation, while the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway demonstrated significant downregulation – all critical regulators of inflammatory responses and fibrotic tissue remodeling. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 4, D–F) confirmed activation of Th17 cell differentiation pathways in the PF group, along with enrichment of Th1/Th2 cell differentiation. In addition, the PPAR signaling pathway was activated in BA group.

Comparative analysis of pulmonary mRNA expression (Fig. 4, G-J) demonstrated that aging BLM-induced mice exhibited significantly reduced PPAR-γ levels and elevated IL-17, IL-6, and TNF-α expression compared to young mice (PF vs. PFY, P < 0.05, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, P < 0.05, respectively), findings that correlated with cytokine detection results. It was also found that the PPAR signaling pathway was inhibited in the PFY group of young mice, and the inhibition was more pronounced in the PF group of aging mice.

B. adolescentis recovered the gut barrier function

Following B. adolescentis intervention, the levels of LBP decreased, gut permeability was reduced, and translocation of LBP from the gut was attenuated (Fig. 5A). Notably, LBP levels in the BA group were significantly lower than those in the BAY group (BA vs. BAY, P < 0.01). The relative mRNA expression levels of MUC-2 and ZO-1 were significantly upregulated post-intervention, and the increase was more obvious in BA group (Fig. 5B and C, BA vs. BAY, P < 0.05, P < 0.05). These findings were corroborated by immunofluorescence staining of ZO-1 and MUC-2 (Fig. 5D), demonstrating that B. adolescentis intervention improve the structure of gut tight junctions and increase the number of goblet cells. Collectively, these results indicate that B. adolescentis enhances both the physical and chemical barrier functions of the gut, especially in the gut of aging mice.

B. adolescentis altered the metabolic levels of PGE2 and suberic acid by modifying the abundance of gut microbial species

B. adolescentis significantly altered the gut microbiota composition in both aging and young mice (Supplemental Fig. 5, A-H). As shown in Fig. 6A, among the top 20 microbial species in terms of abundance, the BA group exhibited decreased relative abundance of Ligilactobacillus, Bacteroides, Prevotellaceae, Parabacteroides, and Lactococcus. Conversely, the B. adolescentis intervention increased the abundance of Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrionaceae, Allobaculum, Rikenella, Dubosiella, Eubacterium ylanophilum, Turicibacter, Muribaculum, and Bifidobacterium.

Comparative metabolomic analysis between the PF and BA groups identified 79 significantly differential metabolites in lung tissue, with the top 40 metabolites shown in Fig. 6B. The differential metabolites encompassed amino acids and peptide derivatives, fatty acids and their conjugates, acylated metabolites, phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidylethanolamine derivatives, glycerophosphocholine metabolites, and prostaglandin derivatives. Metabolomics analysis identified 16 key metabolites related to the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 6B). Subsequent correlation studies of these metabolites and the 20 most abundant microbial communities revealed robust statistically significant associations between specific metabolite profiles and gut microbial community composition in the aging mouse model (Supplemental Fig. 6C). Multifactorial correlation analysis following identification of species differences between age groups pre- and post-intervention demonstrated significant negative correlations between Bifidobacterium, Turicibacter, and norank_f_Desulfovibrionaceae abundance and fibrosis-related gene expression. Conversely, Ligilactobacillus abundance showed a positive correlation with fibrotic gene expression (Supplemental Fig. 5J, Fig. 6C). Furthermore, strong correlations were observed between PGE2 and suberic acid levels with both abundance of Bifidobacterium and Ligilactobacillus, and mRNA expression of α-SMA, collagen I, and collagen III. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly elevated abundance of Bifidobacterium, Turicibacter, and norank_f_Desulfovibrionaceae in the BA group relative to BAY group (Fig. 6, D–F, BA vs. BAY, P < 0.01, P < 0.01, P < 0.05, respectively). Although Ligilactobacillus abundance was elevated in aging mice, B. adolescentis intervention reduced its prevalence (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

Age-related immunosenescence exacerbates proinflammatory cytokine release and reactive oxygen species generation, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses and increasing susceptibility to acute lung injury or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)16,43. In the present follow-up study of post-COVID-19 patients showed that those with residual pulmonary fibrosis lesions were older and had a significantly lower B/S index. A mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis induced by BLM was used, and B. adolescentis was gavage for intervention. The results confirmed that aging mice had higher inflammation and more severe fibrosis than young mice. B. adolescentis intervention reduced fibrosis in aging mice to levels similar to young mice. This effect may be due to B. adolescentis altering gut microbiota by decreasing Ligilactobacillus and increasing Turicibacter, which enhances intestinal barrier function. This change led to reduced pro-inflammatory metabolites (PGE2 and suberic acid) in the lungs, upregulated PPAR signaling, and inhibited Th17 cell differentiation, ultimately alleviating fibrosis.

Clinical studies have found that patients with pulmonary fibrosis have gut microbiota disorder and gut epithelial cell damage, which further leads to the deterioration of fibrosis44.Mendelian analysis found a causal relationship between the occurrence of IPF and the increased abundance of Bacteroidaceae, Gastranaerophilales, Senegalimassilia, and Cyanobacteria45. COVID-19 patients exhibit significant reductions in immunomodulatory gut microbiota, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, and Bifidobacterium species46. Notably, patients developing PCPF demonstrate both prolonged gut microbiota dysbiosis and delayed pulmonary function recovery37,47. Based on our previous findings that decreased Bifidobacterium and Blautia and increased Streptococcus in the gut were linked to acute inflammation and impaired recovery, and proposed the B/S index as a simple prognostic marker due to its close correlation with clinical symptoms48. The results of this study have confirmed the association between the gut microbiota of the elderly population and lung function as well as pulmonary fibrosis lesions. And the results of the mouse experiments also demonstrated the association between the gut microbiota and the development of pulmonary fibrosis, which was consistent with previous research reports. However, after the intervention with B. adolescentis, the results only showed an increasing trend in the B/S index in the BA group and the BAY group, but there was no statistical difference (Supplemental Fig. 5, M). Additionally, the study of the aging mice with pulmonary fibrosis revealed that they were more sensitive to the intervention of B. adolescentis, which also confirmed the susceptibility of the aging mice. Maintaining the gut microbiota is crucial for preventing or treating pulmonary fibrosis.

The occurrence of pulmonary diseases is closely related to the gut, and the gut-lung axis can mediate immune interference through the microbiota49. Dysbiosis of gut microbiota disrupts the gut barrier and immune homeostasis, which in turn triggers an immune response in the distal lung mucosa50,51. Pulmonary injury can activate systemic immune and inflammatory responses, leading to gut barrier damage and bacterial translocation52,53. Furthermore, BLM-induced gut microbiota disruption in mice was accompanied by a significant increase in the levels of pro-fibrotic and pro-apoptotic targets in animal experiments54. In the present study, B. adolescentis was used to reshape gut microbiota, down regulate the abundance of Ligilactobacillus, and up regulate the abundance of Turicibacter and norank_f_Desulfovibrionaceae, and improve the gut bacterial barrier. Previous studies have shown that B. adolescentis could reshape the gut microbiota, enhance the integrity of the gut, and regulate the Treg/Th2 response, thereby slowing down the aging of the colon or chronic inflammation of the colon55,56. Preventive use of B. adolescentis reduces the level of eosinophils in mice and improves asthma symptoms57. Research on muscle atrophy has found that the B. adolescentis can alleviate the muscle atrophy symptoms in aging mice by regulating the level of the key metabolite nicotinic acid58. In addition, B. adolescentis can regulate the immune response to inhibit or improve diseases such as viral hepatitis, fatty liver disease, norovirus infection, and colorectal cancer59–62. Thus, B. adolescentis plays an important role in many diseases. However, there are relatively few studies on the effect of B. adolescentis on pulmonary fibrosis. In this study is the first to demonstrate the therapeutic effect of B. adolescentis against pulmonary fibrosis, revealing its potential as a prophylactic and therapeutic intervention for pulmonary fibrosis complications through the gut-lung axis.

Emerging evidence confirms the involvement of metabolites of gut microbiota in immune-related disease pathogenesis, particularly pulmonary disorders63–65. Correlation analyses integrating microbiome, metabolome and fibrotic indicators in both BLM- and silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis models demonstrated the diagnostic reliability of gut microbiota and metabolite profiling for pulmonary fibrosis identification66. The correlation analysis in the present study revealed significant correlations between PGE2 and suberic acid levels and both pro-fibrotic factors and gut microbiota composition, implicating these metabolites in pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis. The correlation analysis in this study revealed that the levels of PGE2 and suberic acid were significantly correlated with pro-fibrotic factors, and the B. adolescentis intervention reduced the concentration of PGE2 in the lungs and increased the level of linoleic acid, which suggested that these metabolites levels were related to the pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 6C). However, in vitro experiments and animal studies have shown that PGE2 exerts anti-fibrotic effects by inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation67–69. Furthermore, other studies have confirmed the same findings in this study. In the lung tissues of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and in the lung tissues of fibrotic mouse models, the content of PGE2 has increased70. Studies have shown that parecoxib sodium can inhibit the synthesis of PGE2, thereby alleviating pulmonary fibrosis in mice, and it has also proposed that the COX2-PGE2 axis plays a dual role in fibrosis70. Up-regulation of prostaglandin E synthase (PTGES) in lung fibroblasts increases the expression of myofibroblast markers by activating HIF-1α-dependent glycolysis, and promotes the migration and invasion of pulmonary fibroblasts71. PGE2 promoted the expression of α-SMA, fibronectin and Collagen I in rat cardiac fibroblasts cultured in vitro, and promoted myocardial fibrosis72. Notably, the excessive activation pathway of PGE2, including the biosynthesis of PGE2 and its receptor signaling transduction, is a hallmark of diseases such as multiple sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis73. The rat model of rheumatoid arthritis found that Lycium barbarum polysaccharide intervention improved symptoms through specific intestinal flora, and found that suberic acid and other metabolites were increased74. Further investigation in collagen-induced arthritic rats revealed that gut-derived suberic acid exhibits a negative correlation with systemic inflammatory markers while showing positive associations with beneficial commensals including Romboutsia, Lactobacillus, and Turicibacter75. But there are relatively few reports on the effect of suberic acid on pulmonary fibrosis. The experimental data in this research conclusively demonstrate that B. adolescentis significantly reduced pulmonary PGE2 concentrations while elevating suberic acid level. This provides theoretical support for subsequent studies on the roles of PGE2 and suberic acid in pulmonary fibrosis.

The results of transcriptome analysis and mRNA expression indicated that there were significant alterations in the PPAR signaling pathway and Th17 pathway in the aging mice. PPAR-γ is a major regulator of adipogenesis, and its function is mainly to regulate macrophage inflammatory response and cell metabolism76,77. PPAR-γ is expressed in airway smooth muscle cells, and its ligands can reduce the migration and matrix production of human airway smooth muscle, leading to airway remodeling78,79. The PPAR agonist IVA337 reduces the levels of fibrosis markers in animals and, in a dose-dependent manner, inhibits the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts induced by TGF-β in vitro80. Rosiglitazone, as an agonist of PPAR-γ, can restore the decreased arterial oxygen partial pressure, increased wet/dry ratio of lung tissue, and pulmonary fibrosis score induced by paraquat81. In addition, metformin experiments showed that it accelerated the regression of fibrosis by regulating metabolic pathways, activating PPAR-γ signaling pathway, inhibiting TGFβ1 action, inhibiting collagen formation, and inducing myofibroblast-to-lipofibroblast transdifferentiation82. Th17 cells are the most common inflammatory cells infiltrating lung tissue and regulate extracellular matrix deposition by stimulating lung fibroblasts by secreting IL17A83. IL-17A derived from Th17 cells can increase the expression of IL-6 induced by TNF-α, stimulate the secretion of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor and macrophage colony-stimulating factor by fibroblasts and respiratory epithelial cells, and cause damage to alveolar epithelial cells and alveolar basement membrane84,85.It has been reported in studies that PPAR-γ is a key negative regulator of Th17 cell differentiation in humans and mice, and it inhibits Th17 cell differentiation by controlling RORγt induction86. Studies have shown that PPAR-γ agonists such as rosiglitazone and pioglitazone prevent the utilization of glutamine, which can directly limit the Th17 response in the absence of Foxp3 and regulate the immune dysregulation associated with Th1787. Therefore, we propose that B. adolescentis attenuates pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting the differentiation of Th17 cells into IL-17 factors through stimulating PPAR signaling pathway.

However, the size of the clinical sample was relatively small, especially for the elderly, and all were from the same center. And the simple BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, which serves as a mature model for IPF, cannot accurately reflect the pulmonary remodeling phenomenon after COVID-19 infection. Clinical research has discovered the role of Blautia and Bifidobacterium, but we did not conduct further research on Blautia. Our study on the mechanism is somewhat weak due to the lack of comparison of the transcriptome results of young and aging mice lungs. Finally, it may be due to the racial differences between humans and mice that we did not find the role of the B/S index in the mouse model. In addition, this study reports a new finding that B. adolescentis regulates PGE2/PPAR/Th17 signaling pathway through the gut-lung axis, and the specific related mechanisms need to be systematically investigated in the future. We focused on the level of PGE2, although its role in pulmonary fibrosis is controversial and may be related to the age of the aging mice, which deserves further study.

The risk of pulmonary fibrosis in the elderly after COVID-19 is increased, which is related to the decrease of gut Bifidobacterium and Blautia. The animal experiment validated the therapeutic potential of B. adolescentis in BLM-induction pulmonary fibrosis of aging mice, which may be related to improving the gut barrier and regulating the PGE2/PPAR/Th17 pathway. Our study reveals the potential of B. adolescentis for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis and demonstrates the clinical value of age-specific microbiota intervention strategies, which need to be further verified in population-based studies.

Methods

Clinical sample collection

This is a prospective longitudinal follow-up study of COVID-19 survivors discharged from the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China, between February 1 and March 15, 2020. A total of 33 consecutively discharged COVID-19 patients were followed up six months after discharge (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria include a history of inflammatory bowel disease, a history of gastrointestinal surgery, and the use of antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics in the month preceding the six-month follow-up visit48. These patients underwent pulmonary CT scans, pulmonary function tests, and fecal microbiota analysis. Two radiologists with over 5 years of experience independently rated the CT images as described previously7. Pulmonary fibrosis was defined as reticulation, includes fibrous cord shadow, subpleural line, and the presence of bronchiectasis.

The ratings were as follows: 0, 0% involvement; 1, less than 25% involvement; 2, 25%–50% involvement; 3, 50%–75% involvement; and 4, 75% involvement or higher. The sum of the total scores provided the overall lung involvement88. Patients were grouped based on the CT image scores, with scores < 1 classified as the non-fibrosis group (n = 15) and ≥ 1 as the fibrosis group (n = 18).

Previous clinical studies have shown that PCPF exhibits significant age-related differences. The incidence of PCPF is the highest among patients aged 60 to 75 (43.3%), followed by those aged 45 to 60 (28%)89. Therefore, we divided the population into the elderly group (age ≥ 60, n = 8) and the young group (age < 60, n = 25). We then conducted a correlation analysis among the Blautia, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, CT image scores, and lung function. The ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IIT2020-137). Research involving human research participants, material, or data performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Strain and culture conditions

Bifidobacterium adolescentis CGMCC 15058 was acquired from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). The strain was cultured in trypsin-phosphopeptide-yeast (TPY) broth (Qingdao Ruishui, China) under anaerobic conditions at 37°C for 24 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C) and resuspended in sterile physiological saline to a final concentration of 3 × 109 CFU/mL for gavage administration.

Animal model and experiments

Two age groups of C57BL/6 male mice were utilized: young (8-10 weeks) and aging (17–18 months) (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd, China). All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 22 ± 2 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice were randomly allocated into six experimental groups (Fig. 2A). Three groups of aging mice were NC (PBS + saline) group, PF (PBS + BLM) group, and BA (B. adolescentis + BLM) group, and three groups of young mice were NCY (PBS + saline) group, PFY (PBS + BLM) group, and BAY (B. adolescentis + BLM) group.

Prior to model establishment, mice were fasted for 12 h with free access to water. Following body weight measurement, anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (weight-adjusted dose). Anesthetized mice were positioned supine on the surgical platform, with neck hair shaved and disinfected. Bleomycin (BLM, Selleckchem, Houston, Texas, United States) was dissolved in saline (5 mg/ml) and administered via tracheal instillation (5 mg/kg) between cartilage rings. Mice were immediately rotated to optimize pulmonary BLM distribution. The incision was sutured and disinfected. Control groups (NC and NCY) received equivalent volumes of normal saline. Postoperative thermoregulation was maintained, with ad libitum access to food and water upon recovery. All experiments involving animals were conducted according to ethical policies and procedures approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No. 2025-088).

Calculation of lung coefficient

On day 14, lungs were excised en bloc, blotted dry, and immediately weighed to determine wet weight. The lung coefficient was calculated as wet weight/body weight ratio. The left lung was then divided, one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), another snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and the remainder stored in PBS. (n = 8 per group).

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) assay analysis

BALF was collected via endotracheal lavage with PBS following euthanasia. After centrifugation (800 × g, 4°C, 10 min), supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Cell pellets were treated with RBC lysis buffer (5–10 min) and resuspended for counting using a Countess automated cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total protein content was quantified using Pierce™ BCA assay. Cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were measured by ELISA (Abclonal) according to manufacturer’s protocols. (n = 8 per group).

Analysis of serum parameters

Serum was isolated by centrifuging blood samples at 3000×g for 15 min. Inflammatory cytokines were quantified using a mouse 23-Plex cytokine array (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). while lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) levels were determined by ELISA (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). All operations were carried out in accordance with the instructions of the kit. (n = 8 per group).

Hydroxyproline (HYP) in lung tissues

Lung HYP content was determined using the HYP assay kit from Aabcam (ab222941). Collagen deposition was assessed by quantifying HYP content in lung tissues. Tissue samples were homogenized (1:9 w/v) in ice-cold 0.9% saline using a mechanical homogenizer. The 10% homogenate was centrifuged (2500–3000 ×g, 10 min), and supernatants were collected for analysis. (n = 8 per group).

Pathological section analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 2 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius red for histological evaluation of tissue injury, fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration. Immunostaining was performed using primary antibodies against α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen I. Apoptotic cells were detected using TUNEL assay (HKI0010), with nuclei counterstained blue (DAPI) and apoptotic nuclei fluorescing green. Colon sections were immunostained for zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and mucin-2 (MUC-2) to evaluate gut barrier integrity. Positive staining areas were quantified using ImageJ software.The list of antibodies used was provided in the Supplementary Table 1. The dilution ratios for each antibody should be carried out according to the instructions. (n = 3 per group).

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR analysis

We extracted RNA from lung and colon, using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen, CA, USA). The obtained RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), using the PrimeScript™RT kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). Subsequently, the level of expression of target genes was measured using the ViiA7 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) based on the 2 − ΔΔCT method (GAPDH as a control) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II reagent (Takara). The sequences of the primers used are shown in Supplementary Table 2. (n = 8 per group).

16S rRNA sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from feces samples using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH) following manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and concentration were assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific). The V3-V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16S rRNA was amplified with primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 PE300 platform (Illumina) following library preparation. Sequencing data were processed in QIIME2. The DADA2 plugin was used for amplicon sequence variant (ASV) clustering with taxonomic classification against the SILVA 138 database. α- and β-diversity analyses were performed in QIIME2. Differential taxa between groups were identified using LEfSe analysis (LDA score >2, P < 0.05). (n = 8 per group).

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis of lung tissue

Lung tissue samples (n = 5–6/group) were homogenized in 400 μL methanol: water (4:1) using a cryogenic tissue grinder (6 min, −10°C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction (30 min, 5°C, 40 kHz). After incubation at −20°C for 30 min, samples were centrifuged (13,000 × g, 4°C, 15 min), and supernatants were collected for LC-MS analysis. Metabolite profiling was performed using an Orbitrap Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Raw data were processed through Progenesis QI v3.0 (Waters) for peak identification, alignment, and correction, generating a data matrix containing m/z and intensity values. Metabolites were annotated against the KEGG and HMDB databases. Differential metabolites were identified with thresholds of P < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC < 1.

Lung transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was isolated from lung tissues using the previously described method. RNA concentration and purity were measured, and integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Kit (premixed version) following manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were processed to obtain high-quality reads. Sample reproducibility was evaluated by principal component analysis (PCA) using R (v3.2.0). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2, with significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those meeting FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC | >1 threshold. (n = 3 per group)

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.1. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± SD, and those with non-normal distribution were expressed as median (IQR). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess data normality. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used as appropriate. For experiments involving more than two groups, two-way ANOVA was performed to assess the main effects and interaction. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Sidak correction) were conducted as planned to examine treatment effects within each age group (simple effects analysis). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and R (v 4.2). Significance levels are denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
