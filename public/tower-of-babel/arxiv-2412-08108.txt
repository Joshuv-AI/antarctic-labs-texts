Exploiting Vision Encoder Vulnerabilities for Universal Adversarial Perturbations on Large Vision-Language Models
Hee-seon Kim †, Minbeom Kim †, Seokil Ham, Changick Kim

Abstract
Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have achieved remarkable performance on multimodal tasks but remain highly vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations in input images. Existing attacks typically target the vision encoder’s final output embeddings, implicitly treating the encoder as a uniform attack surface, while a systematic analysis of which internal components are most vulnerable has remained largely unexplored. We show such analysis is essential, as adversarial vulnerability in LVLM vision encoders is structurally concentrated rather than uniformly distributed. Building on this, we propose Vision Encoder Vulnerable-Component-Targeted Universal Adversarial Perturbation (VEV-UAP), a task-agnostic and cost-efficient attack framework. Through a component- and layer-wise analysis of attention mechanisms, we identify the value components in middle layers as critical vulnerabilities that strongly influence downstream language model behavior. VEV-UAP selectively targets these components to generate a single universal perturbation shared across images, without involving textual inputs or the language model during optimization. Experiments across multiple LVLMs and tasks show VEV-UAP achieves state-of-the-art attack success rates with reduced computational overhead. Moreover, a single VEV-UAP transfers across LVLMs sharing the same vision encoder, even when paired with different language models, making it a practical framework for scalable robustness evaluation.

Therefore, most existing approaches optimize perturbations against the entire vision encoder through its final output embeddings, implicitly treating the encoder as a uniform attack surface. Yet which internal components are most vulnerable, and how strongly that choice shapes downstream language behavior, has remained largely unexplored.

Through systematic component- and layer-wise analysis, we find that not all attention components within the vision encoder are equally vulnerable to adversarial perturbations. Some components exhibit strong robustness, such that perturbing the entire vision encoder dilutes adversarial effects and fails to sufficiently shift the downstream LLM representations needed to disrupt LVLM outputs. This observation indicates that adversarial vulnerability in LVLM vision encoders is structurally concentrated rather than uniformly distributed. As a result, indiscriminately targeting all components of the vision encoder can be both ineffective and inefficient. Moreover, most prior methods rely on image-specific adversarial perturbations, leading to high computational cost and limited scalability when evaluating robustness over large image sets.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel adversarial attack framework termed

Through this systematic analysis, we find that the value vectors in the middle layers of the vision encoder are especially sensitive to perturbations, inducing pronounced discrepancies in the language feature space between clean and adversarially perturbed images. Motivated by this finding, we design adversarial perturbations that explicitly maximize the divergence between original and perturbed value features at these vulnerable layers.
Furthermore, rather than generating image-specific adversarial perturbations, we adopt a universal adversarial perturbation (UAP) strategy that learns a single perturbation shared across multiple images.
Overall, this design eliminates the need for per-image optimization and task-specific textual inputs or supervision, substantially reducing computational overhead and enabling scalable robustness evaluation over large image sets.

Extensive experiments show that VEV-UAP consistently outperforms existing LVLM attack baselines, achieving state-of-the-art attack success rates with lower computational overhead.
Beyond these performance gains, VEV-UAP provides a practical and scalable framework for robustness assessment in LVLMs by eliminating the need for per-image and per-prompt optimization. Moreover, a single universal perturbation transfers effectively across multiple models that share the same vision encoder, even when paired with different language models.

Structural Vulnerability Analysis of LVLM Vision Encoders

In this section, we conduct a structural vulnerability analysis of LVLM vision encoders to identify which internal components exert the strongest influence on downstream language behavior. This analysis forms the foundation for our component-targeted universal adversarial design.

III-A

Rather than treating the vision encoder as a monolithic attack surface implicitly assuming uniform vulnerability

As a pioneering investigation of this question, we focus on the fundamental components of the attention mechanism, which play a central role in vision encoders.
Specifically, we analyze the following components:

In addition, since attention mechanisms are distributed across multiple layers of the vision encoder, we examine whether the influence of these components on downstream language model behavior varies across layers.

III-B

Representation Robustness (RR) Score as a Proxy for Downstream Influence

We measure deviation at the final token position of the LLM’s last layer, as this representation integrates both visual and textual context before output generation
[
63
]
.
Therefore, changes in this hidden state provide a direct proxy for shifts in the model’s semantic decision space as it directly conditions the final token distribution.
To examine the influence of individual attention components on the hidden states of the LLM module, we analyze each component separately across different layers of the vision encoder.
Specifically, we define a
Representation Robustness (RR) Score
, which measures the cosine similarity between the hidden states of the LLM at the final token position before and after perturbing a specific attention component.

The calculation of the RR Score involves two steps. We first optimize an adversarial perturbation
\delta_{C}^{*}
that targets a set of vision encoder components
C
by maximizing the divergence between the corresponding component representations:

\delta_{C}^{*}=\arg\max_{\delta_{C}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}||C|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{c\in C}\mathrm{CosDist}(E_{c}(x),E_{c}(x+\delta_{C})),

(1)

where
\mathrm{CosDist}(\cdot)
denotes the cosine distance (i.e., 1 - cosine similarity),
\mathcal{X}
is the set of input images, and
E_{c}(\cdot)
represents the output of component
c
extracted from the vision encoder.

We then define the RR score as:

\mathrm{RR}\;\mathrm{Score}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,p)}\big[\mathrm{CosSim}(h_{\text{last}}(E(x+\delta_{C}^{*}),p),h_{\text{last}}(E(x),p))\big],

(2)

where
\mathrm{CosSim}(\cdot)
is cosine similarity,
p
is the corresponding text prompt,
E(\cdot)
is the entire vision encoder, and
h_{\text{last}}(\cdot)
represents the hidden state at the final input token position of the LLM’s last layer.
A lower RR score indicates a larger deviation in the LLM’s internal representation, suggesting a stronger impact on the LVLM and higher adversarial vulnerability of the corresponding component.

Layer-wise Analysis and Empirical Findings

To investigate layer-wise effects, we partition the vision encoder layers into three segments—early, middle, and late—along with an evaluation using all layers.

(1) Layer-dependent vulnerability.

(2) Component-dependent vulnerability.

Within the attention mechanism, value vectors exhibit the highest susceptibility, exerting the strongest downstream influence on the LLM over attention weights, query, and key vectors.

2
Structural vulnerability concentration in mid-layer value vectors

These findings indicate that mid-layer value vectors constitute a structurally central propagation pathway for adversarial effects within the vision encoder. Early layers primarily encode patch-local, low-level visual signals, while late layers operate on already aggregated representations. In contrast, middle layers mediate large-scale feature integration, where contextualized visual information is consolidated before being projected to the language model
[
64
]
.
Within this integration stage, value vectors directly carry the content that is propagated through attention, perturbations at this stage are more likely to coherently alter the semantic information delivered to the LLM, resulting in amplified downstream effects.

Consequently, our analysis reveals that vulnerability in LVLMs is not uniformly distributed, but concentrated in specific perturbation-propagation-sensitive components. Indiscriminately perturbing all components can dilute adversarial effects rather than amplify them, explaining why using all layers
does not lead to the strongest adversarial effect
, despite encompassing potentially vulnerable components.
Instead,
targeting a carefully selected subset of layers and components yields more effective disruption
.
This analysis provides deeper insights into the internal vulnerabilities of LVLMs and directly motivates our component-targeted adversarial design.

IV
Methodology

We propose
Vision Encoder Vulnerable-Component-Targeted Universal Adversarial Perturbation (VEV-UAP)
, the first universal attack framework for LVLMs derived from a systematic component- and layer-wise analysis of vision encoder vulnerability.
While prior attacks target the final image embeddings of the vision encoder, implicitly treating all encoder components as uniformly vulnerable,
our framework is uniquely derived from a component-level structural analysis of vision encoders, revealing and targeting structurally central vulnerability pathways.
Instead of treating the encoder as a monolithic attack surface, VEV-UAP identifies and selectively targets mid-layer value representations that act as central propagation pathways to the language model. This component-aware design enables an architecture-level universal perturbation that generalizes across images and tasks. Although applied only to input images, the perturbation induces coherent downstream disruption without requiring per-image, per-text, or LLM-level optimization, resulting in an efficient and scalable attack strategy.

IV-A

Preliminary

Adversarial attacks are techniques used to subtly modify input data in order to mislead models into producing incorrect predictions.
Considering the open-ended nature of LVLM responses, these attacks introduce imperceptible perturbations to the input image, causing the model to generate semantically incorrect responses.
Given an input image
x\in\mathcal{X}
, a text prompt
p\in\mathcal{P}
, a label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and a LVLM
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Y}
, an adversarial attack seeks to generate a perturbation
\delta
that, when added to the image, causes the model to make incorrect predictions.
The conditions for an adversarial attack can be described as follows:

•

For labeled data:
The perturbation should lead to a semantically incorrect prediction.

\mathrm{Sim}(f(x+\delta,p),y)<\tau.\vskip-2.84526pt

(3)

•

For unlabeled data:
The perturbation is considered successful if it induces a semantically different output.

\mathrm{Sim}(f(x+\delta,p),f(x,p))<\tau.\vskip-2.84526pt

(4)

Here,
\mathrm{Sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes a semantic similarity measure between text outputs, and
\tau
is a predefined threshold.
To make sure the perturbations remain imperceptible, we constrain the perturbation by enforcing
\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
.

IV-B

Vision Encoder Vulnerable-Component-Targeted Universal Adversarial Perturbation (VEV-UAP)

Building on the vulnerability analysis in
Section

III
, we propose
Vision Encoder Vulnerable-Component-Targeted Universal Adversarial Perturbation (VEV-UAP)
. Unlike existing attacks that treat the vision encoder as a monolithic entity, VEV-UAP selectively targets identified high-impact components to effectively disrupt the representations propagated to the downstream language model. By maximizing distortions in the value vectors of the middle layers, VEV-UAP induces consistent degradation in LVLM behavior using a single, image-agnostic perturbation. Rather than optimizing perturbations for specific image–text pairs, VEV-UAP seeks a
universal
perturbation
\delta
that generalizes across different input images, text prompts, and downstream tasks.

Let
V_{l}(x)
denote the value vectors extracted from the
l
-th layer of the vision encoder for an input image
x
.
Our objective is to find an optimal perturbation
\delta^{*}
that maximizes the divergence between the value vectors of clean and perturbed images across a set of vulnerable layers. Formally, we optimize
\delta^{*}
as follows:

L_{vul}=\arg\min_{L}\mathrm{RR}\;\mathrm{Score}(\{V_{l}|l\in L\})

(5)

\delta^{*}=\arg\max_{\delta}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}||L_{vul}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{l^{*}\in L_{vul}}Loss(V_{l^{*}}(x),V_{l^{*}}(x+\delta)),\vskip 0.0pt

(6)

where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the set of images used to learn the universal perturbation,
L_{vul}
denotes the set of target layers identified as vulnerable (i.e., middle layers) with RR Score, and
Loss(\cdot)
denotes the loss function.
To ensure that the perturbation remains visually imperceptible, we impose an
L_{\infty}
constraint
|\delta|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
denotes the perturbation budget.
By solving
Eq.

6
under this constraint, VEV-UAP effectively corrupts the semantic information encoded in the value vectors, leading to distorted vision encoder outputs that propagate erroneous signals to the language model. Importantly, this process does not utilize textual inputs nor require access to the gradients or parameters of the language model, further enhancing the scalability and practicality of the proposed attack.

V-D

Baselines

As the first work to propose vision encoder–based universal adversarial perturbations (UAPs) for LVLMs, we adapt existing image-specific vision encoder–based attacks
[
20
,
23
,
21
,
24
,
62
]
to the universal setting.
As Qwen-ViT is trained from scratch and no publicly available paired text encoder is provided, only the Image Embedding Attack (Img-Emb) baseline can be applied to this model.

(1) Text Embedding Attack (Text-Emb).

Maximizes the cosine distance between the adversarial image embedding and the ground-truth text embedding constructed using the prompt template
“A photo of ⟨class⟩”
, where ⟨class⟩ denotes the ImageNet label. Corresponds to the universal extension of
[
20
]
.

(2) Image Embedding Attack (Img-Emb).

Maximizes the cosine distance between original and adversarial image embeddings produced by the vision encoder. Extends prior image embedding–based attacks to the universal setting
[
23
,
24
,
62
]
.

(3) Both Embedding Attack (Both-Emb).

Jointly maximizes (i) the cosine distance between adversarial image embeddings and ground-truth text embeddings, and (ii) the distance between original and adversarial image embeddings. Serves as the universal counterpart of the dual-embedding attack in
[
21
]
.

V-E

Tasks and Evaluation Metrics

For
VQA
tasks, we adopt standard evaluation metrics from established benchmarks.
We use
soft accuracy
for VQAv2 and TextVQA to account for annotation variability. For ScienceQA, we report standard multiple-choice accuracy. For POPE, we use standard accuracy. Finally, for MME, we report the sum of
Accuracy
and
Accuracy+
across 14 subtasks, where Accuracy+ requires correct responses to both questions in each paired sample, following standard MME evaluation.
VQAv2 uses COCO 2015 test images
[
66
]
, POPE uses COCO 2014 validation images
[
66
]
, and the remaining benchmarks follow their standard splits.

For
classification
, we use the prompt
“What is the main object in this image? Give an answer using a single word or phrase.”
.
For
image captioning
, we use
“What is this image about? Give an answer in a single sentence of about 10 words.”
.
To handle the open-ended outputs of LVLMs, we follow
[
20
]
and compute cosine similarity of text outputs using the CLIP text encoder. This semantic evaluation avoids penalizing conceptually similar predictions (
e.g
.
, predicting
Maltese
instead of
dog
).
For the Clean condition, we sample two responses generated from the same original images and text prompts and compute the cosine similarity between them.
We report the following metrics for classification and captioning:

(1) Attack Success Rate (ASR) (%).

An attack is considered successful if the cosine similarity between the
original
and
adversarial
responses falls below a threshold (
\tau
).

(2) Top-
k
Accuracy (%).

We compute cosine similarity between the
adversarial response embedding
and
label embeddings
. If the ground-truth label is not among the top-
k
most similar labels, the attack is deemed successful. For captioning, embeddings of clean responses serve as pseudo ground-truth labels.

(3) Sim (Avg.).

The average cosine similarity between embeddings of original and adversarial responses, measuring overall semantic degradation.

(4) GPT-4o-as-a-Judge.
We prompt GPT-4o
[
1
]
to assign a score from 1 to 10 indicating the attack success,
where 1 denotes highly similar semantics between clean and adversarial responses (attack failure), and 10 denotes a severe semantic shift (attack success).
We report the average score provided by GPT-4o (
GPT Avg.
). The system prompt used for GPT-4o evaluation is shown in
Fig.

10
.

(range: 1–100). Lower quality factors correspond to stronger compression, while higher values preserve better visual fidelity.

[

]
is a simple yet commonly used defense mechanism that mitigates adversarial perturbations by re-encoding the image and suppressing high-frequency noise.
In our experiments, JPEG compression is applied using the torchvision implementation.
The corresponding results are reported in

.

Overall, when JPEG compression is applied,
VEV-UAP demonstrates substantially stronger robustness
than all baselines, achieving the best attack performance across

.

For the InstructBLIP model, VEV-UAP also shows significantly stronger robustness.

VI
Analysis

In this section, we analyze how perturbations confined to the vision encoder influence downstream language behavior. Our results indicate that mid-layer value representations constitute the central propagation pathway through which this disruption spreads to the downstream language model. Targeting vulnerable components within the vision encoder alone induces degradation across the entire LVLM pipeline, affecting not only visual representations but also downstream language generation and attention. For these analyses, we use VEV-UAPs optimized with different vision encoders depending on the target model: the CLIP-336 vision encoder for LLaVA-1.5, the CLIP-224 vision encoder for LLaVA, and the EVA-CLIP vision encoder for both InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4.

VI-A

Experiment Settings for Analysis

For the analyses in
Sections

III
and
VI
, UAPs are optimized for five epochs using the same dataset as in the main experiments. Specifically, we select 200 images per class from the ImageNet 2012 training set
[
65
]
, resulting in a total of 200,000 images for UAP generation. For classification evaluation, we use one image per class from the ImageNet validation set, yielding 1,000 images in total. For VQA evaluation in
Section

III
, we adopt the first quarter split of the VQAv2 dataset. All other experimental settings are identical to those used in the main experiments. Finally, unlike gradient-based methods such as FGSM
[
28
]
, we employ the Adam optimizer for UAP optimization, as detailed in
Section

V-A
.

VI-B

Impact of Layer Position Shifting and Number of Layers

Building on our earlier observation that the influence of vision encoder components varies substantially across layers, we further investigate how layer selection affects the downstream language model by systematically analyzing both layer position shifting and the number of selected layers. Focusing on the middle layers identified as most effective in the previous section, we shift the layer window along the depth of the vision encoder and vary its size to identify optimal configurations.

The results in
Figure

3
illustrate the effects of layer position shifting and layer number adjustment in the vision encoders, measured by the cosine similarity between the adversarially perturbed and original LLM hidden states. Across both vision encoders, we observe a consistent trend in which the middle-to-late layers of the vision encoder exhibit the strongest influence on the LLM. Based on these findings, we adopt the layer ranges that produce the greatest discrepancy as the default settings.

Notably, after applying VEV-UAP, value vector patterns become increasingly similar across different input images. .

VI-K

Visualization of LLM Attention

In this section, we analyze how VEV-UAP, which is generated solely by perturbing the vision encoder, affects the language model by examining LLM attention patterns. Fig. 14 in the supplementary material visualizes attention maps from the LLMs of LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4, and Qwen2.5-VL under original and adversarial image inputs. For LLaVA-1.5 and LLaVA, we use VEV-UAPs optimized on the CLIP-336 and CLIP-224 vision encoders, respectively. For InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, we use a VEV-UAP optimized on EVA-CLIP, while for Qwen2.5-VL, we use a VEV-UAP optimized on Qwen-ViT.
In each attention map, the vertical axis denotes query token indices and the horizontal axis denotes key token indices, with color intensity indicating attention weights. The red dashed line separates visual tokens produced by the vision encoder from textual tokens processed by the LLM.

Across all evaluated LVLMs, VEV-UAP consistently induces substantial disruptions in LLM attention patterns. Compared to clean inputs, adversarial images lead to blurred, distorted, or redistributed attention across both visual and textual token regions. Tokens that originally receive strong attention are often suppressed, while previously low-attention or irrelevant tokens become overemphasized, resulting in misaligned or overly uniform attention distributions. This effect is consistently observed across different architectures and layers. For instance, in both LLaVA and LLaVA-1.5, VEV-UAP causes pronounced blurring and loss of structure in LLM attention compared to the clean-image setting.

Notably, these disruptions are not limited to visual tokens but propagate into textual tokens, despite the fact that neither the LLM nor the text modality is involved during VEV-UAP optimization. This indicates that adversarial perturbations introduced at the vision encoder level effectively propagate into the language model, degrading its internal attention mechanism. As a result, the LLM struggles to reliably identify and prioritize semantically relevant tokens, leading to erroneous or incoherent interpretations of visual inputs.

Overall, these results demonstrate that although VEV-UAP is generated by perturbing only the vision encoder, its effects propagate deeply into the language model, fundamentally disrupting vision–language alignment and the LLM’s internal attention mechanisms.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we show that adversarial vulnerability in Large Vision-Language Models is structurally concentrated rather than uniformly distributed across the vision encoder. Through component- and layer-wise analysis, we identify mid-layer value representations as a central pathway through which perturbations propagate to the language model. Based on this finding, we propose
Vision Encoder Vulnerable-Component-Targeted Universal Adversarial Perturbation (VEV-UAP)
, a universal perturbation that selectively targets these vulnerable components. Despite operating solely at the vision encoder level and without involving the language model during optimization, VEV-UAP achieves strong and transferable attack performance across tasks and models. These results highlight the importance of component-level analysis for principled robustness evaluation and suggest that improving LVLM robustness may require explicitly reinforcing vulnerable mid-layer value representations.
