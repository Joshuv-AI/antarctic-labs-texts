Woven Endobridge device for ruptured vs. unruptured aneurysms: insights from the WorldWideWEB study
Authors: Franja Dugar, Muhammed Amir Essibayi, Hamza Adel Salim, Basel Musmar, Nimer Adeeb, Mahmoud Dibas, Yan-Lin Li, Oktay Algin, Sherief Ghozy, Sovann V Lay, Adrien Guenego, Leonardo Renieri, Joseph Carnevale, Guillaume Saliou, Panagiotis Mastorakos, Eimad Shotar, Markus Möhlenbruch, Michael Kral, Charlotte Chung, Mohamed M Salem, Ivan Lylyk, Nicole M Cancelliere, Jose Danilo Bengzon Diestro, Paul M Foreman, Hamza Shaikh, Vedran Župančić, Muhammad U Hafeez, Joshua Catapano, Muhammad Waqas, Giyas Ayberk, Muhammet Arslan, James D Rabinov, Onur Ergun, Julian Maingard, Clemens M Schirmer, Mariangela Piano, Anna L Kühn, Caterina Michelozzi, Robert M Starke, Ameer Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough the Woven EndoBridge (WEB) device is increasingly used for the treatment of wide-neck intracranial aneurysms, including in the acute rupture setting, comparative evidence assessing the impact of rupture status remains limited. This study compared angiographic, safety, and clinical outcomes between ruptured and unruptured intracranial aneurysms treated with WEB.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the multicenter cohort registry WorldWideWEB, including consecutive adult patients with intracranial aneurysms treated with the WEB. Patients were stratified into groups of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms. Propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics between both groups. Retreatment rate was the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included mRS, safety events (thromboembolic complications) and angiographic outcomes (periprocedurally and last follow-up).ResultsAmong 1,220 patients, 342 (28.0%) presented with ruptured aneurysms. Propensity-score-matched analyses revealed no significant difference in thromboembolic complications (11.8% vs. 5.9%, p = 0.056), similar periprocedural adequate occlusion (53.3% vs. 53.8%, p > 0.9), and similar retreatment rates (11.8% (95% CI 7.8–17.6%) vs. 7.1% (95% CI 4.1–12.0%), p = 0.14); however, adequate occlusion at follow-up was lower (82.2% vs. 93.3%, p = 0.002) and functional outcomes were worse (mRS ≥ 2 in 34.1% vs. 21.9%, p = 0.012) among patients with ruptured aneurysms.ConclusionRuptured aneurysms demonstrated expected inferior follow-up functional and angiographic outcomes when compared with unruptured aneurysms, but no difference in retreatment rate and procedural safety. These findings support WEB as a safe and effective treatment option for appropriately selected ruptured intracranial aneurysms in routine clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00234-026-04032-6.Keywords: Intracranial aneurysm, Ruptured intracranial aneurysm, Woven EndoBridge device, Endovascular treatment

Introduction

The Woven EndoBridge (WEB) device has become an established endovascular option for the treatment of wide-neck intracranial aneurysms, including selected cases in the acute rupture setting. Compared with techniques such as stent-assisted coiling and flow diversion, its principal advantage is the ability to achieve intrasaccular flow disruption without stent placement in the parent artery, thereby avoiding the need for dual antiplatelet therapy [1].

Since its introduction, the WEB device has been evaluated in multiple prospective trials, and large multicenter registries, demonstrating favorable angiographic outcomes and acceptable safety profiles, especially in wide-neck bifurcation aneurysms [2–5]. Although early investigations focused predominantly on unruptured aneurysms, an increasing number of studies have reported encouraging results in ruptured intracranial aneurysms, including high rates of adequate occlusion, low rebleeding rates, and acceptable complication profiles [6–9]. Meta-analyses and long-term follow-up studies further support the durability of WEB treatment, suggesting that it represents a viable alternative to conventional approaches in appropriately selected ruptured aneurysms [1, 10–12].

Despite these encouraging data, direct comparisons between ruptured and unruptured aneurysms treated with the WEB device remain limited. Many published series of ruptured aneurysms are relatively small or reflect early experience, and differences in baseline clinical status, aneurysm morphology, and treatment strategies may substantially influence angiographic, safety, and functional outcomes [8, 13].

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from a large multicenter registry to compare the safety and efficacy profiles of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms. We hypothesized that ruptured aneurysms would demonstrate inferior angiographic and functional outcomes compared with unruptured aneurysms, while retreatment rates and periprocedural safety profiles would remain comparable between groups after adjusting for baseline differences using propensity score matching.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the WorldWideWEB consortium, a multicenter study including 30 academic medical centers across Europe, North America, South America, and Australia [14]. All consecutive adult patients with both ruptured and unruptured intracranial aneurysms, who underwent endovascular treatment using the WEB device, were included. Patients were excluded if there were missing data on rupture status, occlusion rates, long-term follow-up or retreatment records. A total of 1220 of 1612 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Aneurysms were then categorized based on their rupture status into two groups of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms. The decision to treat aneurysms with the WEB device was made independently at each participating center by the treating neurointerventionalist, based on aneurysm-specific characteristics including rupture status, anatomical location, size, and neck morphology. Institutional review board approval was obtained at the lead institution, with additional approvals secured at each participating center as required. Given the retrospective design and the relatively low risk and retrospective nature of this study, informed consent was waived. Reporting is in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational studies) statement.

Data collection

The following information was collected from institutional medical records and entered into the WorldWideWEB Consortium registry: patient demographics (age, sex, smoking status, prior adjunctive treatment), baseline functional impairment (defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥ 2 prior to treatment), aneurysm-related characteristics (rupture status, anatomical location, size parameters), and treatment characteristics (immediate angiographic occlusion, and immediate Raymond-Roy score). Follow-up data encompassed time since last follow-up, angiographic occlusion outcomes, Raymond-Roy score, and the retreatment rate [15].

Outcomes

Angiographic outcomes included aneurysm occlusion immediately following the treatment and at follow-up, classified using the Raymond–Roy occlusion scale (adequate occlusion: Raymond-Roy occlusion grade I and II; aneurysm remnant: Raymond-Roy occlusion grade III), as well as categorical assessment of occlusion degree at both time points. Angiographic follow-up was performed using digital subtraction angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, or computed tomography angiography, in accordance with local institutional practice.

Safety outcome comprised the occurrence of thromboembolic complications, including both symptomatic and asymptomatic events, which were identified on digital subtraction angiography by angiographic evidence of intraluminal thrombus formation, impaired contrast filling in previously patent vessels, or new vessel occlusion. Efficacy outcomes included functional status at last follow-up, assessed using mRS, and the rate of aneurysm retreatment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, when applicable. Wilson’s score method was used for the calculation of the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), unless otherwise specified [16]. Ordinal variables were analyzed using mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression models. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05. To account for potential confounding between ruptured and unruptured aneurysm groups, propensity score matching was performed adjusted for the variables age, sex, current smoking status, maximum aneurysm diameter, aneurysm neck width, aneurysm width, adjunctive treatment use, and pre-treatment mRS. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with an absolute difference between propensity scores of 0.2. A sensitivity analysis repeating the matching with the addition of aneurysm location (bifurcation vs. non-bifurcation) as a covariate was additionally performed. A subgroup analysis was additionally performed restricting the cohort to bifurcation aneurysms only, in whom propensity score matching was repeated using identical parameters to the primary analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) [17]. Propensity score matching was conducted using the MatchIt package, and descriptive tables were generated using the tableone and gtsummary packages.

Results

A total of 1,220 patients were included, of whom 342/1220 (28.0%) presented with ruptured intracranial aneurysms (Fig. 1). Patients with ruptured aneurysms were younger (median age 56 vs. 61 years), more frequently male, and more often active smokers. The median Hunt–Hess grade among patients with ruptured aneurysms was 2 [IQR 1–3]. Baseline functional impairment (mRS ≥ 2) was significantly more common in the ruptured cohort (98/342 (30.6%) vs. 62/878 (7.5%), p < 0.001) (Table 1).

In a propensity score matched cohort comprising 169 patients with ruptured aneurysms and 169 with unruptured aneurysms, propensity score matching achieved good baseline balance across all variables, with standardized mean difference (SMD) < 0.1 for all covariates after matching (Supplementary Table 1). Angiographic follow-up data were available in 328/338 matched patients (97.0%; ruptured: n = 163/169, unruptured: n = 165/169), and mRS data in 336/338 matched patients (ruptured: n = 167/169, unruptured: n = 169/169). Patients without angiographic or mRS data were retained in all other outcome analyses. Ruptured aneurysms demonstrated significantly lower rates of adequate occlusion (134/163 (82.2%) vs. 154/165 (93.3%), p = 0.002) and lower rates of occlusion status more than 90% (135/163 (82.8%) vs. 153/165 (92.7%), p = 0.006) at follow-up. In addition, patients with ruptured aneurysms showed poorer functional outcomes at follow-up, with a higher proportion having mRS ≥ 2 than in case of unruptured aneurysms (57/167 (34.1%) vs. 37/169 (21.9%), p = 0.012). In the sensitivity analysis including aneurysm location (bifurcation vs. non-bifurcation) as an additional matching covariate, 167 matched pairs were identified (n = 334 total). The primary findings were consistent with the main analysis: adequate occlusion at follow-up remained significantly lower in ruptured aneurysms (133/162 (82.1%) vs. 146/160 (91.2%), p = 0.016), and functional outcomes remained worse (55/165 (33.3%) vs. 36/166 (21.7%), p = 0.018). Retreatment rates were significantly higher in ruptured aneurysms in this analysis (21/167 (12.6%) vs. 10/167 (6.0%), p = 0.038), while occlusion status of more than 90% at follow-up did not reach statistical significance (134/162 (82.7%) vs. 141/160 (88.1%), p = 0.2) (Supplementary Table 2). In the bifurcation aneurysm subgroup (133 matched pairs), findings were consistent with the primary analysis: adequate occlusion at follow-up was significantly lower in ruptured aneurysms (106/130 (81.5%) vs. 117/127 (92.1%), p = 0.012), functional outcomes were worse (45/131 (34.4%) vs. 25/132 (18.9%), p = 0.005), and retreatment rates were significantly higher (17/133 (12.8%) vs. 7/133 (5.3%), p = 0.032) (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the multicenter cohort study, we evaluated whether rupture status influences functional, angiographic, and safety outcomes among patients with intracranial aneurysms treated with the WEB device. By directly comparing ruptured and unruptured aneurysms in a large dataset and adjusting for baseline differences using propensity score matching, our results provide several clinically relevant insights into the performance of the WEB device in the acute rupture setting.

As expected, functional outcomes at follow-up were less favorable in patients with ruptured aneurysms (34.1% with mRS ≥ 2), a finding that persisted after propensity score matching and is most plausibly attributed to the underlying subarachnoid hemorrhage and its complications rather than device-related factors [18].

Immediate angiographic occlusion rates were higher in ruptured than in unruptured aneurysms. This observation is consistent with prior analyses from our multicenter registry, which suggested that acute inflammation and altered hemodynamics in ruptured aneurysms may enhance the prothrombotic effects of the WEB device, potentially promoting more effective early clot formation [19–21]. The paradox of higher immediate but lower follow-up occlusion rates may reflect the transient nature of this prothrombotic environment: in the acute setting of subarachnoid hemorrhage, reduced cerebral blood flow velocity during vasospasm combined with a systemic hypercoagulable state may facilitate early thrombosis within the device, an effect that is unlikely to be sustained once the acute phase resolves. At follow-up, however, ruptured aneurysms demonstrated lower rates of adequate occlusion and a higher prevalence of neck or aneurysm remnants. In the matched analysis, adequate occlusion was achieved in 82.2% of ruptured aneurysms compared with 93.3% of unruptured aneurysms (p = 0.002). These findings are comparable to those reported in the CLEVER study, which demonstrated an adequate occlusion rate of 86.5% in ruptured aneurysms at 12 months [9]. Differences between studies are likely explained by aneurysm characteristics and study design, as CLEVER included smaller aneurysms (mean sac width 4.8 mm), a factor known to be associated with more favorable occlusion outcomes [9]. Other studies have reported similarly high rates of adequate occlusion. Van Rooij et al. observed adequate occlusion in 96% of cases at 3-month angiographic follow-up, a finding likely influenced by smaller aneurysm size and shorter follow-up duration [13]. Meta-analyses by Essibayi et al. and Ferreira et al. reported adequate occlusion rates of 87.3% and 83.8%, respectively, at last or 12-month follow-up, results that are consistent with those observed in our cohort [1, 22]. A sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for aneurysm location (bifurcation vs. non-bifurcation) yielded consistent results, with adequate occlusion remaining significantly lower in ruptured aneurysms (82.1% vs. 91.2%, p = 0.016), supporting the robustness of our primary findings.

Periprocedural thromboembolic complications occurred in 11.8% of ruptured aneurysms in our matched cohort. This rate is comparable to those reported in prior studies, including CLEVER (11.7%) and CLARYS (16.7%), and higher than the pooled thromboembolic complication rate of 4.6% reported in the 2025 meta-analysis by Ferreira et al. [1, 9, 23]. In the unmatched analysis, thromboembolic events were more frequent in ruptured than unruptured aneurysms (11.7% vs. 5.5%), consistent with previous reports from the CLARITY and ARETA studies [24, 25]. This may be related to the pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state during SAH, mirroring the slightly higher risk of coil embolization in the ruptured vs. unruptured setting [26]. However, after propensity score matching, this difference was attenuated and no longer statistically significant. Importantly, the majority of aneurysms in both groups were treated without adjunctive devices, underscoring the feasibility of WEB treatment in ruptured aneurysms while avoiding routine stent use in the acute hemorrhagic setting.

The retreatment rate in ruptured aneurysms was 11.8% in the matched analysis and 9.5% overall. These rates are higher than those reported in prospective studies such as CLEVER (1.8% for ruptured aneurysms) but are comparable to meta-analyses [9]. Caroff et al. reported a global retreatment rate of 7.5%, with significantly higher retreatment rates in ruptured compared to unruptured aneurysms (13% vs. 3.9%) [27]. Other studies have reported retreatment rates ranging from 4.2% to 6.8%, while meta-analyses by Essibayi et al. and Ferreira et al. reported retreatment rates of 5.1% and 7.5%, respectively [1, 6, 13, 22]. Notably, in the sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for bifurcation morphology, retreatment rates reached statistical significance (12.6% vs. 6.0%, p = 0.038), suggesting that after accounting for aneurysm morphology, rupture status may independently influence retreatment risk. This finding was further supported by the bifurcation subgroup analysis, in which retreatment rates were similarly significantly higher in ruptured aneurysms (12.8% vs. 5.3%, p = 0.032). Taken together, these findings suggest that the higher retreatment rate observed in our cohort likely reflects differences in patient selection, aneurysm complexity, follow-up duration, and retreatment thresholds inherent to real-world multicenter practice rather than inferior angiographic durability of the WEB device.

Despite being one of the largest studies on ruptured aneurysms, treated with WEB device, several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. First, retrospective design is subject to selection bias, and treatment strategies were determined at the discretion of individual operators. Second, although propensity score matching was used to reduce confounding, residual unmeasured confounders may persist. Third, follow-up duration was relatively short and slightly different between groups, which may influence the assessment of long-term angiographic outcomes. Fourth, detailed antiplatelet therapy data were not systematically recorded in the WorldWideWEB registry. Given the well-documented heterogeneity of antiplatelet strategies in the acute rupture setting and the variability across participating centers, the potential influence of antiplatelet regimen differences on thromboembolic complication rates cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the safety outcomes of this study. Finally, with only 32 retreatment events in the matched cohort, this analysis may be underpowered, and the absence of a statistically significant difference between groups should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies with standardized follow-up protocols, larger matched cohorts, and mandatory angiographic follow-up of at least 24 months, are warranted to draw definitive conclusions on retreatment rates and to further define the long-term efficacy and safety of the WEB device in ruptured intracranial aneurysms.

Conclusion

Our propensity score matched analysis demonstrated that ruptured aneurysms show lower rates of adequate occlusion and higher prevalence of remnants at follow-up despite comparable immediate periprocedural angiographic results. Importantly, no significant differences were observed in periprocedural thromboembolic complications or retreatment rates after adjustment. These findings support the WEB device as a viable treatment option for appropriately selected ruptured intracranial aneurysms in routine clinical practice.
