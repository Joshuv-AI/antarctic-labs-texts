Hepatitis due to Enterovirus in a Child
Authors: Anna Prete, Sergio Di Fenza, Claudia Mandato, Giovanna D'Urzo, Pasquale Villano, Maria Amendolara, Anna M. Aurino, Marco F. Natale, Antonella De Santis, Giuseppe Avallone, Luigi Masini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnteroviruses are a common cause of infection in neonates, causing wide spectrum of diseases, including myocarditis, myelitis, meningoencephalitis, hepatitis, and sepsis. The enteroviral hepatitis has rarely been recorded in pediatric populations other than neonates. We report a rare case of self-limiting acute hepatitis in a healthy 3-year-old child affected by hand-foot-and-mouth disease caused by Enterovirus. Providers should be aware of hepatitis prevalence in the Enteroviral infection, and should consider it in the differential diagnosis for acute hepatitis of unknown origin.

INTRODUCTION

Viral causes of hepatitis are well known in both adults and children. The most common causes of acute viral hepatitis include hepatitis A and B. Other viruses have also been known to cause acute hepatitis at a young age, including Epstein-Barr virus, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes simplex virus, and Enterovirus. According to the data from the Center for Disease Control and Prevention in America, nonpolio-Enteroviruses cause 10–15 million infections and tens of thousands of hospitalizations in the United States each year.1 Asymptomatic infections account for approximately 50% of the cases, while symptomatic Enteroviral infections range from nonspecific febrile illnesses to life-threatening diseases such as myocarditis or sepsis, and in newborn infants fulminant hepatitis.2–4 However, the main manifestations include hand-foot-mouth disease, acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis, herpangina, etc.5 Enteroviral hepatitis has rarely been recorded in populations other than neonates, so the aim of this paper was to present a rare case of hepatitis due to Enterovirus in a child.

CASE REPORT

A previously healthy 3-year-old boy initially presented to our hospital with febrile seizure and malaise. He had no contact with a sick person, history of travel or use of medications, and family history was noncontributory. He was born full term by cesarean section due to previous cesarean sections, with birth weight of 3,450 kg, and his immunization status was complete and up-to-date. There was no significant pre-, peri- or postnatal complications in the child and the mother. On admission, he presented hyperemic pharynx and tonsillar exudate, there was no abdominal pain, no jaundice or scleral icterus. All other portions of the physical examination were within normal limits. No changes in mental status or in hemodynamic parameters were observed. During the fourth day of stay at the hospital, he developed erythematous papular eruptions on the hands, feet and mouth, and perioral vesicles. From the first day, the patient was treated symptomatically with intravenous (IV) fluids, acetaminophen for fever control, and empirical antibiotic therapy with IV ampicillin-sulbactam, because of neutrophilic leukocytosis and tonsillar exudate.

Initial laboratory testing showed leukocytosis of 14.5 K/µL (neutrophils 77.8%), lactate dehydrogenase 692 U/L, and elevated liver enzymes including alanine transaminase (ALT) 590 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 559 U/L, and γ-Glutamyltranspeptidase (γ- GT) 115 U/L. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and total bilirubin were normal at 74 U/L and 0.35 mg/dL respectively. Prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, lipase, and urinalysis were within normal limits. An abdominal ultrasound, an electrocardiogram, and a transthoracic echocardiogram without abnormalities were obtained in the first day of stay.

Enterovirus was positive via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from nasopharyngeal swab, and confirmed via real-time PCR from blood sample (Tables 4 and 5). Additional workup for other liver pathologies was negative, including alpha-1 antitrypsin, ferritin and iron studies, and ceruloplasmin. Records of autoimmune panel revealed positive antinuclear antibody with 1:160 titer, however anti-smooth muscle and anti-mitochondrial antibodies, anti-tissue transglutaminase antibody were negative. No additional markers of autoimmune hepatitis were tested. The clinical workup suggested a final diagnosis of acute hepatitis secondary to Enteroviral infection, so gastroenterologist was consulted to aid in the assessment of the patient's condition and his care.

DISCUSSION

Enteroviruses are major pathogens in neonates, but in children 90 percent of these infections are nonsymptomatic or manifest simply as an undifferentiated febrile illness.6 These viruses were traditionally classified into the following 4 subgenera: poliovirus, coxsackievirus A and B, and echovirus. In infants, these pathogens can cause varied clinical manifestations, including hand-foot-and-mouth disease, respiratory illness, myocarditis, meningitis, sepsis, and even lethal multisystem organ failure.7–9 Echoviruses particularly may cause severe hepatitis with foci of hepatic necrosis.10

Coxsackievirus group A is well-known to cause hand-foot-and-mouth disease in children, characterized by fever and erythematous papular eruptions on the hands, feet, and mouth, whereas Coxsackie B is known to cause myocarditis, meningitis, and hepatitis in children and neonates.11–13 In our case, real-time-PCR did not discriminate the Enterovirus serotypes, but we can hypothesize a Coxsackievirus group A etiology, due to the typical features of hand-foot-mouth disease showed by the patient.

Very few cases of Enteroviral infection causing abnormal liver function in pediatric patients have been reported, in particular only Qizalbash et al in 2018 reported a pediatric case of hepatitis and meningoencephalitis due to Enterovirus during hand-foot-mouth disease.14,15 Another case of hepatitis due to Coxsackievirus A was reported in 2025 by Surendra et al, but in an adult with underlying liver vulnerability.16 Like these cases, our patient had a similar self-limiting hepatitis during an enteroviral infection, but ours is a unique case of hepatitis manifested in absence of underlying liver damage and during a common paucisymptomatic disease, admitted and evaluated for laboratory testing owing to the febrile seizure.

In summary, we have presented a rare pediatric case of hepatitis due to Enterovirus with the typical manifestations of hand-foot-and-mouth disease, with more severe symptoms characterized by febrile seizure and malaise. In our case, elevated LFTs reflect damage to the liver cells in a healthy child without previous liver illness. Despite the benign course of hepatitis, the acute liver injury complicated the course of a self-limiting disease, requiring a long hospital stay and outpatient serological and clinical follow-up. The incidence of hepatitis during a common hand-foot-and-mouth disease is unknown, because most of children are managed as outpatients. We can hypothesize many patients with paucisymptomatic enteroviral infection may have an unknown self-limiting hepatitis.

Following this case report, providers should consider the Enteroviruses in the differential diagnosis for acute hepatitis of unknown origin, and evaluate liver function in the Enteroviral infection with more severe symptoms.
