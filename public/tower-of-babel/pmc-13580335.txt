Inpatient Deaths in Pediatric Leukodystrophies
Authors: Hannah S. Hart, Kalista L. Vordos, Caroline T. Flood, Darin J. Isakson, Alfredo Novoa, Joshua L. Bonkowsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesLeukodystrophies are neurogenetic diseases affecting the white matter of the central nervous system. The contributing factors for leukodystrophy mortality are incompletely understood. Our objectives were to characterize inpatient deaths of pediatric leukodystrophies, including demographics and risk factors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort analysis utilized the national Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) database. Patients under age 19 years with an (ICD‐10) diagnosis of leukodystrophy and an inpatient encounter at a PHIS hospital between 2016 and 2024 were included. Analysis included descriptive and multivariate statistics.ResultsIn all, 613 patients with 15 different leukodystrophies were identified; 32 patients died (5.2%). The most common diagnoses of patients who died were Krabbe disease (KD) (31%), adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) (28%), metachromatic leukodystrophy (MLD) (28%), and vanishing white matter disease (VWM) (6%). Volume of leukodystrophy admissions at individual hospitals was inversely correlated with mortality. Small numbers limited statistical significance, but mortality rates were higher in females than males (8.3% vs. 3.8%); and Black, Asian, and multiracial patients were more likely to die than White patients, 8.4%, 7.7%, and 8.7%, versus 4.1%.ConclusionsIn this large national administrative database study, we characterized and helped define risks for inpatient mortality of pediatric leukodystrophies. Inpatient mortality was correlated with clinical risk factors, and further, although small numbers precluded statistical significance, mortality disparities by sex and race were identified. The reasons for these differences are not known and warrant further study. Our findings identify directions for new research and underscore the potential impact of specialized institutional experience.

Introduction

Leukodystrophies are a group of genetic diseases affecting the white matter of the central nervous system (CNS), leading to progressive disabilities and death over months to years [1, 2, 3]. Individually, each leukodystrophy is rare, but collectively, there is an incidence of > 1 in 4500 live births [4, 5]. Most leukodystrophies do not have a treatment, and the overall mortality rate in leukodystrophies is approximately 30% by age 10 years [4, 6, 7]. Causes of death and mortality rates are well understood for only a few leukodystrophies, such as the rapid decline associated with cerebral demyelinating adrenoleukodystrophy (cALD). For the majority of leukodystrophies, the mortality rates, demographic characterizations, and risk factors are unknown.

Previous work studying mortality in leukodystrophies has typically used only single‐center data or death record data with statistical modeling [4, 6]. Characterization at a national level, in a more recent time frame, is important for several reasons: the increase in newborn screening for several leukodystrophies, which affects natural history trajectories; the increase in diagnoses with use of next‐generation sequencing [8]; the need for national‐level data to account for the underdiagnosis of children from minority backgrounds [9, 10] and of children in geographic locations lacking access to a major health center [11, 12]; and the lack of granular data for hospital‐associated mortality risks.

The objectives of this work were to characterize mortality in hospitalized pediatric leukodystrophy patients, to identify demographics and risk factors associated with mortality, and to identify any differences in mortality across patient groups. Our approach relied on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD‐10) code‐based identification of the ~15 leukodystrophy subtypes for which a specific code was available (specifically, X‐linked adrenoleukodystrophy; Krabbe disease; metachromatic leukodystrophy; Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher disease; Canavan disease; Aicardi‐Goutières syndrome; tRNA synthetases; hypomyelination‐hypogonadotropic hypogonadism‐hypodontia; vanishing white matter disease; hypomyelination with atrophy of the basal ganglia and cerebellum; Alexander disease; autosomal dominant leukodystrophy; megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts; leukoencephalopathy with calcification and cysts; adult‐onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia). This allowed us to abstract data from a national‐level administrative data set, although, of course, it was limited to only a subset of all leukodystrophies.

Materials and Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This project, using deidentified data, was not considered human subjects research in accordance with the Common Rule [45CFR§46.102(f)] [13] and was exempted by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of Utah and Intermountain Healthcare. Written informed consent from the participants’ legal guardian/next of kin was not required to participate in this study in accordance with the national legislation and the institutional requirements.

Study Design

A retrospective analysis of patients admitted to a Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) hospital was conducted, where the ICD‐10‐CM was used to code each patient's diagnosis of leukodystrophy. The diagnosis criteria were defined by the currently established ICD‐10 codes for leukodystrophy diagnoses (Supplementary Table 1). ICD‐10 codes available for this analysis included childhood cerebral ALD (cALD) (E71.520), adolescent X‐ALD (E71.521), other X‐ALD (E71.528), X‐ALD, unspecified (E71.529), Krabbe disease (KD) (E75.23), metachromatic leukodystrophy (MLD) (E75.25), Pelizaeus‐Merzbacher disease (PMD) (E75.27), Canavan disease (CD) (E75.28), Aicardi‐Goutières syndrome (AGS) (E79.81), tRNA synthetases (E88.43), hypomyelination‐hypogonadotropic hypogonadism‐hypodontia (4H) (G11.5), vanishing white matter disease (VWM) (G11.6), hypomyelination with atrophy of the basal ganglia and cerebellum (H‐ABC) (G23.3), Alexander disease (AxD) (G31.86), autosomal dominant leukodystrophy (ADLD) (G90.B), megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC) (G93.42), leukoencephalopathy with calcification and cysts (LCC) (G93.43), adult‐onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (ALSP) (G93.44) [14, 15].

The PHIS database collects information from 50+ children's hospitals in the United States of America, affiliated with the business alliance of Child Health Corporation of America (Shawnee Mission, Kansas). Each visit has a unique entry and is linked to a unique, deidentified patient. Data collected includes visit demographics such as age, sex, ethnicity, race, mortality, Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) UrbanCore codes, Child Opportunity Index (COI), length of inpatient stay, insurance type, ICD coding, and admission flags. The database collects information about all visit types, including inpatient admission, emergency department (ED), observation, ambulatory surgery, and clinical visits.

Inclusion criteria were limited to patients under 18 years of age who were admitted to a PHIS hospital between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2024, had an inpatient encounter, and had an ICD‐10‐CM diagnosis of leukodystrophy (Figure 1). In all, 1107 unique patients were present in the first data set, with 20 333 encounters recorded. Only inpatient encounters were included in this study, and encounters for ED visits, ambulatory surgery, observation unit, and unspecified visit types were excluded. In all, 494 patients did not have inpatient encounters and were excluded. This includes two who died in non‐inpatient encounters; one in an ED visit and one in the observation unit. To be consistent with and to capture patients who died during their encounter, only the latest recorded encounter for each patient was retained. All prior encounters, 1754 in total, were excluded, leaving one encounter per patient for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (IBM). Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize the characteristics of the study population. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data groups were determined to be non‐parametric. χ 2 tests were used for categorical data where the number of individuals per variable was > 5. Statistics for ordinal data were conducted using Spearman's test. Multivariate analysis was conducted using simple linear regression.

Data Groupings

Race and ethnicity were collected by self‐report of the patient or caregiver on intake. Multiple races could be selected for one patient. Ethnicity categories included Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, or unknown/not reported. PHIS provides a classification of either urban or non‐urban based on the patient's home zip code. An urban area is defined as “encompass[ing] at least 2,000 housing units or hav[ing] a population of at least 5000.” Anything that does not meet the defined population density is considered to be non‐urban [16]. PHIS assigned each participant an “urban flag,” where 1 indicated an urban/suburban area and 0 indicated non‐urban.

PHIS records COI with a scale of 1 to 5, 5 being the highest and 1 being the lowest score. The COI analyzes 44 unique socioeconomic indicators in three domains (Education, Social and Environment, Health and Safety), combined into a single composite score, for 73 000 areas in the United States. A COI score of 5 would include access to certified public schools, ample playgrounds and green spaces, and high median household incomes [17]. PHIS provides insurance information for each visit. Payor type was categorized as commercial, government (Medicaid or other), self‐pay, other (includes TRICARE), or unknown. A leukodystrophy diagnosis was part of the inclusion criteria for the data set. Each patient had a noted ICD‐10 diagnosis during at least one medical encounter. The PHIS database reported the hospital at which the encounter occurred. To ensure patient privacy, each hospital was deidentified and assigned a numerical ID in order of the number of patient cases received.

The PHIS database has a mortality flag and a disposition title that identifies if a patient died during the encounter, described as “expired.” For all encounters that were not flagged as expired, the patient was assumed to be alive at the time of discharge. Clinical characteristics of hospitalizations were recorded in the form of “flags” (diagnosis) attached to an encounter. Multiple flags could be attached to a single encounter.

Results

In all, 613 unique leukodystrophy patients, under age 19, with inpatient encounters recorded from 2016 through 2024, were included in the study. The inpatient mortality rate was 5.2% for children with leukodystrophy, with 32 of the patients dying during an inpatient admission.

Demographics

Across all 613 patients, the average age was 6.7 years [standard deviation (S.D.) = 5.2]. The majority were male (69%), non‐Hispanic (70%), White (56%), lived in an urban area (84%), and insured by Medicaid (49%). The average COI was 2.9 [S.D. = 1.4], and the average hospital stay was 12.3 days [SD = 23.5]. See Table 1 for details of sex, ethnicity, race, UrbanCode, COI, and admission diagnoses.

Comparison of Living and Deceased Patients

There were no differences in age, ethnicity, race, UrbanCode, insurance, COI, or specific leukodystrophy diagnosis (uncorrected for the number of patients). There were significant differences in their length of hospital stay (11 vs. 31 days, respectively) (p < 0.001) and in the diagnoses associated with the admission. There were no differences related to emergency department charges, BMT, operating room charges, medical complications, or NICU charges.

Characteristics of Deceased Patients

Of the individuals who died during the inpatient encounter (Table 2), the most prevalent diagnosis was KD at 31% (10 of 32). 28% (9 of 32) of deceased patients had MLD, 28% (9 of 32) had X‐ X‐ALD, 6% (2 of 32) had VWM, 3% (1 of 32) had AGS, and 3% (1 of 32) had AxD. The average age at admission for patients who died during the encounter was 2.5 years for KD, 5.7 years for MLD, 8.2 years for X‐ALD, 10 years for VWM, 10 years for AGS, and 1 month for AxD. The average length of stay was 33 days for KD, 25 days for MLD, 42 days for X‐ALD, 14 days for VWM, 7 days for AGS, and 33 days for AxD.

Of the 32 deceased individuals, 50% (16 of 32) were male, and 50% (16 of 32) were female. When X‐ALD was removed from the cohort, 35% (8 of 23) were male, and 65% (15 of 23) were female (p = 0.163) (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Mortality Rates

Considering each leukodystrophy individually showed that none had statistically higher mortality rates (Table 4). However, VWM had the highest rate of inpatient mortality at 18.2% (2 of 11 patients), followed by KD at 9.3% (10 of 107), MLD at 7.1% (9 of 127), 5.9% for AxD (1 of 17), 4.0% for X‐ALD (9 of 227), and 1.6% for AGS (1 of 63). No individuals with PMD, Canavan, HABC, or 4H died inpatient.

Mortality Rates and Patient Volume

A simple linear regression was used to test the number of leukodystrophy admissions at each hospital against the mortality rate (Figure 2). The overall regression was statistically significant (R 2 = 0.511, F(1,22) = 22.96, p < 0.001). The number of leukodystrophy patients admitted at a hospital significantly predicted the mortality rate at each hospital (β = −0.715, p < 0.001). Higher admission numbers correlated with a lower mortality rate, and lower admission numbers correlated with a higher mortality rate.

Discussion

We performed a national administrative database characterization of pediatric leukodystrophies, finding that the inpatient mortality of pediatric leukodystrophies was 5%. KD and VWM had the highest mortality rates, although low numbers prevented reaching statistical significance. Although KD, ALD, and MLD were the most common diagnoses of those who died, this reflected the overall higher number of these patients. Mortality rates were higher for females than for males. Further, although also not statistically significant owing to the low overall numbers, mortality was higher in Black, Asian, and multiracial patients than White. The reasons for the higher mortality rates in these groups (females, different races) are not known and were not apparent from our study. Hospitalization factors, including infection and hospital volumes, were associated with risk for mortality and could be targeted for intervention. Our work is the first and largest to date to explore the mortality of hospitalized leukodystrophy patients. In contrast, previous studies of mortality in leukodystrophies have been of overall rates, typically using only single‐center data, or using death record data with statistical modeling [4, 6].

For the patients who died while hospitalized, we did find differences in clinical characteristics, including the presence of infection or need for ventilation. Further, we also found that hospitals with more frequent leukodystrophy admissions had lower overall rates of mortality. However, mortality, unexpectedly, perhaps reassuringly, was not correlated with insurance type or socioeconomic status.

The inpatient mortality rate of 5.2% identified in this study provides a unique counterpoint to the existing body of work characterizing overall mortality in leukodystrophy. These other studies have been center‐based, used statistical modeling, or included both in‐patients and out‐patients, but they included fewer patients. These studies include, from our own group, an overall mortality rate for leukodystrophies of 34% with an average age of death at 8.2 years [4]; an Iranian study of 165 patients, with 39% mortality at an average age of 5.2 years [7]; and a modeling study in estimating mortality at 1.5 per 1 000 000 [6]. In contrast, in this current work, we have larger numbers of patients and more detailed information about clinical mortality causes, but we only have inpatient deaths for analysis.

Inpatient mortality rates were variable across different leukodystrophies. Rates were highest for VWM (18.2%) and KD (9.3%). In comparison, previous overall mortality rates for VWM have been reported as 78.4% for infantile VWM and 8.7% for childhood VWM; our cohort for VWM consisted of a mix of ages [18]. The largest patient group who died inpatient had KD (31%): the inpatient mortality rate for KD was 9.3%. In KD, subtypes include a large spectrum of age of onset, progression, and mortality rates [19]. We previously found that the overall mortality rate for KD was 35% with transplant and 68% without transplant [20]. For MLD, the inpatient mortality rate was 7.1%, while overall mortality rates for MLD have been cited as 15.5% [7]; however, it is also variable based on age of onset, progression, subtype, and treatment [21, 22]. For ALD, the inpatient mortality rate was 4.0%, similar to the previously reported overall mortality of 4.7% [7]. Other data for pediatric ALD are based chiefly on cerebral ALD (cALD): mortality rates for cALD 5 years post‐onset have been cited at 46% when untreated and 5% when treated with BMT [23, 24, 25].

When we reviewed transplant (BMT/HSCT), we examined ALD, MLD, and KD. Among the cohort of living patients, 27% had received BMT, which was not significantly different from the 34% of patients who died inpatient. Within the cohort who died, 88% had a diagnosis of ALD, MLD, or KD. Sixty‐seven percent of children who died with ALD had flags for BMT, while 30% of those who died with K and 22% of those who died with MLD had flags for BMT. These percentages do not reflect the mortality rate for BMT outcomes but rather the percentage of the inpatient cohort who died and had received BMT as part of their disease course. Disease‐specific mortality rates after BMT have been previously reported at 5%–12% for ALD [24, 25, 26], 31% for KD [20], and between 10% and 21% for MLD [21, 22, 27]. Irrespective of diagnosis, the risk for mortality due to graft versus host complications is typically cited around 10% for any child undergoing transplant [28]. Interestingly, however, MLD had higher mortality rates with BMT compared with either ALD or KD: 9.5% compared with 6%, although numbers were too small for statistical significance.

We found significant differences for mortality, including length of hospital stay, and accompanying clinical diagnoses associated with their admission. Deceased individuals had an average hospital stay of 31 days, which was significantly higher than the length of stay for living patients at 11 days. Longer admissions are influenced by many factors, but suggest that these patients were sicker. Clinical factors associated with mortality included ICU admission, infection, mechanical ventilation, TPN, and surgical complications. Specific causes of death were not recorded in these data, although published complications of leukodystrophy include seizures, sepsis, cardiopulmonary complications, and feeding difficulties [4, 7, 10, 29]. For ALD, an important future study could evaluate whether adrenal insufficiency, a known complication of the disease, was associated with a higher risk of infection and/or death. Deceased patients were younger but not significantly so compared with the living (5.6 versus 6.8 years). This could reflect a number of factors, including that more severely affected patients might die at a younger age, or that leukodystrophies with a progressive or demyelinating course might skew toward a younger age of death.

This analysis revealed a disparity between the mortality rate of females compared with males, 8.3% in females compared with 3.8% in males. Analysis of sex in leukodystrophy can be complicated by X‐linked conditions such as ALD and PMD, where males represent the majority of the pediatric cohorts. Indeed, for patients with ALD and PMD in this study, 96% were males. In our study, almost half (42%) of leukodystrophy diagnoses were represented by ALD and PMD, which skewed the sex ratio for the cohort to 69% male. Notably, the overrepresentation of males in the study population was not observed within the cohort of 32 who died, where 50% were male and 50% were female. However, when ALD and PMD were removed from the analysis, only 35% who died were male, and the majority who died, 65%, were female (p = 0.163). Though not significantly different, it is important to recognize the relative overrepresentation of females in the deceased cohort. While previous work has shown that males are more likely than females to receive a diagnosis of leukodystrophy [12, 30], we were not able to identify other factors that would explain the higher mortality rate.

We found that Black, Asian, and multiracial patients were more than twice as likely to die as White patients, although again, because of small numbers, this did not reach statistical significance. This finding is concordant with known racial/ethnic disparities in leukodystrophy diagnosis [9, 12], wait times to receive care [31], and outcomes [10]. Racial disparities have also been identified outside of leukodystrophy patients [32], with insufficient access to healthcare among non‐white children [11, 33].

Inpatient mortality was not significantly correlated with where the patient lived (urban or rural), insurance type, or COI (a measure of socioeconomic status). Although overall mortality was not different, patients with commercial insurance had shorter stays in the hospital than those with Medicaid insurance. Other studies have shown that individuals with private insurance have better access to specialty healthcare than their peers with public insurance [12, 34, 35].

We also performed an analysis of hospitals with at least one death reported. A significant correlation was found between the number of leukodystrophy patients admitted to these hospitals and the percentage of patients who died inpatient. Hospitals with lower leukodystrophy admissions had higher mortality rates, and, inversely, hospitals with higher admissions had lower mortality rates. This supports previous work, which found higher treatment quality and diagnostic rates in leukodystrophy centers of excellence compared with non‐specialty hospitals [11]. Disparities have been previously correlated with distance from leukodystrophy centers of excellence, noting families who are White and privately insured are better able to travel farther distances to receive specialized care [12].

Limitations of this work include that it is retrospective and that it is specific to inpatient encounters; individuals who died at home, in hospice, or in another facility were not captured. This study relied on admission diagnoses for characterizing clinical characteristics of patients, and we were not able to directly review patient charts. The PHIS database used for this study provided demographic, diagnoses, and hospitalization characteristics. However, PHIS is limited in providing granular details, including disease severity, clinical notes, or specific causes of death. Many clinical characteristics described in this paper, such as infection or BMT, were indicated by flags signifying presence or absence, without providing details of timing or severity. There was also no separate means to confirm the correct ICD‐10 coding of the leukodystrophy subtype. We were also not able to determine specific causes of death, such as seizures or sepsis. We were limited in that small numbers often precluded reaching statistical significance. Further, we were only able to analyze leukodystrophies that had their own ICD‐10 code, and for some of the leukodystrophies, the code was only available starting in October 2023, when additional leukodystrophy diagnoses were given their own unique code. Patients with a leukodystrophy diagnosis without an ICD‐10 code recorded before October 2023 may have been absent or underrepresented.

Understanding characteristics of mortality for individuals with leukodystrophy and their interaction with the medical system is necessary to inform better care and quality of life for those affected. Our findings show some disparities in mortality that warrant more study, including sex and race. Also, given the shift to increased newborn screening—almost universally in the United States for ALD, and increasingly for KD and other leukodystrophies—additional changes in mortality characteristics may occur. Further, we identified potentially modifiable risk factors, including hospital volumes and infection, that can be addressed. Although in our current study we did not have measures of clinical disease severity available, it would be valuable for future work to characterize whether and which clinical risk factors of disease severity, such as G‐tubes or scoliosis, act as independent predictors of mortality.

In conclusion, in this large national administrative database study, we found that inpatient mortality of pediatric leukodystrophies was 5%. Inpatient mortality was correlated with clinical risk factors, including higher rates of ICU admission, infections, mechanical ventilation requirements, TPN usage, and surgical complications. We also found demographic disparities, including a higher mortality rate in females than males; and though not statistically significant, Black, Asian, and multiracial pediatric patients were twice as likely to die compared with their white peers. Finally, mortality rates were inversely correlated with leukodystrophy patient volumes. Our findings help define the mortality risks for pediatric leukodystrophies, identify future directions for research, and highlight the impact of specialized leukodystrophy centers and the opportunity to develop care models at lower‐volume hospitals. Characterization of death in leukodystrophy is important to raise awareness and urgency for research, quality of care, and treatment efforts going forward.
