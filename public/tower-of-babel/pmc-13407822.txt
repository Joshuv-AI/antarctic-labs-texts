Clinical and Economic Burden Among People with Knee Osteoarthritis and Obesity in the United States: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: David Schapiro, Magaly Perez-Nieves, Sylvia Gonsahn-Bollie, Kendra Terrell, Ahong Huang, Wenyang Mao, Alexandra Meeks, Pedram Azimzadeh, Julia Fraseur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionObesity and knee osteoarthritis (KOA) are prevalent conditions that significantly impact public health and healthcare costs. We aimed to quantify the economic burden and explore the relationship between obesity and KOA pain.MethodsThis study used Optum’s de-identified Market Clarity Data from October 1, 2015, to March 31, 2024. Adults (≥ 45 years) with moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis (MTS-OA) knee pain (index date: first-observed claim with a KOA diagnosis between October 1, 2016, and March 31, 2023), with continuous enrollment in commercial, Medicare, or Medicaid healthcare plans for 1 year before and after post index, were included. The presence of KOA pain was identified using a previously validated algorithm. Adjusted annualized all-cause and KOA-related healthcare resource utilization and costs were assessed in the first year after index date, stratified by body mass index (BMI) categories: normal (≥ 18.5 to < 25 kg/m2), overweight (≥ 25 to < 30 kg/m2), Class 1 obesity (≥ 30 to < 35 kg/m2), Class 2 obesity (≥ 35 to < 40 kg/m2), and Class 3 obesity (≥ 40 kg/m2). Models were adjusted for age, race, sex, payor type, baseline Charlson Comorbidity Index score, and baseline cost. Adjusted costs were winsorized at 1%.ResultsThe prevalence of obesity-related complications was high, particularly among those with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2. Adjusted total healthcare costs (mean [standard deviation]) SD]) were the highest in individuals with Class 3 ($37,698 [$34,239])) and Class 2 ($33,676 [$29,627]) obesity) compared with those in individuals with normal BMI ($32,402 [$32,290]). Similar trends were observed with pharmacy and medical costs (Class 3: $15,040 [$41,754] and $25,493 [$16,927]; Class 2: $11,088 [$33,879] and $24,075 [$15,540]; normal BMI: $9327 [$32,165] and $23,821 [$17,474]). KOA-specific pharmacy, medical, and total costs followed a similar pattern.ConclusionsIn people with MTS-OA knee pain, this descriptive analysis revealed higher observed average overall and disease-specific healthcare expenditure in Class 2 and 3 obesity than in other BMI categories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40744-026-00856-z.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a multifactorial disease and a leading cause of chronic pain and disability globally [1]. It accounts for more than 80% of the total osteoarthritis burden and affects at least 19% of US adults aged ≥ 45 years [2, 3]. Obesity (defined as body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30 kg/m2) or overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) is a modifiable risk factor associated with the development, progression, and severity of KOA [4–7].

The economic burden of obesity and obesity-related KOA is significant, with mean total 1-year KOA-direct medical costs per person ranging between $3719 and $4518 [8]. Multiple studies have established a direct link between obesity and the risk of KOA; with every 5-unit increase in BMI, the risk of developing KOA increases by 35% [9]. Additionally, increased body weight is associated with greater knee pain, decreased physical function, and lower quality of life in individuals with KOA [10]. Given this strong association, addressing obesity with a comprehensive treatment strategy may help alleviate KOA symptoms, improve physical function, and reduce overall healthcare burden. For people with KOA and obesity, the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and Osteoarthritis Research Society International guidelines recommend a weight reduction of ≥ 5% along with a concomitant exercise program for positive changes in clinical, mechanistic, and economic outcomes of the disease [11, 12].

The relationship between obesity and KOA is well documented; however, most prior studies that have focused broadly on osteoarthritis have not stratified findings by specific BMI categories [13, 14]. Furthermore, many analyses using administrative claims databases have not distinguished the incremental impact of BMI on treatment patterns, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), and costs among individuals with moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis (MTS-OA) knee pain [13, 14]. This leaves a critical gap in understanding how varying degrees of obesity influence the clinical and economic burden of KOA, particularly in real-world settings. Gaining insight into the role of obesity in this relationship may help people and healthcare practitioners manage disease progression, guide treatment options, and implement strategies to optimize patient outcomes. To address this gap, the present study used an extensive administrative claims database to comprehensively evaluate and understand population characteristics, treatment utilization, HCRU, and costs among individuals with MTS-OA knee pain, stratified by BMI class.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective, observational study was conducted over 8 years (October 1, 2015, to March 31, 2024) using de-identified patient health information from Optum’s de-identified Market Clarity Data (Optum® Market Clarity), which deterministically links medical and pharmacy claims with electronic health record (EHR) data. Diagnoses and procedures for outpatient and inpatient visits were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnostic, ICD-10 procedure coding system (ICD-10-PCS), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and Healthcare Financing Administration Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) codes. Pharmacy claims were captured by National Drug Codes (NDCs) and HCPCS codes when appropriate. Optum® Market Clarity standard costs were used for all cost variables. Additionally, costs in the database were Consumer Price Index (CPI)-adjusted for inflation to 2024 via a standard cost variable; therefore, no additional inflation adjustment transformations were carried out.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and is consistent with Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices and applicable US laws and regulations. Participants’ data were de-identified to protect privacy. The de-identified data were fully compliant with US patient confidentiality requirements, including the Health Insurance Health Portability and Accountability Act of 1996. Given the use of retrospective and de-identified data, a formal Consent to Release Information form and Institutional Review Board approval were not required since this was an observational study that used previously collected data and did not impose any intervention.

Study Population

The study population included adults with at least one ICD-10-CM diagnosis code claim for KOA during the index period (October 1, 2016, to March 31, 2023). The date of the first-observed claim with a KOA diagnosis was defined as the index date. Individuals were included if they were ≥ 45 years old on the index date and had MTS-OA knee pain, as determined using criteria outlined and based on a validated approach used in previous literature [14, 15]. Detailed information on this algorithm and the associated medical codes used during the assessment are outlined in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Additionally, individuals were required to have continuous enrollment with medical and pharmacy benefits under a commercial, Medicare, or Medicaid healthcare plan for 12 months pre-index through at least 12 months post-index (≤ 30-day gap allowed) and at least one BMI measurement on or within the 30 days leading up/prior to the index date. Individuals who had claims for pregnancy, childbirth, or malignant cancer, or BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 (underweight) during the study period were excluded from the study.

The study population was stratified into five cohorts based on their baseline BMI: normal (≥ 18.5 to < 25 kg/m2); overweight (≥ 25 to < 30 kg/m2); Class 1 obesity (≥ 30 to < 35 kg/m2); Class 2 obesity (≥ 35 to < 40 kg/m2); and Class 3 obesity (≥ 40 kg/m2).

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to describe the demographics and clinical characteristics of participants with MTS-OA knee pain among commercial- and Medicare-insured groups. Demographic characteristics were assessed on the index date and included age, sex, geographic region, race, ethnicity, and payer type. Clinical characteristics were evaluated in the year leading to the index date and included prevalence of frequently reported obesity-related complications and overall comorbidity burden, summarized using the modified Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score that has been previously validated and published [16]. EHR-based weight and BMI were also assessed during the follow-up period.

The secondary objectives were HCRU, costs, and treatment patterns. Annualized all-cause and OA-related pharmacy, medical, and total healthcare costs were reported for individuals from the index date through the end of the post-index period. HCRU variables included all-cause and OA-specific outpatient, inpatient, and emergency department visits and inpatient days. Treatment patterns included prescription medication use, bariatric surgery, and medical services linked with obesity care (weight management, nutrition counseling, or lifestyle intervention services). These were assessed in the year prior to and the first year after the index date. Additionally, data for commercial-, Medicare-, and Medicaid-insured individuals were analyzed separately. The study used retrospective claims data, with pre-index information used to characterize baseline characteristics, and post-index data used to analyze outcomes during the study period. Total medical and total pharmacy costs were calculated by consolidating all medical and pharmacy payer claims incurred during the study period. KOA-related costs were identified by aggregating all medical payer claims with an ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for KOA in the primary position. KOA pharmacy costs were calculated by using pharmacy claims for KOA-related medications. Total healthcare and total KOA healthcare costs were calculated as the sum of their respective medical and pharmacy costs. Reversed and rejected pharmacy claims were not included in the pharmacy cost estimate. All costs in the Optum Market Clarity database reflect inflation adjustments based on the CPI to the most recent year. All costs mentioned throughout the study are reported in US dollars.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and reported separately for each cohort. Continuous variables were summarized using means [standard deviation (SD)], and categorical variables were summarized using frequency and percentages. Adjusted all-cause and KOA-related healthcare costs were estimated using a generalized linear model with a gamma distribution and a log-link function. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, payor type, baseline CCI score, and baseline cost. Adjusted costs were winsorized at a 1% threshold to mitigate the influence of extreme outliers while preserving the overall data structure. Analysis was conducted using IHD, and data analysis for cost adjustment models was conducted using R version 4.5.0.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of individuals. The mean (SD) age was higher in individuals with normal BMI (67.7 [10.9] years) than in those with overweight or other obesity classes (range: 59.2 [8.5] to 65.4 [10.4] years). More than half of the study population across all five cohorts was female, and approximately 80% of the study population was White (Table 1). The mean baseline BMI range was 23.5–45.7 kg/m2 across the different BMI classifications. Among people with obesity or overweight, a numerical trend of multiple obesity-related complications and higher mean (SD) CCI scores was observed in individuals with higher BMI than in those with lower BMI (CCI scores: normal BMI: 1.3 [1.8]; overweight: 1.1 [1.6]; Class 1 obesity: 1.2 [1.7]; Class 2 obesity: 1.3 [1.7]; and Class 3 obesity: 1.5 [1.8]). Hypertension and dyslipidemia were the most prevalent comorbidities across all cohorts, with a direct increase in prevalence with higher BMI. Similarly, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) was higher in individuals with higher obesity classes (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides insights into the clinical and economic burden of MTS-OA knee pain among people across different BMI categories, identifying a greater burden across multiple dimensions in those with higher BMI. Importantly, this study addresses a key gap in the existing literature by stratifying clinical and economic outcomes by BMI category, an area that has been underexplored in prior research on KOA burden.

In this study population, the burden of comorbidities was notably high, particularly among those with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2. This was accompanied by a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors (such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and T2D in individuals with MTS-OA knee pain across different BMI categories). In congruence with these results, recent studies have also reported that individuals with higher BMI not only report greater KOA pain and disability but also demonstrate a higher prevalence of related comorbidities, exacerbating symptoms of the disease [17, 18].

These findings showed that individuals with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 covered with commercial or Medicare insurance incurred higher observed mean all-cause and KOA-related total healthcare costs than those with BMI < 35 kg/m2. However, individuals with normal BMI incurred higher observed healthcare total costs than those with overweight or Class 1 obesity, reflecting a complex clinical profile of this group. The higher observed costs among individuals with normal BMI may reflect a U-shaped pattern between BMI and frailty, where both low and high BMI are linked to increased vulnerability and healthcare utilization [19]. Prior studies indicate that older adults with normal BMI often exhibit frailty or atypical KOA phenotypes (e.g., sarcopenia, metabolic abnormalities), which can drive greater treatment complexity and costs despite the absence of obesity-related risk factors [20]. The unexpected presentation of KOA in individuals with normal BMI may prompt more extensive diagnostics or specialist referrals, contributing to increased HCRU and costs [21].

Notably, despite these higher medical costs incurred by individuals with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2, all-cause and OA-related HCRU (the number of emergency department, outpatient, and inpatient visits) were relatively similar across obesity classes. A modest increase in inpatient days and outpatient visits among individuals in higher BMI categories (Class 2 and Class 3 obesity) may suggest that these individuals were receiving care for greater clinical complexity or undergoing more resource-intensive healthcare. This is supported by evidence indicating that higher BMI is linked with multimorbidity, including the presence of T2D, OSA, MASH, and greater clinical burden [7, 22]. Consequently, although the frequency of visits may be similar across BMI groups, individuals in higher obesity classes may undergo more intensive assessments or treatments at each visit, leading to more complex billing and higher medical costs. Importantly, these data show that medical costs, rather than pharmacy costs, were the primary driver of the overall cost burden across all groups. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that outpatient visits are increasingly billed at higher levels of complexity, reflecting either greater clinical needs or evolving billing practices [23]. The consistency of this trend of increased all-cause and OA-related costs with the increasing BMI across obesity classes within both Medicare-only and commercial-only cohorts further reinforces the robustness of these findings.

Overall, the results align with a broader concept that higher BMI had higher KOA management expenses, possibly due to a greater need for surgical intervention [24]. However, in this study, individuals with Class 3 obesity had slightly lower observed KOA-related total costs than those with Class 2 obesity. This could be due to the lower rates of KOA surgeries, particularly among individuals with Class 3 obesity. This observation aligns with the recommendations of the American Society of Orthopaedic Surgeons, which advise against elective knee surgeries in people with Class 3 obesity due to the elevated risk of postoperative complications [25].

Across all cohorts, the use of prescription pain medications for KOA pain (NSAIDs, opioids, acetaminophen, injectable corticosteroids, and others) was higher than that of obesity management treatments (GLP1-RA, other OMMs, bariatric surgery) in the first year post-index. Of note, the use of pain medications and these obesity management treatments was higher in individuals with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 than in those with BMI < 35 kg/m2. These findings suggest that BMI and weight may not be fully recognized or implemented into the management of KOA symptoms in clinical practice. According to the ACR guidelines, mechanical overload has a significant impact on obesity-related KOA; therefore, reduced joint loading through weight reduction could be an effective way to prevent KOA onset in people with obesity [11]. Future studies are warranted to understand the impact of weight-reduction interventions and the prevention of obesity progression on the healthcare burden with respect to KOA.

The results of this study have implications for health system planning and policy, and underscore the need for targeted preventive strategies, such as early weight management interventions to reduce obesity-related KOA risk. Integrating these approaches into value-based care models could help optimize resource allocation, improve patient outcomes, and contain costs by incentivizing comprehensive management of both KOA, obesity, including related complications. Health systems may benefit from multidisciplinary care pathways that address multimorbidity and support coordinated, patient-centered care for this high-risk population.

Limitations

Given the use of a retrospective claims database, the analysis has some known limitations. The use of administrative claims data, primarily for billing, may result in diagnostic or treatment inaccuracies due to coding errors or undocumented medication use and exclude free samples and over-the-counter drugs. Additional factors that may impact a patient’s health could not be captured in this study. Of note, the duration of KOA could not be assessed due to the study’s design. Given the chronic nature of KOA, accurately identifying its duration for all patients would have required an extended pre-index period. In addition, the requirement of continuous enrollment for an extended period would have substantially limited the sample size. Additionally, because data were retrospectively analyzed and direct surveying of individuals was not possible, we could not capture individuals’ nicotine use, health literacy, insurance benefit design, socioeconomic status, or educational background. These confounders may influence disease progression and treatment outcomes, and their absence could introduce some residual confounding. Additionally, the higher costs observed in the normal BMI group may be influenced by factors not fully captured in the current analysis, such as misclassification bias, selection bias (where individuals with normal BMI who have other health complications may be more likely to seek medical care and thus be overrepresented in the claims data), lower accuracy of BMI in claims data, and the presence of low muscle mass and higher fat mass, which BMI cannot detect. Furthermore, the high proportion of White individuals in the current study may reflect the demographic composition of the database rather than the actual population distribution of individuals with MTS-OA knee pain. Therefore, the findings may have limited generalizability to racial and ethnic groups that are underrepresented in our dataset. Future studies with more diverse populations are needed to better understand the impact of race and ethnicity on KOA-related outcomes. Notably, this study focuses on insured adults, and the results may not be generalizable to the entire US population. Medication use, especially for NSAIDs and acetaminophen, may be underrepresented, as some are available over the counter, which would not generate claims when purchased.

Conclusions

This study helps characterize the burden of KOA in the US and underscores the interplay of BMI with KOA HCRU. Higher classes of obesity had higher observed multimorbidity, numerically higher overall and disease-specific healthcare expenditure, and greater use of KOA medication. Moreover, by providing a detailed stratification of clinical and economic outcomes across BMI categories, including normal, overweight, and the three obesity classes, this study addresses a key gap in the existing literature, where such analyses have been limited. These insights highlight the need for novel treatment approaches that address both the core disease process of KOA and the overlapping challenges posed by obesity and related comorbidities. Such dual-action treatments could not only improve patient outcomes but also reduce the burden of multimorbidity and curtail the escalating economic impact on healthcare systems.
