Right Ventricular Isovolumetric Relaxation Time is Associated with Pulmonary Vascular Load and Functional Limitation in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
Authors: Fushi Dong, Yosuke Tanaka, Ken Okamura, Katsuyuki Higa, Shota Kaburaki, Yozo Sato, Toru Tanaka, Ayumi Shimizu, Susumu Takeuchi, Yoshiaki Kubota, Namiko Taniuchi, Koichiro Kamio, Kazuo Kasahara, Lina Wu, Masahiro Seike, Mitsunori Hino, Hiroshi Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary hypertension is a frequent complication of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and an independent determinant of prognosis. Increases in pulmonary vascular resistance often precede overt elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure, but early pulmonary vascular involvement remains difficult to detect noninvasively. Conventional echocardiographic assessment in COPD focuses mainly on right ventricular systolic indices, which may remain preserved despite increased pulmonary vascular load.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational study of 58 patients with stable COPD who underwent right heart catheterization and comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography during the same hospitalization. Right ventricular isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT), a Doppler-derived index of diastolic timing, was evaluated in relation to invasively measured pulmonary vascular resistance, dyspnea severity, exercise capacity, and pulmonary function.ResultsA total of 58 patients were included. Patients meeting hemodynamic criteria for pulmonary hypertension exhibited lower diffusing capacity, more severe dyspnea, and reduced exercise capacity despite similar airflow limitation. Pulmonary vascular resistance was significantly higher, while cardiac index remained preserved. IRT was significantly prolonged in patients with pulmonary hypertension and showed a moderate correlation with pulmonary vascular resistance (ρ = 0.56, p < 0.001), six-minute walk distance (ρ = −0.46, p < 0.001), and dyspnea severity (ρ = 0.36, p = 0.006). In contrast, conventional right ventricular systolic indices showed no significant associations with pulmonary vascular resistance.ConclusionEchocardiographic assessment of IRT may provide a physiologically grounded, noninvasive parameter associated with early right ventricular diastolic response to pulmonary vascular load.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized not only by persistent airflow limitation but also by substantial heterogeneity in symptom burden, exercise intolerance, and functional impairment.1–3 In many patients, the degree of clinical limitation cannot be fully explained by spirometric severity alone, suggesting the contribution of additional cardiopulmonary mechanisms beyond airway obstruction.3–5 Among these, pulmonary vascular involvement has increasingly been recognized as an important determinant of dyspnea, reduced exercise capacity, and prognosis in COPD.1–5

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a frequent complication of COPD and represents an important manifestation of pulmonary vascular involvement.1–3 Even in the absence of overt pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary vascular abnormalities may substantially contribute to clinical limitation in COPD, underscoring the need for better assessment of pulmonary vascular load in this population.3–5

Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is a key determinant of right ventricular (RV) afterload and reflects the integrated effects of pulmonary vascular remodeling, loss of vascular bed, hypoxic vasoconstriction, and ventilation–perfusion mismatch in COPD.4,5 Importantly, increases in PVR may occur before mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) reaches the hemodynamic threshold for pulmonary hypertension, rendering early pulmonary vascular involvement difficult to detect in routine clinical practice.4,6 Although right heart catheterization remains the gold standard for assessing pulmonary hemodynamics, its invasive nature limits widespread or repeated use in patients with COPD.6

Transthoracic echocardiography provides a noninvasive and widely available alternative for evaluating right heart function in COPD. However, conventional echocardiographic assessment has largely focused on indices of RV systolic performance, such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and RV fractional area change.7,8 These measures reflect global systolic shortening and may remain preserved despite progressive increases in pulmonary vascular load, particularly in earlier stages of pulmonary vascular involvement.3,5

Right ventricular isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT) is a Doppler-derived parameter that reflects RV diastolic timing and relaxation properties. From a physiological standpoint, increased RV afterload is expected to influence diastolic relaxation before overt systolic dysfunction becomes apparent.9,10

Because conventional echocardiographic indices such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and right ventricular fractional area change primarily reflect global RV systolic performance, they may be less sensitive to early physiological changes associated with increasing pulmonary vascular load. In contrast, IRT reflects RV diastolic timing and relaxation and may provide complementary physiological information during the early adaptation of the right ventricle to increasing pulmonary vascular load, before overt RV systolic dysfunction develops.

Other echocardiographic approaches used to assess pulmonary vascular involvement and RV function include pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAcT), RV free-wall strain, and indices of RV–pulmonary artery coupling. PAAcT is associated with pulmonary vascular load and pulmonary hypertension and primarily reflects pulmonary artery flow timing, whereas RV free-wall strain and RV–pulmonary artery coupling indices assess RV myocardial deformation and the relationship between RV function and afterload, respectively. IRT differs from these parameters by specifically reflecting the timing of RV diastolic relaxation and may therefore provide complementary physiological information that is not directly captured by PAAcT.

Alterations in RV diastolic timing may therefore represent an early manifestation of pulmonary vascular stress and impaired ventricular–vascular interaction. While IRT has been examined in other cardiopulmonary conditions,9,10 its relationship with invasively measured pulmonary vascular resistance and its clinical relevance in patients with COPD have not been systematically investigated.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between RV isovolumetric relaxation time and invasively measured pulmonary vascular resistance in patients with stable COPD who underwent right heart catheterization and comprehensive echocardiographic assessment during the same hospitalization. In addition, we examined the relationships between IRT and clinically relevant outcomes, including dyspnea severity and exercise capacity. By focusing on RV diastolic response rather than diagnostic classification of pulmonary hypertension, this study sought to clarify whether IRT reflects pulmonary vascular load and functional limitation in COPD, thereby providing clinically relevant pathophysiological insight into cardiopulmonary interaction in this disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a single-center, exploratory observational study of consecutive patients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who underwent both comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography and right heart catheterization (RHC) during the same hospitalization for evaluation of exertional dyspnea at Nippon Medical School Hospital between April 2021 and June 2025. According to the institutional study protocol, transthoracic echocardiography was routinely performed on the day of admission, followed by right heart catheterization on the following day in all patients.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nippon Medical School Hospital (Approval No. B-2020-175). The study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000059694) as a non-interventional observational study. Written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design, and an opt-out policy was publicly available on the institutional website.

Given the exploratory nature of this observational study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. At the time of registry, the target sample size was pragmatically set at 60 patients based on feasibility considerations and the anticipated number of evaluable cases required to assess moderate physiologic associations between echocardiographic indices and pulmonary hemodynamics, while allowing for potential exclusions due to incomplete datasets or clinical conditions unsuitable for analysis. Of the 60 registered patients, two were excluded before analysis because one was subsequently considered to have possible concomitant asthma and one had evidence of mild upper respiratory infection at the time of evaluation. Accordingly, 58 patients were included in the final analysis.

Eligibility Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1)age between 20 and 80 years;(2)a clinical diagnosis of COPD established by respiratory specialists according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria11(3)a stable respiratory condition for at least 3 months prior to evaluation, without acute exacerbation or modification of pharmacological therapy;(4)completion of both RHC and transthoracic echocardiography during the same hospitalization;(5)availability of complete hemodynamic data, including mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac output; and(6)availability of analyzable Doppler waveforms for right ventricular indices, particularly isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT) and pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAcT).

Clinical and Functional Assessment

Dyspnea severity was assessed using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale.13 Pulmonary function testing included measurement of percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (%FEV1) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (%DLCO), in accordance with standard recommendations.14

Exercise capacity was evaluated using both the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), performed in accordance with American Thoracic Society guidelines,15 and a treadmill exercise test (TMET) conducted using an institutional incremental protocol. The TMET began at a speed of 1 km/h with 0% incline, with stepwise increases in speed and grade every minute until symptom limitation or exhaustion. Continuous electrocardiographic and pulse oximetry monitoring were performed, and arterial blood lactate levels were measured immediately after test completion. Detailed TMET procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods S1.

Echocardiographic Assessment

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Toshiba Xario system (Toshiba Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) in accordance with American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.7,8 Right ventricular (RV) parameters assessed included pre-ejection period, isovolumetric contraction time (ICT), ejection time (ET), isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT), pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAcT), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV fractional area change (FAC), and the myocardial performance (Tei) index, calculated as (ICT + IRT)/ET.9

All Doppler-derived parameters were averaged from three consecutive cardiac cycles, or from at least five beats when feasible, during spontaneous breathing. Right Heart Catheterization

Right heart catheterization was performed via the right internal jugular vein under standard conditions. Hemodynamic measurements were obtained at end-expiration in the supine position. Cardiac output was measured using the thermodilution method according to standard practice.16

Pulmonary hypertension was defined as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg with a pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤15 mmHg, in accordance with the 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines.6 In the present study, patients meeting these criteria were categorized as the pulmonary hypertension (PH) group irrespective of pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), in order to capture pulmonary vascular involvement characterized by early mPAP elevation before overt increases in PVR. Pulmonary vascular resistance was calculated using standard formulas and analyzed as a continuous variable.17

For the exploratory subgroup analyses presented in Supplementary Table S2, patients without PH (non-PH) were analyzed as a single group irrespective of pulmonary vascular resistance, whereas patients with PH were further stratified according to pulmonary vascular resistance into a High mPAP/Low PVR subgroup (PVR ≤2 Wood units) and a High mPAP/High PVR subgroup (PVR >2 Wood units).

Reproducibility and Statistical Analysis

To assess measurement reliability, 20 echocardiographic studies were randomly selected and independently reanalyzed by two observers blinded to clinical and hemodynamic data. Intra- and interobserver reproducibility for IRT and PAAcT were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients,18 both demonstrating good-to-excellent agreement (ICC ≥0.85). Detailed reproducibility analyses are provided in Supplementary Methods S2.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate, for descriptive purposes. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Associations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP version 14 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

For the exploratory subgroup analyses presented in Supplementary Table S2, comparisons among the three subgroups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Exploratory trend analyses across the ordered subgroups (non-PH, High mPAP/Low PVR, and High mPAP/High PVR) were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 58 patients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) were included in the analysis. All patients had a history of smoking, and none were current smokers. Based on invasively measured hemodynamics obtained by right heart catheterization (RHC), 30 patients met the predefined criteria for pulmonary hypertension (PH), while 28 were classified as non-PH.

PaCO2 values were numerically higher in the PH group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (%DLCO) was significantly reduced, and modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scores were significantly higher in patients with PH. Exploratory Subgroup Analyses

In exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by PH status, the association between PVR and IRT was consistently observed in both the non-PH and PH subgroups. In contrast, associations between IRT and functional parameters were less consistent in the non-PH subgroup (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Figure S3).

Exploratory Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis

Exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated good discrimination of elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR >2 Wood units) by right ventricular isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT), with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.827 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.709–0.925). The optimal cut-off value determined using the Youden index was 50 ms, corresponding to a sensitivity of 75.7% and a specificity of 85.7% (Supplementary Figure S4).

Discussion

In this single-center observational study of patients with stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who underwent right heart catheterization and comprehensive echocardiographic assessment during the same hospitalization, pulmonary vascular involvement was associated with greater symptom burden and reduced exercise capacity. Despite comparable degrees of airflow obstruction and similar levels of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, patients meeting hemodynamic criteria for pulmonary hypertension exhibited lower diffusing capacity, more severe dyspnea, and impaired functional performance. These findings reinforce the concept that clinical limitation in COPD cannot be explained by airflow obstruction alone and likely reflects heterogeneous cardiopulmonary mechanisms, including pulmonary vascular involvement.1–3

Invasive hemodynamic assessment confirmed higher pulmonary arterial pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance in patients with pulmonary hypertension, while cardiac index and mixed venous oxygen saturation remained preserved. Echocardiographic evaluation demonstrated shortening of pulmonary artery acceleration time and prolongation of right ventricular isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT), whereas conventional systolic indices such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and right ventricular fractional area change did not differ between groups. This dissociation suggests that alterations in right ventricular timing and relaxation may accompany increased pulmonary vascular load even before overt impairment of systolic performance becomes apparent, consistent with previous observations in earlier or moderate stages of pulmonary vascular involvement.4,5

Correlation analyses further clarified the relationships among pulmonary function, invasive hemodynamics, and clinical status. Diffusing capacity showed inverse associations with pulmonary arterial pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance, whereas spirometric indices demonstrated only weak relationships with invasive hemodynamic parameters. In contrast, right ventricular IRT consistently correlated with pulmonary vascular resistance, dyspnea severity, and submaximal exercise capacity. These observations suggest that IRT reflects a clinically relevant component of cardiopulmonary impairment that is more closely linked to symptom burden and functional limitation than measures of airflow obstruction alone.

Despite the modest cohort size, the observed associations between IRT and multiple clinically relevant parameters, including pulmonary vascular resistance, dyspnea severity, and exercise limitation, were physiologically coherent and internally consistent across analyses, supporting the robustness of the findings within this exploratory cohort.

From a physiological perspective, pulmonary vascular resistance reflects the pressure–flow relationship of the pulmonary circulation, whereas IRT reflects right ventricular diastolic timing and relaxation properties under increased afterload conditions. Progressive pulmonary vascular involvement may therefore influence right ventricular relaxation before overt systolic dysfunction develops.6–8 The present findings support this concept by demonstrating that prolonged IRT accompanies increased pulmonary vascular resistance and reduced functional capacity in COPD.

In addition to increased pulmonary vascular load, pulmonary hyperinflation and elevated intrathoracic pressure in COPD may reduce venous return and biventricular preload, thereby influencing right ventricular filling and Doppler-derived diastolic time intervals, including IRT. Accordingly, the prolongation of IRT observed in the present study likely reflects the combined effects of pulmonary vascular loading and altered cardiopulmonary mechanics rather than pulmonary vascular resistance alone. This multifactorial physiological background should be considered when interpreting IRT in patients with COPD.

Exploratory ROC analysis further supported the discriminatory ability of IRT for identifying elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, with an optimal cut-off value of 50 ms. However, because this analysis was exploratory and based on a relatively small single-center cohort, the proposed threshold should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires external validation in larger independent cohorts before clinical implementation.

From a clinical perspective, IRT is not intended to replace right heart catheterization, which remains the reference standard for the assessment of pulmonary hemodynamics. Rather, IRT may serve as a complementary noninvasive echocardiographic parameter that could facilitate the identification of patients with possible pulmonary vascular involvement who may benefit from further diagnostic evaluation. Furthermore, when IRT is measured in close temporal proximity to invasively assessed pulmonary vascular resistance, the paired measurements may provide an individualized baseline for subsequent follow-up. Serial prolongation of IRT could potentially prompt consideration of increasing pulmonary vascular load and the need for further cardiopulmonary evaluation before clinically overt pulmonary hypertension or worsening symptoms become apparent. However, because the present study was cross-sectional, this potential monitoring role remains hypothetical and requires prospective validation using serial echocardiographic and hemodynamic assessments.

Exploratory subgroup analyses, in which patients without PH were analyzed as a single group and those with PH were further stratified according to pulmonary vascular resistance, demonstrated progressive changes in pulmonary vascular resistance, pulmonary artery acceleration time, and IRT across the three exploratory subgroups. Although these analyses were exploratory and not intended for diagnostic classification, they support the concept that pulmonary vascular involvement in COPD exists along a continuous spectrum rather than as a binary hemodynamic condition defined solely by pressure thresholds.9

Exercise testing provided additional insight into the clinical relevance of these findings. While maximal exercise performance assessed by treadmill testing showed limited association with IRT, submaximal exercise capacity measured by six-minute walk distance correlated with both pulmonary vascular resistance and IRT. In COPD, maximal exertion is influenced by multiple factors, including ventilatory limitation and peripheral muscle dysfunction, whereas submaximal activity more closely reflects daily functional status and clinically relevant cardiopulmonary impairment.10 The absence of significant differences in lactate responses suggests that reduced activity in this cohort was not primarily driven by peripheral muscle fatigue.

This study benefits from the integration of invasive and noninvasive assessments obtained under standardized clinical conditions at a single specialized center. Although the single-center design limits generalizability, it enabled close temporal alignment of echocardiographic and hemodynamic measurements, allowing detailed assessment of the relationship between echocardiographic findings and invasive pulmonary hemodynamics in COPD.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The modest sample size reflects the exploratory nature of this study and may limit statistical power, thereby precluding causal inference. Tricuspid regurgitation velocity was not consistently available, reflecting known technical challenges in patients with hyperinflated lungs. In addition, echocardiography and catheterization were not performed simultaneously; however, according to the institutional study protocol, echocardiography was routinely performed on the day of admission and right heart catheterization on the following day. Thus, all measurements were obtained within a short predefined interval under stable clinical conditions during the same hospitalization, minimizing the influence of acute physiological variability. Although Doppler-derived time intervals may be influenced by heart rate, heart rate was comparable between the PH and non-PH groups, making it unlikely that the observed prolongation of IRT was attributable to differences in heart rate.

Conclusion

In patients with COPD, increased pulmonary vascular load is accompanied by measurable alterations in right ventricular diastolic timing, reflected by prolongation of isovolumetric relaxation time. IRT is associated with pulmonary vascular resistance, dyspnea severity, and submaximal exercise limitation, highlighting its relevance as a marker of clinically significant cardiopulmonary impairment in COPD. Echocardiographic assessment of IRT may provide complementary insight into pulmonary vascular involvement and functional limitation in COPD, particularly in settings where invasive evaluation or maximal exercise testing is not feasible.
