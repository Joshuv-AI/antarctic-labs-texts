SUMMARY: NHTSA is proposing to amend Federal Motor Vehicle Safety 
Standard (FMVSS) No. 108, ``Lamps, reflective devices, and associated 
equipment.'' The proposed updates to the standard are intended to 
remove unnecessary requirements and to provide design flexibility 
without impact on vehicle safety. The first two proposed changes would 
amend the test voltage specification for integral beam type headlamps 
and remove requirements that apply to vertical headlamp arrangements. 
Further proposed changes address vehicles equipped with Automated 
Driving Systems (ADS) that do not have manually operated driving 
controls. Those modifications would limit the application of certain 
requirements for manually operated turn signal, hazard warning signal, 
and beam switching controls to vehicles with manually operated driving 
controls. The changes are intended to address regulatory burdens and 
costs associated with equipment that does not fulfill the same purpose 
or provide safety benefits in an ADS-equipped vehicle without manually 
operated driving controls as in a vehicle with such controls. The 
changes would also narrow certain requirements for telltales to 
vehicles with manually operated driving controls and incorporate the 
term ``telltale'' where appropriate throughout the standard. Finally, a 
proposed addition to the standard would allow cyan-colored ADS marker 
lamps as optional motor vehicle lighting equipment in ADS-equipped 
vehicles.

I. Executive Summary

    NHTSA proposes to amend Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 
(FMVSS) No. 108, ``Lamps, reflective devices, and associated 
equipment.'' FMVSS No. 108 applies to passenger cars, multipurpose 
passenger vehicles, trucks, buses, trailers (except pole trailers and 
trailer converter dollies), and motorcycles; certain retroreflective 
sheeting and reflex reflectors; and lamps, reflective devices, and 
associated equipment for replacement of like equipment on vehicles to 
which the standard applies. In an effort to enable innovation and to 
provide flexibility in vehicle design, the proposed modifications 
would: (1) update requirements related to headlamp test voltage; (2) 
provide flexibility for headlamp upper and lower beam mounting 
arrangements; (3) narrow the application of certain requirements for 
manually operated turn signal, hazard warning signal, and beam 
switching controls to vehicles with manually operated driving controls; 
(4) narrow certain requirements for telltales to vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls and incorporate the term ``telltale'' where 
appropriate throughout the standard; and (5) allow for equipping 
vehicles with automated driving system (ADS) marker lamps and 
standardizing their performance.
    First, FMVSS No. 108 currently requires headlamps in all vehicles 
for which it applies, except trailers, to meet the applicable 
performance requirements when tested at 12.8 volts. NHTSA proposes to 
revise the test voltages for integral beam headlamps. Because the 
market has moved to integral beam headlamps, NHTSA is proposing to 
eliminate standardized voltage for those lamps. The need for readily 
available replacement lamps, as a routine maintenance item, is limited 
compared to when this requirement was put into place. Other headlamp 
types such as replaceable bulb headlamps and sealed beam headlamps 
would continue to be tested at 12.8 volts.
    Second, FMVSS No. 108 states that where upper and lower beams are 
installed in a vertical orientation, the lower beams must be provided 
by the uppermost headlamp.\1\ NHTSA proposes to remove the current 
requirements associated with vertical

[[Page 67245]]

headlamp arrangements. The minimum and maximum mounting height 
requirements for headlamps would be unaffected by the removal of the 
vertical arrangement requirements. Considering advancements in lighting 
technology, NHTSA has tentatively concluded that it is possible to 
provide adequate illumination for safe driving without requiring the 
lower beam to be placed above the upper beam.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \1\ In FMVSS No. 108, upper beam means a beam intended primarily 
for distance illumination and for use when not meeting or closely 
following other vehicles. Lower beam means a beam intended to 
illuminate the road and its environs ahead of the vehicle when 
meeting or closely following another vehicle.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Third, there are several proposed changes that pertain to vehicles 
equipped with an ADS that do not have any manually operated driving 
controls such as a steering wheel or service brake pedal.\2\ Although 
these vehicles currently are not available for consumer purchase, some 
manufacturers and rideshare operators have deployed such vehicles in a 
number of cities, and several others are developing and testing such 
vehicles throughout the United States. NHTSA is engaging in a process 
to remove unnecessary barriers to technological innovation while 
ensuring motor vehicle safety is not compromised. For FMVSS No. 108, 
NHTSA proposes to remove references to a driver in the definition of 
vehicular hazard warning signal operating unit to clarify its 
application to vehicles operated by a human or an ADS. The requirement 
pertaining to ease of actuation of multiple switches by a person 
driving the vehicle would be limited to vehicles with manually operated 
driving controls.\3\ FMVSS No. 108 requires all vehicles to which it 
applies, except for trailers, to be equipped with a turn signal 
operating unit. NHTSA has tentatively concluded that the self-canceling 
and manually operated control requirements in the current standard are 
unnecessary for turn signal operating units that are installed on 
vehicles without manually operated driving controls; thus the proposed 
modifications narrow those requirements to vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls. The requirement to have a turn signal 
operating unit would be retained, such that vehicles without manually 
operated driving controls would still be required to have a means for 
operating the turn signal lamps (which could be non-manual). The 
proposed modifications would not change the self-canceling and manual 
control specifications for turn signal operating units installed on 
vehicles with manually operated driving controls. FMVSS No. 108 also 
requires all vehicles to which it applies, except trailers, to be 
equipped with a headlamp beam switching device. NHTSA proposes to limit 
the current requirements associated with operation by a driver's hand 
or foot to apply only to vehicles with manually operated driving 
controls. The proposed modifications would not change the requirements 
for vehicles with manually operated driving controls. A vehicle without 
manually operated driving controls would still be required to have a 
means of switching headlamp beams (which could be non-manual).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \2\ Per 49 CFR 571.3, manually operated driving controls means a 
system of controls:
    (i) That are used by an occupant for real-time, sustained,

speed (accelerator and brake); and
    (ii) That is positioned such that they can be used by an 
occupant, regardless of whether the occupant is actively using the 
system to manipulate the vehicle's motion.
    \3\ Edits proposed or adopted in other modernization notices 
have been structured in the negative (i.e., vehicles without 
manually operated driving controls are excepted from certain 
requirements) or in juxtaposition (i.e., separate provisions 
applicable to vehicles with or without manually operated driving 
controls are placed next to each other). This NPRM proposes edits in 
the positive (i.e., requirements are retained on vehicles with 
manually operated driving controls). NHTSA intends for each approach 
to function in the same manner and draw the same distinction between 
vehicles, where each vehicle is categorized as either with or 
without manually operated driving controls.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Fourth, NHTSA proposes to limit the requirements for telltales for 
turn signals and upper beam headlamps to apply only to vehicles with 
manually operated driving controls. NHTSA also proposes to replace the 
words ``pilot indicator'' or ``indicator'' with ``telltale'' as 
appropriate throughout the standard to align with definitions and usage 
in FMVSS No. 101, ``Controls and Displays.''
    Finally, NHTSA proposes to incorporate specifications for an ADS 
marker lamp as optional equipment in ADS-equipped vehicles. FMVSS No. 
108 currently has no specific requirements for a lamp with the purpose 
of communicating to other road users that a vehicle is operating 
without the expectation that an occupant is monitoring the roadway 
(i.e., the vehicle ADS is engaged in full, active control of the 
driving task).\4\ This NPRM proposes ADS marker lamp requirements 
applicable if such a lamp is equipped. The proposal is due to the 
development of a consensus industry standard for ADS marker lamps,\5\ 
the increased use of this type of lamp on vehicles internationally, and 
the agency's need to ensure that the new lighting performs in a manner 
that does not detract from existing levels of motor vehicle safety. 
NHTSA tentatively concludes that a standardized ADS marker lamp will 
mitigate the risk of manufacturers implementing non-standardized lamp 
applications, which may become a nuisance, cause confusion, or 
otherwise have potential negative safety outcomes. Like other lamps 
regulated through FMVSS No. 108, the proposed ADS marker lamp criteria 
include the number of lamps, mounting locations, activation, 
visibility, color, and photometric intensity.

II. Background

    FMVSS No. 108, ``Lamps, reflective devices, and associated 
equipment,'' specifies requirements for original and replacement lamps, 
reflective devices, and associated equipment. The purpose of FMVSS No. 
108 is to reduce traffic crashes, and deaths and injuries resulting 
from traffic crashes, by providing adequate illumination of the roadway 
and by enhancing the conspicuity of motor vehicles on roadways so that 
their presence is perceived, and their signals understood, both in 
daylight and in darkness or other conditions of reduced visibility.

a. Test Voltage

    In 1967, FMVSS No. 108 standardized sealed beam headlamps and 
standardized the voltages at which they were tested to be 12.8 
volts.\6\ This standardization provided commonality and supported 
readily available headlamps as replacement equipment given the 
relatively short lifespan of those headlamps. This voltage was useful 
for the lighting technology at the time and allowed headlamps to 
provide the required lighting output to satisfy the need for safety. As 
lighting technologies evolved from sealed beam headlamps into 
replaceable bulb headlamps, the voltage requirements were carried 
forward to continue providing that commonality and support the 
availability of headlamp replaceable light sources.\7\ The progression 
in headlighting system design has moved from common sealed beams to 
common headlamp replaceable light sources, to integral beam headlamps 
that are specifically designed for the particular vehicle model on 
which they are installed as original equipment. Current integral beam 
headlamps use light sources which have a much longer lifespan than the 
incandescent and halogen sources used in the past. These types of 
illumination sources are not dependent on any particular voltage to 
provide adequate

[[Page 67246]]

illumination and do not routinely fail prior to the end of the 
vehicle's lifespan. As such, NHTSA is proposing to revise the test 
voltage specifications for integral beam headlamps such that NHTSA 
tests integral beam headlamps at the voltage stipulated by the 
manufacturer. NHTSA seeks comment regarding the need to extend this 
revision to test voltage to daytime running lights.

b. Headlamp Mounting Orientation

    At the time FMVSS No. 108 was originally adopted, headlamp light 
source technology provided relatively low luminous flux. To provide the 
maximum illumination of the road in front of the vehicle, it was 
important to place the headlamps as high as practicable within limits 
to avoid excessive glare.\8\ The standard provided minimum and maximum 
heights for the placement of the upper and lower beams. An additional 
requirement for the orientation of the two beams was given for upper 
and lower beams that were vertically oriented. With the intent of 
providing the maximum amount of forward illumination by the lower beam, 
the standard requires that the lower beam be placed above the upper 
beam in a vertical headlamp arrangement.\9\ With the increase in 
luminous flux provided by current headlamp technologies, this 
requirement has become outdated. Therefore, NHTSA is proposing to 
remove the requirements for vertical headlamp arrangements to allow 
lower beam headlamps to be placed below the upper beam when vertically 
arranged.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \8\ 32 FR 2419.
    \9\ 54 FR 20066.

c. ADS-Equipped Vehicles Without Manually Operated Driving Controls

    An ADS commonly is considered to be a combination of hardware and 
software that can perform all real-time operational and tactical 
functions required to operate a motor vehicle on a sustained basis 
\10\--functions that traditionally have been performed by a human 
driver using manually operated driving controls. As defined in 49 CFR 
571.3 and used throughout this document, manually operated driving 
controls means a system of controls: (i) used by an occupant for real-
time, sustained, manual manipulation of the motor vehicle's heading 
(steering) and/or speed (accelerator and brake); and (ii) positioned 
such that they can be used by an occupant, regardless of whether the 
occupant is actively using the system to manipulate the vehicle's 
motion. In an ADS-equipped vehicle designed to be operated only by an 
ADS, manually operated driving controls are not inherently necessary.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

J3016_202104 (April 30, 2021), available at https://www.sae.org/standards/content/j3016_202104/; Tex. Transp. Code Sec.  545.451.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

identifying and addressing regulatory barriers for vehicles that lack 
traditional manual controls or have unconventional seating. The ANPRM 
focused on the challenges of testing and verifying compliance for 
vehicles without traditional manual controls. A separate notice of 
proposed rulemaking (NPRM), ``Occupant Protection for Automated Driving

rule, ``Occupant Protection for Vehicles with Automated Driving 
Systems,'' published on March 30, 2022.\14\ The rulemaking focused on 
crashworthiness standards for ADS-equipped vehicles without manual 
driving controls, revising definitions and updating occupant protection 
standards to exclude vehicles specifically designed not to contain any 
occupants. The 2022 final rule also established the definition for 
manually operated driving controls. On December 3, 2020, NHTSA 
published an ANPRM titled, ``Framework for Automated Driving Systems,'' 
to discuss and request comment on the manner in which the agency would 
define, assess, and manage objectively the safety of ADS performance 
while ensuring the needed flexibility to enable further innovation.\15\ 
The ANPRM included recognition of a phased approach to addressing ADS 
safety, including NHTSA's modernization of the FMVSS for ADS-equipped

    This NPRM continues this task as applied to FMVSS No. 108, which 
was developed at a time when all vehicles were operated by human 
drivers who interacted with the manually operated driving controls 
installed in the vehicle, including the turn signal operating unit and 
the beam switching unit. As such, the standard sets driver-based 
requirements for these systems' controls which have had the unintended 
effect of creating regulatory barriers for ADS-equipped vehicles 
without manually operated driving controls. Therefore, NHTSA is 
proposing to revise the definition of vehicular hazard warning system 
operating unit and narrow the application of certain requirements for 
the turn signal, hazard warning signal, and beam switching controls. 
That said, NHTSA maintains that any vehicle equipped with manually 
operated driving controls, regardless of whether it is equipped with an 
ADS, must continue to meet all existing safety requirements.

d. ADS Marker Lamps

Existing and Emerging Standards
    As ADS technology has developed, some participants in the industry 
have sought to create a standardized indication of active ADS operation 
by means of a marker lamp on the vehicle. SAE Standard J3134, 
originally issued May 2019 and revised December 2025, details the use 
and characteristics of an ``Automated Driving System (ADS) Marker 
Lamp.'' In this notice, reference to SAE Standard J3134 means the 
December 2025 version unless stated otherwise. The 2019 standard cited 
studies ``suggest[ing] that road users prefer ADS-equipped vehicles to 
have additional signals to indicate when the vehicle's ADS is 
engaged.'' The original version also emphasized alignment with NHTSA's 
Federal Automated Vehicles Policy and the United Nations Economic 
Commission for Europe (UNECE) Working Party on Lighting and Light 
Signaling (GRE) for ADS vehicle communication with other road users. 
The 2019 version of SAE J3134 specified requirements for marker lamps 
on the

[[Page 67247]]

front of the vehicle while the updated version of the standard has 
specifications for rear and side ADS marker lamps as well. The updated 
rationale explains that rear and side ADS marker lamps are considered 
for regulation in UNECE and China, and that the American Association of 
Motor Vehicle Administrators (AAMVA) recommends their implementation to 
support law enforcement's ability to identify if an ADS-equipped 
vehicle is being operated by the ADS or the driver.
    The UNECE GRE established a Task Force to develop a harmonized 
standard for ADS lamps and has collaborated with stakeholders, 
including SAE International and Mercedes-Benz. UNECE has defined the 
target beneficiaries of the standard to be law enforcement, 
pedestrians, and other road users.\16\ The UNECE Working Party on 
Automated/Autonomous and Connected Vehicles (GRVA) has consulted with 
the UNECE GRE Task Force on Autonomous Vehicle Signaling Requirements 
(AVSR) and established its own recommendations. In general, ``GRVA 
recommends the establishment of uniform provisions for a light signal 
to communicate the operational status of the ADS,'' noting potential 
benefits for law enforcement.\17\ GRVA, though, does not recommend 
requiring additional lighting for ADS-operated vehicles in UNECE 
regulation due to uncertainty and the need for further research.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

External Light-Signalling,'' Informal Document GRE-90-06, (April 
2024), available at https://unece.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/GRE-90-06e.pdf (last accessed March 31, 2026).

Supporting Research and Recommendations
    The results of an international survey led by the Japanese National 
Traffic Safety and Environment Laboratory were presented at the 92nd 
GRE in April 2025.\18\ The survey included over 4,400 drivers in the 
United Kingdom, Japan, United States, and Germany. These respondents 
were asked to provide their opinion regarding the need for, and use of, 
marker lamps to indicate operation of a vehicle by an ADS. The majority 
of respondents in each country, including upwards of 80 percent of 
respondents in the United States and the United Kingdom, responded 
affirmatively as to whether ADS-equipped vehicles should be equipped 
with a marker lamp. Less specific to marker lamps, around 70 percent of 
respondents in each country affirmed that the operating status (ADS or 
manual driving mode) of an ADS-equipped vehicle should be 
distinguishable from the outside of the vehicle.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Mercedes-Benz expressed interest in increasing other road users' 
acceptance and confidence in ADS-equipped vehicles and claimed that the 
ADS marker lamp is an intuitive way to communicate that the ADS is 
engaged in operating the vehicle. Mercedes-Benz presented 
recommendations in a meeting with NHTSA and at the 89th GRE session via 
the AVSR task force.\19\ Mercedes-Benz provided an analysis with 
respect to the placement and integration of ADS marker lamps and stated 
that a 360-degree view of the cyan lamps would be beneficial to law 
enforcement and other road users without leading to distraction or 
confusion. Mercedes-Benz suggested that concerns regarding ADS marker 
lamps, such as a lack of understanding and confusion about the meaning, 
could be mitigated through educational measures before and during 
market launch. Mercedes-Benz has also conducted a two-year on-road 
validation of ADS marker lamps in California from 2023 to 2025, using 
five vehicles to gather data to understand how other road users 
interacted with vehicles with ADS marker lamps.

    In response to the changing market and materials reviewed and 
submitted, NHTSA is proposing to amend FMVSS No. 108 to incorporate 
standardized specifications for front, rear, and side ADS marker lamps 
if they are installed on a motor vehicle. NHTSA is not proposing to 
mandate ADS marker lamps on all vehicles equipped with ADS. The 
proposal largely, but not entirely, aligns with the specifications in 
SAE J3134.

III. Proposed Changes

a. Integral Beam Headlamp Test Voltage

    FMVSS No. 108 S14.2.5.4 states that headlamps are tested with a 
direct current test voltage of 12.8 volts 20 mv, and does 
not distinguish between headlamp type. The standardization of headlamp 
voltage supported readily available replacement lamps for incandescent 
and halogen technology which did not last the life of the vehicle.\20\ 
In S14.6.4.1.1, the test voltage and current are specified. The product 
of the voltage and current is power. NHTSA is proposing to change the 
test from specific voltage and current values to power in watts. This 
will allow a manufacturer of an integral beam headlamp that operates at 
a design voltage other than 12.8 volts to have an equivalent test to 
systems that operate at 12.8 volts.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \20\ 54 FR 20084.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    FMVSS No. 108 S6.5.3.2 requires that each original and replacement 
equipment headlamp be marked with its voltage and part or trade number. 
In order to ensure that integral beam headlamps are tested at the 
proper voltage for the given device if different from 12.8 volts, NHTSA 
is proposing to test integral beam headlamps at the voltage for which 
they are marked. NHTSA is not proposing to change the marking 
requirements for integral beam headlamp assemblies. As a result, the 
voltage for which a headlamp has been certified will continue to be 
marked on each lamp and can be referenced for independent testing.
    NHTSA has not identified that the same considerations exist for 
replaceable bulb and sealed beam headlamps. Therefore, NHTSA is not 
proposing to change the requirements for those headlamp types, but 
seeks comment on whether doing so is appropriate.

b. Vertical Headlamp Arrangements

    For vehicles with a vertical arrangement of the upper and lower 
beam headlamps, the standard requires that the lower beams be placed 
above upper beams. With the increase in luminous flux provided by 
current headlamp technologies, NHTSA has tentatively concluded that the 
requirement for placing the lower beam above the upper beam is no 
longer necessary and may contribute negatively to glare.\21\ In 
developing this proposal, NHTSA considered increasing the minimum 
luminous flux output requirements to reflect the current market, but 
tentatively concluded that change is not needed because current minimum 
headlamp performance for

[[Page 67248]]

seeing distance is being driven by market forces and not regulation.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \21\ NHTSA, Summary of Headlamp Research at NHTSA, DOT HS 811 
006 (July 2008), available at https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/811006.pdf.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    NHTSA proposes to remove the requirement in S6.1.3.5.1 that lower 
beams be placed above upper beams in a vertical headlamp arrangement. 
Doing so would allow manufacturers design flexibility to change the 
arrangement of the upper and lower beams in a vertical headlamp 
arrangement without affecting safety. NHTSA does not propose any 
changes to the minimum and maximum heights for the placement of the 
upper and lower beams, which ensures that there are no negative safety 
outcomes from the proposed change to the allowed lamp arrangement. The 
requirements for a horizontal headlamp arrangement also remain 
unchanged, as the lower beam being outboard of the upper beam provides 
secondary functionality in showing the width of the vehicle.

c. Turn Signal, Hazard Warning Signal, and Beam Switching Equipment

    NHTSA seeks to address the application of certain existing crash 
avoidance standards to ADS-equipped vehicles without manually operated 
driving controls. In this document, NHTSA proposes to limit the 
application of certain requirements for turn signal, beam switching, 
and hazard warning signal controls to vehicles with manually operated 
driving controls. The proposed changes are not intended to address ADS 
performance, but rather to address which turn signal, beam switching, 
and hazard warning signal equipment is necessary in a vehicle designed 
solely to be operated by an ADS. NHTSA expects all ADS-operated 
vehicles to be equipped with the required vehicle lamps, and to 
activate and deactivate the lamps in accordance with the rules of the 
road. In addition to FMVSS enforcement, NHTSA will not hesitate to use 
its strong and broad defect enforcement authority to address unsafe ADS 
performance.
    FMVSS No. 108 requires vehicles to be equipped with a turn signal 
operating unit that meets the requirements specified in S9. A turn 
signal operating unit is the part of a turn signal system by which the 
operator of a vehicle causes the signal units to function. A turn 
signal operating unit that is installed on passenger cars, multipurpose 
passenger vehicles, trucks, and buses less than 2,032 mm in overall 
width is required to be self-canceling by steering wheel rotation and 
capable of cancellation by a manually operated control. FMVSS No. 108 
also requires all vehicles for which it applies, except for trailers, 
to be equipped with a headlamp beam switching device. The term 
``headlamp beam switching device'' is not defined in S4, Definitions. 
However, S9.4 headlamp beam switching device, requires that each 
vehicle have a means for switching between lower and upper beams 
designed and located so that it may be operated conveniently by a 
simple movement of the driver's hand or foot.\22\

    \22\ 49 CFR 571.108 S9.4.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    For an ADS-equipped vehicle without manually operated driving 
controls, the agency has tentatively concluded there is no safety need 
for turn signal operating units to be self-canceling by steering wheel 
rotation. These vehicles lack steering wheels, and the vehicle's 
heading is controlled by the ADS. Although NHTSA has considered 
creating an equivalent requirement based on measurement within the 
steering system, NHTSA has determined tentatively that such a 
requirement is unnecessary and overly prescriptive because turn signal 
activation and cancellation will be programmed within the ADS to comply 
with traffic laws, and unlike a human driver, the system cannot forget 
that the turn signals are active. Therefore, the safety purpose of the 
self-canceling requirement for turn signal operating units does not 
exist for ADS-equipped vehicles without manually operated driving 
controls.
    In addition, for such vehicles, NHTSA has tentatively concluded 
that there is no safety purpose to require that the turn signal 
operating unit be manually cancelable in vehicles that are operated by 
an ADS and do not have manually operated driving controls. Occupants in 
such vehicles have no responsibility for activating or deactivating the 
turn signal operating units. The operation of the turn signal units is 
managed by the ADS. For these reasons, NHTSA proposes to modify S9.1.1 
to except vehicles without manually operated driving controls from the 
requirements of S9.1.1 related to steering wheel rotation and manual 
cancellation.
    Similarly, the agency has tentatively concluded there is no safety 
need for headlamp beam switching devices installed on ADS-equipped 
vehicles without manually operated driving controls to be operable by 
hand or foot. Headlamp beam selection is an important safety 
responsibility because surrounding vehicles may experience hazardous 
levels of glare if a vehicle operator activates the upper beam when 
meeting or closely following other vehicles. For vehicles without 
manual controls, the ADS, and not a vehicle occupant, would be 
responsible for switching between lower and upper beams consistent with 
the need for visibility and the rules of the road on which the vehicle 
operates. NHTSA proposes to modify S9.4 through reordering of the text 
and additional language to clarify the exception to the design 
requirement for the headlamp beam switching device in vehicles without 
manually operated driving controls.
    If finalized as proposed, vehicles without manually operated 
driving controls still would be required to be equipped with a turn 
signal operating unit, because turn signals are intended to benefit 
other road users, and a headlamp beam switching device (that is not 
required to be designed and located for hand or foot operation), 
because an ADS may still rely on the increased visibility provided by 
high beams.\23\ However, the specifications related to steering wheel 
rotation and to hand or foot operation would no longer apply to the 
turn signal operating units and headlamp beam switching devices in 
those vehicles. In addition, the proposed updates would not affect the 
requirements for vehicles with manually operated driving controls. 
NHTSA seeks comment on whether this approach ensures all vehicles are 
equipped with appropriate lighting equipment while removing design 
barriers to vehicles without manual controls.

    NHTSA also proposes to update the language and clarify the 
requirements for actuation of the vehicular hazard warning signal 
operating unit. In FMVSS No. 108, vehicular hazard warning signal 
operating unit is defined as a driver-controlled device which causes 
all required turn signal lamps to flash simultaneously to indicate to 
approaching drivers the presence of a vehicular hazard. The performance 
requirements in section S9.6.2 require that, if actuation of the hazard 
function requires the operation of more than one switch, a means must 
be provided for actuating all switches simultaneously by a single 
driver action.
    NHTSA proposes to remove references to a driver in the definition 
of ``vehicular hazard warning signal operating unit'' to clarify its 
application to vehicles operated by a person or an ADS. The revised 
definition would refer to the operator of a vehicle rather than the 
driver. The requirement for the vehicular hazard warning signal 
operating unit in S9.6.2 pertaining to ease of actuation of multiple 
switches by a person driving the vehicle would also be amended to apply 
only to vehicles with more manually operated driving controls. NHTSA 
tentatively

[[Page 67249]]

concludes that the requirement only serves a safety purpose in a 
vehicle equipped with manually operated driving controls. The 
requirement is intended to ensure ease of operation for a person 
driving a vehicle, such that multiple actions are not required to 
activate the hazard warning signal. However, an ADS would be capable of 
activating multiple switches simultaneously without issue.
Applicability of Existing NHTSA Chief Counsel Interpretations
    Pursuant to 49 CFR 501.8(d)(5), the Administrator has delegated to 
the Chief Counsel the authority to ``[i]ssue authoritative 
interpretations of the statutes administered by NHTSA and the 
regulations issued by the agency.'' Over the years, NHTSA Chief 
Counsels have issued a number of interpretations related to automated 
activation of the hazard warning signal. NHTSA has tentatively 
determined that, if finalized, this amendment would not change the 
substance of NHTSA's existing interpretations regarding automatic 
activation of the hazard warning signal by vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls. In a letter of interpretation to John 
Frooshani of North American Subaru in 2025, the Chief Counsel explained 
that ``NHTSA's interpretations of `vehicular hazard warning signal 
operating unit' read FMVSS No. 108 as broadly restricting automatic 
activation of the hazard signal . . . but [allow] automatic activation 
on a case-by-case basis under limited circumstances consistent with the 
purpose of the hazard warning signal stated in the standard.'' \24\ 
These interpretations are based on two aspects of FMVSS No. 108. First, 
that the term ``driver controlled device'' in the definition of 
vehicular hazard warning signal operating unit means that the signal 
may not be activated automatically.\25\ Second, that certain automatic 
activations would impair the effectiveness of lighting equipment 
required by the standard in violation of S6.2.1.\26\ If this proposed 
rule is finalized, for vehicles with manually-operated driving 
controls, NHTSA would no longer read the definition of vehicular hazard 
warning signal operating unit to bar automatic activation on the 
grounds that the operating unit must be exclusively driver controlled. 
However, NHTSA would carry forward the conclusions of its 
interpretations on the second basis. That is, in vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls, automatic hazard signal activation is 
supplemental equipment and S6.2.1 therefore limits automatic hazard 
signal activation to circumstances that do not impair required 
lighting.\27\ Under this reading, NHTSA considers whether the 
activations impair other required lighting or if they lack any 
relationship to the original purpose of the hazard warning signal or 
could create confusion about the signal's meaning. Such inappropriate 
activations include, for example, during braking events,\28\ after 
``sudden release of the accelerator pedal,'' \29\ or as a 
``deceleration warning system'' \30\ where it would conflict with the 
purpose of the stop lamps. In vehicles without manually operated 
driving controls, automatic vehicular hazard warning signal operation 
would not be viewed as supplemental equipment subject to S6.2.1. NHTSA 
seeks comment on whether these interpretations are appropriate to 
address ADS activation of the hazard warning signal, or whether 
incorporation into regulatory text is necessary.

d. Telltales

    Section S6.6 of FMVSS No. 108 requires that vehicles for which the 
standard applies be equipped with a turn signal pilot indicator, an 
upper beam headlamp indicator, and a vehicular hazard warning signal 
pilot indicator that shall be in front of and in clear view of the 
driver. Paragraph S9.3.6 also requires a change in the illumination of 
the turn signal pilot indicator to indicate failure of one or more turn 
signal lamps. Paragraph S9.8.4 specifies that if the vehicular hazard 
warning signal pilot indicator is not combined with the turn signal 
pilot indicator, it must emit a red color and have a minimum area 
equivalent to a 0.5 in diameter circle.
    NHTSA proposes to rename the ``indicators'' to ``telltales'' to 
align with the definitions of telltales and indicators found in FMVSS 
No. 101. A telltale is defined in FMVSS No. 101 as ``an optical signal 
that, when illuminated, shows the actuation of a device, a correct or 
improper functioning or condition, or a vehicle system's failure to 
function.'' FMVSS No. 101 defines indicator as a ``device that shows 
the magnitude of the physical characteristics that the instrument is 
designed to sense.'' The turn signal pilot indicator, upper beam 
headlamp indicator, and vehicular hazard warning signal pilot indicator 
convey information related to actuation of a device or failure of a 
device, which aligns with the definition of a telltale rather than an 
indicator. Changing the nomenclature in FMVSS No. 108 for consistency 
will improve clarity of the standard.
    The telltales (referred to as indicators in the current standard) 
for turn signal lamps, upper beam headlamps, and the vehicle hazard 
warning signal specified in FMVSS No. 108 are intended for, and are 
required to be in view of, the driver of the vehicle to show the 
activation state of the respective device. This signal provides 
information to the driver to confirm that the turn signal lamp, upper 
beam headlamp, or hazard warning signal is in the desired state. A 
driver is defined in 49 CFR 571.3 as the occupant of a motor vehicle 
seated immediately behind the steering control system. By this 
definition, a vehicle without manually operated driving controls does 
not have a driver.
    In an ADS-operated vehicle, the ADS, rather than a human driver, 
would control the activation of the turn signals, headlamps, and hazard 
warning signal. NHTSA also expects an ADS to be aware of the 
operational status of each safety critical vehicle system and subsystem 
and respond appropriately to any identified degradations, failures, and 
malfunctions. Because the turn signals and headlamps would not be 
controlled by a person, NHTSA has tentatively concluded that the 
corresponding telltales do not address a safety need in ADS-equipped 
vehicles that do not have manually operated driving controls. The ADS 
would control and monitor the status of the headlamps and signals as 
part of normal vehicle operation. For these reasons, NHTSA proposes to 
limit the requirements to display these telltales to vehicles with 
manually operated driving controls. Manufacturers and fleet operators 
of ADS-equipped vehicles may choose to provide vehicle passengers with 
trip information, and NHTSA anticipates that they would do so to 
satisfy consumer demand even if not strictly necessary to meet the need

[[Page 67250]]

for motor vehicle safety. Because turn signal telltales are also used 
to indicate a lamp failure in S9.3.6, NHTSA is proposing an alternate 
requirement in S9.3.6 to provide a lamp failure telltale in clear view 
of all designated seating positions if the vehicle does not have turn 
signal telltales. NHTSA requests comment on the proposed changes to 
telltale requirements.
    NHTSA also seeks comment on whether the vehicular hazard warning 
signal telltale requirement should apply to vehicles without manual 
controls. The current proposal does not limit the requirement for the 
hazard warning signal telltale to vehicles with manually operated 
driving controls. Whereas turn signals and upper beam headlamps are 
routinely used during normal driving, the vehicular hazard warning 
signals are used to indicate to approaching drivers the presence of a 
vehicular hazard. Vehicle occupants may benefit from knowing that the 
ADS determined it appropriate to activate the hazard warning signal. 
Passengers presented with a hazard warning signal telltale can make 
decisions about whether and how to continue using the vehicle. NHTSA 
plans to initiate a separate rulemaking to address requirements for 
telltales and indicators in ADS-equipped vehicles across multiple 
FMVSS.\31\ NHTSA further encourages the use of supplemental 
notifications communicating safety-relevant information to passengers 
of ADS-operated vehicles via app notifications, in-vehicle alerts, or 
other methods. The specific format and content of such notifications 
would be determined by the manufacturer.

e. ADS Marker Lamps

    NHTSA is proposing to amend FMVSS No. 108 to incorporate 
standardized specifications for front, rear, and side ADS marker lamps 
if they are installed on a motor vehicle. NHTSA has long maintained 
that highway traffic safety is enhanced by the familiarity of drivers 
and other road users (e.g., pedestrians and cyclists) with established 
lighting schemes, which facilitates their ability to recognize the 
meaning that the lamps convey instantly and to respond accordingly. 
FMVSS No. 108 stipulates standardized lamp performance criteria, both 
for required lighting and certain optional lighting equipment, 
including the number of lamps, mounting locations, activation, 
visibility, color and lamp intensity. The standard does not bar 
installation of supplemental lighting equipment. However, under FMVSS 
No. 108 S6.2.1, no additional lamp, reflective device, or other motor 
vehicle equipment is permitted to be installed that impairs the 
effectiveness of lighting equipment required by FMVSS No. 108.\32\ In 
addition, 49 U.S.C. 30122 prohibits a manufacturer, distributor, 
dealer, rental company, or motor vehicle repair business from 
installing aftermarket lighting equipment if doing so would knowingly 
take the vehicle out of compliance with FMVSS No. 108, such as by 
impairing the effectiveness of required lighting.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \32\ For supplemental lighting equipment, impairment is 
generally evaluated on a case-by-case basis and NHTSA typically 
considers four aspects: intensity (i.e., brightness), mounting 
location, activation pattern, and color. E.g., Letter to Paul Schaye 
(Sep. 9, 2019), available at https://www.nhtsa.gov/interpretations/571108-ama-schaye-front-color-changing-light (last accessed January 
29, 2026). These four aspects are non-exhaustive, and NHTSA can 
consider other aspects where relevant.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    NHTSA is concerned that the introduction into the marketplace of 
differing and contradictory supplemental lighting intended to indicate 
ADS operation may generate confusion or distraction among drivers and 
other road users, which could have negative safety outcomes. In 
addition, certain applications, when installed as supplemental 
equipment, may cause impairment of equipment required by FMVSS No. 108, 
such as by masking nearby signal lighting, in violation of S6.2.1. 
Therefore, NHTSA has tentatively concluded that standardizing 
performance aspects for ADS marker lamps in FMVSS No. 108 will ensure 
that ADS marker lamps perform consistently throughout the fleet, limit 
the implementation of non-standardized lamp applications that may have 
potential negative safety outcomes by interfering with current required 
lighting equipment or distracting or confusing drivers, and provide 
regulatory certainty for manufacturers by establishing specifications 
for the lamp that manufacturers can implement without risking a 
violation of S6.2.1.
    NHTSA has tentatively determined that it lacks sufficient 
information to conclude that there is a safety need to require the 
inclusion of ADS marker lamps on all vehicles equipped with an ADS. 
Therefore, NHTSA is proposing an ``if equipped'' standard for ADS 
marker lamps. This is intended to ensure that, if equipped, the lamps 
meet a minimum standard for performance. NHTSA is not proposing to 
mandate that all ADS-equipped vehicles have an ADS marker lamp but 
simply that any ADS marker lamp a manufacturer chooses to include must 
meet the proposed requirements. As this proposal would explicitly 
permit the optional installation and activation of ADS marker lamps in 
accordance with the proposed activation criteria, this would also 
prohibit ADS marker lamp installation on vehicles without ADS 
functionality.
    To determine the appropriate specifications, NHTSA reviewed 
existing research and SAE Standards J3134 and J578. The proposed 
specifications are as follows.
    With regard to activation pattern, NHTSA proposes that the lamp be 
``steady burning'' when activated, which is consistent with SAE J3134 
and with other marker lamps, such as identification lamps and clearance 
lamps, specified in FMVSS No. 108, but seeks comment on allowing ADS 
marker lamps to be flashed for signaling purposes. In addition to 
indicating ADS engagement, ADS marker lamps could potentially be used 
for signaling purposes to indicate the intended movement of the vehicle 
for the benefit of pedestrians and other road users. NHTSA is not aware 
of any research supporting this usage and seeks research-based comments 
related to signaling the vehicle's intended movement to pedestrians and 
other road users.
    With regard to color, NHTSA is proposing that the lamps be cyan, 
the blue-green color defined in SAE J578. This is consistent with SAE 
J3134, which cited studies that support use of a unique color for the 
ADS marker lamp.\33\ NHTSA is concerned that if a current color 
standardized in FMVSS No. 108 for specific uses in required lighting, 
such as amber, red, or white, were to be used for the ADS marker lamp, 
it would not have clear meaning. SAE stated that experts from 
manufacturers, suppliers, and research organizations evaluated 
potential ADS lamp colors and that these evaluations supported the use 
of blue-green light color for ADS lamps.\34\ SAE concluded that a 
unique color allows greater flexibility in mounting location and 
facilitates innovation for new automotive ADS lamps and chose the blue-
green color defined in SAE J578. NHTSA seeks comment on the proposed 
ADS marker lamp color.

    Regarding location and number of lamps, NHTSA proposes that, if a

of the front, sides and rear of the vehicle. Therefore, there would be 
a minimum of four and a maximum of eight ADS marker lamps. The location 
and quantity of ADS marker lamps is consistent with SAE J3134. NHTSA is 
proposing a minimum mounting height of 15 inches. This is consistent 
with other lamps and serves to keep lamps clear of road debris, reduce 
likelihood of damage, and provide visibility. NHTSA proposes that the 
lamps are allowed to be mounted inside or outside of the vehicle within 
the mounting location and height limits as specified in Table I.
    The proposed intensity requirements include two sets of photometric 
intensities, one for when headlamps (or parking lamps) are deactivated 
and the other for when headlamps (or parking lamps) are activated. SAE 
J3134 contains photometry specifications for daytime and nighttime 
operation, which it defines based on ``when exterior ambient light 
conditions warrant (or do not warrant) the use of headlamps.'' FMVSS 
No. 108 does not contain requirements for when headlamps must be active 
based on time of day or ambient lighting conditions. As such, NHTSA is 
proposing to establish photometric performance based on the actual 
activation state of the headlamps. There are common situations during 
daylight conditions, such as rain or fog, where headlamps may be 
illuminated. NHTSA seeks comment on whether the proposed ``headlamp 
activated'' specifications below are sufficiently intense for such 
circumstances.
    NHTSA is also proposing photometric intensity requirements that are 
different from those specified in SAE J3134. One goal of the proposed 
photometry specifications is to ensure that the effectiveness of the 
required lamps is not decreased while the ADS marker lamp is 
illuminated. However, the SAE standard uses photometric intensities 
that NHTSA has tentatively concluded would interfere with the 
visibility of required signal lamps. Therefore, NHTSA proposes that the 
front ADS marker lamps have the same minimum and maximum values as 
parking lamps when the headlamps are activated. Consistent with SAE 
J3134, NHTSA derived the proposed minimum photometry requirements when 
the headlamps are deactivated by multiplying the darkness minimums by 
five times the values for when the headlamps are activated. For 
instance, the minimum value proposed for a front ADS marker lamp at 
test point H-V is 4 cd (headlamps activated). NHTSA proposes a minimum 
value of 20 cd (4 cd times 5) for when the headlamps are deactivated. 
This will permit the lamps to be seen both during the daylight and in 
dark ambient conditions. The maximum value for the front ADS marker 
lamp is proposed to be 125 cd in both daylight and darkness conditions. 
This value is consistent with the maximum allowable intensity for a 
parking lamp. NHTSA has tentatively concluded that the maximum 
intensity value of 125 cd is sufficient for the front mounted ADS 
marker lamp(s) to be seen in both ambient lighting conditions. NHTSA 
also proposes a maximum photometric intensity ratio to the front turn 
signal lamp for installations that combine the front turn signal and 
the front ADS marker lamp to ensure visibility of the turn signal 
lamps. NHTSA proposes that ratio be consistent with the ratios required 
for a parking lamp that is combined with a turn signal lamp.
    The proposed rear ADS marker lamp minimum and maximum photometric 
intensity values are aligned with a taillamp's one lighted section 
value when the headlamps are activated. NHTSA proposes that the minimum 
values be multiplied by five when the headlamps are deactivated. 
Photometric intensity ratios are proposed for when the rear ADS marker 
lamp is combined with a rear turn signal lamp or stop lamp. The 
proposed ratios are equal to the ratios required when a taillamp is 
combined with a stop lamp.
    The proposed side ADS marker lamp minimum photometric intensity 
values are aligned with amber side marker lamp photometric intensities 
for the headlamps activated condition. NHTSA proposes that the minimum 
photometric intensity values for the side ADS marker lamps be 
multiplied by five when the headlamps are deactivated. Since the 
existing side marker lamp photometric intensity requirements do not 
have a maximum, NHTSA is proposing that the side ADS marker lamp 
maximum be the same as the rear ADS marker lamp.
    The proposed geometric visibility requirements for front and rear 
ADS marker lamps are consistent with others shown in FMVSS No. 108 
Table V-c, ``Visibility Requirements of Installed Lighting Devices.'' 
When the front and rear ADS marker lamps are activated, the proposed 
minimum luminous intensity is 0.3 cd when headlamps are deactivated and 
0.05 cd when headlamps are activated. Other considerations for 
photometric intensity include visibility to road users near the 
vehicle, activation in relation to conditions when headlamps are 
deactivated or activated, and mounting location. SAE J3134 incorporates 
the photometric test points from the UNECE signal lamp photometric test 
points.\36\ Consistent with FMVSS No. 108 and J3134, a 2.5 times 
multiplication factor for the minimum values is proposed for ADS lamps 
in close proximity to headlamps, fog lamps, and daytime running lamps.

Effective Date
    As provided by 49 U.S.C. 30111(d), an FMVSS may not become 
effective before the 180th day after the standard is prescribed or 
later than one year after it is prescribed. However, NHTSA may provide 
a different effective date after finding, for good cause shown, that a 
different effective date is in the public interest. NHTSA must publish 
the reasons supporting such a finding. Similarly, 5 U.S.C. 553(d) 
provides that a final rule cannot become effective until at least 30 
days after the date of publication except, among other reasons, the 
rule grants or recognizes an exemption, relieves a restriction, or for 
good cause found and published with the rule. It is in the public 
interest for this proposed rule, if adopted, to be effective 
immediately. This proposed rule would: (1) update requirements related 
to headlamp test voltage; (2) provide flexibility for headlamp upper 
and lower beam mounting arrangements; (3) narrow the application of 
certain requirements for manually operated turn signal, hazard warning 
signal, and beam switching controls to only vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls; (4) narrow certain requirements for 
telltales to vehicles with manually operated driving controls and 
incorporate the term ``telltale'' where appropriate throughout the 
standard; and (5) allow for equipping vehicles with ADS marker lamps 
and standardizing their performance. All of these proposed requirements 
increase flexibility for manufacturers, and as such, there is no need 
for lead time for regulated entities to comply with this proposed rule.

Management and Budget as a significant regulatory action under E.O. 
12866. This proposed rule, if finalized as proposed, is expected to be 
an E.O. 14192 deregulatory action because it removes unnecessary 
regulatory burdens for the reasons discussed above. At this stage, the 
agency has not quantified any potential benefits or costs.
    The proposed changes to the requirements for integral beam headlamp 
test voltage and vertical headlamp arrangements do not have any 
anticipated regulatory costs, as the changes would remove requirements 
that are no longer necessary. Likewise, as it relates to the proposed 
changes to address vehicles equipped with ADS systems that do not have 
manually operated driving controls, NHTSA does not anticipate any 
incremental costs associated with the modifications because they would 
remove unnecessary requirements without adding any new requirements. 
NHTSA does not anticipate any safety disbenefits for the proposed 
changes. In addition to cost savings resulting from reduced compliance 
costs, the benefits of the proposed modifications to the turn signal 
and beam switching control requirements would be potential safety 
benefits from removing the ability of an occupant to interfere with the 
control of the various lamps, which could lead to safety problems for 
other road users. Regarding the new proposed provision that would allow 
for cyan-colored ADS marker lamps as optional motor vehicle lighting 
equipment, NHTSA does not anticipate that any manufacturers would incur 
any incremental costs. NHTSA would only be allowing the use of these 
lamps and not requiring them, and thus manufacturers that do not wish 
to include the cyan lamps would not need to. The agency believes that 
the standardization proposed here may have

[[Page 67253]]

some safety benefits, as it would reduce confusion for other road users 
by making clear that cyan lights mean a vehicle is being operated 
without the expectation that a human occupant is monitoring the roadway 
and ensuring that ADS marker lamp implementations do not impair the 
performance of required lighting. However, due to several limitations, 
NHTSA is unable to estimate the potential safety benefits. Given the 
still-developing nature of this market, NHTSA cannot quantify the 
number of vehicles that may be affected by this proposed rule. Further, 
without this regulatory change, NHTSA believes that these vehicles 
would seek exemptions from this standard, rather than include the 
unnecessary equipment or, in the case of the ADS marker lamps, not 
provide additional lighting. Finally, though NHTSA could seek to 
quantify avoided compliance costs on a per-vehicle basis, any estimate 
based on traditional vehicle configurations may not be accurate, given 
the different design and nature of ADS-equipped vehicles without 
manually operated driving controls. NHTSA requests comment on these 
assumptions and any other information that could help quantify their 
impacts in the final rule.

Executive Order 13132 (Federalism)

    NHTSA has examined this rule pursuant to E.O. 13132, ``Federalism'' 
(64 FR 43255, Aug. 10, 1999), and concluded that no additional 
consultation with States, local governments, or their representatives 
is mandated beyond the rulemaking process. The agency has concluded 
that this rule does not have sufficient federalism implications to 
warrant consultation with State and local officials or the preparation 
of a federalism summary impact statement. The rule does not have 
``substantial direct effects on the States, on the relationship between 
the national government and the States, or on the distribution of power 
and responsibilities among the various levels of government.''
    NHTSA rules can have a preemptive effect in two ways. First, the 
National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act contains an express 
preemption provision: When a motor vehicle safety standard is in effect 
under this chapter, a State or a political subdivision of a State may 
prescribe or continue in effect a standard applicable to the same 
aspect of performance of a motor vehicle or motor vehicle equipment 
only if the standard is identical to the standard prescribed under this 
chapter. 49 U.S.C. 30103(b)(1). This statutory command preempts any 
non-identical State legislative and administrative law addressing the 
same aspect of performance.
    The express preemption provision described above is subject to a 
savings clause under which compliance with a motor vehicle safety 
standard prescribed under this chapter does not exempt a person from 
liability at common law. 49 U.S.C. 30103(e). As a result of this 
provision, State common law tort causes of action against motor vehicle 
manufacturers are generally not expressly preempted by the language in 
49 U.S.C. 30103(b).
    As of January 2026, there is at least one State, California, that 
explicitly permits equipping ADS marker lamps on automated vehicles in 
accordance with SAE J3134 and J578. Cal. Vehicle Code Sec. Sec.  25950 
and 38750. This NPRM proposes to adopt J3134 (and J578 for color) in 
large, but not complete, part. It differs in the following relevant 
items: photometric intensity and headlamp activation state 
specifications, certain restrictions on lamp location, the 
permissibility of multiple lamp arrangements and optical combining, 
manual activation, distance to identification lamps, the chromaticity 
boundaries, and incidental illumination from backup lamps. The preamble 
of this document discusses these items and the reasons for deviating 
from J3134 in further detail. NHTSA tentatively believes that, if 
finalized, this amended

[[Page 67254]]

regulation would preempt Cal. Vehicle Code Sec. Sec.  25950 and 38750 
inasmuch as this proposal deviates from the above aspects of J3134. The 
proposal also differs from J3134 regarding the definition of ``ADS 
marker lamp,'' the activation requirements which utilize the language 
in that definition, and by omitting the requirement that the lamps 
deactivate when in an area that does not permit the lamps. These latter 
deviations are not intended to create a conflict with J3134 or State 
laws that codify that standard, and NHTSA has tentatively concluded 
that the proposed amendments would not preempt these aspects. NHTSA 
seeks comments on these tentative conclusions.
    NHTSA is also considering any potential preemptive effect of this 
proposal and whether an amendment allowing ADS marker lamps would, as a 
matter of law, preempt State laws that do not directly address ADS 
marker lamps, but contain provisions that effectively prohibit them. 
There are a variety of State laws that might affect ADS marker lamps 
for which a State standard may not regulate an aspect of performance in 
an identical manner to that in this proposed amendment. For instance, 
many States have laws limiting the color of motor vehicle lighting used 
in various locations on a motor vehicle, such as by limiting front 
emitting lamps to amber or white or by prohibiting red or blue to the 
front of the vehicle.\38\ NHTSA tentatively believes that such laws 
would likely be preempted by this proposed amendment only inasmuch as 
those laws prevent the installation of an ADS marker lamp that would be 
compliant with the amended standard, as such laws would disrupt the 
uniformity that Congress intended be established by an FMVSS. This 
proposal would have no impact on those laws as applied to lamps other 
than ADS marker lamps or for vehicles that are not equipped with ADS 
marker lamps.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \38\ E.g., Maryland Code, Transportation, Sec.  22-209. Lamp 
colors.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    In addition, many States have laws reserving the color blue for 
certain applications such as law enforcement and towing vehicles. NHTSA 
tentatively believes that the proposed lamps are sufficiently distinct 
from those equipped for those purposes such that no conflict would 
exist. The color selected for the ADS marker lamp, cyan (blue-green 
color defined in SAE J578), was chosen in part because it is distinct 
from the blue hue used by law enforcement. Further, the photometric 
specifications for the ADS marker lamp are substantially less intense 
than law enforcement lighting. In addition, the ADS marker lamps would 
be required to be steady burning. Therefore, NHTSA has tentatively 
concluded no conflict with such requirements would exist.
    NHTSA seeks comment on the referenced State laws and any other 
State laws (for example, those requiring pre-approval of supplemental 
lighting devices) that may be impacted by this proposed amendment. 
NHTSA also seeks comment on any impact on States' ability to enforce 
the remaining scope of their laws (such as those which broadly limit 
lamp color) which would not be preempted, and any associated safety 
impacts.
    This NPRM proposes an ``if-equipped'' requirement that sets 
performance standards but does not require or prohibit whether the ADS 
marker lamps are equipped. NHTSA is not aware of any State laws that 
prohibit or require ADS marker lamps. NHTSA has tentatively determined 
that any future State laws prohibiting equipping vehicles with ADS 
marker lamps in compliance with this proposed amendment would likely be 
preempted. NHTSA seeks comments on whether to preempt States' authority 
to require vehicles be equipped with ADS marker lamps (that is, to 
reserve the decision to equip to manufacturers).
    NHTSA rules can also preempt State law if complying with the FMVSS 
would render the motor vehicle manufacturers liable under State tort 
law. Because most NHTSA standards established by an FMVSS are minimum 
standards, a State common law tort cause of action that seeks to impose 
a higher standard on motor vehicle manufacturers generally will not be 
preempted. If and when such a conflict does exist--for example, when 
the standard at issue is both a minimum and a maximum standard--the 
State common law tort cause of action is impliedly preempted. See Geier 
v. American Honda Motor Co., 529 U.S. 861 (2000).
    Pursuant to E.O. 13132 and E.O. 12988, NHTSA has considered whether 
this proposed rule would preempt State common law causes of action. 
Announcing the agency's position regarding the preemptive effect of one 
of its rules reduces the likelihood that preemption will be an issue in 
any subsequent tort litigation. Regarding the application to vehicles 
without manual driving controls of requirements that specified manually 
operated turn signal, hazard warning signal operating unit, and beam 
switching controls to vehicles with manually operated driving controls 
and the adjustment to the test voltage for integral beam headlamp 
systems, NHTSA believes that the changes will have no effect on safety. 
Regarding removal of the prohibition on arranging a vertically arranged 
headlamp assembly such that the lower beam headlamp is below the upper 
beam headlamp, NHTSA believes that this change will have either no 
impact on safety, or a positive impact of improving safety through a 
small reduction in glare. Thus, NHTSA tentatively concludes that no 
conflict with State common law tort actions would occur from these 
aspects of the proposal. Without any conflict, implied preemption of a 
State common law tort cause of action is unlikely. NHTSA also seeks 
comment on this tentative conclusion.
    The proposal to allow optional installation of ADS marker lamps on 
certain vehicles sets minimum and maximum performance standards if such 
lamps are equipped because the agency has tentatively determined that 
such a standard is necessary to preserve the effectiveness of lighting 
required by the standard and avoid confusion by other road users. It 
would not serve the purposes of this proposed rule if, due to common 
law litigation, a manufacturer was required to include some other type 
of lighting or ADS marker lamps that perform differently. NHTSA has 
therefore tentatively concluded that this aspect of the requirement may 
conflict with State common law tort actions alleging performance 
aspects for ADS marker lamps beyond what is specified in the amended 
standard. If so, the proposed amendments likely would impliedly preempt 
such causes of action. However, given that NHTSA is not proposing to 
mandate ADS marker lamps based in part on a lack of information to 
support such a mandate (as opposed to determination that such a mandate 
would cause a negative outcome), NHTSA has tentatively concluded this 
amended standard likely would not preempt causes of action regarding 
whether an ADS marker lamp is or should have been equipped. NHTSA seeks 
comment on these conclusions.

* * * * *
    S6.6.1 All vehicles to which this standard applies, except 
trailers, must be equipped with a turn signal operating unit, a turn 
signal flasher, and a headlamp beam switching device meeting the 
requirements of S9. All vehicles to which this standard applies, except 
trailers and vehicles without manually operated driving controls, must 
be equipped with a turn signal telltale and an upper beam headlamp 
telltale meeting the requirements of S9.
    S6.6.2 All vehicles to which this standard applies, except trailers 
and motorcycles, must be equipped with a vehicular hazard warning 
signal operating unit, a vehicular hazard warning signal flasher, and a 
vehicular hazard warning signal telltale meeting the requirements of 
S9.
* * * * *
    S7.6.2.2 A backup lamp may project incidental red, yellow, cyan, or 
white light through reflectors or lenses that are adjacent, close to, 
or a part of the lamp assembly.

    S7.12 ADS marker lamps.
    S7.12.1 Number. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.2 Color of light. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.3 Mounting location. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.4 Mounting height. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.5 Activation. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.6 Effective projected luminous lens area. No requirement.
    S7.12.7 Visibility. See Table V-c.
    S7.12.8 Telltale. No requirement.
    S7.12.9 Markings. See S6.5.1.2.
    S7.12.10 Spacing to other lamps.
    S7.12.10.1 Spacing to DRLs. If an ADS marker lamp is mounted within 
100 mm from the lighted edge of a DRL not optically combined with a 
lower beam headlamp or turn signal lamp, the luminous intensity values 
at all test points shown in Table XXIII shall be multiplied at least 
2.5 times when DRLs are activated.
    S7.12.10.2 Spacing to headlamps or fog lamps. If an ADS marker lamp 
is mounted within 100 mm from the lighted edge of a headlamp or fog 
lamp, the luminous intensity values at all test points shown in Table 
XXIII shall be multiplied at least 2.5 times when headlamps or fog 
lamps are on as applicable.
    S7.12.10.3 Spacing to turn signal lamps. Each ADS marker lamp must 
be located on the vehicle so that the distance from its lighted edge to 
the optical center of the nearest turn signal lamp is not less than 100 
mm unless the ADS marker lamp is deactivated when the turn signal or 
hazard warning signal lamp is activated.
    S7.12.11 Multiple compartments and multiple lamps. See Table I-a.
    S7.12.12 Ratio to turn signal and stop lamps.
    S7.12.12.1 When an ADS marker lamp is combined with a turn signal 
or stop lamp, the luminous intensity of the ADS marker lamp at each 
identified test point must not be greater than the luminous intensity 
of the turn signal or stop lamp at that same test point times the 
multiplier shown for that test point in Table XXIII.
    S7.12.13 Photometry. Each ADS marker lamp must be designed to 
conform to the photometry requirements of Table XXIII and Table XXIV, 
when tested according to the procedure of S14.2.1, as specified by this 
section.
    S7.12.13.1 Photometry, headlamps deactivated. For each ADS marker 
lamp operating when the headlamps are deactivated, the maximum 
photometric intensity for front is 125 cd, side is 90 cd and rear is 90 
cd. The minimum photometric intensity applies when the headlamps or 
parking lamps on passenger cars and MPVs, trucks, and buses less than 
2032 mm in overall width are not activated.
    S7.12.13.2 Photometry, headlamps activated. For each ADS marker 
lamp operating when the headlamps are activated, the maximum 
photometric intensity for front is 125 cd, side is 18 cd and rear is 18 
cd. The minimum photometric intensity applies when the headlamps are 
activated in a steady burning state or the parking lamps on passenger 
cars and MPVs, trucks, and buses less than 2032 mm in overall width are 
activated.
    S7.12.14 Physical tests.
    S7.12.14.1 Each ADS marker lamp must be designed to conform to the 
performance requirements of the vibration test of S14.5, and the color 
test and plastic optical material test of S14.4.
    S7.12.14.2 Each ADS marker lamp that is not mounted inside the 
vehicle must be designed to conform to the performance requirements of 
the moisture test, dust test, and corrosion test of S14.5.

    S9.1.1 For vehicles with manually operated driving controls, the 
turn signal operating unit installed on passenger cars, multipurpose 
passenger vehicles, trucks, and buses less than

[[Page 67258]]

2032 mm in overall width must be self-canceling by steering wheel 
rotation and capable of cancellation by a manually operated control.
* * * * *
    S9.3.1 For vehicles with manually operated driving controls, each 
vehicle equipped with a turn signal operating unit where any turn 
signal lamp is not visible to the driver must also have a telltale to 
provide a clear and unmistakable indication that the turn signal system 
is activated.
* * * * *
    S9.3.6 Turn signal lamp failure. Failure of one or more turn signal 
lamps such that the minimum photometric performance specified in Tables 
VI or VII is not being met must be indicated by the turn signal 
telltale by a ``steady on'', ``steady off'', or by a significant change 
in the flashing rate, except when a vehicle does not have turn signal 
telltales or when a variable-load turn signal flasher is used on a 
multipurpose passenger vehicle, truck, or bus 2032 mm or more in 
overall width, on a truck that is capable of accommodating a slide in 
camper, or on any vehicle equipped to tow trailers. For vehicles 
without turn signal telltales, failure of one or more turn signal lamps 
must be indicated by a lamp failure telltale displayed in clear view of 
all designated seating positions.
    S9.4 Headlamp beam switching device. Each vehicle must have a means 
of switching between lower and upper beams. The switch must have no 
dead point and, except as provided by S6.1.5.2, the lower and upper 
beams must not be energized simultaneously except momentarily for 
temporary signaling purposes or during switching between beams. For 
vehicles with manually operated driving controls, the headlamp beam 
switching device must be designed and located so that it may be 
operated conveniently by a simple movement of the driver's hand or 
foot.
    S9.4.1 Semiautomatic headlamp beam switching devices. As an 
alternative to S9.4, a vehicle with manually operated driving controls 
may also be equipped with a semiautomatic means of switching beams that 
complies with 9.4.1.1 though S9.4.1.4 and either 9.4.1.5 (Option 1) or 
9.4.1.6 (Option 2).
* * * * *
    S9.4.1.4 Automatic dimming telltale. There must be a convenient 
means of informing the driver when the device is controlling the 
headlamps automatically. For headlighting systems certified to Option 
1, the device shall not affect the function of the upper beam headlamp 
telltale.
* * * * *
    S9.5 Upper beam headlamp telltale. Each vehicle with manually 
operated driving controls must have a means for indicating to the 
driver when the upper beams of the headlighting system are activated. 
The upper beam headlamp telltale is not required to be activated when 
an Adaptive Driving Beam system is operating in automatic mode.
* * * * *
    S9.6.1 For vehicles with manually operated driving controls, the 
unit may be an independent device or it may be combined with the turn 
signal operating unit. If combined with the turn signal operating unit, 
the actuating motion of the hazard function must differ from the 
actuating motion of the turn signal function.
    S9.6.2 Operating unit switch. The unit must operate independently 
of the ignition or equivalent switch. For vehicles with manually 
operated driving controls, if the actuation of the hazard function 
requires the operation of more than one switch, a means must be 
provided for actuating all switches simultaneously by a single driver 
action.
* * * * *
    S9.8.4 Telltale size and color. If the vehicular hazard warning 
signal telltale is not combined with a turn signal telltale, it must 
emit a red color and have a minimum area equivalent to a 0.5 in 
diameter circle.
* * * * *
    S10.18.9.4.2 If the upper beam is not combined in a headlamp with 
the lower beam and has fixed horizontal aim or has a horizontal VHAD, 
then the headlamp is mounted on a headlamp test fixture which simulates 
its actual design location on any vehicle for which the headlamp is 
intended. The fixture, with the headlamp installed, is attached to the 
goniometer table in such a way that the fixture alignment axes are 
coincident with the goniometer axes. An integral beam headlamp is 
energized at the voltage as marked according to S6.5.3.2 20 
mv. A headlamp other than an integral beam headlamp is energized at 
12.8 v 20 mv. There is no adjustment, shimming, or 
modification of the horizontal axis of the headlamp or test fixture, 
unless the headlamp is equipped with a VHAD. In this case the VHAD is 
adjusted to zero.
* * * * *
    S14.2.1.3 Measurement distance. Photometric measurements are made 
at a distance between the light source and the point of measurement of 
at least 1.2 m for side marker lamps, clearance lamps, identification 
lamps, parking lamps, and ADS marker lamps, and at least 3 m for turn 
signal lamps, stop lamps, taillamps, backup lamps, and school bus 
signal lamps.
* * * * *
    S14.2.5.4 Seasoning and test voltage. All sealed beam headlamps, 
integral beam headlamps, beam contributors, and replaceable light 
sources are seasoned at design voltage for 1% of its average design 
life or 10 hours, whichever is less, prior to a photometry test. An 
integral beam headlamp is tested at the voltage as marked according to 
S6.5.3.2 20 mv. All other headlamps are tested at 12.8 v 
20 mv, DC. Voltage is measured at the terminals of the 
lamp.
* * * * *
    S14.2.5.5.6.2 A headlamp whose upper beam is not combined with a 
lower beam and has a fixed horizontal aim or has a horizontal VHAD must 
be mounted in its normal operating position on a goniometer such that 
the mounting fixture alignment axes are coincident with the goniometer 
axes. An integral beam headlamp is energized at the voltage as marked 
according to S6.5.3.2 20 mv. A headlamp other than an 
integral beam headlamp is energized at 12.8 v 20 mv. There 
must be no adjustment, shimming, or modification of the horizontal axis 
of the headlamp or test fixture, unless the headlamp is equipped with a 
VHAD, in which case the VHAD must be adjusted to zero.
* * * * *
    S14.4.1 Color test. The requirement applies to the overall 
effective color of light emitted by the device and not to the color of 
the light from a small area of the lens. It does not apply to any 
indicator or telltale lights. The color of the sample device must 
comply when tested by either the Visual Method or the Tristimulus 
Method.
* * * * *
    S14.4.1.3.2.6 Cyan. Cyan is not acceptable if it is less saturated 
(paler), greener, or bluer than the limit standards.
* * * * *
    S14.4.1.4.2.7 Cyan. The color of light emitted must fall within the 
following boundaries:

y = 0.32 (blue boundary)
x = 0.20 (white boundary)
y = 0.50-0.50x (green boundary)
* * * * *
    S14.6.4.1.1 A headlamp connector test must be performed on each 
filament circuit of the sample headlamp prior to the test in 
S14.6.4.1.2 according to Figure 4 and S14.6.15. The power source is set 
to provide 128 watts of power.
