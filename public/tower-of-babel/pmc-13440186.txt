Perinatal trajectories of help-seeking stigma and correlates: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Tan Wang, Ying Wang, Jian Peng, Xiaoxv Yin, Qin Yan, Zina Fan, Lei Qiu, Yanhong Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHelp-seeking stigma (stigma of seeking professional psychological help) is a major barrier to perinatal mental health service use. However, longitudinal evidence on heterogeneous stigma trajectories and their baseline correlates remains limited.MethodsPregnant women were recruited in the second trimester from four hospitals in two Chinese cities and completed stigma assessments at four time points (second trimester, third trimester, 1 month postpartum, and 3 months postpartum). Latent growth curve modelling assessed mean change and inter-individual variability. Growth mixture modelling identified latent trajectory classes. Baseline correlates of class membership were examined using R3STEP classification-adjusted multinomial logistic regression.ResultsOf 1,250 enrolled women, 1,063 (85.0%) completed all assessments. Mean stigma remained stable, but significant heterogeneity was observed in both intercept and slope. Four trajectories were identified: low and decreasing (29.4%), moderate and stable (45.4%), high and increasing (18.3%), and moderate and rapidly increasing (6.9%). Compared with the moderate and stable class, elevated depressive symptoms at baseline (second trimester; EPDS ≥ 10) were associated with higher odds of the high and increasing class (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.46, 95% CI 1.63–3.71) and lower odds of the low and decreasing class (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.30–0.84). More positive help-seeking attitudes and higher resilience were associated with lower odds of high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing trajectories. Unmarried/divorced status was associated with higher odds of the two increasing trajectories.ConclusionsMean-level stability in help-seeking stigma concealed clinically meaningful heterogeneity, with some women showing persistently moderate stigma and others following increasing trajectories over time. Baseline depressive symptoms, help-seeking attitudes, resilience, and marital status may support risk stratification within routine antenatal and early postpartum contacts, enabling targeted low-burden stigma-reduction and referral navigation to improve linkage to perinatal mental health care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27884-2.

Introduction

Perinatal mental health problems are a major public health concern worldwide. Meta-analytic evidence indicates a substantial prevalence of perinatal depression [1] and anxiety–depression comorbidity [2], with adverse consequences for maternal health [3, 4], infant development [5–7], family functioning [8], and socioeconomic consequences [9, 10]. Evidence-based professional psychological care, such as counselling and psychotherapy, can reduce symptoms and improve outcomes for mothers, infants, and families [11, 12]. Nevertheless, utilization of professional psychological services during the perinatal period remains low. In a Ghanaian study, 18.9% of pregnant women reported self-initiated mental health help-seeking [13], whereas in a Chinese study of perinatal women with depressive symptoms, only 11.3% had sought psychological help [14]. This gap between burden and service use underscores the need to identify modifiable barriers to engagement with perinatal mental health care.

Help-seeking stigma (stigma of seeking professional psychological help; SSPPH) is a key barrier to engagement with mental health care [15–17]. Help-seeking stigma refers to perceived or anticipated negative social evaluation associated with seeking professional help, as well as the internalization of these judgements as shame, self-devaluation, and reduced self-esteem [15, 18]. It typically includes public stigma (perceived stereotypes and discriminatory attitudes from others) as well as self-stigma (internalized negative beliefs about oneself) [18–20]. During the perinatal period, help-seeking stigma may be intensified by fears of negative judgment, misunderstanding, or negative social evaluation from others [16, 21]. Higher stigma may delay or discourage engagement with professional psychological care [16, 17, 21], with possible implications for the timely recognition and treatment of perinatal mental health problems. Characterizing how help-seeking stigma changes during the perinatal transition may help inform strategies to improve service engagement, particularly among women with elevated mental health need.

Despite growing interest in stigma, evidence in perinatal populations remains limited and is dominated by cross-sectional designs. Prior studies have examined mental health literacy, stigma, and broader social influences on help-seeking during the perinatal period [22–24]. Studies in China suggest that help-seeking stigma among pregnant women is generally mild to moderate and is associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms, lower perceived social support, and some maternal characteristics, including age, education, and parity-related factors [25]. Related work has also shown that attitudes toward professional psychological help-seeking vary across sociodemographic and psychosocial groups [26]. However, cross-sectional designs cannot capture within-person change across the perinatal transition, and mean-level longitudinal trends may still mask clinically meaningful heterogeneity. In particular, women following increasing-stigma trajectories across pregnancy and postpartum may be missed when only average trends are considered, hindering early identification of those at risk of not engaging with care.

Thus, we conducted a prospective cohort study with four help-seeking stigma assessments from the second trimester to three months postpartum to: (1) characterize mean-level changes and inter-individual variability in stigma; (2) identify latent trajectory classes, and (3) examine various baseline sociodemographic and psychological factors associated with trajectory class membership. By identifying women at risk of increasing stigma, these findings may inform risk stratification and scalable referral-linked support within routine maternal health services.

Methods

Participants

We recruited women from four hospitals that provided comprehensive maternal and child health services in two Chinese cities (Haikou, Hainan Province, and Shihezi, Xinjiang). Between April and November 2024, uniformly trained investigators recruited eligible participants based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. We conducted follow-up assessments at three time points: during the third trimester (34–38 weeks of gestation), at one month postpartum, and at three months postpartum. All follow-up assessments were completed by August 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) gestational age 20–24 weeks at enrolment; (3) no severe neuropsychiatric disorders; and (4) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment, inability to communicate effectively due to language or cultural barriers, or inability to complete smartphone-based questionnaires (e.g., via WeChat).

Procedure

Participant recruitment and follow-up across four assessment waves are shown in Fig. 1. We enrolled 1,250 women in the second trimester; 1,180 completed the third-trimester assessment, 1,112 completed the 1-month postpartum assessment, and 1,063 completed the 3-month postpartum assessment (overall attrition, 15.0%). The analytic sample comprised 1,063 women who completed all four assessments.

Measures

Help-seeking stigma was assessed using the Chinese version of the Stigma of Seeking Professional Psychological Help scale (SSPPH), developed within Corrigan’s stigma framework [15] and translated and validated in Chinese populations [27]. The scale comprises 10 items covering two dimensions: self-stigma and public stigma, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Total scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater help-seeking stigma [27]. Help-seeking stigma reflects perceived and internalized negative evaluation associated with seeking professional psychological help. Cronbach’s α across the four waves ranged from 0.959 to 0.974.

Baseline covariates included depressive symptoms, attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help, resilience, perceived social support, and sociodemographic characteristics (age, education, marital status, annual per-capita income, residence, employment, parity, pregnancy complications, and planned pregnancy).

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [28]. The scale includes 10 items scored from 0 to 3, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30; higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. EPDS ≥ 10 was used as the main cutoff [29]. Cronbach’s α was 0.88.

Attitudes toward professional help-seeking were assessed with the Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help Scale–Short Form (ATSPPH-SF) [30]. The scale includes ten items with scores ranging from 0 to 3; items 2, 4, 8, 9, and 10 are reverse-coded [30]. Higher scores indicate more positive attitudes. Help-seeking attitudes reflect the individual’s general evaluative orientation toward professional psychological help-seeking. Cronbach’s α was 0.82. Although both SSPPH and ATSPPH-SF concern professional psychological help-seeking, they assess related but distinct constructs. SSPPH reflects stigma associated with professional help-seeking, whereas ATSPPH-SF reflects attitudes toward professional help-seeking [15, 30]. Baseline ATSPPH-SF was therefore included as a baseline correlate of trajectory class membership rather than as an alternative outcome measure.

Resilience was measured with the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) [31]. Items are rated from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 40; higher scores indicate greater resilience. Resilience refers to the perceived ability to adapt to stress, recover from adversity, and maintain positive coping in the face of challenges. Cronbach’s α was 0.97.

Perceived social support was assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [32]. The scale includes 12 items with scores ranging from 1 to 7, with total scores ranging from 12 to 84; higher scores indicate greater perceived social support. This construct reflects the perceived availability of support from family, friends, and significant others. Cronbach’s α was 0.96.

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses used complete-case data (participants with all four assessments). Because population averages can conceal subgroups with divergent patterns, we used a two-stage latent growth approach to characterize both mean change and heterogeneity in help-seeking stigma over time. First, latent growth curve modelling estimated the average trajectory and inter-individual variability [33]. Second, growth mixture modelling was used to identify latent subgroups with distinct stigma trajectories. The number of classes was selected based on overall model fit, classification quality, minimum class size (≥ 5%), and interpretability, considering likelihood ratio tests and information criteria [34, 35].

To reduce bias from class assignment uncertainty, we used the R3STEP approach, which accounts for classification error when estimating associations between baseline predictors and trajectory membership [36]. Continuous predictors (age, help-seeking attitudes, resilience, and perceived social support) were standardized as z-scores to allow comparison across variables measured on different scales, and their associations with trajectory class membership are reported per 1 standard deviation increase. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, baseline sociodemographic and psychological characteristics were compared across the identified trajectory classes using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Welch ANOVA for continuous variables.

Sensitivity analyses assessed robustness by: (1) characterizing depressive symptoms using EPDS ≥ 13 [29], (2) reference class alterations, and (3) refitting models using time scores scaled to the actual assessment intervals (Supplementary Table S1), and (4) re-running the R3STEP model after excluding ATSPPH-SF from the baseline predictors.

IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 was used for descriptive statistics and baseline comparisons. LGCM and GMM were fitted in Mplus 8.3. The baseline characteristics were compared between completers and non-completers using standardized mean differences (SMDs; |SMD| < 0.10 considered negligible) to evaluate potential attrition bias (Supplementary Table S2).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 1,250 enrolled women, 1,063 (85.0%) completed all four assessments and were included in the analytic sample (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Mean age was 29.66 years (SD = 4.17); 71.68% had a junior college degree or higher; 18.34% reported depressive symptoms (EPDS ≥ 10); and the mean baseline help-seeking stigma score was 21.04 (SD = 8.13). All baseline characteristics were comparable between completers and non-completers (Supplementary Table S2).

Mean-level change in help-seeking stigma

Mean help-seeking stigma scores were similar across assessments: 21.04 (SD 8.13), 20.49 (SD 8.32), 20.28 (SD 8.48), and 20.82 (SD 8.87), respectively. The linear LGCM showed a good fit (χ² = 2.76, RMSEA = 0.04, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.03). However, the mean slope was not statistically significant (-0.11, p = 0.249). Conversely, there was significant inter-individual variability in both the intercept (variance = 35.92, p < 0.001) and slope (variance = 2.32, p < 0.01), indicating substantial heterogeneity in help-seeking stigma change over time.

Heterogeneous help-seeking stigma trajectory classes

We fitted 1- to 5-class GMMs for characterizing heterogeneity (Table 2). The 4-class solution offered the best balance of fit, classification quality, and interpretability. Compared with the 3-class model, the 4-class model had lower AIC/BIC/aBIC (28857.31/28946.75/28889.58), higher entropy (0.969), high average posterior probabilities (0.97–0.99), and all classes ≥ 5% (6.9–45.4%). Although the 5-class model showed slightly lower AIC and aBIC values than the 4-class model, its BIC was higher, the LMR-LRT was non-significant (p = 0.121), and classification quality was poorer (entropy = 0.922; minimum average posterior probability = 0.70). Considering statistical fit, class separation, parsimony, and interpretability together, we retained the 4-class solution.

To improve interpretability, the four classes were named according to their initial stigma levels and patterns of change over time, and their model-estimated SSPPH scores are presented in Table 3 and Fig. 2. The four classes were low and decreasing (n = 313, 29.4%), moderate and stable (n = 483, 45.4%), high and increasing (n = 194, 18.3%), and moderate and rapidly increasing (n = 73, 6.9%). The low and decreasing class started at a relatively low level and declined over time (17.40 to 10.97). The moderate and stable class remained close to the sample mean throughout follow-up (21.22 to 20.41). The high and increasing class had the highest initial stigma level and continued to rise over time (24.82 to 29.79). The moderate and rapidly increasing class began at a moderate level but showed the steepest increase, reaching the highest predicted score at the final wave (22.25 to 41.07).

Baseline characteristics and factors associated with trajectory class membership

Baseline sociodemographic and psychological characteristics across the four trajectory classes are shown in Table 4. The classes differed significantly in age, education, marital status, planned pregnancy, baseline depressive symptoms, help-seeking attitudes, resilience, and perceived social support, as well as in both SSPPH dimensions (all p < 0.05). In general, compared with the low and decreasing and moderate and stable groups, the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing groups showed less favorable help-seeking attitudes, lower resilience, lower perceived social support, and higher baseline levels of depressive symptoms, self-stigma, and public stigma.

Table 5 presents R3STEP classification-adjusted multinomial logistic regression results using the moderate and stable class as the reference. Baseline depressive symptoms (EPDS ≥ 10) were associated with higher odds of the high and increasing trajectory (aOR = 2.46, 95% CI 1.63–3.71) and lower odds of the low and decreasing trajectory (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.30–0.84). More positive help-seeking attitudes (per 1 SD increase) were associated with lower odds of the high and increasing (aOR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.95) and moderate and rapidly increasing trajectories (aOR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.58–0.96), and higher odds of the low and decreasing trajectory (aOR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.20–1.69). Higher resilience (per 1 SD increase) was associated with lower odds of the high and increasing (aOR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.61–0.92) and moderate and rapidly increasing trajectories (aOR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.45–0.80). Older age (per 1 SD increase) was associated with lower odds of the low and decreasing trajectory (aOR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.65–0.94). Unmarried/divorced status was associated with higher odds of the high and increasing (aOR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.17–4.55) and moderate and rapidly increasing trajectories (aOR = 2.55, 95% CI 1.06–6.11). Perceived social support was not independently associated with class membership after adjustment. Taken together, these findings may help identify women at greater risk of following higher or increasing stigma trajectories during routine perinatal care. At baseline, SSPPH and ATSPPH-SF showed a significant negative correlation (Pearson r = -0.279, p < 0.001). In a sensitivity analysis excluding ATSPPH-SF from the baseline predictors, the 4-class solution and the overall pattern of associations remained unchanged (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

Using prospective data, we characterized help-seeking stigma trajectories from the second trimester to three months postpartum and examined baseline factors associated with trajectory class membership. Although stigma showed similar mean values, substantial inter-individual heterogeneity was evident. Four latent trajectory classes were identified: low and decreasing, moderate and stable, high and increasing, and moderate and rapidly increasing, indicating that average trends can mask subgroups with clinically meaningful differences in stigma development. Baseline depressive symptoms, help-seeking attitudes, resilience, age, and marital status were associated with trajectory class membership.

25.1% of women followed increasing trajectories, including a trajectory with a pronounced increase (6.9%). Because no validated SSPPH cut-off is available, these groups should be interpreted as relative patterns rather than as categories defined by cut-offs. Accordingly, the moderate and stable group should not be considered a validated safe group, and the increasing groups should not be interpreted as exceeding a cut-off for deterring help-seeking. Even so, persistently moderate or increasing stigma may still hinder disclosure or referral uptake during the perinatal period.

Beyond identifying heterogeneity, the baseline predictors provide a pragmatic basis for risk stratification in public health practice. Intervention targets should not be restricted to women with increasing stigma trajectories only. The moderate and stable group should not be automatically regarded as low risk or overlooked in perinatal care. Women with depressive symptoms, less favorable help-seeking attitudes, lower resilience, and those who are unmarried or divorced may benefit from prioritized, referral-linked stigma-reduction support delivered through standard maternal health contacts. Our findings are consistent with broader perinatal mental health research showing heterogeneous trajectories and risk profiles during the perinatal transition [37–39]. During the perinatal transition, rapidly changing role expectations, heightened social evaluation, and concerns about being judged may contribute to escalating stigma for some women. Baseline SSPPH dimension scores differed across trajectory classes for both self-stigma and public stigma. This suggests that the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing trajectories may reflect both internalized and perceived social barriers to professional help-seeking.

Implications for public health services

From a public health services perspective, the increasing-stigma subgroups suggest a care-pathway gap. Some women may become progressively less likely to disclose distress or take up referral during a period when early identification and timely linkage to care are most feasible. Embedding brief, low-burden stigma-reduction components within routine antenatal and early postpartum contacts, rather than relying on self-initiated help-seeking, may help mitigate this gap. Practical options include short risk screening linked to stepped referral pathways, normalization messages from maternity staff, brief psychoeducation addressing common fears and confidentiality, and referral-linked navigation support for women at higher risk [40]. Although intervention effectiveness was not tested here, this approach aligns with evidence on multilevel barriers to perinatal mental health care and WHO guidance on integrating perinatal mental health into maternal–child health services [41].

Interpretation of baseline correlates

Baseline depressive symptoms were significantly associated with trajectory class membership. Relative to the moderate and stable class, women exhibiting depressive symptoms displayed higher odds of the high and increasing class and lower odds of the low and decreasing class. This pattern is consistent with cross-sectional evidence linking depressive symptoms to higher stigma [25]. Depressive symptoms may also be accompanied by greater self-stigma and sensitivity to anticipated misunderstanding or bias, which may reinforce help-seeking stigma over time [16, 17, 21]. In additional descriptive analyses (Supplementary Table S4), the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing classes showed persistently higher mean EPDS scores and higher proportions with EPDS ≥ 10 across follow-up. The low and decreasing class had the lowest levels. The moderate and stable class showed intermediate levels. Differences across classes were significant at all time points. These findings support the relevance of stigma trajectories to ongoing mental health need. They also suggest that the two higher-stigma classes may warrant closer attention in perinatal care.

Help-seeking attitudes were also significantly associated with class membership. More positive attitudes were associated with lower odds of the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing classes as well as higher odds of the low and decreasing class. Previous research suggests that help-seeking attitudes are related to stigma, and that both may influence women’s engagement with professional perinatal mental health care [22, 40]. More positive attitudes toward professional psychological help-seeking may indicate greater openness to professional care and lower endorsement of stigma-related beliefs [22]. Future studies could examine whether attitudinal factors mediate or moderate the association between depressive symptoms and stigma trajectories.

Resilience was also associated with class membership; higher resilience was associated with lower odds of the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing classes. Women with greater resilience may have stronger coping resources for managing stress during pregnancy [42, 43], which could be relevant to a lower likelihood of increasing stigma trajectories over time. Interventions that strengthen coping resources may therefore warrant evaluation as potential strategies to reduce rising help-seeking stigma and improve engagement with care.

Among sociodemographic factors, younger age was associated with higher odds of the low and decreasing class, whereas unmarried or divorced status was associated with higher odds of the high and increasing and moderate and rapidly increasing classes. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously, as the underlying mechanisms were not examined in this study. It may reflect differences in interpersonal context [40], support from close others [17], or anticipated social evaluation during the perinatal period [16, 21], rather than a direct effect of marital status itself. Overall, these findings suggest that stigma-related risk may be shaped by both individual psychological characteristics and interpersonal context.

Perceived social support was not consistently associated with trajectory membership after adjustment. Support may influence stigma trajectories indirectly via more proximal determinants such as help-seeking attitudes and coping resources, attenuating its independent association in multivariable models. Moreover, the MSPSS captures perceived availability of support rather than instrumental support that may be more consequential for service engagement. Future studies should incorporate system-level access indicators, such as mental health literacy, service availability or affordability, time and travel constraints, and referral pathways, to clarify how interpersonal resources interact with health-system factors across the care pathway [40, 44].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The prospective design and four repeated assessments allowed us to characterize both average change and latent heterogeneity in help-seeking stigma across the perinatal period. However, several limitations should be noted. First, participants were recruited from two Chinese regions, which may limit generalizability. Second, we assessed only baseline factors. Although repeated depressive symptoms were evaluated descriptively, we did not test whether stigma trajectories independently predict subsequent help-seeking or depressive outcomes. Future studies should examine these associations. Third, other potentially relevant determinants (e.g., mental health literacy, partner support, service accessibility) were not measured and should be incorporated in future research. Additionally, because no validated SSPPH cut-off is available, the identified trajectory classes should be interpreted as relative patterns, not as cut-off-defined categories.

Generalization and external relevance

Although the cohort is not nationally representative, the presence of increasing-stigma subgroups and their associations with depressive symptoms, attitudes, and resilience are plausibly relevant to other settings where perinatal mental health needs are high but service uptake remains low [40, 45]. In many low- and middle-income contexts, constrained specialist capacity makes it particularly important to identify women at higher risk of disengagement and to embed scalable, low-intensity supports within existing maternal health systems [41, 46].

Conclusions

Overall, help-seeking stigma was stable at the mean level from mid-pregnancy to three months postpartum. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed, with 25.1% of women following increasing trajectories, including a trajectory with a rapid increase. Baseline depressive symptoms, help-seeking attitudes, resilience, age, and marital status were significantly associated with trajectory membership. Identifying women at risk of rising help-seeking stigma and addressing modifiable correlates, such as help-seeking attitudes and coping resources, may support engagement with perinatal mental health care.
