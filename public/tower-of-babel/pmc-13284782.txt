T Wave Oversensing in Left Bundle Branch Optimised CRT
Authors: Nadiya Johal, Badrinathan Chandrasekaran, Paul Foley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Five patients who underwent left bundle branch optimised (LOT)-CRT developed T wave oversensing (TWOS) between 2 months and 2 years post implant. LOT-CRT resulted in greater improvement in left ventricular size and function compared with historical case-matched controls. No patients experienced symptoms or inappropriate therapy as a result of TWOS. This report describes the occurrence, potential mechanisms and management of TWOS in patients undergoing LOT-CRT.

Left bundle branch optimised (LOT)-CRT paces the left ventricular (LV) epicardium via the coronary sinus together with a lead implanted into the septum to achieve left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP). Compared with conventional CRT where the right ventricular (RV) is paced via an endocardial lead, LOT-CRT is associated with improved haemodynamics, shortened QRS duration and superior LV remodelling. LOT-CRT appears to result in superior remodelling in patients with left bundle cardiomyopathy.1,2

We describe here five patients who developed T wave oversensing (TWOS) after implantation with LOT-CRT defibrillators (CRTD), ranging between 2 months and 2 years post-implant. While TWOS is not unique to LOT-CRT, this is a relatively new technique. We report these cases to help post-implantation troubleshooting. TWOs may result in inappropriate ICD therapy as well as withheld CRT therapy. This report describes the occurrence, potential mechanisms and management of TWOS in patients undergoing LOT-CRT.

Case Presentation

Between December 2021 and March 2023, 12 patients underwent LOT-CRT implant as part of the Conduction System Pacing Optimised Therapy study (NCT04905290).3 Left bundle branch (LBB) capture was confirmed in eight patients; the remaining four patients had deep septal capture.

LOT-CRTD systems consist of an IS-1 right atrial lead, a DF-1 RV lead, an IS-4 LV lead via the coronary sinus venous tributaries, and an IS-1 lead implanted in the LBB area. The left bundle lead is connected to the pace/sense port of the DF-1 CRTD Cobalt (Medtronic) or Claria (Medtronic) device, with the DF-1 pace/sense terminal end of the RV lead capped. The device obligates bipolar sensing and pacing in the RV port, resulting in bipolar sensing from the LBB area lead.

All patients enrolled in the LOT-CRT study showed a greater improvement in LV ejection fraction (LVEF) at 16.1% and a reduction in LV end systolic volume (LVESV) at 43.5%, compared to traditional CRT therapy.4 Super responders were defined as an increase in LVEF of >14.5%, which was seen in 56% of patients, highlighting the increased likelihood of remodelling in LOT-CRT patients. Within our small sample size of five LOT-CRT patients with TWOS, patients showed an average decrease in LVESV of 44.3% and an average increase in LVEF of 13.7%; Figure 1 shows a transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrating reduction in LV size following LOT-CRT in one of these patients.

Five of the eight patients with confirmed LBB capture developed TWOS during LOT-CRT pacing, with TWOS detected between 2 months and 2 years post implantation. TWOS was mostly identified through home monitoring alerts. In one patient, it was detected incidentally during a routine device check, where the presenting electrogram (EGM) demonstrated TWOS. No patient experienced any symptoms or inappropriate therapy as a result of TWOS. This is a higher incidence than we observe through home monitoring at our institution, where all patients were enrolled, and higher than the reported incidence of 3.4%.5

Discussion

The present cases highlight the occurrence of TWOS during follow-up in patients with LOT-CRT. While TWOS is well recognised, we were struck by the frequency with which we observed these findings when the sensing lead was within the left bundle area, coupled with the high incidence of remodelling together with super-response. We acknowledge these observations have been reported with standard defibrillators; however, it is worth further exploring the potential mechanisms in this population. TWOS might be related to device set-up, specifically increased sensitivity associated with use of the RV port in CRTD devices, or to remodelling with LOT-CRT. The largest multicentre study of LOT-CRT did not report any cases of TWOS, which may indicate that LOT-CRT has no greater propensity to cause TWOS than other pacing modalities.6

No patients received inappropriate therapy or experienced significant reductions in pacing percentage. In all instances of TWOS, RV sensitivity was decreased to 0.45 mV, which mitigated TWOS in three patients. In two patients, post-paced TWOS persisted intermittently. Three patients benefited from the Medtronic TWOS algorithm, which detected the pattern of TWOS and suppressed inappropriate therapy. This algorithm is fully automatic and uses a differential frequency filter to increase the amplitude of the high-frequency R waves, allowing the device to recognise a T wave through the low-frequency waveform pattern. However, this does not eliminate TWOS; it only reduces the risk of inappropriate therapy.

Intracardiac electrogram shows varying far-field morphology with intermittent T wave oversensing.

In Patient B, optimisation of the sensed atrioventricular delay (AVD) eliminated post-paced TWOS (Figure 2). The patient’s original programming involved a sensed AVD of 70 ms to encourage fusion with intrinsic right bundle branch activation (Figure 2A). Ultra-high-frequency ECG (Figure 2D) at the time showed that this shorter sensed AVD encouraged a dyssynchronous pattern of activation (ventricular electrical delay 49 ms). After optimisation, the sensed AVD was lengthened to 90 ms with the LV lead programmed ahead of the LBBA lead by 10 ms (Figures 2A and 2E). This resulted in a synchronous depolarisation pattern (ventricular electrical delay 20.2 ms) and eliminated the post-paced TWOS seen via the device (Figure 2C).

All patients were monitored using home monitoring, and as the patients remained clinically stable, we continued their follow-up. No further changes to therapy or device programming were required.

TWOS can present a challenging problem in patients implanted with defibrillators, responsible for 13% of lead revisions in a multicentre study of patients receiving defibrillator leads.7,8 A higher incidence of TWOS in LOT-CRT might result in more lead revisions in patients with left bundle area leads, which would reduce the attractiveness of this technique. In a study of 30 patients undergoing left bundle area pacing with defibrillator back-up (without LV leads) or LOT-CRT, 11% of 254 detected ventricular arrhythmic events resulted from TWOS, resulting in inappropriate therapy for 5.5% of these episodes.9 When exploring potential mechanisms, the seemingly greater reverse remodelling (LVEF improvement of 13.6%) seen in this group (responders and super-responders) may be associated with ECG changes and increased T wave amplitudes. Importantly, reverse remodelling was higher in patients undergoing LOT-CRT compared with those undergoing conventional CRT series, which may result in a higher incidence of TWOS.

In this series, we implanted all patients with Medtronic defibrillators. In a recent series, 3.4% of patients with Medtronic defibrillators experienced TWOS as the device sensing is capped at a maximum of eight times the programmed value after the R wave blanking period. The sensing algorithm coupled with the filters can result in oversensing of T waves, and the TWOS algorithm prevents unnecessary tachycardia therapies.5 An alternative manufacturer, Abbott, uses a programmable low-frequency attenuation filter. When programmed on, it decreases the amplitude of low-frequency signals and results in a significant reduction in TWOS. However, it increases the risk of oversensing in high-frequency low-amplitude signals, such as myopotentials.10

We observed T wave morphology changes on a 12-lead ECG. Two weeks post-implant, LBBAP testing showed negative T waves in lead 1. However, when these patients were brought back for device optimisation after evidence of TWOS, the 12-lead ECGs taken during LBBAP showed positive or flat T waves in lead 1. Interestingly, these changes in T wave morphology were not present during the 6-month follow-up, when we saw positive clinical and echocardiographic responses to CRT. These findings differ from those reported by Geng et al., where T wave inversion post-LBBAP lead implant was typically resolved within 10 days and commonly seen in inferior and anterior leads in patients with LBB block.11 While we observed surface T wave ECG changes, these may not correlate with the intracardiac T wave EGM signal seen by the device.

In our patients, TWOS was seen post LOT-CRT pacing, with one patient also demonstrating post-sensed TWOS. In a Medtronic device, after a paced event, a blanking period of 200 ms begins, after which sensitivity decays from 4.5 times the programmed value. In devices from other manufacturers, such as Biotronik, post-paced sensitivity depends on the amplitude of the evoked response and then begins to decay from 50% of the measured signal. This means that Medtronic devices use a fixed post-paced sensitivity compared with devices from other manufacturers, suggesting that intermittent post-paced TWOS is more influenced by variability in repolarisation wavefronts. Devices from other manufacturers, such as those from Abbott, have programmable sensitivity adjustments, such as post-sensed and post-paced decay delay.

A common recommendation to avoid TWOS is to use an integrated bipolar sensing configuration; this involves sensing from the tip of the RV lead to the RV coil which attenuates the signal differently than traditional bipolar sensing and can eliminate TWOS.12 In LOT-CRT, integrated bipolar sensing will create a cross-lead sensing vector (LBB lead tip and RV coil), which has the potential for ‘knocking’ artefact from the mechanical interaction between the RV coil and LBBP lead. For this option to be considered, the separation between the RV coil and LBB lead could be assessed radiographically and on intracardiac EGM for knocking artefact with positional changes. This sensing configuration was attempted in one patient but was unsuccessful in resolving TWOS.

TWOS can be avoided by reducing ventricular sensitivity, although potentially under-sensing VF is the trade-off.13 However, evidence regarding LBBAP leads and VF under-sensing is largely unknown. Other options include modification of the programmed ventricular refractory periods, which can limit upper-rate tracking; other manufacturers allow adjustment of the dynamic sensitivity parameter. Less attractive interventional options in patients with functioning leads include repositioning the left bundle lead or switching to RV apical sensing by changing the device and capping the left bundle lead, which again requires opening the pocket. Both options could result in deterioration of electrical parameters with resultant deterioration of LV function.

In our cohort, TWOS occurred at atrial rates between 90 and 154 BPM. Notably, this corresponds to the range in which rate-adaptive AVD (RAAV) adjustments begin in the Medtronic device. RAAV dynamically shortens the AVD based on sensed atrial rates, typically beginning at 90 BPM. While this adaptive shortening preserves AV synchrony and improves atrial tracking, in LOT-CRT it may inadvertently contribute to TWOS. Review of the EGMs from TWOS episodes (Figure 3) revealed variability in the far-field morphology during LOT-CRT pacing at faster atrial rates. This fusion-related variability, combined with known rate-dependent fluctuations in R wave amplitude and repolarisation variability, may increase the likelihood of TWOS during faster atrial rates.14,15 T wave amplitudes and polarity may vary depending on the depolarisation wave front. In LOT-CRT, this may also be altered by adjusting RV port (LB) to LV port delays by pacing LV significantly ahead of RV (LB) or any pacing RV tip to coil.

Conclusion

Unexpected problems can arise with new pacing techniques and occur long after initial implantation even with favourable clinical results. In this series, no significant clinical sequelae were observed. On-going monitoring and individualised programming remain essential in patients with LOT-CRT.

Clinical Perspective

Programming optimisation, particularly atrioventricular delay adjustments and reduced ventricular sensitivity, can effectively manage T wave oversensing (TWOS) without compromising CRT.
