The presence of specific or multiple additional vascular risk factors indicates the need for oral anticoagulant (OAC) therapy in AF patients (according to the CHA2DS2-VASc classification). While OAC treatment significantly reduces the risk of AIS by over 80% in this population, the risk remains higher compared to the general population. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, with its burden expected to increase as the population ages [1]. Intravenous thrombolysis with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) and mechanical thrombectomy have substantially improved outcomes in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [2–4], particularly when applied within appropriate time windows and supported by modern stroke unit care.

Despite these advances, a significant subgroup of AIS patients remains without access to reperfusion therapy. This particularly affects patients receiving non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (DOACs), especially factor Xa inhibitors such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, who present early after symptom onset but are not eligible for thrombolysis due to persistent anticoagulant activity and are not candidates for mechanical thrombectomy due to the absence of large vessel occlusion.

Although DOAC therapy substantially reduces the risk of cardioembolic stroke, AIS still occurs in this population, and therapeutic options in the acute setting remain limited. Observational studies suggest that intravenous thrombolysis in selected patients with recent DOAC exposure may be feasible, but these data are subject to selection bias and cannot replace randomized evidence.

A potential strategy to address this therapeutic gap is the use of specific reversal agents prior to thrombolysis. Andexanet alfa, a recombinant modified factor Xa decoy protein, rapidly reverses the anticoagulant effect of factor Xa inhibitors and may enable safer administration of rtPA. However, this approach remains insufficiently studied in a randomized setting, and concerns persist regarding both hemorrhagic risk and potential prothrombotic effects.

Despite increasing clinical interest, there is currently a lack of prospective randomized evidence guiding intravenous thrombolysis in AIS patients with recent exposure to factor Xa inhibitors and laboratory-confirmed anticoagulant activity. This represents a clinically relevant and increasingly frequent scenario, particularly among patients without large vessel occlusion who are not eligible for mechanical thrombectomy.

The STROACT trial is designed to address this unmet need by evaluating the feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy of reversal-enabled intravenous thrombolysis in this specific patient population.

Objectives {7}

The purpose of this study is to assess the effectiveness and safety of intravenous thrombolysis with rtPA in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) who are on chronic non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy, following the administration of a specific reversal agent.

Trial design {8}

STROACT (STRoke on Oral AntiCoagulants for Thrombolysis) is a multicenter, prospective, parallel-group, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, superiority, non-commercial clinical trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio supported by a grant from the Polish Medical Research Agency. Randomization is stratified by baseline stroke severity (NIHSS score < 6 or ≥ 6) using a computer-generated variable block sequence, with allocation fully concealed via a central electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) system. The trial aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a new intervention involving the sequential intravenous administration of DOAC-specific antidotes (andexanet alfa for apixaban and rivaroxaban) followed by thrombolysis with rtPA. Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This study is being conducted at seven comprehensive stroke centers (CSCs) in Poland. All participating centers have extensive experience in specific acute ischemic stroke treatments. The main center is the Medical University of Gdansk, Department of Adult Neurology.

Eligibility criteria {10}

Patients with symptoms of AIS who were previously functionally independent (0–2 points on the modified Rankin Scale) will be considered for the STROACT study after exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) and large vessel occlusion (LVO) using computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with angiography sequences. Patients with LVO may be eligible for primary mechanical thrombectomy (MT) and will not be included in the study. Patients on DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) mainly for the prevention of cardioembolic events will undergo standard diagnostic procedures (see Participant timeline {13}) and may be recruited into the study.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The STROACT clinical trial is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study that aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a new intervention involving sequential intravenous administration of a DOAC-specific antidote and thrombolysis with rtPA for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) who are actively treated with DOACs and do not have large vessel occlusion (LVO). The study will compare the new intervention to a placebo to determine its effectiveness and safety.

We selected the comparator due to the absence of a specific treatment for this patient group. The STROACT study represents an exploratory randomized approach aimed at evaluating the feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy of this therapeutic strategy in a controlled setting.

Intervention description {11a}

STROACT is a multicenter, parallel-group, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial designed to assess the effectiveness and safety of reperfusion thrombolytic therapy using intravenous rtPA (alteplase) in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) who are on non-vitamin K antagonist anticoagulants (DOAC anti-Xa inhibitors: apixaban and rivaroxaban) and have received a specific antidote/reversal agent (andexanet alfa). The study interventions for each study group are as follows: Study group A (patients on apixaban with high plasma anti-Xa activity): intravenous administration of andexanet alfa followed by an infusion of rtPA (or corresponding placebo for each component of the intervention). Study group B (patients on rivaroxaban with high plasma anti-Xa activity): intravenous administration of andexanet alfa followed by an infusion of rtPA (or corresponding placebo for each component of the intervention).

Participants in groups A and B will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either active treatment or placebo.

Placebo for the antidote and rtPA (0.9% NaCl solution) will be prepared immediately after randomization. All components of the study intervention (sequential therapy) are commercially available medications, and dosages and administration will adhere to local prescribing guidelines.

In the STROACT study, rtPA (both bolus and infusion) will be administered in a vein of the arm contralateral to the ischemic lesion, while the antidote (andexanet alfa) will be administered in a vein of the arm on the same side as the ischemic lesion.

During the recruitment process for the STROACT trial, we observed that patients with AIS who had high plasma levels of anticoagulants and were initially eligible for the study showed significantly improved clinical and neuroimaging outcomes compared to typical stroke cases. Many of these patients experienced rapid neurological recovery to a non-disabling status even before consenting to participate in the trial. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that AIS patients receiving direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) therapy with therapeutic plasma concentrations (> 50 ng/mL) have a more favorable prognosis than those not on this treatment or with suboptimal anticoagulation. To investigate this hypothesis, we expanded the STROACT study with an observational arm to monitor and assess this specific patient population during their hospitalization. For this portion of the project, we plan to recruit 200 patients.

To minimize treatment delays, a standardized workflow is implemented across all participating centers. Following rapid neuroimaging and confirmation of eligibility, anti-factor Xa activity is measured on admission. Once eligibility is confirmed, randomization is performed immediately via the electronic case report form. Study medications are prepared in parallel by unblinded personnel. The andexanet alfa (or placebo) bolus is initiated without delay, followed by continuous infusion. Alteplase (or placebo) bolus is administered as early as possible, without waiting for completion of the reversal infusion, and followed by standard infusion. Separate intravenous lines are used to allow parallel administration of study drugs.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Patients who have had an acute ischemic stroke (AIS) are at a higher risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) during the first 1 to 3 months post-stroke, mainly due to stroke-related immobility. Given the increased thrombotic risk during this period, it is important to consider restarting anticoagulant therapy or implementing of thromboembolism prophylaxis as soon as medically appropriate.

The timing of post-stroke care, including the initiation of anticoagulation or other preventive measures, will be personalized based on each patient’s clinical condition and risk profile, with a careful assessment of the balance between thromboembolic and hemorrhagic risks.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome is the transition from a disabling deficit at admission to a non-disabling deficit (up to two points on the modified Rankin Scale). This will be measured as the proportion of participants achieving this transition from admission to 90 days after receiving the investigational treatment, with a comparison between the treatment and control groups.

The second primary outcome is the change in neurological status, assessed by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, from admission to the 7-day follow-up. The outcome will be presented as the least squares (LS) mean difference in NIHSS score between admission and 7 days post-intervention for each study group.

To address the issue of multiple primary endpoints, additional p-values will be provided using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Participants in each study group (based on the DOAC taken) will be randomized using a computer-generated randomization schedule. The allocation ratio is set at 1:1, randomizing participants to either the active treatment group or the control group. Stratification will be based on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score (< 6 points/≥ 6 points) to ensure balanced generated distribution across the groups. A stratified variable block length randomization, based on a computer-generated randomization schedule integrated into the eCRF software, will be used to ensure consistent and unbiased assignment of participants. The randomization schedule will be incorporated into the electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) system, ensuring consistent and unbiased assignment of participants.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The randomization process will be concealed through the eCRF system to prevent any influence on group allocation. The randomization algorithm is integrated into the eCRF software, ensuring that allocation remains hidden from both the investigator and participants until the appropriate point in the process. Upon entering the required patient information, the system will generate a unique randomization number automatically and notify unblinded personnel responsible for preparing the allocated treatment. The blinding mechanism will uphold the integrity of the randomization process.

Implementation {16c}

The investigator or a trained designee will conduct randomization using the predefined algorithm embedded in the eCRF system. The system is accessible 24/7 for randomization at any time. Prior to randomization, the investigator must verify that all eligibility criteria are met, the patient can receive study treatment (thrombolysis) within the specified time frame (4.5 to 6 h from AIS onset), and the anti-Xa activity results (based on the DOAC taken prior to AIS) are available. The patient’s NIHSS score, used for stratification, must also be provided before randomization. Detailed instructions for randomization will be provided in the eCRF completion manual for each study site.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

This study is a double-blind clinical trial. Once a patient qualifies for the study treatment, unblinded personnel will prepare both components of the sequential treatment. The treating physician, who remains blinded, will receive pre-prepared syringes for administration. These syringes will look identical for both the active treatment and the placebo, ensuring the blinding is maintained.

The syringes will be labeled to distinguish between the antidote and IVT components, allowing for correct sequential administration without compromising the blinding of the treating physician or other blinded study investigators.

Statisticians and outcome assessors will also be blinded to treatment allocation throughout the trial to maintain the study’s integrity. Blinded statisticians will analyze the data to prevent bias in result interpretation, while blinding the outcome assessors ensures unbiased evaluations of study endpoints.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

To maintain the scientific integrity of the study, it is important to avoid unblinding. The Independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC) will regularly review unblinded data to evaluate patient safety, treatment efficacy, and the risk–benefit ratio. Any accidental unblinding should be promptly reported to the site Principal Investigator (PI) and Lead PI.

Unblinding of treatment allocation should only be done in a medical emergency where knowledge of the patient’s treatment group is necessary for proper diagnosis or treatment. If unblinding is necessary, investigators can access individual randomization codes through the electronic Case Report Form (eCRF). The procedure for unblinding is outlined in the eCRF user manual.

In case of unblinding, whether intentional or accidental, the investigator must document the reason and submit a report to the study coordinating center and the Lead PI.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Analysis of primary endpoints

The proportion of subjects achieving a transition from a disabling (at admission) to a non-disabling deficit will be calculated for both the active treatment and placebo groups. The difference in proportions between the groups, along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), will also be reported. A two-sided test for the difference in proportions will be performed. Statistical significance will be defined as a p-value less than 0.0060 at the interim analysis and less than 0.0476 at the final analysis. This analysis will be conducted on two pooled study groups (A and B) to ensure adequate statistical power.

The change in NIHSS score from admission to the 7-day follow-up will be assessed using ANCOVA, with baseline NIHSS score and treatment as independent variables. Least squares (LS) mean differences between the placebo and active treatment groups, along with 95% confidence intervals, will be provided. If the assumptions of equal variance or normality are severely violated, alternative methods, such as ANCOVA on ranks or an appropriate transformation of the endpoint, will be considered.

To account for the issue of multiplicity due to the two primary endpoints, additional p-values will be calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate.

Secondary endpoints

For the secondary efficacy endpoint (the proportion of subjects achieving an excellent functional status, defined as an mRS score 0–1 at 90 days), the difference in proportions between the active treatment and placebo groups will be evaluated using a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The absolute difference in proportions along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be reported. Furthermore, to account for age as a potential confounding factor, both unadjusted and age-adjusted odds ratios will be calculated and presented using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant (α = 0.05). The rate of early (symptomatic and asymptomatic) intracranial hemorrhages detected through neuroimaging, along with the incidence of major extracranial hemorrhages, deaths, recurrent ischemic strokes, and neurological deteriorations, will be analyzed using the same statistical framework as the secondary efficacy endpoint. Specifically, the incidence rates of these safety events will be compared between the study groups using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, and unadjusted as well as age-adjusted odds ratios will be derived via logistic regression, reporting 95% CIs for all estimates.

Exploratory endpoints

Exploratory endpoints include additional cognitive and functional status assessments. These endpoints will be analyzed using descriptive statistics of total scores, presented by treatment group for each study arm and overall, in both tabular and graphical formats. Boxplots will be used for the analysis of exploratory functional assessments where appropriate.

The least squares (LS) mean difference between the placebo and active treatment groups, along with 95% confidence intervals, will be reported. If the assumptions of equal variance or normality are violated, alternative methods, such as ANCOVA on ranks or transformation of the endpoint, will be considered.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Definition of adverse events (AE)

An adverse event (AE) is defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a patient or study participant who has been administered a medicinal product or intervention, regardless of whether a causal relationship exists with the treatment. An AE may include any unfavorable or unintended sign (e.g., abnormal laboratory result), symptom (e.g., nausea, pain), or disease that occurs in temporal association with the use of a medicinal or investigational product or intervention, whether or not it is related to that product or intervention.

AEs encompass complaints reported by the patient (via interview), abnormalities identified during physical examinations, or findings from ancillary tests (e.g., abnormal laboratory results or radiological examinations). Both serious and non-serious AEs are included, as well as the worsening or deterioration (e.g., increased frequency, intensity, or severity) of a pre-existing medical condition, including chronic diseases, present before study inclusion.

AEs can occur at any time, including during screening, even if no study treatment has yet been administered. In this study, AEs will be documented in the designated eCRF section from the moment informed consent is obtained until the end of the study (visit 6; end of study).

Adverse reaction (AR)

An adverse reaction (AR) to an investigational medicinal product is defined as any untoward and unintended response to the investigational product that is related to any dose administered. All adverse events that are assessed by either the reporting investigator or the sponsor as having a reasonable causal relationship to the investigational product qualify as adverse reactions. The term reasonable causal relationship implies that there is evidence or a plausible argument suggesting a link between the investigational product and the adverse event.

Suspected unexpected serious adverse reaction

A suspected unexpected serious adverse reaction (SUSAR) is defined as an untoward and unintended response to a study drug that is not listed in the applicable product information. A SUSAR must also meet one or more of the following serious criteria: results in death, is life-threatening, requires hospitalization or prolongs an existing hospitalization, results in persistent or significant disability or incapacity, or leads to a congenital anomaly or birth defect.

Discussion

To our knowledge, STROACT is the first randomized phase II trial designed to explore the feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy of reversal-enabled intravenous thrombolysis in a highly selected population of acute ischemic stroke patients treated with factor Xa inhibitors. The findings of this study are expected to be hypothesis-generating and may inform the design of future confirmatory randomized trials.

Patients eligible for STROACT represent a clinically relevant subgroup with disabling acute ischemic stroke, laboratory-confirmed anticoagulant activity, and no indication for mechanical thrombectomy. In current practice, many such patients remain without access to reperfusion therapy despite early hospital presentation. The rationale for the intervention is that rapid reversal of anti-factor Xa activity may reduce hemorrhagic risk sufficiently to permit thrombolysis, while acknowledging the potential prothrombotic signal and off-label use of andexanet alfa in this context.

Andexanet alfa reduces circulating anti-factor Xa activity by acting as a decoy receptor; however, it also interacts with tissue factor pathway inhibitor, potentially leading to a transient prothrombotic state. This dual mechanism underscores the importance of careful monitoring of both hemorrhagic and thromboembolic complications in the present trial.

Experience with idarucizumab in dabigatran-treated patients represents the closest clinical analog to the present approach. Observational studies and meta-analyses suggest that reversal-enabled thrombolysis in this setting may be associated with an acceptable safety profile. However, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to factor Xa inhibitors, as andexanet alfa has a distinct pharmacodynamic profile.

Recent observational evidence has increasingly suggested that intravenous thrombolysis may be feasible in selected patients with recent DOAC exposure. In a large international multicenter cohort, recent DOAC ingestion was not associated with excess symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage after IVT compared with no anticoagulation. Subsequent target-trial analyses and real-world cohorts have reported similarly low symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates, including in patients treated after liberalization of local eligibility criteria and in settings with routine calibrated anti-factor IIa/Xa testing.

However, these data should be interpreted with caution. They are derived from observational cohorts, target-trial emulations, and center-specific practice patterns, all of which are inherently susceptible to selection bias, treatment-channeling bias, and confounding by indication. Moreover, these studies address heterogeneous strategies, including plasma-level-guided selection, treatment without reversal, and reversal-enabled approaches, and therefore cannot define the optimal management pathway. In this context, a prospective randomized phase II trial remains justified to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy of a structured reversal-enabled thrombolysis strategy [5–8].

The real-world applicability of this approach is subject to important logistical constraints. The protocol requires rapid laboratory assessment of anti-factor Xa activity, immediate availability of andexanet alfa, and a highly coordinated workflow enabling sequential reversal and thrombolysis without unacceptable delay. Therefore, the findings of this study may initially be generalizable primarily to comprehensive stroke centers with advanced infrastructure. The high cost and limited availability of andexanet alfa may further restrict broader implementation.

Patient heterogeneity, including differences in stroke severity, type of factor Xa inhibitor, renal function, and baseline comorbidities, may influence both safety and treatment response. These factors will be explored in predefined and exploratory analyses, recognizing that such analyses are hypothesis-generating in nature.

This work addresses the European Stroke Organization’s position that a randomized-controlled trial in this area may be difficult to conduct due to logistical and financial constraints. Nevertheless, STROACT represents an effort to generate prospective randomized evidence in a field currently dominated by observational data and heterogeneous clinical practice.

Overall, this phase II trial is designed to generate preliminary evidence on feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy, which may inform the design of larger confirmatory randomized trials, including potential multicenter or adaptive trial frameworks.

Conclusion

The findings of this study are expected to be hypothesis-generating rather than practice-defining. As a phase II trial, STROACT is designed to generate preliminary evidence on feasibility, safety, and potential efficacy, which may inform the design of larger confirmatory randomized studies.
