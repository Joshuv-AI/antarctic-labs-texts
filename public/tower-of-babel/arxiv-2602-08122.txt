Efficient k k -Sign Consistency Verification of Hankel Matrices via Schur Polynomials Thanks: This work was conducted while the first author was a Jane and Larry Sherman Fellow. It was further supported by the Israel Science Foundation (grant no.2406/22).
Christian Grussler, Tobias Damm

Abstract
We consider the problem of certifying (strict) k k -sign consistency of a matrix, that is, whether all of its k k -th order minors share the same (strict) sign. Although this problem is generally of combinatorial complexity, we show that for Hankel matrices it can be significantly simplified: our sufficient condition requires checking only the k k -th order minors of a reshaped Hankel matrix with k k rows. Remarkably, when applied to the Hankel operator, this sufficient condition is also necessary. Comparable results were known only in the setting of (strictly) k k -positive Hankel matrices and operators, in which all minors of order up to k k have the same (strict) sign. More concretely, we derive a formula expressing the k k -th order minors of Hankel matrices as nonnegative integer linear combinations of k k -th order minors with consecutive row indices. Our derivation uses Schur polynomial theory to show that the k k -th order minors of any matrix are nonnegative integer linear combinations of row-consecutive k k -th order minors, meaning minors formed from distinct columns whose consecutive row indices need not coincide across columns. For Hankel matrices, these minors coincide — up to sign changes arising from column swaps — with the usual k k -th order minors with consecutive row indices. Our main result then follows by showing that the sum of certain signed nonnegative integer coefficients equals the corresponding Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. In our problem, the nonnegativity of these coefficients ensures that negatively signed column permutations are cancelled by positively signed ones. Our results also extend naturally to Toeplitz matrices and operators, and we present a partial analogue for circulant matrices.

Introduction

Total positivity theory of linear operators has attracted researchers from various fields over the past centuries. Positivity in the context of nonnegative matrices, i.e., matrices that map the cone of nonnegative vectors to itself, appears in probability theory (Markov chains), economics and biology (compartmental models), statistics (stochastic processes), graph theory (adjacency matrices), etc., where the restriction to nonnegative entries reflects the natural impossibility of negative probabilities, populations, flows, and so on (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
20
,
15
]
).

The framework of total positivity, however, also encompasses mappings with the more general invariance property of variation diminishing/bounding, i.e., reducing or preserving the number of sign changes (which equals zero for nonnegative vectors). This generalization of nonnegative matrix theory underlies, implicitly and explicitly, important developments as early as Descartes’ Rule of Signs (1637)
[
4
]
, the related Routh–Hurwitz criterion
[
14
]
, vibration mechanics
[
6
]
, spline theory
[
15
,
7
]
, geometric modelling
[
7
,
13
]
, and the omnipresent concepts of convexity and monotonicity
[
12
,
15
]
. In recent years, this broader viewpoint has received increasing interest in the study of system behaviors (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
11
,
25
,
26
,
12
,
10
,
24
]
), as well as in low-complexity modelling
[
18
,
9
]
.

The term “total positivity” stems from the characterization of such linear mappings via the sign consistency of their minors
[
15
,
10
]
. A matrix is called (i)
(strictly)
k
-sign consistent
if all its
k
-th order minors share the same (strict) sign, (ii)
(strictly)
k
-positive
if all its minors of order up to
k
are (strictly) positive, and (iii) (strictly) totally positive if it is (strictly)
k
-positive for all
k
. As the number of minors grows combinatorially with the matrix dimensions, a major obstacle to the utility of this framework lies in efficient certification. Fortunately, in the case of (strict)
k
-positivity, it is under mild assumptions sufficient to check the (strict) positivity of consecutive minors of order up to
k
, i.e., minors formed from consecutive column and row indices
[
15
,
11
,
10
]
. This not only significantly reduces the complexity of verifying the property, but when applied to Hankel matrices and operators also yields an exact and tractable characterization
[
5
,
11
,
20
]
.

In this work, we show that similar simplifications can be obtained for (strictly)
k
-sign consistent Hankel matrices and operators. Our main contribution is an explicit formula that expresses each
k
-th order minor of a Hankel matrix as a nonnegative integer linear combination of
k
-th order minors from a reshaped Hankel matrix with
k
rows. As a consequence, it is sufficient to verify (strict) sign consistency only among these
k
-th order minors to conclude (strict)
k
-sign consistency of the original Hankel matrix. In the case of the infinite-dimensional Hankel operator, where all
k
-th order minors of the reshaped Hankel matrix are also
k
-th order minors of the operator, this provides a necessary and sufficient characterization. Our result, in particular, facilitates the use of
[
19
]
, which characterizes
k
-sign consistency of a matrix with
k
rows via the total positivity of an associated transformed matrix. Finally, since column-order inversion of a Hankel matrix yields a Toeplitz matrix, our results also naturally extend to the Toeplitz case and partially to circulant matrices.

In our derivation, we use tools from algebraic combinatorics
[
23
]
such as Schur polynomials, Kostka numbers, and Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. Specifically, we first utilize Schur polynomials to show that
k
-th order minors of a matrix can be expressed as nonnegative combinations of row-consecutive
k
-th order minors, i.e., minors obtained by selecting
k
consecutive rows in each column, without requiring the row indices to coincide across columns. For Hankel matrices, the structure forces all such row-consecutive minors to coincide (up to sign) with the standard
k
-th order minors formed from the first
k
rows of an extended/reshaped Hankel matrix. We then employ the definition of Littlewood–Richardson coefficients to show that all signed contributions arising from column permutations cancel, leaving only nonnegative coefficients in front of the consecutive-row minors. This yields the desired formula and the resulting efficient certificates for
k
-sign consistency.

p\in(1:m)^{r}

a_{\mu}(x)=\begin{cases}\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}\textnormal{sign}_{\mu}(p)x^{p}&\text{if $\mu$ has distinct values},\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(7)

By
[
23
, A1.5.3 Theorem and Eq. (7.35)]
, it holds then for
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{n,r}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{l,r}
that

s_{\lambda}(x)s_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda+\mu}}c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\mu}s_{\gamma}(x)

(8)

and

s_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}m_{\mu}(x),

(9)

with
monomial symmetric function

m_{\mu}(x):=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}x^{p}
.

3
Main Results

In
1
, we observed that
k
-positivity of Hankel matrices
H^{g}(1,M,N)
can be verified efficiently by considering (consecutive) minors only within
H^{g}(1,k,M+N-k)
. The main objective of this work is to extend this result to the setting of
k
-sign consistent
Hankel matrices and operators.

3.1
Row-Consecutive Minor Decomposition

Central to our analysis is the following decomposition of arbitrary minors into nonnegative integer linear combinations of row-consecutive minors.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathds{C}^{M\times n}
,
M\geq n
,
s\in\mathcal{I}_{M,n}
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{s_{n},n}
be defined by
\lambda_{i}:=s_{n-i+1}-(n-i+1)
. Then,

|A_{(s,:)}|=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}D_{\mu}

(10)

where

D_{\mu}:=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}\begin{vmatrix}a_{p_{1}+1,1}&\dots&a_{p_{n}+1,n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{p_{1}+n,1}&\dots&a_{p_{n}+n,n}\end{vmatrix}

(11)

and
K_{\lambda,\mu}
are the Kostka numbers associated with
\lambda
and
\mu
.

Remark 1
.

It suffices to consider the case
s_{1}=1
in
1
; all other cases follow by replacing
A
with
A_{((s_{1}:M),:)}
. This is consistent with the identity
K_{\lambda,\mu}=K_{\lambda-\lambda_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n},\;\mu-\lambda_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}}
from
2
. For the special case
n=2
, the Young diagram of shape
(s_{2}-2,0)
consists of a single row, implying
K_{\lambda,\mu}=1
. Hence,

\begin{vmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}\\
a_{s_{2},1}&a_{s_{2},2}\end{vmatrix}=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{s_{2}-2}{2}\rfloor}D_{(s_{2}-2-i,i)}=\sum_{i=0}^{s_{2}-2}\begin{vmatrix}a_{1+i,1}&a_{s_{2}-i-1,2}\\
a_{2+i,1}&a_{s_{2}-i,2}\end{vmatrix}.

(12)

In other words, for
n=2
,
1
amounts to a telescope-sum expansion.

We prove
1
by first establishing its validity for the specialization
A=V_{M}(x)
, which is a direct reformulation of
eq.

9
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
S:=V_{M}(x)\in\mathds{C}^{M\times n}
,
M\geq n
, for some
x\in\mathds{C}^{n}
,
s\in\mathcal{I}_{M,n}
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{s_{n},n}
be given by
\lambda_{i}:=s_{n-i+1}-(n-i+1)
. Then,

|S_{(s,:)}|=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}d_{\mu}(x)

(13)

where

d_{\mu}(x):=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}^{p_{1}}&\dots&x_{n}^{p_{n}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{p_{1}+n-1}&\dots&x_{n}^{p_{n}+n-1,n}\end{vmatrix}=|S_{(1:n,:)}|m_{\mu}(x).

(14)

and
K_{\lambda,\mu}
are the Kostka numbers with respect to
\lambda
and
\mu
.

Since equality in
eq.

14
follows from the multi-linearity of the determinant, and since
\frac{|S_{(s,:)}|}{|S_{((1:n),:)}|}
is a Schur polynomial, our claim can be restated as

\frac{|S_{(s,:)}|}{|S_{((1:n),:)}|}=\frac{1}{{|S_{((1:n),:)}|}}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}m_{\mu}(x),

which is identical to
eq.

9
.
∎

Let
x\in\mathds{C}^{M}
be such that all
x_{i}
are distinct from each other. Then,
\textnormal{rank}(V_{M}(x))=M
and there exists a
C\in\mathds{C}^{M\times n}
such that
A=V_{M}(x)C
. By the multi-linearity of the determinant and
3
, it follows then that

\displaystyle|A_{(s,:)}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{M}\cdots\sum_{j_{n}=1}^{M}\left[|{V_{M}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})}_{(s,:)}|\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{j_{i}i}\right]=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{M}\cdots\sum_{j_{n}=1}^{M}\left[\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}d_{\mu}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{j_{i}i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}K_{\lambda,\mu}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{M}\cdots\sum_{j_{n}=1}^{M}\left[d_{\mu}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{j_{i}i}\right],

where
d_{\mu}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})
are defined as in
3
. Since all the determinants that compose
d_{\mu}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})
result from the same columns as
{V_{M}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})}_{(s,:)}
, we are allowed to revert our initial expansion such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{M}\cdots\sum_{j_{n}=1}^{M}\left[d_{\mu}(x_{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{j_{i}i}\right]=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}\begin{vmatrix}a_{p_{1}+1,1}&\dots&a_{p_{n}+1,n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{p_{1}+n,1}&\dots&a_{p_{n}+n,n}\end{vmatrix}=D_{\mu}

and our claim follows.
∎Although
1
is a straightforward representation-theoretic identity, the authors are not aware of any prior explicit appearance of this result in the literature.

3.2
k
-Sign Consistency of Hankel Matrices

An immediate consequence of
1
together with the nonnegativity of Kostka numbers is the following certificate for
k
-sign consistency.

Corollary 3
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathds{C}^{M\times n}
with
M\geq n
. If all row-consecutive
k
-minors of
A
share the same (strict) sign, then
A
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent.

Although
3
provides a sufficient certificate, it is typically inefficient: it requires computing far more minors than directly evaluating all
k
-minors, and it is often overly restrictive. For our purposes, however, i.e., the study of
k
-sign consistent Hankel matrices, this criterion is surprisingly close to the favorable checks that we want to show. The case
k=2
already illustrates this phenomenon.

Example 1
.

Let us apply
1
to any two columns of

A:=H^{g}(1,M,N)=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&g_{i+1}&\dots&g_{N}\\
g_{2}&g_{i+2}&\dots&g_{N+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{M}&g_{M+1}&\dots&g_{M+N-1}\end{bmatrix},\;M,N\geq 2.

(15)

Similar to
1
, it sufficies to consider the
2
-minors
|H^{g}(1,s,r)|
,
s\in\mathcal{I}_{M,2}
and

r\in\mathcal{I}_{N,2}
with
s_{1}=r_{1}=1
, because all other cases follow by deleting the first
s_{1}-1
rows and
r_{1}-1
columns of
A
.

Thus, by
1

\displaystyle|A_{(s,r)}|

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}g_{1}&g_{r_{2}}\\
g_{s_{2}}&g_{s_{2}+r_{2}-1}\end{vmatrix}=\sum_{i=0}^{s_{2}-2}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix},

(16)

which shows that all
2
-minors of
A
can be expressed as a linear combination of
2
-minors of the form

b^{g}_{i_{1},i_{2}}:=\begin{vmatrix}g_{i_{1}}&g_{i_{2}}\\
g_{i_{1}+1}&g_{i_{2}+1}\end{vmatrix},\;i\in\mathcal{I}_{M+N-2,2},

with a negative sign if
i+1>s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}
, i.e.,
2i>s_{2}+r_{2}-3
. If
s_{2}\leq r_{2}+1
, then every term in our expansion is a regular
2
-minor of
H^{g}(1,2,M+N-2)
. Moreover, if
s_{2}\geq r_{2}+1
, then

\displaystyle|A_{(s,r)}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{s_{2}-2}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix}=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{s_{2}+r_{2}-3}{2}\rfloor}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix}-\sum_{i=\lfloor\frac{s_{2}+r_{2}-3}{2}\rfloor+1}^{s_{2}-2}\begin{vmatrix}g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}&g_{i+1}\\
g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}&g_{i+2}\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{s_{2}+r_{2}-3}{2}\rfloor}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix}-\sum_{i=r_{2}-1}^{\lceil\frac{s_{2}+r_{2}-3}{2}\rceil-1}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix}=\sum_{i=0}^{r_{2}-2}\begin{vmatrix}g_{i+1}&g_{s_{2}-2-i+r_{2}}\\
g_{i+2}&g_{s_{2}-1-i+r_{2}}\end{vmatrix},

where the last equality removes a zero
2
-minor of the first sum in case that
s_{2}+r_{2}-3
is even. Hence, all
2
-minors of
A
are nonnegative linear integer combinations of the
2
-mionors
b^{g}_{i_{1},i_{2}}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}_{M+N-2,2}
, or equivalently, of the
2
-minors of
H^{g}(1,2,M+N-2)
.

This shows that
H^{g}(1,M,N)
is (strictly)
2
-sign consistent if the reshaped Hankel matrix
H^{g}(1,2,M+N-2)
is (strictly)
2
-sign consistent. In case of
\mathcal{H}^{g}
, this is also a necessary condition, since all
2
-minors of
H^{g}(1,2,2N-2)
are two
2
-minors of
H^{g}(1,2N-2,2N-2)
.

In the following, we extend the conclusions of
example

1
to the cases with
k>2
. Let
A=H^{g}(1,M,N)
,
s\in\mathcal{I}_{M,k}
, and
r\in\mathcal{I}_{N,k}
. Then
eq.

10
specializes to

|A_{(s,r)}|=\sum_{{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}}K_{\lambda,\mu}D_{\mu,r}

(17)

where
\lambda_{i}=s_{k-i+1}-(k-i+1)
and

D_{\mu,r}:=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}b^{g}_{(p_{1}+r_{1},\dots,p_{k}+r_{k})}

(18)

with

b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}:=\begin{vmatrix}g_{i_{1}}&\dots&g_{i_{k}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{i_{1}+k-1}&\dots&g_{i_{k}+k-1}\end{vmatrix},\;i\in(1:M+N-k)^{k}.

As in
example

1
, this shows that every
k
-minor of
A
is a linear combination of the
k
-minors of the reshaped Hankel matrix
H^{g}(1,k,M+N-k)
. Thus, it remains to show that – after possible cancellations – each such
k
-minor appears with a nonnegative coefficient in
eq.

17
.

To this end, we identify which
\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
(up to column permutations) contribute to the same minor
b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}
. For fixed
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
and
p^{\ast}\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu^{\ast})
, the
k
-minor
b^{g}_{(p_{1}^{\ast}+r_{1},\dots,p_{k}^{\ast}+r_{k})}
appears in
eq.

17
for other choices of
\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
and
p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)
, up to column permutation, if and only if

r+p\in\textnormal{Perm}(v^{\ast}),

where
v^{\ast}:=(r+p^{\ast})
. Define
\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast}):=\{q\in\textnormal{Perm}(v^{\ast}):q-r\geq 0\}
, then the set of all such
\mu
is given by

\{(q-r)^{\downarrow}:q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})\}

(19)

with corresponding
k
-minors
b^{g}_{q}
appearing in
D_{(q-r)^{\downarrow},r}
in
eq.

18
. Since (
19
) is nonempty if and only if
{v^{\ast}}^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})
, and since we only need to consider
v^{\ast}
with distinct entries (otherwise
b^{g}_{v^{\ast}}=0
), we may use the identity

\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast}):b^{g}_{q}=\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}^{\uparrow}}(q)b^{g}_{{v^{\ast}}^{\uparrow}}.

Thus,

|A_{(s,r)}|=\sum_{{v^{\ast}\in V^{\ast}}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}}(q)K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}b^{g}_{v^{\ast}},

(20)

where

V^{\ast}:=\{(p+r)^{\uparrow}:p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu),\;\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda},p+r\text{ has distinct entries}\}.

Since each
b^{g}_{v^{\ast}}
is a
k
-minor of
H^{g}(1,k,M+N-k)
, the desired nonnegative integer combination exists provided that

\forall v^{\ast}\in V^{\ast}:\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}}(q)K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}\geq 0.

This inequality follows from the next lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{M,k}
,
r\in\mathcal{I}_{N,k}
, and

v^{\ast}\in\{\,(p+r)^{\uparrow}:p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu),\;\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda},\;(p+r)^{\uparrow}_{i}>(p+r)^{\uparrow}_{i-1}\text{ for all }i\in(2:k)\}.

Then

\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(q)\,K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)\,K_{\lambda,(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}}=c^{\gamma^{\ast}}_{\lambda,\varepsilon},

(21)

where

\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast}):=\{\,q\in\textnormal{Perm}(v^{\ast}):q-r\geq 0\,\},\qquad\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast}):=\{\,p\in\textnormal{Perm}(r):v^{\ast}-p\geq 0\,\},

and

\gamma_{i}^{\ast}:=v^{\ast}_{k-i+1}-k+i,\qquad\varepsilon_{i}:=r_{k-i+1}-k+i,\qquad i\in(1:k).

We begin by showing the first equality in
eq.

21
. Observe that

\{(q-r)^{\downarrow}:q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})\}=\{(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}:p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})\},

since sorting removes any dependence on the order of the entries. By the assumption on
v^{\ast}
, each of the sets

\{q-r:q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{v^{\ast}-p:p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})\}

consists of distinct elements, i.e., for every
p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})
there exist unique
q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})
and
\sigma\in\textnormal{Perm}((1:k))
such that

(v^{\ast}-p)_{\sigma}=v^{\ast}_{\sigma}-p_{\sigma}=q-r.

As this equation is fulfilled by
p_{\sigma}=r
and
q=v^{\ast}_{\sigma}
, we conclude that
\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(q)=\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)
. This proves the first equality in
eq.

21
.
Next, define the polynomial

F(x):=s_{\lambda}(x)\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(r)}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)x^{p}=\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(r)}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}\sum_{q\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)K_{\lambda,\mu}x^{q+p},

(22)

where equality follows by
eq.

9
. Since
\{(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}:p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
and
v^{\ast}-p\in\textnormal{Perm}((v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow})
, we see that
x^{q+p}=x^{v^{\ast}}
for some
q\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu)
if and only if
p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})
and
\mu=(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}
. Therefore,
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)K_{\lambda,(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}}
is exactly the coefficient of
x^{v^{\ast}}
in
F(x)
. By
eqs.

7
and
6
, it follows that

\sum_{p\in\textnormal{Perm}(r)}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)x^{p}=a_{r}(x)=s_{\varepsilon}(x)a_{(0,1,\dots,k-1)}

and, therefore, using
eqs.

8
and
7
,

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=s_{\lambda}(x)s_{\varepsilon}(x)a_{(0,1,\dots,k-1)}(x)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}s_{\gamma}(x)a_{(0,1,\dots,k-1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda+\varepsilon}}c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}a_{(\gamma_{k},\gamma_{k-1}+1,\dots,\gamma_{1}+k-1)}.

Since
v^{\ast}
has strictly increasing entries, the monomial
x^{v^{\ast}}
appears in
F(x)
if and only if

a_{(\gamma_{k},\gamma_{k-1}+1,\dots,\gamma_{1}+k-1)}=a_{v^{\ast}}

for some
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda+\varepsilon}
with
c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\neq 0
. Thus, the coefficient of
x^{v^{\ast}}
in
F(x)
is exactly
c^{\gamma^{\ast}}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
, proving

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{r}(p)\,K_{\lambda,(v^{\ast}-p)^{\downarrow}}=c^{\gamma^{\ast}}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}.

∎We illustrate
4
with two numerical examples.

Example 2
.

Let
\lambda=(4,1,0)
,
r=(1,2,4)
, and consequently
\varepsilon=r^{\downarrow}-(2,1,0)=(2,1,0)
. We consider the following two cases:

1.

Case 1
v^{\ast}=(1,4,7)
:
Here
\gamma^{\ast}=(5,3,1)
, so by
4
it is possible that
eq.

21
is positive.
Q(v^{\ast})
consists of

(0,2,3)=v^{\ast}-r\qquad\text{and}\qquad(0,5,0)=(1,7,4)-r,

where
K_{\lambda,(5,0,0)}=0
by
2
and
K_{\lambda,(3,2,0)}=1
with corresponding
\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(v^{\ast})=1
. Hence,
\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(q)\,K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}=1
, which which agrees with the Littlewood–Richardson coefficient
c^{\gamma^{\ast}}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=1
.

2.

Case 2
v^{\ast}=(2,4,6)
:
In this case,
\gamma^{\ast}=(4,3,2)
, so again
4
allows the possibility of a positive value. However, one can show that
c^{\gamma^{\ast}}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=0
. This can also be verified by noticing that
Q(v^{\ast})
is comprised of

(5,0,0)=(6,2,4)-r,\qquad(3,0,2)=(4,2,6)-r\qquad\text{and}\qquad(1,2,2)=v^{\ast}-r,

with corresponding

\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(6,2,4)K_{\lambda,(3,2,0)}=1,\quad\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(4,2,6)K_{\lambda,(4,1,0)}=-1,\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{sign}_{v^{\ast}}(v^{\ast})K_{\lambda,(2,2,1)}=2.

Thus,
\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}}(q)K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}=0
.

Remark 2
.

Equation

21
also appears in a different form in
[
22
, Corollary 1]
, namely

c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\textnormal{Perm}((1,\dots,k))\\
\psi\trianglelefteq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\textnormal{sign}_{(1,\dots,k)}(\eta)\,K_{\varepsilon,\psi},

(23)

where only nonnegative
\psi=((\gamma+(k-1,\dots,1,0))_{\eta}+\lambda-(k-1,\dots,1,0))^{\downarrow}
are permitted. To see the equivalence with
eq.

21
, note first that
c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=c^{\gamma}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}
by
eq.

8
, so one may interchange the roles of
\lambda
and
\varepsilon
in order to express the sum using
\lambda
-dependent Kostka numbers, as in
eq.

21
. For the choices of
v^{\ast}
and
r
(and corresponding
\gamma
and
\varepsilon
) in
4
, we have

\gamma+(k-1,\dots,1,0)={v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon+(k-1,\dots,1,0)=r^{\downarrow}

so that
\psi={(({v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow})_{\eta}-r^{\downarrow})}^{\downarrow}
. Moreover, since
{v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow}-r^{\downarrow}\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
, the condition
\psi\trianglelefteq\lambda
may be omitted by
2
. Therefore,
eq.

23
becomes

\displaystyle c^{\gamma}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=c^{\gamma}_{\varepsilon,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\textnormal{Perm}((1,\dots,k)),\\
{({v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow})_{\eta}-r^{\downarrow}}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\textnormal{sign}_{(1,\dots,k)}(\eta)\,K_{\lambda,(({v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow})_{\eta}-r^{\downarrow})^{\downarrow}}=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}({v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow})}\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}^{\downarrow}}(q)K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(v^{\ast})}\textnormal{sign}_{{v^{\ast}}}(q)K_{\lambda,(q-r)^{\downarrow}},

where the final equality uses the fact that
v^{\ast}
has distinct entries. Although the resulting identity is the same, our derivation differs substantially from that of
[
22
]
. Our approach is motivated by applications to Hankel matrices and requires significantly fewer prerequisites.

In conclusion, since Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are nonnegative by definition,
eq.

20
shows that each minor
|A_{(s,r)}|
is a nonnegative linear combination of the minors
b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})},\;i\in\mathcal{I}_{M+N-k,k}
. Hence, if all
b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}
share the same sign, then so do all
|A_{(s,r)}|
. The strict case follows similarly, since by

c^{\gamma(\lambda^{\uparrow}+r)}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=c^{\lambda+\varepsilon}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\geq 1

(24)

at least one strictly positive coefficient appears in
eq.

20
.
This is summarized in the following main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
g\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{Z}}
,
A:=H^{g}(1,M,N)\in\mathds{C}^{M\times N}
and
k\leq\min\{M,N\}
. For
s\in\mathcal{I}_{M,k}
and
r\in\mathcal{I}_{N,k}
, define

b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}:=\begin{vmatrix}g_{i_{1}}&\dots&g_{i_{k}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{i_{1}+k-1}&\dots&g_{i_{k}+k-1}\end{vmatrix},\qquad(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in(1:M+N-k)^{k}

Then

|A_{(s,r)}|=\sum_{v^{\ast}\in V^{\ast}}c^{\gamma(v^{\ast})}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\,b^{g}_{v^{\ast}},

(25)

where

\lambda_{i}:=s_{k-i+1}-(k-i+1),\qquad\varepsilon_{i}:=r_{k-i+1}-k+i,\qquad\gamma_{i}(v^{\ast}):=v^{\ast}_{k-i+1}-k+i,\qquad i\in(1:k)

and

V^{\ast}:=\bigl\{(p+r)^{\uparrow}:p\in\textnormal{Perm}(\mu),\;\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda},\;(p+r)^{\uparrow}_{i}>(p+r)^{\uparrow}_{i-1}\text{ for all }i\in(2:k)\bigr\}.

In particular, if the reshaped Hankel matrix
H^{g}(1,k,M+N-k)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent, i.e., all minors
b^{g}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}_{M+N-k,k}
, share the same (strict) sign, then
A
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent.

Note that
2
provides only a sufficient condition, since it requires (strict)
k
-sign consistency of a reshaped Hankel matrix. In the finite-dimensional setting this condition may be stronger than necessary (see the circulant matrix case below). However, in the infinite-dimensional operator case, the situation changes fundamentally: the condition becomes not only sufficient but also necessary. This leads us to the following analogue of
2
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
g\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{Z}}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathcal{H}^{g}
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent.

2.

For all
N\geq k
:
H^{g}(1,k,N)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent.

By definition,
\mathcal{H}^{g}
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent if and only if
H^{g}(1,N,N)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent for every
N\geq k
. However, by
2
, this requirement is already satisfied provided that
H^{g}(1,k,2N-k)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent for all
N\geq k
. Thus the two conditions are equivalent.
∎At this point, it is worth noting that the class of
k
-sign consistent Hankel matrices (and operators) is strictly larger than the class of
k
-positive Hankel matrices (and operators).
Indeed,
k
-sign consistency does not require
1
-sign consistency.
The following example illustrates this.

Example 3
.

Let
g_{t}=\alpha_{1}^{t-1}+k\,\alpha_{2}^{t-1}
for
t\geq 1
, where

k,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathds{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then, for all
t,j\in\mathds{Z}_{>0}
,

b^{g}_{(t,t+j)}=p\,\alpha_{1}^{t-1}\alpha_{2}^{t-1}\bigl(\alpha_{1}^{j}-\alpha_{2}^{j}\bigr)(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}).

(26)

Thus
H^{g}(1,M,N)
is strictly
2
-sign consistent for any
M,N\geq 2

if and only if
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
. However, strict
1
-sign consistency fails in general.
For example, choosing
\alpha_{1}=1
,
\alpha_{2}=0.5
, and
p=-3
yields

(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})=(-2,\,-0.5,\,0.25),

which changes sign.
Hence
H^{g}(1,M,N)
is
2
-sign consistent but not
1
-sign consistent.

Unfortunately, unlike
2
, there is in general no further simplification that would allow us to check only, for example, consecutive
k
-minors of
H^{g}(1,k,M+N-k)
. Indeed, in
example

3
, if one were to impose strict sign consistency solely on consecutive
2
-minors (i.e., by choosing
j=1
in
eq.

26
), then the choice
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{<0}
would also satisfy this restricted condition, despite being incorrect for full
2
-sign consistency.

Yet, it should be noted that
2
enables the use of
1
. In particular, if
H^{g}(1,k,k)
is invertible, then the (strict)
k
-sign consistency of

H^{g}(1,M,N)
,
M,N\geq k
, can be verified by checking the (strict) total positivity of

K_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\,H^{g}(1,k,k)^{-1}\,H^{g}(k+1,k,M+N-k),

where
K_{k}
is defined in
1
.
This verification can be performed efficiently using, for example,
1
.
Then, employing Dodgson condensation as in
[
3
]
, the overall computational complexity reduces to
\mathcal{O}\!\left(k^{2}(M+N)\right)
whenever
k\leq N+M-k
.

3.2.1
Toeplitz Matrices

Since reversing the column order of a Toeplitz matrix produces a Hankel matrix without affecting its (strict)
k
-sign consistency, direct analogues of
2
and
4
hold in the Toeplitz setting.

Corollary 5
.

Let
g\in\mathds{C}^{\mathds{Z}}
, and
A:=T^{g}(t,M,N)\in\mathds{C}^{M\times N}
with
k\leq\min\{M,N\}
. If the reshaped Toeplitz matrix
T^{g}(t+M-k,k,M+N-k)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent, then
A
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent. In particular, the Toeplitz operator
\mathcal{T}^{g}
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent if and only if
T^{g}(N-k,k,2N-k)
is (strictly)
k
-sign consistent for all
N\geq k
.

Unfortunately, for circulant matrices
C^{g}=T^{g}(0,T,T)
with
g\in\ell_{\infty}(T)
,
5
is often too conservative. Indeed, the reshaped Toeplitz
B:=T^{g}(T-k,k,2T-k)
is required to be (strictly)
k
-sign consistent. Due to the
T
-periodicity of
g
, this implies, e.g., that

|B_{(:,(1:k))}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|B_{(:,(2:k)\cup\{T+1\})}|=-|B_{(:,(1:k))}|

must share the same (strict) sign whenever
k<T
. Hence, in the strict case,
5
cannot be applied.

Example 4
.

As shown in
[
12
]
, an example of a
g\in\ell_{\infty}(T)
with strictly
3
-sign consistent
C^{g}
is given by

g_{t}=\frac{1}{1-0.9^{T}}0.9^{t-1}-\frac{2}{1-0.8^{T}}0.8^{t-1}+\frac{1}{1-0.7^{T}}0.7^{t-1},\;t\in(1:T).

In particular, if
T=4
, then

B=T^{g}(1,3,5)=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&g_{0}&g_{3}&g_{2}&g_{1}\\
g_{2}&g_{1}&g_{4}&g_{3}&g_{2}\\
g_{3}&g_{2}&g_{0}&g_{4}&g_{3}\end{bmatrix}

with
0<|B_{(:,(1:3))}|=-|B_{(:,(2:3)\cup\{5\})}|
, i.e.,
B
is not
3
-sign consistent. Thus,
5
cannot be used to certify strict
3
-sign consistentcy of
C^{g}
.

In the non-strict case, however, partial extensions are still possible. To see this, observe that circulant matrices cannot be
k
-sign consistent for even
k
, except in trivial cases. The following example illustrates this obstruction.

Example 5
.

The circulant matrix

C^{g}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}&g_{2}&g_{1}\\
g_{1}&g_{0}&g_{2}\\
g_{2}&g_{1}&g_{0}\end{bmatrix}

contains the
2
-minors

|C^{g}_{((1:2),(2:3))}|=g_{2}^{2}-g_{0}g_{1}=-|C^{g}_{(\{1,3\},(1:2))}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|C^{g}_{((2:3),(1:2))}|=g_{1}^{2}-g_{0}g_{2}=-|C^{g}_{(\{1,3\},(2:3))}|.

C^{g}
can, thus, not be strictly
2
-sign consistent. Moreover, assuming that all
g_{i}\neq 0
,
2
-sign consisteny of
C^{g}
is equivalent to
\frac{g_{1}}{g_{2}}=\frac{g_{2}}{g_{0}}=\frac{g_{0}}{g_{1}}
, i.e., all
g_{i}=\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathds{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

If
k
is odd and such that
2T-k\leq T+k-1
, it follows that, up to additionally created zero
k
-minors, the
k
-minors of
T^{g}(T-k,k,2T-k)
are identical to those of
T^{g}(T-k,k,T)
, or equivalently, to those of
C^{g}_{((1:k),:)}
. Therefore, by application of
5
, the following equivalence holds.

Corollary 6
.

Let
g\in\ell_{\infty}(T)
and
k\in(1:T)
be odd such that
2k\geq T+1
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

C^{g}
is
k
-sign consistent.

2.

C^{g}_{((1:k),:)}
is
k
-sign consistent.

Note that in the cases where
C^{g}
is strictly
1
-sign consistent, it has been shown in
[
12
]
that
6
is valid for
k=3
, independently of
T
.

4
Conclusion

We have developed a framework for efficiently verifying (strict)
k
-sign consistency of Hankel and Toeplitz matrices — meaning that all their
k
-th order minors share the same (strict) sign. While our results provide only sufficient conditions in the finite-dimensional matrix setting, they become necessary and sufficient for the corresponding infinite-dimensional operators. This generalizes the classical certificates for
k
-positivity (i.e., nonnegativity of all minors of order up to
k
) of Hankel and Toeplitz matrices
[
11
,
15
,
5
]
. It, further, enables the direct use of tools such as
[
19
]
, which reduce the verification of
k
-sign consistency for a matrix with
k
rows or columns to a total positivity problem.

Our derivations rely on algebraic combinatorics – specifically Schur polynomials, Kostka numbers, and Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
[
23
]
— to express every
k
-th order minor of a matrix as a nonnegative integer linear combination of intermediate row-consecutive
k
-th order minors. These minors arise from submatrices whose columns are formed from
k
consecutive row indices, which need not coincide across columns. For Hankel matrices, a structural identity links the signed sums of the associated coefficients (Kostka numbers) to Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, allowing us to replace these row-consecutive minors with standard
k
-th order minors of a reshaped Hankel matrix with
k
rows.

Beyond their immediate implications, the connections uncovered here between algebraic combinatorics and total positivity open several promising directions for further research. These include extending analogous decompositions to other structured matrix classes, as well as exploring their consequences in system theory
[
11
,
9
,
17
,
25
]
and sparse optimization
[
18
]
.
