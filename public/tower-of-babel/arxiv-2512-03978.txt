On well-posedness for second-order degenerate parabolic equations with unbounded lower-order terms
Khalid Baadi

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the well-posedness of Cauchy problems for weak solutions to second-order degenerate parabolic equations with a non-smooth, time-dependent degenerate elliptic part that includes both bounded and unbounded lower-order terms. The unbounded lower-order terms are allowed to lie in mixed time-space Lebesgue or even Lorentz spaces. Our notion of weak solutions is formulated under minimal assumptions. We prove the existence and uniqueness of a fundamental solution, which coincides with the associated evolution family for the homogeneous problem ( i.e. , with zero source term) and provides a representation formula for all weak solutions. We also establish L 2 L^{2} off-diagonal estimates for the fundamental solution and derive Gaussian upper bounds under the weak assumption of Moser’s L 2 L^{2} – L ∞ L^{\infty} estimates for weak solutions. Our approach is purely variational and avoids any a priori regularity assumptions on weak solutions or regularization via smooth approximations. Two key ingredients are norm inequalities for fractional powers of the degenerate Laplacian, and a set of embeddings that ensure time continuity of weak solutions, extending the classical Lions regularity theorem and accommodating a wide class of source terms.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to construct solutions to parabolic Cauchy problems of the form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,\mathfrak{T})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\\
u(0)=\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathrm{d}\omega),\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the degenerate elliptic part, which is non-autonomous and includes both bounded and unbounded lower-order terms. More precisely, we consider

(1.3)

\mathcal{B}u=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(A\nabla_{x}u)-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\,au)+b\cdot\nabla_{x}u+cu,

where the coefficients
A
,
a
,
b
, and
c
depend on both time and space variables
(t,x)
. The leading-order term involves a matrix-valued function
A=A(t,x)
with complex measurable entries. The degeneracy of the operator is governed by a spatial weight
\omega=\omega(x)
, which is assumed to be time-independent and to belong to the Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In this setting, we consider
\omega^{-1}A
to be bounded, and we may also assume that it satisfies the classical uniform ellipticity condition. For the lower-order terms
a
,
b
, and
c
, we assume that each can be decomposed as the sum of a bounded term and an unbounded one. Our main interest is in unbounded terms in critical or near-critical spaces, such as mixed time-space Lebesgue or Lorentz spaces, which arise in parabolic PDEs with rough coefficients and degenerate elliptic structures.

The main and natural questions one may ask are the following:

•

What conditions should be imposed on the coefficients
a
,
b
, and
c
to establish the well-posedness of (
1
) ?

•

How may these coefficients depend on the weight
\omega
, and what additional assumptions, beyond the
A_{2}
condition, might be required on
\omega
?

•

In what sense is (
1
) well-posed ?

•

What is the appropriate existence and uniqueness class, and for which classes of source terms can one solve (
1
) ?

•

What is the regularity of weak solutions ?

•

Is there a fundamental solution for the degenerate parabolic operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
that represents weak solutions ?

•

If so, does it satisfy bounds similar to those known in the classical literature when
\omega=1
?

This paper provides us with an answer to all these questions. Before summarizing our results, it is useful to briefly review some classical and well-known results that address these questions in particular cases, such as the unweighted case (
i.e.
,
\omega=1
) or situations without unbounded lower-order terms. The existing literature on the subject is vast, so we will highlight only a few representative works to give context and motivation.

Let us begin with the unweighted case
\omega=1
. When the coefficients are sufficiently regular, several classical methods are available to establish well-posedness and construct the fundamental solution. One of the most effective techniques combines a parametrix construction with the freezing point method
[
18
]
. This approach simplifies the problem by locally freezing the coefficients, making them effectively space-independent, and yields explicit solutions represented by smooth kernels
\Gamma(t,x,s,y)
with Gaussian decay. In the case where the coefficients are merely real, measurable, and possibly unbounded, the classical theory of Ladyzhenskaya, Solonnikov, and Ural’tseva
[
29
]
provides a foundational framework. Along similar lines, and still in the case of real coefficients and an elliptic part of order two, Aronson
[
1
,
2
]
constructed generalized fundamental solutions using Nash’s regularity theory
[
34
]
and proved upper and lower Gaussian bounds. More recently, Auscher and Egert
[
6
]
revisited these problems of constructing solutions and extended the results of
[
29
]
to more general settings, allowing complex coefficients and even unbounded lower-order terms lying in mixed Lorentz spaces. Their work relies on a variational approach that unifies and generalizes many earlier methods. For additional related works, see also
[
7
,
28
]
.

In the weighted case (
\omega\neq 1
) and in the pure second-order setting (
i.e.
,
a=b=c=0
), the existence of a fundamental solution was established by Cruz-Uribe and Rios
[
13
]
, under the additional assumptions that
A
is real-valued, symmetric, and independent of time. In this case, the fundamental solution is constructed via the semigroup
(e^{-t\mathcal{B}})_{t>0}
associated with the operator
\mathcal{B}=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{x})
. More recently, Ataei and Nyström
[
3
]
extended this result to the case where
A=A(t,x)
depends on time, still within the pure second-order framework and under the assumption that
A
remains real-valued. Their construction relies on an approximation argument inspired by Kato’s abstract theory
[
26
]
. Both works make use of Hölder continuity estimates for weak solutions to derive pointwise Gaussian upper bounds. More recently, the author
[
8
]
, using a variational framework, reproved and generalized these results to allow for complex coefficients, and obtained upper pointwise Gaussian bounds using simpler arguments and under weaker assumptions than those required for Hölder continuity estimates of weak solutions. While all of these approaches can be readily extended to include bounded lower-order terms, the situation becomes significantly more delicate in the presence of unbounded ones, and the question of constructing weak solutions has not been addressed.

Although it is a different question, regularity of local weak solutions has been studied. Ishige
[
24
]
establishes Harnack inequalities for nonnegative weak solutions with real coefficients and some unbounded lower-order terms, extending previous works in the purely second-order setting
[
11
,
10
,
12
]
. His assumptions rely on a strong reverse doubling condition on the weight (assumption (A5) in
[
24
]
). We will consider different conditions, so regularity theory under our conditions will require some further work that we plan to do subsequently.

We now turn to the presentation of our results. We restrict ourselves to one representative result in the case where only the lower-order coefficients are unbounded and
\mathfrak{T}=\infty
, corresponding to the homogeneous version of our theory. Further extensions and variants will be discussed in the paper.

Fix
r,q\in[2,\infty)
and assume that

\omega\in A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n}{2q}=\frac{n}{4}.

Note that
r=2
is possible, in which case
n\geq 3
and
q=2^{\star}:=\frac{2n}{n-2}
, but we always have
q>2
since
\frac{1}{r}>0
. For any open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we define the mixed space

\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I):=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})):u\in L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\right\},

and endow it with the norm

\|u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)}:=\|u\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}.

Set

(1.4)

P_{r,q}:=\|a\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}})}+\|b\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}})}+\|c\|_{L^{{\frac{r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}.

Set
M:=\|\omega^{-1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}
. Assuming only
M<\infty
and
P_{r,q}<\infty
allows us to define the operator

\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}:\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

To construct solutions, we begin with the case of
I=\mathbb{R}
, under the hypothesis that the restriction

\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}:\dot{V}_{0}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}

is invertible, where
\dot{V}_{0}
is a variational space that can be viewed as

\dot{V}_{0}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{H}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).

The space
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
is the anti-dual of
\dot{V}_{0}
, and this is what we call the "variational approach".

Let us formulate our representative result. We assume that
A
is elliptic in the sense of Gårding; that is, there exists
\nu>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and all
u\in H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla_{x}u(x)|^{2}\,\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{Re}\left(A(t,x)\nabla_{x}u(x)\cdot\overline{\nabla_{x}u(x)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

We use fractional powers of the degenerate Laplacian
-\Delta_{\omega}=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\nabla_{x})
. See Section
2.2
.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(M,\nu,[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)>0
such that, if
P_{r,q}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then the following assertions hold.

(1)

Let
\rho\in[2,\infty]
, and define
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}\in[0,1]
and let
\rho^{\prime}
denotes the Hölder conjugate of
\rho
. Let
h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. There exists a unique
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}((0,\infty))
solution to the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\\
u(t)\rightarrow\psi\ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \mathrm{as}\ t\rightarrow 0^{+}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, with
u(0)=\psi
,
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|^{2}_{2,\omega}
is absolutely continuous on
[0,\infty)
and we can write the energy equalities. Furthermore, there exists a constant
C\in(0,\infty)
, depending only on the structural constants
M
,
\nu
,
[\omega]_{A_{2}}
,
[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}}
,
n
and
q
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(t)\|_{2,\omega}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}\leq C(\left\|h\right\|_{L^{\rho^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\psi\|_{2,\omega}).

(2)

There exists a unique fundamental solution
\Gamma=(\Gamma(t,s))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}
for
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
. In particular, for all
t\geq 0
, we have the following representation formula for
u
, the solution in item (1):

\displaystyle u(t)=\Gamma(t,0)\psi+\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(t,\tau)(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau,

where the integral is weakly defined in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and also strongly defined in the Bochner sense when
\rho=\infty
.

Let us comment this result. First, it is perturbative in nature with respect to the pure second-order case and follows as a particular instance of Theorem
3.43
. The smallness of
P_{r,q}
is sufficient to guarantee the invertibility and causality of the degenerate parabolic operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
on
\mathbb{R}
, but it is not necessary. For alternative invertibility conditions that do not require a smallness assumption, as well as for the definition of a fundamental solution, the interpretation of the equations, and further details, we refer the reader to Sections
2
and
3
.

Second, note that the quantity
P_{r,q}
does not depend on
\omega
: it is exactly the one taken in the unweighted theory
[
29
,
6
]
. This may seem surprising at first. The reason is that we use the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev estimates in Theorem
2.4
for the degenerate Laplacian
-\Delta_{\omega}
,
and this is precisely where the assumption
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
comes into play. These estimates are a key ingredient of our theory. Third, despite the apparently abstract formulation of our source terms, it covers in fact the following concrete ones and their linear combinations:

•

When
\rho=\infty
, we have
(\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h=h\in L^{1}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

•

When
\rho=2
, we can write, for any
F\in L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
,
-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F)=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2}h
for some
h\in L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. See Lemma
3.1
.

•

When
2\leq\rho<\infty
, set
\tilde{r}=\rho
and let
\tilde{q}\in(2,\infty)
satisfy
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}=\frac{n}{4}
. If
\omega\in RH_{\tilde{q}/2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then any
g\in L^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L_{\omega^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}/2}}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
can be written as
g=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h
for some
h\in L^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L_{\omega}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
,
see Lemma
3.5
. If, in addition,
\rho\geq r
, the condition on
\omega
in the last statement is fulfilled; see Point (2) of Remarks
3.4
.

Note that it is also possible to consider lower-order terms lying in mixed Lorentz spaces. Namely, we may assume that

L_{r,q}:=\|a\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}+\|b\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}+\|c\|_{L^{\frac{r}{r-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty,

where
L^{p,q}
denotes Lorentz spaces. An example of such coefficients is

a(t,x)=\frac{a_{\infty}(t,x)}{|t|^{n\frac{r-2}{2r}}|x|^{n\frac{q-2}{2q}}},\ b(t,x)=\frac{b_{\infty}(t,x)}{|t|^{n\frac{r-2}{2r}}|x|^{n\frac{q-2}{2q}}},\ c(t,x)=\frac{c_{\infty}(t,x)}{|t|^{n\frac{r-2}{r}}|x|^{n\frac{q-2}{q}}}\ \ \ (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n},

where
a_{\infty}
,
b_{\infty}
, and
c_{\infty}
are measurable and bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
taking values in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. For instance, when
r=2
, this gives us
a(t,x)=\frac{a_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|}
,
b(t,x)=\frac{b_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|}
,
c(t,x)=\frac{c_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|^{2}}
, the usual size for drifts and potentials. The assumption
L_{r,q}<\infty
, together with invertibility of
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
on a variational space, is sufficient to construct solutions of equations of the form
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. These solutions belong to a suitable class
\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
, which is contained in
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
, and we allow for a wider class of source terms
f
also measured by mixed Lorentz spaces. In the context of parabolic Cauchy problems, we require the stronger condition

\ell_{r,q}:=\|a\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}((0,\infty);L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}+\|b\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}((0,\infty);L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})}+\|c\|_{L^{\frac{r}{r-2}}((0,\infty);L^{\frac{q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty,

which remains a weaker assumption than
P_{r,q}<\infty
. This distinction is used to ensure causality results. We refer the reader to Section
4
for further details.

Now, when each of the lower-order terms
a
,
b
, and
c
is decomposed into the sum of a bounded part and an unbounded one (“unbounded” meaning belonging to the mixed Lebesgue or Lorentz spaces introduced above), this corresponds to the inhomogeneous version of our theory, which is treated in Section
5
. This is the case, for any pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
with
r\leq\tilde{r}
,
2<\tilde{q}
and
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}>\frac{n}{4}
, when

a,b\in L^{\frac{2\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2\tilde{q}}{\tilde{q}-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad c\in L^{\frac{\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{\tilde{q}}{\tilde{q}-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),

with the additional condition
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}-\frac{1}{2\tilde{q}}<\frac{1}{4}
when
n=1
. See Remark
5.6
for further details and the connection with subcritical exponents appearing in the previously cited literature.

In the final section, Section
6
, we establish
L^{2}
off-diagonal estimates for the fundamental solution. We also derive Gaussian upper bounds under the assumption that Moser’s
L^{2}
–
L^{\infty}
estimates hold for local weak solutions.

It is important to note that in both the homogeneous and inhomogeneous frameworks, we obtain weighted versions of the results in
[
29
,
6
]
, including the case
r=2
, which is excluded by the classical theory
[
29
]
. Conversely, the case
r=\infty
, covered by the unweighted theory
[
29
]
, is excluded from our setting, as in the unweighted version
[
6
]
, since the method is variational.

In
[
6
]
, all the function spaces under consideration are embedded in the ambient space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
, and the analysis relies on the use of the Fourier transform in the
x
-variable. However, this approach is no longer applicable when dealing with weighted spaces and degenerate operators. A key feature of our approach is the use of distribution spaces adapted to the degenerate operators under consideration, following the framework introduced in
[
4
]
. These spaces may appear abstract, as they are tailored to the functional analytic structure of the operator and do not necessarily sit inside
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
in any obvious way. Nonetheless, they provide the appropriate setting to establish existence results for weak solutions. It is important to stress, however, that this level of abstraction is used solely as a technical tool. All our results concerning well-posedness such as existence, uniqueness, and representation formulas are formulated in concrete terms, within explicit functional spaces that preserve a strong connection to the PDE framework.

For further details, we refer the reader to the main text.

2.
Basic Assumptions, Preliminaries, and Parabolic Tools

In preparation for the analysis of the well-posedness of parabolic equations in weighted settings, we begin by specifying the main assumptions on the weights and the underlying functional framework. We then present some preliminaries and parabolic tools that will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

2.1.
Muckenhoupt and reverse Hölder weight classes

We refer to
[
39
, Ch. V]
,
[
19
]
, and
[
20
, Ch. 7]
for classical facts on weights. We say that an almost everywhere (for Lebesgue measure) defined function
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is a weight if it is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as the set of all weights satisfying

(2.1)

[\omega]_{A_{2}}:=\sup_{Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\omega^{-1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)<\infty,

where the supremum is taken with respect to all cubes
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with sides parallel to the axes and
|Q|
is the Lebesgue measure of
Q
.

The reverse Hölder class
RH_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
q\in[1,\infty)
, is defined as the set of all weights
\omega
verifying, for some constant
C
,

\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\omega^{q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq\frac{C}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

for all cubes
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with sides parallel to the axes. The infimum of such constants
C
is what we denote by
[\omega]_{RH_{q}}
. Note that
RH_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the class of all weights.

In the following proposition, we summarize some properties of these classes of weights.

Proposition 2.2
.

We have the following properties.

(1)

RH_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset RH_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
1<p\leq q<\infty
.

(2)

If
\omega\in RH_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
1<q<\infty
, then there exists
p\in(q,\infty)
such that
\omega\in RH_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(3)

A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\bigcup_{1<q<\infty}RH_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2.2.
Function spaces and the degenerate Laplacian

Throughout this paper,
we fix a weight
\omega
belonging to the Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We introduce the measure
\mathrm{d}\omega:=\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x
. If
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a Lebesgue measurable set, we write
\omega(E)
instead of
\int_{E}\mathrm{d}\omega
.

It follows from (
2.1
) that there exists a constant
D=D(n,[\omega]_{A_{2}})
, called the doubling constant for
\omega
, such that

(2.3)

\omega(2Q)\leq D\omega(Q),

for all cubes
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with sides parallel to the axes. We may replace cubes by Euclidean balls. For simplicity, we keep using the same notation and constants.

For every
p\geq 1
and
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a measurable set, we denote by
L^{p}_{\omega}(K)
the space of all measurable functions
f:K\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\omega}(K)}:=\left(\int_{K}|f|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

In particular,
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
\mathrm{d}\omega
. We denote its norm by
{\lVert\cdot\rVert}_{2,\omega}
and its inner product by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2,\omega}
. The class
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\mathrm{d}\omega
is a Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, by (
2.1
), we have

L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We define
H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the space of functions
f\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for which the distributional gradient
\nabla_{x}f
belongs to
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
, and equip this space with the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}_{\omega}}:=(\left\|f\right\|_{2,\omega}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{x}f\right\|_{2,\omega}^{2})^{1/2}
making it a Hilbert space. The class
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and this follows from standard truncation and convolution techniques combined with the boundedness of the (unweighted) maximal operator on
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For a proof, see
[
27
, Thm. 2.5]
.

We define the degenerate Laplacian
-\Delta_{\omega}
as the unbounded self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
associated to the positive symmetric sesquilinear form on
H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined by

(u,v)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla_{x}u\cdot\overline{\nabla_{x}v}\ \mathrm{d}\omega.

The operator
-\Delta_{\omega}
is injective, since the measure
\mathrm{d}\omega
has infinite mass, as it is doubling (
2.3
). For all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta}
as the self-adjoint operator constructed via the functional calculus for sectorial operators, or equivalently, through the Borel functional calculus, since
-\Delta_{\omega}
is self-adjoint. For further details, we refer to
[
36
,
32
,
21
]
. Let the domain of
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta}
be denoted by
D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta})
. For
\beta=0
,
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta}=I
.

The following result concerns norm inequalities for fractional powers of the degenerate Laplacian
-\Delta_{\omega}
. For the proof, see the case
p=2
in
[
5
, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 3.24]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
q\in[2,\infty)
. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(2)

For
\alpha=n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)
, there exists a constant
C
such that

(2.5)

\|\sqrt{\omega}\,f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|f\|_{L^{q}_{{\omega}^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\alpha/2}f\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad\forall f\in D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\alpha/2}).

Moreover, the constant
C
in (2) depends only on
[\omega]_{A_{2}}
,
[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}}
,
n
, and
q
.

Remark 2.6
.

A typical example of a weight
\omega\in A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
2\leq q
, is given by

\omega(x)=|x|^{\beta},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\text{with}\ -\frac{2n}{q}<\beta<n.

2.3.
The distributional anti-duality bracket

In this paper, we adopt the following definition of the distributional anti-duality bracket, which differs from the standard one. All equalities in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are understood in this sense when tested against functions in
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 2.7

(The distributional bracket)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.

(1)

For
F\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
, we define
-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F)\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
by setting, for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\langle\!\langle-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\iint_{I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\overline{\varphi}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(x)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I}\langle F(t),\nabla_{x}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2)

For
h\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\beta\in[0,1]
, we define
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
by setting, for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\langle\!\langle(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\iint_{I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(t,x)(\overline{(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}\varphi}(t,\cdot))(x)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(x)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I}\langle h(t),((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

If
q_{1}\in[2,\infty)
and
q_{2}\in[1,\infty)
are such that
\omega^{q_{1}/2}
is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and if
g\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{q_{2}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}_{1}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, then we define, for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\langle\!\langle g,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\iint_{I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(t,x)\cdot\overline{\varphi}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(x)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I}\langle g(t),\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4)

(Time derivative) If
u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, we define its distributional time derivative
\partial_{t}u\in\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
by setting, for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\langle\!\langle\partial_{t}u,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\iint_{I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}-u(t,x)\cdot\partial_{t}\overline{\varphi}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(x)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I}-\langle u(t),\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

For
\varphi
with spatially compact support, the integral in (1) exists whenever
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, in (2) whenever
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(I;D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}))
, and in (3) whenever
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(I;L^{q^{\prime}_{2}}_{\omega^{q_{1}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Thus, in the same way, and with a slight abuse of notation, we keep writing the distributional anti-duality bracket with the test function
\varphi
on the right-hand side whenever this is meaningful, even if
\varphi\notin\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remark 2.8
.

In the above definition, we use sesquilinear brackets. However, we may transition to a distributional duality bracket without complex conjugation, since

\overline{(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}\,\overline{\phi}}=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}\phi,\quad\text{for all }\beta\in[0,1]\text{ and }\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

2.4.
Parabolic embeddings and integral identities

In this section, we collect some relevant abstract tools taken from
[
4
]
, to which we refer the reader for details. We do so to avoid repetition in both the homogeneous and the inhomogeneous versions of the theory.

Let
H
be a separable complex Hilbert space, and let
S
be a positive, self-adjoint, and injective operator on
H
. There exists a family
(D_{S,\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}
of completions of the domains
D(S^{\alpha})
with respect to the homogeneous norms
\|S^{\alpha}\cdot\|_{H}
. These spaces embed into a common ambient space
E_{\infty}
, the anti-dual of the Fréchet space
E_{-\infty}:=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}D(S^{\alpha})
. Moreover,
D_{S,\alpha}\cap H=D(S^{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

We refer to
[
23
]
for the definition and properties of Banach-valued
L^{p}(I;B)
spaces. We recall the hierarchy of intermediate homogeneous solution and source spaces, for
\alpha\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1}):D_{t}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})\right\},

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\left\{D_{t}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}g\ :\ g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})\right\},

with

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\dot{V}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{2}+\|D_{t}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{W}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle:=\|D_{t}^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}.

We have set
D_{t}^{\gamma}g:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\left|\tau\right|^{\gamma}\mathcal{F}g\right)
where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.9

(Various homogeneous embeddings)
.

We have the following embeddings.

(1)

(Hierarchy and anti-duals)
For all
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
with
\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
, we have a continuous inclusion
\dot{V}_{\alpha}\subset\dot{V}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
\dot{V}_{\alpha}^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+\dot{W}_{-\alpha}
.

(2)

(Extended Lions’ embedding)
For
\alpha\in[-1,0)
, we have
\dot{V}_{\alpha}\hookrightarrow C_{0}(\mathbb{R};H)
.

(3)

(Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev embedding)
Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and let
r=\frac{2}{\alpha}\in[2,\infty)
. Then, we have
\dot{V}_{\alpha}\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})
and there is a constant
C=C(r)
such that for all
u\in\dot{V}_{\alpha}
,

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}\leq C\|D_{t}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}.

Consequently, we have
L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\alpha})\hookrightarrow\dot{W}_{-\alpha}
.

(4)

(Mixed norm embeddings)
For
r\in(2,\infty)
and
\alpha=2/r\in(0,1)
, we have the embeddings

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};H)\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \dot{V}_{0}\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha}),

with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{\alpha}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};H)}^{1-\alpha},

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq C(r)\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{\alpha}\|D_{t}^{{1}/{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};H)}^{1-\alpha}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\alpha})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{1}(\mathbb{R};H)\ \ \text{and}\ \ L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\alpha})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+\dot{W}_{0}=\dot{V}^{\star}_{0}.

Proposition 2.10

(Integral identities)
.

Let
I
be an open interval of
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
u\in L^{2}(I;D_{S,1})
if
I
is unbounded, and that
u\in L^{2}(I;D_{S,1})\cap L^{1}(I;H)
if
I
is bounded. Let
\rho\in(2,\infty]
. Assume that

\partial_{t}u=f+g\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I;E_{\infty}),

with
f\in L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})
and
g\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(I;D_{S,-{\beta}})
, where
{\beta}={2}/{\rho}\in[0,1)
. Then
u\in C(\bar{I},H)
(
C_{0}(\bar{I},H)
if
I
is unbounded),
t\mapsto\left\|u(t)\right\|^{2}_{H}
is absolutely continuous on
\bar{I}
and for all
\sigma,\tau\in\bar{I}
such that
\sigma<\tau
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\left\|u(\tau)\right\|^{2}_{H}-\left\|u(\sigma)\right\|^{2}_{H}=2\mathrm{Re}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\langle f(t),u(t)\rangle_{H,-1}+\langle g(t),u(t)\rangle_{H,-\beta}\ \mathrm{d}t.

3.
Lower-order coefficients in mixed Lebesgue spaces

In this section, we develop our variational framework for solving second-order degenerate parabolic equations with unbounded lower-order terms in mixed Lebesgue spaces, and obtain existence, uniqueness, regularity, and representation for the equations and their associated Cauchy problems.

3.1.
The variational space and an identification

On
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define the following positive, self-adjoint, and injective operator:

S:=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2},\quad\text{with}\quad D(S)=D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2})=H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We recall that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
D(S)=H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with respect to the graph norm.

We adopt the definitions from Section
2.4
, with
S=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2}
. The space
\dot{V}_{0}
will play a central role as the variational space throughout this work.

We record the following useful identification result.

Lemma 3.1

(Linking
L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})
and
L^{2}(I;\dot{H}^{-1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. For any
F\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
, there exists a unique

h\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\|h\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

and

\langle\!\langle-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{I}\langle h(t),S\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\supset\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Conversely, for any
h\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, there exists
F\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\|F\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq\|h\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

and the same identity holds:

\langle\!\langle-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{I}\langle h(t),S\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\supset\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

In both cases, we have
-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F)=Sh=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2}h
in the weak sense against functions
\varphi\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\supset\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

The first result is straightforward by using the duality between
L^{2}(I;D_{S,1})
and
L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})
. The converse also follows by an application of the Hahn-Banach theorem. We omit the details.
∎

3.2.
Embeddings

We adopt the following convention, since
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
will serve as our main ambient space. For a given normed space
E
of Banach-valued functions defined on
I
, we define

E^{\mathrm{loc}}:=E\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),

equipped with the same norm as
E
. This condition is to be understood in a qualitative sense. For example, for a measurable set
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,
r\in[1,\infty]
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, noting that
D_{S,\alpha}\cap L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=D(S^{\alpha})
, we have

L^{r}(I;D_{S,\alpha})^{\mathrm{loc}}=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})):\ u(t)\in D(S^{\alpha})\ \text{for a.e. }t\in I,\ \text{and}\ \|S^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty\right\}.

In the case of
\dot{V}_{\alpha}
for
-1\leq\alpha\leq 1
,
I=\mathbb{R}
automatically when considering
\dot{V}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{loc}}
.

We now recall the definition of mixed spaces. For pairs of exponents
(r,q)\in[1,\infty]^{2}
, intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a weight
\tilde{\omega}
on
\Omega
, we write
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\tilde{\omega}}(\Omega))
for the mixed norm space of measurable functions
u:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with

\|u\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\tilde{\omega}}(\Omega))}:=\left(\int_{I}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,x)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\omega}(x)\right)^{r/q}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/r}<\infty,

with the usual modifications if either
r=\infty
or
q=\infty
, or if the functions take values in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

For any open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we recall that we have set

\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})):u\in L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\right\},

where the gradient is taken in the sense of distributions, with norm

\|u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)}:=\|u\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Note that when
\omega^{q/2}
is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. In particular,
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\mathrm{loc}}
is a dense subspace of
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Moreover, by using the map
u\mapsto(u,\nabla_{x}u)
together with Lemma
3.1
, the duality theory is easily understood by identifying isometrically
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
to a subspace of
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\times L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
. As a consequence, when
(r,q)\in[1,\infty)^{2}
, we have

\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}=L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(I,L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).

More precisely, for
\varphi\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}
, there exists
(F,g)\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\times L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\displaystyle\varphi=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\,F)+g,

that is

\displaystyle\ \varphi(u)=\int_{I}\left(\langle F(t),\nabla_{x}u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle g(t),u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\right)\,\mathrm{dt},\quad\forall u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I),

with

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}}\simeq\inf_{\varphi=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F)+g}\|F\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|g\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(I,L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Remark 3.2
.

Here, the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2,\omega}
serves as the (natural) anti-duality pairing between the spaces
L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Definition 3.3
.

A pair
(r,q)
is admissible if
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n}{2q}=\frac{n}{4}
, with
2\leq r,q<\infty
.

Remarks 3.4
.

(1)

If
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\omega^{q/2}
is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(2)

If
(r,q)
is admissible and
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
is another pair with
r\leq\tilde{r}<\infty
and
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}=\frac{n}{4}
, then it is automatically admissible. Indeed, we have
\tilde{q}\in(2,q]
and
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset RH_{\frac{\tilde{q}}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by Point (1) of Proposition
2.2
.

This definition of admissible pairs is motivated by the forthcoming results.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
(r,q)
is an admissible pair and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval, then we have a continuous inclusion

L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\mathrm{loc}}.

By duality, this yields the continuous embedding

L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime}}(I;D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}}).

Proof.

If
u\in L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
, then
u(t)\in D(S^{\frac{2}{r}})
for almost every
t\in I
. Moreover, the pair
(r,q)
is admissible if and only if
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\frac{2}{r}=n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)
. Therefore, using Theorem
2.4
, there is a constant
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that for almost every
t\in I
,

\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|(S^{\frac{2}{r}}u)(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

The first continuous inclusion then follows by raising both sides of this inequality to the power
r
and integrating with respect to
t\in I
.

To prove the dual embedding result, fix
g\in L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then for all
\varphi\in L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
, we have by Hölder inequality,

|\langle\!\langle g,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle|\leq\|g\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|\varphi\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|\varphi\|_{L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})}.

Moreover, we have
\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
, and it is a dense subspace of both
L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})
. Thus, there exists a unique
h\in L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\|h\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

and

\langle\!\langle g,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle h,S^{\frac{2}{r}}\varphi\rangle\!\rangle,\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{r}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}\supset\mathcal{D}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

∎

Corollary 3.6
.

If
(r,q)
is an admissible pair, then

\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R}).

By duality, this yields the continuous inclusions

\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}})\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}^{\star}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}.

Proof.

For the first embedding, holds because the inclusion
\dot{V}_{0}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}
is continuous. The second embedding is the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev embedding in the time variable, as stated in Point (3) of Proposition
2.9
. The third embedding is precisely the result of Lemma
3.5
. The embeddings of the dual spaces follow straightforwardly, and we skip details.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Roughly speaking, what happens here is that we first apply the time Hardy–Littlewood –Sobolev embedding to obtain the inclusion

\dot{H}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})\hookrightarrow L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})
,
with
\alpha=\frac{2}{r}
. Then, we apply the spatial Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev embedding

D_{S,\alpha}\left(=\dot{H}_{\omega}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)\hookrightarrow L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
\alpha=n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)
. Combining these yields the mixed-norm space
L^{r}L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}
as an endpoint embedding space. Solving for
\alpha
in terms of
r
and
q
leads to the definition of
(r,q)
as an admissible pair.

3.3.
The degenerate parabolic operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}

The parabolic operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a non-autonomous elliptic part
\mathcal{B}
with unbounded lower-order terms, as defined in (
1.3
). Its coefficients
A
,
a
,
b
, and
c
depend on
(t,x)
. The first coefficient is a matrix-valued function
A:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with complex measurable coefficients such that
\omega^{-1}A
is bounded, so for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
u,v\in H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(3.8)

|\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u,\nabla_{x}v\rangle_{2,\omega}|\leq M\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{2,\omega}\|\nabla_{x}v\|_{2,\omega},

with
M:=\|\omega^{-1}A\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}
. In particular, we have

(3.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}|\,\mathrm{d}t\leq M\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|\nabla_{x}v\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

for all distributions
u,v\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
with
\nabla_{x}u,\nabla_{x}v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
. For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
u,v\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\nabla_{x}u,\nabla_{x}v\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
, we set

\mathcal{A}(t)(u,v):=\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}.

For all distributions
u,v\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
with
\nabla_{x}u,\nabla_{x}v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
, we set the form

\mathcal{A}(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{A}(t)(u(t),v(t))\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The lower-order coefficients
a
,
b
are
n
-vectors of complex-valued, measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, and
c
is a complex-valued, measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. Next, we introduce the sesquilinear pairings corresponding to the lower-order terms. For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

\beta(t)(u,v):=\langle a(t)u,\nabla_{x}v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle b(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}u,v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle c(t)u,v\rangle_{2,\omega}.

We also introduce the notation

\beta(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta(t)(u(t),v(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

The formal complex adjoint of
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\mathcal{B}^{\star}u=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(A^{\star}\nabla_{x}u)-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\,\overline{b}u)+\overline{a}\cdot\nabla_{x}u+\overline{c}u.

The quantity
P_{r,q}
defined in (
1.4
), linked to the lower-order terms, arises in the following two results.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(r,q)
be an admissible pair and assume that
P_{r,q}<\infty
. For all
u,v\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}
and almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle|\beta(u,v)(t)|\leq

\displaystyle\|a(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\nabla_{x}v(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle+\|b(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|v(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle+\|c(t)\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|v(t)\|_{L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

In particular,
t\mapsto\beta(u,v)(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and we have

|\beta(u,v)|\leq P_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}\|v\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

For the first inequality, we proceed as follows. Regarding the term involving
a
, we write:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|a(t,x)||u(t,x)||\nabla_{x}v(t,x)|\,\mathrm{d}\omega(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|a(t,x)|\times|u(t,x)|\omega^{1/2}(x)\times|\nabla_{x}v(t,x)|\omega^{1/2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

and the result follows from Hölder inequality, since

\frac{1}{\frac{2q}{q-2}}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{2}=1.

The term involving
b
is treated in the same way. The same applies to the term involving
c
, since

\frac{1}{\frac{q}{q-2}}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

The second inequality is treated in exactly the same way, using Hölder’s inequality, since

\frac{1}{\frac{2r}{r-2}}+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{2}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\frac{r}{r-2}}+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{r}=1.

∎

Remark that at this stage, we have not used the
RH_{q/2}
condition on
\omega
.
By combining Lemma
3.10
and Corollary
3.6
, we arrive at the following result.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
(r,q)
be an admissible pair and assume that
P_{r,q}<\infty
. Then there exists a constant
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

|\beta(u,v)|\leq CP_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}\|v\|_{\dot{V}_{0}},

for all
u,v\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
. In other words,
\beta:\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\times\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a bounded sesquilinear form.

Definition 3.12
.

A pair
(r,q)
is said to be
compatible for lower-order terms
if it is admissible and satisfies
P_{r,q}<\infty
.

Remark 3.13
.

We could have chosen admissible pairs
(r_{i},q_{i})
possibly different for each of the entries

,
b
, and

in Lemma

. In this case, we must assume that

Definition 3.14

(Forward parabolic operator

applied to

.

be a compatible pair for lower-order terms, and let

and all test functions

, we define

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B})u,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle:=-\langle\!\langle u,\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\mathcal{B}u,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int_{I}-\langle u(t),\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{I}\langle A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\beta(t)(u(t),\varphi(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Remark 3.15
.

From (

), we can define the bounded operator, with bound

\mathcal{A}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1}),

Similarly, if

,

Note that we have
\mathcal{B}u=\mathcal{A}u+\beta u
, in

, for all

and
\mathcal{B}u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}

.

We conclude this section with the following result, which states that in suitable settings, the abstract test functions valued in

(adapted to

) can be replaced by standard test functions. This is a key ingredient, as the existence results will be established in abstract spaces.

Proposition 3.16

(From the concrete to the abstract and vice-versa)
.

Let
(r,q)
be a compatible pair for lower-order terms, and let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. Suppose that
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
. For

in a suitable class of source terms, the following holds:
u
is a solution to the equation

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),

if and only if
u
is a solution to the equation

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I;E_{\infty}),

where the latter distributional formulation is interpreted via the anti-duality bracket defined previously,
against test functions
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(I;E_{-\infty})
.

The same equivalence holds for Cauchy problems with initial data in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Such source terms include

f=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F_{1})+F_{2}+(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3},

where
F_{1}\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})
,
F_{2}\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
,
h_{1},h_{2}\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
where
\rho\in(2,\infty)
and
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}\in(0,1)
and
h_{3}\in L^{1}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

Proof.

This follows directly from
[
4
, Lemma 8.2]
, since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
D(S)=H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with respect to the graph norm. Note that
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)\subset L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
by assumption, and we also use Lemma
3.1
, and Lemma
3.5
for the norms involving
\|\cdot\|_{L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
. The details are omitted.
∎

3.4.
The variational approach

We seek weak solutions
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
to the equation
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f
for suitable source terms. We begin with the case
I=\mathbb{R}
by introducing a variational parabolic operator, allowing the use of the Fourier transform in the time variable. We will later restrict to unbounded or bounded time intervals in the context of Cauchy problems.

We write
H_{t}
for the Hilbert transform in the time variable, defined via its symbol
i\tau/|\tau|
. For details, see
[
4
, Section 6.1]
. We define a bounded sesquilinear form

B_{\dot{V}_{0}}:\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\times\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

by

\displaystyle B_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,v):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\beta(u,v)(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{A}(u,v)+\beta(u,v),

for all
u,v\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
. We define
\mathcal{H}\colon\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow(\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}})^{\star}
by

\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}u,v\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}:=B_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,v),\ u,v\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}.

We have

\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}}}=\mathcal{H}\ ,\ \ \left(-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}^{\star}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}}}=\mathcal{H}^{\star},

where
\mathcal{H}^{\star}:\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow({\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}})^{\star}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{H}
. Indeed, we have the equality

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle u(t),\partial_{t}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t

when
u\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
and
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
. Thus, we may refer to
\mathcal{H}
(more precisely, to its unique extension from
\dot{V}_{0}
to
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
) as the variational parabolic operator associated with
\mathcal{B}
, since it arises from the sesquilinear form
B_{\dot{V}_{0}}
(more precisely, from its unique extension to
\dot{V}_{0}\times\dot{V}_{0}
), and the space
\dot{V}_{0}
serves as the underlying variational space.

3.5.
Invertibility and causality

In this section, we give sufficient conditions for invertibility and causality, both of which will be defined rigorously. The first two results are of perturbative nature with respect to the pure second-order case,
i.e.
, they hold when
P_{r,q}
is small enough, while the last one only requires a lower bound.

We start with results of a perturbative nature. We recall that
A
is said to be elliptic in the sense of Gårding if there exists
\nu>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and all
u\in H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(3.17)

\nu\|\nabla_{x}u\|^{2}_{2,\omega}\leq\mathrm{Re}\left(\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u,\nabla_{x}u\rangle_{2,\omega}\right).

A sufficient condition to obtain this ellipticity is to assume that
A
is uniformly elliptic, that is

\nu\left|\xi\right|^{2}\omega(x)\leq\mathrm{Re}(A(t,x)\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}),

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 3.18

(Invertibility)
.

Let
(r,q)
be compatible pair for lower-order terms. Then the operator
\mathcal{H}
extends uniquely to an operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\colon\dot{V}_{0}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
. Moreover, if (
3.17
) holds, there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(M,\nu,[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that if
P_{r,q}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is invertible, with a bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}
depending only on
M
and
\nu
.

Proof.

We start with the extension result. We first recall that
\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
is dense in
\dot{V}_{0}
for this latter space topology. In particular, up to unique abstract extensions, we have
(\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}})^{\star}=\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
and the uniqueness of the extension of
\mathcal{H}
is obvious. Hence, we only need to prove that
B_{\dot{V}_{0}}
extends to a sesquilinear form on
\dot{V}_{0}\times\dot{V}_{0}
. To do so, we notice that the sesquilinear pairing in
B_{\dot{V}_{0}}
involving the half-order time derivative
D_{t}^{1/2}
and the Hilbert transform
H_{t}
is well-defined on
\dot{V}_{0}\times\dot{V}_{0}
, so no need to extend it. Using the bound (
3.9
) and Corollary
3.11
, we see that
\mathcal{A}
and
\beta
extend respectively to sesquilinear forms
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
and
\tilde{\beta}
on
\dot{V}_{0}\times\dot{V}_{0}
with bounds
M
and
CP_{r,q}
, respectively, where
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
is a constant. Thus,
\mathcal{H}
extends to
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\colon\dot{V}_{0}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
, defined by

\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u,v\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}:=\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(u,v)+\tilde{\beta}(u,v),\ u,v\in\dot{V}_{0}.

Now, the invertibility result under a smallness assumption on the lower-order terms follows by a perturbation of the case of pure second-order case. The invertibility in this latter case is essentially due to Kaplan
[
25
]
. For completeness, we provide the full details of this perturbative result.

By the Plancherel theorem and the fact that the Hilbert transform
H_{t}
commutes with
D_{t}^{1/2}
and
S
, it is a bijective isometry on
\dot{V}_{0}
. As it is skew-adjoint, for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
1+\delta H_{t}
is an isomorphism on
\dot{V}_{0}
and
\|(1+\delta H_{t})u\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}^{2}=(1+\delta^{2})\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{V}_{0}}
. The same equality holds on
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
.

Let
\delta>0
to be chosen later. The modified sesquilinear form
\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(\cdot,(1+\delta H_{t})\cdot)
is bounded on
\dot{V}_{0}\times\dot{V}_{0}
and, for all
u\in\dot{V}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u,D_{t}^{1/2}(1+\delta H_{t})u\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)+\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{\beta}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u)

\displaystyle=\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(u,u)+\delta\,\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(u,H_{t}u)+\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{\beta}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u),

where we have used that
H_{t}
is skew-adjoint, hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\ \mathrm{d}t=0.

Using (
3.17
), (
3.8
) (more precisely, their extensions to
D_{S,1}
) together with Corollary
3.11
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u)\geq\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+(\nu-\delta M)\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{2}-CP_{r,q}\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}\|u\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}^{2}.

Choosing first
\delta=\frac{\nu}{1+M}
and then
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
C\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}=\delta/2
, then
P_{r,q}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
implies that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u,(1+\delta H_{t})u)\rangle\!\rangle_{V_{0}^{\star},V_{0}}=\mathrm{Re}(B_{V_{0}}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})u))\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\|u\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}^{2},\ \forall u\in\dot{V}_{0}.

Fix
f\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
. The Lax-Milgram lemma implies that there exists a unique
u\in\dot{V}_{0}
such that

\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,(1+\delta H_{t})\cdot)=(1+\delta H_{t})^{\star}\circ f.

Furthermore, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\left\|(1+\delta H_{t})^{\star}\circ f\right\|_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}}.

Using the fact that
(1+\delta H_{t})^{\star}
is an isomorphism on
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
with operator norm equal to
\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}
, we have that for each
f\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
there exists a unique
u\in\dot{V}_{0}
such that
\tilde{B}_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,\cdot)=f
,
i.e.

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u=f
with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\times\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}}.

∎

Remark 3.20
.

Note that the elliptic part
\mathcal{B}
, initially defined as a bounded operator
\mathcal{B}:\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, when restricted to
L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})^{\mathrm{loc}}
by Corollary
3.6
, extends uniquely to a bounded operator

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})\rightarrow\left(L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})\right)^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}}).

In particular, we have

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\partial_{t}+\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|\dot{V}_{0}}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}.

We now state the following causality result of perturbative nature.

Theorem 3.21

(Causality)
.

Let
(r,q)
be a compatible pair for lower-order terms. Assume that (
3.17
) holds. There exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(M,\nu,[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that
P_{r,q}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
implies that
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
is causal in the following sense: if
I
is an open interval that contains a neighborhood of
-\infty
and
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
satisfies
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
u=0
.

Proof.

If
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
satisfies
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\partial_{t}u=-\mathcal{B}u=-\mathcal{A}u-\beta u
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We have
u\in L^{2}(I;D_{S,1})^{\mathrm{loc}}
and
\mathcal{A}u+\beta u\in L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(I;L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\subset L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(I;D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}})
. By combining Propositions
3.16
and
2.10
, we have
u\in C_{0}(\overline{I};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and, for all
\sigma,\tau\in\overline{I}
such that
\sigma<\tau
,

\displaystyle\left\|u(\tau)\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}-\left\|u(\sigma)\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}=2\mathrm{Re}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\langle\omega^{-1}A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\beta(u,u)(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We choose
\tau\in\overline{I}
such that
\left\|u(\tau)\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}=\sup_{t\in\overline{I}}\left\|u(t)\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}=:\underline{S}
and set
\underline{I}=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\left\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t
. Letting
\sigma\to-\infty
in the above equality and using (
3.17
), we deduce that

(3.22)

\underline{S}\leq-2\nu\,\underline{I}+2\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}|\beta(u,u)(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Now, by combining Lemma
3.5
with the first embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
, we obtain the existence of a constant
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{r}((-\infty,\tau);L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{r}((-\infty,\tau);D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})}\leq C\,\underline{S}^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}\underline{I}^{\frac{1}{r}}.

Combining this inequality together with an estimate as in the proof of Lemma
3.10
, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}|\beta(u,u)(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\leq CP_{r,q}\left(\underline{S}^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}\underline{I}^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{r}}+\underline{S}^{1-\frac{2}{r}}\underline{I}^{\frac{2}{r}}\right).

Going back to (
3.22
), we have

\underline{S}\leq-2\nu\,\underline{I}+2CP_{r,q}\left(\underline{S}^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}\underline{I}^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{r}}+\underline{S}^{1-\frac{2}{r}}\underline{I}^{\frac{2}{r}}\right).

Since
r\in[2,\infty)
, we make use of Young’s convexity inequality to deduce the inequality

\underline{S}\leq-2\nu\,\underline{I}+2CP_{r,q}\left((\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{r})\underline{S}+(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{r})\underline{I}\right).

Now, if
P_{r,q}<\min\left(\frac{\nu}{C(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{r})},\frac{1}{2C(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{r})}\right)=:2\varepsilon_{0}
, we conclude that
\underline{S}=0
, and hence
u=0
.
∎

Finally, we state the following invertibility and causality result based on lower bounds.

Theorem 3.23

(Invertibility and causality through lower bounds)
.

Let
(r,q)
be a compatible pair for lower-order terms.

(1)

Assume that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\!\langle\mathcal{B}u,u\rangle\!\rangle\geq c\|\nabla_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\quad\text{for all}\ u\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}.

Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is invertible.

(2)

Assume that

\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathcal{A}(t)(u,v)+\beta(t)(u,u))\geq 0\quad\text{for all}\ t\in\mathbb{R}\ \ \text{and}\ \ u\in H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Then
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
is causal in the sense of Theorem
3.21
.

Proof.

We follow
[
6
, Theorem 2.42]
. We start with the proof of (1). Using the assumption (more precisely, its extension to
\dot{V}_{0}
) and proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.18
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u,(1+\delta H_{t})u\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+c\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}-\delta M\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}\|H_{t}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-\delta P_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}}\|H_{t}u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}}

\displaystyle\geq\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+(c-\delta\tilde{M})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\delta P_{r,q}\left(\|u\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|H_{t}u\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\right)^{2},

with
\tilde{M}:=M+2P_{r,q}
, where in the second inequality we used that
H_{t}
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})
, and trivial lower bounds together with elementary estimates such as
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
for
a,b\geq 0
. By combining Lemma
3.5
with the second embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
, there are constants
C_{1}=C_{1}([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(r)
such that

(3.24)

\|v\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C_{1}\|v\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})}\leq C_{1}C_{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{\alpha}\|D_{t}^{{1}/{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{1-\alpha},

for all
v\in\dot{V}_{0}
, with
\alpha=\frac{2}{r}
. We set
C:=C_{1}C_{2}
.

If
r=2
, then there is no contribution of
D_{t}^{{1}/{2}}
in (
3.24
) and by combining this inequality with the fact that
H_{t}
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u,(1+\delta H_{t})u\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}\geq\delta\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+(c-\delta(\tilde{M}+4C^{2}P_{r,q}))\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})},

and we choose
\delta>0
such that
c-\delta(\tilde{M}+4C^{2}P_{r,q})>0
and we obtain the invertibility of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.

If
r>2
,
i.e.

\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for
\varepsilon>0
to be fixed later, by convexity, from (
3.24
) we obtain

\|v\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\left(\alpha\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}+(1-\alpha)\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\|D_{t}^{{1}/{2}}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\right),\quad\text{for all}\ v\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}.

Using this inequality with the fact that
H_{t}
is an isometry on
\dot{V}_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}u,(1+\delta H_{t})u\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}\geq

\displaystyle\delta\left(1-8C^{2}P_{r,q}(1-\alpha)^{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right)\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}

\displaystyle+\left(c-\delta\tilde{M}-8\delta C^{2}P_{r,q}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}\right)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}.

We begin by choosing
\varepsilon>0
such that
1-8C^{2}P_{r,q}(1-\alpha)^{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}>0
, and then choose
\delta>0
such that
c-\delta\left(\tilde{M}+8C^{2}P_{r,q}\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}\right)>0
. Under these conditions, we conclude the invertibility of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.

For the proof of (2), we argue as in the proof of Theorem
3.21
. By the energy equality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|u(\tau)\right\|_{2,\omega}^{2}=-2\,\mathrm{Re}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\langle\mathcal{B}u(t),u(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

We then conclude directly from the assumption of (2) that
u=0
.
∎

3.6.
Existence and uniqueness results

In this section, we establish the well-posedness of parabolic equations of the form
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f
on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the source term
f
belongs to various function spaces. We begin by stating the following assumptions, for which we have already provided sufficient conditions in the previous section.

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\ \text{is invertible;}

\displaystyle\textbf{(C)}\quad\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}\ \text{is causal in the sense of Theorem \ref{thm: Causality}.}

Until the end of this Section
3
, we assume that (I) holds and will not repeat this assumption in the statements.
We set
\mathfrak{I}:=\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\|_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}}
. The assumption
(C)
will be invoked only when it is used.
We also fix a pair
(r,q)
that is compatible for lower-order terms.
Note that this includes the assumption
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remarks 3.25
.

(1)

(I)
is equivalent to the statement that
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}^{\star}}
, the unique extension of
\mathcal{H}^{\star}
to
\dot{V}_{0}
, is invertible, and we have
\mathfrak{I}=\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}^{\star}})^{-1}\|_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}}
.

(2)

(C)
is equivalent to the statement that
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}^{\star}
is anti-causal (when
I
is assumed to be a neighborhood of
+\infty
).

Thus, all the results presented here also apply to backward equations.

3.6.1.
Uniqueness

We establish the following uniqueness result, which is the only instance in which we move from the concrete setting to the abstract one.

Proposition 3.26

(Uniqueness)
.

If
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
is a weak solution of
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=0
, then
u=0
.

Proof.

Using the equation, we have
\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{\omega}u=-\Delta_{\omega}u-\mathcal{B}u
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
, with
-\Delta_{\omega}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})
and
\mathcal{B}u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R},L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}})
by Lemma
3.5
. Using Proposition
3.16
, we also have

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{\omega}u=-\Delta_{\omega}u-\mathcal{B}u\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};E_{\infty}).

As
-\Delta_{\omega}u-\mathcal{B}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}})\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}^{\star}=\dot{W}_{-1}+\dot{W}_{-\frac{2}{r}}
by Corollary
3.6
, we use
[
4
, Theorem 4.16]
to conclude that
u\in\dot{V}_{-1}+\dot{V}_{-\frac{2}{r}}=\dot{V}_{-\frac{2}{r}}\subset\dot{V}_{0}
by Proposition
2.9
, Point (1). Thus, we have
u\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
and
\mathcal{H}u=0
, hence
u=0
by the invertibility assumption
(I)
.
∎

3.6.2.
Source term in suitable subspaces of
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}

We recall that we have

\left(\partial_{t}+\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|\dot{V}_{0}}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}},

and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
defined in Remark
3.20
. We also recall that, for any open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,

L^{\rho^{\prime}}(I;D_{S,-\beta})=\left\{(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h,\ h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\}.

We now state the following result.

Proposition 3.27

(Source term in
L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})
,
2\leq\rho<\infty
)
.

Let
\rho\in[2,\infty)
and set
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}
. Let
h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then, there exists a unique
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
solution of

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}).

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{V}_{0}
, and there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q,\rho)
such that

(3.28)

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|h\|_{L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Proof.

Uniqueness follows from Proposition
3.26
. For existence, we observe that the source term
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h
lies in
\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
, so defining
u:=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h)
provides a function
u\in\dot{V}_{0}
that solves
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R},E_{\infty})
.

Moreover, using the equation, we have
\partial_{t}u=-\tilde{\mathcal{B}}u+(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R},E_{\infty})
, which implies that
u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
by Proposition
2.10
. Even better, applying
[
4
, Theorem 4.16]
, we obtain
u\in\dot{V}_{-\min(\frac{2}{r},\beta)}
, and by (2) of Proposition
2.9
, we have the embedding
\dot{V}_{-\min(\frac{2}{r},\beta)}\subset C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{V}_{0}
.

Now, using the first embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
, we have
u\in L^{\tilde{r}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\tilde{\alpha}})^{{\mathrm{loc}}}
for all
\tilde{r}\in[2,\infty)
with
\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{2}{\tilde{r}}
. By taking
\tilde{r}=r
and applying Lemma
3.5
, we conclude that
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
. Furthermore, since
u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\subset L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, it then follows from Proposition
3.16
that
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
.

Finally, to prove (
3.28
), since by definition
u=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h)
, we obtain

(3.29)

\|u\|_{\dot{V_{0}}}=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h)\|_{\dot{V_{0}}}\leq\mathfrak{I}\|(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h\|_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}}\leq\mathfrak{I}\times C(\rho)\,\|h\|_{L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

In this last inequality, we used that
(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})\hookrightarrow\dot{W}_{-\beta}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{-\beta}^{\star}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
, which follows from Points (3) and (1) of Proposition
2.9
. This yields the desired estimate on
\nabla_{x}u
as

\max(\|D_{t}^{1/2}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))})\leq\|u\|_{\dot{V_{0}}}.

To prove the
L^{\infty}
-norm estimate, we use the energy identity (
2.11
) in Proposition
2.10
to deduce

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq

\displaystyle 2M\|\nabla_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+2CP_{r,q}\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{V_{0}}}

\displaystyle+2\|(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}u\|_{L^{\rho}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|h\|_{L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

where
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
is the constant from Corollary
3.11
. Combining (
3.29
) with the second embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
for the norm
\|(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}u\|_{L^{\rho}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
, one then obtains the desired estimate.
∎

By the assumption
(I)
,
\mathcal{H}:\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
is injective. Moreover, by the above result, we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}:\bigcup_{2\leq\rho<\infty}L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})\rightarrow C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{V}_{0}\subset\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}.

As
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is the extension of
\mathcal{H}
to
\dot{V}_{0}
, then
\mathcal{H}^{-1}:\bigcup_{2\leq\rho<\infty}L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})\rightarrow C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{V}_{0}\subset\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
. We stress that whenever we write
u=\mathcal{H}^{-1}f
, for
f\in\bigcup_{2\leq\rho<\infty}L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})
, this means that
u
is the unique element of
\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
satisfying
\mathcal{H}u=f
. We then know
a posteriori
that

u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, etc. Providing such a candidate
u
was the sole purpose of introducing
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
. From this point onward, we work directly with
\mathcal{H}
. We could do the same with sources in
\dot{W}_{\alpha}
for
-1\leq\alpha<0
, but we refrain from doing so: these spaces are defined on the whole real line via the Fourier transform, and we will later need to restrict to intervals.

Corollary 3.30

(Boundedness properties of
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
)
.

Fix
\rho\in[2,\infty)
and set
\beta={2}/{\rho}\in(0,1]
. Then,

\mathcal{H}^{-1}:L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})\rightarrow\dot{V}_{-\min(\frac{2}{r},\beta)}\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\ (\subset\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}})\quad\text{is bounded.}

The same holds for
(\mathcal{H}^{\star})^{-1}
.

Proof.

Refer to Proposition
3.27
above and its proof. We stress that this does not imply that
\mathcal{H}
maps
\dot{V}_{-\min(\frac{2}{r},\beta)}
into
L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\beta})
, since
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
, as mapped above, is not necessarily surjective.
∎

Corollary 3.31
.

Let
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
be a pair with
r\leq\tilde{r}<\infty
and
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}=\frac{n}{4}
. Let
g\in L^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{\omega^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then, there exists a unique
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
solution of

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=g\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}).

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap\dot{V}_{0}
, and there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q,\tilde{q})
such that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{\omega^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Proof.

The pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
is admissible; see Point (2) of Remarks
3.4
. The result then follows directly by combining Lemma
3.5
(the duality result case
I=\mathbb{R}
) with Proposition
3.27
applied with
\rho=\tilde{r}
.
∎

Remark 3.32
.

Since
\omega\in\bigcup_{q_{0}>q}RH_{\frac{q_{0}}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by Point (2) of Proposition
2.2
, when
r>2
we may take
g\in L^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{\omega^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
in the above corollary for any admissible pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
with
\tilde{r}\in[r_{\omega},r)
, for some
r_{\omega}\in[2,r)
.

3.6.3.
Source term in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

The assumption
(I)
cannot be straightforwardly applied to prove existence for a source term in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, since
L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\nsubseteq\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
. However, it is still possible to handle such source terms by employing a duality scheme.

Proposition 3.33

(Source term in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then, there exists a unique
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
solution of

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=f\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}).

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Proof.

Uniqueness is provided by Proposition
3.26
. To prove the existence, we remark that Corollary
3.30
for the backward operator
\mathcal{H}^{\star}
in the case
\rho=2
implies that
(\mathcal{H}^{\star})^{-1}
is bounded from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})
into
C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\rightarrow\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};E_{\infty}),\ \langle\!\langle\mathcal{T}f,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle:=\langle\!\langle f,(\mathcal{H}^{\star})^{-1}\varphi\rangle\!\rangle_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

We have, for
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
a constant,

(3.34)

\left\|\mathcal{T}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Next, let
f
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty})
. We write for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty})
, observing that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty})\subset\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}
,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{T}f,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}^{-1}f,(\mathcal{H}^{\star})^{-1}\varphi\rangle\!\rangle_{\dot{V}_{0}^{\star},\dot{V}_{0}}=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}^{-1}f,\varphi\rangle\!\rangle.

Hence,
\mathcal{T}f=\mathcal{H}^{-1}f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and

(3.35)

\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty}),\quad-\langle\!\langle\mathcal{T}f,\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}f),\varphi\rangle\!\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle f(t),\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\partial_{t}\mathcal{T}f=-\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}f)+f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};E_{\infty})
, the energy equality (
2.11
) in Proposition
2.10
implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq 2M\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}+2P_{r,q}\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}+2\|\mathcal{T}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Next, using the inequality
\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 2\big(\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}+\|\mathcal{T}f\|^{2}_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\big)
, and combining Lemma
3.5
with the first embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
for this second term, together with basic inequalities of the type
ab\leq\varepsilon\frac{a^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{b^{2}}{2}
, we obtain, via this absorption argument, the following estimate holds, with
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
a constant:

(3.36)

\|\mathcal{T}f\|_{C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Now, let us pick
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).
Let
(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty})^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. By (
3.34
) and (
3.36
), we have
\mathcal{T}f_{k}\to\mathcal{T}f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and consequently also in
L^{r}(\mathbb{R};L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and in
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
by combining Lemma
3.5
with the first embedding in (4) of Proposition
2.9
. Using (
3.35
) with
\mathcal{T}f_{k}
for a fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};E_{-\infty})
and letting
k\to\infty
imply that
\partial_{t}(\mathcal{T}f)+\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}f)=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};E_{\infty})
. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{T}f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\subset L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, it then follows from Proposition
3.16
that
\mathcal{T}f
is asolution of
\partial_{t}(\mathcal{T}f)+\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}f)=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
.

∎

3.6.4.

3.7.
The Fundamental solution

The
fundamental solution
is defined as the family of operators that represent the inverse of the degenerate parabolic operator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 3.40

(Fundamental solution for
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
)
.

A fundamental solution for
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
is a family
\Gamma=(\Gamma(t,s))_{t,s\in\mathbb{R}}
of bounded operators on
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that :

(1)

(Boundedness)
\sup_{t,s\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|\Gamma(t,s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<+\infty.

(2)

(Causality)
\Gamma(t,s)=0
if
t<s
.

(3)

(Weak measurability) For all
\psi,\tilde{\psi}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the function
(t,s)\mapsto\langle\Gamma(t,s)\psi,\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}
is Borel measurable on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(4)

(Representation) For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, any
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
solution of the equation
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=\varphi\otimes\psi
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
\langle u(t),\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(s)\langle\Gamma(t,s)\psi,\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}s
, for all
\tilde{\psi}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We have set
(\varphi\otimes\psi)(t,x)=\varphi(t)\psi(x)
.

In the same manner, one defines a fundamental solution
(\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t))_{s,t\in\mathbb{R}}
to the backward operator
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}^{\star}
.

Theorem 3.41

(Existence, uniqueness, and properties of the fundamental solution)
.

Assume that
(C)
holds. There exists a unique fundamental solution
\Gamma
(up to equality almost everywhere) to
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
in the sense of the above definition, and it satisfies the following properties.

(1)

(Estimates for the fundamental solution of
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}
)
For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\Gamma(\cdot,s)\psi\in C_{0}([s,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
\Gamma(s,s)\psi=\psi
, and there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\sup_{s\leq t}\left\|\Gamma(t,s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\nabla_{x}\Gamma(\cdot,s)\psi\right\|_{L^{2}((s,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\left\|\psi\right\|_{2,\omega}.

(2)

(Estimates for the fundamental solution of
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}^{\star}
)
For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{\psi}\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,t)\tilde{\psi}\in C_{0}((-\infty,t];L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
\tilde{\Gamma}(t,t)\psi=\psi
, and there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\sup_{s\leq t}\|\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,t)\tilde{\psi}\|_{L^{2}((-\infty,t);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|\tilde{\psi}\|_{2,\omega}.

(3)

(Full representation) Let
\rho\in[2,\infty]
and set
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}
. Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then the unique solution
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
of the equation

\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=\delta_{s}\otimes\psi+(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h\ \ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})

obtained by combining Proposition
3.27
, Proposition
3.33
and Proposition
3.37
can be represented pointwisely by the equation

u(t)=\Gamma(t,s)\psi+\int_{-\infty}^{t}\Gamma(t,\tau)((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}h(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where the integral is weakly defined in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and also strongly defined in the Bochner sense when
\rho=\infty
. More precisely, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{\psi}\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have the equality with absolutely converging integral

\displaystyle\langle u(t),\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}=\langle\Gamma(t,s)\psi,\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{t}\langle h(\tau),(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau,t)\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(4)

(Adjointness property) For all
s<t
,
\Gamma(t,s)
and
\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t)
are adjoint operators.

(5)

(Chapman–Kolmogorov identities) For all
s<r<t
, we have
\Gamma(t,s)=\Gamma(t,r)\Gamma(r,s)
.

Proof.

The proof of uniqueness proceeds exactly as in
[
4
, Lemma 6.24]
, with
E_{-\infty}
replaced by
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For existence, one defines the Green operator using Proposition
3.37
, as done by J.-L. Lions
[
31
]
. One then proves that it is the fundamental solution and that it satisfies all the listed properties. See
[
6
,
4
]
for details.
∎

3.8.
The Cauchy problem on
(0,\infty)

In this section, we consider the Cauchy problem on the half-line, namely
(0,\infty)
. The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.43

(The Cauchy problem on
(0,\infty)
)
.

Assume that, in addition to the invertibility assumption
(I)
, the causality assumption
(C)
also holds. Let
\rho\in[2,\infty]
, and define
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}\in[0,1]
. Let
h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then there exists a unique
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}((0,\infty))
solution to the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\\
u(t)\rightarrow\psi\ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \mathrm{as}\ t\rightarrow 0^{+}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, with
u(0)=\psi
,
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|^{2}_{2,\omega}
is absolutely continuous on
[0,\infty)
and energy equalities hold. Furthermore, there exists a constant
C=C(M,\mathfrak{I},P_{r,q},[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(t)\|_{2,\omega}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C(\left\|h\right\|_{L^{\rho^{\prime}}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\psi\|_{2,\omega}).

Lastly, for all
t\geq 0
, we have the following representation of
u
:

\displaystyle u(t)=\Gamma(t,0)\psi+\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(t,\tau)(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau,

where the integral is weakly defined in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and strongly defined in the Bochner sense when
\rho=\infty
, as described in Theorem
3.41
, Point (3).

Proof.

We start with the existence of such a solution. We first extend
h
by
0
on
(-\infty,0]
and keep the same notation for the extension. By combining Proposition
3.27
, Proposition
3.33
and Proposition
3.37
, there exists a unique
\tilde{u}\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
solution of the equation

\partial_{t}\tilde{u}+\mathcal{B}\tilde{u}=\delta_{0}\otimes\psi+(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h\ \ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}).

Moreover, by the causality assumption
(C)
,
\tilde{u}\in C_{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
,
\tilde{u}=0
on
(-\infty,0)
and
\tilde{u}(0)=\psi
. The candidate
u:=\tilde{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle{|(0,\infty)}}
satisfies all the required properties of the theorem, thereby proving existence. As for the representation, it follows from that of
\tilde{u}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, using the fundamental solution defined on
\mathbb{R}
. See Theorem
3.41
, Point (3).

Next, we check uniqueness in the space
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}((0,\infty))
. If
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}((0,\infty))
is a solution to (
3.43
) with
h=0
and
\psi=0
, then, by Proposition
2.10
, we have
u\in C_{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
u(0)=0
. We extend
u
by zero on
(-\infty,0)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
to obtain
u\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
, and use its
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-valued continuity on
[0,\infty)
to deduce easily that
u
is a solution to
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}u=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
. Therefore, by Proposition
3.26
, we have
u=0
.

4.
Lower-order coefficients in mixed Lorentz spaces

Let
\mu
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Lorentz space
L^{p,r}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as the set of all measurable functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p,r}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty
, with

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p,r}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\left(\frac{r}{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(t^{1/p}f^{\star}(t)\right)^{r}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\right)^{1/r},\quad\ 1\leq p,r<\infty,\\
\sup_{t>0}t^{1/p}f^{\star}(t),\quad\ 1\leq p\leq\infty,\ r=\infty,\\
\end{matrix}\right.

or equivalently (see
[
20
, Proposition 1.4.9]
)

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p,r}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\left(r\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{r-1}\lambda_{f}(s)^{r/p}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/r},\quad\ 1\leq p,r<\infty,\\
\sup_{s>0}s\,\lambda_{f}(s)^{1/p},\quad\ 1\leq p<\infty,\ r=\infty,\\
\end{matrix}\right.

where

\lambda_{f}(s)=\mu\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|f(x)|>s\right\}\right)\ \ \text{and}\ \ f^{\star}(t)=\inf\left\{s>0:\lambda_{f}(s)\leq t\right\}.

It is known that
L^{p,p}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p,p}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p,r}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is non-increasing as a function of
r
, so that
L^{p,r_{1}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{p,r_{2}}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
. We recall that Hölder’s inequality holds in Lorentz spaces: the product of two functions in
L_{\mu}^{p_{i},r_{i}}
belongs to
L^{1}_{\mu}
if
\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}=1
and
\frac{1}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}=1
. We refer to
[
38
, Ch. V, §3 ]
for more details.

4.1.
Embeddings

We begin by stating the following result, which refines certain abstract embeddings from Proposition
2.9
.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have the following embeddings.

(1)

(Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev embedding)
Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and let
r=\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\in[2,\infty)
. Then, we have
\dot{V}_{\alpha}\hookrightarrow L^{r,2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})
and there is a constant
C=C(r)
such that for all
u\in\dot{V}_{\alpha}
,

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r,2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}\leq C\|D_{t}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}.

Consequently, we have
L^{r^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\alpha})\hookrightarrow\dot{W}_{-\alpha}
, where
r^{\prime}
is the Hölder conjugate of
r
.

(2)

(Mixed norm embedding)
For
r\in(2,\infty)
and
\alpha=2/r\in(0,1)
, we have the embedding
\dot{V}_{0}\hookrightarrow L^{r,2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})
,
with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r,2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\alpha})}

\displaystyle\leq C(r)\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})}^{\alpha}\|D_{t}^{{1}/{2}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};H)}^{1-\alpha}.

Consequently,
L^{r^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\alpha})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+\dot{W}_{0}=\dot{V}^{\star}_{0}
.

Proof.

The first result is obtained, as in the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 5.3]
, by using the fact that the
D_{S,\alpha}
-valued Riesz potential with exponent
\frac{1-\alpha}{2}
is
L^{2}
–
L^{r,2}
bounded, a result established by O’Neil
[
35
]
(see also Tartar
[
40
]
). The second result follows directly from the first, proceeding as in the proof of
[
4
, Proposition 5.4]
. Consequences by duality are immediate, so we omit the details.
∎

We now present the following embedding in the space variable, which extends Theorem
2.4
to boundedness into Lorentz spaces. For the proof, see the case
p=2
in
[
5
, Theorem 3.17]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
q\in[2,\infty)
. Assume that
\omega\in RH_{\frac{q}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and set
\alpha=n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

\|\sqrt{\omega}\,f\|_{L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}f\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad\forall f\in D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}).

Motivated by the above results, we introduce the following space for any open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
:

\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I):=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\ :\ \sqrt{\omega}\,u\in L^{r,2}(I;L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\ \ \text{and}\ \ \nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\right\},

where the gradient is taken in the sense of distributions, with norm

\|u\|_{\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I)}:=\|\sqrt{\omega}\,u\|_{L^{r,2}(I;L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Proceeding as in the previous section for
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(I)
, we have

\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}=L^{2}(I;D_{S,-1})+\sqrt{\omega}\,L^{r^{\prime},2}(I;L^{q^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})).

More precisely, for
\varphi\in\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}
, there exists
(F,g)\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\times L^{r^{\prime},2}(I;L^{q^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\displaystyle\varphi=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\,F)+\sqrt{\omega}\,g,

that is

\displaystyle\ \varphi(u)=\int_{I}\left(\langle F(t),\nabla_{x}u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle g(t),u(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\right)\,\mathrm{dt},\quad\forall u\in\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I),

with

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(I)^{\star}}\simeq\inf_{\varphi=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega F)+g}\|F\|_{L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\sqrt{\omega}\,g\|_{L^{r^{\prime},2}(I;L^{q^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

We record the following lemma, which states the embedding results obtained by combining both time and space embeddings in this new setting.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
(r,q)
is an admissible pair and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval, then we have a continuous inclusion

L^{r,2}(I;D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow\sqrt{\omega}\,L^{r,2}(I;L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\mathrm{loc}}.

By duality, this yields the continuous embedding

\sqrt{\omega}\,L^{r^{\prime},2}(I;L^{q^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime},2}(I;D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}}).

Consequently, we have

\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow L^{r,2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,\frac{2}{r}})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})^{\mathrm{loc}}\hookrightarrow\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R}).

By duality, this yields the continuous embeddings

\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime},2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-\frac{2}{r}})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\frac{2}{r}}^{\star}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{0}^{\star}.

Proof.

This follows by proceeding exactly as in Lemma
3.5
and Corollary
3.6
, using Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
4.1
in place of Theorem
2.4
and Proposition
2.9
, respectively.
∎

4.2.
The variational approach and the resulting theory

For any
(r,q)\in[2,\infty]
, we set

L_{r,q}:=\|a\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}},\infty}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}+\|b\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}},\infty}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}+\|c\|_{L^{{\frac{r}{r-2}},\infty}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}.

Once again, this definition is motivated by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(r,q)
be an admissible pair and assume that
L_{r,q}<\infty
. Then, for all
u,v\in\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
,
t\mapsto\beta(u,v)(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and we have

|\beta(u,v)|\leq L_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}\|v\|_{\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}.

Moreover, there exists a constant
C=C([\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{q}{2}}},n,q)
such that

|\beta(u,v)|\leq CL_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{V}_{0}}\|v\|_{\dot{V}_{0}},

for all
u,v\in\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}
. In other words,
\beta:\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\times\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a bounded sesquilinear form.

Proof.

For all
u,v\in\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})
and almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle|\beta(u,v)(t)|\leq

\displaystyle\|a(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\sqrt{\omega}\,u(t)\|_{L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\nabla_{x}v(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle+\|b(t)\|_{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\sqrt{\omega}\,v(t)\|_{L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\nabla_{x}u(t)\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle+\|c(t)\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\sqrt{\omega}\,u(t)\|_{L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\sqrt{\omega}\,v(t)\|_{L^{q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

This is true as we have Hölder’s inequality in Lorentz spaces: the product of three functions in
L^{q_{i},r_{i}}
belongs to
L^{1}
if
\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}+\frac{1}{q_{3}}=1
and
\frac{1}{r_{1}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}+\frac{1}{r_{2}}=1
. The remainder of the proof follows as in the proof of Lemma
3.10
, and we make use of Lemma
4.3
when it comes to involving
\dot{V}_{0}
.
∎

Now, we can proceed as in the previous section, and all the results hold with
\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}
replacing
\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}
. The only exception concerns the causality assumption
(C)
as stated in Theorem
3.21
. It is based on the first embedding of (4) in Proposition
2.9
, which does not extend to time-boundedness in Lorentz spaces. In this case, we consider the lower-order terms in the Lorentz space only with respect to the space variable. More precisely, we assume the stronger condition

\ell_{r,q}:=\|a\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|b\|_{L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|c\|_{L^{\frac{r}{r-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{q}{q-2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty,

which is still weaker than the case of lower-order terms in mixed Lebesgue spaces.

An example

Let us consider the case
r=2
(hence
n\geq 3
and
q=2^{\star}
),
A=I_{n}
,
a=b=0
, and
c(t,x)=\dfrac{c_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|^{2}}
, where
c_{\infty}
is a bounded, measurable, complex-valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
. In this case, the degenerate parabolic operator reduces to the degenerate parabolic Schrödinger operator

\partial_{t}-\Delta_{\omega}+\frac{c_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|^{2}}.

As the norm
\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{n}{2},\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}

is controlled by
\|c_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}
, we state the following result, which is the version of Theorem
3.43
in this setting and when causality and invertibility are of perturbative nature (see Theorems
3.18
and
3.21
).

Corollary 4.5
.

There exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}\bigl(n,[\omega]_{A_{2}},[\omega]_{RH_{\frac{n}{n-2}}}\bigr)
such that if
\|c_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then the following holds: Let
\rho\in[2,\infty]
and define
\beta=\dfrac{2}{\rho}\in[0,1]
. Let
h\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}\bigl((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)

and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then there exists a unique solution
u\in\dot{\mathcal{L}}^{r,q}((0,\infty))
to the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{\omega}u+\frac{c_{\infty}(t,x)}{|x|^{2}}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\\
u(t)\rightarrow\psi\ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \mathrm{as}\ t\rightarrow 0^{+}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover,
u\in C_{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and the map
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|^{2}_{2,\omega}
is absolutely continuous. The energy equalities, estimates, and the representation (by the fundamental solution of the degenerate parabolic Schrödinger operator) as in Theorem
3.43
also hold.

Here we anticipate a bit, but note that, since
a=b=0
and in the setting of the above result, the fundamental solution of the degenerate parabolic Schrödinger operator satisfies

L^{2}
off-diagonal estimates (see Theorem
6.2
). In the unweighted setting (
\omega=1
), with
c_{\infty}=c
a negative constant, and for non-negative
L^{1}
initial data, the connected yet subtly different question of the existence of a distributional non-negative solution to the above Cauchy problem is also answered positively in
[
9
]
for
c\in[-c_{n},0]
, where
c_{n}=c(n)
is a constant. Always for
\omega=1
, the Hardy inequality provides a lower bound, allowing the recovery of the constant
c_{n}
via the (inhomogeneous) variational method (see
[
6
]
). However, the weighted Hardy inequality requires different conditions on
\omega
than those we imposed (see
[
33
, Theorem 4]
and
[
37
, Theorem 4.1]
). This is why we have restricted ourselves to the perturbative case in Corollary
4.5
, which is, of course, still a new result.

5.
The inhomogeneous version

So far, the results have a homogeneous flavor. We now develop an inhomogeneous version of the theory, covering non-autonomous elliptic parts with bounded and unbounded lower-order terms.

5.1.
Setup

The key ingredient to obtain the inhomogeneous theory from the homogeneous one is to formulate the homogeneous theory for
\tilde{S}:=(1+S^{2})^{1/2}=(1-\Delta_{\omega})^{1/2}
. This formulation naturally leads to the inhomogeneous theory for
S
. In fact, the "homogeneous" fractional domains of
\tilde{S}
are precisely the "inhomogeneous" fractional domains of
S
. More precisely, for all
\alpha\geq 0
, we have
D_{\tilde{S},\alpha}=D(S^{\alpha})=D((-\Delta_{\omega})^{\alpha/2})
and
\|\cdot\|_{\tilde{S},\alpha}\simeq\|\cdot\|_{S,\alpha}+\|\cdot\|_{2,\omega}=\|S^{\alpha}\cdot\|_{2,\omega}+\|\cdot\|_{2,\omega}
. For
\alpha<0
, we have
D_{\tilde{S},\alpha}=D_{S,\alpha}+L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with a norm equivalent to the corresponding quotient norm.

We denote by
(V_{\alpha})_{-1\leq\alpha\leq 1}
and
(W_{\alpha})_{-1\leq\alpha\leq 1}
the universal solution and source spaces associated with
\tilde{S}
. Note that for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have the continuous embedding
V_{\alpha}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{\alpha}
, since the operator
D_{t}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
is the same in both spaces. By duality, this implies the dual embedding
\dot{V}_{\alpha}^{\star}\hookrightarrow V_{\alpha}^{\star}
, where the duality is understood with respect to the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
pairing, as usual. As a consequence, the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev-type embeddings stated in Proposition
2.9
, both in the homogeneous and inhomogeneous settings, together with their duals, hold. Moreover, for the variational spaces
\dot{V}_{0}
and
V_{0}
, we have

V_{0}=\dot{V}_{0}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\ (\subset\dot{V}_{0}^{\mathrm{loc}})\quad\text{and}\quad\|\cdot\|^{2}_{V_{0}}\simeq\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\dot{V}_{0}}+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

Formulating Proposition
2.10
for
\tilde{S}
yields the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. Let
u\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Let
\rho\in[2,\infty]
and set
\beta=\frac{2}{\rho}\in[0,1]
. Assume that
\partial_{t}u=(-\Delta_{\omega})^{\beta/2}h_{1}+h_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
with
h_{1},h_{2}\in L^{\rho^{\prime}}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Then
u\in C_{0}(\overline{I};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and the map
t\mapsto\|u(t)\|^{2}_{2,\omega}
is absolutely continuous on
\overline{I}
. Moreover, the integral identities, as in (
2.11
), hold for all
\sigma,\tau\in\overline{I}
with
\sigma<\tau
.

Remarks 5.2
.

(1)

In the case where
I
is a bounded open interval, the conclusion of the above proposition remains valid by viewing
h_{2}
as an element of
L^{1}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and by applying Proposition
2.10
(
i.e.
, the homogeneous theory for
S
, rather than for
\widetilde{S}
). Moreover, in this setting, it suffices to assume that
u\in L^{1}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
\nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, rather than requiring
u\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

(2)

Note that, since the embedding
H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous and dense, the Lions embedding theorem
[
30
]
applies only in the case where
I
is a bounded interval and
\rho=2
, and one must
a priori
assume that
u\in L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Point (1) above is a better result.

5.2.
The degenerate parabolic operator and the variational approach

We consider degenerate parabolic operators of type
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
with a time-dependent degenerate elliptic part
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
in divergence form perturbed with both bounded and unbounded lower-order terms. We shall only consider the case of unbounded lower-order terms in mixed Lebesgue spaces, since the case of mixed Lorentz spaces can be treated in exactly the same way, with the modifications already mentioned in Section
4
when proving causality results. Thus, we consider

(5.3)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}u=-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(A\nabla_{x}u)-\omega^{-1}\mathrm{div}_{x}(\omega\,au)+b\cdot\nabla_{x}u+cu.

Let
(r,q)
be an admissible pair. We assume that
a
,
b
,
c
can be decomposed as
a=a_{\mathrm{h}}+a_{\infty}
,
b=b_{\mathrm{h}}+b_{\infty}
,
c=c_{\mathrm{h}}+c_{\infty}
with

(5.4)

P_{r,q}:=\|a_{\mathrm{h}}\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}+\|b_{\mathrm{h}}\|_{L^{{\frac{2r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}+\|c_{\mathrm{h}}\|_{L^{{\frac{r}{r-2}}}(\mathbb{R};{L^{\frac{q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})}<\infty,

(5.5)

P_{\infty,\infty}:=\|a_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}+\|b_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}+\|c_{\infty}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}<\infty.

The power
1/2
on
\|c_{\infty}\|^{1/2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}
is relevant for scaling and for a cleaner presentation, as we will see.

Remark 5.6

(Example: subcritical exponents)
.

Let
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
be a pair with
r\leq\tilde{r}
,
2<\tilde{q}
and
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}>\frac{n}{4}
, with the additional condition
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}-\frac{1}{2\tilde{q}}<\frac{1}{4}
when
n=1
. Any coefficients

a,b\in L^{\frac{2\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2\tilde{q}}{\tilde{q}-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad c\in L^{\frac{\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{\tilde{q}}{\tilde{q}-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),

can be decomposed as above, that is,
a=a_{\mathrm{h}}+a_{\infty}
,
b=b_{\mathrm{h}}+b_{\infty}
, and
c=c_{\mathrm{h}}+c_{\infty}
, with

a_{\mathrm{h}},b_{\mathrm{h}}\in L^{\frac{2r}{r-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{2q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}),\ c_{\mathrm{h}}\in L^{\frac{r}{r-2}}(\mathbb{R};L^{\frac{q}{q-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),\ \ a_{\infty},b_{\infty},\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})^{n}\ \ \text{and}\ c_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n}),

by visualizing exponents in a (
\frac{\tilde{r}-2}{\tilde{r}}
,
\frac{\tilde{q}-2}{\tilde{q}}
)-plane and truncating each coefficient at a fixed level
\ell>0
(namely,
y_{\mathrm{h}}=y\,\mathbb{1}_{\{|y|>\ell\}}
and
y_{\infty}=y\,\mathbb{1}_{\{|y|\leq\ell\}}
). In fact, setting
r^{\circ}=\frac{\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}-2}\in(1,\infty]
and
q^{\circ}=\frac{\tilde{q}}{\tilde{q}-2}\in(1,\infty]
, then the condition
\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{n}{2\tilde{q}}>\frac{n}{4}
is equivalent to

\frac{1}{r^{\circ}}+\frac{n}{2q^{\circ}}<1,

which are the subcritical exponents appearing in
[
2
,
29
,
7
,
6
]
. For the additional assumption when
n=1
, it is equivalent to
\frac{1}{r^{\circ}}>\frac{1}{2q^{\circ}}
.

Next, we introduce the sesquilinear forms associated with the lower-order terms. For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
u,v\in H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we set

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(t)(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\langle a_{\mathrm{h}}(t)u,\nabla_{x}v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle b_{\mathrm{h}}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}u,v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle c_{\mathrm{h}}(t)u,v\rangle_{2,\omega},

\displaystyle\beta_{\infty}(t)(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\langle a_{\infty}(t)u,\nabla_{x}v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle b_{\infty}(t)\cdot\nabla_{x}u,v\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle c_{\infty}(t)u,v\rangle_{2,\omega},

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{inh}}(t)(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(t)(u,v)+\beta_{\infty}(t)(u,v).

Now, for all
u,v\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, we set

\beta_{\mathrm{inh}}(u,v):=\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(u,v)+\beta_{\infty}(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(t)(u(t),v(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\beta_{\infty}(t)(u(t),v(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

We record the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
u,v\in\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, we have

\displaystyle\star\ |\beta_{\infty}(u,v)|\leq P_{\infty,\infty}(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|\nabla_{x}v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))})

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+P_{\infty,\infty}^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))},

\displaystyle\star\ |\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(u,v)|\leq P_{r,q}\|u\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}\|v\|_{\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})}.

In particular,
\beta_{\mathrm{inh}}:V_{0}\times V_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a bounded sesquilinear form.

Proof.

The bound on
\beta_{\infty}
follows directly from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities. For
\beta_{\mathrm{h}}
, this is exactly Lemma
3.10
. The statement on
\beta_{\mathrm{inh}}
follows directly by combining Corollary
3.6
with the embedding
V_{0}\hookrightarrow\dot{V}_{0}
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

We may define the bounded operator, with bound
P_{\infty,\infty}+P_{\infty,\infty}^{2}
,

\beta_{\infty}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R};H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};H^{1}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))+L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1}).

We also define the operators
\beta_{\mathrm{h}}:\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\dot{\Sigma}^{r,q}(\mathbb{R})^{\star}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})+L^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R},L^{q^{\prime}}_{\omega^{q^{\prime}/2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\mathcal{A}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,1})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R};D_{S,-1})
as in Remark
3.15
.

In order to employ the variational approach, we introduce the following bounded sesquilinear form
B_{V_{0}}:V_{0}\times V_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
, defined for all
u,v\in V_{0}
by

\displaystyle B_{V_{0}}(u,v):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\langle A(t)\nabla_{x}u(t),\nabla_{x}\varphi(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}+\beta_{\mathrm{inh}}(t)(u(t),v(t))\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle H_{t}D_{t}^{1/2}u(t),D_{t}^{1/2}v(t)\rangle_{2,\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{A}(u,v)+\beta_{\mathrm{h}}(u,v)+\beta_{\infty}(u,v)

\displaystyle=:B_{\dot{V}_{0}}(u,v)+\beta_{\infty}(u,v).

Next, we define
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inh}}\colon V_{0}\to V_{0}^{\star}
by
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inh}}u,v\rangle\!\rangle_{V_{0}^{\star},V_{0}}:=B_{V_{0}}(u,v)
for all
u,v\in V_{0}
. Note that, as before, we have
\left(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|V_{0}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inh}}
and
\left(-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{|V_{0}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}
. Note also that we have the decomposition

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{inh}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{h}}+\beta_{\infty},

where

6.1.

-decay for the fundamental solution

Let us first recall some relevant literature concerning

[

[

]

-weights, such estimates appear in
[

]
under the additional assumptions that

in
[

]

[
8
]
, which employs the Grönwall lemma as a key tool. While similar ideas apply when lower-order coefficients are bounded, handling unbounded lower-order terms is more subtle, even in the unweighted case. A novel approach addressing this in the unweighted setting is presented in
[
6
]
, and we will see that these ideas extend to the weighted setting, providing complete details due to the presence of the weight
\omega
, while refining and adding more precision to certain arguments.

Taking into account the invertibility and causality results from the previous section,
we consider an elliptic operator
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
as in (

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by
\mathrm{d}\mu(t,x):=\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Definition 6.9

(Local weak solutions)
.

Fix an admissible pair
(r,q)
and assume that
P_{r,q}<\infty
and
P_{\infty,\infty}<\infty
, as in the previous section on Gaffney estimates. We say that
u
is a local weak solution to the equation
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}u=0
in an open set
\Omega=I\times\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, with
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open sets, if
u\in L^{\infty}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathcal{O}))
with
\nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}(I;L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathcal{O})^{n})
and satisfies the equation
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}u=0
in
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(\Omega)
as in Definition
2.7
with
\mathcal{O}
replacing
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Local weak solutions to the equation
-\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}v=0
are defined similarly.

Remark 6.10
.

For a local weak solution
u
as above, localizing in space and applying the first embedding of (4) in Proposition
2.9
together with Lemma
3.5
yield
u\in L^{r}(I;L^{q}_{\omega^{q/2},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O}))
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{O^{\prime}}
is an open set with
\overline{\mathcal{O^{\prime}}}\subset\mathcal{O}
, then such local weak solutions are continuous in time with values in
L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}})
. This follows directly from Point (2) of Remark
5.2
.

Definition 6.11

(Moser’s
L^{2}
-
L^{\infty}
estimates)
.

We say that
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
, respectively
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}
, satisfies Moser’s
L^{2}
-
L^{\infty}
estimates if there exist constants
B>0
and
0<R_{0}\leq\infty
such that that for all
R\in(0,R_{0})
,
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, and all local weak solutions of
\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}u=0
on a neighborhood of
Q_{2R}(t_{0},x_{0})
, respectively
-\partial_{t}v+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}v=0
and
Q_{2R}^{\star}(t_{0},x_{0})
, respectively, have local bounds of the form, respectively,

(6.12)

\supess_{Q_{R}(t_{0},x_{0})}\left|u\right|=\sup_{t\in(t_{0}-R^{2},t_{0}]}\left(\supess_{B(x_{0},R)}\left|u(t,\cdot)\right|\right)\leq B\left(\frac{1}{\mu(Q_{2R}(t_{0},x_{0}))}\int_{Q_{2R}(t_{0},x_{0})}\left|u\right|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2},

(6.13)

\supess_{Q_{R}^{\star}(t_{0},x_{0})}\left|v\right|=\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+R^{2})}\left(\supess_{B(x_{0},R)}\left|v(t,\cdot)\right|\right)\leq B\left(\frac{1}{\mu(Q_{2R}^{\star}(t_{0},x_{0}))}\int_{Q_{2R}^{\star}(t_{0},x_{0})}\left|v\right|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2}.

The above equalities follow from the local
L^{2}_{\omega}
-valued continuity of local weak solutions. See
[
8
, Appendix A]
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.14

(Pointwise Gaussian upper bounds)
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
6.2
. If
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
and
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}^{\star}
satisfy Moser’s
L^{2}
-
L^{\infty}
estimates, then
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
has Gaussian upper bounds, that is, for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<t-s<4R_{0}^{2}
,
\Gamma(t,s)
is an integral operator with a kernel
\Gamma(t,x;s,y)
satisfying a pointwise Gaussian upper bound, that is, for almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,

(6.15)

\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{-\frac{16}{c}\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{t-s}+cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)},

where
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(D,B,C)
,
C
is the constant from Theorem
6.2
, and
c
is the constant from the same theorem. Moreover, in the case
R_{0}<\infty
, there exist constants
K_{0}=K_{0}([\omega]_{A_{2}},n)\geq 1
and
k_{0}=k_{0}([\omega]_{A_{2}},n)\geq 1
such that, for all
\ell\geq 1
and all
t,s
with
\ell(4R_{0}^{2})\leq t-s<(\ell+1)(4R_{0}^{2})
, the operator
\Gamma(t,s)
is an integral operator with kernel
\Gamma(t,x;s,y)
, which satisfies, for almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,

(6.16)

\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{\ell+1}K_{0}^{\ell+2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{-\tfrac{16|x-y|^{2}}{k_{0}^{2}c(t-s)}+cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}.

The function
\Gamma(t,x;s,y)
is referred to as the generalized fundamental solution of
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{inh}}
.

Remark 6.17
.

The factor
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}
appearing in (
6.15
) may be replaced by one of

\frac{1}{\omega_{t-s}(x)},\ \ \frac{1}{\omega_{t-s}(y)},\ \ \frac{1}{\max(\omega_{t-s}(x),\omega_{t-s}(y))},

and
\tilde{C}
and
c
are replaced respectively by
C^{\circ}=C^{\circ}(\tilde{C},c,D)
and
2c
. See
[
13
, Rem. 3]
.

Proof of Theorem
6.14
.

Let
\gamma\geq 0
and
\chi\in\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
a bounded Lipschitz function such that
\left\|\nabla_{x}\chi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\gamma.
We fix
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and also fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Under the hypothesis of Theorem
6.2
, we rewrite (
6.6
) and (
6.8
) (after scaling) as

(6.18)

\|e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,s)e^{-\chi}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq Ce^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{c\|\nabla_{x}\chi\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t-s)}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\quad\text{for all}\ t>s,

with
C
,
c
the constants obtained there that are independent of
\chi
. By duality, for all
t>s
, we have

\|e^{-\chi}\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t)e^{\chi}\psi\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq Ce^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{c\|\nabla_{x}\chi\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t-s)}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

For all
t>s
, we set

U(t):=\Gamma(t,s)e^{-\chi}\psi.

For all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<t-s<4R_{0}^{2}
, Moser’s
L^{2}
–
L^{\infty}
estimate (
6.12
) with
R=\frac{\sqrt{t-s}}{2}<R_{0}
implies that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for almost every
z\in B(x,R)
,

\displaystyle\left|U(t,z)\right|^{2}\leq\frac{B^{2}}{\mu(Q_{\sqrt{t-s}}(t,x))}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t-s})}\left|U(\tau,y)\right|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\mu,

hence,

\displaystyle|e^{\chi(z)}U(t,z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B^{2}}{(t-s)\omega_{t-s}(x)}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t-s})}e^{2(\chi(z)-\chi(y)}|e^{\psi(y)}U(\tau,y)|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\omega(y)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B^{2}e^{4\gamma\sqrt{t-s}}}{(t-s)\omega_{t-s}(x)}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{B(x,\sqrt{t-s})}|e^{\chi(y)}U(\tau,y)|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\omega(y)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B^{2}e^{4\gamma\sqrt{t-s}}}{(t-s)\omega_{t-s}(x)}\int_{s}^{t}\|e^{\chi}U(\tau)\|^{2}_{2,\omega}\ \mathrm{d}\tau.

Using (
6.18
), we have for almost every
z\in B(x,R)
,

(6.19)

|e^{\chi(z)}U(t,z)|^{2}\leq\frac{C^{2}B^{2}e^{4\gamma\sqrt{t-s}}}{(t-s)\omega_{t-s}(x)}\left(\int_{s}^{t}e^{2cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(\tau-s)}e^{2c\gamma^{2}(\tau-s)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\left\|\psi\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}.

Remark that for all
z\in B(x,R)
, we have
B(z,\frac{\sqrt{t-s}}{2})\subset B(x,\sqrt{t-s})
and using (
2.3
) we obtain

\omega_{t-s}(z)=\omega(B(z,\sqrt{t-s}))\leq D\,\omega(B(z,\frac{\sqrt{t-s}}{2}))\leq D\omega_{t-s}(x).

As
\tau-s\leq t-s\ (<4R_{0}^{2})
, then in (
6.19
) we can majorate, for almost every
z\in B(x,R)
, with

|e^{\chi(z)}U(t,z)|^{2}\leq\frac{DC^{2}B^{2}e^{4\gamma\sqrt{t-s}}}{\omega_{t-s}(z)}e^{2cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\left\|\psi\right\|^{2}_{2,\omega}.

Therefore,

(6.20)

\|\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,s)e^{-\chi}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\sqrt{D}CBe^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2\gamma\sqrt{t-s}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{2,\omega}.

Using (
6.20
) and a duality argument, we obtain

\|e^{-\chi}\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t)(\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{\chi}\psi)\|_{2,\omega}\leq\sqrt{D}CBe^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2\gamma\sqrt{t-s}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{L_{\omega}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Using the same computations and arguments with
\tilde{\Gamma}(s,t)
(by using (
6.13
)), a duality argument yields

(6.21)

\|e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,s)(\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{-\chi}\psi)\|_{2,\omega}\leq\sqrt{D}CBe^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2\gamma\sqrt{t-s}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{L_{\omega}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Using the Chapman-Kolmogorov identities, we write
\Gamma(t,s)=\Gamma(t,\frac{t+s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{t+s}{2},s).
Hence,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\

\displaystyle e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,s)(\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{-\chi}\psi)=\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,\frac{t+s}{2})e^{-\chi}(e^{\chi}\Gamma(\frac{t+s}{2},t)\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{-\chi}\psi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{t-s}}{\omega_{\frac{t-s}{2}}}}\sqrt{\omega_{\frac{t-s}{2}}}\ e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,\frac{t+s}{2})e^{-\chi}\left(e^{\chi}\Gamma(\frac{t+s}{2},t)\sqrt{\omega_{\frac{t-s}{2}}}\ e^{-\chi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{t-s}}{\omega_{\frac{t-s}{2}}}}\,\psi\right).

Notice that by the doubling property (
2.3
), we have

(6.22)

\left\|\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{t-s}}{\omega_{\frac{t-s}{2}}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq D^{1/2}.

Combining (
6.20
), (
6.21
), (
6.22
) and the above Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, we deduce that

(6.23)

\|\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{\chi}\Gamma(t,s)(\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}}\ e^{-\chi}\psi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2\sqrt{2}\gamma\sqrt{t-s}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}\left\|\psi\right\|_{L_{\omega}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

Since
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=L^{\infty}_{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the estimate (
6.23
) and the Dunford-Pettis theorem
[
16
]
ensures that, for all
0<t-s<4R_{0}^{2}
,
\Gamma(t,s)
is an integral operator with a unique kernel
\Gamma(t,x;s,y)
satisfying, for almost all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the estimate

(6.24)

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}\times e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{2\sqrt{2}\gamma\sqrt{t-s}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}e^{\chi(y)-\chi(x)}.

When
\gamma=0
, that is
\chi
is constant, we obtain the following bound

(6.25)

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}\times e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}.

To prove (
6.15
), we fix
s,t
with
0<t-s<4R_{0}^{2}
and
x\neq y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which (
6.24
) is valid. If
\frac{|x-y|}{2\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{t-s}}<2
, we have
1\leq e^{\frac{2}{c}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{16c(t-s)}}
and using simply (
6.25
), we obtain

\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{\frac{2}{c}}\times e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{16c(t-s)}+cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}.

Now, assume that
\frac{|x-y|}{2\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{t-s}}\geq 2
. Let
\gamma:=\frac{|x-y|}{4c(t-s)}
, take
N>\gamma|x-y|
and set, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\chi(z):=\inf(\gamma|z-y|,N).

It is a bounded Lipschitz function satisfying
\|\nabla_{x}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\gamma
,
\chi(x)=\gamma|x-y|
and
\chi(y)=0
. Remark that
2\sqrt{2}\gamma\sqrt{t-s}\leq\tfrac{\chi(x)}{2}
, thus, (
6.24
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}\times e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{-\frac{\chi(x)}{2}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)}.

Remark also that
-\tfrac{\chi(x)}{2}+c\gamma^{2}(t-s)=-\tfrac{|x-y|^{2}}{16c(t-s)}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\frac{D^{3/2}C^{2}B^{2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}\times e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{-\tfrac{|x-y|^{2}}{16c(t-s)}}.

This concludes the proof in the case
R_{0}=\infty
.

Iteration in the case
R_{0}<\infty
:

We now suggest a way to iterate and thereby obtain (
6.16
). First, it is known that, for all
s<t
, the degenerate heat semigroup
e^{(t-s)\Delta_{\omega}}
has a kernel
\mathcal{K}_{t-s}(x,y)
satisfying two-sided Gaussian bounds
[
13
,
3
,
8
]
. More precisely, there exist constants
K_{0}=K_{0}([\omega]_{A_{2}},n)>1
and
k_{0}=k_{0}([\omega]_{A_{2}},n)>1
such that, for almost all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6.26)

\frac{K_{0}^{-1}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{-\frac{k_{0}|x-y|^{2}}{t-s}}\leq\mathcal{K}_{t-s}(x,y)\leq\frac{K_{0}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{k_{0}(t-s)}}.

The case
0<t-s<4R_{0}^{2}
is covered by (
6.15
), which has now been established. In this case, using the lower bound in (
6.26
), we have, for almost all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6.27)

\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|\leq\tilde{C}\,K_{0}\,e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}\,\mathcal{K}_{\frac{ck_{0}}{16}(t-s)}(x,y).

Fix
\ell\geq 1
and let
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\ell(4R_{0}^{2})\leq t-s<(\ell+1)(4R_{0}^{2})
. For each
0\leq j\leq\ell+1
, define

r_{j}:=s+\frac{j(t-s)}{\ell+1},

so that
r_{0}=s
,
r_{\ell+1}=t
, and
r_{j+1}-r_{j}=\frac{t-s}{\ell+1}\in(0,4R_{0}^{2})
. By the Chapman–Kolmogorov identities (see Theorem
3.41
, Point (5)), we have

\Gamma(t,s)=\Gamma(t,r_{\ell})\Gamma(r_{\ell},r_{\ell-1})\dots\Gamma(r_{1},s).

In particular,
\Gamma(t,s)
is an integral operator with kernel
\Gamma(t,x;s,y)
. Moreover, using the Chapman–Kolmogorov identities for
\Gamma
and for the semigroup kernel (equivalently, the additivity of the semigroup), together with inequality (
6.27
), we have, for almost every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma(t,x;s,y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ell}}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\left|\Gamma(r_{j+1},z_{j+1};r_{j},z_{j})\right|\right)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{\ell})\,\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{\ell-1})\dots\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{1})\quad(z_{\ell+1}:=x\ \text{and}\ z_{0}:=y)

\displaystyle\leq(\tilde{C}K_{0})^{\ell+1}\left(e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}\frac{t-s}{\ell+1}}\right)^{\ell+1}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ell}}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\mathcal{K}_{\frac{ck_{0}}{16}(r_{j+1}-r_{j})}(z_{j+1},z_{j})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{\ell})\,\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{\ell-1})\dots\mathrm{d}\omega(z_{1})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{C}K_{0})^{\ell+1}e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}\mathcal{K}_{\frac{ck_{0}}{16}(t-s)}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\tilde{C}K_{0})^{\ell+1}K_{0}}{{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}}e^{cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)}e^{-\tfrac{16|x-y|^{2}}{ck_{0}^{2}(t-s)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{C}^{\ell+1}K_{0}^{\ell+2}}{\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{t-s}(y)}}e^{-\tfrac{16|x-y|^{2}}{ck_{0}^{2}(t-s)}+cP_{\infty,\infty}^{2}(t-s)},

where, in the penultimate line, we used the upper bound in (
6.26
).
∎

Remark 6.28
.

The factor
k_{0}^{-2}
in (
6.16
) arises from the lack of explicit formulas for the degenerate heat semigroup kernels, unlike in the unweighted case, where the kernels are explicit Gaussians and the factor
\frac{16}{c}
in (
6.15
) remains unchanged under iteration.

Corollary 6.29
.

Assume that we are in the setting of Theorem
6.14
. Then, for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
with
s<t
, the following properties hold.

(1)

(Adjointess property) For almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, we have

\tilde{\Gamma}(s,y;t,x)=\overline{\Gamma(t,x;s,y)},

where
\tilde{\Gamma}(s,y;t,x)
is generalized fundamental solution of the adjoint operator
-\partial_{t}+\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{\mathrm{inh}}
.

(2)

(Chapman-Kolmogorov identities) If
r\in(s,t)
, then for almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Gamma(t,x;r,z)\Gamma(r,z;s,y)\ \mathrm{d}\omega(z)=\Gamma(t,x;s,y).

Proof.

This follows directly from the adjointness property together with the Chapman–Kolmogorov identities, as stated in Theorem
3.41
, Points (4) and (5).
