Strengthening Bulow-Klemperer-Style Results for Multi-Unit Auctions
Moshe Babaioff, Yiding Feng, Zihan Luo

Abstract
The classic result of Bulow and Klemperer, 1996 shows that in multi-unit auctions with m m units and n ≥ m n\geq m buyers whose values are sampled i.i.d. from a regular distribution, the revenue of the VCG auction with m m additional buyers is at least as large as the optimal revenue. Unfortunately, for regular distributions, adding m m additional buyers is sometimes indeed necessary, so the “competition complexity” of the VCG auction is m m . We seek proving better competition complexity results in two dimensions. First, under stronger distributional assumptions—specifically, λ \lambda -regularity, which interpolates between regularity and monotone hazard rate (MHR)—the competition complexity of VCG auction drops dramatically. In balanced markets (where m = n m=n ) with MHR distributions, it is sufficient to only add ( e 1 / e − 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ n ≈ 0.4447 ​ n (e^{1/e}-1+o(1))n\approx 0.4447n additional buyers to match the optimal revenue—less than half the number that is necessary under regularity—and this bound is asymptotically tight. We provide both exact finite-market results for small value of n n , and closed-form asymptotic formulas for general market with any m ≤ n m\leq n , and any target fraction of the optimal revenue. Second, we analyze a supply-limiting variant of VCG auction that caps the number of units sold in a prior-independent way. Whenever the goal is to achieve almost the optimal revenue—say, 90% or 95%—this mechanism strictly improves upon standard VCG auction, requiring significantly fewer additional buyers. Together, our results show that both stronger distributional assumptions, as well as a simple prior-independent refinement to the VCG auction, can each substantially reduce the number of additional buyers that is sufficient to achieve (near-)optimal revenue. Our analysis hinges on a unified worst-case reduction to truncated generalized Pareto distributions, enabling both numerical computation and analytical tractability.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental goals in mechanism design is revenue maximization.
Optimizing over the complex space of mechanisms to maximize the revenue is challenging, but for the single-item setting,
Myerson, 1981
has presented his celebrate mechanism, which maximizes the seller’s expected revenue in the Bayesian environment. However, this mechanism relies on the knowledge of the buyers’ prior value distribution—a requirement that may be impractical in many real-world settings.
To address this limitation,
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
showed that for a single-item auction, when buyer values are drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution, it suffices to recruit just one additional buyer from the same distribution and run the
VCG Auction
(i.e., the second-price auction). This simple, prior-independent mechanism—requiring no knowledge of the prior—guarantees expected revenue at least as high as that of Myerson’s optimal auction. This result suggests that, rather than optimizing over the complex, prior-dependent mechanism-design space to maximize revenue, a firm may instead benefit from market expansion and the use of simple and practical prior-independent mechanisms, such as the second-price auction.

In many practical applications, the seller may have multiple units of a homogeneous good to allocate among
n
buyers. Prominent examples include online advertising (where an ad platform sells multiple impressions of the same ad slot), spectrum auctions (where a regulator allocates several identical frequency blocks), cloud computing markets (where a provider offers multiple instances of a virtual machine type), and emerging AI service platforms (where providers sell access to multiple identical queries or compute tokens from a large language model). As a natural extension of the single-unit setting,
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
show that when the number of units is
m\leq n
and buyers’ values are drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution, recruiting
m
additional buyers and then running the
VCG Auction

By considering prior-independent variants of the

Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism

Indeed, our work shows that to approximately outperform the optimal revenue, it is sufficient for a prior-independent supply-limiting variant of
VCG Auction
to add significantly fewer additional buyers.

1.2
Our Techniques

In most prior work on Bulow–Klemperer-style results for regular distributions
(
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
;
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2009
;
Roughgarden et al., 2020
;
Fu et al., 2019
, e.g.,)
, the analysis typically relies on charging arguments or virtual surplus comparisons, and an explicit characterization of worst-case distributions is unnecessary. In contrast, to obtain tight or nearly tight characterizations of competition complexity under stronger distributional assumptions or alternative mechanisms, we require a more refined approach.

A key ingredient in our analysis is a
worst-case reduction
(
Propositions

3.1
,
4.1
and
5.1
): we show that to understand the competition complexity of the
VCG Auction
(and its supply-limiting variant), it suffices to consider a very simple family of distributions—namely, truncated
\lambda
-generalized Pareto distributions (
Definition

4.1
). This family is parameterized by just a single parameter (the truncation point), which makes both numerical and analytical treatment tractable.

The main challenge in proving this reduction stems from a subtle technical issue: in multi-unit settings, the revenue gap between
VCG Auction
and the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
does
not
behave monotonically with respect to standard stochastic dominance. In other words, a “worse” distribution (in the usual sense) does not always lead to a larger revenue gap, since the expected revenue of both mechanisms decreases. This breaks a common proof strategy used in the literature.

To overcome this, we shift our focus from the multiplicative revenue gap to an additive one, and uncover a clean
structured three-interval property
: the behavior of the gap can be decomposed into three quantile intervals. While non-monotonicity appears in the middle interval, it does so in a consistent and controllable way. Leveraging the concavity of revenue curves under
\lambda
-regularity, we show that the worst case is always attained within the class of truncated
\lambda
-generalized Pareto distributions. The proof proceeds via a careful case analysis based on the location of the monopoly quantile relative to the three quantile intervals.

This reduction not only resolves the technical obstacle but also serves as a powerful tool: it allows us to compute exact competition complexity for small markets via numerical search. In addition, it also serves as a powerful analytical tool. For large markets, where the target approximation ratio
\Gamma<1
, it enables clean asymptotic results (
Theorems

4.2
and
5.2
) via standard concentration inequalities and the slack introduced by
\Gamma<1
. However, when the goal is
exact
optimality (
\Gamma=1
)—as in
Theorem

3.2
for balanced markets under MHR distributions—the lack of slack makes the analysis substantially more delicate.

We next explain the key challenge and technical approach behind establishing the uniform upper bound in
Theorem

3.2
. Even after reducing the problem to the one-parameter family of truncated exponential distributions, a significant obstacle remains: the worst-case truncation point
r\in[1,e]
admits no closed-form expression and varies with the market size
n
(
Remark

3.2
). This rules out simple endpoint analyses.

To overcome this, we develop a refined four-part case analysis over the interval
[1,e]
, leveraging precise derivative estimates and concentration properties of order statistics (see
Lemmas

E.2
,
E.3
,
E.4
,
E.5
and
E.6
). This careful decomposition allows us to certify that the bound stated in the theorem suffices for all market size
n\geq 594
. The competition complexity for market size
n\leq 593
is covered by the numerical computations.

Preliminaries

In this work, we study revenue maximization in a Bayesian multi-unit multi-buyer setting, aiming to match the optimal revenue by
a prior-independent mechanism with some additional buyers.

Model.

A seller has
m
identical units of a good to sell to
n\geq m
unit-demand buyers.
Each buyer
i
has a private value
v_{i}\geq 0
for receiving one unit, where the values are independently and identically (i.i.d.) drawn from a publicly known valuation distribution
F
, supported on non-negative numbers.
We use
F
interchangeably to denote both the distribution and its cumulative distribution function (CDF), i.e.,
F(v)=\Pr_{x\sim F}[x<v]
.
We assume that
F
has a probability density function and denote its corresponding probability density function (PDF) by
f
. We also assume that there exists a price
p\in{\sf supp}(F)
that maximizes the expected revenue
p\cdot(1-F(p))
for the single-buyer problem.

Mechanisms.

A selling
mechanism
consists of an
allocation rule

\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{b})=\left(x_{1}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right),\ldots,x_{n}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right)
and a
payment rule

\boldsymbol{p}(\boldsymbol{b})=\left(p_{1}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right),\ldots,p_{n}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right)
, where
x_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\in[0,1]
denotes the probability that buyer
i
receives one unit of the good when the reported bid profile is
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
, and
p_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the payment charged to buyer
i
. Feasibility relies on that at most
m
units are allocated ex post,
i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\leq m
for all
\boldsymbol{b}
. Buyers aim to maximize their quasi-linear utility, which is
x_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)\cdot b_{i}-p_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)
for buyer
i
with allocation
x_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)
that is charged a payment of
p_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{b}\right)
.

A mechanism satisfies
Bayesian incentive compatible (BIC)
if every buyer maximizes her interim utility (that is, her expected utility over the randomness of all other buyers’ value realizations and randomness of the mechanism) by bidding her value truthfully when all other buyers bids truthfully; and satisfies
dominant strategy incentive compatible (DSIC)
if every buyer maximizes her ex post utility regardless of all other buyers’ reports. For mechanisms that are DSIC or BIC, we assume buyers report their private values truthfully, and thus interpret the allocation and payment rules
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p})
of a given mechanism as functions of the true valuation profile
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, mapping it to buyers’ allocations and payments, respectively.

A DSIC/BIC mechanism satisfies
interim individual rational (interim IR)
if every buyer’s interim utility is non-negative for all her values; and satisfies
ex post individual rational (ex post IR)
if every buyer’s ex post utility is non-negative for all valuation profiles.

Classic distribution families.

In the mechanism design literature, the regularity and monotone hazard rate (MHR) conditions are two classic distributional assumptions that have been studied extensively:

•

(Regularity)
A distribution
F
satisfies the
regularity condition
if its
virtual value

v-\frac{1-F(v)}{f(v)}
is non-decreasing in
v
.

•

(MHR)
A distribution
F
satisfies the
monotone hazard rate (MHR) condition
if its
hazard rate

\frac{f(v)}{1-F(v)}

is non-decreasing in
v
.

We also recall the
\lambda
-regularity condition which interpolates between these two conditions in a parametric way.
The
\lambda
-regularity condition is also known as the
\alpha
-strongly regular condition
in the literature
(
Cole and Roughgarden, 2014
;
Cole and Rao, 2017
)
.

•

(
\lambda
-Regularity)
For any
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
a distribution
F
satisfies the
\lambda
-regularity condition
if its
\lambda
-generalized virtual value

\lambda\cdot v-\frac{1-F(v)}{f(v)}
is non-decreasing in
v
.

By definition, it can be verified that the
\lambda
-regularity with
\lambda=1
and
\lambda=0
recover the classic regularity and MHR conditions, respectively.
We also denote the classes of regular distributions, MHR distributions, and
\lambda
-regular distributions, by
\mathcal{F}_{\text{Reg}}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{(\lambda\text{-Reg})}
, respectively.

Bayesian revenue maximization.

The seller’s objective is to maximize her expected
revenue
, defined as the sum of payments collected from all buyers:
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\!\left[{\sum_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)}\right]
. For ease of presentation, we let
{\sf RevX}_{m:n}(F)
denote the expected revenue of truthful (DSIC/BIC) mechanism
{\sf X}
when selling
m
units of an identical good to
n
truthful buyers whose values are drawn i.i.d. from
F
.
When the distribution is clear from the context, we omit
F
and simply write
{\sf RevX}_{m:n}
.

We recall Myerson’s characterization of the Bayesian-optimal auction for selling
m
identical units when buyer valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution (Myerson’s
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
maximizes the revenue under the BIC and interim IR constraints).

Lemma 2.1

(
Myerson, 1981
)
.

Consider the setting of selling
m
units of an identical item to
n\geq m
buyers with values drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution
F
.
Fix a monopoly reserve
r^{*}\in\argmax\limits_{p\geq 0}\,p\cdot(1-F(p))
.
The
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
with monopoly reserve
r^{*}
operates as follows: allocate one unit to each of the
m
highest-valued buyers whose value is at least monopoly reserve
r^{*}
. Each allocated buyer pays the maximum of the monopoly reserve
r^{*}
and the
(m+1)
-th highest value; all other buyers pay
0
.

Notably, the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
is
distribution-dependent
: implementing the monopoly reserve
r^{*}
relies on the knowledge of the valuation distribution
F
.
Motivated by the Wilson doctrine
(
Wilson, 1987
)
, it is therefore desirable to design mechanisms whose performance guarantees do not rely on such detailed prior information, which leads to the study of
prior-independent mechanisms
.
One important prior-independent mechanism studied in the literature—and the main focus of our paper—is the
VCG Auction
:

Definition 2.1
.

In an
m
-unit
n
-buyer market with
m\leq n
, the
VCG Auction
allocates one unit to each of the
m
highest-value buyers, and charges each of them the
(m+1)
-highest value as the price.

The
VCG Auction
is DSIC, ex post IR, and welfare-maximizing by construction. Remarkably, its implementation does not relies on any knowledge regarding the valuation distribution
F
, and hence it is prior-independent. When the valuation distribution is regular, it is similar to the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
and the only difference is that it does not have a prior-dependent monopoly reserve.
While the
VCG Auction
is welfare-maximizing, its revenue is in general strictly smaller than the revenue of the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
.

The Bulow-Klemperer result and competition complexity.

The seminal work of
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
shows that it is possible to obtain revenue that is as large as the Bayesian optimal revenue, by the
VCG Auction
with some additional buyers.
Following their approach, we also use the
VCG Auction
as our canonical prior-independent mechanism for this purpose.

Theorem 2.2

(
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
)
.

Consider the setting of selling
m
units of an identical item to
n\geq m
buyers with values drawn i.i.d. from a regular distribution
F

with
m
additional buyers has revenue at least as large as
the original
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
, i.e.,

{\sf RevVCG}_{m:n+m}(F)\geq{\sf RevOPT}_{m:n}(F)
. Moreover, there exists an instance with a regular distribution such that
m
additional buyers are necessary for this to hold.

Following the result of
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996

Eden et al., 2017

\displaystyle\texttt{CC}\left(m,n,\mathcal{F}_{\text{Reg}},1\right)=m

Revenue curves.
We follow the revenue-curve viewpoint of
Bulow and Roberts, 1989
to describe the expected revenue obtained by posting a take-it-or-leave-it price when buyers’ values are drawn from valuation distribution
F
.

Definition 2.3

(Revenue curve,
Bulow and Roberts, 1989
)
.

Fix a valuation distribution
F
. The
revenue curve

R:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined as

\displaystyle R(q)\triangleq q\cdot F^{-1}(1-q)\quad\text{for all $q\in(0,1]$}~,\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R(0)\triangleq\lim\nolimits_{q\to 0^{+}}R(q)

where
F^{-1}(1-q)\triangleq\sup\{v:F(v)\leq 1-q\}
.

We define the
monopoly revenue
as the largest revenue
\max_{q\in[0,1]}R(q)
and denote every quantile (resp. price) attaining this largest revenue as a
monopoly quantile

q^{*}
(resp.
monopoly reserve
r^{*}
).
5
5

5

When the distribution
F
is regular, its revenue curve is bounded and continuous, so the monopoly revenue and monopoly quantile are well-defined. When
F
is
\lambda
-regular with
\lambda\in[0,1)
, the monopoly quantile is not only well-defined but also unique and strictly positive
(
Schweizer and Szech, 2019
)
, which implies that the monopoly reserve is uniquely defined as well.
For regular distributions, it is possible that the monopoly quantile is zero. In this case, we adopt the convention that the corresponding monopoly reserve is
\infty
, and the monopoly revenue is given by
R(0)
.

The regularity of a valuation distribution has the following nice geometric interpretation on the induced revenue curve.

Lemma 2.3

(
Bulow and Roberts, 1989
)
.

A valuation distribution
F
of the buyer is regular if and only if the induced revenue curve
R
is weakly concave.

3
Balanced Markets with an MHR Distribution

As we illustrate in
Example

1.1
, for every
n
, there exists an
n
-unit
n
-buyer (balanced) market with values sampled i.i.d. from some regular distribution, for which
n
additional buyers are necessary for the
VCG Auction
to outperform the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
, i.e.,
a number equal to the market size (and, by
Theorem

2.2
,
n
additional buyers are also sufficient).
Notably, while the distribution in
Example

1.1
satisfies regularity, it does
not
satisfy the MHR condition. This raises a natural question:
when the valuation distribution is MHR rather than merely regular, is it sufficient to add significantly fewer than
n
buyers?

We answer this question affirmatively: when
n
is large enough, it suffices to add fewer than half as many additional buyers. Specifically, in
Section

3.1
, we first show that, rather than optimizing over the entire (infinite-dimensional) space of MHR distributions, the tight competition complexity can be determined by restricting attention to a specific subclass of worst-case MHR distributions that admits a simple closed-form characterization (
Proposition

3.1
).

This worst-case reduction serves as a powerful tool for subsequent analyses. We leverage it to numerically compute the (nearly) exact competition complexity for any given finite market size
n
. Moreover, we use the same characterization to analytically derive a uniform upper bound on the growth rate of the competition complexity as a function of
n
, as stated in
Theorem

3.2
(
Section

3.2
). We show that the growth rate is asymptotically at most
(e^{1/e}-1)\cdot n\approx 0.4447\cdot n
, which is less than half the rate that is sufficient and necessary for regular distributions. We also show that this rate is asymptotically tight in the same theorem.

3.1
Characterization of a Worst-Case Sub-class of Distributions

As in much of the literature, a common approach to studying revenue approximation is to identify either a worst-case distribution or a succinct subclass of distributions that contains the worst case. Our analysis follows this high-level agenda.

In a balanced market (
m=n
), the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
(see
Lemma

2.1
) simplifies to posting the monopoly reserve to all
n
buyers and allocating one unit to each buyer whose value exceeds this reserve. Consequently, the optimal revenue is simply
n
times the monopoly revenue.
Combining this with the revenue monotonicity of the
VCG Auction
(i.e.,
{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n}(F_{1})\leq{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n}(F_{2})
whenever
F_{1}
is first-order stochastically dominated by
F_{2}
), we observe that a distribution can be worst-case for the competition complexity
\texttt{CC}(n,\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}})
only if it does not strictly first-order stochastically dominates any other MHR distribution with the same monopoly revenue. (Otherwise, such a dominated distribution would yield the same optimal revenue but lower VCG revenue, making it worse for competition complexity.)
After normalizing the monopoly revenue to
1
(which is without loss of generality for competition complexity analysis), we show that, for every market size
n
, the worst-case distribution belongs to the following class of truncated exponential distributions, a natural subclass of the MHR distributions.

Definition 3.1

(Truncated exponential distribution)
.

For any
r\in[1,e]
, the
r
-truncated exponential distribution
F_{(0,r)}
has support
{\sf supp}(F_{(0,r)})=[0,r]
, and its CDF
F_{(0,r)}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
is defined as

\displaystyle F_{(0,r)}(v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-r^{-\frac{v}{r}}&v\in[0,r]\\
1&v\in(r,\infty)\end{array}\right.

The
class of Truncated Exponential Distributions
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}\triangleq\left\{F_{(0,r)}:r\in[1,e]\right\}.

It can be verified (by its definition) that for every
r\in[1,e]
, the
r
-truncated exponential distribution is MHR, and thus
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}}
.
The fact that the competition complexity of the class of MHR distributions is the same at the one for the subclass of truncated exponential distributions
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}
is formalized by the following result.
As it is a special case of the more general result presented in
Proposition

4.1
(covering imbalanced markets, arbitrary target approximation ratios
\Gamma
, and
\lambda
-regular distributions),
we omit its proof (we prove the more general result of
Proposition

4.1
in
Section

Proposition 3.1

(Characterization of a subclass of worst-case MHR distributions)
.

The competition complexity of the
VCG Auction
in the
n
-unit,
n
-buyer balanced market over the class of MHR distributions
\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}}
coincides with competition complexity
over the subclass of truncated exponential distributions
\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}}
; that is,

\displaystyle\texttt{CC}\left(n,\mathcal{F}_{\text{MHR}}\right)=\texttt{CC}\left(n,\mathcal{F}_{\textsc{TEXP}}\right).

Remark 3.2
.

One might wonder whether an even stronger characterization of the worst-case is possible—for example, whether the worst-case distribution always occurs at a boundary of the allowable truncation range (i.e.,
r=1
or
r=e
) or whether the same truncation value
r
is worst for all market sizes

We show that neither is true: the worst-case truncation

Appendix

Although the definition of competition complexity quantifies over all MHR distributions, the proposition above guarantees that it suffices to consider a much smaller subclass. Specifically, the class of truncated exponential distributions consists of distributions that admit a simple closed-form expression parameterized by a single scalar
r\in[1,e]
.

Leveraging our characterization we can numerically compute the competition complexity

for any given market size

as follows: we pick some large

and perform a grid search over a discretization of the truncation

with discretization step size

and report the results in
Appendix

D

In the next subsection, we further exploit our characterization to derive a uniform upper and lower bound on the competition complexity that holds for all market sizes
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.2
Uniform Upper and Lower Bound on the Competition Complexity

In this section, we provide a uniform and lower upper bound on the competition complexity for MHR distributions in the balanced-market setting (

Theorem 3.2

For any

4
General Markets with a
\lambda
-Regular Distribution

In this section, we generalize our analysis in
Section

3
along three natural dimensions.

First, we move beyond the MHR assumption to the broader class of
\lambda
-regular distributions, which smoothly interpolates between regularity (
\lambda=1
) and MHR (
\lambda=0
). This allows us to quantify precisely how stronger distributional assumptions reduce the number of additional buyers.

Second, we consider general (possibly imbalanced) markets with
n
buyers and
m\leq n
units, rather than restricting to the balanced case (
m=n
). This captures a wider range of practical settings—from scarce goods (
m\ll n
) to abundant supply (
m\approx n
).

Third, instead of requiring the
VCG Auction
to fully match the optimal Bayesian revenue (obtaining a
\Gamma
approximation with
\Gamma=1
), we relax the goal to achieving a

\Gamma
-fraction of it, i.e., for
\varepsilon>0
, getting a fraction of only
\Gamma=1-\varepsilon<1
.
This shift is not only theoretically natural but also practically relevant: if a seller can recruit only a limited number of extra buyers—say,
0.2n
instead of the
0.4447n
(that is necessary for full optimality under MHR)—what revenue guarantee can they still expect? Our framework provides a complete answer: by inverting our competition complexity bounds, one can determine the best approximation ratio
\Gamma
achievable for any given number of additional buyers.

Together, these extensions yield a unified and fine-grained understanding of how market size, distributional strength, and revenue targets jointly shape the cost of prior independence, in the sense of the number of additional buyers that is sufficient and necessary for the
VCG Auction
to beat the optimum in revenue.

Similar to
Section

3
, we first show that, rather than searching over the entire (infinite dimensional) space of
\lambda
-regular distributions, the tight competition complexity can be determined by considering a specific subclass of
\lambda
-regular distributions that contains the worst-case distribution and admits a simple closed-form characterization (
Proposition

4.1
). This “worst-case” characterization then serves as a powerful tool for subsequent analyses: we apply it to analytically derive the tight bound on the competition complexity in (possibly imbalanced) large markets, for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
and any
\Gamma\in(0,1)
(
Theorem

4.2
) in
Section

4.2
.

4.1
Characterization of a Worst-Case Sub-class of Distributions

9

It is known that, for any
\lambda

(which is without loss of generality in competition complexity analysis), the class

can be viewed as the smallest distributions—in the FOSD sense—among all
\lambda
-regular distributions.

Since the
r
-truncated
\lambda
-generalized Pareto distribution

is first-order stochastically dominated by every other
\lambda
-regular distribution with the same monopoly reserve and monopoly quantile, a natural approach to establishing its worst-case nature would be to show monotonicity of the
multiplicative revenue gap
in the FOSD order.
Specifically, one might hope that the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf RevVCG}_{m:n+k}(F)}{{\sf RevOPT}_{m:n}(F)}

becomes larger in the FOSD sense—while keeping the monopoly reserve and quantile fixed.
Unfortunately, this monotonicity does not hold in general when

Indeed, one can construct
\lambda
-regular distributions

(see such an example in

The underlying reason is that, although both the

and the

share similar structural forms, the addition of
k
extra buyers alters the distribution of the
(m+1)
-th highest value, which affects the revenues of both mechanisms in non-monotonic ways.

To overcome this obstacle, we shift our focus from the multiplicative revenue gap to the
additive revenue gap
:

\displaystyle{\sf RevVCG}_{m:n+k}(F)-\Gamma\cdot{\sf RevOPT}_{m:n}(F).

This shift is not merely a technical convenience—it is
without loss of generality
for the purpose of identifying worst-case distributions. Recall that the competition complexity is defined as the smallest
k
such that this additive gap is non-negative for
all
distributions in the class. Thus, any distribution that minimizes the additive gap is also a candidate for maximizing the required
k
, making it sufficient to analyze this additive form.

This change of perspective reveals a crucial
three-interval structure
in the behavior of the additive gap. Specifically, there exist quantile thresholds
q^{\dagger}\leq q^{\ddagger}
in
[0,1]
such that the following holds. Consider any two
\lambda
-regular distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
that share the same monopoly reserve and monopoly quantile, with
F_{1}
first-order stochastically dominated by
F_{2}
. Suppose further that their CDFs differ only on values corresponding to a single quantile interval. Then:

•

If the CDFs differ only on
[0,q^{\dagger}]
, the additive revenue gap is
smaller
under
F_{1}
;

•

If they differ only on
[q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}]

larger
under
F_{1}
;

•

If they differ only on
[q^{\ddagger},1]
, the gap is again
smaller
under
F_{1}
.

In other words, the failure of monotonicity is confined exclusively to the middle interval
[q^{\dagger},q^{\ddagger}]
—and even there, it behaves in a consistent and analyzable direction: the additive gap

under the FOSD-dominated distribution. (In the special case of a balanced market (
n=m

q^{\dagger}=q^{\ddagger}
, and full monotonicity is restored.)

Leveraging the concavity of revenue curves under
\lambda
-regularity, we show that this structure allows us to “compensate” for the non-monotonicity in the middle interval using the monotonicity in the lower interval
[0,q^{\dagger}]
. This enables a precise comparison between an arbitrary
\lambda
-regular distribution and its truncated generalized Pareto counterpart, ultimately proving that the worst case always lies within our parametric subclass.

Unlike the single-unit setting, where the

Definition 5.1

(

)

In an

We remark that the supply

It can be verified that the

corresponds to a special case of the

We define its competition complexity as follows:

Definition 5.2

\displaystyle\texttt{CC}^{\texttt{SL}}\left(m,n,\mathcal{F},\Gamma\right)

Both arguments closely mirror their counterparts for the classic

Appendix A
Analysis of
Example

1.1

In this section, we analyze
Example

1.1
.
First, we consider the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
. Since the market is balanced,
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
reduces to offer the monopoly price to all buyers, and thus, the expected revenue is

\displaystyle{\sf RevOPT}_{n:n}(F)=n\cdot H\cdot\frac{1}{H+1}=n-\frac{1}{3}

where the last equality holds since
H=3n-1
.

Next we analyze the
VCG Auction
with
2N-1
buyers. Since all buyers are ex ante symmetric. It suffices to analyze a fixed buyer. Notably, a fixed buyer
i
receives the item if and only if her value
v_{i}
is higher than the
n
-th highest values among others
2n-2
buyers. Thus, by considering the interim allocation and revenue curve in the quantile space, the expected payment from a fixed agent
i
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}R(q)\cdot\eta_{(n:2n-2)}(q)\,{\mathrm{d}}q\leq\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(1-q)\cdot\eta_{(n:2n-2)}(q)\,{\mathrm{d}}q\leq\frac{1}{2}

where
R
is the revenue curve induced by distribution
F
, and
\eta_{a:b}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the probability density function (PDF) of
a
-th order statistics from
b
i.i.d. samples from the uniform distribution
U[0,1]
. Here the first inequality holds due to the fact that
R(q)\leq 1-q
for all
q\in[0,1]
(implied by the construction of
F
), and
the second inequality holds due to the facts that
\int_{0}^{1}\eta_{(n:2n-2)}(q)\,{\mathrm{d}}q=1
and
\int_{0}^{1}q\cdot\eta_{(n:2n-2)}(q)\,{\mathrm{d}}q\geq\frac{1}{2}
(guaranteed since
\eta_{(n:2n-2)}
is a PDF, and its expectation is larger than
1/2
due to the symmetry of uniform distribution).
Thus, the expected revenue in the
VCG Auction
with
2n-1
buyers is

\displaystyle{\sf RevVCG}_{n:2n-1}(F)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot(2n-1)=n-\frac{1}{2}

which is strictly less than the expected revenue in the
Bayesian-Optimal Mechanism
.

VCG Auction
in the
n

(n+t_{n})

Throughout
Section

E.1

{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})

We first establish a sequence of technical lemmas needed for our final analysis.

Lemma E.1
.

Define a random variable
X\sim Beta\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)
with density

p\left(x\right)\triangleq\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot x^{n}\cdot\left(1-x\right)^{t_{n}-1}
.
Let
p_{+}(q^{*})\triangleq\text{Pr}\!\left[{\left(X\geq q^{*}\right)}\right]=\int_{q^{*}}^{1}p\left(x\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

and

h(q^{*})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left(\left(-\ln X\right)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\left\{X\geq q^{*}\right\}}\right)}\right]=\int_{q^{*}}^{1}\left(-\ln x\right)\cdot p\left(x\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x
.
Then,

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\ln\left({\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}\left(q^{*}\right)\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}=-\frac{1}{q^{*}}+\frac{1}{q^{*}}\cdot\frac{h(q^{*})}{\left(\ln q^{*}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-p_{+}(q^{*})-\frac{h(q^{*})}{\ln q^{*}}\right)}.

Proof of
Lemma

E.1
.

Define the quantity
a\triangleq(n+t_{n})\cdot(n+t_{n}-1)\cdot\binom{n+t_{n}-2}{n-1}
, which does not depend on
q^{*}
.
The expression for
{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}\left(q^{*}\right)
is as follows (recall
\eta_{a:b}
is the PDF of
a
-th smallest order statistics of
b
i.i.d. samples from uniform distribution
U[0,1]
):

\displaystyle{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})={}

\displaystyle\left(n+t_{n}\right)\cdot\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}R_{r^{*}}\left(q\right)\cdot\eta_{n:n+t_{n}-1}\left(q\right){\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(n+t_{n})\cdot\left[\displaystyle\int_{0}^{q^{*}}r^{*}\cdot q\cdot\eta_{n:n+t_{n}-1}{\mathrm{d}}q+\displaystyle\int_{q^{*}}^{1}\frac{r^{*}}{\ln r^{*}}\cdot q\cdot\ln\frac{1}{q}\cdot\eta_{n:n+t_{n}-1}{\mathrm{d}}q\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{a}{q^{*}}\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{q^{*}}(1-q)^{t_{n}-1}\cdot q^{n}{\mathrm{d}}q-\frac{1}{\ln q^{*}}\cdot\displaystyle\int_{q^{*}}^{1}-\ln q\cdot(1-q)^{t_{n}-1}\cdot q^{n}{\mathrm{d}}q\right)

By the definition of the Beta function, we know that
B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}q^{n}\cdot\left(1-q\right)^{t_{n}-1}{\mathrm{d}}q
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{q^{*}}(1-q)^{t_{n}-1}\cdot q^{n}{\mathrm{d}}q=B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)\cdot(1-p_{+}(q^{*}))

\displaystyle\int_{q^{*}}^{1}-\ln q\cdot(1-q)^{t_{n}-1}\cdot q^{n}{\mathrm{d}}q=B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)\cdot h(q^{*})

These probabilistic representations allow us to rewrite the key terms in a compact form:

\displaystyle{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})=\frac{a}{q^{*}}\cdot B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)\cdot\left(1-p_{+}(q^{*})-\frac{h(q^{*})}{\ln q^{*}}\right)

We will analyze the monotonicity of
{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})
by studying the sign of its logarithmic derivative.
Since
{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})>0
for all
q^{*}
, it suffices to study the sign of

{\mathrm{d}}\ln({\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*}))/{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}={{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})^{\prime}}/{{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})}
,
where

\displaystyle\ln({\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*}))=\ln\frac{a\cdot B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}{q^{*}}+\ln\left(1-p_{+}(q^{*})-\frac{h(q^{*})}{\ln q^{*}}\right)

(2)

Since
a\cdot B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)
is independent of
q^{*}
, in
Equation

2
, the derivative of the first term with respect to
q^{*}
equals
-1/q^{*}
.

Let
F(q^{*})\triangleq 1-p_{+}(q^{*})-{h(q^{*})}/{\ln q^{*}}
denote the argument of the logarithm in the second term of
Equation

2
.
Differentiating
F(q^{*})
with respect to
q^{*}
, we obtain

F^{\prime}(q^{*})=-p_{+}^{\prime}(q^{*})+\frac{h^{\prime}(q^{*})}{{\left|\ln q^{*}\right|}}+\frac{h(q^{*})}{q^{*}\cdot(\ln q^{*})^{2}}=\frac{h(q^{*})}{q^{*}\cdot(\ln q^{*})^{2}}

The last equality follows from Leibniz’ rule, which yields
p_{+}^{\prime}(q^{*})=-p(q^{*})
and
h^{\prime}(q^{*})=-(-\ln q^{*})\,p(q^{*})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\ln({\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*}))}{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}={}-\frac{1}{q^{*}}+\frac{F^{\prime}(q^{*})}{F(q^{*})}={}-\frac{1}{q^{*}}+\frac{1}{q^{*}}\cdot\frac{h(q^{*})}{\left(\ln q^{*}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-p_{+}(q^{*})-\frac{h(q^{*})}{\ln q^{*}}\right)}

This complete the proof of
Lemma

E.1

Lemma E.2
.

For any

and

Proof of
Lemma

E.2
.

In this proof, we will always consider on
{\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*})
, where
q^{*}
is between
[0.8,1]
.

Let
\mu\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{X}\right]=(n+1)/(n+t_{n}+1)
, which is approximately
0.69
when
n\geq 594
. By Hoeffding’s inequality, for
\epsilon=0.1
we have

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\!\left[{{\left|X-\mu\right|}\geq\epsilon}\right]\leq 2\cdot e^{-(n+t_{n}+1)\cdot\epsilon^{2}}=2\cdot e^{-0.01\cdot(n+t_{n}+1)}

when

q^{*}\geq\mu+\epsilon

We will prove that
{{\mathrm{d}}\ln({\sf RevVCG}_{n:n+t_{n}}(q^{*}))}/{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}<0

E.1
,
it is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{h(q^{*})}{(\ln q^{*})^{2}}\cdot(1+\ln q^{*})<1-p_{+}(q^{*}).

(3)

Case (i)

When
q^{*}\to 1^{-}

\displaystyle\lim_{q^{*}\to 1^{-}}\frac{h(q^{*})}{(\ln q^{*})^{2}}=\lim_{q^{*}\to 1^{-}}\frac{\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}h(q^{*})}{\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}q^{*}}(\ln q^{*})^{2}}=\lim_{q^{*}\to 1^{-}}\frac{(\ln q^{*})\cdot p(q^{*})}{2\ln q^{*}/q^{*}}=\lim_{q^{*}\to 1^{-}}\frac{q^{*}\cdot p(q^{*})}{2}=0

The last equality holds since
p(q^{*})=0
when
q^{*}=1
.
Therefore, condition
3
holds when
q^{*}=1
.

Case (ii)

When
q^{*}\in[0.99,1]
,
we apply the substitutions
q^{*}=1-u
and
x=1-y
. Accordingly,
h(q^{*})
and
p(q^{*})
are rewritten as

and

\displaystyle h\left(1-u\right)=\int_{0}^{u}-\ln\left(1-y\right)\cdot p\left(1-y\right){\mathrm{d}}y\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,p\left(1-y\right)=\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\left(1-y\right)^{n}\cdot y^{t_{n}-1}

Since
0\leq y\leq u\leq 0.01
, using the Taylor expansion of

, we have

\displaystyle-\ln\left(1-y\right)=y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{y^{j}}{j}\leq y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}y^{j}=y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{3}}{3\left(1-y\right)}\leq y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{3}}{3\left(1-u\right)}

Moreover, since
\left(1-y\right)^{n}\leq 1
, it follows that

\displaystyle h\left(1-u\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\int_{0}^{u}-\ln\left(1-y\right)\cdot\left(1-y\right)^{n}\cdot y^{t_{n}-1}{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\int_{0}^{u}\left(y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{3}}{3\cdot\left(1-u\right)}\right)\cdot y^{t_{n}-1}{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\left(\frac{u^{t_{n}+1}}{t_{n}+1}+\frac{u^{t_{n}+2}}{2\cdot\left(t_{n}+2\right)}+\frac{u^{t_{n}+3}}{3\cdot\left(1-u\right)\cdot\left(t_{n}+3\right)}\right)

Therefore, we conclude that for all
q^{*}\in\left[0.99,1\right]
,

\displaystyle\frac{h\left(q^{*}\right)}{\left(\ln q^{*}\right)^{2}}={}\frac{h\left(1-u\right)}{\left(\ln\left(1-u\right)\right)^{2}}\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\left(\frac{u^{t_{n}-1}}{t_{n}+1}+\frac{u^{t_{n}}}{2\cdot\left(t_{n}+2\right)}+\frac{u^{t_{n}+1}}{3\cdot 0.99\cdot\left(t_{n}+3\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{(0.01)^{t_{n}-1}}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{t_{n}+1}+\frac{0.01}{2\cdot\left(t_{n}+2\right)}+\frac{(0.01)^{2}}{3\cdot 0.99\cdot\left(t_{n}+3\right)}\right),

the first inequality holds since the inequality
-\ln\left(1-u\right)\geq u
implies

\left(\ln\left(1-u\right)\right)^{2}\geq u^{2}
, and
\frac{1}{1-u}\leq\frac{1}{0.99}
for all
u\in\left[0,0.01\right]
.
The second inequality holds since
for fixed
n
,
u^{t_{n}-1}
,
u^{t_{n}}
and
u^{t_{n}+1}
are all monotonically increasing in
u
, and hence attain their maximum at
u=0.01
.
Using Stirling’s approximation for the Beta function, when
n\geq 594
, let
\theta\triangleq 0.444\leq\frac{t_{n}}{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B\!\left(n+1,t_{n}\right)}\leq 0.32\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot e^{\left(\left(1+\theta\right)\cdot\ln\left(1+\theta\right)-\theta\cdot\ln\theta\right)\cdot n}\leq 0.32\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot e^{n}

Moreover,
(0.01)^{t_{n}-1}\leq(0.01)^{\theta\cdot n-1}=100\cdot e^{-\theta\cdot n\cdot\ln 100}\leq 100\cdot e^{-2\cdot n}
.
Combining the two bounds, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{h\left(q^{*}\right)}{\left(\ln q^{*}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 32\cdot\sqrt{n}\cdot e^{-n}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{t_{n}+1}+\frac{0.01}{2\cdot\left(t_{n}+2\right)}+\frac{(0.01)^{2}}{3\cdot 0.99\cdot\left(t_{n}+3\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 32\cdot\sqrt{594}\cdot e^{-594}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{271+1}+\frac{0.01}{2\cdot\left(271+2\right)}+\frac{(0.01)^{2}}{3\cdot 0.99\cdot\left(271+3\right)}\right)\approx 3.08\cdot 10^{-258},

the second inequality holds since

grows linearly in
n
, the fuctions
{1}/(t_{n}+1)+{0.01}/(2\cdot\left(t_{n}+2\right))+{(0.01)^{2}}/(3\cdot 0.99\cdot\left(t_{n}+3\right))
and

are both decreasing in

, and hence attain their maximum at

and

.

Since
1-p_{+}(q^{*})\geq 0.5

.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\sf RevVCG}_{s:n+k_{n}}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(n+k_{n}\right)\cdot\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(q\right)\cdot\eta_{s:n+k_{n}-1}\left(q\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(n+k_{n}\right)\cdot R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\right)~,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\sf RevOPT}_{m_{n}:n}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\cdot\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\min\left\{q,\frac{1}{r}\right\}\right)\cdot\eta_{m_{n}:n-1}\left(q\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}q

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\cdot R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\min\left\{\frac{m_{n}}{n},\frac{1}{r}\right\}\right)~,

where
R_{(\lambda,r)}
is the induced revenue curve of distribution
F_{(\lambda,r)}
, and both equalities (a) and (b) hold due to the concentration of the order statistics (
\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\eta_{s:n+k_{n}-1}}\!\left[{q}\right]=s/(n+k_{n})
and
\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\eta_{m_{n}:n-1}}\!\left[{q}\right]=m_{n}/n
) and the Lipschitz continuity (implied by the
\lambda
-regularity of
F_{(\lambda,r)}
) of the revenue curve
R_{(\lambda,r)}(\cdot)
and
R_{(\lambda,r)}(\min\{\cdot,1/r\})
at
s/(n+k_{n})
and
m_{n}/n
, respectively.

Below, we consider two cases depending on the value of the supply-to-demand ratio
\alpha
separately.

Case (i) [Supply-to-demand ratio
\alpha\in[(1-\lambda)^{1/\lambda},1]
]:

In this case, we further consider three subcases depending on the value of truncation
r
(which is also the monopoly reserve by construction) in distribution
F_{(\lambda,r)}
.

Case (i.a) [Truncation
r\in[1,n/m_{n}]
]:
In this subcase, the revenue curve
R_{(\lambda,r)}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\right)=r\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\min\left\{\frac{m_{n}}{n},\frac{1}{r}\right\}\right)=r\cdot\frac{m_{n}}{n}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{{\sf RevVCG}_{s:n+k_{n}}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}{{\sf RevOPT}_{m_{n}:n}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{\left(n+k_{n}\right)\cdot r\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}}{n\cdot r\cdot\frac{m_{n}}{n}}=\frac{s}{m_{n}}

Let
s/m_{n}\geq\Gamma
, then
s\geq\lceil\Gamma\cdot m_{n}\rceil=\lceil\Gamma\cdot\lceil\alpha\cdot n\rceil\rceil
. In this case, condition
8
holds for any
k_{n}\geq 0
, in particular for
k_{n}=0
.

Case (i.b) [Truncation
r\in(n/m_{n},(n+k_{n})/s]
]:
In this subcase, the revenue curve
R_{(\lambda,r)}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\right)=r\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\min\left\{\frac{m_{n}}{n},\frac{1}{r}\right\}\right)=1

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{{\sf RevVCG}_{s:n+k_{n}}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}{{\sf RevOPT}_{m_{n}:n}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{\left(n+k_{n}\right)\cdot r\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}}{n\cdot 1}=\frac{s}{n}\cdot r>\frac{s}{m_{n}}

As in the analysis of

Case (i.a)
, when
s\geq\lceil\Gamma\cdot m_{n}\rceil=\lceil\Gamma\cdot\lceil\alpha\cdot n\rceil\rceil
, condition
8
holds for any
k_{n}\geq 0
, in particular for
k_{n}=0
.

Case (i.c) [Truncation
r\in((n+k_{n})/s,(1-\lambda)^{-1/\lambda}]
]:
In this subcase, the revenue curve
R_{(\lambda,r)}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\right)=\frac{r}{r^{\lambda}-1}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{n+k_{n}}{s}\right)^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{n+k_{n}}{s}\right)^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}

and

\displaystyle R_{(\lambda,r)}\left(\min\left\{\frac{m_{n}}{n},\frac{1}{r}\right\}\right)=1

where the inequality holds since
r/(r^{\lambda}-1)
is decreasing in
r\in((n+k_{n})/s,(1-\lambda)^{-1/\lambda}]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{{\sf RevVCG}_{s:n+k_{n}}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}{{\sf RevOPT}_{m_{n}:n}\left(F_{(\lambda,r)}\right)}\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{(n+k_{n})\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{n+k_{n}}{s}\right)^{\lambda}-1\right)\cdot\frac{s}{n+k_{n}}}{n\cdot 1}\geq\Gamma

where the last inequality holds when
s=\lceil\Gamma\cdot\lceil\alpha\cdot n\rceil\rceil
due to the value of
k_{n}
provided in the theorem statement.

Case (ii) [Supply-to-demand ratio
\alpha\in(0,(1-\lambda)^{1/\lambda})
]:

In this case, since truncation
r\leq(1-\lambda)^{-1/\lambda}
by construction (
Definition

4.1
), it guarantees that
r\in[1,n/m_{n}]
and thus the same argument as Case (i.a) applies.

Combining all the pieces together, we complete the proof of
Theorem

5.2
as desired.
∎
