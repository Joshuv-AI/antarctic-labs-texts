Quantitative analysis of airflow velocity distribution and average wind speed line localization in irregular mine roadways
Authors: Yingdi Yang, Yu Niu, Zhihao Li, Jiahao Wang, Xinsheng Huang, Chao Sun, Changxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate measurement of airflow velocity in mine roadways is critical for underground ventilation management, hazardous substance (methane, dust) migration control, and sensor optimization placement. However, turbulent flow dominance and cross-sectional irregularities (e.g., deformation, wall roughness) lead to complex spatiotemporal variations in velocity fields, posing great challenges to precise velocity estimation. To address this issue, this study systematically investigates airflow velocity distribution characteristics in irregular mine roadways using a combination of numerical simulation and field testing. First, Fluent software was employed to establish 3D models of semi-circular arch and trapezoidal roadways with different semi-circular convex radii (0–500 mm), cross-sectional dimensions, inlet velocities (0.5–8 m/s), and support types. Velocity distribution patterns in fully developed turbulent regions (120 m from the inlet) were analyzed, and nonlinear fitting was used to clarify the logarithmic relationship between point wind speed and distance from the roadway wall. The position of the average wind speed line was quantitatively determined: approximately 0.11 times the roadway width/height from the walls for regular cross-sections, and 0.1 times for irregular cross-sections. Field tests were conducted in Taoyuan Coal Mine, using a hot-wire anemometer (STA2) for point measurement and the traverse method for verification. Results show that the relative error between numerical simulation and field measurement is within 5%, confirming the reliability of the simulation model. This research provides a scientific basis for optimizing sensor placement and improving the accuracy of airflow velocity measurement in irregular mine roadways, contributing to the enhancement of underground ventilation safety and intelligent management.Keywords: Irregular mine roadways, Airflow velocity distribution, Numerical simulation, Field test, Average wind speed lineSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The distribution of airflow velocity in underground roadways serves as the foundation for calculating the migration patterns of methane gas, fire-related gases, and dust, as well as for analyzing temperature, humidity distribution, and heat and mass transfer processes1–3. It is of great significance for the placement of airflow velocity sensors and the development of anemometers4. Accurate measurement of mine ventilation parameters is a fundamental task in underground ventilation management5. However, due to the predominance of turbulent flow in coal mine ventilation, the velocity and pressure fields exhibit highly complex spatial and temporal variations, making precise measurement a challenging endeavor6. Currently, airflow velocity measurement primarily relies on two methods: manual measurement and real-time monitoring using airflow velocity sensors. Manual measurement is labor-intensive and prone to errors, whereas sensor-based monitoring, despite its stability, often reflects only local rather than average airflow velocity due to improper sensor placement7. Moreover, the pulsating characteristics of turbulence make single-point measurements inadequate for accurately representing the overall velocity distribution8.

In recent years, research on airflow velocity measurement in coal mine roadways has made considerable progress. Li et al.9 designed and implemented a system for measuring line-averaged velocity across roadway cross-sections based on the ultrasonic time-difference principle. Zhou et al.10 investigated correction factors for real-time air velocity monitoring in underground mines, while Belle B11 emphasized the critical role of air velocity monitoring in mining health and safety management. Zou et al.12 explored the influence of wall roughness on flow characteristics in channels, Zhang et al.13 conducted numerical simulations to assess ventilation efficiency in rough-wall models during roadway construction, and Milici14 investigated near-wall turbulence in rough channels filled with granular materials. Liu et al.15 analyzed airflow velocity distribution in symmetrical rectangular roadways using laser Doppler velocimetry, and Luo et al.16 investigated the influence of different support methods on velocity distribution. Jia17 examined the effects of inlet velocity, roof roughness, and roadway height on velocity distribution, revealing a significant impact of wall roughness on airflow characteristics. Additionally, Hu18 theoretically defined roadway wall roughness and constructed a three-center arch roadway model.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) techniques have been widely applied in studies on airflow velocity distribution in underground roadways. Heerden and Sullivan19 were among the first to introduce CFD into mine ventilation studies, and Moloney and Lowndes20 validated CFD simulation results against experimental data. Zhou et al.21 employed CFD to analyze the impact of obstacles on underground airflow. Ding22 conducted numerical analysis of the average wind speed distribution characteristics in trapezoidal-section roadways based on a 3D numerical model. Kazakova and Shalimov23 studied the stability of mine ventilation systems under natural ventilation conditions, while Wang et al.24 developed a computational simulation tool for automated coal mine ventilation adjustment, validating its feasibility. Wacław Dziurzyński et al.25 proposed a modeling approach for evaluating the sensitivity of aerodynamic resistance and air density variations to major airflow directions in mine ventilation networks. Uchino and Inoue26 developed a CFD model for auxiliary ventilation, Suglo and Frimpong27 evaluated the efficiency of auxiliary ventilation systems using empirical methods, and Toraño et al.28 and Wala et al.29 validated CFD models of roadway and longwall ventilation through numerical simulations and experimental data.

Despite progress in CFD modeling and ventilation monitoring, accurate estimation of average airflow in mine roadways remains challenging. Deformations and wall roughness create strong velocity gradients, making conventional sensor placement unreliable30–32. This study aims to systematically investigate the airflow velocity distribution characteristics under the combined effects of cross-sectional irregularities and different ventilation conditions. Specifically, Fluent software is employed to establish numerical models of roadways with different convex radii, cross-sectional dimensions, inlet velocities, and support types, focusing on the velocity distribution patterns in fully developed turbulent regions. Field tests at Taoyuan Coal Mine are carried out to validate the reliability of the numerical simulation results, ensuring the accuracy of the proposed average wind speed line position. The findings are expected to overcome the limitations of traditional single-point measurement methods caused by improper sensor placement and turbulent pulsation, thereby contributing to the intelligent and precise development of mine ventilation monitoring systems.

Materials & methods

Establishment of the irregular roadway model

Mathematical model development

This study employs Fluent software to perform numerical simulations of airflow distribution within the roadway. During normal underground ventilation in coal mines, airflow velocity typically does not exceed 8 m/s. Thus, air can be approximated as an incompressible fluid according to the Mach number33. The Mach number is defined as the ratio of flow velocity to the speed of sound in the medium. Under typical underground mine environmental conditions, for an airflow velocity of 8 m/s, the corresponding Mach number ≈ 0.023. It is widely accepted in engineering practice that when the Mach number is less than 0.3, compressibility effects are negligible, and the fluid can be accurately modeled as incompressible without introducing significant error.

Although mine air contains trace amounts of toxic gases, dust, and moisture, their concentrations are generally low under normal ventilation conditions. Therefore, the airflow is considered homogeneous and incompressible in this study. While moisture may induce slight density variations, turbulent mixing in actively ventilated roadways (typically > 0.5 m/s) effectively suppresses significant thermal or hygral stratification33,34. Hence, the influence of humidity layering is deemed negligible for the purpose of analyzing geometric effects on velocity distribution. Similarly, the impact of interlayer viscosity and three-dimensional turbulence structures is simplified to focus on the primary hydrodynamic behavior, and airflow is treated as one-dimensional in the numerical model.

Numerical model development

This study primarily employs numerical simulation to investigate the airflow distribution characteristics in roadways with irregular cross-sections. In practical underground roadways, the surfaces exhibit a certain degree of irregularity.

To facilitate the analysis of the impact of surface irregularities on the cross-sectional velocity distribution, the irregularities were quantified and idealized. Different semi-circular convex structures with radii of 0 mm, 100 mm, 200 mm, 300 mm, 400 mm, and 500 mm were selected to represent the irregularities of the roadway walls. These convex structures were positioned in regions where turbulence was fully developed. The distribution of roadway cross-sections with different convex structure radii is shown in Figs. 1 ~ 2.

To ensure the accuracy of velocity gradient calculation near convex structures and roadway walls, local mesh refinement was implemented on the surfaces of convex bodies and near-wall regions with large velocity gradients.

As indicated in Table 2, the maximum fully developed turbulence length in the roadway models is 115 m. Therefore, velocity distribution analyses were conducted at a cross-section located 120 m from the roadway entrance, ensuring that turbulence was fully developed at this location.

For numerical computations, standard turbulence equations were used to model turbulent flow. Gravitational effects were considered by default in the present numerical simulations. Under these conditions, the airflow distribution characteristics across the irregular cross-sections of the roadway in the fully developed turbulence region were analyzed.

Field Test Method

To verify the average wind speed line position and the wind speed distribution patterns obtained from the numerical simulations for irregular semi-circular arch and trapezoidal roadway cross-sections, the Taoyuan Coal Mine roadway was selected as the field test location for this study. The roadway shape is an irregular semi-circular arch and trapezoidal shape, with measurements taken under different wind speed conditions.

A hot-wire anemometer (STA2) was used for point measurement of wind speed. It is equipped with a hot wire and a standard thermistor, capable of recording and displaying both maximum and minimum readings. The large LCD dual-function display allows simultaneous reading of both wind speed and temperature. The technical specifications are shown in Table 3.

Results & analysis

Wind speed distribution in roadway cross-sections

Semi-circular arch roadway cross-section

1) Regular Cross-Section: Wind Speed Distribution under Different Inlet Wind Speeds

Taking a semi-circular arch roadway with a waist width of 5 m and a midline height of 4.5 m as an example, the velocity contour plots under different inlet wind speeds are presented in Fig. 5. The results indicate that in a regular cross-section, the velocity contours exhibit an annular distribution, closely resembling the roadway cross-section shape. The wind speed increases progressively from the roadway walls toward the center, reaching its maximum at the center. The contour lines near the roadway walls are densely packed, indicating a steep velocity gradient, whereas those near the center are more widely spaced, suggesting a lower velocity gradient and a gradual stabilization of airflow velocity.

Regular cross-section wind speed distribution under different inlet wind speeds.

2) Irregular Cross-Section: Wind Speed Distribution with a Constant Protrusion Radius under Different Inlet Wind Speeds.

For a semi-circular arch roadway with a waist width of 5 m, a midline height of 4.5 m, and a protrusion radius of 300 mm, the velocity contour plots under different inlet wind speeds are presented in Fig. 6. Due to space constraints, only the computational cloud diagram at 3 m/s is displayed. The results show that deformation in the roadway cross-section leads to significant wind speed variations near the deformed regions, whereas the airflow in the regular regions remains largely unchanged. Similar to the regular cross-section, velocity gradients are higher near the roadway walls and lower towards the center, where the velocity stabilizes.

Irregular cross-section wind velocity distribution of convex body with 300 mm radius.

As the inlet wind speed increases, the thickness of the boundary layer near the walls decreases. For a constant protrusion radius, regardless of the inlet wind speed, the overall wind speed distribution remains relatively smooth, with a distinct stratification pattern. The airflow exhibits a concentric ring distribution from the roadway walls toward the center, with zero velocity near the walls and increasing velocity toward the center. At fully developed turbulence sections, the presence of protrusions does not significantly alter the velocity distribution curves but does affect the velocity gradient.

3) Irregular Cross-Section: Wind Speed Distribution with Different Protrusion Radii at a Constant Inlet Wind Speed.

Taking a semi-circular arch roadway with a waist width of 5 m, a midline height of 4.5 m, and an inlet wind speed of 3 m/s as an example, the velocity contour plots for different protrusion radii are presented in Fig. 7. Due to space constraints, only the computational cloud diagram for a protrusion radius of 500 mm is shown. The results indicate that velocity contours expand outward from the roadway center toward the walls. Similar to previous observations, the contour lines are denser near the walls, indicating a steeper velocity gradient, while they become sparser toward the center, suggesting a lower velocity gradient and a stabilization of central airflow velocity.

Irregular cross-section wind velocity distribution of with different protrusion radii at 3 m/s inlet velocity.

Under the same inlet wind speed, as the protrusion size increases, higher velocity values appear on the protrusion surface. In the regular regions, wind speed distribution remains relatively smooth with a distinct stratification pattern, following a concentric ring distribution from the roadway walls toward the center. The fully developed turbulence section demonstrates that as the protrusion size increases, velocity distribution is affected, with an increase in velocity at the protrusion surface.

Analysis of wind speed distribution along the central axis

This section primarily analyzes the functional relationship between point wind speed along the central axis of the roadway and the distance from the roadway wall. Based on the cross-sectional wind speed distribution obtained in the previous section, a key technique is to determine the relationship between point wind speed along the central axis and the distance from the roadway wall. Using Origin software, nonlinear fitting is performed between point velocity and the distance to the roadway wall. Considering the selected roadway type in the simulation scheme, a cross-section located 120 m from the roadway entrance, where turbulence is fully developed, is chosen. The central axis of the roadway serves as the data monitoring line.

Regular cross-section of the roadway

For a regular cross-section, where no deformation occurs, a semi-circular arched roadway with a width of 5 m and a midline height of 4.5 m is taken as an example. The wind speed distribution along the central axis of the roadway cross-section is shown in Fig. 11.

Discussion

The present study provides new insights into airflow behavior in irregular mine roadways by combining numerical simulation with field validation. Compared with previous studies that primarily focused on regular or idealized roadway geometries15,16,22, our results highlight the additional complexity introduced by roadway deformation and convex wall structures. The logarithmic relationship between wind speed and wall distance observed in both regular and deformed sections confirms earlier findings on velocity distribution in symmetrical roadways, while also extending them to irregular geometries where the mean-velocity line shifts from approximately 0.11B or H to ~ 0.10B or H.

Furthermore, the finding that convex deformation increases local velocity gradients but does not fundamentally alter the overall flow pattern complements CFD investigations on rough-walled roadways18–20. Our work bridges the gap between idealized simulation studies and practical field conditions by systematically validating the numerical results against in-situ measurements. The demonstrated agreement, with errors consistently within 5%, confirms the robustness of the fixed-point method for estimating average airflow velocity in deformed roadways, an aspect rarely verified in previous research.

From an engineering perspective, these results provide practical guidance for optimizing the placement of airflow sensors in irregular sections of coal mine roadways. The identification of a stable mean-velocity line location under varying geometrical and inflow conditions ensures that monitoring strategies can be standardized even in roadways subject to deformation. This contributes to more reliable airflow monitoring, enhanced ventilation control, and ultimately safer underground working environments.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. The numerical simulations adopt appropriate simplifications for auxiliary facilities and boundary conditions, which may differ from the complex real-world underground environment with time-varying ventilation, dynamic equipment operation, and uneven wall roughness. In practical roadway monitoring, the velocity field is also subject to real-time disturbances from personnel flow, roadway support, material accumulation, and the passage of mine trucks, which may cause the previously calibrated average wind speed line position to deviate.

Field tests in this study were conducted under typical roadway sections and stable, undisturbed ventilation conditions; therefore, broader verification under more mine types, severe deformation conditions, and unsteady flow states is still required to further improve the generalizability of the conclusions. In addition, the proposed positioning method for the average wind speed line is applicable to conventional fully developed turbulent flow, and its long-term adaptability under dynamic on-site disturbances needs to be further studied in the future.

Moreover, the logarithmic relationship between wind speed and air volume is established under steady flow and typical deformation conditions. How to establish reasonable correction strategies for severely distorted velocity fields and dynamically changing underground working conditions will be an important focus of our future research to further improve the accuracy of dynamic air volume calculation.

In conclusion, while the present work establishes a foundational understanding of airflow behavior in irregular mine roadways and validates a practical approach for average wind speed determination, ongoing efforts will emphasize enhanced model realism, broader experimental validation across diverse geological and operational settings, and the translation of theoretical insights into robust engineering solutions for safer and more efficient underground operations.

Conclusions

In response to the challenge of accurate average wind speed measurement in coal mine roadways with deformation-prone cross-sectional geometries, this study systematically investigates airflow distribution characteristics in irregular semi-circular arch and trapezoidal roadways through a combined theoretical, numerical, and experimental approach. By integrating CFD simulations with field validation, the following conclusions are drawn:

1) Numerical simulations reveal that convex deformations—common in aging or stressed mine roadways—significantly distort local airflow fields. The magnitude of disturbance increases with the size of the deformation, leading to localized acceleration of airflow near the protruding zone. In contrast, in the regular (undeformed) wall regions, velocity distribution remains relatively stable, with isovelocity contours aligning parallel to the boundaries. This indicates that the flow structure in non-deformed zones retains predictable behavior despite global geometric irregularity.

2) A logarithmic relationship between wind speed and distance from the roadway boundary is established based on boundary layer theory and simulation data. In uniform cross-sections, the average wind speed line is located at approximately 0.11 times the local width or height from the wall. When deformation occurs, this position shifts slightly inward and stabilizes at ~ 0.1 times the effective dimension from the undistorted wall. This consistent pattern provides a geometry-adaptive rule for identifying representative measurement points in practical mine environments.

3) Field measurements were conducted in both regular and deformed roadways to validate the proposed fixed-point measurement method. Measurement locations were determined using on-site geometric surveys and the derived 0.1-rule. Comparative analysis against the standard multi-point line method shows that the proposed approach achieves an average error of less than 5%, confirming its accuracy and reliability for engineering applications.
