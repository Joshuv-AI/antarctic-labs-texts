Evaluating astigmatid mites as supplemental food for Macrolophus pygmaeus : contrasting responses between commercial and wild populations
Authors: María del Carmen Reche, Carolina Graválos, Amador Rodríguez‐Gómez, Virginia Balanza, Ana Belén Abelaira, Pablo Bielza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEarly establishment of Macrolophus pygmaeus in crops depends on the availability of supplemental food. However, Ephestia kuehniella eggs are costly, and although Artemia franciscana cysts are much cheaper, they may show lower and more variable nutritional quality. We therefore evaluated seven astigmatid mite species as factitious diets for two populations of M. pygmaeus (commercial and wild).RESULTSSurvival, development time, adult weight, longevity, fecundity, and demographic parameters were assessed and compared with the standard E. kuehniella diet. As expected, E. kuehniella resulted in the highest biological performance in both populations. Nevertheless, several mite species supported moderate development. Acarus siro and Carpoglyphus lactis emerged as the most promising alternatives, showing relatively high survival, acceptable developmental rates, and less pronounced reductions in fecundity. The wild population showed greater tolerance to suboptimal diets, particularly when fed Lepidoglyphus destructor, suggesting population‐specific nutritional responses likely linked to genetic variation. Life table analyses supported these findings.CONCLUSIONAlthough none of the mite species matched the nutritional quality of E. kuehniella, some represent viable and economically competitive supplemental foods capable of promoting M. pygmaeus establishment in the absence of prey. These results highlight the potential to optimize alternative diets and to develop predator strains better adapted to cost‐effective food sources. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Since the 1990s, the generalist predatory bug Macrolophus pygmaeus (Rambur) (Hemiptera: Miridae) has been produced commercially for use in integrated pest management programs. 1 Its widespread adoption is due to its high effectiveness as a biological control agent in greenhouse‐grown horticultural crops, particularly tomato, eggplant, and pepper. 2 , 3 , 4 This predator is capable of regulating a wide variety of pest species, including whiteflies such as Bemisia tabaci (Gennadius) and Trialeurodes vaporiorum (Westwood) (Hemiptera: Aleyrodidae), aphids such as Myzus persicae (Sulzer) (Hemiptera: Aphididae), lepidopterans such as Tuta absoluta (Meyrick) (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae), and thrips such as Frankliniella occidentalis (Pergande) (Thysanoptera: Thripidae). 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

In addition to its predatory activity, M. pygmaeus exhibits zoophytophagous feeding habits, allowing it to consume plant tissue, nectar, and pollen. 9 This dietary flexibility enables the predator to persist in the crop even under conditions of prey scarcity or complete absence. However, the lack of animal‐based food leads to reduced survival, slower development, and decreased fecundity. 9 , 10

Because releases of M. pygmaeus are typically carried out preventively, before pests are present, it is common to provide supplemental food sources to promote its establishment in the crop. 4 Among the most widely used artificial food sources are sterilized Ephestia kuehniella (Zeller) (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) eggs and Artemia franciscana (Kellog) (Anostraca: Artemiidae) cysts, both of which are considered highly suitable for generalist predatory bugs. 11 , 12 However, the current price of E. kuehniella eggs strongly limits their practical use in the field, 13 where they are generally restricted to nurseries or localized release points rather than full‐field applications. Artemia cysts are much cheaper and therefore more compatible with full‐field supplementation, but they can exhibit considerable variability in their suitability for rearing M. pygmaeus, depending on their source. 14

Other research lines have explored the use of pollen as a food supplement, a low‐cost alternative capable of maintaining predator populations. However, although pollen supports the survival of M. pygmaeus, the resulting individuals generally exhibit reduced biological quality, which diminishes their effectiveness as biological control agents. 13 Similarly, several artificial diets have been developed that, despite the advantage of being more affordable, are commonly associated with a pronounced decline in both the biological quality and performance of the predator. 15 , 16

Astigmatid mites (mites associated with stored products) represent another potential supplemental food source that can be produced continuously throughout the year within existing biocontrol rearing systems and supplied as living prey. These features may provide practical advantages for crop supplementation. However, to date, no studies have evaluated their suitability as a supplemental food source for M. pygmaeus, nor are they typically considered in recent reviews on alternative diets for this predator. 17 In contrast, several species of these mites have been studied as a food source for members of the genus Orius, with variable results, including studies reporting good biological parameters in predators reared on them. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the effect of various astigmatid mite species as a food source for two populations of M. pygmaeus. To this end, assays were conducted to assess survival, development time, fecundity, longevity, and adult body size to determine whether these mites could serve as a suboptimal yet viable diet for field supplementation of M. pygmaeus.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Insects

Two populations of M. pygmaeus were used. One was a commercial population supplied by Agrobio (Almeria, Spain) and the other was a wild population collected on wild plants in Cabrils (Barcelona, Spain). We included both a commercial and a wild population because inter‐population variation in feeding responses has been documented in other predatory hemipterans. 22 Moreover, commercial colonies maintained for many generations on optimized rearing diets may differ from wild populations in their nutritional responses due to the loss of heterozygosity and reduced overall genetic variation. 23 The populations were reared in the laboratory by using 1‐L plastic containers with filter paper on the lid, with ad libitum access to E. kuehniella eggs as food, green bean pods (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) as moisture source and egg‐laying substrate, and pieces of paper as hideout to avoid cannibalism. The population was maintained under controlled conditions at 26 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity (RH), and a 16 h light:8 h dark light regime. The populations were reproduced in the laboratory for at least two to four generations before the experiments.

Factitious foods

Eight factitious foods supplied by the company Agrobio (Almeria, Spain) were tested: frozen eggs of E. kuehniella and seven species of astigmatid mites, supplied alive and comprising a mixture of all develop stages (egg, larvae, nymph, and adults). The species of astigmatid mites were Aleuroglyphus ovatus (Troupeau), Acarus siro (L.), Thyreophagus entomophagus (Laboulbene), Tyrolichus casei (Oudemans) (Acari: Acaridae), Suidasia medanensis (Oudemans) (Acari: Suidasiidae), Carpoglyphus lactis (Linnaeus) (Acari: Carpoglyphidae), and Lepidoglyphus destructor (Schrank) (Acari: Glycyphagidae). The different diets were tested in all trials described below.

All mite species, at all developmental stages, were received fresh on a weekly basis from Agrobio (Spain). The astigmatid mites were maintained on a standard diet consisting of wheat bran supplemented with yeast, which is the storage medium used by the company to ensure their survival during transport and to provide a suitable substrate and food source. On arrival, the mites were handled according to the supplier's recommendations to preserve their quality; specifically, containers were kept horizontal and maintained at 14–18 °C, protected from direct sunlight until use. Only freshly received mites were used in the experiments, ensuring consistent and optimal conditions throughout the study.

Nymphal survival and developmental trial

Green bean pods were introduced in the rearing containers for the females to lay eggs. After 24 h, pods were removed and held until hatching. Once eggs hatched, first‐instar nymphs (<24 h old) were collected and used to set up the replicates: for each diet, four replicates were established, each replicate being a single 1‐L plastic container with a filter‐paper lid containing 75 first‐instar nymphs (300 nymphs per diet in total). Each replicate container received one green bean pod (as a water source) and the assigned diet. Maintenance was performed twice weekly by replacing the bean pod and adding fresh food of the corresponding diet. On adult emergence, survivors per diet were recorded daily; adults were sexed and weighed using a microbalance (Sartorius M‐pact, Gottingen, Germany, d = 0.001 mg). The experiment was conducted under standard laboratory conditions (26 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% RH, photoperiod 16 h light:8 h dark).

Female fertility trial

Green bean pods were introduced into the rearing containers to allow females to oviposit. After 24 h, the green bean pods were collected and transferred to plastic containers (1 L) with a filter paper‐covered lid. The individuals were reared on E. kuehniella eggs and green beans, and the containers were checked twice a week, replacing the bean pod and adding fresh food of the corresponding diet. This diet was used to standardize the nutritional background of the individuals before the adult assays. Once the nymphs reached adulthood, they were allowed to mate for 5 days. After mating, adults were sexed and females were isolated to assess fertility when fed on different astigmatid mite species or E. kuehniella eggs. Forty females per treatment were placed individually in polypropylene cups (200 mL) covered with paper secured with a rubber band, supplied with green bean pods as oviposition and hydration substrate, and the corresponding diet. Every 2–3 days, the bean pod bearing eggs was removed and replaced with a fresh pod and the corresponding diet until the female died. Macrolophus pygmaeus eggs cannot be easily observed with a stereomicroscope, so fertility was assessed by counting first‐instar nymphs. At each pod change for each female, the removed pod was deposited in an individual 300‐mL plastic cup with a small amount of E. kuehniella eggs, and first‐instar nymphs emerging from that pod were counted 10 days later.

The first fertility measurement was discarded for all diets to reduce the influence of the initial feeding with E. kuehniella eggs during the 5‐day period prior to mating. The trial was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions of 26 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% RH, and a photoperiod of 16 h light:8 h dark.

Data analysis

The two M. pygmaeus populations were assayed in independent runs on different dates, as the extensive set of diets included in the study demanded the management of a large number of individuals per experimental trial. As a result, no direct comparisons were made between populations. For each population, data on survival, body size, longevity, and fertility were analyzed using a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with diet as the main factor. Prior to analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were verified. Survival data (percentages) were arcsine square‐root transformed to meet ANOVA assumptions, whereas body size, longevity, and fertility required no transformation. When significant differences were detected, means were compared using Tukey's honestly significant difference test. Because the results of the biological parameters varied considerably among the different diets and between the two populations, determining which mite species was most suitable for use as supplemental food in crops required a more integrative approach. Therefore, we generated life tables following the age‐stage analysis, two‐sex model proposed by Chi and Liu 24 and Chi, 25 using TWOSEX‐MSChart software. 26 This analysis enabled the calculation of key demographic parameters, including the intrinsic rate of increase (r), the finite rate of increase (λ), the net reproductive rate (R₀), and the mean generation time (T). Standard errors were estimated using the bootstrap method with 100 000 resamplings. 27

RESULTS

Survival experiment during nymphal development

For the Agrobio population, survival (Table 1) was significantly affected by diet (F = 9.92; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05). The highest survival rates were obtained with E. kuehniella eggs (65.8%) and C. lactis (60.5%), followed by T. entomophagus, S. medanensis, and A. siro (55.5–57.7%). The diets that most reduced survival were T. casei, L. destructor, and A. ovatus (40.9–47.4%). In the Cabrils population, although significant differences in survival were also detected (F = 4.17; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05), the ranking of diets differed from that of the Agrobio population. The optimal factitious diet based on E. kuehniella eggs resulted in the highest survival (65.3%), as expected, but T. casei and L. destructor achieved similar values (around 60%). The lowest survival was recorded with S. medanensis (46.8%), while A. siro produced intermediate results (56%).

Development time (Table 1) was also influenced by diet. In the Agrobio population (F = 28.75; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05), the shortest development time was observed with E. kuehniella eggs (13.9 days), followed by A. siro (15 days). The longest development times were recorded with A. ovatus, T. casei, and L. destructor (17–18 days), while the remaining diets produced intermediate values (around 16 days). Similarly, in the Cabrils population (F = 34.67; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05), E. kuehniella eggs again supported the shortest development time (15.7 days), followed by T. entomophagus, C. lactis, and A. siro (approximately 17 days). The longest development time was recorded with S. medanensis (20 days). Overall, the Cabrils population exhibited slower development than the commercial Agrobio population.

Female body weight (Table 1) in the Agrobio population was also influenced by diet (F = 13.85; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05). The heaviest females were produced on E. kuehniella eggs (0.90 mg), whereas the lightest were those fed T. casei mites (0.51 mg). The remaining diets produced females around 0.6 mg, with the heaviest among them in the L. destructor and A. siro treatments (0.66 and 0.62 mg, respectively). In the Cabrils population, diet also affected female weight (F = 23.17; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05). The trend was consistent with that of the commercial colony, with females reared on E. kuehniella eggs being the heaviest (1.03 mg) and those reared on T. casei being the lightest (0.66 mg). The other diets resulted in intermediate values, with the heaviest among them in the L. destructor, A. siro, and C. lactis treatments (0.79, 0.74, and 0.73 mg, respectively).

Male body weight (Table 1) in the Agrobio population was affected by diet (F = 27.35; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05). The heaviest males were those fed on E. kuehniella eggs, while the lightest were reared on T. casei. Intermediate values were observed for T. entomophagus, C. lactis, A. siro, and S. medanensis. In the Cabrils population, male body weight was also significantly affected by diet (F = 9.52; df = 7, 24; P < 0.05). Males reared on E. kuehniella eggs (0.64 mg) were significantly heavier than those from the other treatments (0.47–0.52 mg). Adult body weight in the Cabrils population was generally higher than in the Agrobio population, which may be related to the longer development time observed.

Longevity and fertility parameters

Female longevity in the Agrobio population was significantly influenced by diet (F = 9.90; df = 7, 261; P < 0.05) (Table 2). Females fed on E. kuehniella eggs lived the longest (18.9 days), followed by those on C. lactis (16.0 days). The shortest‐lived females were those fed on T. casei (10.2 days), while the remaining mite diets resulted in intermediate longevity (≈12–13 days). In the Cabrils population, diet also affected female longevity (F = 2.35; df = 7, 280; P < 0.05). In contrast, all mite diets resulted in significantly longer‐lived females (≈19–22 days), indicating that the nutritional response of the Cabrils population differed markedly from that of the commercial population.

Female fertility in the Agrobio population was strongly affected by diet (F = 39.87; df = 7, 312; P < 0.05) (Table 2). Females fed on E. kuehniella eggs produced the highest number of first‐stage nymphs (N1) (36.6 N1/female), while all mite diets drastically reduced fertility. Among them, C. lactis supported the greatest number of offspring (9.5 N1), followed by A. siro and L. destructor (6.6 and 5.0 N1, respectively), with the remaining diets producing <4 N1 per female. Fertility was also diet‐dependent in the Cabrils population (F = 19.86; df = 7, 312; P < 0.05). Although E. kuehniella eggs supported relatively high fertility (23.5 N1), L. destructor yielded an even higher reproductive output (31.6 N1). All other mite diets significantly reduced fertility, with A. siro showing the best performance among them (10.0 N1). Overall, fertility was more negatively impacted by mite diets in the commercial Agrobio population than in the wild Cabrils population, highlighting clear population‐specific differences in nutritional suitability.

Due to the unexpected results observed in the fertility of the Cabrils population when fed the L. destructor diet, we repeated the trial using E. kuehniella eggs and L. destructor for both populations. Fertility (Table 3) was significantly affected by diet in the Agrobio population (F = 20.21; df = 1/98; P < 0.05), whereas no significant effect was observed in the Cabrils population (F = 0.00; df = 1/78; P > 0.05). As shown, fertility in the Agrobio population was slightly higher than in the first trial, but remained considerably greater when individuals were fed on E. kuehniella eggs compared to L. destructor mites. In contrast, in the Cabrils population, the fertility achieved with L. destructor was very similar to that observed with E. kuehniella eggs, although it did not surpass it in this trial.

Population growth

Because the results of the biological parameters varied considerably among the different diets and between the two populations, determining which mite species was most suitable for use as supplemental food in crops required a more integrative approach. The intrinsic rate of increase (r), finite rate of increase (λ), net reproductive rate (R₀), and mean generation time (T) differed significantly among the diet treatments in both populations (Table 4), with individuals fed E. kuehniella eggs exhibiting the highest rates. In the Agrobio population, among the mite diets the T. casei and A. ovatus diets resulted in the lowest reproductive performance, whereas the highest values were obtained with the A. siro diet. In the Cabrils population, although L. destructor achieved reproductive rates comparable to the optimal E. kuehniella diet, it displayed one of the longest mean generation times. Among the prey mites, the next best‐performing diets were A. siro, T. entomophagus, and A. ovatus.

DISCUSSION

To ensure the successful use of M. pygmaeus in augmentative biological control, release strategies must guarantee access to suitable alternative foods during periods of prey scarcity. In this study we evaluated seven astigmatid mite species as factitious diets for two populations of M. pygmaeus and compared them with the standard egg diet of E. kuehniella. As expected, the egg diet consistently yielded the highest survival, fastest development, largest adult size, greatest fecundity, and most favorable life‐table parameters in both populations. Nevertheless, several mite species supported positive population growth. Acarus siro and C. lactis emerged as the most robust options across populations, and L. destructor produced demographic parameters in the Cabrils population that were comparable to those obtained on E. kuehniella eggs. These findings show that astigmatid mites, while nutritionally inferior to the optimal egg diet, have potential as economically viable supplemental foods for M. pygmaeus.

A recurring theme in previous work on zoophytophagous mirids is the trade‐off between diet quality and cost. 17 , 28 Sterilized E. kuehniella eggs remain the benchmark for mass rearing of M. pygmaeus, but their current price strongly constrains routine field supplementation. 13 Artemia cysts partially alleviate this economic constraint but often yield lower‐quality predators and show batch‐to‐batch variation in suitability, 14 although such variation may be less relevant when used as supplemental food under crop conditions. Pollen and artificial diets are cheaper alternatives, but they typically produce smaller, less fecund, and less efficient predators. 13 , 15 , 16 The economic cost of supplemental feeding is therefore central to the practical implementation of biological control programmes. Current information from major biocontrol suppliers indicates that E. kuehniella eggs cost approximately 1500–2000 € kg−1, Artemia cysts about 130 € kg−1, and commercial astigmatid mite preparations about 80 € ha−1 per full‐field application at 10 L ha−1. Under current commercial practice, Ephestia is usually applied locally in nurseries or at release points rather than over the whole crop; at a dose of 10 g per 1000 released predators and a release rate of 1 predator/m2 this represents approximately 150–200 € ha−1. By contrast, full‐field application following the protocol of Brenard et al. 29 (0.04 g per plant; 1.33 plants/m2) would require about 532 g ha−1 per application, corresponding to approximately 69 € ha−1 for Artemia and 798–1064 € ha−1 for Ephestia. Ephestia eggs also deteriorate rapidly after application, which further limits their suitability for full‐field use. Thus, prey mites are not necessarily cheaper than Artemia on a pure per‐application basis, but they remain far less costly than Ephestia under full‐field use and offer additional operational advantages. As living prey they may remain available in the crop for longer than inert foods, and because they are already produced in the same biofactories used for mass rearing predatory mites, they may also be less exposed to supply bottlenecks and quality fluctuations. Overall, astigmatid mites appear to be economically competitive and operationally attractive alternatives.

Astigmatid mites have been repeatedly evaluated as food for Orius spp., and nutritional responses differ markedly among both predator and mite species. 18 , 19 , 21 , 30 A similar pattern emerged for M. pygmaeus. Across diets, survival of nymphs fed on the better‐performing mites was only moderately lower than on E. kuehniella eggs, whereas development time and adult body size showed more pronounced deterioration and fertility was the most sensitive trait. In the Agrobio population, for example, fertility decreased by 75–90% on most mite diets compared with the egg diet, whereas the Cabrils population experienced smaller reductions. Within this generally suboptimal performance, A. siro and C. lactis stood out by combining relatively high survival, moderate development times, and positive intrinsic rates of increase in both populations, while T. casei and A. ovatus frequently produced near‐zero or negative population growth. Life‐table parameters of M. pygmaeus on E. kuehniella eggs in our study were broadly similar to those reported by Mollá et al., 31 although the higher intrinsic rates of increase and shorter generation times we observed suggest that our strains or rearing conditions may have favoured faster population growth.

The contrasting responses of the two M. pygmaeus populations highlight the importance of intraspecific variation in diet suitability. While both populations were able to develop on several astigmatid mites, their performance differed markedly depending on their origin, suggesting that population history or adaptation to rearing conditions may influence the suitability of alternative prey. Rather than reflecting prior exposure to astigmatid mites, this pattern may indicate that the wild population retains a greater capacity to cope with nutritionally suboptimal or variable food sources, whereas the commercial strain, maintained for multiple generations on a high‐quality diet, may be more specialised and thus less efficient at exploiting lower‐quality resources. The Cabrils (wild) population generally tolerated astigmatid diets better than the Agrobio (commercial) strain: fertility and longevity were less severely reduced by mites, and the life‐table analysis revealed that L. destructor provided demographic parameters closely matching those of the egg diet, albeit with a somewhat longer generation time. In the commercial population, by contrast, L. destructor was clearly inferior to E. kuehniella. In addition, the Cabrils population consistently exhibited slower development across diets, including E. kuehniella, which may be associated with its larger body size and reflects intrinsic differences in life‐history traits between populations rather than diet‐specific effects. These results partially support our initial hypothesis that wild populations would show greater capacity to cope with suboptimal diets, while also illustrating that the ranking of alternative foods can depend strongly on predator genotype. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that commercial natural enemy stocks may become genetically depauperate and specialised on the diets used for mass rearing 23 and that considerable intraspecific genetic variation in life‐history traits is available for exploitation in biological control agents. 32 , 33 , 34

Such nutritional variation can be harnessed through genetic improvement programmes. In Orius laevigatus (Fieber), artificial selection has yielded strains with enhanced resistance to several insecticides, 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 larger body size, 39 and better performance on pollen and other suboptimal foods. 21 , 22 Our data suggest that similar approaches could be applied to M. pygmaeus to increase fitness on astigmatid mite diets, focusing on species such as A. siro, C. lactis, and, for certain wild genotypes, L. destructor. Selective breeding could be combined with diet optimisation—such as mixtures of mites with pollen or Artemia cysts—to mitigate the strong negative effects on fecundity observed in the commercial population and tailor diets to specific crop systems. In addition, the omnivorous feeding behaviour of M. pygmaeus should be considered when interpreting these results. Zoophytophagous predators can adjust their diet depending on the availability and quality of plant and animal resources. In our experiments, individuals were maintained on green bean pods, which were used as a water source and oviposition substrate. However, plant species can influence the feeding behaviour and prey choice of omnivorous predators, as plant quality may alter the balance between plant feeding and prey consumption. Previous studies have shown that plant‐mediated effects can modify feeding preferences and the performance of omnivorous mirids. 40 Therefore, the suitability of astigmatid mites as supplemental prey could differ in other cropping systems, particularly in tomato, the main crop where M. pygmaeus is used for biological control.

Beyond their nutritional properties, astigmatid mites offer several practical advantages that are highly relevant for augmentative biocontrol programmes. They can be produced inexpensively on simple substrates (e.g., wheat bran), supplied continuously throughout the year and that already form the basis of rearing systems for phytoseiid predators. 41 In greenhouse crops, these mites could therefore be deployed as operationally attractive supplements during the establishment phase of M. pygmaeus populations or during temporary prey shortages. Under such conditions, the moderate growth rates provided by A. siro or C. lactis might be sufficient, particularly if predators later switch to natural prey such as whiteflies, aphids or lepidopteran eggs. Importantly, some astigmatid species do not support key pests such as western flower thrips, 41 reducing the risk that supplemental foods inadvertently promote pest outbreaks. Furthermore, exploring the use of mixed diets could be a promising approach to optimize predator development while balancing cost. For example, combining prey mites (a suboptimal but inexpensive diet) with a small proportion of E. kuehniella eggs (an optimal but costly diet) or Artemia cysts could enhance growth and performance, as suggested by previous studies. 13 , 42 Additionally, evaluating combinations of different prey, mite species may provide further insights into improving diet quality, availability, and cost‐effectiveness, which could have practical implications for mass‐rearing and biological control programmes.

Our experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions and with all individuals reared on E. kuehniella eggs before their exposure to mite diets in the fertility assays. This design allowed us to disentangle juvenile from adult diet effects but does not capture potential long‐term adaptation to mites or interactions with plant quality and natural prey. Moreover, only two predator populations were tested. Future work should therefore evaluate complete life cycles and multiple generations on mite‐based diets, include a broader set of M. pygmaeus genotypes, and assess predator performance and pest suppression in semi‐field and commercial greenhouse trials where predators can exploit a mixture of mites, natural prey, and plant resources.

In conclusion, this study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of astigmatid mites as supplemental foods for M. pygmaeus and demonstrates that, although none of the tested species matched the performance of E. kuehniella eggs, several—particularly A. siro and C. lactis, and L. destructor for specific wild genotypes—can sustain positive population growth.

When combined with the deliberate use of intraspecific genetic variation and selective breeding, these alternative foods could underpin the development of M. pygmaeus strains tailored to economically viable factitious diets. Such advances would reduce production and implementation costs, and thereby enhance the economic viability and robustness of augmentative biological control programmes in protected horticultural crops.
