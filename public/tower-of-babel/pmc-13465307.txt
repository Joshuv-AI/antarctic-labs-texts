Molecular classification of primary Sjögren’s syndrome based on salivary gland omics
Authors: Wuwei Zhuang, Qi Zhang, Jie Shen, Lin Zhu, Wenyan Shen, Xinyu Pu, Tingting Yan, Qiao Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary Sjögren’s syndrome is a complex and heterogeneous disease, often involving multiple organ systems and carrying an increased risk of lymphoma development. In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk RNA-seq data to deconvolute the disease heterogeneity using non-negative matrix factorization.This analysis identified four distinct molecular subtypes, which we designated as the Classical Immune Type, Ion Channel Type, Fibrotic Senescent Type, and Immune Desert Type. Through differential expression analysis, we further identified characteristic gene sets for each of these subtypes. The Gene Set Variation Analysis scores of these subtype-specific gene signatures in patient minor salivary gland RNA-seq data may potentially inform patient stratification. We preliminarily characterized the clinical features and local immune microenvironment profiles associated with each subtype. These findings may have implications for personalized therapeutic strategies and potentially inform the selection of patients for future clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03843-5.

Background

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by marked hyperactivity of B-cell function [1]. The breakdown of immune tolerance leads to the characteristic destruction of the body’s exocrine glands, particularly the lacrimal and salivary glands, in pSS [2]. The progressive loss of glandular function leads to symptoms of dry mouth and dry eyes in patients. However, the nature of pSS extends well beyond exocrine gland pathology. More than half of patients experience involvement of other organ systems, and nearly 5% may progress to lymphoma, making it a highly heterogeneous systemic disease [3, 4].

Currently, the diagnosis of primary pSS follows the 2016 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR-EULAR) classification criteria (hereinafter referred to as the 2016 criteria) [5]. The criteria comprise five assessment items: anti-SSA/Ro antibody positivity, minor salivary gland biopsy showing focal lymphocytic sialadenitis (FLS), an ocular staining score (OSS) of ≥ 5, a Schirmer’s test result of ≤ 5 mm/5 min, and an unstimulated whole saliva flow rate of ≤ 0.1 mL/min. Among these, an MSG biopsy with an FLS focus score of ≥ 1 and anti-SSA/Ro antibody positivity are designated as major items, each assigned 3 points; the remaining three items are assigned 1 point each. A total score of ≥ 4 points qualifies for the diagnosis of pSS [5].

However, the aforementioned diagnostic criteria fail to cover all patients and carry the risk of misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. For instance, the prevalence of xerostomia is higher in elderly patients and increases significantly with age. This is primarily attributed to an age-related decline in salivary gland function and reduced saliva production [6, 7]. A 2002 study involving minor salivary gland biopsies in healthy individuals and a 2024 meta-analysis indicate that a certain proportion of the general population also exhibits FLS, with some cases e FLS > 1 [8, 9]. According to the 2016 criteria, this subset of patients—despite being seronegative—would be highly susceptible to misdiagnosis as pSS.

Furthermore, although patients who are antiSSA-positive typically exhibit symptoms characteristic of Sjögren’s syndrome—such as elevated EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) scores, rheumatoid factor positivity, ANA positivity, low C4 levels, and hypergammaglobulinemia [10], —a certain proportion of the general population is also antiSSA-positive, with prevalence rates ranging from 0.5% to 2.5% [11, 12]. If such healthy individuals also experience dry mouth and dry eyes, they may be misdiagnosed with pSS. It remains unclear whether antiSSA-positive healthy individuals represent a pre‑clinical stage of the disease or simply carry the antibody without clinical significance.

Therapeutic options for pSS are also limited. Some secretory analogues for saliva and tears merely alleviate symptoms without halting further glandular damage [13]. Furthermore, several biologics effective in diseases of a similar type have failed in clinical trials for pSS. For example, as a B-cell-depleting antibody, rituximab (RTX) was one of the earliest biologics used to treat SS. Two large-scale phase II and III RCTs conducted in France and the UK (the TEARS and TRACTISS studies, respectively) both failed to meet the primary endpoint based on Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores (for fatigue, dryness, and pain), with no significant changes observed in the ESSDAI [14, 15]. Another example is the failed phase II TWINSS trial, in which a CD40 monoclonal antibody did not meet the primary endpoint for treating moderate-to-severe pSS (for some dosage groups, the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 3-point reduction in ESSDAI score from baseline at 24 weeks was not significantly greater than that in the placebo group) [16].

The failure of these clinical trials may be attributed to the following reasons:1.Some patients diagnosed with pSS may already be in the advanced stages, with irreversible glandular dysfunction, which directly impacts improvements in patient-reported outcomes (such as the ESSPRI score) in clinical trials.2.Regarding the selection and assessment of clinical endpoints, although ESSDAI is widely used in pSS clinical trials, this index primarily evaluates systemic disease activity and lacks sensitivity in assessing exocrine gland function. Systemic evaluation metrics are insufficient for capturing subtle changes in glandular function.3.pSS exhibits significant heterogeneity. Different clinical subtypes of pSS involve distinct pathophysiological processes, and key pathogenic factors may vary across subtypes. Therefore, the appropriate therapeutic targets should also differ accordingly. Due to this high degree of heterogeneity, it is difficult for clinical trial cohorts to encompass all pSS subtypes [17, 18].

Therefore, subtyping pSS to guide diagnosis, treatment, and clinical trials is fully warranted. Previous subclassification analyses of pSS patients have either been based on clinical manifestations—for example, Ioannidis et al. classified patients presenting with purpura and low C4 levels at initial visit as high-risk, with a higher probability of developing lymphoma, while the remaining patients were categorized into a low-risk subgroup [19], —or have integrated peripheral blood RNA-seq data, such as Perrine Soret et al., who stratified 304 pSS patients into four distinct subgroups based on peripheral blood RNA-seq, with these subgroups differing in interferon gene enrichment and B‑cell activation levels; flow cytometry further confirmed differences in immune cell frequencies among these subgroups [20].

Alternatively, some approaches have combined both clinical symptoms and RNA‑seq data. For instance, Jessica R. Tarn et al. performed hierarchical clustering based on five common symptoms associated with pSS (pain, fatigue, dryness, anxiety, and depression) and used multinomial logistic regression to identify subgroups within the UK Primary Sjögren’s Syndrome Registry (UKPSSR): Low Symptom Burden (LSB), High Symptom Burden (HSB), Dryness Dominant with Fatigue (DDF), and Pain Dominant with Fatigue (PDF). Significant differences were observed among these subgroups in peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, positivity rates for anti‑SSA and anti‑SSB antibodies, serum IgG and CXCL13 concentrations, as well as in peripheral blood RNA‑seq profiles. The interferon (IFN) module activity score was significantly higher in the LSB and DDF subgroups compared to the HSB and PDF subgroups [21]. Although the TRACTISS trial evaluating rituximab for pSS did not meet its primary endpoint overall [15], a subgroup analysis revealed that at week 48, patients in the DDF subgroup treated with rituximab showed significantly greater improvement in both unstimulated and stimulated salivary flow rates compared to DDF patients receiving placebo [21]. This suggests that, given the substantial heterogeneity among pSS patients, it is feasible to characterize patients using clinical features and omics data, perform subtype classification, and subsequently tailor diagnostic, therapeutic, or clinical trial strategies according to the specific subtype.

In this study, we applied the non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm to the RNA sequencing results of minor salivary glands from pSS patients. We stratified the patients into four distinct subtypes, designated as the Classical Immune Type, Ion Channel Type, Fibrotic Senescent Type, and Immune Desert Type. Through differential analysis, we identified characteristic gene sets for each of these four subtypes of pSS. The Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) scores of these subtype-specific gene signatures in the patients’ labial gland RNA-seq data may potentially inform patient stratification. Our study, through molecular subtyping of patients with pSS, partially explains the reasons for the heterogeneity of these patients at the level of the salivary immune gland microenvironment, which may contribute to precision medicine for pSS.

Methods

Acquisition of single-cell RNA-seq and RNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA-seq data of minor salivary glands from Sjögren’s syndrome patients were obtained from( https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/21bbfaec-6958-46bc-b1cd-1535752f6304), which includes samples from 3 healthy volunteers, 12 patients with dry symptoms who did not meet the diagnostic criteria (sicca controls), 8 patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome, and 2 patients with secondary Sjögren’s syndrome associated with systemic sclerosis and arthritis, respectively [22].

The minor salivary gland RNA-seq dataset GSE171896 originates from a study by Xiaofang Zhu et al. [23]. This dataset comprises 36 patients with interstitial lung disease-associated primary Sjögren’s syndrome (ILD-pSS) and 128 patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome without ILD (non-ILD-pSS). All patients fulfilled the 2016 criteria. Minor salivary gland samples were obtained via biopsy before the initiation of any systemic immunosuppressive or glucocorticoid therapy.

The minor salivary gland RNA-seq dataset GSE173808 originates from a study by Gwenny M. Verstappen et al. [24]. This dataset includes 39 patients with pSS and 20 non‑pSS sicca patients. All patients met the 2016 criteria.

Acquisition of senescence-associated gene sets

Aging itself can impair salivary gland function, leading to xerostomia. Age-related structural and functional changes in salivary glands include acinar cell atrophy, fibrosis, reduced vascular supply, and impaired tight junction integrity, all of which contribute to secretory dysfunction [25–27]. With advancing age, the glandular parenchyma is progressively replaced by fibrotic and adipose tissue, leading to reduced saliva secretion and altered salivary composition [28]. These age-related aging changes in the salivary glands themselves may synergistically interact with the salivary gland symptoms caused by pSS.

In healthy salivary glands, tissue-resident salivary gland stem cells (SGSCs) maintain glandular homeostasis through their proliferation and differentiation. Recent studies have focused on the senescent phenotype of SGSCs. The number of these cells correlates with patients’ unstimulated whole saliva flow rate, stimulated whole saliva flow rate, stimulated parotid saliva flow rate, and CD45 + cell infiltration [29, 30]. Therefore, we included senescence‑associated genes as one of the gene sets for molecular subtyping. SenMayo is a senescence‑associated gene set constructed by integrating genes previously reported to be enriched in senescent cells and/or SASP‑secreting cells, provided they were experimentally validated in at least human or mouse cells. The authors screened 1,656 studies, excluded duplicates, case reports, and studies on non‑human/non‑mouse genes, and ultimately selected 15 studies from which 125 genes were identified, constituting the SenMayo gene set [31]. An additional gene set of senescence-associated upregulated genes was obtained from the official Gene Set Enrichment Analysis(GSEA) website(https://www.gseamsigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/collections.jsp). The relevant gene sets are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Acquisition of the ion channel gene set

The primary trigger for salivary secretion is the rise in cytosolic calcium concentration within acinar cells induced by neurotransmitter stimulation. Defects in calcium signaling have been prominently observed in the epithelial cells of patients with pSS [32, 33]. In addition to calcium signaling, ion channels such as chloride channels and aquaporins are downregulated in pSS [34], suggesting that ion channel dysfunction may be a key factor contributing to impaired secretory function in the salivary glands of these patients [35–37].

Through a literature review, we curated a gene dataset related to ion channels, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Acquisition of the interferon-related gene set

The activation of the type I interferon pathway has been markedly observed in both the blood and salivary gland tissues of patients with pSS [38]. Immunohistochemistry has confirmed the presence of type I interferon within the lymphoid foci of pSS patients [39], and the involvement of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which produce type I interferon, has been noted [40]. RNA sequencing of peripheral blood and labial gland tissues indicates a significant upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes [24]. In pSS patients, enhanced type I interferon activity is associated with higher disease activity, the presence of autoantibodies, hypergammaglobulinemia, and lower complement levels. In mouse models of pSS, type I interferon has been shown to directly affect glandular function [41] .These findings strongly underscore the crucial role of type I interferon in the pathogenesis of pSS. Therefore, we included type I interferon-related genes as one of the bases for molecular subtyping. The type I interferon signature gene set was obtained from the GSEA website(https://www.gseamsigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/collections.jsp). The relevant genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Acquisition of gene sets related to high endothelial venules and tertiary lymphoid structures

High Endothelial Venules (HEV) are a specialized type of venous endothelial cells. The surface of these high endothelial cells abundantly expresses a class of adhesion molecules known as vascular addressins, most typically Peripheral Node Addressin (PNAd) and Mucosal Addressin Cell Adhesion Molecule-1 (MAdCAM-1). These addressins specifically recognize homing receptors on the surface of lymphocytes, such as L-selectin, mediating the recruitment of immune cells to sites of inflammation and ultimately contributing to the formation of lymphoid structures [42] .Experiments have demonstrated that glandular endothelial cells in patients with pSS exhibit HEV characteristics, a phenomenon not observed in control groups [43] .Based on a literature review, we obtained characteristic genes associated with HEV [44] and incorporated them as a basis for molecular subtyping. The specific genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

In the affected glands of some patients with pSS, infiltrating lymphocytes can organize into tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) [45]. TLS are ectopic lymphoid tissues capable of driving antigen-specific immune responses at sites of chronic inflammation. Unlike secondary lymphoid organs such as lymph nodes, TLS lack a capsule and possess distinct characteristics and functions. These structures are frequently observed in tissues from cancers and autoimmune diseases [46, 47] TLS disrupt the normal architecture of salivary glands and exacerbate glandular damage by promoting the activation of autoreactive immune cells [48, 49]. The persistent antigenic stimulation within these structures may also increase the risk of lymphoma development [50] .Through a literature review, we acquired a set of genes associated with TLS formation [51] and included them as one of the bases for molecular subtyping. The specific genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Acquisition of the anti-SSA signature gene set

Positivity for anti-SSA/Ro antibody is a major diagnostic criterion in the 2016 criteria [5, 52]. However, anti-SSA/Ro antibodies are positive in only approximately 70% of patients with pSS [53, 54]. Patients who are positive for anti-SSA antibodies (with or without concomitant anti-SSB antibodies) exhibit a higher prevalence of key pSS-related clinical manifestations and greater disease [55]. In contrast, patients negative for both anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies possess a distinct immunophenotype and clinical presentation [56–58], and are defined as having seronegative Sjögren’s syndrome [52].These seronegative patients may represent a unique subtype within the disease spectrum [52].Therefore, to characterize this distinction, we performed a re-analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing data from minor salivary glands of antiSSA-positive and antiSSA-negative patients, as published by Thomas J.F. Pranzatelli et al. [22]. Using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with parameters set to min.pct = 0.25 and logfc.threshold = 0.5, we identified upregulated signature genes specific to antiSSA-positive patients. This gene set was subsequently included as one of the bases for molecular subtyping. The relevant genes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Single-cell sequencing analysis of minor salivary glands

We analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing data from labial salivary glands of patients with Sjögren’s syndrome and controls using the Seurat R package (v4.2.0) [59]. A uniform quality control standard was applied to all samples: cells expressing fewer than 500 genes or with a mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10% were excluded, and genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were also removed. The SCTransform function in Seurat was used to integrate single-cell data from different samples to remove batch effects, and 3000 highly variable genes were selected for downstream analysis. For the integrated single-cell expression matrix, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed for linear dimensionality reduction. The FindClusters function (resolution = 0.5) was employed to identify distinct cell clusters. Subsequently, we applied two nonlinear dimensionality reduction techniques, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), for the two-dimensional visualization of all cell types [60].

Following the annotation of all cell types, the FindAllMarkers function (parameters set as: min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.5) was used to identify the top 30 highly expressed genes for each cell type as cell-type-specific marker genes. This set of marker genes served as one basis for molecular subtyping. A higher enrichment score for a specific cell-type gene set indicates an increased abundance of that particular cell type.

GSVA enrichment analysis and correlation analysis

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) is another single-sample gene set enrichment method, which is a nonparametric approach based on kernel density estimation [61]. For each sample, it assesses the deviation of the expression distribution within a specific gene set from the overall background gene expression distribution [61]. Compared to ssGSEA, its enrichment scores allow for a more sensitive detection of inter-sample variation. Therefore, We applied GSVA to the GSE171896 dataset using the aforementioned gene sets, resulting in an enrichment score matrix for each gene signature type. As this matrix contained negative values, we performed a matrix translation to remove them. Specifically, the minimum value in the GSVA matrix was calculated, and this value was subtracted from each element, thereby generating a new GSVA matrix with a minimum value of 0 (i.e., Y = X - min(X)).

Then, we performed GSVA on the validation dataset GSE173808 using the subtype-specific gene signatures and other characteristic gene sets. We employed Pearson correlation analysis to examine the relationships between the GSVA enrichment scores of the subtype-specific gene signatures and the clinical variables in the GSE173808 dataset.

Identification of four distinct molecular subtypes in primary Sjögren’s syndrome via non-negative matrix factorization based on GSVA results

NMF is a mathematical method for analyzing high-dimensional non-negative data. It achieves dimensionality reduction, feature extraction, and pattern recognition by decomposing the original data matrix into the product of two or more non-negative matrices. Initially developed for applications in image processing, such as face recognition [62], it has been increasingly applied in recent years for dimensionality reduction and clustering of high-dimensional biological data [63, 64].

This analysis was implemented using the “NMF” R package [65]. NMF aims to identify latent features within gene expression profiles by decomposing the original matrix into two non-negative matrices. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of the cophenetic coefficient, dispersion coefficient, and silhouette coefficient, the optimal rank (k) was determined to be 5(corresponding to the point preceding the maximum drop in the cophenetic correlation coefficient as k varied), and the number of iterations was set to 200. Consequently, patients with pSS were successfully stratified into four distinct molecular subtypes (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A-B).

Differential gene expression analysis of primary Sjögren’s syndrome subtypes and establishment of subtype-specific gene signatures

In the GSE171896 dataset, we performed differential gene expression analysis across the four distinct pSS subtypes using the limma package. Genes were considered differentially expressed and selected based on the following thresholds: an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂(fold change) ≥ 1.5. This analysis yielded four subtype-specific gene signatures. These signatures hold potential for guiding clinical decision-making and patient stratification in future clinical trials for Sjögren’s syndrome (Supplementary Table 3) [66].

Immunoffuorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on samples from patients with Sjögren’s syndrome and non-SS sicca patients. Details of the antibodies and instruments used for immunofluorescence staining are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

When assessing whether the differences in ILD incidence among different pSS molecular subtypes are statistically significant, we employed both Fisher’s exact test and the chi-squared test due to the small sample size of GSE173808, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [67].

Results

Single-cell sequencing analysis of minor salivary glands in Sjögren’s syndrome patients identifies molecular signatures of distinct cell types

We performed a batch-corrected integration of single-cell RNA sequencing data from minor salivary glands, encompassing samples from 3 healthy volunteers, 12 patients with sicca symptoms not meeting formal diagnostic criteria (sicca controls), 8 patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome, and 2 patients with secondary Sjögren’s syndrome (1 associated with systemic sclerosis and 1 with arthritis). The following quality control criteria were applied: cells expressing fewer than 500 genes or with a mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10% were excluded, and genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were removed. After quality control, a total of 71,399 cells were retained for downstream analysis. Using the FindClusters function in Seurat (resolution = 0.5), we identified 19 distinct cell clusters. The FindAllMarkers function was employed to obtain characteristic genes for each cluster, with the top 30 highly expressed genes defined as the cell-type-specific gene signature (see Supplementary Table 2).

As shown in Fig. 1A-B and Supplementary Fig. 1A-C, different cell types were annotated based on previously reported salivary gland markers [37, 68–70]. Proportional changes in cell types among antiSSA-negative Sjögren's syndrome patients

Compared to the classical SSA-positive Sjögren’s syndrome patients, the SSA-negative group represents an even more heterogeneous population. Their primary diagnosis often relies more heavily on the FLS. A subset of these patients may harbor other specific autoantibodies [71], while others, such as those positive for anti-centromere antibodies, may progress to other autoimmune diseases like systemic sclerosis [72].

Single-cell sequencing revealed differences among SSA-positive patients, SSA-negative patients, and controls at the cellular composition level. As shown in Fig. 2A-D, compared to the control group, both SSA-positive and SSA-negative patients exhibited a significant reduction in salivary epithelial cells, with the loss being more pronounced in the SSA-negative group. In contrast, the proportion of ductal cells was increased in SSA-negative patients. Furthermore, SSA-negative patients showed a decreased proportion of infiltrating immune cells—such as GZMK + T cells, total T cells, B cells, and plasma cells—relative to SSA-positive patients. Conversely, the proportions of stromal cells, including FAP+fibroblasts, total fibroblasts, ACKR1 + endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, were increased in SSA-negative patients. This indicates that the local immune microenvironment within the glands is fundamentally distinct between the two patient types.

Identification of molecular subtypes in primary Sjögren’s syndrome patients

As shown in Fig. 3A-C, NMF successfully identified four distinct molecular subtypes of pSS. The subtype containing only two RNA-seq samples (type 3) was excluded from the final classification. The remaining subtypes were defined as follows: We designated Type 1 as the “Classical Immune Type.” This subtype is characterized by significant activation of the type I interferon pathway, prominent immune cell infiltration, and substantial glandular destruction. This is reflected by increased enrichment scores for immune cells such as GZMK + T cells and B cells. Concurrently, decreased enrichment scores are observed for salivary acinar cells (serous, mucous, and seromucous) as well as for both CFTR+ ductal cells and general ductal epithelial cells. The enrichment of senescence- and fibroblast-related gene sets indicates the presence of aging and fibrotic pathology within the glands of this subtype.

We designated Type 2 as the “Ion Channel Type.” This subtype is characterized by reduced expression of ion channel proteins, yet it exhibits relatively mild glandular damage, suggesting that salivary gland impairment may still be in a reversible state. Concurrently, this subtype shows moderate enrichment for the anti-SSA gene signature, type‑I interferon‑related genes, and T‑/B‑cell immune gene sets, while demonstrating the highest degree of plasma cell infiltration within the glands.

Type 4 was designated as the “Fibrotic Senescent Type.” This subtype is characterized by mild immune infiltration but pronounced senescence and fibrosis. Salivary epithelial cells are markedly reduced, whereas ductal epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells are relatively increased. Both immune and senescence factors likely contribute to the pathogenesis in this subtype.

Type 5 was designated as the “Immune Desert Type.” This subtype is characterized by the absence of immune-mediated damage to acinar and ductal cells, along with minimal infiltration of both immune cells and stromal components such as fibroblasts.

As shown in Fig. 3D, analysis of Hallmark gene sets revealed significant differences in signaling pathway activation across the four molecular subtypes of pSS.In the Classical Immune Type, pathways including the interferon-alpha response, interferon-gamma response, apoptosis, complement signaling, IL-2/STAT5 signaling, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling were markedly activated. This pattern indicates a robust immune and inflammatory response within the glands.Previous clinical trials targeting TNF with monoclonal antibodies or TNF receptor fusion proteins, which used improvement in VAS scores for systemic dryness, pain, and fatigue as the primary endpoint, yielded negative results and failed to meet therapeutic expectations [73, 74]. This discrepancy may be because patients with the Classical Immune Type, characterized by prominent TNF activation, constitute only approximately 25% of the total pSS population. Furthermore, in this subtype, the upregulation of complement signaling-related genes coincides with significant infiltration of GZMK + T cells. As highlighted in a recent study, GZMK can activate the complement system [75], suggesting that this mechanism may be a key contributor to glandular damage in pSS. Consequently, complement inhibitors might represent a potential therapeutic strategy to halt the progressive glandular injury in these patients.

In contrast, the Ion Channel Type showed significant enrichment for pathways involved in oxidative phosphorylation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, and DNA repair. The enrichment of these pathways suggests the presence of DNA damage in this subtype, and whether this is closely linked to lymphomagenesis warrants further investigation.Furthermore, this subtype exhibited marked enrichment in various metabolism-related pathways, including glycolysis, cholesterol synthesis, the proteasome pathway, adipogenesis, fatty acid synthesis, and mTORC1 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1) signaling. This pattern indicates that patients with this subtype may have fatty deposition in their salivary glands. Previous imaging and histopathological studies have noted the presence of fatty deposits in the salivary glands of patients with pSS, and the extent of this fatty infiltration has been closely correlated with reduced salivary flow in these patients [76–80].

In patients with the Fibrotic Senescent Type, significant enrichment was observed in pathways related to coagulation, hypoxia and angiogenesis, epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT), and TGF‑β signaling, indicating active fibrotic processes within the salivary glands of this subtype. Drugs aimed at mitigating or reversing fibrosis might therefore offer therapeutic potential for addressing glandular fibrosis in these patients.

Finally, differential gene expression analysis performed across the four subtypes enabled the successful construction of subtype-specific gene signatures for pSS, detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

This further supports the notion that aging is likely a key pathological factor in this subtype.

As shown in Fig. 4A and E, the GSVA enrichment scores of the Immune Desert Type gene signature showed significant negative correlations with age, the proportion of CD45 + cells, and the FLS score, but no correlation with ESSDAI.

In the validation dataset GSE173808, we performed GSVA using the pSS subtype-specific gene signatures, the antiSSA gene signature, the senescence-specific gene set, the type I interferon-related gene set, the tertiary lymphoid structure-related gene set, the high endothelial venule-related gene set, the ion channel-related gene set, and marker gene sets for various cell types derived from single-cell sequencing data.

Unsupervised clustering based on the resulting GSVA enrichment scores clearly separated the patients into four distinct clusters (Fig. 4F). Cluster 1 (C1) resembled the Classical Immune Type, C2 resembled the Ion Channel Type, and C3 resembled the Fibrotic Senescent Type. Cluster 4 (C4), which included a substantial number of sicca patients who did not meet the 2016 criteria, resembled the Immune Desert Type.

As shown in Fig. 4F, both Clusters C1 and C2 were characterized by a high frequency of autoantibody positivity, activation of type I interferon signaling, a significant reduction in glandular cells, and decreased expression of ion channel proteins. These clusters were associated with higher disease severity (elevated ESSDAI scores) and pronounced immune infiltration, warranting consideration for immunosuppressive therapy.

Cluster C3 exhibited prominent senescence features, low levels of immune infiltration and inflammatory response, an increased proportion of stromal cells (e.g., fibroblasts, endothelial cells), and more severe glandular fibrosis. This cluster also had a higher proportion of patients seronegative for anti-SSA antibodies (Fig. 4F). For patients in this subtype without systemic or major organ involvement (e.g., interstitial lung disease, hypergammaglobulinemia), immunosuppressive drugs may have limited efficacy. In contrast, regenerative approaches such as multipotent stem cell-derived salivary gland organoids may offer potential for improving salivary gland function.

A subset of patients within Cluster C4 did not fulfill the 2016 criteria. Their salivary gland transcriptomic profiles were similar to healthy controls, showing preserved salivary epithelial cells, normal ion channel expression, and no aberrant immune cell infiltration. Some patients in this cluster might be misdiagnosed with Sjögren’s syndrome based on incidental, clinically insignificant FLS (even if anti-SSA negative) or based on incidental anti-SSA positivity combined with isolated sicca symptoms. Furthermore, some patients in C4 may represent a very early disease stage prior to significant glandular involvement.

Discussion

pSS represents a heterogeneous disease spectrum encompassing diverse clinical manifestations. We performed a preliminary molecular classification of patients with pSS by integrating single-cell sequencing and RNA-seq data. We have tentatively identified four potentially distinct molecular subtypes of pSS.

The Classical Immune Type is characterized by significant activation of the type I interferon pathway, positivity for autoantibodies, and strong expression of genes associated with tertiary lymphoid structures. This subtype also exhibits marked immune cell infiltration, including GZMK + T cells and B cells. Salivary gland cells (serous acinar, mucous acinar, and mixed acinar cells) and ductal cells show significant damage. In the validation dataset GSE173808,The GSVA scores of the subtype-specific gene set demonstrate a significant positive correlation with ESSDAI scores, the proportion of CD45 + cells in the glands, and the FLS score. Patients belonging to this subtype present with more severe disease, higher disease activity, and greater systemic involvement. Early diagnosis and immunosuppressive therapy may provide substantial clinical benefit for these individuals [81].

Among these, the Ion Channel Type may involve a unique pathogenic pathway. This subtype is characterized by reduced expression of ion channel proteins and infiltration of plasma cells, though the overall degree of immune infiltration is lower than in the Classical Immune Type.

For the Fibrotic Senescent Type, the GSVA scores of its characteristic gene set show a significant correlation with age, indicating that aging factors contribute to disease progression in this subtype. While immune involvement is relatively weak, glandular damage and fibrosis are severe. Current treatments for salivary gland hypofunction primarily focus on symptom management, such as saliva substitutes, sialogogues, and medications aimed at alleviating symptoms rather than addressing the underlying glandular damage and fibrosis [82]. However, organoid-based technologies have emerged as a promising alternative. Recent research highlights strategies for modeling salivary gland dysfunction and restoring normal function, suggesting this approach as a potential therapeutic avenue for patients with the Fibrotic Senescent Type of pSS [83]. Upon reviewing the literature, we found that two retrospective clinical studies have identified advanced age as a predictor of the onset of ILD and a risk factor for its progression [84].Compared to pSS-no-ILD patients, those with pSS-ILD were older at diagnosis (55 ± 13 vs. 70 ± 7, p = 0.001) and had higher ESSDAI scores (3.9 ± 4.7 vs. 12.3 ± 4.3, p = 0.001) [85].Interestingly, we found that patients in the Fibrotic Senescent Type exhibited a strong correlation between their gene set GSVA scores and age (see Fig. 4A/D). Furthermore, although not statistically significant (see Supplementary Table 4/5),the incidence of ILD in this group was the highest among the four clinical subtypes, at approximately 32% (compared to 19% in the Classical Immune Type, 21% in the Ion Channel Type and 11% in the Immune Desert Type).

The Immune Desert Type is characterized by the absence of immune-mediated damage to acinar and ductal cells, along with minimal immune infiltration within the glands. The GSVA scores for its characteristic gene set show a significant negative correlation with age, the proportion of CD45 + cells in the glands, and the FLS score, but no correlation with the ESSDAI score. This suggests that patients of this type may represent atypical pSS cases or be in the early stages of the disease. Further research is needed to investigate the prognosis of these patients and their potential transitions to or relationships with the other three subtypes.

As mentioned earlier, existing studies on subtype classification of pSS patients are mostly based on clinical manifestations and peripheral blood RNA-seq data. For example, Jianbin Li et al. used unsupervised K-means clustering analysis to classify Sjögren’s syndrome patients into the multi-system inflammatory subtype and the glandular-limited high immunoglobulin subtype based on differences in clinical manifestations [86]. Sara S. McCoy et al. performed a hierarchical clustering analysis of Sjögren’s syndrome patients based on relatively subjective questionnaires assessing the degree of dryness, fatigue, and pain. They identified four symptom-based subtypes of Sjögren’s syndrome, which include the high-symptom burden (HSB), pain dominant with fatigue (PDF), dryness dominant with fatigue (DDF), and low symptom burden (LSB) subtypes [87]. Subsequently, a re-analysis of the JOQUER trial—a randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 120 pSS patients randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either HCQ (400 mg once daily) or a placebo from baseline to 24 weeks—was conducted based on the above findings. The results suggested that in the PDF and DDF subgroups, the reduction in the interferon enrichment score based on peripheral blood RNA-seq was statistically significant [88, 89]. Soret et al. utilized clinical and peripheral blood RNA-seq data from the PRECISESADS European pSS cohort to identify four distinct disease subtypes, which differed in terms of IFN signatures, B-cell activity, and clinical features [20]. Furthermore, research indicates that different patient subgroups exhibit varying responsiveness to drugs [90].

Although the above findings hold significant scientific value for understanding the pathogenesis and heterogeneity of different pSS patients, subgrouping based on clinical symptoms struggles to fully elucidate the heterogeneity of pSS patients. Meanwhile, peripheral blood RNA-seq data are influenced by numerous factors, such as aging and viral infections. Furthermore, scores based on patients’ ratings of dryness, fatigue, and pain are relatively subjective.

To date, no studies have classified patients based on the salivary gland immune microenvironment in a clinical setting or used such classification for enrollment in clinical trials. In contrast to the aforementioned research, this study integrates multi-omics data to qualitatively and quantitatively demonstrate the differences in the labial salivary gland immune microenvironment among different pSS patients. It utilizes the NMF algorithm to identify distinct salivary gland immune microenvironments, thereby performing molecular subtyping of pSS patients. This classification objectively assesses the patient’s salivary gland immune microenvironment, is subject to fewer subjective and external confounding factors, and may provide reference value for evaluating patients’ disease status from multiple perspectives and for informing treatment decisions.

This study has certain limitations:1,It is based on public sequencing databases containing only minor salivary gland RNA-seq data and partial clinical information, resulting in incomplete patient profiles. 2,The database scale is relatively small, necessitating validation of these findings in larger, multi-center cohorts. The proposed classification requires further confirmation through additional research. Integrating more comprehensive clinical data to explore the core pathogenic mechanisms of each subtype will be helpful for guiding future clinical diagnosis and treatment.3,In the Methods section, we performed both ssGSEA and GSVA analyses on the original GSE171896 matrix using predefined gene sets, and the resulting scores were used for NMF analysis. (The chosen K value was the point preceding the maximum change in the cophenetic coefficient as K varied [91].) We found that the NMF analysis of the ssGSEA matrix yielded only two disease subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 4), whereas the NMF analysis of the GSVA matrix identified five subtypes (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2), which better highlighted differences in salivary gland gene expression among patients with Sjögren’s syndrome. However, we removed one subtype (type 3) because it contained only two samples; although this might be attributable to sequencing technology bias, it remains an important limitation of this study.4, During our initial NMF analysis of the GSE171896 dataset, we did not consider whether ILD might affect salivary gland gene expression.5, Our preliminary findings on the molecular subtypes of Sjögren’s syndrome and their potential therapeutic approaches are speculative and require confirmation through large-scale clinical studies and experimental validation.6, Although we have identified four distinct molecular subtypes of pSS in two cohorts simultaneously, the biological validity or stability of these four subtypes themselves requires further research and confirmation.
