Algebraic cycles of some Fano varieties with Hodge structure of level one
Pedro Montero, Iván Rosas-Soto

Abstract
. We study Chow groups and étale motivic cohomology groups of smooth complete intersections with Hodge structures of level one, classified by Deligne and Rapoport, with particular attention to fivefolds. We extend these results to an étale motivic context and recover an analogous finite-dimensionality in the sense of Kimura. We further analyse algebraic cycles on other smooth Fano manifolds with Hodge structures of level one and, as an application, we prove the integral Hodge conjecture for smooth quartic double fivefolds by means of the étale motivic approach.

1.
Introduction

Chow groups provide important information about the algebro-geometric structure of a variety, which can be used to address different problems in algebraic geometry, such as rationality questions, enumerative problems, and the behaviour of such properties in families, among others.

One of the open questions about algebraic cycles in a complex smooth projective variety
X
is the so-called
Hodge conjecture
, which relates the algebro-geometric information of the variety with its analytic counterpart. We refer the interested reader to the survey book
[
Lew99
]
for a comprehensive introduction to the subject. Originally, the version of the conjecture with integral coefficients was stated as follows: for all smooth projective varieties
X
over
\mathbb{C}
and for all
0\leq i\leq\operatorname{dim}(X)
, the cycle class map

c^{i}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\to\operatorname{H}^{2i}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z})

surjects onto the integral Hodge classes
\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. In this form, Atiyah and Hirzebruch
[
AH62
]
provided a counterexample in 1962, consisting of a torsion class in
H^{4}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, for a Godeaux–Serre variety
X
of dimension
7
, which is not algebraic. The situation does not improve if one considers only the torsion-free part of Hodge classes of a smooth projective variety: Kollár, in
[
BCC92
]
, produced the so-called
Trento examples
, obtained by considering very general hypersurfaces of dimension
4
and degree
125
, where the generator class of
\operatorname{H}^{4}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is not algebraic.

Despite this negative evidence for the integral version of the Hodge conjecture, its rational counterpart remains open to this day: for all smooth projective varieties
X
over
\mathbb{C}
and for all
0\leq i\leq\operatorname{dim}(X)
the cycle class map

c_{\mathbb{Q}}^{i}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Q})

is surjective. There exists a generalised version, concerning Hodge structures of different weights, which may be stated as follows: let
X
be a smooth projective variety, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
H\subset\operatorname{H}^{k}(X,\mathbb{Q})
be a sub-Hodge structure of coniveau
\geq c
(see also §
2
and
[
Lew99
, Lecture 7]
). Then there exists a closed algebraic subset
Y\subset X
of codimension
c
such that

H\subset\ker\big(j^{*}:\operatorname{H}_{B}^{k}(X,\mathbb{Q})\to\operatorname{H}_{B}^{k}(X\setminus Y,\mathbb{Q})\big).

As we have mentioned, algebraic cycles and topological information also play a role in the study of rationality (or birational) problems. The first birational invariant of smooth projective varieties to recall is the Artin–Mumford invariant, given by
\operatorname{H}^{3}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\operatorname{tors}}
, which encodes the topological information of the Brauer group of
X
, in other words, it provides information about the Severi-Brauer varieties of
X
. Another invariant of topological nature is the obstruction to the integral Hodge conjecture in codimension
2
, i.e.
(\operatorname{Hdg}^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z})/\operatorname{Im}(c^{2}))_{\operatorname{tors}}
. Similarly, for curve classes, one has that
\operatorname{Hdg}^{2n-2}(X,\mathbb{Z})/\operatorname{Im}(c^{n-1})
is a birational invariant of smooth projective varieties. All of these invariants are related to another broad family of birational invariants, the so-called
unramified cohomology groups
introduced by Colliot-Thélène and Ojanguren in
[
CO89
]
, which have been successfully used to measure the failure of the Hodge conjecture for degree four integral classes
[
CV12
]
and to produce new counterexamples to rationality questions
[
CP16
]
by means of the decomposition of the diagonal (see §
2
). We refer the interested reader to the survey articles
[
Pey19
,
Voi19
]
for an introduction to the subject.

Another important variant is to consider algebraic cycles while changing the base topology in the following sense: we can obtain Chow groups by taking the hypercohomology of Bloch’s complexes. More precisely, for each
j
, the presheaf
z^{i}(-,j)\colon U\mapsto z^{i}(U,j)
associated with Bloch’s complex is a sheaf for all topologies
t\in\left\{\operatorname{flat},\ \textup{\'{e}t},\ \operatorname{Nis},\ \operatorname{Zar}\right\}
(see
[
Gei04
]
), and hence
z^{i}(-,\bullet)
is a complex of sheaves on the small étale, Nisnevich, and Zariski sites of
X
. We define the complex of
t
-sheaves

\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)_{t}:=z^{i}(-,\bullet)_{t}[-2i].

In this case, by taking
t=\operatorname{Zar}
, the hypercohomology of the complex recovers Chow groups:

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\simeq\mathbb{H}^{2i}_{\operatorname{Zar}}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i)).

From the perspective of triangulated categories, we can recover Chow groups by using the category of Nisnevich motives as

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{DM}(k,\mathbb{Z})}(M(X),\mathbf{1}(i)[2i]).

All of these isomorphisms are related to the Zariski or Nisnevich topology. As we have mentioned, we may also proceed by considering the étale topology and obtain an étale version of Chow groups. Depending on the reference, this is called
Lichtenbaum cohomology
or
étale motivic cohomology
, which we denote by
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
. This new approach provides additional tools to study problems about algebraic cycles on smooth projective varieties, in particular the Hodge conjecture. For a smooth projective variety over a field of characteristic zero, we define

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X):=\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z})}(M_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X),\mathbb{Z}(i)[2i])\simeq\mathbb{H}^{2i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i)).

A first important feature (see
[
Kah12
,
CV12
]
) of the étale Chow groups is that they fit into the following commutative diagram

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{nr}}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))}

thus measuring the failure of the integral Hodge conjecture in degree
4
.

More generally, one of the results that illustrate the relation between algebraic cycles and étale motivic cohomology was given by Rosenschon and Srinivas in
[
RS16
]
, concerning the Hodge and Tate conjectures. In brief, for a smooth projective variety
X
over
\mathbb{C}
, and for fixed
i\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a cycle class map
c_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}:\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\to\operatorname{H}_{B}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, whose image is contained in
\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore, we can formulate an étale analogue of the Hodge conjecture, namely: to ask whether the map
c_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}:\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\to\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is surjective. The first point to note is that, in contrast with the usual cycle class map, on torsion classes we have a surjection

c_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}\Big|_{\operatorname{tors}}:\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\operatorname{tors}}\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\operatorname{tors}}.

This follows from the isomorphism between étale motivic cohomology groups with finite coefficients and étale cohomology, together with the comparison between étale and Betti cohomology with finite coefficients, see
[
Mil80
]
. Consequently, we obtain the equivalence

c^{i}_{\mathbb{Q}}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Q})\iff c_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}:\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

This relation can be extended to a more general setting by considering Chow motives and étale motives with different coefficients. To this end, let us recall that if
G
is an abelian group, there is a well-known result stating that if
G\otimes\mathbb{Q}=0
and
G\otimes\mathbb{Z}/p=0
for all prime numbers
p
, then
G=0
. In a slightly more general form, thanks to the universal coefficient theorem, for a complex smooth projective variety
X
we have that if
\operatorname{H}^{n}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Q})=0
and
\operatorname{H}^{n}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}/p)=0
for all prime numbers
p
, then
\operatorname{H}^{n}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
.

In our motivic context, a similar principle still holds, concerning a family of conservative functors between triangulated categories of motives with different coefficients. Let us consider the category of étale motives over
k
(for simplicity we assume
k=\bar{k}
), denoted
\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z})
, together with the functors associated with the change of coefficients

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z})}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Q})}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z}/\ell)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\mathbb{Q}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\ell}}

This forms a conservative family of functors (see
[
CD16
]
), i.e. a morphism of étale motives
f:M\to N\in\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism if and only if, for all prime numbers
\ell
, the morphism
f_{\ell}:M/\ell\to N/\ell\in\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z}/\ell)
and
f_{\mathbb{Q}}:M_{\mathbb{Q}}\to N_{\mathbb{Q}}\in\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Q})
are isomorphisms.

A priori, this result does not appear to simplify the characterisation or decomposition of integral étale motives. However, the work of Cisinski and Déglise on étale motives provides a useful perspective by using the following equivalences:

•

According to
[
CD16
]
, the category of motives with finite coefficients is equivalent to the derived category of étale sheaves. This yields the equivalences

\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z}/\ell)\simeq D(k_{\textup{\'{e}t}},\mathbb{Z}/\ell)\simeq(\mathbb{F}_{\ell}\text{-vect. spaces})^{\mathbb{Z}}.

We are not aware of such an explicit description for other motivic theories with finite coefficients.

•

There is an equivalence of categories between étale and Nisnevich motives when working with
\mathbb{Q}
-coefficients, again by
[
CD16
]
:

\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Q})\simeq\operatorname{DM}(k,\mathbb{Q}).

With this idea in mind, it is natural to ask whether studying problems concerning algebraic cycles from an étale point of view may yield new refinements, tools, or techniques that could improve our understanding. These results may also suggest that it is easier to characterise étale Chow groups and to decompose integral étale motives than to achieve the corresponding goals with integral Chow motives.

In connection with rationality problems, one of the major breakthroughs was obtained by Clemens and Griffiths
[
CG72
]
, who proved that a smooth cubic threefold is unirational but not rational, relying heavily on the Hodge structure of these varieties. More precisely, for such a threefold
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
, the Hodge decomposition of its middle cohomology,
\operatorname{H}^{3}(X,\mathbb{C})=V\oplus\overline{V}
, splits into two conjugate spaces. Consequently, as in the case of algebraic curves, one can associate to
X
an
intermediate Jacobian
, which is a principally polarised abelian variety of dimension
5
in this case. The proof of Clemens and Griffiths proceeds by showing that this abelian variety is not the Jacobian of a curve. More generally, such a decomposition of the middle cohomology gives rise to a weight-one Hodge structure, which can be associated with a principally polarised abelian variety related to homologically trivial algebraic cycles, just as for an algebraic curve
C
, where the Jacobian coincides with the Albanese variety, the latter being dual to the group
\operatorname{Pic}^{\circ}(C)
of degree-zero
0
-cycles.

On the other hand, from the perspective of
0
-cycles, an important class of algebraic varieties is given by Fano varieties (i.e. smooth projective varieties with ample anticanonical divisor). These form a concrete class of rationally connected varieties, meaning that all of their
0
-cycles are rationally equivalent, and they arise naturally in the birational classification of higher-dimensional algebraic varieties (see, for instance,
[
Mur04
]
for a survey on algebraic cycles on Fano varieties). In regard of these considerations, in this article we focus on Fano varieties with Hodge structures of level one.

From a classification perspective, the simplest Fano varieties arise as hypersurfaces or, more generally, as smooth complete intersections of small degree in projective space. Regarding the classification of varieties of complete intersection with Hodge structures of level one given by Deligne and Rapoport in
[
Del71
]
and
[
Rap72
]
:

•

Fano threefolds given by:

–

Cubic hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
,

–

Complete intersections of a quadric and a cubic in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
, and

–

Quartic hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
,

where the associated intermediate Jacobian is a principally polarised variety of dimension
5
,
20
and
30
, respectively.

•

Intersections of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
. In this case, the intermediate Jacobian is the Jacobian of a hyperelliptic curve of genus
m+1
, see
[
Rei72
]
.

•

Intersections of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+4}
. In this case, the intermediate Jacobian is the Prym variety of a double cover of a plane curve of degree
2m+5
, see
[
Bea77
]
.

•

If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{6}
is a smooth cubic hypersurface, then its intermediate Jacobian is a
21
-dimensional principally polarised abelian variety.

In this article, we push further the study of algebraic cycles on such varieties, which,
grosso modo
, may be described as follows: for fivefolds, their Chow groups are fully characterised by their structure as complete intersections and their intermediate Jacobians. For further details about the classification of algebraic cycles of such varieties, we refer to
[
Rei72
,
Bea77
,
Mur04
]
, and more recently to the work of Fu and Tian
[
FT19
]
, who analysed the case of cubic fivefolds. This latter work provided the starting point for our study of algebraic cycles on Fano fivefolds from the point of view of classification of Fano varieties.

Following the classification of del Pezzo surfaces in the birational geometry of surfaces initiated by the Italian school, Gino Fano obtained classification results in dimension three in the early twentieth century. With the subsequent development of birational geometry in dimension three, Iskovskikh and Mori-Mukai achieved the classification of all smooth Fano threefolds into
108
deformation families, marking one of the first fundamental applications of the Minimal Model Program. Using the theory of adjunction for polarised varieties and techniques related to the moduli of vector bundles on K3 surfaces, Fujita and Mukai extended this classification to Fano varieties of arbitrary dimension with anticanonical divisor sufficiently divisible in the Picard group. The first currently open case is the classification of Fano fourfolds whose anticanonical divisor is not divisible by another ample divisor; we refer to
[
IP99
]
for a general account on the classification problem and properties of Fano varieties. Beyond the complete intersections mentioned earlier, a particularly notable class of Fano varieties in which rationality and Hodge theory have been extensively studied is given by the Gushel-Mukai varieties, which in odd dimensions also carry level one Hodge structures (see, e.g.,
[
DK18
,
DK20
]
).

Another interesting case of Fano varieties with sufficiently divisible anticanonical divisor and level one Hodge structure is given by double covers
X
of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched along a smooth quartic, i.e. smooth
quartic double fivefolds
. In this situation, the rational Hodge conjecture follows from the double covering structure, which allows one to lift rational classes of linear cycles from projective space. From this perspective, one may ask whether such cycles admit an interpretation as linear subspaces inside
X
, in analogy with the classical study of linear subspaces in complete intersections in projective space (see, e.g.,
[
DM98
]
). This approach was first adopted by Tihomirov
[
Tih80
]
, who studied lines in the three-dimensional case, and later extended in the doctoral thesis of Skauli
[
Ska23
]
, who investigated lines in double covers of arbitrary dimension, as well as Griffiths groups and the coniveau filtration in these varieties.

As in the case of complete intersections, one of our main results is the study of Chow groups and étale Chow groups of quartic double fivefolds, through the analysis of linear subspaces (see Appendix
A
), induced rational maps, and Bloch–Ogus type spectral sequences. The principal applications of this analysis are the verification of the integral Hodge conjecture and the étale motivic decomposition for quartic double fivefolds.

The article is structured as follows. In §
2
we introduce Chow groups, providing definitions of different notions of intermediate Jacobians and the decomposition of the diagonal. In §
3
we present an introduction to the notion of étale Chow groups, while §
4
and §
5
are devoted to the description of Chow and étale Chow groups of smooth complete intersections with Hodge structures of level one, with special emphasis on fivefolds. In §
6
we observe that some of these results, particularly those concerning the middle cohomology and its associated intermediate Jacobian, lift to the motivic setting. Finally, in §
7
we study the algebraic cycles of a quartic double fivefold and we obtain the following result:

Theorem

(Theorem
7.15
)
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{P}^{5}
be a smooth double cover branched along a smooth quartic hypersurface

We also include an Appendix, where by means of classical computations using incidence varieties, we obtain a description of certain Fano schemes of linear-like subvarieties on smooth double covers of projective space.

2.
Preliminaries on Chow groups

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over a field
k
of dimension
n
. We denote the Chow group of
X
in codimension
i
by
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
, i.e., the group of closed subvarieties of
X
of codimension
i
modulo rational equivalence. We can grade Chow groups of
X
by dimension of subvarieties, in which case we have
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X):=\operatorname{CH}_{n-i}(X)
.

In the category of smooth projective varieties, Chow groups have nice functorial properties, here we present some of the most common properties used in the article:

(1)

If
f:X\to Y
is any morphism, then there exists a pull-back morphism
f^{*}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(Y)\to\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
.

(2)

If
f:X\to Y
is a proper map, then we obtain a push-forward map
f_{*}:\operatorname{CH}_{k}(X)\to\operatorname{CH}_{k}(Y)
given by
f_{*}(Z)=\deg(Z/f(Z))[f(Z)]
.

(3)

Chow groups are dotated of a monoidal structure
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)\to\operatorname{CH}^{i+j}(X\times X)
. Taking the diagonal embedding
\Delta_{X}:X\hookrightarrow X\times X
we obtain the intersection product for two cycles
Z\in\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
and
Z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)
by

Z\cdot Z^{\prime}=\Delta^{*}_{X}(Z\times Z^{\prime})\in\operatorname{CH}^{i+j}(X).

We remark that the intersection product gives a ring structure to
\operatorname{CH}^{*}(X)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
. If again
f:X\to Y
is any morphism, then
f^{*}
is functorial with respect to intersection product, i.e.,
f^{*}(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})=f^{*}(Z)\cdot f^{*}(Z^{\prime})
.

(4)

For a projective morphism
f:X\to Y
, we have a projection formula (which is an analogue for push-forwards of the functoriality of pull-back with respect to intersection produts):

\displaystyle f_{*}(Z\cdot f^{*}(Z^{\prime}))=f_{*}(Z)\cdot Z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CH}(Y),

where
Z\in\operatorname{CH}(X)
and
Z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CH}(Y)
.

Let
Z\subset X
be a smooth projective subvariety of
X
, let us denote the
U
to be the open complement of
Z
. We have a short exact sequence, called the
localization exact sequence

\displaystyle\operatorname{CH}_{i}(Z)\xrightarrow{i_{*}}\operatorname{CH}_{i}(X)\xrightarrow{j^{*}}\operatorname{CH}_{i}(U)\to 0.

A correspondence from
X
to
Y
is a cycle
Z\in\operatorname{CH}(X\times Y)
. A correspondence
Z\in\operatorname{CH}^{t}(X\times Y)
acts on the cycles on
X
as follows

\displaystyle Z(T)=(\text{pr}_{Y})_{*}(Z\cdot(T\times Y))\in\operatorname{CH}^{i+t-n}(Y)

where
T\in\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
and
n=\text{dim}(X)
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a cycle
Z\in\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
is
algebraically trivial
if there is a smooth irreducible curve
C
and
W\in\operatorname{CH}^{i}(C\times X)
and two points
a
,
b\in C
such that
W(a)=0
and
W(b)=Z
. We denote by
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\operatorname{alg}}
the subgroup of algebraically trivial cycles of codimension
i
on
X
.

When
k=\mathbb{C}
there is a well-defined cycle map
c^{i}_{\mathbb{A}}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{A}}\to\operatorname{H}^{2i}_{B}(X,\mathbb{A})
to Betti cohomology for
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{Z},\ \mathbb{Q}
, whose image is a subset of the Hodge classes

\displaystyle\text{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{A}):=\left\{\alpha\in\operatorname{H}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{A})\ |\ \alpha_{\mathbb{C}}\in\operatorname{H}^{i,i}(X)\right\}.

We denote
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\hom}:=\text{ker}(c^{i})
, where
c^{i}:=c^{i}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. It is clear that
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\text{alg}}\subset\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\text{hom}}
, and the quotient group is called the
Griffiths group
, denoted by

\displaystyle\text{Griff}^{i}(X):=\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\hom}/\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\operatorname{alg}}.

Definition 2.2
.

Assume that
k=\bar{k}
, and let
\Omega/k
be a field extension of infinite transcendence degree. A map

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X_{\Omega})_{\operatorname{alg}}\to T(\Omega)

with
T
a variety is said to be
regular
if, for every smooth connected variety
S
with base point
s_{0}\in S(\Omega)
, and for every cycle
\Gamma\in\operatorname{CH}^{i}(S\times X)
, the composition

\displaystyle S(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X_{\Omega})_{\operatorname{alg}}\to T(\Omega)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\mapsto\Gamma((s)-(s_{0}))

is a morphism of algebraic varieties.

We say that
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\operatorname{alg}}
is
weakly representable
if there exists an abelian variety
A/k
and a regular isomorphism of groups
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X_{\Omega})_{\operatorname{alg}}\xrightarrow{\simeq}A(\Omega)
.

We recall some relations between birational geometry and Chow groups. We begin by reviewing a few definitions from birational geometry.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
of dimension
n
. We say that
X
is:

(1)

Rational
if
X
is birational to
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}
.

(2)

Stably rational
if there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X\times\mathbb{P}^{m}_{k}
is rational.

(3)

Unirational
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a dominant rational map
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{k}\dashrightarrow X
.

(4)

Rationally connected
if any two general points
p,q\in X
can be connected by a chain of rational curves.

These notions satisfy the following implications:

\displaystyle\textup{(1)}\Longrightarrow\textup{(2)}\Longrightarrow\textup{(3)}\Longrightarrow\textup{(4)}.

In the present article, we use properties of birational invariants related to algebraic cycles. The first and most important of these is that the group of zero-cycles of a smooth projective variety is a stably birational invariant. In particular, for a stably rational variety
X
, we have
\operatorname{CH}_{0}(X)=\mathbb{Z}
. The converse does not hold in general; however, this condition can still be used to detect when a variety is not (stably) rational, through the so-called
decomposition of the diagonal
. To that end, we introduce the notion of a universally trivial
\operatorname{CH}_{0}
-group.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
of dimension
n
.

(1)

We say that
X
has a
universally trivial
\operatorname{CH}_{0}
-group
if
X
has a zero-cycle
x
of degree 1 such that
\operatorname{CH}_{0}(X_{K})=\mathbb{Z}x
for any field extension
k\subset K
.

(2)

We say that
X
admits a
Chow decomposition of the diagonal
if one can write

\Delta_{X}=X\times x+Z\in\operatorname{CH}^{n}(X\times X),

where
Z
is a cycle on
X\times X
supported on
D\times X
, with
D\subset X
a proper closed algebraic subset.

These two conditions are equivalent (see eg.
[
Voi19
, Proposition 3.11]
). Zero-cycles also encode information about codimension 2 cycles and curves in
X

3.
Preliminaries on étale Chow groups

In this section, we recall the definition and fundamental properties of the étale analogues of Chow groups, and gather some general results concerning these groups for smooth complete intersections of Hodge level one.

3.1.
Étale Chow groups

We consider an étale version of Chow groups, namely the well-known Lichtenbaum cohomology groups, which are defined as the hypercohomology of the étale sheafification of Bloch’s cycle complex. These groups were characterized by Rosenschon and Srinivas in
[
RS16
]
using étale hypercoverings.

In this context, we let
\text{Sm}_{k}
denote the category of smooth separated
k
-schemes over a field
k
. We denote by
z^{i}(X,\bullet)
the cycle complex of abelian groups defined by Bloch:

\displaystyle z^{i}(X,\bullet):\cdots\to z^{i}(X,j)\to\cdots\to z^{i}(X,1)\to z^{i}(X,0)\to 0,

where
z^{i}(X,j)
is the subgroup generated by closed subvarieties of codimension
i
that intersect properly with
X\times F
for each face
F
of the standard simplex
\Delta^{j}
, and the differentials are given by the alternating sum of the pullbacks of the face maps.

We remind the reader that the homology groups of this complex define the higher Chow groups, namely
\text{CH}^{i}(X,2i-j)=\text{H}_{j}(z^{i}(X,\bullet))
, and in particular,
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X,0)
.

Let us recall that
z^{i}(X,j)
and the complex
z^{i}(X,\bullet)
are covariantly functorial for proper morphisms and contravariantly functorial for flat morphisms between smooth
k
-schemes (see
[
Blo86
, Proposition 1.3]
). Therefore, for any topology
t\in\left\{\text{flat},\ \textup{\'{e}t},\ \text{Nis},\ \text{Zar}\right\}
, we obtain a complex of
t
-presheaves

z^{i}(-,\bullet)\colon U\mapsto z^{i}(U,\bullet).

In particular, for each
j
, the presheaf
z^{i}(-,j)\colon U\mapsto z^{i}(U,j)
is a sheaf for all topologies
t\in\left\{\text{flat},\ \textup{\'{e}t},\ \text{Nis},\ \text{Zar}\right\}
(see
[
Gei04
, Lemma 3.1]
), and hence
z^{i}(-,\bullet)
is a complex of sheaves on the small étale, Nisnevich, and Zariski sites of
X
.

We define the complex of
t
-sheaves

R_{X}(i)_{t}:=\left(z^{i}(-,\bullet)_{t}\otimes R\right)[-2i],

where
R
is an abelian group. In what follows, we will restrict to the cases
t=\text{Zar}
or
t=\textup{\'{e}t}
, and compute the corresponding hypercohomology groups
\mathbb{H}^{j}_{t}(X,R_{X}(i)_{t})
.

For instance, taking
t=\text{Zar}
and
R=\mathbb{Z}
, the hypercohomology of the complex recovers the higher Chow groups:

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X,2i-j)\simeq\mathbb{H}^{j}_{\text{Zar}}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i)).

We denote the motivic and Lichtenbaum cohomology groups with coefficients in
R
as

\text{H}_{M}^{j}(X,R(i))=\mathbb{H}^{j}_{\text{Zar}}(X,R(i)),\quad\text{H}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}^{j}(X,R(i))=\mathbb{H}^{j}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X,R(i)),

and in particular, we set
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X):=\text{H}^{2i}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))
.

Let
\pi\colon X_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\to X_{\text{Zar}}
be the canonical morphism of sites. The adjunction morphism
\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)\to R\pi_{*}\pi^{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)=R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}
induces the
comparison morphisms

\kappa^{j,i}\colon\text{H}_{M}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))\longrightarrow\text{H}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))

for all bidegrees
(j,i)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Thanks to
[
Voe11
, Theorem 6.18]
, we know that the comparison map
\kappa^{j,i}
is an isomorphism for
j\leq i+1
and a monomorphism for
j\leq i+2
. For simplicity, we write

\kappa^{i}:=\kappa^{2i,i}:\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\to\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)

for the comparison map between Chow and Chow étale groups.

In some cases, it is possible to obtain more information about the Lichtenbaum cohomology groups and the comparison map between them and the higher Chow groups. For instance, there is a quasi-isomorphism
R_{X}(0)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}=R
, where the right-hand side is regarded as an étale sheaf. Thus, the Lichtenbaum cohomology agrees with the usual étale cohomology, i.e.,

\text{H}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}^{j}(X,R(0))\simeq\text{H}^{j}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X,R)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and in particular,
\text{CH}^{0}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)=\mathbb{Z}^{\pi_{0}(X)}
.

In the next case,
i=1
, since there is a quasi-isomorphism of complexes
\mathbb{Z}_{X}(1)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}[-1]
, we obtain the following isomorphisms:

\text{CH}^{1}(X)\simeq\text{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{1}(X)=\text{Pic}(X),\quad\text{H}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z}(1))\simeq\text{H}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{3}(X,\mathbb{G}_{m}[-1])=\text{Br}(X),

where
\text{Pic}(X)
and
\text{Br}(X)
denote the Picard and Grothendieck-Brauer groups of
X
, respectively. In fact, for bidegree
(j,1)
, by
[
VSF00
, Corollary 3.4.3]
, there exists an isomorphism

\text{H}_{M}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Z}(1))\simeq\text{H}_{\text{Zar}}^{j-1}(X,\mathbb{G}_{m}),

because the quasi-isomorphism
\mathbb{Z}_{X}(1)\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}[-1]
also holds in the Zariski topology.

As a particular case, consider

\text{H}_{M}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z}(1))\simeq\text{H}_{\text{Zar}}^{2}(X,\mathbb{G}_{m})=0,

since
\text{H}_{M}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))=0
for
j>2i
, whereas the Grothendieck-Brauer group
\text{Br}(X)
is not always trivial (for instance, if
X
is an Enriques surface; see eg.
[
Bea09
]
).

In bi-degree
(4,2)
, the comparison map is known to be injective but, in general, not surjective. More precisely, we have a short exact sequence

0\to\text{CH}^{2}(X)\xrightarrow{\kappa^{2}}\text{CH}^{2}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))\to 0,

where
\mathscr{H}^{3}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))
is the Zariski sheaf associated to the presheaf
U\mapsto\text{H}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{3}(U,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))
, and the unramified part is given by the global sections
\text{H}_{\text{nr}}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))=\Gamma(X,\mathscr{H}^{3}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2)))
. For a proof, see
[
Kah12
, Proposition 2.9]
.

If
k=\mathbb{C}
, the latter group surjects onto the torsion subgroup of the obstruction, in codimension 4, to the integral Hodge conjecture, i.e.,

\text{H}_{\text{nr}}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))\twoheadrightarrow\left(\text{Hdg}^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z})/\operatorname{Im}\left\{c^{2}:\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\to\text{H}_{B}^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z}(2))\right\}\right)_{\text{tors}},

and hence, in general, it is nonzero and the comparison map
\kappa^{2}
is not surjective. For further details, see
[
CV12
, Théorème 3.7]
.

Remark 3.1
.

For rational coefficients, the adjunction morphism

\mathbb{Q}_{X}(i)\to R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Q}_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}

turns out to be an isomorphism (see
[
Kah12
, Théorème 2.6]
). Therefore, we have

\text{H}_{M}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Q}(i))\simeq\text{H}_{M,\textup{\'{e}t}}^{j}(X,\mathbb{Q}(i))

for all
(j,i)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. In particular,

\text{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\text{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
R
is a torsion group, then the Lichtenbaum cohomology can be computed via étale cohomology. More precisely, let
\ell
be a prime number,
r\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
, and
R=\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{r}
. Then there is a quasi-isomorphism

(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{r})_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mu_{\ell^{r}}^{\otimes i}.

Passing to the direct limit, we also obtain a quasi-isomorphism

(\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}/\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\varinjlim_{r}\mu_{\ell^{r}}^{\otimes i},

and finally we define

(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}:=\bigoplus_{\ell}(\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}/\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}.

The following result is a well-known consequence, referred to as the
Suslin rigidity theorem
, concerning the morphism
\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)\to R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}
for
i\geq\dim(X)
over
k=\bar{k}
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
Sus00
, Theorem 4.2]
,
[
Gei18
, Section 2]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
over an algebraically closed field
k
. Then, for
i\geq n
, the canonical morphism of sites
\pi\colon X_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\to X_{\text{Zar}}
induces a quasi-isomorphism of complexes of Zariski sheaves:

\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)\to R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(i)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}.

In particular,
\operatorname{CH}^{n}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{n}(X)
.

Throughout the present article, we will use various cycle class maps, ranging from étale motivic and Lichtenbaum cohomology groups to Betti and étale cohomology:

\displaystyle c^{i}\colon\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\to\text{H}^{2i}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i)),\quad(\text{resp. }c^{i}_{\mathbb{Q}}\colon\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\text{H}^{2i}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Q}(i)))\quad\textbf{(Cycle class map)}

\displaystyle c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\colon\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\text{H}^{2i}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}(i))\quad\textbf{(Lichtenbaum cycle class map; see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{RS}{}{}]})}

These maps satisfy the property that, as in the classical case of Chow groups, we have

\operatorname{Im}(c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}})\subset\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z}),\quad\text{but}\quad\operatorname{Im}(c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}})_{\text{tors}}\simeq\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\text{tors}}.

Remark 3.3
.

Let us briefly discuss the functoriality of étale Chow groups: Since we work over a base field
k
with
\operatorname{char}(k)=0
, we can use an equivalent definition (see
[

, Theorem 7.1.2]
) of étale Chow groups in terms of the triangulated category of étale motives
\operatorname{DM}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k,\mathbb{Z})
. For more details, we refer the reader to
[
Ayo14
]
and
[

]
. One defines the

Thanks to the six functor formalism (see
[
CD19
]
), we can recover the standard formalism for Chow groups (pull-backs, push-forwards of proper maps, intersection products, and the projection formula), now in the context of étale Chow groups. See
[
Ros22
]
for further details on the construction and compatibility properties.

An
étale correspondence
from
X
to
Y
is a cycle
Z\in\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X\times Y)
. A correspondence
Z\in\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{t}(X\times Y)
acts on cycles in

Z(T):=(\operatorname{pr}_{Y})_{*}(Z\cdot(T\times Y))\in\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i+t-n}(Y),

where

and

.

Definition 3.4
.

We say that a cycle

is

if there exists a smooth irreducible curve
C
, a cycle
W\in\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(C\times X)
, and two points

such that
W(a)=0
and
W(b)=Z
. We denote the subgroup of algebraically trivial cycles of codimension

by
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\operatorname{alg}}
.

We denote by
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{hom}}:=\ker(c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}})
. It is clear that

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{alg}}\subset\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{hom}},

although it is not always obvious that this inclusion is strict. However,
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{alg}}
is always a divisible group, since
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{hom}}
is the maximal divisible subgroup of
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)
. The corresponding quotient is called the

, and is denoted as

\operatorname{Griff}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X):=\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{hom}}/\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\text{alg}}.

3.2.
Étale motivic cohomology of complete intersections

Let us consider
X=X_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
to be a smooth complete intersection of multidegree
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
and dimension
n
, defined over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. By Whitney’s formula, the total Chern class of the tangent bundle of
X
, denoted by
\mathscr{T}_{X}
, is given by

(1)

\displaystyle c(\mathscr{T}_{X})=\left.\frac{(1+h)^{n+r+1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+d_{i}h)}\right|_{X}=\frac{(1+\xi_{X})^{n+r+1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+d_{i}\xi_{X})},

where
\xi_{X}
denotes the hyperplane class restricted to
X

Then, the Euler characteristic
\chi(X)
is equal to
\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}
times the coefficient of
h^{n}
in the expression (
1
), since
\xi_{X}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\right)h\in\operatorname{CH}^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{n+r})
. This coefficient can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+d_{i}h)}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1-d_{i}h+d_{i}^{2}h^{2}-\cdots+(-1)^{k}d_{i}^{k}h^{k}+\cdots\right).

Let us define

p_{n}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}):=(-1)^{n}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}\leq r}d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{n}}.

We also expand:

(1+h)^{n+r+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n+r+1}\binom{n+r+1}{i}h^{i}.

Hence, the Euler characteristic of
X
is given by:

\chi(X)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n+r+1}{i}\cdot p_{n-i}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\right).

Since the cohomology groups of a smooth complete intersection are torsion-free, and the alternating sum of their Betti numbers equals
\chi(X)
, we deduce:

b_{n}(X)=(-1)^{n}\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n+r+1}{i}\cdot p_{n-i}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})\right)-n-1\right).

Proposition 3.5

Let
X=X_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+r}

is odd and

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\text{tors}}\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)^{\text{hom}}_{\text{tors}},

independently of

. In particular, we have an isomorphism

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\text{tors}}\simeq J^{2i-1}(X)_{\text{tors}},

where

is the intermediate Jacobian. By our assumptions on

, we have
\text{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
, and thus
J^{2i+1}(X)=0
. Hence,
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)^{\text{hom}}
is torsion-free for every

. Since this group is divisible
2
2

2

In fact, it is the maximal divisible subgroup of
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\simeq\operatorname{Im}(c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}})\oplus\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\text{hom}}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}},

where
\operatorname{Im}(c^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}})_{\text{tors}}\simeq\operatorname{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\text{tors}}=0
in this case.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

(1)

In the excluded case
\dim(X)=2m+1
and
i=m+1
, we have:

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{m+1}(X)_{\text{tors}}\simeq J^{2m+1}(X)_{\text{tors}}\simeq(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{\oplus b_{2m+1}(X)}.

(2)

If
\dim(X)=2m+1
, then the Hodge conjecture holds (rationally). By the equivalence in
[
RS16
]
, we have:

0\to\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\text{hom}}\to\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\mathbb{Z}[h^{i}]\to 0,

which splits via a natural section. The same holds in even dimension, except possibly in the middle degree, where the Hodge conjecture remains open.

4.
Chow groups of complete intersections with Hodge level one

Smooth complete intersection in the complex projective space whose Hodge structures have level one were fully classified by Deligne and Rapoport in
[
Del71
]
and
[
Rap72
]
, respectively. These varieties are the following:

(1)

Fano threefolds given by:

•

Cubic hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
,

•

Quartic hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, and

•

Complete intersections of a quadric and a cubic in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.

(2)

Intersections of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
.

(3)

Intersections of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+4}
.

(4)

Cubic fivefolds in
\mathbb{P}^{6}
.

Recall that if
G
is an abelian group and
X
a complex algebraic variety, then
\mathscr{H}^{q}(G)
denotes the Zariski sheaf associated to the presheaf
U\mapsto\operatorname{H}_{B}^{q}(U,G)
. The unramified cohomology groups are defined as

\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{nr}}^{i}(X,G):=\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{Zar}}^{0}(X,\mathscr{H}^{i}(G)),

which are birational invariants and relate to the torsion in Chow groups. More generally, if
\mathscr{G}
is an étale sheaf on
X
, we set

\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{nr}}^{i}(X,\mathscr{G}):=\operatorname{H}^{0}_{\operatorname{Zar}}(X,\mathscr{H}^{i}(\mathscr{G})),

where
\mathscr{H}^{i}(\mathscr{G})
is the Zariski sheaf associated to
U\mapsto\operatorname{H}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(U,\mathscr{G})
.

4.1.
Chow groups of an intersection of two quadrics

Let
X=X_{(2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
. When
m\geq 1
, the variety
X
is rational: by
[
DM98
, Theorem 2.1]
we know that
X
contains a line
\ell\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset X
, and more generally,
X
contains
j
-planes
H\simeq\mathbb{P}^{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq m
. Here, the linear projection
\pi_{\ell}:X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}
from a line induces a birational map (cf.
[
HT21
]
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X=X_{(2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
with
m\geq 1
. Fix an integer
p\neq m
. Then for all
k\geq 1
, the groups
\operatorname{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{p+k}(\mathbb{Z}))
are torsion.

Proof.

The result holds for
p=0
and
p=1
. Let us now fix
p\leq m-1
. The hypotheses of Theorem
2.5
are satisfied for
X
with
c=p+1
, so we have a decomposition:

N_{p}\Delta_{X}=Z_{0}+Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{p}+Z^{\prime},

where
Z^{\prime}
is supported on
X\times T
with
\operatorname{codim}_{X}(T)\geq p+1
. Denote by
\widetilde{W}_{i}
and
\widetilde{T}
the desingularizations of
W_{i}
and
T
, respectively.

Since the cycle
\Delta_{X}
acts as the identity on cohomology, we have that
N_{p}\Delta_{X}^{\ast}
acts on
\text{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{q}(\mathbb{Z}))
as
N_{p}\cdot\operatorname{Id}
. Thanks to the decomposition of the diagonal, the action of the diagonal (times the constant
N_{p}
) factors through the following terms:

•

\text{H}^{p}(\widetilde{W}_{i},\mathscr{H}^{p+k}_{\widetilde{W}_{i}}(\mathbb{Z}))
, which vanishes since
p+k>\dim(\widetilde{W}_{i})=i
, so
\mathscr{H}^{p+k}_{\widetilde{W}_{i}}(\mathbb{Z})=0
.

•

\text{H}^{p-c}(\widetilde{T},\mathscr{H}^{p+k-c}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathbb{Z}))
, where
c=\operatorname{codim}_{X}(T)
. As long as
p-c<0
we have that the latter group vanishes.

Therefore,
N_{p}\cdot\Delta_{X}
acts as zero on
\text{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{p+k}(\mathbb{Z}))
, and the result follows.

Now suppose
p\geq m+1
. Set
c_{p}:=2m+1-p
so that the hypotheses are verified for
c=c_{p}+1
. Then, the
N_{c_{p}}\Delta_{X}^{\ast}
factors through:

•

\text{H}^{p-d_{i}}(\widetilde{W}_{i},\mathscr{H}^{q-d_{i}}_{\widetilde{W}_{i}}(\mathbb{Z}))
, where
d_{i}=2m+1-i
and again
p-d_{i}<0
, so these terms vanish.

•

\text{H}^{p}(\widetilde{T},\mathscr{H}^{p+k}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathbb{Z}))
, and since
\operatorname{codim}_{X}(T)\geq c_{p}+1
, we get
\dim(T)\leq p-1
, so again the cohomology group vanishes.

As before, we deduce that the groups
\text{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{p+k}(\mathbb{Z}))
are torsion.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
X=X_{(2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
with
m\geq 1
. Then, for any fixed integer
p\neq m
and for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{p+k}(\mathbb{Z}))=0.

Proof.

Let
\phi:X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}
be a birational map which is an isomorphism onto its image outside a line
\ell\subset X
, where
\operatorname{codim}_{X}(\ell)=2m
(i.e.,
\dim(\ell)=1
). Then:

\Delta_{X}=\phi^{*}(\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}})+Z\in\operatorname{CH}^{2m+1}(X\times X),

where
Z
is a cycle supported on
H\times H
, since
\phi
is an isomorphism outside a linear subspace
H\subset X
of
\operatorname{codim}_{X}(H)=m+1
. Indeed, the decomposition of the diagonal of
\Delta_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}}
gives
N_{p}=1
. Finally, the result follows directly from Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.2.

Chow groups of

Now consider

. For

, the variety
X
is rational, with a birational map
\phi:X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}
induced by projection from a linear space

. Therefore, for all

.

, we have

\operatorname{H}^{p}(X,\mathscr{H}^{p+k}(\mathbb{Z}))=0.

Remark 4.5
.

Since

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
X=X_{(2,2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+4}
with

. Then for all

with

, the groups

The argument relies on the surjectivity of

5.
Étale Chow groups of complete intersection with Hodge level one

In this section we compute the étale Chow groups for smooth complete intersections in the projective space with Hodge structure of level one. We will first discuss the case of Fano threefolds (which includes, in particular, cubic threefolds, quartic threefolds, and complete intersections of type
(2,3)
in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
), and then turn to the case of higher-dimensional complete intersections.

5.1.
Fano threefolds

Let
X
be a smooth Fano threefold over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. Such varieties satisfy the following two properties: Grabowski proved in
[
Gra04
]
that the integral Hodge conjecture holds for
X
, and that its Chow groups are representable. Using these facts, we can readily deduce the following isomorphisms:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth Fano threefold. Then:

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\textup{ for all }i\geq 0.

Proof.

For codimensions
0
and
1
, the result holds for all smooth projective varieties over a field
k
with
\operatorname{char}(k)=0
. If
Y
is a smooth projective variety over a field
k=\bar{k}
of characteristic zero, then
\operatorname{CH}^{\dim(Y)}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(Y)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{\dim(Y)}(Y)
. For the last étale Chow group, we consider the short exact sequence:

0\to\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\to\operatorname{CH}^{2}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))\to 0.

By
[
CV12
, Théorème 3.9]
, if
\operatorname{CH}_{0}(Y)
is supported on a subvariety of
Y
of dimension at most
2
, then the obstruction to the integral Hodge conjecture is isomorphic to
\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))
.

Furthermore, by
[
Jan94
, Proposition 1.6]
, the condition that
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)_{\text{alg}}
is representable is equivalent to the condition that
\operatorname{CH}_{0}(X)
is supported on a subvariety of
X
of dimension
1
. Since the integral Hodge conjecture holds for
X
, it follows that
\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))=0
, and therefore
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{2}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
.
∎

5.2.
Cubic fivefolds

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{6}
be a smooth cubic fivefold defined over
\mathbb{C}
. Its classical Chow groups are characterized as follows:

•

\operatorname{CH}^{0}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the fundamental class of
X
.

•

\operatorname{CH}^{1}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the class of a hyperplane section
h:=c_{1}(\mathscr{O}_{X}(1))
.

•

\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by
h^{2}
. Since
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}=0
, the group
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)
is isomorphic to the image of the cycle class map.

•

\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}\oplus J^{5}(X)
, where
J^{5}(X)
is a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
21
(see
[
FT19
, Théorème 1]
).

•

\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the class of a line contained in
X
.

•

\operatorname{CH}^{5}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the class of a point, since
X
is unirational.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X
be a smooth cubic fivefold defined over
\mathbb{C}
. Then

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\quad\text{for all }i\geq 0.

Proof.

For
i=0,1,5
, the comparison maps are isomorphisms:

\operatorname{CH}^{0}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{0}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X),\quad\operatorname{CH}^{1}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X),\quad\operatorname{CH}^{5}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{5}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X).

Since
\text{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})=0
, it follows that
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{2}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
. For
i=4
, the comparison map
\kappa^{4}:\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{CH}^{4}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
is
a priori
injective because
\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)
is torsion-free. The cokernel of
\kappa^{4}
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{H}^{8}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\tau_{\geq 6}R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(4)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\right),

and there exists a spectral sequence

E^{p,q}_{2}:=\text{H}^{p}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,R^{q}\tau_{\geq 6}R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(4)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\right)\Longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{p+q}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\tau_{\geq 6}R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(4)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\right).

Moreover,

\text{H}^{p}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,R^{q}\tau_{\geq 6}R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(4)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\right)\simeq\begin{cases}\text{H}^{p}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\mathscr{H}^{q-1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(4))\right),&\text{if }q\geq 6,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

From this, we deduce that the cokernel of
\kappa^{4}
vanishes, hence
\operatorname{CH}^{4}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
. For
i=3
, the cokernel of
\kappa^{3}
vanishes, so
\kappa^{3}
is surjective. Its kernel is a subgroup of

\mathbb{H}^{5}_{\mathrm{Zar}}\!\left(X,\tau_{\geq 5}R\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}_{X}(3)_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\right),

which, by the same reasoning, is trivial. Therefore,
\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{3}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
.
∎

5.3.
Intersections of two and three quadrics

In contrast to the classical setting, where one typically has explicit control of some of the Chow groups only, in the case of étale Chow groups we can compute
all
of them. This is due to the fact that, for the varieties considered here, the rational Hodge conjecture holds, and we can apply the results of Rosenschon and Srinivas
[
RS16
]
to obtain a complete description in every codimension.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
X_{(2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
be a smooth intersection of two quadrics. Then the étale Chow groups of
X
are given by:

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\simeq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}[h^{i}],&\text{if }i\neq m+1,\\
\mathbb{Z}[h^{m+1}]\oplus J^{m+1}(X),&\text{if }i=m+1,\end{cases}

where
h
is a hyperplane section, and
J^{m+1}(X)
is the
(m+1)
-intermediate Jacobian, which is isomorphic to the Jacobian of a smooth projective curve
C
.

Proof.

This follows directly from
[
RS16
]
. Since
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\mathrm{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is surjective if and only if
\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathrm{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is surjective, we have that
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\mathbb{Z}
is surjective. Moreover,
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathrm{tor}}
, and the torsion part of
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
(by
[
RS16
, Proposition 5.1]
) is isomorphic to
J^{i}(X)_{\mathrm{tors}}
. For the torsion-free part, we use that
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
and that the torsion-free part of
J^{m+1}(X)
is uniquely divisible.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
X_{(2,2,2)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2m+4}
be a smooth intersection of three quadrics. Then the étale Chow groups of
X
are given by:

\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\simeq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}[h^{i}],&\text{if }i\neq m+1,\\
\mathbb{Z}[h^{m+1}]\oplus J^{m+1}(X),&\text{if }i=m+1,\end{cases}

where
h
is a hyperplane section, and
J^{m+1}(X)
is the
(m+1)
-intermediate Jacobian of
X
.

Proof.

Again, this follows from
[
RS16
]
. The same argument as in the previous proposition applies: the surjectivity of
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\mathrm{Hdg}^{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
follows from its rational analogue, and the identification of
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
with its torsion part reduces the problem to understanding
J^{2i-1}(X)_{\mathrm{tors}}
. The torsion-free part is obtained from the equality
\operatorname{CH}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The above result, together with the discussion in §
4.1
and §
4.2
, implies that if
X
is a
5
-dimensional intersection of two or three quadrics then
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)
for all
i\geq 0
.

6.
Decomposition of finite dimensional motives

In this section, we show that the previous results on the characterization of Chow groups (and étale Chow groups) also hold in a motivic framework. The key point is the finite dimensionality of these motives in the sense of Kimura, see
[
Kim05
]
. This condition is more easily verified for varieties with Hodge structures of level 1. For instance, the motives of cubic threefolds and fivefolds are finite-dimensional
[
Huy23
, Chapter 7, §4]
, whereas the finite dimensionality of the cubic fourfold motive remains unknown. We briefly recall the construction of the category of Chow motives and some of the main properties of their decompositions for the reader’s convenience.

Let
\text{SmProj}_{k}
denote the category of smooth projective varieties over a field
k
. If
X\in\text{SmProj}_{k}
has pure dimension
d
, the group of degree
r
correspondences from
X
to
Y
is defined by
\text{Corr}^{r}(X,Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}=\operatorname{CH}^{d+r}(X\times Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. More generally, if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}
are the irreducible components of
X
, we set
\text{Corr}^{r}(X,Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{j}\text{Corr}^{r}(X_{i},Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Given elements
f\in\text{Corr}^{r}(X,Y)
and
g\in\text{Corr}^{s}(Y,Z)
, their composition
g\circ f\in\text{Corr}^{r+s}(X,Z)
is given by

g\circ f=(\text{pr}_{XZ})_{*}\left(\text{pr}_{XY}^{*}(f)\cdot\text{pr}_{YZ}^{*}(g)\right),

where
\text{pr}_{XZ}
,
\text{pr}_{XY}
, and
\text{pr}_{YZ}
denote the projections of
X\times Y\times Z
onto
X\times Z
,
X\times Y
, and
Y\times Z
, respectively. If
X
and
Y
are objects in
\text{SmProj}_{k}
of pure dimensions
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
, and
f:X\to Y
is a morphism, then for the graph
\Gamma_{f}\subset X\times Y
we define

f_{*}:=\Gamma_{f}\in\text{Corr}^{d_{Y}-d_{X}}(X,Y),\qquad f^{*}:={}^{t}\Gamma_{f}\in\text{Corr}^{0}(X,Y),

where
{}^{t}\Gamma_{f}
denotes the transpose of
\Gamma_{f}
.

We can now recall the construction of the category of Chow effective motives over
k
, denoted
\text{Chow}^{\operatorname{eff}}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Its objects are pairs
(X,p)
with
X\in\text{SmProj}_{k}
and
p\in\text{Corr}^{0}(X,X)
satisfying
p\circ p=p
. Morphisms between two objects
(X,p)
and
(Y,q)
are given by

\mathrm{Hom}_{\text{Chow}^{\operatorname{eff}}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}}\big((X,p),(Y,q)\big)=q\circ\text{Corr}^{0}(X,Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}\circ p.

The category of Chow motives
\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
has as objects triplets
(X,p,m)
with
X\in\text{SmProj}_{k}
,
p\in\text{Corr}^{0}(X,X)
idempotent, and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Morphisms between
(X,p,m)
and
(Y,q,n)
are defined by

\mathrm{Hom}_{\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}}\big((X,p,m),(Y,q,n)\big)=q\circ\text{Corr}^{\,n-m}(X,Y)_{\mathbb{Q}}\circ p.

Finally, there is a contravariant functor

\displaystyle h:\text{SmProj}_{k}

\displaystyle\to\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto h(X):=(X,\Delta_{X},0)

\displaystyle f:X\to Y

\displaystyle\mapsto h(f)={}^{t}\Gamma_{f}:h(Y)\to h(X).

Remark 6.1
.

The category
\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
is additive and pseudo-abelian (i.e. every idempotent morphism has both a kernel and a cokernel). Moreover, if we consider the triangulated category of motives
\operatorname{DM}(k,\mathbb{Q})
, there is a fully faithful embedding of
1
-categories

\text{Chow}(k)^{\mathrm{opp}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{DM}(k,\mathbb{Q}).

Another important point is that Chow motives appear in
\operatorname{DM}(k,\mathbb{Q})
as the weight-
0
objects, with respect to the weight structure constructed by Bondarko
[
Bon14
]
.

Examples and further properties of motives.

In what follows we assume that
X
has pure dimension
d
:

(1)

The motive of a point is
\mathbf{1}:=(\mathrm{Spec}(k),\mathrm{id},0)
.

(2)

If
X\in\text{SmProj}_{k}
with
X(k)\neq\emptyset
, define
p_{0}(X):=e\times X
and
p_{2d}(X):={}^{t}p_{0}(X)=X\times e
. These projectors are orthogonal, i.e.
p_{2d}(X)\circ p_{0}(X)=p_{0}(X)\circ p_{2d}(X)=0
. This yields the motives

h^{0}(X):=(X,p_{0}(X),0),\qquad h^{2d}(X):=(X,p_{2d}(X),0).

(3)

The Lefschetz and Tate motives are defined by

\mathbf{L}:=(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k},\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}\times e,0),\qquad\mathbf{T}:=(\mathrm{Spec}(k),\mathrm{id},1).

(4)

Direct sums of motives: if
M=(X,p,m)
and
N=(Y,q,m)
are two motives, then

M\oplus N=\big(X\coprod Y,p\oplus q,m\big).

(5)

Monoidal structure: the category
\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a tensor category with

\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}\times\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}},\quad(X,p,m)\otimes(Y,q,n)\mapsto(X\times Y,p\otimes q,m+n).

Under this structure, the Lefschetz and Tate motives are inverses:
\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{1}
. Moreover,
h^{2d}(X)\simeq\mathbf{L}^{\otimes d}
.

(6)

Duality operator:

{}^{\vee}:\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{opp}}\to\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}},\quad M=(X,p,m)\mapsto M^{\vee}=(X,{}^{t}p,d-m).

(7)

Chow groups and cohomology of motives: for
M=(X,p,m)
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(M):=p_{\ast}\big(\operatorname{CH}^{i+m}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\big)\subset\operatorname{CH}^{i+m}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}.

For a Weil cohomology theory
\operatorname{H}(-)
we define
\operatorname{H}^{i}(M):=p_{\ast}(\operatorname{H}^{i+2m}(X))\subset\operatorname{H}^{i+2m}(X)
.

(8)

If
X\in\text{SmProj}_{k}
with
X(k)\neq\emptyset
, the correspondence
p^{+}(X)=\Delta_{X}-p_{0}(X)-p_{2d}(X)
is a projector, since
p_{0}(X)
and
p_{2d}(X)
are orthogonal. Defining
h^{+}(X):=(X,p^{+}(X),0)
, we obtain a decomposition

h(X)\simeq h^{0}(X)\oplus h^{+}(X)\oplus h^{2d}(X)\simeq\mathbf{1}\oplus h^{+}(X)\oplus\mathbf{L}^{\otimes d}.

We now add a few remarks on the latter point. Fix a Weil cohomology theory
\operatorname{H}(-)
and consider the diagonal class
\Delta_{X}
of
X
. Let
\gamma_{X\times X}
denote the cycle class map to
\operatorname{H}(-)
. By the Künneth formula one has

\gamma_{X\times X}(\Delta_{X})=\sum_{i=0}^{2d}\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{topo}}(X)\in H^{2d}(X\times X)\simeq\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2d}H^{2d-i}(X)\otimes H^{i}(X),

with the convention that
\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{topo}}(X)\in\operatorname{H}^{2d-i}(X)\otimes\operatorname{H}^{i}(X)
. Since
\operatorname{H}^{2d-i}(X)\otimes\operatorname{H}^{i}(X)\simeq\mathrm{End}(\operatorname{H}^{i}(X))
, we may regard
\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{topo}}(X)
as the identity on
\operatorname{H}^{i}(X)
. Thus,
\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{topo}}(X)
acts on
\operatorname{H}^{*}(X)
as the identity on
\operatorname{H}^{i}(X)
and as zero on all other summands of the above decomposition. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
X\in\mathrm{SmProj}_{k}
be of pure dimension
d
. We say that
X
admits a
Chow–Künneth decomposition
if there exist projectors
p_{i}(X)\in\mathrm{Corr}^{0}(X,X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
for
0\leq i\leq 2d
such that:

(1)

\sum_{i=0}^{2d}p_{i}(X)=\Delta_{X}
.

(2)

The projectors
p_{i}(X)
are pairwise orthogonal, i.e.

p_{i}(X)\circ p_{j}(X)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
p_{i}(X)&\text{if }i=j,\end{cases}

(3)

\gamma_{X\times X}(p_{i}(X))=\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{topo}}(X)
.

It is conjectured that every smooth projective variety
X
admits a Chow–Künneth decomposition. A result of Murre shows that, for a smooth projective variety
X\in\mathrm{SmProj}_{k}
, there exist projectors
p_{1}(X)
and
p_{2d-1}(X)
with the following properties:

Theorem 6.3

(
[
Mur90
]
)
.

Let
X\in\mathrm{SmProj}_{k}
be smooth projective of dimension
d
. Then there exist
p_{1}(X),p_{2d-1}(X)\in\mathrm{Corr}^{0}(X,X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that:

(1)

p_{1}(X)
and
p_{2d-1}(X)
are orthogonal to each other and to
p_{0}(X)
and
p_{2d}(X)
. They lift the Künneth components
\Delta_{1}^{\mathrm{topo}}
and
\Delta_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{topo}}
, respectively, and satisfy
p_{2d-1}(X)={}^{t}p_{1}(X)
.

(2)

Let
h^{1}(X)=(X,p_{1}(X),0)\in\operatorname{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then

\operatorname{H}^{i}(h^{1}(X))=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\neq 1,\\
\operatorname{H}^{1}(X)&\text{if }i=1,\end{cases}\qquad\operatorname{CH}^{i}(h^{1}(X))_{\mathbb{Q}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\neq 1,\\
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}&\text{if }i=1.\end{cases}

(3)

Let
h^{2d-1}(X)=(X,p_{2d-1}(X),0)\in\operatorname{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then

\operatorname{H}^{i}(h^{2d-1}(X))=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\neq 2d-1,\\
\operatorname{H}^{2d-1}(X)&\text{if }i=2d-1,\end{cases}\qquad\operatorname{CH}^{i}(h^{2d-1}(X))_{\mathbb{Q}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\neq 2d-1,\\
\mathrm{Alb}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}&\text{if }i=2d-1.\end{cases}

.

We now return to the decomposition of motives for varieties with Hodge structures of level 1, focusing in particular on varieties that are complete intersections. In all these cases, we have Chow–Künneth decomposition.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
X
be a smooth projective variety with
\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)=2m+1
in one of the following cases:

(1)

a complete intersection of two quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+3}
;

(2)

a complete intersection of three quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{2m+4}

(
with smooth discriminant curve
)
;

(3)

a cubic fivefold in
\mathbb{P}^{6}
;

(4)

a Fano threefold.

Then

h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{2m+1}(X)\simeq h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}.

Proof.

The statement compiles several known results, each corresponding to one of the cases for
X
. All arguments and properties involved (notably finite dimensionality and motivic decompositions) can be found in the following references:

(1)

For complete intersections of two quadrics, see
[
Rei72
, Theorem 4.8]
and
[
Lat21
, Theorem 2.1]
.

(2)

For complete intersections of three quadrics, see
[
NS09
, Theorem 1]
and
[
Bou16
, Theorem 4.3]
.

(3)

For cubic fivefolds in
\mathbb{P}^{6}
, the result follows from the criterion in
[
Via13
, Theorem 4]
, together with the fact that
\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\mathbb{Q}
for all
0\leq i\leq 5
.

(4)

For Fano threefolds, the statement follows from
[
GG12
, Theorems 8 and 15]
concerning the representability of zero-cycles.

If
X
is one of the above cases, then the motive
h(X)\in\text{Chow}(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{Q}}
is finite dimensional in the sense of Kimura. By
[
MNP13
, Corollary 5.6.4]
and the non-existence of phantom motives
4
4

4

i.e., non-trivial Chow motives which are homologically trivial.
, we obtain an integral étale Chow–Künneth decomposition of
h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
with

h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{2n-1}(X):=(X,p),\qquad p=\Delta_{X}-\sum_{i=0}^{2n-2}p_{2i}^{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X).

This reflects the fact that there are no transcendental cohomology classes in codimensions other than
2m+1
. In each case, the Hodge decomposition

\operatorname{H}^{2m+1}_{B}(X,\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{i+j=2m+1}\operatorname{H}^{i,j}(X)

has nonzero components only in
\operatorname{H}^{m+1,m}(X)
and
\operatorname{H}^{m,m+1}(X)
. Moreover, the intermediate Jacobians
J^{m+1}(X)
are abelian varieties. Thus, there exists a Chow–Künneth decomposition of the motive

h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}\to h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X),

so in particular we obtain a morphism
h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}\to h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
. The realization of the motive
h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}
corresponds to the first homology group of
J^{m+1}(X)
. Indeed, there is an isomorphism

\operatorname{H}^{B}_{1}(J^{m+1}(X),\mathbb{Z})(-m)\simeq\operatorname{H}^{2m+1}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

Explicitly, the
1
-motive associated to
J^{m+1}(X)
can be written as

h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}=(J^{m+1}(X),q,-m),

where
q\in\operatorname{End}(h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X)))
is the idempotent defining
h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}
as a direct factor of
h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}
. At the level of homological motives there is an isomorphism

h^{2m+1}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq h^{1}(J^{m+1}(X))_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}\in\text{Chow}_{\operatorname{hom}}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Since these motives are finite dimensional in the sense of Kimura, this isomorphism lifts to the category of Chow motives:

h^{2m+1}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq h^{1}(J^{m+1}(X))_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}\in\text{Chow}(k)_{\mathbb{Q}}.

As this is induced by an algebraic cycle, its integral image is generated by an integral étale class, by
[
Ros22
, Proposition 3.1.7]
. Therefore, the isomorphism lifts further to an integral isomorphism of étale Chow motives
h^{2m+1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)\simeq h^{1}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(J^{m+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{m}\in\text{Chow}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k)
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

We remark that both

From now on we restrict to varieties
X\in\operatorname{SmProj}_{\mathbb{C}}
for which the standard Lefschetz conjecture

holds. Let us recall the statement. Let

Its

\Lambda\circ\cdots\circ\Lambda
(

Conjecture 6.8

(Conjecture
B(X)
)
.

The topological correspondence

is algebraic, i.e. there exists

The conjecture

is known in the following cases (see e.g.

(1)

Curves and surfaces (consequences of the Lefschetz

(2)

Abelian varieties.

(3)

Varieties

(4)

Complete intersections in products of projective spaces.

(5)

Grassmannians.

(6)

Uniruled threefolds and unirational fourfolds.

Lemma 6.9
.

The conjecture
B(X)
holds when
X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a smooth double cover ramified over a hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of degree
2d
, with

.

Proof.

The argument is analogous to the proof that conjecture
B(X)
holds when

is an ample hypersurface in projective space (see, e.g.,
[

, Proposition 4.3]
). As explained in

[

, Proposition 1.4.7.(vii)]
, the key point is that the rational version of the weak Lefschetz theorem allows one to construct an algebraic class representing

from the corresponding class in the ambient space. Since

can be realized as an ample hypersurface in the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(1^{n+1},d)
, the argument carries over verbatim by invoking the weak Lefschetz theorem for rational cohomology of toric varieties due to Batyrev and Cox
[

]
(cf. the recent preprint
[

]
, where the pullback map is described at the level of integral cohomology).
∎

For

with

and

, consider the following coniveau-like filtration

\operatorname{N}^{i}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}\operatorname{H}_{B}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\operatorname{SmProj}_{\mathbb{C}}\\
\Gamma\in\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i+1}(C\times X)\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Im}\!\left\{\Gamma_{*}:\operatorname{H}^{1}(C,\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})\right\},

where

runs over all smooth projective curves. This defines a sub-Hodge structure of
\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})
of coniveau
i
. In addition, we consider the maximal sub-Hodge structure of
\operatorname{H}^{i+1,i}(X)\oplus\operatorname{H}^{i,i+1}(X)
, which we denote by

. Each of these Hodge structures has an associated intermediate Jacobian, denoted

and

, respectively. In these cases we quotient by the lattices

\operatorname{N}^{i}\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{Z}):=\operatorname{N}^{i}\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})\cap\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}),\qquad V_{\mathbb{Z}}:=V\cap\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}_{B}(X,\mathbb{Z}),

in the corresponding definitions. Both constructions yield abelian varieties, since their tangent spaces are contained in
\operatorname{H}^{i+1,i}(X)\oplus\operatorname{H}^{i,i+1}(X)
. Conjecturally, they coincide by the generalized Hodge conjecture in weight
2i+1
and level

.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension

. Then for all

is a direct factor of the motive
h^{+}(X)
, where

is associated with the projector

p^{+}(X):=\Delta-p_{0}(X)-p_{1}(X)-p_{2d-1}(X)-p_{2d}(X).

Moreover, the generalized Hodge conjecture holds in weight
2i+1
and level

is a submotive of

Proof.

The fact that the motives
h_{1}(J_{\operatorname{alg}}^{i+1}(X))\otimes\mathbf{L}^{i}

follows from
[
Via13
]
; we briefly recall the construction of the relevant projectors.

Consider
\operatorname{N}^{i}\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})
and
V
as the sub-Hodge structure of
\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{C})
described above. By definition, there exists a curve

(not necessarily connected) such that for some

we obtain a surjection

\Gamma_{*}:\operatorname{H}^{1}(C,\mathbb{Z})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{N}^{i}\operatorname{H}^{2i+1}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

Thus the induced map of abelian varieties

is also surjective. By the semisimplicity of the category of abelian varieties up to isogeny, there exists

such that

for some

\Gamma^{t}_{*}\circ\Lambda:J^{i}_{a}(X)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(C)

Now consider the dual morphism

for a polarization
\lambda:J^{i+1}_{\operatorname{alg}}(X)^{\vee}\to J^{i+1}_{\operatorname{alg}}(X)

\lambda
such that
\Gamma_{\lambda}=\alpha\circ\Lambda^{-1}\circ\alpha^{\vee}
, set
\gamma:=\Gamma_{\lambda}
. Then define the projector

\pi_{2i+1,i}:=\Gamma_{*}\circ\gamma\circ\Gamma^{t}_{*}\circ L^{2i+1}\in\operatorname{CH}^{d}(X\times X)_{\mathbb{Q}}.

By
[
Via13
, Theorem 2.8]
there is an isomorphism of motives

\pi_{2i+1,i}\circ L^{2i+1}\circ\pi_{2(d-i)-1,d-i-1}:(X,\pi_{2i,i},0)\to(X,\pi_{2(d-i)-1,d-i-1},2i+1-d),

while by
[
Via13
, Theorem 2.9]

\pi_{2i+1,i+1}
we obtain an isomorphism of motives

(X,\pi_{2i+1,i+1})\simeq h_{1}(J^{i+1}_{\operatorname{alg}}(X))(i).

By the theory of
[
RS16
]
, these projectors and morphisms lift to
\text{Chow}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(k)

h_{1}(J^{i+1}_{\operatorname{alg}}(X))(i)
is indeed a subobject of
h_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
.

For the last statement, assume the generalized Hodge conjecture holds in level
1
and weight
2i+1
. Then
J_{\operatorname{alg}}^{i+1}(X)\simeq J_{H}^{i+1}(X)
, hence

is a direct factor of

Here

with both isomorphisms induced by algebraic cycles, due to the Lefschetz-type assumptions. Considering the injection

yielding the commutative diagram

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{H}_{1}(C,\mathbb{Q}(1))}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{H}_{1}(J_{H}^{i+1}(X),\mathbb{Q}(i))}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i\circ h^{i}}

7.
Further Fano manifolds with Hodge level one

7.1.
Gushel–Mukai varieties

A Gushel–Mukai variety of dimension
n\in\{3,4,5,6\}
over a field
k
is a smooth projective variety of the form

X=\mathrm{CGr}(2,V_{5})\cap\mathbb{P}(W_{n+5})\cap Q,

where
V_{5}
is a
5
-dimensional
k
-vector space,
\mathrm{CGr}(2,V_{5})\subset\mathbb{P}(k\oplus\wedge^{2}V_{5})
denotes the cone over the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,V_{5})
,
W_{n+5}\subset k\oplus\wedge^{2}V_{5}
is a linear subspace of dimension
n+5
, and
Q\subset\mathbb{P}(k\oplus\wedge^{2}V_{5})
is a quadric of dimension
9
.

Since our focus is on odd-dimensional varieties whose middle cohomology carries a Hodge structure of level one, it suffices to restrict attention to the cases of threefolds and fivefolds.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
X
be a Gushel–Mukai threefold or fivefold over
\mathbb{C}
. Then

\operatorname{CH}^{i}(X)\ \simeq\ \operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{i}(X)\quad\text{for all }i\geq 0.

Proof.

When
\dim(X)=3
, the variety is a Fano threefold, and the statement follows from the discussion in §5.1.
For
\dim(X)=5
, the argument parallels that of the case of a five-dimensional complete intersection of three quadrics, with the additional observation that
N=1
in the decomposition of the diagonal from Theorem
2.5
.
∎

7.2.
Quartic double fivefolds

We say that
X
is a
quartic double fivefold
if
X
is a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched along a smooth quartic hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Such a variety
X
is a Fano fivefold of Hodge level
1
, whose intermediate Jacobian
J(X)
is a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
142
.

Proof.

For a double (ramified) covering, by
[
EV92
, Lemma 3.22]
one has

\text{H}^{q}(X,\Omega^{p}_{X})\simeq\text{H}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{2m+1},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}})\ \oplus\ \text{H}^{q}\big(\mathbb{P}^{2m+1},\Omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}}^{p}(\log B)(-3)\big).

When
p+q=2m+1
, Bott’s vanishing theorem implies
\text{H}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{2m+1},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}})=0
.
To compute the logarithmic term we use the Poincaré residue sequence

0\to\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-3)\to\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-3)\to\Omega^{p-1}_{\mathbb{P}}(-3)\to 0,

whose associated long exact sequence in cohomology reads

\displaystyle 0\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-3)\big)\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-3)\big)\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-3)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\to\text{H}^{1}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-3)\big)\to\cdots\to\text{H}^{2m+1}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-3)\big)\to\text{H}^{2m+1}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-3)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\to\text{H}^{2m+1}\big(\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-3)\big)\to 0.

Bott’s theorem gives
\text{H}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{2m+1},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}})=0
unless
p=q
, hence for
p+q=2m+1
we obtain

\text{H}^{q}\big(\mathbb{P}^{2m+1},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2m+1}}(\log B)(-3)\big)\simeq\text{H}^{q}\big(B,\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-3)\big).

By
[
Vo24
, Theorem D.(2)]
,
\text{H}^{q}(B,\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-3))=0
if
p+q=2m+1
and either
\frac{m-1}{3}\geq p
or
-\frac{m+2}{3}+(2m+2)\leq p
.
Thus the potentially nonzero Hodge numbers
h^{p,\,2m+1-p}
occur only for
p\in\big(\frac{m-1}{3},\frac{5m+4}{3}\big)
.

Now consider the specific case of a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched over a quartic hypersurface
B
.
In this setting, the analogous residue sequence becomes

0\to\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-2)\to\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-2)\to\Omega^{p-1}_{\mathbb{P}}(-2)\to 0,

and its long exact sequence in cohomology is

\displaystyle 0\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-2)\big)\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-2)\big)\to\text{H}^{0}\big(\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-2)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\to\text{H}^{1}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-2)\big)\to\cdots\to\text{H}^{5}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(-2)\big)\to\text{H}^{5}\big(\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}}(\log B)(-2)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\to\text{H}^{5}\big(\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-2)\big)\to 0.

By Bott’s vanishing theorem on
\mathbb{P}^{5}
,
\text{H}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{5},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{5}}(l))
vanishes except in the following cases:
(1)
p=q
and
l=0
; (2)
q=0
and
l>p
; (3)
q=5
,
l<-5+p
.
In our case this implies

\text{H}^{q}(X,\Omega^{p}_{X})\simeq\text{H}^{q}\big(\mathbb{P}^{5},\Omega_{\mathbb{P}^{5}}^{p}(\log B)(-2)\big),

and for
p+q=5
we have

\text{H}^{q}\big(\mathbb{P}^{5},\Omega^{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{5}}(\log B)(-2)\big)\simeq\text{H}^{q}\big(B,\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-2)\big).

By
[
Vo24
, Theorem D.(2)]
,
\text{H}^{q}(B,\Omega^{p-1}_{B}(-2))=0
for
p+q=5
whenever
|q-p|>1
.
The only nontrivial Hodge number in this range is
h^{2,2}(B)=142
, which is the Hodge number of a quartic hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
From this, one deduces the cohomology groups of
X
:

\text{H}^{k}(X,\mathbb{Z})\ \simeq\ \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus 284},&\text{if $k=5$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This shows that
X
has Hodge level
1
, and its intermediate Jacobian
J(X)
is a principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
142
, as claimed.
∎

We summarize here the cycle-theoretic properties of quartic double fivefolds.

Let
X
be a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched along a smooth quartic hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
The covering map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{5}
restricts to a Galois cover of degree
2
,

X\setminus f^{-1}(B)\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb{P}^{5}\setminus B.

We have the following:

•

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{0}(X)\ \simeq\ \operatorname{CH}^{0}(X)\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}
, generated by the fundamental class of
X
.

•

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{1}(X)\ \simeq\ \operatorname{CH}^{1}(X)\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}
, generated by the class of a hyperplane section, by Lefschetz’s
(1,1)
theorem.

•

\operatorname{CH}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}^{2}(X)\ \simeq\ \operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}
, and consequently
\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))=0
.
Indeed, since
X
is unirational (
[
Bea15
, Proposition 5]
), we have
\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Z}(2))=0
.
Then, by
[
CV12
, Théorème 2.7]
,
\mathrm{Griff}^{2}(X)=0
.
Using the decomposition of the diagonal,
[
BS83
, Theorem 1]
implies that
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}
is weakly representable, and since
\text{H}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is trivial, we conclude
\operatorname{CH}^{2}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}=0
(by the bound on the dimension of the representative in
[
Mur85
, Theorem 1.9]
).
Finally, by
[
CV12
, Théorème 3.7]
,
\text{H}^{3}_{\text{nr}}(X,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))
is isomorphic to the obstruction to the integral Hodge conjecture in degree
4
, which vanishes by the decomposition of the diagonal.

•

Since
X
contains a line, the natural map
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)\to\text{H}_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is surjective.
Moreover,
\mathrm{Griff}^{4}(X)=0
, so
\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
.
By
[
Suz20
, Theorem 1.1]
,
\operatorname{CH}^{\mathrm{alg}}_{1}(X)_{\mathrm{tors}}=0
, hence
\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
is uniquely divisible.
Therefore
\operatorname{CH}^{4}(X)\simeq\operatorname{CH}^{4}_{\textup{\'{e}t}}(X)
.
In particular, any curve
C\subset X
is algebraically equivalent to a sum of lines contained in
X
.

•

\operatorname{CH}^{5}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the class of a point, since
X
is unirational.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
X
be a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched along a quartic hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
. Then
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\mathbb{Q}
.
For such
X
, there exists a multiplicative Chow–Künneth decomposition of the form

\pi_{X}^{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\,{}^{t}\Gamma_{f}\circ\pi^{i}_{\mathbb{P}^{5}}\circ\Gamma_{f},&\text{if }i<5,\\[3.99994pt]
\pi_{X}^{10-i},&\text{if }i>5,\\[3.99994pt]
\Delta_{X}-\sum_{k\neq 5}\pi_{X}^{k},&\text{if }i=5,\end{cases}

where
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{5}
is the covering map and
\pi^{i}_{\mathbb{P}^{5}}
denotes the Chow–Künneth projectors of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
. Since the action of
\pi_{X}^{i}
on
\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
factors through
\operatorname{CH}^{j}(\mathbb{P}^{5})_{\mathbb{Q}}
, we have

(\pi_{X}^{2i})_{*}\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\ \simeq\ \begin{cases}\mathbb{Q},&\text{if }i=j,\\[1.99997pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad(\pi_{X}^{2i+1})_{*}\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\ \simeq\ \begin{cases}\operatorname{CH}^{j}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}},&\text{if }i=2,\\[1.99997pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It follows that
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
must be torsion.
Indeed, if it were not, then we would have

\text{H}^{3}(X,\mathbb{Q})\otimes\text{H}^{7}(X,\mathbb{Q})\ \neq\ 0,

since
\pi_{X}^{5}\neq 0
, which is impossible in this case.
Therefore
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\mathbb{Q}
, and hence
\operatorname{CH}_{1}(X)\simeq\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
X
be a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{5}
branched along a quartic hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{5}
. Then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
and a decomposition of the diagonal

m\Delta_{X}=m[x\times X]+m\ell\times h+Z^{\prime},

where
x\in X
is a point,
\ell\subset X
is a line,
h\in\operatorname{CH}^{1}(X)
is the generator, and
Z^{\prime}
is supported on
X\times T
with
T
a closed subset of pure dimension
3
in
X
.

Proposition 7.5
.

The Griffiths group
\mathrm{Griff}^{3}(X)
is torsion.

Proof.

Note first that
T
is not necessarily smooth nor connected. Let
\widetilde{T}\to T
be a desingularization, with
\widetilde{T}=\coprod_{i}\widetilde{T}_{i}
, and define the composite map
\widetilde{i}\colon\widetilde{T}\to T\hookrightarrow X
. Let
\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X\times\widetilde{T})
be such that its image is a multiple
m^{\prime}Z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X\times T)
. We have

\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(\widetilde{T})\simeq\prod_{i}\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(\widetilde{T}_{i}),\qquad\mathrm{Alb}(\widetilde{T})\simeq\prod_{i}\mathrm{Alb}(\widetilde{T}_{i}).

Now let
\alpha\in\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X)
. The action of
mm^{\prime}\Delta_{X}
gives

(mm^{\prime}\Delta_{X})_{*}(\alpha)=mm^{\prime}\alpha=\widetilde{i}_{*}(\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{*}(\alpha))\in\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X).

Since
\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{*}(\alpha)\in\operatorname{CH}^{1}(\widetilde{T})
, it is homologically equivalent to zero, hence also algebraically equivalent to zero. This implies that

mm^{\prime}\alpha=\widetilde{i}_{*}(\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{*}(\alpha))

is algebraically equivalent to zero, and therefore
\mathrm{Griff}^{3}(X)
is a torsion group of order dividing
mm^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 7.6
.

The restriction of the Abel–Jacobi map

\Phi_{X}\colon\operatorname{CH}^{3}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}\ \longrightarrow\ J^{5}(X)

is surjective and has finite kernel.

Proof.

Using the same notation as in the previous proposition, we first prove the surjectivity of
\Phi_{X}
, following an argument similar to
[
FT19
, Proposition 18]

Proof.

Consider the Cartesian square

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi^{\prime}:=\Phi\times_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}P}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

and set
t=\psi\circ u:\Phi^{\prime}\to\mathbb{G}(r,n)
. A fiber of
\gamma:G_{P}(r)\to\mathbb{G}(r,n)
is the restriction of
|\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)|
to
Z_{h}:=p^{-1}(h)
, i.e., the set of
r
-planes in
t^{-1}(h)
. Thus

\gamma^{-1}(h)\simeq\mathbb{P}\left(\left(t|_{Z_{h}}\right)_{*}\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\right)\simeq\mathbb{P}\left(t_{*}\rho^{\prime*}\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\big|_{h}\right).

By base change and the formula for projective bundles,

G_{P}(r)\simeq\mathbb{P}\left(\psi_{*}\rho^{*}p_{*}\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\right)\simeq\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^{d}Q^{\vee}).

The dimension formula follows since this is the projectivization of a vector bundle of rank
\binom{d+r}{d}+1
over
\mathbb{G}(r,n)
.
∎

Definition A.4
.

Let
X
be a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
ramified along a hypersurface of degree
2d
. We define the
Fano scheme of
r
-planes
of
X
as

F_{r}(X):=\{\,h_{r}\in G_{P}(r)\mid h_{r}\subset X\,\}\subset G_{P}(r).

At a smooth point
h_{r}\in F_{r}(X)
, the Zariski tangent space is
H^{0}(N_{h_{r}/X})
, where
N_{h_{r}/X}
denotes the normal bundle. From the short exact sequence

0\to N_{h_{r}/X}\to N_{h_{r}/P}|_{h_{r}}\to N_{X/P}|_{h_{r}}\to 0,

and using that
X
and
h_{r}
are cut out respectively by sections of
\mathscr{O}_{P}(2)
and
\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\oplus p^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)^{\oplus(n-r)}
, we obtain

N_{X/P}=\mathscr{O}_{P}(2)|_{X},\qquad N_{h_{r}/P}=\left(\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\oplus p^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)^{\oplus(n-r)}\right)\big|_{h_{r}}.

Thus,

0\to N_{h_{r}/X}\to\mathscr{O}_{h_{r}}(d)\oplus\mathscr{O}_{h_{r}}(1)^{\oplus(n-r)}\xrightarrow{\alpha}\mathscr{O}_{h_{r}}(2d)\to 0.

Let
\mathfrak{X}\subset\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(\mathscr{O}_{P}(2)))
be the parameter space of double covers of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Consider the incidence correspondence

I_{r}:=\{\,(h_{r},X)\in G_{P}(r)\times\mathfrak{X}\mid h_{r}\subset X\,\}

with projections
p_{G}:I_{r}\to G_{P}(r)
and
p_{\mathfrak{X}}:I_{r}\to\mathfrak{X}
. The fiber
p_{\mathfrak{X}}^{-1}(X)
is
F_{r}(X)
, while
p_{G}^{-1}(h_{r})
consists of double covers containing
h_{r}
.

Since
\mathfrak{X}\simeq\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(d))
, one finds

p_{G}^{-1}(h_{r})\simeq\ker\left\{\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}\!\left((\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(d))^{\vee}\right)\to\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}\!\left((\mathscr{O}_{P}(1)\oplus p^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)^{\oplus(n-r)})^{\vee}\right)\right\}.

This kernel has codimension
\binom{2d+r}{2d}
, hence
I_{r}
has codimension
\binom{2d+r}{2d}
in
G_{P}(r)\times\mathfrak{X}
. It follows that the
expected dimension
of
F_{r}(X)
is

\delta(n,d,r):=(r+1)(n-r)+\binom{d+r}{d}-\binom{2d+r}{2d}.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
X
be a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
of degree
d
.

(1)

If
\delta(n,d,r)<0
, then for a general
X
the scheme
F_{r}(X)
is empty.

(2)

If
\delta(n,d,r)\geq 0
, then
F_{r}(X)
is non-empty.

Proof.

If
\delta(n,d,r)<0
, then
\dim I_{r}<\dim\mathfrak{X}
, so for a general
X
the fiber
p_{\mathfrak{X}}^{-1}(X)
is empty. The second statement follows from the the standard argument consisting in showing the existence of an explicit
(h_{r},X)\in I_{r}
such that
p_{\mathfrak{X}}
is smooth at this point (see eg.
[
Ska23
, Proposition V.3.8]
or
[
DM98
, Théorème 2.1]
).
∎

Remark A.6
.

(1)

The Fano scheme
F_{r}(X)
is the zero locus of a section of
\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}(Q)
. Thus

[F_{r}(X)]=c_{\binom{2d+r}{2d}}\!\left(\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}(Q)\right)\in\operatorname{CH}^{*}(G_{P}(r)).

(2)

If
X\xrightarrow{m:1}\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a cyclic covering of order
m
ramified along a hypersurface of degree
d
, then
X
can be described as the zero locus of a section of
\mathscr{O}_{P}(m)
. In this case, the expected dimension formula generalizes to

\dim F_{r}(X)=(r+1)(n-r)+\binom{d+r}{d}-\binom{md+r}{md}.

Proposition A.7
.

Let
X\xrightarrow{2:1}\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a smooth double covering branched along a hypersurface
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of degree
2d
. Fix
r\geq 1
. Assume
F_{r}(X)
is smooth, then the canonical bundle of
F_{r}(X)
is

\omega_{F_{r}(X)}\simeq\left(\gamma^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(a+b-n-1)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}\!\left(2\binom{2d+r}{2d}-\binom{d+r}{d}-1\right)\right)\big|_{F_{r}(X)},

where

a=\begin{cases}\frac{d(d+1)}{2},&r=1,\\
\frac{r^{d+1}-r(d+1)+d}{(r-1)^{2}},&r\neq 1,\end{cases}\quad b=\begin{cases}d(2d+1),&r=1,\\
\frac{r^{2d+1}-r(2d+1)+2d}{(r-1)^{2}},&r\neq 1.\end{cases}

Proof.

By Remark
A.6
, there exists
s\in H^{0}(G_{P}(r),\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}(Q))
with
F_{r}(X)\simeq V(s)
. Thus

\omega_{F_{r}(X)}\simeq\left(\omega_{G_{P}(r)}\otimes\det\mathscr{F}\right)\big|_{F_{r}(X)},

where
\mathscr{F}:=\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}(2)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathrm{Sym}^{2d}(Q)
.

The morphism
\gamma:G_{P}(r)\to\mathbb{G}(r,n)
is the
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{d+r}{d}}
-bundle obtained as the projectivization of
\mathscr{E}:=\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^{d}(Q^{\vee})
. Using the splitting principle, one checks

\det(\mathscr{E})\simeq\det(\mathrm{Sym}^{d}(Q^{\vee}))\simeq\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(a).

Since
\omega_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}\simeq\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(-n-1)
, we have

\omega_{G_{P}(r)}=\gamma^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(a-n-1)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}\!\left(-\binom{d+r}{d}-1\right).

Similarly,

\det(\mathscr{F})\simeq\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}\!\left(2\binom{2d+r}{2d}\right)\otimes\gamma^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(b).

Combining, we obtain

\omega_{F_{r}(X)}\simeq\left(\gamma^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{G}(r,n)}(a+b-n-1)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{G_{P}(r)}\!\left(2\binom{2d+r}{2d}-\binom{d+r}{d}-1\right)\right)\big|_{F_{r}(X)},

as long as
F_{r}(X)
is smooth.
∎
