Introduction

Conflicts, wars and mass shootings, along with natural disasters like earthquakes and wildfires, are creating ‘blood deserts’ worldwide. A ‘blood desert’ is a geographical area where it is impossible to meet the local demand for blood components timely and affordably in at least 75% of transfusion cases.1 2 Notably, 40% of annual blood donations originate from high-income countries, which serve only 16% of the world’s population.1 This disparity leaves the majority of the global community underserved. Recent modelling studies suggest that the commonly cited donation target rate of 10–20 units per 1000 people significantly underestimates the actual need.1 Moreover, no research has effectively captured the true need in regions with heightened blood demands, especially those experiencing conflicts and disasters, which pose significant challenges.

Managing blood supplies during emergencies

The WHO recently issued guidance on managing blood supplies during emergencies.3 It highlights the need for a coordinated approach, including risk assessment, preparedness and operational continuity. These measures are important not only for meeting immediate demands but also for ensuring the resilience of blood supply systems against recurring disasters. The WHO actively promotes dialogue on these strategies among global healthcare providers. This follows the adoption of the integrated emergency, critical and operative care (ECO) resolution. The resolution aims for universal health coverage and protection from health emergencies (Resolution WHA76.2), adopted in May 2023. Over 80 member states supported this resolution, highlighting the urgent need to enhance health systems, ensuring the provision of emergency care, including safe and timely blood transfusions for everyone.

Walking blood banks

A ‘walking blood bank’ (WBB) is an emergency transfusion protocol in which blood donors are mobilised on demand from a predefined pool. The collected blood is then tested, typically using rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) to verify donor eligibility and ensure compatibility between the donor’s and recipient’s blood.4 Innovative solutions like the ‘Heroes in Arms’ programme showcase a proactive approach to this protocol, providing a clear implementation roadmap inspired by military-style ‘WBBs’.5 They mobilise a preselected cohort of whole-blood donors rapidly during mass casualty incidents (MCIs), bridging the gap between immediate blood demand and traditional supply mechanisms. The programme has led to a 48% reduction in mortality rates among traumatic injuries, showcasing its potential to save lives in crisis situations.5 These strategies are crucial for addressing blood shortages in emergencies and demonstrate the need for collaborative efforts, including community involvement, to improve emergency care outcomes. Healthcare providers in the USA validate and use prehospital whole blood transfusion programmes.6 They consider them essential for treating unstable trauma patients with ongoing blood loss. It is the preferred resuscitative fluid for combat casualties.6 However, many blood deserts still lack this critical, lifesaving resource. This unavailability diminishes the survival chances for patients with active bleeding. Low-titre O-positive whole blood is an effective strategy employed in many trauma centres and lower-tier community hospitals during MCIs, showing no difference in patient outcomes and ensuring safe transfusions.7 In countries like Malawi, Papua New Guinea and South Africa, blood donation relies on leveraging social connections, which is especially important for rare blood types.8 Based on this concept, the Smart Blood Query project in the South East Asia region used Rapid Short Messaging Services to link a database of prescreened donors, thereby improving blood donations during disasters and other times of need. This platform allows both donors and recipients to access it during crises, facilitating just-in-time processes similar to WBB.9 The frameworks from the ‘Heroes in Arms’ initiative and the Smart Blood Query project show significant potential to enhance blood availability and transfusion capabilities during MCIs globally.5 9

Drone-based blood delivery

Drones are powered aircraft that operate without a human onboard. They can be designed to carry various types of medical or non-medical supplies and can overcome geographical obstacles, decrease carbon emissions and simplify emergency response logistics. Drones can cover long distances quickly, access remote and isolated regions, avoid road blockages and closures that can obstruct conventional blood delivery methods, and eliminate the risks associated with human transport in hazardous conditions, such as natural disasters or conflict zones.4 The response to the 2008 Wenchuan Earthquake in China is a critical example of learning from natural disasters.10 These lessons have led to improvements in MCI response strategies and resource management. During this catastrophic event, emergency teams collected 117 000 units of blood products but only distributed 70 415 units.10 This discrepancy highlights the need for better and faster emergency blood allocation methods in disaster scenarios. Equipping prehospital systems in blood deserts with whole blood and rapid transfusion equipment is crucial for improving outcomes and reducing deaths. Innovations like drone-based blood delivery, used before or alongside Emergency Medical Services, can transform such settings.1 11 In Rwanda, drones have been employed to transport blood products directly to regional centres, enabling physicians to treat patients requiring transfusions locally, rather than transporting them to the capital city. This approach eliminates the need for storage of blood products, as they are delivered on demand, facilitating the treatment of medical emergencies.11 Despite being more expensive per minute than ambulance transport, drones’ faster delivery times have proven cost-effective overall.12 There are however certain challenges, including rapidly identifying and mobilising donors, and ensuring the safe and sterile collection and transport of blood. Legally, drone operators must navigate stringent aviation laws and healthcare regulations that govern the transport and handling of blood products. Logistically, maintaining the integrity of blood supplies during rapid transport requires advanced solutions. These include developing specialised containers that preserve temperature and sterility, and robust tracking systems to oversee the journey from donor to recipient.

Framework for improved blood availability

In this article, we propose a comprehensive framework that has the potential to improve blood availability and transfusion capabilities during MCIs (figure 1). This framework combines innovative strategies from the literature to help navigate the complexities of a globally unstable landscape marked by environmental and geopolitical disasters. It involves assessing blood availability at trauma centres and nearby facilities. If supplies are insufficient, alternative strategies such as activating civilian WBBs or drone blood delivery are employed. Rapid infectious disease testing ensures the safety of freshly donated blood, enabling immediate crossmatching and transfusion. One significant concern is the risk of undetected transfusion-transmitted infections, as RDTs often have lower sensitivity and specificity for detecting HIV, hepatitis B and C, and syphilis compared with more advanced screening methods like nucleic acid testing, which are not routinely available in low-resource settings.13 Due to resource constraints, hospital blood banks in South Asia and Africa have adopted RDTs for their WBBs, a practice acknowledged and endorsed by the WHO and various national transfusion services.13 The FDA has also recently issued a statement supporting the transfusion of blood from HIV patients deemed low risk based on individual risk assessment, prioritising the immediate need to save lives in MCIs over potential long-term disease concerns.14 This comprehensive framework enhances resilience and responsiveness in blood supply management, critical in regions prone to environmental and geopolitical disasters. Bringing whole blood to patients in resource-poor and disaster-stricken locations via drone-based delivery and WBBs urgently demands our attention. Programmes like ‘Heroes in Arms’ show significant potential for adapting innovative strategies to diverse healthcare environments globally. Healthcare providers can modify these programmes to align with varying levels of infrastructure and medical capability. Collaborative efforts with technology firms are leading to pilot projects that explore the feasibility of drones swiftly and safely transporting medical supplies across different settings. Successful case studies from Rwanda show how drones can significantly cut delivery times for critical blood products.11 While innovative approaches like WBBs and drone-based delivery systems offer immediate solutions in emergency settings, increasing blood collection from voluntary non-remunerated blood donors remains essential for improving long-term supply, safety and sustainability.15

Conclusion

As healthcare leaders gather at the World Health Assembly in May every year, and at other high-level meetings throughout the year, we have an opportunity to address the critical issues of ‘blood deserts’ and emergency medical responses on a global scale. Governments and non-governmental organisations need to prioritise these issues in national and international health agendas, ensuring that the frameworks for emergency healthcare delivery are not only proposed but actively implemented. This concerted effort is essential for transforming the landscape of global health emergency preparedness and response, making universal and timely access to essential medical resources a reality for all, particularly in areas hardest hit by disasters and conflicts today. To effectively address blood deserts in disaster settings, the WHO and other international bodies must actively endorse and implement a comprehensive framework that integrates innovative strategies. This fosters collaboration between scientific, clinical and local policy-making communities. Additionally, integrating these strategies into national health policies is essential, ensuring that all countries are well-prepared to handle medical crises.
