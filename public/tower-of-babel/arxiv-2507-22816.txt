Kan Approximations of the Persistent Homology Transform
Shreya Arya, Justin Curry

Abstract
. The persistent homology transform (PHT) of a subset M ⊂ ℝ d M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} is a map PHT ​ ( M ) : 𝕊 d − 1 → 𝐃𝐠𝐦 \text{PHT}(M):\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbf{Dgm} from the unit sphere to the space of persistence diagrams. This map assigns to each direction v ∈ 𝕊 d − 1 v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1} the persistent homology of the filtration of M M in direction v v . In practice, one can only sample the map PHT ​ ( M ) \text{PHT}(M) at a finite set of directions A ⊂ 𝕊 d − 1 A\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1} . This suggests two natural questions: (1) Can we interpolate the PHT from this finite sample of directions to the entire sphere? If so, (2) can we prove that the resulting interpolation is close to the true PHT? In this paper we show that if we can sample the PHT at the module level, where we have information about how homology from each direction interacts, a ready-made interpolation theory due to Bubenik, de Silva, and Nanda using Kan extensions can answer both of these questions in the affirmative. A close inspection of those techniques shows that we can infer the PHT from a finite sample of heights from each direction as well. Our paper presents the first known results for approximating the PHT from finite directional and scalar data.

Although the original motivation for this Euler calculus

[

With the advent of persistent homology in the early 2000s

One major drawback of topological transforms is that interpolation and approximation is inherently difficult.
At first glance, the discrete nature of topological invariants seems to provide an insurmountable obstacle to interpolation.
For example, if a shape
M
has
\chi(M\cap W)=3
and
\chi(M\cap W^{\prime})=4
for two nearby hyperplanes
W
and
W^{\prime}
it makes no sense to linearly interpolate or average these values for a third hyperplane
W^{\prime\prime}
because the Euler characteristic

is inherently integer-valued.
Modern applied topology has learned to sidestep these kinds of apparent instabilities by working with 1-parameter families of topological invariants—suitably called
persistent
topological invariants—where continuity between these signatures can be proved.
For example, if one filters a shape
M
in direction
v
and studies either the Euler curve or the Betti curves (one for each
n\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
)

\text{ECT}(M)(v,t):=\chi(h_{v}(-\infty,t])\qquad\text{or}\qquad\text{BCT}_{n}(M)(v,t):=\beta_{n}(h_{v}(-\infty,t]),

where

is the height function in direction

[

shows that all of these curves are continuous in the argument

, for target

and

.
However, once again, averaging or interpolating these curves between nearby directions does not make sense due to their piece-wise constant integer nature.
Surprisingly, another shift in perspective, where one presents the Euler and Betti curves as sums of carefully defined indicator functions---indicator functions supported on the bars in the persistent homology barcode---allows a continuous interpolation theory, explained below.
This also serves as a pedagogical point for demonstrating why the PHT’s extra structure provides a distinct advantage over the ECT, even though both are equally expressive

As reviewed in

in direction

in direction

with the property that

can be computed by counting the intervals in
\mathcal{D}_{n}(v)
containing
[s,t]

Specializing this property to

, and recalling that the Betti number
\beta_{n}
is the dimension of the homology group

, shows that the Betti curve can be expressed as a sum of indicator functions supported on intervals in the diagram
\mathcal{D}_{n}(v)
:

\text{BCT}_{n}(v,\bullet)=\sum_{[b_{i},d_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}(v)}\mathbbm{1}_{[b_{i},d_{i})}(\bullet)

This perspective suggests a path forward to interpolating topological information between directions.
If one is given a partial matching between diagrams
\mathcal{D}_{n}(v)
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}(v^{\prime})
for nearby directions

and
v^{\prime}
, then the straight line interpolation
(1-\lambda)(b_{i},d_{i})+\lambda(b^{\prime}_{i},d^{\prime}_{i})
between the endpoints of each pair of matched intervals provides a path between these persistence diagrams, and hence a candidate extension of the PHT along a path between
v
and
v^{\prime}
.
Unfortunately, the data of a matching between persistence diagrams is often missing and even when one can be found, it might not be unique.
This lack of canonical matchings is at the heart of several subtle technical difficulties that one encounters when trying to perform regression in the space of persistence diagrams
\mathbf{Dgm}
:

[
22
]
shows that sets of persistence diagrams do not have unique Fréchet means and
[
23
]
shows that even when these means are unique they might not vary continuously.

In this paper we circumvent these problems and answer two fundamental questions about the PHT, both in the affirmative:

(1)

(Interpolation) Given a set of directions
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\text{PHT}(M)
for these directions, does there exist a continuous extension
\overline{\text{PHT}}(M):\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbf{Dgm}
of this information?

(2)

(Approximation) Given an extension
\overline{\text{PHT}}(M)
, can we prove reasonable error bounds between it and the true
\text{PHT}(M)
?

We achieve these results by utilizing pre-existing work
[
24
,
25
]
by Bubenik, de Silva, and Nanda, which shows how category theory can provide canonical methods—Kan extensions—for interpolating between persistence diagrams,
as long as one works on the module level
.
These methods are especially nice because the provided extensions are Lipschitz, thus ensuring that small changes in direction correspond to small changes in persistent homology.
In answering the second question, we prove that the distance between the true PHT of shape
M
and the extension
\overline{\text{PHT}}(M)
is bounded by twice the Hausdorff distance between the sphere and the approximating direction set
A
.
This has a nice online learning interpretation: as persistent homology from more directions is learned, we can update our inferred
\overline{\text{PHT}}(M)
to more closely approximate the truth.

We note that
[
24
,
25
]
also provides some flexibility when choosing a metric on the sphere of directions.
There are two obvious choices here—the Euclidean metric
d_{2}
and the round (or geodesic) metric
d_{g}
—and many of our results are stated for both choices.
The choice of the round metric achieves optimal error bounds between the Kan-extended
\overline{\text{PHT}}(M)
and the true PHT, but this optimality comes at a conceptual cost.
Because the round metric is best understood in terms of an angular coordinate system on the sphere, our persistence modules are naturally indexed by the totally ordered set
\Theta=[-\pi,0]
, instead of
\mathbb{R}
.
This requires invoking the language of generalized persistence modules
[
26
]
, which introduces some subtleties when defining the interleaving distance.
On the other hand, working with the Euclidean metric trades optimal error bounds for conceptual simplicity and better matches existing literature on the PHT.
For this reason, we choose to present both using the notation
\text{PHT}^{X}
to indicate that the indexing set
X
can change, depending on one’s preference.
For experts tempted to skip
Section

2
, we caution them to pay careful attention starting at

, as our
\Theta
-coordinate system for the PHT is original to this work.

In
Section

3
, we demonstrate how the theory of Kan extensions provides a constructive answer to our first question (on interpolation) and compute the left, right, and center Kan extensions for a few simple examples.
In
Section

4
, we answer our second question (on the approximation error) by deriving precise error bounds.
We first prove a point-wise, direction-by-direction interleaving bound, between the true PHT and its extension and provide an example showing that our bound is tight.
This then leads naturally to a global error bound between the PHT and it’s approximation in the
L^{\infty}
(sup) norm.
Finally, we generalize our results to the most practical setting by providing an error bound for extensions from
fully discrete data
where both directions and filtration values are sampled.

2.
Background

In this section, we establish the mathematical framework necessary for our main results.

2.1.
O-minimality

We work within the framework of o-minimal structures due to its tame topological properties. This framework encompasses a broad class of geometric objects while excluding pathological constructions such as the Cantor set.

Definition 2.1
.

An
o-minimal structure

\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{d}\}
, is a specification of a boolean algebra of subsets
\mathcal{O}_{d}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each natural number
d\geq 0
, satisfying certain axioms.
These are: we assume that
\mathcal{O}_{1}
contains only finite unions of points and intervals.
We further require that
\mathcal{O}
be closed under certain product and projection operators, i.e. if
A\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
, then
A\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}\times A
are in
\mathcal{O}_{d+1}
;
and if
A\in\mathcal{O}_{d+1}
, then
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is axis-aligned projection.
It is a fact that
\mathcal{O}
contains all semi-algebraic sets, but may contain certain regular expansions of these sets.
Elements of
\mathcal{O}
are called
definable sets
and
compact
definable sets are called
constructible sets
.
The subcollection of constructible subsets in
\mathcal{O}_{d}
is denoted
\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Every constructible set is triangulable
[
27
]
, which allows us to define algebraic topological invariants such as Euler characteristic and homology. Importantly, any shape that can be faithfully represented via a mesh on a computer is a constructible set.

2.2.
Persistence Modules

For a constructible set
M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the homology group
H_{n}(M;\Bbbk)
in degree
n
with coefficients in a field
\Bbbk
provides an algebraic summary of
M
. Persistent homology extends this concept by capturing the evolution of homology groups across an
\mathbb{R}
-indexed filtration of
M
. Let
\mathbb{R}
denote the category whose objects are the real numbers and which admits a unique morphism
a\to b
whenever
a\leq b
.

Definition 2.2

(Persistence Modules)
.

A persistence module is a functor
U:\mathbb{R}\to\text{Vect}
, where
Vect
is the category of vector spaces over a fixed field
\Bbbk
.

In other words, a persistence module
U
assigns to each real number
t
a vector space
U(t)
, and to each pair
s\leq t
a linear map
U(s\leq t):U(s)\to U(t)
, such that
U(t\leq t)
is the identity and
U(r\leq t)=U(s\leq t)\circ U(r\leq s)
for
r\leq s\leq t
.

Persistence modules arise naturally from filtered topological spaces. Given a constructible set
M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a constructible function
2
2

2

A constructible function is one with a constructible graph.

h:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we obtain a filtration of
M
by considering the sublevel sets
M_{t}=h^{-1}(-\infty,t]
. For each homology degree
n
, the homology groups of these sublevel sets, together with the maps induced by inclusions, form a persistence module
\text{PH}_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\text{Vect}
that sends
t\mapsto H_{n}(M_{t})
.

It is a remarkable fact of representation theory
[
28
]
that whenever this functor lands in the sub-category of finite-dimensional vector spaces and maps, then the persistent module decomposes into a collection of indecomposable “building block” functors called interval modules, i.e.,
U\cong\bigoplus_{I}I^{n_{I}}_{\Bbbk}
, where each
I^{n_{I}}_{\Bbbk}:(\mathbb{R},\leq)\to\text{Vect}
assigns
\Bbbk^{n_{I}}
to points in the interval
I=[b,d)
, the identity map to
I^{n_{I}}_{\Bbbk}(s\leq t)
whenever
s,t\in[b,d)
, and outside of this interval the vector spaces and maps are
0
.
Each interval
[b,d)
represents a homological feature that appears at time
b
and disappears by time
d
.
We remark that the interval
I
could also have one of the three forms: open-open
(b,d)
, open-closed
(b,d]
, and closed-closed
[b,d]
, but in practice only closed-open intervals
[b,d)
appear.

2.3.
The Interleaving Distance

The category of persistence modules,

\text{Vect}^{\mathbb{R}}
, consists of functors of the form
\mathbb{R}\to\text{Vect}
with morphisms as natural transformations.
This category is equipped with an interleaving distance
d_{\mathrm{Int}}
, which measures the similarity between persistence modules.
We note that this distance measures “approximate isomorphism” between persistence modules.

Definition 2.3

(Interleaving distance)
.

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
, one defines the
\varepsilon
-shift
operation as the map
T_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
where
T_{\varepsilon}(x)=x+\varepsilon
. This is an endofunctor of
\mathbb{R}
, when
\mathbb{R}
is viewed as a category.
Pre-composition of a persistence module
U\in\text{Vect}^{\mathbb{R}}
with
T_{\varepsilon}
is called the
\varepsilon
-shift of
U
.
An
\varepsilon
-interleaving
between two persistence modules
U
and
V
is simply a pair of morphisms from each original module to the other one’s shift, i.e., a pair of natural transformations
\Phi:U\Rightarrow VT_{\varepsilon}
and
\Psi:V\Rightarrow UT_{\varepsilon}
such that
(\Psi T_{\varepsilon})\Phi=U\sigma_{2\varepsilon}
and
(\Phi T_{\varepsilon})\Psi=V\sigma_{2\varepsilon}
, where
\sigma_{2\varepsilon}
is the canonical natural transformation from the identity to
T_{2\varepsilon}
.
The
interleaving distance
between
U
and
V
is then defined as:

d_{\text{Int}}(U,V)=\inf\{\varepsilon\geq 0\mid U\text{ and }V\text{ are }\varepsilon\text{-interleaved}\}

If there is no interleaving between
U
and
V
, we set
d_{\mathrm{Int}}(U,V)=\infty.

Persistence modules can be generalized by considering different choices of indexing categories, see the theory of
generalized persistence modules

[
26
]
.
For the purposes of our paper, we are particularly interested in the case where the indexing category is an interval
I=[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
More generally, for any interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.4

(
I
-persistence module)
.

An
I
-persistence module is a functor
U:I\to\text{Vect}
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval of
\mathbb{R}
.

The category of
I
-persistence modules denoted as
\text{Vect}^{I}
.
The interleaving distance between
I
-persistence modules can be defined by letting the translation functor
T_{\epsilon}(x)=\min(x+\epsilon,b)
so that translations by
\epsilon
are capped at
b
.

2.4.
Persistent Homology Transform

We now introduce the central object of our study, the Persistent Homology Transform (PHT).
The PHT captures the persistent homology of a constructible subset
M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by considering filtrations induced by height functions in all directions.
Specifically, for each direction
v
, we define a height function
h_{v}:M\to\mathbb{R}
as
h_{v}(x)=x\cdot v
.
This function induces a filtration of
M
by sublevel sets

M_{v,t}=\{x\in M\mid x\cdot v\leq t\}=h_{v}^{-1}(-\infty,t].

Definition 2.5

(PHT: Version 1)
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a constructible set. The
persistent homology transform
of
M
in degree
n
is defined as the map

\text{PHT}^{\mathbb{R}}_{n}(M):\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\text{Vect}^{\mathbb{R}}\qquad v\mapsto(t\mapsto H_{n}(M_{v,t}))

where
H_{n}
denotes the
n
-th homology functor.

Remark 2.6

(Rescaling Shapes)
.

As observed in
[
1
, Rmk. 2.2]
and reviewed in
Section

B.1
, the PHT is Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and the interleaving distance on
\text{Vect}^{\mathbb{R}}
.
However, this Lipschitz constant is
\sup_{x\in M}||x||_{2}
, which is the largest distance between
0
and any point in
M
.
To provide a uniform Lipschitz bound across all shapes, we assume that every shape
M
in this paper has been rescaled so that
M
is contained in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid||x||_{2}\leq 1\}
.

Building off
2.6
, we observe that PHT: Version 1 can be rewritten as
\text{PHT}^{\mathbb{I}}_{i}(M)
, where
\mathbb{I}=[-1,1]
contains the range of height values where topological changes can occur.
This observation allows us to introduce an alternative coordinate system for the PHT based on the angle
\theta
between the direction
v
and
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that
x\cdot v=t
.
We adopt the convention of using
-\theta
to represent the angular defect from direction
v
so that
\theta=0
corresponds to the case where
t=1
, and so
M_{v,t=1}=M
, and
t=0
corresponds to
\theta=-\pi/2
.
Because we want increasing values of
t
to correspond to increasing values of
\theta
, the choice of
\Theta=[-\pi,0]
as the principal domain for
\arccos
is most natural here.
We solidify this second formulation of the PHT, as it will lead to improved stability constants later in the paper.

Definition 2.7

(PHT: Version 2)
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a constructible set. The
angular persistent homology transform
of
M
in degree
n
is defined as the map

\text{PHT}^{\Theta}_{n}(M):\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\text{Vect}^{\Theta}\qquad v\mapsto(\theta\mapsto H_{n}(M_{v,\cos{\theta}}))

Remark 2.8

(Change of coordinates)
.

For any
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\theta\in[-\pi,0]
and
t\in[-1,1]
, the angular PHT can also be written as
\text{PHT}^{\Theta}_{n}(M)(v)(\theta)=\text{PHT}^{\mathbb{I}}_{n}(M)(v)(\cos{\theta})
and similarly the ordinary PHT can be written as
\text{PHT}^{\mathbb{I}}_{n}(M)(v)(t)=\text{PHT}_{n}^{\Theta}(M)(v)(-\arccos{t})
.
Note that the negative sign comes from the the convention of taking angles in
[-\pi,0]
.

2.5.
Distances on PHTs

We can define a distance for the PHTs of two shapes
M
and
N
by considering the maximum interleaving distance over all directions on the sphere; this comports with various
L^{\infty}
-type stability results.
Since we have two versions of the PHT—
2.5
and
2.7
above—we introduce some extra notation to allow us to streamline our results.
Let
X
be an interval of
\mathbb{R}
, e.g.,

X=\mathbb{I}=[-1,1]
or
X=\Theta=[-\pi,0]
.

Definition 2.9

(PHT interleaving distance)
.

Let
M,N\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
PHT interleaving distance
between
\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)
and
\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(N)
is defined as

d_{I}(\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(N))=\max_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}d_{\mathrm{Int}}\left(\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(v),\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(N)(v)\right),

where
d_{\mathrm{Int}}
denotes the interleaving distance between persistence modules.

Remark 2.10
.

When
X=\mathbb{R}
we have the usual persistent homology transform, and by the isometry theorem
[
29
]
, the PHT interleaving distance coincides with the PHT bottleneck distance as defined in
[
30
]
.

We equip
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with two metrics, which reflects our choice of
X=\mathbb{I}
or
X=\Theta
.
The choice of
X=\mathbb{I}
leads naturally to stability results for the Euclidean metric
d_{2}(v,w)=\|v-w\|_{2}
on the sphere.
By contrast, the choice of
X=\Theta
leads naturally to stability results stated in terms of the round (or geodesic) metric
d_{g}(v,w)=\arccos(v\cdot w)
.

2.6.
Lipshitz Stability of the PHT

It was shown in
[
1
]
that the PHT is 1-Lipschitz (and hence also continuous) with respect to the interleaving distance. It is straightforward to see (cf.
2.13
) that the angular-coordinatized version
\text{PHT}^{\Theta}
is also 1-Lipschitz. The proof of the following lemma is provided in
Section

B.1
.

Lemma 2.11
.

\text{PHT}_{n}^{\mathbb{I}}(M)
and
\text{PHT}_{n}^{\Theta}(M)
are 1-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{2}
and
d_{g}
.

2.7.
Nesting Relations between Sub-Level Sets

We note here the relationships between sublevel sets of a shape
M\subseteq\mathbb{D}^{d}
when viewed in different directions. A key observation is that the Euclidean metric is overly conservative for witnessing inclusions of sublevel sets. By contrast, the round metric is tight.

Lemma 2.12
.

For directions
v,w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and heights
s,t\in[-1,1]
, suppose
t+d_{2}(v,w)\leq s
then
M_{v,t}\subseteq M_{w,s}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in M
such that
x\cdot v\leq t
. Then
|x\cdot v-x\cdot w|\leq\|v-w\|_{2}\leq s-t
, and so
x\cdot w\leq s-t+x\cdot v\leq s-t+t\leq s
.
∎

It is important to note that the converse is not always true.
If
M_{v,t}\subset M_{w,s}
then
d_{2}(v,w)
may be larger than
s-t
; as the reader is encouraged to find for themselves.
This is one of several reasons to consider the more natural round (geodesic) metric on the sphere, where a converse result can be proved; cf.
2.14
.

Lemma 2.13
.

For directions
v,w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and angles
\theta,\phi\in[-\pi,0]
where
\phi+d_{g}(v,w)\leq\theta
, then
M_{v,\cos{\phi}}\subseteq M_{w,\cos{\theta}}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in M_{v,\phi}
i.e.,
x\cdot v\leq\cos\phi
. Taking arc cosine on both sides gives,

\arccos{x\cdot v}\geq\arccos{\cos{\phi}}=-\phi
since
\phi\in[-\pi,0]
. We are further given that
d_{g}(v,w)\leq\theta-\phi
and so
\arccos{x\cdot v}\geq-\phi\geq\arccos{v\cdot w}-\theta
. Rearranging gives
\theta\geq\arccos{v\cdot w}-\arccos{x\cdot v}\geq-\arccos{x\cdot w}
by
B.1
. Taking cosine on both sides yields
x\cdot w\leq\cos\theta
.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
M=\mathbb{D}^{d}
, which is the closed unit ball.
Given arbitrary
v,w,\theta
, the smallest
\phi
(in absolute value) satisfying
M_{v,\cos\phi}\subseteq M_{w,\cos\theta}
is
\phi=\theta-\arccos(v\cdot w)
.

3.
Lipschitz Extensions of the PHT

We now proceed to answer our first question from the introduction: How do we take a finite collection of persistence modules
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
, each of which we assume records the persistent homology of some unknown shape
M\subseteq\mathbb{D}^{d}
when filtered along directions
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, e.g.,
U_{i}\cong H_{n}(M_{v_{i},t})
, and predict the persistent homology for an unknown direction
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
?
The method we advance here is to adapt the work of
[
24
,
25
]
on Kan extensions to the specifics of the PHT.

3.3.
Right and Center Kan Extensions for the PHT

As

This leads to undesirable estimates for the homology in those parameter ranges—either zero or a direct sum of homologies, respectively.
The reader who is sensitive to duality may ask if there is a way to exploit the

The

Specifically, the right Kan extension assigns to

This assignment is slightly counterintuitive, because formally to construct this limit, one would need to take the product of all homologies of “future” sub-level sets that
(w,\beta)
might map to, and then assign a best approximating sub-vector space of this product.

\operatorname*{\underrightarrow{\mathrm{colim}}}_{(v,\alpha)\in L^{-}(w,\beta)}\text{PHT}^{X}(M)(v,\alpha)\to\varprojlim_{(v,\alpha)\in L^{+}(w,\beta)}\text{PHT}^{X}(M)(v,\alpha).

Remark 3.11

]
.
Using coherent matchings to compute Kan extensions is left to future work.

4.
Error Bounds for Kan Approximations

In this section, we establish precise error bounds between the true PHT of a shape
M
and the Kan extension of the PHT computed at a finite set of directions.
This result is proved in two steps:
4.1
proves a point-wise bound for any direction
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
4.6
then converts this result into a distance bound between PHTs, cf.
2.9
.
Subtle differences between the geodesic
X=\Theta
and Euclidean
X=\mathbb{I}
coordinate systems for the PHT are exhibited through a few computations and remarks.
Finally, we generalize our error bound further by considering both a finite set of directions and scalar heights.
The machinery of Kan extensions works flawlessly here and produces a linear error term in our approximation.

4.1.
Direction-wise Bound

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
M
be a constructible set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For any direction
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and homological degree
n
, there exists an
\epsilon=2d(v,w)
-interleaving between
\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
and
\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
, where
v\in A
is a direction where
\text{PHT}(M)
is sampled.

Remark 4.2

(Simplifying Assumptions and Notation)
.

For the proof of
4.1
we will make several simplifying assumptions.
First, we will assume that
\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(w)
is given by the left Kan extension, as all we really need for the proof is a construction that is functorially constructed in terms of limits and colimits.
Additionally, we will assume
X=\mathbb{I}
since
\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)(\beta)=H_{n}(M_{w,\beta})
is more straightforward to write. Recall that when
X=\Theta
the “sub-level set at parameter
\beta
” is actually
M_{w,\cos\beta}
in the usual Euclidean coordinate system.
Finally, as
M
and
i\geq 0
are constant throughout, we will ignore these as well.

Proof.

In view of
4.2
we will write
C_{w}:=\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(w)
to emphasize that
C_{w}(\beta)
is given by the colimit of
\text{PHT}^{X}_{i}
over the past light cone
L^{-}(w,\beta)
.
Our task is to compare this with the usual persistent homology module of
M
in direction
w
, which we will write as
H_{w}
.
The reader is urged to recall
2.3
and the definition of our translation functor
T_{\epsilon}:X\to X
, given by
T_{\epsilon}(\beta)=\min\{\beta+\epsilon,\sup X\}
.
Our task in this proof is to construct two natural transformations

\Phi:C_{w}\Rightarrow H_{w}T_{\epsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi:H_{w}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{\epsilon}

such that
(\Psi T_{\epsilon})\Phi=C_{w}\sigma_{2\epsilon}
and
(\Phi T_{\epsilon})\Psi=H_{w}\sigma_{2\epsilon}
, where
\sigma_{2\epsilon}
is the canonical natural transformation from the identity to
T_{2\epsilon}
.

We first note that
\Phi:C_{w}\Rightarrow H_{w}T_{\epsilon}
exists for every
\epsilon\geq 0
.
Indeed, since
C_{w}(\beta)
is the colimit over all homology vector spaces
H_{n}(M_{v,\alpha})
such that
(v,\alpha)\to(w,\beta)
exists in the under comma category
\iota\downarrow(w,\beta)
for the inclusion
\iota:\mathbb{AX}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mathbb{X}
, then we can guarantee that
M_{v,\alpha}\subseteq M_{w,\beta}
is a valid geometric inclusion, hence
H_{n}(M_{w,\beta})=:H_{w}(\beta)
stands as a co-cone to the diagram over
L^{-}(w,\beta)
.
By definition,
C_{w}(\beta)
is the universal co-cone over
L^{-}(w,\beta)
, so there is a unique map
C_{w}(\beta)\to H_{w}(\beta)
.
As this argument is functorial in
\beta
, we conclude the existence of a natural transformation
\Phi:C_{w}\Rightarrow H_{w}
and hence a natural transformation to any shift
H_{w}T_{\epsilon}
because
H_{w}T_{\epsilon}(\beta):=H_{n}(M_{w,\beta+\epsilon})
is also a co-cone.

The construction of
\Psi:H_{w}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{\epsilon}
is the main difficulty of the proof, and illustrates how
\epsilon
depends on the choice of
v\in A
.
First we note that for any pair of vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
there is always a natural transformation
\Delta_{w,v}:H_{w}\Rightarrow H_{v}T_{d(v,w)}
because of the nesting relationship
M_{w,\beta}\subseteq M_{v,\beta+d(v,w)}
proved in
Section

2.7
.
Moreover, if
v\in A
there is always a natural transformation
\Sigma_{v,w}:H_{v}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{d(v,w)}
because
H_{n}(M_{v,\beta})
participates in the diagram over
L^{-}(w,\beta+d(v,w))
that defines
C_{w}(\beta+d(v,w))
.
Shifting this natural transformation yields a natural transformation
\Sigma_{v,w}T_{d(v,w)}:H_{v}T_{d(v,w)}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{d(v,w)}T_{d(v,w)}=C_{w}T_{2d(v,w)}
.
Setting

\Psi:=(\Sigma_{v,w}T_{d(v,w)})\Delta_{w,v}:H_{w}\Rightarrow H_{v}T_{d(v,w)}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{2d(v,w)}

reveals an
\epsilon
-interleaving for all
\epsilon\geq 2d(v,w)
.
∎

4.2.
Global Distance Bound

Theorem 4.6

(Stability of the Kan Extension)
.

Let
M
be a fixed constructible set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be a subset of directions.
The PHT interleaving distance between the persistent homology transform
\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)
and its Kan extension approximation
\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)
is bounded by twice the Hausdorff distance between
A
and the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for every degree
n
, i.e.,

d_{I}(\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M))\leq 2d_{H}(A,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

Here, the distance metric on the sphere is the Euclidean metric when
X=\mathbb{I}
, and the geodesic metric when
X=\Theta
.
In particular, this result implies that if
A
is an
\epsilon
-net of the sphere, then

d_{I}(\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M))\leq 2\epsilon.

Proof.

Suppose
\epsilon=d_{H}(A,\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, which implies that for every
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exists a point
v\in A
such that
d(v,w)\leq\epsilon
. By
4.1
, for any such pair
(v,w)
, there is a
2\epsilon
-interleaving between
\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
and
\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
for every homological degree
n
. Since this holds for every
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we conclude that the interleaving distance between the true PHT and its Kan extension approximation, measured over all directions in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, is at most
2\epsilon
. Thus,

d_{I}(\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M))=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}d_{\mathrm{Int}}\left(\mathrm{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(w),\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)\right)\leq 2\epsilon.

∎

(Bottleneck Distance Bounds)

Following

, one could repeat this analysis for

and this would give a bound on the bottleneck distance for the PHT.

. This can be seen by noting that the proportion
\frac{2\pi}{6}
in the interval
[-\pi,0]
is much smaller than the proportion

in the interval

.

4.3.
Comparing Euclidean and Angular Coordinates

A direct comparison of the error bounds for the Euclidean (
\mathbb{I}
-indexed) and angular (
\Theta
-indexed) versions of the PHT is ill-defined, as their interleaving distances are computed over different indexing categories. To facilitate a meaningful comparison, we can re-parameterize the angular PHT to the Euclidean domain. Specifically, compute the extension in angular coordinates
\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{\Theta}(M)
and then apply a change of coordinates as described in
2.8
to obtain a
\mathbb{I}
-persistence module. Recall the change of coordinates mentioned earlier,
\text{PHT}^{\mathbb{I}}_{n}(M)(v)(t)=\text{PHT}_{n}^{\Theta}(M)(v)(-\arccos{t})
. We may then compute the interleaving distance for this re-parameterized
\mathbb{I}
-module, and in doing so, we observe the advantage of these angular coordinates. The stability of this re-parameterized
\mathbb{I}
-module is given by the following lemma, which is analogous to
4.1
. The proof is relegated to
Section

B.2
.

Lemma 4.10

(Stability of the Re-parameterized Angular PHT)
.

For any direction
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and homological degree
n
, there exists an
\epsilon=2\sin(d_{g}(v,w))
-interleaving between
U^{\prime}_{w}=\mathrm{PHT}^{\Theta}_{n}(M)(w)\circ(-\arccos)
and
\overline{U^{\prime}}_{w}=\overline{\mathrm{PHT}}^{\Theta}_{n}(M)(w)\circ(-\arccos)
, where
v\in A
is any direction where
\text{PHT}(M)
is sampled.
Here we are using
\mathbb{I}
-interleaving coordinates, as
U^{\prime}_{w}
and
\overline{U^{\prime}}_{w}
are now treated as
\mathbb{I}
-modules.

Remark 4.11
.

A comparison between
\sin(\arccos(v\cdot w))
and
\|v-w\|
highlights the difference between two types of interleavings (cf. case
X=\mathbb{I}
in
4.1
and
4.10
). The former represents the perpendicular distance between vectors
v
and
w
on the unit sphere, while the latter represents the Euclidean distance between them. For instance, when the angle
\theta=\pi/2
, we have
\sin(\theta)=1
, whereas the Euclidean distance between
v
and
w
is
\sqrt{2}
. This demonstrates that using angular coordinates for a PHT provides a tighter stability bound. In fact, it is the tightest bound one can obtain without having any other information about the shape.

4.4.
Extension from Fully Discrete Data

In practice, the Persistent Homology Transform is often computed from data that are discrete in both directions and filtration values, i.e., not just the directions
A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
but for each direction
v\in A
, a finite set of filtration values
\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{X}
.
We can handle this by defining a new category
\mathbb{AT}
that is a full subcategory of
\mathbb{AX}
, defined previously.
Our problem is to extend a functor
G^{X}:\mathbb{AT}\to\text{Vect}
, representing the doubly-sampled PHT, to the continuous category
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mathbb{X}
. The Kan extension formulation is identical, with
\mathbb{AT}
as the source category:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Vect}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mathbb{X}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{G}^{X}}

An argument very similar to the proof of

yields the following, the strongest discrete approximation result known for the PHT.
We relegate its proof to

.

Theorem 4.12

(Stability of the fully discrete Kan Extension)
.

Let
M
be a constructible set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
A
be an
\epsilon_{A}
-net of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and let
\mathbb{T}
be an
\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
-net of

. The PHT interleaving distance between the true PHT,
\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)
, and its Kan extension from the discrete data on
\mathbb{AT}
,
\overline{\text{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)
, is bounded for every degree

as follows:

d_{I}(\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\overline{\text{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M))\leq 2\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}.

Appendix A
Kan Extensions

We provide a brief review of Kan extensions. For a comprehensive treatment, see
[
33
]
.

Definition A.1

(Left Kan Extension)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
, and
\mathcal{E}
be categories, and let
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
and
G:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be functors. A left Kan extension of
F
along
G
is a pair
(\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F,\eta)
, where
\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{E}
is a functor and
\eta:F\implies\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F\circ G
is a natural transformation, such that for any other pair
(K,\gamma)
with
K:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{E}
and
\gamma:F\implies K\circ G
, there exists a unique natural transformation
\tau:\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F\implies K
making the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}

The right Kan extension is defined dually and we refer the reader to
[
33
]
for a complete treatment.
We are interested in computing the pointwise Kan extensions, i.e. given any object
d\in\mathcal{D}
in the domain, how do find the corresponding object
\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F(d)
in
\mathcal{E}
.
To do so, we first give the definition of a comma category and its dual.

Definition A.2

(Comma Categories)
.

For a functor
G:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
and object
d\in\mathcal{D}
the
under comma category
, written
G\downarrow d
, consists of those objects in the image of
G
that sit “under” a target object
d
, i.e.,

•

objects are pairs
(c,f)
where
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
f:Gc\to d
is a morphism in
\mathcal{D}
,

•

morphisms between
(c,f)
and
(c^{\prime},f^{\prime})
is a morphism
h:c\to c^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}
such that the following triangle commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Gc}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{Gc^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Gh}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\prime}}

Dually, the
over comma category
, written
d\downarrow G
, consists of those objects in the image of
G
that sit “over” a target object
d
, i.e.,

•

objects are pairs
(c,f)
where
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
f:d\to Gc
is a morphism in
\mathcal{D}
,

•

morphisms between
(c,f)
and
(c^{\prime},f^{\prime})
is a morphism
h:c\to c^{\prime}

\mathcal{C}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Gc}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{Gc^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Gh}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{\prime}}

Both comma categories have a projection functor to

that sends an object

and a morphism

We call the projection functor from the under comma category

and the projection functor from the over comma category

Theorem A.3

(Existence of Pointwise Kan Extensions)
.

For
\mathcal{C}
small and
\mathcal{E}
cocomplete, the left Kan extension
\mathrm{Lan}_{G}F
exists and is defined by the map

Finally, the following definition provides an important criterion for reducing colimit calculations to simpler (sub)categories.

Definition A.4

(Cofinality)
.

A functor
H:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
is said to be

if for every object

the over comma category
(b\downarrow H)
is non-empty and connected.

.

The utility of the above definition only comes to light when one learns that the above “topological” stipulation on the fibers of a functor

Lemma B.1
.

For any
x\in M\in\mathcal{CS}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

with

,

|\arccos{x\cdot v}-\arccos{x\cdot w}|\leq\arccos{v\cdot w}.

If
x=0
, then
\arccos(x\cdot v)=\arccos(x\cdot w)=\pi/2
, so the inequality holds. Let
u=x/\|x\|
be a unit vector. Set

\alpha=\arccos(u\cdot v),\quad\beta=\arccos(u\cdot w),\quad\gamma=\arccos(v\cdot w),

so
|\alpha-\beta|\leq\gamma
by the triangle inequality for the geodesic distance on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Define
g(t)=\arccos(r\cos t)
for

, then its derivative satisfies

g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{r\sin t}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}\cos^{2}t}}\leq 1,

so
|g(\alpha)-g(\beta)|\leq|\alpha-\beta|\leq\gamma
. Hence,
|\arccos{x\cdot v}-\arccos{x\cdot w}|\leq\arccos{v\cdot w}.

∎

Proof.

Lemma B.2

(Reparameterization of Interleavings)
.

Let
U,V:\Theta\to\text{Vect}
be two persistence modules over the indexing category
\Theta=[-\pi,0]
that are
\epsilon
-interleaved. Let their re-parameterizations to the indexing category
\mathbb{I}=[-1,1]
be defined by
U^{\prime}(t)=U(-\arccos t)
and
V^{\prime}(t)=V(-\arccos t)
. Then the modules
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
are
\delta
-interleaved, where
\delta=2\sin(\epsilon/2)
.

Proof.

An
\epsilon
-interleaving between
U
and
V
consists of a pair of natural transformations,
\Phi:U\Rightarrow VT_{\epsilon}
and
\Psi:V\Rightarrow UT_{\epsilon}
, satisfying the necessary commutative diagrams. We seek the minimal
\delta\geq 0
for which a
\delta
-interleaving
(\Phi^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime})
exists for the re-parameterized modules
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
.
Under the re-parameterization
t=\cos\theta
, the morphism
\Phi
induces maps
\phi^{\prime}_{t}:U^{\prime}(t)\to V^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
, where
t^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\cos(T_{\epsilon}(-\arccos t))
. The natural transformation
\Phi^{\prime}:U^{\prime}\Rightarrow V^{\prime}T_{\delta}
is constructed by composing these maps with the internal morphisms of
V^{\prime}
. This construction is well-defined if and only if
t^{\prime}\leq T_{\delta}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{I}
, which requires that
\delta\geq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{I}}(t^{\prime}-t)
. The commutativity conditions for the
(\Phi^{\prime},\Psi^{\prime})
interleaving are satisfied because these transformations are constructed functorially from
(\Phi,\Psi)
, which already form an interleaving. The problem thus reduces to the optimization:

\delta=\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}\left[\cos(T_{\epsilon}(-\arccos{t}))-t\right].

Letting
\theta=-\arccos t
, we seek to maximize
f(\theta)=\cos(T_{\epsilon}(\theta))-\cos\theta
for
\theta\in[-\pi,0]
. To evaluate this supremum, we must consider the definition of the shift functor on the bounded domain
\Theta
, namely
T_{\epsilon}(\theta)=\min(\theta+\epsilon,0)
. We have two cases.

First, consider the domain
\theta\in[-\pi,-\epsilon]
, where
T_{\epsilon}(\theta)=\theta+\epsilon
. The function to maximize is
f(\theta)=\cos(\theta+\epsilon)-\cos\theta
. This quantity represents the horizontal distance between the points on the unit circle at angles
\theta
and
\theta+\epsilon
. This distance is maximized when the chord connecting these points is horizontal, which requires the angles to be symmetric about
-\pi/2
. This configuration yields a maximal value of
2\sin(\epsilon/2)
by the calculation below:

\displaystyle\delta=\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)-\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)=\sin(\epsilon/2)-(-\sin(\epsilon/2))=2\sin(\epsilon/2).

Second, consider the domain
\theta\in(-\epsilon,0]
, where
T_{\epsilon}(\theta)=0
. The function becomes
f(\theta)=\cos(0)-\cos\theta=1-\cos\theta
. On
[-\pi,0]
, this function is decreasing, so its supremum on this sub-interval occurs as
\theta
approaches the infimum of the domain,
-\epsilon
. This yields a value of
1-\cos(-\epsilon)=1-\cos\epsilon
.

To find the global maximum, we compare the results from the two cases. Using the identity
1-\cos\epsilon=2\sin^{2}(\epsilon/2)
, we see that
2\sin(\epsilon/2)\geq 2\sin^{2}(\epsilon/2)
since
\sin(\epsilon/2)\in[0,1]
. The global supremum for
f(\theta)
is therefore
2\sin(\epsilon/2)
. This choice of
\delta
satisfies the required inequality, thereby establishing the interleaving distance.
∎

B.2.
Proof of
4.10

Proof.

Let
U_{w}=\text{PHT}^{\Theta}_{n}(M)(w)
and
\overline{U}_{w}=\overline{\text{PHT}}^{\Theta}_{n}(M)(w)
denote the original persistence module and the extended persistence module over the indexing category
\Theta=[-\pi,0]
. By Lemma
4.1
, for any
v\in A
, the modules
U_{w}
and
\overline{U}_{w}
are
\epsilon_{g}
-interleaved, where
\epsilon_{g}=2d_{g}(v,w)
.

The modules in the statement of the lemma,
U^{\prime}_{w}
and
\overline{U^{\prime}}_{w}
, are the re-parameterizations of
U_{w}
and
\overline{U}_{w}
to the indexing category
\mathbb{I}=[-1,1]
. We have established in Lemma
B.2
that if two
\Theta
-persistence modules are
\epsilon_{g}
-interleaved, their re-parameterized counterparts in the
\mathbb{I}
-domain are
\delta
-interleaved, where
\delta=2\sin(\epsilon_{g}/2)
.
Substituting
\epsilon_{g}=2d_{g}(v,w)
into the reparameterization formula yields the interleaving distance:

\epsilon=2\sin\left(\frac{2d_{g}(v,w)}{2}\right)=2\sin(d_{g}(v,w)).

This completes the proof.
∎

B.3.
Proof of
4.12

Proof.

In line with the simplifying assumptions from
4.2
, we denote the true persistence module in direction
w
as
H_{w}:=\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
and the fully discrete Kan extension as
C_{w}:=\overline{\text{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M)(w)
. We note that
C_{w}(\beta)
is given by the colimit of
\text{PHT}_{n}^{X}(M)
over the discrete past light cone
L^{-}_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{T}}(w,\beta)
, which consists of pairs
(v,t)\in A\times\mathbb{T}
such that
t+d(v,w)\leq\beta
. Our goal is to show a
(2\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}})
-interleaving between
H_{w}
and
C_{w}
. Let
\epsilon=2\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
. We must construct two natural transformations

\Phi:C_{w}\Rightarrow H_{w}T_{\epsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi:H_{w}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{\epsilon}

that satisfy the interleaving conditions.

The construction of
\Phi:C_{w}\Rightarrow H_{w}T_{\epsilon}
is straightforward. By definition,
C_{w}(\beta)
is the colimit over a diagram whose objects are pairs
(v,t)\in A\times\mathbb{T}
in the discrete past light cone of
(w,\beta)
. For any such object, the inequality
t+d(v,w)\leq\beta
holds. Since
\epsilon\geq 0
, this implies
t+d(v,w)\leq T_{\epsilon}(\beta)
. This latter inequality defines a morphism in the full space-time category, which induces a map from the homology group
H_{v}(t)
to
H_{w}(T_{\epsilon}(\beta))
. Consequently,
H_{w}(T_{\epsilon}(\beta))
forms a co-cone over the diagram for
C_{w}(\beta)
. The universal property of the colimit then guarantees the existence of a unique map
\Phi_{\beta}:C_{w}(\beta)\to H_{w}(T_{\epsilon}(\beta))
, which defines the natural transformation
\Phi
.

As in the proof of
4.1
, the construction of
\Psi:H_{w}\Rightarrow C_{w}T_{\epsilon}
is more involved. We define the map
\Psi_{\beta}:H_{w}(\beta)\to C_{w}(T_{\epsilon}(\beta))
for each
\beta
as a composition of maps arising from the discretizations. First, since
A
is an
\epsilon_{A}
-net, we choose a
v\in A
with
d(v,w)\leq\epsilon_{A}
. The geometric inclusion
M_{w,\beta}\subseteq M_{v,\beta+\epsilon_{A}}
induces a map on homology,
H_{w}(\beta)\to H_{v}(\beta+\epsilon_{A})
. Second, since the filtration value
\beta+\epsilon_{A}
may not be in the discrete set
\mathbb{T}
, we use the fact that
\mathbb{T}
is an
\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
-net to choose a
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
where
\beta+\epsilon_{A}\leq t^{\prime}\leq\beta+\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
. This gives a second map,
H_{v}(\beta+\epsilon_{A})\to H_{v}(t^{\prime})
. The term
\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
-net in this context means that for any value
y
in the parameter space, there exists a point
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
in the interval
[y,y+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}]
.

The resulting pair
(v,t^{\prime})
is now an element of the discrete sample space
A\times\mathbb{T}
. To define a map from
H_{v}(t^{\prime})
into the colimit
C_{w}(T_{\epsilon}(\beta))
, we must verify that
(v,t^{\prime})
is an object in the diagram defining this colimit. The following calculation reveals this.

t^{\prime}+d(v,w)\leq(\beta+\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}})+\epsilon_{A}=\beta+2\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}=T_{\epsilon}(\beta).

So, a canonical map from
H_{v}(t^{\prime})
into the colimit exists and the composition of these three maps defines
\Psi_{\beta}
. The functoriality of this construction yields the natural transformation
\Psi
.

Finally, verifying that the compositions
(\Psi T_{\epsilon})\Phi
and
(\Phi T_{\epsilon})\Psi
commute with the canonical shift morphisms follows from the functoriality of homology and the universal property of the colimit. Since the bound holds for any direction
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we conclude that
d_{I}(\text{PHT}^{X}_{n}(M),\overline{\text{PHT}}^{X}_{n}(M))\leq 2\epsilon_{A}+\epsilon_{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎
