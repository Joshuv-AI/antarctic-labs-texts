A culturally adapted, theory-based workplace educational intervention for weight management among university employees with overweight and obesity in Iraq: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Nasir Khadir Khalaf Hassan, Yousif Bahaadeen Ahmed Abdulla, Chnar Salahadddin Qadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the effectiveness of a brief, Theory of Planned Behavior-based educational program on weight management and related health outcomes among university employees.MethodsIn this quasi-experimental study conducted at two major universities in Erbil, Iraq, 200 employees with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m² self-selected into an intervention (n = 100) or control (n = 100) group. The intervention consisted of five individual 35–40-minute sessions delivered over 12 weeks and covered obesity awareness, culturally adapted nutrition education, physical activity, and behavior-change strategies. The control group received only standard written materials. Primary outcomes were changes in body weight, BMI, and waist circumference. Secondary outcomes included lipid profile, fasting glucose, quality of life (Impact of Weight on Quality of Life–Lite [IWQOL-Lite]), dietary quality, and physical activity. All assessments were performed at baseline and 12 weeks.ResultsThe intervention was associated with a mean weight loss of 7.46 kg (95% CI 6.44–8.48) compared with a gain of 0.58 kg in the control group (adjusted difference − 8.04 kg; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 2.40). 79% of intervention participants lost ≥ 5% of their initial body weight (versus 0% in controls), and 41% lost ≥ 10%. Significant improvements were also observed in BMI, waist circumference, lipid profile, quality of life, and dietary quality (all p < 0.001; d > 1.8). Mediation analysis indicated that improvement in dietary quality accounted for 82% of the observed association between group assignment and change in BMI.ConclusionsA brief, low-cost, culturally adapted educational intervention delivered in the workplace was associated with exceptionally large weight loss, cardiometabolic benefits, and psychosocial gains, with perfect retention. These findings suggest that this model may offer a promising approach for obesity management in Middle Eastern settings. However, confirmation in randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up is required before firm conclusions regarding scalability and effectiveness can be drawn.Clinical trial registrationThe study was not prospectively registered in a clinical trial registry because it employed a quasi-experimental design with participant self-selection rather than random allocation. However, the full study protocol including all primary and secondary outcomes, eligibility criteria, intervention details, and the statistical analysis plan was finalized, approved by the Hawler Medical University Ethics Committee (reference HMU-REC-2024-18, 15 September 2024), and locked prior to the start of participant recruitment and data collection. No outcomes were added, removed, or modified after data inspection, and no post-hoc analyses were conducted beyond those pre-specified in the protocol. The manuscript adheres fully to the TREND reporting standards for non-randomized evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01360-x.Keywords: Obesity, Overweight, Weight Loss, Health Education, Workplace

Introduction

Overweight and obesity remain among the most formidable public health challenges of the 21st century. As of the latest World Health Organization data, 2.5 billion adults worldwide are overweight, of whom 890 million are obese. These conditions drive substantial increases in type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, selected cancers, and reduced health-related quality of life, imposing enormous economic costs on health systems globally [1].

The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region have experienced one of the most rapid rises in obesity prevalence. National surveys indicate that approximately 65.7% of adults in Iraq are overweight or obese, with obesity prevalence around 34% overall and consistently higher among women [2]. Obesity has become the dominant driver of the non-communicable disease burden in the Eastern Mediterranean Region and is the leading contributor to ischemic heart disease mortality [3, 4].

Despite this alarming epidemiological picture, high-quality intervention research on overweight and obesity in Iraq remains scarce. The limited available studies, such as a randomized controlled trial among university employees in Mosul that applied the Health Belief Model, produced only modest BMI reductions of approximately 0.5 kg/m² over three months. Broader systematic reviews of dietary interventions across the MENA region confirm similarly modest average weight loss (around 4.8 kg) and highlight major methodological limitations, including short follow-up, predominance of Iranian trials, and absence of theory-driven or culturally tailored approaches. No previous Iraqi study has evaluated a culturally adapted, theory-based educational intervention delivered in a workplace setting [5, 6].

Despite this alarming epidemiological picture, high-quality intervention research on overweight and obesity in Iraq remains scarce. The limited available studies have produced only modest results, and no previous Iraqi study has evaluated a culturally adapted, Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)-based educational intervention delivered in a workplace setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study in Iraq to test such an approach.

Workplace-based programs represent a pragmatic, scalable strategy, particularly within universities where staff combine relatively high educational levels with predominantly sedentary occupations. Iraqi university employees exhibit high obesity rates together with low self-efficacy for weight control and prolonged screen-based sedentary behavior [7]. TPB offers a strong theoretical foundation for such interventions; multiple meta-analyses have shown TPB-based approaches to be superior in predicting and changing health behaviors compared with non-theory-guided methods. Its three core constructs—attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control lend themselves particularly well to cultural adaptation within Middle Eastern family- and community-oriented contexts [8, 9].

To address these critical evidence gaps, we conducted a quasi-experimental study evaluating the effect of a theory-based, individually tailored educational intervention on weight management among university employees with overweight or obesity in Erbil City, Iraq. The quasi-experimental design was selected as the most ethical and feasible approach in a real-world university environment, where full randomization risks contamination between groups and withholding potentially beneficial programs from motivated volunteers would be difficult to justify. We hypothesized that a culturally adapted TPB-guided intervention delivered through five structured sessions would produce clinically meaningful improvements in anthropometric, biochemical, psychosocial, and behavioral outcomes compared with standard written materials alone.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a 12-week quasi-experimental pre–post study with a non-equivalent control group to assess the effectiveness of a theory-based educational intervention for weight management among university employees with overweight or obesity. This design was chosen as the most feasible and practical option in a real-world university setting. Although randomized controlled trials are the gold standard for evaluating intervention effectiveness, a fully randomized design was deemed inappropriate in this specific context for both methodological and ethical reasons. First, because many employees worked in the same departments and shared offices, random allocation would have carried a high risk of contamination between intervention and control participants. Second, withholding a potentially beneficial program from employees who expressed strong interest in participating raised ethical concerns. Therefore, after careful consideration, we adopted a pragmatic quasi-experimental design in which participants self-selected into the intervention or control arm. While this approach introduces the possibility of selection bias, it is a recognized and accepted design in workplace health intervention research when randomization is impractical or ethically problematic. The limitations associated with self-selection are explicitly acknowledged and discussed in the Limitations section.

Self-selection into groups according to preference and availability was therefore permitted after a university-wide information session. This approach aligns with recent pragmatic quasi-experimental workplace trials in occupational settings where randomization is impractical [10, 11]. We acknowledge that self-selection introduces potential systematic bias (e.g., more motivated individuals choosing the intervention). However, baseline imbalances were modest and were rigorously addressed through analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusting for gender, baseline weight, LDL-cholesterol, and total cholesterol, plus sensitivity analyses (complete-case, per-protocol, and outlier removal). Outcome assessors were blinded, intervention fidelity was 100%, and retention was 100%, all of which strengthen internal validity despite the design. Post-study, the program has been offered to all control participants and other university staff as part of routine wellness services. The study was reported in accordance with the TREND statement for non-randomized evaluations of behavioral and public health interventions [12] and the TIDieR checklist for intervention description and replication [13]. A completed TREND checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2.

Setting and participants

The study took place at Hawler Medical University and Salahaddin University in Erbil City, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, between October 2024 and June 2025. We recruited full- or part-time university employees aged 18–75 years who had a measured body-mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m². After an introductory information session open to all staff, interested employees self-selected into either the intervention or control group according to their preference and availability.

Inclusion criteria were confirmed BMI ≥ 25 kg/m², willingness to attend all study visits, and ability to give informed consent in Kurdish or Arabic. We excluded individuals who were pregnant or breastfeeding, had chronic conditions or were taking medications known to markedly affect body weight (e.g., systemic corticosteroids, antipsychotics, or thyroid hormone therapy started within the previous 6 months), were already enrolled in any structured weight-loss program, or had any medical condition that contraindicated moderate physical activity.

Sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. Based on the largest previous workplace intervention in the MENA region, we assumed a conservative medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50), α = 0.05 (two-sided), and 90% power, which indicated a minimum of 85 participants per group. To allow for subgroup analyses and any unforeseen attrition, we aimed for 100 per arm. We ultimately recruited exactly 100 participants per group, with zero loss to follow-up. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed that with the observed large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 2.0) and 100% retention, the study achieved > 99% power to detect the effects reported.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention group attended five individual face-to-face counselling sessions (35–40 min each) spread over 12 weeks. All sessions were delivered by the same trained health educator (the first author, a registered nurse with advanced training in behavior-change counselling). Sessions were held in quiet, private rooms at the participant’s workplace or the university clinic to maximize convenience and minimize disruption to work schedules.

The intervention was systematically culturally adapted using the framework of Bernal et al. (updated in recent 2024–2025 applications for Arab and Middle Eastern populations; [14]). Adaptation occurred at both surface level (language, visual imagery, and examples using locally available and culturally familiar foods such as low-oil dolma, lentil stew, grilled chicken with traditional spices, and date-based desserts) and deep level (incorporating family-oriented decision-making norms, religious considerations around halal foods and Ramadan fasting, gender-sensitive physical activity options, and collective family eating patterns). A pilot group of 20 university employees provided iterative feedback on cultural appropriateness, readability, and practicality before finalization.

The entire program was explicitly grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Each TPB construct was operationalized through specific Behavior Change Techniques (BCT Taxonomy v1; Michie et al., 2013 [15]) as follows:

Attitude → BCT 5.1 (information about health consequences) and 9.2 (pros and cons);

A detailed, standardized protocol (Supplementary File 1) covered five core topics delivered in sequence: [1] understanding obesity, its causes, and health consequences (BCT 5.1); [2] energy balance, portion control, and a practical 2-week culturally tailored meal plan featuring traditional Iraqi/Kurdish dishes; [3] physical activity recommendations aligned with current WHO guidelines (BCT 1.4 and 2.3); [4] personalized goal setting and self-monitoring tools (daily logs, illustrated portion guides, and simple smartphone reminders); and [5] relapse prevention and long-term maintenance strategies (BCT 1.2 and 6.2). Every participant received all materials in their preferred language (Kurdish or Arabic), including color booklets, visual portion plates, and basic step counters. To ensure intervention quality and replicability, fidelity was monitored rigorously: 10% of sessions were randomly audio-recorded and independently scored against a 25-item fidelity checklist by an external observer blinded to participant outcomes. Attendance was 100% and fidelity reached 100%. No protocol deviations occurred. The control group received only one 12-page standard written booklet on general healthy eating and physical activity (issued by the Iraqi Ministry of Health) at baseline. They had no further contact or support until the final assessment at 12 weeks. No additional advice was given to either group during the study period.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were changes from baseline to 12 weeks in body weight (kg), BMI (kg/m²), and waist circumference (cm). Secondary outcomes included the full lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol), fasting blood glucose, obesity-related knowledge (Obesity Awareness Questionnaire [OAQ-20], 20-item scale), health-related quality of life (IWQOL-Lite total score) [16], dietary quality (adapted and validated Food Frequency Questionnaire), physical activity (International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form, MET-min/week), and use of weight management strategies (Oxford Food and Activity Behaviours [OxFAB] questionnaire, 20-item scale). The OxFAB questionnaire is a validated instrument that assesses the frequency with which individuals employ 20 specific, evidence-based behavioral strategies for weight control (e.g., meal planning and preparation, self-monitoring of intake/weight, stimulus control to avoid triggers, seeking social/family support, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention planning). Items are scored on frequency scales (0 = never to 4 = always) and summed to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 20; higher scores indicate greater overall engagement in weight management strategies. This tool aligns directly with core constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior (particularly perceived behavioral control) and has demonstrated good reliability and validity in prior obesity research. For the present study, the OxFAB underwent rigorous forward–backward translation into Kurdish and Arabic by independent bilingual experts, review by a local endocrinologist and nutritionist for cultural relevance (incorporating Iraqi/Kurdish family eating patterns, religious fasting considerations, and locally available foods), and pilot testing in 20 university employees (Cronbach’s α = 0.82). All other questionnaires had either been previously validated in Arabic- or Kurdish-speaking populations or underwent identical rigorous forward–backward translation and pilot testing in 20 local employees (Cronbach’s α > 0.82 for all scales).

Data collection

All measurements were taken at baseline and 12 weeks by trained research assistants who were blinded to group allocation. Socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, level of education, occupation, and family monthly income in Iraqi Dinars) were collected at baseline using a brief, structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed specifically for this study and culturally adapted to the Iraqi/Kurdish context. Responses were self-reported and cross-checked against university personnel records where feasible to enhance accuracy. Dietary quality was assessed using a semi-quantitative 48-item food frequency questionnaire adapted for the Kurdish/Iraqi dietary context [17, 18]. The questionnaire included food categories commonly consumed in the local diet across major food groups (meats, grains, dairy, vegetables, fruits, fats, sweets, legumes, beverages, and nuts). Response options ranged from “never” to “at all meals.” Frequency responses were converted to estimated daily servings and scored to create a total dietary quality score. The total dietary quality score ranged from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating better overall dietary quality (i.e., greater consumption of nutrient-dense foods such as vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, and nuts, and lower consumption of fats, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages).The instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Physical activity was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [19], which assesses time spent in vigorous-intensity activity, moderate-intensity activity, walking, and sitting during the past seven days. The IPAQ-SF has demonstrated good test-retest reliability (Spearman’s ρ = 0.80) and acceptable criterion validity (ρ = 0.30) in international validation studies [16]. Responses were converted to Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) minutes per week and categorized as low, moderate, or high activity levels according to IPAQ scoring guidelines. Internal consistency reliability in the current sample was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [20]. Responses were converted to Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) minutes per week and categorized as low, moderate, or high activity levels according to IPAQ scoring guidelines. Internal consistency reliability in the current sample was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.85). Quality of life was measured using a culturally adapted 27-item version of the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite (IWQOL-Lite; available at https://www.qualityoflifeconsulting.com/). The original 31-item instrument was modified by removing 4 items pertaining to sexual life, as such questions were deemed culturally inappropriate for the target population. The adapted instrument assessed four domains: Physical Function (11 items), Self-Esteem (7 items), Public Distress (5 items), and Work (4 items). Scores were transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. Reliability was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.86) [21].

Obesity-related knowledge was assessed using the Obesity Awareness Questionnaire (OAQ-20), a 20-item instrument covering obesity as a disease and risk factors, complications, diagnosis, and management. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater obesity awareness. The instrument demonstrated excellent reliability in the pilot study (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) [22].

Anthropometry and body composition assessment followed International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK) standards. Height was measured with a calibrated stadiometer (Seca 213, ± 0.1 cm), weight with a calibrated digital scale (Seca 813, ± 0.1 kg), and waist circumference with a non-stretchable tape at the midpoint between the lowest rib and iliac crest. Segmental body composition (fat mass, fat-free mass, and visceral fat area) was assessed using the InBody 770 bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) according to manufacturer protocols. Biochemical tests (total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, and fasting blood glucose) were performed on 12-hour fasting venous samples collected in a single certified clinical laboratory. Analyses were conducted using standard enzymatic colorimetric methods on an automated chemistry analyzer. LDL-cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald equation when triglycerides were below 400 mg/dL. The laboratory participates in internal quality control and external proficiency testing programs in accordance with national laboratory accreditation standards.

Statistical analysis

We adhered strictly to the SAMPL guidelines for statistical reporting. The statistical analysis plan was pre-specified in the study protocol before any participant enrollment, data collection, or outcome assessment began. No primary or secondary outcomes were added, removed, or modified after data inspection, and no post-hoc analyses were performed beyond those outlined in the protocol. Change-score distributions were examined both visually (histograms and Q–Q plots) and with the Shapiro–Wilk test; because most variables were non-normal, we report both parametric and non-parametric results for transparency. The OxFAB total score exhibited a non-normal distribution and was therefore analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (within-group) and Mann–Whitney U tests (between-group) on change scores, consistent with the approach applied to other non-normally distributed variables. Rank-biserial correlations were reported as effect sizes.

Within-group changes were analyzed with paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. Between-group differences in change scores were compared using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. To address baseline imbalances (gender, weight, and lipid levels), we additionally ran analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) on the 12-week values, adjusting for baseline measurements and the three imbalanced covariates; adjusted means and 95% confidence intervals are presented alongside unadjusted results.

Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) for parametric data and rank-biserial correlation for non-parametric data. We predefined clinically meaningful thresholds as ≥ 5% weight loss, ≥ 5 cm waist reduction, and ≥ 10-point gain on the IWQOL-Lite. Mediation of the intervention effect on BMI change through knowledge, dietary quality, and physical activity was tested with structural equation modelling (maximum-likelihood estimation in IBM AMOS 28), with excellent fit confirmed by multiple indices (χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR). All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. With 100% retention, no data was missing and no imputation was needed. Sensitivity analyses included complete-case (identical to primary analysis), per-protocol, and removal of any statistical outliers (none found). Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 21.0 and AMOS 28.0.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hawler Medical University (reference HMU-REC-2024-18, 15 September 2024) and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Every participant gave written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the procedures, potential benefits and risks, and their unconditional right to withdraw at any time. Data was anonymized and stored securely. Participants received a small reimbursement for travel costs (10,000 IQD per visit).

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

All 200 participants (100 per group) who completed baseline assessments were retained through the 12-week follow-up, resulting in zero attrition and identical intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses. The perfect retention rate was achieved through deliberate design features tailored to the local context: convenient workplace delivery (sessions held in private rooms at participants’ own departments or the university clinic), flexible individual scheduling around work hours and family obligations, twice-weekly SMS and phone reminders, a small but culturally meaningful travel reimbursement (10,000 IQD ≈ 7.5 USD per visit), consistent delivery by a single trained local educator who built rapport and emphasized cultural values of commitment, family health responsibility, and perseverance, and the brief 12-week duration that minimized cumulative burden. No participant withdrew due to time constraints, perceived side effects, loss of interest, or logistical barriers.

Participant flow is presented in Fig. 1 (CONSORT/TREND-compliant diagram). The intervention and control groups were well balanced at baseline on most characteristics (Table 1). The only statistically significant differences were a higher proportion of women and a slightly lower mean weight in the intervention group, as well as modest differences in LDL-cholesterol and total cholesterol. Significant differences were observed for gender distribution (chi-squared [χ²] = 4.52, p = 0.033), with more males in the control group (54% vs. 39%). The control group had higher baseline weight (93.70 vs. 89.86 kg; p = 0.024), while the intervention group had higher baseline LDL-cholesterol (138.53 vs. 120.99 mg/dL; p < 0.001) and total cholesterol (204.60 vs. 187.75 mg/dL; p = 0.023). These baseline imbalances were addressed in all subsequent analyses using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).

Primary anthropometric outcomes

The intervention was associated with large, clinically meaningful reductions in all primary outcomes (Table 2). Mean body weight decreased by 7.46 kg (95% CI − 8.48 to − 6.44) in the intervention group compared with a small gain of 0.58 kg (95% CI 0.38 to 0.78) in the control group (adjusted between-group difference − 8.04 kg, 95% CI − 9.12 to − 6.96; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 2.40, 95% CI 2.07 to 2.73). Similarly, BMI fell by 2.79 kg/m² (95% CI − 3.17 to − 2.41) versus an increase of 0.20 kg/m² in controls (adjusted difference − 2.99 kg/m², 95% CI − 3.41 to − 2.57; p < 0.001; d = 2.38). Waist circumference decreased by 8.75 cm (95% CI − 9.76 to − 7.74) in the intervention arm compared with a negligible increase of 0.38 cm in controls (adjusted difference − 9.13 cm, 95% CI − 10.29 to − 7.97; p < 0.001; d = 2.45).

Seventy-nine participants (79%) in the intervention group achieved the predefined clinically important threshold of ≥ 5% weight loss, compared with zero participants in the control group (χ² = 130.6, p < 0.001, φ = 0.81). Furthermore, 41% of intervention participants lost ≥ 10% of their initial body weight, an outcome rarely reported in short-term workplace trials. Within-group changes were also highly significant in the intervention arm (all p < 0.001 by Wilcoxon signed-rank test), while controls showed small but statistically significant increases in weight and BMI (p < 0.001).

Secondary outcomes

Marked improvements were observed across biochemical, psychosocial, and behavioural domains (Table 3). The intervention group showed substantial reductions in total cholesterol (− 26.90 mg/dL), triglycerides (− 37.56 mg/dL), and LDL-cholesterol (− 17.00 mg/dL), all significantly greater than the small increases seen in controls (all adjusted between-group p < 0.001; d > 1.8). Fasting blood glucose decreased modestly but significantly more in the intervention arm (adjusted difference − 2.1 mg/dL, 95% CI − 4.3 to − 0.1; p = 0.041). HDL-cholesterol showed a small temporary decline in the intervention group (− 2.93 mg/dL), consistent with rapid weight loss physiology, while controls experienced a slight rise.

Psychosocial and behavioral outcomes improved dramatically. Obesity knowledge scores rose by 6.88 points (95% CI 6.41 to 7.35) versus a small decline in controls (adjusted difference 7.28 points, p < 0.001; d = 3.12). Health-related quality of life (IWQOL-Lite total score) increased by 27.06 points (95% CI 24.15 to 29.97)—well above the established minimally important difference of 7.7–12 points—while controls declined slightly (adjusted difference 29.46 points, p < 0.001; d = 2.91). Dietary quality improved by 6.68 points and physical activity rose by 1,296 MET-min/week in the intervention group, both markedly superior to controls (d = 3.05 and 1.85, respectively).

Weight management strategy use (OxFAB) showed minimal change in both groups. The control group decreased slightly from 16.43 ± 3.28 to 16.15 ± 2.95 (p = 0.124), while the intervention group showed a non-significant increase from 16.02 ± 2.94 to 16.28 ± 4.72 (p = 0.256). Although the between-group difference in change scores was statistically significant (U = 4,034, p = 0.018), the effect size was small (rank-biserial correlation = 0.19).

Clinically meaningful categorical changes

At 12 weeks, 68% of intervention participants moved down at least one obesity class (e.g., from class II to class I or overweight), compared with 4% in the control group (p < 0.001). Waist circumference fell below the high-risk threshold (< 102 cm for men, < 88 cm for women) in 52% of intervention participants versus 6% of controls.

Mediation analysis

Structural equation modelling demonstrated excellent fit (χ² = 2.23, df = 3, p = 0.526; CFI = 1.00; TLI = 1.00; RMSEA = 0.00 [90% CI 0.00–0.11]; SRMR = 0.009). The intervention significantly improved all three proposed mediators (knowledge, dietary quality, and physical activity). However, only change in dietary quality showed a significant association with BMI reduction and statistically accounted for 82% of the observed association between group assignment and BMI change (indirect effect β = −2.46, 95% CI − 3.07 to − 1.84, p < 0.001). This finding should be interpreted as exploratory. Because the study was non-randomized and the mediator (dietary quality) was self-reported, the analysis demonstrates statistical association rather than definitive causal mechanism. The model explained 69% of the variance in BMI change. Full path coefficients and covariance matrix are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 2.

Sensitivity and additional analyses

Results remained virtually identical in sensitivity analyses (complete-case, per-protocol, and after exclusion of any potential outliers; none were identified). Subgroup analyses by gender and baseline BMI category showed consistent large effects in both men and women and across overweight, class I, and class II obesity, with slightly greater absolute weight loss among women (mean − 8.1 kg vs. −6.7 kg in men; interaction p = 0.12). No serious adverse events occurred. Three participants in the intervention group reported mild transient muscle soreness after starting physical activity, all of which resolved without intervention.

Discussion

The present quasi-experimental study demonstrated that a relatively brief, theory-based educational intervention delivered in the workplace can yield large and clinically meaningful improvements in weight, cardiometabolic risk markers, quality of life, and health behaviors among university employees with overweight and obesity in Iraq. Over 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group lost an average of 7.46 kg, with 79% achieving at least 5% weight reduction and 41% reaching ≥ 10% loss outcomes that far exceed those reported in most previous lifestyle interventions in the Middle East and North Africa region.

The magnitude of the observed effects particularly a mean 7.46 kg loss, 79% of participants achieving ≥ 5% weight loss, and 41% achieving ≥ 10% loss within only 12 weeks will understandably invite skepticism, especially given the quasi-experimental design with self-selection. Several factors could plausibly have contributed to inflation of apparent benefit. First, participants who chose the intervention arm may have been more motivated or ready for change than those who selected the control arm, despite statistical adjustment for observed baseline differences. Second, the intervention group received five individual face-to-face sessions plus ongoing materials and reminders, while the control group received only a single written booklet; this substantial difference in contact time and attention could have produced expectancy or Hawthorne effects. Third, although we adjusted for measured confounders via ANCOVA and conducted sensitivity analyses, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., baseline motivation, social support, or concurrent lifestyle changes) cannot be excluded. Finally, mediation analysis in a non-randomized context can only demonstrate that dietary quality change was statistically associated with BMI change; it cannot prove that the intervention caused the dietary improvement or that the dietary change was the sole mechanism. These limitations are real and must be weighed when interpreting the findings.

Nevertheless, several design features increase confidence in the results. Retention was 100%, outcome assessors were blinded, intervention fidelity reached 100%, and the large effect was mechanistically coherent (82% of the group difference in BMI change was statistically accounted for by improvement in dietary quality). The intervention was low-cost, culturally grounded, and delivered in a real-world workplace setting—features that enhance external validity even while limiting internal validity relative to an RCT.

When these results are placed against the existing literature, the contrast is stark. Systematic reviews of lifestyle interventions across the Middle East and North Africa have consistently reported mean weight losses of only 4–5 kg over 3–6 months, with high dropout and modest effect sizes [23, 24]. Even the strongest previous Iraqi trial, a randomized controlled study among university employees in Mosul that used the Health Belief Model achieved only a 0.5 kg/m² BMI reduction and suffered considerable attrition [25]. Broader global meta-analyses of non-surgical weight-loss programs similarly show average losses of 3–5 kg at 12 weeks in comparable populations [26, 27]. Our effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 2.0 across primary outcomes) and absolute weight reduction therefore rank among the largest ever reported for a non-pharmacological, short-term intervention in any low- or middle-income setting, and certainly within the Arab world.

Structural equation modelling demonstrated excellent fit. The intervention significantly improved all three proposed mediators. However, only change in dietary quality showed a significant association with BMI reduction and accounted for 82% of the observed association between group assignment and BMI change (indirect effect β = −2.46, 95% CI − 3.07 to − 1.84, p < 0.001). Neither knowledge gain nor physical activity change independently mediated the association. The model explained 69% of the variance in BMI change [28, 29]. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the non-randomized design; they indicate statistical mediation of the observed group difference rather than definitive proof of causal mechanism. This result is consistent with emerging evidence that enhanced obesity awareness can drive meaningful changes in food-related behavior and metabolic outcomes, supporting the value of the awareness-building component in our intervention [30, 31].

Beyond weight, the improvements in cardiometabolic markers and quality of life are clinically meaningful. The reductions in total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-cholesterol were of a magnitude associated with measurable cardiovascular risk reduction in other populations [32]. The 27-point increase on the IWQOL-Lite scale greatly exceeded the established minimally important difference of 7.7–12 points and is among the largest psychosocial gains reported in any non-surgical trial of this duration [33, 34]. In a cultural context where obesity carries significant social stigma and affects work performance and family life, these psychosocial benefits may be as important as the physical ones. Recent research highlights how weight-related stigma and emotional eating both prevalent in Middle Eastern populations can be alleviated by effective interventions, offering a plausible explanation for the dramatic psychosocial improvements observed here [35, 36].

While the 12-week results are encouraging, the sustainability of weight loss and behavioral changes beyond the active intervention period remains uncertain. Several mechanisms may support longer-term maintenance. First, the strong emphasis on practical skills (meal planning, portion control, and culturally appropriate recipes) and the provision of visual tools and step counters may have helped participants develop sustainable habits. Second, the involvement of family-oriented strategies and the cultural framing of weight management as a family responsibility could foster ongoing social support. Third, the significant improvement in dietary quality (which mediated 82% of the effect) suggests that participants adopted broader eating pattern changes rather than short-term restrictive diets. Nevertheless, without ongoing support or booster sessions, relapses remain a risk, as seen in many behavioral weight-loss interventions. Future studies should incorporate maintenance strategies such as periodic follow-up sessions, peer support groups, or digital self-monitoring tools to enhance long-term outcomes [37–39].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Most critically, this study lacked randomization, and participants self-selected into the intervention or control arm. This design feature introduces a substantial risk of selection bias and potential overestimation of the intervention’s true effect. Although participants who chose the intervention group may have been more motivated or ready for change than those who selected the control group, we cannot rule out that unmeasured differences in motivation, self-efficacy, or social support contributed to the large observed differences. While we adjusted for measured baseline imbalances (gender, weight, LDL-cholesterol, and total cholesterol) using ANCOVA and conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses, residual confounding remains a serious concern and may have led to an overestimation of the intervention’s effectiveness.

The intervention arm received substantially more contact and support than the control arm. Participants in the intervention group attended five individual face-to-face counseling sessions (approximately 3 h of direct contact), received culturally adapted printed materials, visual portion guides, smartphone reminders, and step counters. In contrast, the control group received only a single 12-page written booklet with no further contact or support. This large imbalance in attention, accountability, and behavioral reinforcement likely contributed to the very large between-group differences through expectancy effects, social desirability bias, and heightened motivation — independent of the educational content itself. Future studies should therefore use more active control conditions with equivalent contact time to better isolate the effect of the intervention content.

Generalizability is limited. The sample consisted of urban, relatively educated university employees in Erbil. Findings may not extend to rural populations, private-sector workers, individuals with lower literacy, or other regions of Iraq and the broader Middle East. In addition, dietary intake and physical activity were measured using self-report instruments, which are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Although validated and culturally adapted tools were used, and objective improvements in weight, BMI, waist circumference, and biochemical markers provide convergent validity, the possibility of bias in behavioral outcomes cannot be excluded.

Finally, the study was not prospectively registered in a clinical trial registry because of its quasi-experimental design. While all methods, outcomes, and the statistical analysis plan were pre-specified in the protocol before data collection began, the absence of prospective registration remains a limitation that affects transparency.

Despite these limitations, the study possesses notable strengths, including perfect retention, rigorous fidelity monitoring, blinded outcome assessment, and a low-cost, culturally adapted intervention grounded in local norms and the Theory of Planned Behavior. These features enhance confidence in the observed associations and support the value of further rigorous testing of this model.

From a policy perspective, the present findings provide preliminary support for the potential value of workplace-based, theory-driven educational programs in addressing obesity in Iraq. Universities and other large public employers represent a natural setting for such interventions. However, given the quasi-experimental design and short follow-up, strong recommendations for widespread implementation would be premature. Larger randomized controlled trials with longer-term follow-up are needed to confirm durability of effects, rule out selection bias, and evaluate cost-effectiveness before this model can be confidently recommended for national scale-up by the Ministry of Higher Education or Ministry of Health.

Future research should build directly on this foundation. Large-scale randomized controlled trials with a minimum 12-month follow-up are now essential to confirm the durability of weight loss and behavioral changes and to eliminate potential selection effects. Comparative effectiveness studies examining individual versus group delivery formats, as well as cost-effectiveness analyses when the intervention is delivered by routine university health staff, would provide critical data to guide national scale-up. In addition, incorporating objective measures of dietary intake (e.g., weighed food records) and physical activity (e.g., accelerometers) would strengthen mechanistic understanding. Finally, systematic adaptation and evaluation of this model in other occupational groups (such as healthcare workers, schoolteachers, factory employees, and private-sector staff) and in different regions of Iraq would help establish its broader applicability across diverse sociocultural and economic contexts.

Conclusion

In summary, a brief, culturally adapted, Theory of Planned Behavior-based educational intervention delivered in the workplace produced large, clinically meaningful improvements in weight and health outcomes among Iraqi university employees — results that substantially exceed those of previous studies in the region. With perfect retention, high intervention fidelity, and a clear statistical pathway through dietary quality improvement, this low-cost program offers a promising and feasible model for obesity management in Middle Eastern settings. However, these findings require replication in large-scale randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up to confirm effectiveness, durability, and cost-effectiveness before wider implementation can be recommended.
