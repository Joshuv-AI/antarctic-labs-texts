Sugammadex interference with serum progesterone measurement: an in‐vitro study
Authors: Tamblyn Devoy, Theresa Larkin, Miles Allan, Kevin Mantik, Monika McShane, Natalie A. Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntroductionSugammadex may interfere with serum progesterone immunoassays, potentially rendering measured concentrations and resulting recommendations unreliable.MethodsWe investigated the effect of a range of sugammadex concentrations on serum progesterone concentrations in samples from pre‐menopausal female volunteers. Samples were spiked with sugammadex concentrations (15–120 μM), corresponding to clinical doses between 2 mg.kg‐1 and 19 mg.kg‐1. Total progesterone concentrations in each sample were determined using the routine electrochemiluminescence immunoassay method. A subgroup of 15 samples underwent further testing using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectroscopy, comparing baseline with sugammadex 120 μM.ResultsThirty‐five participants provided blood samples. Increasing concentrations of sugammadex were associated with lower measured serum progesterone, with an approximate 10% reduction with each doubling of sugammadex concentration (F(2,30) = 4.86, p = 0.015). Overall, progesterone concentrations decreased by a mean (SD) of 34.6% (12.4%) between control samples and those spiked with sugammadex 120 μM (p = 0.015). In contrast, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectroscopy showed no statistically significant biochemical change in progesterone concentrations between baseline and sugammadex 120 μM.DiscussionSugammadex produced a dose‐dependent reduction in the progesterone concentrations when measured by immunoassay techniques, but not by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectroscopy. This suggests that an immunoassay interference occurs across clinically relevant doses. Progesterone results from routine laboratory testing should be interpreted with caution in the presence of sugammadex.

Introduction

Sugammadex is a modified gamma cyclodextrin that forms complexes with aminosteroid neuromuscular blocking drugs [1, 2]. Exploratory experiments by Merck Sharp & Dohme (Oss, the Netherlands), the original manufacturer of sugammadex, showed an affinity between sugammadex and other steroidal molecules, such as progestogens, which may have clinical implications in everyday use [1, 3, 4]. In addition, Merck Sharp & Dohme suggested that an interaction may also exist between sugammadex and the immunoassay used to measure serum progesterone concentrations [1, 4, 5].

An early spiking experiment using the AxSYM™ Progesterone Enzyme Immunoassay (Abbott Laboratories®, Abbott Park, IL, USA) showed a statistically significant 12.8% reduction in progesterone concentrations after blood samples from three female volunteers were spiked with sugammadex 100 μg.ml‐1 (46 μM), comparable to a clinical dose of 8 mg.kg‐1 [4, 5]. The authors concluded that “this assay may be compromised when used in the presence of sugammadex for the purpose of identifying reproductive phases” [4]. No further studies investigating this interaction have been published. Inaccurate measurement of endogenous hormone levels following sugammadex exposure may question the reliability of conclusions from previous studies in premenopausal women.

The primary aim of this in‐vitro study was to determine whether sugammadex interferes with the measurement of serum progesterone over a range of clinically relevant doses. We hypothesised that measured progesterone concentrations would vary according to sugammadex concentration if assay interference occurred.

Methods

This study was approved by the Joint University of Wollongong and Illawarra Shoalhaven Local Health District and Medical Human Research Ethics Committee.

Female volunteers aged 18–50 y were recruited through staff advertisements at the Wollongong Hospital, NSW, Australia. Eligibility screening, participant information, informed consent and data storage were managed through the REDCap (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA) electronic database. We did not study participants meeting any of the following criteria: cis‐male or transgender persons; age < 18 y or > 50 y; self‐reported menopause; women who may have been pregnant; women with bilateral oophorectomies; and participants with incomplete or missing key details in the eligibility questionnaire form. Women who were on any form of hormonal contraception were also excluded as serum progesterone concentrations are markedly lower in these individuals [6]. Participants with progesterone concentrations below the assay's lower limit of detection were excluded post analysis.

Serum samples (15 ml) were collected from each participant by routine venepuncture. Samples were centrifuged on‐site, stored at 2–8°C, and then transferred on ice to the University of Wollongong, on the day of collection. Samples were spiked on day 5 post‐collection with solutions containing increasing concentrations of sugammadex (Juno Pharmaceuticals, Cremorne, VIC, Australia).

An initial stock dilution of sugammadex 4800 μM was prepared and final concentrations were achieved by serial dilutions. Serum samples were spiked with 50 μl of 0.9% saline (control) or 50 μl of sugammadex to produce final concentrations of 15 μM, 30 μM, 60 μM, 100 μM and 120 μM. These concentrations are approximately comparable to clinical doses of sugammadex of 2–19 mg.kg‐1. Comparability was based on plasma concentrations reported by the manufacturer, with 46 μM corresponding to 8 mg.kg‐1, and other doses proportionally derived by increasing or decreasing the concentration [4, 5].

Following spiking, samples were stored at ‐80°C and transferred to a central laboratory, where progesterone levels were measured using the Cobas® Elecsys™ Progesterone II electroluminescence immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics, North Ryde, NSW, Australia). This is the standard measurement technique used in this laboratory that services our local health district. The limit of quantification for this assay is 0.2 nmol.l‐1 [7]. All samples were analysed using the Roche Cobas® Progesterone Reagent with calibration performed using three‐level quality control (Biorad™ Immunoassay Plus, Hercules, CA, USA), yielding a coefficient of variation of 5–6%. Samples were analysed in duplicate and the mean progesterone concentration used for statistical analysis. Samples underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle.

An unspiked serum sample was used to assign the menstrual phase of participants. Progesterone concentrations were classified as luteal or follicular menstrual phase according to the reported reference ranges of the immunoassay. Participants were classified as being in the luteal phase if progesterone concentration was 5.8–75.9 nmol.l‐1, and in the follicular phase if concentration was 0.2–2.8 nmol.l‐1 [7].

Serum samples from the 15 participants that showed the greatest discrepancy in progesterone concentrations between the lowest and highest concentrations of sugammadex as measured by immunoassay, and with sufficient remaining volume, were subsequently retested using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectroscopy (LC–MS/MS). This method, including progesterone quantitation, was developed and validated locally [8]. Traceable steroid panel calibrators (MassChrom®, Chromsystems, Munich, Germany) and in‐house serum pools were used for calibration and quality control. The stable‐isotope labelled internal standard progesterone‐13C3, (IsoSciences LLC, Ambler, PA, USA) was used. Samples were extracted using supported liquid extraction (Biotage, Uppsala, Sweden) and analysed on an ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a tandem mass spectrometer (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed using a modified C18 analytical column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) with ammonium fluoride and methanol mobile phases, and progesterone was detected using positive electrospray ionisation. Calibrators consisted of six levels between 0.5–77.0 nmol.l‐1, with a lower limit of quantitation of 0.078 nmol.l‐1. On the day of analysis, samples were thawed and split for spiking with 0.9% saline (control) or sugammadex at 120 μM. Samples were analysed immediately using both this mass spectroscopy method and a repeated electroluminescence immunoassay.

The sample size was determined using an ANOVA repeated measures analysis of variance, assuming 80% power and an effect size of 0.25. This recommended a sample size of 27 participants. Recruitment exceeded this target to allow for post‐recruitment exclusions. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). ANOVA with repeated measures with Bonferroni post‐hoc testing was used to compare progesterone concentrations between control samples and those spiked with increasing concentrations of sugammadex measured by immunoassay. F‐statistics were used for multiple group comparisons in addition to pairwise testing. Bland–Altman plots were used to compare control samples and those spiked with 120 μM sugammadex as measured by immunoassay and mass spectrometry.

Results

Participants were recruited in January 2024 and blood samples were obtained from 35 participants. One participant had a serum progesterone concentration below the lower limit of the assay and was excluded (Fig. 1). The mean (SD) age of the included participants was 33 (9) y. Eleven participants were in the luteal phase and 22 in the follicular phase. One participant could not be assigned a menstrual cycle phase as their baseline progesterone concentration fell between the reference ranges and was therefore excluded from the phase‐based analysis.

Sugammadex spiking had a statistically and biochemically significant effect on progesterone concentration (F (5,29) = 2.898, p = 0.031). This effect remained statistically significant when analysed separately by menstrual cycle phase for those in the follicular phase (F (5,17) = 4.676, p = 0.007) and those in the luteal phase (F (5,6) = 6.043, p = 0.024). For the full sample, post‐hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction showed a statistically significant difference between each pairwise comparison of progesterone concentrations across the 15 μM, 30 μM, 60 μM and 120 μM concentrations. The concentrations in the samples spiked with sugammadex 100 μM and 120 μM were not statistically significantly different (p = 0.081). A Bonferroni‐adjusted post‐hoc analysis showed that mean (SD) serum progesterone concentrations were statistically and biochemically lower in samples spiked with sugammadex 120 μM compared with controls: reductions of 34.6% (12.4)% in the full cohort (p = 0.015); 29.2% (12.2)% in the follicular phase (p = 0.002); and 44.7% (2.0)% in the luteal phase (p = 0.002).

Doubling sugammadex concentrations was associated with a proportional reduction in progesterone concentrations (F (2,32) = 4.762, p = 0.015). For the overall cohort, spiking with sugammadex 15 μM reduced progesterone concentrations by a mean (SD) of 8.0% (9.7%). Subsequent doublings in sugammadex concentration produced further decreases of 6.5% (15.4%), 8.3% (16.3%) and 14.8% (14.4%).

The mass spectrometry sub‐study showed a different pattern (Table 1 and online Supporting Information Table S2). Progesterone concentrations in samples containing sugammadex 120 μM were lower than control samples (p = 0.034), although the mean difference was only 1%, and unlikely to be biochemically significant. There were no statistically significant differences between the initial and the repeated immunoassay measurements in samples with sugammadex 120 μM (p = 0.183) (Fig. 3). Bland–Altman analysis showed no consistent correlation between the two measurement methods. For the control samples, the mean difference was 15.6% with 95%CI within the limits of agreement between ‐31.5% and 62.8%. For samples containing 120 μM, the mean difference was ‐46% with 95%CI within the limits of agreement between ‐125.6% to 33.5% (Fig. 4). Mass spectrometry measurements were used as the reference method in this analysis.

Discussion

This is the first in‐vitro study to report the effect of a range of sugammadex concentrations on serum progesterone measurements in premenopausal women. The original study by Merck Sharp & Dohme examined only one concentration of sugammadex and reported a 12.8% reduction in progesterone concentration between baseline and a dose comparable to 8 mg.kg‐1 in the three female participants [4, 5]. We observed an overall mean reduction of 44% in total progesterone concentrations between the control samples and those containing the highest concentration of sugammadex. A reduction of 35% was observed at concentrations corresponding to 8 mg.kg‐1, which is around twice the value reported in the manufacturer's original study. For the most clinically relevant sugammadex doses of 2–4 mg.kg‐1 used in everyday practice, the concentrations were 10–20% lower than controls.

Our findings support previous reports of potential interference between sugammadex and total progesterone concentrations measured by immunoassay, and suggest that the magnitude of this effect may be greater than previously described.

As immunoassays measure total progesterone, sugammadex would not be expected to interfere with the progesterone measurements directly and total levels should remain stable across a range of sugammadex concentrations. However, using electrochemiluminescence assays, progesterone concentrations decreased with increasing concentrations of sugammadex. Possible mechanisms include direct binding of sugammadex, a sugammadex‐progesterone complex or to assay reagents.

To investigate this further, we compared the immunoassay analysis with the mass spectrometry analysis. Whilst a statistically significant difference was observed with mass spectrometry analysis, the magnitude and relevance of this difference were considered to be minimal. The Bland–Altman analysis showed no consistent agreement between the two methods, which supports the interpretation that immunoassay interference, rather than a true change in progesterone, accounts for the observed reduction after sugammadex exposure.

Immunoassays are used widely as their advantages include: user‐friendly instruments; minimal sampling volumes; reasonable costs; simplified pre‐analysis requirements; rapid throughput; and availability [9, 10]. However, their potential for interference with the measurement of hormones is well recognised [11, 12]. The electrochemiluminescence assay information sheet from the manufacturer lists substances with potential cross‐reactivity, and sugammadex is not included [7]. Modern mass spectrometry techniques, including LC–MS/MS, are considered more reliable for measuring steroid compounds as they do not rely on antibody‐based detection and are therefore less susceptible to cross‐reactivity and specificity issues [9]. As a result, they are the reference method for steroid hormone measurement [9, 10]. However, wider use has been limited due to: high equipment costs; need for operator expertise; and slower throughput [9].

A direct comparison of these techniques has been reported in studies measuring sex hormones in saliva, where enzyme‐linked immunosorbent immunoassays performed poorly when compared with mass spectrometry, particularly for progesterone [13]. To our knowledge, a comparison of these methods for measuring female sex steroid hormones in a peri‐operative context has not previously been reported.

Important strengths of the current study include an appropriately powered sample size and evaluation of multiple sugammadex concentrations across a clinically relevant range. The use of a non‐immunoassay method for measuring progesterone also strengthens the findings and supports the presence of an interaction between sugammadex and the immunoassay.

Our study has limitations. Our sub‐study re‐analysed a smaller number of samples, which likely contributed to the large standard deviation observed in the LC–MS/MS results. This was due to budget constraints, which limited re‐analysis to approximately half of the samples at only two concentrations. We chose the highest and lowest sugammadex concentrations to maximise the likelihood of detecting an effect. Given the dramatic difference in results with LC–MS/MS analysis, we are reassured that this sample size was sufficient to support our conclusions of a difference between the two techniques. As an in‐vitro study, another limitation of both the current and the original studies by the manufacturer is the absence of any aminosteroid neuromuscular blocking drugs – such as rocuronium – which is the clinical context in which sugammadex is administered.

We can postulate that in a clinical setting, sugammadex would have complexed with a rocuronium molecule before being exposed to a progesterone assay. As such, the amount of sugammadex available for subsequent complexation would be less than in serum without rocuronium. Initial pharmacokinetic data determined that after administering the recommended dose of sugammadex, the ratio of sugammadex to rocuronium is always >1 for the following 24 h [14]. It is therefore likely that, in vivo, there will be an excess of sugammadex molecules available after complexation with rocuronium that could interact with other molecules and any subsequent assays.

The observed unreliability of immunoassay‐based measurements raises questions about the validity of conclusions from previous studies in this field. For example, one in‐vivo observational study of peri‐operative progesterone concentrations in premenopausal women who were not on a hormonal contraceptive reported decreases of 35% from pre‐operative values to 4 h postoperatively in patients who did not receive sugammadex and 43% in those who did [6]. Although direct comparisons are challenging due to differences in dosing and study design, these findings may partly reflect sugammadex‐assay interference. Whether such changes represent laboratory artefact, true pharmacological effects or physiological responses to surgery remains unclear.

In summary, this in‐vitro study showed that adding sugammadex to serum samples produced a dose‐dependent reduction in total progesterone concentrations measured by immunoassay, but not by mass spectrometry in a limited sub‐study. This suggests interference of the sugammadex molecule with the progesterone immunoassay across a range of clinically relevant drug concentrations. Laboratories should recognise sugammadex as a potential source of immunoassay interference when interpreting progesterone results. Future clinical studies should consider using mass spectrometry‐based methods to ensure accurate hormone measurement.
