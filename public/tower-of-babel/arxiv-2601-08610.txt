Permutation Inference under Multi-way Clustering and Missing Data
Wenxuan Guo, Panos Toulis, Yuhao Wang

Abstract
Econometric applications with multi-way clustering often feature a small number of effective clusters or heavy-tailed data, making standard cluster-robust and bootstrap inference unreliable in finite samples. In this paper, we develop a framework for finite-sample valid permutation inference in linear regression with multi-way clustering under an assumption of conditional exchangeability of the errors. Our assumption is closely related to the notion of separate exchangeability studied in earlier work, but can be more realistic in many economic settings as it imposes minimal restrictions on the covariate distribution. We construct permutation tests of significance that are valid in finite samples and establish theoretical power guarantees, in contrast to existing methods that are justified only asymptotically. We also extend our methodology to settings with missing data and derive power results that reveal phase transitions in detectability. Through simulation studies, we demonstrate that the proposed tests maintain correct size and competitive power, while standard cluster-robust and bootstrap procedures can exhibit substantial size distortions.



1
Introduction

Cluster-robust standard errors are ubiquitous in applied econometrics and statistics whenever data exhibit within-cluster dependence. In many empirical settings, clustering occurs along more than one dimension, such as importer–exporter country pairs in international trade. This leads to dyadic (or multi-way) regression specifications, where standard one-way cluster-robust standard errors
(
26
;
1
;
34
)
are no longer sufficient.

To address this issue,
4
proposed a multi-way extension of standard cluster-robust variance estimators that is relatively simple to implement and has since become standard practice. However, as pointed out by
29
, the multi-way variance formulas of
4
are largely heuristic: they lack formal proofs of validity and can even yield non-positive definite variance matrices. Such numerical issues tend to arise precisely in settings where robust inference is needed, such as when clusters are few, small, or unbalanced.

Building on this literature,
29
provided formal justifications for multi-way cluster-robust inference. Their analysis follows the theoretical framework of
separately exchangeable arrays
developed by
8
;
32
, which treats the data as drawn from a two-dimensional array.
One clustering dimension corresponds to the rows and the other to the columns of this array, with each “cell” containing the data from the corresponding cluster pair.
Within this setup,
29
proved the asymptotic validity of
t
-statistics based on multi-way cluster-robust standard errors and proposed a wild cluster bootstrap procedure that performs well in simulations.

While elegant, the analysis of
29
relies on several assumptions inherited from the separately exchangeable framework: (i) the number of rows and columns must grow without bound, (ii) covariates and errors are jointly exchangeable in the two dimensions, (iii) data are independent across distinct rows and across distinct columns, and (iv) cell sizes are positive in expectation. These conditions are analytically convenient but can exclude many empirically relevant settings. For instance, in international trade data, importer–exporter pairs are finite, exhibit geographic dependence, and covariates are typically nonexchangeable. Such features are common in real data and violate the assumptions in earlier work.

In this paper, we develop an alternative framework for inference under multi-way clustering that replaces all four assumptions above with a single assumption of conditional separate exchangeability of the errors given covariates. Under this symmetry, we construct a permutation test of significance that is valid in finite samples and comes with theoretical power guarantees under plausible alternatives. To our knowledge, this is the first test to deliver finite-sample validity for regression models with multi-way clustered errors. The key restriction, relative to existing work, is that our exchangeability condition applies to the finite sample rather than to the population. See Section
2
for a detailed comparison.

Owing to its construction, our permutation test is conceptually simple and straightforward to implement, especially when compared to multi-way wild cluster bootstrap procedures. Our tests also accommodate a finite number of clusters and impose no assumptions on the covariate distribution, thus allowing for irregular and heavy-tailed data. The extension to missing data can be handled naturally within our framework, with the permutation test performed on a carefully chosen subset of the data consisting of fully observed cells—that is, a clique in graph-theoretic terms. This novel connection to graph theory facilitates the analysis of power properties under Erdös–Rényi-type missingness.

In summary, a key contribution of our paper is to show that a small modification of the separate exchangeability condition in earlier work can lead to finite-sample valid inference via permutation tests and can provide a potentially valuable addition to the standard econometric toolkit.

2
Main Idea and Overview of Results

To illustrate the main idea, consider the following
regression model:

y_{ij}=x_{ij}^{\top}\gamma+d_{ij}^{\top}\beta+\varepsilon_{ij}\;,\quad i,j=1,\dots,n\;,

(1)

where
y_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
are the outcomes,
d_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the covariates of interest, and
x_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are auxiliary covariates;
\varepsilon_{ij}
are the unobserved errors. The parameter
\beta
is of primary interest, while
\gamma
is a nuisance parameter.
Equation (
1
) describes a dyadic regression specification
(
19
)
, such as trade data between importer country
i
and exporter country
j
.
In the array representation of (
1
), index
i
is the “row cluster”,
j
is the “column cluster”, and
there is a single observation per cell
(i,j)
. We will consider extensions in Section
6
.

Our goal is to test the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\beta=0
and perform inference via test inversion. Throughout the paper, our main assumption is the conditional exchangeability of the errors:

(\varepsilon_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)})_{i,j=1}^{n}\mid\{x_{ij},d_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}~,~\text{for all permutations $\pi,\sigma$ on $\{1,\dots,n\}$.}

(2)

That is, the error disturbances have an importer–exporter “random effects” structure conditional on the covariates. Relative to earlier work, this assumption is weaker in that it imposes no restrictions on the covariate distribution, but stronger in that it holds in finite samples rather than the population. We give a detailed comparison in the following section.

Under this invariance assumption, we propose a finite-sample valid permutation test for
H_{0}
.
The procedure, detailed in Section
3
, consists of the following three steps:

1.

(
Partialling-out.
) We construct a group
\mathcal{G}
of two-way permutations and matrices
V_{g}
for each
(\pi,\sigma)\coloneqq g\in\mathcal{G}
such that

V_{g}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=0\;,~\text{and}~V_{g}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{g}=0\;,

(3)

where
\mathbf{X}=[x_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times p}
are the auxiliary covariates in matrix form, and
\mathbf{X}^{g}
is obtained from
\mathbf{X}
after permuting the rows according to
\pi
and
the columns according to
\sigma
. Similarly, define
\mathbf{y}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
as outcome and error vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
, respectively, following the same stacking operation as for
\mathbf{X}
.

This step constructs the matrices
\{V_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
, which act as orthonormal projections to partial out nuisance effects, similar to the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem
(
17
;
28
)
. Section
3
provides simple conditions under which such matrices exist.

2.

(
Minorized statistics.
) We consider test statistics
s(\mathbf{y},V)
that depend on
\mathbf{y}
only through
V\mathbf{y}
. Following from Equation (
3
) and the linear model (
1
), we can thus write
s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})=f(V_{g}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{g})
under the null hypothesis, for some known function
f
. Next, define the statistic’s
minorized
version as
s^{*}(\mathbf{y})=\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}s(\mathbf{y},V_{g})
.
Then, under the null hypothesis, we have

s^{*}(\mathbf{y})=\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}f(V_{g}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}):=f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\;.

(4)

Thus, minorization provides an explicit function of the unobserved errors.

3.

(
Permutation test.
) We compare
s^{*}(\mathbf{y})
with the values

\{s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
, i.e., the randomization
distribution constructed from two-way permutations
\mathcal{G}
on the outcomes.
For instance, we may define the
p
-value through the function

\mathrm{pval}(s^{*}(\mathbf{y}),\{s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}})=\frac{1}{1+|\mathcal{G}|}\Bigl(1+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbbm{1}\{s^{*}(\mathbf{y})\leq s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})\}\Bigr)\;.

It is straightforward to verify that the resulting testing procedure is valid in finite samples for any
n>0
since

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathrm{pval}(s^{*}(\mathbf{y}),\{s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}})}_{\text{feasible test}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{pval}(f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}),\{s(\mathbf{y}^{g},V_{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{pval}(f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}),\{f(V_{g}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}})\;,

\displaystyle\geq\underbrace{\mathrm{pval}(f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}),\{f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{g})\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}})}_{\text{finite-sample valid, infeasible}}\;.

(5)

In the above derivation, the first equality follows from (
4
). The second step follows from the null hypothesis, as argued above.
The last step follows from the minorization property.
Note that the last
p
-value is finite-sample exact due to the invariant hypothesis (
2
). However, such a
p
-value is infeasible because it depends on
transformations of the errors,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{g}
, which are unobserved.
The minorization step yields a feasible test that remains finite-sample valid, albeit slightly conservative due to (
2
); we quantify this conservativeness through simulations.
The full proof for validity is given in Theorem
1
.

An important feature of our construction is that the derivation in (
2
) is not specific to two-way permutations and applies under any invariance group
\mathcal{G}
, provided that an invariance assumption like (
2
) holds. We exploit this flexibility to handle missing data and multi-way clustering in Sections
5
and
6
.

Beyond type I error control, in Theorem
2
we derive a power theory for our test, which is asymptotic and characterizes the minimal signal strength of
|\beta|
required to ensure a diminishing type II error. This result depends explicitly on a parameter of the tail behavior of regression errors and can be adapted to missing data (Section
5
).
This new test preserves finite-sample validity if two-way exchangeability holds conditional on the pattern of missingness (Theorem
4
). To maintain high power, we propose an efficient block-construction algorithm based on graph methods. Using this construction, we derive theoretical power bounds under Erdös–Rényi-type missingness. This analysis quantifies how the density of cells with observed data as compared to all possible cells in the data array affects treatment effect detection (Theorem
5
). Finally, in Section
6
, we generalize our procedure to multi-way clustering, including classical two-way layouts and panel data.

2.1
Related Work

Our work contributes a new perspective to a long line of research on cluster-robust standard errors, developed mainly in econometrics
(
26
;
1
;
3
;
23
;
22
)
. In subsequent work,
4
extended these methods to multi-way clustering. A rigorous analysis of the two-way clustering regime was initiated by
8
;
32
, based on the notion of separate exchangeability of array-valued data.

In a significant development,
29
built upon this theory to establish the asymptotic properties of
t
-statistics based on two-way cluster-robust standard errors; see also
12
;
7
.
Their analysis relies on four key assumptions: (i) both cluster dimensions grow without bound, (ii) covariates and error disturbances are jointly exchangeable, (iii) data are independent across distinct cluster pairs, and (iv) the number of observations within each cluster pair is positive in expectation.
Assumption (ii), in particular, entails an invariance condition that can be written as

(d_{ij},x_{ij},\varepsilon_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(d_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)},x_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)},\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)})_{i,j=1}^{n}\;,

(6)

for all permutations
\pi,\sigma
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
This condition is closely related to our invariance assumption (
2
), as both impose a form of two-way (double) exchangeability on the data.

However, this comparison also underscores key conceptual differences between our framework and the separate exchangeability approach adopted in earlier work.
Specifically, unlike assumption (
6
), our invariance assumption (
2
) imposes no restrictions on the covariate distribution.
Our approach is therefore more realistic in empirical econometric settings where the covariates differ systematically across clusters and cannot reasonably be modeled as random effects.
For instance, in international trade data,
x_{ij}
may represent the distance between countries
i
and
j
.
In this context, it is not realistic to assume that distance is exchangeable with respect to
i
or
j
, and it is even unclear what a superpopulation distribution of between-country distances would really represent.
By contrast, our approach in this paper is to conduct inference conditional on the observed
x_{ij}
.

At the same time, our invariance assumption (
2
) is required to hold for any fixed covariates in the sample, whereas the restriction in (
6
) is on the hypothetical superpopulation. For example, suppose
x_{ij}=f(\eta_{ij})
and
\varepsilon_{ij}=g(\eta_{ij},u_{ij})
for some functions
f,g
and doubly exchangeable arrays
\eta_{ij},u_{ij}
.
In this case, our assumption could be violated because
\varepsilon_{ij}
may no longer be exchangeable conditional on
f(\eta_{ij})
, whereas condition (
6
) would continue to hold assuming the superpopulation is well defined.
The benefit of assuming in-sample invariance is that it enables the construction of permutation tests that are valid in finite samples, while dispensing with all other assumptions (i)–(iv) outlined above.
In short, the distinction arises because our assumption corresponds to a “fixed-design” invariance, whereas earlier work imposes a “random-design” invariance.
Thus, neither framework is a special case of the other, and from that perspective they are best viewed as complementary.

In terms of methodology, our paper is more closely related to an emerging literature in statistics that studies testing and inference under data invariance assumptions. Building on the seminal work of
15
,
43
;
44
formalized early ideas in invariance-based inference through the construction of test statistics that, under the null hypothesis, become explicit functions of the errors, as in Equation (
4
).
The two-way exchangeability invariance (
2
) that we use in this paper was originally introduced by
43
. However, the randomization tests developed in that paper depend on OLS residuals and are generally not finite-sample valid, except in special cases. An important advance toward this goal was achieved by
25
and later substantially extended by
45
. Our construction based on partialling-out and minorization, as described earlier, and the theoretical framework we use to study power follow closely
45
. However, those papers rely on simple one-dimensional exchangeability and do not account for clustering in the regression errors. In addition, they do not address the problem of missing data.

In the social sciences and psychology,
30
;
24
proposed permutation tests for dyadic regression—a special case of two-way clustering—known as quadratic assignment procedures (QAP). However, these tests are not finite-sample valid for the general regressions considered in our paper; see also
9
;
40
.
More broadly, permutation tests frequently arise in experimental and causal inference settings as Fisherian randomization tests
(
14
)
. These procedures are finite-sample valid under sharp null hypotheses, even for complex test statistics
(
18
;
21
)
, but generally require additional modifications to maintain validity under weaker null hypotheses (e.g., regression tests of signifiance), at least in an asymptotic sense
(
39
;
6
;
46
;
11
;
2
;
38
)
. A notable exception is the use of the minorization trick to construct finite-sample valid tests of certain weak null hypotheses
(
5
)
, which also serves as an inspiration for our work. In settings with missing data, we follow a similar approach to
38
by transforming the problem of constructing block permutations into a biclique search problem in graph theory.

3
Main Test under Dyadic Regression

In this section, we remain focused on the dyadic regression setting (
1
) with
n
row clusters,
n
column clusters, and each cell
(i,j)
containing a single observation. We will generalize this setup to multi-way clustering in Section
6
.
For notational simplicity, we express the dyadic regression model (
1
) in matrix form,

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\gamma+\mathbf{D}\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\;.

(7)

Here,
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{D}
, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
collect all dyadic observations into long matrices (or vectors) through a stacking operation. Concretely, we stack the dyadic entries in lexicographic order, and create vectors when stacking scalar values and matrices when stacking vectors; i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=(y_{11},\dots,y_{1n},y_{21},\dots,y_{2n},\dots,y_{n1},\dots,y_{nn})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}\;,

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle=(x_{11},\dots,x_{1n},x_{21},\dots,x_{2n},\dots,x_{n1},\dots,x_{nn})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times p}\;.

That is, the observation associated with the cell
(i,j)
occupies row
(i-1)n+j
in the stacked objects. In this notation, the error vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is defined similarly as
\mathbf{y}
.
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times d}
is a long matrix when
d>1
and reduces to a vector when
d=1
. Our procedure below will handle both cases.

Our goal is to test
H_{0}:\beta=0
under the key assumption of double exchangeability discussed in the introduction. We repeat the definition here for completeness.

Assumption 1
.

For any permutations
\pi,\sigma
on
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, the errors satisfy

(\varepsilon_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)})_{i,j\in[n]}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}\;.

By Assumption
1
, the
n\times n
matrix of dyadic errors is both row-wise and column-wise exchangeable. Double exchangeability holds, for instance, in “random effects” models,

\varepsilon_{ij}=\eta_{i}+\xi_{j}+u_{ij}~,

(8)

where
\eta_{i},\xi_{j},u_{ij}
are i.i.d. random variables conditional on covariates.
Then, Assumption
1
holds since, conditional on covariates,

\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)}=\eta_{\pi(i)}+\xi_{\sigma(j)}+u_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\eta_{i}+\xi_{j}+u_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}\;.

Note that the assumption does not require that
\varepsilon_{ij}
are independent of the covariates
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}
. For instance, the assumption allows
\xi_{j}=f(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})\tilde{\xi}_{j}+g(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})
, where
\{\tilde{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in[n]}
represent generic i.i.d. random variables, and
f,g
are arbitrary functions.

We also note that under Assumption
1
, we may further assume
\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{ij}|x_{ij},d_{ij})=0
without loss of generality, since the invariance is maintained under additive shifts.
As a result, parameters
\beta
and
\gamma
retain their standard regression interpretation.

3.1
Main Testing Procedure

Our main procedure now follows the three steps outlined in Section
2
.
First, we randomly generate
K
row permutations
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K}
and
K
column permutations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{K}
, and define the full set of two-way permutations as

\mathcal{G}=\left\{\left(\pi_{0},\sigma_{0}\right),\left(\pi_{1},\sigma_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(\pi_{K},\sigma_{K}\right)\right\}\;,

(9)

where
\pi_{0}=\sigma_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
is the identity mapping on
[n]
. For now, we assume that
\mathcal{G}
forms an algebraic group in both dimensions. That is, for any
r,s\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
, there exists
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
such that
\pi_{k}=\pi_{r}\circ\pi_{s}
,
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{r}\circ\sigma_{s}
, where
\circ
denotes function composition.
In the following section, we will provide a specific algorithmic construction of
\mathcal{G}
, which will guarantee the group property.

Given a row permutation
\pi
and a column permutation
\sigma
, we can apply them to
outcomes
\mathbf{y}
by replacing
y_{ij}
with
y_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)}
. Hence, we define the permuted data by
\mathbf{y}_{\pi,\sigma}
, where the
[(i-1)n+j]
-th entry of
\mathbf{y}_{\pi,\sigma}
is equal to
y_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)}
, and define the permuted covariates
\mathbf{X}_{\pi,\sigma}
similarly. Finally, let
N=n^{2}
denote the total number of observations in this setup. We are now ready to describe our main testing procedure.

Procedure 1: Invariant Permutation Test under Dyadic Regression.

1.

For
k=1,\dots,K
, find an orthonormal matrix
V_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(N-2p)}
such that
V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=0

and
V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\pi_{k},\sigma_{k}}=0
. Compute

a_{k}=\|\mathbf{D}^{\top}V_{k}V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\|\;,\quad b_{k}=\|\mathbf{D}^{\top}V_{k}V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{k},\sigma_{k}}\|\;.

For
d=1
, using
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote inner product, the quantities above reduce to

a_{k}=|\langle V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{D},V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\rangle|\;,\quad b_{k}=|\langle V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{D},V_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{k},\sigma_{k}}\rangle|\;.

2.

Compute the randomization
p
-value

\mathrm{pval}\coloneqq\frac{1}{K+1}\Bigl(1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\bigl\{\min_{1\leq j\leq K}a_{j}\leq b_{k}\bigr\}\Bigr)\;.

(10)

3.

Inference.
Let
\mathrm{pval}(b)
denote the randomization
p
-value for
H_{0}^{b}:\beta=b
, and define the confidence region

\mathrm{CI}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathrm{pval}(x)>\alpha\}\;.

Then,
\mathrm{CI}
is the
100\times(1-\alpha)\%
confidence region for
\beta
.

As described in the introduction, Step 1 of the procedure constructs an orthonormal matrix
V_{k}
that projects
\mathbf{y}
onto the orthogonal complement of the nuisance-parameter space.
This removes nuisance effects from
\mathbf{X}
(partialling-out step). The other key ingredient is the minorization trick in Equation (
10
) of Step 2, which guarantees
that the reference distribution of the test stochastically dominates the true reference distribution of the idealized test.
The resulting
p
-value is therefore finite-sample valid conditionally on the data according to the following theorem, and thus it is valid marginally as well. See Section
D
in the Appendix for the proof.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{y})
be the data from model (
7
) with
p<N/2
and suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Under
H_{0}:\beta=0
, the
p
-value defined in (
10
) satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{pval}\leq\alpha\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})\leq\alpha~,~\text{for all}~\alpha\in[0,1],~n>0\;.

To our best knowledge, Procedure
3.1
delivers the first general finite-sample valid test of significance in dyadic regression. Under Assumption
1
, it controls the type I error at the nominal level for any error and covariate distribution, allowing heavy-tailed distributions and strong cluster-level dependence.
We demonstrate these points through simulations in Section
7
and the empirical application in Section
8
.

Remark 1
.

We note that Procedure
3.1
requires a permutation group
\mathcal{G}
that is random, as described in the following section. Other tests proposed in this paper also involve certain random components. As a result, running our procedures with different random seeds may yield slightly different
p
-values. A practical remedy is to compute multiple
p
-values and take their median, as suggested by
10
;
20
. We will adopt this strategy for the test applied in Section
B
of the Appendix.

3.2
Construction of Permutation Subgroup

In this section, we propose an algorithm as a concrete way to construct
\mathcal{G}
that has the appropriate group structure required for Procedure
3.1
. The concrete algorithm is adapted from Algorithm 1 of
45
to the dyadic regression setting, and is presented in Algorithm
1
that follows.

Data:

The index set
I
to permute and the number of permutations
K
.

begin

n\leftarrow|I|

Generate a random one-to-one mapping
\pi:I\to[n]

for

k=1,\dots,K

do

Construct a permutation function
\psi_{k}:=\pi^{-1}\circ\tilde{\psi}_{k}\circ\pi
, where
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
is a permutation function such that

1.

If
i>(K+1)\lfloor\frac{n}{K+1}\rfloor
, set
\tilde{\psi}_{k}(i)=i
,

2.

If
i\leq(K+1)\lfloor\frac{n}{K+1}\rfloor
, set

\tilde{\psi}_{k}(i):=\begin{cases}i+k&~\text{if}~i\bmod(K+1)\leq K+1-k\\
i-(K+1-k)&~\text{if}~i\bmod(K+1)>K+1-k\end{cases}\;.

Result:

Set of permutations
\psi_{k}
,
k=0,\dots,K
, where
\psi_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
is the identity mapping on
I
.

Algorithm 1

Permutation set construction

To understand the algorithm, consider
I=[n]
,
\pi=\mathrm{Id}
, and suppose
n
can be divided by
K+1
. Then, our algorithm divides the
n
indices into
m\coloneqq n/(K+1)
ordered lists of indices and performs cyclic permutations on each sub-list. Specifically, we first denote
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
as
m
ordered lists of indices such that

(1,\ldots,n)\coloneqq(\underbrace{1,\ldots,K+1}_{S_{1}},\underbrace{K+2,\ldots,2(K+1)}_{S_{2}},\underbrace{(m-1)(K+1)+1,\ldots,m(K+1)}_{S_{m}})\;,

Then we define
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
as the permutation for which

\left(\tilde{\psi}_{k}(1),\ldots,\tilde{\psi}_{k}(n)\right):=\left(S_{1}^{k},\ldots,S_{m}^{k}\right)\;,

where each
S_{i}^{k}
is created via shifting all the elements in
S_{i}
by
k
places. Taking
S_{1}^{k}
for example, we have
S_{1}^{k}\coloneqq(K+2-k,\ldots,K+1,1,2,\ldots,K+1-k)
. One can easily verify that the resulting set of permutations
\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{K}
formalizes a group, since it is constructed by cyclic permutations. We give a formal proof in Section
D
. For dyadic regression, it suffices to consider
I=[n]
, while in the next few sections, we will apply Algorithm
1
with other choices of index sets.

For dyadic regression and Procedure
3.1
, we just need to apply Algorithm
1
twice and store the outputs as
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
and
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
, which represent the row and column permutations, respectively. Then, one can verify that
\mathcal{G}
defined in (
9
) is an algebraic group, as its row and column permutations are both algebraic groups.
Importantly, Algorithm
1
provides meaningful permutations that lead to a provably powerful test as we show in the next section.

4
Power Analysis

In this section, we focus on the
d=1
setting (testing a single coefficient) and study the statistical power of Procedure
3.1
combined with the construction from Algorithm
1
. To obtain meaningful results, we impose some necessary yet mild moment conditions on dyadic errors. First, rewrite the covariate vector
\mathbf{D}
as a linear model on
\mathbf{X}
:

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{X}\beta^{D}+e\;,

where
e\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an error vector associated with
\mathbf{D}
. Note that this representation is without loss of generality, as one can always set
e\coloneqq\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{X}\beta^{D}
for a given vector
\beta^{D}
.
Next, we impose the following assumption on
e
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
.

Assumption 2
.

The error vectors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
and
e
are from a random effects model:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle=\eta_{i}+\xi_{j}+\zeta_{ij}\;,\quad e_{ij}=\eta^{e}_{i}+\xi^{e}_{j}+\zeta^{e}_{ij}\;.

Here,
\eta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\eta}
,
\xi_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}
,
\zeta_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\zeta}
and
\eta^{e}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\eta}^{e},\xi^{e}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{e},\zeta^{e}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\zeta}^{e}
, all with mean zero.

Assumption
2
requires that the errors associated with
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{y}
are both generated from random features, implying that the dyadic errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
are doubly exchangeable. Additionally, we impose certain moment conditions on the distribution of random features.

Assumption 3
.

The random features of
\{\varepsilon_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
in Assumption
2
satisfy

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\mathbb{E}|\eta_{1}|^{1+t}<\infty,\quad 0<\mathbb{E}|\xi_{1}|^{1+t}<\infty,\quad 0<\mathbb{E}|\zeta_{11}|^{1+t}<\infty\;,

for some constant
t\in[0,1]
. For some
\kappa>0
, the random features of
\{e_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
satisfy

\displaystyle 0<\mathbb{E}|\eta^{e}_{1}|^{2}<\infty,\quad 0<\mathbb{E}|\xi^{e}_{1}|^{2}<\infty,\quad 0<\mathbb{E}|\zeta^{e}_{11}|^{2}<\infty

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\zeta^{e}_{11})^{2}\geq\kappa(\mathbb{E}(\eta_{1}^{e})^{2}+\mathbb{E}(\xi_{1}^{e})^{2})\;.

Assumption
3
requires that the random features in the error vector
e
have finite second moments, and the idiosyncratic variance dominates the cluster-level variances by a factor
\kappa
.
Crucially, our power analysis accommodates heavy-tailed components in dyadic errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, and our result depends explicitly on the moment parameter
t
. Similar assumptions have been employed for power analysis of randomization tests in standard linear models
(
45
)
; here, we extend their assumption to doubly exchangeable errors and perform a refined power analysis. Concretely, the following theorem shows that the type II error of Procedure
3.1
vanishes asymptotically. Throughout the paper,
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
means that
a_{n}\geq cb_{n}
for some constant
c
and all sufficiently large
n
, whereas
a_{n}=\omega(b_{n})
indicates
a_{n}/b_{n}\to\infty
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
K
is fixed and
n
can be divided by
K+1
. Suppose that Assumptions
2
,
3
hold. Let
\mathrm{pval}
be the randomization
p
-value from Procedure
3.1
with the permutation set generated from Algorithm
1
. In the asymptotic regime where
\beta
and
p
vary with
n
in a way such that
n>(\max\{2/\kappa,2\}+m)p
for some constant
m>0
and

|\beta|=\Omega\left(n^{-\frac{t}{1+t}}\right)~\text{if}~t<1\quad~\text{or}~\quad|\beta|=\omega\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)~\text{if}~t=1\;,

we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{pval}>\frac{1}{K+1}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})=0
.

Intuitively, Theorem
2
characterizes the minimal signal strength
|\beta|
to guarantee a diminishing type II error, and a smaller threshold on
|\beta|
indicates higher statistical power. Notably, the threshold increases as the moment parameter
t
decreases, reflecting that heavy-tailed dyadic errors (smaller
t
) make the testing problem more difficult. Note that Assumptions
2
and
3
are used only in power analysis, and are not required for validity.

Interestingly, the minimal signal strength in Theorem
2
is identical to that for linear regression with
n
observations under simple error exchangeability
(
45
, Theorem 3)
.
This implies that testing in the dyadic regression model with
n
clusters is —in terms of detectability— as hard as testing the linear model with
n
observations. This result may be viewed as an analogue of asymptotic results for cluster-robust statistics, where the convergence rate depends on the number of clusters rather than the total number of observations
(
8
;
29
)
.
In our analysis, this phenomenon is a result of Assumption
2
, which introduces cluster-level error components
(\eta,\xi)
and thus reduces the effective sample size from
n^{2}
to
n
. Under a stronger assumption that
\eta=\xi=0
, the dyadic structure can be better exploited to obtain strictly better signal strengths as shown below.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose the assumptions in Theorem
2
hold and that
\eta=\xi=0
with probability one. In the asymptotic regime where
\beta
and
p
vary with
n
in a way such that
n>(\max\{2/\kappa,2\}+m)p
for some constant
m>0
and

|\beta|=\Omega\left(n^{-\frac{2t}{1+t}}\right)~\text{if}~t<1\quad~\text{or}~\quad|\beta|=\omega\left(n^{-1}\right)~\text{if}~t=1\;,

we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{pval}>\frac{1}{K+1}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})=0
.

Direct calculation shows that the minimal signal strength in Theorem
3
equals the square of the corresponding threshold in Theorem
2
; this is because the effective sample size increases from
n
to
n^{2}
. See Section
E
for detailed proofs.

5
Dyadic Regression with Missing Data

In this section, we turn our attention to the problem of missing data. We
remain focused on testing
H_{0}
under the dyadic regression model (
1
), but now only a subset of cells are observed:

(y_{ij},x_{ij},d_{ij})~\text{is observed if and only if}~(i,j)~\text{satisfies that}~M_{ij}=1~.

(11)

In the expression above,
M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is a mask matrix, where
M_{ij}=1
indicates an observed cell
(i,j)
and
M_{ij}=0
indicates a missing cell. In general, it is not possible to conduct valid inference if
M
has an arbitrary distribution with respect to the observed data
(
27
)
. To make progress, we make the following assumption on the missingness mechanism.

Assumption 4
.

The missing mask matrix
M
satisfies

M\perp\!\!\!\perp(\varepsilon_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}\;.

In plain language, under Assumption
4
, the missing mechanism is conditionally independent of dyadic errors, such that the double exchangeability is preserved conditional on the observed data.
Note that this assumption allows the missing data mechanism to depend on
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}
in an arbitrary way. However, the assumption will be violated, in general, if

M
depends on the observed outcomes
\mathbf{y}
.

H_{0}:\beta=0
under missing data, we first study why Procedure
3.1
can fail. By way of illustration, in Figure

However, other permutations (e.g., permutation 2) remain feasible because they only permute data between fully observed entries.

Definition 1
.

Let

for all

\mathcal{F}_{M}=\{I_{q}\times J_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}

I_{q}\,\cap\,I_{q^{\prime}}=J_{q}\,\cap\,J_{q^{\prime}}\;=\;\emptyset\quad\text{for all}~q\neq q^{\prime}\;.

Given a disjoint set of fully observed blocks
\mathcal{F}_{M}=\{I_{q}\times J_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
, we may decompose the original data into sub-arrays—each determined by

I_{q}
and columns in
J_{q}
—and then run Procedure
3.1
separately within each sub-array. This is possible because there are no missing data within each sub-array, and errors remain doubly exchangeable under Assumption
4
.
This idea is implemented in Procedure
5
below.

Procedure 2: Conditional Permutation Test with Missing Data.

1.

Construct a set of fully observed blocks,
\mathcal{F}_{M}=\{I_{q}\times J_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
, under mask
M
.

2.

For each
q=1,\ldots,Q
:

(a)

Subset the data by rows
I_{q}
and columns
J_{q}
. Denote the data sub-array as
(\mathbf{X}_{q},\mathbf{D}_{q},\mathbf{y}_{q})
.

(b)

Apply Algorithm
1
on index sets
I_{q},J_{q}
to generate the permutation set
\mathcal{G}_{q}=\{(\pi_{0,q},\sigma_{0,q}),\ldots,(\pi_{K,q},\sigma_{K,q})\}
for data sub-array
q
.

3.

Construct
\mathcal{G}=\{(\pi_{0},\sigma_{0}),\ldots,(\pi_{K},\sigma_{K})\}

by concatenating the sub-array permutations,

\pi_{k}=(\pi_{k,1}:\ldots:\pi_{k,Q})
, for
k=0,\ldots,K
.

In this notation,
\pi_{k}
is a permutation of
[n]
, such that elements in
I_{q}
are permuted according to
\pi_{k,q}
, for each

, while all other elements of
[n]
remain fixed.

Similarly, define
\sigma_{k}=(\sigma_{k,1}:\ldots:\sigma_{k,Q})
.

4.

Apply Procedure
3.1
to the stacked data
\{(\mathbf{X}_{q},\mathbf{D}_{q},\mathbf{y}_{q})\}_{q=1}^{Q}
and the
permutation set
\mathcal{G}
from step 3.

The following theorem establishes the finite-sample validity of Procedure

.

for a proof.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
4
hold for the dyadic regression model (
1
). Suppose also that
p<\widetilde{N}/2
, where

\widetilde{N}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}|I_{q}|\cdot|J_{q}|:=|\mathcal{F}_{M}|
. Then, under
H_{0}:\beta=0
, the
p
-value obtained from Procedure
5
satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{pval}\leq\alpha\mid M,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})\leq\alpha~\text{for all}~\alpha\in[0,1],~n>0\;.

To maintain finite-sample validity, Procedure
5
uses a subset of the data contained in the fully observed blocks
\mathcal{F}_{M}
, but this may result in efficiency loss. We will study such power loss in Section
5.1
under assumptions on the missingness mechanism.

Remark 2

(Construction of
\mathcal{F}_{M}
)
.

Procedure
5
requires a set of fully observed blocks
\mathcal{F}_{M}
.
In Section
A
of the Appendix, we propose a graph-theoretic construction of
\mathcal{F}_{M}
and provide a concrete algorithm.
At a high level, we view the mask matrix
M
as an adjacency matrix that defines edges between rows and columns; i.e., row
i
is connected with column
j
if and only if
M_{ij}=1
.
Under this representation, a fully observed block corresponds to a complete bipartite subgraph (a biclique), and we find a biclique decomposition of the graph by iteratively solving the maximum biclique problem. Although this problem is generally NP-hard
(
36
;
47
)
, Procedure
5
remains valid even when the biclique maximization is approximate, e.g., using software packages such as the
Bimax
method
(
37
)
.

Finally, we note an interesting connection to randomization tests that rely on biclique decompositions under interference
(
38
)
. In that setting, a biclique represents a subset of the data for which a weak null hypothesis reduces to a sharp one, which is analogous to how a biclique in our setting represents a subset of the data with no missing information.

5.1
Power Analysis of Procedure
5

Similar to Section
4
, we focus on the
d=1
setting of testing a single coefficient. The power properties of Procedure
5
necessarily depend on the missing-data mechanism. Because this mechanism can be arbitrary, we impose additional assumptions to enable theoretical analysis. Specifically, we assume that the missing cells are generated independently with missingness probability
1-\rho
, corresponding to the
missing completely at random
(MCAR) scenario of
27
.
We therefore proceed with the following assumption.

Assumption 5
.

The design graph
G
is generated from mask
M
, where
\{M_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
are independent Bernoulli variables with probability
\mathbb{P}(M_{ij}=1)=\rho
.

The type of random bipartite graphs related to Assumption
5
has been widely studied in random graph theory
(
16
)
, and can be viewed as a natural generalization of Erdős–Rényi-type graphs
(
13
)
. Under this random graph model, we establish the following result on the size of the largest biclique, which is a determinant factor for the test power.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
5
, with probability converging to one, the largest biclique in
G
has size
s\times s
with

s=\Omega\Bigl(\min\Bigl\{\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\log n,\frac{n^{1/3}}{\log n}\Bigr\}\Bigr)\;.

The rate of the largest biclique depends on
\rho/(1-\rho)
, the probability odds of observing any data cell—let
r_{\mathrm{odds}}\coloneqq\rho/(1-\rho)
. Proposition
1
is motivated by the classical literature on the maximum clique size in random graphs, and its proof relies on concentration bounds on clique counts. More concretely, the first component (
\log n
) is consistent with the classical result that the maximum clique in an Erdös–Rényi graph grows at the order of
\log n

(
31
)
; additionally, we quantify how the clique size grows with
r_{\mathrm{odds}}
. The second component, to our best knowledge, is a new result characterizing the limiting behavior of the maximum clique, with the rate
n^{1/3}
stemming from the technical proof.

Next, we apply Proposition
1
to characterize the statistical power of the conditional permutation test of Procedure
5
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that
K
is fixed and that Assumptions
1
-
3
,
5
hold. Let
\mathrm{pval}
be the randomization
p
-value from Procedure
5
on a single permutation block from the largest biclique of the design graph.
In the asymptotic regime where
\beta
and
p
vary with
n
in a way such that
s>(\max\{2/\kappa,2\}+C)p
for some constant
C>0
,
s
is divisible by
K+1
, and

|\beta|=\Omega\left(s^{-\frac{t}{1+t}}\right)~\text{if}~t<1\quad~\text{or}~\quad|\beta|=\omega\left(s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)~\text{if}~t=1\;,

we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{pval}>\frac{1}{K+1}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D})=0
.

Theorem
5
follows directly from Theorem
2
by replacing the sample size
n
with the largest biclique size
s
defined in Proposition
1
.
Although Procedure
5
allows a sequence of disjoint bicliques in practice, we condition on a single largest clique to simplify the analysis.
Under missing data, the minimum detectable signal strength scales with the missingness factor captured by
s
.
To better understand these results, consider the following scenarios:

(i)

Constant missingness probability.

When
\rho\in(0,1)
is fixed, the largest biclique has size of order
\log n
.
This is a well-established result in random graph theory
(
16
)
.
Consequently, our test requires exponentially larger sample sizes to achieve a vanishing type II error.
That is, loosely speaking, to detect a signal of magnitude
|\beta|=\epsilon
, we require
e^{1/\epsilon}
data points.
This illustrates the fundamental impact of MCAR data on efficiency loss.

(ii)

Diminishing missingness probability I.

Suppose that
1-\rho\to 0
and, in addition,
r_{\mathrm{odds}}=O(n^{1/3}/\log^{2}n)
; that is, the odds of observation increase slowly at this rate.
Then, the biclique size satisfies

s\sim r_{\mathrm{odds}}\log n\;.

In other words,
s
grows proportionally to the odds of observation up to a logarithmic factor.
Compared with the first case,
s
can grow polynomially in
n
,
and thus the minimum detectable signal can decay at a polynomial rate, though not necessarily at the canonical
n^{-1/2}
rate.

(iii)

Diminishing missingness probability II.

Suppose that
1-\rho\to 0
and
r_{\mathrm{odds}}>n^{1/3}/\log^{2}n
,
corresponding to rapidly increasing odds of observation.
Then, the biclique size saturates at order
n^{1/3}/\log n
.
Beyond this threshold, further increases in the odds of observation do not enlarge the biclique size.

We note that our power analysis under missing data is novel and potentially of independent interest.
Prior work on random bipartite graphs has analyzed biclique sizes primarily under the assumption of a constant edge probability
\rho

(
16
;
31
)
.
Our results extend this setting in two directions.
First, we establish a conceptual link between missing-data mechanisms and random-graph theory, which proves useful for conducting power analysis under missingness.
Second, we refine the graph-theoretic analysis by allowing
\rho
to vary with
n
, yielding a more complete characterization of how missingness affects biclique size—namely, the second and third regimes discussed above.
Additional details and technical proofs are provided in Section
G
of the Appendix.

6
Generalization to Multi-way Clustering

In this section, we extend our methodology to settings with multi-way clustered errors.
The extension is straightforward within our framework because the key argument for finite-sample validity, given in Equation (
2
), does not rely on the specific structure of double exchangeability.
Accordingly, our discussion focuses on suitable modifications of the invariance property (
2
) for each new domain, while implementation follows the same structure as Procedures
3.1
and
5
.

As a starting point, consider the following three-way regression model:

y_{ijl}=x_{ijl}^{\top}\gamma+d_{ijl}^{\top}\,\beta+\varepsilon_{ijl},\quad i\in[m],~j\in[n],~l\in[\ell]\;.

(12)

In the trade context,
i
and
j
may denote importer and exporter countries, and
l
may denote industry, product category, or time in the case of repeated observations.
Beyond the three-way structure, note that model (
12
) generalizes the baseline dyadic model (
1
) by allowing for clusters of different sizes. In the following sections, we discuss several representative scenarios.

6.1
Random effects

Suppose that the errors obey a full random-effects structure:

\varepsilon_{ijl}=\eta_{i}+\xi_{j}+\zeta_{l}+u_{ijl}\;,

where, following (
8
), the random effects
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in[m]},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in[n]},\{\zeta_{l}\}_{l\in[\ell]}
and the idiosyncratic errors
\{u_{ijl}\}_{i\in[m],j\in[n],l\in[\ell]}
are i.i.d. within each family and mutually independent. Then, the errors satisfy three-way exchangeability: for any permutations
\pi,\sigma,\psi
on
[m]
,
[n]
, and
[\ell]
, we have

(\varepsilon_{ijl})_{i\in[m],j\in[n],l\in[\ell]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)\psi(l)})_{i\in[m],j\in[n],l\in[\ell]}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}\;,

(InvA)

where
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}
denote the stacked matrix (or vector) for
x_{ijl}
and
d_{ijl}
, respectively. To test
H_{0}\!:\beta=0
under this invariance, we can adapt our main test as follows:

(i)

Apply Algorithm
1
three times to construct a three-way permutation group
\mathcal{G}
.

(ii)

Apply Procedure
3.1
using the group
\mathcal{G}
.

The resulting permutation test is finite-sample valid.

6.2
Panel models

In panel models, one clustering dimension (e.g.,
l
) corresponds to time:

y_{ijt}=x_{ijt}^{\top}\gamma+d_{ijt}^{\top}\,\beta+\varepsilon_{ijt}\;.

In the trade model, where
i
and
j
denote importer and exporter countries,
t
may represent the year of observation.
In such settings, full three-way exchangeability (
InvA
) is implausible because errors are typically not exchangeable over
t
due to autocorrelation.
Suppose instead that the errors remain exchangeable across the first two dimensions:

(\varepsilon_{ijt})_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\varepsilon_{\pi(i)\sigma(j)t})_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{D}\;.

(InvB)

This holds, for instance, under the model

\varepsilon_{ijt}=\eta_{i}+\xi_{j}+\zeta_{t}+u_{ijt}
,
where
\eta_{i},\xi_{j}
are random effects as before, but
\zeta_{t}
represents an arbitrary time trend.
To test the null hypothesis for
\beta
under (
InvB
), we proceed as follows:

(i)

Apply Algorithm
1
twice—once for each of the index sets
[m]
and
[n]
—to construct a two-way permutation subgroup
\mathcal{G}
.

(ii)

Apply Procedure
3.1
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
.

The resulting permutation test is finite-sample valid because it applies the baseline dyadic test within each time period
t
, thereby controlling for the unknown time trend.
To our knowledge, this is the first instance of a finite-sample valid test for testing
\beta=0
under exchangeable errors (
InvB
) in panel data models, which we discuss further in Section
9
.

6.3
Two-way layouts

In some settings, the number of observations per cell
(i,j)
in model (
12
), denoted
\ell_{ij}
, may vary across
i
and
j
.
In such cases, the above procedure cannot be applied directly, as it requires data blocks of equal size.

An alternative is to perform permutations only with respect to cluster
l
, effectively permuting data independently within each cell
(i,j)
.
The full procedure is as follows:

(i)

For each cell
(i,j)
, apply Algorithm
1
on index set
[\ell_{ij}]
.
Denote the resulting permutation group as
\mathcal{G}_{ij}=\{\pi_{k}^{ij}\}_{k=0}^{K}
, where
\pi_{0}^{ij}=\mathrm{Id}
.

(ii)

Apply Procedure
3.1
using
\mathcal{G}
as the permutation group, where
\mathcal{G}
is formed by concatenating
(\mathcal{G}_{ij})_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}
as in Procedure
5
.

The resulting permutation test is finite-sample valid, even under a relaxation of (
InvA
) in which exchangeability holds only with respect to cluster
l
.
For example, the error structure
\varepsilon_{ijl}=\eta_{ij}+\zeta_{l}+u_{ijl}
satisfies this condition if
\eta_{ij}
are arbitrary but
\zeta_{l}
are i.i.d.
This test is thus appropriate in settings where
l
indexes independent replications, such as two-way layouts in randomized experiments
(
33
)
.

6.4
Irregular designs

In certain two-way layouts, permuting data within each cell
(i,j)
may not be feasible.
For instance, if cluster
l
denotes time, as in the panel case (B), then invariance (
InvB
) is not plausible, and cell-level permutations would lead to an invalid test.
Moreover, if
d_{ijl}
is fixed within a given cell
(i,j)
—that is, a dyad-level covariate—then within-cell permutations yield a trivial test with zero power.

A practical solution for such irregular designs is to combine the conditional permutation test for missing data (Section
5
) with the panel-data test from Case (B). For a fixed threshold
L_{0}
, define

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\ell_{ij}\geq L_{0},\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We then propose the following procedure:

(i)

Apply Algorithm
2
using
M
as the mask matrix to obtain a set of fully observed blocks,
\mathcal{F}_{M}
. For each cell
(i,j)
in
\mathcal{F}_{M}
, randomly drop
\ell_{ij}-L_{0}

9
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we propose permutation tests of significance that are finite-sample valid under a suitable double invariance assumption on the errors. This framework of invariance-based inference provides an attractive alternative to standard asymptotic methods, particularly in settings with complex dependence structures. As we have shown, however, such complexity inevitably entails a trade-off between finite-sample validity and statistical efficiency. For example, our procedure for irregular designs—see scenario (D) in Section
6
—relies on subsampling the original data, which can result in efficiency loss. Similarly, our conditional permutation tests for missing data require a pre-specified mask matrix, whose construction involves balancing the number of cells against their sizes. Developing practical algorithms to optimally design this mask matrix is an important next step. More broadly, providing concrete guidance on how to navigate the trade-off between validity and efficiency is a valuable direction for future research.

An important extension is to analyze the performance of our test under the specific setting of panel data. On one hand, applying the invariance-based procedure to real panel data would provide practical insights beyond the simulations and applications considered in the current paper. On the other hand, a formal characterization of the test’s power under various panel-specific error structures remains open. It remains a compelling direction for future work to develop such theoretical guarantees and to understand how temporal dependence in panel data shapes the test’s efficiency.

Here, we explain the graph-theoretic construction of

in Remark

Then,

\mathcal{F}_{M}

Remark 3
.

The biclique decomposition problem also arises in
38
in the context of randomization tests under interference. In that paper, a biclique represents a subset of the data for which a weak null hypothesis reduces to a sharp one that can be tested via a Fisher randomization test. This interpretation is conceptually distinct from our setting, where a biclique corresponds to a fully observed subset of the data.
Nevertheless, in both contexts, achieving finite-sample validity requires conditioning on a biclique, so the task of identifying bicliques is common to both approaches.

G.1
Proof of Proposition
1

Given a random bipartite graph
G
in Assumption
5
, let
X_{s}
denote the number of bicliques of size
s\times s
. Recall that
r_{\mathrm{odds}}=\rho/(1-\rho)
. Lemma
4
shows that
X_{s}>0
with high probability, therefore a biclique of size
s\times s
exists.

Lemma 4
.

Let
s=\min\{(2/3-b_{0})r_{\mathrm{odds}}\log n,a_{n}{n}^{1/3}\}
for any constant
b_{0}>0
and a diminishing sequence
a_{n}=o(1)
. We have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{s}>0)=1
.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

By definition,
X_{s}=\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}\{(S,S^{\prime})~\text{is a biclique}\}
, where
S
and
S^{\prime}
are subsets of
[n]
with size
s
. To analyze the tail behavior of
X_{s}
, let
S,S^{\prime},T,T^{\prime}
be subsets of
[n]
with size
s
, and denote
S\times S^{\prime}\sim T\times T^{\prime}
if
S\times S^{\prime}\cap T\times T^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Then, define

\bar{\Delta}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(S,S^{\prime})\sim(T,T^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left((S,S^{\prime}),(T,T^{\prime})\text{ are $s\times s$ bicliques in }G\right)\;.

Then, by Janson’s inequality (Theorem 27.13 of
16
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{s}=0\right)\leq\exp(-\frac{\left(\mathbb{E} X_s\right)^2}{2 \bar{\Delta}})\;.

It suffices to show that the ratio
\bar{\Delta}/(\mathbb{E}X_{s})^{2}
goes to zero. By definition of
X_{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}((S,S^{\prime})~\text{is a biclique})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{=}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\rho^{s^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(ii)}}}{{=}}\binom{n}{s}^{2}\rho^{s^{2}}\;.

Here (i) holds because
(S,S^{\prime})
has
s^{2}
edges in total and each edge occurs with probability
\rho
. (ii) follows from the fact that there are
\binom{n}{s}
different choices for
S
and
S^{\prime}
, and hence we have
\binom{n}{s}^{2}
combinations of
(S,S^{\prime})
. Similarly, to compute
\bar{\Delta}
, we first enumerate all possible pair of
(S,S^{\prime})
, then enumerate
(T,T^{\prime})
where the overlapping indices vary from
1
to
s
.

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(S,S^{\prime})\sim(T,T^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left((S,S^{\prime}),(T,T^{\prime})\text{ are $s\times s$ bicliques}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\sum_{T,T^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}\{|S\cap T|\geq 1\}\mathbb{I}\{|S^{\prime}\cap T^{\prime}|\geq 1\}\mathbb{P}\left((S,S^{\prime}),(T,T^{\prime})\text{ are $s\times s$ bicliques}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\sum_{T,T^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{I}\{|S\cap T|=i\}\mathbb{I}\{|S^{\prime}\cap T^{\prime}|=j\}\mathbb{P}\left((S,S^{\prime}),(T,T^{\prime})\text{ are $s\times s$ bicliques}\right)\;.

For each pair of
i
and
j
, it requires
s^{2}
edges for
(S,S^{\prime})
to be a biclique, and
(s^{2}-ij)
additional edges for
(T,T^{\prime})
to be a biclique. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}((S,S^{\prime}),(T,T^{\prime})\text{ are $s\times s$ bicliques})=\rho^{s^{2}}\times\rho^{s^{2}-ij}
and

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\sum_{T,T^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{I}\{|S\cap T|=i\}\mathbb{I}\{|S^{\prime}\cap T^{\prime}|=j\}\rho^{s^{2}-ij}

\displaystyle=\rho^{s^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{n-s}{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\binom{n-s}{s-j}\binom{s}{j}\rho^{s^{2}-ij}\;.

In the last equality, the number of choices for
T
is
\binom{n-s}{s-i}\binom{s}{i}
since we select
i
indices from
S
and
s-i
indices from the complement of
S
. Similarly, the number of choices for
T^{\prime}
is
\binom{n-s}{s-j}\binom{s}{j}
. Lastly, as shown earlier, there are
\binom{n}{s}
choices for
S
and
S^{\prime}
, which leads to

\bar{\Delta}=\binom{n}{s}^{2}\rho^{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{n-s}{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\binom{n-s}{s-j}\binom{s}{j}\rho^{s^{2}-ij}\;.

Based on the expression of
\mathbb{E}X_{s}
and
\bar{\Delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\Delta}}{\left(\mathbb{E}X_{s}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{n}{s}^{2}\rho^{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{n-s}{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\binom{n-s}{s-j}\binom{s}{j}\rho^{s^{2}-ij}}{\left(\binom{n}{s}^{2}\rho^{s^{2}}\right)^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\binom{n-s}{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\binom{n-s}{s-j}\binom{s}{j}}{\binom{n}{s}^{2}}\rho^{-ij}\eqqcolon\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}u_{i,j}.

In the following we show that
u_{i,j}\leq u_{1,1}
. By definition, for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\frac{u_{i,j+1}}{u_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s-j)^{2}}{(n-2s+j+1)(j+1)}\rho^{-i}\leq\frac{s^{2}}{(n-2s)(j+1)}\rho^{-i}=\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)\frac{s^{2}}{n(j+1)}\rho^{-i}\;.

Similarly,

\frac{u_{i+1,j}}{u_{i,j}}\leq\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)\frac{s^{2}}{n(i+1)}\rho^{-j}\;.

Therefore, for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\frac{u_{i,j}}{u_{1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)^{i+j}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n}\right)^{i+j}\frac{\rho^{-ij-1}}{i!j!}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{\leq}}\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)^{i+j}\left(\frac{es^{2}}{in}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{es^{2}}{jn}\right)^{j}\rho^{-ij-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(ii)}}}{{\leq}}\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)^{i+j}\left(\frac{es^{2}}{in}\cdot\rho^{-i/2}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{es^{2}}{jn}\cdot\rho^{-j/2}\right)^{j}\rho^{-1}

where (i), (ii) follow from
i!\geq(i/e)^{i}
and
ij\leq(i^{2}+j^{2})/2
, respectively. Since

\displaystyle\frac{es^{2}}{in}\cdot\rho^{-i/2}=\exp(1 + 2 \log s - \log i - \log n + \frac{i}{2} \log\frac{1}{\rho})

and
1/\rho=1+1/r_{\mathrm{odds}}
and
\log(1+x)\asymp x
, we have
\log\frac{1}{\rho}\asymp\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{odds}}}
and

\displaystyle\frac{es^{2}}{in}\cdot\rho^{-i/2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2 \log s - \log n + \frac{s}{2\rodds})\leq\exp(2 \log s - \log n + \frac{(2/3-b_0)\rodds\log n}{2\rodds})

\displaystyle=\exp(2 \log s - \log n + (1/3-b_0/2)\log n)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2/3 \log n - \log n + 1/3 \log n - b_0 \log n / 2)=\exp(- b_0 \log n / 2)=o(1)\;,

where the last inequality follows from
s\leq n^{1/3}
. Similarly, we can show that
\frac{es^{2}}{jn}\cdot\rho^{-j/2}=o(1)
. In addition, by the inequality
1+x\leq\exp(x)
, we have

\left(1+\frac{2s}{n-2s}\right)^{i+j}\leq\exp(\frac{2s}{n-2s} (i+j))\leq\exp(\frac{4s^2}{n-2s})=O(1)\;,

where the last equality follows form
s\leq n^{1/3}
. Therefore, for
n
large enough, we have
u_{i,j}/u_{1,1}<1
, i.e.,
0<u_{i,j}<u_{1,1}
.

Based on the analysis above, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\Delta}}{\left(\mathbb{E}X_{s}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}u_{i,j}\leq s^{2}u_{1,1}=s^{2}\frac{\binom{n-s}{s-1}\binom{s}{1}\binom{n-s}{s-1}\binom{s}{1}}{\binom{n}{s}^{2}}\rho^{-1}=O\Bigl(\frac{s^{6}}{n^{2}}\Bigr)\;.

Since
s=o(n^{1/3})
, the above quantity is
o(1)
, which completes the proof.
∎

By setting
b_{0}=1/3
and

in the parameter

Suppose the largest biclique for the permutation block is of size

Lemma 5
.

Consider a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a permutation matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have
|\tr(MP)|\leq\tr(M)
.

Proof.

Using the positive semidefiniteness and symmetry of
M
, we have for any
i,j
,
|{M}_{ij}|\leq\frac{{M}_{ii}+{M}_{jj}}{2}
. Let
\sigma
be the permutation induced by
P
. We have

\tr(MP)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i,\sigma(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|M_{i,\sigma(i)}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{M_{ii}+{M}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)}}{2}=\tr(M)\;.

∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a random vector with i.i.d. entries and
\mathbb{E}u_{1}=0
,
\mathbb{E}u_{1}^{2}\leq 2
. For a symmetric matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with zero diagonal entries, we have for any fixed
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{u^{\top}Mu}{n^{2}}>\delta\right)\leq\frac{8\|M\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{4}\delta^{2}}\;.

(28)

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be another random vector with i.i.d. entries and
\mathbb{E}v_{1}=0
,
\mathbb{E}v_{1}^{2}\leq 2
,
u\perp\!\!\!\perp v
. For any matrix
N\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
, we have for any fixed
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{u^{\top}Nv}{n^{2}}>\delta\right)\leq\frac{4\|N\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{4}\delta^{2}}\;.

(29)

Proof.

To prove (
28
), observe that

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{u^{\top}Mu}{n^{2}}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}M_{ij}\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{n^{2}}\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{=}}2\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}M_{ij}^{2}\frac{u_{i}^{2}u_{j}^{2}}{n^{4}}\right)\leq\frac{8\|M\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{4}}\;.

where (i) follows from
u_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp u_{j}
for any
i\neq j
. Then by applying Chebyshev’s inequality, we obtain (
28
).
To prove (
29
), since
u_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp v_{j}
for any
i,j
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{u^{\top}Nv}{n^{2}}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{k}N_{ij}\frac{u_{i}v_{j}}{n^{2}}\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{k}N_{ij}^{2}\frac{u_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}}{n^{4}}\right)\leq\frac{4\|N\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{4}}\;.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, we obtain (
29
).
∎

The next Lemma gives the upper and lower bounds for the trace of projection matrix products, which is used in our analysis.

Lemma 7
.

Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two projection matrices and
P
be a
n\times n
permutation matrix. We have

\displaystyle\tr(M_1 M_2)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{0,\tr(M_1)+\tr(M_2)-n\}\;,

(30)

\displaystyle\tr(M_1 M_2 P)

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\tr(M_1),\tr(M_2)\}\;.

(31)

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{span}(M_{1})
and
\mathrm{span}(M_{2})
be the linear spaces spanned by projection matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, and let
t=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{1})\cap\mathrm{span}(M_{2})),r=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{1})),s=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{2}))
. Then, we express the projection matrices by
M_{1}=UU^{\top},M_{2}=WW^{\top}
. Here,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
are orthonormal matrices corresponding to
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, and
U
,
W
have the same top
t
columns from their intersection space. Then,
\tr(M_1 M_2)=\|U^{\top}W\|_{F}^{2}\geq 0
. Note that
U^{\top}W
is a
r\times s
matrix, and one can easily verify that the top
t\times t
block is the identity matrix by construction. Therefore,
\|U^{\top}W\|_{F}^{2}\geq t
. In addition, by the Grassmann’s formula,

t=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{1}))+\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{2}))-\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(M_{1})\cup\mathrm{span}(M_{2}))\geq r+s-n\;.

For projection matrices, we have
r=\tr(M_1)
and
s=\tr(M_2)
. Hence we obtain (
30
).

Secondly, let
\sigma_{i}(A)
be the
i
-th singular value of a matrix
A
. By von Neumann’s trace inequality, for
n\times n
matrices
A
and
B
, we have
|\tr(AB)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(A)\sigma_{i}(B)
. By setting
A=M_{1}M_{2}
and
B=P
, we have

|\tr(M_1 M_2 P)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M_{1}M_{2})\sigma_{i}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M_{1}M_{2})\;,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
P
is an orthogonal matrix and
\sigma_{i}(P)=1
. Then, by the AM-GM inequality we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M_{1}M_{2})\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{rank}(M_{1}M_{2})}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M_{1}M_{2})^{2}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{rank}(M_{1}M_{2})}\|M_{1}M_{2}\|_{F}\;.

The basic rank inequality justifies that
\mathrm{rank}(M_{1}M_{2})\leq\min\{\tr(M_1),\tr(M_2)\}
. Additionally,
\|M_{1}M_{2}\|_{F}^{2}=\tr(M_1 M_2)\leq\min\{\tr(M_1),\tr(M_2)\}
. To summarize, we have
\tr(M_1 M_2 P)\leq\min\{\tr(M_1),\tr(M_2)\}
and the second result is proved.
∎
