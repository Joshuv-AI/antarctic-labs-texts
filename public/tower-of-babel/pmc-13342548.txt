Superior protection against tuberculosis using heterologous mRNA nanoadjuvant vaccines
Authors: Bubacarr J. B. Touray, Alieu K. Faburay, Fakry F. Mohamed, Sherein S. Abdelgayed, Adel M. Talaat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health threat, underscoring the need for vaccines that surpass BCG efficacy. We developed QTAP-R, a novel mRNA–lipid nanoparticle (LNP) vaccine encoding Ag85B, Hsp70, and ESAT-6, to enhance immunity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. QTAP efficiently encapsulated and delivered mRNA with high transfection efficiency and low cytotoxicity. In C57BL/6 mice, QTAP-R induced strong antigen-specific IgG and T-cell responses, including elevated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ activation and increased polyfunctional cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-17A). When combined with BCG (BCG + QTAP-R), the vaccine elicited enhanced immune memory, reduced bacterial burden in lungs and spleen, and minimized lung pathology following M. tuberculosis challenge. Subcutaneous QTAP-R (QTAP-SQ) provided partial protection under high-dose challenge, outperforming intranasal delivery. Transcriptomic profiling revealed upregulation of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-12) and chemokines (CCL3, CCL4, CXCL9, CXCL10), indicating enhanced immune recruitment and activation. CD4⁺ T-cell depletion abolished protection, confirming their critical role in QTAP-R–mediated immunity. Overall, QTAP-R demonstrates potent immunogenicity and synergistic efficacy with BCG, positioning it as a promising mRNA-based TB vaccine candidate.

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains one of the most significant infectious diseases globally, posing a substantial burden on public health systems and socioeconomic development1,2. Despite the availability of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine for over a century, TB continues to affect millions of people each year, with an estimated 10 million new cases and 1.4 million deaths reported worldwide in 20191. The challenges in TB control are exacerbated by the emergence of drug-resistant strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and the complex immunopathogenesis of the disease, which involves intricate interactions between the host immune system and the pathogen3–6. The BCG vaccine, although effective in protecting against severe forms of childhood TB, has variable efficacy against pulmonary TB in adults and does not confer lifelong protection3,7. Moreover, the BCG vaccine is contraindicated in immunocompromised individuals, and its widespread use has not led to a substantial reduction in TB incidence in high-burden countries3,7. Therefore, there is an urgent need for the development of new and more effective TB vaccines that can overcome the limitations of the current BCG vaccine and provide durable protection against TB in all age groups and populations, including those with compromised immune systems. The primary findings of this report highlight the development of a highly effective vaccine against tuberculosis, which could be used as a primary vaccine or as a secondary vaccine following BCG primary immunization.

Previously, live attenuated vaccines, including modified BCG constructs, showed promise in controlling tuberculosis in several animal models8–11. Live-attenuated vaccines are advantageous due to their ability to elicit strong and durable immune responses by mimicking natural infections12. Despite their effectiveness, concerns exist regarding their safety profile, particularly the risk of reversion to virulence or uncontrolled replication in immunocompromised hosts13. However, this class of vaccines (live-attenuated) can be problematic when administered to individuals with immune-compromised conditions or those suffering from other infections13,14. Similarly, subunit vaccine candidates developed against M. tuberculosis, such as M72/AS01E and H56:IC31, have demonstrated varying immunogenicity and protection in preclinical and clinical studies15,16. However, despite promising early-phase results, most have failed to confer robust and long-lasting protection in large-scale clinical trials, highlighting the need for novel vaccine strategies15,16. In recent years, mRNA vaccines have emerged as a revolutionary platform for infectious disease vaccination, as demonstrated by the successful development and rapid deployment of mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines17–19. The mRNA vaccines offer several advantages over traditional vaccine platforms, including rapid development, production, scalability, and the potential to induce potent and durable immune responses against a wide range of pathogens18. The ability to encode multiple antigens and incorporate adjuvants into the mRNA vaccine formulation further enhances their immunogenicity and protective efficacy18,20.

Recent studies have further reinforced the potential of mRNA-based vaccines for tuberculosis (TB). De Voss et al. demonstrated that a lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-formulated mRNA vaccine encoding a mix of five M. tuberculosis antigens (PPE15, ESAT6, EspC, EsxI, MetE) induced potent immune responses in murine models and conferred significant protection against TB infection when administered either alone or in combination with a viral vector-based prime-boost approach21. Similarly, Lukeman et al. explored the efficacy of an LNP-mRNA vaccine encoding the CysVac2 fusion protein, which enhanced polyfunctional Th1 CD4⁺ T cell responses, improved BCG-induced immunity, and provided significant bacterial load reduction in the lungs of infected mice22. These findings support the notion that mRNA vaccines can be engineered to elicit strong cellular and humoral responses against TB, thereby complementing or even enhancing the protective efficacy of BCG.

Given these advancements, our study seeks to contribute to this growing field by designing an mRNA vaccine (QTAP-R) encoding key M. tuberculosis antigens Ag85B, Hsp70, and ESAT-6 and assessing its protective efficacy in murine models. These antigens were selected based on their critical roles in M. tuberculosis pathogenesis and their potential as vaccine candidates to induce protective immune responses against TB23–25. The combined antigen formula represents complementary stages of infection with Ag85B for promoting cell wall biosynthesis and early macrophage infection while Hsp70 for its high conservation, facilitating antigen processing and cross-presentation while ESAT-6 for mediating virulence and immune activation26–28. Together, they provide a balanced repertoire of immunodominant epitopes capable of eliciting both Th1 and Th17 responses, outperforming single-antigen formulations in prior preclinical studies23–25. Incorporating these antigens into an mRNA nanoadjuvant vaccine aims to exploit the synergistic effects of antigen presentation and adjuvant activity to enhance the induction of robust cellular and humoral immune responses against M. tuberculosis. This study describes the design, formulation, and preclinical evaluation of an mRNA nanoadjuvant vaccine encoding Ag85B, Hsp70, and ESAT-6 in a murine model. We present compelling evidence demonstrating the immunogenicity of the vaccine and its ability to confer protection against M. tuberculosis Erdman infection in C57BL/6 mice. This protection was superior when the mRNA vaccines were combined with BCG as a priming vaccine. Our findings provide a strong foundation for further developing and optimizing this mRNA vaccine candidate for TB. They also pave the way for its evaluation in larger animal models and clinical trials to assess its long-term efficacy, safety, and potential for human use.

Results

Optimized nanoparticle formulation and efficient mRNA encapsulation

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were successfully formulated to encapsulate the synthesized mRNA and incorporate Quil-A as an adjuvant (QTAP) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P)-dependent size distribution and encapsulation efficiency. The lower N/P ratio (0.25) resulted in a smaller nanoparticle size (91 nm ± 3) (Supplementary Fig. 11B). However, this ratio yielded the least encapsulation efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Moreover, the encapsulation efficiency did not change significantly beyond an N/P ratio of 0.45, as almost all the mRNA is encapsulated at this ratio (~96%). We also observed similar expression of GFP in HEK 293 T cells at both 1- and 3-day post-transfection for all N/P ratios examined (Supplementary Fig. 11B), suggesting no net gain in transfection efficiency with increasing N/P ratio in the 0.45–1.67 range, and encapsulation efficiency of 96% is not different from 100% in terms of protein expression. Hence, we used the N/P ratio of 0.45 throughout this study. The in vitro transfection efficiency of the QTAP was also evaluated in multiple cell lines using the GFP reporter gene. Cells transfected with QTAP exhibited a robust increase in GFP expression compared to those transfected with three different commercial transfection reagents (Supplementary Fig. 11C), highlighting our delivery platform’s superior mRNA delivery and expression capabilities. Additionally, cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTT assay. Consistent with our previous findings29, the QTAP demonstrated significantly lower cytotoxicity compared to commercial transfection reagents, indicating a favorable safety profile and biocompatibility for efficient gene delivery applications (Supplementary Fig. 12A).

Overall, we observed that subtle variance in the N/P ratio affects the size and encapsulation efficiency of the QTAP. However, our data suggest that while a higher N/P ratio generates nanoparticles with higher encapsulation efficiency, this does not translate to higher transfection efficiency in cells, probably due to the increase in nanoparticle size.

QTAP-R vaccine induces robust humoral immune response, resulting in enhanced mycobacterial opsonization in vitro

To generate the QTAP-R vaccine, three individual mRNA constructs were synthesized, each encoding a distinct M. tuberculosis antigen, Ag85B, Hsp70, or ESAT-6, optimized for mouse codon usage and transcribed with modified nucleotides to enhance stability and translation efficiency (Fig. 1A). Each construct was separately transfected into HEK 293 T cells, and protein expression was verified by western blot analysis, which revealed distinct bands at the expected molecular weights for Ag85B (~31 kDa), Hsp70 (~70 kDa), and ESAT-6 (~6 kDa). These findings confirmed that all three mRNAs were properly expressed and suitable for formulation into the lipid nanoparticle (QTAP) system for subsequent immunization studies. Antibody responses to M. tuberculosis antigens are shown to play an essential role in its pathogenesis. C57BL/6 mice were immunized twice at 4-week intervals, either subcutaneously (SQ) or intranasally (IN), with the QTAP nanovaccine to evaluate route-dependent immune responses and BCG to compare the potency of the QTAP nanovaccine to elicit an antibody response. The BCG + QTAP group was first given BCG and boosted with QTAP-R 4 weeks later. Blood samples were collected at regular intervals post-immunization to assess antigen-specific antibody responses by ELISA. We observed a significant increase in PPD and Ag85B-specific IgG responses in serum at four weeks after the prime and booster dose for all groups, except for the intranasal immunization route (Fig. 1B). Following the second vaccine dose, we observed elevated levels of PPD and Ag85 B-specific IgG in serum above the levels detected after the first vaccine dose, suggesting that re-vaccination with either of the mRNA-LNP-TB vaccine increases humoral immune responses. At either time, similar differences were detected when ESAT-6 was used as the antigen, except for the BCG vaccinated group (Fig. 1B). To determine the role of serum factors on the phagocytosis and growth of mycobacteria, we pretreated M. bovis BCG with sera from each group. We incubated serum-pre-treated BCG with activated THP-1 macrophages and examined the percentage of phagocytosis by flow cytometry and the bacterial growth rate by CFU enumeration. Following the first vaccine dose, phagocytosis of serum-treated BCG by activated THP-1 macrophages was significantly increased in all vaccinated groups. This effect was amplified following the second vaccine dose (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 12B). This enhanced phagocytosis of serum-treated M. bovis BCG led to an increased bacterial burden in the macrophages, as reflected in the two-day post-macrophage infection CFU counts (Fig. 1C). Together, these results suggest that vaccination with QTAP-R-induced serum factors enhances the uptake of mycobacteria by macrophages. QTAP-R enhances BCG-induced immunity by amplifying T-cell activation, polyfunctional responses and memory formation

Analysis of immune cell populations and cytokine levels in spleens of vaccinated mice revealed a robust activation of innate and adaptive immune pathways, indicative of a potent vaccine-induced systemic immune responses. Splenocytes isolated from immunized mice 4 weeks after the booster dose were subjected to ex vivo stimulation with M. tuberculosis PPD to evaluate cellular immune responses. Flow cytometric analysis of CD103 and CD69 expression revealed that the combination of BCG and QTAP significantly increased the frequency of CD103⁺ and CD69⁺ T cells compared to BCG alone (Fig. 1D). The highest level of CD103⁺ expression was observed in the BCG + QTAP-R group, particularly in the CD8⁺ T-cell population, suggesting an enhanced capacity for tissue residency (Supplementary Fig. 13A). A similar trend was noted for CD69⁺ expression, indicating a higher activation status of T cells in the presence of QTAP (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that QTAP-R enhances the ability of BCG to induce tissue-resident-like T-cell populations. A distinct T cell phenotypic profile emerged in analyzing splenocytes from vaccinated mice, revealing notable increases in specific T cell subsets.

We also investigated the types of cytokines produced in mice splenocytes after vaccination with QTAP, BCG, and BCG + QTAP-R. We observed polyfunctional T-cell populations in vaccinated mice, present in both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets (Fig. 1E). While this indicates the overall polyfunctional profile at the prime stage, we expanded this analysis after the booster dose, quantifying the same cytokines to demonstrate functional maturation and persistence of the T-cell response (Fig. 2A). A substantial increase in IFN-γ⁺, TNF-α⁺, IL-2⁺, and IL-17A⁺ in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells was observed when QTAP-R was used as a booster to BCG. Notably, we saw a higher IL-17A production in both T cells subsets of all vaccinated mice, suggesting their potential role in Th17-mediated immunity, critical for mucosal defense against tuberculosis (Fig. 2A). The CD8⁺ T-cell compartment showed significant increases in TNF-α⁺ and IL-2⁺ populations in the BCG + QTAP-R group, indicating a robust cytotoxic and helper function enhancement over BCG alone (Fig. 2A). The increased frequencies of cytokine-producing T cells further demonstrated the enhanced immune response in the BCG + QTAP-R group. QTAP-R and BCG vaccination strategies reduce tuberculosis burden and lung pathology

Within each time point, group differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test (familywise α = 0.05). Across the standard immunization and challenge model, all vaccinated groups showed significantly reduced bacterial burden compared with PBS controls in both lung and spleen (Fig. 4B, C). By 8 WPC, lung CFU continued to decline across vaccinated groups, with BCG + QTAP-R again showing the lowest observed bacterial load; however, differences among vaccinated regimens were not statistically significant under the multiple-comparisons framework. To evaluate vaccine efficacy under a more stringent challenge, the infection dose was increased from 50 CFUs to 750 CFUs. In this high-dose challenge model, all groups exhibited a higher bacterial burden compared to the standard challenge (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B). Nevertheless, BCG vaccination significantly reduced the counts of CFU in the lungs and spleens compared to PBS. QTAP-SQ also demonstrated a protective effect, with lower CFU counts than PBS, although not as pronounced as that of BCG. QTAP-IN, while offering some reduction, was the least effective among the vaccinated groups (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B). These findings suggest that while QTAP alone provides partial protection, BCG remains the most effective strategy under high-dose challenge conditions.

Histopathological analysis of the lung tissue further supported these findings (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. 14C). In the standard challenge model (50 CFUs), mice vaccinated with BCG + QTAP-R exhibited the least pulmonary pathology over time. At 4 WPC, extensive inflammatory lesions were observed in the PBS group, as indicated by large granulomatous areas, while QTAP-IN and QTAP-SQ groups showed milder inflammation. BCG alone reduced the extent of lung damage; however, the BCG + QTAP-R group displayed the most preserved lung architecture with minimal inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 4D). At 8- and 16-weeks post-infection, granulomatous lesions persisted in the mice in the PBS group, and in one mouse in the BCG group. In contrast, all mice in the QTAP-IN, QTAP-SQ, and BCG + QTAP-R groups exhibited reduced lung pathology (Fig. 4D).

Under high-dose challenge conditions, histopathological assessment revealed more extensive lung pathology across all groups, consistent with the higher bacterial burden (Supplementary Fig. 14C). While BCG vaccination mitigated lung damage compared to PBS, inflammation and granuloma formation were still evident, suggesting that a higher infection load overwhelms vaccine-induced protection to some extent. QTAP-SQ showed moderate protection, whereas QTAP-IN provided the least histological improvement (Supplementary Fig. 14C). These findings indicate that while QTAP alone offers some benefits, it is less effective than BCG in preventing severe lung pathology under high-dose challenge conditions. The superior protective effect observed with BCG + QTAP-R in the standard challenge model suggests that further evaluation under high-dose conditions is warranted.

QTAP-R and BCG vaccination sustain immune responses and memory T-cell populations following M. tuberculosis challenge

To evaluate whether vaccine-induced immunity was maintained after the M. tuberculosis challenge, cytokine responses, T-cell activation, and memory formation were assessed following infection with the standard challenge dose (50 CFUs).

Analysis of cytokine secretion in serum revealed that IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 levels remained elevated in vaccinated groups after challenge, with the highest levels observed in the BCG + QTAP-R group (Supplementary Fig. 14D). QTAP-SQ and BCG alone induced significantly higher cytokine levels than PBS and QTAP-IN, although their responses were slightly lower than those of the BCG + QTAP-R combination. QTAP-IN failed to cause a significant increase in IFN-γ or TNF-α levels post-challenge, suggesting a weaker ability to sustain immunity (Supplementary Fig. 14D). These results indicate that vaccine-induced immune responses persist following infection, with the most robust cytokine production observed in the BCG + QTAP-R group.

Further examination of T-cell functionality post-challenge revealed that T-cell responses were well maintained in vaccinated groups. The BCG + QTAP-R group exhibited the highest frequencies of IFN-γ⁺, TNF-α⁺, and IL-17A⁺ CD4⁺and CD8⁺ T cells, followed closely by BCG and QTAP-SQ (Fig. 5A). Memory T-cell analysis showed that vaccine-induced memory populations persisted following the challenge. The BCG + QTAP-R group retained the highest proportion of CD44⁺CD62L⁺ central memory and CD44⁺CD62L⁻ effector memory T cells in both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ populations (Fig. 5B). BCG and QTAP-SQ also maintained significant memory T-cell populations, while QTAP-IN induced only a modest expansion of memory subsets (Fig. 5B). The persistence of memory T cells post-challenge suggests that vaccination with QTAP and BCG effectively primes the immune system for long-term protection, with the combination of BCG and QTAP-R providing the most robust recall response.

These findings demonstrate that vaccine-induced immunity remains sustained following infection with M. tuberculosis. The persistence of cytokine secretion, polyfunctional T-cell responses, and memory T-cell populations post-challenge indicates that the protective immune responses elicited by vaccination are durable, with the strongest maintenance observed in the BCG + QTAP-R group.

CD4 T cells are essential for optimal vaccine-mediated protection against tuberculosis

To determine whether the protective immunity conferred by QTAP-R vaccination was dependent on CD4⁺ T cells, we performed depletion studies in vaccinated mice. Although CD4⁺ T cells are well known to be critical for controlling M. tuberculosis infection, this experiment aimed to specifically evaluate whether QTAP-R-induced protection relies on CD4-mediated mechanisms or can be maintained through CD8⁺ or antibody-driven responses in their absence.

The impact of CD4 T-cell depletion on vaccine-induced protection was evaluated by comparing bacterial burden and lung pathology in QTAP-SQ vaccinated mice with and without CD4 T cells at four weeks post-challenge (Fig. 5C). Mice that underwent CD4 depletion (CD4−) exhibited a substantial increase in lung bacterial burden compared to their CD4-sufficient (CD4 + ) counterparts, as evidenced by significantly higher CFU counts (Fig. 5C). Notably, the bacterial load in the lungs of CD4-depleted, QTAP-SQ vaccinated mice was significantly higher than in CD4-sufficient, QTAP-SQ vaccinated, and the PBS control group, suggesting a reliance on CD4 T cells for bacterial control. The histopathological analysis corroborated these findings, showing exacerbated lung pathology in CD4-depleted mice (Fig. 5C). Granulomatous lesions were markedly more extensive and widespread in the QTAP-SQ-vaccinated, CD4-depleted group, compared to the inflammation observed in the PBS group. In contrast, CD4-sufficient, QTAP-SQ vaccinated mice exhibited well-preserved lung architecture with reduced inflammatory infiltrates, further emphasizing the role of CD4 T cells in modulating vaccine-induced protection (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that QTAP-SQ vaccination primarily induces a CD4 T cell response for bacterial containment and limiting pathology.

Discussion

This study addresses the persistent challenge of tuberculosis (TB), a global health threat that remains one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide. Despite the widespread use of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine, its variable efficacy in adults and limited ability to induce long-term protection against pulmonary Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection necessitate the development of improved vaccination strategies24,30. Recent advances in mRNA vaccine technology have demonstrated the potential to induce robust and durable immune responses, prompting the exploration of novel formulations to enhance TB vaccine efficacy18. In this study, we investigated the immunogenic potential of QTAP, an mRNA-lipid nanoparticle vaccine adjuvanted with Quil-A, compared to BCG alone and a BCG + QTAP-R combination to evaluate its capacity to induce protective immunity.

The optimized formulation of QTAP nanoparticles demonstrated efficient mRNA encapsulation and delivery, with an N/P ratio of 0.45, yielding the highest encapsulation efficiency (~96%) while maintaining a favorable nanoparticle size. Despite a further increase in encapsulation efficiency at higher N/P ratios, no significant improvement in transfection efficiency was observed, suggesting that nanoparticle size may influence cellular uptake and expression rather than encapsulation alone. Our findings align with studies demonstrating that lower N/P ratios result in smaller nanoparticles, attributed to the reduced electrostatic interaction between cationic lipids and negatively charged mRNA molecules31–33. Consistently, QTAP outperformed commercial transfection reagents in in vitro transfection studies while exhibiting lower cytotoxicity, reinforcing its potential as a safe and effective gene delivery platform.

The immunogenicity of QTAP was assessed in a murine tuberculosis (TB) model, revealing robust humoral responses. Both subcutaneous and intranasal immunization induced significant increases in antigen-specific IgG responses, with the SQ route being demonstrating better performance. This pattern aligns with previous studies showing that subcutaneous administration of vaccines enhances systemic antibody responses, while mucosal routes tend to favor local immunity34. Notably, ESAT-6-specific IgG responses were lower in BCG-immunized mice, consistent with its lack of ESAT-6 expression35. The elevated antibody responses in QTAP-vaccinated mice correlated with enhanced opsonization of mycobacteria, increasing their uptake by THP-1 macrophages. This phenomenon, leading to increased bacterial burden within macrophages, has been previously described as antibody-mediated enhancement of phagocytosis, a potential mechanism of protection in TB immunity36,37.

Further analysis of cellular immune responses revealed that QTAP augmented BCG-induced T-cell activation, primarily through the increase in CD103⁺ and CD69⁺ T cells. CD103⁺ T cells are associated with tissue residency, particularly in the lungs, which play a critical role in local immune surveillance38. The increased presence of these cells suggests that QTAP enhances the ability of BCG to induce tissue-resident-like T cell populations, which are crucial for long-term protection against respiratory pathogens. Moreover, QTAP enhanced the polyfunctional T-cell response, significantly increasing IFN-γ⁺, TNF-α⁺, IL-2⁺, and IL-17A⁺ T cells, particularly in the BCG + QTAP-R group. Polyfunctional T cells are considered key correlates of protection in TB, as they exhibit superior cytotoxicity and cytokine-mediated antimicrobial activity39. The enhanced IL-17A response is of particular interest, given its role in recruiting neutrophils and maintaining mucosal immunity, both critical for controlling M. tuberculosis infection40,41.

Memory T-cell formation was markedly enhanced across the vaccinated groups, particularly in mice that received QTAP, BCG, or the BCG + QTAP-R combination. The expansion of both central (CD44⁺CD62L⁺) and effector (CD44⁺CD62L⁻) memory populations was significantly higher than the PBS control, indicating a strong potential for long-term immunity. Among these groups, BCG + QTAP-R vaccination led to the most pronounced increase in memory T-cell populations, with CD8⁺ T cells showing a robust effector memory response, which is crucial for cytotoxic activity against infected cells. CD4⁺ central memory T cells were also significantly expanded, suggesting that QTAP enhances the durability of vaccine-induced immunity by promoting long-lived memory subsets that can rapidly respond to secondary infections. A limitation of this study is that flow cytometric analysis of lung cells was not performed but could be attempted in future studies to better evaluate the generated localized immunity by QTAP-R. Another limitation is the exclusion of intramuscular (IM) immunization. While SQ and IM are both parenteral routes that can elicit systemic immunity, differences in muscle versus subcutaneous tissue microenvironments (including antigen expression kinetics, local inflammation, and antigen-presenting cell populations) may influence immunogenicity and reactogenicity. Accordingly, extrapolation of SQ performance to IM should be made cautiously.

Transcriptomic profiling was performed using splenocytes to evaluate systemic immune signatures associated with vaccine-induced protection. We selected the spleen because it provides a robust and reproducible source of RNA for detecting broad cytokine and chemokine modulation following subcutaneous vaccination. Future studies will focus on local pulmonary responses to further dissect the tissue-specific mechanisms of QTAP-R-induced protection. Nonetheless, transcriptional profiling of a focused list of immune-related genes provided evidence of an enhanced and sustained immune response. Notably, genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and IL-12 were significantly upregulated in vaccinated groups, particularly those receiving BCG + QTAP-R. These cytokines are crucial for T-cell differentiation and survival, underscoring that QTAP promotes the long-term maintenance of vaccine-induced immunity. Chemokines such as CCL3, CCL4, CXCL9, and CXCL10 were also elevated, suggesting enhanced recruitment of immune cells to the site of infection, which could contribute to more efficient bacterial clearance and sustained immunological protection. The persistence of these immune mediators post-vaccination highlights the durability of the immune response elicited by QTAP-R, making it a promising candidate for enhancing the protective efficacy of BCG.

Our findings align with recent research on mRNA vaccines for TB, highlighting their ability to elicit robust protective immunity. For instance, De Voss et al. reported that an LNP-mRNA vaccine encoding multiple M. tuberculosis antigens induced strong IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 production, like the polyfunctional T-cell responses observed with QTAP-R 21. However, unlike QTAP-R, which significantly enhanced BCG-induced immunity, their mRNA construct did not show additional efficacy when used as a booster for BCG, suggesting that antigen selection and delivery strategy may influence synergy with existing vaccines. Furthermore, Lukeman et al. demonstrated that an mRNA vaccine encoding the CysVac2 fusion protein improved antigen-specific CD4⁺ T cell responses and reduced bacterial burden comparable to our BCG + QTAP-R combination22. Their study particularly emphasized the importance of early innate immune activation and antigen presentation in driving adaptive immunity. This aligns with our observation that QTAP-R induces early upregulation of IL-1, IL-6, and chemokines in immune cell recruitment.

A key distinction between our study and previous reports is the antigen selection strategy. While De Voss et al. used a multi-antigen mix, and Lukeman et al. focused on a fusion protein, our vaccine incorporated Ag85B, Hsp70, and ESAT-6, three well-characterized immunodominant antigens known for their roles in M. tuberculosis infection virulence and immune evasion. Hsp70-specific responses using protein or overlapping peptide pools was not performed because Hsp70 antigenic reagents were not available at the time of the study; this will be addressed in future work using recombinant Hsp70 and/or synthesized peptide pools. The efficacy of QTAP-R, especially in combination with BCG, suggests that a targeted antigen approach combined with potent nano-adjuvants can enhance protective immunity more effectively than broader antigen cocktails. Moreover, our study provides additional insights into the administration route’s impact on vaccine efficacy. De Voss et al. administered their vaccine intramuscularly21, while Lukeman et al. used both intramuscular and subcutaneous routes22. Our results indicate that subcutaneous QTAP-R delivery offers superior protection compared to intranasal administration, likely due to enhanced systemic immune priming and antigen persistence. This underscores the need for further optimization of delivery routes to maximize mRNA vaccine efficacy for TB.

The protective efficacy of QTAP-R was observed across both the standard and high-dose TB challenge models. In the standard challenge model (50 CFU), vaccination significantly reduced bacterial burden in both lungs and spleen compared with PBS at all post-challenge time points (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test on log10-transformed CFU). Among vaccinated regimens, the BCG + QTAP-R group consistently exhibited the lowest observed bacterial loads across organs and time points, suggesting enhanced durability of control; however, pairwise differences among vaccinated groups did not consistently reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons. Reduced bacterial burden was accompanied by improved pulmonary histopathology, with vaccinated animals demonstrating qualitatively reduced granulomatous inflammation relative to PBS controls. QTAP-R alone also conferred protection relative to PBS, and the numerically lower burdens observed with the BCG + QTAP-R regimen are consistent with a potential additive or synergistic interaction between QTAP-R and BCG, though confirmatory evidence will require adequately powered direct comparisons. In the high-dose challenge model (750 CFUs), QTAP-SQ continued to provide partial protection, though BCG remained the most effective strategy under these conditions. The absence of a BCG + QTAP-R group in this model leaves the question of whether the combination would retain its superior protective effect under severe infection conditions.

To assess the durability of vaccine-induced immunity post-infection, we examined cytokine responses, T-cell activation, and memory formation. Vaccinated mice maintained high levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 post-challenge, with the most potent response observed in the BCG + QTAP-R group. Cytokine production was measured following ex vivo antigen-specific stimulation, with percentages gated on stimulated live lymphocytes rather than total T cells. This gating strategy inherently enriches for antigen-responding cells, and the high cytokine-producing fractions observed within this gate reflect a robust vaccine-induced recall response; resting naive and central memory T cells that do not respond to stimulation fall outside this gate but remain present in the animal. Additionally, strong T-cell responses persisted, suggesting that vaccination not only elicits a strong primary response but also primes the immune system for robust recall responses upon infection. Memory T-cell phenotyping analysis was performed on unstimulated cells to preserve native surface marker expression, as T cell activation causes rapid CD62L shedding which would otherwise artifactually distort memory subset identification. This analysis confirmed that both central and effector memory T cells were maintained post-challenge, and together with the functional cytokine data, demonstrates that BCG + QTAP-R vaccination induces both strong antigen-specific effector responses and a robust central memory compartment in the total T cell pool, findings that are complementary rather than contradictory, indicating the potential for long-term immunity. While these data indicate that vaccination sustains Th1-biased cellular immunity during early post-challenge periods, long-term durability and true recall capacity (e.g., longitudinal immune profiling beyond 4 weeks and/or secondary challenge studies) were not directly assessed here. Notably, however, bacterial burden and lung pathology were quantified at 8 and 16 weeks post-challenge, and these outcomes remained improved in vaccinated groups, supporting maintenance of protective efficacy within the timeframe of this study.

CD4 depletion studies further highlighted the critical role of CD4 T cells in vaccine-induced immunity. Mice vaccinated with QTAP-SQ and depleted of CD4 T cells before the TB challenge exhibited significantly higher bacterial burden than non-depleted QTAP-SQ-vaccinated mice, as evidenced by lung CFU enumeration. Histopathological analysis revealed extensive granulomatous inflammation, like that observed in the unvaccinated PBS group, suggesting a loss of vaccine-induced protection. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating the essential role of CD4 T cells in controlling M. tuberculosis infection and their contribution to vaccine-mediated immunity42. The failure of QTAP-SQ to confer protection in CD4-depleted mice underscores the reliance of this vaccine platform on CD4-dependent mechanisms, which may include cytokine production, B-cell help, and direct cytotoxicity against infected macrophages.

These findings demonstrate that QTAP-R enhances BCG-induced immunity by amplifying T-cell activation, polyfunctional cytokine responses, and memory formation. The vaccine-mediated reduction in bacterial burden and lung pathology underscores its protective potential. However, the reliance on CD4 T cells suggests that future strategies should explore approaches to bolster CD8 T cell-mediated immunity and innate immune components to ensure protection in scenarios where CD4 responses may be compromised. Furthermore, while this study employed a murine model to establish proof-of-concept efficacy, we recognize the importance of validating these findings in more human-like models of TB, such as non-human primates or humanized mouse models, which more closely recapitulate the complexity of human immune responses and disease pathology. Such experiments will be critical for advancing QTAP-R toward clinical translation. These results provide compelling evidence that QTAP-R represents a promising mRNA-LNP-based tuberculosis vaccine strategy capable of augmenting current BCG vaccination efforts.

Methods

Animal studies

All animal experiments were conducted by institutional guidelines and approved protocols from the University of Wisconsin-Madison’s Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (USA) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. For vaccination studies, mice were immunized subcutaneously with QTAP-R (10 µg of mRNA encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles, 100 µl/mouse) or intranasally as indicated. Because of the permissible injection volume for a single site in young mice at the first dose, the intramuscular route was not attempted. Control groups received either the BCG vaccine (Pasteur strain, 1 × 106 CFU per dose) or PBS. All immunization procedures were done under general anesthesia using 4–5% induction by inhalation for less than 5 minutes. For infection experiments, vaccinated and control mice were challenged with 50 or 750 CFU of M. tuberculosis Erdman via the aerosol route using a Glas-Col inhalation exposure system (Glas-Col, USA). Mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose through inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation after observing no detectable heartbeats. Lung and spleen tissues were harvested at defined time points post-challenge to evaluate bacterial burden, immune responses, and histopathological changes. For the CD4 depletion experiment, three groups of female C57BL/6 mice (n = 12/group) aged 6–8 weeks were used in this study, where one group received PBS, and the remaining two groups were immunized through the subcutaneous route with the mRNA LNPs encoding Ag85B, Hsp70, and ESAT-6 antigens. Four weeks after the prime dose, a booster dose was given through the same route, and two doses (1-day intervals) of anti-mouse CD4 monoclonal antibody (InVivo MAb anti-mouse CD4) (Bio x cell, NH, USA) were given to one of the vaccine groups after 4 weeks post-boost. Before CD4 depletion, mice (n = 4) from one of the vaccine groups were euthanized to determine CD4 counts pre-depletion by flow cytometry. One day after receiving both doses of anti-mouse CD4, four mice were euthanized to determine the successful depletion of CD4 T cells in the spleen and lungs by flow cytometry, as described below.

Histopathological examination

Lungs were collected from the various experimental groups and processed in a routine manner. The paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned at 5-micron thickness and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin, and the stained slides were then digitally scanned43.

Bacterial culture

M. tuberculosis Erdman strain (originally from ATCC, no. 35801) was used for the in vivo and in vitro infection experiments. Bacteria were cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (BD Biosciences, USA) supplemented with 10% ADC (albumin-dextrose-catalase), 0.2% glycerol, and 0.05% Tween-80. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with gentle agitation until they reached the logarithmic phase of growth. For infection experiments, bacterial stocks were prepared by centrifuging the culture at 3000 x g for 10 minutes, resuspending the pellet in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-80, and passing the suspension through a 5-μm syringe filter to obtain a single-cell suspension. The concentration of bacteria was determined by plating serial dilutions on Middlebrook 7H10 agar plates and enumerating colony-forming units (CFUs) after 3–4 weeks of incubation at 37 °C.

Gene synthesis and cloning

The sequences encoding M. tuberculosis genes Ag85B (GI:410812180), Hsp70 (GI:1190665804), and ESAT-6 (GI:90811461) were downloaded from GenBank and optimized for mouse codon usage to enhance expression efficiency in C57BL/6 mice. Codon optimization was performed using established algorithms to maximize translational efficiency and minimize potential mRNA instability44. The optimized gene sequences were synthesized and cloned into a plasmid vector containing cytomegalovirus (CMV) and T7 promoters to drive mRNA transcription. Green fluorescent protein45 from jellyfish Aequorea Victoria and Luciferase (Luc) gene from firefly luciferase were also downloaded from GenBank and amplified, followed by cloning onto an expression vector pCMV from our laboratory collection, as described before.

For mRNA synthesis, modified mRNA was synthesized using the HiScribe™ T7 ARCA mRNA Kit (with tailing) (New England BioLabs, E2060S) with Pseudouridine-5’-Triphosphate (TriLink, N-1019). In brief, mRNA was generated through co-transcriptional incorporation of Anti-Reverse Cap Analog (ARCA) (NEB, S1411) facilitated by T7 RNA Polymerase in 10 mM Pseudo-UTP. Following transcription, the reaction underwent DNase I treatment to remove the DNA template, followed by poly(A) tail addition using poly(A) polymerase. The synthesized mRNA was purified using a column-based purification method to remove unincorporated nucleotides, enzymes, and template DNA.

Vaccine formulation and characterization

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were utilized as a delivery system for mRNA encapsulation and co-delivery of adjuvants. As published elsewhere, LNPs were prepared using a proprietary formulation of ionizable cationic lipid DOTAP and Quil-A29. Briefly, the purified mRNA was mixed with LNPs at an optimized ratio and subjected to a brief vortexing and sonication step to facilitate mRNA encapsulation within the lipid bilayer of the nanoparticles. To incorporate the adjuvant, Quil-A (VET-SAP, Desert King), into the LNPs, Quil-A was dissolved in the aqueous phase during LNP formulation. Quil-A is a saponin-based adjuvant that enhances immune responses by promoting antigen uptake and presentation29,46–50. The size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the formulated LNPs were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic mobility measurements, respectively. The encapsulation efficiency of mRNA within the LNPs was assessed by a fluorometric assay using a fluorescent dye that binds specifically to RNA. Finally, to examine antigen expression from plasmid constructs, we employed in vitro transfection of mRNA LNPs in HEK 293 T, BHK, and THP-1 cell lines using either luciferase or GFP reporter genes, as reporter genes. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and transfected with mRNA LNPs at varying concentrations. After 24 hours, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using a luminometer to assess mRNA delivery and expression efficiency. Fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry were used to generate qualitative and quantitative data.

MTT cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of the test compounds was evaluated using the MTT assay as described previously with minor modifications. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/well in 100 μL of complete growth medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After 24 hours, cells were treated with varying concentrations of the nanoadjuvant vaccine in triplicate and then incubated for an additional 12 hours. Control wells received an equivalent volume of vehicle DMSO.

Following the incubation period, 10 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for an additional 3–4 hours at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by carefully aspirating the medium, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader with a reference wavelength of 630 nm to correct for background.

ELISA for antibody responses

Blood samples obtained before (week 0) and after vaccination (4 and 8 weeks), and post-challenge (4 weeks) were subjected to enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to evaluate antigen-specific antibody responses and serum cytokine levels. IgG targeting purified protein derivative (PPD), ESAT6, and Ag85B were precisely quantified in serum samples.

Phagocytosis assay

To examine the effect of vaccination on antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, 1 × 106 THP-1 monocytes (ATCC TIB-202) were seeded per well in 6-well plates and stimulated to differentiate into phagocytic macrophage-like cells using 20 nM phorbol 12-myristate acetate (PMA) (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) for 48 hours. Before infection, BCG-GFP was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in 2 ml of RPMI 1640 medium with serum from mice in the experimental groups. For in vitro infection, adherent cells were exposed to BCG-GFP at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5:1 (CFU: cell) and incubated for 4 hours. After infection, macrophages were washed three times with cold, sterile PBS to eliminate extracellular BCG-GFP and then detached using Accutase cell detachment solution (BD Biosciences, UK) for 15 minutes before recovery with sterile cell scrapers. Before fixation, cells were stained with a Live-or-Dead fixable viability stain (V450 fluorochrome, Biotium) and washed with PBS. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes. Quantification of BCG-GFP+ total live cells was performed using flow cytometry. Samples were analyzed using an LSR II flow cytometer, and data analysis was conducted using FlowJo (TreeStar Inc, Ashland, USA). Each sample was run in duplicate, and the results presented represent the average of three independent experiments.

Flow cytometry

Lungs and spleens from vaccinated mice were processed for flow cytometric analysis. Tissues were finely minced and placed in a gentleMACS dissociator M Tube (Miltenyi 130-093-236, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) with 3 mL of collagenase B (1 mg/mL) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The tube was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by passing the digested tissues through a 70-µm cell strainer (Falcon) and lysing red blood cells (RBCs) with BD Pharm Lyse™ (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). For intracellular cytokine staining, 106 cells were stimulated with M. tuberculosis purified protein derivative (PPD) (1 mg/ml) and IL-2 (400 U/ml), while the same concentration of IL-2 was added to unstimulated control samples. Following a 5-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, Brefeldin A (1 µL/mL, GolgiPlug, BD Biosciences) was added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 6 hours under the same conditions.

Fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for cell-surface markers CD4 (BUV 496, GK1.5), CD8a (BUV395, 53-6.7), CD44 (BV510, IM7), CD3e (BV786, 145-2C11), CD103 (FITC, 2E7), CD62L (PE-CF594), CD69 (PE-Cy7, H1.2F3), and intracellular cytokines IFN-γ (APC-Cy7, XMG1.2), TNF-α (BV421, MP6-XT22), IL-2 (PE-CF594, JES6-5H4), and IL-17 (FITC, TC11-18H10.1) were obtained from BD Biosciences, Biolegend, eBioscience, or Invitrogen. Cell viability was assessed using Dye eFluor 780 (eBiosciences). After staining for surface markers, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences) and subsequently stained for intracellular cytokines. Flow cytometric data were acquired using an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar, Woodburn, OR, USA). Results were expressed as the percentage difference between stimulated and unstimulated cells. A minimum of 100,000 events was collected per sample, and a Boolean gating strategy was applied to identify cytokine-secreting T cells.

Statistics

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For most datasets, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used to assess differences among groups. When two groups were compared directly, data were analyzed assuming a normal (Gaussian) distribution using an unpaired two-tailed Welch’s t test. Prior to analysis, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Each dot in the graphs represents an individual mouse, and bar heights denote the group means.

For immunogenicity assays (ELISA, ELISPOT, flow cytometry), sample sizes of n = 4 mice per group were used, with experiments repeated independently at least twice to ensure reproducibility. For bacterial burden (CFU) analyses following M. tuberculosis challenge (excluding CD4-depletion study where n = 4), n = 8 mice per group per time point were analyzed. Outliers beyond two standard deviations of the group mean were excluded only if justified by technical error (e.g., sample processing loss).

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with the following notations: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and *p < 0.0001 (****). All statistical tests used are indicated in the corresponding Figure legends. The study design was not randomized but followed consistent group allocation based on vaccination type and route, and all analyses were conducted in a blinded manner with respect to treatment group identity during CFU counting and histological scoring.
