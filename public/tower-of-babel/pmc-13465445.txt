Chromosome-level genome assembly of Trichiurus japonicus resolves species boundaries and evolutionary history of cutlassfishes in Northwest Pacific
Authors: Vanthu Giap, Tianqin Wu, Liang Lu, Jiantao Hu, Mingwei Zhou, Chenhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persistent taxonomic uncertainty has plagued the Trichiuridae family (hairtails), a group of ecologically and commercially significant fishes, a result of conflicting morphological criteria and historical misclassifications. While molecular approaches have advanced understanding their taxonomy, mitochondrial-based analyses and misapplied nomenclature have obscured species boundaries and population dynamics. Here, we integrate a chromosome-level genome assembly of Trichiurus japonicus with whole-genome resequencing of congeneric species to resolve the taxonomic ambiguity in Trichiurus. Combining phylogenomic, demographic, and population genetic analyses, we resolve taxonomic uncertainties by redefining species boundaries, reconstructing population dynamics, and identifying genomic divergence between populations. Our analyses suggest that T. japonicus and T. nanhaiensis are distinct species. Crucially, we identify a cryptic, genetically distinct lineage putatively derived from the Indian Ocean, misclassified as T. lepturus in previous studies, underscoring the impact of commercial trade on taxonomic confusion. Our analyses reveal low spatial genetic differentiation in T. japonicus across the sampled Northwest Pacific localities, consistent with weak population structure within our sampling frame. This study establishes a genomic framework of Trichiurus to resolve taxonomic conflicts in morphologically cryptic taxa and advances strategies for sustainable fisheries management under climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13009-x.

Significance statement

This study employs genomic approaches to resolve taxonomic uncertainties in the Trichiurus genus, providing genome-wide evidence of T. japonicus and T. nanhaiensis as distinct species while identifying a cryptic, genetically distinct lineage putatively derived from the Indian Ocean (T. sp.). Our analyses indicate that T. japonicus maintained stable population dynamics throughout Pleistocene glaciations. These results may advance management strategies for commercially important cutlassfish species and establish a crucial genomic framework for evaluating marine organisms’ vulnerability to contemporary climate-driven environmental changes.

Introduction

The Trichiuridae family (cutlassfishes or hairtails) comprises ecologically and economically significant fish [1, 2]. Their role as mid-to-upper trophic level predators makes them vital for maintaining the structure and function of marine ecosystems. Fisheries in the Northwest Pacific rely heavily on species of the genus Trichiurus, including T. japonicus, T. lepturus, T. nanhaiensis, and T. brevis [3, 4], although pronounced morphological conservatism among these taxa has long complicated accurate identification [5–7]. This taxonomic ambiguity directly undermines stock assessments and sustainable management, as effective conservation strategies relies on the accurate delineation of species and their population units.

Initial taxonomic work regarded cutlassfishes as a single cosmopolitan species [8, 9], but subsequent morphological revisions recognized multiple East Asian taxa [10, 11]. The advent of molecular markers, particularly mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), revealed deep evolutionary divergences within the genus, challenging the traditional view and supporting the existence of cryptic species [8, 12, 13]. While mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is useful for initial screening and barcoding, its maternal, non-recombining, single-locus nature makes it susceptible to stochastic processes such as genetic drift and incomplete lineage sorting. These can produce evolutionary signals that conflict with genome-wide divergence patterns and true species relationships [14, 15]. Historical nomenclatural acts established the foundational framework for current taxonomy of Trichiurus [16–20], yet the relationships and validity of many nominal species remained contested [21].

Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps persist due to methodological limitations. First, earlier investigations were often constrained by geographically restricted sampling and a primary reliance on mitochondrial markers, which has frequently resulted in cytonuclear discordance and ambiguous species boundaries [22–24] .This discordance arises when genealogies from nuclear and mitochondrial genomes conflict, a common issue in recently diverged species or those with complex demographic histories, obscuring true evolutionary relationships [22, 25]. Second, several described taxa retain uncertain morphological diagnoses [3, 8, 26], and key lineages, including T. brevis [16, 26], T. sp.2 [27–30], and an unresolved Indian Ocean lineage [21, 31], lack clear phylogenetic placement and genomic characterization. Third, while early isozyme studies suggested fine-scale population structure for T. japonicus [4, 32], subsequent genetic data with limited resolution supported only two broad groups in the East and South China Seas [11, 33], leaving its contemporary population connectivity and historical demography poorly understood.

In this study, we have three objectives: firstly, using genome-wide nuclear data to resolve long-standing species boundaries in the Trichiurus genus, which have been obscured by morphological conservatism and mitonuclear discordance in previous studies; secondly, test whether Pleistocene sea-level fluctuations in the Northwest Pacific are a key driver of allopatric speciation in Trichiurus; thirdly, evaluate spatial genetic structure of T. japonicus exhibits across its Northwest Pacific range, if it is consistent with a panmictic population hypothesis. To address these interconnected limitations in taxonomy, species delimitation, and population genomics, we established a robust phylogenomic framework for Northwest Pacific Trichiurus. By integrating extensive specimen collections across the region’s major marine basins with a chromosome-level genome assembly and whole-genome resequencing, we apply genome-wide SNP analysis and coalescent-based modeling. The specific goal of this study is to clarify the phylogenetic status of ambiguous lineages; delineate the population structure, connectivity, and demographic history of the commercially dominant T. japonicus; and thereby create an empirical genomic foundation for informed regional fisheries management and conservation.

Result

Genome assembly and annotation

We present a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for T. japonicus (Fig. 1C). This 964.99 Mbp assembly, anchored to 24 chromosomes (98.87% anchoring rate), was generated by integrating 112 GB of Oxford Nanopore long reads, 66.37 GB of Illumina short reads, and 104.11 GB of Hi-C scaffolding data, resulting in scaffold and contig N50 values of 39.19 Mbp and 6.07 Mbp respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The genome exhibits a GC content of 41.21% and achieved a high BUSCO completeness score of 96.9%.

Genome composition and repetitive elements. Repetitive elements constituted a substantial portion (40.48%) of the T. japonicus genome. DNA transposons were the most abundant class (10.95%), followed by retroelements (10.50%), which comprised primarily long terminal repeats (LTRs; 6.10%) and long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs; 3.89%). Unclassified repeats accounted for an additional 14.08% of the genome. Repetitive elements dominated the T. japonicus genome, accounting for nearly half of its composition (40.48%). DNA transposons (10.95%) and retroelements (10.50%) were the two major repeat classes, with unclassified repeats comprising the remainder (14.08%). Integrating transcriptomic, protein homology, and ab initio evidence, we annotated 23,993 high-confidence protein-coding genes. This gene set provides a robust foundation for comparative genomic analyses.

Compared with the published HiFi-based T. japonicus genome of Hsu et al. (2025) [34], our Nanopore-based assembly differs in assembly size, gene count, and BUSCO completeness, but both studies consistently recover 24 chromosomes, indicating consistency in chromosome number; furthermore. Synteny mapping between the assemblies also suggest structural concordance (Supplementary Fig. 5), despite differences in total size (964.99 Mb vs. 1,138 Mb), gene count (23,993 vs. 26,541), and BUSCO completeness (96.9% vs. 99.4%). Beyond genome description, our study further links the reference assembly to population genomics, phylogenomics, and demographic inference to address species boundaries and evolutionary history within Trichiurus, whereas Hsu et al. primarily presented a genomic resource. Our comparative analyses also reveal strong chromosomal synteny conservation with related perciforms (Scomber japonicus, Thunnus albacares), as evidenced by large collinear blocks spanning entire chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Furthermore, spatial mapping uncovers significant correlations between syntenic regions, GC content gradients, gene density, and transposable element distribution (Supplementary Fig. 1B), highlighting the interplay between genome architecture and sequence composition.

Population genomics and phylogenomics

Northwest Pacific Trichiurus taxa revealed clear genetic divergence in PCA (Fig. 2A, left). Trichiurus japonicus formed a tight cluster, indicating low genomic diversity. Trichiurus lepturus separated distinctly from all other species. While closely grouped, T. sp. and T. nanhaiensis separate from both T. japonicus and T. lepturus. Trichiurus nanhaiensis and Trichiurus sp. formed two distinct genetic clusters, which is more apparent in the two-species PCA (Fig. 2A, right). Applying a MAF threshold of 0.05 further accentuated this genetic separation. Because MAF thresholds can affect population-structure inference, this pattern should be interpreted together with the unfiltered PCA, FST, and phylogenomic analyses.

Genetic differentiation among the four Trichiurus species was medium to high (FST = 0.1728–0.3800; Fig. 2B), Divergence was greatest between T. lepturus and T. sp., and lowest between T. lepturus and T. japonicus. In contrast, northern and southern T. japonicus populations showed negligible differentiation (FST = 0.0188), consistent with a single cohesive coastal population within our 2022–2023 multi-season sampling frame. Genome-wide Dxy, FST, and π patterns supported divergence among Trichiurus species and low differentiation within the sampled T. japonicus populations (Supplementary Fig. 2). The apparent difference between FST and PCA/ADMIXTURE patterns likely reflects the use of different SNP-filtering schemes and the fact that PCA and ADMIXTURE emphasize clustering patterns, whereas FST measures allele-frequency differentiation. Therefore, these analyses were interpreted as complementary but method-dependent lines of evidence.

ADMIXTURE analysis revealed three major genetic clusters at K = 3, corresponding to T. lepturus, T. japonicus, and a combined T. nanhaiensis –Trichiurus sp. group (Fig. 2C). These clusters were broadly consistent with the major species-level groupings inferred from PCA and phylogenomic analyses. At higher K values (K = 5 to K = 7), T. japonicus individuals formed distinct subclusters, but these showed no correlation with geographic sampling locations.

Intraspecific genomic variation within T. japonicus was low compared with interspecific differentiation. The weighted FST between northern and southern populations was only 0.01181, suggesting that T. japonicus individuals across the sampled range share a relatively uniform genetic background and are consistent with a single, weakly differentiated population within our sampling frame. After applying a MAF threshold of 0.05, ADMIXTURE analysis showed clearer separation between T. nanhaiensis and Trichiurus sp. This threshold-dependent pattern should be interpreted cautiously and in combination with other analyses.

Two individuals showed possible evidence of genetic admixture: T. japonicus h11 and Trichiurus sp. 661 (Dandong Fig. 1A). Because these individuals differ in sequencing depth, these signals should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation with additional high-coverage samples. These findings may reflect localized gene flow, historical hybridization or coverage related uncertainty. Most T. japonicus across its range showed a relatively uniform genetic background, consistent with weak population structure in the present dataset. Trichiurus brevis showed as a distinct cluster, although this result may be influenced by the small sample size (n = 1).

Overall, the ADMIXTURE results support three principal genetic patterns: distinction between T. lepturus, T. japonicus, and the T. nanhaiensis–T. sp. group; a cohesive T. japonicus population along the Chinese coast; and localized admixture suggesting past or limited contemporary gene flow at species boundaries.

Phylogenomics

For clarity, we present the nuclear phylogeny first (Fig. 3A) and the mitochondrial phylogeny second (Fig. 3B), allowing direct comparison between genome-wide and mitochondrial signals. Phylogenomic analysis resolved the evolutionary relationships within Trichiurus but revealed cytonuclear discordance (Fig. 3). The nuclear phylogeny (Fig. 3A) supported a basal lineage of South China Sea T. lepturus and resolved T. japonicus, T. nanhaiensis, and T. sp. as distinct genomic lineages, with T. japonicus sister to a clade containing the latter two. The mitochondrial gene tree (Fig. 3B) recovered three well-supported monophyletic clades of T. lepturus (West African, American, Vietnamese). Nuclear sequences for the West African and American clades were unavailable, so their nuclear differentiation remains unassessed. All three lineages are still called T. lepturus because Linnaeus originally applied this name to three distinct hairtail genera in 1758. Other related taxa have since been resolved, leaving only these three monophyletic lineages as T. lepturus in the strict sense. The two trees are largely congruent at the species level (e.g., T. japonicus forms a consistent clade in both analyses). The difference we refer to is primarily within T. lepturus, where the mitochondrial tree shows a more admixed and geographically inconsistent structure compared to the more resolved nuclear phylogeny. These discrepancies are reflected in local topology and node support rather than in major species-level relationships. Given that the mitochondrial inference is based on a single locus (COX1), this pattern may result from incomplete lineage sorting or introgression. Therefore, we interpret the mitochondrial signal cautiously as provisional evidence of lineage complexity rather than definitive mito-nuclear discordance.

The analysis also clarified the identity of market specimens. Specimens labeled Trichiurus sp. from Dandong (China) and Busan (South Korea) clustered with an unnamed Indian Ocean lineage, matching the GenBank sequence MK333401 identified as T. lepturus [35]. Furthermore, COX1 data supported the interpretation that specimens previously called Trichiurus sp. 2 correspond to T. nanhaiensis.

Gene flow and divergence history

D-statistic analyses detected significant asymmetric allele sharing between T. nanhaiensis and Trichiurus sp., whereas comparable signals involving T. japonicus and other congeners were weak or not significant (Fig. 4A). Divergence timing estimates revealed that most species pairs separated approximately 383,000–398,000 generations ago. In contrast, T. nanhaiensis and Trichiurus sp. diverged much more recently, at only 4,903 generations ago. Using a generation time of 2.9 years for Trichiurus, this recent divergence is consistent with incomplete lineage separation between the two lineages into the Holocene.

Our analysis identified a major historical population bottleneck in the genus Trichiurus occurring approximately 474,010 generations ago, equivalent to ~ 1.12 million years ago using a generation time of 2.9 years (Fig. 4B). This bottleneck coincided with Pleistocene glacial-interglacial cycles and associated sea-level fluctuations. Subsequent major divergence events followed this bottleneck. The separation of T. japonicus from its relatives (~ 0.39–0.80 Mya) coincided with the closure of the Taiwan Strait during glacial periods, indicating geographic isolation as the driver of speciation.

Discussion

This study helps resolve long-standing taxonomic ambiguity within the genus Trichiurus in the Northwest Pacific by employing a phylogenomic approach. Our integration of chromosome-level genome assembly with genome-wide SNP data reduces the limitations of previous studies that relied solely on morphology or mitochondrial DNA, which can be inadequate for delineating closely related marine species due to phenotypic plasticity and incomplete lineage sorting. We delineate four distinct evolutionary lineages: T. japonicus, T. nanhaiensis, T. lepturus, and a newly identified cryptic lineage (T. sp.), thereby providing a robust genomic framework for the genus. Our research presents the first chromosome-level genome assembly for T. japonicus, an ecologically and commercially vital Northwest Pacific hairtail. Our high-quality assembly anchors to 24 chromosomes with good completeness (BUSCO: 96.9%), providing a great genomic resource for the understudied Trichiuridae family. Our assembly (GCA_046254865.1) was submitted to NCBI in December 2024 and released on bioRxiv shortly thereafter, predating the recently reported T. japonicus genome (Sci. Data, 2025, 12:1860). Prior to this work, no high-quality chromosome-level reference or genome-wide SNP data existed for Trichiurus, precluding robust analysis of nuclear divergence, population structure, and demographic history.

Compared to the HiFi-based assembly by Hsu et al. (2025) [34] (1,138 Mb; 26,541 genes; 99.4% BUSCO), our Nanopore-based assembly is more compact (931.81 Mb; 23,993 genes; 96.9% BUSCO), reflecting differences in sequencing platform, assembly algorithms, and haplotype resolution. Whole-genome synteny analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5) revealed high structural concordance across all chromosomes, confirming the overall karyotype stability of T. japonicus. Importantly, both assemblies converge on 24 chromosomes, confirming karyotype stability and supporting the chromosome level organization of our assembly. While Hsu et al. focused on genome description, our study extends to comparative genomics, gene family evolution, and adaptive signatures, filling a critical interpretive gap.

Our divergence estimates of Trichiurus species (383,689–398,073 generations ago, corresponding to ~ 1.11–1.15 million years ago with a generation time of 2.9 years) align with the timeline of Pleistocene glacial lowstands, when the emergence of the Taiwan Strait Land Bridge acted as a vicariant barrier. This geographic isolation would have separated ancestral populations between the East China Sea and the South China Sea/Indo-Pacific, initiating genetic divergence, a plausible scenario that is consistent with the timing of known glacial-interglacial cycles and the observed species divergence pattern. Our genomic data support the recognition of T. japonicus as a distinct species, consistent with Temminck & Schlegel (1844) [36] and rejecting the long-misapplied synonym with T. haumela Forsskål, (1775) [37]. This controversy arises because the name T. haumela has been historically misapplied as a catch-all label for T. japonicus in regional collections, creating nomenclatural inconsistency where a single biological entity is referenced by multiple names [3, 38]. Thus, we clarify that T. haumela is not a true junior synonym but a distinct name erroneously assigned to T. japonicus lineages due to phenotypic similarity [27]. The persistence of this taxonomic error stems from the pronounced morphological conservatism within Trichiurus, which confounds traditional identification [4, 21]., This initial ambiguity was then cemented through the uncritical repetition of misidentifications in the literature [39, 40] and practical challenges in accessing verifiable type specimens for comparison [41]. Our genome-wide clustering and species delimitation analyses argue against conspecificity under the name haumela, and provide evidence to help resolve this long-standing confusion. Consequently, this work underscores the utility of genome-scale data in clarifying species boundaries.

Furthermore, we uncovered a significant case of market mislabeling with direct implications for trade and conservation [42, 43]. Specimens sold as locally caught were genetically identified as a novel lineage (T. sp.) that clusters with Indian Ocean populations, matching the GenBank sequence MK333401 [35] (Fig. 3, B). This suggests that these specimens may represent imported products, rather than a resident Yellow Sea population. This finding highlights the critical need for forensic seafood science, demonstrating how genetic tools can be leveraged for market surveillance to combat fraud, ensure accurate labeling, and ultimately support sustainable fisheries management [44, 45].

Finally, we document two concurrent speciation processes (Fig. 4A). The first involves T. nanhaiensis and T. sp., where high gene flow across weak ecological gradients maintains incomplete divergence, trapping them in an early stage of speciation [46]. In contrast, T. japonicus and T. nanhaiensis exhibit a classic pattern of allopatric speciation: Pleistocene isolation in separate glacial refugia established strong reproductive barriers [47], and although subsequent secondary contact permitted limited hybridization [48, 49], species integrity remains intact, a pattern observed in numerous other taxa [50–52]. This evolutionary dynamics mirrors patterns seen in terrestrial taxa (e.g., human [53] or brown bears [54]) and demonstrates how cyclical climate-driven isolation and reconnection have shaped marine biodiversity.

The main limitations of this study are as follows: first, sequencing depth was uneven among resequenced individuals, which may influence genotype accuracy and downstream PCA or ADMIXTURE patterns, particularly for individuals showing possible admixture. Second, the use of different filtering thresholds, especially MAF thresholds, can affect population-structure inference; therefore, ADMIXTURE patterns based on MAF = 0.05 should be interpreted together with PCA, FST, D-statistics, and phylogenomic results. Third, the single sample of T. brevis (n = 1) precludes a definitive assessment of its taxonomic status. While this individual formed a distinct cluster at high K-values in our structure analysis, this result requires validation with additional specimens to rule out potential artifacts or exceptional individual variation. Fourth, sampling gaps persist in the northern distribution range of T. lepturus, the southern South China Sea, and the broader Indian Ocean, which limits our ability to fully assess the global phylogeographic pattern of the T. lepturus complex. Fifth, our demographic inference is based on a single high-coverage individual per species and a fixed mutation rate and generation time, which introduces uncertainty in the absolute timing of demographic events; however, bootstrap validation supported the relative population size trajectory and bottleneck signal. Sixth, our sampling was conducted within a limited seasonal window, which does not capture potential temporal population dynamics of T. japonicus. Nevertheless, the four major evolutionary lineages (T. japonicus, T. nanhaiensis, T. lepturus, and the cryptic T. sp. lineage), are supported by genome-wide SNP data and show general congruence across multiple analytical frameworks (e.g., FST, PCA, ADMIXTURE, and phylogenomic analysis) [55].

Beyond resolving taxonomy, this study demonstrates the decisive advantage of nuclear genomic data for understanding marine diversification. Genome-wide SNPs provide a biparentally inherited, multilocus signal [56], that can help disentangle contemporary gene flow from ancestral polymorphism [57], enabling accurate reconstructions of evolutionary history and revealing cryptic diversity [58–60].

Although the nuclear and mitochondrial phylogenies are largely congruent at the species level (Fig. 3), we observe localized discordance within T. lepturus, where the COX1-based tree exhibits a more admixed (Fig. 3B) and geographically inconsistent structure compared to the well-resolved nuclear phylogeny (Fig. 3A). Importantly, this discrepancy is restricted to shallow nodes and is not associated with conflicts in major species-level relationships. Such patterns are expected under incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) [61], particularly in lineages that have undergone recent divergence and retain large effective population sizes [14, 62, 63], as is likely for the widely distributed T. lepturus. In addition, the possibility of historical or ongoing introgression cannot be excluded, given the overlapping distributions and ecological continuity across the Northwest Pacific.

We emphasize that the mitochondrial inference is based on a single locus and is therefore inherently more susceptible to stochastic lineage sorting, selective sweeps, and introgression, whereas the nuclear phylogeny integrates signals from 4,434 independent loci and thus provides a more robust estimate of the species tree. The consistency of species-level relationships across both datasets further supports the reliability of the nuclear framework.

Taken together, we interpret the mitochondrial pattern not as definitive evidence of mito-nuclear discordance, but as an expected outcome of lineage complexity in a recently diversified and potentially reticulated system. Resolving this complexity will require broader population-level sampling and the application of coalescent-based approaches that explicitly model gene tree heterogeneity. Until such analyses are conducted, the mitochondrial signal should be viewed as provisional and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Our phylogenomic, population structure, and demographic inference results converge to support a coherent evolutionary history of Trichiurus in the Northwest Pacific. The nuclear phylogeny resolved four distinct monophyletic lineages, which are further supported by clear species-level separation in PCA and FST analyses. MSMC-IM results revealed that the major interspecific divergence events occurred immediately after the genus-wide population bottleneck at ~ 474,010 generations ago, consistent with allopatric speciation driven by glacial vicariance. The only exception is the very recent divergence between T. nanhaiensis and T. sp., which is accompanied by persistent gene flow, as evidenced by D-statistic and MSMC-IM migration rate results. The apparent mito-nuclear incongruence within the T. lepturus complex may reflect mitochondrial introgression, incomplete lineage sorting, or limited resolution of the current nuclear sampling, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously until denser geographic and genomic sampling becomes available.

Our findings have direct, actionable implications for fisheries science and management. The low genetic differentiation observed in T. japonicus across the sampled localities suggests that this taxon may be managed as a weakly differentiated regional fishery stock, pending broader temporal and geographic validation. Conversely, the recognition of T. nanhaiensis and T. sp. as distinct evolutionary and likely ecological units necessitates the development of species-specific assessment frameworks and trade regulations to prevent overexploitation and mislabeling, thereby aligning conservation policy with biological reality.

In summary, this integrated phylogenomic study establishes a resolved framework for Trichiurus in the Northwest Pacific and provides a concrete model of how Pleistocene sea-level fluctuations catalyzed marine speciation. Looking forward, we propose three priorities: (1) expanded sampling of T. brevis and under-represented regions to refine species boundaries and distributions; (2) the integration of high-throughput morphometric or otolith geochemical analyses with genomic data to develop rapid, diagnostic tools for fisheries monitoring; and (3) a broader biogeographic study of the T. lepturus complex across the Indo-Pacific to elucidate global diversification drivers. Collectively, our work underscores the power of phylogenomics to unveil hidden marine biodiversity and provides the essential scientific foundation for evidence-based management of these ecologically and economically vital fisheries.

Conclusion

This study presents a chromosome-level genome assembly for T. japonicus. Analysis of population resequencing data from China and Vietnam reveals low genetic differentiation across the Chinese coast, suggesting a weakly differentiated stock within the present sampling frame. In contrast, genetic divergence supports T. japonicus, T. lepturus, T. nanhaiensis, and a putative Indian Ocean lineage (T. sp.) as distinct species. Multiple analytical approaches suggest a recent divergence (~ 10,000 years ago) between T. sp. and T. nanhaiensis, accompanied by substantial ongoing gene flow. These findings help clarify species boundaries within Trichiurus and provide a scientific basis for improved fisheries management.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection and sampling

A single specimen of T. japonicus was captured by fishing rod-at Gouqi Island, China (30°N, 122°E) on 23 October 2023 (Fig. 1A). Following capture, tissues from ten distinct organs were dissected and preserved for downstream genomic analyses [64]. The liver was designated for long-read Nanopore sequencing, and skeletal muscle was reserved for Hi-C library construction [65]. Additional samples from the gill, heart, kidney, spleen, brain, gas bladder, skin, and blood were collected for subsequent transcriptomic profiling [64]. For population-level morphological and molecular analyses, an additional 439 ethanol-preserved specimens from the genus Trichiurus were examined [3]. These specimens originated from coastal waters of Vietnam (n = 151), China (n = 223), and South Korea (n = 65).

DNA extraction and genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from tissue samples using a standard cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol [66], followed by purification with a commercial QIAGEN kit [67]. Long-read data were generated using Oxford Nanopore sequencing technology to aid in genome assembly [68]. To support assembly polishing and provide deep coverage, short-read data were also generated from the same DNA sample using the Illumina HiSeq platform [69].

Mitochondrial DNA amplification and species identification

To confirm species identity and assess phylogenetic relationships, the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COXI) gene segment of the mitochondrial genome was amplified and Sanger sequenced from 81 representative specimens, using universal primers (Forward: 5′-CTACAACCCACCGCTTACTC-3′; Reverse: 5′-GTTGTAATAAAGTTAATGGCGC-3′).This analysis confirmed the presence of T. lepturus, T. nanhaiensis, T. japonicus (from Zhoushan, Xiamen, Qingdao, and Hainan, China), and an undescribed T. sp. within the collection from Vietnam, China, and Korea. For a broader phylogenetic context, all publicly available COX1 sequences for the genus Trichiurus were retrieved from the NCBI database. This comparative dataset included sequences annotated as T. lepturus (e.g., KM538620, MT455315), putative “T. lepturus” or T. sp. (e.g., LC085645, MZ272011), and Trichiurus sp2 (e.g., FM998058, LC269196).

Whole-genome resequencing and sample composition

To investigate genomic variation across the genus, shallow whole-genome resequencing was performed on 56 individuals. Genomic DNA libraries were prepared according to an inline-indexing protocol following ultrasonic shearing to a fragment size range of 250–1000 base pairs [70]. All libraries were sequenced as 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina HiSeq2500 platform, achieving an average genomic coverage of approximately 8× per individual [71]. Per-sample read numbers and mapping statistics are provided in Supplementary Tables 3 to allow independent verification of coverage estimates. The resequenced panel included one individual of Tentoriceps cristatus from Hainan, China, and eight individuals of Lepturacanthus savala from Xiamen, China as outgroups. The remaining 47 individuals comprised multiple Trichiurus species: T. lepturus (n = 11 from Vietnam), T. sp. (n = 6 from South Korea and China), T. nanhaiensis (n = 9 from Vietnam and China), T. brevis (n = 1 from China), and T. japonicus (n = 20 from China and South Korea).

Genome assembly and scaffolding

The genome size was estimated using GenomeScope 2.0 by analyzing 21-mer frequency profiles generated with Jellyfish (v2.3.0) [71]. A de novo assembly was constructed from Nanopore long-read data using NextDenovo with non-default parameters: read_cutoff = 1k, seed_cutoff = 25k. The same individual used for Nanopore long-read sequencing also provided the DNA for Illumina short-read polishing and Hi-C library construction. The initial assembly was subsequently polished in a two-stage process; the first polishing step was performed using Illumina short-read data with NextPolish (v1.4.1) with non-default parameters: sgs_options = -max_depth 100, to correct sequencing errors.

Hi-C-based chromosome construction

To order and orient contigs into chromosome-scale scaffolds, Hi-C scaffolding was performed [72]. The Hi-C sequencing data were processed and aligned to the polished assembly using HiC-Pro (v3.1.0) [73] and Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) [74]. Chromosomal-level scaffolding was then executed using Juicer (v1.6.2) and 3D-DNA (v180922) [74]. The resulting scaffolds were visually inspected and manually curated in Juicebox (v1.11.08) [75].

Assembly quality

The completeness of the assembled genome was assessed using BUSCO (v5.4.3) [76] against the actinopterygii_odb10 database [77].

Repeat annotation and structural gene prediction

Repetitive elements within the genome were identified and characterized using a combination of RepeatModeler v2.0.3 [78] for de novo repeat library construction and RepeatMasker v4.1.2-pl [79] for genome-wide annotation.

Genome structural annotation was performed using MAKER3 v3.01.03 [80]. A cleaned protein database was first prepared from five closely related teleost species: Danio rerio, Perca flavescens, Scomber japonicus, Thunnus albacares, and Thunnus maccoyii. Transcriptomic evidence was generated by aligning RNA-Seq reads and assembling them into a deduplicated transcriptome using Trinity and CD-HIT-EST [81]. The MAKER pipeline was executed iteratively in three rounds: an initial annotation run; a second round incorporating ab initio gene predictions from Augustus v3.4.0 and SNAP, which were trained using the outputs of the first round; and a final third round integrating all available transcriptomic, protein homology, and ab initio evidence [82]. Post-processing of the resulting GFF file included filtering, extraction of coding sequences and protein sequences, and functional annotation. Evidence-based gene prioritization was performed using InterProScan v5.56-89.0 [83].

Comparative genomics

For evolutionary context, a comparative genomic analysis focused on synteny was performed [84]. Genome synteny between Scomber japonicus and Thunnus albacare was investigated using JCVI (v1.1.14) [85], and the results were visualized using pyCircos [86].

Resequencing data processing and variant calling

Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed using unique dual-index identifiers, which comprised 8-bp i7/i5 adapters paired with 6-bp inline barcodes [87]. Demultiplexed reads were then processed for adapter removal and quality trimming [88] using TrimGalore (v0.6.7) [89] and Cutadapt (v1.2.1) [90], with a quality threshold of Phred score < 20 [91]. Variant calling was performed with GATK (v4.2.0.0) to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [92]. The resulting variant calls were filtered with the following thresholds: QUAL < 30, QD < 2, FS > 60, and SOR > 4,. We describe these variants as a filtered SNP dataset rather than as definitively high–quality SNPs, because low and uneven sequencing depth can affect heterozygote recovery and genotype accuracy [93].

Population structure analysis

The filtered SNP dataset was used to investigate population structure [94]. Principal component analysis was conducted using PLINK (v1.9) [95]. Ancestry proportions and population clustering were inferred with ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) [96], testing a range of hypothetical ancestral populations from K = 1 to K = 7 [97].

For downstream analyses, the filtered SNP dataset was further processed with analysis-specific thresholds: For PCA analysis: 1,875,043 SNPs were retained with a maximum missing data rate of 20% per site (--geno 0.2) and no minor allele frequency (MAF) filtering, to maximize interspecific variation information; For ADMIXTURE analysis: 120,445 filtered SNPs were retained after filtering with a MAF threshold of 0.05, depth and missingness filtering with minimum depth (minDP) = 2, maximum depth (maxDP) = 80, and a maximum missing data rate of 30% per site (--geno 0.3).; For D-statistic analysis: 793,598 SNPs were retained after applying MAF > 0.01, removing sites deviating from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium at P < 1 × 10⁻⁶, filtering sites with missing genotype rate ≥ 0.8, and pruning linkage disequilibrium with --indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2. For FST, Dxy, and π analyses, the same 793,598-SNP dataset was used for 50-kb window-based divergence and diversity estimates. For MSMC-IM, 834,650 SNPs were retained following the standard filtering protocol of the MSMC-IM pipeline.(Supplymentary Table 4).

Demographic history inference

The demographic histories of T. japonicus, T. brevis, T. nanhaiensis, and T. sp. were reconstructed using MSMC2 and MSMC-IM to model changes in effective population size and migration over time [98]. For this analysis, the individual from each of the four species with the deepest sequencing coverage (> 20×) was selected [99]. Data phasing was performed with WhatsHap (v2.8) prior to analysis [100]. Two primary comparison groups were analyzed jointly:T. japonicus versus the other three species, and T. sp. versus T. nanhaiensis. Analyses followed the standard MSMC2 and MSMC-IM protocols, using an average mutation rate (µ) of 5.97 × 10⁻⁹ per base per generation, and a standard generation time of 2.9 years for Trichiurus obtained from the authoritative FishBase database. Bootstrap validation was performed using MSMC-Tools, with 50 bootstrap datasets generated from per-chromosome data, and MSMC2 and MSMC-IM analyses were re-run for each bootstrap replicate to assess the robustness of the demographic inference.

Phylogenetic analysis of orthologous genes

To reconstruct a species phylogeny, single-copy orthologous genes were identified and analyzed [101]. A modified version of the Assexon pipeline [102], adapted for whole-genome resequencing data, was employed. Reads were aligned to a conserved set of 4,434 single-copy orthologs derived from the Oreochromis niloticus reference genome [103]. Consensus sequences were assembled using UBLAST and SGA, and exon boundaries were precisely refined with Exonerate [102]. A maximum likelihood phylogeny was reconstructed from the concatenated alignment using RAxML (v8.2.12) [104] under a GTR+GAMMA model of sequence evolution, with branch support assessed from 1,000 bootstrap replicates [105].

Admixture and population divergence analysis

For tests of admixture and window-based divergence, the SNP dataset was further filtered using PLINK [105] by retaining variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 [105], removing sites deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at P < 1 × 10⁻⁶ [106], and excluding sites with missing genotype rate (≥ 0.8) [107], resulting in 793,598 filtered SNPs. Linkage disequilibrium was pruned with the parameter --indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2. The D-statistic (ABBA-BABA test) was then calculated using the qpDstat program from the Admixtools package [108], with Lepturacanthus savala designated as the outgroup. Each test involved a trio of ingroup populations selected from Trichiurus lepturus, T. japonicus, T. nanhaiensis, and T. sp. Finally, to quantify genome-wide patterns of divergence and diversity, pairwise genetic differentiation (Dxy, FST) and nucleotide diversity (π) were calculated from this 793, 598 – SMP dataset in 50-kb non-overlapping windows across all chromosomes using pixy [108]. These results were summarized and visualized using the CMplot R package [109].
