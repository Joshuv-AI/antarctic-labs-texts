Analysis of the Role of N‐Linked Glycans on Human CD1d Molecules
Authors: Syamdas Bandyopadhyay, Sayantan Pradhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCD1d is a cell‐surface expressed glycoprotein that presents a variety of lipid and glycolipid antigens to invariant natural killer T‐cells (iNKT). We have examined the role of the N‐linked glycans on the human CD1d (hCD1d) in modulating the responses of iNKT cells using a combination of cell‐free, cell‐based assays and in silico analyses. Deficiency of one or more glycans on hCD1d diminished the activation of iNKT cells. Soluble glycan mutants did not have significant differences in terms of binding to the nickel‐coated 96‐well plates. Our cell‐free assay, together with docking and MM‐GBSA analyses, confirms that Glycan 1 and Glycan 2 are critical determinants of optimal iNKT cell activation. Taken together, our data emphasized an essential role of the hCD1d N‐linked glycans in modulating iNKT cell response.

Introduction

There are five CD1 family genes organized in a cluster on human chromosome 1 but only one of them, encoding CD1d, is shared by mice and rats [1, 2, 3, 4]. CD1d is expressed in multiple cell types including monocytes, circulating and splenic human B cells, dendritic cells, cortical thymocytes and PHA‐activated human T‐cells [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. It is a type I integral membrane glycoprotein that is structurally similar to MHC I molecules, possessing luminal α1, α2 and α3 domains, and it associates non‐covalently with β2‐microglobulin (β2m) [9, 10]. hCD1d has four N‐linked glycosylation sites flanking the α1 and α2 domains which combine to form the antigen‐binding groove that binds lipid and glycoprotein antigens [1, 2] (Figure 1A).

CD1d molecules present an array of endogenous or, less frequently, pathogen‐derived lipids which are recognized by Natural Killer T (NKT) cells [11, 12]. The major subset of NKT cells in mice, and a significant subset in humans, are known as invariant NKT (iNKT) cells. They express a fixed repertoire of T cell receptors (Vα24 JαQ with Vβ11 in humans and Vα14 Jα18 with a restricted Vβ sets in mice) that recognize CD1d molecules associated with the specific lipid agonist α‐galactosyl ceramide (α‐GalCer) [11, 13, 14]. Importantly, NKT cells are also able to recognize one or more autologous lipids and exist in a preactivated state with the potential for direct cytotoxicity [15, 16]. Given the preactivated nature of these cells and the potentially deleterious effects of widespread activation, their responses must be tightly regulated.

Glycosylation of proteins is important in a variety of ways for cellular immune responses. The addition of N‐linked glycans during biosynthesis of proteins in the ER plays an important role in their folding, trafficking, and stability [17, 18]. Although MHC I molecules serve as a well‐studied example of the importance of N‐linked glycans in immune functions, there is much less information on the role of N‐linked glycans in hCD1d function. Previous work suggested they have a role in modulating the interaction between the heavy chain and the β2m [1], but data on the potential effects of glycosylation on iNKT cell recognition is lacking (Figure 1B). Here we use a combination of cell‐free and cell‐based assays supported by in silico analyses to examine this question. While experimental studies highlight the importance of N‐linked glycans in hCD1d function, their direct contribution to receptor binding remains insufficiently understood. Computational approaches such as protein–protein docking and binding energy calculations provide molecular‐level insights into glycan‐mediated modulation of hCD1d interactions. Overall, we find that the N‐linked glycans particularly glycans 1 and 2 play important roles in iNKT cell recognition of hCD1d molecules.

Materials and Methods

Antibodies, Cell Lines and Reagents

The antibodies to hCD1d, CD1d51.1, and D5 have been described previously [19, 20]. The polyclonal rabbit anti‐human β2m antibody was from Dako Cytomation. α−6xHis was from BD Biosciences and MBL International Corporation, respectively. The reagents used for sandwich ELISA (anti‐human IL‐2 mAb) were from BD Biosciences. Neuraminidase enzyme was procured from Roche. Ni‐NTA beads were from Qiagen. α‐GalCer was synthesized as described [21]. The DN32D3 hybridoma (RRID not available) represents a mouse NKT cell line that recognizes α‐GalCer presented in the context of hCD1d, and was kindly provided by Dr. Albert Bendelac, University of Chicago. C1R cell line (RRID:CVCL C1R0) is an Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) transformed lymphoblastoid cell line, and Expi293 cell line is derived from human embryonic kidney 293 cells that have been optimized for confluent suspension cultures. Expi293 cells (RRID not available) were procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific; C1R cells were purchased from ATCC. All cell lines were authenticated for the described experiments and have not been previously reported as misidentified or contaminated. Moreover, all cell lines used were free of mycoplasma contamination for the described experiment.

Cloning, Mutagenesis, Protein Purification and De‐Glycosylation Assay

6xHis tagged‐soluble and membrane expressed hCD1d were cloned in pcDNA3.1 and MSCV2.2 vectors, respectively. hCD1d cDNAs containing N‐glycosylation site mutations were generated by the PCR method using 325 ng of template DNA and Turbo Pfu (Agilent) [22]. The reaction was digested using 5 μl of Dpn I for 5 h at 37°C, of which 3 μl was used to transform E. coli Top10 cells which were screened for the correct insert and confirmed by DNA sequencing. Soluble wild‐type and glycan mutants of hCD1d were purified from the supernatant of Expi293 cells by the Ni‐NTA method, followed by size exclusion chromatography. Purified proteins were run in SDS‐PAGE and stained by Coomassie Brilliant Blue to visualize the protein. hCD1d protein was digested by Neuraminidase I at 4°C overnight.

ELISA

Supernatant was collected from overnight culture, centrifuged, and used in a sandwich ELISA experiment. Capture antibody for IL‐2 was incubated at 1:250 dilution at 4°C overnight. Plates were blocked with 1x PBS containing 5% FBS for 1 h at room temperature followed by addition of standard IL‐2 or culture supernatant. Supernatant was diluted appropriately whenever required to ensure that the measured values fell within the assay's linear detection range. Detection antibody was used at a dilution of 1:500. OD450 was measured using a BioTek microplate reader.

Flowcytometry

CD1d51.1 antibody (0.25 μg per 50,000 cells) was used for 30 min, followed by incubation with 1:500 Alexa 647 goat α‐mouse secondary antibody (Life Technologies). Data were collected using BD Accuri C6 and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Generation of Stable Transfectant

C1R cells were transfected with the vector MSCV2.2 containing the appropriate insert using the retroviral transduction method. Cells were sorted as required for equal expression of GFP encoded by the bicistronic vector used to express the constructs. The cells were assayed for hCD1d surface expression by flow cytometry using CD1d51.1 antibody. Cells were maintained in RPMI media (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone), penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen), 1 M HEPES (Life Technologies), l‐glutamine (Life Technologies) and β‐ME (Life Technologies) in 5% CO2 atmosphere [23].

Immunoblotting

SDS‐PAGE was run to resolve the protein and electrophoretically transferred to Immobilon‐P membranes (Millipore). Blots were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with α−6xHis antibody (1:5000) followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG (1:5000) antibody. The proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescent Western blotting substrate (SuperSignal West Pico, Pierce) on X‐ray film [23].

Plate‐Bound Assay

100 ng of purified CD1d protein in 1× PBS was added to each well of nickel‐coated 96‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for immobilization overnight at 4°C. The next day, plates were washed five times with 1x PBS. CD1d51.1, D5 or α‐β2 m antibody diluted in 1x PBS with 5% FBS was incubated at different concentrations as indicated for 1 h in sterile 1xPBS with 5% FBS. HRP‐conjugated α‐mouse IgG (for CD1d51.1 and D5 antibody) or α‐rabbit IgG antibody (for α‐β2 m antibody) was used as a secondary antibody for 1 h at 37°C at 1:10,000 dilution followed by five washes with 1x PBS and then incubation with 100 μL/well TMB single solution substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL/well 1 M H3PO4. OD450 was measured using a BioTek microplate reader (Figure 1C).

For iNKT cell stimulation assay, CD1d protein (100 ng per well) diluted in 1× PBS was immobilized on sterile nickel‐coated 96‐well plates overnight at 4°C, as described previously. The next day, the plate was brought to room temperature, and after five washes with 1x PBS, α‐GalCer was incubated in the wells of 96‐well plates at the indicated doses for 4 h at room temperature, and 50,000 DN32D3 cells were added to each well. The plate was incubated in the CO2 incubator for 24 h. Supernatant was collected the next day. ELISA was performed on the supernatant to measure the amount of IL‐2 (Figure 1C).

In Silico Analysis

In silico analysis was performed to complement and compare the experimental findings, providing molecular‐level insights into hCD1d–iNKT cell receptor interactions. The workflow is outlined as follows. Protein Preparation—The crystal structure of the hCD1d–iNKT‐cell receptor complex (PDB ID: 8SGB) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank [24]. Chain A, corresponding to hCD1d, and chain C, corresponding to the NKT cell receptor, were extracted and used for the study. The structure was pre‐processed by removing water molecules and other non‐essential heteroatoms, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms. Glycan deletion mutants (Glycan 1 at N2°, Glycan 2 at N42, Glycan 3 at N1°8, and Glycan 4 at N163) were generated by selectively removing the respective N‐linked glycans from chain A using PyMOL software [25].

Protein–Protein Docking and Binding Energy Calculation

Docking calculations were carried out using the ClusPro server [26, 27]. Chain A (CD1d, wild‐type and mutants) was used as the receptor, while chain C (NKT cell receptor) was used as the ligand. Docking was performed using default parameters under the balanced scoring mode. The generated docked conformations were clustered based on RMSD, and the top‐ranked model from the largest cluster with the lowest energy score was selected for further analysis. Binding free energy calculations were carried out using the MM‐GBSA method implemented in GROMACS software [28, 29]. More negative values indicate stronger binding affinity. The docking and MM‐GBSA results were compared across the wild‐type and glycan‐deleted hCD1d systems. Variations in binding affinity were analyzed in relation to the structural and functional roles of individual glycans and correlated with available experimental observations on hCD1d‐mediated activation of iNKT‐cells.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicates. The data, presented as mean values ± standard deviations (SD), are from at least three independent experiments. Student's two‐tailed t‐test was employed to assess the significance of the differences. p < 0.05 (*) was considered to be significant, p < 0.01 (**) and (***) p < 0.001 were considered to be highly significant.

Results

Near‐Complete Loss of hCD1d N‐Linked Glycans Reduces iNKT Cell Stimulation

The four N‐linked glycans flanking the antigen‐binding groove at residues N20, N42, N108 and N163 are numbered 1, 2, 3 and 4 as previously described (Figure 1A) [1]. Removal of all four glycans completely inhibited hCD1d surface expression, so to address their role, we produced a mutant lacking three of them. A wild‐type cDNA construct and a construct lacking glycans 1, 3 and 4 (Δ134 CD1d) were expressed in C1R cells by retroviral transduction [23]. Cell surface levels were measured using a CD1d51.1 antibody, specific for CD1d.β2 m dimers, by flow cytometry. A dramatic reduction of surface‐expressed hCD1d was observed in case of Δ134 hCD1d compared to WT hCD1d (Figure 2A). To compare the abilities of the mutant and WT constructs to stimulate iNKT cells, cell surface expression levels must be similar. To attain this goal, we manipulated the Kozak sequence, which regulates the initiation and translation of an mRNA [30, 31, 32], of WT and mutant hCD1d to produce variants with matched cell surface expression levels. Bases corresponding less well to the consensus (5’ CCG TAT TTT ATG GGG 3’) were introduced into the Kozak sequence of the WT hCD1d (WT CD1dlo) and bases closer to the consensus (5’ CCG GCC ACC ATG GGG 3’) were introduced into the Kozak sequence of Δ134 CD1d (Δ134 CD1dhi) (Figure 2B) and the new constructs expressed in C1R cells. FACS analysis showed that Δ134 CD1dhi was expressed at a much higher level than the unmodified Δ134 CD1d, and importantly expression was very close to the level of WT CD1dlo (Figure 2A). Next, we determined the effect of the near‐complete loss of glycan on the iNKT cell response. C1R cells bearing either WT CD1dlo, Δ134 CD1d or Δ134 CD1dhi were incubated with varying doses of α‐GalCer for 4 h and assayed for their ability to stimulate the α‐GalCer‐specific hybridoma DN32D3, a mouse iNKT cell hybridoma that recognizes human CD1d. There were significant differences in the functional capacity of the Δ134 CD1dhi or Δ134 CD1d compared to the WT CD1dlo: Δ134 CD1dhi or Δ134 exhibited inferior stimulation compared to WT CD1dlo (Figure 2C). We concluded that N‐linked glycans of hCD1d are necessary for optimal stimulation of the iNKT cell line. While fully glycosylated hCD1d.β2m dimers can physiologically interact with the iNKT cell receptor and evoke immune functions, removal of three of them seriously impaired recognition.

Glycan(s) 1 and 2 Are Important for iNKT Cell Stimulation

Although the data above suggest that glycosylation is required for efficient iNKT cell recognition, narrowing down the particular glycan(s) responsible by using Kozak sequence manipulation to adjust cell surface expression is complicated. In addition, it is difficult to exclude alternative unexpected explanations for the results, for example, a potential role for ER stress induced by degradation of over‐expressed mutant hCD1d heavy chains. We therefore opted for a cell‐free system using recombinant hCD1d glycosylation mutants to test the contribution of individual glycans to the modulation of the iNKT cell response. We generated single glycan mutants of 6xHis‐tagged hCD1d lacking the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains, disrupting the individual N‐X‐S/T glycosylation motifs by replacing the serine or threonine residue with alanine. The purified proteins were further purified in association with b2m from Expi 293 cell supernatants using nickel‐beads followed by size exclusion chromatography (Figure 3A). Each individual glycan mutant lacks only one of the four N‐linked glycans. Consequently, the difference in molecular mass relative to the WT hCD1d protein is minimal, resulting in a migration pattern that is comparable to that of the WT counterpart. The WT and mutant proteins were tested to assess their conformational status by binding them overnight to nickel‐coated plates and comparing their capacities to bind the conformation‐dependent mAb CD1d51.1, the heavy chain specific mAb D5, and a rabbit α‐β2 m antibody. Titration profiles of the antibodies showed no major differences between WT hCD1d and the mutants (Figure 3B). We next investigated the impact of the glycan deficiencies on recognition by the iNKT cell line DN32D3. After immobilization as above α‐GalCer was added for 4 h at 37°C followed by DN32D3 cells, and after further overnight incubation released IL‐2 was measured by ELISA. While Δ1 CD1d and Δ2 CD1d showed a significant reduction in stimulatory capacity compared to the wild‐type protein, there was no significant attenuation of IL‐2 production in the case of Δ3 and Δ4 CD1d proteins (Figure 3C). Altogether, our data suggested that glycans 1 and 2 are important for optimal iNKT cell stimulation.

Sialic Acid of hCD1d Is Required During iNKT T Cell Receptor Function

The data presented so far indicates that glycans, in particular glycans 1 and 2, are important for iNKT cell recognition. A remaining question is what features of the glycan are important: is it the simple bulk of the carbohydrate or a specific aspect of the glycan structure? A terminal modification of glycans is the addition of sialic acid, which introduces a negative charge. To ask if sialic acid is important, we adapted the cell‐free assay using purified soluble recombinant hCD1d‐b2m dimers from which sialic acid residues were removed using neuraminidase. Comparison by SDS‐PAGE and western blotting of the neuraminidase‐treated hCD1d with untreated hCD1d showed as expected that the heavy chain band was more compact after neuraminidase treatment (Figure 4A,B). Neuraminidase treatment had no effect on hCD1d binding by any of the three antibodies (CD1d51.1, D5 and rabbit anti‐β2m) (Figure 4C). However, when control and neuraminidase‐treated hCD1d molecules were immobilized and tested for iNKT stimulation, it was clear that loss of sialic acid impaired recognition. (Figure 4D).

Docking Analysis Reveals the Structural Role of N‐Linked Glycans in hCD1d–iNKT Cell Receptor Interaction

The docking analysis of the hCD1d–iNKT‐cell receptor complex (PDB ID: 8SGB) reveals a strong correlation with experimentally observed glycan‐dependent modulation of iNKT cell activation. The native (fully glycosylated) hCD1d structure exhibited the most favorable binding affinity (−90.25 kcal/mol), supporting the experimental observation that complete glycosylation is essential for optimal recognition and activation of iNKT cells (Table 1).

Consistent with the experimental findings, deletion of individual glycans resulted in reduced docking affinity, indicating destabilization of the hCD1d–receptor interface. Among all mutants, deletion of Glycan 2 showed the most pronounced loss in binding affinity (−62.92 kcal/mol). This aligns directly with the reported role of Glycan 2 in stabilizing the interaction between the CD1d heavy chain and β2‐microglobulin, as well as its critical contribution to iNKT cell stimulation [1].

Deletion of Glycan 1 also resulted in a significant reduction in binding affinity (−72.55 kcal/mol), supporting experimental evidence that Glycan 1, along with Glycan 2, plays a dominant role in modulating iNKT cell responses.

In contrast, deletion of Glycan 4 and Glycan 3 showed a comparatively smaller effect on docking affinity (−85.93 kcal/mol and −88.82 kcal/mol, respectively), closely resembling their native structure (Figure 5). While the former docking results suggest that once the protein is structurally intact, Glycan 4 contributes less directly to receptor binding, the later indicate no significant reduction in iNKT cell stimulation upon its deletion, confirming its role as an accessory glycan with minimal involvement in direct receptor interaction.

Discussion

Glycosylation is one of the most important modifications found on eukaryotic proteins [1, 33, 34, 35]. N‐linked glycans are critical for the functions of several immunologically important glycoproteins, including CD4, CD26 and CD45 [36, 37, 38]. Our study aimed to decipher the role of glycosylation in hCD1d function. hCD1d has four N‐linked glycans at residues flanking the antigen‐binding groove, and although glycan 2, positioned at N42 has been shown to interact with β2m [1], no data are available on the potential effects of the glycans on iNKT cell responses. Our data indicate a role for the glycans beyond expected effects on folding and expression, and specifically suggest that they are important for iNKT cell recognition.

We initially opted to assess the functions of the glycans by expressing mutants lacking them in the b2m‐positive but HLA class I‐deficient B lymphoblast cell line C1R, to eliminate potential effects of MHC‐I heavy chain competition for b2m association. Complete elimination of all four glycans prevented cell surface expression of hCD1d, while hCD1d lacking glycans 1, 3 and 4 were selected because of the known interaction of glycan 2 with b2m [1], was detectable but low (Figure 2A). These results are not particularly surprising because glycosylation facilitates the folding and assembly of many glycoproteins. To enable the comparison of wild‐type and mutant hCD1d, we modified the Kozak sequences of the expression vector, reducing translation of the wild‐type and increasing translation of the mutant to achieve comparable surface levels of both. Measurement of their relative abilities to stimulate an iNKT hybridoma clearly showed that complete glycosylation is essential for optimal recognition.

Attempts to extend the functional analysis of individual glycans by expressing additional glycosylation mutants did not succeed because of variability in surface expression that we were unable to resolve using the Kozak mutagenesis approach. We therefore resorted to expressing and purifying soluble hCD1d‐b2m dimers lacking individual glycans and evaluating their ability to stimulate the iNKT hybridoma after immobilization via a C‐terminal His tag. We found that none of the individual glycans were essential for normal folding, based on the reactivities of hCD1d variants lacking individual glycans with the conformation‐dependent mAb CD1d51.1. However, two of the glycans, 1 and 2, independently affected iNKT cell recognition when they were eliminated. Glycan 2 appears to interact with b2m, based on earlier data [1], but glycan 1 does not, making this interaction unlikely to be the cause of the effect. Notably, glycans 1 and 2 are at each end of the α1 α‐helix and lie on the same side of the lipid‐binding groove. Whether an hCD1d mutant deprived of all glycans is still produced by the cells—could be interesting to investigate but is beyond our scope since that does not align with the primary objective of the present study. The fully de‐glycosylated mutant was not detected on the cell surface, as a result we did not further investigate its intracellular expression. Moreover, the conclusions of the present study are based on the comparative analyses of the individual glycan mutants and are not dependent on the intracellular expression of the fully de‐glycosylated construct.

We also provide evidence that sialic acid is particularly important for optimal stimulation of the iNKT cell hybridoma. This may have physiological significance: in dendritic cells, the surface level of sialic acid decreases upon treatment with proinflammatory cytokines [39], which could affect CD1d function. In addition, bacterial sialidases can cleave terminal neuraminic acid residues from N‐linked glycans [40], potentially those on surface CD1d molecules. This could negatively affect iNKT cell recognition during infection. Overall, the docking‐derived prioritization of glycan contributions: Glycan 2 > Glycan 1 > Glycan 4 ≥ Glycan 3 presented in Table 1 is in strong agreement with experimental functional data, particularly highlighting the dominant roles of Glycans 1 and 2 in iNKT cell activation. The results collectively demonstrate that glycans directly influence receptor binding reinforcing the concept that glycosylation is a critical determinant of antigen presentation and immune recognition, and that computational docking can effectively capture these biologically relevant trends when interpreted alongside experimental data.

Altogether, using a combination of cell‐based, cell‐free, and in silico analyses, we show that the glycans of hCD1d, more specifically glycan 1 and glycan 2, play important roles in determining the outcome of the responses of the iNKT cells (Figure 6), furthering our understanding of CD1d biology.
