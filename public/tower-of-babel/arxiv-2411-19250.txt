Parametric Lattices Are Better Quantizers in Dimensions 13 and 14
Daniel Pook-Kolb, Erik Agrell, Bruce Allen

Abstract
New lattice quantizers with lower normalized second moments than previously reported are constructed in 13 and 14 dimensions and conjectured to be optimal. Our construction combines an initial numerical optimization with a subsequent analytical optimization of families of lattices, whose Voronoi regions are constructed exactly. The new lattices are constructed from glued products of previously known lattices, by scaling the component lattices and then optimizing the scale factors. A two-parameter family of lattices in 13 dimensions reveals an intricate landscape of phase changes as the parameters are varied.



I
Introduction

The

lattice quantizer
problem is to find a lattice with the minimal
normalized second moment in a given dimension
[
1
, Ch. 2]
.
Geometrically, this minimizes the mean squared distance from a random point
to the closest lattice point, when the number of lattice points per unit
volume is fixed.
It is one of the classical problems in lattice theory and has applications in
fields ranging from
data compression
[
2
]
,
[
3
, Chs. 1, 3–5]

and cryptography
[
4
]

to machine learning
[
5
]

and data analysis
[
6
]
.

Proofs of optimality of a lattice for quantization exist only in dimensions up
to three.
In two dimensions, the optimal lattice quantizer is the hexagonal lattice

[
7
]
and in three dimensions
it is the body-centered cubic lattice
[
8
]
.
Together with these proven results,
the lattices with the lowest known normalized second moments
in dimensions
4
–
8
,
12
,
16
, and
24

were listed in 1984 by Conway and Sloane
[
9
]
and
conjectured optimal by the same authors in 1999
[
1
, p. 13]
.
Remarkably, the conjectured optimal lattice quantizer in
12
dimensions, the
Coxeter–Todd lattice
K_{12}
, is surpassed by lattices found in 2010 by
Kudryashov and Yurkov
[
10
]
and rediscovered and analyzed
by the present authors
using a different construction technique
[
11
]
;
see Appendix
B-B
.

In dimensions
13
and
14
, the focus of the present paper, the best known
lattice quantizers were for a long time the duals of the checkerboard lattice

D^{*}_{13}
and
D^{*}_{14}

[
9
, Fig. 2]
,
[
1
, Fig. 2.9]
.
Both were surpassed by a product of
K_{12}
and a low-dimensional lattice
[
12
]
.
Using a better quantizer than
K_{12}
in
12
dimensions gives
a slightly improved product lattice in
13
dimensions
[
11
]
.
A lamination of
K_{12}
led to a non-product lattice with an even lower NSM
[
13
]
.
Lyu et al. obtained a better
14
-dimensional lattice from a complex lattice
over the Eisenstein integers in 2023
[
14
]
.

With one exception, the best known lattice quantizers are
integral lattices,

i.e., lattices such that the inner product between any pair of lattice vectors is an integer.
The exception is in
9
dimensions, where a conjectured optimal lattice
was found in 1998 by Agrell and Eriksson using numerical optimization
[
15
]
.
It was defined using a
9\times 9
generator matrix, where
80
components are
exact and one is “just a computer number,” as the lattice pioneer Neil Sloane once
commented
[
16
]
. It took until 2021 to identify the square of this “computer number”
as the root of a ninth-order polynomial
[
17
]
.
We call such lattices, which are exact functions
of a small number of real parameters,
parametric lattices.

The work reported here employs both numerical approximation methods and
exact analytic ones. While this mixed approach to finding optimal lattice quantizers is decades old, there has been recent progress.
In
[
18
]
, numerical optimization was performed in dimensions

10
–
16
using an improved
stochastic gradient descent method.
This is complemented in the same work by a new identification technique to
find exact lattices from approximate numerical representations.
In all dimensions except
13
,
14
, and
15
, the previously known lattices
with the lowest normalized second moment
were recovered, providing strong numerical evidence for the conjecture that these are
the optimal lattice quantizers in their dimension.
In dimension
15
, the lattice
\Lambda^{*}_{15}
, i.e., the dual of the
laminated lattice
\Lambda_{15}
, was identified and conjectured optimal.
Results in the only remaining dimensions
13
and
14
were reported
numerically, with further analysis deferred to future work.
That is the purpose of the present paper.

First, the lattices found numerically in dimensions
13
and
14
are
identified using the technique described in
[
18
]
:
solving systems of equations derived from their theta images.
The technique does not return exact representations of these two lattices,
but rather reveals them to be parametric lattices,
somewhat similar to the best
9
-dimensional one
[
17
]
.
Then, by optimizing the parameters using the methods developed in

[
17
,
13
]
, we obtain exact lattice quantizers with the lowest
normalized second moments reported in dimensions
13
and
14
,
and conjecture these lattices to be optimal in their dimensions.

II
Lattice Definitions

II-A

Basic notions

An
n
-dimensional lattice
L
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
m\geq n
, is the set of all linear
combinations of
n
linearly independent
basis vectors
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with integer
coefficients.
With the convention that all vectors are row vectors, we can write

L\triangleq\left\{{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\colon{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\right\},

(1)

where
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is an
n\times m

generator matrix
of
L
whose rows
are the basis vectors.
There are infinitely many generator matrices for the same lattice
L
.

The
dual lattice

L^{*}
of a lattice
L
with generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}

is generated by the matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{*}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{A}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{B}}
, where

{\boldsymbol{A}}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{B}}^{T}
is a
Gram matrix
of
L
.
Similar to generator matrices, Gram matrices are not unique for a given
lattice, but their determinants depend only on
L

[
1
, p. 4]
.
A lattice is an integral lattice if and only if its Gram matrix has only
integer entries.

Two lattices
L
and
L^{\prime}
are called
equivalent
if one can be
transformed into the other by a combination of rotations, reflections, and/or changes of scale

[
1
, p. 10]
.
This means that for any
n
-dimensional lattice
L
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
m>n
,
there exists an equivalent lattice
L^{\prime}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
To simplify the discussion and without loss of generality, we henceforth assume
that
m=n
, making
{\boldsymbol{B}}
a square generator matrix.
1
1

1

This notational assumption will however not prevent us from using
nonsquare submatrices as building blocks in the square generator matrices
(
46
) and (
VI-B
).

In that case,
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{B}}^{T})^{-1}
.

The
Voronoi region
of a lattice point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in L
is the set of all
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
closer to
{\boldsymbol{x}}
than to any other lattice point.
By definition, the origin
{\boldsymbol{0}}
is always a lattice point, and we denote
the Voronoi region of
{\boldsymbol{0}}
by
\Omega
.
The volume of
\Omega
is
V=\sqrt{\det{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\lvert\det{\boldsymbol{B}}\rvert
.

The lattice quantizer problem is to find a lattice

L
with the minimal
normalized second moment
(NSM) or

quantizer constant

[
1
, pp. 34, 56–62]

G(L)\triangleq\frac{1}{nV^{1+2/n}}\int_{\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(2)

The normalization by
1/V^{1+2/n}
makes
G
independent of the
lattice’s scale, i.e.,
G(L)=G(aL)
for all
a>0
.
The factor of
1/n
is a convention that ensures that the
n
-dimensional
cubic lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
has the same NSM in all dimensions, with
G({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})=1/12
.
It also has the consequence that

G(L\times L)=G(L),

(3)

where
L\times L
is a product lattice defined in
Sec.
II-B
.
If
L
and
L^{\prime}
are equivalent lattices, then their respective NSMs are
the same,
G(L)=G(L^{\prime})
.

In 1996, Zamir and Feder
[
19
]
proved a useful necessary condition
that a globally optimal lattice quantizer satisfies.
Let
{\boldsymbol{U}}
denote the
second moment matrix
of
L
defined by

{\boldsymbol{U}}\triangleq\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(4)

Then, if
L
is a globally optimal lattice quantizer,

{\boldsymbol{U}}
is proportional to the
n
-dimensional identity matrix
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
.
This result is generalized in
[
12
]

to apply to
locally
optimal lattice quantizers as well.
Note that this is only a necessary condition.
If
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is not proportional to the identity, then

L

is not even locally optimal.
This will become an important point in our analysis, especially for the novel

13
-dimensional lattice.

II-B

Product lattices

The Cartesian product of
k
scaled lattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
is

L_{\textrm{p}}\triangleq a_{1}L_{1}\times\cdots\times a_{k}L_{k}=\{[a_{1}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\;\cdots\;a_{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}]\colon{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\in L_{i},\forall i\},

(5)

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
are real positive scale factors.

L_{\textrm{p}}
is called a
product lattice
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
the
(possibly scaled)
component lattices
.
It is a special case of a parametric lattice with
k
parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
.
The dimension of
L_{\textrm{p}}
is
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}
, where
n_{i}
is the dimension of
L_{i}
.

Product lattices were first considered for quantization in
[
20
]
.
It was shown in
[
12
]

that there exist optimal choices of factors
a_{i}
in (
5
) that
result in the lowest possible NSM of the product lattice.
Such an
optimal product lattice
has an NSM satisfying

G(L_{\textrm{p}})^{n}=G(L_{1})^{n_{1}}\cdots G(L_{k})^{n_{k}}.

(6)

By combining various lattices in this way and selecting the result with
the lowest NSM for a given dimension
n
, many new lattices were found with
lower NSMs than previously reported
[
12
,
14
]
.

A crucial result in
[
12
]
is that a product lattice (
5
)
is never optimal.
There always exists a small perturbation of
the generator matrix of a product lattice that reduces the NSM,
so that product lattices are not even locally optimal.

II-C

Glued lattices

The concept of “gluing” a lattice was originally developed by
Conway and Sloane in
[
21
]

and later considered for quantization in
[
11
]
.
It becomes especially useful when applied to product lattices, where it has
led to two different
12
-dimensional lattice quantizers with a lower NSM than the
Coxeter–Todd lattice
K_{12}
.

Intuitively, gluing interpolates between a lattice
L
and its dual
L^{*}
,

L\subseteq L^{\prime}\subseteq L^{*},

(7)

where
L^{\prime}
is the glued lattice.
Since this requires that
L\subseteq L^{*}
, it implies that
L
must be
an integral lattice.
In general, the dual
L^{*}
of an integral lattice
will not be an integral lattice unless
L
is self-dual.

Since an integral lattice
L
is contained in its dual
L^{*}
,
the dual can be written as a finite number of unions of its translates

L^{*}=\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{g}}\in L^{*}/L}(L+{\boldsymbol{g}}).

(8)

The quotient
L^{*}/L
is a largest set of vectors in
L^{*}

that are all distinct modulo translations by
vectors in
L
.
This set is not unique, but possible choices differ only by translations in
L

and therefore leave (
8
) invariant.
Conventionally, the vectors are selected as short as possible within their
coset
L+{\boldsymbol{g}}
, and by this convention,
{\boldsymbol{0}}
is always one of the vectors.
Thus,

L^{*}/L
is a finite set of coset representatives of
L
in

L^{*}

[
1
, p. 48]
.
The representatives
{\boldsymbol{g}}
in (
8
) form a group
under addition modulo
L
and
are called the
glue vectors
of
L

The number of glue vectors, i.e., the index of
L
in
L^{*}
, is

\det{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

We use the term “gluing” when only a subset of glue vectors is used in
(
8
),

L^{\prime}\triangleq\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\Gamma}(L+{\boldsymbol{g}}),

(9)

where
\Gamma\subseteq L^{*}/L
is a group under addition
modulo
L
, called the
glue group
.
(If
\Gamma
is not a group, then (
9
) produces a nonlattice
packing.)

A product lattice
L_{\textrm{p}}
with integral component lattices
L_{i}
,

i=1,\ldots,k
, and
a_{i}=1
for all
i
, is itself an integral lattice and can thus be used for
gluing.
The glue vectors of
L_{\textrm{p}}
consist of concatenations of the glue vectors of

L_{i}
,

{\boldsymbol{g}}=[{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1}\;\ldots\;{\boldsymbol{g}}_{k}],

(10)

where
{\boldsymbol{g}}_{i}\in L_{i}^{*}/L_{i}
, and are called
glue words
.

II-D

Gluing of scaled lattices

We generalize the gluing construction (
9
) by applying it to
product lattices of the form (
5
) for arbitrary positive

a_{i}
, where the (unscaled) component lattices are integral
lattices.
For this, a useful identity that holds for any lattice
L
and scale factor

a>0
is that

(aL)^{*}=a^{-1}L^{*},

(11)

which follows from the definition of the dual.

First, we define the gluing of scaled integral lattices.
Let
\bar{L}
be an integral lattice
and

L=a\bar{L}
for some
a>0
.
In general,
L\not\subseteq L^{*}
, so it cannot be glued as defined in Sec.
II-C

since
L^{*}/L
does not exist.
However,
by (
11
) we have
L^{*}=(a\bar{L})^{*}=a^{-1}\bar{L}^{*}

and thus
L=a\bar{L}\subseteq a\bar{L}^{*}=a^{2}L^{*}
.
This means that there exist cosets
(a^{2}L^{*})/L
and

a^{2}L^{*}=\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{g}}\in(a^{2}L^{*})/L}(L+{\boldsymbol{g}}).

(12)

The glue vectors
{\boldsymbol{g}}
are the coset representatives in

(a^{2}L^{*})/L=(a\bar{L}^{*})/(a\bar{L})=a\,\left(\bar{L}^{*}/\bar{L}\right)

(13)

(the factor
a
does not cancel since “
/
” forms the quotient space)
and are therefore simply the usual glue vectors of
\bar{L}

multiplied by the scale factor
a
.
If
\bar{\Gamma}
is a glue group of
\bar{L}
and
\bar{L}^{\prime}
the corresponding
glued lattice obtained from (
9
), then we define the
glued
scaled
lattice via

L^{\prime}\triangleq\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\Gamma}(L+{\boldsymbol{g}})=\bigcup_{\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\bar{\Gamma}}(a\bar{L}+a\bar{\boldsymbol{g}})=a\bar{L}^{\prime},

(14)

where the glue group
\Gamma=a\bar{\Gamma}
.

For a product lattice
L_{\textrm{p}}
of the form (
5
), i.e.,

L_{\textrm{p}}=a_{1}\bar{L}_{1}\times\ldots\times a_{k}\bar{L}_{k},

(15)

with
k

integral component lattices
\bar{L}_{1},\ldots,\bar{L}_{k}
,
we define gluing by composing glue words using the
glue vectors of the scaled component lattices,

L^{\prime}\triangleq\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\Gamma}(L_{\textrm{p}}+{\boldsymbol{g}})=\bigcup_{[\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1}\;\ldots\;\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}_{k}]\in\bar{\Gamma}}(L_{\textrm{p}}+[a_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1}\;\ldots\;a_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{g}}_{k}]).

(16)

In (
16
), the glue group
\Gamma

is a subset of

a_{1}\,(\bar{L}_{1}^{*}/\bar{L}_{1})\times\ldots\times a_{k}\,(\bar{L}_{k}^{*}/\bar{L}_{k})

(17)

and is a group under addition modulo
L_{\textrm{p}}
.
The group
\bar{\Gamma}
is a subset of

\bar{L}_{1}^{*}/\bar{L}_{1}\times\ldots\times\bar{L}_{k}^{*}/\bar{L}_{k}

(18)

and a group under addition modulo the unscaled lattice

\bar{L}_{1}\times\cdots\times\bar{L}_{k}
.
Note that
L^{\prime}
is not a sublattice of the dual
L_{\textrm{p}}^{*}
or of a
scaled version of
L_{\textrm{p}}^{*}
, unless the individual factors
a_{i}
are all equal.

II-E

Generalized gluing

The gluing constructions (
9
) and (
16
)
can be generalized further as follows. We no longer require any
relationship between
\Gamma
and glue vectors of any constituent lattice.
If
\Gamma
is a finite group under addition modulo
L
, then
(
9
) still produces a lattice
L^{\prime}
containing
L
.
This general case was already mentioned in
[
11
, Sec. III]
but not
considered further.
It contains the gluing of products of scaled integral lattices as
a special case but also permits glue vectors not in
L^{*}
or non-integral
L
.

III
Parametric Voronoi Regions

We analyze lattices using the methods
described in
[
13
]
with the optimizations in
[
22
]
.
This is done by exactly constructing the full Voronoi region of the lattice up
to symmetry.
Using the recursion relations of
[
13
, Sec. 3]
,
which build upon
[
17
, Sec. 3]
,
the NSM (
2
) and second moment matrix (
4
)
can then be calculated.

The Voronoi region of an
n
-dimensional lattice
L
is represented as a
hierarchy
of faces and
subfaces, starting with the single
n
-dimensional face, the Voronoi region

\Omega
, at the top with no parents
(see, e.g.,
[
13
, Fig. 4]
).
The facets are the
(n-1)
-dimensional subfaces of
\Omega
.
Their only parent face is
\Omega
.
These facets are, like
\Omega
, convex polytopes and each have
(n-2)
-dimensional
subfaces (i.e., their respective facets), which are the
(n-2)
-faces of
\Omega
.
Our method builds the complete face hierarchy of
\Omega
from
the Voronoi region itself down to the
0
-dimensional faces, which are the vertices of
\Omega
.
This hierarchy is sometimes called the
Hasse diagram.

The automorphism group
\Aut(L)
of the lattice is a subgroup of the
rotations
O(n)
that takes
L
into
L
.
If
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is a generator of
L
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}\in\Aut(L)
,
then
{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{M}}
is another generator of the same lattice
L
.
Symmetries are exploited in the following way.
Since
\Aut(L)
consists of the symmetries of
\Omega
, it takes each

d
-dimensional face of
\Omega
into another
d
-dimensional face of

\Omega
.
Two faces of
\Omega
are called
equivalent
under
\Aut(L)
if an
element of
\Aut(L)
takes one into the other.
The hierarchy of faces we construct now only includes one face per class of
equivalent faces, and additionally all their direct subfaces.
(The latter are required to identify all classes of faces and to evaluate the
recursion relations.)

Some issues are unique to analyzing a parametric lattice.
Let

a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
be its
s
real parameters,
s\geq 1
, and
{\boldsymbol{a}}\triangleq[a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}]
.
The generator matrix of a parametric lattice is then a function of the
parameters, and we write it as
{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
.
Let further
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
be the Voronoi region of the lattice generated by
{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
.
As the parameters approach certain critical points,
vertices of
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
may split into multiple vertices, or
several vertices may merge into one or disappear.
Such topological changes have to be considered when constructing a
representation of the Voronoi region.

Our method builds the face hierarchy at a fixed point
{\boldsymbol{a}}
in parameter space.
We obtain the positions of all vertices,
relevant vectors, and other quantities like the volumes of all faces and the NSM
as functions of the parameters.
However, since the face hierarchy is assumed fixed, these functions are valid
only when
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
undergoes no topological changes.
If
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
does undergo a topological change, our
representation does not agree anymore with
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
and may, in fact, become
geometrically ill-defined.

To account for topological changes of the Voronoi region, we
determine the region
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
that contains
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and where
the Voronoi region has the same topology throughout.
The region
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is called the
phase
of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
An example where phases of a parametric lattice have been analyzed can be found in

[
17
, Sec. 4]
.
In particular,
[
17
, Tab. 3]
shows how the numbers of vertices and
facets of the Voronoi region of the parametric lattice
\text{AE}_{9}
change across phases.

The phase
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is identified in two steps
[
17
,
13
]
.
First, we identify the region
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
, with
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
, where the lengths of all

1
-faces are positive
(recall that those lengths,
i.e., the volumes of the
1
-faces, are functions of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
).
If the length of a
1
-face vanishes, then two vertices merge and
the face hierarchy must change.
The second step is to test
\Omega
for further topological
changes in
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
as described in the next paragraph.
If no change occurs, then
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}=P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
.
Otherwise, the boundaries of
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
are located and the phase is determined as
the interior region

P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\subset P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
.
For the lattices studied here, no such changes were found away from
points of vanishing
1
-face lengths,
and thus for these lattices
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}=P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
.

We use the following tests to identify topological changes of
\Omega
within

P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
.
Each test is carried out at a large number of points
(a few hundred for the lattices studied here)

{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}\in P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
.
2
2

2

This works only if the number
s
of parameters is small.
In cases of many parameters, testing the conditions on a dense enough
set of points in
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
might be too expensive.

(
i
)

The set of parametric facets is the same throughout
P_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\prime}
.
This is tested by writing the relevant vectors
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{a}})
of
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
as

{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
, for integer vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, and checking that

{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
are the relevant vectors of
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
for the same
set of integer vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
This requires finding all relevant vectors once at each test point
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}

and writing them as linear combinations of the rows of

{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
.

(
ii
)

Parametric vertices do not move outside of
\Omega
.
More precisely,
let
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
be the set of parametric vertices of
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}})
and

{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})\triangleq\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})\colon{\boldsymbol{v}}({\boldsymbol{a}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right\}.

(19)

Then we test if
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})\subset\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})

as follows.
The set of parametric vertices
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is constructed once at
{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
Each vertex is then evaluated at
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}
, which produces the set
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
.
With the relevant vectors
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
known (see (
i
)), the set of
inequalities that defines
\Omega({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
is evaluated, i.e., we test

{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{n}}\|^{2},\ \ \forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}),{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}).

(20)

(
iii
)

Vertices do not move outside the facets they lie in; i.e.,
if a vertex
{\boldsymbol{v}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
lies in a facet
\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{a}})
, then
{\boldsymbol{v}}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}({\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime})
.
This is tested as in (
ii
), but instead of checking for inequality in
(

The output of the numerical optimization algorithm is an approximation of a locally
optimal lattice. Therefore,
a heuristic method to identify the exact form of a lattice whose
generator or Gram matrix is known only numerically was also proposed in

[
18

optimal lattices exhibit, specifically the arrangement of lattice points on a discrete
set of separated, spherical shells.

To characterize, visualize, and identify these shells,
the cumulative distribution of lattice point norms

N({\boldsymbol{B}},r)\triangleq\left|\{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\colon\|{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\|\leq r\}\right|

{\boldsymbol{B}}

The plot of

Vertical steps in this plot, which represent the shells of lattice points with almost
equal norms, are identified visually. They are turned into an
overdetermined system of equations, which is then solved analytically using
common mathematical software.
This procedure is described in detail in
[
18
, Sec. V-B]
.
Three possible outcomes can occur now:
the system has one, multiple, or no solution.
If it has one solution, then the exact lattice is recovered.
The case of multiple solutions corresponds to a parametric lattice.
In the rare cases when the target lattice
does not have a structure of well separated shells, the identification technique fails.
The first
such case we have observed appears in
13
dimensions and will be
described in detail in Sec.
VI
.

In the remainder of this section, we introduce a deterministic algorithm
to fine-tune and exactly identify a locally optimal lattice from a close
approximation, without relying on any presumed lattice structure.
It is very demanding in terms of computer time and memory, and we
apply it only as a last resort when the identification method
in
[
18
]
fails to return an exact, locally optimal lattice.

A second moment matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}
that is not proportional to the identity can be used to
find a deformation
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
of any lattice
L
with generator
{\boldsymbol{B}}

towards a lower NSM.
This idea is introduced in the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 2]
.
Let

\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{U}}-\frac{\tr{\boldsymbol{U}}}{n}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}

(22)

be the traceless part of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
,
which is nonzero if
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is not proportional to
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
,
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}\triangleq\exp(-\epsilon\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}})
.
It is then shown in
[
12
]

that for small enough positive
\epsilon
, the lattice
L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}

with generator
{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
has a lower NSM than
L
.

An optimization algorithm based on this insight operates as follows.
Starting from an arbitrary lattice
L
with generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
,
the full face hierarchy of the Voronoi region, from dimension
0
to
n
, is computed.
From this description, the NSM
G
and the second moment matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}

are computed exactly.
A perturbation matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}=\exp(-\epsilon\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}})
is computed with
\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}

given by (
22
).
An alternative is
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}-\epsilon\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
,
which is approximately equivalent to
\exp(-\epsilon\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}})

if the elements of
\epsilon\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
have small enough magnitude.
Then the generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is perturbed into
{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
.
This yields a parametric lattice
L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
, with

\epsilon
as a parameter.

To select
\epsilon
, we consider the
deformed Voronoi region
\Omega{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
, where
\Omega
is the Voronoi
region of
L

L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
,
but it is easy to compute from
\Omega
when
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
is known.
The second moment matrix of
\Omega{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
is
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}^{T}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}

[
12
, Theorem 2]
. In general, there exists no perturbation
\epsilon
that makes

{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}^{T}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
proportional to
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
,
but
\epsilon
can nevertheless be chosen to move
the off-diagonal elements closer to zero and the diagonal elements closer to each other.
This provides
a good enough approximation for the next step.

At this point it may be useful to introduce additional parameters into the
parametric lattice.
This is not strictly necessary but can significantly reduce the number of
steps needed for the optimization algorithm to converge.
It is motivated by the fact that there may be no value of
\epsilon
for which

L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
has a second moment matrix proportional to
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
.
An example is the
13
-dimensional case studied in Sec.
VI
, which
is a generalized glued product of three integral lattices.
There, a natural choice of additional parameters are the scale factors

[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}]
of the component lattices.

Next, the face hierarchy of
L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}
is computed for the selected

\epsilon
.
This yields a complete description of the
Voronoi region at
\epsilon
and within the phase
P_{\epsilon,}
.
This is why the exact value of
\epsilon
is not critical.
The NSM is computed as a function of
\epsilon
and any additional parameters in the phase
and the minimum is found analytically. If this minimum lies in
P_{\epsilon,}
,
then the algorithm has converged; otherwise the search continues in another phase.
There are at least three methods to select the next phase to investigate.

One is to consider the phase in which the minimum of the NSM expression of
P_{\epsilon,}
lies.
Although this expression does not give the NSM in other phases,
it is often a very accurate approximation. For example, (
77
)
shows that the second moment of the
13
-dimensional parametric lattice in
Sec.
VI
is
13
times differentiable on the boundary between the
two analyzed phases.

The second method is to compute the second moment matrix of
L{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\epsilon}

and again perturb the lattice to move this matrix closer to a scaled identity.
With this and the previous method, it needs to be verified that the exact NSM at the new point is
less than the previous NSM.

If this is not the case (which is theoretically possible although we have
not experienced it in practice), then a third, safer, method is to
numerically find the minimum NSM in
P_{\epsilon,}
, which
by assumption lies on the phase boundary, and next investigate the other
phases that touch the boundary at the same point, proceeding
with the ones containing the smallest NSM.

We successfully used the first of these three options to optimize the

13
-dimensional lattice studied here.
This required computing the face hierarchy at the initial point and in
two additional phases.
The
14
-dimensional lattice was optimized in just a single step, since its
second moment matrix was proportional to
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
within the initial phase.

VII
Conclusions

By combining several analysis methods,
we obtain new lattice quantizers
in dimensions
13
and
14
, which we conjecture are optimal,
based on numerical and analytic evidence.
The key steps are to first find an approximate lattice using
stochastic gradient descent.
This is followed by the exact identification as a
generalized glued product lattice.
The second moment matrix then provides a clue whether further refinement is
necessary, which was an important step in the analysis of the

13
-dimensional lattice.
Parameterizing the product lattice by scaling the components has proved
to be a successful route.
The optimal parameters are determined via the common root of two polynomials
of degree
14
(in dimension
13
)
and a root of a single polynomial of degree
15
(in dimension
14
).

A somewhat surprising result is that
the optimal parameters in
13
dimensions are close but not equal to unity.
At exactly
1
, the lattice is not even locally optimal, since its
second moment matrix is not proportional to the identity.
The difference is insignificant in practice but demonstrates
the elusive nature of optimal lattice quantizers.
Furthermore,
by visualizing the points in parameter space where lengths of
1
-faces
become zero (Fig.
7
), we found that Voronoi regions
can undergo a much higher number of topological changes than we initially
expected.

We conjecture that the optimal
13
-dimensional lattice quantizer is
generated by
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{13}(1,\sqrt{v_{2,0}},\sqrt{v_{3,0}})
in (
VI-B
),
where
[v_{2,0},v_{3,0}]
is the common root of the polynomials
f^{B}_{2}
and

f^{B}_{3}
closest to
[1,1]
. The full expressions of
f^{B}_{2}
and
f^{B}_{3}
are
given in the online supplementary material
[
27
]
.
We also conjecture that the optimal
14
-dimensional lattice quantizer is
generated by
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{14}(\sqrt{v_{0}})
in (
23
),
where
v_{0}
is the second positive root of
f(v)
in (
41
).

Together with the conjectured optimal lattices
in dimensions below
13
and in dimension
16

[
1
, Chap. 1]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, we now believe that the list of optimal lattices quantizers
is complete up to dimension
16
.

Appendix B
Proofs of equivalences

Two lattices with Gram matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime}
are equivalent
if and only if there exists a real positive scalar
c

and an integer matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}
with determinant
\pm 1
such that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime}=c{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(89)

To determine if two given lattices are equivalent is a nontrivial task.
In this appendix, we prove hitherto unknown equivalences
between known lattices, which were discovered during this work
and are mentioned in the text.
The search for constructive mapping rules between different lattice representations
was facilitated by a heuristic algorithm that selects a set of short vectors in each lattice
and attempts to map the sets onto each other.
