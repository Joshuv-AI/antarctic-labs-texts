Antiviral therapy and hidden predictors of postoperative outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Nadeem Ashraf, Qingyong Hong, Zhidong Guo, Kun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a global health burden and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a common cause. Antiviral therapy has the possibility to reduce the incidence of HCC, and recurrence of HCC after surgical resection.MethodsA total of 410 HCC patients had hepatectomy at Zhongnan hospital of Wuhan University. Categorization of these patients was done on the basis of HBV antiviral therapy. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and the overall survival (OS) rates were assessed during follow-up period. Tumor necrosis percentage, recovery of liver functions, and tumor-related characteristics were also examined in these patients. Statistical analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox regression models.Results After applying strict selection criteria, only 93 patients formed the observation group. These patients had comparatively smaller tumor size ≤5 cm(76.3% vs. 45.1%) and less tumor necrosis (30.1% vs. 54.6%). OS (p = 0.035) and RFS (p = 0.034) was also improved in the observation group. However, antiviral therapy did not show any significant effect on OS (p = 0.188) and RFS (p = 0.527) after a multivariate adjustment. Microvascular invasion of more than 5 foci (OS HR = 2.96; RFS HR = 1.97), poor differentiation (RFS HR = 2.70), and stage C versus 0 of BCLC (OS HR = 3.75; RFS HR = 4.40) were independent adverse prognostic factors. Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated that tumour necrosis was associated with overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS), and this finding was validated in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionWhile antiviral therapy correlated with improved tumor pathology and univariate survival outcomes, it was not an independent prognostic factor after adjustment. Tumor necrosis independently predicted worse OS and RFS. Prognosis was primarily driven by microvascular invasion, poor differentiation, and advanced BCLC stage, suggesting antiviral benefits may operate indirectly through tumor biology modulation.

Introduction

Among primary liver cancers, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common types [1]. It is responsible for approximately 90% of cases and the global incidence is expected to be more than 1 million cases by the end of 2025 [2]. Globally, it is the third prominent reason of deaths related to cancer, and a 5-year survival rate is only 18% [3]. In Asian countries, it is the second commonest reason of deaths related to cancer [4]. Although significant advancements have been made in management techniques including surgical hepatectomy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, arterial chemoembolization, and liver transplant, high recurrence rates and poor long-term outcomes are still major challenges for these patients [5–7]. These outcomes highlight the importance of exploring adjunctive therapies that can improve tumor control and enhance patient prognosis after surgery.

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a known causative factor for HCC development [6]. According to the World Health Organization, chronic HBV infection was found in approximately 254 million people in 2022, and more than 1 million new infections occur annually [8]. Earlier research has shown a significant recurrence rate of HCC in cases with elevated viral load of HBV [6, 9, 10]. Persistent viral replication contributes to ongoing liver inflammation, fibrosis, and genomic instability, all of which promote tumorigenesis and postoperative recurrence [11, 12].

HBV antiviral therapy has been demonstrated to have beneficial effects in improving hepatic function and postoperative outcomes in patients with HBV-related HCC. This is mainly because of the viral suppression caused by antiviral therapy [10, 13, 14]. Emerging evidence further reports that antiviral agents exert additional oncological effects. These include modulation of the tumor microenvironment, reduction of hepatic inflammation, limitation of hepatocyte turnover, and enhancement of tumor necrosis. All this results in reducing early recurrence after curative resection [15, 16].

Nonetheless, the existing literature has inconsistent findings regarding the biological impact of antiviral therapy on long-term postoperative outcomes. Therefore, this study was designed to address this gap by evaluating the effect of pre- and postoperative antiviral treatment on the degree of tumor necrosis and long-term prognosis including mortality in patients undergoing curative-intent resection for HBV-related HCC. This study also examines associations between antiviral therapy and other prognostic factors.

Materials and methods

Study design

Retrospective cohort study.

Settings

Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University.

Duration

It was completed in 96 months, from March 2016 to March 2024.

Sampling technique

Consecutive Sampling Technique.

Sample size

Four hundred ten patients who underwent hepatectomy.

Preoperative evaluation and pathological assessment

Several imaging tests (chest x-ray, abdominal ultrasound, CT scan, and MRI) were carried out on each patient before surgical hepatic resection. Various blood tests were also performed comprising HBsAg, complete blood cell count, serum albumin (ALB), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), serum total bilirubin (TBIL), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), glutamate aminotransferase (ALT), Carbohydrate Antigen 19 − 9 (CA-19-9), and Alpha fetoprotein (AFP).

Exposure definition

Patients were categorized based on the effectiveness of antiviral exposure. Those who received nucleos(t)ide analog therapy for at least three months prior to surgery and achieved sustained virological suppression (both pre- and post-operative HBV DNA < 2000 IU/mL) were considered effectively treated. Patients, who demonstrated poor adherence or irregular follow-up, were excluded from outcome analyses. Patients who did not receive antiviral therapy, received treatment for < 3 months, or did not achieve viral suppression despite therapy were classified to have no or insufficient antiviral exposure. Drug class, specific agent, dose, treatment duration, adherence, and on-treatment viral load trajectories were included in data related antiviral therapy. The treatment included nucleos(t)ide analogues (tenofovir disoproxil fumarate 300 mg once daily) and the duration was a minimum of 12 months, with continuation based on virological and biochemical response criteria. Therapy adherence was assessed through monthly pill counts, pharmacy refill records, and self-reported compliance during follow-up visits.

Diagnosis of HCC and criteria for tumor recurrence

The preoperative criteria to confirm HCC included dynamic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI presenting > 1 cm nodules along with arterial phase enhancement and portal or delayed phase washout, and/or a constant rise in serum AFP concentration. An experienced histopathologist assessed the degree of tumor necrosis on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained sections. Semi-quantitative estimation of the extent of necrosis was done and expressed as the percentage of necrotic area within the total tumor tissue. In cases where the estimation was not certain, a second pathologist independently reviewed the specimen. The final grading was based on consensus between two pathologists. This approach made standardized evaluation of necrosis across all samples possible and reduced inter-observer variation. Curative liver resection was defined as complete excision of all macroscopically visible tumors. It was accompanied by histopathological confirmation of tumor-free resection margins and postoperative imaging showing no residual disease. Tumor recurrence was supposed to be present when there were new intrahepatic lesions on enhanced CT or MRI, and their features appeared similar to HCC. Extrahepatic metastasis was considered when other organs had new lesions, accompanied by raised AFP levels.

Outcomes

All patients were followed up one month after surgery, every 3 months during the first 2 years, and every 6 months after that. Follow-up evaluations consisted of CT scans, liver function tests, and HBV-DNA viral load measurements. If any abnormality was detected, contrast-enhanced CT or MRI was carried out promptly to check tumor recurrence. Primary endpoints included tumor necrosis percentage on the resection specimen and RFS, computed from the day the resection was performed to radiologic or pathologic recurrence. In our study, tumor necrosis was defined as the proportion of necrotic area relative to the total tumor area on histopathological examination of resected specimens. Necrosis was quantified by two experienced pathologists using hematoxylin–eosin-stained slides by estimating the percentage of necrotic tissue (≥ 5% necrotic area). For ease of statistical comparison in baseline characteristics, necrosis was presented as a binary variable (Yes/No). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), early recurrence within two years, liver function recovery assessed by ALT, AST, ALB, TBIL, GGT, ALP, variations in tumor markers such as CEA, CA 19 − 9, AFP, and tumor features including the tumor count, tumor diameter, microvascular invasion (MVI), histological differentiation, presence of satellite nodules, and BCLC stage.

Statistical analyses

Median and interquartile range were calculated to express continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Independent samples t-test was performed to make group comparisons between observation group and control group patients for normally distributed continuous variables while Mann–Whitney U test was carried out for non-normally distributed variables. Similarly, chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test were performed for normally and non-normally distributed categorical variables. P-values were reported for each comparison. OS and RFS were measured using the Kaplan–Meier method, and log-rank test was used to evaluate variations between groups. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression to recognize predictors of survival outcomes. Results were reported as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Multivariate models included variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis, and the proportional hazards assumption was assessed. Subgroup analyses comparing survival according to tumor necrosis were performed separately in both groups using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test.

An exploratory cutoff value of 50% tumor necrosis was applied for subgroup analyses based on pathological assessment. This cutoff was used to compare overall survival and recurrence-free survival. Receiver operating characteristic analysis and area under the curve estimation were not performed, as tumor necrosis was evaluated as a pathological prognostic characteristic rather than as a diagnostic or predictive marker.

Given the retrospective nature of this study and the unequal sample sizes between the antiviral and non-antiviral groups, propensity score matching was not performed. Instead, potential baseline imbalances were addressed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models incorporating major clinical, biochemical, and pathological covariates.

Results

Among the 410 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma included in this study, only 93 met the strict selection criteria (antiviral therapy at least 3 months plus confirmed HBV DNA below 2000 IU/mL) and formed the observation group; patients with incomplete viral suppression or poor adherence were excluded to ensure clean group separation. Of those in the observation group, 83 (89.24%) were administered entecavir and 10 (10.75%) received tenofovir. Standard-dose treatment was followed in 89 patients (95.70%). The median duration of antiviral therapy was 2 years (IQR: 1–10).

Table 1 shows a total of 410 eligible patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 56.48 ± 10.90 years; 84.90% were male. At baseline, several significant differences were observed between the observation group (n = 93) and control group (n = 317) in the occurrence of tumor necrosis, presence of satellite lesions, BCLC staging, tumor diameter, hepatitis B surface antigen titre, albumin, white blood cell count, platelet count, neutrophil count, platelet-monocyte ratio, CA199, ALT, AST, GGT, and ALP levels (all p < 0.05). Patients in the observation group had lower median white blood cell counts [4.68 × 10⁹/L (IQR 4.03–5.64) vs. 5.23 × 10⁹/L (IQR 4.30–6.58), p = 0.001], lower platelet counts [142 × 10⁹/L (IQR 109–176) vs. 156 × 10⁹/L (IQR 116–201), p = 0.016], lower neutrophil counts [2.61 × 10⁹/L (IQR 2.16–3.29) vs. 3.23 × 10⁹/L (IQR 2.36–4.30), p < 0.001], and lower monocyte counts [0.40 × 10⁹/L (IQR 0.34–0.50) vs. 0.45 × 10⁹/L (IQR 0.35–0.60), p = 0.021], but higher albumin levels [39.80 g/L (IQR 37.40–42.40) vs. 38.30 g/L (IQR 35.10–42.10), p = 0.006]. Liver function markers ALT, AST, GGT, and ALP were substantially lesser in the observation group (all p < 0.01). Median CA199 levels were also lower [6.51 ng/mL vs. 9.23 ng/mL, p = 0.006], and majority of patients in the observation group had HBsAg ≤ 250 IU/mL (48.4% vs. 34.7%, p = 0.023). Tumor characteristics also differed: patients more often had smaller tumors ≤ 5 cm (76.3% vs. 45.1%, p < 0.001), less tumor necrosis (30.1% vs. 54.6%, p < 0.001), fewer satellite nodules (7.5% vs. 24.6%, p = 0.001), and more frequent BCLC stage 0 disease (15.1% vs. 4.7%, p = 0.001), (Table 1).

Factors influencing OS in HCC patients

Death was used as the outcome measure. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed the following factors to be statistically significant (p < 0.05): antiviral treatment status, occurrence of tumour necrosis, tumour micrometastases, tumour diameter, white blood cell count, haemoglobin level, haematocrit, neutrophil count, hepatitis B surface antigen titre, AST, and ALB. Further multivariate analysis of significant variables indicated that tumour microinvasion > 5 sites was an independent risk factor for death compared with no invasion (HR = 2.96, 1.84–4.75, p < 0.001). BCLC stage C was an independent risk factor for mortality compared with stage 0 (HR = 3.75, 1.54–9.10, p < 0.05), whereas antiviral treatment status showed no statistical significance (p = 0.132). Factors influencing RFS in HCC patients

Antiviral therapy did not independently reduce recurrence risk (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.58–1.32, p = 0.527). (Table 3)

Cox regression analysis of factors influencing recurrence-free survival in HCC patients

Subgroup analysis using an exploratory cutoff of 50% tumor necrosis demonstrated that patients with more than 50% necrosis experienced significantly poorer overall survival and recurrence-free survival. Subsequently, univariate analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between clinicopathological factors including tumor necrosis and survival outcomes. Variables that reached significance in the univariate analysis were then entered into a multivariate model to identify independent predictors of survival.

The relationship between antiviral therapy and prognosis

The median follow-up duration was 46 months. At study termination, 176 deaths had occurred. At the end of follow-up, the cumulative survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 88.2%, 75.3%, and 40.0% respectively in the control group, and 96.7%, 86.2%, and 45.8% respectively in the observation group. Kaplan-Meier estimates demonstrated superior survival rates in the observation group compared to the control group (p = 0.035, Fig. 1A), alongside a lower tumor recurrence rate in the observation group (p = 0.034, Fig. 1B). Sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding patients with BCLC stage C. Kaplan-Meier estimates demonstrated superior survival rates in the observation group compared to the control group (p = 0.0078, Fig. 1C), alongside a lower tumor recurrence rate in the observation group (p = 0.013, Fig. 1D).

The relationship between tumour necrosis and prognosis

Grouped according to tumor necrosis occurrence, Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that the overall survival rate and recurrence rates were worse in the tumor necrosis group compared to the non-necrosis group (Fig. 2A, p = 0.0017; Fig. 2B, p = 0.002). Subgroup analysis was conducted using a 50% tumor necrosis threshold. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that the overall survival rate and recurrence rate were both poorer in the group with > 50% tumor necrosis compared to the non-necrotic group (Fig. 2C, p = 0.0016; Fig. 2D, p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

After excluding patients with BCLC stage C, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify factors influencing overall survival. Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that the following variables were statistically significant (p < 0.05): antiviral therapy status, white blood cell count, hemoglobin level, packed cell volume, neutrophil count, AST, ALB, occurrence of tumor necrosis, tumor microinvasion, and tumor diameter. Further multivariate analysis of significant variables revealed that white blood cell count (HR = 1.11, 1.00–1.22, p = 0.040), poorly differentiated tumors compared to well-differentiated tumor (HR = 2.06, 0.84–5.05, p = 0.022), and tumor micro-invasion > 5 sites compared to no invasion were independent risk factors for mortality (HR = 2.36, 1.40–3.97, p = 0.001). The administration of antiviral therapy showed no statistical significance (p = 0.132). The detailed subgroup Cox regression results for overall survival are summarized in Table 4. Sensitivity analysis yielded stable conclusions.

Factors influencing Overall survival in subgroups: cox regression analysis

Using tumor recurrence as the outcome, univariate and univariate Cox regression analyses were conducted. The analyses indicated that the following factors were statistically significant (p < 0.05): receipt of antiviral therapy, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, neutrophil count, monocyte count, ALT, AST, GGT, ALP, AFP, occurrence of tumor necrosis, presence of micro metastases, tumor diameter, tumor differentiation grade, and BCLC staging. After accounting for collinearity among these significant factors, multivariate regression analysis revealed that white blood cell count and tumor micro-invasion involving > 5 sites, compared to no invasion (HR = 1.97, 1.24–3.12, p = 0.004), tumor diameter compared with well-differentiated tumors (HR = 2.70, 1.04–7.05, p = 0.045) were independent risk factors for recurrence, whereas antiviral therapy status showed no statistical significance (p < 0.05). Discussion

Although complete clinical cure remains uncommon, the majority of patients with HBV infection obtain constant viral suppression and longstanding control of the disease after receiving potent nucleos(t)ide analogues [17, 18]. However, even after achieving good control, HBV patients have a higher likelihood of causing HCC, particularly in cases with advanced liver damage. Additionally, ongoing viral replication, active hepatitis, tumor-related characteristics, underlying liver fibrosis, and the host’s immune condition are considered key contributors to tumor recurrence following surgical resection [19–21]. Several studies have identified elevated HBV-DNA level as a major determinant influencing the prognosis in patients with HBV-related HCC following resection [22–24]. Reduced mortality rate has also been reported in individuals with low levels of HBV-DNA [25].

Antiviral therapy can not only prevent DNA replication of HBV but also increase normalization of liver functions, and reduce liver inflammation, necrosis, and fibrosis in these patients [25, 26]. Antiviral therapy has also been shown to markedly suppress protein X mRNA expression within tumor tissues, thereby slowing the progression of the tumor [27–29]. Our study also showed that the median HBV DNA level was significantly higher in the control group than in the observation group, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.368). Previous research has shown better prognosis in HCC patients who were treated with antivirals prior to their tumor resection surgery [6, 17, 25, 26].

The present study analyzed 410 patients with HBV-related HCC who had hepatic resection to measure the influence of antiviral therapy on tumor necrosis and prognosis in these cases. Kaplan-Meier estimates demonstrated superior survival rates in the observation group compared to the control group (p = 0.0078), alongside a lower tumor recurrence rate in the observation group (p = 0.013). Several observational studies and reviews have documented improved postoperative outcomes with sustained viral suppression, attributed to reduced ongoing hepatic inflammation, lower risk of de novo tumor formation, and preserved liver reserve [13, 17, 30, 31]. Ke et al. revealed in a study that the 1-, 3-, and 5-year RFS rates exhibited no substantial variation between two groups (P = 0.778); however, the OS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were higher in the treatment group juxtaposed to the control group (P = 0.002) [32]. Similarly, another study documented that early combination therapy (entecavir plus Peg-IFNα-2a during year 1) achieved significantly higher 2- and 8-year RFS and 8-year OS rates than late combination or nucleos(t)ide analogue monotherapy (P < 0.05) [10].

Early recurrence of HCC is primarily linked to tumor-related factors such as capsular invasion, satellite nodules, vascular infiltration, and tumor size or number, whereas late recurrence is more often attributed to ongoing hepatitis activity and the host’s immunity [33, 34]. In our study, independent risk factors for recurrence included MVI > 5 foci (HR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.24–3.12, p = 0.004), poor differentiation compared with well differentiation (HR = 2.70, 95% CI: 1.04–7.05, p = 0.045), and BCLC stage C vs. stage 0 (HR = 4.40, 95% CI: 1.69–11.42, p = 0.002), comparable to the outcomes of the earlier research [9, 33, 34]. Nevertheless, antiviral treatment did not independently lessen recurrence risk (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.58–1.32, p = 0.527). In a research by Li et al., early recurrence was associated with platelet count more than 100 × 109 /L, tumor size more than 5 cm, and tumor multiplicity (p < 0.05 each) [9]. However, in contrast to our results, this study reported that antiviral treatment was a protective aspect against early recurrence (p < 0.001) [9]. Additionally, our study demonstrated that although tumor necrosis was associated with recurrence in univariate analysis, it lost statistical significance in multivariate analysis, mainly because its effect was mediated by other aggressive tumor features such as microvascular invasion and tumor diameter.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations were also encountered. First, potential drug resistance was not evaluated in detail. Second, although we controlled for major clinical and pathological variables, residual confounders cannot be entirely excluded. Contrary to the findings of some previous studies, our findings suggest that the benefit of antiviral therapy is mainly indirect through better liver function and more favorable tumor characteristics instead of direct survival impact. This variation is due to differences in patient population, inclusion criteria, timing and duration of antiviral therapy, and use of postoperative adjuvant treatments. Also, we acknowledge that subgroup analyses based on the use of postoperative adjuvant antiviral therapy can clarify its impact on long-term outcomes. This serves as a major limitation of our study. Future multicenter prospective studies are necessitated to authenticate our results and further investigate the mechanistic links between viral suppression, liver microenvironment changes, and HCC recurrence. Moreover, the potential role of combining antiviral therapy with emerging adjuvant strategies should be explored.

Several additional limitations should be noted. Baseline differences were observed between the observation group and control group, particularly in tumor size and BCLC stage, reflecting real-world treatment patterns. Although propensity score matching could reduce selection bias, it was not applied in this study because matching on key tumor-related variables may result in over-adjustment and a substantial reduction in statistical power. Importantly, after multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses, antiviral therapy was not identified as an independent predictor of survival, supporting the robustness of our conclusions.

In addition, the 50% tumor necrosis cutoff used in subgroup analyses was exploratory and not derived from a validated prognostic classification system. Receiver operating characteristic analysis and AUC estimation were not performed because tumor necrosis was evaluated as a pathological prognostic characteristic rather than as a diagnostic or predictive test.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that antiviral therapy plays a critical role in stabilizing the underlying liver disease, improving hepatic reserve, and optimizing patients for surgical resection. Patients in the observation group had a better prognosis at baseline. However, its impact of preventing post-resection recurrence appears to be indirect and is often outweighed by factors reflecting intrinsic tumor aggressiveness. This difference is clinically important as it emphasizes that although antiviral therapy improves the host environment, it does not fully counteract the biological behavior of an already established tumor. Future studies or subgroup analyses are warranted to explore the impact of postoperative antiviral therapy or combination adjuvant strategies.
