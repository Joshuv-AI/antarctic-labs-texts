Abstract
We present the first deep radio continuum observations of Pa 30, a nebula hosting a unique optical source driven by an ultrafast outflow with a velocity of 16,000 km s -1 . The nebula was proposed to be the remnant of a white dwarf merger that occurred in 1181CE. We report no detection of the radio diffuse emission from Pa 30 or radio emission from the central source, setting 3 ​ σ 3\sigma upper limit flux densities of 0.84 ​ mJy 0.84\,\rm mJy and 0.29 ​ mJy 0.29\,\rm mJy at 1.5 GHz and 6 GHz, respectively, for Pa 30. The radio surface brightness of Pa 30 is ∼ 3 \sim 3 orders of magnitude smaller than that of typical supernova remnants (SNRs) with comparable angular size. If Pa 30 is an SNR, our observations show it to be the faintest known in the radio band. Considering that 10% of the supernova (SN) kinetic energy is transferred to cosmic rays (CRs), the absence of radio synchrotron emission suggests that the SN kinetic energy ≲ 3 × 10 47 ​ ( B / 10 ​ μ ​ G ) − 1.65 \lesssim 3\times 10^{47}(B/10~\mu\rm G)^{-1.65} erg , which is 3 to 4 orders of magnitude lower than that of typical SNRs and the lowest measured among Galactic SNRs. There is also an indication of inefficient CR acceleration for this source. The low SN kinetic energy either implies the potential existence of many more radio-faint, sub-energetic SNRs in our Galaxy or challenges the SNR interpretation of Pa 30.

E_{\rm SN}
is believed to be around
10^{51}
erg, some SNRs are found to have much lower -energy SN explosions, such as the Crab Nebula with
E_{\rm SN}\lesssim 10^{50}
erg
(
N. Smith 2013

H. Yang & R. A. Chevalier 2015

, DA 530 with
E_{\rm SN}\lesssim 10^{50}
erg

Thermonuclear SNe, with Type Ia being the most well-known subgroup, are diverse, as demonstrated by both supernova observations
(
S. W. Jha et al. 2019
)
and SNR studies
(
P. Zhou et al. 2021
;
J. Weng et al. 2024
, see e.g.,)
.

White dwarfs (WDs) are created by stars with masses less than 8–10
M_{\odot}
. When approaching or exceeding the Chandrasekhar mass limit, WDs in binary systems can lead to
thermonuclear
explosions as a type Ia SN disrupting the merger product,
a type Ia SN with a neutron star (for massive WD binaries)
, or an accretion-induced collapse to form a neutron star
(
I. Iben & A. V. Tutukov 1984
;
R. Pakmor et al. 2011
;
A. R. King et al. 2001
;
H. Saio & K. Nomoto 2004
;
K. J. Shen et al. 2012
)
. However, there is increasing evidence that WDs may produce peculiar thermonuclear SNe with properties distinguished from normal Type Ia, such as the subluminous groups Type Iax, which may leave a stellar remnant behind
(
S. W. Jha et al. 2019
;
M. Kromer et al. 2013
)

and Calcium-rich transients
(
H. B. Perets et al. 2010
;
M. M. Kasliwal et al. 2012
)
.
SNR observations have identified two Galactic Type Iax SNR candidates, Pa 30
(
L. M. Oskinova et al. 2020
)
and Sgr A East near the Galactic center
(
P. Zhou et al. 2021
)
, and a Calcium-rich transient SNR candidate G306.3
-0.9

(
J. Weng et al. 2022
)
.

Pa 30,
with a diameter of
\sim 220^{\prime\prime}

at a distance of
2.3\ \rm kpc

(
C. A. L. Bailer-Jones et al. 2021
)
,
is a newly discovered Galactic infrared (IR) nebula (a.k.a. IRAS 00500+6713) hosting a hot center object J005311 at R.A.=00h53m11.2s, Decl.=
+67^{\circ}30^{\prime}2.1^{\prime\prime}

(
V. V. Gvaramadze et al. 2019
)
.

The central hot source, with a temperature of around
210000\,\rm K
, drives ultra-fast winds of a velocity
v_{\infty}\approx 16000\,\rm km\ s^{-1}
, suggesting a super-Chandrasekhar mass object due to a WD merger
(
V. V. Gvaramadze et al. 2019
;
F. Lykou et al. 2023
)
.

The following up X-ray observations revealed O and C enhancements of the ejecta, supporting the merger of an ONe and a CO WD for creating Pa 30
(
L. M. Oskinova et al. 2020
)
.

The optical measurements show that Pa 30 is expanding with a peak velocity of
\approx 1400\,\rm km\ s^{-1}

(
A. Ritter et al. 2021
;
R. A. Fesen et al. 2023
;
T. Cunningham et al. 2024
)
and imply an explosive event
\sim 10^{3}
kyr ago. This young age, along with the nebula’s position in the sky, establishes a connection between the nebula and the 1181 CE “Guest star” recorded in ancient Chinese and Japanese texts
(
A. Ritter et al. 2021
;
B. E. Schaefer 2023
)
. This association is strengthened by recent Keck IFU observations, which provided a refined explosion time of
1152^{+77}_{-72}
yr for Pa 30
(
T. Cunningham et al. 2024
)
.

Moreover, Pa 30 reveals a remarkably peculiar morphology, containing an infrared (NIR) ring and radially aligned [S II] filaments pointing to the central object. This morphology is unlike any other
nova
or young Galactic
SNR

(
A. Ritter et al. 2021
;
R. A. Fesen et al. 2023
)
.

Despite the young age, Pa 30 has not been detected in the radio band based on the shallow radio survey data (e.g., NVSS), setting the upper limit of radio flux density to
\sim 4.1\ \rm mJy
at the L band.

Radio emissions in SNRs are synchrotron radiation generated by GeV-energy electrons, which can be easily accelerated in even old SNRs. Most SNRs in our Galaxy have been identified through radio surveys
(
D. A. Green 2025
)
, though a fraction are initially discovered through other wavebands. It is puzzling that Pa 30, which has an expansion velocity of
\sim 10^{3}~\rm km~s^{-1}
, is not bright in the radio band if it is indeed an SNR. Therefore, we conducted dedicated radio observations toward Pa 30, aiming to detect radio emission or study its puzzling properties in the radio band.

In this
letter
, we report the first deep radio observation results of our VLA observations of Pa 30 in L and C bands. In Section
II
we describe our observations and the data reduction threads. The results of the new observation are presented in Section
III
. We discuss the emission models and their implications for different scenarios in Section
IV
. Section
V
is a summary of this work.

II
Observations and Data Reduction

Our Pa 30 observations were conducted with the Very Large Array (VLA),
during August and October 2022 (22A-256; PI: Ping Zhou). The observations were
done with two epochs (executions), for
1.0
–
2.0
GHz (L band) under C
configuration and
4.0
–
8.0\,\rm GHz
(C band) under D configuration,
respectively. The choice for such configurations is optimised for their
comparable angular resolutions. The largest angular scales are
\theta_{\rm LAS}=970^{\prime\prime}
and
\theta_{\rm LAS}=240^{\prime\prime}
for L band and C band, respectively.
For both L- and C bands, the flux and bandpass calibrator was 3C 48, and the gain
calibrator was J0102+5824. We loop between Pa 30 and J0102+5824 every 15 and 10
minutes, for the L and C bands, with 90 seconds on the gain calibrator. The
on-source time was 85.5 minutes and 143.5 minutes at L band and C band,
respectively.

We processed the data using the standard VLA pipeline (ver 2024.1.0.8) with the
Common Astronomy Software Applications package
(
CASA Team et al. 2022
, CASA
v6.6.1;)
. After the standard pipeline processing, we applied additional manual flagging to remove low-level radio frequency
interference (RFI) and noisy scans on the source.

III
Results

The L and C bands images do not reveal significant diffuse emission from Pa 30, although a few point-like sources are found in the vicinity (see the lower panels of Figure
1
).

We marked these sources with at least
5\sigma
detection in either of the two bands in Figure
1
. We used the CASA
imfit
task to fit the brightness profiles of these point-like sources and found they
can be well fitted by a 2-D Gaussian model,
with sizes and orientations consistent with the beam within
1\sigma
uncertainties
.

This supports that they are indeed unresolved sources, and the corresponding
flux density measurements are summarized in Table
1
.

Since no significant extended structures are found within or around the field of Pa 30, we regard it as a non-detection and proceed with the analysis using a
3\sigma
upper limit. Using the
radius
of
\sim 114.5^{\prime\prime}
, we can estimate the
3\sigma
upper limit of Pa 30’s
flux density

to be
0.84\rm\,mJy
at
1.5\ \rm GHz
and
0.29\rm\,mJy
at
6\ \rm GHz
. The left panel of Figure
2
compares the 1 GHz radio surface brightness
\Sigma
and angular area between Pa 30 and known radio shell-type SNRs. The radio surface brightness of Pa 30 is
\sim 3
orders of magnitude smaller than that of typical SNRs with comparable angular size, making it the faintest among all known Galactic SNRs.

Moreover, there is no significant radio counterpart of the central source J005311. The RMS noise levels of the central source are
17.2\rm\,\mu Jy
and
2.09\rm\,\mu Jy
on L band and C band, respectively.

IV
Discussions

The absence of detectable radio emissions adds to the mystery of Pa 30, as all known young SNRs with ages less than 2 kyr in our Galaxy are radio sources
(
D. A. Green 2025
)
, including another Type Iax SNR at the Galactic center
(
P. Zhou et al. 2021
, Sgr A East,)
. Here we
adopt the SNR explanation for Pa 30 and calculate the expected SNR
synchrotron emission from energy injection and particle number injection perspectives, respectively.
These nonthermal particles can be accelerated by the SNR shocks via the diffusive shock acceleration (DSA) mechanism
(
A. R. Bell 1978
;
R. D. Blandford & J. P. Ostriker 1978
)
.
In addition, we also provide constraints on bremsstrahlung emission and discuss the wind-blown bubble scenario.

IV.1
Synchrotron emission

SNRs generate synchrotron radio emission as their relativistic electrons gyrate in the magnetic fields.
We assumed that the shock-accelerated particles have a power law distribution

\frac{dN_{i}}{dE_{i}}=N_{0,i}\left(\frac{E_{i}}{1\,{\rm GeV}}\right)^{-s},

(1)

where
i=\rm e,p
, for electron and proton, respectively.
s
is the particle energy spectral index and
N_{0,i}
is the electron or proton number. The total energy of the particle can be determined as

W_{i}=\int_{E_{\text{min}}}^{E_{\text{max}}}E_{i}N_{0,i}(E_{i}/1\,{\rm GeV})^{-s}dE_{i},

(2)

where
E_{\rm min}
and
E_{\rm max}
represent the minimum and maximum
particle
energy, respectively.
Then the synchrotron emission luminosity
L_{\nu}
is mainly a function of the total electron energy
W_{\text{e}}
, the electron energy distribution, and the magnetic fields
B
determined by

L_{\nu}\propto W_{\rm e}B^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\nu^{\frac{1-s}{2}},

(3)

In the subsequent analysis, we adopt an arbitrary maximum electron energy of
E_{\text{max}}=10^{5}\,\text{GeV}
, while

varying
E_{\rm max}
has negligible effects on the estimated luminosity when
E_{\text{max}}\gg E_{\text{min}}
.

In this section, we adopt
E_{\text{min}}=10\,\rm MeV

(
S. P. Reynolds et al. 2021
)
. Then we can calculate the synchrotron luminosity using the synchrotron module in the python package
Naima
3
3

3

https://github.com/zblz/naima

(
V. Zabalza 2015
;
F. A. Aharonian et al. 2010
)
.

The standard DSA theory predicts the
electron energy spectral
index is 2.0 for the strong shock of the SNR
(
A. R. Bell 1978
, e.g.,)
, which is consistent with the index distribution of the radio spectra of SNRs
(
D. A. Green 2025
)
.
However, young SNRs show radio frequency spectral index (
=(s-1)/2
) from
0.6
to
0.77
, inferring
s=2.2
–2.5. Therefore, adopting
s=2.3
,
the 6 GHz upper limit suggests:

\left(\frac{W_{\rm e}}{\rm erg}\right)\left(\frac{B}{10~\rm\mu G}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\lesssim 2.7\times 10^{44}.

(4)

This value varies from
1.6\times 10^{44}
to
6.2\times 10^{44}
as
s
increases from 2 to 2.5.

Below, we calculate
L_{\nu}
and also compare with
previous
models.

If we
suppose 10% of the
SN kinetic
energy converts to CR proton energy
W_{\rm p}
.

Adopting an electron-to-proton number ratio
K_{\rm ep}=N_{\rm e}/N_{\rm p}=0.01

(
B. Katz & E. Waxman 2008
,
K_{\rm ep}
may range from
10^{-4}
to
10^{-2}
, with
10^{-2}
being a typical value for SNRs)
and the same particle energy spectral index,
we obtain
W_{\rm e}\approx 0.01W_{\rm p}=0.001E_{\rm SN}
.
This links the synchrotron luminosity
L_{\nu}
with the SN kinetic energy
E_{\rm SN}
.

On the contrary, we can derive the upper limit of
E_{\text{SN}}
based on the radio upper limit and the assumed
B
and
E_{\rm min}
.

For
s=2.3
, equations (
3
) and (
4
) are
transformed into

L_{\nu}\propto E_{\text{SN}}B^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\nu^{\frac{1-s}{2}}K_{\rm ep}

(5)

and the
6\,\rm GHz
flux density upper limit provide:

\displaystyle E_{\text{SN}}\lesssim 2.7\times 10^{47}\left(\frac{B}{10~\rm\mu G}\right)^{-1.65}\left(\frac{K_{\rm ep}}{0.01}\right)^{-1}\,{\rm erg}.

(6)

The lack of radio detection indicates
that
Pa 30 may have a low
kinetic energy
of

E_{\text{SN}}\lesssim 2.7\times 10^{47}\,\rm erg

(
J. Vink 2020
, typically
\sim 10^{51}\rm\,erg
;)
for the typical magnetic fields
B=10
–
10^{3}~\mu\rm G
in SNR shells
(
S. P. Reynolds et al. 2012
)
.

This is lower than ejecta kinematic energy of
\sim 10^{48}
erg given in previous X-ray studies
(
F. Lykou et al. 2023
;
T. Ko et al. 2024b
, with large uncertainties in ambient density and ejecta mass)
.
For
s=2
–
2.5
, and
B=3
–
10\,\rm\mu G
, the
6\,\rm GHz
upper limit gives
E_{\text{SN}}
between
8.5\times 10^{46}\,{\rm erg}
and
2.6\times 10^{48}\,{\rm erg}
.

The magnetic field strength is unknown for Pa 30, which lies 190 pc above the Galactic plane. While typical magnetic field strength in the Galactic disk is a few
\mu
G
(
J. L. Han 2017
)
, those in SNRs can be strongly amplified
(
S. P. Reynolds et al. 2012
, see a review by)
. The magnetic amplification efficiency,
\epsilon_{B}\equiv(B^{2}/8\pi)/(\rho_{0}v_{s}^{2})
, relates magnetic energy density to the postshock pressure, where
v_{s}
is the shock velocity,
\rho_{0}=\mu m_{\rm H}n_{0}
is the ambient gas density,
\mu=1.4
as the mean molecular weight and
n_{0}
is the ambient number density. For Pa 30, the ambient density is poorly constrained, although X-ray measurements suggest
n_{0}\sim 0.1~\rm cm^{-3}

(
T. Ko et al. 2024b
)
. This yields
\epsilon_{B}=0.9\times 10^{-3}(B/10~\mu{\rm G})^{2}(n_{0}/0.1~~{\rm cm^{-3}})^{-1}(v_{s}/1.4\times 10^{3}~{\rm km\ s^{-1}})^{-2}
, falling at the lower end of
\epsilon_{B}=10^{-3}
–
10^{-1}
found in young SNRs
(
S. P. Reynolds & R. A. Chevalier 1981
;
S. P. Reynolds et al. 2021
)
.
In the extreme case that Pa 30 lies in a very rarefied region and has an ultra-low magnetic field
B\sim 3\mu
G, the radio measurements still suggest a low kinetic energy
\lesssim 10^{48}
erg.

The low
SN kinetic
energy can explain the peculiar position of Pa 30 at the lower-left corner in the
\Sigma
–D diagram (right panel of Figure
2
).
Previous studies have shown a positive correlation between the radio
surface brightness

\Sigma
and
E_{\rm SN}

(
E. G. Berezhko & H. J. Völk 2004
;
M. Z. Pavlović et al. 2018
)
.

One can predict
\Sigma
–D relation of SNRs by combining Equation
5
and the SNR dynamic evolution equations.
We use the self-similar solutions of
J. K. Truelove & C. F. McKee 1999
for describing the dynamic evolution of SNRs in the ejecta-dominated phase and Sedov-Taylor phase. Pa 30 is probably in the ejecta-dominated phase with little velocity deceleration
(
T. Cunningham et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, we adopt the model with a uniform ejecta distribution, while the models with a sharp ejecta density profile (power-law index
n>3
) predict significant deceleration in the ejecta-dominated phase and are thus inconsistent with the case in Pa 30.

In the ejecta-dominated phase, the shock velocity scales as

v_{s}\propto E_{\rm SN}^{1/2}M_{\rm ej}^{-1/2},

(7)

Combining this with equation
5
,
adopting
s=2.3
, we derive

\Sigma=L_{\nu}(\pi^{2}D^{2})^{-1}\propto E_{\text{SN}}^{3.3/4}M_{\rm ej}^{0.7/4}n_{0}^{3.3/4}\epsilon_{B}^{3.3/4}K_{\text{ep}}.

(8)

The right panel of Figure
2
shows the
\Sigma
-
D
relationship of some
shell-type
SNRs and our prediction. The observed values of SNRs can be reproduced by our model with normal SNR parameters:

s=2
–
2.5
,
E_{\text{SN}}=10^{50}
–
2\times 10^{51}\,\rm erg
, ejecta mass
M_{\rm ej}=1
–
4M_{\odot}
,
n_{0}=0.05
–
1\,\rm cm^{-3}
, and
\epsilon_{B}=0.001
–

0.1
. Pa 30 is obviously an outlier in this figure.
Given the
3\sigma
radio upper limit of Pa 30 and
v_{s}\simeq 1400\,\rm km\,s^{-1}
, our model yields the constraint

\left(\frac{E_{\text{SN}}}{10^{48}\,\rm erg}\right)\left(\frac{n_{0}}{0.1\,\rm cm^{-3}}\right)^{3.3/4}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{B}}{0.001}\right)^{3.3/4}\left(\frac{K_{\text{ep}}}{0.01}\right)\lesssim 0.2.

For
\epsilon_{B}=0.001

Comparison with previous model and recalculation of
\epsilon_{e}

Considering the atypical morphology of Pa 30 compared to normal SNRs,
T. Ko et al. 2024b

\epsilon_{\text{e}}

4.13\,\rm mJy

With deep, targeted observation in L and C bands, we refined the

With our new data, we can rule out the previously assumed optimistic luminosity of the outer shock (see Figure

The equations used for the calculation of

For

IV.3
Bremsstrahlung

Our radio observations have not established the existence of synchrotron emission from the SNR shock.

Previous optical study suggested that Pa 30’s unusual appearance with radially aligned filaments may arise from the photoionization of wind-driven ejecta
(
R. A. Fesen et al. 2023
)
. In this case, the hot, ionized bubble could produce thermal bremsstrahlung emission.

The non-detection of radio emission from Pa 30 thus sets an upper limit on this process and offers constraints on ionized gas.

Assuming that the thermally emitting gas is dispersed within a sphere of radius
R
, and the volume filling factor is
f
.

Then, the bremsstrahlung intensity of an optically thin, spherically distributed gas is calculated as

I_{\nu}\approx\frac{2}{3}B_{\nu}\tau

where
\tau
is the optical depth measured across the center
of the nebula

(
D. E. Osterbrock 1965
;
F. M. Olnon 1975
)
.
Considering Rayleigh–Jeans regime (
h\nu\ll kT
)
, we obtain
the bremsstrahlung luminosity

\displaystyle L_{\nu}

\displaystyle\approx 1.9\times 10^{18}\sum\limits_{\rm i}Z_{\text{i}}^{2}n_{\text{i}}n_{\rm e}f\left(\frac{T}{\text{K}}\right)^{-0.35}\left(\frac{\nu}{\text{GHz}}\right)^{-0.1}

(10)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle erg\,s^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}},

where
Z_{\text{i}}

and

n_{\text{i}}
represent the charge and number density of the
i
-th
ion specie
, respectively.

For the fully ionized, solar abundance gas (
dominated by H and He
) that fully fills the nebula,
the radio observation sets a maximum density of

n_{\text{e}}=1.6\sim 5.3~\rm cm^{-3}
for
T=10^{4}\sim 10^{7}
K.
It is more likely that Pa 30 consists of weakly ionized gas that fills only a fraction of the volume and has over-solar abundances, since the optical structures are filamentary and weak in H
\alpha
emission

(
R. A. Fesen et al. 2023
)
.
Pa 30 is also filled with hot, highly ionized plasma, which emits the bremsstrahlung emission mainly in the X-ray bands, rather than in the radio bands.
Therefore, we consider the radio-emitting gas to be weakly ionized (
Z_{\text{i}}=1

and
n_{\rm e}=\sum\limits_{\rm i}n_{\rm i}
), as also inferred by the strong [S II], [N II], and [O II] lines.
Given the 0.1 mJy upper limit in the C band,
we estimate

f(\frac{n_{\rm e}}{\rm cm^{-3}})^{2}(\frac{T}{\rm K})^{-0.35}\lesssim 0.1.

(11)

Using the average density derived from [S II] lines
n_{\rm e}=120\,\rm cm^{-3}

(
F. Lykou et al. 2023
)
, the filling factor of the weakly ionized gas is derived as

f\lesssim 2\times 10^{-4}(T/10^{4}~\rm K)^{0.35}
.

Therefore, the lack of radio emission suggests that the optical structures may fill less than

\sim 2\times 10^{-4}

of the total nebula’s volume. This is consistent with the unresolved, filamentary structures in the optical band
(
R. A. Fesen et al. 2023
)
.

However, the actual temperature is uncertain and may deviate from the
10^{4}\,\rm K
, introducing extra uncertainty in the estimate of the filling factor.

We can roughly estimate the upper limit of the total mass of the gas that could produce radio bremsstrahlung radiation as

M_{\rm gas}\lesssim 5\times 10^{-2}M_{\odot}\left(\frac{T}{10^{4}\,\rm K}\right)^{0.35}\left(\frac{n_{\rm e}}{120\,\rm cm^{-3}}\right)\left(\frac{\mu}{10}\right),

(12)

where a large mean molecular weight is assumed to count for the metal-rich gas. Considering the metal composition and the ambient density carry large uncertainties, we therefore do not discuss the gas mass further.

IV.4
Sub-energetic SN origin of Pa 30 or not?

Pa 30 has been regarded as a young SNR, although this interpretation remains unconfirmed.
This nebula challenges our understanding of SNe/SNRs in several aspects if it is indeed an SNR.
Unlike a normal Ia SNR without a central source or core-collapse SNR with a neutron star or black hole, it consists of a central source exhibiting ultra-strong outflow (16,000 km s
-1
) and the mass-loss rate (
\dot{M}
) of
3.5\times 10^{-6}M_{\odot}\,\rm yr^{-1}

(
V. V. Gvaramadze et al. 2019
)
.
A Type Iax origin has therefore been suggested, as this subclass of subluminous SNe may leave behind a hot, partially unburnt white dwarf
(
L. M. Oskinova et al. 2020
)
.

Additionally, the absence of detectable radio emission renders Pa 30 the least luminous known SNR in the radio band. It is particularly puzzling that an 844-year-old remnant is so faint at radio wavelengths, while other young SNRs are typically bright radio sources (see Figure
2
).
The extremely low radio synchrotron luminosity implies that few CR electrons are being accelerated and suggests an unusually low kinetic energy of
\lesssim 2.7\times 10^{47}(B/10~\mu\rm G)^{-1.65}
erg — 3–4 orders of magnitude lower than the canonical SN kinetic energy.

Since most Galactic SNRs have been identified through radio surveys, radio-faint remnants like Pa 30 are likely to be missed unless detected in other wavebands. This introduces an observational bias, implying that the current Galactic SNR sample is highly incomplete and that a larger population of sub-energetic SNRs remains undiscovered. Such low-kinetic-energy SNRs are intrinsically difficult to detect, but they may constitute a non-negligible fraction of the Galactic SNR population. Since Pa 30 may link to a historical supernova, this implies that such events are not extremely rare. In fact, type Iax supernovae are estimated to account for
\sim 10\%
of Type Ia explosions
(
X. Ma et al. 2025
, see
R. J. Foley et al. 2013
for a larger fraction)
. Moreover, given the non-detection at such a close distance, similar remnants located farther away in the Galaxy would likely remain undetected
(
S. Ranasinghe & D. Leahy 2022
)
. Identifying these remnants is crucial for understanding the true distribution of SN kinetic energy and metal feedback into the Galaxy, as well as the diverse outcomes of stellar or white dwarf evolution.

On the other hand, the low kinetic energy further challenges our definition of SNe, which are canonically characterized by a kinetic energy of
\sim 10^{51}
erg, even though a population of sub-energetic SN is emerging
(
R. J. Foley et al. 2009
, e.g., Type Iax SN 2008ha with
E_{\rm SN}\sim 2\times 10^{48}
erg,)
.

Therefore, it remains unclear whether Pa 30 is a remnant of a Type Iax SN or a wind nebula powered by the central source. For the latter case, our calculation of the synchrotron emission is inappropriate since it is based on SNR parameters.
Notably, the central source of Pa 30 is an energetic source and should at least partially shape Pa 30. It gives a wind luminosity of
L_{w}=1/2\dot{M}v_{w}^{2}=2.8\times 10^{38}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}
and can supply an energy of
10^{48}
erg in
\sim 110
years. However, it is known that the wind luminosity is not constant over time
(
B. E. Schaefer 2023
)
.
There is also a possibility that Pa 30 is a complex nebula powered by both an earlier explosion and the central source.
We anticipate that future multi-wavelength observations will unveil the radiative mechanism and material composition, thereby shedding light on the nature of Pa 30.

V
Summary

We report the VLA observation of Pa 30,

which hosts a central source with extraordinarily fast outflow.

Our observations
in
L band and C band resulted in the non-detection of shell-like extended structures that resemble their infrared or X-ray morphology, at the rms noise level of
18.2\,\rm\mu Jy\,beam^{-1}
in L band and
4.6\,\rm\mu Jy\,beam^{-1}
in C band. Some of the bright radio sources in the Pa 30 field are likely irrelevant background radio-emitting objects. We employed different methods to constrain the properties of Pa 30 using the upper limits on the radio
flux density
.

First, we considered the SNR scenario of Pa 30 and
our radio observation provides an independent way to infer the low
kinetic energy to
E_{\text{SN}}\lesssim 2.7\times 10^{47}(B/10\,\rm\mu G)^{-1.65}\,\rm erg
.

We also considered the wind nebula
case and the radio emission is
dominated by bremsstrahlung emission of thermal electrons. Our radio observation helped to provide constraints on the gas density and filling factor.

While our radio observations support Pa 30’s being a remnant of a peculiar, sub-energetic supernova explosion, other scenarios, such as a hot stellar bubble, remain plausible. Deep radio exploration and multi-wavelength observations of Pa 30 and other potential sub-energetic SNRs could provide definitive characterization.
