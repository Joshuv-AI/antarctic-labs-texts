Cocycle stability in permutations of random simplicial complexes
Michael Chapman, Yuval Peled

Abstract
. The cocycle stability rate of a simplicial complex measures how far cochains with small coboundaries are from actual cocycles. In this paper, we study the 1 1 -dimensional cocycle stability rate with permutation coefficients of random 2 2 -dimensional Linial–Meshulam complexes. Our main contribution is the following: If, in the middle traingle density range, these random complexes asymptotically almost surely have a linear cocycle stability rate, then there exists a non-sofic hyperbolic group. As no non-sofic group, nor any non-residually finite hyperbolic group, are known to exist, this opens a potential approach to both problems simultaneously. Our proof method is inspired by a well known fact about the non local testability of Sipser–Spielman expander codes.

2.1.
Simplicial complexes and non-commutative cohomology

A
simplicial complex

\mathcal{X}
is a downwards closed system of finite sets, namely, if
A\in\mathcal{X}
and
B\subseteq A
, then
B\in\mathcal{X}
. One can associate a CW complex with
\mathcal{X}
by letting each set of size
d+1
be a
d
-dimensional simplex, and gluing these simplices along their intersections. We say that
\mathcal{X}
is
d
-dimensional if the size of all of its sets is bounded by
d+1
. The
i
-cells
\mathcal{X}(i)
are the sets of size
i+1
in
\mathcal{X}
.

We study non-commutative cohomology, and thus focus only on
2
-dimensional complexes.
When
d=2
, we have three types of cells — vertices, edges and triangles. Thus, we can think of the complex
\mathcal{X}
as a graph
G(\mathcal{X})
with triangles pasted on it. The edge
[e]=\{x,y\}
can be oriented in two ways
xy=x\xrightarrow{e}y
and
yx=y\xrightarrow{\bar{e}}x
. The triangle
[\Delta]=\{x,y,z\}
can be oriented in
6
different ways

xyz,xzy,yzx,yxz,zxy,zyx
.
We interpret the orientation
xyz
as the closed path
x\to y\to z\to x
in the underlying graph. Given the orientation
\Delta=xyz
, we denote by
\bar{\Delta}
the reverse orientation
xzy
(note that this is indeed the same path traversed backwards). We denote by
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)
the oriented
i
-cells. We use square brackets
[\cdot]
to emphasize the use of an un-oriented cell, though we may abuse notations and remove the square brackets while still referring to the un-oriented versions — it should be clear from context.

Let
\Gamma
be a group,
d\colon\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a bi-invariant metric on
\Gamma
and let
\|\gamma\|=d(\gamma,{\rm Id})
for
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
The
i
-cochains of
\mathcal{X}
with
\Gamma
coefficients
are the anti-symmetric assignments of elements of

i
-cells. Namely,

C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\{\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\to\Gamma\mid\forall c\in\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(i)\colon\alpha(\bar{c})=\alpha(c)^{-1}\}.

Since we did not define orientations on vertices, there are no anti-symmetricity conditions on
0
-cochains. Moreover, for every
2
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(2)\to\Gamma
and triangle
\{x,y,z\}

The
coboundary maps
are defined as follows. For a

is the
1
-cochain

\forall xy\in\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(1)\ \colon\ \ \delta\alpha(xy)=\alpha(x)^{-1}\alpha(y).

For a
1
-cochain
\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(1)\to\Gamma
, its coboundary
\delta\alpha
is the
2
-cochain

\forall xyz\in\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(2)\ \colon\ \ \delta\alpha(xyz)=\alpha(xy)\alpha(yz)\alpha(zx).

Remark 2.1
.

Every
1
-cochain extends naturally to paths in

as follows: If
\pi=x\xrightarrow{e_{1}}...\xrightarrow{e_{\ell}}y
, then
\alpha(\pi)=\alpha(e_{1})\cdot...\cdot\alpha(e_{\ell})
. In this perspective,
\delta\alpha
is the restriction of the extended

.

We assume from now on that any simplicial complex
\mathcal{X}
is given to us with probability distributions

over its oriented

Definition 2.2
.

The collection of probability measures

on the

is said to be

if

is proportional to
\sum_{\tau\supset\sigma}\mu_{i+1}(\tau)
. In this case,
\mu_{2}

\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{0}
.

For any
\alpha

which are
i
-cochains with
\Gamma
coefficients, the

between them is

(2.1)

d(\alpha,\beta)=\Ex_{x\sim\mu_{i}}[d(\alpha(x),\beta(x))].

Given a subset

, we define the distance of

(2.2)

The

\Gamma
coefficients

is its distance to the constant identity cochain, namely

\lVert\alpha\rVert=\Ex_{x\sim\mu_{i}}[\|\alpha(x)\|].

An

is an
i
-cochain

is the constant identity function. We denote the collection of
i
-cocycles by

.
A

. A

if it is in the image of the coboundary operator

. Namely, there exists a
0

. We denote by

the collection of
i
-coboundaries of
\mathcal{X}
. Note that the only indices for which we define

.
We say that the

There is a natural action of

with

(2.4)

\forall\alpha\colon\overrightarrow{\mathcal{X}}(1)\to\Gamma,\ \beta\colon\mathcal{X}(0)\to\Gamma\ \colon\ \ \beta.\alpha(x\xrightarrow{e}y)=\beta(x)^{-1}\alpha(e)\beta(y).

Note that for every path

\beta.\alpha(\pi)=\beta(x)^{-1}\alpha(\pi)\beta(y).

In particular,

Let

A complex is said to be

We call

of

.
The

of a complex

with
\Gamma
coefficients is

(2.8)

h_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\|\delta\alpha\|}{d(\alpha,Z^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma))}\ \middle|\ {\alpha\in C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)},\ \|\delta\alpha\|\neq 0}}\right\}.

Similarly, the
i^{\rm th}

coboundary Cheeger constant
of a complex

with
\Gamma

(2.9)

h^{B}_{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\|\delta\alpha\|}{d(\alpha,B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma))}\ \middle|\ {\alpha\in C^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)}\setminus B^{i}(\mathcal{X},\Gamma)}}\right\}.

-cocycle expander with

, and when

2.2.
Cohomology with permutation coefficients

Though all the cohomological definitions we just listed were defined for a general group

For a positive integer

be the

-cochains with permutation coefficients
, the
i
-cocycles with permutation coefficients
and the

-coboundaries with permutation coefficients
respectively. Thus,

defines a metric on

as in (
2.1
), and henceforth also a norm as in (

). Furthermore, the definitions of cocycle stability and Cheeger constants extend naturally to the permutation coefficicents setup.

2.5.
Local spectral expansion

This section discusses local spectral expansion, which using a local to global result a la Garland
[
17
]
, specifically in the form known as Oppenheim’s trickling down theorem
[
38
]
,
implies global spectral expansion.
We follow
[
12
]
in our presentation — see also Appendix A of
[
21
]
, and the original paper by Oppenheim for the uniform case

38
]
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected
2
-dimensional simplicial complex. Assume
\mu_{2}
is a fully supported distribution on the triangles of
\mathcal{X}
. Furthermore, assume
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{0}
are descendent (as in Definition
2.2
) from

, and are fully supported. This implies in particular that the complex
\mathcal{X}
is
pure
: Every edge (and vertex) participates in some triangle. The link of a cell

is the simplicial complex

(2.11)

\mathcal{X}_{\sigma}=\{\tau\in\mathcal{X}\mid\tau\cup\sigma\in\mathcal{X},\tau\cap\sigma=\emptyset\}.

As this paper focuses on two dimensional complexes, mostly vertex links will be used, which are themselves graphs (and sometimes edge links will be used, which are collections of vertices). The links inherit a distribution on their edges as follows: For every vertex
v
and edge
e\in\mathcal{X}_{v}
, we have
\mu_{v,1}(e)=\frac{\mu_{2}(v\cup e)}{\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{v}(1)}\mu_{2}(v\cup e^{\prime})}
. The measure on vertices of
\mathcal{X}_{v}
descends from
\mu_{v,1}
. Given a simplical complex
\mathcal{X}
, we can define an adjacency operator

on the complex valued functions on its vertices: Given

,

Af(v)=\Ex_{u\sim\mu_{v,0}}[f(u)],

namely, we sample an edge
vu
according to
\mu_{1}
conditioned on the edge containing
v
. Furthermore, we choose the following inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{X}(0)}
:

\langle f,g\rangle=\Ex_{v\sim\mu_{0}}[\overline{f(v)}g(v)].

The adjacency operator
A
has the following properties:

\bullet
The constant functions are eigenfunctions of it with eigenvalue
1
.

\bullet
It is self adjoint with respect to the chosen inner product.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that
\mathcal{X}
is a
\lambda
-spectral expander
if the second eigenvalue of

is bounded by
\lambda
from above. We say that

is a
\lambda
-local spectral expander
if the links

are

-spectral expanders for all

.

[

38
]

The idea is to use a Breadth First Search (BFS) algorithm on a

Recall that in a BFS algorithm, one starts with a fixed vertex, in our case

, as

.
Therefore,

\Ex_{C\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})}[\|\delta f_{C}\|]\geq\sum_{C\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{A})}\mu_{0}(C\cap D)\frac{1-2\lambda}{1-\lambda}=\mu_{0}(D)\frac{1-2\lambda}{1-\lambda}.

All in all,

But this means that the global defect of

∎

6.
The subcritical regime

In this section we study
h_{1}(\mathcal{Y},{\rm Sym})
where
\mathcal{Y}\sim Y(n,p)
and
p=n^{-1-\eta}
for some fixed
\eta>0
. In this regime, the simplicial complex
Y(n,p)
has a well-understood
subcritical
structure: it is comprised of small strongly-connected components, that are collapsible to graphs
[
10
]
.

6.1.
Strongly-connected components, collapsibility, and Cheeger constant

We start by a general study of collapsible strongly-connected components, and their Cheeger constants.

Given a
2
-dimensional simplicial complex
X
, its dual graph is a graph whose vertices are the triangles of
X
, and two triangles are adjacent if they share an edge. The complex
X
is called
strongly-connected
if its dual graph is connected. More generally, a subcomplex of
X
which is comprised of all the triangles in a connected component of the dual graph, and the vertices and edges they contain, is called a strongly-connected component. Clearly, every
2
-complex
X
has an edge-disjoint decomposition to strongly-connected components and isolated edges and vertices.

The Cheeger constant
h_{1}(X,\Gamma)
can be read off the Cheeger constants of the strongly-connected components of
X
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
X
be a
2
-complex and
\mathcal{C}
the set of its strongly-connected components. Then,

h_{1}(X,\Gamma)=\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}}h_{1}(C,\Gamma)\,.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be the measure on
\mathcal{C}
given by sampling a triangle from
X
by the uniform measure
\mu_{2}
and selecting its component. Note that since
\mu_{1}
is descending from
\mu_{2}
then
\mu
is also obtained by sampling an edge by the measure
\mu_{1}
and selecting its component.

For an
i
-cochain
\alpha
of
X
, we denote by
\alpha_{C}
its restriction to a strongly-connected component
C\in\mathcal{C}
. A direct computation yields that

d(\alpha,\beta)=\Ex_{C\sim\mu}[d(\alpha_{C},\beta_{C})]

for every two
i
-cochains
\alpha,\beta
. Therefore,

\|\delta\alpha\|=\Ex_{C\sim\mu}[\|\delta\alpha_{C}\|]
for every
1
-cochain
\alpha
, and, in addition,
d(\alpha,Z^{1}(X,\Gamma))=\Ex_{C\sim\mu}[d(\alpha_{C},Z^{1}(C,\Gamma))]
, since the closest
1
-cocycle to
\alpha
is obtained by choosing the closest
1
-cocycle to
\alpha_{C}
in every component
C
.

In conclusion, for every
1
-cochain
\alpha
,

\|\delta\alpha\|\geq\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}}h_{1}(C,\Gamma)\cdot\Ex_{C\sim\mu}[d(\alpha_{C},Z^{1}(C,\Gamma))]=\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}}h_{1}(C,\Gamma)d(\alpha,Z^{1}(X,\Gamma))]\,,

whence
h_{1}(X,\Gamma)\geq\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}}h_{1}(C,\Gamma).

On the other hand, let
C_{0}
be a component attaining the minimum
h_{1}(C,\Gamma)
, using the
1
-cochain
\alpha_{0}\in C^{1}(C_{0},\Gamma)
. Extend
\alpha_{0}
to a
1
-cochain
\alpha
on
X
by setting
\alpha(e)={\rm Id}
for every
e\notin C_{0}
. Then,

\|\delta\alpha\|=\mu(C_{0})\cdot\|\delta\alpha_{0}\|=\mu(C_{0})\cdot h_{1}(C_{0},\Gamma)\cdot d(\alpha_{0},Z^{1}(C_{0},\Gamma))=h_{1}(C_{0},\Gamma)\cdot d(\alpha,Z^{1}(X,\Gamma))\,,

whence
h_{1}(X,\Gamma)\leq h_{1}(C_{0},\Gamma),
which concludes the proof.
∎

Collapsibility is one of the most well-studied concepts in the theory of random simplicial complexes. An edge
e
in a simplicial complex
X
is called
free
if it is contained in a unique triangle
\Delta
of
X
. In such a case, the removal
X\searrow X\setminus\{e,\Delta\}
of
e
and
\Delta
from
X
is called an elementary collapse. If all the triangles in
X
can be removed in a sequence of elementary collapses, we say that
X
is collapsible to a graph.

Observe that every strongly-connected
2
-complex
C
satisfies
|C(2)|+2\geq|C(0)|
. Indeed, explore its dual graph by some graph-search algorithm. Initially, one triangle and
3
vertices are exposed. Afterwards, in every step the algorithm exposes one additional triangle and at most one new vertex. The following claim discusses the collapsibility of strongly-connected
2
-complexes with a few faces.

Claim 6.2
.

Let
C
be a strongly-connected component satisfying
|C(0)|>|C(2)|
. Then,
C
is collapsible to a graph
G
that intersects the boundary of every triangle in
C
.

Proof.

We construct a sequence of elementary collapses that establishes the claim. Suppose that in graph-search algorithm on the dual-graph of
C
the triangles are exposed in the order
\Delta_{1},...,\Delta_{f}
. The fact that
|C(2)|<|C(0)|
implies that each step exposes a new vertex, except perhaps one step.

Case 1.
If
|C(0)|=|C(2)|+2
then in every step
j
, 2 new edges of
\Delta_{j}
are exposed (in the first step
3
new edges are exposed, but we consider only 2 of them). We arbitrarily select one of them, denote it
e_{j}
, and we denote the other new edge by
\tilde{e}_{j}
. We collapse
C
in the reverse order to which it was exposed, using
e_{j}
as the free edge of
\Delta_{j}
. Note that
\tilde{e}_{j}
has not been collapsed, thus it belongs to the resulting graph
G
, and so
G
intersects the boundary of every triangle
\Delta_{j}
in
C
.

Case 2.
If
|C(0)|=|C(2)|+1
then there exists a unique special step
s
in which no new vertex has been exposed. If
2
new edges have been exposed in this step, then the same construction in Case 1 works. Otherwise, note that
1
new edge have been exposed in step
s
. Indeed, otherwise the three boundary edges of
\Delta_{s}
belong to the subcomplex with triangles
\Delta_{1},...,\Delta_{s-1}
, but this cannot occur since in every step
1\leq j<s
a new vertex is exposed. In such a case, we amend our construction as follows: Start by letting
e_{s}
be the unique newly exposed edge in step
s
, and arbitrarily select
\tilde{e}_{s}
from the other edges in the boundary of
\Delta_{s}
. Then, sequentially, for each
j<s
we select a newly exposed edge in step
j
that is not
\tilde{e}_{s}
as
e_{j}
, and the other newly exposed edge in this step as
\tilde{e}_{j}
. This can always be done since there are two new edges and only one edge,
\tilde{e}_{s}
, we may need to avoid when choosing
e_{j}
. For
j>s
, we select
e_{j},\tilde{e}_{j}
arbitrarily from the new edges in step
j
.

As before, we collapse
C
in the reverse order to which it was exposed, using
e_{j}
as the free edge of
\Delta_{j}
. This is possible since
e_{j}
is a new edge step
j
for every
1\leq j\leq f
. In addition, no
\tilde{e}_{j}
has been collapsed, thusthe resulting graph
G
intersects the boundary of every triangle
\Delta_{j}
in
C
.
∎

We now give a lower bound for the Cheeger constant of such complexes

Lemma 6.3
.

If
C
is a strongly-connected
2
-complex with
f
triangles that is collapsible to a graph that intersects the boundary of every triangle in
C
. Then
h_{1}(C,\Gamma)\geq 1/f\,.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in C^{1}(C,\Gamma)
. Suppose that
C
admits a sequence of elementary collapses

C=C^{(m)}\searrow C^{(m-1)}\searrow\cdots\searrow C^{(1)}\,,

where
C^{(1)}
is a graph that intersects the boundary of every triangle in
C
. We use the collapse sequence to inductively construct a
1
-cocycle
\beta
that is close to
\alpha
. First, for every edge
e\in C^{(1)}
we set
\beta(e)=\alpha(e)
. For every
1<j\leq m
, denote by
e_{j}=xy,\penalty\ \Delta_{j}=xyz
the edge and triangle that are removed in the elementary collapse
C^{(j)}\searrow C^{(j-1)}\,.
Assume, by induction, that
\beta(e)
is already defined for every
e\in C^{(1)}\cup\{e_{2},...,e_{j-1}\}
. In particular,
\beta(xz),\beta(yz)
are already defined, and we define
\beta(xy)=\beta(xz)\beta(zy)
so that
\delta\beta(\Delta_{j})={\rm Id}
. Therefore, by the triangle inequality,

d(\alpha(xy),\beta(xy))\leq d(\alpha(xz),\beta(xz))+d(\alpha(yz),\beta(yz))+d(\delta\alpha(\Delta_{j}),{\rm Id}).

Since
C^{(1)}
intersects the boundary of every triangle in
C
, we assume, without loss of generality, that
xz\in C^{(1)},
hence
\alpha(xz)=\beta(xz)
. Therefore, a straightforward induction gives

d(\alpha(e_{j}),\beta(e_{j}))\leq\sum_{i\geq j}d(\delta\alpha(\Delta_{j}),{\rm Id}).

In consequence, since
\beta\in Z^{1}(C,\Gamma)
, we find

d(\alpha,Z^{1}(C,\Gamma))\leq\sum_{\Delta\in C}d(\delta\alpha(\Delta),{\rm Id})=f\cdot\|\delta\alpha\|\,,

as claimed.
∎

The following example shows that the lower bound from Lemma
6.3
is of the right order.

Example 6.4
.

Let
C
be a
2
-complex comprised of a triangle
xyz
and for each edge of delta,
m
additional triangles (which we call wings) attahced to it:
xyv_{i},xzu_{i},yzw_{i}
for

. In particular,
|C(0)|=3+3m
and
|C(2)|=1+3m
. Let
\Gamma
be a group and
\sigma\in\Gamma
of distance
\varepsilon>0
from
{\rm Id}
. Let
\alpha\in C^{1}(C,\Gamma)
defined by
\alpha(xy)=\alpha(xv_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(xv_{m})=\sigma
and
{\rm Id}
elsewhere. Then,
\delta\alpha(xyz)=\sigma
and

for every other triangle. In consequence,

\|\delta\alpha\|=\varepsilon/(3m+1)\,.

Let

Note that the cycles

6.2.
Subcritical Linial-Meshulam Complexes

Consider
\mathcal{Y}\sim Y(n,p)
, where

for

. In this regime, all the strongly-connected components of

are typically small and structurally simple.

Claim 6.5
.

Fix
\eta>0
, and let
\mathcal{Y}\sim Y(n,p)
, where

. Then, a.a.s.  every strongly-connected component

of
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies

|C(2)|\leq 2/\eta
.

Proof.

Set
f
to be the smallest integer greater than

. Let
C
be a strongly-connected complex with

. explore its dual graph by some graph-search algorithm. Initially, one triangle and
3
vertices are exposed. Afterwards, in every step the algorithm exposes one additional triangle and at most one new vertex. Terminate the search when
f

are exposed. Therefore, the event that
\mathcal{Y}
has a strongly-connected component

with

is contained in the event that some

\mathcal{Y}

\sum_{3\leq v\leq f+2}\binom{n}{v}\binom{\binom{v}{3}}{v}p^{f}=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{f+2-(1+\eta)f}}}\right)\to 0\,,

as

, as claimed.
∎

Claim 6.6
.

Fix
\eta>0
, and let
\mathcal{Y}\sim Y(n,p)
, where

. Then, a.a.s.  every strongly-connected component

of
\mathcal{Y}

|C(0)|>|C(2)|.

Proof.

By the previous claim, we may assume that

. The probability that there exist integers

, and
v
vertices that span

triangles in the

is at most

\sum_{4\leq v\leq f\leq 2/\eta}\binom{n}{v}\binom{\binom{v}{3}}{v}p^{f}=O(n^{-4\eta})\to 0\,,

as

, as claimed.
∎

These two claims will let us prove a lower bound for

. For the upper bound we will show that
\mathcal{Y}
a.a.s. contains the strongly-connected component that appears in Example

Claim 6.7
.

Fix

, and let
\mathcal{Y}\sim Y(n,p)
, where

. Then, a.a.s.
\mathcal{Y}
has a strongly-connected component that is isomorphic to the complex

in Example

…0

Proof.

There are

different subcomplexes that are isomorphic to

. Denote them by

and let

be the event that

is a strongly-connected component of
\mathcal{Y}
. Therefore, the expectation of the number

of strongly-connected components isomorphic to

is at least

\Ex[X]\geq Np^{3m+1}(1-p)^{(6m+3)n}\,.

since, for each

, each of the

edges in

is contained in most

so that the

and triangle disjoint, and therefore

We obtain that

By Checbyshev’s inequality,

\Pro(X=0)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\Ex[X]^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\Ex[X]}+(1-p)^{-2(6m+3)n}-1\,.

Note that

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We start with the lower bound. Let

For the upper bound, Lemma
