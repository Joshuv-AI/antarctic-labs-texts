Genome scale CRISPRi reveals both shared and strain-specific vulnerabilities in genetically diverse drug-resistant strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: XinYue Wang, William J Jowsey, Chen-Yi Cheung, Nina Dickerhof, Cassandra L Chapman, Jamie R H Taka, Mark B Hampton, Ghader Bashiri, Paul P Gardner, Peter C Fineran, Gregory M Cook, Simon A Jackson, Matthew B McNeil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global health burden caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis is aggravated by the emergence and spread of drug resistance. Mutations that cause drug resistance can have collateral effects that increase the vulnerability of downstream pathways to inhibition. Here, using genome scale CRISPR interference we identified collateral effects associated with different drug-resistant genotypes of M. tuberculosis. We demonstrate that drug resistance generated shared vulnerabilities in several overlapping functional pathways. Most drug-resistant strains were more sensitive to tRNA synthetase knockdowns than the parental drug-sensitive strain, highlighting the potential of tRNA synthetases as high-value drug targets. Additionally, the rifampicin-resistant mutant RpoB(S450L) had increased sensitivity to the dysregulation of sulphur metabolism due to transcriptional dysregulation. This increased vulnerability did not translate to all rpoB genotypes but was linked to predicted effects on transcriptional dynamics. Amongst clinical isolates, non-synonymous mutations in sulphur metabolism genes have evolved in a geographic lineage specific manner to mitigate fitness costs associated with the collateral phenotypes of drug resistance. Combined, our findings highlight the power of functional genomics in pinpointing highly vulnerable drug targets across drug-resistant strains.Subject terms: Microbial genetics, Pathogens, Antibacterial drug resistance

Introduction

Drug-resistant (DR) strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis are a major challenge to global efforts to control Tuberculosis (TB)1,2. While first-line treatments such as isoniazid (INH) and rifampicin (RIF) have historically been effective, mutations conferring drug resistance have severely compromised therapeutic efficacy3,4. Drug resistance often arises through mutations in core biological pathways. Such mutations can impose fitness costs and create collateral vulnerabilities in which different genes or pathways become more important for the growth and survival of resistant strains5–11. High-throughput and systematic approaches to identifying collateral vulnerabilities have provided fundamental insights into the biology and evolutionary trajectories of drug resistance. Yet whether vulnerabilities extend to diverse DR genotypes and even phenotypes remains poorly understood, limiting the potential of collateral vulnerabilities to inform new therapeutic strategies.

Here, we used comparative genome-scale CRISPR interference (GS-CRISPRi) transcriptional knockdowns to identify collateral vulnerabilities that are either unique or shared between genetically and phenotypically distinct DR strains of M. tuberculosis. We focused on isogenic yet clinically relevant DR genotypes: a strain resistant to the front-line drug rifampicin (RpoBS450L, RIFR-RpoB); a levofloxacin (LEV)-resistant strain (GyrAD94Y, LEVR-GyrA); a linezolid (LZD)-resistant strain (RplCC154R, LZDR-RplC); and two strains that are resistant to bedaquiline (BDQ), which is a part of the newly FDA-approved all oral treatment (AtpEA63P, BDQR-AtpE and Rv0678G65GfsX10, BDQR-Rv0678). With the exception of the loss-of-function mutation in the transcriptional regulator Rv0678, all of these mutations are in core biological pathways, including DNA replication (gyrA), transcription (rpoB), translation (rplC) and energy generation (atpE).

By using GS-CRISPRi we have identified vulnerabilities that are shared amongst the majority, and in some cases all DR strains. These include tRNA synthetases, for which there are several preclinical drug candidates reinforcing their clinical potential as part of future TB-therapies. Furthermore, sulphur metabolism was uniquely vulnerable to inhibition in RIFR-RpoB, which is linked to predicted effects of rpoB mutations on transcriptional dynamics. Amongst clinical isolates, we observed that mutations in sulphur metabolism genes have evolved in both a geographic lineage-specific manner to limit fitness costs associated with cysteine limitation and as a potential compensatory strategy to overcome collateral phenotypes of drug resistance. By building upon prior GS-CRISPRi screens in drug susceptible and resistant (i.e. isoniazid-katG null and rifampicin-RpoBS450L) strains of M. tuberculosis5,8,12, this work provides fundamental insights into how drug resistance simultaneously changes cellular physiology and produces druggable vulnerabilities in M. tuberculosis that could drive the development of new therapeutic regimens.

Results

Genome-scale CRISPRi screening across diverse DR genotypes

We hypothesised that DR mutations in unique, yet core, biological pathways would have global effects on cellular physiology with overlapping collateral vulnerabilities. To investigate this, we applied the GS-CRISPRi platform to five DR strains of M. tuberculosis (Fig. 1A, Table 1)5. Four strains harboured clinically relevant mutations in antibiotic targets in core biological pathways (e.g. RIFR-RpoBS450L, LZDR-RplCC154R, LEVR-GyrAD94Y and BDQR-AtpEA63P). Whilst ATP synthase is the target of BDQ, clinical resistance is predominantly observed via inactivation of the transcriptional regulator Rv06787,13–15. Therefore, we also included an Rv0678 mutant, BDQR-Rv0678G65GfsX10. All strains are isogenic, being previously isolated in the avirulent auxotrophic strain of M. tuberculosis strain mc262067. Each GS-CRISPRi experiment involved screening a 22,996 guide RNA (gRNA) library that targets 3,991 distinct protein-coding genes. CRISPRi-mediated knockdown was conducted using 300 ng/ml of the inducer anhydrotetracycline (ATc). DNA was extracted on days 5, 10, and 14 for library preparation and amplicon sequencing. Three independent GS-CRISPRi experiments were performed in parallel, each including a drug-susceptible (DS) parent as an internal control. Due to the inherent technical variability of pooled CRISPRi screens16–19, we implemented a normalisation workflow to allow comparisons between experiments. After normalisation, a total of 2492, 2462, and 2467 gRNAs were identified as being depleted in the DS parent across the three screens (Supplementary Data 1). This represents 531–564 essential genes in the DS parent (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 2), consistent with a prior screen of the DS parent which identified 540 essential genes5. The essentiality calls for these DS parents demonstrated 92.3%–93.4% agreement with a previous GS-CRISPRi screen12, consistent with our previous screen of the DS parent, which showed 93.3% agreement5.

To identify collateral vulnerabilities across DR strains, we compared gRNA depletion profiles between the DR strains and the DS parent using day 14 data. Genes were classified as being more vulnerable if (i) the gene was essential in the DR strain and (ii) had ≥ 2 gRNAs that were depleted by at least two-fold more than in the DS-parent5. We identified 278, 272, 245, 205, and 137 genes that were more vulnerable in BDQR-AtpE, RIFR-RpoB, BDQR-Rv0678, LEVR-GyrA, and LZDR-RplC, respectively, relative to their DS parent (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 3). Genes involved in protein synthesis, nucleotide metabolism, respiration and amino acid metabolism were consistently more sensitive to inhibition across all DR strains (Fig. 1C). However, there was variation in the order of the most sensitive pathways, with protein synthesis being the most vulnerable in BDQR-Rv0678, LEVR-GyrA and LZDR-RplC, while nucleotide metabolism was the most vulnerable in BDQR-AtpE and amino acid metabolism the most vulnerable in RIFR-RpoB (Fig. 1C). In conclusion, GS-CRISPRi can identify differences in genetic essentiality across distinct DR strains of M. tuberculosis.

GS-CRISPRi identifies shared vulnerabilities among drug-resistant genotypes

To identify unique and shared vulnerabilities we examined the distribution of genetic vulnerabilities across the five DR M. tuberculosis genotypes (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Data 3). A total of 254 genes were identified as being strain-specific (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Data 4). RIFR-RpoB contained the greatest number of strain-specific vulnerabilities, with 91 genes more susceptible to inhibition followed by BDQR-AtpE with 71 genes (Fig. 2A). Amongst the shared vulnerabilities 117, 37 and 12 genes were identified as being more vulnerable to inhibition in three, four and all five DR strains respectively (Fig. 2A). The twelve vulnerabilities common to all DR strains were all essential genes in M. tuberculosis12, suggesting that these genes had increased importance for the growth or survival of DR strains (Table 2). Three universally shared vulnerabilities were involved in protein synthesis: encoding aspartate aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (aspS), a large ribosomal subunit (rplQ), and elongation factor (tsf). In addition to aspartyl-tRNA synthetase, which was a universally shared vulnerability among all five DR strains, several other aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, except for argS and tyrS, were found to be more vulnerable to inhibition in two or more DR strains (Fig. 2B). As aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases catalyse the attachment of amino acids to tRNA during protein synthesis it suggests that these proteins are more important for maintaining translational fidelity in DR strains compared to a DS parent. In conclusion, GS-CRISPRi can identify shared genetic vulnerabilities across genetically and phenotypically diverse DR strains of M. tuberculosis.

Mutations in antibiotic targets compromise function at the whole-cell level

The increased sensitivity of LEVR-GyrA to inhibition of gyrA and LZDR-RplC to inhibition of genes involved in protein synthesis suggested that resistance mutations compromise the function of target enzymes. We hypothesised that, for this principle of compromised function to apply to other resistance mutations, RIFR-RpoB and BDQR-AtpE should be more sensitive to inhibition of RNA polymerase and ATP synthase, respectively. In agreement with this, as RpoBS450L leads to impaired RNA-polymerase function multiple RNA polymerase genes (rpoA, rpoB, and rpoZ) were more vulnerable to transcriptional repression in RIFR-RpoB (Fig. 3A). This observation is consistent with recent data showing that RpoBS450L mutants in M. tuberculosis have increased sensitivity to non-rifampicin-based inhibitors of RNA polymerase20. GS-CRISPRi screening also demonstrated that multiple ATP synthase genes (atpB, atpC, atpD, atpG, atpH) were more vulnerable to transcriptional repression in the BDQR-AtpE, but not BDQR-Rv0678 mutant (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Data 3). The genes atpE, atpA and atpF were maximally depleted by day 14, and unable to be used to discern differences in vulnerability (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Data 1)7. Consistent with screening data, BDQR-AtpE was more sensitive to transcriptional inhibition of atpF in ATc dose response assays, requiring less ATc to inhibit growth (Fig. 3C). We hypothesised that if BDQR-AtpE had impaired ATP synthase activity, there would be a downturn in consumption of protons, leading to an increase in the membrane potential component of the proton motive force (PMF)21. Consistent with this, BDQR-AtpE had increased membrane potential when quantitatively measured using radiolabelled probes (Fig. 3D). Clinical resistance to BDQ is predominantly observed through inactivation of the transcriptional regulator Rv0678 and upregulation of the efflux pump MmpS5L5. MmpS5L5 is a resistance-nodulation-division transporter and, like ATP synthase, consumes PMF22. Consequently, and in contrast to BDQR-AtpE, the BDQR-Rv0678 strain that overexpresses MmpS5L5 showed reduced membrane potential (Fig. 3D). Combined, this demonstrates how distinct bedaquiline resistance mechanisms have opposing effects on membrane bioenergetics. In conclusion, these combined results demonstrate how resistance mutations in antibiotic targets compromise protein function and make them more sensitive to targeted inhibition23.

RIFR-RpoB is more vulnerable to the loss of sulphur metabolism

The RIFR-RpoB mutant (here after referred to as RIFR-RpoBS450L) exhibited the highest proportion of amino acid metabolism genes with increased vulnerability to transcriptional inhibition (Fig. 1C). Specifically, this related to genes involved in lysine, threonine, methionine, and cysteine biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 4A). In M. tuberculosis sulphate assimilation is necessary for the synthesis of multiple biological products including the amino acids cysteine and methionine, the redox thiols mycothiol and ergothioneine and sulfolipids (Fig. 4B). Sulphate is imported via the ABC transporter SubI-CysTWA1 (Fig. 4B), with gRNAs targeting all four transporter-encoding genes being significantly depleted in RIFR-RpoBS450L (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 1). In addition, gRNAs targeting downstream steps in the conversion of inorganic sulphate into cysteine (cysD, cysNC and cysH) were also more vulnerable to transcriptional inhibition (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 1). Consistent with our data, a prior CRISPRi screen had identified increased sensitivity to the loss sulphate metabolism in a RpoBS450L mutant8. Validating GS-CRISPRi, individual gRNAs targeting the ABC transporter (i.e. cysT) and the adenosine-5′-phosphosulphate (APS) reductase (i.e. cysH) both had a greater growth impairment against RIFR-RpoBS450L (Fig. 4C–F). Exogenous cysteine supplementation was able to complement the growth impairment caused by repression of cysH and cysT in the DS-parent and RIFR- RpoBS450L mutant in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating that this fitness cost is due to depleted cysteine synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D).

The RpoBS450L mutation is the most prevalent (~64%) among RIFR clinical isolates24. Other RpoB mutations, such as RpoBS441L and RpoBH445Y, though less common, are also associated with RIFR24. To investigate if vulnerability to impaired sulphate metabolism extended to other RpoB mutants, we examined the sensitivity of two additional clinically-relevant RpoB RIF mutants (RIFR-RpoBS441L, RIFR-RpoBH445Y) and a RpoB fidaxomicin(FIX)R-RpoBQ1080R mutant to transcriptional inhibition of sulphate metabolism. Consistent with RIFR-RpoBS450L the RIFR-RpoBS441L mutant was more sensitive to transcriptional inhibition of sulphate metabolism (Fig. 4C, E). In contrast, RIFR-RpoBH445Y and FIXR-RpoBQ1080R were insensitive, with inhibition of cysT or cysH causing comparable fitness defects to the DS parent (Fig. 4D, F). A loss of CRISPRi repression is unlikely to be responsible for variable levels of vulnerability between rpoB mutants, as CRISPRi repression is maintained during growth assays as detected by RT-qPCR and the repeated addition of ATc did not alter fitness costs of either gRNA against the DS-parent or RpoBS450L mutant (Supplementary Fig. 2E-H). In conclusion, RpoB rifampicin-resistant mutants have altered vulnerability to the inhibition of sulphate metabolism, with increased vulnerability being RpoB genotype-dependent.

Cysteine metabolism genes are dysregulated in RIFR-RpoBS450L

Transcriptional attenuation is a regulatory strategy in bacteria where the availability of a metabolite influences the expression of genes responsible for its biosynthesis25–27. Transcriptional attenuation is facilitated by short open reading frames (sORF) found within 5′ untranslated regions (UTR) upstream of many metabolic genes25–27. For amino acid synthesis genes, when there are sufficient concentrations of amino acids, ribosomes translate through poly-amino acid sequences of sORF, leading to the formation of a transcriptional terminator in the 5′UTR that inhibits transcription of downstream genes. Under conditions of amino acid starvation, insufficient levels of charged tRNA lead to ribosomal stalling at poly amino-acid sequences, allowing for the formation of an anti-terminator and the transcription of downstream biosynthetic genes. Several cysteine biosynthesis genes that were more vulnerable to inhibition in RIFR-RpoB have a 5′UTR preceded by a predicted sORF that contains poly cysteine (poly-C) sequences (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. S2)28. The methionine biosynthesis gene metE that was more vulnerable in RIFR-RpoB also has sORF and a cobalamin-dependent riboswitch to regulate gene expression29.

RpoBS450L reduces RNA polymerase elongation rates and increases termination at regulatory RNA sequences23,30. Conversely, RpoBH445Y, which was less vulnerable to inhibition of cysteine biosynthesis is transcriptionally faster with reduced termination rates23. We hypothesised that the differential vulnerability of RpoB mutants to inhibition of sulphur metabolism was due to either altered basal transcription levels or differences in transcriptional responses following cysteine depletion. To test this we investigated the expression of cysteine metabolism genes that contained poly-C sORF. Under conditions of sufficient cysteine (i.e. expression of a non-targeting gRNA), genes that contained long poly-C sequences (mshA, cysA2 and cysK1) had comparable expression levels between RpoB mutants and the DS-parent (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 2). Conversely, genes with short or no predicted poly-C sequences (i.e. cysT, subI, cysD) were expressed to lower levels in the vulnerable RIFR-RpoBS450L and expressed to higher levels in the insensitive RIFR-RpoBH445Y and FIXR-RpoBQ1080R (Fig. 5B, C and Supplementary Fig. S2). Under conditions of cysteine depletion (i.e. expression of a cysT or cysH targeting gRNA) many genes with poly-C sequences (i.e. subI, cysA2, cysT, and cysK1) were upregulated in the DS-parent, supporting the role of cysteine in regulating the transcription of these genes (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. S2). In many instances the upregulation of cysteine metabolism genes including cysA2, subI, mshA, cysT and cysK1 was greater in RIFR-RpoBS450L compared to the DS parent (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. S2). The cysteine metabolism genes subI, cysT, cysK1, and mshA were also upregulated more in the invulnerable RpoBH445Y and RpoBQ1080R mutants compared to the DS parent, although it was variable depending on the specific RpoB mutant and gRNA used (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. S2). The cysD gene was not upregulated in any strain, supporting the prediction of no cysteine responsive element (Fig. 5B).

To investigate if altered transcriptional responses translated to cellular effects, we measured the intracellular levels of cysteine and the cysteine-containing low molecular weight thiols (LMWT) mycothiol, γ-glutamyl-cysteine and ergothioneine in the DS parent and RIFR-RpoBS450L following inhibition of cysT or cysH (Fig. 5D). When expressing a non-targeting (NT) gRNA, the levels of cysteine and LMWTs were comparable between RIFR-RpoBS450L and the DS parent (Fig. 5E–H)31–34. Depletion of either cysT or cysH decreased cysteine and LMWTs levels in the DS parent (Fig. 5E–H). Depletion of cysT or cysH had a greater effect on cysteine synthesis in RIFR-RpoBS450L (i.e. 67 and 66% decreased compared to 49 and 59% for the DS parent), with cysteine depleted to the limit of detection in the majority of RIFR-RpoBS450L replicates (Fig. 5C). RIFR-RpoBS450L also had greater depletions of γ-glutamyl-cysteine (96 and 95% reduction vs 55 and 84% for DS- parent) and mycothiol (91 and 88% reduction vs 45 and 70% for the DS parent) following inhibition of cysT or cysH (Fig. 5D, F). Ergothioneine was substantially depleted in both strains (Fig. 5E). LMWTs such as mycothiol and ergothioneine typically accumulate during exponential growth32. Whilst RIFR-RpoBS450L has a greater fitness cost following repression of cysT and cysH we hypothesise that this is not a confounding factor as we observed selective differences, with some LMWTs being more affected than others. In conclusion, the differential expression of key biosynthetic genes, and responses to cysteine limitation influence the vulnerability of RpoB mutants to the inhibition of sulphur metabolism.

Sulphur metabolism influences in-host evolution of RIFR isolates

Secondary mutations in RpoA and RpoC restore impaired RNA polymerase (RNAP) activity in RIFR clinical isolates35–38. Mutations in nusG have also been identified as a compensatory mechanism in RIFR that reduces excessive RNAP pausing and termination8. We hypothesised that the increased vulnerability of sulphur metabolism in RIFR-RpoBS450L would select for compensatory mutations in sulphur metabolism genes to overcome the downstream metabolic consequences of impaired RNAP function, or select against mutations that exacerbate this vulnerability. To investigate this, we compared changes in non-synonymous mutation rates between drug-sensitive and RIFR populations39. We used this approach as the low numbers of mutations in some genes precluded analysis based on ratio of non-synonymous (dN) to synonymous (dS) substitution rates (dN/dS). We focused on clinical isolates from the CRyPTIC consortium, a publicly available collection of >10 K clinical isolates with matched whole genome sequencing and phenotypic resistance profiles40,41. This dataset was filtered to remove redundancy while minimising the loss of phylogenetic diversity amongst the RIFR and DS populations (Scripting parameter: RTL = 0.97). We analysed all rpoB genotypes from this database, including both RIF mono-resistant and RIF multidrug-resistant strains. Overall, there was a higher occurrence of non-synonymous mutations in both RpoA and RpoC amongst RIFR isolates, consistent with the selection for compensatory mutations (Fig. 6A)35–37. We also observed an increase in non-synonymous mutations in both subI and cysA1 that encode components of the sulphate ABC importer, sirA and cysD that are enzymatic steps in the conversion of sulphate into cysteine and in metE that converts cysteine into methionine (Fig. 6A, B and Supplementary Fig. 3A–E). We also observed a reduction in non-synonymous mutations in cysK1, as well as mshC and mshD, two genes required for the synthesis of the LWMT mycothiol (Fig. 6A, B and Supplementary Fig. 3A–E). To further understand the influence of collateral vulnerabilities on mycobacterial evolution we searched for SNPs that were over-represented, and potentially fixed, in RIFR isolates (Fig. 6A, B and Supplementary Fig. 3F–K). With the exception of rpoC mutations, a SubI A76T mutation was the most over-represented in RIFR isolates (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Data 6). Whilst not a homoplastic variant, it was found in 57% of RIFR isolates compared to 35% of DS strains. Within the M. tuberculosis complex, geographic lineages have evolved in a clonal manner, allowing mutations to evolve independently or exclusively within lineages. When assessing the distribution of SubIA76T we observed that it was exclusively present in lineage 2 and lineage 3 isolates within the CRyPTIC database (Fig. 6C, D). The enrichment of A76T in RIFR isolates is likely a result of drug-sensitive strains from lineage 4 accounting for approximately 33% of the analysed CRyPTIC database isolates (Fig. 6C). We hypothesised that the lack of SubIA76T variants outside lineage 2 and 3 was because SubIA76T imposed fitness costs on non-lineage 2 or 3 strains. To test this, we engineered SubIA76T into the DS-parent and RIFR-RpoBS450L mutant in a M. tuberculosis mc26206 background that is derived from H37Rv (i.e., a lineage 4 derivative). We reproducibly observed that the SubIA76T mutation in the DS-parent increased vulnerability to the transcriptional inhibition of cysT and cysH (Fig. 6E–H and Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Conversely, the SubIA76T mutation in RIFR-RpoBS450L had no reproducible effect on vulnerability to the transcriptional inhibition of cysT or cysH (Fig. 6E–H and Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). The SubIA76T mutation had no impact on the growth of either strain in the absence of gRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 4A). As SubI is the substrate-binding protein of the CysTWA1SubI importer we hypothesised that this variant impacted the binding and delivery of sulphate to the import apparatus. However, A76 is distal to both putative ligand and complex interaction sites and did not alter the affinity of SubI for sulphate, with both WT and A76T variants having similar dissociation constants (Fig. 6I and Supplementary Fig. 4D). In conclusion, the increased vulnerability of sulphate metabolism in RIFR isolates influences the within host evolution of M. tuberculosis by potentially selecting for adaptative mutations to overcome metabolic vulnerabilities. Furthermore, the evolution of lineage-specific mutations in sulphur metabolism genes is the result of lineage-specific metabolic characteristics and variation in the presentation of fitness costs.

Discussion

Fitness costs associated with drug resistance produce collateral effects on cellular physiology that result in other pathways becoming more vulnerable to inhibition. Here, by using GS-CRISPRi we demonstrated that different drug resistance mutations in M. tuberculosis generate shared vulnerabilities that represent high-value targets for combating antimicrobial resistance. We further show how strain specific vulnerabilities in some RIFR mutants is driven by dysregulated expression of metabolic genes in response to nutrient limitation.

We identified hundreds of genes more vulnerable to inhibition in DR strains than an isogenic DS parent strain. Most shared vulnerabilities were related to protein synthesis, nucleotide metabolism, respiration and amino acid metabolism. These shared vulnerabilities, consisting of core biological processes, suggests that resistance-conferring mutations, despite their mechanistic diversity, impose common burdens. Amino-acyl tRNA synthetases that we identified as having shared vulnerabilities across multiple DR strains have previously been identified as highly vulnerable genes in M. tuberculosis42. Consistent with our observation that lysS was more vulnerable to transcriptional inhibition in DR strains, the Lysyl-tRNA synthetase inhibitor DDD02049209 has increased potency against DR M. tuberculosis clinical isolates relative to a DS strain43. This demonstrates that amino-acyl tRNA synthetase vulnerability extends beyond aspS and more importantly into clinical isolates. The leucyl-tRNA synthetase inhibitor ganfeborole has also advanced to clinical evaluation, although whether it is more active against DR strains requires testing12,44. We hypothesise that as each drug-resistant strain has a mutation likely to impact DNA replication/unwinding (gyrA), transcription (rpoB) or translation (rplC), the shared vulnerability with amino-acyl tRNA synthetases is a result of the major role that transcription-translation coupling and ribosomal pausing play in controlling mycobacterial gene expression. Whilst ATP synthase is not directly involved in transcription-translation coupling, the loss of cellular ATP is likely to affect these pathways. Combined, these results highlight the potential clinical impact of novel aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase inhibitors in combating mycobacterial drug resistance.

Resistance to bedaquiline is provided by direct and indirect resistance mechanisms45. A better understanding of both is needed to combat rising resistance levels. BDQR-AtpE was more sensitive to the inhibition of ATP synthase subunits (e.g. atpB, atpC, atpD, atpG, atpH), highlighting that impaired ATP synthase in BDQR-AtpE makes it more sensitive to disruptions in energy production. BDQR-AtpE also had increased vulnerability to inhibition of icl1 and glnA1, both of which are consistent with bedaquiline rerouting carbon through the glyoxylate shunt and having collateral effects on glutamine synthetase activity44,46,47. Furthermore, the increased vulnerability of purine biosynthesis (e.g. purCDFHKNQ) in BDQR-AtpE suggests that impaired energy production increases the vulnerability of energy-intensive biochemical pathways, including nucleotide metabolism and protein synthesis47. These broad effects on cell physiology are potentially why bedaquiline has synergistic interactions with a wide range of other chemical inhibitors, many with distinct mechanisms of action48–52. Moreover, unique BDQ-resistance mutations can have contrasting effects on membrane bioenergetics. Mutations that reduce the efficacy of the ATP synthase increased PMF, whereas mutations that upregulate the efflux pump MmpS5L5, being the most clinically relevant24, reduce PMF. Harnessing these unique differences may identify novel strategies for selecting against the emergence of bedaquiline resistance.

The RpoBS450L mutation alters the structure of RNA polymerase β subunit, leading to reduced transcriptional speed and increased termination rates23,53. RpoBS450L is the dominant mutation in clinical isolates of RIFR M. tuberculosis, suggesting that it has the lowest fitness cost compared to other clinically relevant RIFR RpoB mutations24,54. Here, we identified that RIFR-RpoBS450L was more vulnerable to the inhibition of genes involved in sulphate metabolism. This observation is consistent with prior reports of increased sulphate sensitivity in a RpoBS450L mutant8. These results are also in line with prior CRISPRi screens reporting that the loss of sulphate import increased sensitivity of M. tuberculosis to rifampicin, whilst prior TnSeq experiments have identified cysteine (cysA1, cysT, cysW, subI, cysN) and mycothiol (mshA, mshC) biosynthetic genes as being more essential in drug-susceptible clinical isolates of M. tuberculosis compared to the H37Rv laboratory strains55. There are also numerous reported links between RNA polymerase function, cysteine biosynthesis and nutrient limitation including (i) mutations in RpoB upregulating cysteine biosynthesis genes (e.g. cysE, and cysM) in Escherichia coli56, (ii) the loss of a serine/threonine protein kinase (STPK) upregulating genes associated with sulphur metabolism and a decrease in rifampicin susceptibility in Brucella melitensis57, (iii) genes involved in sulphur import and assimilation being upregulated in response to redox stress, with a cysH deletion mutant being more sensitive to redox stress in mycobacteria and (iv) the S534L substitution in RpoB improving growth under nutrient limitation in Myxococcus xanthus58. Rifampicin-resistant mutants of M. tuberculosis also have reduced levels of sulfoglycolipids, a downstream product of sulphate metabolism59. In our current study, the RIFR-RpoBS450L mutant was able to maintain the synthesis of cysteine and the LWMTs mycothiol, ergothioneine and γ-glutamyl-cysteine when grown in nutrient-rich media. However, under conditions of cysteine limitation (i.e., transcriptional inhibition of cysT or cysH), greater growth impairment and greater reductions in intracellular levels of cysteine and LMWTs were observed in RIFR-RpoBS450L. In conclusion, RIFR-RpoBS450L mutants in M. tuberculosis have an impaired ability to upregulate the synthesis of intracellular cysteine in response to cysteine limitation.

Many genes involved in cysteine biosynthesis are under the regulatory control of transcriptional attenuators that respond to cysteine availability28,60. RpoB mutants that have reported anti-termination or fast RNA-polymerase elongation rates (i.e. RpoBH445Y) were insensitive to cysteine depletion, over expressed cysteine biosynthetic genes and had greater upregulation in response to cysteine starvation. Conversely RpoB mutants that have reported pro-pausing or slow elongation rates (i.e. RpoBS450L) were vulnerable to cysteine depletion and under expressed key biosynthetic genes. We hypothesise that the greater upregulation of cysteine-responsive genes following transcriptional repression of cysT or cysH in the vulnerable RpoBS450L mutant compared to the DS-parent was due to increased cysteine starvation, as observed by mass spectrometry, inducing greater transcriptional upregulation. Conversely, the increased upregulation in RpoBH445Y was due to reduced transcriptional attenuation in these genotypes. Transcriptional attenuation, facilitated by sORF found within 5′ UTR of many metabolic genes, is a widespread regulatory strategy to control the expression of metabolic genes in response to metabolite availability. Whilst further investigations are required, in our current study cysteine biosynthesis genes with either no or short poly-C sORF were the most dysregulated between RpoB mutants suggestive of a link between the strength of transcriptional attenuation, dynamics of ribosomal pausing and metabolic perturbation. Conversely, the longer the poly-C sequence, the tighter the coupling between expression and intracellular cysteine levels, irrespective of RpoB genotype. Whilst our current study has focused on cysteine biosynthesis genes, because transcriptional attenuators control the expression of many metabolic pathways the transcriptional defects of RpoBS450L are likely to extend beyond cysteine biosynthesis possibly to ileS, trpE, trpC and glnA1 each of which have long 5′ UTR and were more vulnerable to inhibition in RpoBS450L. In conclusion, transcriptional defects in RpoB result in impaired expression of metabolic genes and the ability to respond to nutrient limitation.

RpoBS450L is the fittest rifampicin-resistant clinical isolate yet also has high rates of compensatory RpoC mutations in clinical settings, in many cases up to 80% of all samples2,38,54,61,62. Many other RIFR genotypes, including RpoBH445Y, that are of reduced clinical prevalence have a proposed higher fitness cost yet paradoxically have lower rates of compensatory mutations63. Whilst there are multiple possible explanations for this discrepancy, we propose that the increased vulnerability of RpoBS450L to nutritional limitation has profound effects within a nutritionally limited host environment. Specifically, anti-termination or fast RNA-polymerase elongation rate mutants would prime or increase the ability of M. tuberculosis to respond to nutrient limitation whilst pro-pausing or slow RNA-polymerase elongation mutants are under prepared or have a reduced ability to respond to nutrient limitation. This would generate a strong selective pressure for slow mutants (i.e., RpoBS450L)) to evolve compensatory mutations that repair RNAP function. Whilst fast mutants that overestimate nutritional limitation (i.e. RpoBH445Y) expend resources synthesising products not needed under nutrient rich conditions resulting in a greater fitness cost. Yet, these fast mutants are “primed” for nutritional limitation resulting in reduced selective pressure to evolve compensatory mutations under changing environmental conditions. Further work is required to investigate this hypothesis.

The collateral effects of drug resistance have important evolutionary consequences. Compensatory fitness mutations typically restore the underlying functional impairment64. For RIFR RpoB mutants’ compensatory fitness mutations typically occur via adaptive mutations in RNA polymerase (i.e. rpoABC) or in functionally related transcription-translation proteins (i.e. nusG). The dominance of compensatory mutations in rpoABC over nusG suggest a hierarchy of compensation, in which the level of fitness benefit provided results in a stronger selective advantage leading to higher abundance in clinical isolates. Adding to this prior knowledge our data suggests that collateral vulnerabilities also select for potentially adaptive mutations in genes that restore the metabolic dysregulation (i.e sulphur metabolism genes) without correcting the primary cause of this phenotype (i.e. RNA-polymerase dysfunction). The fitness benefit provided by these compensatory mutations is likely to be lower than rpoC mutations leading to reduced prevalence in clinical samples relative to rpoC compensatory mutations. Supporting this hypothesis, multiple metabolic genes including cysN have recently been identified as being under positive selection in a cohort of clinical isolates of M. tuberculosis from Moldova65. Further functional experimentation and longitudinal data from clinical isolates is required to investigate this hypothesis.

The clonal evolution of the M. tuberculosis complex into distinct geographic lineages has resulted in mutations becoming fixed to specific lineages. These phylogenetic mutations are useful barcoding tools and prior systems biology studies have shown that they can have functional influences on lineage-specific metabolic characteristics66. However, outside of drug resistance the influence of these phylogenetic mutations on mycobacterial physiology have been largely ignored. Here we demonstrate that the SubIA76T variant has not evolved in lineage 4 strains as it imposes fitness costs on M. tuberculosis specifically in response to cysteine limitation. Additional validation is required to demonstrate that the SubIA76T variant does not impose a fitness cost in lineage 2 or 3 strains. This highlights the need to investigate the influence of these natural genetic variants on the physiology of different M. tuberculosis lineages and their possible influence on treatment, evolution and transmission.

In conclusion, by identifying shared and unique metabolic vulnerabilities this study advances our understanding of the fitness costs and compensatory strategies in DR M. tuberculosis. These insights pave the way for targeted and broad-spectrum therapies that exploit the metabolic vulnerabilities of DR strains, improve treatment outcomes and can combat the growing threat of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. For example, recent reports in M. tuberculosis have demonstrated that the deletion of the sulphate SubI-CysTWA1 has significant effects on mycobacterial survival in murine infection models67. Not only does this demonstrate that SubI-CysTWA1 is a promising therapeutic target, but that targeting sulphate uptake could be used to eliminate RIF-resistant mutants.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The attenuated avirulent M. tuberculosis strain mc26206 (H37Rv derived, ΔpanCD, ΔleuCD) and DR derivatives used in this study are listed in Table 1. All strains were grown and maintained at 37 °C in Middlebrook 7H9 liquid medium or on 7H11 solid medium supplemented with OADC (0.005% oleic acid, 0.5% BSA (sigma A-7906), dextrose (0.2% v/v), 0.085% catalase), pantothenic acid (25 µg/mL), leucine (50 µg/mL), and kanamycin (20 µg/mL). Tyloxapol (0.05% v/v) was supplemented to liquid medium only. Escherichia coli strain MC1061 was used for the cloning of CRISPRi plasmids. E. coli MC1061 was grown at 37 °C in LB or on 1.5% LB-agar supplemented with kanamycin at 50 µg/ml.

Pooled M. tuberculosis CRISPRi library construction and genome-wide CRISPRi screening

The pooled CRISPRi library was constructed using established methods5. Briefly, 100 ml of glycerol stock of each strain was inoculated to 10 ml of 7H9 media individually and grown in a T25 flask until confluent. Twelve replicates per strain were then made, each with 100 ml of the outgrowth sub-cultured into 10 ml 7H9 media until an OD600 of 0.4, then supplemented with 2 M glycine and grown overnight. All cultures were then harvested in three equal volumes and washed with 10% glycerol. The washed cell pellets were then resuspended, electroporated, and incubated in 10 ml 7H9 at 37 °C for recovery. All recovered transformants were then harvested and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.4 in 7H9 media with 20 µg/mL kanamycin (7H9-K). A 200 µl aliquot was taken from each transformation to test transformation efficiency via serial dilution and plate counting. For the remaining culture, 2.5 ml was added to a T75 flask with 50 ml of 7H9-K and grown at 37 °C until an OD600 of 0.5. Then, the remaining kanamycin (Kan) resistant populations of each strain were determined, harvested, pooled, adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0, and stored at −80 °C until further process.

The genome-scale CRISPR screening was performed for each DR-strain and their DS-parent, as previously described. Briefly, seven seeds of the pooled library stock for each strain were thawed, combined, and cultured to give an OD600 of about 0.5. Stock viability was determined via CFU counts. Twelve replicates of each start-up culture were then grown for four days to an OD600 of approximately 1 in 7H9-K. Cultures of each strain were then pooled, from which 500 µl was added to 10 ml of 7H9-K media in T25 flasks under 300 ng/ml ATc to induce CRISPRi or no ATc as a control. For each ATc concentration, five replicate cultures were performed. All T25 flasks were grown at 37 °C without shaking for 14 days, and cultures were back-diluted via a 20-fold dilution on day 5 and day 10 into fresh 7H9-K media with the same concentration of ATc as the initial settings to ensure log-phase growth. The remaining culture of each sample was harvested on day 5, day 10, and day 14 via centrifugation at 3220 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and cell pellets were stored at 20 °C for gDNA extraction.

DNA extraction, library construction, and amplicon sequencing

For each cell pellet obtained from the CRISPRi screens, genomic DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, #D4300) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with the following modifications. Cell pellets were resuspended in 750 µl of ZymoBIOMICS lysis solution and lysed by bead beating for 5 minutes using a SPEX SamplePrep MiniG 1600 tissue homogenizer (SPEX, Metuchen, NJ, USA) at 1,500 rpm. Genomic DNA was eluted in 50 µl of preheated (60 °C) nuclease-free water. DNA concentration was measured using an Invitrogen Qubit 4 Fluorometer with the Broad Range Qubit kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresing 5 µl of each sample on a 1% agarose gel.

To amplify the gRNA, the extracted gDNA was diluted to 25 ng/µl and used as a template for PCR amplification. Samples with gDNA concentrations below 25 ng/µl were used without further dilution. For each sample, 100 ng of gDNA was used in a 50 µl PCR reaction. The PCR master mix was prepared using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England BioLabs, #M0493L) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each strain per screen, 60 individual PCR reactions were performed, consisting of five replicates of 4 ATc concentrations across three time points. The PCR setup included (i) one of five forward primers, each staggered by a single nucleotide to facilitate sequencing library dephasing, and (ii) a reverse primer designed without dephasing capability. Primer sequences and PCR conditions are detailed as previously described5.

Analysis of deep sequencing data from CRISPRi screens

Demultiplexed FASTQ files were converted to tabular format using SeqKit68. Sequence features, including promoter, gRNA, and scaffold sequences, were identified through stepwise fuzzy string matching, irrespective of sequencing quality scores, as the expected promoter and gRNA sequences were predefined; thus, low-quality base calls did not affect downstream analyses. Reads containing identifiable promoter regions upstream of the expected gRNA sequence were retained for subsequent analyses, typically accounting for over 99% of raw reads. To address variable gRNA sequence lengths in the CRISPRi pool, a 34-nt sequence starting from the +1 site was used as a unique sequence tag to match against the expected gRNA sequence pool. Only tags with perfect matches to the expected gRNA sequences were included in generating gRNA count tables for further analysis.

For each gRNA, differential abundance testing was conducted with counts of all five replicates via the classic exact test (edgeR package v3.42.4), and fold changes in gRNA abundance at each time point were calculated relative to the corresponding ATc-0 samples68. False discovery rate for the multiple exact test results were controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (e.g. method = ”BH”). These adjusted p-values, along with log foldchange (eg. logFC) of gRNA abundance, were then used to determine gRNA depletion in downstream analyses.

To ensure equitable comparisons across different CRISPRi experiments, we implemented a normalization in which gRNA depletion thresholds were customized for each screen using the day 14 ATc-300 condition. This approach ensured that the mc26206 control strain yielded a comparable number of depleted gRNAs to our previously validated screen5 before essential genes were identified. Essentiality was defined as cases where at least two gRNAs targeting the same gene were depleted. Because the number of genes classified as essential scales with the overall number of depleted gRNAs, a fixed threshold (≥2 depleted gRNAs per gene) would artificially inflate essential gene counts in experiments with higher global depletion. To correct for this, we applied experiment-specific thresholds that normalized guide depletion across screens, thereby producing comparable mc26206 controls among experiments. These experiment-specific thresholds were then subsequently applied to the corresponding DR strain in each experiment. Collateral vulnerabilities were subsequently assessed by comparing gRNA depletion profiles of DR strains with those of the DS parent under the day 14 ATc-300 condition. Genes were classified as vulnerable if essential in the DR background and supported by ≥2 gRNAs showing at least a two-fold stronger depletion than in the DS parent.

The metabolic pathways of all genes screened in this study were assigned using a manually curated PATRIC database derived from the bacterial and viral bioinformatics resource centre69. Class and subclass information for each gene was obtained via the subsystems table of the H37rv PATRIC database. Where functional classification was missing for a gene, manual classification was applied based on its end products and biological processes as previously described5.

Mutation analyses in clinical isolates

To investigate if genetic vulnerabilities identified from the screen have clinical relevance, we used gene variation data from the CRyPTIC database40. The CRyPTIC data compendium consists of whole-genome sequences and quantitative resistant phenotypes of over 10,000 M. tuberculosis isolates collected worldwide. From these CRyPTIC isolates, we selected clinical isolates that are either resistant to RIF or drug-susceptible to all drugs based on the drug resistance data available from the CRyPTIC database40. To avoid potential bias caused by phylogenetic redundancy, we trimmed the selected samples using Treemmer (v0.3, RTL = 0.97) to remove redundant samples that are genotypically highly similar without sacrificing taxonomic diversity70. After trimming, a total of 4747 M. tuberculosis clinical isolates were included in downstream analyses, including 3058 DS and 1689 RIF-resistant isolates. Of these isolates, we identified mutations from 23 genes involved in sulphur metabolism pathways, and rpoA, rpoB, and rpoC as controls (Supplementary Data 6). Mutation distribution of the proportion of nonsynonymous and synonymous SNPs within a given DS or RIF-resistant group was calculated instead of the conventional dN/dS metric because the majority of genes in our dataset contained only one or a few SNPs across the clinical isolates, for which estimates of dN/dS become unstable or undefined (e.g., when all SNPs fall exclusively into either synonymous or nonsynonymous categories). With all the mutations, only SNP data were used for calculating synonymous and non-synonymous changes (Supplementary Data 7). For each gene per Ds or RIF-resistant group, the mutation frequencies of synonymous and non-synonymous mutations were normalised based on the population size of clinical isolates that had at least 1 SNP in the target gene:

Ng = the number of clinical isolates that had at least 1 SNP in gene g

ng,i= number of nonsynonymous SNPs observed in gene g in isolate i

sg.i= number of synonymous SNPs observed in gene g in isolate i.

Construction and transformation of CRISPRi plasmids that target genes of interest

The gRNA sequences of interest from the pooled-CRISPRi library were cloned as individual CRISPRi plasmids. Briefly, the gRNA sequence and a complementary sequence were ordered with GGGA and AAAC overhangs. Oligos were annealed and cloned into the CRISPRi plasmid pLJR965 using BsmB1 and confirmed using Sanger sequencing as previously described. Constructed CRISPRi plasmids are listed in Supplementary Data 7. CRISPRi plasmids were transformed into M. tuberculosis DS parent and DR strains as previously described71,72.

CRISPRi phenotypic assessment of essentiality under continuous log phase growth

The increased vulnerability of target genes was assessed using growth kinetics following target depletion. Experiments were performed by diluting M. tuberculosis mc26206 strains containing CRISPRi plasmids in 7H9-Kan to an OD600 of 0.5. 1 ml of culture was added to 9 ml of 7H9-Kan in a T25-flasks with ATc-300 to a starting OD600 of 0.05. Cultures were grown without shaking at 37 °C. At day 5 the OD600 was determined and 0.5 ml of culture was back-diluted into 9.5 ml of 7H9-K in a T25-flasks with ATc-300 and grown without shaking at 37 °C. Cultures were grown for an additional 14-21 days and OD600 was determined on stated days.

Measurement of the membrane potential in Mycobacterium tuberculosis

M. tuberculosis mc26206 WT and BDQ-resistant strains were grown routinely in 7H9 medium supplemented with OADC, tyloxapol, 50 µg/mL leucine and 20 µg/mL pantothenate. Cultures were centrifuged at 3220 × g for 10 min and cells were resuspended in 7H9 base medium with no added supplements to an OD600 of 1. Resuspended cells were incubated at 37 °C and the membrane potential determinations were performed as previously described.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

M. tuberculosis mc26206 strains containing either NT or targeting CRISPRi plasmids were diluted in 7H9-K to an OD600 of 0.5. In biological triplicate, 3 ml of OD600-adjusted culture was added to 27 ml of 7H9-K in a T75 culture flask with ATc-300, making a starting OD600 of 0.05. Cultures were grown without shaking at 37 °C for5 days before 1.5 ml of culture was back-diluted into 28.5 ml of 7H9-K in T25 culture flasks with ATc-300 and grown for a further 10 days. A volume of culture equal to 1 ml of OD600 culture was pelleted by centrifugation at 3220 × g at 4 °C for 10 min, then resuspended in 1 ml TRIzol and transferred to 2-ml bead-beating tubes containing 200 µl of 0.1 mm silica beads. Cultures were lysed by bead-beating at 4800 rpm for 30 s 3×, rested on ice between cycles. RNA was extracted by removing the top aqueous layer after the addition of 200 µl chloroform, followed by a 2-10 min incubation and centrifugation for 15 min at 13000 × g at 4 °C. The RNA was purified using the Zymo-RNA Clean and Concentrator (Cat No: R1019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was removed using the Invitrogen Turbo DNA-free kit (Cat No: AM1907) as previously described73–75. The removal of DNA was confirmed by gel electrophoresis of Taq polymerase PCR using 1 μl of extracted RNA as template and the primer combination MMO200 + MMO201. Extracted RNA, confirmed as DNA-free, was converted into cDNA using 100 ng of RNA and the Invitrogen SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Cat No: 11756050). qRT-PCR reactions were performed in 384 well plates in a ViiA7 Thermocycler using Invitrogen PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat No: A25780) with 3 μL of 1/100-diluted cDNA and 500 nM of forward and reverse primers. Signals were normalised to the housekeeping sigA transcript and quantified by the 2ΔΔCt method. Error bars are the standard deviation of three biological replicates, each comprised of two technical replicates.

Quantification of LMWT levels in Mycobacterium tuberculosis lysates using LC-MS/MS

M. tuberculosis mc26206 strains containing either NT or targeting CRISPRi plasmids were diluted in 7H9-K to an OD600 of 0.5. One ml of culture was added to 9 ml of 7H9-K in a T25 culture flasks with ATc-300 to a starting OD600 of 0.05. This was performed in triplicate over three consecutive days. Cultures were grown without shaking at 37 °C. At day 5, 2.5 ml of culture was back-diluted into 47.5 ml of 7H9-K in a T75-culture flasks with ATc-300 and grown without shaking at 37 °C for 10 days. Metabolites were extracted as previously stated5 with the following modifications. Metabolites were extracted on the day 10 time point from a volume of culture equal to 3 ml at an OD600 of 1. The extraction buffer consisted of 5 ml of HEPES buffer (50 mM HEPES (Fisher Scientific, #BP310), 2.25 mM diethylenetriamine pentaacetate (Sigma, #D6518), pH 8.05) and acetonitrile (Sigma, #900667), with 1.1 ml of freshly made 200 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) (Sigma, #04259). Tubes containing cell-laden filters were rested on ice between bead-beating cycles and once lysed, cell lysates were incubated at 60 °C for 10 min to allow for LMWTs to be alkylated by NEM.

Lysates were diluted 1:5 with PBS and 10 µl was injected for analysis by targeted multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM)-based LC-MS/MS analysis of ergothioneine and the NEM-adducts of cysteine, γ-glutamyl-cysteine and mycothiol on an Ultimate 3000 RS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled to a 4000 QTrap mass spectrometer (Sciex, Framingham, MA). HPLC, voltage, temperature and gas settings were as described previously76,77, but here all analytes were detected in the same run with the mass spectrometer operating in positive ion mode. The quantifier / qualifier mass transitions (parent ion→fragment ion) for ergothioneine, cysteine-NEM, γ-glutamyl-cysteine-NEM, and MSH-NEM were 230.1 → 127.1 / 230.1 → 186.2, 247.2 → 201.1 / 247.15 → 212.1, 376.0 → 247.0 / 376.0 → 130.0 and 612.2 → 243.0 / 612.2 → 201.0, respectively. L-cysteine and γ-glutamyl-cysteine (Biosynth) were treated with 40-fold molar access of NEM, and together with ergothioneine (Sigma) served as external standards. Standards were serially diluted and co-injected with the diluted lysates. The area under the curve (AUC) was determined for the quantifier mass transition of each analyte and concentrations were interpolated from a linear standard curve generated using the external standards and corrected for sample dilution. The limit of detection was 0.02, 0.002 and 0.8 µM (lysate concentration) for cysteine, γ-glutamyl-cysteine and ergothioneine, respectively. A standard could not be obtained for mycothiol and its abundance is expressed as the raw AUC data.

Mycobacterial recombineering

The RpoBS441L and RpoBH445Y mutant were constructed using mycobacterial recombineering78. Briefly, M. tuberculosis strain mc26206 parent was transformed with pKM461 (KanR). M. tuberculosis with pKM461 was grown, ATc was added to induce the Che9C annealase and cells were made electrocompetent as previously described. 380 µl of electrocompetent cells were transformed with 1 µg/ml of RpoBS441L oligo (CAGCGCCGACAGTCGGCGCTTGTGGGTCAACCCCAACAGCGGGTTGTTCTGGTCCATGAATTGGCTCAGC). RpoBH445Y oligo (ACCGCCGGGCCCCAGCGCCGACAGTCGGCGCTTGTAGGTCAACCCCGACAGCGGGTTGTTCTGGTCCATG). Electroporated cells were recovered in 5 ml 7H9 media with no antibiotics at 37 C for 48 hours. Cells were harvested and plated onto 7H11 supplemented agar containing RIF at 0.5 µM. After 3 weeks incubation, colonies were inoculated into 10 ml 7H9 + antibiotic in T25 tissue culture flasks. Growing cultures were screened for RIF resistance. Genomic DNA was extracted from phenotypically positive colonies and PCR screen was used to confirm mutation in RpoB. From genetically confirmed strains, whole genome sequencing was used to confirm mutation as previously described6,7. To cure strains of pKM461, RpoBS441L mutant was grown in 10 ml 7H9 in T25 tissue culture flaks to an OD of approximately 0.1. Culture was diluted and plated on to 7H11 agar containing OADC and 3% sucrose. After 2 weeks of incubation, 8 colonies were transferred to 24-well plates and once grown, screened for both Kan sensitivity, to confirm the loss of pKM461, and RIF to confirm the phenotype was retained. Genomic DNA was extracted from KanS + RIFR clones and mutation in RpoB was again confirmed by sanger sequencing and whole genome sequencing. The SubIA76T mutant was also made using recombineering with some minor adjustments. First we used a DS-parent and RpoBS450L mutant M. tuberculosis containing the plasmids pKM461 and pKM427. pKM427 Integrates at the L5att site and contains a broken hygromycin cassette. After induction with ATc a (i) SubIA76T oligo (CTGGACTCCCCGGCCCTGTTCGGAAGCGTTGAACGtcGGAATCACCGCACTCCAGCCGGGTTCCGGGACG) and (ii) hygromycin repair oligo (CGGTCCAGCAGCCGGGGCGAGAGGTAGCCCCACCCGCGGTGGTCCTCGACGGTCGCCGCG) were transformed into either strain. After recovery, cells were plated onto 7H11 supplemented media containing hygromycin (50 µg/ml). After 3 weeks incubation, at least 50 colonies were inoculated into 10 ml 7H9 + Hyg in 24 well plates. Once turbid boil prep lysates were prepared and used to PCR screened for the SubIA76T mutation using the fwd primers (TGGAGTGCGGTGATTCCGAC or TGGAGTGCGGTGATTCAGAC) with the reverse primer (GGCGCAATTTGACGTGTTCA). Positive PCR colonies were outgrown and whole genome sequencing was used to confirm mutation as previously described6,7. Strains were cured of pKM461 as above.

Protein expression and purification

DNA encoding SubI (41-356) (SubI_WT) and SubI (41-356) A76T (SubI_A76T) were synthesized and cloned into the pProEX HTb expression vector by GenScript. All proteins were expressed in LOBSTR (DE3) cells grown in Terrific Broth media supplemented with 0.15 g/L glucose79. Expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM isopropyl β-D−1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to log phase cultures prior to subsequent growth overnight at 18 °C. Cells were lysed by cell disruption in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100. The His-tagged SubI_WT and SubI_A76T proteins were purified using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography, and further enriched using size exclusion chromatography equilibrated in buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Binding studies titrating sodium sulfate with SubI proteins were undertaken using a MicroCal VP-ITC. A 1 mM sodium sulfate solution was prepared in the same size exclusion chromatography buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl) used to purify the SubI proteins. Sodium sulfate was titrated through 29×10 µL injections using the stirring syringe into the sample cell containing 1.4 ml of 100 µM SubI_WT or 100 µM SubI_A76T, maintained at 20 °C. Each injection was spaced 300 s apart to allow a baseline to be reached, and the reference power was set to 15 µCal sec−1. Additionally, 1 mM sodium sulfate was titrated into the buffer solution to assess the heat of dilution and the resulting heats subtracted from the binding data. The calorimetric data were analyzed using Origin® software, and the best fit was achieved using a binding model that allowed for a set of identical and independent sites.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. The experiments were not randomized. For experimental validations, the number of replicates (two to three) was used mainly due to the practicality of allowing horizontal comparisons within the same experimental setups. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
