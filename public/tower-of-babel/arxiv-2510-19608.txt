Abstract
This paper presents a novel structure-preserving, Kron-based reduction framework for unbalanced distribution feeders. The method aggregates electrically similar nodes within a mixed-integer optimization (MIP) problem to engender reduced networks that optimally reproduce the voltage profiles of the original full network. To overcome computational bottlenecks of MIP formulations, we motivate an exhaustive-search formulation to identify optimal aggregation decisions, while enforcing voltage margin limits. The proposed exhaustive network reduction algorithm is parallelizable on GPUs, which enables scalable network reduction. The resulting reduced networks approximate the full system’s voltage profiles with low errors, and are suitable for steady-state analysis and optimal power flow studies. The framework is validated on two real utility distribution feeders with 5,991 5{,}991 and 8,381 8{,}381 nodes. The reduced models achieve up to 90 % 90\% and 80 % 80\% network reduction, respectively, while the maximum voltage-magnitude error remains below 0.003 0.003 p.u. Furthermore, on a 1000-node version of the network, the GPU-accelerated reduction algorithm runs up to 15x faster than its CPU-based counterpart.

Introduction

The rapid integration of distributed energy resources (DERs) and advanced control schemes has significantly increased the complexity of distribution networks, posing challenges for optimization and real-time decision-making
[

Consequently, research on network reduction has emerged, where proposed network reduction tools seek to simplify network models while preserving essential electrical characteristics.

One network reduction technique is bus elimination via Kron reduction
[
2
]
, where selected nodes are eliminated via the Schur complement of the nodal admittance matrix, resulting in an equivalent network that preserves the impedance seen between retained nodes. However, the set of reducible nodes is restricted to those with zero current injection.

and

References

propose methods for network reduction on distribution systems considering multiphase unbalanced models.
Nevertheless, a common limitation across these studies lies in their dependence on a predefined set of nodes that must be kept. Whether these nodes are specified as critical nodes or boundary nodes, this requirement restricts flexibility and may reduce the accuracy of the reduced networks.

In prior works
[
9
]
and
[

]
, an optimal Kron-based reduction of networks (Opti-KRON) framework has been developed for single-phase balanced feeders, where the reduced networks do not rely on any predefined set of nodes and explicitly trades off reduction level against accuracy via tunable bounds on voltage magnitude error. A mixed-integer linear program (MILP) was proposed to identify the optimal set of nodes and clusters that maximize reduction while minimizing voltage deviations across representative loading scenarios. In addition, because Kron reduction can yield dense, meshed networks
[
2
]
,
[

), the framework introduced a radialization step to ensure the reduced network remains radial.
However, the previous Opti-KRON formulation is limited to single-phase networks.
As a result, no existing
optimal

In this paper, we extend the Opti-KRON framework of
[
9
]
to unbalanced three-phase distribution networks. We develop a three-phase Kron-based reduction and radialization procedure.
To formulate the optimal reduction, we first present that generalizes the single-phase Opti-KRON to three-phase feeders.
Furthermore, we introduce an exhaustive-search algorithm that serves as a benchmark to the MILP formulation. The proposed exhaustive search offers several advantages. It operates directly on complex values without requiring decomposition to rectangular coordinates. Second, it can evaluate nonconvex objective functions. Third, its structure supports parallel computation on GPUs.
Consequently, the proposed method achieves significant computational speedups on large-scale feeders while increasing reduction accuracy. This paper makes the following contributions:

•

Three-phase MILP extension: The single-phase optimal Kron-based network reduction is extended to unbalanced three-phase radial feeders.

•

Exhaustive search framework: An alternative exhaustive search based algorithm, with the ability to handle nonconvex objective functions, is proposed to increase scalability and accuracy.

•

Three-phase radialization: The radialization procedure is extended to the three-phase setting to ensure that the reduced networks preserve both topological structure and phase connectivity.

II-C

Clustering for Kron-based Reduction

As shown in (
II-B
) and (
9
), Kron reduction preserves voltage profile when the reduced nodes have zero current injection.
To enable reduction beyond strictly zero-injection nodes, we introduce an
assignment

\mathcal{K}
as

These assignments, however, must obey several rules:

1.

Connectivity: Each child must be connected to its super-node through existing branches, so that every cluster forms a connected sub-graph of the original network.

2.

Phase availability: a reduced node
i
can only be assigned to a super-node
k
if
\phi(i)\subseteq\phi(k)
.

3.

Uniqueness: every reduced node
i\in\mathcal{R}
is assigned to exactly one super-node.

A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}

A_{i,j}=1

\displaystyle I^{\rm agg}\;=\;(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})\,I\,\in\mathbb{C}^{3n},

\displaystyle(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})\;I\;=\;Y\;V^{\rm agg}.

(11)

Now, the

\mathcal{R}=\{i\in\mathcal{V}|I^{\rm agg}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\}

Consider assignment of nodes with nonzero injections results in

III
Optimal Kron-based Reduction of Networks

The choice of
A
determines how injections are aggregated and how voltages are represented, and thus directly affects both the reduction level and the accuracy of the reduced model. In general, greater reduction introduces larger errors.
The objective is to find the assignment matrix
A
by finding a desired balance between reduction and accuracy.
One way to achieve this is to cast the search for
A
as an optimization problem, where the assignment rules – phase availability, connectivity, and uniqueness – are enforced as constraints, as was done in Opti-KRON
[
9
]
for single phase networks.

In this section, we extend the formulation to three-phase feeders.
The core optimization framework follows the single-phase structure but is reformulated to handle three-phase feeders.
Let
\mathcal{L}
denote a library of operating scenarios given from AC load flow solutions on the full network. For each scenario
l\in\mathcal{L}
,
\hat{V}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{3n}
and
\hat{I}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{3n}
are the three-phase nodal voltage and current injection vectors of the full network that satisfy
Y\hat{V}_{l}=\hat{I}_{l}
. The voltage of the network under aggregated current is denoted by
V_{l}
, such that
YV_{l}=A\hat{I}_{l}
.
In this paper, error is defined as the difference between the nodal voltage in the full and in the reduced network. In the reduced network, super-node voltages are obtained through (
11
), while each reduced node is assigned the voltage of its associated super-node. Accordingly, for
l\in\mathcal{L}
, error is computed as

\displaystyle{E_{l}}=\overbrace{(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}){\rm diag}\{\hat{V}_{l}\}}^{\text{Full}}-\overbrace{{\rm diag}\{V_{l}\}(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})}^{\text{ Reduced}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}.

(12)

From (
12
),
A_{i,j}=1\rightarrow E_{l,i,j}=\hat{V}_{l,j}-V_{l,i}
and
A_{i,j}=0\rightarrow E_{l,i,j}=0
. To quantify the maximum voltage deviation within each cluster and evaluate the quality of a given reduction, we define the maximum intra-cluster error (MICE) as

\displaystyle\text{MICE}=\left\|\vec{e}_{i}^{T}E_{l}\right\|_{\infty}.

(13)

Note that
\vec{e}_{i}
represent the
i^{\rm th}
standard basis vector. Using MICE as the measure of accuracy, we formulate the three-phase Opti-KRON problem, to find the assignment matrix that as follows.

\displaystyle\min_{A}\quad

\displaystyle{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}{\left\|\vec{e}_{i}^{T}E_{l}\right\|_{\infty}}}-\alpha\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(1-A_{i,i})}

(14a)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;\quad

\displaystyle{E_{l}}=(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}){\rm diag}\{\hat{V}_{l}\}-{\rm diag}\{V_{l}\}(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})\quad

(14b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}V_{l}=(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})\hat{I_{l}}

(14c)

\displaystyle-\bar{E}\leq|(A\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3})^{\rm T}V_{l}|-|\hat{V}_{l}|\leq\bar{E}

(14d)

\displaystyle A^{\rm T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}

(14e)

\displaystyle A_{i,j}\leq A_{i,i}\quad\forall i,j\in\mathcal{V}

(14f)

\displaystyle A_{i,j}\leq\Lambda_{i,j}\quad\forall i,j\in\mathcal{V},i\neq j

(14g)

\displaystyle A_{i,j}=0\quad\forall i,j\in\mathcal{V},\phi_{j}\nsubseteq\phi_{i}

(14h)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(1-A_{i,i})\leq q

(14i)

\displaystyle A_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall i,j\in\mathcal{V}.

(14j)

The objective function(
14a
) minimizes the sum MICE across all scenarios, while simultaneously minimizing the number of super-nodes. The trade-off is tuned by the parameter
\alpha
. The KCL equation is enforced in (
14c
).
Voltage magnitude deviations are constrained in (
14d
) to lie within a tolerance
\bar{E}
.
Unique assignments are enforced by (
14e
) to ensure each reduced node maps to exactly one super-node.
Equation (
14f
) allows assignments only to super-nodes.
To keep the clusters connected, (
14g
) restricts clustering to adjacent nodes through the adjacency matrix
\Lambda
, while (
14h
) enforces availability of phases of reduced nodes at super-nodes.
The constraint (
14i
) bounds the number of reduced nodes, to speed-up the solve times.
Finally, (
14j
) declares all assignment variables as binary.

To solve (
14
), we follow the methodology adapted from
[
9
]
, which addressed the same challenges:

1.

The problem is a mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP), due to the nonlinear voltage constraints (
14b
) and (
14d
). This can be relaxed to a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) by introducing auxiliary variables and Big M method.

2.

All continuous variables are expressed in complex form, which prevents us to solve it with standard solvers. There are two possible formulations to tackle this. In the first approach, the voltage magnitude is expressed as
|V|=||V^{\rm real}+V^{\rm imag}||_{2}
, which adds a nonconvex and nonlinear constraint. Otherwise, we can adopt the standard rectangular decomposition to decompose complex variables into real and imaginary components and approximate (
14d
). In this work, we adopt the rectangular formulation to preserve linearity.

3.

The reduction level is limited by (
14g
) and (
14i
). Therefore, we solve the problem iteratively: at each stage, the model is resolved with an updated adjacency matrix that reflects the evolving cluster structure. The process terminates when no reduction occurs, i.e., no further reduction satisfies the voltage error threshold.

To iteratively solve (
14

t

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(t+1)}=(A^{*}\Lambda{A^{*}}^{\rm T})\odot(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\rm T}-I),

(15)

where,
A^{*}
represents the optimal assignment matrix obtained at iteration
t

\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.
It connects each super-node to the neighbors of itself and its assigned nodes. If the original network is radial,
\Lambda
remains radial throughout. The number of reduced nodes
n_{r}^{(t)}
and super-nodes
n_{s}^{(q,t)}
at iteration

are both functions of the reduction limit
q
.
The number of nodes that can be reduced in iteration
t
is bounded by
n_{r}^{(t)}\leq q
,
and the number of remaining super-nodes evolves as

n_{s}^{(q,t)}=n-\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}n_{r}^{(\tau)}.

(16)

Since
\Lambda^{t}
is radial, the number of lines at each iteration is
n_{s}^{(q,t)}-1

2(n_{s}^{(q,t)}-1)

(i,j)
that satisfy (
14g

The number of iterations in (
14

q
in (
14i
).
Setting
q
to a larger value allows the optimizer to make more reductions per Opti-KRON iteration, leading to potentially fewer total iterations until convergence is achieved. However, since reductions can only be made on direct neighbors at each iteration (due to the connectivity constraint (
14g
)), we have observed that the optimizer can start to make “greedy” decisions for
q>1

Specifically, it can trade long-term reduction quality (in terms of voltage prediction error) for short-term nodal reduction gains which, while feasible and locally optimal, eliminate the possibility of higher quality reduction decisions on future iterations. This will be illustrated directly in Fig.
3
. This behavior arises because larger

When reducing many nodes at one step,
n_{s}^{(q,t)}
decreases sharply and leads to a smaller set of distinct feasible choices. The following lemma formalizes the impact of
q
on the total number of feasible reduction scenarios explored throughout the reduction process.

.

Consider iteratively reducing a network of size

\displaystyle W^{T(q_{1})}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{T(q_{1})}2(n_{s}^{(1,t)}-1)

\displaystyle W^{T(q_{2})}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{T(q_{2})}2(n_{s}^{(q_{2},t)}-1).

IV
Exhaustive Search for Reduction

Building on the MILP formulation of Section III, which optimally determines reduction assignments through an MILP, we now introduce a complementary approach designed for parallel execution on GPUs.
Motivated by Lemma
1
, reducing one node per iteration let us evaluate each distinct reduction scenario independently and in parallel.
Additionally, while solving the MILP requires decomposing complex variables into their real and imaginary parts, the exhaustive search approach operates directly in the complex domain and enables exact computation of voltage deviations.
Furthermore, unlike the MILP, which minimizes a linear objective, the exhaustive-search framework allows direct evaluation of nonlinear error measures. In particular, objectives based on voltage magnitudes, while inherently nonconvex, can be assessed exactly for each candidate reduction. Accordingly, we adjust the objective of each iteration to minimize the sum of MICE (SMICE), where error is measured as voltage
magnitude

We initialize the assignment set such that every node is a super-node and is assigned to itself. At each iteration, the set of candidate assignments must satisfy phase availability and connectivity constraints. The algorithm then evaluates the error of every potential assignment, selects the candidate with the minimum sum of MICE (SMICE) across all loading scenarios, and proceeds with updated adjacency, current vector, and assignment set.
For all iterations,
Y
remains fixed, while the current vector
I^{c}
is updated for each assignment. Solving
I^{c}=YV^{c}
results in the same super-nodes voltage that Kron reduction would yield.
After each solve, reduced nodes are assigned the voltage of their super-node, both for new and previously reduced nodes.
The parallelizable part of the algorithm is the evaluation of candidate assignments, where each candidate assignment is evaluated independently with no data dependencies. This independency makes this block suitable for parallel computation of the resulting error of each candidate assignments (
{\rm SMICE}
), which, in turn, makes the proposed approach scalable.
However, the Kron-based reduction of a radial network generates smaller, but dense meshed network
[
2
,
9
]
. To recover a radial three-phase distribution network, we now review and extend the radialization approach introduced in
[
9
]
.

V
Three-phase Radialization

Kron-based reduced networks are prone to being meshed and densely connected.
This structure makes us unable to use power flow algorithms optimized for three-phase radial networks. Density of the reduced networks increases the computational burden of subsequent optimization problems.
In
[
9
]
, radialization was presented to find a radial equivalent of a single-phase Kron reduced network.
Here, we extend that approach to three-phase networks.

The topology of a Kron reduced graph can be realized through its admittance matrix from (

). In single-phase networks, any nonzero entry
{Y_{\rm Kron}}_{i,j}
indicates a direct connection between nodes
i
and
j
. For three-phase feeders, we extend this definition: a connection between nodes
i
and
j
exists if the corresponding
3\times 3

contains at least one nonzero entry. In the following, we first demonstrate that topology of a three-phase Kron reduced network can be found through its Kron reduced graph Laplacian as Lemma
2
. Then, we leverage this observation to extend single-phase radialization to three-phase cases.

Reference
[
11
]
proves that Kron reduction of radial networks is an edge-disjoint of maximal cliques.
Lemma
2
extends this property to three-phase networks. A clique is a sub-graph that all the nodes in the sub-graph are mutually connected. When a clique is not included in any larger clique it is call a maximal clique. Any maximal cliques with three or more nodes creates meshes in Kron reduced networks, which is the consequence of eliminating nodes with degree
\geq 3
. These properties are identical between single and three-phase networks. Therefore, we summarize the radialization procedure of
[
9
]
, which can be extended directly into three-phase networks.

Radialization consists of identifying and re-inserting critical nodes for each maximal clique in a Kron reduced network. Critical nodes are defined as those with degree
\geq 3
in a sub-tree of the original feeder that spans the nodes of each maximal clique. Adding these nodes to the set of super-nodes ensures that the reduced network is radial.
This does not mean all nodes with degree
\geq 3
must be reinserted, only the minimal subset whose absence would otherwise create cliques. Thus, if
\mathcal{K}
is the set of super-nodes in the reduced meshed network and
\mathcal{R}_{c}
is the set of critical nodes for all the maximal cliques, then the set of super-nodes in the radialized network is
\mathcal{K}_{\rm radial}=\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}_{c}
.
Finally, if injections at critical nodes are not reassigned, the pre-radialized and radialized networks are electrically equivalent in terms of the voltage profile at the super-nodes.

VI
Simulation and Results

To evaluate the performance of the proposed framework, simulations are carried out on two realistic three-phase unbalanced distribution feeders from Vermont, USA: feeder A with 8,381 nodes and feeder B with 5,591 nodes. To investigate performance across different network scales, multiple sub-feeders of varying sizes (100, 300, 500, and 1000 nodes) are extracted from these systems. For each network, two representative operating scenarios are considered corresponding to the highest and lowest loading conditions from a week of historical hourly data, i.e. 168 loading scenarios. All computations were performed on the Vermont Advanced Computing Center (VACC) at the University of Vermont. The simulation codes used in this study were implemented in Julia 1.11.5. To solve the optimization problems, JuMP v1.29.1
[
12
]
and the Gurobi Optimizer 12.0 were employed, with the optimality gap set to 0%. The GPU implementation was carried out using CUDA package
[
13
]
.

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

This work extended the network-reduction framework previously developed for single-phase feeders to unbalanced three-phase distribution systems. The proposed formulation enables accurate aggregation of large feeders while maintaining the voltage profile of the original networks across all phases. By adopting a direct exhaustive-search approach and leveraging parallel execution on GPUs, the method achieves high reduction levels with negligible voltage deviation. We applied the proposed framework into two real-word unbalance three-phase networks and demonstrated the scalability and practicality of the technique.

Future work will generalize the Opti-KRON framework to transmission systems, with emphasis on preserving line flow accuracy between the reduced and original networks. We are also interested in investigating how optimization and control tasks—such as optimal power flow and hosting capacity assessment—performed on reduced models, can be consistently lifted back to the full network.
