The chain mediation effects of psychological resilience and active coping style between discrimination perceptions and subjective well being among college students with disability
Authors: Nairun Zhou, Zhiwei Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExploring the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities and the chain-mediated effects of psychological resilience and positive coping styles. Questionnaires were administered to 528 college students with disabilities using the Discrimination Perceptions Scale, Index of Well-Being Scale, Psychological Resilience Scale, and Simple Coping Styles Scale. SPSS 24.0 and Amos 26.0 were used to analyze chained mediation effects and construct structural equation modeling tests. ①Discrimination perception was significantly negatively correlated with subjective well-being (r = -0.294, p < 0.01), psychological resilience(r = -0.427, p < 0.01) and all 3 of its dimensions, and positive coping styles (r = -0.328, p <0.01); subjective well-being was significantly positively correlated with psychological resilience (r = 0.334, p < 0.01) and all 3 of its dimensions, and positive coping styles (r = 0.363, p<0.01); and positive coping styles was significantly positively correlated with psychological resilience(r = 0.433, p < 0.01) and all 3 of its dimensions.② The direct effect of discrimination perceptions on college students with disabilities was significant, and the mediating effect value was-0.191.③Psychological resilience and positive coping styles not only play a significant separate mediating role between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being of college students with disabilities, with mediation effect values of -0.138 (49.11%) and − 0.087 (30.96%), respectively but also play a significant chain mediating role between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being, with an effect value of -0.056 (19.93%). Discrimination perceptions can directly affect the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities, and can also indirectly affect the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities through the separate and chain-mediated effects of psychological resilience and positive coping styles.Keywords: Chain mediation effect, Discrimination perceptions, Positive coping styles, Psychological resilience, Subjective well-beingSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

As the expansion of higher education in China continues, the number of students with disabilities has shown a steady growth trend, forming an educational group that cannot be ignored. According to the 2022 Statistical Bulletin on the Development of the Cause of Persons with Disabilities published by the China Disabled Persons’ Federation, a cumulative total of 30,035 students with disabilities were admitted at the higher education level in the period 2021–2025. However, physical disability is an unavoidable reality for college students with disabilities. As an external factor, disability not only directly affects an individual’s subjective well-being1, but may also trigger discrimination, which in turn negatively affects an individual’s subjective well-being2,3.

Subjective well-being, as an overall evaluation and subjective experience of an individual’s own quality of life, is one of the important psychological parameters reflecting the level of an individual’s mental health4. Increasing the level of subjective well-being can positively impact mental health by reducing individuals’ adverse emotional experiences5. It has been shown that individuals with higher levels of subjective well-being are more likely to have a high quality of life, establish stable relationships, and maintain good physical health6. For college students with disabilities, subjective well-being also has a profound impact on their academic and social skills7. However, physical impairments can lead to negative psychological experiences, such as discrimination perceptions and psychological stress, to which individuals are particularly susceptible8. These experiences continuously undermine their subjective well-being9. At present, how to cope with the generally low subjective well-being of college students with disabilities has become an urgent issue for colleges and universities. Although there have been studies focusing on the relationship between discrimination perceptions and the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities8, there is still a lack of in-depth research on the mechanisms of the specific effects of discrimination perceptions on the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities. Therefore, actively studying the mechanism of discrimination perceptions’ influence on the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities can help to improve their subjective well-being level, which in turn improves their mental health.

Discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being

Discrimination perceptions refers to an individual’s subjective experience of unfair, negative, or harmful treatment based on their group identity10. It implies a vulnerable “critical psychological reality“11, and can be extremely damaging to an individual’s mental health12,13. Relative deprivation theory states that when an individual evaluates their social status and situation based on social comparisons with others and discrimination perceptions, they feels deprived of their basic rights, which leads to adverse emotions such as anxiety and depression14, and consequently, lowers their level of subjective well-being. Relevant studies have shown that discrimination perceptions are a key factor in lowering an individual’s level of subjective well-being15. It can directly affect an individual’s level of subjective well-being16, or it can negatively affect an individual’s level of subjective well-being indirectly by decreasing life satisfaction, self-assessment of health, quality of life, which leads to depressive symptoms, and undermining an individual’s protective resources and positive emotions17,18. The Phenomenological Variant of Ecological Systems Theory(PVEST) identifies discrimination perceptions as an important risk factor affecting the development of disadvantaged adolescents19. Numerous studies have shown that discrimination perceptions are an important factor contributing to low levels of subjective well-being in specific populations such as refugees20, immigrant children and adolescents21, and obese individuals22. Therefore, this study proposes research hypothesis 1: Discrimination perceptions negatively predicts subjective well-being among college students with disabilities.

Discrimination perceptions and psychological resilience

Psychological resilience refers to the ability of an individual to adjust their state in the face of adversity and effectively respond to changes in the external environment23, which is a psychological trait possessed by an individual in a difficult situation24. Psychological resilience serves as a crucial determinant in facilitating effective adversity management and stress adaptation25. This adaptive capacity significantly contributes to enhanced positive emotional experiences and elevated26. Multiple investigations of specialized populations reveal that psychological resilience levels are associated with adaptive responses to diverse stressors, and these patterns correlate with subjective well-being27. Based on Psychological Stress Theory, discrimination perceptions as a persistent chronic stressor, is associated with sustained stress states, whereby individuals with prolonged exposure to discriminatory experiences correlate with decreased positive psychological resources28, and these patterns are linked to lower levels of subjective well-being. Based on the Organism-environment Interaction Model, individuals with different characteristics demonstrate varied responses under identical environmental conditions29. Psychological resilience, as a positive psychological resource, is associated with psychological equilibrium maintenance under stress conditions30. Studies indicate correlations between resilience levels and the development of adaptive cognitive patterns, including emotional recognition and regulation capabilities31. Therefore, this study proposes hypothesis 2: Among college students with disabilities, psychological resilience is a mediating variable between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being.

Discrimination perceptions and positive coping styles

Coping styles encompass observable cognitive and behavioral responses to frustration, stress, and emotional challenges32. Research categorizes these patterns into two primary categories of coping patterns: positive and negative styles. According to the Pressure-Cognitive Interaction Theory, there are associations between coping styles, stressful experiences, and subsequent outcomes33. Studies indicate relationships between discrimination perceptions and individual outcomes measures across different coping styles34, particularly in assessments of subjective well-being35. Positive coping styles are regarded as a protective factor for subjective well-being36, which can provide protection for individuals in stressful situations such as feeling discrimination, and help to eliminate the negative emotional experiences of individuals37, thus reducing the impact of discrimination perceptions on subjective well-being. Workplace observational studies suggest associations between coping styles and perceptions of occupational stress perceptions among employees38. Therefore, this study proposes hypothesis 3: Positive coping styles are a mediating variable between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities.

Chain mediation of psychological resilience and positive coping styles

Empirical investigations have established distinct associations between psychological resilience and coping styles. Individuals with enhanced psychological resilience exhibit a preferential engagement in adaptive coping mechanisms when confronting stressors. Conversely, those with attenuated resilience demonstrate a pronounced tendency toward maladaptive response patterns, manifesting in avoidance behaviors and compromised stress management strategies39,40. Furthermore, a clinical investigation conducted among frontline healthcare professionals substantiates a significant positive correlation between psychological resilience and adaptive coping mechanisms41. According to the rejection-identification model, when individuals with special needs experience discrimination, their subjective well-being is adversely affected42. However, individuals with higher psychological resilience tend to adopt adaptive coping styles, effectively buffering or protecting their subjective well-being40. Based on these analyses, the present study proposes the research hypothesis that among college students with disabilities, psychological resilience and positive coping styles play a chain mediating role in the effect of discrimination perceptions on subjective well-being.

Prior research suggests associations among discrimination perceptions, psychological resilience, positive coping styles, and subjective well-being. However, the relationships and potential pathways connecting these variables require further investigation. Current empirical evidence is limited regarding how these psychological factors relate to subjective well-being among college students with disabilities. Therefore, this study examines the association between these variables in this population, with particular focus on the mediating roles of psychological resilience and positive coping styles in the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities. Materials and methods

Participants

Following the principles of voluntariness, confidentiality, and anonymity, a random sampling method was used to select students with disabilities from one of the ten colleges in Guangzhou as participants. The questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site by the investigators with the cooperation of the university’s research department. The final study sample consisted of 528 participants, of which 271 (51.3%) were male and 257 (48.7%) were female. By grade level, 133 (25.2%) were freshmen, 141 (26.7%) were sophomores, and 127 (24.1%) were juniors and seniors. The outcomes are presented in Table 1.

Measurement

Discrimination perceptions scale

The Individual discrimination perceptions Scale, developed by Shen et al.43, was employed to assess discrimination perceptions. The scale consists of 3 items (e.g., “I feel I have been treated unfairly”) rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). This instrument has demonstrated robust psychometric properties among adolescent populations, with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of individual discrimination. In the present study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.836) and sampling validity (KMO = 0.724, Bartlett’s test of sphericity: p < 0.001).

Index of well-being scale

The Index of Well-Being Scale compiled by Wang et al. was used44, with a total of 9 entries. 8 entries constituted the overall affective index, which was in the form of a semantic differential scale, with a 7-point scale in 8 groups of bipolar adjectives(e.g., “bored - interesting”),with a weight of 1; and 1 entry for the life satisfaction index, with a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all satisfied) to 7 (very satisfied), with a weight of 1.1; The higher the overall score, the higher the individual’s subjective well-being level. The higher the overall score, the higher the degree of subjective well-being of the individual. In the present study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.909) and sampling validity (KMO = 0.941, Bartlett’s test of sphericity: p < 0.001).

Psychological resilience scale

Psychological resilience was assessed using the Psychological Resilience Scale developed by Yu and Zhang45,the scale consists of 25-item(e.g.,“I am capable of adapting to change”).The scale comprises three dimensions: toughness, strength, and optimism, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = always). Higher total scores indicate greater psychological resilience. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the present study (overall Cronbach’s α = 0.958; subscale Cronbach’s α: toughness = 0.929, strength = 0.918, optimism = 0.873).

Simplified coping styles questionnaire, SCSQ)

Positive coping styles were assessed using the 12-item Positive Coping Subscale from the Simple Coping Style Scale developed by Xie et al.46, with sample items such as “Seeking relief through work, study, or other activities.” Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never use, 4 = frequently use), with higher scores indicating stronger characteristics of positive coping. In the present study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.943) and sampling validity (KMO = 0.958, Bartlett’s test of sphericity: p < 0.001).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis of the study data were performed using SPSS 24.0 software, and the chain mediator model was developed and fitted to the chain mediator effect model using Amos 26.0, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Common method bias test

Since the data were obtained from the self-reports of the subject students, there may be an issue of common method bias that cannot be avoided. Therefore, after conducting an exploratory factor analysis using Harman’s one-factor test, it was found that the number of common factors with an extracted eigenvalue greater than one was one, and the variance explained by the first factor was 34.373%, which is less than the critical value of 40%. Thus, the common method bias is not significant.

Reliability test

The KMO test and Bartlett’s test of spherical were performed on the data, and the results showed that each scale passed the reliability test, which Cronbach’s alpha values all greater than 0.8, indicating that the overall reliability of each scale was excellent. The significance results of the Bartlett’s test sphericity showed that all values were less than 0.01, indicating that the reliability of the scales was high and met the standard of conducting factor analysis. Suppose verification

The associations among discrimination perceptions, psychological resilience, positive coping styles and subjective well-being were analyzed using structural equation modeling in AMOS 26.0. Mediation analyses examined the pathways through psychological resilience and positive coping styles. The model revealed significant associations among variables: discrimination perceptions was negatively associated with psychological resilience(β = -0.397, p<0.001), while psychological resilience showed positive associations with positive coping styles(β = 0.289, p<0.001) and subjective well-being (β = 0.347, p < 0.001). Discrimination perceptions were negatively associated with both positive coping styles(β = -0.178, p<0.05) and subjective well-being (β = -0.191, p <0.001). Additionally, positive coping styles demonstrated a positive association with subjective well-being (β = 0.492, p<0.001). Complete pathway analyses are presented in Table 5.

Test of mediated effects

The significance of the mediation effect was further tested using the bootstrapping mediation effect test of Preacher and Hayes (2008) (sample size set to 5,000 trials)48, with bias-corrected confidence intervals set to 95%. Mediation analyses identified three distinct indirect pathways with a total indirect effect of -0.281. The pathways were: ①Discrimination perceptions → psychological resilience → subjective well-being (indirect effect = -0.138, 49.11%); ②Discrimination perceptions → positive coping styles → subjective well-being(indirect effect = -0.087, 30.96%); ③Discrimination perceptions → psychological resilience → positive coping styles → subjective well-being (indirect effect = -0.056, 19.93%). The 95% confidence intervals for all indirect effects excluded zero, indicating significant mediating effects of psychological resilience and positive coping styles among college students with disabilities. Discussion

Subjective well-being serves as a critical indicator of psychological status, life quality, and satisfaction, reflecting individuals’ comprehensive self-evaluation49. For college students with disabilities, subjective well-being particularly influences both health outcomes and academic performance, especially when facing discriminatory. This study investigated the relationships between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities, examining the mediating effects of psychological resilience and positive coping styles. Analysis revealed negative associations between discrimination perceptions and psychological resilience, adaptive coping, and subjective well-being. Conversely, both psychological resilience and positive coping styles positive correlations with subjective well-being. Further analysis indicated that psychological resilience and positive coping styles independently mediate the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities. More importantly, psychological resilience and positive coping styles play a chain-mediated role in the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subject well-being of college students with disabilities.

The direct relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being of college students with disabilities

This study demonstrates an inverse relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities. Additionally, the data indicates a negative association between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being of college students with disabilities, supporting hypothesis 1. The results of the study were consistent with the rejection-identification model and previous research50, showing patterns similar to earlier studies that indicated decreased subjective well-being levels accompanying increased discrimination perceptions15. Discrimination perceptions, as a potential stressor, may be associated with elevated psychological stress and adverse emotional experiences among individuals with disabilities, potentially influencing their subjective well-being4. Understanding these relationships suggests that addressing discrimination perceptions among college students with disabilities could contribute to their subjective well-being and psychosocial development.

The mediating role of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience partially mediates the effect of discrimination perceptions on the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities, thus validating research hypothesis 2. According to reciprocal determinism, individuals with high levels of psychological resilience are more likely to remain emotionally stable in the face of discrimination perceptions, thereby effectively mitigating the negative effects of discrimination on mental health51, and thus maintaining higher levels of subjective well-being28,29. In addition, the compensatory model of psychological resilience suggests that psychological resilience, as a protective factor for maintaining mental health, mitigates the negative effects of threatening factors on individuals52. As a result, individuals with high psychological resilience usually demonstrate higher levels of self-adaptation and problem-solving abilities in the face of stress53.

The mediating role of positive coping styles

Positive coping styles partially mediate the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities, supporting hypothesis 3. Discrimination perceptions are linked to adverse emotional experiences and demonstrates an inverse association with subjective well-being among college students with disabilities15. Psychological Stress Theory posits that coping styles function as mediating variables in stress responses28. Evidence suggests that positive coping styles may buffer the adverse effects of discrimination perceptions on subjective well-being in children and adolescents, potentially through effective stress management and adaptive mental states54.

Chain mediation of psychological resilience and positive coping styles

This study demonstrates that psychological resilience and positive coping styles act as chain mediators in the relationship between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities, supporting hypothesis 4. The analysis reveals three pathways between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being: through the independent mediation of psychological resilience and positive coping styles, and through their chained mediation. According to the Organism-environment Interaction Model, individuals with distinct characteristics exhibit differential response patterns under identical environmental conditions29. In addition, the psychological resilience model posits that resilience functions as a crucial psychological resource, enabling individuals with high resilience to implement adaptive strategies efficiently. These individuals demonstrate enhanced problem-solving capabilities and maintain optimistic attitudes, effectively moderating negative emotional responses30. Based on the empirical findings, individuals exhibiting higher levels of psychological resilience demonstrate an increased tendency to utilize positive coping styles when confronted with discriminatory experiences39, which may be associated with reduced adverse effects on subjective well-being55.

Conclusions

This investigation reveals the complex interplay between discrimination perceptions and subjective well-being among college students with disabilities, mediated by psychological resilience and positive coping styles. By employing structural equation modeling, we validated a chain mediation model that elucidates the mechanisms underlying this relationship. Our findings provide robust evidence that psychological resilience and positive coping styles serve as chain mediators, establishing a clear pathway: Discrimination perceptions → psychological resilience → positive coping styles → subjective well-being. These results not only advance our theoretical understanding of psychological protective mechanisms but also provide empirically grounded insights for developing targeted interventions to enhance the subjective well-being of students with disabilities.

Applications value

This study holds significant theoretical and practical implications for understanding the factors influencing the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities. At the theoretical level, this investigation integrates Psychological Stress Theory28, Pressure-Cognitive Interaction Theory33, and the Rejection-Identification Model42, thereby deepening our understanding of the mechanisms linking discrimination perceptions to subjective well-being. Additionally, it enriches the theoretical frameworks of Relative Deprivation Theory14 and the compensatory model of psychological resilience52 in the context of psychological health research among college students with disabilities. At the practical level, this research offers significant implications for promoting the subjective well-being of college students with disabilities. First, addressing the negative impact of discrimination perceptions on subjective well-being, we recommend that higher education institutions establish anti-discrimination mechanisms and create inclusive, supportive campus environments through multicultural education initiatives to enhance understanding and acceptance. Second, the mediating role of psychological resilience suggests that educators should prioritize cultivating resilience among students with disabilities through mindfulness training and resilience-building workshops to enhance their capacity to face adversity56. The mediating effect of positive coping styles indicates the necessity to strengthen psychological counseling services, helping students master adaptive coping strategies and transform discriminatory experiences into opportunities for personal growth.

Limitations and prospectives

Despite the significant findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the exclusive reliance on self-report measures may introduce potential response bias and common method variance57. Future studies would benefit from incorporating both self-report and observer-rated assessments to enhance measurement objectivity. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships among variables. Longitudinal investigations are needed to establish temporal precedence and causal mechanisms underlying these associations. Additionally, the geographical constraint of sampling exclusively from Guangdong Province may limit the generalizability of our findings.
