Effect of Perinatal and Maternal Factors on Blood Pressure and Adiposity at Age 3
Authors: Katie Margolis, Ashley Trinh, Peter K Gregersen, Dorota Gruber, Christine B Sethna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe specific aim of the project was to evaluate the relationship between perinatal and maternal factors on childhood blood pressure (BP) and adiposity outcomes at Age 3. A retrospective case‐control study of 128 children and their mothers enrolled in the Generations Project was completed to examine the association between different perinatal and maternal factors on childhood BP and adiposity outcomes at Age 3. The Generations Project of the Northwell Health System is a longitudinal, observational, population‐based study following infants and their biological mothers. Using multivariable regression analysis, cesarean delivery (β = 0.07, CI = 0.04–0.10, p < 0.001) and small for gestational age (β = 0.07, CI = 0.002–0.13, p = 0.04) were individually associated with greater systolic blood pressure index during childhood. In adjusted regression analysis, IVF was associated with greater odds of elevated BP status (OR = 3.52, CI = 1.04–12.0, p = 0.04). Additionally, low birth weight (β = −0.86, CI = −1.64 to −0.08, p = 0.03) was associated with lower childhood body mass index Z‐scores. It can be concluded that adverse perinatal and maternal conditions can create a suboptimal intrauterine environment that is ideal for immediate survival but can alter the trajectory of the developing fetus and lead to negative health consequences. This study builds upon the existing research examining the effect different perinatal and maternal conditions have on childhood health and expands on the limited research investigating the potential impact cesarean delivery and in vitro fertilization have on offspring BP and adiposity.

Introduction

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease is the field of study that seeks to understand how early life environmental conditions can affect a critical window of development and long‐term health outcomes [1]. In particular, the intrauterine environment is crucial for optimizing fetal growth. Adverse maternal and gestational conditions can create a suboptimal intrauterine setting that is ideal for immediate survival but changes the trajectory of the developing fetus through epigenetic modification [2]. These modifications have been shown to alter nephron endowment, oxidative stress, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), and sodium transportation [1, 3]. However, the fetal programming response can be maladaptive and increase risk for chronic disease later in life [2, 3].

There are many perinatal and maternal factors that have been identified and associated with a higher likelihood of abnormal blood pressure (BP) and adiposity. These conditions are both increasingly important to identify and address as they are linked to numerous adverse health outcomes such as diabetes mellitus (DM), liver dysfunction, obstructive sleep apnea, orthopedic pain, ophthalmologic changes, and left ventricular hypertrophy [4, 5]. Low birth weight (LBW) is a well‐established risk factor for developing hypertension [6, 7]. In addition, a study conducted by Lawlor et al. found that maternal age, maternal body mass index (BMI), and smoking during pregnancy are associated with elevated systolic BP in childhood (2004) [8]. In relation to various factors influencing childhood adiposity, a comprehensive meta‐analysis demonstrated that breastfeeding is inversely associated with obesity [9]. Conversely, elevated BP and smoking during pregnancy have been linked to childhood obesity [10, 11].

Cesarean delivery rates are consistently rising worldwide, with it now being one of the most frequently performed surgeries in the United States. From 1970 to 2016, the rate of cesarean delivery increased from 5% in 1970 to 31.9% in 2016 [12]. If completed under appropriate indications, cesarean delivery has been known to prevent maternal and neonatal illness and death. However, cesarean delivery can also render the newborn at risk for potential adverse health outcomes [13]. Several health consequences have been identified in children born via cesarean delivery, including a higher likelihood of developing respiratory tract infections and asthma when compared to those born via vaginal delivery [14]. Despite these findings, only a few studies have focused on investigating the connection between cesarean delivery and BP in offspring. Ferraro et al. found that cesarean delivery increased the odds of having hypertension in young adults even when adjusted for confounders (2019) [15]. However, additional research is needed to further support these findings. Furthermore, there have been mixed results on the relationship between cesarean delivery and childhood obesity [14, 16, 17, 18].

In vitro fertilization (IVF) has also become an increasingly prevalent treatment for infertility. However, the specific health outcomes of children conceived by IVF are not well‐studied. Evidence suggests children born via IVF are at higher risk for visual impairment, autism spectrum disorder, hospitalizations, and being stillborn [19, 20, 21]. However, there are few studies investigating the effect of IVF on offspring health outcomes, and current results are mixed. One study found systolic and diastolic BP, as well as triglyceride levels, to be significantly increased in children conceived by IVF compared to spontaneously conceived children [22]. Using animal models, Rexhaj et al. demonstrated that mice conceived by IVF had significantly increased endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, hypertension, and premature death when compared to controls [23]. Interestingly, the IVF mice were also shown to have methylation of genes implicated in vascular nitric oxide (NO) production, a well‐known vasodilator involved in mediating kidney physiology and regulating blood pressure [24]. Despite these findings, further investigation is necessary to define the effect of IVF on offspring BP. Similarly, there are a few studies investigating the relationship between IVF and offspring adiposity. It has been demonstrated that children conceived by IVF are at increased risk for LBW and have higher peripheral body mass and body fat compared to other children [25, 26].

The aim of this study was to build upon the existing limited research investigating the potential impact of cesarean delivery and conception via IVF on offspring BP and adiposity. Furthermore, this study delved deeper into the impact of perinatal and maternal factors (smoking, obesity, elevated BP/hypertension, DM, and birth weight) on offspring BP and adiposity through a retrospective cohort study that followed a diverse population. Understanding the relationships between delivery and conception modality and childhood BP and adiposity will allow for enhanced monitoring and intervention, ultimately leading to a reduced risk of developing cardiovascular disease later in life.

Methods

Patient Population

The Generations Project of the Northwell Health System is a longitudinal, observational, population‐based study of infants born at Long Island Jewish Medical Center, North Shore University Hospital, or Lenox Hill Hospital, and their biological mothers. These hospitals draw from a diverse population base in Queens Borough, Brooklyn Borough, Manhattan, and Nassau County in New York. Approximately, 600 nuclear families (mother and newborn(s)) were enrolled and followed annually beginning in 2017. Obstetricians served as the primary source of referral for families prior to the birth of the newborn(s). A retrospective chart review of 128 children and their mothers enrolled in the Generations Project was conducted to evaluate the effect of perinatal and maternal factors on childhood BP and adiposity at Age 3. A total of 472 participants were excluded due to loss to follow‐up, failure to attend the 3‐year well‐child appointment, and/or missing blood pressure measurement at the appointment (Figure 1). This ancillary study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Northwell Health. Children followed outside of Northwell Health, and those who were born with congenital malformations including cardiac and kidney abnormalities, were excluded from analysis.

Participant flow diagram.

Perinatal and Maternal Exposures

Perinatal exposures of interest included mode of delivery (cesarean vs. vaginal), mode of conception (IVF vs. not IVF), birth weight, and birth weight for gestational age. LBW was defined as birth weight <2500 g. High birth weight (HBW) was defined as >4000 g. Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as birth weight <10th percentile of newborns of the same gestational age at birth. Large for gestational age (LGA) was defined as birth weight >90th percentile of newborns of the same gestational age at birth (Table 1).

Maternal exposures investigated included self‐identified race, obesity, smoking status, DM, and BP. Maternal obesity was defined as BMI ≥30 [27]. Childhood Outcome Measures

Child height, weight, and BP readings were extracted from the medical records of individuals under the care of the Northwell Health System. Only a single BP measurement was documented in the medical record at the time of evaluation. BP was obtained using an oscillometric device. BP outcomes included casual systolic and diastolic BP, elevated BP, and hypertensive BP at Age 3. BP was indexed to the 95th percentile for age, sex, and height. Elevated BP was defined as SBP or DBP 90%–95% for age, sex, and height. Hypertensive BP was defined as casual SBP or DBP ≥95% for age, sex, and height [29]. Child adiposity outcomes included BMI Z‐scores and obesity status at Age 3. Obesity was defined as BMI >95th percentile [30] (Table 3).

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Data on race, insurance type (private vs. public/no insurance), education of the mother, sex of offspring, and mode of delivery (cesarean vs. vaginal) and mode of conception (IVF or not IVF) were collected. The rates of LBWs, HBWs, births SGA, and births LGA were also collected.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics included medians with interquartile range for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables to describe demographic and clinical characteristics of the maternal–child dyads. Linear regressions were used to determine the relationship between individual perinatal and maternal factors and SBP index, DBP index, and BMI Z‐score (Table 4). Logistic regressions were used to determine the association between individual perinatal and maternal factors and childhood elevated BP, hypertensive BP, and obesity (Table 5). Models were adjusted for the following variables, chosen a priori based on previous literature review and clinical judgment: child sex, child race, maternal education, and insurance. All assumptions for the regression models were met. Multiple imputation using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method was used for missing values. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and two‐sided tests of hypotheses were used throughout. SPSS 28.0 (IBM SPSS) statistical software was used to conduct the analyses.

Results

Demographic Information

There were 128 mother–child dyads analyzed for this study with a wide distribution of race amongst the participants. Cesarean delivery accounted for 40% of deliveries. Among the cohort, 14.8% of children were conceived via IVF. There was an equal dispersion of male and female children. Having LBW was twice as common as HBW. Less than 15% of participants met criteria for SGA or LGA (Table 1). An even distribution was seen for private and public/no insurance. The majority of mothers had an education beyond a high school degree. Obesity was present in over 25% of mothers, while DM, hypertension, and positive smoking history were present in less than a quarter of mothers (Table 2).

Blood Pressure

Average systolic and diastolic BP indices were 0.87 (SD = 0.09) and 0.88 (SD = 0.12) for children at Age 3, respectively. It was found that 25.8% of children had elevated BP and 17.2% of children had hypertensive BP (Table 3). In multivariable regression analysis, cesarean delivery (beta = 0.07, CI = 0.04–0.10, p < 0.001) and being born SGA (beta = 0.07, CI = 0.002–0.13, p = 0.04) were individually associated with greater SBP index at Age 3 (Table 4 and Figure 2). In adjusted regression analysis, IVF was associated with greater odds of elevated BP status (OR = 3.52, CI = 1.04–12.0, p = 0.04; Table 5 and Figure 3). No other perinatal or maternal factors were associated with changes in SBP or DBP index, elevated BP, or hypertensive BP in childhood.

Adiposity

Average BMI Z‐score of the children was 0.17 (SD = 1.31) at Age 3. Of the children, 11.6% were overweight, and 12.5% were obese (Table 3). In multivariable regression analysis, LBW (beta = −0.86, CI = −1.64 to −0.08, p = 0.03) was associated with lower BMI Z‐scores at Age 3 (Table 4 and Figure 4). No other perinatal or maternal factors were associated with childhood BMI Z‐score or obesity.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, cesarean delivery, conception via IVF, and being born SGA were found to be associated with greater systolic BP at Age 3. Regarding adiposity, LBW was associated with lower BMI Z‐scores at Age 3. The findings may be explained by the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease. Based on this theory, the growing fetus undergoes developmental programming in order to adapt to changes and stressors presented to its environment [1]. The fetal modifications that take place can be maladaptive and lead to issues with BP and obesity for the offspring later in life [2, 3]. Hypertension and obesity are well‐established risk factors for cardiovascular disease, which is difficult and expensive to treat. The results of this study suggest that specific perinatal and maternal conditions are linked to adverse BP and adiposity outcomes, underscoring the importance of identifying and improving these factors for maternal health before and during pregnancy.

Children born via cesarean delivery showed a notable rise in SBP compared to those born vaginally, a phenomenon that could be attributed to the absence of exposure to the maternal vaginal canal microbiome and feces. Using animal models, a study by Lin et al. found that several microorganisms, including Lactobacillus, a common bacterium established in intestinal tracts and vaginal canals, promote production of butyrate (2020) [31]. Significantly decreased fecal levels of butyrate were found in hypertensive patients when compared to normotensive patients [32]. In addition, the presence of Lactobacillus has been positively linked with lower BP variability [33]. The association between lack of butyrate and hypertension may be mediated through endothelial dysfunction [34]. As the utilization of cesarean delivery rises, it becomes more imperative to investigate its health outcomes on children.

Fetal programming and environmental stressors may be responsible for the relationship between IVF and BP abnormalities. Maternal factors such as smoking, BMI, and nutrition have been linked to epigenetic modifications in offspring, such as DNA methylation [35, 36]. Alterations in the NO pathway may be one mechanism that contributes to BP issues. Using animal models, mice conceived by IVF were shown to have methylation of the gene eNOS, which correlated with decreased vascular eNOS expression and decreased NO synthesis, increased endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, hypertension, and early death compared to the control mice [23]. Several human studies have demonstrated that children conceived by IVF had vascular abnormalities, including increased carotid thickness and increased pulse wave velocity [37, 38].

The study also revealed that being born SGA was linked to a higher SBP index at the age of 3. Similarly, a study by Weres et al. found that children who were born SGA had an increased risk for primary hypertension (2019) [39]. This finding may be due to lower birth weight being associated with abnormal vasculature and endothelial dysfunction [40]. Conversely, a meta‐analysis showed no substantial difference in SGA preterm infants and non‐SGA births [41].

Children who were born with LBW were observed to have lower BMI Z‐scores at Age 3. This finding is contradictory to prior studies that have established that LBW increases risk for obesity [42]. The relationship is mediated by changes in nephron endowment, increased RAAS activity, metabolism regulation, and sympathetic hyperactivity [39, 40, 43]. Casey et al. demonstrated that the prevalence of obesity in LBW patients increased as the children aged (2012) [44]. The findings in this study showing that LBW was found to be associated with lower BMI Z‐scores at Age 3, which can be attributed to the metabolic and endocrine outcomes of LBW being more prominent in middle‐late childhood rather than early childhood.

There were several limitations of this study. We utilized a single health system and a small sample size. However, our cohort comprises a diverse population from various facilities across the New York metropolitan area, utilizing a longitudinal design. Additional limitations include the observational design of the study as opposed to an experimental design. In addition, BP was measured only once at the well‐child visits using an oscillometric device instead of ambulatory BP monitoring. The associations observed in this study were characterized by wide confidence intervals, indicating limited precision and possible model instability. Future studies with larger samples are needed to improve the stability of these estimates. This study experienced substantial loss to follow‐up, which may be related to the COVID‐19 pandemic, as many participants reached the 3‐year follow‐up period during 2020 when in‐person clinical visits were significantly disrupted. Further, variables such as maternal age, gestational age, and postnatal factors were not collected. Therefore, these variables could not be adjusted for in the analysis. For future research efforts, it is essential to gather a larger sample size to examine how various types of maternal hypertension (such as gestational, chronic, pre‐eclampsia, eclampsia) and different reasons for cesarean delivery (such as repeat/scheduled, emergent) impact childhood BP and adiposity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that different perinatal and maternal factors are associated with changes in childhood BP and adiposity at Age 3. Uncovering the conditions that shape the intrauterine environment for the growing fetus has profound implications for closer monitoring and intervention in children who are at higher risk for hypertension and obesity. Both of these cardiometabolic conditions have been described as a trans‐generational vicious cycle, as indicated by the rising prevalence over time [45]. To address the increasing prevalence of hypertension and obesity, it is important to explore these factors and develop more effective strategies for managing elevated BP and obesity in children. Specifically, enhancing maternal and perinatal health presents a critical opportunity to prevent the occurrence of these adverse health outcomes in future generations.
