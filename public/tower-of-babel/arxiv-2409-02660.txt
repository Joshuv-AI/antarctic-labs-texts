A min-max random game on a graph that is not a tree
Natalia Cardona-Tobón, Anja Sturm, Jan M. Swart

Abstract
We study a random game in which two players in turn play a fixed number of moves. For each move, there are two possible choices. To each possible outcome of the game we assign a winner in an i.i.d. fashion with a fixed parameter p p . In the case where all different game histories lead to different outcomes, a classical result due to Pearl (1980) says that in the limit when the number of moves is large, there is a sharp threshold in the parameter p p that separates the regimes in which either player has with high probability a winning strategy. We are interested in a modification of this game where the outcome is determined by the exact sequence of moves played by the first player and by the number of times the second player has played each of the two possible moves. We show that also in this case, there is a sharp threshold in the parameter p p that separates the regimes in which either player has with high probability a winning strategy. Since in the modified game, different game histories can lead to the same outcome, the graph associated with the game is no longer a tree which means independence is lost. As a result, the analysis becomes more complicated and open problems remain.

Main results

Imagine now that we change the game in such a way that for the outcome of the game, the whole history of the moves played by Alice is relevant as before, but all that matters of Bob’s moves is the total number of times he plays each of the two possible moves. In this case, there are

It seems that if for the outcome of a game, all that matters is how often a player has played each of the two possible moves, then this player has less influence on the outcome compared to the game in which the whole history of moves played by this player matters. It is natural to conjecture that this gives such a player a disadvantage. Our first result partially confirms this.

[

]

]

P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }p<1/64,\\
1&\mbox{if }p>15/16.\end{array}\right.

1.2
The graph structure of the games

To describe the graph structure of our games of interest, we first introduce some general notation. Recall that a directed graph is a pair
(G,\vec{E})
where
G
is a set whose elements are called vertices and
\vec{E}
is a subset of
G\times G
whose elements are called directed edges. By a slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes use
G
as a shorthand for
(G,\vec{E})
, when it is clear how the set of directed edges is defined. We say that
(G,\vec{E})
is finite if
G
is a finite set. For
v,w\in G
we write
v\leadsto w
if there exist
v=v_{0},\ldots,v_{n}=w
such that
(v_{k-1},v_{k})\in\vec{E}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. We let
{\rm dist}(v,w)
denote the smallest integer
n\geq 0
for which such
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n}
exist. We say that
G
is
acyclic
if there do not exist
v,w\in G
with
v\neq w
such that
v\leadsto w\leadsto v
. For
v\in G
, we adopt the notation
{\cal O}(v):=\{w\in V:(v,w)\in\vec{E}\}
and we write

\ring{G}:=\big\{v\in V:{\cal O}(v)\neq\emptyset\big\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\partial G:=\big\{v\in V:{\cal O}(v)=\emptyset\big\}.

(1.4)

Quite generally, we can describe the graph structure of a finite turn-based game played by two players by means of a quadruple
(G,\vec{E},0,\tau)
where

(i)

(G,\vec{E})
is a finite acyclic directed graph,

(ii)

0\in G
satisfies
0\leadsto v
for all
v\in G
,

(iii)

\tau:\ring{G}\to\{1,2\}
is a function.

For the sake of the exposition, let us call such a quadruple a
game-graph
. Slightly abusing the notation, we will sometimes write
G
as shorthand for
(G,\vec{E},0,\tau)
, when it is clear how
G
is equipped with the structure of a game-graph. We call
0
the
root
. We interpret
G
as the set of possible states of the game,
0
as the state at the beginning of the game,
\partial G
as the set of possible outcomes of the game,
\ring{G}
as the non-final states of the game, and
\tau
as a function that tells us whose turn it is in each non-final state of the game. We write

\ring{G}^{1}:=\big\{v\in\ring{G}:\tau(v)=1\big\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ring{G}^{2}:=\big\{v\in\ring{G}:\tau(v)=2\big\}.

(1.5)

A (pure) strategy for Alice is a function

\ring{G}^{1}\ni v\mapsto\sigma_{1}(v)\in{\cal O}(v),

(1.6)

which has the interpretation that in the state
v
, Alice always plays the move that makes the game progress to the state
\sigma_{1}(v)
. Likewise, a strategy for Bob is a function
\ring{G}^{2}\ni v\mapsto\sigma_{2}(v)\in{\cal O}(v)
. We let
{\cal S}_{1}
(resp.
{\cal S}_{2}
) denote the set of all strategies for Alice (resp. Bob). Given a strategy
\sigma_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}
of Alice and a strategy
\sigma_{2}\in{\cal S}_{2}
of Bob, there is a unique sequence of vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n}\in G
with
v_{0}=0
and
v_{n}\in\partial G
such that for each
0\leq k<n
one has
v_{k+1}=\sigma_{1}(v_{k})
if
v_{k}\in\ring{G}^{1}
and
v_{k+1}=\sigma_{2}(v_{k})
if
v_{k}\in\ring{G}^{2}
. Then

o(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}):=v_{n}

(1.7)

is the outcome of the game if Alice plays strategy
\sigma_{1}
and Bob plays strategy
\sigma_{2}
. Given a function
x:\partial G\to\{0,1\}
that assigns a winner to each possible outcome, with
x(v)=0
meaning that Alice is the winner and
x(v)=1
meaning that Bob is the winner, we say that a strategy
\sigma_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}
is a
winning strategy
for Alice given
x
if

x\big(o(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\big)=0\qquad\forall\sigma_{2}\in{\cal S}_{2}.

(1.8)

Winning strategies for Bob are defined similarly (with
1
instead of
0
). To find out who has a winning strategy given
x
, we can work our way back from the set of all possible outcomes to the initial state of the game. Given a function
x:\partial G\to\{0,1\}
that assigns a winner to each possible outcome, we can define
\overline{x}:\partial G\cup\ring{G}\to\{0,1\}
by setting
\overline{x}(v):=x(v)
for
v\in\partial G
and then defining inductively

\overline{x}(v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\bigwedge_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{x}(w)&\mbox{if }v\in\ring{G}^{1},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\bigvee_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{x}(w)&\mbox{if }v\in\ring{G}^{2}.\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

Then it is not hard to see that Alice (resp. Bob) has a winning strategy if and only if
\overline{x}(0)=0
(resp.
\overline{x}(0)=1
). We let
L:\{0,1\}^{\partial G}\to\{0,1\}
denote the monotone Boolean function defined as

L(x):=\overline{x}(0)\quad\mbox{where $\overline{x}$ solves (\ref{minmax}) with $\overline{x}=x$ on $\partial G$.}

(1.10)

Then
L(x)=0
(resp.
=1
) if Alice (resp. Bob) has a winning strategy given
x
.

The game-graphs of the games that we are interested in have a special structure: the players alternate turns and play a fixed number of turns. Game graphs with various numbers of turns are consistent, in the sense that the game-graph of the game with
n
turns can be obtained by truncating a certain infinite graph at height
n

Let us define a
decision graph
to be a triple
(D,\vec{F},0)
such that

(i)

(D,\vec{F})
is a directed graph,

(ii)

0\in D
satisfies
0\leadsto w
for all
w\in D

(iii)

|w|=|v|+1
for all
(v,w)\in\vec{F}

|w|:={\rm dist}(0,w)

(w\in D)
,

(iv)

{\cal O}(w)\neq\emptyset

w\in D

Note that (iii) implies that
(D,\vec{F})
is acyclic. For a decision graph
D

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\partial_{n}D:=\big\{w\in D:|w|=n\big\},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\langle D\rangle_{n}:=\big\{w\in D:|w|<n\big\},\quad\mbox{and}\quad[D]_{n}:=\big\{w\in D:|w|\leq n\big\}.\end{array}

(1.11)

Given a decision graph
D
and integer
n\geq 1

G_{n}(D)=(G_{n},\vec{E}_{n},0,\tau)

(1.12)

by setting

G_{n}:=[D]_{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\vec{E}_{n}:=\big\{(v,w)\in G_{n}\times G_{n}:(v,w)\in\vec{F}\big\},

(1.13)

which has the effect that
\ring{G}_{n}=\langle D\rangle_{n}

, and then defining

\tau(v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 1&\displaystyle\mbox{if }|v|\mbox{ is even,}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle 2&\displaystyle\mbox{if }|v|\mbox{ is odd.}\end{array}\right.\qquad(v\in\ring{G}_{n}).

(1.14)

This corresponds to a game where Alice and Bob take turns to play
n
moves together, and Alice starts. For the games we are interested in,
D
has a product structure, in the sense that elements of

are of the form
(a,b)
where
a
and

record all that is relevant of Alice’s and Bob’s moves up to this point in the game. To formalise this, given two decision graphs
(D^{i},\vec{F}^{i},0^{i})

(i=1,2

\left.\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}c@{\,}l}\displaystyle\partial_{2n}D&:=&\displaystyle\big\{(a,b):a\in\partial_{n}D^{1},\ b\in\partial_{n}D^{2}\big\},\\[5.0pt]

(1.15)

and

\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}c@{\,}l}\displaystyle\vec{F}&:=&\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\{\big((a,b),(a^{\prime},b)\big):(a,b)\in\partial_{2n}D,\ (a,a^{\prime})\in\vec{F}^{1}\big\}\\[5.0pt]
&&\displaystyle\cup\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\{\big((a,b),(a,b^{\prime})\big):(a,b)\in\partial_{2n+1}D,\ (b,b^{\prime})\in\vec{F}^{2}\big\}.\end{array}

(1.16)

The game-graphs of the games

1.3
Cellular automata

In Subsection
1.1
we gave a somewhat informal description of the functions
P^{\rm AB}_{n}
,
P^{\rm Ab}_{n}
,
P^{\rm aB}_{n}
, and
P^{\rm ab}_{n}
in terms of winning strategies. In formula (
1.21
) of Subsection
1.2

Let
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
be the set of functions
x:{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
. Given a random variable
X_{0}
with values in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
, we will be interested in a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that evolves in a deterministic way. At even times
t>0
, we define
X_{t}
in terms of
X_{t-1}
according to one of the following two rules

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad}l}{\rm A}.&\displaystyle X_{t}(i,j)=X_{t-1}(2i,j)\wedge X_{t-1}(2i+1,j),\\[5.0pt]
{\rm a.}&\displaystyle X_{t}(i,j)=X_{t-1}(i,j)\wedge X_{t-1}(i+1,j),\end{array}\qquad\big((i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\big),

(1.22)

and at odd times
t>0
, we define
X_{t}
according to one of the rules

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad}l}{\rm B}.&\displaystyle X_{t}(i,j)=X_{t-1}(i,2j)\vee X_{t-1}(i,2j+1),\\[5.0pt]

1.4
Discussion and open problems

The game-graph of Pearl’s
[
Pea80
]
original game
{\rm AB}_{n}(p)
is the deterministic tree
G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}})
. Several authors have studied variants of the game
{\rm AB}_{n}(p)
where this tree is replaced by a random tree. Martin and Stasiński in
[
MS18
]
studied min-max games on a Galton-Watson tree, truncated at some height
2n
. Pemantle and Ward in
[
PW06
]
studied a model on a binary tree where i.i.d. random variables attached to the nodes determine whether the minimum or maximum function should be applied. We refer to Broutin and Mailler
[
BM18
]
for an overview of the literature on this type of problems.

For games with a large number of moves, it may be computationally unfeasible to find a winning strategy. This motivates the study of efficient algorithms that can play games near optimally. Indeed, this was the main motivation for Pearl
[
Pea80
]
to study the game
{\rm AB}_{n}(p)
. There is an extensive literature on this topic. We cite
[
DG95
]
for a somewhat older overview and
[
SN15
,
MS18
]
for more recent contributions.

When trying to play a game near optimally, a natural approach is to find all possible states that can be reached after a fixed number
N
of moves, then attach a utility to each possible state using some statistical procedure, and finally use minimaxing in the spirit of (
1.9
) to compute utility values for all states that lie less than
N
moves in the future. Surprisingly, it was discovered by Nau
[
Nau80
]
that when the game-graph is a tree, such algorithms can sometimes behave pathologically, in the sense that increasing
N
leads to worse, rather than better game playing. This sort of pathology has been the subject of much further research
[
LBG12
]
.

An early suggestion, that has not been pursued much, is that this sort of pathologies may be resolved if different game histories can lead to the same outcome
[
Nau83
]
. When the game no longer has the structure of a tree, however, independence is lost. For this reason, such games are harder to analyse and much less is known. There seems to be a general agreement, however, that introducing dependence may resolve game-playing pathology. For this reason, it would seem interesting to study game-playing algorithms for the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
introduced in the present paper.

Our own motivation for studying these games comes from a different direction, which is related to the cellular automata from Subsection
1.3
. Attractive spin systems have two special invariant laws, called the lower and upper invariant laws, that are the long-time limit laws starting from the all zero and all one initial states
[
Lig85
, Thm III.2.3]
. If the dynamics of the spin system are invariant under automorphisms of the lattice (such as translations on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
), then it is natural to ask if all invariant laws that are moreover invariant under automorphisms of the lattice are convex combinations of the lower and upper invariant laws. Frequently, one finds that mean-field models have an additional “intermediate” invariant law that lies between the lower and upper invariant laws, but very little is known about the existence of such intermediate invariant laws in a truly spatial setting. A notable exception are stochastic Ising models, where such intermediate invariant laws are known to exist if the lattice is a Cayley tree
[
Geo11
, Section 12.2]
but not on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. (The latter follows from
[
Bod06
]
(in dimensions
d\geq 3
) and
[
Hig81
]
(in dimension
d=2
) combined with
[
Lig85
, Thm IV.5.12]
; see also the discussion on
[
FV18
, page 166]
).

The cellular automaton
X^{\rm AB}
from Subsection
1.3
is a mean-field model.
3
3

3

The cellular automaton
X^{\rm AB}
, started from an i.i.d. law, remains i.i.d. for all time. This is characteristic of mean-field models (sometimes called “propagation of chaos”) and is closely related to the tree structure of the “genealogy” of
X^{\rm AB}
. Compare the relation between recursive tree processes and mean-field limits in
[
MSS20
]
.
It has an intermediate invariant law, which is the product measure with intensity
p_{\rm c}^{{\rm AB}}
. Our original plan was to prove that the cellular automata
X^{\rm Ab}
and
X^{\rm aB}
also have an intermediate invariant law, but this is still unresolved. This is closely related to the question about the limit behaviour of
P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
at
p=p^{\rm Ab}_{\rm c}
. We have shown that this quantity does not tend to zero. It seems there are two plausible scenarios: either this quantity tends to one and the cellular automaton
X^{\rm Ab}
has no intermediate invariant law, or it tends to a value strictly between zero and one and such an intermediate invariant law exists.

Another open problem concerns the size of the “critical window” where the function
P^{\rm Ab}_{n}
changes from a value close to zero to a value close to one. In Theorem
14
below, we prove an upper bound of order
1/n
, but it is doubtful this is sharp, given that for the game
{\rm AB}_{n}(p)
the size of the critical window decreases exponentially in
n
. For the mean-field game
{\rm AB}_{n}(p)
, a detailed analysis of the critical behaviour, which involves a nontrivial limit law, has been carried out in
[
ADN05
]
.

There are lots of open problems concerning the game
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
. Below Proposition
1
we already mentioned the conjecture that
P^{ab}_{n}(p)\leq P^{aB}_{n}(p)
and
P^{ab}_{n}(p)\geq P^{Ab}_{n}(p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
and
n\geq 1
. Another obvious conjecture is the existence of a critical value
p^{\rm ab}_{\rm c}
such that

P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }p<p^{\rm ab}_{\rm c},\\
1&\mbox{if }p>p^{\rm ab}_{\rm c}.\end{array}\right.

(1.34)

Our proof of Theorem
2
uses the special structure of the game-graphs associated with the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
in two places: namely, in the preparatory Lemmas
15
and
18
we use that the game-graphs have a product structure with one component a binary tree. Based on numerical simulations, we conjecture that a critical value
p^{\rm ab}_{\rm c}
as in (
1.34
) exists and that the cellular automaton
X^{\rm ab}
started in product law with this intensity converges as time tends to infinity to a convex combination of the delta-measures on the all-zero and all-one states. Based on this, we conjecture that the cellular automaton

has no intermediate invariant law.

\forall A^{\prime}\in{\cal A}(L\circ\Psi)\ \exists A\in{\cal A}(L)\mbox{ s.t.\ }A^{\prime}\subset\psi(A).

(2.11)

Indeed, for each
y\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, one has

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\exists A^{\prime}\in{\cal A}(L\circ\Psi)\mbox{ s.t.\ }y(j)=1\ \forall j\in A^{\prime}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad L\circ\Psi(y)=1\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad L(y\circ\psi)=1\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists A\in{\cal A}(L)\mbox{ s.t.\ }y\big(\psi(i)\big)=1\ \forall i\in A\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists A\in{\cal A}(L)\mbox{ s.t.\ }y(j)=1\ \forall j\in\psi(A),\end{array}

(2.12)

and the implication
\Rightarrow
here can only hold for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{T}
if (
2.11
) is satisfied.

We now prove that (
2.1
) implies (
2.10
). We can without loss of generality assume that
\psi
is surjective. Then we can find an integer
n\geq 0
, finite sets
S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}
with
S_{0}=S
and
S_{n}=T
, and pair contractions
\phi_{k}:S_{k-1}\to S_{k}

(1\leq k\leq n)
, such that
\psi=\phi_{n}\circ\cdots\phi_{1}
. For each
0\leq k\leq n
, let
L_{k}:\{0,1\}^{S_{k}}\to\{0,1\}
and
\psi_{k}:S\to S_{k}
be defined as

L_{k}:=L\circ\Psi_{k}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\psi_{k}:=\phi_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Psi_{k}(x):=x\circ\psi_{k}\qquad\big(x\in\{0,1\}^{S_{k}}\big),

(2.13)

where
\psi_{0}
is the identity map. For each
0\leq k\leq n
, let
X_{k}=(X_{k}(i))_{i\in S_{k}}
be an i.i.d. collection of Boolean random variables with intensity
q
. We will prove (
2.10
) by showing that

{\mathbb{P}}\big[L\circ\Psi(Y)=1\big]={\mathbb{P}}\big[L_{n}(X_{n})=1\big]\leq\cdots\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big[L_{0}(X_{0})=1\big]={\mathbb{P}}\big[L(X)=1\big].

(2.14)

Since
L_{k}=L_{k-1}\circ\Phi_{k}

(1\leq k\leq n)
with
\Phi_{k}(x):=x\circ\phi_{k}
, it suffices to show that

{\mathbb{P}}\big[L_{k-1}\circ\Phi_{k}(X_{k})=1\big]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big[L_{k-1}(X_{k-1})=1\big]\qquad(1\leq k\leq n).

(2.15)

Since
\phi_{k}
is a pair contraction, by what we have already proved, it suffices to show that

\big|\phi_{k}(A^{\prime})\big|=\big|A^{\prime}\big|\qquad\forall A^{\prime}\in{\cal A}(L_{k-1}).

(2.16)

The assumption (
2.1
) implies that
|\psi_{n}(A)|=|\psi(A)|=|A|=|\psi_{0}(A)|
and hence
|\psi_{0}(A)|=\cdots=|\psi_{n}(A)|
for all
A\in{\cal A}(L)
. In view of (
2.11
), for each
A^{\prime}\in{\cal A}(L_{k-1})
, there exists an
A\in{\cal A}(L)
such that
A^{\prime}\subset\psi_{k-1}(A)
. The fact that
|\phi_{k}\circ\psi_{k-1}(A)|=|\psi_{k}(A)|=|\psi_{k-1}(A)|
now implies (
2.16
) and the proof is complete.

Lemma 7 (Strategies and zero-sets)

Let

(2.19)

Proof
By symmetry, it suffices to prove the statement for zero-sets. We claim that

{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L)=\big\{Z\subset\partial G:\exists Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\mbox{ s.t.\ }Z^{\prime}\subset Z\big\}.

(2.20)

Indeed, using the fact that
L(x)=0
if and only if Alice has a winning strategy given

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle Z\in{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad L(x)=0\mbox{ with }x(v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }v\in Z\\
1&\mbox{if }v\not\in Z\end{array}\right.\\
\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mbox{Alice has a winning strategy given $x$}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists\sigma_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}\mbox{ s.t.\ }x(v)=0\ \forall v\in Z(\sigma_{1})\\
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\mbox{ s.t.\ }x(v)=0\ \forall x\in Z^{\prime}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\mbox{ s.t.\ }Z^{\prime}\subset Z,\end{array}

which proves (
2.20
). As an immediate consequence, we obtain

Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\quad\Rightarrow\quad Z^{\prime}:=Z\mbox{ satisfies }Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\mbox{ and }Z^{\prime}\subset Z\quad\Rightarrow\quad Z\in{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L),

i.e.,
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\subset{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L)
.

To prove that
{\cal Z}(L)\subset{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}

. Since

Lemma 8 (Projection property)

Let
(D,\vec{F},0)
and
(D^{\prime},\vec{F}^{\prime},0^{\prime})
be decision graphs and assume that
\ell:D\to D^{\prime}
satisfies

\ell(0)=0^{\prime}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{$\ell$ maps ${\cal O}(v)$ surjectively into ${\cal O}(\ell(v))$ for all $v\in D$}.

(2.21)

Let
n\geq 1
and let
L_{n}
and
L^{\prime}_{n}
be the Boolean functions defined as in (
1.10
) in terms of the game-graphs
G_{n}(D)
and
G_{n}(D^{\prime})
, respectively. Then for each
n\geq 1
,
\ell
maps
\partial G_{n}(D)
into
\partial G_{n}(D^{\prime})
, and

L^{\prime}_{n}(x)=L_{n}(x\circ\ell)\qquad\big(x\in\{0,1\}^{\partial G_{n}(D^{\prime})}\big),

(2.22)

where
x\circ\ell\in\{0,1\}^{\partial G_{n}(D)}
is defined as
x\circ\ell(v):=x\big(\ell(v)\big)

\big(v\in\partial G_{n}(D)\big)
.

Proof
It is easy to see that (
2.21
) implies that
\big|\ell(v)\big|=|v|

(v\in D)
. As a result,
\ell
maps
\partial G_{n}(D)=\partial_{n}D
into
\partial G_{n}(D^{\prime})=\partial_{n}D^{\prime}
. We can inductively extend the function
x:\partial_{n}D^{\prime}\to\{0,1\}
to a function
\overline{x}:[D^{\prime}]_{n}\to\{0,1\}
such that for all
v\in\langle D^{\prime}\rangle_{n}
,

\overline{x}(v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\bigwedge_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{x}(w)&\mbox{if $|v|$ is even,}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\bigvee_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{x}(w)&\mbox{if $|v|$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

(2.23)

Then
L^{\prime}_{n}(x)=\overline{x}(0^{\prime})
. Define
\overline{y}:[D]_{n}\to\{0,1\}
by
\overline{y}(v):=\overline{x}\big(\ell(v)\big)

(v\in[D]_{n})
. Then as a result of (
2.21
), we see that
\overline{y}
satisfies the inductive relation

\overline{y}(v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\bigwedge_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{y}(w)&\mbox{if $|v|$ is even,}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\bigvee_{w\in{\cal O}(v)}\overline{y}(w)&\mbox{if $|v|$ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

(2.24)

Letting
y
denote the restriction of
\overline{y}

L_{n}(x\circ\ell)=L_{n}(y)=\overline{y}(0)=\overline{x}\big(\ell(0)\big)=\overline{x}(0^{\prime})=L^{\prime}_{n}(x).

(2.25)

An example of a function satisfying (

) is the function

\ell(\mathbf{i}):=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{i_{k}=1\}},\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{i_{k}=2\}}\Big)\qquad(\mathbf{i}\in{\mathbb{T}}).

(2.26)

Further examples are the functions

\ell_{1}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}):=\big(\ell(\mathbf{i}),\mathbf{j}\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ell_{2}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}):=\big(\mathbf{i},\ell(\mathbf{j})\big)\qquad\big((\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})\in{\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}}\big),

(2.27)

where

2.26

\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}c@{\,}ll}\displaystyle L^{\rm aB}_{n}(x)&=&\displaystyle L^{\rm AB}_{n}(x\circ\ell_{1})&\displaystyle\big(x\in\partial G_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}})\big),\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle L^{\rm Ab}_{n}(x)&=&\displaystyle L^{\rm AB}_{n}(x\circ\ell_{2})&\displaystyle\big(x\in\partial G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})\big).\end{array}

(2.28)

We wish to apply Proposition

\psi=\ell_{1}
or

Lemma 9 (The game-graph that is a tree)

For the game-graph
G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}})
, let
\sigma_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}\in{\cal S}_{2}
be strategies for Alice and Bob, respectively, and let
Z(\sigma_{1})
and
A(\sigma_{2})
be defined as in (

). Then for each
\mathbf{j}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
, there exists precisely one
\mathbf{i}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
such that
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})\in Z(\sigma_{1})
. Likewise, for each
\mathbf{i}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
, there exists precisely one
\mathbf{j}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
such that
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})\in A(\sigma_{2})
.

Proof
To prove the first statement, let
\sigma_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}
be a strategy for Alice and let
\mathbf{j}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
. Because of the tree structure of

Proof of Proposition
1

Let

X=\big(X(a,b)\big)_{a\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}},\ b\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Y=\big(Y(a,b)\big)_{a\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}},\ b\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}

), the second equality follows from formula (

\big|\ell_{2}(A)\big|=|A|\qquad\forall A\in{\cal A}(L^{\rm AB}_{2n}).

(2.31)

We claim that (

. Indeed, Lemma

2.3
Toom cycles

In Subsection
2.5
, we will derive upper bounds on the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
, and on the probability that Bob has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
, which then translate into some of the bounds in Propositions
3
and
4
. To have a unified set-up, it will be convenient to introduce games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
that are identical to the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
except that instead of Alice, Bob starts. Then the probability that Bob has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
is equal to the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(1-p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(1-p)
, respectively. Therefore, it suffices to derive upper bounds on the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
,
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
,
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
, and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
.

We will use a Peierls argument first invented by Toom
[
Too80
]
and further developed in
[
SST22
]
. To prepare for this, in the present subsection, we formulate a theorem that says that if Alice has a winning strategy in any of these games, then a certain structure must be present in the game-graph that following
[
SST22
]
we will call a “Toom cycle”. We first need to introduce notation for the game-graphs of the games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
.

Given a decision graph
D
and integer
n\geq 1
, we define a game-graph

G^{\prime}_{n}(D)=(G^{\prime}_{n},\vec{E}^{\prime}_{n},0,\tau^{\prime})

(2.32)

exactly as in (
1.13
), but with (
1.14
) replaced by

\tau^{\prime}(v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 1&\displaystyle\mbox{if }|v|\mbox{ is odd,}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle 2&\displaystyle\mbox{if }|v|\mbox{ is even.}\end{array}\right.\qquad(v\in\ring{G}_{n}).

(2.33)

In (
1.15
) and (
1.16
), we defined a sort of “product”
D^{1}\ltimes D^{2}
of two decision graphs
D^{1}
and
D^{2}
. Elements of
D^{1}\ltimes D^{2}
are pairs
(a,b)
with
a\in D^{1}
and
b\in D^{2}
. If
(a,b)
is at distance
n
from the root, then to reach a new state at distance
n+1
from the root, we replace either
a
or
b
by a new state
a^{\prime}
or
b^{\prime}
in
D^{1}
or
D^{2}
that lies one step further from the root. Here we replace
a
in case
n
is even and
b
in case
n
is odd, as is natural for a game where Alice starts. For games where Bob starts, very much in the same spirit, we can define a second “product”
D^{1}\rtimes D^{2}
which differs from
D^{1}\ltimes D^{2}
only in the fact that we replace
a
in case
n
is odd and
b
in case
n
is even. Formally, we set

D^{1}\rtimes D^{2}:=\big\{(a,b):(b,a)\in D^{2}\ltimes D^{1}\big\},

(2.34)

which is then equipped with the structure of a decision graph in the obvious way. We will be interested in the game-graphs

G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}),\quad G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}),\quad G_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}).

(2.35)

Note that in each case, the decision graph of Bob is
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
. We do not insist that
n
is even, so we also allow for games where the same player has both the first and the last move. We will show that given a function that assigns winners to possible outcomes, if Alice has a winning strategy, then a “Toom cycle” must be present in these game-graphs.

Fix
n\geq 1
and let
(G,\vec{E},0,\tau)
be any of the game-graphs in (
2.35
). Elements of
G
are pairs
(a,b)
with
a
an element of
{\mathbb{T}}
or
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
and
b
an element of
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
. Recall that if
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\cdots i_{m}\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
k\in\{1,2\}
, then
\mathbf{i}k\in{\mathbb{T}}
denotes the word obtained by appending the letter
k
to the word
\mathbf{i}
. To have a unified notation, it will be convenient to introduce for the decision-graph
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
the notation

(i,j)k:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(i+1,j)&\mbox{if }k=1,\\
(i,j+1)&\mbox{if }k=2.\end{array}\right.

(2.36)

Recall that
\ring{G}^{1}
and
\ring{G}^{2}
are the sets of internal states when it is Alice’s and Bob’s turn, respectively. Using notation as in (
2.36
), we set for
k\in\{1,2\}

\vec{A}_{k}:=\big\{\big((a,b),(ak,b)\big):(a,b)\in\ring{G}^{1}\big\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\vec{B}_{k}:=\big\{\big((a,b),(a,bk)\big):(a,b)\in\ring{G}^{2}\big\}

(2.37)

and write
\vec{A}:=\vec{A}_{1}\cup\vec{A}_{2}
and
\vec{B}:=\vec{B}_{1}\cup\vec{B}_{2}
so that
\vec{E}=\vec{A}\cup\vec{B}
. For any set of directed edges
\vec{F}
, we let

\accentset{\leftarrow}{F}:=\big\{(w,v):(v,w)\in\vec{F}\big\}

(2.38)

denote the set of directed edges obtained by reversing the direction of all edges in
\vec{F}
. A
walk
in
G
of
length

l
is a finite word
\psi=\psi_{0}\cdots\psi_{l}
made from the alphabet
G
such that
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\vec{E}\cup\accentset{\leftarrow}{E}
for each
0<k\leq l
. We call the substring
\psi_{k-1}\psi_{k}
the
k
-th step
of the walk
\psi
and we say that the
k
-th step is:

straight-up
(su) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\rightarrow}{A}
,
straight-down
(sd) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\leftarrow}{A}
,

left-up
(lu) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\rightarrow}{B}_{2}
,
left-down
(ld) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\leftarrow}{B}_{1}
,

right-up
(ru) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\rightarrow}{B}_{1}
,
right-down
(rd) if
(\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\in\accentset{\leftarrow}{B}_{2}
.

This terminology is inspired by the right picture in Figure
6
. Steps of the types su, ru, and lu are called
up
and steps of the types sd, ld, and rd are called
down
. Steps of the types su and sd are called
straight
, those of the types lu and ld
left
, and those of the types ru and rd
right
. For any walk
\psi
, we set
N(\psi)=N:=\{0,\ldots,l\}
and we partition
N
into subsets
N_{\uparrow},N_{\circ},N_{\downarrow},N_{\ast}
defined as

\begin{array}[]{r@{\,}c@{\,}l}\displaystyle N_{\uparrow}&:=&\displaystyle\big\{k:0<k<l,\ (\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\mbox{ is up},\ (\psi_{k},\psi_{k+1})\mbox{ is up}\big\}\cup\{0\},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle N_{\circ}&:=&\displaystyle\big\{k:0<k<l,\ (\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\mbox{ is down},\ (\psi_{k},\psi_{k+1})\mbox{ is up}\big\},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle N_{\downarrow}&:=&\displaystyle\big\{k:0<k<l,\ (\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\mbox{ is down},\ (\psi_{k},\psi_{k+1})\mbox{ is down}\big\}\cup\{l\},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle N_{\ast}&:=&\displaystyle\big\{k:0<k<l,\ (\psi_{k-1},\psi_{k})\mbox{ is up},\ (\psi_{k},\psi_{k+1})\mbox{ is down}\big\}.\end{array}

(2.39)

We next define Toom cycles. A
Toom cycle
in
G
is a walk in
G
of length
l\geq 2
that must satisfy a number of requirements. First, we require that

\psi_{0}=\psi_{l}=0,\mbox{ the first step is of type su or ru, and the last step is of type sd or rd.}

(2.40)

Next for
0<k<l
, depending on the type of the
k
-th step and on whether
\psi_{k}\in\ring{G}
or
\in\partial G
, we will put constraints on the type of the
k+1
-th step. More precisely, if
\psi_{k}\in\ring{G}
, then only the following combinations are allowed for the types of the
k
-th and the
k+1
-th step:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle{\rm su}\to{\rm ru},&\displaystyle{\rm ru}\to{\rm su},&\displaystyle{\rm lu}\to{\rm su},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle{\rm sd}\to{\rm rd}/{\rm ld},&\displaystyle{\rm rd}\to{\rm sd},&\displaystyle{\rm ld}\to{\rm lu}.\end{array}

(2.41)

If, on the other hand,
\psi_{k}\in\partial G
, then only the following combinations are allowed:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle{\rm su}\to{\rm sd},&\displaystyle{\rm ru}\to{\rm rd}/{\rm ld},&\displaystyle{\rm lu}\to{\rm rd}/{\rm ld}.\end{array}

(2.42)

See Figure
6
for an illustration. Note that these rules imply that
N_{\ast}(\psi)=\{k:\psi_{k}\in\partial G\}
. In addition to the requirements above, a Toom cycle has to fulfill the following two additional, final requirements:

(i)

\psi_{k}\neq\psi_{m}
for all
k,m\in N_{\ast}
with
k\neq m
,

(ii)

if
\psi_{k}=\psi_{m}
for some
k\in N_{s}
and
m\in N_{t}
with
s,t\in\{\uparrow,\circ,\downarrow\}
and
s\leq t
with respect to the total order on
\{\uparrow,\circ,\downarrow\}
defined by
\uparrow\,<\circ<\,\downarrow
, then
k\leq m
.

Requirement (i) says that vertices in
\partial G
are visited only once. Applying (ii) twice we see that if
\psi_{k}=\psi_{m}
for some
k,m\in N_{s}
with
s\in\{\uparrow,\circ,\downarrow\}
, then
k=m
, so (ii) can roughly be described by saying that internal vertices can be visited at most three times, and these visits have to take place in the right order depending on whether the walk is going up, is in a local minimum, or goes down.

. The pictures on the left and right show two projections of the same Toom cycle (compare Figure

(a,b)
of the game that the Toom cycle passes through. If Alice has a winning strategy, then there must exist a Toom cycle for which all these points correspond to a win for Alice.

Given a function
x:\partial G\to\{0,1\}
that assigns a winner to each possible outcome, we say that a Toom cycle
\psi=\psi_{0}\cdots\psi_{l}
is
present
in
(G,x)
if

x(\psi_{k})=0\quad\mbox{for all $0\leq k\leq l$ such that }\psi_{k}\in\partial G.

(2.43)

We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 10 (Toom cycles)

Let
G
be any of the game-graphs in (
2.35
) and let
x:\partial G\to\{0,1\}
be a function that assigns a winner to each possible outcome. If Alice has a winning strategy given
x
, then a Toom cycle is present in
(G,x)
.

One can check that in Theorem
10
, the converse implication does not hold, i.e., the presence of a Toom cycle does not imply that Alice has a winning strategy. A counterexample for the game-graph
G_{4}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
is shown in Figure
7
. Intriguingly, we have not been able to construct a counterexample for the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, leaving open the possibility that the converse implication holds in these cases. Theorem
10
is very similar to
[
SST22
, Thm 9]
and so is its proof (see in particular
[
SST22
, Figure 4]
for the idea of loop erasion), but since our setting is different and we will need some special properties of Toom cycles that are specific to our setting we will give an independent proof here. Strategies for Alice have an analogue for general monotone cellular automata. In this general context, they correspond to the so-called “minimal explanations” of
[
SST22
, Section 7.1]
, whose relation to Toom cycles is discussed in more detail in that article. We prove Theorem
10
in Subsection
2.4
and then apply it in Subsection
2.5
.

2.4
Presence of Toom cycles

In this subsection we prove Theorem
10
. Let
G
be any of the game-graphs in (
2.35
) and let
L
be defined as in (
1.10
), i.e., this is the Boolean function defined by the requirement that if
x:\partial G\to\{0,1\}
be a function that assigns a winner to each possible outcome, then
L(x)=0
if and only if Alice has a winning strategy given
x
. Using notation introduced in Subsection
2.1
, let
{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L)
denote the set of zero-sets of
L
and let
{\cal Z}(L)
denote its set of minimal zero-sets. Defining
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}
as in (
2.18
), we recall from Lemma
7
that

{\cal Z}(L)\subset{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\subset{\cal Z}^{\uparrow}(L).

(2.44)

Remark
One can check that both inclusions in (
2.44
) are strict. We say that a strategy
\sigma_{1}
for Alice is
minimal
if the set
Z(\sigma_{1})
from (
2.17
) satisfies
Z(\sigma_{1})\in{\cal Z}(L)
. Note that regardless of how one assigns winners to possible outcomes, it actually never makes sense for Alice to play a strategy that is not minimal, since for each non-minimal strategy she has at her disposal another strategy that is guaranteed to be at least as good in each situation. For the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, it seems that with some effort, it is possible to give a precise description of all minimal strategies for Alice, but for the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, classifying the minimal strategies seems a hard task.

Proof of Theorem
10

For each Toom cycle
\psi=\psi_{0}\cdots\psi_{l}
, we write

Z_{\psi}:=\big\{\psi_{k}:0<k<l,\ \psi_{k}\in\partial G\big\},

(2.45)

and we set

{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}:=\big\{Z_{\psi}:\psi\mbox{ is a Toom cycle}\big\}.

(2.46)

We will prove that

\forall Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}\ \exists Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}\mbox{ s.t.\ }Z^{\prime}\subset Z,

(2.47)

which clearly implies the statement of the theorem. The proof is by induction on
n
. For concreteness, we write down the proof for the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, and at the end remark on how the proof has to be adapted for the other two game-graphs. To have a unified notation, we write
D\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
where
D={\mathbb{T}}
or
={\mathbb{N}}^{2}
. Let
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
and
{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}(n)
denote the sets defined in (
2.18
) and (
2.46
) for a given value of
n
. It will be convenient to also include the case
n=0
. For this purpose, we use the convention that in this case there is precise one Toom cycle, which is the walk of length
l=0
given by
\psi=\psi_{0}=(0,0)
, where
(0,0)
denotes the root of
D\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
. Then
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(0)
and
{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}(0)
both have a single element, which is the singleton
\{(0,0)\}
, and (
2.47
) holds for
n=0
.

If
n
is even,
Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
is given, and
Z\ni(a,b)\mapsto\kappa_{(a,b)}\in\{1,2\}
is a function, then we can define
\tilde{Z}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
by

\tilde{Z}:=\big\{(a\kappa_{(a,b)},b):(a,b)\in Z\big\},

(2.48)

where
a\kappa_{(a,b)}
is defined as in (
2.36
) if the decision graph of Alice is
{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
. Formula (
2.48
) corresponds to Alice playing the strategy that defined
Z
up to the
n
-th turn of the game and then playing the move
\kappa_{(a,b)}
if at that moment the state of the game is
(a,b)
. We see from this that
\tilde{Z}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
, and each element of
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
is of this form for some
Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
. Similarly, if
n
is odd and
Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
is given, then we can define
\tilde{Z}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
by

\tilde{Z}:=\big\{(a,b1),(a,b2):(a,b)\in Z\big\}.

(2.49)

Indeed, this corresponds to Alice playing the strategy that defined
Z
up to the
n
-th turn of the game, while Bob has now one extra final move. We see from this that
\tilde{Z}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
, and each element of
{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n+1)
is of this form for some
Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
. In view of this, to complete the induction step of the proof, it suffices to show that:

If for
Z\in{\cal Z}_{\rm strat}(n)
there exists a
Z^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}(n)
such that
Z^{\prime}\subset Z
and
\tilde{Z}
is defined as in (
2.48
) or (
2.49
) depending on whether
n
is even or odd, then there exists a
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\in{\cal Z}_{\rm Toom}(n+1)
such that
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\subset\tilde{Z}
.

To prove this, let
\psi=\psi_{0}\cdots\psi_{l}
be a Toom cycle such that
Z^{\prime}=Z_{\psi}
. If
n
is even, then we modify
\psi
in such a way that for each
k
such that
\psi_{k}\in\partial G_{n}(D\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, in place of
\psi_{k}=(a,b)
we insert the string
(a,b)(a\kappa_{(a,b)},b)(a,b)
. Note that this adds two steps to the Toom cycle, the first of which is straight-up and the second straight-down. If
n
is odd, then we modify
\psi
in such a way that for each
k
such that
\psi_{k}\in\partial G_{n}(D\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, in place of
\psi_{k}=(a,b)
we insert the string
(a,b)(a,b1)(a,b)(a,b2)(a,b)
. Note that this adds four steps to the Toom cycle: right-up, left-down, left-up, and right-down.

Let
\tilde{\psi}
denote the modified walk. Then it is straightforward to check that
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}:=Z_{\tilde{\psi}}\subset\tilde{Z}
and
\tilde{\psi}
satisfies all requirements of a Toom cycle, except possibly for requirement (i), i.e., it may now be the case that some potential outcomes of the game are visited more than once. To remedy this, we apply the procedure of
loop erasion
which is also the main idea behind the proof of
[
SST22
, Thm 9]
. If
\tilde{\psi}_{k}=\tilde{\psi}_{k^{\prime}}\in\partial G_{n+1}
for some
k<k^{\prime}
, then we remove the string
\tilde{\psi}_{k+1}\cdots\tilde{\psi}_{k^{\prime}}
from
\tilde{\psi}
. One can check that this preserves all the requirements of a Toom cycle that
\tilde{\psi}
satisfies. Moreover, by applying this procedure till it is no longer possible to do so, we can make sure that also requirement (i) becomes valid again. In this way, we find a new modified Toom cycle
\tilde{\psi}
such that
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}:=Z_{\tilde{\psi}}\subset\tilde{Z}
. Note, however, that because of the loop erasion, even if we started with
Z^{\prime}=Z
, it may happen that
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}
is a strict subset of
\tilde{Z}
.

The proof for the game-graphs
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
is essentially the same. It differs only in the sense that the roles of even and odd
n
are interchanged.

Remark
For the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, one can check that because of the loop erasion, the construction in the proof of Theorem
10
can yield Toom cycles in which a right-up step is followed by a right-down step, as explicitly allowed in (
2.42
). For the game-graphs
G_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, it seems that on the other hand such transitions never arise from the construction in the proof of Theorem
10
, so for these game-graphs it should be possible to prove a stronger version of the theorem in which the Toom cycle satisfies additional properties. Since we do not need this for our main results, we do not pursue this further.

2.5
The Peierls argument

Recall the definition of the games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
at the beginning of Subsection
2.3
. In this subsection, we apply Theorem
10
to derive upper bounds on the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
,
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
,
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
, and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
. We start by considering game-graphs where Alice starts, since in this case the argument is slightly easier.

Lemma 11 (Toom cycles if Alice starts)

If
\psi
is a Toom cycle in
G=G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
or
G_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, then there exists an integer
m\geq 1
such that the number of steps of each of the types su, sd, lu, ld, ru, rd equals
m
, and the Toom cycle passes through precisely
m+1
different possible outcomes of the game.

Proof
We see from (
2.41
) and (
2.42
) that after a step of a type in
\{{\rm su},{\rm ld},{\rm rd}\}
there always follows a step of a type in
\{{\rm ru},{\rm lu},{\rm sd}\}
and vice versa. Therefore, since the first step of the Toom cycle is straight-up and the last step is straight-down, the number of steps of the Toom cycle is even and if we divide its steps in pairs of two consecutive steps, then the first step of such a pair is always of a type in
\{{\rm su},{\rm ld},{\rm rd}\}
and the second step of a type in
\{{\rm ru},{\rm lu},{\rm sd}\}
. It follows from the structure of
G=G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
that a straight-up step cannot end in
\partial G_{2n}
, so by (
2.41
) only three types of pairs of two consecutive steps are possible:

{\rm su}\mbox{ followed by }{\rm ru},\quad{\rm ld}\mbox{ followed by }{\rm lu},\quad\mbox{or}\quad{\rm rd}\mbox{ followed by }{\rm sd}.

(2.50)

By a slight abuse of notation, let us also write
{\rm su}
to denote the number of straight-up steps in the Toom cycle, and similarly for the other five types of steps. Then (
2.50
) implies that

{\rm su}={\rm ru},\quad{\rm ld}={\rm lu},\quad{\rm rd}={\rm sd}.

(2.51)

Since the distance from the root increases by one in each up move and decreases by one in each down move, and the cycle ends where it began, the number of up moves must equal the number of down moves, which gives

{\rm su}+{\rm lu}+{\rm ru}={\rm sd}+{\rm ld}+{\rm rd}.

(2.52)

Similarly, if
f\big(a,(i,j)\big):=i-j
denotes the difference between number of 1 moves and 2 moves played by Bob when the state of the game is
(a,(i,j))
, then the function
f
increases by one in each right move, decreases by one in each left move, and stays the same in straight moves, so we see that the number of right moves must equal the number of left moves, which gives

{\rm lu}+{\rm ld}={\rm ru}+{\rm rd}.

(2.53)

Combining (
2.51
), (
2.52
), and (
2.53
), we see that there exists an integer
m\geq 1
such that

{\rm su}={\rm sd}={\rm lu}={\rm ld}={\rm ru}={\rm rd}=:m.

(2.54)

In view of (
2.41
), each change from the down direction to the up direction is associated with a pair consisting of a left-down step followed by a left-up step, so the number of these changes is precisely
m
. Since the first step is up and the last step is down, we change from the up direction to the down direction once more than the other way around. Because of (
2.41
) and (
2.42
), changes from the up direction to the down direction can only happen in states that are a possible outcome of the game, and because of the crucial condition (i) in the definition of a Toom cycle, the walk visits each possible outcome at most once. It follows that the Toom cycle passes through precisely
m+1
different possible outcomes of the game.

Proposition 12 (Peierls estimates if Alice starts)

For each
n\geq 1
, the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
can be estimated from above by

1-P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)\leq\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{3m}(1-p)^{m+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad 1-P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)\leq\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{4m}(1-p)^{m+1}.

(2.55)

In particular,

P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1\quad\mbox{for all }p>\frac{7}{8}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1\quad\mbox{for all }p>\frac{15}{16}.

(2.56)

Proof
Let
G=G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and let
\big(X(a,b)\big)_{(a,b)\in\partial G}
be i.i.d. Boolean random variables with intensity
p
. By Theorem
10
, the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the game
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
can be estimated from above by the probability that a Toom cycle is present in
(G,X)
, which in turn can be estimated from above by the expected number of such Toom cycles. By Lemma
11
, the length of such a Toom cycle must be of the form
6m
with
m
an integer. We have
m\geq n
since the cycle must make at least
n
straight-up steps before it can start walking down. Since by Lemma
11
, a Toom cycle of length
6m
visits precisely
m+1
possible outcomes, the probability that a given Toom cycle of length
6m
is present in
(G,X)
is precisely
(1-p)^{m+1}
. This gives the bound

1-P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)\leq\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}M_{m}(1-p)^{m+1},

(2.57)

where
M_{m}
denotes the number of distinct Toom cycles of length
6m
in
G
. We claim that

M_{m}\leq 2^{m}\cdot 2^{m+1}\cdot 2^{m-1}.

(2.58)

Here the first factor comes from the fact that for each straight-up step we have two choices (corresponding to the two possible moves of Alice), the second factor comes from the fact that each time we arrive in a possible outcome of the game, we have to choose whether the next step is left-down or right-down, and the third factor comes from the fact that after each straight-down step except the very last one, we have to choose whether the next step is left-down or right-down. By (
2.41
) and (
2.42
) these are the only choices we need to make to uniquely determine a Toom cycle.

The argument for the game
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
is precisely the same, except that now the decision graph of Alice is no longer a tree which has the effect that also at the beginning of each straight-down step we may have to choose between two possible ways of making a straight-down step (see Figure
7
).

For the game-graphs where Bob starts, the argument is only slightly more complicated.

Proposition 13 (Peierls estimates if Bob starts)

For each
n\geq 1
, the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(p)
can be estimated from above by

\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{4m}(1-p)^{m+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}2^{6m}(1-p)^{m+1}.

(2.59)

As a result,

P^{\rm aB}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\mbox{for all }p<\frac{1}{16}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\mbox{for all }p<\frac{1}{64}.

(2.60)

Proof
The proof is very similar to the proof of Proposition
12
, but we have to modify Lemma
11
. Toom cycles in the game-graphs
G^{\prime}_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G^{\prime}_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
can be obtained from Toom cycles in the game-graphs
G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
by removing the first and last steps (which were straight-up and straight-down), and then inserting at each instance where the Toom cycle changes from the up to the down direction one extra straight-up step followed my a straight-down step. The argument then proceeds as before up to the point where we count the number of modified Toom cycles for which the original (unmodified) Toom cycle had length
6m
. For the game-graph
G^{\prime}_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, we get a factor
2
for each straight-up step. Due to the modification, the number of straight-up steps has decreased by one and increased by
m+1
, so we gain an extra factor
2^{m}
. For the game-graph
G^{\prime}_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\rtimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, we also get a factor
2
for each straight-down step, so we gain a factor
2^{2m}
. This explains (
2.59
).

Formula (
2.60
) follows from (
2.59
) and the fact that the probability that Bob has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
is equal to the probability that Alice has a winning strategy in the games
{\rm Ab}^{\prime}_{n}(1-p)
and
{\rm ab}^{\prime}_{n}(1-p)
, respectively.

The bounds for
P^{\rm ab}_{n}(p)
from Propositions
12
and
13
immediately imply Proposition
4
, while the bounds for
P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
P^{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
will translate into the upper bound on
p^{\rm Ab}_{\rm c}
and lower bound on
p^{\rm aB}_{\rm c}
from Proposition
3
, once sharpness of the transition is established.

2.6
Sharpness of the transition

Throughout this subsection,
G_{2n}
denotes any of the game-graphs
G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
and
G_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}})
, which are the game-graphs of the games
{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)
and
{\rm aB}_{n}(p)
, respectively, and
L_{2n}
denotes any of the Boolean functions
L^{\rm Ab}_{2n}
and
L^{\rm aB}_{2n}
that tell us who has a winning strategy for each given assignment
x=\big(x(v)\big)_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}
of winners to possible outcomes, with
0
and
1
indicating a win for Alice and Bob, respectively.

For each
p\in[0,1]
, we let
\big(X^{p}(v)\big)_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}
be i.i.d. Boolean variables with intensity
p
and for each
v\in\partial G_{2n}
, we define

X^{p}_{v,0}(w):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }w=v,\\[5.0pt]
X^{p}(w)&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{and}\quad X^{p}_{v,1}(w):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }w=v,\\[5.0pt]
X^{p}(w)&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(2.61)

(w\in\partial G_{2n})
. By definition, a possible outcome
v\in\partial G_{2n}
is
pivotal
for
L_{2n}
if
L_{2n}(X^{p}_{v,0})\neq L_{2n}(X^{p}_{v,1})
, which by the monotonicity of
L_{2n}
means that
L_{2n}(X^{p}_{v,0})=0
and
L_{2n}(X^{p}_{v,1})=1
. The
influence
of
v
is defined as

I^{p}_{2n}(v):={\mathbb{P}}\big[v\mbox{ is pivotal for }L_{2n}\big]\qquad(v\in\partial G_{2n})

(2.62)

and Russo’s formula (see, e.g.,
[
GS14
, Thm 3.2]
) tells us that the probability
P_{n}(p):={\mathbb{P}}[L_{2n}(X^{p})=1]
that Bob has a winning strategy is continuously differentiable with

{\textstyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial{p}}}}P_{n}(p)=\sum_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}I^{p}_{2n}(v).

(2.63)

It is easy to see that each possible outcome has a positive influence, so Russo’s formula implies that
P_{n}
is strictly increasing. Since moreover
P_{n}(0)=0
and
P_{n}(1)=1
, we conclude that the function
P_{n}
is invertible. We will prove the following result.

Theorem 14 (Sharpness of the transition)

For each
0<\varepsilon\leq{\textstyle\frac{{1}}{{2}}}
, there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

P_{n}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)-P_{n}^{-1}(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{C}{n}\qquad(n\geq 1).

(2.64)

We start with a preparatory lemma.

Lemma 15 (Lower bound on the total influence)

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
p\in[0,1]
, one has

\sum_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}I^{p}_{2n}(v)\geq\inf_{J>0}\big[2^{n}J\vee c{\rm Var}\big(L_{2n}(X^{p})\big)\log(1/J)\big].

(2.65)

Proof
Let us write

I^{p}_{2n}:=\sum_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}I^{p}_{2n}(v)\quad\mbox{and}\quad J^{p}_{2n}:=\max_{v\in\partial G_{2n}}I^{p}_{2n}(v).

(2.66)

For the game-graph
G_{2n}=G_{2n}({\mathbb{T}}\ltimes{\mathbb{N}}^{2})
, the set of possible outcomes is
\partial G_{2n}=\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}\times\partial_{n}{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
, where
\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
has
2^{n}
elements that play a completely symmetric role in
G_{2n}
, so that the influence
I^{p}_{2n}(a,b)
of an element
(a,b)\in\partial G_{2n}
depends on
b
but not
a
. Choosing
b_{\ast}\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
such that
I^{p}_{2n}(a,b_{\ast})=J^{p}_{2n}
for all
a\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}
gives the trivial bound

I^{p}_{2n}\geq\sum_{a\in\partial_{n}{\mathbb{T}}}I^{p}_{2n}(a,b_{\ast})=2^{n}J^{p}_{2n}.

(2.67)

For the game-graph
G_{2n}=G_{2n}({\mathbb{N}}^{2}\ltimes{\mathbb{T}})
, we get the same bound by reversing the roles of
a
and
b
. To complete the proof, we apply an inequality from the theory of noise sensitivity. By
[
GS14
, Thm 3.4]
, which is based on
[
BK+92
]
, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that

I^{p}_{2n}\geq c{\rm Var}\big(L_{2n}(X^{p})\big)\log(1/J^{p}_{2n}).

(2.68)

Combining these two estimates one arrives at (
2.65
).

Proof of Theorem
14

Since
P_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is strictly increasing we have

{\rm Var}\big(L_{2n}(X^{p})\big)=P_{n}(p)\big(1-P_{n}(p)\big)\geq\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\qquad\big(p\in[P_{n}^{-1}(\varepsilon),P_{n}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)]\big).

(2.69)

Russo’s formula (
2.63
) and Lemma
15
now tell us that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

{\textstyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial{p}}}}P_{n}(p)\geq\inf_{J>0}\big[2^{n}J\vee c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log(1/J)\big]\qquad\big(p\in[P_{n}^{-1}(\varepsilon),P_{n}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)]\big).

(2.70)

Making
c
smaller if necessary, we can without loss of generality assume that

c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log 2\leq 1.

(2.71)

To estimate the right-hand side of (
2.70
) from below, we set
J_{n}:=n2^{-n}
. Since the function
J\mapsto 2^{n}J
is increasing and
J\mapsto c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log(1/J)
is decreasing, we have

2^{n}J\vee c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log(1/J)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\geq 2^{n}J_{n}&\mbox{for }J\geq J_{n},\\[5.0pt]
\geq c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log(1/J_{n})&\mbox{for }J\leq J_{n}.\end{array}\right.

(2.72)

We observe that by (
2.71
),

c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log(1/J_{n})=c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)(n\log 2-\log n)\leq n=2^{n}J_{n}.

(2.73)

Choose
0<c^{\prime}<c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\log 2
. Then (
2.70
), (
2.72
), and (
2.71
) combine to give

{\textstyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial{p}}}}P_{n}(p)\geq c\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)(n\log 2-\log n)\geq c^{\prime}n\qquad\big(p\in[P_{n}^{-1}(\varepsilon),P_{n}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)]\big).

(2.74)

Setting
C:=1/c^{\prime}
, we obtain (
2.64
).

2.7
The cellular automata

In this subsection we study the cellular automata
(X^{{\rm Ab},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X^{{\rm aB},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from Subsection
1.3
. It will be convenient to change the notation a bit. Given a random variable
X_{0}
with values in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
, we can define a discrete-time process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with values in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
by setting

X_{t}(i,j):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle X_{t-1}(2i,j)\wedge X_{t-1}(2i+1,j)&\mbox{if $t$ is even},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle X_{t-1}(i,j)\vee X_{t-1}(i,j+1)&\mbox{if $t$ is odd},\end{array}\right.\qquad\big(t>0,\ (i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\big).

(2.75)

In particular, if
\big(X_{0}(i,j)\big)_{(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
are i.i.d. with intensity
p
, then this is the cellular automaton
(X^{{\rm Ab},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined in Subsection
1.3
. Similarly, given a random variable
\widehat{X}_{0}
with values in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
, we can define a discrete-time process
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with values in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
by setting

\widehat{X}_{t}(i,j):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{t-1}(2i,j)\wedge\widehat{X}_{t-1}(2i+1,j)&\mbox{if $t$ is odd},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{t-1}(i,j)\vee\widehat{X}_{t-1}(i,j+1)&\mbox{if $t$ is even},\end{array}\right.\qquad\big(t>0,\ (i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\big),

(2.76)

where compared to (
2.75
) all we have done is that we have interchanged the rules at even and odd times. The cellular automaton
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is similar to the cellular automaton
(X^{{\rm aB},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from Subsection
1.3
, but compared to the definitions there, the roles of
i
and
j
and the roles of
0
and
1
are interchanged. More precisely, if
\big(\widehat{X}_{0}(i,j)\big)_{(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
are i.i.d. with intensity
1-p
, then setting

X^{{\rm aB},p}_{t}(i,j):=1-\widehat{X}_{t}(j,i)\qquad\big(t\geq 0,\ (i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\big)

(2.77)

yields a cellular automaton
(X^{{\rm aB},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as defined in Subsection
1.3
.

As observed in (
1.28
), the cellular automaton
(X^{{\rm Ab},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has the property that at each time its columns are independent. This holds not only for product initial laws but more generally if the initial state
X_{0}
has the property that its columns are independent, and the same holds for
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This motivates the following definitions.

We let
S:=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the space of all functions
y:{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}
. We equip
S
with the product topology and the associated Borel-
\sigma
-field and we set

{\cal M}:=\big\{\mu:\mu\mbox{ is a probability law on }S\big\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\cal S}:=\big\{\mu\in{\cal M}:\mu\mbox{ is stationary}\big\}.

(2.78)

We equip
{\cal M}
with the topology of weak convergence of probability laws. Then
{\cal S}
is a closed subset of
{\cal M}
. We define maps
\Phi_{\rm A}:S^{2}\to S
and
\Phi_{\rm b}:S\to S
by

\Phi_{\rm A}(y,z)(k):=y(k)\wedge z(k)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{\rm b}(y)(k):=y(k)\vee y(k+1)\qquad(k\in{\mathbb{N}}),

(2.79)

we define maps
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
and
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
from
{\cal M}
to
{\cal M}
by

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu):={\mathbb{P}}\big[\Phi_{\rm A}(Y,Z)\in\,\cdot\,\big]\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu):={\mathbb{P}}\big[\Phi_{\rm b}(Y)\in\,\cdot\,\big]\\[5.0pt]
\qquad\mbox{where $Y$ and $Z$ are independent with law $\mu\in{\cal M}$,}\end{array}

(2.80)

and we set

{\cal F}:={\cal F}_{\rm A}\circ{\cal F}_{\rm b}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\cal F}:={\cal F}_{\rm b}\circ{\cal F}_{\rm A}.

(2.81)

The following lemma is a more precise formulation of the observation (
1.28
).

Lemma 16 (Law of the columns)

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the cellular automaton defined in (
2.75
) started in an initial law such that

\big(X_{0}(i,\,\cdot\,)\big)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mbox{ are i.i.d.\ with common law }\mu.

(2.82)

Then for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\big(X_{2n}(i,\,\cdot\,)\big)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mbox{ are i.i.d.\ with common law }{\cal F}^{n}(\mu),

(2.83)

where
{\cal F}^{n}
denotes the
n
-th iterate of the map defined in (
2.81
). The same is true with
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
replaced by
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
{\cal F}
replaced by
\widehat{\cal F}
.

Proof
The proof is by induction on
n
. Assuming that the statement holds for
n-1
, we see from (
2.75
) that

X_{2n-1}(i,\,\cdot\,)=\Phi_{\rm b}\big(X_{2n-2}(i,\,\cdot\,)\big)\qquad(i\in{\mathbb{N}}),

(2.84)

which implies (compare Figure
4
) that

\big(X_{2n-1}(i,\,\cdot\,)\big)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mbox{ are i.i.d.\ with common law }{\cal F}_{\rm b}\circ{\cal F}^{n-1}(\mu).

(2.85)

Using again (
2.75
), we see that

X_{2n}(i,\,\cdot\,)=\Phi_{\rm A}\big(X_{2n-1}(2i,\,\cdot\,),X_{2n-1}(2i+1,\,\cdot\,)\big)\qquad(i\in{\mathbb{N}}),

(2.86)

which implies (
2.83
). The proof for
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the same, but we apply
\Phi_{\rm A}
and
\Phi_{\rm b}
in the opposite order.

We let
\pi_{p}
denote the product measure on
S=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
with intensity
p
and for any
\mu\in{\cal S}
, we let
\langle\mu\rangle:=\int\mu(\mathrm{d}y)y(i)
which by stationarity does not depend on
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then our main functions of interest can be expressed in terms of the “column maps”
{\cal F}
and
\widehat{\cal F}
as

P^{\rm Ab}_{n}(p)=\langle{\cal F}^{n}(\pi_{p})\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad P^{\rm aB}_{n}(p)=1-\langle\widehat{\cal F}^{n}(\pi_{1-p})\rangle\qquad\big(p\in[0,1],\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\big),

(2.87)

where the first equality follows from (
1.24
), Lemma
16
, and the fact that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
started in product measure with intensity
p
is the cellular automaton
(X^{{\rm Ab},p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from Subsection
1.3
, and in the second equality we have used (
2.77
).

Recall that
S=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is equipped with the product topology,
{\cal M}
is equipped with the topology of weak convergence, and
{\cal S}\subset{\cal M}
denotes the set of stationary measures.

Lemma 17 (Properties of the column maps)

The maps
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
,
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
,
{\cal F}
, and
\widehat{\cal F}
are continuous with respect to the topology on
{\cal M}
and map
{\cal S}
into itself.

Proof
In view of (
2.81
) it suffices to prove the statement for
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
and
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
. Preservation of stationarity is obvious. To prove continuity, assume that
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
. By Skorohod’s representation theorem
[
Bil99
, Thm 6.7]
, we can find random variables
Y_{n},Y
with laws
\mu_{n},\mu
such that
Y_{n}\to Y
a.s. with respect to the topology on
S
. Let
(Y^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be an independent copy of
(Y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and let
Y^{\prime}
be the a.s. limit of
Y^{\prime}_{n}
. Then
\Phi_{\rm A}(Y_{n},Y^{\prime}_{n})\to\Phi_{\rm A}(Y,Y^{\prime})
a.s. and
\Phi_{\rm b}(Y_{n})\to\Phi_{\rm b}(Y)
a.s. with respect to the topology on
S
, which implies that
{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu_{n})\Rightarrow{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu)
and
{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu_{n})\Rightarrow{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu)
.

Lemma 18 (Upper bound on the density)

One has

\langle{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu)\rangle=\langle\mu\rangle^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu)\rangle\leq 2\langle\mu\rangle\qquad(\mu\in{\cal S}),

(2.88)

and consequently,

\langle{\cal F}(\mu)\rangle\leq 4\langle\mu\rangle^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle\widehat{\cal F}(\mu)\rangle\leq 2\langle\mu\rangle^{2}\qquad(\mu\in{\cal S}).

(2.89)

Proof
Let
Y
and
Z
be independent random variables with law
\mu\in{\cal S}
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}\big[\Phi_{\rm A}(Y,Z)(j)=1\big]={\mathbb{P}}\big[Y(j)\wedge Z(j)=1\big]=\langle\mu\rangle^{2}\qquad(j\in{\mathbb{N}}),

(2.90)

and

{\mathbb{P}}\big[\Phi_{\rm b}(Y)(j)=1\big]={\mathbb{P}}\big[Y(j)\vee Y(j+1)=1\big]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big[Y(j)=1\big]+{\mathbb{P}}\big[Y(j+1)=1\big]=2\langle\mu\rangle

(2.91)

(j\in{\mathbb{N}})
, proving (
2.88
). It follows that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\langle{\cal F}(\mu)\rangle=\langle{\cal F}_{\rm A}\circ{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu)\rangle=\langle{\cal F}_{\rm b}(\mu)\rangle^{2}\leq\big(2\langle\mu\rangle\big)^{2},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\cal F}(\mu)\rangle=\langle{\cal F}_{\rm b}\circ{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu)\rangle\leq 2\langle{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu)\rangle=2\langle\mu\rangle^{2}.\end{array}

(2.92)

Let
\underline{0},\underline{1}\in S
denote the configurations that are constantly zero and one, respectively. Since
\Phi_{\rm A}(\underline{0},\underline{0})=\underline{0}
and
\Phi_{\rm b}(\underline{0})=\underline{0}
, the delta-measure
\delta_{\underline{0}}
on
\underline{0}
is a fixed point of the maps
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
and
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
and hence also of
{\cal F}
and
\widehat{\cal F}
. The same applies to the delta-measure
\delta_{\underline{1}}
on
\underline{1}
. We define the domains of attraction of these fixed points as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\cal D}_{0}:=\big\{\mu\in{\cal S}:{\cal F}^{n}(\mu)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\delta_{\underline{0}}\big\},&\displaystyle{\cal D}_{1}:=\big\{\mu\in{\cal S}:{\cal F}^{n}(\mu)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\delta_{\underline{1}}\big\},\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal D}\hskip-0.5pt}\hskip 0.5pt_{0}:=\big\{\mu\in{\cal S}:\widehat{\cal F}^{n}(\mu)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\delta_{\underline{0}}\big\},&\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal D}\hskip-0.5pt}\hskip 0.5pt_{1}:=\big\{\mu\in{\cal S}:\widehat{\cal F}^{n}(\mu)\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\delta_{\underline{1}}\big\},\end{array}

(2.93)

where
\Rightarrow
denotes weak convergence of probability measures on
S
. The following proposition is the main result of this subsection.

Proposition 19 (Domains of attraction)

{\cal D}_{0}
and
\widehat{{\cal D}\hskip-0.5pt}\hskip 0.5pt_{0}
are open, dense subsets of
{\cal S}
.

Proof
Let
{\cal O}:=\{\mu\in{\cal S}:\langle\mu\rangle<1/4\}
. Since
S\ni x\mapsto x(0)
is a bounded continuous function, the map
{\cal S}\ni\mu\mapsto\langle\mu\rangle:=\int_{S}x(0)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
is continuous with respect to weak convergence of probability measures, and hence
{\cal O}
, being the inverse image of
[0,1/4)
under this map, is an open subset of
{\cal S}
. We claim that

{\cal D}_{0}=\big\{\mu\in{\cal S}:{\cal F}^{n}(\mu)\in{\cal O}\mbox{ for some }n\geq 1\big\}.

(2.94)

Indeed, the inclusion
\subset
follows from the fact that
{\cal O}
is an open set containing
\delta_{\underline{0}}
and the inclusion
\supset
follows from the fact that
{\cal O}\subset{\cal D}_{0}
by Lemma
18
. Since the map
{\cal F}
is continuous by Lemma
17
, the inverse image
{\cal F}^{-n}({\cal O})
of
{\cal O}
under the map
{\cal F}^{n}
is open for each
n
, and hence

{\cal D}_{0}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}{\cal F}^{-n}({\cal O}),

(2.95)

being a union of open sets, is open. The same argument shows that also
\widehat{{\cal D}\hskip-0.5pt}\hskip 0.5pt_{0}
is an open subset of
{\cal S}
.

We observe that the map
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
is linear but
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
is not. It is easy to check that for any
\mu\in{\cal S}
, we have

{\cal F}_{\rm A}\big(r\mu+(1-r)\pi_{0}\big)=r^{2}{\cal F}_{\rm A}(\mu)+(1-r^{2})\pi_{0}.

(2.96)

Using the linearity of
{\cal F}_{\rm b}
, we see that the same formula holds with
{\cal F}_{\rm A}
replaced by
{\cal F}
or
\widehat{\cal F}
. It follows that

{\cal F}^{n}\big(r\mu+(1-r)\pi_{0}\big)=r^{n+1}\widehat{\cal F}(\mu)+(1-r^{n+1})\pi_{0}\underset{{n}\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\pi_{0}\quad\forall r\in[0,1),

(2.97)

which shows that
r\mu+(1-r)\pi_{0}\in{\cal D}_{0}
for all
r\in[0,1)
. Since
r\mu+(1-r)\pi_{0}\Rightarrow\mu
as
r\to 1
, and
\mu\in{\cal S}
is arbitrary, we conclude that
{\cal D}_{0}
is dense in
{\cal M}
. The proof for
\widehat{{\cal D}\hskip-0.5pt}\hskip 0.5pt_{0}
is the same.

Proof of Theorem

and Proposition
