The roles of surround inhibition for the intrinsic function of the striatum, analyzed in silico
Authors: Johanna Frost Nylén, J. J. Johannes Hjorth, Alexander Kozlov, Ilaria Carannante, Jeanette Hellgren Kotaleski, Sten Grillner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe impact of surround inhibition between striatal projection neurons has been studied in silico. We show that it has a major impact on the distal dendrites with regard to shunting, and thereby reducing the amplitude of the corticostriatal EPSPs and on dendritic plateau potentials. Furthermore, surround inhibition can contribute to action selection within striatal modules like that of the fore-limb area in the dorsolateral striatum.

In this study, we explore the function of the input stage of the basal ganglia, the striatum, through modelling, using a recently developed simulation platform (1) in which all neurons are modelled to be similar to their biological counterparts. The basal ganglia, a subcortical group of nuclei, are of critical importance for motor learning, action selection/execution, and formation of habitual movements (2–4). The main input structure of the basal ganglia is the striatum, the largest compartment, which receives cortical and thalamic inputs together with projections from other basal ganglia nuclei and subcortical structures including the dopaminergic innervation from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), and the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) (5–8). The striatum consists of 95% striatal projection neurons (SPNs) divided into those of the direct and indirect pathways (dSPN and iSPN, respectively), depending on their canonical projections to downstream nuclei and the expression of either the D1 dopamine receptor (D1R) or the D2 dopamine receptor (D2R). The remaining 5% are GABAergic interneurons except for the cholinergic interneurons (ChINs) (9). The fast-spiking interneurons (FS) within the striatum are similar to the cortical FS and project to soma and proximal dendrites on both dSPNs and iSPNs. FS–FS interact via both gap junctions and GABAergic synapses (10–12). SPNs were first shown to interact through sparse interconnections by Tunstall et al. (13). Later studies show that the dSPN and iSPN are interconnected via GABAergic synapses onto their distal dendrites and the connection probability between pairs of SPNs is distance-dependent (11, 12). The lowest connection probability is between dSPN to iSPN while iSPN to iSPN has the highest connection probability (Fig. 1A). Pairs of SPNs have been estimated to be connected via 2-5 synapses per pair, while FS to SPN have approximately twice the number of synapses per connection, with a similar ratio for the strength of the connection (1).

The cortical projections to the striatum are delineated into rostrocaudal and mediolateral areas, which correspond to distinct cortical areas (14). In Foster et al. (14), the projections through the cortex and the basal ganglia were investigated by anterograde tracer injections. They demonstrated that specific areas of the cortex, for example, the forelimb area, project to a specific area within the dorsolateral striatum (DLS; the somatosensory and motor part of the striatum). In turn, the direct pathway SPNs (dSPNs) from this area project to a specific part within the output nucleus of the basal ganglia, the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) (15). Throughout the basal ganglia, the striatal projection patterns are maintained within modules although projections between nuclei reverse in the dorsoventral and mediolateral axis. These subdivisions into hindlimb, trunk, forelimb, and orofacial areas in the striatum demonstrate the somatotopic organization of the DLS region (16).

The individual DLS modules have an estimated size of 500 to 700 µm with around 40,000 neurons [estimated from Foster et al. (14)]. Assuming a uniform density throughout the module this would result in around 38,000 SPNs and 2,000 interneurons. Based on Hjorth et al. (1), a majority of the SPN pairs have a maximum intersomatic distance of 250 µm, hence most of the presynaptic neurons would originate within the same module. Therefore, mutual input is important for the inhibition between dSPNs, iSPNs, and FS. As the majority of the SPN synapses occur on distal dendrites, an investigation into surround inhibition could elucidate the interaction that occurs within a module and its role in the striatal processing of cortical and other inputs.

One aim of this study is to explore the role of the inhibition mediated by SPNs onto the distal dendrites of the surrounding SPNs. We focus on the processing within one or two populations of SPNs following the activation by cortical input and modulation by dopamine. We utilize detailed simulations of the striatal network with multicompartmental model neurons of SPNs and most of the interneuron subtypes, with validated intrinsic properties and models of GABAergic and glutamatergic synapses with validated synaptic properties. We make use of the detailed striatal simulation platform (Snudda) (1, 17–19). Snudda was developed to facilitate investigations into the function and intrinsic complexity of the striatal microcircuit with its neuronal subtypes and their connection probability, morphology, and intrinsic properties as well as the extrastriatal inputs.

We show that the SPN-induced surround inhibition has a profound effect on the distal dendrites of surrounding SPNs both regarding the shunting of excitatory cortical or thalamic input and the effects on dendritic non-linearities such as plateau-like potentials. Furthermore, following the activation of two populations of SPNs, there is also an extensive interaction, which results in the suppression of populations with weaker activity. The activation of equally strong populations leads to a smaller effect, which is dependent on the interconnectivity of the units, their position and size. A phasic dopamine activation promotes dSPN and reduces iSPN activity and modulates the population unit interaction accordingly. Last, focal activation within a module, such as the fore- and hindlimb areas, would lead to a spatially restricted surround inhibition and hence would not affect the activity in surrounding functional modules.

Results

The striatal microcircuit model utilized for the simulations is illustrated in Fig. 1A. The detailed somatodendritic reconstructions and the distribution of synaptic contacts result in potential functional impact on network activity. The connectivity within the striatal model is primarily based on paired recordings (1, 11, 12) (Fig. 1A). The connection probability is distance dependent as illustrated in Fig. 1A, where the connectivity between dSPNs and iSPNs and FS to SPNs is indicated for paired recordings at 50 µm (red) and 100 µm (blue). The simulations included excitatory input from the cortex and thalamus as well as the dopaminergic modulation from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc). Furthermore, the SPNs receive most of their input from SPNs, LTS (low threshold spiking interneurons), cortex, and thalamus on distal dendrites (Fig. 1B), while FS targets the soma and proximal dendrites hence influencing the spike initiation. The influence of a single SPN is constrained by the axonal and dendritic morphology. The axonal arbors of SPNs are distributed in a spherical manner around the somata. A population of SPNs can therefore convey surround inhibition onto other SPNs as illustrated in Fig. 1C. The Snudda detection algorithms employ touch detection on the axons and dendrites within the network and via several pruning rules (Materials and Methods and refs. 1 and 17) synaptic contacts are established according to the experimental data in Fig. 1A. These anatomical characteristics could therefore have functional implications on the behavior of the striatal microcircuit, especially the spatially restricted surround inhibition mediated by SPNs.

To investigate the influence of surround inhibition, we used the tool Snudda to simulate large-scale networks of the striatal microcircuit with appropriate densities of dSPN and iSPN, FS, ChINs, and LTS (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The networks consisted of 20,000 to 40,000 neurons with detailed multicompartmental models with somatodendritic morphologies. The simulation tool, Snudda, offers several features to set up and simulate (using NEURON) synaptic input with varying properties such as dendritic location, frequency, and the number of inputs (1, 17). Furthermore, the networks can be manipulated with current injections, synaptic input, and neuromodulatory effects from, for example, dopamine (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Within each simulation, the tool offers the ability to record different variables including dendritic membrane potentials through the whole cell, ion channel currents, synaptic currents, and somatic membrane potential (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Each simulation and recording within the network can be further specified based on neuron type including dendritic compartment, position (x, y, z), population unit (ensembles of cells), and dendritic position (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D).

Inhibition of Distal Dendrites of Neurons Surrounding an Active Cluster of SPNs.

To investigate the impact of SPN inhibition on surrounding non-active (non-spiking) SPNs, a network of 40,000 neurons was simulated with a stimulation of a central population unit of 350 neurons considered a realistic number. However, this will of course vary depending on behavior and input from the cortex/thalamus (blue in Fig. 2A). They were active at a rate of 20 to 30 Hz similar to the physiological condition. The impact exerted on the distal dendrites of the surrounding SPNs was recorded throughout the simulation. The activation caused a depolarization in the SPNs, since the resting membrane potential is below the reversal potential of GABA (Fig. 2 B and C). The amplitude of the depolarization ranged between 0.1 and 11 mV depending mostly on the distance from the active cluster of neurons. Fig. 2B shows the mean depolarization within the entire population of surrounding SPNs. The large distribution of dendritic synaptic potentials in Fig. 2C suggests that certain neurons received more synapses from the active population unit. The mean depolarization based on the distance between the center of the population unit and the somatic position of the surrounding SPNs was calculated (Fig. 2D) and demonstrated that the mean depolarization was the largest within 200 µm, and progressively smaller in the next interval up to 300 µm and even smaller at 400 µm. This follows from the size of the axonal arbor around each SPN. The influence of SPNs is clearly distance-dependent and decreases 200 µm beyond the center of the focal activation.

Corticostriatal EPSPs Become Shunted by SPN-Induced Surround Inhibition.

The activation of the GABAergic synapses at the distal dendrites will inevitably lead to a conductance increase locally that would be expected to affect the local excitatory synaptic transmission from the thalamus or cortex, hence shunt the excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs). To investigate how effective the distal SPN synapses are at shunting corticostriatal input, we simulated a corticostriatal activation of the central population unit, while recording the dendritic EPSP (Fig. 3A, black trace) and subsequently activated the surrounding SPNs (red trace), which led to a marked reduction of the depolarization. The gray trace shows the activation of surrounding SPNs alone (only inhibition). The corticostriatal EPSPs were thus markedly shunted when a concurrent surround inhibition occurred, as shown in Fig. 3B when comparing the area under the curve (AUC). The GABAergic inhibition decreased the overall depolarization in the distal dendrites by 60%. Furthermore, we simulated individual dSPN and iSPN and found that the excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials were attenuated up to 80% from the distal dendrites to the soma, which emphasizes the importance of dendritic dynamics on the resultant effect in the somatic compartment. In a subset of SPNs, the activation led to spiking, which was completely shunted by the inhibition. Hence, shunting inhibition can modulate both subthreshold and suprathreshold responses during extended corticostriatal activations.

Effect of Surround Inhibition on the Dendritic Plateau-Like Potentials.

To further investigate the dendritic plateau-like potential and its dynamics in the distal dendrites of SPNs (20, 21), a simulation with a network of 20,000 neurons was created and the input was designed to induce plateau-like potentials in the distal dendrites. The population was a centrally located subpopulation of 1000 dSPNs and iSPNs and received a clustered group of 20 synapses within a distal dendritic section located approximately 100 µm from the soma [Fig. 4 A and B (black trace)]. A separate group of presynaptic SPNs (1,000) within the striatal network were activated for 2 s to induce SPN to SPN inhibition onto the plateau-induced population of SPNs (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). SI Appendix, Fig. S2B shows the mean firing frequency of the presynaptic SPNs. The inhibition caused a reduction of 20% in the AUC of the total depolarization (Fig. 4B).

The plateau-like potential lasts for approximately 250 ms within the dendritic section; hence, the AUC was calculated for 250 ms following the activation (Fig. 4C). The AUC with and without inhibition (AUCdiff) showed a symmetrical distribution within SPNs, with negative mean AUCdiff overall. The negative AUCdiff demonstrates that the inhibition reduces the amplitude of the plateau-like potentials, also shown in the example in the left panel in Fig. 4C. In the other group, the inhibition had the opposite effect. The results clearly imply that when the starting point of the initiated plateau response is below the chloride equilibrium potential for GABA (set to −65 mV), the GABA input will initially provide a depolarization that can facilitate the plateau response, but if it starts above the equilibrium potential, it will instead reduce the amplitude. This excitatory effect of GABA has previously been observed (22, 23). May et al. (23) demonstrated that optogenetic and GABA uncaging led to depolarization in SPNs.

The NMDA current is an important part of the dendritic plateaus, thus the NMDA current in the dendrites was recorded with and without concurrent inhibition (Fig. 4D). The distribution was similar to the dendritic potential AUCdiff, with a population of negatively and positively modulated neurons. The neurons which had a negative AUCdiff using the membrane potential response (red in Fig. 4C) in general showed a negative AUCdiff when measuring the NMDA current. We performed an additional simulation with three clusters of 20 corticostriatal synapses within the dendritic tree. The majority of SPNs still only showed subthreshold responses. However, while the action potentials were shunted in Fig. 3, here, the inhibition caused either a transition from a subthreshold to a suprathreshold response or the addition of action potentials within the suprathreshold responses.

Interaction between Two Competing Populations of SPNs.

In the previous simulations, we investigated the effect of surround inhibition from a single focal activation and the inhibition from presynaptic neurons. Next, we examined how two separate populations of SPNs that are active simultaneously interact and how they affect each other (referred to as population units). This is important in the context of action selection and when there are competing alternatives. In the context of action selection, one can select only one action during the competition between different potential actions within a spatially confined volume. The simplest scenario for action selection within the striatum would be between two competing subpopulations (units), resulting from the activation of input from two separate groups of neurons, for instance in the motor areas within the cortex.

Initially, a network with 40,000 neurons was created with two population units. Each population unit consisted of 4,000 SPNs and received a separate activation for 2 s. To investigate their interaction, one population unit [P2 (blue) in Fig. 5A] could be activated and achieved a maximum firing frequency of 40 Hz, and the effect on the (competing) population unit (P1), which was stimulated to a lesser degree at three different levels of frequencies of 3, 5, and 10 Hz. The degree of suppression of P1 by P2 was measured as the number of P1-neurons that ceased spiking following the activation of the primary population unit (Fig. 5B). In the 3 Hz population unit, 60% of the SPNs stopped firing, while for the 10-Hz unit the reduction was only 15% (P1 in Fig. 5C). Hence, the model shows that a strongly activated population unit can suppress the activity of nearby weaker activated population units. Moreover, the depressing effect was somewhat larger on iSPNs then on dSPNs (Fig. 5B).

Effect of Size and Spatial Distribution of Competing Population Units.

A separate question is how important is the spatial distribution of the competing population units and the relative size of these population units? Different scenarios were simulated using striatal networks of 40,000 neurons represented by spatially clustered and by randomly assigned population units.

To investigate the importance of the spatial distribution, two population units of 350 SPNs both activated at the same (high) level were separated with increasing interunit distance (Fig. 6A). The mean change in frequency following the activation of the secondary population unit was measured in the primary population unit (Fig. 6B). If the two populations completely overlapped, the suppression of the SPN spiking activity was the largest. The networks with larger interunit distances (77 and 155) showed reduced effect by the secondary population unit, with the 155 µm causing the smallest effect.

The effect of the distance between population units (350 neurons). (A) An illustration of the three networks with increasing interunit distance from left to right (population units in red and blue). (B) The change in mean firing frequency in the primary population unit following the activation of the secondary population unit within the three scenarios (black, green, and blue in A). (C) The number of synapses from the secondary population unit onto the primary population unit within the three scenarios (black, green, and blue in A).

The number of SPN synapses between the neurons was quantified in the competing population units (Fig. 6C). The decrease in firing frequency was clearly correlated with the number of synapses from the secondary population unit onto neurons within the primary population unit (Fig. 6C). Hence, an increased overlap between the axonal and dendritic trees increases the probability of connections between SPNs and thereby a larger degree of surround inhibition. This in turn leads to a larger modulation exerted by the secondary population unit. Clearly, the degree of interconnectivity, that is, the number of synapses operating between the two populations determines the strength of interaction.

In a second set of population unit simulations, we sought to investigate how a randomly distributed set of population units influence each other. SI Appendix, Fig. S3A shows the three networks with an increasing number of neurons within each population unit (10%, 20%, and 40% of the total population) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). The neurons were randomly chosen within the population, and the number of incoming synapses from the secondary population unit onto the primary population was measured (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). The number of incoming synapses were similar to the clustered populations in Fig. 6, although these population units were 10× (the number of neurons). Hence, the spatial distribution of SPNs is important for the overlap between units. Not surprisingly, as the size of the population unit increased, the number of incoming synapses increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). SI Appendix, Fig. S3B shows that even though these population units are large (10% is equal to 4,000 SPNs), the effect of the secondary population unit is comparable.

From the population unit simulations in Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3, we can conclude that the spatial distribution and the size of population units are critical factors in determining the degree of suppression between units. Hence, the smallest unit from SI Appendix, Fig. S3 (10% of the total number of neurons) was simulated in a clustered distribution similar to Fig. 6. This would reveal to what extent the spatial clustering of units could contribute to the inhibition of the secondary unit. SI Appendix, Fig. S4A shows the intersomatic distances of the population units (each consisting of 4,000 units). In the leftmost panel, the distributions show that when the units are completely overlapping the intersomatic distance within P1 and between P1 and P2 is similar. This distribution shifts to larger intersomatic distances as the population units are further away. Compared to the random assignment in SI Appendix, Fig. S3, the number of incoming synapses is almost doubled when the population unit is clustered. The effect on the primary population unit is also increasing, which is consistent with the increased number of synapses from the secondary population unit (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). In the group with an interunit distance of 155 µm, the connectivity between the units is similar to the network in SI Appendix, Fig. S3. This is also reflected in the frequency change, which is comparable. Although the size of the population unit contributes to the level of suppression, spatially clustered units increase the connectivity between population units even further.

In conclusion, the population unit simulations demonstrate that the competition between units is affected by the degree of interconnectivity as further illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S5. This showed that the interaction from clustered populations is limited to 400 to 500 µm (with weakening strength). Although randomly distributed populations can produce similar effects, these require a larger number of participating neurons. Both scenarios are possible for the striatal microcircuit, although the randomly distributed populations are limited by the extent of their axonal and dendritic tree as well. Furthermore, within a population, such as an area assigned to the forelimb, both clustered and randomly assigned population units could be competing with other population units, but interaction would mostly be within the same striatal modules. Last, the current striatal simulations were performed on a network, which has not experienced any learning. Learning could obviously modify several of the components, which could affect the interaction significantly.

The Action of Dopamine on Network Activity.

The dSPNs express the D1R and the iSPNs the D2R. D1R mediates a net excitatory effect, while D2R instead produces a net inhibitory effect (24). To investigate how dopaminergic modulation would interact with the population units, a large-scale dopaminergic modulation was simulated (19) through the striatal microcircuit as described in Fig. 7A. The dopaminergic transient (Materials and Methods) is illustrated in red with a cortical command, which lasts for 500 ms. The simulations were repeated with and without dopamine. Fig. 7B shows a single dSPN from the primary population unit without dopamine (black) and with a dopamine transient (red); note the marked facilitation mediated by D1R. Following the activation of the secondary population, there is inhibition of several units within P1 (Fig. 7B; in blue). The number of active neurons in P1 was quantified within the different simulation conditions, and the ratio of active neurons was calculated. On the population level (P1 in Fig. 7C), there is a marked increase in the number of spiking dSPNs, while the iSPNs become inhibited following dopaminergic modulation. If two competing population units are activated simultaneously, they inhibit each other to some degree (P1 and P2), while dopamine enhances activity in the dSPN populations; dopamine causes an inhibition of iSPNs. Hence, dopamine acts as a modulator of the relative strength of the dSPN and iSPN response respectively within a population unit. The decreased iSPN response would result in a decreased inhibition from iSPN both within the primary population unit and toward SPNs in the secondary population unit.

Effects of Ablations of Connectivity between FS and SPNs on Network Activity.

In an additional set of simulations, we investigated the general effect of the inhibitory intrastriatal microcircuit by performing a set of ablations of SPN to SPN and FS to SPN connections. The simulation consisted of activating all neurons and subsequently ablating the local GABAergic connectivity. The resultant effect on the SPN activity was measured within iSPN and dSPN separately (Fig. 8). Fig. 8A shows the network of 20,000 neurons which was simulated with an activation of 2 and 0.5 s. The simulations were performed with ablations of the FS–SPN, SPN–SPN connectivity, and a complete removal of all intrastriatal synapses. In Fig. 8 B1 and B2, the ratio of spiking neurons within the population following the ablation is compared to control. The ratio of spiking neurons increases in each group with SPN–SPN ablations contributing to most of the inhibition in both dSPNs and iSPNs. The mean firing frequency was also measured in both populations of SPNs. The ablations increased the mean firing frequency and the distribution of firing frequency changed in each ablation group. In both Fig. 8 B and C, the SPN–SPN ablation showed the greatest effect on population activity. The FS–SPN ablation had a time-dependent effect as seen in Fig. 8 B3 and C3, where the instantaneous rate of dSPN and iSPN were measured during the activation. The FS–SPN inhibition decreased after 100 ms, which is consistent with the short-term depression at FS–SPN synapses (11). In addition to the complete SPN ablation, iSPN ablation was performed and the effect was measured within the SPN population (like Fig. 8). SI Appendix, Fig. S6 demonstrates that the iSPN ablation dominates within SPN population for iSPN, while for dSPN the dSPN–dSPN contributes to the suppression. This is consistent with the connection probability within the dSPN and iSPN populations (Fig. 1). We explore here the effect of a simultaneous activation of SPNs and FS. To what degree the FS receives a separate corticostriatal input is not clear, but they can be strongly activated during initiation of behavior (25, 26).

In conclusion, the ablation of GABAergic connectivity showed that the striatal microcircuit can influence the activity of SPNs with a varying degree of suppression. The population unit simulations additionally showed that the distance-dependent connectivity limits the extent of focal activation effects on local SPNs. Although, the determining factor is the number of synapses between units. Furthermore, the inhibition had a dual effect on dendritic membrane potentials. Focal activation showed that the GABAergic synapses are depolarizing due to the hyperpolarized membrane potential. Additionally, the inhibition can shunt corticostriatal activation, while induced plateau-like potentials have a dual response to inhibitory activation. More than half of the population is shunted and inhibited while the other population induces larger plateau-like potentials. Hence, the striatal microcircuit has several different responses to inhibitory activation varying from spatially restricted population units to interference with dendritic processing.

Discussion

The overarching goal of this study is to investigate the role of the surround inhibition for the processing within the striatum at both the cellular and network level. We have used the detailed network model of the mouse striatum developed earlier, which contains the two types of SPNs, FS, LTS, and ChINs represented by multicompartmental models being very close to their biological counterparts with appropriate sets of ion channels and synaptic connectivity (1, 17, 19). This platform represents as close as possible the dynamic operation of the striatum. The simulated network is similar in size to that of the fore- or hindlimb areas within the striatum with around 40,000 neurons [based on Foster et al. (14)]. Many of the questions addressed here would have been very difficult to investigate using experimental methods.

Effects on the Surround Inhibition of SPNs—Synaptic Transmission and Plateau-Like Potentials.

First, the impact of the inhibition within the striatum following a focal activation of a population of SPNs within a large network was investigated. We observed that the impact is mostly within 200 µm but can reach up to 400 µm from the center of the focal activation. This corresponds to a striatal volume like the functional modules for the forelimb or hindlimb areas (14), and consequently, the impact of the surround inhibition mediated by SPNs is limited to the local interaction within one module but not across different modules.

We then investigated how SPN–SPN GABAergic synapses would affect the corticostriatal inputs to individual distal dendrites of SPNs, by comparing the amplitude of the simulated corticostriatal EPSPs with and without surround inhibition. Through current clamp recordings of the population, we could show that inhibition could shunt 40 to 60% of the amplitude of the corticostriatal synaptic input. Hence, a strong reduction in the excitatory input to these cells would take place. This would also apply to the thalamic input to SPNs.

Furthermore, the surround inhibition also modulated plateau-like potentials within a population through both shunting and direct inhibition. Here, we observe that preceding surround inhibition could either depress or enhance the size of plateau-like potentials (Fig. 4). In the latter case, it is due to the GABA reversal being set to −65 mV, while varying membrane potential could be more negative, which results in that GABA will have a depolarizing effect within the SPNs. Within the SPNs, which had depolarizing GABA effects, the depolarization was clearly visible prior to the induction of the plateau-like potentials. In the converse case, when the plateau-like potential was induced from a more depolarized level the plateaus were reduced in amplitude, as observed previously and which also impacted the calcium transients (20, 27). Additionally, the simulation assumed clustered spatial positions between the excitatory synapses while the inhibitory synapses were more distributed. In a realistic setting, the activity of the presynaptic neurons and the feedforward inhibition following a cortical or thalamic activation would have to be positioned with precise timing to achieve such a detailed form of inhibition. A possibility is that such a spatially and timed interaction between excitation and inhibition and its position on the dendritic tree of the SPN occurs during learning and development of the striatal microcircuit, but evidence for such an extensive organization is currently lacking.

Competition between Striatal Subpopulations Activated Concurrently.

In different behavioral contexts, input to striatum may arrive from separate parts of the cortex or thalamus and will then activate different subpopulations within the striatum. These subpopulations may be involved in the control of different movements (28, 29). The question here is whether the surround inhibition around each subpopulation can contribute to the selection of one action over the other. The simulations of two SPN population units activated from different parts of the cortex (within, e.g., the forelimb module), show that the unit with a higher level of activity will suppress the activity of the “weaker” subpopulation being active at a lower level and inhibit these and thereby contribute to action selection. On the other hand, if the population units are of similar strength and at a high level of activity, the impact is smaller. Also, in this case, the spatial positioning of such units has an impact on the degree of inhibition, which it exerts on the neighboring units.

In the current simulations (Figs. 5 and 6), we activated the SPNs of each subpopulation directly, by excitation applied on the soma-level to observe the impact of the surround inhibition of the subpopulations. This may have led to an underestimation of the impact of the surround inhibition since the shunting inhibition on the distal dendrites will have less impact (Fig. 3). Further simulations are required to disentangle the contribution of shunting inhibition during competition between populations.

The dopaminergic modulation of SPN population units will change the relative strength between dSPN and iSPN. Moreover, it will affect the degree to which the population units inhibit each other and even override the effect of surround inhibition. The dopaminergic modulation of population units showed the role of changing the relative competition between units and between dSPNs and iSPNs (Fig. 7C). Dopamine may act both at the presynaptic and postsynaptic levels within the striatum (30) and can also modulate the intrastriatal GABAergic connections (31).

Finally, we investigated the importance of surround inhibition by ablating the synaptic connectivity between SPNs or the effect of FS on SPNs. The effect on the striatal network is related to the number of SPNs, its connection probability, and short-term synaptic dynamics. In the current model, we could show that the inhibitory SPN–SPN interaction is the dominating factor, which controls network activity following the activation of the whole network. During behavior, the number of SPNs which are active during a specific task will be a subpopulation within the network. Hence, as several have shown (32, 33), FS are activated more generally and modulate, for example, the expression of habitual behavior. Hence, in Fig. 8, if half of the population of SPN would be active, the ablation of FS–SPN and SPN–SPN would be comparable. Although, the SPN–SPN connectivity could in addition modulate the distal dendrites which could have an impact on network activity.

In a very different setting, Ponci and Wickens (34) modeled SPNs and used small populations of point neurons activated so that they fired at a very low rate and were connected via surround inhibition. SPNs then tended to arrange themselves in separate assemblies and become active out of phase with each other. We have not observed this in the present experiments, in which, however, subpopulations of SPNs were activated for shorter periods and at a higher level.

SPNs Elicit their Behavioral Effects via SNr to Downstream Motor-Centers.

The fundamentals of action selection depend on an activation of a subset of striatonigral projections that via dSPNs inhibit subpopulations of cells within the SNr, which can thereby disinhibit different downstream brainstem motor centers (15). The interaction between individual populations of SPNs within the striatum and their projections to the SNr (in terms of convergence or divergence to different populations of neurons) will be affected by the processing of corticostriatal input and by the intrastriatal inhibition. The striatal output via SPNs will in parallel be channeled through the SNr not only to downstream motor centers in the midbrain–brainstem but in addition forwarded back to the cortex and striatum via the thalamus, providing an efference copy of the commands directed to downstream motor centers.

Surround Inhibition Can Contribute to Action Selection in a Striatal Functional Module Like that of the Forelimb but Not for Global Movement Control.

We have noted above that the SPN-induced surround inhibition between populations of SPNs can contribute only within a distance less than 400 μm, that is, within a module like that of the forelimb. The forelimb movements concerned here relate to reaching, grasping, fine manipulations, or corrections during locomotor movements. Within such a module, competition between different population units activated from different cortical or thalamic centers can contribute to the selection of one population unit over another. However, complex movements like playing tennis involve many parts of the body in an integrated way and will have to rely on other mechanisms than lateral inhibition within striatal modules for the coordination (ref. 35).

Limitations and Future Perspectives.

The striatal network itself has been simulated with high accuracy regarding the cellular properties and the connectivity of SPNs, FS, LTS, and ChINs to form the baseline network. The synaptic properties of corticostriatal neurons with a blend of NMDA and AMPA receptors are also detailed. However, the pattern of activity in different corticostriatal populations of neurons is only approximated, due to a lack of detailed experimental data. Further experiments will be required. This includes both the input from layer 5 neurons that target the striatum on their way to downstream motor centers and the intratelencephalic neurons in layer 5 that target only neurons in the striatum and cortex. The pattern of activity in the prominent thalamostriatal population is even less well described and has been approximated. Moreover, there is also a major GABAergic input to the striatum from the arkypallidal neurons in GPe that are spontaneously active and under the control of both motor cortex and dSPNs (36).

The effect of dopamine activation on the different SPNs, and the population activity has been simulated with a transient activation as would normally occur during initiation of a bout motor activity (28). We have, however, not yet considered reward and the possible induction of synaptic plasticity, important for motor learning. The other neuromodulator systems (5-HT, histamine, and noradrenaline) that also terminate in the striatum but with lesser density than for the massive dopamine termination have not yet been included. They have the potential of affecting the intrinsic properties of the striatal neurons (36, 37). Our current striatal network is designed for fast synaptic interaction (GABAA, AMPA, and NMDA) and has not included the G-protein-mediated slow synaptic transmission (mGluRs, GABAB, M1, M4, or adenosine), but the subroutines for this are now available (18, 19, 38–40). These neuromodulators could modulate plateau-like potentials and synaptic transmission and affect the balance between the different neuron types within the striatum.

Although, within the present model around 98% of the neurons in the striatum were included, a few additional interneuron types that synapse on the distal dendrites of SPNs have been described and could further contribute to the shunting and the inhibition of corticostriatal EPSPs and plateau-like potentials. Especially, interneurons with slow dynamics could filter activity along the dendrite. They include NPY-NGF interneurons or other spontaneously active neurons such as tyrosine hydroxylase-expressing interneurons (ThINs) (39, 40).

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the present detailed striatal model can simulate the activity in modules of 40,000 neurons similar to that of the forelimb area and highlights the impact of surround inhibition on intrastriatal processing and its contribution to action selection.

Materials and Methods

Snudda is a platform for large-scale simulation of multicompartmental neuron models. The platform is written in Python, and the neuron models are simulated in NEURON (https://github.com/Hjorthmedh/Snudda). The tool consists of frameworks for creating networks via placement, touch detection, and pruning, which have been validated against experimental data on neuron densities, distance-dependent connection probabilities, and the distribution of glutamatergic and GABAergic on the somatodendritic morphology of each cell type. The simulations were performed on supercomputers Piz Daint (Cray XC40/XC50) at Swiss National Supercomputing Centre (CSCS) and Beskow (CRAY XC40) and Dardel (HPE Cray EX) at Swedish National Infrastructure for Computing (SNIC) at the PDC Center for High-Performance Computing, KTH Royal Institute of Technology.

The simulations consisted of custom applications of the features available within Snudda including development of new recordings and tools for modifying the microcircuit. These additional features were included into the Snudda framework, and the code base for each simulation with documentation is available (https://github.com/jofrony/current_bgd2_analysis), including the analysis of each simulation.

Models.

The multicompartmental models used for the simulations are available on the online data repository (https://zenodo.org/records/10067236) and perform very close to their biological counterpart. The neuron types included in the striatal simulations are dSPN, iSPN, FS, LTS, and ChIN. The repository includes the models for synaptic dynamics of corticostriatal and thalamostriatal for each neuron type. Details on the development, variability, and the validation can be found in previous publications (1, 17, 19).

Creating Networks.

The network sizes used in the simulations were 20.000 and 40.000 multicompartmental neurons. The different networks were generated using snudda.init module with define_striatum with default values for network parameters (see snudda.init). The assembly of the circuit consisted of place, detect, and prune using the wrapper script (snudda.core). The assembly was parallelized using IPython for parallel computing, running an ipcluster and Snudda with the --parallel option.

For the simulations, which included population units, the creation of the networks involved the module add_population_unit_density. The population units were defined based on spatial coordinates within the circuit and the distance to a center point in each unit. The neurons within the population unit sphere were added to either population unit 1 or 2 (with all neurons being population unit 0 by default). The parameters for the population unit were radius, the distance between centers of units, and the size of units (see https://github.com/jofrony/current_bgd2_analysis).

Synaptic Input.

The striatal microcircuit network was stimulated in several different modes to investigate the effect of the surrounding inhibition for the operation of the striatal network. Synaptic inputs were simulated by corticostriatal and thalamostriatal models of synaptic dynamics (using snudda.input). The inputs for the background simulation were uncorrelated with 1 to 2 Hz. The activation used 40 corticostriatal synapses with 20 Hz and were placed randomly within the distance-dependent distribution on the dendritic tree of dSPNs and iSPNs. In the plateau-like potential simulations, a population unit of dSPNs and iSPNs were stimulated with corticostriatal synapses clustered on a dendritic segment. The clustered synapses were stimulated with 50 Hz for 25 ms using the add_external_input function of the Snudda.simulate class.

Current Injection.

To simulate populations with a specific frequency, the networks were simulated with a current injection in each somatic compartment during 2-s stimulation.

Network Ablations.

The network was ablated using the ablation class which removes the synaptic connections between neuron types and produces modulated network-synapses.hdf5-files.

Dopaminergic Modulation.

The functionality to simulate dopaminergic modulation (19) was utilized, and for the ion channels modulated within each neuron type, see https://zenodo.org/records/10067236.

Network Output.

In each simulation, the spiking and voltage of each somatic compartment of each neuron within the network were recorded. In addition, to the default recordings, the additional functionality was utilized to record the membrane potential from dendritic compartments of each neuron. The simulations with plateau-like potentials included recordings of the NMDA current on each synapse (tmglut.mod).

Network Analysis.

The analysis of each simulation utilizes the SnuddaLoad functionality with custom code (https://github.com/jofrony/current_bgd2_analysis). The analysis consisted of spike times, membrane potential of somatic, and dendritic compartments of each neuron (SPN and FS) within the network. The depolarization of subthreshold activation in distal dendrites was measured and normalized to response prior to the activation of the focal population unit. The degree of shunting was estimated by calculating the AUC following the corticostriatal input with and without inhibition. The population unit simulations measured the mean firing frequency change during the stimulation of the second population unit (utilizing custom code in https://github.com/jofrony/current_bgd2_analysis). The connectivity between population units was quantified using custom code (connectivity_analysis). The number of active neurons was classified based on the presence or absence of action potentials within the period of stimulation, and the ratio was calculated. The instantaneous frequency and mean frequency of each neuron were calculated for each ablation scenario. The plateau-like potentials and NMDA current were evaluated using the AUC and the difference in the AUC (AUCdiff).
