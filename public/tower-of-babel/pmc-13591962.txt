Determinants of community pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services in selected Arabic-speaking countries: a theory of planned behaviour-based structural equation modelling study
Authors: Ammar A Jairoun, Abeer M Al-Ghananeem, Moyad Shahwan, Heba Jeroudy, Sabaa S Al-Hemyari, Faris El-Dahiyat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCommunity pharmacists are increasingly recognised as accessible healthcare providers who can contribute to immunisation programmes and improve vaccine coverage. However, pharmacist-driven vaccination services remain underutilised in many Arabic-speaking countries, and factors influencing pharmacists’ intention to provide these services are not fully understood.ObjectiveTo translate, culturally adapt, validate, and evaluate the Pharmacist-Driven Vaccination Service Intention Model (PDV-SIM) among community pharmacists in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar and identify factors influencing their intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among community pharmacists practicing in Arabic-speaking countries between February 2024 and November 2024. The PDV-SIM was translated and culturally adapted using a forward–backward translation process. Confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modelling were performed using EQS 6.4. Reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, average variance extracted, and the Fornell–Larcker criterion.ResultsA total of 305 pharmacists participated. The measurement model demonstrated acceptable fit under robust estimation (CFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.937, NNFI = 0.918, RMSEA = 0.066). Reliability and convergent validity were satisfactory across all constructs. The structural model also showed acceptable fit (CFI = 0.934, IFI = 0.935, TLI = 0.918, RMSEA = 0.065). Personal development attitude was the strongest predictor of intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services (β = 0.724, p < 0.001), followed by subjective norm (β = 0.180, p < 0.01) and perceived behavioural control (β = 0.152, p < 0.05). The model explained 67.2% of the variance in intention.ConclusionProfessional development attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control significantly influenced pharmacists’ intention to provide vaccination services. Strengthening training, professional support, and regulatory frameworks may facilitate implementation of pharmacist-driven vaccination services across Arabic-speaking countries.KEYWORDS: Community pharmacy, vaccination services, confirmatory factor analysis, subjective norm, theory of planned behaviour, perceived behavioural control

Introduction

Community pharmacists have become increasingly recognised as accessible healthcare professionals who contribute substantially to disease prevention and health promotion through the delivery of vaccination services. Their accessibility extended opening hours, and frequent interactions with patients position community pharmacies as convenient settings for improving vaccine uptake, particularly among adults. Beyond vaccine administration, pharmacists play an important role in educating patients, addressing misconceptions, reducing vaccine hesitancy, and promoting informed vaccination decisions through trusted professional relationships (Agomo et al., 2018; Shen & Peterson, 2020).

Evidence from many healthcare systems demonstrates that pharmacist-administered vaccination services improve immunisation coverage, increase patient convenience and satisfaction, and strengthen public health initiatives, particularly for seasonal influenza and other adult immunisation programmes (Grzegorczyk-Karolak et al., 2022). The COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted the value of expanding vaccination services beyond traditional healthcare settings and emphasised the important contribution that community pharmacists can make to emergency preparedness and population health. However, despite these demonstrated benefits, implementation remains inconsistent across countries because of differences in legislation, professional scope of practice, reimbursement mechanisms, workforce capacity, training opportunities, and organisational infrastructure (Agomo et al., 2018; Ayenew et al., 2024).

Within the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), community pharmacists are increasingly being integrated into vaccination services, although implementation remains heterogeneous. Community pharmacies provide convenient access to healthcare and can improve vaccination uptake by reducing geographical, logistical, and appointment-related barriers while facilitating patient education and vaccine advocacy (Noorsaeed et al., 2025). Recent developments in digital healthcare and telepharmacy have further expanded opportunities for supporting vaccination programmes; however, challenges related to healthcare system integration and regulatory implementation remain (Noorsaeed et al., 2025). Although several GCC countries have introduced pharmacist-administered vaccination through regulated initiatives, the extent of pharmacists’ involvement continues to vary according to national legislation, professional authorisation, and healthcare policies (Noorsaeed et al., 2025).

Despite growing recognition of the pharmacist's role in immunisation, community pharmacies remain underutilised as vaccination providers in many GCC countries. Legislative restrictions, limited professional authorisation, insufficient immunisation training, and uncertainty regarding pharmacists’ legal responsibilities continue to hinder broader implementation of pharmacist-administered vaccination services (Alrasheedy et al., 2024). In addition, operational barriers – including staffing shortages, limited consultation time, inadequate reimbursement models, lack of private counselling areas, and insufficient vaccine storage facilities – restrict the ability of many community pharmacies to deliver vaccination services effectively (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Ayenew et al., 2024). Studies conducted in the UAE have similarly reported positive professional attitudes toward pharmacist-administered vaccination while highlighting the need for improved infrastructure, practical training, and supportive regulatory frameworks before widespread implementation can be achieved (Ali et al., 2022; Alzubaidi et al., 2018; Hasan et al., 2018).

Expanding community pharmacists’ participation in vaccination programmes has particular relevance for countries seeking to improve adult immunisation coverage and strengthen preventive healthcare services. Limited healthcare accessibility, vaccine hesitancy, shortages of trained healthcare professionals, and socioeconomic disparities continue to contribute to vaccination gaps in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), underscoring the importance of utilising community pharmacies as accessible vaccination sites (Agomo et al., 2018; Ali et al., 2022). Within the UAE and neighbouring GCC countries, pharmacists are gradually transitioning from traditional dispensing roles toward broader patient-centered clinical services, including vaccination, chronic disease management, and medication therapy management (Alzubaidi et al., 2018). Nevertheless, successful implementation requires supportive legislation, sustainable reimbursement mechanisms, standardised immunisation training and certification, and stronger interprofessional collaboration (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Alzubaidi et al., 2018; Hasan et al., 2018; Noorsaeed et al., 2025).

Although previous studies consistently demonstrate that community pharmacists generally support expanding their role in vaccination services, willingness alone does not ensure successful implementation. Pharmacists continue to report barriers related to insufficient knowledge, limited practical training, heavy workload, lack of formal certification, and uncertainty regarding professional responsibilities (Alrasheedy et al., 2024). Understanding the psychological and behavioural factors that influence pharmacists’ intention to provide vaccination services is therefore essential for developing effective implementation strategies and informing policy decisions.

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) provides a well-established theoretical framework for explaining behavioural intention through three principal constructs: attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control (Ajzen, 1985). TPB has been widely applied to investigate healthcare professionals’ adoption of new clinical services and has consistently demonstrated good explanatory performance in pharmacy practice research (Eccles et al., 2005; Godin et al., 2008; Rawy et al., 2021). Building on this framework, Gülpınar and Uzun (2022) developed the Pharmacist-Driven Vaccination Service Intention Model (PDV-SIM), which extends the traditional TPB model by incorporating pharmacists’ perceptions of patient-related benefits, professional benefits, and potential negative consequences associated with pharmacist-administered vaccination services (Gülpınar & Uzun, 2022). Although the original PDV-SIM demonstrated satisfactory psychometric performance, it has not previously been translated, culturally adapted, or validated for Arabic-speaking community pharmacists.

Therefore, this study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the Arabic version of the Pharmacist-Driven Vaccination Service Intention Model (PDV-SIM) among community pharmacists practicing in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar. In addition, the study examined the determinants of pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services using the Theory of Planned Behavior framework.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional investigation assessed the factors that shape community pharmacists’ willingness to deliver pharmacist-driven vaccination services across Arabic-speaking countries. Specifically, community pharmacists practicing in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Saudi Arabia, and Qatar were invited to participate. Of the final study sample, approximately 50% practiced in the UAE, 30% in Saudi Arabia, and 20% in Qatar. The study also involved the Arabic translation, cultural adaptation, and validation of the Pharmacist-Driven Vaccination Service Intention Model (PDV-SIM). This model was originally developed by Gülpınar and Uzun (2022) and is theoretically based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB).

Data collection took place from February 2024 to November 2024. An Arabic-language online questionnaire was distributed to eligible community pharmacists practicing in the three participating countries using a convenience sampling approach.

Target population

The study targeted community pharmacists who had completed at least three months of professional practice. Pharmacists still undergoing probation were not included, as they may not yet have had enough practical exposure to routine pharmacy work, professional responsibilities, or local healthcare regulations.

Hypotheses and research model

The study adopted the PDV-SIM, originally proposed by Gülpınar and Uzun (2022). In the present research, the model was translated into Arabic, adapted to Arabic-speaking healthcare contexts, and assessed psychometrically among community pharmacists. The study also examined whether the model could be applied appropriately across Arabic countries.

The original PDV-SIM draws on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which is widely used to explain why individuals intend to perform particular health-related behaviours. In TPB, intention is regarded as the most direct antecedent of behaviour. Intention is shaped by three main components: attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control. Attitude refers to whether a person evaluates the behaviour positively or negatively. Subjective norm reflects the influence of perceived expectations from important people or groups. Perceived behavioural control describes the extent to which a person believes that the behaviour is easy or difficult to perform (Ajzen, 1985).

TPB was an appropriate theoretical basis for the original model because it has been used extensively in studies examining whether healthcare professionals intend to adopt new services, practices, or clinical roles (Eccles et al., 2005; Godin et al., 2008; Rawy et al., 2021). The framework is also suitable for pharmacist vaccination research because it accounts for both personal influences and external conditions that can affect professional behaviour.

The current study followed the conceptual structure of the original PDV-SIM and retained its proposed relationships between constructs. The model assumes that pharmacists’ intention to provide vaccination services is shaped by perceived patient benefits, perceived professional benefits, concerns about possible negative consequences, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control. The following hypotheses, adapted from the original PDV-SIM developed by Gülpınar and Uzun (2022), were therefore examined:

H1: Positive attitudes toward patient-related benefits are associated with stronger community pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services.

H2: Positive attitudes toward professional benefits are associated with stronger community pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services.

H3: Concerns about the negative consequences of pharmacist-driven vaccination services are associated with weaker community pharmacists’ intention to provide such services.

H4: Subjective norm is positively associated with community pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services.

H5: Perceived behavioral control is positively associated with community pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services.

Translation and cultural adaptation

The questionnaire was translated and adapted using a forward and backward translation procedure, following accepted guidance for cross-cultural instrument adaptation (Sousa & Rojjanasrirat, 2011; Wild et al., 2005). First, two bilingual translators independently translated the English questionnaire into Arabic. The two Arabic versions were then compared, and differences were discussed until a preliminary Arabic version was agreed.

Next, two independent different bilingual translators, who had not seen the original questionnaire, translated the Arabic version back into English. These back-translated versions were compared with the source questionnaire to check whether the translated items retained the intended concepts, wording accuracy, and meaning. Any differences were reviewed by the translators and research team until agreement was reached.

The translated questionnaire was then culturally adapted to ensure that the items were appropriate for Arabic-speaking pharmacists and reflected the language, cultural expectations, and healthcare practice contexts of Arabic countries.

Pilot study

Prior to the main survey, the translated questionnaire was pilot tested among 20 community pharmacists from the participating countries (United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar). Pilot participants were excluded from the final study sample. The pilot assessed clarity, cultural appropriateness, questionnaire flow, and feasibility. The average completion time was approximately 15 min. Feedback resulted in minor wording and formatting revisions to improve clarity before the main survey.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was estimated using recommendations for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) (Hair et al., n.d.; Kline, 2023). The PDV-SIM contained six latent variables and 21 observed indicators. Using the commonly applied minimum ratio of 10 participants per item, at least 210 participants were required (Hair et al., n.d).

The final dataset included 305 complete responses. This sample exceeded commonly recommended minimum sample sizes for covariance-based structural equation modelling and was considered adequate for model validation and hypothesis testing (Hair et al., n.d.; Wolf et al., 2013).

Sampling technique and data collection

A convenience sampling strategy was employed. The online questionnaire was distributed to approximately 350 eligible community pharmacists through professional pharmacist networks and social media platforms in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar. Of the 350 pharmacists invited, 308 completed the questionnaire, corresponding to an overall response rate of 88.0%. After data screening, three questionnaires were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 305 pharmacists.

Data quality, confidentiality, and ethical considerations

Data quality was supported through predefined eligibility criteria and systematic screening of completed questionnaires for duplicate entries, unusually short completion times, and inconsistent response patterns before statistical analysis. The questionnaire was completed anonymously through a secure online platform, and no personally identifiable information was collected. Survey data were stored in password-protected databases accessible only to authorised members of the research team, and all findings are presented in aggregate form to preserve participant confidentiality.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Committee of Ajman University. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained before survey commencement. Participants received information describing the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional ethical requirements for research involving human participants.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS version 26 and EQS version 6.4. Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the demographic profile of the participants.

For descriptive interpretation of the latent construct scores, item responses were transformed to percentage scores (0–100%) and categorised according to the standard three-level Bloom's cut-off for attitude assessment (Azhar et al., 2024; Shaipuzaman & Rahman, 2022; Umbreen et al., 2023). Scores of 80–100% were classified as positive, 60–79% as moderate, and <60% as negative. These categories were used solely to describe the distribution of participants’ responses across the study constructs and were not used in the structural equation modelling analyses (Azhar et al., 2024; Shaipuzaman & Rahman, 2022; Umbreen et al., 2023).

Before inferential testing, the dataset was examined for missing data, normality, and outliers. Skewness and kurtosis values were used to assess univariate normality. Mardia’s coefficient was used to evaluate multivariate normality. As the data showed evidence of multivariate non-normality, robust maximum likelihood estimation was used for all subsequent SEM analyses. This approach was selected to produce more reliable parameter estimates and standard errors under non-normal data conditions.

The measurement model was tested through CFA using a covariance-based SEM approach. Model fit was examined using several fit statistics, including the chi-square statistic (χ²), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Because the data were not multivariate normal, the main model fit results were based on robust indices derived from the Satorra-Bentler scaled chi-square. Model fit was considered acceptable when CFI, TLI, and IFI were at least 0.90 and RMSEA was 0.08 or lower (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Reliability and validity were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). Internal consistency was considered acceptable when Cronbach’s alpha and CR values were 0.70 or above. Convergent validity was supported when AVE values reached or exceeded 0.50. Discriminant validity was examined using the Fornell-Larcker criterion by comparing the square root of each construct’s AVE with the correlations between constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hu & Bentler, 1999).

After the measurement model had been confirmed, the structural model was tested using robust maximum likelihood estimation. The hypothesised paths were evaluated using standardised path coefficients (β), robust standard errors, and Z-statistics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The amount of variance explained in the dependent construct was assessed using R².

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 305 community pharmacists from three Arabic-speaking countries were included in the final analysis. Participants were recruited from the United Arab Emirates (n = 153, 50%), Saudi Arabia (n = 91, 30%), and Qatar (n = 61, 20%). The nearly balanced gender distribution (51.8% female and 48.2% male). Most respondents held a bachelor’s degree (75.4%), while 24.6% had postgraduate qualifications. Slightly more than half worked in independent pharmacies (55.1%), compared to 44.9% in chain pharmacies. The majority were pharmacists in charge (60.0%), followed by assistant pharmacists (22.0%) and chief pharmacists (18.0%). In terms of experience, most participants had 6–10 years (43.9%) or 1–5 years (26.9%) of practice, with fewer having over 10 years of experience. Regarding educational background, most graduated from local universities (45.2%), followed by regional (31.8%) and international institutions (23.0%) (Table 1).

Measurement model evaluation

A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted in EQS 6.4 to evaluate the psychometric performance of the proposed six-factor measurement model comprising patient-related attitude toward PDV services, personal development attitude toward Service (PDV) services, attitude toward negative consequences of PDV services, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control, and intention toward providing PDV services. The analysis was based on 305 complete cases and 21 observed items. Initial distributional assessment indicated substantial multivariate non-normality, as evidenced by Mardia’s coefficient of 268.148 and a normalised estimate of 75.337; therefore, both ML and robust ML estimators were examined, with primary emphasis placed on the robust solution.

The final respecified measurement model incorporated ten correlated residuals retained in the EQS syntax. Under conventional ML estimation, the model yielded χ² (164) = 588.260, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.934, IFI = 0.935, NNFI = 0.916, and RMSEA = 0.092 (90% CI: 0.084–0.100). Under robust ML estimation, the model demonstrated improved fit, with Satorra–Bentler scaled χ² (164) = 377.934, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.937, NNFI = 0.918, and RMSEA = 0.066 (90% CI: 0.057–0.074). These robust fit statistics indicate an acceptable to good overall fit of the measurement model to the data, particularly when accounting for non-normality (Table 3 and Figure 1).

Causal model evaluation

The structural model was assessed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and robust maximum likelihood estimation to account for deviations from multivariate normality. The robust solution demonstrated acceptable model fit, with CFI = 0.934, IFI = 0.935, and TLI = 0.918, all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90. The RMSEA value of 0.065 (90% CI: 0.057–0.074) further indicated a good approximation of model fit. Although the chi-square statistic was statistically significant (Satorra–Bentler χ² = 388.72, df = 169, p < 0.001), this was expected given the sensitivity of the test to sample size (Table 6 and Figure 2).

Structural relationships and hypothesis testing

The hypothesised relationships among the constructs were evaluated using standardised path coefficients (β) and robust Z-statistics (Table 7). The results revealed that personal development attitude (F2) had a strong and statistically significant positive effect on intention to provide PDV services (β = 0.724, p < 0.001), representing the most influential predictor in the model. Additionally, subjective norm (F4) (β = 0.180, p < 0.01) and perceived behavioural control (F5) (β = 0.152, p < 0.05) were also found to significantly influence intention, supporting the theoretical expectations of the model.

Discussion

The present study successfully translated, culturally adapted, and validated the Arabic version of the PDV-SIM among community pharmacists practicing in three Arabic-speaking countries. The measurement model demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, including acceptable model fit, internal consistency, convergent validity, and overall construct validity, supporting its suitability for evaluating determinants of pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services. The structural model subsequently explained 67.2% of the variance in intention, indicating good explanatory performance. This suggests that the selected constructs provide a useful basis for understanding pharmacists’ readiness to participate in vaccination service provision.

Among the predictors examined, personal development attitude had the strongest effect on intention. Subjective norm and perceived behavioural control also significantly influenced intention. Patient-related attitude and attitude toward negative consequences did not show significant effects in the structural model. More than four-fifths of participants demonstrated positive personal development attitudes, whereas approximately half reported positive subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.

The main finding was that personal development attitude was the strongest positive predictor of intention to provide PDV services. Pharmacists who viewed vaccination services as a route to professional growth, career development, wider recognition, and broader clinical responsibility were more likely to express willingness to provide these services. This outcome is consistent with earlier research showing that pharmacists increasingly support expansion of their professional roles and regard vaccination as a way to make a stronger contribution to public health and patient care (Ayenew et al., 2024; Francis et al., 2026; Qamar et al., 2022). For instance, research among Malaysian pharmacists found substantial willingness to provide vaccination services and support for broader professional responsibilities (Qamar et al., 2022). Other studies have also shown that involvement in vaccination activities can improve pharmacists’ professional image, job satisfaction, and perceived value within the healthcare system (Huston et al., 2019; Katoue, 2021).

The strong effect of personal development attitude may also reflect wider changes in pharmacy practice. The profession has been moving away from a mainly product-focused role toward a more patient-centered clinical model (Schindel et al., 2012; Seidling & Moecker, 2023). In many Arabic countries, pharmacist-administered vaccination is still not widely established. Pharmacists may therefore see vaccination services as a way to strengthen their clinical identity and demonstrate their relevance within modern healthcare systems.

Prior studies have reported that pharmacists are more willing to provide vaccination services when they have higher educational attainment, better vaccine-related knowledge, and more significant exposure to immunisation training (Jarab et al., 2022; Meraya et al., 2024). Pharmacists also frequently identify continuing education, certification pathways, and practical vaccine-administration training as necessary supports for expanded practice (Francis et al., 2026; Meraya et al., 2024; Sae-Lim et al., 2025).

Subjective norm was also a significant predictor of intention. This means that pharmacists’ willingness to provide vaccination services is affected by perceived expectations and support from groups such as patients, physicians, pharmacy owners, professional bodies, and regulatory authorities. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where social expectations were shown to increase pharmacists’ willingness to provide vaccination services (Alnahar et al., 2022; Gülpınar & Uzun, 2022).

In many healthcare systems, pharmacists consider vaccine advocacy to be part of their professional role and view disease prevention as an area where they should contribute more actively (Al Hail et al., 2023; Ozdemir et al., 2022). The significance of subjective norm in this study indicates that pharmacist-led vaccination programmes will be more likely to succeed when they are supported by healthcare professionals, policymakers, professional organisations, and the public. Patient trust is especially important in countries where vaccination has traditionally been provided by physicians, as some patients may be unsure about receiving vaccines from pharmacists (Alnahar et al., 2022; Ayenew et al., 2024).

Perceived behavioural control was another significant predictor. Pharmacists who believed that they had the knowledge, confidence, and practical ability to administer vaccines were more likely to intend to provide PDV services. This finding is consistent with evidence showing that training programmes can improve pharmacists’ confidence, competence, and readiness to vaccinate (Carroll et al., 2020; Hastings et al., 2020). Pharmacists who complete structured immunisation training often report greater confidence in vaccine administration and patient communication, which can increase their willingness to participate in immunisation services (Ezeala et al., 2025; Luthin et al., 2011). Practical exposure and hands-on vaccine administration training have also been linked to greater comfort and preparedness among pharmacy students and practicing pharmacists (Doyle-Campbell et al., 2022; Lau et al., 2017).

The role of perceived behavioural control underlines the need to build pharmacists’ practical competence in vaccination services. Previous studies have identified inadequate knowledge, limited training, and insufficient practical experience as barriers to confidence (Au et al., 2024; Kristina & Hanifah, 2021). Greater access to certification programmes, continuing professional development, and vaccination-specific training is therefore likely to increase pharmacists’ readiness and support broader implementation of vaccination services in Arabic countries.

Patient-related attitude was not a significant predictor of intention, contrary to the expected relationship. Pharmacists may recognise that vaccination services can improve access, convenience, and immunisation coverage for patients. However, these perceived patient benefits did not independently explain intention once the other constructs were included in the model. A likely explanation is the close relationship between patient-related attitude and personal development attitude. The discriminant validity assessment showed a strong correlation between these two constructs. This indicates that pharmacists who see patient benefits may also see professional benefits. In the structural model, the stronger personal development construct may therefore have captured much of the explanatory role that patient-related attitude would otherwise have contributed.

Attitude toward negative consequences was also not a significant predictor. This suggests that concerns about workload, legal responsibility, adverse events, limited resources, or operational difficulties were not the strongest drivers of pharmacists’ intention in this sample. Previous research has identified many barriers to pharmacist vaccination services, including lack of legal authorisation, staff shortages, inadequate facilities, limited reimbursement, and workload pressures (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Ayenew et al., 2024; Francis et al., 2026; Jarab et al., 2022; Kadhim, 2024; Kelling et al., 2016; Meraya et al., 2024; Shahin et al., 2024). Legal authority remains a major issue in several countries, where pharmacists report that they are not permitted to administer vaccines (Ayenew et al., 2024; Kadhim, 2024; Kristina & Hanifah, 2021). Other barriers include insufficient vaccine storage capacity, weak links with immunisation information systems, limited collaboration with physicians, and lack of financial incentives (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Chadi et al., 2024; Jarab et al., 2022; MacBrayne et al., 2021; Qamar et al., 2022; Shahin et al., 2024; Westrick et al., 2018).

Despite these barriers, their non-significant effect in the present model indicates that pharmacists may still intend to provide vaccination services when they feel professionally motivated, socially supported, and confident in their abilities. The findings suggest that enabling factors had more influence on intention than perceived barriers. Nevertheless, these findings should not be interpreted as indicating that pharmacists are willing or able to provide vaccination services irrespective of legal authorisation. Rather, they demonstrate that pharmacists’ psychological intention remained positive despite existing regulatory and operational barriers. Actual implementation continues to depend on national legislation, professional certification, organisational support, and regulatory approval (Alnahar et al., 2022; Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Meraya et al., 2024).

Authorisation of community pharmacists to administer vaccines remains heterogeneous across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) region and has not yet evolved into a fully harmonised regional model. The available evidence indicates that implementation differs substantially between countries with respect to regulatory authorisation, service settings, and professional requirements. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), pharmacist-administered vaccination has progressed through a regulated, emirate-level approach rather than a unified national framework. For example, the Abu Dhabi Department of Health (DoH) has established regulatory standards permitting designated Vaccination Qualified Pharmacies to administer seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines to adults, provided that pharmacists complete formal training and obtain the required certification. This authorisation remains restricted to approved pharmacies operating within the DoH regulatory framework rather than representing unrestricted nationwide community pharmacy practice (Aldajani & Aldosari, 2023; Department of Health–Abu Dhabi, n.d.). Consistent with this evolving regulatory environment, studies conducted among UAE community pharmacists have demonstrated strong willingness to expand their professional role into vaccination services, although implementation continues to depend on regulatory approval, structured training, and adequate pharmacy infrastructure (Rahma et al., 2025; Thomas et al., 2024). Additional evidence among healthcare professionals in the UAE has also highlighted important educational needs, including strengthening vaccine-related knowledge, communication skills, and confidence in addressing vaccine hesitancy, further supporting the importance of competency-based training before wider implementation of pharmacist-administered vaccination services (Elbarazi et al., 2021; Kampani et al., 2025).

In Saudi Arabia, the literature indicates that community pharmacists have become increasingly involved in national vaccination initiatives, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, reflecting a progressive expansion of pharmacy-based immunisation services(Aldajani & Aldosari, 2023). Nevertheless, implementation remains closely regulated through Ministry of Health policies and requires appropriate professional training and competency development. Several studies have shown that Saudi pharmacists generally support an expanded vaccination role and recognise its potential contribution to improving public health and increasing vaccine accessibility (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Alshahrani, 2022; Alshahrani et al., 2023). However, important implementation barriers persist, including insufficient vaccination-specific knowledge, lack of standardised certification, inadequate infrastructure, staffing constraints, limited coordination with healthcare facilities, and uncertainty regarding regulatory requirements (Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Alshahrani, 2022; Alshahrani et al., 2023). Notably, less than half of pharmacists surveyed in the Qassim region were aware of their legal authorisation to provide adult vaccination services, highlighting the need for clearer regulatory communication alongside standardised education and certification programmes (Alrasheedy et al., 2024).

Compared with the UAE and Saudi Arabia, pharmacist-administered vaccination in Qatar remains more limited. Existing evidence indicates that adult vaccination services continue to be delivered primarily through primary healthcare centres and institutional settings rather than routine commercial community pharmacies (Gardenia Medical Centre, n.d.). Although community pharmacists recognise vaccination as an important component of their professional role, most report limited practical experience, inadequate training, and low confidence in vaccine administration (El Hajj et al., 2023). More than four-fifths of pharmacists had not received formal vaccination training, and only a minority considered themselves competent to administer vaccines, emphasising the need for comprehensive training programmes, organisational support, and structural changes before broader implementation can be achieved (El Hajj et al., 2023).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that although community pharmacists across the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar consistently express positive attitudes toward expanding their role in immunisation services, the transition from intention to routine clinical practice depends largely on supportive regulatory frameworks, formal authorisation, standardised competency-based training, certification, and appropriate pharmacy infrastructure (Aldajani & Aldosari, 2023; Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Alshahrani, 2022; Alshahrani et al., 2023; El Hajj et al., 2023; Elbarazi et al., 2021; Kampani et al., 2025; Rahma et al., 2025; Thomas et al., 2024). These regional differences are also important for interpreting the present findings. Because participating pharmacists were recruited from healthcare systems operating under different regulatory environments, legal authority to administer vaccines may have influenced pharmacists’ perceived behavioural control and professional readiness. Consequently, the observed intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services should be interpreted as reflecting pharmacists’ psychological readiness to undertake this expanded clinical role rather than indicating that vaccination services were already legally authorised or routinely implemented across all participating countries.

The findings carry practical implications for pharmacy education, professional practice, and health policy in Arabic countries. Because personal development attitude was the strongest predictor, strategies to promote pharmacist-led vaccination should emphasise professional advancement, expanded clinical responsibilities, and the value of pharmacists as immunisation providers. Training and certification programmes should therefore cover technical vaccine-administration skills while also reinforcing pharmacists’ clinical identity.

The effect of subjective norm shows that professional and public support will be important for implementation. Professional organisations, healthcare authorities, physicians, and patients all have a role in shaping acceptance of pharmacist-administered vaccination. Collaborative practice models and public information campaigns may help increase confidence in pharmacists’ ability to provide safe and effective vaccination services.

The influence of perceived behavioural control points to the need for competency-based training, continuing professional education, and practical vaccination experience. Regulatory bodies should also address structural barriers through clear legislation, practice guidelines, liability protections, reimbursement arrangements, and infrastructure support.

These actions are especially relevant in Arabic countries, where prior studies have reported positive attitudes toward pharmacist-administered vaccination but mixed levels of preparedness, training, and regulatory support (Alnahar et al., 2022; Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Kadhim, 2024; Meraya et al., 2024; Thomas et al., 2024; Youssef et al., 2021). Research performed in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Lebanon, and Iraq has shown that pharmacists are often willing to provide vaccination services when they receive appropriate training, legal authorisation, and institutional support (Alnahar et al., 2022; Alrasheedy et al., 2024; Kadhim, 2024; Meraya et al., 2024; Thomas et al., 2024; Youssef et al., 2021).

This study has several strengths. It used structural equation modelling, assessed reliability and validity thoroughly, and applied a theory-based framework that explained a large proportion of the variance in intention.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of cause-and-effect relationships. Second, data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, which may be subject to social desirability and response biases. Third, the study evaluated pharmacists’ intention to provide pharmacist-driven vaccination services rather than their actual vaccination practice or future service implementation. Furthermore, although participants were recruited from three Arabic-speaking countries (the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar), the sample does not represent all Arabic-speaking countries, and the findings should therefore be interpreted within the context of the participating healthcare systems. Finally, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as pharmacists with greater interest in vaccination services or professional development may have been more likely to participate. Consequently, the study sample cannot be considered nationally representative of community pharmacists in the participating countries, and caution should be exercised when generalising the findings beyond the study population. Future research should examine whether pharmacists who report strong intention to provide vaccination services go on to deliver these services in practice. Further studies should also test interventions designed to improve pharmacists’ training, confidence, professional motivation, and long-term involvement in vaccination programmes.

Conclusion

This study examined the factors associated with community pharmacists’ intention to offer pharmacist-driven vaccination services in Arabic countries. The structural equation framework explained 67.2% of the variance in intention, showing strong explanatory capacity. Personal development attitude was the strongest predictor, followed by subjective norm and perceived behavioural control. These outcomes suggest that pharmacists are more likely to intend to provide vaccination services when they see the role as professionally beneficial, feel supported by important stakeholders, and believe they have the skills and confidence to perform the service.

Patient-related attitude and attitude toward negative consequences were not significant predictors. This indicates that pharmacists’ intentions were shaped more strongly by professional motivation, social influence, and self-efficacy than by perceived patient benefits or concerns about barriers and risks.

The findings support the need for competency-based training, continuing professional development, supportive regulation, and stronger collaboration between pharmacists and other healthcare professionals. Because community pharmacies are accessible and many pharmacists are willing to expand their clinical role, including pharmacists in national immunisation strategies can improve vaccine access and support public health across Arabic countries.

Future studies should determine whether intention leads to actual vaccination practice and evaluate interventions that strengthen pharmacists’ readiness, competence, and sustained participation in pharmacist-driven vaccination services.
