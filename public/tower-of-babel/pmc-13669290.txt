The Current State of Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction: Graft Selection, Lateral Augmentation, Rehabilitation, and Return-to-Play
Authors: Jeffrey George, Andrew J Moyal, Isha K Shah, Robert J Burkhart, Louis S Kang, Jacob G Calcei, James E Voos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewAnterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) reliably restores anteroposterior stability; however, the rate of return to prior level of play and incidence of recurrent ACL injury have improved little over the past two decades. The purpose of this review is to describe the current state of ACLR with respect to graft selection, lateral extra-articular augmentation, rehabilitation and return to play, including the role of psychological readiness, and the specific rehabilitation protocol utilized by the senior author.Recent FindingsGraft utilization has shifted substantially. Quadriceps tendon autograft accounted for less than 5% of primary reconstructions prior to 2014 and approximately 20% by 2023. Contemporary registry data demonstrate smaller differences in revision risk between autograft types than previously reported. Randomized and comparative data support lateral extra-articular tenodesis in selected high-risk knees, with the largest effect reported in the revision setting. Return-to-play practice has shifted from fixed timelines toward criteria-based clearance. However, a subset of athletes do not pass a full clearance battery, and some athletes who do pass and observe recommended timeframes still sustain reinjury. Limb symmetry index may overestimate functional recovery, while test batteries and thresholds vary considerably between institutions. Psychological readiness is independently associated with both return to sport and second ACL injury.Summary Graft selection should be matched to the patient profile, and lateral augmentation should be reserved for high-risk knees. Return to play should be determined by a combination of time from surgery, functional criteria, and psychological readiness. Future study should focus on validating return-to-play criteria against reinjury endpoints and on standardizing how these criteria are defined and reported.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Graft selection, Lateral extra-articular augmentation, Return to play, Psychological readiness, Reinjury

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is among the most common and most consequential injuries sustained by athletes [1, 2]. Analysis of a United States national insurance database identified 931,186 ACL tears between 2010 and 2020 [1]. Incidence is highest among adolescents, and adolescent female athletes sustain ACL injuries at approximately 1.56 times the rate of male athletes, with the greatest per-season risk in soccer, basketball, and gymnastics [2]. Injury rates are higher in club sport than in school-sponsored sport [2].

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is among the most frequently performed procedures in orthopaedic sports medicine and successive technical refinements have been aimed at optimizing outcomes in high-demand athletes [3]. Despite this, functional outcomes have not improved proportionally. Approximately 65% of athletes return to preinjury level of sport and 55% return to competitive sport [4]. Among athletes younger than 25 years who return to pivoting sport, the combined rate of ipsilateral graft rupture and contralateral ACL injury approaches 23% [5]. This discrepancy has led to increasing emphasis on discussion of graft type, incorporation of lateral ligamentous repair/reconstruction, and parameters for return to play clearance.

The purpose of this narrative review is to describe the current state of ACLR with respect to graft selection, lateral augmentation, rehabilitation, and return to play. In doing so, the authors sought to answer the following questions: (1) Which graft should be selected for a given patient profile, and what accounts for the recent increase in quadriceps tendon utilization? (2) Which patients benefit from lateral extra-articular augmentation? (3) Does the senior author’s rehabilitation protocol differ according to graft choice or the addition of LET? (4) Should return to play be governed by time from surgery, by functional criteria, or by both? (5) What is the role of psychological readiness in return-to-play decision-making?

Graft Selection

Utilization Trends

Serial survey data from the ACL Study Group spanning 1998 to 2023 document longitudinal changes in graft preference. Over that period, bone–patellar tendon–bone (BTB) autograft use declined gradually, hamstring tendon (HT) autograft use rose and subsequently fell, allograft use remained low among surveyed surgeons, and quadriceps tendon (QT) autograft use rose sharply [6]. QT accounted for no more than 5% of primary reconstructions between 1998 and 2014 but approached 20% by 2023 [6]. In the revision setting, hamstring use declined abruptly after 2016, with concurrent increases in both BTB and QT utilization [6].

Bone–Patellar Tendon–Bone

BTB autograft remains the reference standard for the young, high-demand pivoting athlete. Its advantages are both mechanical and biological: bone-to-bone incorporation at both tunnels, predictable and rigid fixation, and an extensive track record in competitive populations [3]. A recent meta-analysis restricted to patients younger than 25 years found graft failure rates of 11.1% for HT, 5.1% for BTB, and 2.5% for QT. Failure was significantly higher with HT than with BTB, whereas QT did not differ significantly from either graft [7].

The trade-off is donor-site morbidity. Anterior knee pain and pain with kneeling remain the most frequent complaints, and patellar fracture and patellar tendon rupture are rare but consequential [3]. These considerations are particularly relevant in athletes whose sport involves frequent kneeling or direct patellar contact. Graft characteristics and typical indications are summarized in Table 1.

Hamstring Tendon

Preference for hamstring autograft has declined among surveyed ACL Study Group surgeons, while a recent meta-analysis found higher failure rates with HT than BTB in patients younger than 25 years [6, 7]. Residual knee flexion weakness and tunnel widening are recognized limitations [3, 8]. Contemporary registry data, however, temper the concerns regarding revision risks. In a Swedish Knee Ligament Registry analysis of 44,513 patients, two-year revision rates were 2.2% for QT, 2.5% for BTB, and 2.3% for HT, while graft type was not associated with revision hazard in adjusted analysis [9]. A separate cohort of 27,715 patients undergoing ACLR between 2012 and 2023 found that QT was associated with lower revision risk than HT only in females younger than 22 years [10].

Quadriceps Tendon

QT autograft has been the principal beneficiary of the shift away from hamstring graft. Advantages include a large and reliably obtainable cross-sectional area, the option of an all–soft tissue or bone-block configuration, lower donor-site morbidity than BTB, and donor graft-site pain reported in 0% to 11.5% of patients across studies [3, 11, 12]. A systematic review of randomized trials has demonstrated comparable graft failure, laxity, complication rates, and patient-reported outcomes across QT, HT, and BTB [12].

Two caveats warrant emphasis. First, graft-specific strength recovery is not neutral. Recent synthesis of randomized data indicates that hamstring autografts favor recovery of knee extension strength while quadriceps autografts favor recovery of flexion strength, with direct implications for rehabilitation programming and interpretation of strength-based clearance criteria [13]. Second, QT outcomes appear sensitive to surgeon volume. Danish registry data demonstrated a QT revision rate of 6.4% at low-volume centers compared with 2.9% at high-volume centers, with an adjusted revision hazard ratio of 2.3 for low-volume practice [14].

The current state of QT literature is continuing to grow, with increasing evidence supporting its use as a viable graft, especially when an all soft-tissue graft is needed. The aggregate signal is that QT performs comparably to established autografts in experienced hands [9–12]. The volume-dependence finding is the more actionable of the two [14]. A surgeon adopting QT should anticipate a learning curve and should not assume that outcomes published from high-volume centers apply to early experience.

Allograft

Allograft should be avoided in young athletes. Comparative data demonstrate substantially higher failure rates with allograft in patients 19 years or younger, with pooled failure rates of 25.5% for allograft, 16.6% for HT autograft, and 8.5% for BTB autograft [15]. More recent propensity-matched analysis in adolescents has suggested the difference narrows after adjustment for baseline characteristics, but the weight of evidence continues to favor autograft in adolescent patients returning to sport [15]. Allograft retains a defensible role in older or lower-demand patients, in multiligament reconstruction where autograft supply is limited, and in selected revision scenarios [3].

Lateral Extra-Articular Augmentation

Isolated intra-articular reconstruction may not fully restore anterolateral rotatory stability within high-risk patients. Persistent pivot shift after appropriately performed ACLR has been associated with worse patient-reported outcomes and graft failure, driving renewed interest in lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) and anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction [16].

The STABILITY randomized clinical trial provides the strongest primary evidence. The trial enrolled 618 patients aged between 14 and 25 years with at least two of three risk features (grade 2 or greater pivot shift, intention to return to high-risk pivoting sport, and generalized ligamentous laxity) and randomized patients to hamstring autograft ACLR with or without a modified Lemaire LET [17]. At two-year follow-up, the addition of LET significantly reduced graft rupture and similarly the clinical-failure endpoint of graft rupture or persistent rotatory laxity [17]. Though LET did not significantly increase overall rates of minor medical events, minor surgical events, or reoperation, hardware irritation and removal were more frequent [18].

The revision setting shows an even larger effect. A retrospective comparative series with a mean follow-up beyond six years reported graft failure in 3.3% of revision ACLR performed with LET compared with 18.3% of isolated revision ACLR [19]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies in revision ACLR similarly found that the addition of LET or ALL reconstruction improved subjective IKDC scores, restored rotational stability, and reduced failure rates relative to isolated revision reconstruction [20]. A large administrative database analysis found LET was correlated with a reduced risk of subsequent revision ACLR [21].

Contemporary indications for LET, reflected in both published series and current practice patterns, include revision reconstruction, high-grade pivot shift, generalized ligamentous laxity or knee hyperextension, and the young athlete returning to pivoting or contact sport [16, 22]. Technique selection, including modified Lemaire, MacIntosh, and anatomic ALL reconstruction, remains an area of debate and has recently been reviewed in detail [16].

The authors perform lateral augmentation routinely in revision reconstruction, consistent with the magnitude of benefit reported during revision ACLR, and selectively in primary reconstruction when two or more stability risk features are present [17]. Importantly, LET is not a substitute for appropriately performed primary reconstruction. Attention to tunnel position, graft selection, and treatment of concomitant meniscal and slope-related pathology remains essential.

Return to Play: Timing and Criteria

The Case for Time-Based Clearance

Temporal evidence is among the most robust in this literature. In the Delaware–Oslo ACL cohort, each additional month of delayed return to sport up to nine months was associated with an approximately 51% reduction in reinjury risk [23]. Application of time-based and functional return-to-sport criteria was associated with an estimated 84% lower reinjury risk, although the comparison did not reach statistical significance [23]. In a separate cohort, young athletes returning before nine months sustained secondary ACL injuries at approximately seven times the rate of athletes returning greater than nine months following ACLR [24]. A meta-analysis found that patients who sustained a second ACL injury returned to sport 25 days earlier (95% CI, 9.5–40.4 days) than those who did not; however, this trend was not seen in professional athletes [25]. Subsequent work from the Delaware–Oslo cohort has further suggested that activity level and functional readiness, rather than chronological age itself, are the critical determinants [26]. Graft ligamentization may continue for twelve to twenty-four months and varies considerably between individuals [27].

The Case for Criteria-Based Clearance

Criteria-based clearance rests on the premise that measurable functional recovery, not elapsed time, should govern the decision. The most frequently cited evidence comes from a cohort of 158 male professional athletes, of whom 26 (16.5%) sustained graft rupture at a mean of 105 days after return to sport [28]. Failure to meet all six discharge criteria before returning to team training was associated with a hazard ratio of 4.1 for graft rupture [28]. A typical contemporary battery includes isokinetic quadriceps and hamstring strength testing, a hop test battery, assessment of movement quality, and increasingly a patient-reported measure of psychological readiness when determining appropriate return to sport [28, 29]. Remaining Questions in Return-to-Play Assessment

Criteria-based clearance represents the best available synthesis of the return-to-play literature and has meaningfully advanced practice [29]. Given the widespread adoption of these criteria, their remaining limitations warrant examination.

First, a subset of athletes do not pass a full clearance battery, while athletes who do pass criteria while observing recommended timeframes still sustain reinjury [30]. A systematic review with meta-analysis reported that 42.7% of patients passed return-to-sport criteria and that 14.4% of those who passed sustained a second ACL injury, with no statistically significant association between passing criteria and reduced second injury risk [31]. The authors of that review noted that the quality of available evidence precludes a definitive conclusion. This finding should not be interpreted as an argument against criteria-based clearance, but rather as an open question regarding what current batteries measure and which residual factors they fail to capture.

Second, limb symmetry index (LSI) may overestimate functional recovery, as the uninvolved limb also deconditions during rehabilitation [30]. When preoperative uninvolved-limb performance is used as the reference standard, the difference is substantial. In one cohort, 57.1% of patients achieved 90% LSI across quadriceps strength and all hop tests, whereas only 28.6% met the same 90% threshold against estimated preinjury capacity, and estimated preinjury capacity was more sensitive than LSI in predicting second ACL injury [30]. This offers one explanation for reinjury among athletes who have met symmetry thresholds, and supports preinjury or estimated preinjury referencing where baseline testing is feasible.

Third, batteries, thresholds, and integration of patient-reported outcome measures vary considerably between institutions. Systematic and scoping reviews have documented marked heterogeneity and a lack of standardization in how return to sport is assessed and reported [29, 32]. This limits comparison across published cohorts and may account for variability in reinjury rates reported between centers [32]. Standardization of reporting would meaningfully improve comparability of future studies.

Psychological Readiness

Kinesiophobia and fear of reinjury are commonly seen following ACLR and have been shown to impede return to sport, alter measurable knee biomechanics, and potentially increase the risk of re-rupture [33]. Although current clinical practice guidelines recognize the role of psychological factors in rehabilitation, patient-reported outcome measures and psychological readiness are infrequently incorporated into rehabilitation protocols [33]. This may contribute in part to the discrepancy between high rates of successful surgical reconstruction and considerably lower rates of return to competitive play [4].

The ACL-Return to Sport after Injury (ACL-RSI) scale is the most widely used instrument in this domain, assessing emotion, confidence in performance, and risk appraisal. The predictive value of ACL-RSI extends beyond return to sport alone. Among 329 patients who returned to sport, 52 (16%) sustained a second ACL injury, and lower psychological readiness plus younger age were identified as risk factors [34]. Importantly, the relationship between psychological readiness and reinjury may vary by assessment timing, with higher psychological readiness at three months postoperatively associated with a higher incidence of second ACL injury [35].

The trajectory of these scores affects how the instrument should be used. A systematic review with meta-analysis of ACL-RSI scores over time demonstrates that psychological readiness improves early after reconstruction, with comparatively little subsequent improvement until two years or beyond [36]. A single low score at the point of clearance may therefore reflect a stable feature of that athlete’s recovery rather than a transient state. This supports serial measurement and early intervention rather than a single assessment at the end of rehabilitation [36].

Interest has accordingly shifted toward psychologically informed rehabilitation, graded exposure to sport-specific movements, and targeted interventions such as sports-specific visualization, knee bracing, and kinesiotaping [33]. Network meta-analysis has supported integration of psychological and physical assessment for optimizing return-to-sport outcomes [37]. The authors administer the ACL-RSI as part of the clearance battery (Table 2) and regard lower scores as an indication for further targeted rehabilitation rather than for clearance with reassurance alone [33, 37].

Key Principles

Given the above, several key principles can be applied to return-to-play decision-making following ACLR (Fig. 1):

Preoperative assessment of patient age, sport demand, pivot shift grade, and ligamentous laxity informs graft selection and the decision regarding lateral extra-articular augmentation. Postoperative clearance requires concurrent satisfaction of three domains: a temporal floor of approximately nine months, passage of a functional criteria battery referenced where possible to estimated preinjury capacity, and formal assessment of psychological readiness. Failure in any single domain returns the athlete to targeted rehabilitation rather than to sport

Our Protocol

The senior author implements the aforementioned principles in the institution-wide protocol for ACLR rehabilitation. Regardless of graft choice, rehabilitation progression consists of time- and performance-based metrics. As ligamentization occurs within a specific timeframe, the 8–9-month time point is utilized as a baseline for clearance, along with performance metrics [23–25, 27, 28]. The senior author’s protocol is not independently modified by the addition of LET.

Patients will partake in closed- and open-kinetic-chain work for three months, with consistent and heavy use of occlusion training for quadriceps strength and fan-bike cycling for cardiovascular endurance and quadriceps strength. At three months, patients transition to turf work with straight-line activities, including jogging, with a goal of building back cardiovascular endurance. At six months, patients begin cutting and pivoting activities, along with limb-symmetry index testing to track progression of quadriceps and hamstring strength. Despite the initiation of functional testing, patients are not cleared until the 8–9-month time point. Patients will continue with their rehabilitation until they reach 80–90% LSI on functional and strength-based testing. Once greater than 90% is achieved on LSIs, patients will be cleared for return to sport [23, 28, 29]. If needed, we will continue to assess progression through the 12-month time point.

Along with this rehabilitation protocol, the senior author emphasizes a slow and gradual increase in sport-specific volume, with multiple weeks of practice or simulation prior to competitive play. In patients undergoing more complex procedures, including multiligament reconstruction or revision ACLR, a similar protocol is used with a slower progression.

Conclusions

Contemporary ACLR is best understood as three linked decisions rather than a single operation. Graft selection should be matched to patient profile rather than applied uniformly (Table 1). BTB remains the reference standard in the young high-demand athlete. QT performs comparably in experienced hands with the caveat that outcomes appear volume-dependent. Hamstring autograft performs better in registry data than current North American practice patterns would suggest. Allograft should be avoided in young pivoting athletes. Lateral extra-articular augmentation is now an evidence-supported adjunct, with the largest benefit reported in revision reconstruction and in primary reconstruction of the high-risk knee. Return to play should be governed by a combination of time from surgery, functional criteria, and psychological readiness, none of which is sufficient alone (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Future study should focus on validation of return-to-play criteria against reinjury endpoints, as the batteries in widest use have not been shown to reduce second injury; standardization of how these criteria are defined and reported, in order to allow comparison between cohorts; continued registry surveillance of quadriceps tendon autograft as adoption broadens beyond high-volume centers; and completion of ongoing randomized work extending lateral augmentation to patellar and quadriceps tendon grafts.
