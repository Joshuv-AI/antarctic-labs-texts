Distinct Roles of Directional and Positional Experience in De Novo Visuomotor Learning
Authors: Tomoya Kawano, Shota Hagio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humans can flexibly acquire entirely new sensorimotor mappings, a process known as de novo motor learning. A central challenge in de novo motor learning is that the learner must discover a viable solution from scratch within a highly redundant control space, without predefined task constraints. Understanding what types of sensorimotor information contribute to the formation of accurate motor behavior in such situations is therefore critical for explaining how novel sensorimotor skills are acquired. While previous studies have suggested that novel visuomotor mappings can be formed based on movement direction and target position, it remains unclear how these two types of information contribute to the learning process. To address this question, we trained 25 human participants (13 males and 12 females) to learn arbitrary joystick-to-cursor mapping. We then employed a generalization paradigm to selectively restrict learning experience to either movement direction or target position. Three distinct target conditions were designed: one emphasized target position (P), another emphasized movement direction (D), and a third (P&D) encouraged learning of both components separately. Results showed direction experience reduced initial direction error, whereas the lack of position experience was associated with increased final position error. However, in the P&D condition, combining these experiences did not yield additive generalization. Instead, endpoint accuracy was positively correlated with the alignment between direction- and position-based outputs in the redundant control space. These results suggest that accurate formation of a novel sensorimotor map depends on the coordinated use of directional and positional experiences.

Significance Statement

How do humans build entirely new sensorimotor relationships from scratch? This study examined how distinct sensorimotor experiences (movement direction and target position) contribute to the acquisition of a novel joystick-to-cursor mapping. By isolating these experiences, we found that direction experience improved movement initiation, while the lack of position experience reduced movement termination accuracy. However, combining these experiences did not lead to more accurate movements as a whole. Instead, the accuracy was related to how well directional and positional joystick outputs were aligned in redundant control space. These findings suggest that de novo motor learning requires the coordinated use of directional and positional information.

Introduction

Humans possess a remarkable ability to learn arbitrary sensorimotor maps, a process known as de novo motor learning (Krakauer et al., 2019). This capability underlies many everyday skills, such as playing musical instruments or using unfamiliar tools. For example, when first operating a remote-controlled device (e.g., a drone or robotic manipulator), individuals must learn how their motor commands applied to a handheld device translate into movements of a spatially separate external effector. De novo motor learning refers to the process of establishing this novel relationship (i.e., forming a new sensorimotor map). Previous studies have primarily focused on motor adaptation, where pre-existing sensorimotor maps are recalibrated in response to environmental changes (Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi, 1994; Krakauer et al., 2000). However, information regarding how such sensorimotor maps are built from scratch remains limited.

To experimentally induce de novo motor learning, researchers have devised a variety of tasks. A commonly used approach is to create an arbitrary mapping between the position of a body part and on-screen cursor position (Mosier et al., 2005; Haith et al., 2022; Kawano et al., 2024). For example, in a bimanual task, vertical movements of one hand control horizontal cursor movements and vice versa, forcing participants to acquire a novel visuomotor map through practice (Yang et al., 2021; Haith et al., 2022). While participants initially struggle to direct the cursor toward intended targets, they gradually acquire precise control with training. Although these tasks evoke de novo motor learning, how the brain constructs a new sensorimotor map remains poorly understood. Unlike motor adaptation, de novo motor learning requires establishing entirely new mappings between actions and sensory consequences. This distinction raises a fundamental question: what aspects of the sensorimotor experience does the brain rely on when constructing a novel sensorimotor mapping from scratch?

It has been proposed that the brain can represent visuomotor map based on two distinct types of information (Wang and Sainburg, 2005; Wu and Smith, 2013; Van der Graaff et al., 2017). One is to transform a movement direction into a change in hand posture (vector coding); and the other is to transform a target position into a specific hand posture (position coding). Several studies suggested that de novo motor learning relies on vector coding (Mosier et al., 2005; Ranganathan et al., 2013). Supporting this idea, it has been observed that the cursor trajectory tended to became straight even without explicit instruction (Mosier et al., 2005). In addition, the final hand posture at the target has been shown to depend on the movement direction toward the target (Ranganathan et al., 2013). Conversely, other findings suggested the involvement of position coding. Several studies have demonstrated that de novo motor learning tasks can be learned without continuous visual feedback of a cursor (Liu and Scheidt, 2008; Kim et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024). This finding implies that the brain can construct visuomotor maps using only information about the target position (i.e., position coding). Together, these findings suggest that the brain can build a novel visuomotor map using information about both movement direction and target position.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate how two types of information—movement direction and target position—contribute to de novo motor learning. To this end, we employed a generalization paradigm (Malfait et al., 2002; Donchin et al., 2003; Shadmehr, 2004), which allows us to dissociate the contributions of direction and position experiences by examining how restricted learning experience generalizes to untrained movements. We conducted two experiments. In Experiment 1, three distinct target conditions were designed to elicit generalization from experience with specific components: one target emphasized target position alone, another emphasized movement direction alone, and a third encouraged learning of both elements separately. We define “separately” as experiencing both the matched position and direction of an untrained movement through distinct training contexts, whereas “alone” refers to experiencing only one of these matched components. This experimental design is crucial for examining whether the sensorimotor system can integrate separate learning experiences. We reasoned that if direction and position information additively contribute to the construction of a sensorimotor map, then acquiring both components should lead to greater generalization than experiencing either component alone. Experiment 2 served as a control experiment in which a separate group of participants was trained only on one target condition. This experiment tested whether the generalization pattern observed in Experiment 1 was influenced by interleaving different target conditions within the same participants.

Materials and Methods

Experiment 1

Participants

Twenty-five right-handed individuals without a history of neurological or motor disorders were included in this study [13 males and 12 females; aged 20.8 ± 1.87 years, mean ± standard deviation (SD)]. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed description of the purpose, potential benefits, and risks of the experiment. One female participant was excluded due to failure to complete the whole task within the 1.5-h experiment time (complete up to the 12th block out of 20). All procedures used in this study were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee for Human Experimentation at the Graduate School of Human and Environmental Studies, Kyoto University (24-H-15).

Experimental setup

The participants sat in front of two three-axis joysticks (Model: 9SA50RR-22-47; APEM) equipped on an adjustable table with a 27-in LCD monitor (1,920 × 1,080-pixel resolution, 165 Hz refresh rate; ASUS; Fig. 1A). Both joysticks were positioned symmetrically with respect to the participant’s midline, placed 30 cm forward from the face, and separated laterally by 22.5 cm. Participants could not see their hands, and their head motion was limited by a chinrest. Participants manipulated the two joysticks with both hands using three fingers (thumb, index, and middle finger). Each joystick measured 3-axis positions (i.e., X: horizontal, Y: vertical, and Z: roll axis), acquiring a total of 6 input signals in 16-bit at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. Both joysticks were spring-loaded, meaning they passively returned to the center position when the fingers were removed. We performed preprocessing on these inputs in real time using a custom LabVIEW program as follows: Initially, joystick inputs were normalized such that each axis could vary independently within the range of [ − 1, 1], where ±1 corresponded to ±14.4° tilt for the X- and Y-axes, and ±27° rotation for the Z-axis. Thereafter, the five-point moving average was applied to reduce the intrinsic noise of the joysticks. The post-processing 6-dimensional vector h, representing the joystick positions, was linearly mapped onto the lateral and vertical cursor position p using the equation below (Mosier et al., 2005; Ranganathan et al., 2013; Choi et al., 2020; Fig. 1B): [xy]=[a1,1a1,2…a1,6a2,1a2,2…a2,6]×[h1h2…h5h6]T, i.e., p = Ah, where the mapping matrix A was determined from the calibration task (described below).

Experimental task

There were two tasks in the experiment: a calibration task and point-to-point reaching task.

Calibration task

To determine the mapping matrix A for each participant, calibration task was conducted. In this task, participants were instructed to move the joysticks freely for 1 min. To encourage participants to explore various joystick movements, we presented six bar graphs visualizing the real-time variance of joystick positions for each axis (Choi et al., 2020). The acquired calibration data was standardized for each axis to ensure that all axes contributed to the mapping matrix A. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on the standardized calibration data. PCA identifies orthogonal directions in the data that successively capture the largest variance, such that each principal component represents a dominant pattern of coordinated joystick movements. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to form the first and second rows of the mapping matrix A. Therefore, the scores of the PC1 and PC2 were converted to the lateral and vertical cursor position, respectively. We used first two PCs to avoid requiring participants to perform unfamiliar joystick movements in the following reaching task. Individual mapping matrices for all participants are shown in Figure 1-1.

Point-to-point reaching task

Participants were required to move the on-screen cursor (5 mm radius) to a series of targets (10 mm radius). During the task, targets were presented sequentially, with a sequence of trials referring to a group of consecutive reaches toward these targets (Fig. 2A). Details regarding the locations and order of the targets are described below (Experimental conditions). All trials were classified into either a training trial or a test trial. Each trial was defined as one reaching movement toward a single target. In the training trial, participants were given continuous visual feedback of the cursor. Based on this visual information, participants moved the cursor toward the target. When the cursor stayed within a target for 500 ms, it moved to a new location. At the end of every 2 or 3 training trials (referred to as a sequence of trials), the visual feedback of the cursor was removed and participants were instructed to relax the joysticks (i.e., release their hands so that the spring force passively returned each joystick to the center position) to reset the cursor position at the center of the workspace (Fig. 2B). The number of trials in each sequence differed across experimental conditions (see Experimental conditions). To control the amount of time spent across participants, a time limit of 12 s was imposed only on the final trial of each sequence. If the elapsed time exceeded 12 s, the trial was terminated and participants were instructed to relax the joysticks. Participants were instructed to move the cursor to the target as fast as possible. To motivate them to follow this instruction, we provided a score calculated based on the elapsed time after the start of the trial, which reflects how quickly they reached the target. The score was calculated as follows: score={100(time≤1.5)100−10012−1.5×(time−1.5)(1.5<time<12)0(time≥12). In the test trial, the visual feedback of the cursor was removed to evaluate the open-loop movement learned during the training trial. The test trials were always initiated from the center of the workspace and directed toward one of the eight targets (Fig. 2C). Experimental conditions

We designed four experimental conditions to test how learning generalized across untrained movements. To clarify how movement direction and target position contribute to the acquisition of a de novo visuomotor map, we employed a generalization paradigm (Malfait et al., 2002; Donchin et al., 2003; Shadmehr, 2004). This approach makes it possible to disentangle the respective roles of direction and position by testing how learning under restricted experience transfers to novel, untrained movements. Within this framework, we defined four conditions that varied in the type of information provided. The Control (C) condition involved direct practice of the test movement, serving as a baseline. The Position (P) condition exposed participants only to the final position of the test movement, while the Direction (D) condition exposed them only to movement direction. The Position and Direction (P&D) condition combined these two components, allowing participants to experience target position and movement direction separately. The aim of this experimental design is to evaluate how well learning generalizes to the P, D, and P&D conditions relative to the common baseline provided by the C condition, in which the test movement is directly trained. Figure 2C shows an example configuration of the test targets for the four conditions. The following explanations will be based on this configuration. There are 12 potential targets that could be presented in training trials (Fig. 2A, T1–T12). Participants were trained to move between these targets.

Control condition

In this condition, the movement in the test trial was a movement upward from the center of the workspace (Fig. 2C). This condition served as a baseline to assess the amount of learning when participants were directly trained on the test movement. To this end, participants were trained to perform the same upward movement as in the test trial. The training was conducted using a sequence of three trials. Initially, the cursor was located at T1. Then, targets were presented in the sequence of either T2→T1→T6 or T5→T1→T6 (Fig. 2A). The inclusion of the T2→T1 (or T5→T1) movements was intended to balance the number of movements toward the targets (T2–T5) surrounding the test target. These consecutive three trials are referred to as the C (Control) sequence.

Position condition

In this condition, the movement in the test trial was a movement to the left from the center of the workspace (Fig. 2C). During training, the target position was kept consistent while the movement direction was varied. To achieve this, they were trained to move to the same position, but from a different direction. The training was conducted using a sequence of two trials. Initially, the cursor was located at T1. Then, targets were presented in the sequence of either T2→T7 or T3→T7 (Fig. 2A). Importantly, T7 was the same as the test target of this condition (Fig. 2C). These consecutive two trials are referred to as the P (Position) sequence.

Direction condition

In this condition, the movement in the test trial was a movement to the right from the center of the workspace (Fig. 2C). During training, the movement direction was kept consistent while the target position was varied. To this end, they were trained to move from the same direction to different positions. The training was conducted using a sequence of two trials. Initially, the cursor was located at T1. Then, targets were presented in the sequence of either T5→T12 or T4→T11 (Fig. 2A). Crucially, T5→T12 and T4→T11 movements matched the movement direction of the test trial (Fig. 2C). This consecutive two trials are referred to as the D (Direction) sequence.

Position and direction condition

In this condition, the movement in the test trial was a movement downward from the center of the workspace (Fig. 2C). Participants were allowed to separately experience both the position and direction of the movement. To this end, participants were trained using both the P- and D-sequences. Initially, the cursor was located at T1. Then, targets were presented in the sequence of either T3→T8, T3→T9, T4→T8, or T4→T10 (Fig. 2A). Essentially, T8 was the same as the test target of this condition. Additionally, T3→T9 and T4→T10 movements matched the movement direction of the test trial (Fig. 2C). These consecutive two trials are referred to as the P&D (Position and Direction) sequence. If the effects of direction and position experience additively contribute to the acquisition of a de novo sensorimotor map, learning both components separately would yield greater generalization than learning either component alone (P condition and D condition).

Experimental procedure

The experimental task consisted of 20 blocks (Fig. 2E). Each block consisted of 20 training sequences and 8 test trials (Fig. 2E). The sequences for each condition were presented in a pseudorandom order every four sequences. In block 1, test trials were presented at the beginning of the block to test movements with minimal training effects. In the subsequent blocks, test trials were randomly interleaved between sequences. The rest period between blocks was 30 s, with a longer 120 s rest period provided every five blocks.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using MATLAB R2025a (MathWorks, Inc.). We recorded cursor position, joystick position, and joystick tangential velocity at 50 Hz. The joystick tangential velocity was low-pass filtered (5 Hz) using a fourth-order Butterworth filter.

Task performance

The task performance in the training trials was assessed by movement time. The movement time was the time elapsed from movement onset to the end of the trial. The movement onset was defined as the time when joystick tangential velocity exceeded a threshold of 0.5 for 10 consecutive time steps. In the test trial, we characterized each reaching movement by separating it into two continuous phases: movement initiation and movement termination. To quantify the accuracy of these phases, we computed the initial direction error and the final position error, respectively. The final position error was calculated as the Euclidean distance between the final cursor position and the target position. The initial direction error was quantified as the absolute angle between the direction from the center of the workspace to the cursor position at the time when the joystick tangential velocity reached its first peak after movement onset and the direction from the center of the workspace to the target position. The peak of the joystick tangential velocity was identified using the MATLAB function findpeaks, with the argument MinPeakProminence set to 5% of the maximum velocity in each trial.

Position- and direction-based visuomotor outputs in joystick control space

In the P&D condition, the effects of position and direction experiences appear additive in the workspace since the direction experience matches the movement direction of the test trial and the position experience matches the target position (Fig. 5A). However, in the joystick control space, which includes task-irrelevant dimensions, visuomotor outputs based on direction and position are not necessarily aligned (Fig. 5B). We therefore tested how the degree of alignment between position- and direction-based visuomotor outputs influenced generalization in the P&D condition. To this end, we calculated how target positions or movement directions were transformed into joystick positions or joystick movement directions, respectively. The former was defined as the position-based visuomotor output (VOpos), and the latter as the direction-based visuomotor output (VOdir). To estimate VOpos, we averaged the final joystick positions across trials directed to the same target position. Similarly, to estimate VOdir, we calculated joystick movement vectors as the difference between the final and initial joystick positions in each trial, and then averaged these vectors across trials with the same movement direction in the workspace. For this analysis, we used training trials from the last five blocks. Because the number of trials for averaging was small (12 or 13), a bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples was used to obtain stable estimates of these averages. To quantify the alignment between the two outputs, we calculated the angle between VOdir and VOpos as arccos(VOdir⋅VOpos‖VOdir‖‖VOpos‖), where a smaller value indicates greater alignment. Finally, to examine whether this alignment explains individual differences in the final position error in the P&D condition while controlling for individual differences in movement initiation, we performed a linear regression at the participant level with the alignment and initial direction error as predictors of the final position error.

Outlier criteria

To avoid outlier influenced bias during hypothesis testing, outliers were removed using the quartile method. Specifically, this procedure was applied to the group data pooled across all participants within each condition. First, the first quartile (Q1) and the third quartile (Q3), which represent the 25th and 75th percentiles of the data, respectively, were calculated. Thereafter the interquartile range (IQR) was calculated by subtracting Q1 from Q3: IQR=Q3−Q1. Next, the lower- and upper bound for outliers were calculated: Lower bound=Q1−1.5×IQR,Upper bound=Q3+1.5×IQR. Any data points outside this range were considered outliers. Outliers were identified separately for the initial direction error and the final position error, as these measures quantify distinct phases of the movement (initiation and termination). Accordingly, a trial that is extreme in one measure is not necessarily anomalous in the other.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were calculated using R version 4.2.2 (Rstudio, Inc.). Data from 24 participants were analyzed. To investigate the effects of condition and trial on the error in last 5 test trials (either final position error or initial direction error), we conducted a linear mixed-effects model analysis using the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015). For the initial direction error, the fixed effects included condition and trial. For the final position error, we additionally included initial direction error as a covariate. This was motivated by the fact that the final cursor position is geometrically influenced by the initial movement direction; statistically controlling for initial direction error therefore allowed us to isolate the contribution of each condition to movement termination beyond what was already explained by movement initiation. First, outliers within each condition were excluded using the quartile method (a total of 3.1% and 2.1% of the data was removed for initial direction error and final position error, respectively). After outlier removal, the analyses included 465 observations for initial direction error and 470 observations for final position error. The random effects included both random intercepts and random slopes for participants. Statistical significance of the fixed effects was determined using Type III F-tests. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using t-tests on estimated marginal means derived from the linear mixed-effects models (using the emmeans package in R), and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. In all statistical analyses, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cohen’s d was computed as effect size. Experiment 2

To assess whether interleaving the four training conditions within each block in the Experiment 1 introduced interference among them, we conducted Experiment 2. In Experiment 2, a separate group of participants was trained exclusively on the D (Direction) sequence.

Participants

Twenty-four right-handed individuals without a history of neurological or motor disorders participated in this control experiment (10 males and 14 females; aged 20 ± 2.82 years, mean ± SD). The sample size was matched to that of the Experiment 1. All participants provided written informed consent.

Task

The experimental setup, and calibration procedure were identical to those of the Experiment 1. The only differences were the composition of training targets and training sequences within each block (Fig. 6A). Participants were trained exclusively on the D (Direction) sequence. Accordingly, the training targets were reduced to five (Fig. 6A, T1–T5). The two trained movements (T4 → T5 and T2 → T3) were directionally matched to the test direction, in the same manner as the D condition of the Experiment 1. The assignment of the D condition to one of the four cardinal test directions was counterbalanced across participants. The task consisted of 10 blocks (Fig. 6B). Each block consisted of 20 D training sequences and 8 test trials that were randomly interleaved between the sequences. Of the 8 test trials per block, 4 were directed to the trained target positions (2 trials for each of the two trained movements, T2 → T3 and T4 → T5), which is defined as C condition. The remaining 4 trials were directed to the D condition test target (Fig. 6A right).

Data analysis

The initial direction error and final position error were quantified using the same methods as in Experiment 1. Because participants in Experiment 2 were trained exclusively on the D-sequence with 20 D sequences per block (compared with 5 per block in Experiment 1), the same amount of practice accumulated within fewer blocks.

To align practice experience across conditions and experiments, two matched reference points were defined in Experiment 2. The mid-training reference for the D condition (Dmid) was established at the end of block 5 (Fig. 6B), at which point participants had completed 100 D sequences, matching the total D-sequence experience of the D condition in Experiment 1. The last-training reference for C condition (Clast) was measured at the end of block 10 (Fig. 6B). Because the two trained movements (T2 → T3 and T4 → T5) were each presented 10 times per block, participants had accumulated 100 direct practices for each by block 10, matching the direct practice amount of the C condition in Experiment 1 (100 C sequences). A comparison between Dmid and Clast therefore assessed whether directional training alone produced performance comparable to that achieved through an equivalent amount of direct practice. In addition, Dmid was compared with the D condition of Experiment 1 to verify whether matching the total amount of D-sequence practice across different experimental designs yielded comparable task performance.

To examine the effect of additional directional training, a late-training reference for the D condition (Dlast) was defined at the end of block 10 (Fig. 6B), by which point participants had completed 200 D sequences. Comparing Dlast with Clast allowed us to determine whether further practice enables generalization.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R, following the same framework as in Experiment 1. For each reference point (Clast, Dmid, and Dlast), five test trials at the corresponding stage of training were used. As in Experiment 1, outliers within each condition were excluded using the quartile (IQR) method. The proportion of data removed for the initial direction error was 3.3%, 1.7%, and 3.3% in Clast, Dmid, and Dlast, respectively, and 11.7%, 1.7%, and 2.5% for the final position error. Both errors were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, and pairwise comparisons were conducted on the estimated marginal means using the emmeans package. Cohen’s d was computed as the effect size. The initial direction error was included as a covariate when analyzing the final position error, as in Experiment 1. In the within-subject comparisons within Experiment 2 (Clast vs Dmid and Clast vs Dlast), the models included participant-specific random intercepts and random slopes, whereas in the between-subject comparison between the D condition of Experiment 1 and Dmid in Experiment 2, only random intercepts were included.

Results

Experiment 1

Task performance improved in the training trials

Participants learned the de novo visuomotor task through repeated training. Figure 3A shows the cursor trajectories of a representative participant in training trials. In the early stages, the cursor movements were erratic under all conditions. In contrast, in the later stages, the cursor moved directly toward the target more efficiently. The movement time decreased as the training progressed, indicating that participants learned to move the cursor to the target more quickly (Fig. 3B).

Generalization in the test trials

During the course of training, participants performed test trials on eight targets. Among the eight targets, four targets were assigned to four different conditions. Each condition was different in terms of the experience of movement direction and target position. To assess the ability to reach the target in the test trials, we characterized each reaching movement by separating it into two continuous phases: movement initiation and movement termination. The accuracy of these phases was quantified by the initial direction error and the final position error, respectively. Through training, both errors in the test trials decreased across trials (Fig. 4A,D). Because the test trials were performed without online visual feedback, this reduction reflects improvements in feedforward control. Notably, despite the lack of direct training, error reduction was observed in the P, D, and P&D conditions, indicating that generalization had occurred (Fig. 4A,D).

Generalization at movement initiation

Although generalization occurred in untrained conditions, the pattern of errors varied depending on the condition. We first examined the initial movement trajectory in the test trials. Figure 4B shows the distribution of the cursor position when the joystick velocity reached its peak. In particular, the distributions differed in shape between the P and D conditions. In the D condition, the distribution was elongated along the target direction, whereas in the P condition, it was elongated perpendicular to the target direction. This suggests that movement initiation in the D condition was aligned with the target direction. These observations were supported by a quantitative comparison of the initial direction error across conditions (Fig. 4C). Statistical analysis on the last 5 test trials showed that there was no significant effect of trial, indicating that the learning reached plateau [ F(1,370.76)a=0.570, p = 0.451], whereas there was a significant main effect of condition [ F(3,23.08)b=3.98, p = 0.0201]. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that the lack of direction experience markedly impaired movement initiation: the initial direction error in the P condition was substantially larger than in the C condition (C − P: t(23)c=−3.43, p = 0.0136, d = −0.862, 95% CI [−16.9, −1.46]). In contrast, the lack of position experience had little effect on movement initiation. The initial direction error in the D condition was comparable to that in the C condition (C − D: t(23)d=−0.861, p = 0.398, d = −0.201, 95% CI [−9.33, 5.04]), and although the P&D condition showed a slightly larger initial direction error than the C condition (C − P&D: t(23)e=−1.83, p = 0.162, d = −0.472, 95% CI [−13.0, 2.91]), this difference did not reach statistical significance. Adding position experience to direction experience produced only a small change between the D and P&D conditions (D − P&D: t(23)f=−1.09, p = 0.346, d = −0.270, 95% CI [−10.5, 4.77]). Consistent with these patterns, the comparison between the P and D conditions showed a medium effect size in favor of the D condition, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P − D: t(23)g=2.37, p = 0.0790, d = 0.661, 95% CI [−1.52, 15.6]). Altogether, these results suggest that the experience of movement direction contributed to improved accuracy of movement initiation, while the experience of target position had little effect on it.

Generalization at movement termination

To evaluate generalization at the termination phase under comparable initial conditions, we focused on the D and P&D conditions, in which the initial direction error was not significantly different from that in the C condition. We further included the initial direction error as a covariate in the statistical model so that the contribution of each condition to movement termination could be evaluated beyond what was already explained by movement initiation. The P condition was excluded from this focused comparison because its initial direction error was substantially larger than that of the C condition, making the termination comparison difficult to interpret under matched initial conditions. Figure 4E shows the distribution of the final cursor position across all four conditions. In the D condition, although the distribution was centered on the target, frequent overshooting occurred. In the P&D condition, the distribution was similarly distributed around the target with frequent deviation. Statistical analysis on the last 5 test trials showed no significant effect of trial, indicating that the learning converged [ F(1,365.31)h=0.172, p = 0.679], whereas there was a significant main effect of condition [ F(3,23.28)i=7.98, p = 7.81 × 10−4]. The covariate, initial direction error, also showed a strong effect [ F(1,436.52)j=82.97, p < 2.2 × 10−16], confirming that initial direction error was tightly linked to final position error. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that, even when initial direction error was statistically controlled, the final position error in the D condition was substantially larger than in the C condition (C − D: t(23.0)k=−3.09, p = 0.0106, d = −0.850, 95% CI [−1.86, −0.064]). This indicates that, although the D condition showed comparable movement initiation to the C condition, its movement termination was less accurate. The lack of position experience thus appears to be relevant to the accuracy of movement termination, even when the movement starts from a comparable initial direction. Notably, providing position experience separately, in addition to direction experience, was not sufficient to support accurate movement termination. The final position error in the P&D condition was also significantly larger than in the C condition (C − P&D: t(23.2)l=−4.18, p = 0.0021, d = −0.988, 95% CI [−1.89, −0.347]), and was comparable to that in the D condition (D − P&D: t(23.0)m=−0.529, p = 0.879, d = −0.138, 95% CI [−1.01, 0.696]). These results indicate that, even after experiencing both target position and movement direction in separate training contexts, participants were unable to reach the level of termination accuracy achieved through direct training. What might explain this lack of additive benefit?

Use of the redundant control space relates to generalization at movement termination

To examine why the additive benefit was not observed at the group level, we focused on the redundancy of the joystick control space. Although position and direction experiences appear additive in the workspace, the same target position can be achieved by infinitely many joystick configurations along the task-irrelevant dimensions (Fig. 5A,B). Consequently, the visuomotor outputs derived from direction and position experiences do not necessarily coincide. We therefore quantified the alignment between the direction-based and position-based visuomotor outputs in the joystick control space, defined as the angle between the two outputs. To examine whether this alignment explains individual differences in the final position error in the P&D condition, we performed a linear regression at the participant level with the alignment and initial direction error as predictors of the final position error. After controlling for initial direction error, the alignment was a significant predictor of the final position error (βn = 0.0501, p = 0.00692; Fig. 5C), indicating that participants with greater alignment exhibited smaller final position errors in the P&D condition. These results indicate that the alignment between direction- and position-based outputs in the redundant control space underlies how effectively position experience and direction experience are combined to support accurate movement termination.

Experiment 2

Generalization in the D condition under single-condition training

The D condition provides the central evidence for our main findings—that directional experience contributes to movement initiation, whereas the lack of positional experience is associated with reduced accuracy of movement termination. To examine whether interleaving the four training conditions in Experiment 1 interfered with the generalization observed in the D condition, we trained a separate group of participants exclusively on the D (Direction) sequence. Clast and Dmid were matched to the C and D conditions of Experiment 1 in the amount of practice.

Results showed the initial direction error in Dmid was larger than in Clast (Clast − Dmid: t(23)o=−4.05, p = 0.0005, d = −0.99, 95% CI [−16.0, −5.16]; Fig. 6C). Thus, at this matched-practice point, Experiment 2 did not yet reproduce the comparability between the D and C conditions that had been observed in Experiment 1. However, with continued training the initial direction error in Dlast converged to a level comparable to Clast (Clast − Dlast: t(22.9)p=−1.49, p = 0.150, d = −0.21, 95% CI [−4.71, 0.77]; Fig. 6D), with an effect size closely matching that of the C − D comparison in Experiment 1 (d = −0.201). The comparable movement initiation between the D and C conditions observed in Experiment 1 was therefore reproduced after additional training. A direct comparison of the initial direction error in the D condition between the two experiments revealed no significant difference, although the error tended to be smaller in Experiment 1 (D − Dmid: t(46)q=−1.80, p = 0.079, d = −0.60, 95% CI [−13.6, 0.77]; Fig. 6E), suggesting that interleaving the four conditions in Experiment 1 may have influenced the rate of learning. Together, these results indicate that, although the within participants design in Experiment 1 may have accelerated the decrease of the initial direction error, directional experience alone was ultimately sufficient to achieve movement initiation comparable to the C condition, reproducing the corresponding finding of Experiment 1.

A similar pattern was observed for the final position error. The final position error in Dmid was larger than in Clast (Clast − Dmid: t(25.9)r=−6.27, p < 0.0001, d = −1.63, 95% CI [−2.70, −1.37]; Fig. 6F), reproducing the impaired movement termination under directional experience alone observed in Experiment 1. This error remained larger than in Clast even with continued training (Clast − Dlast: t(23.1)s=−5.09, p < 0.0001, d = −2.09, 95% CI [−3.02, −1.27]; Fig. 6G), indicating that additional practice did not improve movement termination. As with the initial direction error, a direct comparison between the two experiments revealed no significant difference (D − Dmid: t(46.6)t=−0.901, p = 0.372, d = −0.301, 95% CI [−1.4, 0.532]; Fig. 6H).

In summary, these results confirm that the central dissociation of Experiment 1 is not attributable to the interleaving of the four conditions: even with training on the D sequence alone, directional experience was sufficient to learn accurate movement initiation but not accurate movement termination, reproducing the central claim of Experiment 1. At the same time, we acknowledge that interleaving the four conditions did affect the learning of the D condition in Experiment 1, indicating that some interference was present, possibly because participants spent more total time engaged in the task across the interleaved conditions.

Discussion

This study aimed to clarify how two types of information, movement direction and target position, contribute to de novo motor learning. To this end, we adopted a generalization paradigm to dissociate the respective contributions of direction and position experiences (Malfait et al., 2002; Donchin et al., 2003; Shadmehr, 2004). The results suggested that direction experience enhanced the accuracy of movement initiation, whereas the absence of position experience led to reduced accuracy of movement termination (Fig. 4). However, experiencing position and direction separately did not yield additive effects at the group level (Fig. 4F). Instead, participants whose position- and direction-based visuomotor outputs were more closely aligned in the redundant control space exhibited smaller final position errors (Fig. 5C).

Contribution of direction experience

Our results demonstrate that directional experience selectively enhances the accuracy of movement initiation (Fig. 4C), supporting the long-standing view that motor learning primarily relies on direction-based representations. This view is supported by classical findings in motor adaptation showing that adaptation to visuomotor rotations involves the remapping of movement vectors rather than final limb positions (Wang and Sainburg, 2005). Evidence from de novo motor learning studies further supports this direction-based representation. When participants acquired novel hand-to-cursor mappings, their trajectories became progressively straighter with practice, indicating the establishment of a direction-based transport map linking changes in hand posture to cursor motion (Mosier et al., 2005). Moreover, Ranganathan et al. (2013) demonstrated that the motor system exploits redundancy not by converging to a single final posture, but by adopting distinct hand postures for a single target location, depending on the starting hand configuration. This dependence on the initial state reinforces the dominance of a transport map, i.e., associating cursor displacement with changes in hand posture (Ranganathan et al., 2013). Consistent with these studies, our results showed that isolated experience of movement direction improved the accuracy of movement initiation to a level comparable to that achieved through direct practice (Fig. 4C). Altogether, these findings suggest that directional experience contributes to the refinement of direction-based control mechanisms that support accurate movement initiation.

Contribution of position experience

Although initial direction error in the Direction (D) condition was comparable to the Control (C) condition, the final position error was significantly larger in the D condition, indicating impaired accuracy in movement termination (Fig. 4). This dissociation between movement initiation and termination can be explained by the existence of two complementary control processes: a transient movement controller that generates vectorial commands for reaching, and a sustained hold controller that stabilizes the limb at the desired endpoint (Shadmehr, 2017; Albert et al., 2020). Evidence for this distinction comes from multiple lines of research. In adaptation studies, learning acquired in visuomotor rotation tasks generalizes poorly between trajectory-reversal (slicing) and positioning (reaching) tasks, suggesting that initial trajectory and final position rely on separate neural mechanisms (Ghez et al., 2007; Scheidt and Ghez, 2007). Neurophysiological data further indicate that neurons in the primary motor cortex exhibit separate load-related tuning during posture and movement, reinforcing the functional separation between control phases (Kurtzer et al., 2005). Within this framework, the impaired termination observed in the D condition is consistent with insufficient formation of the hold controller, which appears to rely on positional experience. Although the D condition supported accurate movement initiation, the lack of positional experience was associated with reduced accuracy at movement termination. This pattern suggests that positional experience plays a role in the formation of the hold controller, which becomes relevant after movement initiation has been guided by directional experience.

Conditional contribution of position experience to movement termination

The previous section suggested that position experience plays a role in supporting accurate movement termination. If this were the case, providing position experience in addition to direction experience (as in the P&D condition) would be expected to improve movement termination compared with direction experience alone (D condition). However, additive benefits of providing both experiences were not observed at the group level. This is likely because, in the P&D condition, the visuomotor outputs derived from direction and position experiences do not necessarily coincide due to the redundancy in the control space. In support of this view, participants with higher alignment between direction- and position-based visuomotor outputs in the joystick control space exhibited smaller final position errors (Fig. 5C). This finding suggests that, even when both direction and position experiences are acquired, the benefit of position experience emerges only when the position-based and direction-based visuomotor maps are aligned with each other in the redundant control space. This account also fits within the framework of movement and hold controllers (Shadmehr, 2017; Albert et al., 2020), in which the alignment between the two visuomotor maps may reflect the agreement between the states specified by these controllers. Taken together, these results suggest that position experience does not uniformly support movement termination, but its effect is conditional—specifically when it is integrated with direction experience within a coherent visuomotor representation.

Distinction between de novo motor learning and motor adaptation

De novo motor learning fundamentally differs from motor adaptation in that it necessitates establishing entirely new mappings between motor commands and sensory outcomes, rather than merely recalibrating pre-existing ones. Although prior motor adaptation studies have revealed the existence of separate movement and hold controllers (Ghez et al., 2007; Scheidt and Ghez, 2007), the specific sensorimotor experiences required for their initial construction remain largely unexplored within that traditional paradigm. By utilizing de novo motor learning, we were able to precisely control the available experiences, revealing that different types of experience support the acquisition of these two distinct controllers. A second key distinction lies in the treatment of redundancy. Motor adaptation typically involves recalibration within a constrained control space (Berger et al., 2013; Sadtler et al., 2014; Berger et al., 2022), whereas de novo motor learning requires the exploration of solutions in an unconstrained high-dimensional space (Mosier et al., 2005; Choi et al., 2020; Dal’Bello and Izawa, 2021). This fundamental difference introduces a distinct computational challenge. When direction- and position-based visuomotor maps are constructed separately in a high-dimensional space, the motor commands specified by each map are not guaranteed to be mutually consistent. As a result, the state reached by the direction-based map may not coincide with the state stabilized by the position-based map, leading to ineffective movement termination. Consequently, in de novo motor learning, achieving coordination between these newly acquired visuomotor maps emerges as a critical computational requirement for accurate reaching movements. From the perspective of ecological dynamics (Gibson, 1979; Jacobs and Michaels, 2007), this distinction can also be framed in terms of the formation of the task-specific perception–action couplings. Whereas motor adaptation can be viewed as the recalibration of pre-existing couplings under novel task constraints, de novo motor learning requires learners to establish entirely new couplings between their actions and visual consequences. The present findings suggest that the formation of these new couplings is not unitary, but involves the parallel establishment of distinct couplings: one between cursor-direction information and movement initiation-related actions, and another between cursor position information and movement termination-related actions.

Neural representations underlying direction- and position-based learning

Direction- and position-based learning may rely on distinct representations of the target, such as a cursor-centered displacement vector specifying movement direction and an absolute target position anchored in external space. These two forms of information are broadly consistent with the distinction between allocentric and egocentric spatial representations (Schenk, 2006; Milner and Goodale, 2008; Chen and Crawford, 2020). From this perspective, the two forms of learning observed in our study can be interpreted in relation to these spatial codes: position-based learning may reflect reliance on egocentric representations of target location, whereas direction-based learning may reflect reliance on allocentric representations of the spatial relation between the cursor and the target. This interpretation is consistent with the idea that multiple spatial representations can be maintained in parallel to support flexible visuomotor behavior.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results show that de novo motor learning relies on both movement direction and target position experiences. Direction experience determined the accuracy of movement initiation, whereas the accuracy of movement termination depended on the alignment between direction- and position-based outputs in the redundant control space. These findings suggest that accurate formation of novel sensorimotor maps requires the coordinated use of directional and positional experiences.

Our data revealed that this coordination varied considerably across individuals and was associated with endpoint accuracy. Building on this observation, future work will examine the origin of these individual differences and whether such coordination can be actively trained through targeted practice. Investigating these questions will deepen our understanding of how the brain integrates multiple types of sensorimotor experience to construct novel sensorimotor maps.
