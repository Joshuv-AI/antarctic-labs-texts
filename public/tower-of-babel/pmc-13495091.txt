Long-Term Outcomes and Risk Factors for Implant Failure After Distal Femoral Megaprosthesis in Orthopaedic Oncology: A Single-Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Seiji Shimomura, Koichi Ogura, Eisuke Kobayashi, Shintaro Iwata, Shuhei Osaki, Hiroya Kondo, Toshiyuki Takemori, Satoshi Kamio, Akira Kawai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistal femoral replacement (DFR) is a standard reconstruction technique following malignant bone tumor resection, yet long-term outcomes and implant-related complications remain insufficiently reviewed.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 88 patients who underwent DFR for malignant or aggressive benign tumors from 1990 to 2023. The cumulative incidences of implant revision, removal, or amputation were estimated using competing risk analysis with death as a competing event. Risk factors for major reoperation were identified through univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 8.0 years, 71 reoperations were performed in 41 patients (30 major and 41 minor). The cumulative incidence of implant removal or revision was 19.8% at 5 years, 36.8% at 10 years, and 52.2% at 20 years. Type 4 complications (infection) were the most common cause of failure, including late-onset infections occurring more than 10 years postoperatively. In multivariate analysis, the presence of a residual epiphyseal plate (hazard ratio: 4.17, P = .012) and use of fixed hinge (hazard ratio: 2.63, P = .042) were significantly associated with increased risk of implant failure. Complex surgical procedures (eg, extra-articular resection or vascular reconstruction) did not significantly increase the risk of implant failure.ConclusionsDFR provides durable long-term outcomes, though the risk of revision persists beyond 10 years, primarily due to infection. Residual epiphyseal plate presence is a significant risk factor for implant failure. Complex surgical procedures were not independently associated with implant failure in the adjusted analysis.

Introduction

The distal femur is the most common site of malignant bone tumors. Various reconstruction options are available following tumor resection, including allografts; devitalized autografts treated with liquid nitrogen, pasteurization, or radiation; and endoprosthesis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Of these, prosthetic replacement remains the most common reconstruction method following distal femoral bone tumor resection [5,6]. Due to variations in resection procedure, various types of implant designs are utilized. However, there is limited evidence regarding the long-term outcomes and risk of revision surgery associated with different implant designs and surgical techniques [[7], [8], [9]]. On the other hand, bone sarcomas frequently affect the children, adolescent, and young adult population, and patients undergoing distal femoral replacement (DFR) are occasionally skeletally immature [10]. It may influence canal diameter and stem fit, diaphyseal geometry, and activity levels, as well as the indication for expandable devices. Therefore, it may be clinically relevant to implant survival. We aimed to document the long-term outcomes after DFR, focusing specifically on (1) the proportion of patients who experienced complications requiring surgery; (2) the cumulative incidence of implant removal or revision and amputation at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years as shown by competing risk analyses; and (3) the risk factors for implant removal or revision, and amputation.

Material and methods

The institutional review board of the National Cancer Center Hospital approved this study (2017-336). The requirement for informed consent was waived, and an opt-out method was used. We enrolled patients in whom a distal femoral megaprosthesis had been implanted after distal femoral resection of a malignant or aggressive benign tumor at our hospital, including revision of a failed prior oncologic reconstruction. DFRs performed for nononcologic indications, such as periprosthetic fracture or aseptic loosening after primary arthroplasty, were not included. All surgeries were performed by our experienced bone and soft-tissue tumor surgeons. Patients were eligible for the study if they underwent DFR for primary reconstruction after tumor resection or as a revision of a failed prior reconstruction. Patients who received total femoral replacement for their primary reconstruction were ineligible. Patients lost to follow-up after <1 years without meeting any end points or having a competing event (death) were also ineligible.

We identified 98 patients undergoing DFR between 1990 and 2023. Of these, 10 patients who received reconstruction with a total femur prosthesis were excluded from our analysis, resulting in a final cohort of 88 patients (Fig. 1). The study end points were postoperative complications requiring any unplanned surgery. Reoperations were classified as “major” if they involved (1) removal of the metal-body femoral or tibial component; (2) major revision (exchange of the metal-body femoral or tibial component); or (3) amputation. “Minor” reoperations were defined as those other than major reoperations. Postoperative complications were recorded according to the definitions suggested by Henderson et al. [11].

Since 21 (23.9%) of the 88 patients died, and death is a competing event of implant removal/revision, the Kaplan–Meier method overestimates the cumulative incidence of implant removal/revision. Therefore, we used Fine–Gray competing risk analysis, considering death and removal or revision for reasons other than prosthetic failure as competing events so as to estimate accurately the incidence of prosthetic failure [12]. Univariable and multivariable Fine–Gray regression models were used to examine associations between covariates and major reoperation. The multivariable model included age, residual epiphyseal plate status, hinge type, joint resection type, and use of an expandable prosthesis. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.0.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated that the cumulative incidence of major reoperations following DFR was comparable to previous reports [[7], [8], [9],13]. Notably, the risk of major reoperation was higher in cases where the epiphyseal plate was preserved.

In this study, the highest incidence of major complications occurred between the first and second postoperative years, most likely attributable to the concentration of type 5 complications (tumor progression) during this period. This finding is consistent with the oncological tendency for recurrence to occur within 1–2 years after surgery [14]. At most time points, however, type 4 complications (infection) were the leading cause of implant removal or revision. Importantly, these infections were not limited to the early postoperative phase but continued to occur even more than 10 years after the initial surgery, highlighting the persistent risk of infection throughout the life of the implant. Types 2 and 3 complications also contributed to implant removal and revision procedures up to 10 years postoperatively. After 10 years, type 2 complications were no longer observed, whereas type 3 complications, similar to type 4, continued to appear during long-term follow-up. Among the eight type 3 complications, 1 was caused by a periprosthetic fracture following a fall, while the remaining cases were implant fractures. Regarding minor reoperations, infection (a type 4 complication) was the most common cause and occurred at all postoperative time points. In addition, type 1 complications (soft-tissue failures) developed during the early postoperative period (within 6 months); however, none of these cases required major reoperation and all were successfully managed with local treatment.

Several studies have previously reported on the long-term outcomes of DFR. Grimer et al. (2016) [13] demonstrated an implant survival rate of less than 10% at 20 years postoperatively in 102 cases of fixed-hinge DFR. Ogura et al. reported that the implant removal rate at 10 years following reconstruction with a rotating-hinge megaprosthesis was 30.1%. Although most implant removals and revisions occurred within the first 10 years, the risk persisted beyond 10 years at a rate of 1.24% per year, primarily due to deep infection (1.06% per year) [9]. In the present study, the implant removal rate at 10 years, considering both fixed-hinge and rotating-hinge designs, was 36.8%, with an annual removal risk of 1.57% thereafter. The most common cause was infection, classified as a type 4 complication, occurring at a rate of 1.06% per year, which is consistent with previous reports.

Regarding hinge types, fixed-hinge knee tumor endoprostheses have been associated with high rates of mechanical failure, as they do not distribute forces appropriately around the knee, thereby increasing the risk of periprosthetic fractures [15]. In contrast, newer rotating-hinge knee mechanisms allow for flexion–extension and external–internal rotations of the knee, providing better function and lower rates of loosening. Myers et al. reported that the transition from fixed-hinge to rotating-hinge prostheses reduced the risk of revision by 52% [8]. Furthermore, Pala et al. reported that aseptic loosening occurred more frequently in fixed-hinge prostheses compared to rotating-hinge prostheses (5.9% vs 2%), whereas other complications, such as infections, were comparable between the two designs [16]. In the present study, although the association was attenuated after multivariable adjustment, fixed-hinge prostheses were associated with more than twice the rate of revision, suggesting that the transition to rotating-hinge designs has improved implant survival.

In this study, the presence of a residual epiphyseal plate was identified as a potential risk factor for implant revision. This variable likely acts as a proxy for extreme youth and skeletal immaturity, which may influence canal diameter, stem fit, growth-related bone enlargement, alignment changes, implant choice, and the need for repeated procedures. To date, no studies have directly reported an association between residual epiphyseal plates and implant survival. However, Eckardt et al. [17] reported normal tibial and epiphyseal growth when the tibial component was cemented through the physis, while Tsuda et al. [18], in their study on physeal-preserving endoprosthetic replacement, observed a mean growth of 3.3 cm. In contrast, Masrouha et al. reported tibial physeal growth disturbances in 2 out of 4 cases of DFR [19]. Nevertheless, none of these studies directly associated the presence of a residual epiphyseal plate with an increased risk of prosthesis revision. In the present study, of the 19 patients with a residual epiphyseal plate, 10 received expandable implants and 9 received nonexpandable implants. Implant failure occurred in 5 of the 10 patients with expandable implants and in 6 of the 9 patients with nonexpandable implants. Mechanical failure was observed in 3 of the 19 cases with a residual epiphyseal plate, but no clear relationship between epiphyseal plate retention and mechanical failure was identified. However, implant failure due to infection was documented in 6 cases among those with a residual epiphyseal plate. These findings suggest that multiple factors related to skeletal immaturity—including infection, repeated surgeries, bone enlargement, and alignment alterations—may contribute to an increased risk of implant failure in cases involving a residual epiphyseal plate.

Postoperative implant failure is generally reported to be more frequent in complex surgery involving extra-articular resection and vascular reconstruction. Ogura et al. [20] reported that extra-articular resection is an independent risk factor for type 4 failure. Hardes et al. [21] suggested that limb salvage after extra-articular resection with a tumor prosthesis can achieve good functional results in most patients, although the rates of complications and subsequent amputation are higher than in patients treated with intra-articular resection. Furthermore, Teixeira et al. [22] reported that arthroplasty with vascular reconstruction for osteosarcoma carries a high risk of amputation. In this study, implant failure risk was not significantly different among patients who required extra-articular resection (hazard ratio: 1.01). Moreover, there are no implant failures after surgery with vascular reconstruction; however, the small number of such cases precludes definitive conclusions. Although flap reconstruction was frequently used (29 of 88, 33.0%), it was not identified as an independent predictor in the multivariate analysis. The risk of implant failure was not increased even in cases requiring extensive soft-tissue resection.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, nonrandomized case series, which is subject to selection and recall bias. Given the retrospective design of this study, the presence of missing data represents a potential limitation. However, case series studies have the advantage of enabling analysis of multiple outcomes and addressing rare diseases that would otherwise require extremely large cohorts in prospective studies. Second, this study spans more than 3 decades, during which adjuvant treatments and prosthetic designs have evolved. Third, the number of cases and failures is not sufficient, so the risk factor analysis is not available for each type of failure. Fourth, the present study was limited to DFR performed after tumor resection. In oncologic DFR, resection of the surrounding soft tissues is frequently required; therefore, the outcomes observed in this cohort may not be generalizable to DFR performed for common nononcologic indications, such as periprosthetic fracture or nonunion. Fifth, this study did not evaluate mechanical factors such as resection length/resection ratio and the diaphyseal–stem ratio, which have previously been reported to be associated with implant failure [20]. In the present cohort, no significant difference in implant survival was observed according to resection length. Because various types of implants were used in our cohort and fixation methods also differed, including both cemented and cementless fixation, these factors were not included in the present analysis. Further evaluation will be conducted when a greater number of cases of mechanical failure becomes available.

Conclusions

Long-term outcomes of DFR after tumor resection in our institution were comparable to prior reports, with implant revision probabilities of 19.8% at 5 years, 36.8% at 10 years, and 52.2% at 20 years. Infection was the leading cause of failure, with a persistent annual risk of 1.06% even beyond 10 years postoperatively. Residual epiphyseal plate presence and fixed-hinge design were associated with increased risk of major reoperation. These findings support the need for long-term surveillance after DFR, particularly in skeletally immature patients.
