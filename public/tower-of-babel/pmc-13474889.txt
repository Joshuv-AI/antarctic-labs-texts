Predictive clinicopathologic factors for para-aortic nodal involvement in endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Yakup Yalcin, Kemal Ozerkan, Serenat Eris Yalcin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo identify clinicopathologic risk factors predicting para-aortic lymph node (PALN) metastasis in patients with endometrial carcinoma.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 598 patients with FIGO 2009 stage I–III endometrial carcinoma who underwent comprehensive surgical staging, including pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy, at Bursa Uludag University Hospital between January 2000 and May 2025. Demographic, surgical, and histopathological parameters were extracted from medical records. Univariable and multivariable regression analyses were performed to determine predictors of PALN metastasis.ResultsPALN metastasis was identified in 64 patients (10.7%). Isolated PALN involvement occurred in 3.5% of cases. On multivariable analysis, elevated preoperative CA-125 (> 35 U/mL) (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.11–4.84), deep myometrial invasion (> 50%) (OR 2.62, 95% CI 1.09–6.30), lymphovascular space invasion (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.12–4.75), and pelvic lymph node metastasis (OR 25.5, 95% CI 12.8–50.9) were independently associated with PALN involvement. Patients with pelvic nodal metastasis had a markedly increased likelihood of para-aortic spread.ConclusionsPara-aortic lymph node metastasis in endometrial carcinoma is independently associated with pelvic lymph node positivity, elevated preoperative CA-125, deep myometrial invasion, and LVSI. These parameters may guide risk stratification and support the development of predictive models to minimize unnecessary lymphadenectomy while ensuring accurate staging and tailored adjuvant therapy.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in developed countries, with an incidence that continues to rise [1]. In patients with endometrial cancer, surgical staging has been universally established as the primary approach, serving both as the definitive treatment of the disease and as a means of obtaining accurate prognostic information. Standard surgical staging includes total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and the evaluation of regional lymph nodes, thereby allowing for precise determination of disease extent and guiding decision-making regarding adjuvant therapy [2].

Lymphatic metastasis is the most important route of spread in endometrial cancer and is one of the critical prognostic factors determining survival. Although some retrospective studies have demonstrated improved survival following lymphadenectomy [3, 4], two randomized controlled trials failed to show any survival benefit [5, 6]. Nevertheless, several studies have highlighted the potential therapeutic value of para-aortic lymphadenectomy, suggesting that it may improve survival, particularly in selected patient groups [7, 8].

In the literature, pelvic lymph node metastasis has been reported in 5–20% of endometrial cancer cases, reflecting the relatively common pattern of regional nodal spread. In contrast, para-aortic lymph node involvement is less frequent, with an incidence ranging between 3.4 and 12%, yet it carries greater clinical significance given its strong association with advanced disease and poorer survival outcomes [9–11]. Lymph node (LN) evaluation in endometrial cancer can be performed using various surgical approaches. These include sentinel lymph node (SLN) mapping and systematic pelvic and paraaortic lymphadenectomy extending to the level of the renal vessels [12–15]. The 2009 FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) staging update emphasized the prognostic importance of lymph node involvement, dividing stage IIIC into two subgroups: Stage IIIC1, pelvic lymph node positivity only; Stage IIIC2 defines paraaortic lymph node positivity along with pelvic lymph node positivity or isolated paraaortic lymph node positivity despite pelvic lymph node negativity [16].

Although lymphadenectomy performed as part of surgical staging provides prognostic information guiding adjuvant therapy selection, paraaortic lymphadenectomy prolongs operative time, increases blood loss, and increases the risk of perioperative morbidity. In contrast, SLN mapping is increasingly being accepted as a method with lower morbidity, higher sensitivity, and a lower false-negative rate. However, the inability of SLN mapping to detect isolated paraaortic metastases is considered a significant disadvantage [17].

Therefore, a better understanding of the risk factors associated with paraaortic lymph node metastases in endometrial cancer is necessary. Accurately identifying high-risk patients will contribute to optimizing surgical strategies, personalizing the scope of lymph node assessment, and more effective adjuvant treatment planning. The aim of this study was to investigate clinicopathological risk factors predicting para-aortic lymph node metastasis in endometrial cancer.

Materials and methods

The data of patients with endometrial cancer who underwent surgical treatment at Bursa Uludag University Hospital between January 2000 and May 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. This study protocol was reviewed and approved by our hospital’s ethics committee (Approval No. 2025/842/15–33). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived in accordance with ethical principles. The research was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, which defines internationally accepted ethical standards for studies involving human participants.

Medical records of patients diagnosed with FIGO stage I-III endometrial carcinoma who underwent comprehensive surgical staging, including hysterectomy and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy, in our hospital were retrospectively analyzed. To standardize patients diagnosed before and after 2009, the FIGO 2009 staging system was applied. The study included patients aged 18–90 years with histologically verified endometrioid or non-endometrioid endometrial carcinoma classified as FIGO 2009 stages I–III. The exclusion criteria comprised presence of synchronous malignancies, receipt of neoadjuvant treatment (hormonal therapy or chemotherapy), and insufficient medical documentation. Patients with FIGO stage IV disease were excluded in order to minimize clinical heterogeneity and avoid confounding related to distant metastatic spread.

Data collection involved detailed review of medical charts, surgical notes, and pathology reports. Demographic parameters (such as age and body mass index [BMI]) and histopathological characteristics (including histologic subtype, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), FIGO stage, number of lymph nodes removed, and presence of metastatic involvement) were systematically recorded. Surgical procedures were performed by gynecologic oncologists with expertise in oncologic surgery. Comprehensive surgical staging consisted of total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, collection of peritoneal cytology, pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy, and omentectomy when indicated. Para-aortic lymphadenectomy was selectively performed according to patient-specific risk factors or at the surgeon’s discretion. Risk factors in the cohort that underwent systematic lymphadenectomy included non-endometrioid histology, ≥ 50% myometrial invasion, grade 3 tumors, cervical involvement, and tumor size ≥ 2 cm.

The surgical approach was either laparotomy or laparoscopy, depending on clinical circumstances. A total of 151 patients (25.2%) underwent laparoscopic surgery, whereas 447 patients (74.8%) underwent laparotomy. Pelvic lymphadenectomy was defined as the removal of lymphatic tissue from the external, internal, and common iliac chains, together with the obturator region. Para-aortic dissection encompassed the pericaval, aortocaval, and left para-aortic regions, extending superiorly up to the level of the left renal vein.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Mann–Whitney U tests were used for comparison of continuous variables between groups, while Pearson’s Chi- square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed for categorical variables, depending on cell counts. Descriptive statistics were presented as median (range) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between clinicopathologic parameters and PALN metastasis. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

A total of 598 patients with endometrial carcinoma were included in the analysis. The median age at diagnosis was 62 years (range, 27–90), the median BMI was 34.9 kg/m² (range, 18.4–64.0), and the median preoperative CA-125 level was 21 U/mL (range, 3.3–1885). The majority of patients presented with early-stage disease, with 425 (71.5%) classified as FIGO stage I, 44 (7.4%) as stage II, and 129 (21.1%) as stage III. Histologically, 478 (79.9%) tumors were of endometrioid type, while 120 (20.1%) were non-endometrioid. Among the 120 patients with non-endometrioid histology, 51 (8.5%) had serous carcinoma, 12 (2.0%) mucinous carcinoma, 13 (2.2%) clear cell carcinoma, 28 (4.7%) carcinosarcoma, 11 (1.8%) undifferentiated carcinoma, and 5 (0.9%) mixed-type carcinoma. Since formal histologic grading is not routinely applicable to non-endometrioid histologic subtypes, these tumors were classified as high-grade (grade 3) tumors in the analysis. High-grade (grade 3) tumors accounted for 196 (32.8%) of cases. Deep myometrial invasion (≥ 50%) was observed in 242 (40.5%) patients, and LVSI was present in 173 (28.9%). Adnexal and para-uterine/vaginal involvement were infrequent, reported in 27 (4.5%) and 11 (1.8%) patients, respectively. Cervical stromal invasion was identified in 79 (13.2%), and positive peritoneal cytology in only 8 (1.3%) cases (Table 1).

Paraaortic lymph node (PALN) metastasis was identified in 64 patients (10.7%), whereas 534 (89.3%) had no paraaortic involvement (PALN–). Compared with the PALN– cohort, PALN+ patients were significantly older (median 65.5 vs. 62.0 years, p = 0.004), had lower BMI (32.5 vs. 35.0 kg/m², p = 0.041), and presented with higher median preoperative CA-125 levels (42.7 vs. 19.7 U/mL, p < 0.001). Tumor size was also larger in PALN+ patients (median 4.5 vs. 3.8 cm, p < 0.001). Advanced disease was strikingly overrepresented in the PALN+ group, with 98.4% of patients staged as FIGO III, compared to 11.4% in the PALN– group (p < 0.001). Non-endometrioid histologies, high-grade tumors, deep myometrial invasion, LVSI positivity, cervical stromal invasion, and positive peritoneal cytology were all significantly more common in the PALN+ cohort (all p < 0.05). While adnexal and para-uterine/vaginal invasion were infrequent overall, they also appeared at higher rates in the PALN+ group. In terms of nodal assessment, PALN+ patients had a higher median number of both pelvic (24.5 vs. 20.0, p = 0.013) and paraaortic nodes removed (17.0 vs. 11.0, p = 0.001). Concomitant pelvic lymph node metastasis was strongly associated with PALN involvement, present in 67.2% of PALN+ patients compared with only 6.4% of PALN– patients (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Univariate analysis identified age > 60 years (OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.07–3.29, p = 0.028), pre-operative CA-125 level (OR 5.70, 95% CI 3.32–9.79, p < 0.001), non-endometrioid histology (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.58–4.76, p < 0.001), grade 3 disease (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.24–3.55, p = 0.005), deep myometrial invasion (OR 5.26, 95% CI 2.91–9.51, p < 0.001), LVSI (OR 4.31, 95% CI 2.50–7.42, p < 0.001), cervical stromal invasion (OR 4.01, 95% CI 2.22–7.22, p < 0.001), positive peritoneal cytology (OR 9.30, 95% CI 2.26–38.22, p = 0.002), pelvic LN metastasis (OR 30.1, 95% CI 16.1–56.4, p < 0.001), and paraaortic LN yield > 10 (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.31–4.10, p = 0.003) as significant predictors of PALN involvement.

On multivariate analysis, pre-operative CA-125 level (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.11–4.84, p = 0.012), deep myometrial invasion (OR 2.62, 95% CI 1.09–6.30, p = 0.032), LVSI (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.12–4.75, p = 0.021) and pelvic LN metastasis (OR 25.5, 95% CI 12.8–50.9, p < 0.001) retained independent significance for predicting PALN involvement. Other factors such as age, histology, cervical stromal invasion, peritoneal cytology, and nodal harvest counts did not remain significant after adjustment (Table 3).

Discussion

In the cohort of 598 endometrial cancer patients who underwent systematic lymph node dissection, the incidence of pelvic lymph node metastasis (LNM) was 12.8% (77/598), and the incidence of para-aortic LNM was 10.7% (64/598). Isolated para-aortic lymph node metastasis was identified in 3.5% of patients, whereas the majority of patients who underwent systematic lymphadenectomy had no histopathologic evidence of para-aortic nodal involvement. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that systematic lymphadenectomy may result in overtreatment in a substantial proportion of patients, particularly in low-risk populations. In the study using the SEER database, nodular metastases were detected in only approximately 6% of patients who underwent lymphadenectomy, while no evidence of lymph node metastases was found in 94% of those who underwent the procedure [18]. Li et al., in a study including both endometrioid and non-endometrioid patients, reported a pelvic lymph node metastasis rate of 12.7% (50/393) and emphasized that 83% of patients underwent unnecessary lymphadenectomy [19]. Therefore, in order for systematic lymphadenectomy to be applied to appropriately selected patients, it is essential to have a clear understanding of the risk factors for lymph node metastasis. Identification of these factors is crucial, as it prevents unnecessary lymphadenectomy in low-risk patients while ensuring accurate staging and appropriate adjuvant therapy in high-risk cases, thereby playing a critical role in improving individualized outcomes.

In addition, unnecessary lymphadenectomy increases the risk of serious morbidity, including intraoperative bleeding, nerve injury, lymphocele formation, and chylous ascites [5, 6]. Compared with pelvic lymphadenectomy, para-aortic lymphadenectomy is generally considered a more complex procedure with higher complication rates [20]. Given the risks associated with intraoperative and postoperative complications of pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy, the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) guidelines recommend SLN biopsy as the standard staging method in patients with low- and intermediate-risk early-stage endometrial cancer, while systematic lymphadenectomy is considered an acceptable alternative in patients with intermediate- and high-risk disease [13]. This recommendation is based on the rationale that para-aortic lymphadenectomy allows for the removal of micrometastases and skip metastases, thereby improving the accuracy of staging and guiding adjuvant therapy more effectively [21].

The SEPAL study demonstrated that combined pelvic and para-aortic dissection conferred a survival advantage compared with pelvic dissection alone in intermediate- and high-risk endometrial cancer, leading to a 10.6% increase in overall survival [7]. Similarly, other retrospective studies have supported this hypothesis, reporting higher survival rates and lower recurrence rates in patients who underwent combined pelvic and para-aortic dissection [22–26]. In addition, Tokalioglu et al. evaluated supramesenteric lymph node metastasis in patients with endometrioid-type endometrial cancer and reported that metastasis above the inferior mesenteric artery was observed in 7.3% of intermediate- and high-risk patients undergoing systematic lymphadenectomy [27]. In multivariate analysis, tumor size, LVSI, pelvic lymph node metastasis, and inframesenteric lymph node metastasis were identified as independent predictors of supramesenteric nodal involvement. These findings further support the concept that para-aortic lymph node spread is closely associated with aggressive uterine factors and concomitant pelvic nodal disease.

Although many of the identified risk factors have previously been reported in the literature, our findings provide additional confirmatory evidence derived from a relatively large and homogeneous single-center cohort. In particular, the present study supports the reproducibility of established clinicopathologic predictors of para-aortic lymph node involvement across different patient populations and surgical settings. Independent predictors of para-aortic lymph node involvement included elevated preoperative CA-125 levels, LVSI positivity, deep myometrial invasion (≥ 50%), and pelvic lymph node positivity. According to our findings, women with positive pelvic LNs were 25 times more likely to have para-aortic LN metastasis, whereas patients with LVSI positivity were twice as likely to have positive para-aortic LNs. Similarly, both elevated preoperative CA-125 and > 50% myometrial invasion were associated with a twofold increased risk of para-aortic LN metastasis.

The most significant risk factor for para-aortic lymph node involvement is the presence of positive pelvic lymph nodes. In patients with pelvic lymph node–positive endometrial cancer, the rate of para-aortic metastasis has been reported to range between 50% and 67% [28–30]. Consistent with the literature, in our study, 67.2% of patients with positive pelvic nodes had para-aortic lymph node involvement, which increased the risk of para-aortic metastasis by 25-fold. Luomaranta et al. demonstrated that the presence of macroscopically positive pelvic lymph nodes has a sensitivity of 52.4% and a specificity of 93.8% in predicting para-aortic lymph node metastasis [31]. This finding highlights the potential value of intraoperative macroscopic pelvic node assessment as a practical indicator for anticipating para-aortic spread. However, while the high specificity underscores its reliability in ruling in disease when macroscopically positive nodes are observed, the relatively modest sensitivity indicates that a significant proportion of para-aortic metastases may remain undetected if reliance is placed solely on this parameter. Therefore, although macroscopic pelvic nodal involvement may serve as a useful intraoperative guide, it should be interpreted in conjunction with other established risk factors—such as tumor grade, lymphovascular space invasion, and depth of myometrial invasion—to optimize patient selection for para-aortic lymphadenectomy and ensure comprehensive staging in endometrial cancer.

In our cohort, 57.1% of PALN-positive patients also had LVSI, and multivariate analysis identified LVSI as an independent predictor of para-aortic involvement, conferring a twofold increased risk. Consistent with the literature, LVSI has also been identified as an independent predictor of para-aortic lymph node positivity [10, 32]. Sari et al. demonstrated that LVSI, when assessed through intraoperative frozen section analysis, serves as a critical predictor to guide para-aortic lymphadenectomy, reporting that the incidence of para-aortic metastasis in LVSI-negative patients was as low as 0.5–0.8% [10]. Similarly, Solmaz et al. proposed a combined evaluation of pelvic lymph node status and LVSI, showing that when both parameters were negative, the incidence of para-aortic metastasis decreased to only 0.1% [33].

The median preoperative CA-125 level among patients with PALN metastasis was 42.7 U/mL, and 59.1% (38/64) of PALN-positive patients had levels exceeding 35 U/mL. These findings further support the role of CA-125 as a preoperative surrogate marker of high tumor burden and potential nodal spread. The strong association between elevated CA-125 and PALN metastasis likely reflects underlying biological mechanisms. CA-125, a glycoprotein secreted by epithelial tumors, is an indicator of tumor burden, peritoneal dissemination, and aggressive molecular subtypes [34]. Elevated CA-125 has been independently associated with poor disease-free survival and overall survival, particularly in NSMP, MMRd, and p53-abnormal subtypes [35, 36].

A key finding of our study was the identification of isolated para-aortic lymph node metastasis in the absence of pelvic lymph node involvement, observed in 3.5% of the cohort (21/598). Todo et al., in a systematic review of 25 studies, reported an overall incidence of isolated para-aortic LNM of 2.7% [30]. However, in intermediate- or high-risk patients, the incidence of isolated para-aortic lymph node metastasis may rise to as high as 7–8% [22]. Isolated para-aortic lymph node involvement is closely associated with the lymphatic pathways of the uterus and may vary according to the anatomical location of the tumor. It has been demonstrated that such metastases may originate from lesions located in the uterine fundus, the left uterine horn, or the left intramural muscular layer, with direct spread to the para-aortic lymph nodes via the presacral route or gonadal vessels [37]. Since SLN mapping via cervical injection may not reliably detect isolated PALN metastases, such cases may be underdiagnosed. Our findings are consistent with those of Benseler et al., who emphasized the limitations of SLN biopsy in accurately assessing para-aortic involvement [38]. Missing these PALN-positive patients may result in the omission of adjuvant therapy. In line with this, Bogani et al. evaluated 123 patients with stage IIIC2 EC and reported that 8 of 9 patients (89%) who declined adjuvant therapy died within a median of 5 months [39].

The main strengths of our study lie in the inclusion of a relatively large patient cohort and the homogeneous nature of this population. Furthermore, the fact that all patients were treated at a single institution with consistent surgical and pathological evaluation protocols enhanced the reliability and internal validity of the data. These factors reduce heterogeneity and strengthen the robustness of our findings in the context of endometrial cancer research.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of the study inevitably introduces the possibility of methodological constraints, including a certain degree of selection bias, despite our efforts to minimize it. Second, molecular classification data were not routinely available for a substantial proportion of patients, particularly those treated during the earlier years of the study period. Given the increasing importance of molecular subtypes in risk stratification and prediction of lymph node metastasis in endometrial cancer, the absence of molecular profiling represents an important limitation of the present study. Third, SLN mapping was not systematically performed in our cohort, as only a limited number of patients underwent this procedure; consequently, these patients were excluded from the analysis. This omission restricts our ability to compare the diagnostic and prognostic performance of systematic lymphadenectomy with SLN biopsy, which is now considered a standard approach in many centers. In addition, para-aortic lymphadenectomy was not performed systematically in all patients but rather selectively according to intraoperative findings and clinicopathologic risk factors. Therefore, selection bias may have influenced both the observed incidence of para-aortic lymph node metastasis and the strength of associated risk factors.

In summary, our study demonstrates that para-aortic lymph node metastasis in patients with endometrial cancer is significantly associated with pelvic lymph node involvement, elevated CA-125 levels, positive lymphovascular space invasion, and deep myometrial invasion. Based on these findings, the development of a predictive model or scoring system for assessing the risk of para-aortic metastasis in endometrial cancer could be considered. Additionally, it can contribute to improved risk stratification by supporting individualized decision-making processes regarding the scope of lymph node assessment in patients with endometrial cancer.
