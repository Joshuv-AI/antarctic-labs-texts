Effect of Saliva on Compressive Strength of Mineral Trioxide Aggregate, Biod+, and Biodentine: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Swarnima Bhatnagar, Ganesh Jeevanandan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to evaluate the effect of saliva on the compressive strength of three commonly used dental materials—mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), Biod+, and Biodentine—used for root canal fillings.Materials and MethodsThirty-six extracted primary molars were divided into two groups: a control group (group A) with no saliva exposure and an experimental group (group B) subjected to saliva exposure. Each group was further divided into three subgroups (n = 6), with specimens filled with MTA, Biod+, or Biodentine. After a standardized pulpotomy procedure, the materials were placed within the prepared cavities. The compressive strength of each material was tested using an Instron universal testing machine after a 4-hour setting period. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).ResultsBiod+ demonstrated the highest compressive strength among the three materials, both in the presence and absence of saliva. Biodentine exhibited the least variation in compressive strength following saliva exposure, indicating superior stability in an oral environment. MTA showed lower compressive strength compared to Biod+ and Biodentine. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant difference in compressive strength between the groups (p = 0.00532).ConclusionBiod+ exhibited the highest durability in terms of compressive strength, making it a promising material for endodontic applications requiring high mechanical resistance. Biodentine, on the other hand, demonstrated greater stability under salivary exposure, making it a reliable choice for clinical scenarios where moisture contamination is a concern. These findings provide valuable insights into the selection of optimal root canal filling materials based on specific clinical requirements.How to cite this articleBhatnagar S, Jeevanandan G. Introduction

The material of choice after a root canal treatment, pulpectomy, or pulpotomy has been a point of debate as each of the materials available in the market has different parameters in terms of its nature and its capabilities to act as a sealer and to withstand the occlusal load during mastication. The ideal root end filling material must possess the following characteristics: noncarcinogenic properties, radiopacity, the ability to set in wet conditions, dimensional stability, antibacterial activity, nontoxicity, ease of handling, and physical properties such as bond strength, hardness, adhesion to root canal dentin, and sufficient compressive strength to ensure a reliable apical seal.1 Intracoronal restorative materials used post-pulpotomy, including resin-modified glass ionomer cement (GIC) and glass ionomers (GIs), have also been evaluated. These have grown in popularity and provided patients with esthetically pleasing outcomes. However, low wear resistance and fracture resistance are some drawbacks of these cements.2 Pulpotomy, which involves the removal of the coronal pulp, is recommended for vital, asymptomatic primary teeth with carious pulp exposures.3 Candidates for pulpotomy include teeth with reversible pulpitis, extensive caries, carious pulp exposure, no radicular pathology, no spontaneous pain, and those traumatized to expose the pulp. There is little chance of failure with this procedure. When failure occurs, it is often attributed to leakage, a critical factor resulting from incomplete tooth coverage. This condition permits bacteria to infiltrate the pulp through exposed dentinal tubules and the salivary environment. Therefore, the longevity of a pulpotomized tooth relies on a properly sealed restoration.4 As a result, the selection of a post-pulpotomy restoration is influenced by factors such as its sealing capability, durability, esthetics, and acceptance by both the parent and child.5 The demand for esthetics has increased in both the adult and pediatric populations, but patient preference also plays a role in the choice of dental material. More so than in the past, parents participate in clinical decision-making, and their preferences for dental materials have changed over time depending on factors such as cost, durability, toxicity, and esthetic value.6 Another popular dental material is GIC, which has benefits such as bonding to dentin and enamel and the capacity to absorb and release fluoride. Nevertheless, some of their disadvantages include low compressive strength and sensitivity to moisture. Glass ionomer cements underwent compositional changes that led to the introduction of GICs modified with resin.7 Vitrebond, a commercially available GI resin-modified liner, is frequently used in pulp capping procedures. The modification of GI powders led to the incorporation of bioactive glass into GICs. To enhance the mechanical properties of light-cured GICs further, a resin matrix was added, giving rise to products such as Activa Bioactive base/liner.8

Due to its favorable properties, including antimicrobial activity, biocompatibility, radiopacity, a high pH, and its ability to induce dentinal bridge formation, mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) was introduced to the market. However, limitations such as difficult handling, an extended setting time, and inadequate mechanical properties, low compressive strength9 led to the introduction of Biodentine as an alternative calcium silicate-based cement and dentin substitute. Biodentine, similar to MTA, is used as a dentin replacement material and is formulated as a powder-liquid system. The liquid contains calcium chloride as an accelerator and a hydrosoluble polymer as a water-reducing agent, while the powder consists of calcium carbonate, zirconium oxide as a radiopacifier, tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, calcium oxide, and iron oxide. Biodentine has a setting time of approximately 9–12 minutes. Compared to MTA, it is easier to handle, offers better sealing than calcium hydroxide (CH), promotes dentinal bridge formation, has antimicrobial properties, and requires a shorter working time.

The mechanical properties of restorative materials are often assessed through compressive strength tests.10 However, conflicting results have been reported in previous studies regarding the compressive strength of different liners available on the dental market.11 Additionally, limited research exists on the compressive strength of newly developed cements such as Biod+. Since these liners provide sufficient compressive strength, they can function independently for pulp capping without requiring an additional base beneath the final restorative material. To withstand parafunctional and functional stresses in the oral cavity, all the aforementioned liners must demonstrate adequate compressive strength when placed under ceramic, metal, amalgam, or composite restorations.

Materials and Methods

The study was carried out within the Department of Periodontics at Saveetha Dental College. It began following the required approval from the ethical committee of the Saveetha Research Board, Saveetha University, Chennai, India. The results were obtained from the white lab of Saveetha Dental College.

Specimen Collection

A total of 36 tooth specimens were obtained from the Department of Pedodontics at Saveetha Dental College. The selection criteria required the extracted teeth to be primary molars without abnormalities such as malformation, gross decay, or fractures. The specimens were then divided into two groups, each containing 18 samples.

Protocol

Pulpotomy procedure was performed in the teeth specimens sorted in both the groups. Group A being the control, and group B which comprises 18 teeth specimens that are to be subjected to saliva exposure. An access cavity was created in all specimens, followed by the excavation of the coronal pulp.

Fillings

After excavating the coronal pulp in both groups of freshly extracted primary molars, the selected materials for the study were applied. Each group with 18 specimens was further divided into three subgroups within group A and group B, denoting that six samples of teeth from each group are used for filling the canals with MTA, Biod+, and Biodentine, respectively. In control group (group A), all the teeth samples were filled with MTA, Biod+, and Biodentine without mixing it with saliva. In group B, the specimens were mixed in saliva and were filled with the three materials in sequence among six allotted to each material within the 18 samples. The filling was placed till the occlusal surface of the tooth. Adequate occlusal clearance was also done.

Compressive Strength

The specimens were kept undisturbed for a period of 4 hours, after which the specimens were subjected to pressure in terms of compressive strength. The tooth specimens were subjected to compressive strength testing using an Instron universal testing machine.

Statistics

The obtained data were sorted and stored in MS Excel. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was done to obtain the significance of the results between group A and group B. SPSS was used to analyze significance and to chart the mean value represented in N/(force).

Results

The results of the data pertaining to the compressive strength of materials used as root canal sealers, namely MTA, Biod+, and Biodentine, show that Biod+ has the highest compressive strength of the three materials under conditions where saliva is present as well as absent. The material's increased capacity to bear large compressive forces without deforming or failing mechanically is indicated by its superior compressive strength, and this is an essential attribute for guaranteeing the longevity and toughness of dental restorations. Regarding the effect of saliva on compressive strength, Biodentine exhibits the least amount of fluctuation both prior to and following exposure to saliva, suggesting that it functions consistently in salivary environments. This property indicates that Biodentine maintains its mechanical integrity more effectively than both Biod+ and MTA, which is crucial for materials that are frequently exposed to the oral environment. Consequently, Biodentine is distinguished by its consistent performance even in the presence of saliva. However, Biod+ demonstrates the highest durability in terms of compressive strength. A comparison of the compressive strengths of the three materials, both with and without saliva exposure, showed a statistically significant difference. The one-way ANOVA analysis yielded a p-value of 0.00532, confirming that the observed variations in compressive strength are not due to random variation. In conclusion, compressive strength is a crucial component of dental materials that directly affects how well they work and how reliable they are in dental restorations. Based on their individual characteristics and clinical uses, the results indicate that Biod+ is the most resilient material in terms of compressive strength, and Biodentine provides the most reliable performance when saliva is present. As such, these materials are the best options for root canal fillings (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Discussion

Typically, a deep and extensive cavity prepared during the endodontic access procedure removes the arched roof of the pulp chamber, significantly reduces the cusps and ridges in the dentin, and ultimately weakens the tooth.12 Following its introduction by Torabijenad, MTA received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval and was subsequently made available for purchase as ProRoot MTA in 1998.13 MTA has good compressive strength, outstanding sealing ability, biocompatibility, and, once set, is insoluble in bodily fluids.14 Composite resins are another group of materials that have been widely used for restoring teeth following pulpotomy. Because composite resins bond to the remaining tooth structure micromechanically, they can strengthen the weaker tooth structure that remains.15 Polymerization shrinkage is a drawback of traditional composite resins, though. To reduce polymerization stress in composite materials, the use of an incremental composite placement technique has been recommended. Although incremental filling helps alleviate polymerization shrinkage stress in conventional composite resin restorations, it has drawbacks, including being time-consuming, increasing the risk of interlayer contamination, and potentially leading to void formation in the restorations.16 To address these limitations, bulk-fill composite resins have been introduced, which allow for application and light-curing in a single step.17 Giomer is an additional material that can be utilized for pulpotomized teeth restoration. A prior study18 demonstrated that giomer, which was used in primary molars restoration that were pulpotomized, exhibited greater fracture strength compared to those restored with amalgam or GIC. Two recently developed bulk-fill giomers with different viscosities, Beautifil-Bulk Flowable and Beautifil-Bulk by SHOFU, Kyoto, Japan, have been introduced.19 Flowable composite resins are believed to reduce stress, enhance elasticity, and prevent fractures.20 In the present study, a 2 mm layer of high-viscosity bulk-fill giomer (Beautifil-Bulk, SHOFU) was applied over a low-viscosity bulk-fill giomer. The development of Biod+ represents a notable innovation, offering benefits such as reduced stress and improved fracture resistance. Studies show that giomer-restored pulpotomized teeth have higher fracture strength than those restored with amalgam or GIC. Specifically, the combination of low and high-viscosity bulk-fill materials, such as MTA and Biodentine, enhances elasticity and prevents fractures, making it an effective restoration approach. This two-layer method improves the longevity and integrity of the restored teeth while also streamlining the restoration procedure. All things considered, these improvements in methods and materials lead to more consistent and durable results post-pulpotomy.

In conclusion, the development of sealer materials and application methods has improved the course of treatment for teeth that have had pulpotomies. Even though endodontic access cavity preparation initially weakened the tooth structure, materials such as MTA and Biodentine have made it possible to strengthen the tooth structure that is still present. MTA is a dependable option for endodontic treatment because of its exceptional sealing ability, biocompatibility, and insolubility in physiological fluids. Biod+ performed admirably in the confinement of this setting. More evidence supporting the compressive strength of Biod+ is encouraged, which makes it easier to adapt to better materials through more research.
