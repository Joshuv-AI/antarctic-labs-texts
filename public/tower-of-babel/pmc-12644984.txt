PASCAL repair of severe mitral regurgitation from posterior leaflet cleft in a critical patient: a case report

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is one of the most prevalent valvular heart diseases and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality.1,2 In primary MR, surgical repair remains first-line therapy.3 In high-risk patients, mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has emerged as a guideline-endorsed alternative.4 However, certain anatomical variations, such as mitral leaflet clefts, pose challenges for M-TEER due to disrupted coaptation and the risk of residual regurgitation. Experience in such anatomies remains limited but is expanding with newer devices.5,6

Case presentation

A 65-year-old male with a known history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation, and coronary artery disease presented in acute respiratory distress [SaO₂ 45% on room air, blood pressure 188/119 mmHg, NT-proBNP 1282 ng/L (normal < 125 ng/L), lactate 3.1 mmol/L (normal < 2.0 mmol/L)]. One week earlier, he had been hospitalized externally for pneumonia complicated by hypertensive pulmonary oedema and severe MR. Following that admission, elective surgical mitral valve replacement had been scheduled. However, before the scheduled date, he was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) because of acute respiratory failure, where non-invasive ventilation was initiated but failed to provide adequate oxygenation, necessitating intubation and 4 days of veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) with prone positioning. He was diagnosed with coronavirus disease 2019 pneumonia and suspected bacterial superinfection, requiring broad-spectrum antibiotics and steroids. He was extubated after 6 days and remained in the ICU for 7 more days due to delirium.

The initial transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) confirmed severe MR (effective regurgitant orifice area 0.5 cm², vena contracta 6 mm, regurgitant volume 86 mL) with a mitral valve area of 5 cm², a mean gradient of 3 mmHg, and preserved biventricular function. Given the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk following VV-ECMO and critical illness, the multidisciplinary Heart Team opted for M-TEER using the PASCAL ACE system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA).

Under general anaesthesia, left femoral venous access was obtained to spare the former ECMO site. Standard transseptal puncture was performed using a steerable sheath and dilator system. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a v-wave of 28 mmHg, a mean left atrial pressure of 8 mmHg, and a cardiac index of 2.4 L/min/m². Intraprocedural 3D-TOE demonstrated a significant posterior mitral leaflet cleft (Figure 1).

Intraprocedural 3D transoesophageal echocardiography of the mitral valve. Surgeon’s-view en face images demonstrating a posterior mitral leaflet cleft (arrow) between P2 and P3 scallops (A) and the resulting jet in colour Doppler (B). A1–3, anterior leaflet scallops; P1–3, posterior leaflet scallops.

Initial attempts were made to place the PASCAL ACE medial and lateral to the cleft in the coaptation line (Figure 2). Despite successful leaflet grasping, these configurations resulted in unacceptably high mean transmitral gradients of 7–8 mmHg, likely due to excessive tissue approximation. The team then realigned the device parallel to the coaptation plane, guided by a 2D intercommissural short-axis view as well as 3D imaging for clocking orientation. Sequential independent grasping was performed of segments P2 and P3 adjacent to the cleft. The device was deployed after confirming adequate tissue capture, stability, and absence of significant residual regurgitation (Figure 3). Post-deployment TOE showed a stable device position with marked reduction of MR from Grade IV to mild and a final mean gradient of 3 mmHg.

The PASCAL ACE device was then positioned parallel to the coaptation plane in the posterior mitral leaflet cleft (C1) resulting in mild MR (C2) with a final mean gradient of 3 mmHg.

Intraprocedural transoesophageal echocardiography: before and after perpendicular PASCAL ACE deployment. (A) 2D and 3D transcatheter edge-to-edge images before device introduction, demonstrating the posterior leaflet cleft and severe regurgitation. (B) The PASCAL ACE device positioned orthogonally across the cleft in capture-ready configuration. (C) The final post-deployment result with the cleft bridged, coaptation restored, and a functional bileaflet valve achieved.

The patient recovered well post-procedure. Repeat echocardiography showed stable device position with mild residual MR and preserved transmitral gradients. He was discharged after further clinical stabilization. Within 30 days, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class improved from initial IV to II. Given the excellent procedural result and the patient’s improved clinical condition, the initially planned surgical valve replacement was deferred. At 90 days, he showed sustained recovery, no MR recurrence (Figure 4), and no readmissions.

Discussion

This case illustrates several important considerations regarding the management of complex MR in high-risk patients. Mitral leaflet clefts often contraindicate M-TEER due to coaptation difficulty and residual MR risk.3 According to the 2025 European guidelines, surgical repair remains first-line for severe primary MR (Class I, Level B), whereas M-TEER is a Class IIa option in high-risk/inoperable patients.4 This case demonstrates successful off-label M-TEER, emphasizing the need for flexible guideline application in unstable patients.

Several case reports have described the feasibility of M-TEER in patients with leaflet clefts, particularly using the MitraClip (Abbott, Chicago, Il) system. Russo et al.7 reported successful perpendicular clipping of an anterior cleft in a patient with prior atrioventricular canal repair and with excellent haemodynamic results and no need for reintervention. Stys et al.8 described a case of post-surgical cleft MR managed with MitraClip implantation across the cleft, resulting in significant symptomatic improvement. In a study of primary MR, Gerçek et al.5 demonstrated that both MitraClip and PASCAL were effective in cleft or multi-scallop anatomies, with similar procedural success.

Previous reports have confirmed the feasibility of PASCAL repair in anatomically complex MR cases, including those with large flail segments, or multijet regurgitation.9,10 Its central spacer distributes tension across the coaptation line, reducing leaflet stress. Independent leaflet grasping allows staged grasping of asymmetrical or gapped leaflet segments. This is especially important when one needs to grasp very close to a cleft edge, where simultaneous grasping systems such as the MitraClip system may not achieve secure capture of both sides. Although limited, early data suggest promising PASCAL outcomes in non-standard coaptation anatomy. The European guidelines do not yet provide a specific recommendation for cleft anatomies.4

In our case, initial attempts to place the device lateral and medial to the cleft failed due to elevated gradients, highlighting a common dilemma in leaflet cleft interventions. Realigning the device perpendicularly across the cleft reduced MR without inducing stenosis, lowering left atrial v-wave/mean pressure and thereby the left atrial-left ventricular mean gradient (7–8 → 3 mmHg). This strategy resembles surgical cleft repair, approximating cleft margins to restore coaptation.

The patient’s critical illness added further complexity. Mounting evidence supports the use of M-TEER in cardiogenic shock or severe decompensation.11 A study by Jung et al.12 showed improved survival in patients in shock undergoing TEER compared to conservative management.

The procedure was performed by experienced operators at a high-volume Heart Valve Centre (>170 M-TEER/year; >70 PASCAL/year). Given the complexity and risk of leaflet trauma or single-leaflet device attachment, this strategy should be confined to teams with advanced imaging and TEER expertise.

Transcatheter mitral valve replacement is an emerging option in prohibitive-risk MR. Contemporary series (Tendyne, Intrepid) demonstrate near elimination of MR but non-trivial early risk, particularly with transapical access (bleeding, early mortality) and patient selection constraints (left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, annular/LV geometry).13 In this unstable patient shortly after VV-ECMO, the Heart Team prioritized a less invasive M-TEER strategy.

This case demonstrates the feasibility of PASCAL repair in cleft anatomy and critically ill patients, emphasizing the importance of individualized procedural strategies. While surgical intervention remains the standard of care for cleft MR, patients with contraindications to surgery may benefit from a percutaneous approach. The PASCAL ACE, with its spacer and independent grasping, offers unique advantages and may enable effective MR reduction in anatomically complex cases. As real-world experience with PASCAL expands, especially in high-risk and off-label anatomies, future studies should further define patient selection and long-term outcomes.

Patient perspective

The patient reports clear symptomatic improvement and is satisfied with the outcome.
