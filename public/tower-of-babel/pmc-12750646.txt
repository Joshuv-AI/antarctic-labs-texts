Retroperitoneal hibernoma mimicking a metastatic germ cell tumor: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Sarra Ben Rejeb, Marwa Aloui, Nadia Kouki, Yasmine Ben Romdhane, Haifa Rachdi, Hassen Khouni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHibernomas are very rare fat-derived benign tumors most commonly developed from residual brown fat sited in the interscapular region, the neck, the axillary, and the mediastinal areas. Retroperitoneal location is extremely rare and is usually confused with other malignancies such as liposarcoma and teratoma. We herein described an additional case of retroperitoneal hibernoma and discussed the challenging diagnosis.Case presentationA 32-year-old White North African man presented with a left-sided testicular tumor mass. The testicular ultrasonography showed an ill‐defined hypoechoic lobulated left-sided tumor mass with necrosis and calcifications. Abdominal computed tomography scan revealed a left para-aortic lymph node enlargement (17/26 mm). The patient underwent left orchidectomy, and the pathological report concluded that it was a mixed germ cell tumor. Adjuvant chemotherapy was performed. At 4 months, the abdominal computed tomography scan control revealed incomplete regression of the left para-aortic lymph node (10 × 15 mm), as it was thought to be a metastatic lymph node, surgical removal was performed. No germ cell component, lymphoid tissue, calcifications, or necrosis was found. A final diagnosis of hibernoma was made.ConclusionThe present case highlighted the challenging diagnosis of a retroperitoneal mass in the context of a known germ cell tumor. Introduction

Hibernoma is a rare benign tumor derived from brown fat cells. It was described for the first time by Merkel in 1905 and was named pseudolipoma [1]. The thigh, the shoulder, the axillar region, the back, and the neck are the most commonly involved sites [2–4]. Retroperitoneal hibernoma (RH) is however extremely rare, with only few cases described in the literature [5–10]. We herein report a misleading case of a retroperitoneal hibernoma incidentally discovered in a patient with a testicular germ cell tumor.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old North African White man presented with a swollen and painful left testicle without fever or hematuria. Physical examination of the genital organs revealed a hard painless mass of the left testis. Laboratory tests including testicular tumor markers were unremarkable (AFP: 1.43 UI/mL [< 15 UI/mL], HCG: 1.02 UI/mL [< 10 UI/mL]. Testicular ultrasonography showed an ill‐defined hypoechoic lobulated left-sided tumor mass with necrosis and calcifications. On computed tomography (CT) scan, enlargement of a left para-aortic lymph node was found (17/26 mm) (Fig. 1). No other retroperitoneal or pelvic tumor mass was found. The patient underwent left orchidectomy, and pathological examination concluded the presence of a mixed germ cell tumor composed of seminomatous and yolk sac components. The tumor measured 9 cm and was confined to the testis, it was totally removed, and no vascular invasion was found. The patient received adjuvant chemotherapy. Four months later, he underwent a CT scan control, which revealed incomplete regression of the para-aortic lymph node (10 × 15 mm). As the lymph node was highly suspicious of metastasis from the testicular germ cell tumor, surgical removal of this swelling was performed. On the gross examination, the specimen was tan-yellow, soft, and spongy on cut surface. No solid or whitish areas were found. Microscopic examination revealed a neoplastic lesion composed of polygonal eosinophilic brown fat cells admixed with mature univacuolated adipocytes [Figs. 2–3]. There was no lymph node tissue or metastatic germ cell component. No calcification or teratomatous tissue was identified. Pathological examination concluded to a benign hibernoma. At 6 months, the patient is free from disease.

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only 20 cases of retroperitoneal hibernomas have been described in the literature, and the present case is the first published report of a regressive retroperitoneal hibernoma occurring in a patient with a germ cell tumor [5–23] [Table 1]. Indeed, our case was somewhat challenging because of the patient’s medical history of a germ cell tumor of the left testis, and the retroperitoneal mass was thought to be a metastatic lymph node.

Hibernoma is a very rare neoplasm accounting for < 1% of fat-derived tumor that is developed from the vestiges of fetal brown adipose tissue [24]. Hibernomas are most often located in the thigh, trunk, chest, upper extremity, head, and neck. Retroperitoneal location is however infrequent, accounting for < 10% [8–10]. This tumor typically occurs in young adults (mean age: 38 years; range: 2–75 years). In the present paper, the authors reviewed all cases of retroperitoneal hibernomas, the mean age of patients was 35 years old (3 months–63 years old) [5–23]. Hibernomas are slightly more common in male patients; nevertheless, according to the reported cases in this paper, a higher tendency of RH in women was found, with a sex ratio of 0:5 [5–23]. In most cases, the clinical presentation was an abdominal mass leading to abdominal distension and/or abdominal pain or symptoms due to compression of adjacent structures. In this reported case, there were no symptoms related to the retroperitoneal hibernoma, which was incidentally discovered on radiological exploration of a testicular tumor mass. The radiologic features of hibernomas are still not pathognomonic; thus, ultrasound, CT scan, and even magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are not specific and depend on the amount of the lipid component. Although some findings could be highly suggestive of the benign nature of this tumor, such as the good demarcation and fatty nature, preoperative diagnosis is still difficult. Indeed, the main differential diagnoses include not only benign tumors such as atypical lipoma, hibernoma, angiomyolipoma, and teratoma but also malignant entities such as liposarcoma and malignant fibrous histiocytoma, and as in the present case, a metastatic lymph node should also be considered. Preoperatively, a core needle biopsy (CNB) can be performed, although the final pathology report after surgical excision is necessary to provide the definitive diagnosis and to rule out other differential diagnoses [24–28]. In the present case, it was not possible to perform preoperative biopsy considering the high risk of germ cell tumor dissemination, as the mass was thought to be a metastatic lymph node from the testicular malignancy. On gross examination, hibernoma is typically well circumscribed and encapsulated with a red-brown color. Microscopically, it has a pathognomonic appearance, composed of multivacuolated cells with variably eosinophilic cytoplasm and small central nuclei, smaller granular eosinophilic cells, and a variable amount of univacuolated mature adipocytes. Nuclear atypia and mitosis are usually absent [1, 24].

The etiopathogenesis of hibernomas is not clearly established; however, a possible link between hibernoma and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 has been advanced [24, 28]. In the present case, the retroperitoneal hibernoma occurred in association with a germ cell tumor of the testis. The main pathological challenge was to rule out a germ cell component, especially a teratomatous tissue. For this purpose, the entire specimen was submitted, and microscopic examination did not reveal any other tissue, calcifications, or necrosis.

Although this is the first reported case of a retroperitoneal hibernoma associated with a germ cell tumor of the testis, other associated malignancy has been reported in the paper published by Garcia et al [15], in which they had the coincidental association of RH with breast tumors (carcinoma and phyllode tumor). Further case reports and literature reviews are highly recommended to better understand the mechanism of these associations.

Hibernomas are entirely benign and typically do not recur after complete surgical removal [5–23]. A remarkable fact that should be noted however in this reported case, is that the hibernoma has decreased in size after the adjuvant chemotherapy performed for the testicular germ cell tumor. However, considering the high toxicity and side effects of chemotherapy, it is not clinically relevant or safe to use it for the treatment of such benign tumors.

Conclusion

Retroperitoneal hibernomas are very rare. However further case reports and series studies are highly recommended to increase awareness among surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists about this entity to include it in the differential diagnosis of retroperitoneal soft tissue tumors and to focus on radiologic characteristics that could help with preoperative diagnosis.
