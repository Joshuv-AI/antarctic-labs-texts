Network Analysis of Health‐Related Quality of Life by Frailty Status Among Korean Older Adults: Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2019–2021
Authors: SoYoung Kim, BeLong Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeFrailty in older adults is a multidimensional syndrome associated with adverse outcomes and reduced health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). However, differences in HRQoL structure by frailty status are not well understood, and this study aimed to compare HRQoL network structures between frail and non‐frail older adults and identify the most influential components in each group.MethodsWe analyzed data from 1311 adults aged ≥ 65 years in the 2019–2021 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Frailty was defined using a modified Fried phenotype, and HRQoL was measured with the Health‐Related Quality of Life Instrument with Eight Items. Regularized partial correlation networks were estimated, and centrality, accuracy, and stability were assessed.ResultsThe Frail group was older, more often female, socioeconomically disadvantaged, and had higher comorbidity burdens than non‐frail group. In the frail group, depression–happiness was the strongest link, with working, depression, happiness, and sleep showing high centrality (strength); in non‐frail group, climbing stairs–pain was strongest, with working, depression, climbing stairs, and pain central. Across groups, HRQoL was positively associated with better self‐rated health, absence of unmet medical needs, and better oral health.ConclusionHRQoL is organized differently according to frailty status: mental–emotional domains dominate the frail network, whereas physical‐social domains dominate the non‐frail network. The consistently high centrality of Work across groups highlights perceived role participation as a key lever for improving HRQoL in older adults. These findings support frailty‐specific, network‐informed strategies—emphasizing emotional support in frail older adults and physical resilience in non‐frail older adults.

Introduction

Globally, population aging is accelerating, and South Korea is expected to become a super‐aged society by 2025, showing one of the fastest aging rates worldwide [1, 2]. This demographic shift brings diverse health challenges and poses a growing public health concern.

Among these, frailty affects about one‐sixth of community‐dwelling older adults [3] and represents a critical geriatric syndrome marked by reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to stressors [4, 5]. It is closely linked to falls, hospitalization, mortality, and impaired quality of life (QoL) [6, 7]. Frailty is now recognized as a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, psychological, and social domains, highlighting the need for an integrative approach [8].

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) has emerged as a key indicator of overall health in older adults and a meaningful outcome in elderly care [9]. Lower HRQoL is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, dementia, and mortality [10]. However, most previous studies examined determinants of HRQoL in isolation, without exploring their interrelationships [11]. Network analyses have been applied to HRQoL, but typically without stratification by frailty, limiting their relevance to frail populations [12, 13, 14]. Thus, given the heightened vulnerability of frail older adults [5], the pattern and strength of HRQoL determinants may substantially differ between frail and non‐frail older adults.

In Korea, HRQoL has often been measured with the EuroQol‐5 Dimension (EQ‐5D) [15]; but this tool has notable limitations such as ceiling effects, limited descriptive richness, and cultural inadaptability [16]. To overcome these issues, the Health‐Related Quality of Life Instrument with Eight Items (HINT‐8) was developed for the Korean context.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the HRQoL network structures between frail and non‐frail older adults using the HINT‐8 and to identify key determinants within each group. By adopting a frailty‐stratified network approach, this study provides insights into how HRQoL domains interact differently according to frailty status, supporting targeted strategies to promote healthy and dignified aging in rapidly aging societies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study used data from the 2019–2021 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). KNHANES is an annual, nationally representative survey employing a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to assess the health and nutrition of non‐institutionalized Koreans. Further methodological details are available on the official website (http://knhanes.kdca.go.kr, accessed on 3 April 2025) [17].

Of 22 559 participants, those aged ≥ 65 years were eligible. Participants with missing data on frailty phenotype, HINT‐8 responses, or key covariates were excluded, yielding 1311 participants in the final analysis. All participants provided written informed consent, and the data were anonymized before analysis. The KNHANES protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the KDCA (IRB Nos.: 2018‐01‐03‐2C‐A, 2018‐01‐03‐5C‐A).

Frailty Assessment

Frailty was defined using a modified Fried phenotype [18] adapted to KNHANES data. Five criteria were applied: (1) Unintentional weight loss ≥ 3 kg in the past year; (2) Weakness (mean handgrip strength < 28 kg for men or < 18 kg for women) [19]; (3) Slowness (reporting difficulty walking or being confined to bed, from EQ‐5D mobility items); (4) Exhaustion (feeling “extremely fatigued” on the stress question); and (5) Low physical activity (< 2 h/week moderate or < 1 h/week vigorous activity).

Participants meeting ≥ 3 criteria were classified as frail, 1–2 as pre‐frail, and none as robust. Because the robust group was too small for stable network estimation, pre‐frail and robust participants were combined as non‐frail.

Health‐Related Quality of Life (HINT‐8)

HRQoL was measured using the Health‐Related Quality of Life Instrument with Eight Items (HINT‐8), developed to minimize ceiling effects and enhance cultural relevance in Korean populations. The instrument includes eight items—Climbing stairs, Pain, Vitality, Working, Depression, Memory, Sleep, and Happiness—rated on a four‐point Likert scale (1 = no problem, 4 = severe problem). Items are grouped into four domains: physical (Climbing stairs, Pain, Vitality), social (Working), mental (Depression, Memory, Sleep), and positive well‐being (Happiness). The HINT‐8 has shown good construct validity and test–retest reliability in Korean adults [16].

Other Measurements

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, region (metropolitan/urban/rural), education (≤ middle school, high school, ≥ college), household income (quartiles), and family size (single vs. ≥ 2 members).

Comorbidity burden was evaluated using a modified Charlson Comorbidity Index [20], assigning a weight of 2 to cancer, kidney disease, and liver cirrhosis, and 1 to other conditions. Additional variables included BMI (continuous), self‐rated health (poor/fair/good), chewing difficulty (severe/some/none), and unmet medical needs (yes/no).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics: categorical variables as counts and weighted percentages, continuous variables as weighted means ± SDs. Differences by frailty status were tested using Rao–Scott χ2 tests for categorical and design‐based ANOVA for continuous variables, accounting for survey design. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Network analysis is a systems‐oriented approach that models variables as interconnected nodes within a network, where edges represent the strength of pairwise associations after controlling for all other variables. Unlike traditional regression models that examine individual predictors in isolation, network analysis allows simultaneous visualization of how multiple domains relate to and influence one another, providing a holistic view of complex constructs such as HRQoL. The EBICglasso method applies LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regularization to partial correlations, producing sparse networks that retain only meaningful connections while minimizing false positives. Centrality indices quantify the relative importance of each node: strength reflects the total connectivity of a node, closeness indicates how efficiently a node is connected to all others, and betweenness measures how frequently a node lies on the shortest path between other nodes, thereby serving as a potential bridge. This approach is particularly advantageous for identifying leverage points for intervention within multidimensional health outcomes.

A partial correlation network (Network I) of HINT‐8 items was estimated using the EBICglasso method in bootnet (R package), applying LASSO regularization to identify robust edges. Networks were visualized with qgraph using the Fruchterman–Reingold algorithm, where edge thickness and color indicated correlation strength. Centrality indices (strength, closeness, betweenness) were calculated and standardized. Network II extended Network I by adding sociodemographic and health‐related factors. Network accuracy was assessed via nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations), and stability via case‐dropping subset bootstrapping. The correlation stability (CS) coefficient was used to evaluate reliability (acceptable > 0.25; good > 0.50) [21, 22].

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Sociodemographic, Health‐Related Characteristics, and HRQoL

A total of 1311 participants aged 65 years or older were included in the analysis, categorized into frail (n = 388), pre‐frail (n = 903), and robust (n = 20) groups (Supporting Information S1).

The mean age of the study population was 72.9±5.1 years. The frail group was significantly older compared to the pre‐frail and robust groups (p < 0.001). The proportion of females was higher in the frail group compared to the pre‐frail and robust groups (p < 0.001).

Significant differences were observed in household income, education level, family size (all p < 0.001), and region of residence (p = 0.017) across frailty groups. Frail group was more likely to live in rural areas compared to pre‐frail and robust groups, while robust group was most commonly metropolitan residents. Frail group was also more likely to belong to the lowest income quartile and have middle school education or lower. Single‐person households were more prevalent among frail group than in the pre‐frail and robust groups.

The comorbidity burden was higher in the frail group compared to the pre‐frail and robust groups (p < 0.001). Regarding self‐rated health, poor health was more common in the frail group (p < 0.001). Severe chewing problems were also more frequent among frail group (p < 0.001). Unmet medical needs were more prevalent in the frail group (p < 0.001).

Significant differences were observed in all eight HINT‐8 items (all p < 0.001). The frail group demonstrated higher scores, indicating poorer quality of life. The total HINT‐8 score was highest in the frail group (18.2 ± 4.3), followed by pre‐frail (14.1± 3.7) and robust (11.6± 2.1) groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Network Structure

The network density was slightly lower in the frail group (0.82) compared to the non‐frail group (0.93), suggesting weaker interconnections among HINT‐8 items in the frail group. In the frail group, the strongest edge was between depression and happiness, whereas in the non‐frail group, the strongest edge was between climbing stairs and pain, followed by working and vitality (Figure 1). Centrality analysis demonstrated that working had the highest strength centrality in both groups (frail: 0.87; non‐frail: 0.91). In the frail group, depression (0.82) and climbing stairs (0.70) followed, with happiness (0.69) and sleep (0.69) also showing relatively high values. In the non‐frail group, depression (0.82), climbing stairs (0.76), and pain (0.72) were notable. Closeness centrality was highest for work in both groups (frail: 0.109; non‐frail: 0.132). Regarding betweenness centrality, work (0.143) and sleep (0.143) had the highest values in the frail group, whereas in the non‐frail group, work (0.333) was markedly higher than other nodes (Figure 2). However, given that the CS‐coefficient for strength centrality in the frail group was below the recommended threshold of 0.25, the ranking of individual nodes by strength centrality in this group should be interpreted with caution.

Network Accuracy and Stability

As shown in Figure 3, the CIs around most edge weights were relatively narrow, indicating acceptable precision of the estimated edges. In the frail group, the CS‐coefficient was 0.206 for strength, whereas closeness and betweenness had values close to the lowest tested level. In contrast, the non‐frail group demonstrated higher CS‐coefficients: 0.595 for strength, 0.361 for closeness, and 0.05 for betweenness.

Network Structure of HRQoL and Associated Factors

We examined the network structure focusing on connections between HRQoL (HINT‐8) and sociodemographic or health‐related factors (Figure 4). In the frail group, HRQoL was most strongly associated with self‐rated health (SRH) (weight = 0.316), followed by positive associations with fewer chewing problems (weight = 0.217) and the absence of unmet medical needs (UMN) (weight = 0.122). In the non‐frail group, HRQoL showed the strongest positive association with better SRH (weight = 0.409), followed by notable positive links with the absence of UMN (weight = 0.237) and fewer chewing problems (weight = 0.078). Compared with the frail group, the non‐frail group showed more evident positive links with education level and household income.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the structure of HRQoL among older adults by frailty status using a network analysis approach. Our findings revealed distinct patterns in network structure, centrality indices, and associations with sociodemographic and health‐related factors between frail and non‐frail older adults, suggesting that the organization of perceived quality of life varies according to frailty status.

As expected, the frail group tended to be older, more likely female, socioeconomically disadvantaged, and had a higher comorbidity burden, accompanied by poorer perceived health and greater healthcare vulnerability—patterns consistent with prior literature and supporting the validity of our frailty classification [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Across the frailty continuum, HINT‐8 scores showed a clear gradient, with poorer HRQoL in the frail group and better HRQoL in the robust group. This stepwise pattern reflects the well‐established notion that frailty progressively impairs multiple aspects of well‐being, including physical, psychological, and social domains [4].

The HRQoL network was slightly less dense in the frail group (density = 0.82) compared to the non‐frail group (density = 0.93), suggesting a modestly lower level of interconnection and a potential tendency toward a more fragmented network structure. In the frail group, the strongest edge emerged between depression and happiness, and centrality indices revealed the dominance of emotional and mental health nodes—especially depression, happiness, and sleep (strength). These findings imply that emotional well‐being plays a pivotal role in shaping overall HRQoL among frail older adults. This aligns with prior studies emphasizing the emotional vulnerability of frail older adults and the importance of psychological support in this group. Specifically, previous research has shown that physical frailty can lead to the onset and progression of late‐life depression, and that frailty and depression may interact bidirectionally, reinforcing each other in a potential vicious cycle [28, 29]. This may be because frailty diminishes both physical and psychological reserves, making older adults more vulnerable to emotional distress. As physiological resilience declines, the ability to cope with stress, maintain social connections, and regulate mood becomes increasingly important. In this context, emotional well‐being may serve as a critical buffer against further decline, helping to preserve overall quality of life despite physical limitations. Our findings expand on these associations by demonstrating that emotional domains—particularly depression and happiness—occupy central positions within the HRQoL network of frail older adults. This structural prominence supports the clinical relevance of targeting emotional well‐being as a core component of frailty care. Indeed, recent findings suggest that even among frail older adults, interventions designed to strengthen emotional support can lead to meaningful improvements in mood and perceived social connection [30]. Interestingly, sleep showed the highest betweenness centrality in the frail group, acting as a potential “bridge” node. This suggests that interventions improving sleep quality could have a ripple effect on multiple HRQoL domains among frail older adults. Given its bridging role, sleep may represent a high‐leverage intervention target in frail older adults. Evidence‐based sleep hygiene strategies—including maintaining consistent sleep–wake schedules, creating a conducive sleep environment, limiting daytime napping, and cognitive‐behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT‐I)—are generally safe, non‐pharmacological interventions that can be readily integrated into routine clinical care for frail populations [31]. By improving sleep quality, these interventions may exert downstream effects on both emotional domains and physical domains, thereby offering a practical and multidimensional approach to enhancing HRQoL in frail older adults. Nevertheless, the CS‐coefficients in the frail group were below the recommended threshold of 0.25 and near the lowest tested level for closeness and betweenness, so the specific rankings of node importance—including the bridging role of sleep—should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive, and future studies with larger frail populations are needed to validate these findings. Nonetheless, these results offer valuable insights into the potentially fragmented and emotionally centered experience of frailty.

In contrast, the non‐frail group's HRQoL network was denser and more integrated, with strong connections observed between physical‐function items such as climbing stairs and pain, and between vitality and working. Centrality indices confirmed that physical functioning plays a more prominent role in perceived HRQoL in this group. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that physical function is a key determinant of quality of life in healthier older adults, as better mobility, vitality, and pain management are closely linked to maintaining independence and daily functioning [32, 33]. The strong interconnections among these nodes may also reflect greater adaptability and resilience in this group, where improvements in one domain can more readily influence other aspects of well‐being. Interventions aimed at preserving physical capability—such as strength maintenance, mobility support, and fatigue reduction—may thus have broad benefits for HRQoL in non‐frail older adults. However, it should be noted that the non‐frail group in this study predominantly comprised pre‐frail older adults rather than truly robust older adults. Because pre‐frailty itself often involves early physical decline, the physical‐domain dominance observed in the non‐frail network may partly reflect the heightened awareness of and concern about physical functioning characteristic of pre‐frail older adults, rather than the HRQoL structure of fully robust older adults. Future studies with larger robust samples are needed to disentangle the network structures of truly robust versus pre‐frail older adults.

Working emerged as the most central node across both frail and non‐frail groups, underscoring the critical role of meaningful social participation—even beyond formal employment—in shaping HRQoL. Social prescriptions, which promote engagement in community‐based activities, have been shown to enhance social connection, mental well‐being, and self‐management of health in later life [34]. The centrality of the ‘working’ node likely reflects dimensions of identity, routine, and purpose that extend beyond paid labor into broader domains of social role and contribution [35]. Facilitating older adults' continued engagement in socially and personally meaningful activities may therefore serve as a powerful strategy for maintaining and improving HRQoL in aging populations. This finding may be particularly salient in the Korean cultural context, where Confucian values place great emphasis on social roles, familial contributions, and productive engagement as central components of self‐ worth and identity in later life [36]. According to role theory, older adults perceive themselves as social beings and develop self‐concept through role performance; however, old age brings difficulties caused by role loss due to environmental changes such as retirement. For Korean older adults, the loss of socially recognized roles—through retirement—can be a particularly significant driver of perceived decline in quality of life, extending beyond economic consequences to encompass a sense of purposelessness and diminished social standing [37].

Importantly, our analysis of the extended network incorporating sociodemographic and health‐related variables revealed that both groups showed strong positive associations between self‐rated health and HRQoL, as well as with the absence of unmet medical needs and better oral health (fewer chewing problems), consistent with previous findings [38, 39, 40]. However, given their limited physiological and psychological reserves, frail older adults may be more susceptible to problem cascades, where unmet medical needs or oral health issues trigger a chain reaction of worsening health, function, and well‐being, underscoring the importance of identifying and addressing these barriers early through targeted and proactive support [41]. Higher education level and household income were positively associated with HRQoL only in the non‐frail group, suggesting that non‐frail older adults may be better able to leverage their social and financial resources to maintain well‐being, whereas frail older adults may face overwhelming physical and emotional challenges that limit the protective effects of such resources.

Our findings have several implications. First, they support a differential intervention approach based on frailty status: psychological and emotional support may yield greater benefits for frail older adults, while maintaining physical function and preventing decline may be more effective in non‐frail older adults. Second, the central role of “work” as a node across all groups suggests that preserving older adults' ability to engage in productive or socially meaningful roles—be it formal employment, volunteering, or household roles—could be a high‐leverage target for improving overall quality of life. From a policy perspective, this aligns with growing interest in social prescribing strategies and could be especially valuable in community‐based aging programs. In frail older adults, where physical interventions may be limited, strategies that enhance emotional resilience—including mood management and sleep interventions—could play a key role. Emerging evidence suggests that frailty may be more reversible than previously thought [42]. Building on our findings, tailored intervention strategies that reflect the distinct HRQoL characteristics of frail older adults may contribute to improving frailty outcomes.

This study has several strengths. We used data from the nationally representative KNHANES, ensuring strong generalizability to the Korean older adult population. In addition, a key strength of this study lies in its ability to move beyond the traditional, reductionist approach that examines individual variables in isolation. Instead, by applying network analysis, we were able to illuminate the underlying architecture of how different aspects of HRQoL are interlinked within the context of frailty. This systems‐oriented perspective allowed us to identify not just which domains are affected, but how they interact, reinforce, or buffer each other. In doing so, our findings reveal the internal logic and vulnerability points within the HRQoL structure of older adults, offering a strategic map for future intervention development tailored to frailty status.

However, some limitations should be noted. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Future longitudinal studies or interventional designs are needed to confirm these findings and test the effectiveness of targeted strategies. Second, our frailty classification was based on a proxy measure, which, although supported by previous research, may not capture all dimensions of frailty. Third, HINT‐8, while culturally tailored and validated for Korean, may not be readily applicable to international populations; further research is warranted to assess its cross‐cultural validity. Fourth, centrality stability in the frail group was low, likely due to a smaller sample size and greater heterogeneity; thus these findings should thus be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, the data used in this study were collected during the COVID‐19 pandemic (2019–2021), a period characterized by social distancing measures, reduced healthcare access, and heightened psychological distress among older adults, and these pandemic‐related factors may have influenced the network structures observed [43]. While the KNHANES maintained rigorous sampling during this period, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to non‐pandemic contexts, and future studies using post‐pandemic data are needed to confirm the robustness of these network structures.

In conclusion, our findings underscore the importance of tailoring interventions based on frailty status—emphasizing mental health support for frail older adults and promoting physical resilience in non‐frail older adults. Social role engagement remains a crucial anchor point across groups, highlighting the need for community and policy initiatives that foster meaningful participation and equitable access to supportive resources for healthy aging.
