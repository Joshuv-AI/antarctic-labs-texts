Trends and Burden of Human Rabies in Iran 2012–2021: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Aliasghar Fakhri‐Demeshghieh, Hesameddin Akbarein, Rouzbeh Bashar, Mohammad Reza Shirzadi, Zahra Boluki, Narges Ghasemzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsRabies is an endemic disease in Iran. The aim of this cross‐sectional study was to determine the association between sex and age of rabies death cases, detect the trends of the incidence of human rabies, and to compute the burden of human rabies between 2012 and 2021.MethodsThe age was categorized, and the association between the sex and the age groups was determined with Fisher's exact test and post hoc test with adjusted standardized residuals method. The trends of the incidence of overall, female, and male rabies were visually examined. The statistical significance of the trends were determined with joinpoint regression. Disability‐adjusted life years (DALY) was separately computed for males and females.ResultsBetween 2012 and 2021, 69 rabies deaths occurred. The highest frequency was reported in males aged 10–14 years. The association between sex and age was statistically significant (p = 0.003). An increase was observed for the entire period with several fluctuations and a sudden rise in 2021. The joinpoint regression failed to detect any statistically significant joinpoints or trends. The highest DALYs occurred in 2021. DALYs for females and males increased between 2019 and 2021.ConclusionThe association between the sex and age of rabies cases in Iran was statistically significant. The incidence of deaths had an increasing trend, while the findings of the joinpoint regression were probably due to the large variation in the data.Keywords: DALY, epidemiology, Iran, rabies

Introduction

Rabies is caused by the rabies virus, which is neurotropic and belongs to the Rhabdoviridae family, genus Lyssavirus [1, 2]. Rabies virus is predominantly transmitted by the saliva of an infected animal through bites or scratches caused by an infected animal [3], especially by dogs and a variety of species of bats, with the majority of human cases of rabies being caused by the bite of infected dogs [4]. However, despite their being rare, nonbite rabies transmission including via organ transplant, aerosol transmission, and the contact of scratches, abrasions, open wounds, eyes, or the mouth with the saliva, brain tissue, or spinal fluid from an infected animal have also been reported [5, 6, 7].

Rabies has been classified as a neglected tropical disease by the World Health Organization [8]. Canine rabies causes human deaths prominently in Asia and Africa [9, 10], while in North America, with the near elimination of canine rabies by the implementation of effective vaccination programs, the major rabies reservoirs have now become bats and mesocarnivores [11].

In 2019, the global human rabies deaths, with an estimated 47.20% decrease were lower compared to 1990 [12, 13]. The declining global trend of rabies deaths and the subsequent decrease in the global burden of rabies was also observed in the results of the Global Burden of Disease study 2021 [14]. However, in certain countries such as Vietnam and Iran, rabies outbreaks have increased in recent years [15].

Rabies is endemic in Iran [16] and remains a public health concern [17], especially in the northern settings close to forested regions [18]. However, almost all provinces of Iran are involved, with rabies being reported in both wildlife and domestic animals [19]. In Iran, the majority of animal bites occur due to dog bites, with the reports of the relative frequencies of dog bites among animal bites ranging from 70.3% to 89.5% [20, 21, 22, 23]. In Iran, dogs are the main cause of human rabies [24].

According to the data of the Global Burden of Disease study 2021, the incidence of human rabies in Iran (0.0002 per 100,000 individuals, 95% Uncertainty Interval: 0.0001, 0.0004) was lower than the global estimates (0.13 per 100,000 individuals, 95% Uncertainty Interval: 0.077, 0.18) [25]. However, in recent years, the incidence of dog bites have increased in Iran, and the number of human rabies deaths spiked to 16 in 2021 [26, 27, 28]. Moreover, it has been estimated that in 2021, 59.7 million US dollars was lost due to the costs of rabies postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) in Iran [29]. Due to the costs of rabies and its being a public health concern in Iran, it is necessary to determine the temporal trends and the burden of rabies in Iran.

This study aimed to determine the statistical association between sex and age groups of death cases due to rabies, detect and determine the statistically significant trends in the incidence of human rabies deaths, and to compute the burden of human rabies based on sex in Iran between 2012 and 2021.

Methods

The study proposal received ethical approval from Research Ethics Committees of Faculty of Veterinary Medicine—University of Tehran [IR.UT.VETMED.REC.1403.012]. Informed consent was not obtained as the population under investigation was unavailable. The data of the participants were de‐identified for analysis. All ethical principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to. This research was reported according to the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross‐sectional studies.

Data Collection

Data of all human rabies cases that occurred between 2012 and 2021 were acquired from the Ministry of Health and Medical Education of Iran. This data was collected from Iran's surveillance system for animal bites and rabies. The estimated annual population sizes of Iran between 2012 and 2021 were acquired from the Statistical Centre of Iran. This data is based on the censuses of Iran's population in 2011 and 2016, with approximations for the rest of the years.

Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria was defined as human rabies deaths occurring between 2012 and 2021, recorded by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education of Iran.

Mortality

The mean, standard deviation (SD), and median were used for the description of demographic data of human rabies deaths. The counts of deaths due to human rabies were categorized based on sex and 5‐year age groups. Due to the expected values of more than 20% of the cells in the contingency table being less than five, Fisher's exact test was used for the determination of the association between sex and the age groups of rabies human deaths. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post hoc test with adjusted standardized residuals method was applied to detect the cells that were statistically different from their expected values [30]. For the post hoc test, the Bonferroni‐corrected p‐value of less than 0.0013 was considered as statistically significant for the differences in the observed cell values and the expected cell values. SPSS version 24 was used for the statistical description and the determination of the association between age categories and the sex of human deaths due to rabies.

Trend Analysis

The annual incidences of overall, female, and male rabies deaths were calculated. Line charts were drawn to visually inspect the trends of the incidences of overall, female, and male rabies deaths. All annual incidences were expressed per 100,000 individuals.

Joinpoint regression with a log‐linear model was used for the examination and the assessment of the significance of changes in trends, and the identification of periods with statistically significant log‐linear trends of the annual incidence of human rabies deaths. Due to the zero counts of female rabies deaths between 2014 and 2016, a constant of 0.5 was added to the nominators of annual incidences of overall, female, and male rabies deaths in order to stabilize the variance. The maximum number of joinpoints was set at one. The minimum number of observations from a joinpoint to either end of the data was set at one. And, the minimum number of observations between two joinpoints was set at zero. The Bayesian Information Criterion 3 (BIC3) method was used for selecting the optimal number of joinpoints. The sex of human rabies cases was used as a covariate. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant [31]. Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.3.0.0 was used.

Burden

DALY was calculated as the sum of years of healthy life lost due to disability (YLD) and Years of life lost (YLL) from mortality [8] for each age category based on the year. For cases with a missing date of death, the date of the report was used instead. YLD was considered zero due to the near 100% fatality of human rabies. For the calculation of YLL, the standard loss functions for each age category in Global Health Estimates 2019 (GHE 2019) were used [32]. DALY was calculated for females and males separately.

Results

Mortality

Between 2012 and 2021, overall, 69 human deaths due to rabies occurred, of which 11 belonged to females, and 58 were males. The mean (SD) age of female and male human rabies cases was 35.82 ± 30.50 and 34.14 ± 20.93 years, respectively. While the median age was 46 and 30 years, respectively. The highest frequency of human rabies deaths was observed in males aged between 10 and 14 (Table 1). Fisher's exact test was statistically significant (p = 0.003). Post hoc testing depicted that the observed values in the cells of females aged 1–4 (p = 0.0009) and 80–84 years (p = 0.0009), and males aged 1–4 (p = 0.0009) and 80–84 years (p = 0.0009) were significantly different from their expected values. For females, 3 out of 11 (27.7%) rabies deaths occurred in the 1–4 years age group, and two out of 11 rabies deaths (18.18%) occurred in the 80–84 years age group. The observed values for the aforementioned age groups were higher in comparison to the expected values. For males, 1 out of 58 rabies deaths (1.72%) occurred in 1–4 years age group and 0 out of 58 (0%) rabies deaths were observed in 80–84 years age group. The observed values for the aforementioned age groups were lower in comparison to the expected values.

Discussion

Based on our findings, between 2012 and 2021, 69 deaths due to rabies occurred in Iran, with the highest frequency of human rabies deaths observed in males aged between 10 and 14. This finding is consistent with the findings of Gan et al. where it was reported that in 2019, the highest incidence of human rabies was observed in males and 0–14 year age‐group worldwide [12].

The low counts of human rabies deaths in children aged 1–4 years and 5–9 could be due to Iranian children spending their time indoors in these age groups in Iran [33], which could decrease the incidence of animal bites and therefore, reduce the exposure of these age groups to rabies. The low counts of human rabies deaths in the age categories of 50–54 to 85+ could be due to less exposures of these age groups to animal bites due to their reaching the age of retirement and reduced occupational exposure to animal bites. However, human rabies deaths in elderly Iranian individuals still occur. It has been shown that antibody titers after receiving PEP in elderly populations aged ≥ 70 years were inadequate in 6.5% of participants [34]. It has been shown that geometric mean titers of rabies antibodies in individuals aged > 50 years compared to individuals aged < 50 years were significantly lower, and for individuals aged 60–62 years, maximum levels of geometric mean titers of rabies antibodies were reached later than younger individuals (42 days vs. 30 days) [35]. Therefore, to eliminate human rabies deaths among elderly individuals in Iran, receiving timely rabies PEP may not be enough, and reducing their exposure via reducing the incidence of animal bites particularly dog bites is recommended.

The observed higher frequency of rabies deaths in males could be due to the higher likelihood of their possessing occupational activities exposing them to dog bites or due to their being more adventurous and aggressive [36]. In Iran, in 2021, approximately 77.2% of animal bites were reported in males. However, it was also observed that the delay in receiving rabies PEP was 1.43 times more likely in females [37]. In the Persian Gulf and Saudi Arabia region including Iran, young males in urban areas are at most risk of animal bites particularly by domestic dogs and cats [38, 39]. This finding together with the contrast of a higher likelihood of delay in receiving PEP in Iranian females supports the notion that males experience higher exposure to rabid animals compared to females in Iran. Due to the higher incidence of human rabies in Iranian males, particularly in the age‐group of 10–14 years, it is recommended to target males particularly males aged 10–14 years in future rabies prevention programs in Iran. It is recommended to increase the public awareness about rabies, the importance of timely receiving rabies PEP, and animal‐bites in Iran with more focus on children in the age group of 10–14 years since in a study assessing the knowledge of male rural middle‐school students in Kalat County in Iran, it was observed that only 50% of participants had prior awareness about animal bites [17].

The association between the sex and the age‐groups of rabies human deaths was statistically significant (p = 0.003) with the observed values for females and males aged 1–4 and 80–84 years were significantly different from the expected values (p = 0.0009). 27.27% and 18.18% of female deaths occurred in the 1–4 and 80–84 years age groups, which were higher than the expected values. 1.72% and 0% of male deaths occured in 1–4 years and 80–84 years age groups, which were lower than the expected values.

In visual inspection, the incidences of overall, female, and male human rabies death for the entire time period had fluctuations, with the most increase seen in 2021. However, no statistically significant trends were detected. The findings of the joinpoint regression were in contrast with the findings of the visual inspection. This could be due to the large variations in the data [40]. Our findings are in contradiction with the findings of Ruveshkumar et al. that determined the trends of secular incidence of human rabies deaths in India with joinpoint regression, reporting the secular incidence of human rabies between 2005 and 2020 declined (p < 0.001) [41]. The trends of human deaths due to rabies have decreased globally with 13,743 deaths in 2019, among which the majority of deaths were reported in India, Nigeria, Pakistan, Ethiopia, and China [12].

The sudden increase in rabies human deaths to 16 cases in 2021 in Iran could be due to the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic that caused reduction in receiving rabies PEP in dog‐bite victims in India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Thailand due to the fear of COVID‐19 infection [42]. The COVID‐19 pandemic decreased access to rabies PEP in many countries [43]. Nonadherence to rabies PEP in a clinical trial conducted in India was higher in the period of the COVID‐19 lockdown (19%) compared to prepandemic (6%) and postlockdown (2%) [44]. Therefore, it is possible that in Iran, failure, or delay in receiving rabies PEP was also affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic, as it was noted that in 2021, the consumption of human rabies vaccines decreased significantly [29], which could suggest that receiving the complete regime of rabies PEP in Iran was decreased due to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Based on our findings, the highest number of DALYs lost due to rabies was observed in males in 2021. This finding is explainable by the drastic rise in the number of male deaths in 2021. DALYs lost in females and males both increased between 2019 and 2021. This finding was in contrast with the estimated burden of rabies in China, showing a decrease between 2004 and 2020 with an annual average of 47,000 DALYs [45]. The lower burden of rabies in Iran is due to the lower incidence of rabies in Iran. Moreover, the global burden of rabies has shown downward trends with an estimated APC of −0.55% (95% Uncertainty Interval: −0.68 to −0.24%) compared to 1990 [12].

In Iran, although the number of human deaths due to rabies is relatively low (69 cases between 2012 and 2021), rabies remains a significant concern with potential to cause substantial economic losses [18, 46]. The costs of rabies PEP and canine rabies programs have been noted as the most vital factors in the social costs of rabies in endemic countries [16]. Annually, circa 29 million people receive PEP worldwide [47]. And, in Iran, in 2021, 260,470 animal bite victims received rabies PEP [37]. However, the direct costs of anti‐rabies serum and vaccine have been increasing in Iran. With the increase in the incidence of animal bites in Iran and since the PEP is provided free of charge, the management of animal bites cause a great financial burden on the health system of Iran [48, 49].

The combination of receiving rabies PEP and mass dog vaccination campaigns have shown to be highly cost‐effective in saving the lives of animal‐bite victims attacked by rabid dogs and to reduce the costs of managing animal‐bite wounds [50]. Due to the zoonotic nature of rabies [51] and its urban wildlife cycles, there is a necessity for continued rabies surveillance along wildlife–human interfaces via multidisciplinary implementations (One Health) to entail stakeholders from health, veterinary, wildlife, social sciences, and the leaders of communities [52]. However, Iran faces numerous challenges in this regard, including the lack of awareness about rabies, especially in rural areas, insufficient rabies vaccination of dogs and cats, and the ease in the spread of rabies over borders into Iran [53]. Moreover, in a study in Golestan province of Iran, it was observed that the coordination between different sectors responsible for rabies control, animal population management, and animal bite control has been insufficient [54]. Therefore, it is recommended to enhance the coordination between different organizations that play a role in rabies control programs in Iran to address the zoonotic nature of this disease.

Limitations

This study faced several limitations: First, the possibility of under‐reporting of rabies, diagnostic delays, and missing data could lead to the under‐estimation and the misclassification of rabies cases in our models. Second, the number of rabies deaths especially for females was low. Therefore, the findings of Fischer's exact test and post hoc test should be interpreted with caution. Third, due to the fact that the data of 10 years were acquired and modeled, the maximum number of joinpoints was limited to one. Fourth, due to the rareness of rabies in Iran, the sample size for our study was small. Therefore, it is challenging to model its temporal trends. Fifth, there are several methods of calculating DALYs with differing components. Since GHE 2019 methods were used for the calculation of DALYs, our computed DALYs are not directly comparable to studies that utilized other methods. The generalizability of this research is limited to human rabies deaths in Iran.

Conclusions

Based on our findings, between 2012 and 2021, 69 cases of human rabies were reported in Iran, with the highest frequency of rabies cases reported in males aged 10–14 years. Fisher's exact test showed that the association between the sex and the age groups of human rabies deaths was statistically significant (p = 0.003). The post hoc test depicted that for females, the observed number of deaths in the 1–4 and 80–84 year age groups were statistically different and higher than the expected values. And, for males, 1–4 and 80–84 years age groups were statistically significant with lower than expected values. In joinpoint regression analysis, no statistically‐significant trends were detected, probably due to the large variations in the data. The highest observed rabies deaths and DALYs were observed in males in 2021.
