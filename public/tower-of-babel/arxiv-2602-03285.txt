MeetBench-XL: Calibrated Multi-Dimensional Evaluation and Learned Dual-Policy Agents for Real-Time Meetings
Yuelin Hu, Jun Xu, Bingcong Lu, Zhengxue Cheng, Hongwei Hu, Ronghua Wu, Li Song

Abstract
Enterprise meeting environments demand AI assistants that can handle diverse operational tasks—from rapid fact-checking during live discussions to deep cross-meeting analysis for strategic planning—under strict latency, cost, and privacy constraints. Existing meeting benchmarks focus on simplified QA tasks and lack alignment with real-world enterprise workflows where questions arise organically from multi-stakeholder collaboration, span temporal contexts, and require tool-augmented reasoning. We address this gap through a grounded dataset construction and learned agent framework. First, we present MeetAll: a bilingual, multimodal corpus derived from 231 meetings (140 hours) with enterprise-informed question injection validated through domain expert review ( κ = 0.82 \kappa=0.82 ) and human discriminability studies (56% detection rate, near-random). Unlike purely synthetic benchmarks, our injection protocol anchors on four enterprise-critical dimensions—cognitive load, temporal context span, domain expertise, and actionable task execution—calibrated via interviews with 15 enterprise meeting stakeholders across finance, healthcare, and tech sectors. Second, we introduce MeetBench-XL: a multi-dimensional evaluation protocol calibrated against human judgment (Pearson r = 0.78 r=0.78 , p < 0.01 p<0.01 ) that measures factual fidelity, user intent alignment, response efficiency, structural clarity, and completeness—criteria prioritized by enterprise users in our needs assessment. Third, we propose MeetMaster-XL: a learned dual-policy agent that jointly optimizes routing (fast Talker vs. slow Planner) and tool invocation (retrieval, cross-meeting aggregation, web search) through offline policy learning on quality–latency–cost objectives. Unlike prior rule-based fast/slow systems, our lightweight classifier (1ms overhead) achieves 81.2% routing accuracy and unlocks a superior Pareto frontier: 30% latency reduction on simple queries and 12.4% quality gain on complex tasks over single-model baselines, while remaining deployable on consumer GPUs (RTX 4090, 22GB VRAM). We benchmark against commercial APIs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro) and provide ablations, robustness tests (noise, overlap, multilingual splits), and a field deployment case study. Resources: https://github.com/huyuelin/MeetBench.

Task Definition and Success Criteria

Inputs.
A streaming multimodal meeting context consisting of: (i) ASR transcripts with timestamps and speaker tags; (ii) optional audio features for low-latency streaming; (iii) an optional enterprise KnowledgeBase (KB) and cross-meeting index for retrieval; and (iv) an optional user query (text or speech). The system may access web search if permitted.

Outputs.
A task-complete response containing: (i) final textual answer and optional structured fields (bullets, itemized action items); (ii) optional spoken reply via TTS for real-time interaction; (iii) tool traces when tools are invoked (retrieval sources, web links) for auditability.

Objective.
Maximize quality under latency and cost constraints. Quality is measured by MeetBench-XL’s five dimensions and a calibrated overall score (§
Calibration and Human Alignment
). For action-oriented requests, we additionally measure
Task-Execution Effort (TE)
success.

Hard constraints.
(i)
Latency
: P50/P90
\leq

\leq
20s/30s for complex queries. (ii)
Compute budget
: single 24GB GPU must be supported with bounded throughput (§
Resource and Deployability
). (iii)
Privacy
: no cross-split leakage; KB and cross-meeting retrieval restricted to authorized data.

TE metrics.

Enterprise Task Taxonomy and Multi-Dimensional Annotation

Design rationale: beyond single-axis complexity.

Prior meeting QA datasets annotate questions along a single dimension, failing to capture enterprise task heterogeneity. Our stakeholder interviews revealed that
operational value
depends on four orthogonal factors: reasoning depth (CL), temporal scope (CD), specialized knowledge (DK), and actionability (TE).

Four-dimensional taxonomy.

We formalize each axis with explicit operational definitions:

Cognitive Load (CL)
:
Low
(direct fact extraction, ¡5s),
Medium
(synthesis across 2–5 utterances, 15–30s),
High
(multi-hop inference, ¿45s).

Context Dependency (CD)
:
None
(self-contained),
Recent
(last 3–5 utterances),
Long-range
(15+ min earlier),
Cross-meeting
(references prior sessions).

Domain Knowledge (DK)
:
General
(common sense),
Basic
(field-specific terminology),
Expert
(deep technical/regulatory knowledge).

Task-Execution Effort (TE)
:
Low
(passive recording),
Medium
(structured organization),
High
(strategic planning with tool calls).

13-class consolidation and enterprise frequency alignment.

The 3×4×3×3=108 Cartesian cells suffer from data sparsity (39 cells have ¡5 samples in pilot annotation). We consolidate into 13 representative classes via axis-level grouping that preserves diagnostic power while ensuring
\geq
50 samples per class (Table in Appendix B). Consolidation is informed by: (1) enterprise frequency (stakeholder interviews: 38% low-CL/no-CD, 15% cross-meeting), (2) error correlation (pilot annotation shows high-CL and expert-DK errors cluster similarly). Class distribution in MeetAll: matches real-world deployment query frequencies (Jensen-Shannon divergence 0.087,
p=0.104
).

Cross-meeting injections are performed strictly within the same split (TRAIN-to-TRAIN, TEST-to-TEST) with disjoint source/target meetings to prevent leakage. Injection scripts and class mappings are released for reproducibility.

Ground Truth Annotation

We first used strong LLMs
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Achiam et al. 2023
)
to generate
candidate
answers for each meeting query and context. Annotators then produced
final human‑verified references
by editing or rewriting these candidates; thus, the released ground truths are human‑curated rather than LLM‑generated. Each reference contains text, type labels, and alignment metadata.
For audio responses, we used voice cloning to generate natural spoken replies for playback and user studies.
Using the F5-TTS framework
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, we clone the timbre of the
most active speaker
in every meeting.
Cloned replies therefore blend seamlessly into the dialogue and preserve conversational realism.

Automatic candidates & human‑finalized references.

For each injected query, we generate candidates with two strong LLMs (GPT‑4o
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
and DeepSeek‑R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
).
Then five trained annotators independently review and correct the outputs to obtain the
final
ground truth references, judged on
adequacy, faithfulness, and applicability
(4‑point Likert). In a blind re‑scoring of
n=100
samples, human‑finalized references are preferred over raw outputs in 91% of cases.

Subjective validation study.

20 external participants rated 150 randomly sampled (query, GT) pairs from three complexity bands. Mean plausibility scores on a 5-point scale were 4.38 (low), 4.24 (medium), and 4.07 (high), demonstrating that GT answers remain appropriate even for analytical questions.

Planner Module.
The
Planner
module functions as the system’s slow-thinking counterpart, tailored for handling complex and deliberative queries that require multi-step reasoning and strategic planning. It utilizes our supervised finetuned model
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
(for both more accurate classification of meeting problems and creating solution for complex problems), which classifies query type and complexity and offers enhanced reasoning capabilities and superior performance in complex task execution.

Tool‑oriented plan synthesis.
When a query is routed to planner, the Planner assembles a structured “plan card” listing the tool chain, evidence sources, stop criteria. This plan is fed to the planning loop in Step 3 above, ensuring reliable decision‑making.

Parallel Processing for Enhanced Real-Time Performance:

This parallel deployment allows both agents to process queries simultaneously without mutual interference, significantly improving the system’s responsiveness and scalability. In scenarios with bursty traffic, we cap Talker prefill and use a token-level interrupt for Planner decisions to ensure bounded tail latency.

Talker Module.
The
Talker
module serves as the system’s fast-thinking component, optimized for handling simple and routine queries encountered during meetings. It leverages our finetuned multimodal model
(
Chu et al. 2024
)
(finetuned for better quality of fast-response) to facilitate multimodal input processing, supporting both text and audio inputs. The design philosophy of the Talker is grounded in mimicking human-like quick thinking, enabling swift and efficient responses that align with natural cognitive processes.

Talker module processes the data through its multimodal pipeline. The prewarming mechanism ensures that the initial token generation is expedited, enabling the system to provide immediate feedback. Subsequent tokens are generated at a consistent rate of 30ms per token, ensuring timely and coherent responses that align with the flow of the meeting.

Learned Dual-Policy: Joint Routing and Tool Optimization

Problem formulation.

Given a query
q
and meeting context
c
, we seek policies
\pi_{\text{route}}
and
\pi_{\text{tool}}
that maximize a composite objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}\Big[

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot\text{TaskSuccess}(q,a)-\beta\cdot\text{Latency}(a)

\displaystyle-\gamma\cdot\text{TokenCost}(a)\Big]

where
a
is the agent’s answer,
\alpha
/
\beta
/
\gamma
trade off quality/latency/cost (we set
\alpha=1.0
,
\beta=0.05
,
\gamma=0.01
based on enterprise tolerance thresholds from surveys), and TaskSuccess is a proxy reward derived from task completion heuristics (not circular LLM-judge scores, see below).

Architecture.

A lightweight
routing head
(300K parameters: 2-layer MLP with 512 hidden units) consumes frozen encoder features (mean-pooled hidden states from Planner’s first 3 tokens) and outputs a policy over four actions:

•

Fast
: Route to Talker, no tools (for low-CL, no-CD queries).

•

Slow
: Route to Planner, in-meeting context only.

•

Slow+RAG
: Planner + knowledge base retrieval (for domain-DK queries).

•

Slow+CrossSession

Inference and token-level gating.

At deployment, the routing head runs in 1ms (GPU) after ASR. For ambiguous cases (router confidence
<0.75
), we employ a token-level early-exit gate: Planner emits a sentinel token “T” within 400ms if it classifies the query as simple (target ¡300ms on A100). If “T” detected, return cached Talker response; else, interrupt Talker and proceed with Planner. Miss-trigger rate: 1.8%; late-trigger (¿400ms): 3.1% (5k trials). Both fallback to conservative Planner path, ensuring safety.

Why learned policies outperform heuristics.

Heuristics fail on: (1)
ambiguous queries
(“What did we discuss about security?”—could be fast in-meeting scan or slow cross-session synthesis depending on context); (2)
partial observability
(whether cross-meeting cache has relevant data unknown until retrieval); (3)
non-stationary trade-offs
(latency budgets vary by meeting urgency). Learned policies adapt via training signals from real task outcomes and enterprise constraints (Table

Field Deployment Case Study

To validate real-world applicability beyond controlled benchmarking, we conducted a 4-week pilot deployment with a 35-person product team at an anonymized SaaS company (Nov–Dec 2024, IRB-approved, opt-in consent). The team held 3 recurring meeting types: daily standups (15 min, 5–8 participants), weekly planning sessions (60 min, 12–15 participants), and biweekly architecture reviews (90 min, 8–12 participants, technical depth).

Deployment setup.

MeetMaster-XL ran on a single RTX 4090 (on-premise server) with streaming ASR (Whisper-large-v3, 3s latency) and wake-word activation (“Hey assistant”). Users could ask questions via voice or text during meetings; responses were delivered as text overlays (Zoom plugin) with optional TTS playback. We logged: (1) query text and complexity labels (auto-classified), (2) routing decisions (Talker/Planner), (3) latency (ASR-to-first-token, end-to-end), (4) user feedback (thumbs up/down, optional freeform comments).

Organic query distribution and alignment with MeetAll.

Over 4 weeks, users issued 1,247 queries. Distribution by complexity: low-CL/no-CD 41%, medium-CL/recent-CD 32%, high-CL/long-CD 16%, cross-meeting 11% (Jensen-Shannon divergence vs. MeetAll: 0.087,
p=0.104
, confirming dataset representativeness). Domain breakdown: product planning 38%, technical discussion 34%, operations 19%, compliance/legal 9%—closely matching MeetAll domain splits.

Latency tolerance and user satisfaction.

Post-deployment survey (
n=28
respondents, 80% response rate):

•

Latency tolerance
: 82% rated sub-2s responses for simple queries as “essential,” while 71% accepted 15–20s for complex cross-meeting synthesis (“reasonable trade-off for accuracy”).

•

Routing accuracy perception
: 76% agreed “the assistant correctly identifies when to give quick vs. detailed answers.” Manual audit of 200 sampled queries: 81% routing accuracy (matching offline test set).

•

Overall utility
: Mean satisfaction 4.2/5 (Likert scale). Positive feedback themes: “Saves time tracking action items” (63%), “Helps onboard new members to past discussions” (54%). Negative feedback: “Occasionally misses context when speakers overlap” (31%), “Domain-specific jargon sometimes requires clarification” (22%).

Comparison with prior baseline (uniform RAG).

Prior to MeetMaster-XL, the team used a commercial meeting assistant (anonymized, uniform retrieval pipeline). Head-to-head comparison on 100 matched queries (same meetings, similar complexity distribution):

MeetMaster-XL wins on simple queries (63% of test set) via fast Talker; baseline slightly faster on medium-complexity (uniform pipeline optimized for mid-range), but MeetMaster-XL dominates on high-complexity (Planner’s tool calls outweigh routing overhead).

Failure modes and future work.

Error analysis of 54 downvoted responses (4.3% downvote rate): 31% ASR errors (speaker overlap, accents), 26% domain knowledge gaps (niche regulatory terms not in training KB), 22% routing errors (ambiguous queries misclassified as simple), 21% tool retrieval failures (cross-meeting links broken due to meeting ID changes). We discuss mitigation strategies (confidence-based escalation, active learning for domain KB) in Appendix E.

Conclusion

We introduce
MeetAll
(140h bilingual audio, 1,180 enterprise-validated questions spanning cognitive, temporal, domain, and execution dimensions) and
MeetBench-XL
(human-calibrated five-dimensional evaluation, Pearson
r=0.78
with expert judgment). Unlike purely synthetic datasets, MeetAll’s hybrid construction—authentic audio + stakeholder-informed injection + discriminability validation (56% human detection, near-random)—ensures both acoustic realism and task coverage rare in organic data (28% cross-meeting queries vs. ¡5% in prior work).

We further contribute
MeetMaster-XL
: a
learned
dual-policy agent that jointly optimizes routing (fast Talker vs. slow Planner, 81.2% accuracy) and tool invocation through offline policy learning on quality–latency–cost objectives. This subsumes prior heuristic systems and achieves deployment-ready performance: 30% latency reduction on simple queries, 12.4% quality gain on complex tasks, single-GPU operation (RTX 4090, 22GB VRAM), and validated effectiveness in a 4-week enterprise pilot (35 users, 1,247 organic queries, 4.2/5 satisfaction).

Our work addresses the workshop’s call for robust benchmarks (calibrated evaluation), agentic enterprise applications (multi-meeting synthesis, actionable planning), and deployment-constrained design (on-premise, cost-effective). By bridging dataset realism, evaluation rigor, and field validation, we aim to catalyze research that translates to production-grade meeting assistants. Limitations: current scope limited to Mandarin/English; tool library expandable (calendar, task trackers); robustness to extreme noise/overlap requires further study. Future work: active learning for domain KB expansion, multi-turn clarification dialogues, integration with enterprise workflow systems (Jira, Confluence).
