Isolation and characterization of seven phages infecting gut Bacteroides
Authors: Krushe Mundru, Khalida Khatun, Yolanda Y. Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite the importance of the gut microbiota in human health, isolation and studies of phages infecting abundant gut bacteria have increased only in the past few years. The majority of phages isolated and characterized thus far infect facultative or strict aerobic bacteria. The aim of this study is to isolate and sequence new phages that infect Bacteroides species, which are abundant and prevalent members of the human gut microbiota across populations. Here, we report the isolation and genomes of seven phages infecting three human gut Bacteroides species from the United States. These phages are within the Caudoviricetes class and have siphovirus structural morphology. We observe a variable host range among five B. fragilis-infecting phages. This work provides resources to support further characterizations of gut-resident phages and their biology.

Introduction

Viruses that infect bacteria, bacteriophages or phages, are ubiquitous and play key roles in diverse environments. Despite decades of research on the human gut microbiome, focused efforts to study the viral community have grown in recent years. Metagenomic datasets have provided a wealth of information from which multiple bioinformatics studies have discovered new viral groups with genomes highly divergent from previously sequenced phages. 1-3 Most protein clusters from these assembled genomes had little to no similarity to protein sequences in common databases. 1 Most gut phages are predicted to infect anaerobes from the abundant phyla Bacteroidota (Bacteroidetes) and Bacillota (Firmicutes). 1-4 Altogether, these recent studies have exposed a major knowledge gap in the biology of abundant phages in the human gut.

Large-scale isolation efforts have drastically expanded the collection of anaerobic phages, providing valuable resources for research on phage biology and advancing beyond sequence-based predictions. 5 , 6 In particular, an increasing number of phages infecting Bacteroidales have been isolated and sequenced. 7-12 However, these phages remain a small percentage of public isolate collections and limited access to cultivated phages remain a barrier to mechanistic studies. 13 Members of the Bacteroidales order are highly abundant and prevalent across populations globally. Their ecological niches in the human gut include glycan degradation, nutrient release, immune development and modulation, and pathogenic exclusion. Therefore, a better understanding of how phages persist and infect their hosts is crucial for advancing gut microbiota research.

Here, we report the isolation of seven phages infecting Bacteroides caccae, Bacteroides fragilis, and Bacteroides stercoris from Buffalo, NY, USA. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed siphovirus structural morphology. Genome sequences show phages are within the taxonomic class Caudoviricetes. The five B. fragilis phages exhibit variable host range and differences in their genomes. This work adds to the growing number of phage isolates and provides key resources for molecular characterization of viral life cycle and mechanisms of infection among Bacteroides phages.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and standard cultivation conditions

Bacteroides strains were grown at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber (Coy) maintained with a gas mix of 10% H2, 5% CO2, 85% N2. Bacteria were grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHIS, Criterion) and were supplemented with 5 mg/L hemin and 2.5 μL/L vitamin K1. The following human gut isolates were used in this study: B. caccae ATCC 43185, B. caccae CL03T12C61, B. fragilis CL03T12C07, B. fragilis CL05T00C42, B. fragilis CL07T00C01, B. fragilis CL07T12C05, B. fragilis P207, B. fragilis 3_1_12, B. stercoris ATCC 43183, and B. stercoris CC31F. SM buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4, and 0.01% gelatin) was used for phage isolation, propagation, and dilutions. Plaque images were obtained using an Epson Perfection V600 Photo Scanner.

Phage isolation and propagation

Phages were isolated from wastewater samples collected in May of 2024 and May–June of 2025. In 2024, influent, two stages of reactivated sludge, and effluent were collected from the Town of Amherst Wastewater Treatment Facility (Amherst, NY). In 2025, influent and effluent samples were collected from the Town of Amherst Wastewater Treatment Facility and Buffalo Sewage Treatment Plant (Amherst and Buffalo, NY). All wastewater samples were centrifuged at 18,590 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove solid debris. Supernatants were filtered through chromatography paper to remove remaining solid particles. Finally, the filtrates were filtered through either 0.45 μm or 0.22 μm polyether sulfone (PES) membranes to collect the final processed filtrates, which were used to isolate phage plaques.

Three methods of isolation were applied to detect plaques. First, phages were enriched by mixing equal volumes (5 mL) of processed filtrates, and BHIS was inoculated with 1% overnight bacterial culture. After overnight growth, cells were removed through centrifugation, and the phage-enriched supernatant was collected. In the second method, processed filtrates were spotted onto a top agar layer inoculated with bacteria. In the third method, processed wastewater was concentrated (375–590-fold) using tangential flow filtration (Minimate TFF system, Cytivia) and a 100 kDa MWCO membrane. Both phage-enriched filtrates and concentrated wastewater samples were used to screen plaque formations.

Initial screening for plaques was done by spotting wastewater samples onto double-layer agar plates, where the bottom layer consists of 1.5% agar and the top layer consists of 0.5% agar mixed with 5% inoculum of an overnight culture of the bacterial host. 2 μL of processed wastewater or enrichment culture filtrate was spotted. Samples that yielded plaques in this initial screen were used to isolate phages.

Individual plaques with diverse morphologies were streaked three or more times for isolation. Single plaques were resuspended in 100 μL SM buffer. Each plaque was propagated first in liquid media and then in double-layer agar plates to generate high-titer phage stocks. After overnight incubation, ~5 mL of SM buffer was added to each plate to harvest and resuspend the top agar layer. This was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Lysates were centrifuged at 7,500 × g for 10 min and then filtered using 0.45 μm PES membrane to obtain the final phage solutions.

Structural morphology elucidation using transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Carbon Film 300 Mesh copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) were used for TEM imaging. 10 μL of high-titer phage solutions were transferred onto each grid and allowed to adsorb for 1 min. Excess sample was removed by blotting with Whatman filter paper. Grids were washed by pipetting 10 μL of 0.22 μm filtered dH2O, which was removed using Whatman filter paper. Washing was repeated two more times. Grids were then stained using 5 μL of 0.22 μm filtered 1% or 2% uranyl acetate stain for 30 s. The stain was removed using filter paper. Grids were then washed three times using dH2O as previously described. This was allowed to air dry for at least 10 min before imaging. Images were obtained using the Hitachi HT7800 Electron Microscope.

The capsid size and tail length measurements of phage particles were performed using Fiji. Two measurements were taken for each icosahedral capsid: latitudinally opposing vertices. Three measurements were taken for each tail length. The mean and standard deviation values from 2 to 5 particles of the same phage sample are provided in Table S2.

Determination of phage host range and efficiency of plaquing (EOP)

Ten Bacteroides strains were used in the host range assays, including the original strains used to isolate each phage (see first methods section). 5 µL of phage solution was spotted onto double-layer agar plates. If plaque formation was observed, the efficiency of plating (EOP) was quantified using serially diluted phage stocks (101–108-fold in SM buffer). 2 µL of each dilution was spotted onto a double-layer plate three times against each bacterial strain. PFU/mL values for each bacterial strain are normalized against the PFU/mL obtained for the original strain used in isolation. EOP values for each phage-bacteria combination were confirmed in at least 2 experiments.

Phage DNA extraction

Phage lysates were incubated with 2 μL of nuclease solution (5 mg/mL DNase I, 5 mg/mL RNase A) per mL of lysate for 30 min at 37 °C. Precipitant solution was then added to obtain a final concentration of 10% w/v PEG-8000 and 1 M NaCl, mixed well, and left at 4 °C for 16–18 h. Phage solutions were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was decanted, and the precipitated phage particles were resuspended in 500 μL of 5 mM MgSO4. The solution was centrifuged again, and the supernatant was collected. 10 μL of 0.5 M EDTA and 2.5 μL of 20 mg/mL Proteinase K were added to the phage resuspension and incubated at 50 °C for 30 min. vDNA was extracted using the Promega Wizard DNA cleanup kit (Cat. A7280) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Extracted DNA was then purified and concentrated using the Zymo DNA Clean and Concentrator.

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Purified phage DNA was submitted to and sequenced using Nanopore through the service provided by Plasmidsaurus. Genome assemblies were generated using Flye and were circularized during this step. 14 Each genome was rearranged with the large subunit terminase as the last gene. Genomes were annotated with Pharokka v1.7.5. 15 Specifically, coding sequences (CDS) were predicted with PHANOTATE. 16 Functional annotation was generated by matching each CDS to the PHROGs. 17 The output from Pharokka was then used as input for Phold and Phynteny to integrate protein structural similarity and gene synteny data to improve annotations. 18 , 19 The annotated genomes are available on NCBI Genbank with accession numbers: PZ319851–PZ319857.

Results

Isolation of seven Bacteroides phages and structural morphology analysis

We isolated phages against Bacteroides species using wastewater (primary influent, reactivated sludge, and primary effluent) collected from treatment facilities in the cities of Amherst and Buffalo, NY, USA. Wastewater samples were centrifuged and filtered to remove particulates and most bacterial cells. We tested three isolation methods in combination with the classic plaque formation assay to detect phage lytic activity. First, we set up enrichment cultures of wastewater filtrates with each bacterial strain to enrich for phages. Second, we directly plated wastewater filtrates on a bacterial lawn to detect plaque formation. Third, we concentrated wastewater samples ~400–600-fold prior to plating. To isolate phages, we restreaked each plaque on the original host strain for purification and verified through genome sequencing.

Overall, supernatants of enrichment cultures yielded more plaques, but these also led to repeated isolations of identical phages due to reduced phage diversity. We had more success isolating unique phages from concentrated wastewater samples. Due to tiny phage plaque sizes and instability of phage solutions, we were unable to propagate all plaques initially detected. Despite these challenges, we isolated 7 phages against three species: Bacteroides caccae, B. fragilis, and B. stercoris (Figure 1A−G, Table 1, S1). Five of these phages were isolated from B. fragilis P207 or B. fragilis CL07T12C05.

TEM imaging revealed all phages to have the structural morphology of siphoviruses – icosahedral capsids with long flexible tails (Figure 1A–G). The five B. fragilis phages are similar in size, with tail lengths of 141–154 nm and capsid sizes of ~53–63 nm. In comparison, B. caccae and B. stercoris phages have larger capsid sizes at 66 and 71 nm, and shorter tails at 117 and 116 nm, respectively (Table S2).

Genome sequencing and annotations

All phages were sequenced to obtain genome coverage of 42–240x with genome lengths of 35,855–47,730 bp and GC content of 38.4%–46.1% (Table S1). These genomes are high-quality based on genome completeness of ≥98% predicted using CheckV. 20 All phages are dsDNA viruses in the taxonomic class Caudoviricetes (Figure 2A–C). To assess genomic similarity against previously isolated phages, we computed their genome similarity against top hits from the NCBI nr/nt database (isolates only, excluding MAGs), the Gut Phage Isolate Collection, and the Gut Phage Biobank using the Virus Intergenomic Distance Calculator (VIRIDIC) (Table S3). 5 , 6 , 21 HLBC001 has high genome similarity (90%) to only one phage, BcRU1, which was isolated against host B. caccae JCM 9498 (Genbank: LC918553.1, unpublished). HLBST001 is similar to just one phage as well, BS162P3 from the Gut Phage Isolate Collection, at 91% (Genbank: OP172707.1). This phage was initially isolated using B. salyersiae BSC162. In contrast to the limited number of similar genomes for HLBC001 and HLBST001, B. fragilis phages matched dozens of phages with ~77%–90% similarity from all three databases.

Genomes were annotated using the Pharokka, Phold, and Phynteny pipeline (Figure 2A–C). 15 , 18 , 19 All phages encode a holin-endolysin system to support host cell lysis. No genome is predicted to encode tRNA, tmRNA, and CRISPR. B. caccae phage HLBC001 and B. stercoris phage HLBST001 do not encode any genes associated with a temperate life cycle, thus we conclude they are virulent phages. Both phages encode a DNA polymerase and DNA helicases that are absent in the five B. fragilis phages. The five B. fragilis phage genomes encode an anti-repressor Ant gene; however, there is no indication of an integrase or excisionase essential for lysogeny. Of note, HLBF002 encodes a second putative anti-repressor gene. Additionally, the GC content of phage genomes (~38.5%) differs from bacterial strains used for isolation (GC content of ~43.5% for B. fragilis P207 and CL07T12C05). Based on these features, we conclude that the B. fragilis phages are likely virulent.

Genome comparisons of B. fragilis phages

We aligned B. fragilis phage genomes from this study with two similar published phage genomes: phages Barc2635 (accession: MN078104) and vB_BfrS_23 (accession: MN630433) (Figure 3). 11 , 12 Absolutely conserved genes include a resolvase and a NinB/Orf homologous recombination mediator. Although we are unable to annotate these genes with precise functions, they likely encode proteins involved in DNA recombination and repair. These gene annotations are present in previously isolated B. fragilis phages (Barc2635, vB_BfrS_23) where the presence of anti-repressor and recombinase genes were also reported. A thymidylate synthase gene is conserved within a putative replication and regulation module, which may help to increase nucleotide synthesis during phage replication.

HLBF001 and HLBF003 are more similar to Barc2635 and vB_BfrS_23 than the other phages isolated in our study. They encode an SMC-domain containing protein (structural maintenance of chromosomes, TIGR02169) that is absent in HLBF002, HLBF004, and HLBF005. HLBF002, HLBF004, and HLBF005 encode 1–2 DNA methylases. One of the DNA methylases is highly conserved between HLBF004 and HLBF005 at 98% amino acid identity.

Genome alignment of five new B. fragilis phage genomes and two reference genomes with terL oriented as the last gene. An alignment plot was generated using Clinker. 23 Genes are colored based on the predicted PHROG categories. Annotations are edited to remove low-confidence assignments.

Intergenomic similarities revealed HLBF004 and HLBF005 to be the most similar at 87%. Notably, both phages were isolated from the same wastewater sample using B. fragilis P207. HLBF001 is the most similar to vB_BfrS_23 and Barc2635 (86%), which were isolated in the United Kingdom and Spain, respectively. HLBF003 has the highest similarity (89%) to Barc2635. The global geographical locations (Spain, UK, and USA) from which these phages have been isolated support a widespread distribution and high abundance among different populations.

Variable host range across phages

The host range of each phage isolate was determined using spot titration assays. A clear zone of clearance demonstrates lytic activity against the host strain. In contrast, turbid zones or plaques may indicate incomplete lysis or abortive infection where productive phage replication was halted. If no zone of clearance is observed, the phages are deemed to be inactive against the strain. First, we tested each phage in cross-species plaque assays against all bacterial strains used in this study and observed no lytic activity beyond the original host species (data not shown).

Next, we tested each phage against multiple strains of the same species as the host used for isolation. HLBC001 exhibited lytic activity only against its original host B. caccae ATCC 43185, and not CL03T12C61. HLBST001 can lyse B. stercoris ATCC 43183 in addition to the original host, B. stercoris CC31F, although at a much lower efficiency (efficiency of plating or EOP of <0.1%) (Figure 4A).

All five B. fragilis phages showed either complete or incomplete lytic activity against three of the six B. fragilis strains tested (Figure 4B). For HLBF001 infected against B. fragilis CL03T12C07, CL05T00C42, and 3_1_12, turbid plaques formed at higher phage titers that did not resolve into individual plaques after dilutions. Therefore, these combinations are not included in our EOP calculations. Turbid plaques can indicate abortive infections at high phage titers that do not lead to productive phage replication. EOP was calculated for B. fragilis host-phage pairs where clear plaques are observed (Figure 4C).

Phage HLBF003 has the broadest host range with high infectivity across three strains: P207, CL07T12C05, and CL07T00C01. B. fragilis CL07T12C05 and CL07T00C01 were isolated from the same individual as part of the Human Microbiome Project and are highly genomically similar (~99.8% average nucleotide identity/ANI), whereas P207 was isolated from an ulcerative colitis patient with pouch inflammation (pouchitis) (~99% ANI). 24 , 25 HLBF001 and HLBF002 have a similar host range, and both infect B. fragilis CL07T12C05 and B. fragilis CL07T00C01. Phages HLBF004 and HLBF005 have a narrow host range and high infectivity only against the host strain P207, with HLBF005 displaying minimal lytic activity toward B. fragilis CL07T12C05. Notably, a previously characterized phage, vB_BfrS_23 (>79% intergenomic similarity), exhibited lytic activity against a single strain out of the 9 B. fragilis strains tested. 11 Thus, in comparison, phages HLBF001-003 can infect against multiple B. fragilis strains.

Phage defense systems and CRISPR-Cas systems were predicted in all six B. fragilis strains included in our host range test (DefenseFinder and CRISPRCasFinder, accessed 2026-02). 26 , 27 Strains CL07T12C05 and CL07T00C01 encode the least number of defense systems (9 compared to the highest number of 20 predicted systems), which correlates with the higher phage susceptibility (Figure 4C). 13 defense systems are predicted in P207, from which three phages were isolated from Strains P207 and CL03T12C07 both encode CRISPR-cas systems, however, a search of spacer sequences against phage genomes did not lead to any match. Additional studies are required to identify potential roles of defense systems in phage susceptibility among these isolates.

Discussion

In summary, we isolated and sequenced seven phages that infect B. caccae, B. fragilis, and B. stercoris. HLBST001 is infective against both B. stercoris strains tested (CC31F and ATCC 43183). Five B. fragilis phages varied in infectivity across six B. fragilis strains. These phages are related to two previously studied phages, vB_BfrS_23 and Barc2635 (79%–89% genome similarity). 11 , 12 The global geographical locations (Spain, UK, and USA) from which similar B. fragilis phages have been isolated support a broad distribution and high abundances across populations. The new B. fragilis phage isolates display a broader host range with lytic activity against three of the six B. fragilis strains tested. This includes two B. fragilis strains from the Human Microbiome Project (CL07T12C05 and CL07T00C01) that are highly similar and isolated from the same individual, as well as P207, an isolate from an ulcerative colitis patient with pouchitis. 24 In contrast, a previously isolated B. fragilis phage vB_BfrS_23 displayed lytic activity against a single strain out of a panel of 9 B. fragilis strains. 11 A future goal is to identify the underlying mechanism driving host specificity among these phages when infecting bacterial strains with high genomic similarity.

The isolation and characterization of seven Bacteroides phages contribute to the collection of isolated gut phages and the rapidly expanding databases of phage genomes. Phages infecting strict anaerobes are severely underrepresented in public isolate collections. This work provides crucial resources to support research on elucidating diverse infection mechanisms, engineering phages to alter host specificity, and developing phages as therapeutic agents.
