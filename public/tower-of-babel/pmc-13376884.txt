EMReady2: improvement of cryo-EM and cryo-ET maps by local quality-aware deep learning with Mamba
Authors: Hong Cao, Yueting Zhu, Tao Li, Ji Chen, Jiahua He, Xinggang Wang, Sheng-You Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a leading technology for determining the structures of biological macromolecules. However, map quality issues such as noise and loss of contrast hinder accurate map interpretation. Traditional and deep learning-based post-processing methods offer improvements but face limitations particularly in handling map heterogeneity. Here, we present EMReady2, an extension of our previous EMReady cryo-EM map improvement method. EMReady2 introduces a fast Mamba-based dual-branch UNet architecture to jointly capture local and global features. In addition, EMReady2 also uses a local resolution-guided learning strategy to address map local quality heterogeneity, and significantly extends the training set. These advances render EMReady2 applicable to a broader range of cryo-EM maps, including those containing nucleic acids, medium-resolution maps, and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) maps. EMReady2 is evaluated on 136 diverse maps at 2.0–10.0 Å resolutions, and compared with existing map post-processing methods. Our results demonstrate that EMReady2 exhibits state-of-the-art performance in both map quality and map interpretability improvement while much reducing the computational cost.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Machine learning, Software

Introduction

The rapid development of hardware1, sample preparation2 and image processing algorithms3–6 has positioned cryo-EM as one of the mainstream technologies for elucidating the structural basis of biological molecules as well as the interactions among them7–9. In order to build accurate atomic models of biological macromolecules using cryo-EM10–20, the quality and interpretability of the reconstructed three-dimensional (3D) density maps are critical. However, owing to radiation damage, particle flexibility, preferred orientation, and reconstruction error, the first line of reconstructed 3D density maps often suffer from a high level of background noise, loss of contrast at high frequencies, as well as heterogeneity of resolution across the map21–24, which pose significant obstacles to accurate structural interpretation.

To reduce these defects and improve the quality of cryo-EM density maps, various post-processing algorithms have been developed, which can be broadly categorized into two classes: traditional methods25–30 and deep learning-based methods31–35. Traditional approaches for map post-processing are often performed in Fourier space of density maps, where Fourier coefficients/amplitudes at different frequencies are re-weighted to enhance contrast and reduce noise. Leveraging the advancements in deep-learning algorithms and their proven efficacy in image restoration tasks, deep learning-based post-processing methods have also been developed recently31–35. Unlike traditional map sharpening methods, deep learning-based post-processing directly modifies the input map in real space, offering the advantage of leveraging complex, data-driven models to accurately capture and enhance underlying structural details while maintaining the ability of contrast enhancement and noise reduction. More recently, specialized deep-learning architectures have been developed to specifically address anisotropic resolution issues arising from preferred particle orientations. Notably, methods such as spIsoNet36 and AR-Decon37 utilize independent half-maps to facilitate density recovery. These approaches operate primarily in Fourier space, where networks learn to restore missing or attenuated information by leveraging the statistical properties of independent half-maps. This Fourier-centric strategy is distinct from, yet highly complementary to, real-space optimization methods like EMReady. While the former mitigates anisotropy by restoring global frequency components, the latter excels at enhancing local density connectivity and side-chain definition in real space. Current studies indicate that synergistic pipelines combining Fourier-space deconvolution with real-space refinement improve map quality compared to either approach alone37.

EMReady is a deep-learning framework to enhance map interpretability based on a three-dimensional Swin-Conv-UNet (SCUNet) architecture through both local and non-local strategies. Its Swin-Conv blocks integrate the local feature-extraction capabilities of residual convolutions with the long-range modeling of Swin Transformers38. To ensure robustness and prevent overfitting, the framework employs a hybrid loss function incorporating smooth L1 loss for local fidelity and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) for non-local context. As a robust community resource, EMReady has been widely used in the structural determination pipelines of cryo-EM maps. For instance, it has been employed to resolve complex assemblies such as the SARS-CoV-2 entry complexes39 and the HCV envelope protein complex40. In addition, in the study of the Plasmodium falciparum fertilisation complex41, EMReady mitigates the effects of preferred orientation bias and local-resolution variations. By processing unsharpened maps, it significantly enhanced the interpretability of regions with weak density, enabling confident backbone tracing and the generation of high-quality composite maps for model building. These applications underscore the reliability of EMReady in enhancing map interpretability across diverse biological systems.

Among current deep-learning approaches, EMReady exhibits state-of-the-art performance34. However, EMReady still faces limitations. First, one major limitation is that EMReady neglects the inherent density heterogeneity in cryo-EM maps, like the variation of local resolution across a map, during training. It generates the target simulated map by applying a uniform resolution across the entire structure. Second, EMReady mainly focuses on cryo-EM maps for proteins, and therefore its performance is limited for nucleic acid cases. Third, EMReady is trained with maps of 3–6 Å resolutions and therefore not effective enough for lower-resolution maps like cryo-ET subtomogram averaging (STA) maps. Finally, the Swin-Conv-UNet (SCUNet) architecture of EMReady is relatively slow in computation and high demand for GPU memory, which introduces a heavy computational burden for large maps.

In this work, we present EMReady2, a local quality-aware deep-learning method for the post-processing of protein and nucleic acid maps across a 2–10 Å resolution range by taking advantage of the latest Bidirectional Mamba network architecture. Compared with the previous EMReady method, several major improvements are made in EMReady2. First, the density heterogeneity is considered by constructing local resolution-dependent simulated maps during training. Second, protein and nucleic acid maps are well balanced in the training set. Third, lower-resolution (i.e. 6–10 Å) maps, including cryo-ET cases, are added to the training set. Finally, we develop a bidirectional Mamba-conv UNet (BiMCUnet), which integrates a bidirectional Mamba branch42,43 and a convolutional branch within a UNet architecture, is developed to greatly accelerate the computational efficiency without sacrificing the accuracy. EMReady2 is extensively validated on 136 cryo-EM and cryo-ET maps of proteins and nucleic acids with resolutions ranging from 2.0 to 10.0 Å. The evaluation results demonstrate that EMReady2 achieves significant improvements compared to existing methods including EMReady.

Results

Overview of EMReady2

Figure 1 shows an overview of EMReady2. The input for EMReady2 is a single EM density map, which can originate from either cryo-EM single-particle analysis (SPA) or cryo-electron tomography subtomogram averaging (STA) and may include proteins, nucleic acids, or their complexes. The input map is first partitioned into a series of overlapping 3D subvolumes. These subvolumes are then fed into the BiMCUNet network to generate corresponding post-processed subvolumes. Finally, the processed subvolumes are reassembled to produce the output map.

The overview of EMReady2.

a The workflow of EMReady2. The input to EMReady2 is an electron microscopy (EM) density map derived from cryo-EM single-particle analysis (SPA) or cryo-ET subtomogram averaging (STA), which may include proteins, nucleic acids, or their complexes. Initially, the density map is partitioned into a series of overlapping 3D subvolumes. These subvolumes are then processed using the BiMamba-Conv-UNet model to generate enhanced, post-processed subvolumes. Finally, the processed subvolumes are reassembled to produce the refined output density map. b The BiMamba-Conv block architecture: The input features are processed through both the bidirectional Mamba block and the convolutional block, followed by feature fusion. c The BiMamba layer: Input features undergo simultaneous forward and backward updates, enabling more comprehensive contextual learning.

While convolutional layers effectively extract local features and transformers44 capture global context, the latter often face quadratic complexity. Drawing on the performance of Mamba42 in language modeling, we incorporate this architecture into the cryo-EM processing pipeline to balance local and global feature extraction with linear scaling. However, the original Mamba design, tailored for sequential data, presents two key challenges in this context: unidirectional modeling and limited sensitivity to local structural details. Specifically, the original Mamba architecture processes information sequentially based on preceding elements, which is suboptimal for three-dimensional volumes where each voxel is inherently correlated with contextual information from both preceding and subsequent regions. To address these challenges, we developed the BiMCUNet, which employs bidirectional state space models (SSMs)42,43,45–48 to achieve comprehensive global context modeling while seamlessly integrating convolutional modules to enhance local feature extraction. Figure 1a shows the overall network architecture, consisting of three encoders, a bottleneck, and three decoders connected via skip connections. Figure 1b details the workflow within each bidirectional Mamba-conv (BiMC) Block. Further methodological details are provided in the Methods section.

Overall performances

We first evaluated the performance of EMReady2 on a test set of 118 cryo-EM maps at 2–10 Å. As shown in Fig. 2a, EMReady2 markedly improves the unmasked map-model FSC-0.5 relative to the deposited maps, outperforming all other post-processing methods. It should be noticed that by default we calculate the unmasked FSC in this work, unless otherwise specified. Specifically, EMReady2 improves the FSC-0.527 for 113 of the 118 maps in the test set (Supplementary Data 1). On average, EMReady2 achieves a mean FSC-0.5 of 4.65 Å, compared with 5.78 Å for the deposited maps, 5.41 Å for DeepEMhancer, 5.78 Å for phenix.auto_sharpen, 5.04 Å for EMReady, and 5.08 Å for CryoTEN (Table 1). In addition to FSC-0.5, the Q-score49 of maps processed by EMReady2 is also improved (Fig. 2b). Specifically, the mean Q-score for EMReady2 is 0.493, compared with 0.454 for the deposited maps, 0.368 for DeepEMhancer, 0.449 for phenix.auto_sharpen, 0.477 for EMReady, and 0.452 for CryoTEN (Table 1).

In addition, we further assessed the main-chain (MC) Q-score to characterize map continuity (Fig. 2c), which is critical for reliable backbone tracing in subsequent model building. It is shown that EMReady2 improves the main-chain Q-score in 110 of the 118 maps (Supplementary Data 1). On average, EMReady2 achieves a main-chain Q-score of 0.557, which is considerably higher than 0.491 of the deposited maps and other methods (Table 1). Furthermore, EMReady2 is also capable of improving CC_box, CC_mask, and CC_peaks. The maps processed by EMReady2 achieve average CC_box, CC_mask, and CC_peaks values of 0.859, 0.748, and 0.717, respectively, which are significantly higher than 0.758, 0.721, and 0.614 for the deposited maps (Table 1), and those of the other post-processing approaches including EMReady (Fig. 2d–f). Specifically, EMReady2 improved CC_box in 109 maps, CC_mask in 92 maps, and CC_peaks in 101 maps within the test set (Supplementary Data 1).

Besides single-particle analysis (SPA) approach, another important domain of EM-based structural biology is cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), which produce density maps at sub-nanometre resolution through subtomogram averaging (STA). We further evaluated the performance of EMReady2 on a test set of 18 cryo-ET STA maps at 3–10 Å, and compared it with DeepEMhancer, phenix.auto_sharpen, EMReady, and CryoTEN (Supplementary Data 1). Given that most cryo-ET STA maps in this set contain only partially modeled regions, we selected masked map-model FSC-0.5, Q-score, CC_mask, and main-chain Q-score as evaluation metrics. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, EMReady2 achieves a masked map-model FSC-0.5 of 6.26 Å, a Q-score of 0.396, a CC_mask of 0.698, and a main-chain Q-score of 0.451, all better than those for the deposited maps (i.e. 7.06 Å, 0.360, 0.673, and 0.397, respectively) as well as other methods. These results demonstrate the robust performance of EMReady2 on STA datasets.

Improvement of nucleic acid maps

To evaluate the ability of EMReady2 in improving nucleic acid density of electron microscopy (EM) maps, we further assessed its performance on a test set of 18 raw cryo-EM maps in which nucleic acid residues account for more than 10% of the corresponding structures (Supplementary Data 2). The resolutions of these maps span a range from 2.60 Å to 6.62 Å. The nucleic acid content for each example is detailed in Supplementary Data 6. Overall, nucleic acid residues constitute approximately 40% of the total residue count. It should be noted that since the average molecular volume of a nucleic acid residue is roughly three-fold that of a protein residue, the nucleic acid density constitutes the majority of the structural volume in this test set. EMReady2 is compared against DeepEMhancer, phenix.auto_sharpen, EMReady, and CryoTEN on this test set. Performance is measured using six distinct metrics: FSC-0.5, CC_box, CC_mask, CC_peaks, Q-score, and main-chain Q-score.

As shown in Fig. 4a, EMReady2 achieves a mean Q-score of 0.499, which is significantly higher than 0.453 for the deposited maps, 0.368 for DeepEMhancer, and 0.446 for phenix.auto_sharpen, 0.458 for EMReady and 0.445 for CryoTEN (Table 2). Figure 4b, c shows the results of FSC-0.5 across different methods. Overall, EMReady2 obtains a mean value of 4.20 Å, representing an improvement over the 5.70 Å for deposited maps, 4.94 Å for DeepEMhancer, 5.70 Å for phenix.auto_sharpen, 4.59 Å for EMReady, and 4.69 Å for CryoTEN (Table 2). Examining individual cases in the test set further demonstrates that EMReady2 exhibits consistent and broadly applicable improvements (Supplementary Data 2). Figure 4d–f shows the average map-model correlation coefficients (CC_box, CC_mask, and CC_peaks). Specifically, the average CC_box, CC_mask, and CC_peaks values for the maps processed by EMReady2 are 0.889, 0.786, and 0.767, respectively, which are higher than 0.820, 0.765, and 0.706 for the deposited maps (Table 2). Considering all the evaluation metrics, EMReady and EMReady2 are both capable of improving the deposited primary maps, and significantly outperform DeepEMhancer and phenix.auto_sharpen. Furthermore, EMReady2 outperforms the original EMReady, indicating that the integration of local quality information and nucleic acid data during training enhances model generalization. In terms of main-chain Q-score, EMReady2 also shows a striking performance, achieving a value of 0.543, compared with 0.501 for the best result among the other methods, EMReady (Table 2). Figure 4g, h shows an example of EMD-26260, a 2.6 Å cryo-EM map of the LIN28b nucleosome bound to OCT4. At low contour level, EMReady2 effectively preserves the high-quality regions of the original map while refining side-chain densities. At higher contour levels, EMReady2 also demonstrates impressive improvements in the nucleic acid backbone density. The FSC-0.5 curves further confirm the enhancements across all spatial frequencies in Fourier space. Quantitatively, the EMReady2-processed map achieves an improved Q-score of 0.740, significantly higher than 0.698 for deposited map, 0.656 for DeepEMhancer, and 0.701 for phenix.auto_sharpen, 0.720 for EMReady, and 0.710 for CryoTEN. EMReady2 also achieves the highest unmasked map–model FSC-0.5 value of 2.05 Å.

To evaluate the impact on nucleic acid components, we segmented and analyzed the density regions corresponding to nucleic acid residues in the 18 map test set (Supplementary Data 7). As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4, EMReady2 consistently achieves the highest performance for these nucleic acid regions. To quantify this effect, we compared the magnitude of the improvement (relative to the deposited maps) between the global map and the segmented nucleic acid regions (Supplementary Table 2). For the global maps, EMReady2 improves the mean FSC-0.5, Q-score, CC_mask, and main-chain Q-score by 0.881 Å, 0.046, 0.021, and 0.082, respectively. The enhancements are even more pronounced within nucleic acid-specific regions: the mean FSC-0.5 improves by 1.246 Å (from 5.834 Å to 4.589 Å), the Q-score by 0.051 (from 0.449 to 0.500), the CC_mask by 0.024 (from 0.782 to 0.807), and the main-chain Q-score by 0.086 (from 0.414 to 0.500). These results demonstrate that the enhancement capability of EMReady2 comes more from nucleic acids than from proteins, suggesting the necessity of including nucleic acid maps in training.

Impact of resolution on performance

To assess the generalizability and robustness of EMReady2, we performed a systematic resolution-stratified analysis on the test set of 118 cryo-EM maps (Supplementary Data 8). These maps are categorized into five distinct bins: 2–3, 3–4, 4–5, 5–6, and 6–10 Å. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a–f, EMReady2 consistently outperforms existing methods across all resolution bins and evaluation metrics. Overall, the performance exhibits a characteristic decline as resolution worsens, reflecting the inherent loss of high-frequency structural information. It is also revealed that some metrics like the Q-score and main-chain Q-score, which are sensitive to atomistic detail, show a more pronounced sensitivity to resolution changes compared to correlation-based metrics like CC_box. Notably, for maps with a resolution lower than 5.0 Å, EMReady2 is the only method that consistently and significantly improves both the Q-score and CC_mask relative to the deposited maps. In contrast, other post-processing tools often fail to maintain their efficacy under these challenging conditions.

Visual inspection corroborates these quantitative improvements across various structural contexts (Supplementary Fig. 5g). At high resolutions, such as the 2.70 Å map of EMD-21997, EMReady2 markedly sharpens side-chain definitions and clarifies atomistic details. As the resolution decreases, the impact on structural connectivity becomes increasingly pronounced. For intermediate-resolution cases like the 5.01 Å map (EMD-4366), the processed density exhibits significantly improved helical continuity. Most notably, in low-resolution examples such as the 6.10 Å map of EMD-13378, EMReady2 effectively restores the continuity of the backbone and secondary structure elements that are fragmented or ambiguous in the original reconstruction. These results confirm that EMReady2 provides robust density improvement across the entire resolution range.

For the 18 cryo-ET subtomogram averaging (STA) maps, we investigated the relationship between evaluation metrics and resolution (Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with the trends observed in our cryo-EM datasets, the masked map-model FSC-0.5, Q-score, and main-chain Q-score exhibit a characteristic decline as resolution worsens. By contrast, CC_mask appears largely insensitive to resolution. This insensitivity, which is also observed for CC metrics in the larger cryo-EM test set, can be understood because these metrics prioritize overall density envelopes and contour agreement over fine-grained atomistic details.

Assessment of potential structural hallucinations

To address the risk of structural hallucination where supervised models may erroneously introduce canonical features in ambiguous density regions, we specifically evaluated EMReady2’s performance on intrinsically disordered segments. While our training pipeline incorporates local-resolution-dependent simulations to decouple learned priors from atomic models, the risk of overfitting to ideal secondary structures remains a critical concern for data-driven methods. Supplementary Fig. 7 shows an example of EMD-6536, a 4.8 Å cryo-EM map of the Saccharomyces cerevisiae CMG complex. At low contour levels, the global signal contrast and density connectivity of the EMReady2-processed map are significantly improved. As shown in the magnified views, the deposited map exhibits fragmented and near-noise-level density in this region, with several helices and loops lacking discernible signals. It is worthy noting that EMReady2 does not hallucinat or force these disordered densities into regular α-helices or β-strands. Analysis at high contour levels further corroborate this conclusion, demonstrating that EMReady2 respects the experimental signal boundaries and provides improvements that prioritize structural fidelity over aesthetic regularization.

Comparison with phenix.resolve_cryo_em

While many map enhancement tools utilize primary cryo-EM maps, certain established methods require half-maps, which possess nominally independent errors, to perform density modification. To benchmark our framework in this context, we compared EMReady2 with phenix.resolve_cryo_em (density modification)29 across 42 examples from our test set for which half-maps are available (Supplementary Data 9). For this comparison, the average of the two half-maps serves as the input for EMReady2 (and as the baseline deposited map), while phenix.resolve_cryo_em is executed using the two half-maps alongside their corresponding amino acid sequences. Results are reported in Supplementary Table 3 and shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. On average, EMReady2 achieves an FSC-0.5 of 4.48 Å, a Q-score of 0.485, and a main-chain Q-score of 0.551, with correlation coefficients of 0.862 (CC_box), 0.765 (CC_mask), and 0.701 (CC_peaks). In comparison, the original deposited maps yield the corresponding values of 6.18 Å, 0.397, 0.761, 0.715, 0.587, and 0.447, respectively, while phenix.resolve_cryo_em produced 5.22 Å, 0.437, 0.461, 0.647, 0.415, and 0.478. Conceptually, traditional density modification typically operates in Fourier space; however, while amplitude modulation can boost high-frequency signals, it inherently risks amplifying high-frequency noise. In contrast, our real-space approach is designed to simultaneously enhance structural signals and suppress background noise, thereby improving local map quality. This advantage is quantitatively reflected in our results: the high CC_box values highlight the denoising capacity of EMReady2, while the improved Q-scores underscore its ability to refine atomistic signal details.

Improvement in map interpretability

The true enhancement of density map quality should improve the accuracy of the built models, or namely enhance the interpretability of the maps. The previous sections have demonstrated the overall performance of EMReady2 using general map quality metrics (e.g., Phenix FSC, Phenix CC, and Q-score for the overall map density quality). Here, we present a detailed analysis focusing on its effectiveness in addressing maps afflicted by major local quality deficiencies, which pose a disproportionate challenge to accurate interpretation of the density.

The FSC curve displayed in Fig. 6b also demonstrates that EMReady2 is able to enhance the signals along a broad range of spatial frequencies.

Ablation experiments

Compared with EMReady, EMReady2 exhibits significant differences in three key aspects: the training framework (encompassing network architecture and input density box size), the training dataset (incorporating a mix of diverse data types), and the generation method of simulated maps (utilizing local resolution). To investigate how these factors influence the performance of EMReady, we conducted extensive ablation experiments (Supplementary Data 5), including: generating simulated maps using the reported resolution (model : reported resolution); adopting the same SCUNet network architecture as EMReady (model : SCUNet); removing the bidirectional mechanism of Mamba (model : mamba); excluding medium-resolution density maps from training (model : w/o medium); excluding cryo-ET density maps from training (model : w/o cryo-ET); modifying the input box size (model : box_size = 48).

We first performed ablation experiments on the test set of 118 cryo-EM maps. Six ablation models are presented in Supplementary Table 5, from which it can be observed that the baseline model achieves the best performance in all metrics, although some ablation models also demonstrate competitive performance in terms of CC (correlation coefficient) values. Figure 7a, b show the performance comparisons of all models in terms of FSC-0.5 and Q-score, revealing the key factors affecting performance. First, the generation of simulated maps based on local resolution endows the model with enhanced capability for tailored optimization of local densities, thereby alleviating overfitting. Second, the bidirectional Mamba architecture enables efficient information interaction while maintaining high computational speed, which also allows us to train the model with a larger box size. Since the range of secondary structures can span tens of angstroms, a larger input box better captures such long-range structural information during model training, ultimately leading to improved performance. Additionally, the exclusion of either medium-resolution or cryo-ET density maps results in a significant drop in performance highlighting the necessity of training with a mixed dataset of density maps with varying resolutions. The inferior performance of the unidirectional Mamba variant further validates that a bidirectional scanning strategy is required to fully integrate the 3D spatial context. Overall, the baseline model achieves an FSC-0.5 of 4.65 Å and a Q-score of 0.493, outperforming all ablation models. Such trends are also found in our previous study for structural classification of cryo-EM density maps50, where using an increased box size for local image patches and incorporating a broader resolution range (e.g., 2–10 Å rather than only 2–5 Å) significantly improves the performance.

Notably, while metrics such as Q-score and dFSC show improvements, the gains in CC-based benchmarks (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6) appear incremental. This discrepancy can be attributed to the different focus of these metrics: CC-based measures are largely reflective of the global density agreement and structural envelope, which are already reasonably well-captured by the baseline and ablation variants. However, Q-scores and dFSC are more sensitive to local high-frequency details, such as side-chain sharpness and backbone continuity. The marginal change in CC values, alongside the significant boost in local metrics, suggests that EMReady2 effectively refines fine-grained atomic features while maintaining the overall integrity of the global density distribution.

Discussion

In this study, we have proposed EMReady2, a universally applicable deep learning-based post-processing method for EM density maps. EMReady2 pioneers the application of the Mamba architecture within the cryo-EM processing pipeline and extend it with a bidirectional design and convolutional integration, proposing the innovative BiMamba-Conv-UNet (BiMCUnet) architecture. During training, we generate the target simulated map using varying local-resolution values derived from Q-scores for different atoms within the structure. This mitigates training biases introduced by poorly modeled regions and allows the model to adapt its density modification in a local quality-aware manner during inference. Furthermore, our training and evaluation now encompass maps containing nucleic acid molecules, intermediate-resolution maps, and cryo-ET maps.

Compared with the first version of EMReady, EMReady2 improves in two folds. First, EMReady2 adopts the network architecture of BiMCUnet instead of the SCUNet in EMReady. Due the high computational cost of 3D attention scales for SCUNet, the original EMReady is restricted to minimal window sizes (3 or 4), hindering the optimal integration of global structural features. However, BiMCUnet overcomes this limitation by utilizing a Selective Scan Mechanism, which facilitates the extraction of global features through BiMamba’s long-range dependency modeling and bidirectional context integration, while preserving fine-grained local details via the residual convolutional branch. In addition, the linear scaling of the Mamba architecture markedly reduces computational overhead compared to traditional attention-based transformers. Therefore, EMReady2 not only improves the map but also dramatically reduces the computational cost. Second, EMReady2 considerably extends the training set that includes additional low-resolution cases like cryo-ET maps and nucleic acid maps. As such, EMReady2 achieves a better performance than EMReady in both low-resolution and nucleic acid maps.

EMReady2 is extensively evaluated on three diverse test sets covering different types of maps at 2–10 Å, and compared with state-of-the-art methods, including DeepEMhancer, phenix.auto_sharpen, EMReady, and CryoTEN. The evaluation results demonstrate that EMReady2 significantly outperforms the existing methods. EMReady2 can enhance the quality of maps for both proteins and nucleic acids. Also, EMReady2 shows effectiveness in enhancing maps across a wide range of resolutions, from 2 to 10 Å. Moreover, EMReady2 can be applied to the maps reconstructed not only by cryo-EM SPA but also by cryo-ET STA. We also demonstrate that EMReady2 improves the map interpretability in terms of de novo model building and is robust to map local quality heterogeneity. The runtime analysis confirms its computational efficiency, supporting the processing of larger and more complex maps. It is anticipated that EMReady2 will serve as an enormously valuable tool in the workflow of cryo-EM model building in structural biology.

Regarding its scope, EMReady2 is specifically designed to mitigate stochastic background noise, signal attenuation (blurring), and fragmented density connectivity in real space. However, these real-space noise and suboptimal density features must be distinguished from compositional or conformational heterogeneity. The latter arise from the inherent flexibility of macromolecules or incomplete particle sorting during 3D reconstruction and must be addressed through upstream image processing and classification pipelines. While our method excels at restoring the connectivity of the predominant structural state, it does not decouple overlapping densities from multiple conformations or correct fundamental alignment errors in the original reconstruction.

Despite these advancements, certain limitations inherent to data-driven methods remain. One main limitation of current machine learning-based density enhancement approaches lies in the potential difficulty of accurately improving the density of water molecules, ions, and small ligands51. In many PDB entries, these components are either absent or under-sampled. Furthermore, for cryo-EM at 2–10 Å resolutions, the signals for these small molecules are inherently weak and poorly defined. While our results demonstrate that including more nucleotide data improves the model’s robustness in low-signal regions, the intrinsic data-driven nature of EMReady2 means it remains challenging to generalize to rare chemical entities that seldom appear in the training set.

In addition, users should remain vigilant regarding the potential for structural hallucination likethe inadvertent reinforcement of canonical secondary structure features in regions where experimental evidence is ambiguous. While our training strategy specifically incorporates local-resolution variations to mitigate this effect, no supervised model is entirely immune to the bias of its learned priors. Therefore, while EMReady2 provides reliable density modifications for protein and nucleic acid macromolecules, we strongly recommend that users cross-verify the processed densities, especially those for ions, ligands, and disordered domains, against the original maps and corresponding atomic models. Future iterations could benefit from hybrid physics-informed approaches or uncertainty-weighted loss functions to further safeguard against spurious feature generation and enhance the framework’s versatility across the entire molecular landscape.

Methods

Network architecture

Figure 1a shows the overall architecture of bidirectional Mamba-conv UNet (BiMCUnet). Given an input density map, an initial 3D convolution extracts low-level volumetric features and expands the channel dimension. The encoder contains three downsampling stages, each of which composes of BiMamba-Conv (BiMC) Blocks followed by a convolution that halves the resolution and doubles the channels. At the bottleneck, BiMC Blocks capture contextual information at the coarsest scale. The decoder mirrors the encoder with transposed convolutions and BiMC Blocks, and incorporates skip connections via element-wise addition to preserve spatial detail. Finally, a 3D convolutional tail projects the features to the output domain.

Mamba

Recently, State Space Models (SSMs)45–48 have emerged as a powerful class of architectures for sequence modeling. Unlike Transformers44, which scales quadratic computational complexity relative to sequence length, SSMs provide linear scaling, making them exceptionally efficient for processing high-resolution volumetric data like cryo-EM density maps. At its core, the SSM framework treats data as a linear continuous-time dynamical system. It utilizes a hidden state, h(t), which acts as a memory to integrate and propagate structural context throughout the spatial sequence, enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies such as backbone connectivity in fragmented maps.

Data collection

In this study, all EM density maps and the corresponding atomic structures in our dataset were downloaded from the EMDB53 and the PDB54, respectively. To build a consensus and high-quality dataset that covers different types of EM density maps, all maps and PDB structures that meet the following criteria were collected: (i) resolution between 2.0–10.0 Å; (ii) reconstructed by single-particle analysis (SPA) or subtomogram averaging (STA); (iii) a unique correspondence between structure and density map; (iv) without any unknown atom/residue; (v) the total number of Cα and C4′ atoms falls within the range of 20–2000; (vi) cross-correlation coefficient calculated by UCSF Chimera55 between the structure and the density map is greater than 0.6; (vii) CC_mask calculated by phenix.map_model_cc56 between the structure and the density map is greater than 0.5. Further manual inspection is conducted to exclude maps that exhibit obvious inconsistencies with the associated PDB model from our dataset.

To remove the redundancy in our dataset, we use MMseqs257 to cluster the samples in our dataset by using a coverage and a sequence similarity thresholds of 0.8 and 0.3 for proteins and 0.8 and 0.8 for nucleic acids, respectively. The clustering results in a non-redundant set of 549 maps, which were then divided into 348 training samples, 65 validation samples, and 136 testing samples. We further divided the test samples into three distinct sets to address different evaluation needs. The first test set comprises all 118 cryo-EM single-particle analysis (SPA) maps with resolutions ranging from 2.0 Å to 10.0 Å. The second test set is a subset of this first test set, consisting of 18 maps with resolutions of 3.0–10.0 Å, where more than 10% of the residues in the corresponding PDB structure are nucleic acids. The third test set focuses on nucleic acid-containing cryo-EM SPA maps and consists of 18 maps with resolutions between 2.0 Å and 7.0 Å. To evaluate the improvement in map interpretability achieved by EMReady2, we extracted all chains from test-set structures with resolutions better than 5.0 Å, yielding a total of 832 chains, including 753 protein chains and 79 nucleic acid chains.

Network training

To train EMReady2, we first resample the grid size of all maps in the training set to 1.0 Å through cubic interpolation. Next, the density values of each map are clipped to the range from 0 to the 99.999th percentile of the entire map. Finally, each pair of experimental and simulated maps is segmented into a series of 3D subvolume pairs using a sliding window of shape 80 × 80 × 80 and a stride of 48. Non-positive subvolumes are excluded to ensure effective training.

EMReady2 employs a BiMamba-Conv-UNet architecture for post-processing. The network consists of three encoder, one bottleneck, and three decoder BiMamba-conv (BiMC) blocks with skip connections. EMReady2 is implemented using PyTorch 2.3.158. Data augmentation is applied during training: input subvolume pairs are augmented by random 90∘ rotations, followed by randomly cropping a smaller subvolume of shape 64 × 64 × 64 from the original 80 × 80 × 80 subvolume.

Evaluations of map quality and interpretability

We conduct comprehensive evaluations of EMReady2 on a total of 136 EM maps. For each density map, we first chunk it into overlapping 3D subvolumes of shape 64 × 64 × 64 with a sliding stride of 24. Subsequently, these subvolumes are fed into the selected EMReady2 model with the lowest validation loss. Finally, the resulting subvolumes are re-assembled into the output post-processed map, where overlapping sections are averaged.

Three metrics are used to measure map quality before and after post-processing. The first is FSC-0.5, the resolution at which the map-model Fourier shell correlation falls to 0.5, calculated via phenix.mtriage56. The third is the real-space map correlation coefficient (CC) calculated by phenix.map_model_cc56, which assesses the similarity between the experimental map (ρexp) and an idealized map generated from atomic coordinates (ρcalc):

Three variants are used: CCbox evaluates the correlation across the entire volume; CCmask focuses on the density within a specified radius (mask) around the atomic model; and CCpeaks emphasizes high-intensity regions. For FSC-0.5, lower values indicate better performance, while higher values are better for all other metrics.

In addition to map quality, we assess the ability of EMReady2 to improve map interpretability. We use phenix.map_to_model11 to build atomic models de novo from map regions segmented within 4.0 Å of the chains in our first test set. Residue coverage and sequence recall, calculated by phenix.chain_comparison, assess the accuracy of the built models relative to the PDB model. Protein residues are represented by Cα atoms, while nucleic acid residues are represented by C4’ atoms. Residue coverage is the proportion of residues in the built model with matched residues (within 3.0 Å) in the PDB model. Sequence recall is the fraction of residues in the built model with correct residue types.

Comparison with related methods
