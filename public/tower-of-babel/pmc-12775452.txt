Rumen metabolic environment of dairy cows varying in susceptibility of subacute ruminal acidosis modifies in vitro bacterial composition and fermentation
Authors: H Yang, S Heirbaut, J Jeyanathan, X P Jing, M Q Zhang, N De Neve, L Vandaele, V Fievez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rumen metabolic environment from either SARA susceptible (S) or unsusceptible (U) cows, was hypothesized to alter the fermentative capacity and bacterial community composition. This was evaluated in vitro through exposure for a period of 24 h of the microbial inoculum of three donor cows to sterile ruminal supernatant obtained from a subset of 15 out of a group of 38 early lactating dairy cows which were either SARA S (n = 8) or U (n = 7) cows at pH 5.8 and 6.8. Compared to exposure to sterile supernatant from S cows, microbiota exposed to sterile supernatant from U cows produced more total volatile fatty acids, irrespective of the in vitro pH (6.8 or 5.8). Specifically, branched-chain volatile fatty acids (BCVFA), such as isobutyrate and total BCVFA are elevated/tended to be elevated in incubations with the sterile supernatant from U cows. Net lactate accumulation in incubations with U cows’ supernatant at pH 6.8 was higher than with S cows’ supernatant. Nevertheless, a lower relative abundance of Firmicutes, uncultured Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008, and Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium, as well as a higher relative abundance of Prevotella was observed after incubation with sterile supernatant from SARA S cows as compared with SARA U cows.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30106-1.Keywords: Subacute ruminal acidosis, Inter-animal variation, Rumen microbiota, Microbial metabolites, In vitro simulationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Microbiology, Physiology

Introduction

Dairy cows differ in susceptibility to develop subacute ruminal acidosis (SARA)1–3, a digestive disorder without overt clinical symptoms but potentially resulting in health problems and production losses4. Although inter-animal variation in SARA is considered a multifactorial and complex trait5–7, its occurrence has been linked to differences in the microbial structure, i.e., increased abundance of specific taxonomic groups8,9, similar to the inter-individual differences in intestinal microbes observed in human phenotypes either or not suffering from gastro-intestinal disorders10,11.

Obviously, variation in metabolic activity of these unique microbial communities might result in the presence (or production) of specific metabolites. Indeed, animal-dependent alterations occur in the rumen metabolome during a SARA-induction protocol12,13. E.g., in the study by Saleem et al.13, an increase in the production of the rumen metabolite ethanolamine was suggested to result from a combination of changes in microbial activity, turnover of epithelial cells, and cell lysis of ruminal microbiota caused by grain feeding. Such metabolites might provide essential growth factors (e.g., branched-chain volatile fatty acids, BCVFA) which facilitate growth and functionality of specific microbial communities14,15, while antimicrobial compounds excreted by microbiota can actively interfere with other species’ growth16,17. For example, ethanolamine can be used by Escherichia coli, as a nitrogen source and thus, could provide a growth advantage over other commensal microbiota, resulting in the proliferation of E. coli18. In support of this, a grain-based SARA induction protocol has been shown to increase the proportion of E. coli19,20 in the rumen. Alternatively, rumen metabolites might also retard the growth of bacteria, as shown for ethanol and Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens21. While metabolites like ethanolamine and BCVFA may influence microbial dynamics13, we focused on lactate, BCVFA and LPS as key indicators due to their documented links to SARA14,22,23. Our hypothesis is that sterile ruminal supernatant from SARA-susceptible (S) and unsusceptible (U) cows differentially alters the microbial composition and metabolic outputs (e.g., VFA, lactate).

This hypothesis was investigated using sterile ruminal supernatant (containing microbial metabolites) obtained from a subset of 15 out of a group of 38 early lactating dairy cows. This subset of cows responded differently to the early lactation diet, rich in rapidly fermentable carbohydrates: the first group of 8 SARA-susceptible cows (S group) experienced a decreased rumen pH and developed SARA, during at least 2 days in the first 3 weeks after calving. Sterile ruminal supernatant has been used from these cows as well as from the second group of 7 animals which were unsusceptible to SARA development (U group). Each of the microbial inocula from 3 rumen fistulated cows, not suffering from SARA, were exposed to the supernatant of each of the S and U cows during in vitro incubations at normal (pH = 6.8) and low (pH = 5.8) pH. After 24 h of incubation, changes in the bacterial composition, as well as volatile fatty acid (VFA) production and lactate accumulation, were assessed. The latter were targeted as a first attempt to assess the effect of the various supernatants on the metabolic capacity of the microbial population.

Results

Lactate, volatile fatty acids, and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in the sterile ruminal supernatant from S and U cows

The fermentation products in the original sterile supernatant collected from either SARA S (n = 8) or SARA U (n = 7) cows are shown in Fig. 1 (a-j). Lactate concentration tended to increase in S cows (Fig. 1b, P = 0.081), whereas the proportion of butyrate tended to increase in U cows (Fig. 1f, P = 0.082). In contrast, no differences were observed in total VFA concentration, the proportions of acetate and propionate, isobutyrate, isovalerate, total minor VFA (isobutyrate, isovalerate, valerate and caproate) and LPS (Fig. 1c, d, e, g, h, i and j; P > 0.05).

Net VFA production and net lactate accumulation after 24 h in vitro incubation

As shown in the schematic diagram (Fig. 2a), microbiota from three dairy cows were exposed in vitro to sterile supernatant of rumen fluid from either SARA S or SARA U cows at pH 5.8 or 6.8. Net lactate accumulation of U cows at pH 6.8 was higher than S cows, whereas no difference was observed between groups when microbiota were exposed to pH 5.8 (Fig. 2c, P interaction = 0.033). Compared to exposure to sterile supernatant from S cows, microbiota exposed to sterile supernatant from U cows produced/tended to produce more total VFA (Fig. 2d, P group = 0.046), isobutyrate (Fig. 2h, P group = 0.050), and total BCVFA (Fig. 2j, P group = 0.075), irrespective of the in vitro pH (6.8 or 5.8). The acetate proportion in total VFA was higher after exposure to supernatant from S rather than U cows in pH 5.8 (Fig. 2e, P interaction = 0.041), whereas the propionate proportion tended to be higher in pH 6.8 (Fig. 2f, P interaction = 0.054). In contrast, no differences were found in pH (Fig. 2b, P group = 0.460), the proportion of butyrate (Fig. 2g, P group = 0.990), isovalerate (Fig. 2i, P group = 0.202) and sum of valerate and caproate (Fig. 2k, P group = 0.339) between groups of sterile supernatant at both pH 6.8 and pH 5.8.

Bacterial community composition before and after in vitro incubation

Figure 3 (b-e) represents the bacterial community composition assessed using qPCR and Illumina sequence after 24 h in vitro incubation when microbiota from 3 donor cows were exposed to sterile supernatant from either S or U cows at pH 6.8 or pH 5.8 (Fig. 3a). Gene copy numbers of bacteria were higher in pH 6.8 than pH 5.8, irrespective of the origin of the sterile supernatant (Fig. 3b, P pH < 0.001). Also, PERMANOVA analysis revealed differences in β-diversity between inoculum before incubation and the bacterial community after incubation at pH 6.8 and pH 5.8 (Fig. 3c, P 6.8 vs. 5.8 < 0.001; P 6.8 vs. inoculum < 0.001; P 5.8 vs. inoculum = 0.004). In contrast, no differences were observed in bacterial β-diversity related to the origin of the sterile supernatant at both pH after incubation (P 6.8_S vs. 6.8_U = 0.808; P 5.8_S vs. 5.8_U = 0.347). Similarly, no differences were found in α-diversity between groups, whereas pH 6.8 differed from pH 5.8 in terms of ASV, Faith_pd, evenness, and Shannon index (Table 1, P pH < 0.001).

Seven genera were influenced by the origin of the sterile supernatant after 24 h incubation at pH 6.8 and/or pH 5.8 (Table 2). Briefly, the relative abundances of uncultured Ruminococcaceae and Firmicutes were higher when microbiota from 3 donors had been exposed over 24 h to sterile supernatant from U cows as compared with sterile supernatant from S cows, irrespective of pH (P group ≤ 0.05). The interaction between pH and supernatant origin significantly influenced the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008, Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium and Prevotella (P group × pH < 0.05). The relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008 and Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium was higher when microbiota were exposed to sterile supernatant from U than from S cows under the pH 5.8 condition (P group × pH = 0.04 and 0.027). In contrast, the relative abundance of genera Prevotella increased when microbiota were exposed to sterile supernatant from S cows than from U cows at pH 5.8 (P group × pH = 0.002). The relative abundance of Streptococcus (P group = 0.071) and uncultured Christensenellaceae (P group = 0.070) tended to be higher after incubation with sterile supernatant from U cows than with supernatant from S cows at both pH levels, whereas Succiniclasticum tended to show relatively lower abundances when bacteria from donors were exposed to sterile supernatant from U cows than to supernatant from S cows at pH 5.8 (P group × pH = 0.087).

Discussion

As ruminal metabolites (e.g., LPS, BCVFA) differ under SARA conditions between SARA S and U cows12,22, our study specifically tested the collective effect of sterile supernatant (containing these and other unmeasured metabolites) from either SARA S cows or U cows on the fermentative capacity and bacterial community composition. This was assessed in vitro using sterile ruminal supernatant from S and U cows, which showed a distinct rumen pH pattern. Furthermore, two in vitro pH conditions were created (i.e., pH 6.8 and 5.8), resulting in a 2 × 2 experimental design. However, results of the in vitro condition at pH 5.8 should be considered with caution as the net total VFA production and changes in the bacterial composition were limited (Bray-Curtis dissimilarities of bacterial composition at low pH after 24 h incubation were clustered with the donor-inoculum, Fig. 3c) after the 24 h incubation. In the current study, net total VFA production at pH 5.8 is much lower than at pH 6.8 (24.5 µmol/flask vs. 203 µmol/flask, respectively). Similarly, in previous in vitro experiments, severe decreases in VFA production have been observed when pH dropped below 6.0 and when these pH conditions were maintained for an extended period24–26, as low pH decreased fiber degradation and fermentation by shifting the microbial community and altering the gene expression of carbohydrate-active enzymes27. Thus, further optimization of in vitro pH circumstances is needed to better represent in vivo SARA conditions e.g., include a shorter incubation time of pH below 6 and the creation of diurnal pH variation.

Microbiota exposed to sterile ruminal supernatant from U cows rather than S cows produced more VFA, irrespective of the in vitro pH. Also, ruminal supernatant from U cows stimulated lactate accumulation compared with S cows at pH 6.8. This might indicate an inhibitory effect of the sterile ruminal supernatant from S cows or inversely, a stimulatory effect of the U cows’ supernatant on the microbial fermentative activity. VFA production and lactate accumulation, as measured in the current study, are major parameters to assess the metabolic capacity, particularly the fermentative capacity. Nevertheless, methane production is another parameter to characterize the fermentative capacity, while rumen microbiota also have other essential functionalities, such as microbial protein production and synthesis of vitamins. As such, the current study only provides a first indication of a potential effect of various supernatants on rumen function. In a similar set-up, Shaani et al. assessed the effect of sterile ruminal supernatant on in vitro methane production14. In that study, sterile ruminal supernatant was collected at two diurnal time points (i.e., morning [06:00, 1 h prior feeding] and evening [17:00, 10 h after feeding]) from cows fed 70% concentrate14. Microbiota incubated with sterile ruminal supernatant from the evening collection produced 40–50% more methane as compared with incubations with morning supernatant, which indicated that diurnal variation in the metabolic environment might affect fermentative capacity, with methane production used as a proxy in that study14. Inhibitory factors in the ruminal metabolic environment of S cows could be biogenic amines or bacterial endotoxins such as LPS19,22. These compounds can actively interfere with the growth and hence functionality of specific microbial communities16,28,29. Biogenic amines have not been analyzed in the current study, while ruminal LPS concentrations on average were more than 10-fold lower in the current study as compared with the study by Khafipour et al. (15400 EU/mL, Fig. 1j vs. 179762 EU/mL)19. This may be related to the milder SARA conditions encountered in S cows in our study as compared with the SARA conditions created in the study by Khafipour et al.18, where cows experiencing SARA had a rumen pH below 5.6 of at least 337 min/d. In our study, a reticular pH of 6.0 (equivalent to rumen pH of 5.8) was taken as SARA-threshold30,31, with half of the S cows experiencing SARA for one week or less during the first 3 weeks in lactation (supplemental Table S1). Although SARA conditions were not necessarily present on the sampling day, reticular pH at that time still differed significantly between groups (average pH: S, 6.11; U, 6.45; time of pH below 6: S, 144 min; U, 0.00 min; P ≤ 0.05). As supported by our data, it is important to emphasize that SARA is a condition that has an impact beyond the days on which SARA was effectively observed. E.g. Dohme et al.32 reported that repeated SARA challenges in lactating dairy cows resulted in progressively more severe acidosis episodes, indicative of a lasting impairment in rumen function. Furthermore, the effect of LPS on ruminal fermentative activity may be systemic, as suggested by the reduction in organic matter degradability of alfalfa hay through intravenous LPS infusion33.

On the other hand, stimulatory growth factors, such as BCVFA, are elevated/tend to be elevated in the sterile supernatant from U cows. BCVFA are important precursors for cellulolytic bacteria and cellulolytic bacteria rely on other bacteria for the provision of BCAA and BCVFA34–36. The increased proportions of isobutyrate and tendency of higher total BCVFA might have stimulated the growth of cellulolytic bacteria, which could have contributed to the increased abundance in the incubations with supernatant of U cows of the genus of uncultured Ruminococcaceae, which are mainly associated with degradation of cellulose36,37. Also, other single metabolites (e.g., lactate38 and endotoxin (i.e., LPS31,39 have been shown to potentially alter bacterial communities in vivo and in vitro. However, we observed no differences in richness and diversity of bacterial communities between microbiota incubated with sterile supernatant from S cows and U cows at both pH conditions which might be due to the relatively mild SARA conditions in the current animal cohort. In contrast, at the phylum and genus level, some differences in the relative abundance of microbiota were observed after 24 h incubations with sterile ruminal supernatant from S and U cows. The relative abundances of the phylum Firmicutes and the genus of uncultured Ruminococcaceae were higher after 24 h in vitro incubation with sterile ruminal supernatant from U cows as compared with supernatant from S cows, irrespective of pH.

Interestingly, the interaction between pH and supernatant origin significantly influenced the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008, Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium and Prevotella (Table 2, P group × pH < 0.05). Previously, the relative abundance of starch-degrading bacteria (e.g., Prevotella spp.) thrives in acidic, high-starch environments Zhang et al. (2022)9,40. In the current study, the relative abundance of the genus Prevotella, containing a large number of known amylolytic bacteria, was higher after adding sterile supernatant from SARA S cows (enriched in SARA-associated metabolites) as compared with SARA U cows under pH 5.8 condition, which might suggest a stimulatory effect of ruminal supernatant from SARA S cows on amylolytic bacteria especially in acidic condition (pH 5.8). Moreover, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008 also showed a relatively higher abundance in incubations with sterile supernatant from U than S cows under pH 5.8. Lachnospiraceae spp. were shown to have the most transcriptional activity in the digestion of forage during the secondary phase of bacterial colonization of feed particles, which is particularly associated with the degradation of (structural) carbohydrates41. This suggests, despite low pH, U-cow supernatant supported cellulolytic bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae spp.), likely due to higher BCVFA (Fig. 2j), which are critical for fibrolytic microbes34. In line with this in vitro study, a lower abundance of cellulolytic bacteria is reported during dietary SARA-induction as well as in SARA susceptible cows19,42. Sterile supernatant from SARA U cows also was associated with the higher relative abundance of Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium, compared with supernatant from SARA S cows. This could be related to a former observation that Anaerovoracaceae bacteria were negatively correlated with tyramine43, one of the main biogenic amines often associated with SARA (sterile supernatant from SARA U and S cows impacts the fermentative activity and bacterial community. However, a limitation of this study is the lack of comprehensive metabolite (e.g., ethanolamine, biogenic amines) profiling beyond lactate, LPS and BCVFA. The specific metabolites and biological mechanisms underlying this modification still need to be clarified in the future. Additionally, in vitro pH circumstances (5.8 vs. 6.8) may not fully replicate in vivo SARA dynamics. In the future, such optimization could include a shorter incubation time of pH below 6 and the creation of diurnal pH variation.

In conclusion, inter-animal variation in reticular pH parameters within a group of 38 cows over the first 3 weeks postpartum allowed to select 2 groups of cows that differed most in SARA susceptibility (i.e., S and U cows). Microbiota exposed to sterile ruminal supernatant from U cows rather than S cows produced more total VFA, irrespective of the in vitro pH. Also, ruminal supernatant from U cows stimulated lactate accumulation compared with S cows at pH 6.8. On the other hand, stimulatory growth factors, such as BCVFA, are elevated/tended to be elevated in incubations with sterile supernatant from U cows. This was accompanied by a lower relative abundance of Firmicutes, uncultured Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-008, and Anaerovoracaceae Mogibacterium, as well as a higher relative abundance of Prevotella in sterile supernatant from SARA S cows as compared with SARA U cows.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets, and pH measurement

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Flanders Research Institute for Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (ILVO), Belgium (EC 2018/329and EC 2014/241). All experimental methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Thirty-eight Holstein-Friesian multiparous dairy cows were included in the monitoring experiment which took place at the research farm of ILVO (Melle, Belgium) from 2 wk before the predicted calving date to 3 wk after calving from March 2019 to October 2020.

The rations were formulated following common practice on dairy farms without the attempt for SARA induction. From 3 wk prior to calving onwards, cows received the same partial mixed ration as the lactating cows supplemented with a dry cow mineral premix (AVEVE, Deinze, Belgium) and on average 1 kg of balanced concentrate per cow per day. The partial mixed ration of the lactating cows was calculated to fulfill the needs of an average adult cow of 650 kg, producing 26 kg of fat-protein corrected milk, and was based on maize silage, pre-wilted grass silage, pressed beet pulp, soybean meal, and balanced compound feed (Table 3). The latter was linearly build-up from d 3 to d 20 in lactation according to the scheme given in Table 4. Cows were given the basal diet as 2 equal offerings at roughly 07:30 and 16:30 h ad libitum and had free access to water. Dry cows and lactating cows were housed separately in a naturally ventilated freestall barn with a slatted floor. Stocking density was always < 1 cow/stall. Cows were milked twice daily, had ad libitum access to roughage and concentrate in the concentrate boxes. Individual feed intake was monitored throughout the trial using roughage intake control (RIC) feeding bins (Insentec, Hokofarm Group), except during the period around calving. Cows had ad libitum access to the RIC bins. During lactation, concentrate intake was monitored at the automatic concentrate providers (Greenfeed, C-Lock Inc.; DeLaval) and in a 2 × 7 herringbone milking parlor (DeLaval). Dairy cows were milked twice daily, at 0530 and 1630 h.

Thus, we switched to smaXtec boluses (smaXtec Animal Care GmbH; n = 20) for subsequent monitoring.

Collection of sterile ruminal supernatant

Collection of rumen samples. On 21 d (+/- 1 d) postpartum, rumen samples were collected via oral-esophageal intubation using a stomach tube 2 h after morning feeding44. This standardized time point was chosen for several reasons. First, given our focus on evaluating the long-term effects of prior SARA conditions on rumen metabolite composition, we considered this interval more appropriate than immediate post-feeding sampling to minimize the influence of short-term postprandial fluctuations. Second, sampling during this period helps reduce inter-animal variation that could arise from differences in individual fermentation dynamics following feeding. Unfractionated rumen contents were collected and immediately filtered through a 1-mm pore size sieve. To maximally prevent contamination with saliva, a florascope was used, with the stomach tube inserted into the ventral ruminal sac according to the method from Geishauser et al.44 to collect rumen fluid. In a single case, the first aliquot was discarded due to visual saliva contamination. After homogenizing, 2 sterile falcon tube of 15 ml was completely filled with rumen fluid and immediately transferred to the lab (within 30 min) in a thermos flask filled with water at a temperature of 39 °C for collection of sterile supernatant.

Obtaining sterile ruminal supernatant from rumen fluid of cows sampled on 21 days postpartum. The method to collect sterile supernatant was based on Shaani et al.14 and the procedures were the same as described by Yang et al.9 unless otherwise specified. Rumen fluid samples collected from cows on 21 d postpartum were centrifuged at 25 °C, 14,000 × g for 15 min for the collection of sterile supernatant. The supernatant was then sterilized by filtering through a series of sterile vacuum filters (0.8, 0.45, and 0.2 μm filters, Sigma-Aldrich, Overijse, Belgium). The filtered supernatant was stored at − 20 °C until use in the in vitro experiment. On the incubation day, the number of aliquots required to experiment was thawed and kept at 39 °C under CO2 flushing. The sterile supernatant was kept in a 39 °C warm water bath under continuous flushing with CO2 until the start of the in vitro experiment.

Group assignment

Twenty cows were selected from the 38 animals, based on pH criteria. For this, mean and median daily pH values were assessed against a pH threshold. A pH threshold of 6.0 was chosen in the current study as the reticular pH is generally 0.2 units higher than the rumen pH30,31. Further, the mean and median duration that pH dropped below 6 was calculated for each cow on a daily basis during the 3-wk postpartum period. Then, the thirty-eight cows were ranked from high to low based on mean and median pH below 6 and the 20 most extreme cows were selected:

Susceptible group (S; n = 10): mean or median time of pH below 6 at least 180 min/d over the first three weeks after calving,

Susceptible group (S; n = 10): mean or median time of pH below 6 at least 180 min/d over the first three weeks after calving,

Unsusceptible group (U; n = 10): median time of pH below 6 = 0 min/d and mean time pH below 6 < 10 min/d over the first three weeks after calving.

Unsusceptible group (U; n = 10): median time of pH below 6 = 0 min/d and mean time pH below 6 < 10 min/d over the first three weeks after calving.

For the comparison of the ‘extreme cows’, it was considered to include bolus type as a co-factor in the model. However, this inclusion did not make a difference as the two bolus types (smaXtec and eCow)) were equally distributed in the S (smaXtec, n = 5 cows; eCow, n = 5 cows) and U groups (smaXtec, n = 5 cows; eCow, n = 5 cows). Daily median and mean time of pH below 6 as well as the number of days with more than 330 min with pH below 6 and mean daily area under the curve of pH below 6.0 during the 3-wk postpartum period are given in Table 5, which indicates both groups differ in all rumen pH-related parameters (P < 0.001). The reticular pH and time of pH below 6 via pH bolus at the same time of rumen fluid collection (d 21 postpartum) showed differences between groups (average pH: S, 6.11; U, 6.45; time of pH below 6: S, 144 min; U, 0.00 min; P ≤ 0.05). Three cows in the S group showed some clinical disease signs (i.e., severe hypocalcemia, severe lameness, and hypocalcemia) on some days during the 21-day monitoring period. The average time of pH below 6 of clinically diseased cows is 370 ± 149 min/d, while other S-cows which were not diseased is 442 ± 161 min/d. For five cows (including one clinically diseased cow), difficulties were experienced in sampling rumen fluid resulting in the collection of a too limited amount of rumen fluid to run the incubations. Accordingly, incubations were carried out with sterile ruminal supernatant from 8 out of 10 cows from the S group and 7 out of 10 cows from the U group (Fig. 4; S, n = 8 and U, n = 7).

Experimental procedures. The 20 most extreme cows, i.e., cows susceptible (S, n = 10) or unsusceptible (U, n = 10) were selected from 38 cows based on mean and median of pH below 6. Then, sterile rumen supernatant was collected from the rumen fluid of these cows sampled on 21 d postpartum.

In-vitro experiment with microbiota from 3 donor cows and sterile ruminal supernatant from S and U cows

An in vitro experiment was performed using inoculum collected from 3 late-lactation cows. The inoculum collected from donor cows was exposed to a buffer solution at pH 5.8 or pH 6.8 and either S cows’ or the U cows’ rumen fluid supernatant (Fig. 4; S, n = 8 and U, n = 7, 15 supernatant × 3 donor inoculum × 2 pH × 3 replicates + 6 negative controls = 276 flasks).

Obtaining microbiota from rumen fluid of late-lactating donor cows. About 500 mL/cow of rumen fluid was collected from three fistulated Holstein cows in late-lactation (312d–371d DIM; 6–7 years old) housed in the same barn. The rumen fluid was immediately transferred to the lab (within 30 min) in a thermos flask filled with water at a temperature of 39 °C for collection of microbiota. These cows were fed 7.2 kg of maize silage (on a dry matter basis) and 6.5 kg of grass silage per day, supplemented with 1.1 kg of concentrate. Additionally, sub-samples of rumen fluid were collected in cryovials and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for microbial analysis. Snap frozen rumen fluid samples were stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. The method to collect microbiota was based on Shaani et al.14 and the procedures were the same as described by Yang et al.9 unless otherwise specified. Accordingly, the collected rumen fluid was filtered through a kitchen sieve, mixed, and centrifuged (200 mL) at 25 °C and 14,000 g for 15 min. The pellets, containing the rumen microbes, were recovered after centrifugation (collected from 200 ml of rumen fluid) and were washed with 200 mL autoclaved buffer, through brief vortexing, and mixing the pellets with buffer through pipette trituration. Afterward, this mixture was centrifuged at 25 °C, 14,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet containing the microbiota was suspended in 66.6 ml of buffer at pH 6.8. The microbiota were thereby concentrated 3.3 folds as compared with the original rumen fluid sample. Oxygen exposure was minimized during all the above-mentioned steps by continuous flushing of CO2.

In-vitro incubation of mixed microbiota and sterile supernatant. The microbes, collected from the three fistulated Holstein late-lactation cows, were incubated with sterile supernatant from the S cows as well as the U cows (Fig. 4).

For each incubation, 3.7 mL of anaerobic MES sodium salt buffer (pH 6.8 or 5.7; per L: 51.59 g 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid, 0.118 g MgCl2.6H2O, 8.30 g NaHCO3, and 0.95 g NH4HCO3) was added to a 25 mL flask, followed 1 mL of anaerobic sterile supernatant from one of the 15 selected cows. After flushing the incubation flasks with CO2, 0.3 mL of the rumen inoculum-buffer mixture was introduced into the flask via needle injection, resulting in a total volume of 5 mL and a 1:4 ratio of (supernatant + microbiota) to buffer. Buffer pH had been adjusted with 6 M of HCl to 6.8 or 5.7. The final pH of the incubation mixture was 5.8 when using the buffer initially adjusted to pH 5.7, and 6.8 when using the buffer at pH 6.8. The samples were incubated in triplicate for 24 h at 39 °C in a shaking incubator (Edmund Bühler TH30; Edmund Bühler GmbH, Hechingen, Germany). Fifty mg maize silage (proximate chemical composition, g/kg DM: crude protein, 67.3; crude fat, 35.5; NDF, 451; ADF, 218; ash, 56.0), lyophilized and stored at ambient temperature was used as a substrate for the incubation.

After 24 h incubation, the flasks were taken out of the incubator and kept in an ice bath to stop the microbial activity. Final pH (HannaInstruments) was measured. Two mL of the incubation fluid was collected for analysis of volatile fatty acids (VFA) and lactate. Additionally, sub-samples of incubation fluid were collected in cryovials and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for microbial analysis. Snap frozen fluid samples were stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction.

Fermentative profiles

One mL of the incubation fluid was collected and acidified with 100 µl of formic acid which contained the internal standard (10 mg 2-ethyl butyric acid/mL formic acid) for analysis of volatile fatty acids (VFA). After 15 min centrifugation at 4 °C and 22,000 × g, the supernatant was filtered, and an aliquot was transferred into a 1.5-ml glass vial. VFA was analyzed through gas chromatography on an HP 7890 A (Agilent Technologies, Diegem, Belgium) equipped with a Nukol column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Supelco Analytical, Bellefonte, PA) with a flame ionization detector45. Lactate concentrations were determined according to Conway46. Net production of VFA and net accumulation of lactate was calculated by subtracting the amount in the sterile ruminal supernatant before incubation from the amount after incubation.

Bacterial community analysis based on 16 S rRNA sequencing

DNA Extraction. A total of 1 mL of both the incubated medium and of the rumen fluid from the 3 donor cows were centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min at 16,000 × g to collect the pellets before genomic DNA extraction by using the repeated bead beating plus column purification (RBB + C) method47. The concentration and quality of extracted DNA were checked in a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (VWR International BVBA, Leuven, Belgium).

Bacterial 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and data mining. Extracted gDNA was submitted to Macrogen Sequencing Service (Macrogen Korea, Seoul, Korea) for library preparation and bacterial 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (V3–V4 region, primers: 344 F and 806R48. Preparation of the amplicons barcoded library was based on the Illumina 16 S metagenomic sequencing library preparation protocol (https://support.illumina.com) and the sequencing was performed using Illumina MiSeq V3-technology (2 × 300 bp).

The amplicon sequencing data were analyzed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2, version 2020.08)49. The sequences were demultiplexed, barcodes were removed, and forward and reverse reads were imported into Qiime2. The DADA2 pipeline was used to detect and correct Illumina amplicon sequences, to remove primers and chimeric reads, and for the assembly into amplicon sequence variants (ASV)50. A further filtering step was performed to remove low abundance sequences with frequencies below 0.01% or present in less than 2 out of all 96 samples (90 incubated media + 6 original inocula). Finally, to normalize the number of sequences per sample, a cut-off value (30114) was chosen based on alpha rarefaction curves for all samples. Taxonomy was assigned using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the Silva database (SILVA Release 138, https://www.arb-silva.de/silva-license-information/)51 at 99% similarity followed by removal of the features of archaea and unassigned taxa.

Four measures of α diversity were calculated in QIIME249: (1) observed ASV, a measure of community richness, (2) Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (Faith_pd), a measure of community richness that incorporates phylogenetic relationships between the features, (3) Evenness (i.e., Pielou’s Evenness), a measure of community evenness, and (4) Shannon index, an indicator of species richness and evenness in community structure. Next to this, β diversity indices between samples were determined in QIIME249 based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity52.

Bacterial population analysis by qPCR

The abundance of the 16 S rRNA gene of total bacteria was quantified by qPCR. The primers used for the qPCR are presented in supplemental Table S2. Primer sets and qPCR conditions used were the same as reported for general bacteria53. The qPCR reactions were assayed in a 12.5 µL reaction mixture containing 6.25 µL of Maxima® SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix (2X) (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA), 1 µL of primer mixture containing 0.5 µM of each primer, DNA (20 ng) and molecular water. Amplification of each target group was carried out in a two-step cycling protocol (StepOneTM Real Time PCR System, Applied Biosystems, CA) with the following program: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, 35 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s (denaturation), 60 °C for 1 min for annealing/extension. The melting curve was built by measuring the fluorescence emissions with increased temperature from 60 to 95 °C with ramps of 0.5 °C every 15 s. Duplicate qPCR quantification was performed on 20 ng of extracted DNA. A plasmid containing a single copy of the targeted gene was used as qPCR standards. The copy numbers in the standards were calculated based on the DNA concentrations determined by the NanoDrop spectrophotometer (VWR International BVBA, Leuven, Belgium). External standards were prepared and used in every qPCR run to enumerate the gene copies in the samples. The absolute quantity of each group of microorganisms was calculated using the respective standards and expressed as corresponding gene copies/mL of sample.

The potential influence of bolus type (eCow bolus, n = 18; smaXtec boluses, n = 20) was included as a co-factor in the data analysis. However, this co-factor was not significant (All P Bolus > 0.05). Thus, it was removed from the final model. Tukey’s post hoc analysis was performed to evaluate the differences among means when the lme4 test indicated a significant two-way interaction. Normality of residuals in the model was assessed via quantile-quantile plots and Shapiro-Wilk’s test, while homogeneity of variances was verified based on Levene-test. When the above assumptions were not met, the nonparametric Scheirer-Ray-Hare extension of the Kruskal-Wallis test was performed in the R (R Core Team, 2020) package rcompanion55,56.

Microbial data. Bacterial sequencing profiles were analyzed in QIIME2 (v. 2020.08) and R (R Core Team, 2020)49,55. Distance-based (Bray-Curtis distances) permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was carried out to check whether bacterial composition varies between groups in QIIME257. Analysis of the composition of microbiomes (ANCOM) tests were run in R (R Core Team, 2020) at phylum and genus level to the first screen which bacterial taxa were differentially abundant between different groups of cows at either pH 6.8 or 5.854,58. Further, the taxa that differed significantly across groups detected by the ANCOM test and α─diversity indices were considered for further analysis in R (R Core Team, 2020)55 by the model described above.

For all tests, a P-value of 0.05 was used to define significance, with trends declared at 0.05 < P-value < 0.10.
