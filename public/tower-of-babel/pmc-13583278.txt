How to Build Health Practitioners Research Capacity: An Umbrella Review
Authors: Victoria Headlam, Jacqueline Penney, Ha Hoang, Elaine Hart, Sarah Prior, Marie-Louise Bird, Suzanne Waddingham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBuilding research capacity, culture and capability (RCCC) among healthcare practitioners is essential for evidence-informed care. No umbrella review has synthesised review-level evidence on RCCC across professions and settings.ObjectiveTo synthesise and critically appraise systematic reviews on documented factors influencing RCCC among healthcare practitioners, and to identify strategies that strengthen these factors within healthcare settings.MethodsMEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus and Cochrane (2005–2025; English) were searched for systematic reviews on RCCC among healthcare practitioners. Reviews underwent two-stage screening and were then appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Data were synthesised thematically and mapped to an ecological model (individual, interpersonal, organisational, community/network, policy/system).ResultsNineteen reviews were included; most were high quality. Barriers included time constraints, limited skills, lack of mentorship, infrastructure gaps, and short-term funding. Strategies spanned multiple levels: education, mentoring, protected time, dedicated roles, partnerships, practice-based research networks, and policy supports such as multi-year funding and streamlined ethics. Evidence on long-term implementation of research capacity building interventions and low-resource settings was limited.ConclusionsRCCC in healthcare settings is shaped by multi-level and interconnected factors, requiring coordinated and context-sensitive strategies to overcome persistent challenges and harness key enablers. Viewing these factors through an ecological lens is a valuable reference for understanding and organising the information gathered about research support initiatives to align them with established research in healthcare frameworks. To ensure sustainable impact, rigorous evaluation systems must be applied to demonstrate the successful adoption of research facilitators and their contribution to strengthening evidence-based practice.RegistrationPROSPERO: CRD420251007398.

Introduction

Building the research capacity, culture and capabilities of healthcare practitioners is essential for advancing evidence-informed practice, improving patient outcomes, and fostering a sustainable culture of inquiry and innovation within healthcare systems.1,2 This process involves the development of individual skills, supportive infrastructure, and organisational culture that enable both practitioners and health systems to conduct, apply, and sustain high-quality research.1,3

Research is broadly defined as the generation of new knowledge or the reinterpretation of existing knowledge in innovative ways to produce fresh insights, concepts, or methods.1,4 Within healthcare, strengthening RCCC supports the production of translational research, which bridges the gap between evidence and practice through innovation, collaboration, and system responsiveness, ultimately benefiting patients, clinicians, and communities.5-7

To fully realise the benefits of research-informed practice, healthcare organisations must demonstrate a sustained commitment to valuing and investing in research. Central to this commitment is the cultivation of a positive research culture, which empowers practitioners to engage meaningfully with research and embeds research activity as a core function of healthcare delivery.8-10 Over the past three decades, several frameworks have been developed to guide organisations in creating and sustaining research-enabling environments.1,8 Despite the availability of these frameworks, the literature consistently highlights that many healthcare professionals lack opportunities to engage in research activities.11-14 Persistent gaps in practitioner engagement suggest that structural and organisational interventions alone are insufficient. A deeper understanding of the multilevel and interconnected factors influencing research participation is needed.

Such insights are critical for informing effective research capacity development strategies and enhancing the impact of translational research within evidence-informed health systems.15-18 This need is reinforced by implementation science literature highlighting that translating evidence into routine practice is often complex, non-linear and context-dependent, rather than a simple or predictable time-lagged process. 19

While an expanding body of systematic reviews has examined frameworks, strategies, and outcomes related to building research capacity among healthcare practitioners, no umbrella review has yet synthesised and critically appraised the barriers, facilitators, and interventions that shape health workers’ engagement in research capacity development.

In this review, ‘research capacity, capabilities and culture’ (RCCC) was used as a core construct and ‘research engagement’ used only to describe behavioural participation in research activities as reported in included reviews.

Aims and Review Questions

This umbrella review aims to synthesise and critically appraise systematic reviews on factors influencing RCCC among healthcare practitioners, and to identify strategies that strengthen these domains within healthcare settings.

The review was guided by two key questions:• What are the barriers and enablers to building RCCC among healthcare workers?• What strategies support the development of RCCC in healthcare?

Methods

This review is an umbrella review, designed to collate and critically appraise evidence from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses to provide a comprehensive synthesis on a defined topic.20,21 This methodology enables evaluation across diverse contexts, clarifies inconsistencies, and informs policy, practice, and investment in research capacity, culture, and capability-building. To guide the development of this umbrella review, the authors followed established methodological guidance from the Johanna Briggs Institute’s Critical Appraisal Tool 22 and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Checklist. 23

A protocol for this umbrella review has been registered with International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) in July 2025 (ID: CRD420251007398).

Inclusion Criteria

Table 2 outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria used to guide the selection of studies for this umbrella review. Only systematic reviews published in English between 2005 and 2025 were considered. The timeframe was set from 2005 onwards to capture contemporary evidence following the publication of an influential framework for research capacity building in healthcare. 1 Eligible studies focused on health care practitioners including those in medicine, nursing, allied health, and broader healthcare roles and addressed topics related to research capacity building, professional development, organisational research culture, and individual research engagement. Reviews that did not pertain to research capacity in health, involved non-health-related populations, or were published outside the specified date range were excluded. Study Selection

All identified citations were imported into the cloud-based screening tool Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/). Two reviewers (VH and JP) independently screened titles and abstracts using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 2). Conflicts were resolved through discussion, with input from the wider authorship team when necessary. Articles deemed potentially eligible at this stage were retrieved for full-text review, and a final set of studies meeting all criteria was retained for data extraction and synthesis.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted using Covidence (Supplemental Table 2) and included information from each included review, including databases searched, aims, number and type of primary studies included, healthcare settings and provider types, geographical and population contexts, identified barriers and enablers to research engagement, and implemented or proposed strategies to support research capacity building.

Three reviewers (VH, JP and HH) independently completed the data extraction process. All extracted data were reviewed for completeness and accuracy. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions, and any unresolved issues were referred to the broader authorship team for clarification.

Data Synthesis

To guide the synthesis and interpretation of findings, this umbrella review applies the ecological model as a conceptual framework.24,25 Widely used in implementation science and health research, the ecological model conceptualises behaviour and capacity as shaped by multiple, interacting levels: individual, interpersonal, organisational, community/network, and policy/system.24,25 This multilevel perspective enables a structured analysis of barriers, enablers, and strategies across diverse healthcare contexts.

A convergent qualitative synthesis with a hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis was conducted to identify and synthesise patterns across the extracted data.26,27 Three reviewers (VH, JP, HH) independently coded the extracted data inductively into broad categories of barriers, enablers and strategies. Differences in coding, interpretation, or theme development were discussed and resolved through consensus, with input from the wider authorship team where required. The reviewers then collaboratively grouped related codes into preliminary descriptive themes.

The agreed themes were subsequently mapped deductively to the five levels of the ecological model. To enhance rigour, analytic decisions were documented throughout the process, and the final themes were checked against the extracted data to ensure they were grounded in the included reviews.

Assessment of Methodological Quality

In this umbrella review, the authors used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses to assess the methodological quality of the included studies (Supplemental Table 3). 28 This checklist comprises 11 items that evaluate key aspects of methodology of a systematic review, including the clarity of the review question, appropriateness of inclusion criteria, adequacy of the search strategy and sources, transparency in data extraction and synthesis, assessment of publication bias, and the relevance of recommendations for practice and future research.

Each item was scored as one point if adequately addressed, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 11 for each review. Based on their total scores, reviews were categorised as low quality (0–4 points), medium quality (5–8 points), or high quality (9–11 points). Two reviewers (HH and JP) independently conducted the appraisal, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus among the review team. Methodological appraisal informed the interpretation of findings rather than study inclusion. Overall and item-level appraisal information was used to contextualise the synthesis and support transparent interpretation across reviews.

Assessment of Overlap

Overlap of primary (index) studies across included systematic reviews was assessed using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA), a validated metric for quantifying overlap in umbrella reviews. 29 A citation matrix was constructed to identify repeated study inclusion. The CCA was calculated using the formula described by Pieper et al (2014) 29 : CCA = (N − r)/(rc − r), where N is the total number of study occurrences, r is the number of unique studies, and c is the number of reviews. Overlap was interpreted using established thresholds: slight (0–5%), moderate (6–10%), high (11–15%), and very high (>15%). These findings informed interpretation of the results and the potential for bias due to overlapping evidence.

Results

Overview of Included Studies

A total of 406 systematic reviews were initially identified for consideration in this umbrella review. After removing duplicates (n = 14), titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to health practitioner research capacity development. This resulted in 51 reviews being assessed in full text against the predefined inclusion criteria. Of these, 32 studies were excluded due to one or more of the following reasons: inappropriate target population, protocol-only publication, absence of relevant outcomes, unsuitable study design, or classification as commentary or editorial material.

Ultimately, 19 systematic reviews met all eligibility criteria and were included in this umbrella review to synthesise the existing evidence on research capacity building among healthcare professionals. Characteristics of the Included Studies

This umbrella review included 19 systematic reviews published between 2006 and 2023. The systematic reviews sourced primary papers from 3 to 12 databases, with a median of 7 databases. CINAHL, Medline, EMBASE, PubMed, and Scopus are the most frequently used databases.

The number of primary studies per review ranged from 6 to 240, with most including between 10 and 45 studies. The reviews collectively examined over 700 primary studies, with methodologies spanning quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods, and case study designs. Most reviews focused on nurses (42%) and allied health professionals (16%) working in hospital, tertiary, or community settings, while others explored RCCC among pharmacists, clinical educators, and postdoctoral scholars. Geographically, the studies covered predominantly high-income countries such as Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada, as well as low and middle-income countries (LMICs), including African and Southeast Asian nations.

The methodological quality of the 19 included systematic reviews (Supplemental Table 3) was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist. The majority of reviews (n = 16) were rated as high quality (scores ≥9), while the remaining three were classified as moderate quality (scores between 6 and 8). No reviews were rated as low quality.

Assessment of Overlap

A total of 633 primary studies were identified across 19 included systematic reviews, with 16 instances of overlapping study occurrences as shown in (Supplemental Table 4). The calculated Corrected Covered Area (CCA) was 2.14%, indicating slight overlap according to established thresholds. 29 This suggests minimal duplication of primary studies across reviews and a low risk of bias arising from overlapping evidence. Accordingly, the influence of overlap on the interpretation of findings was considered to be limited.

Although findings are presented by ecological level, many barriers, enablers and strategies operate across multiple levels. For synthesis, themes were mapped to the primary level of influence, recognising that some factors may be shaped or reinforced at other levels. This approach reflects the interconnected nature of research capacity, culture and capability.

Individual Level

At the individual level, RCCC is shaped by clinicians’ knowledge, skills, attitudes, motivations, confidence and competing role demands. Across reviews, time constraints emerged as the most pervasive barrier.2,31-44 Clinicians frequently reported that research was deprioritised amid heavy clinical workloads, staffing shortages, and administrative responsibilities.31,40-43,45 Even when clinicians held positive attitudes toward research, the absence of protected time (also referred to as dedicated or quarantined time) limited sustained participation.34,35,38

Lack of research skills were also prominent. Reviews highlighted limited methodological knowledge, low confidence in formulating research questions, and difficulty appraising evidence, particularly among early-career clinicians and those with limited prior exposure.36,39,41,44 In LMICs and non-English-speaking contexts, challenges related to digital literacy and language proficiency further constrained access to evidence.41,44

Attitudinal barriers included scepticism about the relevance of research to clinical practice, fear of criticism, and resistance to change.36,41,43 Emotional responses such as anxiety and perceived lack of recognition also undermined motivation. 34 Role conflict, particularly for those juggling clinical, teaching, and administrative duties, was a recurring theme, with postdoctoral clinicians reporting difficulty maintaining research momentum in the absence of strategic support.31,35

Despite these barriers, several enablers were consistently identified. Intrinsic motivation, curiosity, a commitment to improving patient care and a valuing of research and its contribution to evidence-based practice were powerful drivers of engagement, especially when research was perceived as meaningful and professionally affirming.32,34,38,42,45 Structured education, through formal qualifications, short courses, and experiential learning, was linked to increased competence and readiness for evidence-based practice.10,31,36,37 Exposure to credible role models and mentoring relationships reinforced a research-oriented identity and supported career progression.34-36

Recommended strategies included flexible, targeted education (e.g., short courses, online modules), tuition support, and experiential learning embedded in clinical practice. Providing structured opportunities for clinicians to develop research capacity, enabling the enhancement of research skills and abilities to build confidence in dissemination, implementation, and the practical, hands-on experience necessary for producing high-quality research.2,31,33,34,39,40,46 Recognition mechanisms, such as career advancement pathways and incentives, were also highlighted as important levers to translate motivation into sustained engagement.31,36,42,44,45

Interpersonal Level

The interpersonal layer of the ecological model highlighted the influence of mentors, peers, and immediate professional networks on clinicians’ research engagement. These relationships were reported to shape confidence, skill development, and a sense of belonging within research-active communities.

A consistent barrier across reviews was limited access to mentors with research expertise, particularly in clinical environments lacking formal academic–clinical partnerships.31,35,40 In LMICs, this gap was exacerbated by systemic shortages of research leaders and weak institutional linkages. 46 Even where mentorship existed, it was often informal, lacking structure, continuity, and clear objectives. Such arrangements provided limited support for complex tasks like grant writing, ethics approval, and publication, leading to stalled projects and diminished confidence among early-career clinicians.34,41

Weak academic–clinical linkages further constrained opportunities for supervision and collaborative problem-solving. Reviews noted that clinicians in resource-constrained settings were often isolated from research networks and methodological expertise, limiting their ability to initiate or sustain research activity.41,46

In contrast, structured mentoring programs embedded within research institutes or health services were consistently associated with improved research productivity, confidence, and career progression. Programs such as the Summer Nursing Research Institute (SNRI) combined mentorship with leadership development, grant writing, and scholarly publishing, demonstrating measurable gains in research output. 35 Teaching scholar programs and writing groups also provided goal-oriented support for novice researchers. 35 Teaching scholar programs and writing groups also provided goal-oriented support for novice researchers. 31

Peer networks and Communities of Practice (CoP) emerged as powerful enablers. These collaborative structures created safe spaces for shared learning, troubleshooting, and normalising research as part of professional identity. CoPs facilitated sustained engagement beyond individual projects and fostered a culture of inquiry within clinical teams.38,47 Peer mentoring models were particularly effective in reducing isolation and enhancing motivation, especially for clinicians in geographically dispersed or resource-limited settings. 35

Strategies to strengthen interpersonal supports included formalised mentorship structures with clear matching processes and accountability mechanisms, near-peer mentoring models that foster reciprocal learning, and embedded CoPs within clinical services. Academic–clinical co-supervision arrangements helped bridge gaps between theory and practice, while distance mentoring and structured writing groups provided flexible support for clinicians in dispersed settings.31,38,46

Organisational Level

The organisational layer captures how institutional structures, leadership, resources, and processes shape the conditions under which clinicians engage in research. Reviews consistently identified modifiable organisational barriers alongside enablers and strategies that embed research within core service delivery.

Infrastructure and resource constraints were among the most frequently cited barriers. Clinicians reported limited access to essential supports such as research databases, statistical software, libraries, administrative assistance, and dedicated research staff.31,33,41,44 Without these foundational resources, even motivated individuals struggled to progress projects beyond the conceptual stage.

Leadership gaps and strategic misalignment further hindered engagement. In organisations where research was not visibly prioritised or integrated into strategic plans, it was perceived as peripheral to core business. This lack of endorsement reduced uptake of evidence-based initiatives and weakened the diffusion of research practices across teams.10,42,43,45

Workforce pressures including staff shortages, high turnover, and siloed workflows meant that clinical demands often displaced research time. These pressures also limited opportunities for interdisciplinary collaboration and team-based inquiry.33,38,41 Additionally, complex and opaque administrative processes, such as ethics approvals and grant management, created friction and discouraged participation, particularly among novice investigators.44,46

Conversely, visible leadership and a research-positive culture were strong enablers. When managers and executives actively endorsed research and aligned it with service goals, clinicians were more likely to engage in scholarly activity. Recognition mechanisms and leader role-modelling reinforced research as a valued component of professional practice.10,40,45

Embedding dedicated research roles, such as facilitators, champions, and managers within clinical services provided hands-on support and mentorship, improving project delivery and research output.33,42,45 Academic affiliations, including conjoint appointments and co-funded positions, extended supervision capacity and created clearer pathways for education and project pipelines.31,38

Effective strategies included formal allocation of protected time through flexible rostering and funded backfill, which was consistently identified as essential for translating intent into action.35,37,38 Small-grant schemes and seed funding supported project initiation and signalled institutional commitment and supported project initiation and signalled institutional commitment.35,37,38 Establishing centralised research offices with digital portals and standard operating procedures streamlined ethics and grant processes, reducing administrative burden.44,46

Culture-building activities, such as journal clubs, internal research days, and seminar series reinforced a shared identity around inquiry and facilitated diffusion of good practice across departments.2,45 Institutions that implemented integrated capability-building programs combining education, mentorship, and peer learning reported stronger research culture and sustained engagement.38,47

Community/Network Level

The community/network layer reflected the role of inter-institutional collaboration, professional linkages, and supra-organisational structures in enabling research engagement. These partnerships extended beyond individual organisations and were shown to be critical for resource sharing, sustainability, and scaling of capacity-building initiatives.

A key barrier identified across reviews was institutional siloing. Health services often operated in isolation, limiting opportunities for interdisciplinary research and knowledge exchange.33,46 This fragmentation was particularly detrimental for smaller or rural services, where access to mentorship, methodological expertise, and infrastructure was already constrained.33,46 This fragmentation was particularly detrimental for smaller or rural services, where access to mentorship, methodological expertise, and infrastructure was already constrained. In LMICs, weak national coordination and the absence of regional research networks further restricted collaboration and perpetuated reliance on externally driven projects. 46

Limited visibility of external research opportunities and inconsistent communication channels also hindered engagement. Clinicians frequently lacked awareness of available networks or mechanisms to connect with academic partners, resulting in missed opportunities for collaboration and professional development.32,40

In contrast, formal academic–practice partnerships were consistently identified as critical enablers. These partnerships provided access to supervision, methodological expertise, and infrastructure, while creating pathways for joint appointments and shared governance.31,38,45 PBRNs emerged as particularly effective models, especially in pharmacy and primary care contexts. They also facilitated co-production of research, continuing education, and access to clinical tools and mentorship, thereby reducing isolation and enhancing sustainability. 32

Regional and international networks extended the reach of research initiatives, supported resource pooling, and promoted standardisation of capacity-building approaches. These networks were especially valuable in LMICs, where local infrastructure was limited and external collaboration provided essential scaffolding for research development. 46

Recommended strategies included the development of formal partnership agreements with clear roles and governance arrangements, joint appointments to strengthen integration of research into clinical practice, and active participation in PBRNs. Knowledge-sharing platforms, such as online portals, newsletters, and webinars, were also highlighted as effective tools to improve visibility and foster engagement across sectors. 40

Policy/System Level

At the system level, governance, funding, regulation, and national coordination shaped the enabling conditions for research capacity. Reviews highlighted fragmented governance, especially in LMICs as a major barrier, with weak national coordination and ad hoc priority setting undermining continuity and alignment.39,46

Short-term, donor-driven funding models limited sustainability, often ending before capacity could be institutionalised. Under-investment in infrastructure, such as data systems and research offices, further constrained scale-up and access to evidence. 46 Slow ethics approvals and lack of digital systems discouraged novice investigators and delayed project start-up.33,46 Furthermore, externally led research agendas with limited local ownership reduced relevance and uptake, while weak knowledge translation (KT) ecosystems and policy-maker capacity hindered evidence use in practice.43,46

In contrast, national research agendas, long-term funding, and strengthened governance were associated with more sustainable gains. Systems approaches that linked macro policy to institutional arrangements and workforce development were recommended.2,46 KT platforms, digital ethics systems, and equitable partnerships such as PBRNs enabled broader engagement and resource sharing.31,32,40

Key strategies included establishing coordinating bodies, shifting to multi-year funding, streamlining ethics processes, building policy-maker capacity, and incentivising locally led collaborations. Monitoring frameworks and national dashboards supported transparency and iterative learning.40,46

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review to synthesise evidence on barriers, enablers, and strategies influencing research capacity, culture, and capability among healthcare practitioners across all five levels of the ecological model. The synthesis of 19 systematic reviews revealed that barriers and enablers operate at multiple and interacting levels. At the individual level, time constraints and limited research skills were the most pervasive barriers, while intrinsic motivation and structured education emerged as key enablers. This is supported by a recent review on evidence-based practice uptake by primary care staff 48 which reported that staff generally viewed research useful but optional and were impeded by time and funding. The review also highlighted a need for training and organisational support that enables clinicians to conduct research. Even when clinicians express positive attitudes toward research, the absence of protected time limits sustained engagement. 49 Limited research skills and confidence, particularly in formulating research questions and appraising evidence, further constrain participation. 12

Mentorship and peer networks emerged as critical enablers in this umbrella review. Structured mentoring programmes and CoPs not only build methodological and research skills but also normalize research as part of routine clinical roles by creating shared expectations, governance, and interactional routines that facilitate knowledge exchange and translation.12,50 The observed prominence of near-peer mentoring maps onto a growing evidence base that it offers scalable, context-sensitive pathways to capability building, delivering dual benefits to mentors and mentees and adapts effectively in resource-limited settings through hybrid/virtual formats and peer supports. 51

Leadership commitment, demonstrated by informed action such as allocating protected time, embedding research roles and aligning organisational priorities with research goals, is a decisive factor for embedding research in routine practice at the organisational level. This is consistent with implementation science, where the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research repeatedly identifies leadership engagement and available resources among the strongest determinants of implementation success.52,53 Conversely, infrastructure gaps and administrative burden (e.g. complex, duplicative processes) continue to slow progress, reflecting mismatched organisational readiness despite capacity-building efforts. 54 Further, academic–practice partnerships and PBRNs expand access to expertise and infrastructure, mitigate isolation for rural and remote clinicians, and support co-production and implementation of research. 55 Finally, at Policy/System level, fragmented governance and short-term funding cycles undermine continuity and scale-up, whereas national research agendas, multi-year investment, and streamlined ethics processes are system-level enablers.

The findings of this umbrella review strongly align with the framework for evaluating research capacity building (RCB) in healthcare. 1 The six principles outlined by Cooke including developing skills and confidence, supporting research close to practice, fostering linkages and partnerships, ensuring appropriate dissemination, promoting sustainability, and investing in infrastructure were consistently reflected across the ecological levels examined in this umbrella review. 1 For example, barriers such as limited research skills, time constraints, and infrastructure gaps were matched by strategies that built individual capabilities, strengthened mentorship and peer networks, and embedded research roles within organisations. Similarly, the emphasis on academic–practice partnerships, PBRNs, and policy-level coordination directly supports Cooke’s multi-level approach to capacity building. By applying the ecological model, this review reinforces the interconnectedness of individual, organisational, and systemic factors in shaping research capacity, culture, and capability. Results from this umbrella review provides empirical support for the Cooke framework as a guiding structure for planning and evaluating RCB initiatives.

These results suggest that effective strategies should be implemented as multi-level bundles that combine individual capability building (short courses, near-peer mentoring) with interpersonal supports (structured mentoring, CoPs), organisational enablers (protected time, a dedicated support role that acts as a single point of contact for clinicians engaging in research), and network activation (academic–practice partnerships, PBRNs), while policy-level actions (multi-year investment, streamlined ethics) remove system barriers. Sequencing quick-start enablers (near-peer mentoring, writing groups, protected-time pilots) alongside partnership and ethics improvements will generate early outcomes, build momentum, and support scale-up, particularly in rural or resource-constrained settings where hybrid/virtual models can sustain mentoring and collaboration.

Future research should prioritize rigorous, theory-informed evaluations of multi-level strategies to build research capacity across diverse health professions and settings. Longitudinal and experimental designs could be used to evaluate the sustainability and causal impact of interventions such as mentoring models, leadership programs, and integrated frameworks informed by learning theories. Future research could also assess the robustness of measurement tools for research capacity, competence, and performance, and examine their links to patient and system outcomes. Greater attention is required to equity and context, including underrepresented disciplines (e.g., allied health, pharmacy) and low-and middle-income countries, where evidence remains sparse. Research should also explore mechanisms of influence, such as motivational factors, organisational culture, and policy levers, and evaluate the scalability of strategies like protected time, digital mentorship, and PBRNs. Finally, future work should incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses and implementation science approaches to inform sustainable, system-wide capacity strengthening.

This umbrella review has several limitations. First, it relied exclusively on systematic reviews published in English, which may introduce language bias and limit the inclusion of evidence from non-English-speaking contexts. Second, the 19 systematic reviews varied in scope, populations, healthcare settings, and definitions of research capacity and culture. This heterogeneity limited our ability to conduct quantitative synthesis and required reliance on thematic analysis, which may reduce comparability across contexts. Third, some primary studies were likely included in multiple systematic reviews, which could have amplified certain findings. Fourth, the review was restricted to systematic reviews, consistent with umbrella review methodology.20,21 However, excluding other forms of evidence synthesis, such as scoping may have limited the conceptual breadth of the findings. This is particularly relevant in research capacity building, where scoping reviews often map frameworks, intervention components and emerging models of practice that may not yet have been evaluated through systematic review evidence. Finally, although some reviews included studies from low- and middle-income countries and rural contexts, the evidence base remains dominated by high-income settings. This limits the generalisability of findings to resource-constrained environments. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and their applicability to diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This umbrella review highlights that challenges such as time constraints, limited research skills, and organisational barriers persist across settings, while enablers include leadership support, structured education, mentorship, and academic–practice partnerships. Strategies to strengthen research capacity are most effective when implemented as multi-level approaches spanning individual, organisational, and system domains. However, evidence remains uneven, with gaps in low-resource and rural contexts, limited evaluation of long-term sustainability, and a lack of robust outcome measures. Addressing these gaps through coordinated, context-sensitive and sequenced multi-level interventions, alongside rigorous and longitudinal evaluation will be critical to embedding a sustainable research culture within healthcare systems.
