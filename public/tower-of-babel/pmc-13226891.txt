Intensification of lupin protein solubilization using high-power ultrasound
Authors: Paola Navarro-Vozmediano, Jose Benedito, Rubén Domínguez-Valencia, Maria Teresa Blay, Jose V. Garcia-Perez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lupin’s nutritional value can be reduced by anti-nutritional factors (ANF), including alkaloids and saponins, as well as by anti-technological factors (ATF), such as fat and polyphenols. This study investigated the use of high-power ultrasound (HPU) during alkaline solubilization of lupin flour with the aim of reducing ANF and ATF while enhancing protein yield and techno-functional properties of lupin protein isolates (LPI). For that purpose, the influence of time (15–30–45 min), temperature (30–45-60°C) and HPU amplitude (0–50-100%) was assessed. The impact of lupin flour (LF) addition to the alkalinized water and temperature on the ultrasonic field was evaluated from frequency spectra analysis. HPU enhanced lupin protein solubilization at elevated temperatures, resulting in higher extraction and protein yields (avg. 6 and 3%, respectively), compared to conventional alkaline solubilization without HPU at 60°C. This effect was consistent with the temperature-dependent cavitation behavior observed in LF dispersions. In addition, HPU also reduced the content of fat (avg. 47%), saponins (avg. 10%) and polyphenols (avg. 17%), and enhanced techno-functional treats, including water absorption (avg. 23%), foaming stability after 2 h (avg. 68%), emulsifying activity index (avg. 18%) and emulsifying stability index (avg. 119%), compared to non-sonicated conditions. High-amplitude HPU application (∼100%), combined with elevated temperatures (∼60°C) and prolonged solubilization times (∼45 min) were identified as the most suitable conditions to enhance protein recovery and techno-functional properties while reducing undesirable components. These results indicate that HPU-assisted solubilization represents a promising and efficient strategy for the sustainable production of high-quality LPI suitable for industrial applications.

Introduction

In recent decades, dietary patterns in developed societies have shifted towards a high consumption of animal-derived proteins. Although these proteins are valued for their nutritional quality, their large-scale production entails considerable environmental issues, including substantial greenhouse gas emissions, excessive demand for arable land and freshwater resources and growing ethical and sustainability concerns. These challenges have intensified the search for alternative and more sustainable protein sources. Among plant-based proteins, soybean remains the dominant raw material in the global market [14]. However, its production is linked to important environmental limitations, including land-use change, soil fertility loss and intensive input requirements [41]. In this context, Lupinus luteus has recently emerged as a promising alternative crop due to its high protein concentration (approximately 40%) and its comparatively lower impact on the environment [30]. Despite these advantages, lupin seeds are known to possess several anti-nutritional factors (ANF), including alkaloids, saponins and polyphenolic compounds, which may impair protein digestibility and bioavailability by forming complexes with proteins. Additionally, anti-technological factors (ATF), such as residual lipids and polyphenols, can adversely affect protein purity, color, oxidative stability, processing behavior and extraction performance [7], [48].

Protein isolates from plant materials are commonly obtained using wet extraction approaches, which usually involve alkaline solubilization, isoelectric precipitation and subsequent drying of the recovered protein fraction [5]. Nevertheless, traditional extraction techniques frequently do not effectively remove ANF and ATF, which may persist in the isolates or, in some cases, become concentrated based on the processing conditions used [33]. As a result, both the nutritional value of the product and the efficiency of protein extraction and recovery are negatively affected.. To address these limitations, different strategies have been explored. Optimization of traditional extraction parameters, such as pH, temperature, extraction time and type of solvent, has been shown to improve protein yield and quality [11]. In parallel, innovative processing technologies, including enzyme treatments [42], microwaves [40]or pulse electric fields[53] have attracted increasing interest due to their ability to intensify mass transfer, enhance protein solubilization and reduce the co-extraction of undesirable compounds. These emerging technologies can alter the structure of proteins, leading to changes in their techno-functional properties, including emulsifying capacity, foaming behavior and water and fat absorption, key attributes for the application of protein isolates in complex food matrices [25], [38], [58].

High-power ultrasound (HPU) has proven to be an efficient and innovative extraction technology. By applying acoustic waves at frequencies typically between 20 and 40 kHz, HPU induces cavitation and intense microagitation, which can disrupt cell structures and enhance mass transfer [6], [9], [66]. The use of HPU has become a widely adopted strategy to extract ANF and ATF from diverse plant matrices. This approach has been applied to recover saponins from lentils, fenugreek and lupin [37], alkaloids from lupin [1], polyphenols from red beans [67], antioxidant compounds from chickpeas [18]and lipids from rapeseed flakes [43]. Navarro-Vozmediano, Dalmau, et al. [35] explored the use of HPU as a pretreatment for lupin flour before alkaline protein extraction, with the aim of minimizing ANF and ATF in the resulting protein isolate. HPU has also been explored as a downstream strategy to enhance techno-functional properties of plant proteins. For instance, HPU has been reported to improve foaming capacity and stability of millet protein concentrate [38]and foaming and emulsifying properties of soybean isolates [21].

Related to HPU-assisted protein solubilization, several studies have shown that ultrasound can modify protein yield and techno-functional properties of different plant sources including pequi almond [51], rice bran [31], pea (F. [60], pumpkin seeds [55], walnut [17], moringa seeds [15], Malaysian ginseng [13]and ganxet beans [26]. Although HPU-assisted extraction has demonstrated high efficiency for recovering proteins from various plant sources, studies on lupin remain limited. To date, the only study addressing this matrix has only focused on extraction yield and alkaloid reduction [1]. To our knowledge, no study on HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization of lupin has evaluated other ANF and ATF such as saponins, polyphenols, fat content and antioxidant activity, nor assessed the techno-functional properties of the resulting isolates. The effects of ultrasound on process intensification depend strongly on the amount of energy transmitted to the medium, which is influenced not only by process parameters such as temperature, extraction time, and solvent type, but also by the physicochemical properties of the medium, including viscosity and density [2]. However, most studies report HPU effects without quantitatively characterizing the ultrasonic field, limiting the understanding of how process conditions affect protein solubilization. Several approaches can be used to characterize the acoustic field: calorimetry, widely applied in previous studies [2], estimates the total energy delivered to the medium, whereas hydrophone measurements could provide more detailed information on cavitation activity and local energy distribution. To date, only Navarro-Vozmediano, Dalmau, et al. [35] has applied hydrophone measurements for ultrasonic field characterization in food-related processes. In this context, the present study investigates HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization of lupin flour as a strategy to maximize protein yield, reduce the content of ANF and ATF and enhance the techno-functional properties of the resulting protein isolates. Additionally, this study aims to characterize the ultrasonic field to evaluate the relationship between delivered acoustic energy and HPU effects on lupin protein.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Seeds of Lupinus luteus L. var. Tremosilla, a yellow lupin variety, were purchased from a commercial supplier (Semillas Batlle S.A, Spain). The seeds were milled in a 22 kW vertical hammer mill (Sitem-gran Ibérica S.L., Spain) to yield flour with particle size between 200 and 1000 µm. To avoid loss of material, sieving was omitted, as the experimental design targeted a closer approximation to large-scale industrial processing.

Protein isolation

Alkaline solubilization

Fig. 1 depicts the experimental setup used for isolating proteins from lupin. The procedure was adapted from Domínguez et al. [11]. In brief, 40 g of lupin flour (LF) was combined with 320 mL of distilled water and homogenized at 12000 rpm for 5 min using an Ultra-Turrax DI 25 Basic (IKA, Staufen, Germany). The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 10.3 with 2 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) to enhance protein solubility under alkaline conditions. Protein extraction was carried out under different times (15, 30 and 45 min), temperatures (30, 45 and 60°C) and HPU amplitudes (0, 50 and 100%), as defined by the BBD. HPU treatments at 45 and 60°C may be considered as thermosonication due to the simultaneous application of high power ultrasound and mild heating. HPU-assisted treatments were performed using an ultrasonic sonotrode (UP400St, Heilscher Ultrasound Technology, Germany) fitted with a 2 cm diameter tip and operating at 24 kHz. Solubilization treatments without HPU were conducted with a mechanical stirrer (RZR 2021, Heidolph, Germany) set at 952 rpm. To maintain precise temperature conditions, all extractions were carried out in a closed 600 mL jacketed vessel connected to a chiller-heating system (Model 89202–974, VWR, Pennsylvania, United States).Fig. 1Scheme of protein extraction.

Isoelectric precipitation

After finishing the alkaline solubilization treatments, all samples were processed identically. Each mixture was centrifuged (Digicen 21 R, Orto Alresa, Madrid, Spain) at 4053 xg for 10 min and the supernatant was filtered through a Büchner funnel using Filterlab-1238 filter paper (20–25 µm pore size). Protein precipitation was then induced by adjusting the pH to the isoelectric point (4.7) with 4 N HCl (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). The suspensions were magnetically stirred for 30 min before a second centrifugation under identical conditions (4053 x g for 10 min). The isolated lupin proteins (LPI) were then freeze-dried (LyoMicron, Coolvacuum, Barcelona, Spain) for 48 h to achieve a final moisture content near 7% w.b. and stored at −26°C. Chemical analysis

The determination of protein, fat, polyphenol, saponin and alkaloid contents, along with the assessment of antioxidant activity, was performed according to the methodologies outlined by Navarro-Vozmediano, Bou, et al. (2025).

Water and fat absorption indexes

The water and fat absorption indexes (WAI and FAI) were determined according to the procedure described by Lian et al. [29], incorporating slight adjustments based on Navarro-Vozmediano, Bou, et al. (2025).

Results and discussion

Effect of temperature and flour dispersion on the ultrasonic field

Acoustic pressure measurements following the experimental set-up (Fig. 2) described in Section 2.4 were used to analyze the effect of temperature and flour addition in the alkalinized water medium. As shown in Fig. 2B, increasing temperature significantly (p < 0.05) reduced all acoustic-pressure components measured in alkalinized water. As an illustrative example, when temperature increased from 30 to 45°C, P0, Ps, Pt and PT were reduced from 41.7, 26.2, 22.2, 54.2 to 24.9, 11.7, 9.4, 29.3 kPa, respectively. This reduction is illustrated in Fig. 2C and 2D for the acoustic pressure linked to transient cavitation and the total acoustic pressure, respectively. The negative effect of temperature on acoustic pressure was also reported in a previous work by Navarro-Vozmediano, Dalmau, et al. [35]. Although cavitation inception is generally favored at higher temperatures due to the increase in vapor pressure, bubble collapses become less violent and more damped, which ultimately reduces the acoustic pressure transmitted through the medium and detected by the sensor. In addition, as the number of cavitation bubbles increases, the resulting bubble cloud can scatter and absorb part of the incident acoustic energy, thereby reducing the transmitted pressure, an effect known as bubble-cloud shielding [59]. Collectively, these effects contribute to a reduction in measured acoustic pressure. The effect of temperature on acoustic pressure in the LF dispersion differed from that observed in alkalinized water. At low temperatures, the limited available energy strongly constrains cavitation bubble formation, as reflected by the low acoustic pressures measured for both stable and transient cavitation (Fig. 2B). At intermediate and high temperatures, however, the elevated vapor pressure promotes bubble formation even at low acoustic energy, resulting in increased acoustic pressures, particularly for transient cavitation, as shown in Fig. 2C.

The addition of the flour to the alkalinized water to prepare the LF dispersion involved a large reduction in all acoustic pressure components, as observed in Fig. 2B, 2C and 2D. Notably, PT, averaged over the studied temperature range, significantly (p < 0.05) decreased from 35.1 to 17.1 kPa, when LF was added to the medium. The addition of the flour increases the content of both soluble and non-soluble solids, which leads to a larger attenuation due to reflection, scattering and energy absorption. This results are consistent with Navarro-Vozmediano, Dalmau, et al. [35] who reported a reduction of 66 and 48% for Pt and PT, respectively, when LF was added to water. Particle size may have a significant effect on the acoustic field, although this effect is often difficult to assess, since it also depends on the solid–liquid ratio used. In the case of smaller particles, the release and dispersion of soluble compounds may be enhanced, potentially increasing the viscosity of the medium and, consequently, ultrasonic attenuation. Conversely, larger particles may enhance scattering phenomena, also contributing to acoustic energy losses. Therefore, it is difficult to predict a priori how the use of particles with a different size would have affected the acoustic pressure. This again highlights the need for an appropriate characterization of the acoustic field under the actual experimental conditions, as carried out in the present study.

The ultrasonic attenuation and velocity measurements used to characterize alkalinized water and flour dispersions are shown in Fig. 2B. Ultrasonic velocity was influenced by both temperature and LF addition. For alkalinized water, increasing the temperature from 30 to 60°C raised the velocity from 1486 to 1522 m/s, a trend that was similarly observed in the LF dispersion. In addition, LF incorporation increased ultrasonic velocity at all temperatures, consistent with the higher solids content; for instance, at 30°C the velocity increased from 1486 m/s in alkalinized water to 1500 m/s in the LF dispersion.

Regarding the ultrasonic attenuation coefficient in the alkalinized water, a slight decrease with increasing temperature was observed, which is consistent with the lower intrinsic absorption of ultrasound in water at higher temperatures. Accordingly, attenuation decreased from 0.87 dB/cm at 30°C to 0.84 dB/cm at 60°C (Fig. 2B), in agreement with previous reports [44]. This trend contrasts with the behavior of acoustic pressure in the ultrasonic bath (Fig. 2C and 2D), likely because attenuation was measured under near-linear propagation conditions (1 MHz), whereas bath acoustic pressure is strongly influenced by non-linear effects, particularly cavitation and bubble-cloud shielding. Attenuation coefficient could not be measured in the LF dispersion, as secondary echoes of the direct wave were undetectable, further illustrating the high attenuation of LF dispersions, consistent with the acoustic pressure measurements.

Effect of HPU-assisted solubilization on the protein extraction

Extraction yields (EY) of LPI ranged from 16.6 (30 min-45°C-50%) to 18.5 g/100 g LF (30 min-60°C-100%), with an average of 17.5 g/100 g LF (Table 3). These values were lower than those reported for the same lupin variety in previous studies, where extraction yields of 23.2 g/100 g LF [11]and 22.4 g/100 g LF [36]were achieved. However, it should be noted that those studies used conventional extraction methods with longer solubilization times of 90 min under continuous agitation. Time, temperature and HPU amplitude showed no significant (p > 0.05) effects on LPI yield. Only, the interaction between temperature and HPU amplitude had a significant (p < 0.05, Table 5) influence, as illustrated in Fig. 4A. Thereby, when HPU is not applied, the higher the temperature, the lower the LPI yield. This effect could be attributed to the influence of high temperatures, which may reduce protein solubilization by promoting protein aggregation and partial unfolding, as well as disrupting hydration interactions that help maintain protein solubility, ultimately limiting extraction [24]. A similar effect has been reported for lupin protein extraction assisted by moderate electric fields, where higher temperatures reduced extraction efficiency [34]. Protein content (PC) of LPI ranged from 77.5 (45 min-60°C-50%) to 82.5 g/100 g LPI (45 min-45°C-0%), with an average of 80.4 g/100 g LPI (Table 3). No significant (p > 0.05) effects of time, temperature or HPU amplitude were observed and none of their quadratic or interaction effects were significant (p > 0.05, Table 5). Similar findings were reported by Ribeiro et al. [51], who observed that HPU alkaline extraction of pequi almond did not result in significant (p > 0.05) differences in PC compared to conventional alkaline extraction. In contrast, significant enhancements in PC have been reported for HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization of grass pea [64], walnut [17]and pea (F. [60], compared to conventional alkaline solubilization. These differences are likely attributable to the type of plant material and the specific process variables applied, including solid-to-liquid ratio and sample size, which strongly influence ultrasonic attenuation and extraction efficiency.

Total protein yields (PY), which combines the above mentioned LPI extraction yield and its protein content, ranged from 13.4 (30 min-45°C-50%) to 14.9 g/100 g LF (30 min-30°C-0%), with an average of 14.1 g/100 g LF (Table 3). A quadratic HPU effect was found (p < 0.01, Table 5), indicating that PY increased with increasing ultrasonic amplitude (Fig. 4B). Moreover, interactions of HPU amplitude with time and temperature also presented significant effects (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). PY was noticeably reduced at higher temperatures when sonication was not applied, whereas at 100% HPU amplitude, temperature had a positive effect on PY (Fig. 4C). As for solubilization time, PY remained fairly constant without sonication but decreased at high temperatures under 100% HPU amplitude when treatment time was extended (Fig. 4B). Prolonged ultrasound exposure could break covalent bonds and induce aggregation, which would reduce protein solubility and, consequently, the PY of LPI [31], [49]. Ly et al. [31] and Sert et al. [55] stated that prolonged and excessive HPU application during alkaline solubilization significantly (p < 0.05) decreased PY for defatted rice bran and pumpkin seeds, respectively. Similar effects have been observed in soybean [19]and black bean [23]isolates, where extended HPU treatment time reduced protein solubility.

Impact of HPU-assisted solubilization on ANF and ATF

Fat content

Fat content (FC) of LPI ranged between 2.28 (45 min-45°C-100%) to 7.20 g/100 g dm (30 min-30°C-0%) (Table 3). The HPU application exhibited a linear negative effect on FC (p < 0.01, Table 5), while the temperature exhibited a quadratic relationship (p < 0.05, Table 5). In the case of HPU, increasing the applied amplitude led to lower FC, likely due to the higher acoustic pressures at elevated amplitudes promoting more intense cavitation. In the case of the temperature, FC reduction is promoted at 45°C compared to 30 and 60°C. Thereby, in order to obtain LPI with low FC, HPU-assisted solubilization of the flour has to be conducted at high HPU amplitude and mild temperature (Fig. 4D). Under these conditions, FC might show values close to 1.33 g/100 g dm at 41.2 min (Table 2), which represents a noticeable reduction compared to 7.20 g/100 g dm achieved at 30 min-30°C-0% (Table 3). Conversely, other studies reported no significant (p > 0.05) effect of HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization on FC for pea[60]and walnut[17]. According to Prempeh et al. [45], cavitation generated by ultrasound promotes the formation of lipid droplets through acoustic destabilization. Accordingly, it is plausible that subsequent centrifugation prior to protein precipitation removes these droplets, thereby reducing the FC in the final LPI.

Polyphenols content and antioxidant activity

Total polyphenol content (TPC) of LPI ranged between 0.74 (30 min-60°C-100%) to 2.15 mg gallic acid/g dm (30 min-30°C-0%) (Table 3) and was largely affected by solubilization conditions. Thus, time, temperature and HPU had a linear effect (p < 0.05, Table 5) on TPC. The most intensive solubilization conditions (high temperature, long treatment time and high HPU amplitude) resulted in the lowest TPC, which could reach values as low as 0.63 mg gallic acid/g dm (Fig. 4E, Table 2). Antioxidant activity (AA) of LPI ranged between 3.40 (30 min-60°C-100%) to 4.66 µM Trolox/g dm (15 min-45°C-0%) (Table 3) and it was only significantly (p < 0.05) affected by solubilization time (Table 5). No significant (p > 0.05) effects of temperature or HPU were observed (Table 5). These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that HPU application improves polyphenol extraction through cavitation and microagitation mechanisms, while simultaneously promoting polyphenol modifications due to ultrasonic energy exposure [9], [65], which may result in a AA decrease. Moreover, elevated temperatures have been shown to enhance TPC reduction [20], [22].

Saponins and alkaloids content

Total saponin content (TSC) of LPI ranged between 2.27 (30 min-30°C-100%) and 3.78 g oleanolic acid/100 g dm (30 min-60°C-0%) (Table 3). Both time and temperature showed no significant (p > 0.05) effect on TSC, whereas HPU had a significant (p < 0.05, Table 5) influence. As illustrated in Fig. 4F, higher HPU amplitudes led to lower TSC values. Comparable results were described by Navarro-Vozmediano, Dalmau, et al. [35] after HPU lupin flour pretreatment. Navarro del Hierro et al. [37] also showed similar findings for lentil, fenugreek and lupin flours treated with HPU. As discussed in Section 3.3.2., the reduction in TSC at high HPU amplitudes may result from the increase of ultrasound cavitation and microagitation, which may enhance solvent penetration and promote saponin diffusion and solubilization [37].

In all treatments, the alkaloid content (AC) of LPI was below the detection limit (Table 3). This result is likely due to the high water solubility of lupin alkaloids, which facilitates their effective removal during protein extraction and reduces potential toxicity [1], [12]. Lupin flour initially contained 0.44 g lupinine/100 g dm, confirming that the protein extraction process successfully removed a substantial fraction of these compounds.

Effect of HPU-assisted solubilization on techno-functional properties

Water and fat absorption indexes

The water absorption index (WAI) is mainly affected by the presence of hydrophilic amino acids, non-protein constituents and the molecular organization and composition of proteins [54]. Conversely, the fat absorption index (FAI) describes the ability of proteins to retain lipids through capillary action, a phenomenon governed by hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic attractions and hydrogen bonding [27]. WAI and FAI of LPI ranged between 1.39 (30 min-30°C-0%) to 2.01 g water/g dm (30 min-60°C-100% and 45 min-45°C-100%) and 1.68 (15 min-30°C-50%) to 2.28 g oil/g dm (30 min-60°C-0%), respectively (Table 4). Although none of the studied factors significantly (p > 0.05) influenced FAI, both HPU and temperature showed a linear (p < 0.05) effect on WAI (Table 5). Thus, thermosonication at high HPU amplitude and solubilization temperature led to an improvement of WAI (Fig. 5A). Similar findings were reported by Ribeiro et al. [51], who observed that HPU solubilization significantly (p < 0.05) increased the WAI of pequi almond isolate, without significantly (p > 0.05) affecting the FAI. In contrast, Chittapalo & Noomhorm, [8] found that HPU solubilization enhanced both WAI and FAI of defatted rice bran isolates, whereas Sun et al. [57] reported a significant (p < 0.05) enhancement of FAI and a significant (p < 0.05) reduction of WAI in rice bran isolates from HPU-assisted solubilization. HPU may induce structural modifications in proteins, such as partial denaturation and unfolding, which expose hydrophilic sites and increase surface area, facilitating water binding [4], [46], [51], [57]. Similarly, Navarro-Vozmediano et al. [34] reported that elevated temperatures during alkaline solubilization significantly (p < 0.05) improved WAI of LPI, likely due to heat-induced partial unfolding that exposes additional hydrophilic Foaming properties

The foaming capacity (FCA) of LPI ranged from 87.7 (30 min-60°C-0%) to 128.2% (15 min-45°C-100%), while foam stability (FS) varied from 58.0 (30 min-45°C-50%) to 98.3% (45 min-60°C-50%) and from 19.3 (30 min-60°C-0%) to 78.7% (30 min-30°C-100%) for 1 h and 2 h, respectively (Table 4). Both FCA and FS were significantly influenced by the solubilization conditions. The linear effects of time and temperature were not significant (p > 0.05, Table 5). However, a significant (p < 0.05) quadratic effect of temperature was observed for both FCA and FS1h. As shown in Fig. 5B and 5C, a solubilization temperature of 45°C resulted in improved FCA and reduced FS1h. Moreover, as for FS2h, higher HPU amplitudes led to more stable foams (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5D), suggesting that intensive ultrasonic solubilization promotes the formation of more persistent foams over extended periods.

As previously discussed, HPU treatment can induce structural modifications in proteins, increasing the exposure of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions. This promotes more efficient protein adsorption at the air–water interface, reduces surface tension, and helps prevent bubble coalescence [17], [49], [55]. Additionally, a lower lipid content in the isolates may enhance foaming properties by minimizing interference with protein adsorption and foam stabilization [28], [51], which is consistent with the significant (p < 0.05) linear reduction in FC observed with increasing HPU amplitude during solubilization (Section 3.3.1).

Although HPU did not significantly (p > 0.05) affect FCA in our study, several reports have demonstrated significant (p < 0.05) improvements in FCA and FS for different protein isolates obtained by HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization, such as moringa seeds [15], walnut [17], pequi almond [51], rice bran [57], hempseeds [63], pumpkin seeds [55], grass pea [64]and pea [60].

It is noteworthy that HPU efficiency is strongly influenced by both processing conditions and raw material characteristics, as reported in various studies (Ozuna et al., 2014). This variability provides a plausible explanation for the discrepancies between the results of the present work and those reported in previous studies.

Emulsifying properties

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) of LPI ranged from 49.2 (30 min-45°C-50%) to 108.0 m2/g (45 min-45°C-100%) and 40.2 (30 min-30°C-0%) to 401.7 min (30 min-60°C-100%), respectively (Table 4). HPU amplitude had a linear effect (p < 0.05, Table 5) on EAI and ESI. Thus, the higher HPU amplitude, the higher EAI and ESI (Fig. 5E and 5F). Additionally, the ESI also showed a linear effect with temperature and a quadratic effect with time (p < 0.01, Table 5). As depicted in Fig. 5F, the higher the temperature, the higher the ESI. While, treatments exceeding 30 min exhibited lower stability. These findings aligns well with the results presented by Wang et al. [60], who stated that HPU alkaline solubilization significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced both EAI and ESI of pea protein isolates. Emulsifying properties of grass pea protein isolates were also significantly (p < 0.05) improved by HPU application during solubilization [64]. Similar results were obtained for hempseed [63], rice bran [57]and moringa [15]protein isolates. Whereas, Ly et al. [31] and Ribeiro et al. [51] reported no significant (p > 0.05) differences in emulsifying properties between ultrasonic-assisted and conventional protein solubilization of defatted rice bran and pequi almond, respectively. The application of HPU likely induced protein structural changes, promoting partial unfolding and increasing flexibility. These modifications may have facilitated protein adsorption at the oil–water interface, enhancing interactions with lipids and thereby improving emulsifying properties [39], [47], [62].

Color properties

All LPI presented a brownish-yellow appearance, consistent with the values reported by Domínguez et al. [11] (L* 74.9; a* 4.4; b* 45.3). Color analysis revealed a linear effect (p < 0.05, Table 5) of temperature for lightness (L*), yellowness (b*), chroma (C*) and hue angle (h*). Moreover, temperature also exhibited a quadratic (p < 0.05, Table 5) effect for b* and C*, indicating that solubilization at 45°C resulted in LPI with bluer tones. While, HPU application showed a quadratic effect for a* and h*, leading to slightly greener tones when 50% HPU amplitude was applied.

The color variations observed in this work are likely the result of multiple contributing factors. One key factor may be carotenoids, a group of lipid-soluble pigments whose behavior is closely related to the fat fraction of food systems and which can substantially affect the a* (redness) and b* (yellowness) values[5]. Lupin seeds contain several carotenoids, with lutein being the predominant compound, accounting for approximately 70–90% of their total carotenoid profile [56]. Although carotenoids were not directly quantified in the present study, their potential contribution to the observed color changes can be inferred from existing literature and from the variations in FC, since lipids are known to affect the retention of these pigments in food matrices[11].In the present work, both FC and the b* parameter exhibited a quadratic temperature response (Section 3.3.1.), with LPI obtained at 45°C showing lower both variables. This pattern suggests that a decrease in FC may reflect decreased carotenoid retention, which in turn leads to diminished yellowness. Nevertheless, this interpretation remains hypothetical and should be validated by direct measurement of carotenoid compounds in future investigations. In addition, HPU could have affected color parameters by promoting structural modifications of proteins and pigments through cavitation, which may have contributed to the observed changes[10], [32].

Model performance and selection of optimal process conditions

Explained variance (R2) of the regression models ranged from 0.64 (FAI) to 0.93 (FC) (Table 5). Low R2 values indicate that, at a 95% confidence level, the studied process factors had minimal influence on the observed variability of the corresponding response variable. As illustrated in the previous sections, FAI (R2 = 0.64) and PC (R2 = 0.70) were not significantly (p > 0.05) affected by solubilization time, temperature, or HPU application (Table 2, Table 4). A mild influence of process factors was found for responses with R2 around 0.75; for instance FS2h (R2 = 0.73) and TSC (R2 = 0.75) were only significantly affected by HPU (Table 2, Table 4), whereas AA (R2 = 0.75) was only dependent on solubilization time. The solubilization temperature was the only significant (p < 0.05) factor for FCA (R2 = 0.86) and FS1h (R2=0.78) and its interaction with HPU modulated the EY (R2 = 0.77). The strongest effects of process variables were found for PY (R2 = 0.91), FC (R2 = 0.93), TPC (R2 0.91), WAI (R2 = 0.92) and ESI (R2 = 0.91). For all response variables, the Durbin-Watson test indicated no evidence of residual autocorrelation, with p-values > 0.05 in every case.

Regression models were optimized to maximize extraction and protein yields, minimize ANF and ATF content and maximize techno-functional properties, in order to identify the overall most desirable conditions for HPU-assisted alkaline solubilization (Table 2). Overall, thermosonication at longer extraction times (∼ 45 min), higher temperatures (∼ 60°C) and higher HPU amplitudes (∼ 100%) were identified as optimal, as these conditions promoted favorable responses for 8, 9, and 10 of the evaluated variables, respectively. Additionally, significant interaction effects indicated that high HPU amplitudes were particularly effective at elevated temperatures, consistent with the acoustic pressure results reported in Section 3.1. For responses whose individual optima did not exactly coincide with 45 min, 60°C or 100% HPU amplitude, the predicted values remained close to their respective maxima or minima, indicating that satisfactory response levels can still be achieved under the selected conditions. In particular, FC, TPC, AA, and TSC were consistently minimized at long extraction times, high temperature, and high HPU amplitude. At the same settings, most techno-functional properties were maximized, confirming that these conditions provide an effective strategy to enhance protein functionality while limiting the co-extraction of non-protein components.

Conclusions

The ultrasonic field was influenced by both temperature and the addition of lupin flour (LF). The presence of LF attenuated the acoustic field, whereas the effect of temperature differed between media, with higher temperatures in the LF dispersions promoting stronger cavitation and microagitation. High-power ultrasound (HPU) assisted solubilization proved to be an effective strategy for enhancing protein extraction from lupin flour while positively modulating its composition and functional properties. Extraction and total protein yields significantly increased by thermosonication at high HPU amplitude and temperature (60°C), whereas protein content remained fairly stable across treatments. HPU also contributed to reduce undesirable components, such as fat, saponins and polyphenols, while improving key techno-functional properties, including water absorption, foaming capacity and stability, and emulsifying activity.

Based on the optimization strategy, long solubilization times, high temperature and high HPU amplitude are recommended to maximize protein recovery and techno-functional performance while minimizing anti-nutritional and anti-technological factors, consistent with observations from ultrasonic field measurements. Overall, these findings demonstrate that HPU-assisted solubilization represents a practical and sustainable approach for industrial-scale lupin protein extraction, allowing efficient processing without the use of organic solvents and supporting both product quality and process sustainability.
