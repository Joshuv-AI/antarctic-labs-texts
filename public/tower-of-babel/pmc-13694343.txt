External Validation of SELECT Trial-Derived Cardiovascular Risk Equations in a UK Population with Overweight or Obesity and Established Cardiovascular Disease Without Diabetes
Authors: Felice Gragnano, Martin Bøg, Anders Bo Bojesen, Milana Ivkovic, Christopher Lübker, Francesco Fusco, Resham Baruah, Joseph Whitaker, Nia C Jenkins, Iestyn Lloyd, Steffan Hill, Peter E Weeke, A Michael Lincoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth economic models, many of which rely on risk equations, often play an important role in informing local healthcare decision making for the management of obesity. Risk equations for predicting cardiovascular outcomes have previously been derived from SELECT trial data. Validation of risk equations in real-world populations can provide confidence in the accuracy and reliability of health economic models.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to validate the predicted outcomes of the novel, SELECT trial-derived cardiovascular risk equations in a real-world UK population with overweight or obesity and established CVD without diabetes using the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) database.MethodsThe SELECT risk equations for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and stroke were externally validated in a CPRD cohort; records were assessed between 24 October 2008 and 29 March 2021 for patients aged ≥ 45 years with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 27 kg/m2 and established CVD without diabetes. The risk equations were validated both in their original form, and after re-calibration to the CPRD analysis population. Discrimination and calibration were evaluated to assess the predictive performance of the risk equations. The SELECT risk equations, without recalibration, were also compared with published risk equations in terms of their discrimination and calibration.ResultsThe discrimination C-indices at 4 years were 0.65 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.64–0.65) for ACS and 0.71 (95% CI 0.71–0.71) for stroke. After re-calibration, at 4 years calibration, observed/expected ratio was 1.02 (95% CI 1.00–1.03) for ACS and 0.98 (95% CI 0.96–1.00) for stroke. Prediction accuracy was lower for those with a higher baseline risk of stroke, with over-prediction observed in these patients. For both ACS and stroke, the SELECT risk equations showed better discrimination than Framingham (ACS) (C-index 0.68 vs 0.54) and SMART-REACH (stroke) (C-index 0.74 vs 0.50).ConclusionThe recalibrated SELECT risk equations showed acceptable discrimination and good calibration when applied to a real-world population of patients with overweight or obesity and established CVD without diabetes from the CPRD database. These risk equations with calibration also demonstrated better predictive performance compared with published risk equations, supporting their use in health economic evaluations in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01649-6.

Key Points for Decision Makers

The SELECT trial-derived secondary cardiovascular (CV) risk equations for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and stroke were validated in a real-world UK population, using data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink database. Discrimination was acceptable across 1- to 4-year horizons (C-index 0.65 for ACS; 0.71 for stroke); good calibration was achieved after recalibrating baseline hazards to the validation population.Compared with Framingham (ACS) and SMART-REACH (stroke), the SELECT trial-derived equations, without recalibration, demonstrated higher discrimination (C-index 0.68 vs 0.54 for ACS; 0.73 vs 0.50 for stroke) and a consistent trend of increasing observed risk across estimated risk deciles; Framingham and SMART-REACH did not show this pattern.These results support the use of the recalibrated SELECT trial-derived CV risk equations in health-economic evaluations of populations with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes.

Introduction

Obesity is classed as a global epidemic by the World Health Organisation, with 43% of adults living with overweight or obesity, on course to rise to 51% by 2035 [1, 2]. It is well established that obesity contributes directly to the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality [3, 4]. Recent pharmacological advances, in particular with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), have demonstrated efficacy in promoting clinically significant weight loss, and some have documented cardiovascular (CV) benefits [5–7]. The Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity (SELECT) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03574597) demonstrated a reduction in the instantaneous risk of CV outcomes (death from CV causes, nonfatal MI, or nonfatal stroke) in patients with established CV disease living with overweight or obesity when receiving weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg compared with standard of care (hazard ratio [HR] 0.80; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.72–0.90; p < 0.001) at a mean follow-up of 39.8 months, with a maximum follow-up of 56 months [8], with the magnitude of the reduction in CV independent of weight loss [9]. Obesity-related CVD is associated with significant healthcare resource use and costs; patients with class III obesity and established CVD experience healthcare costs ten times greater than those with normal weight and low CVD risk [10, 11]. As the prevalence of obesity continues to rise, the clinical and economic burden is also expected to rise; with the emergence of additional weight-loss interventions, particularly those with broader CV implications, economic evaluations will play an important role, supporting the efficient allocation of limited resources in the management of overweight and obesity and associated CV risk.

Health economic modelling is an important tool for evaluating and comparing the costs and outcomes associated with using different interventions. For chronic and progressive conditions where clinical outcomes are influenced by multiple risk factors, health economic models often utilise risk equations, and selecting the most appropriate equations is paramount when adapting the model for a specific decision problem [12]. Previously validated health economic models [13–16] have used established risk equations, including those from the Framingham Heart Study for primary and secondary coronary heart disease (CHD), to estimate the impact of risk predictors on the occurrence of CV events and type 2 diabetes (T2D) [17]. However, these risk equations were derived from a historical population, where the standard of care and risk factors may not align with current healthcare practice, and further, the population is not specific to people with overweight or obesity, but without diabetes, and therefore may misrepresent risk in this population. In a recently published study, risk equations were developed using SELECT trial data to improve the predictive accuracy of secondary CV events in this population [18].

Validation of risk equations using external populations is an essential step to demonstrate their predictive performance in populations outside the dataset used to derive them, and thus their generalisability to these populations [19]. This study aims to externally validate SELECT trial-derived CV risk equations for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and stroke in a real-world UK population with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes.

Methods

Overview

Previous research derived and internally validated risk equations for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and stroke using the SELECT trial data (depicted in the grey section of Fig. 1) [18]. In this study, these risk equations were externally validated, using a Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) cohort selected using key inclusion and exclusion criteria of the SELECT trial, assessing discrimination and calibration, with and without re-calibration. Predictive performance was also compared with other published risk equations (Fig. 1).

Study Design and Data Source

A retrospective cohort study design was used, using data from the CPRD Aurum database, a large, representative, population-based dataset that reflects real-world clinical practice in the UK, from 24 October 2008 until either the first in-study occurrence of loss to follow-up, end of study (29 March 2021), or death [20]. CPRD collects anonymised patient data from primary care practices for over 19 million patients across England, designed to support real-world evidence research. The CPRD database was linked to secondary care data via Hospital Episode Statistics and to mortality data via the Office of National Statistics using established linkages, enhancing the range of data available to extract relevant health outcomes to better align populations and definitions of risk factors and outcomes with the derivation dataset, compared with using data provided by the CPRD database alone [20]. For these reasons, CPRD is widely used for external validation of prognostic models [21–23].

Validation Cohort

Patients matching SELECT trial eligibility criteria were selected from the CPRD population; criteria requiring variables not captured as structured fields in CPRD were not applied (Online Resource 1 [Table S1], see electronic supplementary material [ESM]) [8]. Included patients were aged ≥ 45 years, with overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 27 kg/m2) and established CVD (one or more of prior MI, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease [PAD]) on the index date (24 October 2008). Patients who had prior MI, stroke, hospitalisation for unstable angina or transient ischaemic attack [TIA] within 60 days prior to index, or had diabetes were excluded. As semaglutide received marketing authorisation for use for the treatment of overweight and obesity in the UK in 2023, no patients received semaglutide at index; patients who received semaglutide or any GLP-1RA at any point during follow-up were also excluded. No other restrictions for background medication were applied to reflect the standard of care for CV comorbidities (full eligibility criteria included in Online Resource 1 [Table S1], ESM). Patients were followed up from the index date until either the first in-study occurrence of loss to follow-up, end of study (29 March 2021), or death.

Risk Equations

The risk equations validated in this study were previously derived from patient-level data from SELECT and estimate event-specific absolute risk up to the maximum trial follow-up, 4.6 years, for the first in-trial occurrence of each of the following composite endpoints:

ACS (consisting of MI and unstable angina requiring hospitalisation),

ACS (consisting of MI and unstable angina requiring hospitalisation),

Stroke (defined as any event of an acute ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, not including TIA).

Stroke (defined as any event of an acute ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, not including TIA).

The risk equations were developed using cause-specific Cox proportional hazard models for survival data, with all-cause mortality considered as a competing risk. The following time-invariant risk factors measured at baseline were included in one or more of the risk equations: treatment arm (semaglutide versus placebo); age; current smoking status; male sex; sleep apnoea; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); CHD; history of ACS; revascularisation; atrial fibrillation (AF); cerebrovascular disorder; history of stroke; TIA; systolic blood pressure (SBP); total/high density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol ratio; high sensitivity C-reactive protein in serum (hsCRP); urine albumin creatinine ratio (uACR); angina medications; and antiplatelet therapy.

As described in the population inclusion section, semaglutide first received marketing approval for the treatment of overweight and obesity in 2023; at the time of this study (2024), 4.6 years of follow-up data post-treatment initiation were not available from the CPRD database. Therefore, this study only validated the risk equations derived from the SELECT placebo arm, as the validation cohort received standard of care without GLP-1RA exposure.

Baseline Variables

Patient demographics were extracted from the most recent records at index in primary care (using read codes) and secondary care (using International Classification of Diseases, tenth version [ICD-10] codes) and were used as inputs to the risk equations. Clinicians validated the definitions, read codes and ICD-10 codes for the variables in the analysis set, which are detailed in Online Resource 1 (Table S2) and Online Resource 2 (see ESM).

Outcomes

Outcomes measured from the validation cohort (CPRD) were defined to align with those recorded in the derivation cohort (SELECT). Endpoints were defined as the earliest post-index recorded ACS or stroke, either in primary care or secondary care. Clinicians validated definitions, read codes and ICD-10 codes for outcomes in the analysis set, which are outlined in Online Resource 1 (Table S3) and Online Resource 2 (see ESM).

Missing Data

Multiple imputation by chained equations was used to impute missing data from the validation cohort [24]. Five imputed datasets, which are generally considered sufficient [25, 26], were created using 20 iterations. Baseline data across the five imputed datasets are reported in Online Resource 1 (Table S6, see ESM). Analyses were conducted separately across the five imputed datasets, and results were summarised using Rubin’s rules [27]. For variables that were missing for all or the majority of patients (hsCRP and uACR), the input to the risk equation was replaced with the mean value from the risk equation’s derivation dataset [28].

Statistical Analysis

Baseline risk factors from the SELECT risk equations and other key characteristics were summarised in the CPRD population (validation cohort) and compared with the SELECT trial population (derivation cohort). The total number of observed events was compared between populations using descriptive statistics.

The risk of ACS and stroke at specified time points was predicted for each patient using the SELECT risk equations with SELECT trial baseline hazards. Due to the higher baseline hazard of ACS and stroke in the overall CPRD population compared with SELECT, baseline hazards were re-calibrated to prevent underestimation of absolute risk in the validation cohort (Online Resource 1 [Figures S1 and S2], see ESM).[29] Baseline hazards for ACS, stroke and the competing risk of mortality were estimated in the validation cohort, assuming equal coefficients for the risk factors as seen in the SELECT risk equations, using the Breslow estimator. The absolute risk of each event at specified time points was predicted for each patient using the estimated validation-cohort baseline hazard and the SELECT risk equation coefficients.

Discrimination and calibration of the SELECT risk equations were tested to evaluate the predictive performance in the validation cohort, both before and after re-calibration (analyses without re-calibration, using the SELECT baseline hazards, are presented in Online Resource 1, ESM). Discrimination evaluates the risk equation’s ability to discriminate between those who experience the event and those who do not. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the associated area under the ROC curve (AUC) were obtained for 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year time horizons. Multiple time horizons were used to investigate whether discrimination was time-dependent. A diagonal line through the origin, where y=x, represents a model which is no better than random prediction, with a corresponding AUC of 0.5. Harrell’s concordance (C)-index considers every possible pairing of patients and measures the proportion of patient pairs whose predicted event times have the same relative order as their true event times. A C-index of 0.5 indicates a model which is no better than random prediction, while a value of 1 indicates perfect discrimination.

Calibration evaluates how accurately observed absolute risk is predicted at a cohort level. Three methods were used to assess calibration: first, overlaying the predicted (average risk at 6-monthly intervals) and observed cumulative incidence across a 4-year time horizon; second, plotting predicted incidence against observed incidence per patient by predicted risk decile at 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year time horizons; and third, calculating the observed/expected (O/E) ratio of events at 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year time horizons by dividing the number of observed events in the CPRD population by the number of predicted events. The observed cumulative incidence was calculated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator, accounting for the competing risk of mortality.

Comparison with Published Risk Equations

The predictive ability of published risk equations for secondary CV events was compared with the SELECT risk equations. The predictive performance was compared, without re-calibration of the SELECT or published risk equations in order to understand the predictive ability of each risk equation when applying it to a new data set. All risk equations were validated using the competing risks setting, using published baseline hazards where applicable. The following criteria were used to identify risk equations suitable for comparison: (i) derived from a cohort with prior CVD, (ii) were able to predict in a population without diabetes (either through a non-diabetes population, or a diabetes risk factor), and (iii) either predicted the outcomes of interest, or predicted composite endpoints which included outcomes of interest. A pragmatic literature search identified two suitable risk equations for analysis, Framingham [17] and SMART-REACH [30]. Outcomes and risk factors were defined slightly differently between the published risk equations and the SELECT trial (Online Resource 1 [Table S4 and S5], see ESM). Specifically, for stroke, SMART-REACH only included ischaemic stroke, whilst SELECT considered both ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke. Additionally, SELECT considered both fatal and non-fatal ACS events, whilst it is unclear whether Framingham considered fatal MI and angina events to be MI and angina events or CHD-related death events. Where the outcome of interest was a component of a composite endpoint, event proportions from the risk equation derivation data were used to separate the outcome into its component parts (Online Resource 1 [Table S6 and Table S7], see ESM).

The Framingham risk equation predicts a composite outcome of MI, angina, coronary insufficiency, sudden, and non-sudden CHD-related death; the combined proportions of MI and angina events (0.639 for males, 0.692 for females) were used to reweight predicted probabilities and compared with the SELECT ACS risk equation. The SMART-REACH risk equation predicts a composite outcome of MI, stroke and CV death; the proportion of stroke events (0.263) was used to reweight predicted probabilities and was compared with the SELECT stroke risk equation. The three risk equations were derived from populations of different ages: SELECT for those aged ≥45 years; Framingham for those aged 35–74 years; SMART-REACH for those aged 45–89 years. Therefore, each risk equation was validated in a patient subgroup matching the age group of the derivation cohort for the corresponding risk equations: 45–74 years for ACS, and 45–89 years for stroke. The risk of each outcome was predicted per patient at the 1-year prediction horizon, the longest prediction horizon compatible with all three risk equations (i.e. the maximum prediction period of the risk equation with the shortest horizon, SMART-REACH), and a cycle length often used for modelling long-term health economic outcomes; discrimination and calibration was evaluated, as described previously.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis tested the impact of varying hsCRP and uACR values on the performance of the SELECT ACS risk equation, since these measures are only selectively available in the CPRD. ACS risk scores were predicted per patient using 1000 repeated random samples from obtained distributions of hsCRP and uACR. Distributions were obtained from a sample of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [31] from January 2017 to March 2020, where patients were aged ≥ 45 years, BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2, with a history of MI or stroke, and no diabetes (see Online Resource 1 in the ESM for details). For every 1000 samples of risk score, the AUC, C-index and calibration plot data were calculated, and their distributions were summarised. This was performed on the first imputed dataset only.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 169,823 patients with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 and established CVD at index, and no MI, stroke, hospitalisation for unstable angina or TIA within 60 days prior to index were extracted from the CPRD database. After applying exclusion criteria, 93,575 patients were eligible for the validation cohort (Online Resource 1 [Fig. S3], see ESM).

Across the whole study period (12.5 years), 22% of patients experienced an ACS event (average follow-up 7.7 years) and 15% of patients experienced a stroke event (average follow-up 8.2 years) (Table 1). Considering only the first 4.6 years of follow-up, the CPRD validation cohort has a much higher incidence of events than the SELECT trial (ACS: 13.3% vs 4.5%; stroke: 8.7% vs 1.9%, respectively) [8].

Baseline characteristics for the risk factors used in the SELECT risk equations are presented for the CPRD validation cohort and compared with the SELECT trial population in Table 1 [8]. Additional baseline characteristics are reported in the ESM, Online Resource 1 [Table S8]. The CPRD validation cohort was older (mean age 70.9 years vs 61.6 years), had higher rates of a history of cerebrovascular disease (21.7% vs 6.7%), stroke (29.5% vs 23.3%), current smokers (25.3% vs 16.8%), and TIA (9.8% vs 4.3%), and lower rates of a history of CHD (70.4% vs 82.1%), ACS (59.5% vs 76.4%), revascularisation (27.8% vs 67.3), and sleep apnoea (1.5% vs 14.6%). Baseline values of SBP and total/HDL cholesterol ratio were comparable between the two populations (Table 1). Proportions of patients receiving anti-angina medications were comparable (39.4% vs 39.9%) but the proportion of antiplatelet agents at baseline was considerably lower in the CPRD validation cohort than in the SELECT trial population (39.3% vs 86.2%, respectively). Levels of missingness were highest for hsCRP (100%) and uACR (98%) but low in all other continuous variables.

Discrimination

ROC curves with associated AUC metrics for 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year time horizons using re-calibrated risk equations (Fig. 2) show little variation across the time horizons (0.65–0.66 for ACS and 0.72–0.73 for stroke). The C-indices with 95% CI at the 4-year time horizon were 0.65 (0.65–0.65) for ACS and 0.71 (0.71–0.71) for stroke. These demonstrate that 65% of all possible patient pairs had predicted event times with the same relative order as their true event times for ACS, and 71% of patient pairs for stroke. As discrimination is not affected by re-calibration, the SELECT risk equations without re-calibration produced equal discrimination metrics (Online Resource 1 [Figure S4], see ESM).

Calibration

Calibration plots for 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year prediction time horizons visualise the predicted probabilities of event occurrence with the re-calibrated risk equations versus the observed incidence (Fig. 3), where the diagonal (45°) line represents perfect agreement between the two. Both re-calibrated risk equations demonstrated alignment between predicted and observed risk, with points lying on or close to the diagonal line of perfect calibration. For stroke, the predicted incidence was under-predicted for intermediate deciles (7th–9th), while at the highest decile (10th) incidence was over-predicted. The magnitude of over-prediction of risk increased with longer prediction time horizons. The O/E ratio with 95% CIs ranged between 1.01 (0.98–1.03) at 1 year and 1.02 (1.00–1.03) at 4 years for ACS, and between 1.00 (0.97–1.03) at 1 year and 0.98 (0.96–1.00) at 4 years for stroke. Plots of observed versus predicted cumulative incidence (Fig. 4) corroborate these results; the re-calibrated risk equations predicted a cumulative incidence curve which largely overlaps with the observed curve across the study period, with marginal deviation from day 750 onwards. As expected, re-calibrated risk equations demonstrated much closer alignment of predicted and observed cumulative incidence than the risk equations without re-calibration (Online Resource 1 [Fig. S5 and Fig. S6], see ESM).

Comparison with Published Risk Equations

The discrimination of the SELECT risk equations was compared with Framingham (for ACS) and SMART-REACH (for stroke) risk equations without re-calibration using ROC curves at the 1-year time horizon (Fig. 5). For both ACS and stroke, the SELECT risk equations demonstrated better discriminative ability than the published risk equations, as shown by higher AUC values (0.68 vs 0.54 for ACS, 0.74 vs 0.50 for stroke, respectively) and higher C-indices (0.68 vs 0.54 for ACS, 0.73 vs 0.50 for stroke, respectively).

Calibration plots for the SELECT risk equations, without re-calibration, were compared with published risk equations at the 1-year time horizon (Fig. 6). Both risk equations underestimated ACS risk across all deciles, indicated by the points for predicted incidence versus observed incidence lying above the diagonal line for both risk equations. Whilst the points for Framingham generally lie closer to the diagonal, observed incidence does not follow a consistent increasing trend with increasing risk deciles, indicating poor risk stratification. The observed stroke risk, with the SMART-REACH risk equation, was similar across all estimated risk deciles, demonstrating an inability to differentiate between those with high and low risk of stroke, reflecting the poor discrimination seen previously. However, for both outcomes, the SELECT risk equation data points follow the trend of increasing observed risk with increasing estimated risk, demonstrating that they can differentiate between high-risk and low-risk patients.

Sensitivity Analysis

Across the 1000 samples of predicted ACS risk when varying hsCRP and uACR, there was little variation in the AUC (mean [95% CI]: 0.66 [0.66–0.67]) and C-index (mean [95% CI]: 0.66 [0.66–0.66]). The calibration plot (Online Resource 1 [Fig. S8], see ESM) also showed narrow confidence intervals for each risk decile, showing the high level of missingness in hsCRP and uACR did not significantly impact the validation results.

Discussion

The current study utilised real-world UK patient data from the CPRD database to externally validate risk equations for predicting secondary CV events in people with overweight or obesity and established CVD without diabetes, derived using the SELECT trial data. Overall, the recalibrated SELECT risk equations demonstrated acceptable discrimination ability and agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, in an external real-world population. The ability of the SELECT risk equations to discriminate between those who experienced an event, and those who did not, demonstrated acceptable accuracy for stroke (AUC 0.72–0.73) and for ACS (AUC 0.65–0.66), and remained consistent across varying time horizons in both outcomes. The observed AUC values closely aligned with those reported in the derivation cohort (AUC 0.68–0.71 for stroke and 0.66–0.68 for ACS), indicating the generalisability of the SELECT risk equations to this external UK population, and were between 0.6 and 0.7, which has previously been defined as acceptable. This level of predictive performance is in line with other secondary CV risk equations [32–35]. Following re-calibration, the risk equations displayed overall good alignment between predicted and observed risk, upon visual inspection, with points lying on or close to the diagonal line of perfect calibration, with a slight deviation for points of higher risk deciles for stroke. Predicted versus observed cumulative incidence curves aligned across the study period. Compared with Framingham and SMART-REACH risk equations, the SELECT risk equations without recalibration showed better discriminative ability with higher AUC values and C-indices, and minor improvements in calibration, demonstrating greater ability to distinguish between patients with high and low event risk.

The validation of these trial-derived risk equations in an external UK population from the CPRD database supports their predictive ability and generalisability in populations aligned with the SELECT trial eligibility criteria, while also contributing to the broader validation of the economic models that utilise these risk equations. Furthermore, the comparisons with the evaluated published risk equations demonstrate that these SELECT trial-derived risk equations more closely predict outcomes in this population.

Strengths and Limitations

The external validation population was derived from the CPRD Aurum database; CPRD is a large database frequently used for external validation of risk equations and is considered to be representative of the UK population in terms of patient demographics and health characteristics [36–38]. Additionally, ready linkage of CPRD primary care data with secondary care data (Hospital Episode Statistics) and mortality data (Office of National Statistics) further enhances the completeness and relevance of the data, enabling a more robust evaluation [39, 40].

This study validated the SELECT trial risk equations, derived from a multinational cohort, in an external UK population from the CPRD database. Further external validation studies are required to assess performance in other healthcare systems or geographic populations to assess generalisability beyond the UK. However, the external validation of risk equations using real-world clinical data is associated with inherent limitations, such as the predictive performance of trial-derived risk equations when applied to a real-world population being limited by key differences in these populations. In addition, clinical trials are closely monitored and subject to stringent selection criteria, which may not reflect real-world clinical practice [41]. In the current study, although SELECT eligibility criteria were applied, baseline characteristics of the populations varied. For example, the CPRD population was older (70.9 years vs 61.6 years) and had differing rates of comorbidity history (lower rates of CHD, history of ACS and revascularisation but higher rates of cerebrovascular disorder and TIA) and medication use (lower use of antiplatelet agents), which may signify differences in the baseline risks of ACS and stroke. To adjust for this, the risk equations were re-calibrated to the baseline hazard of the CPRD population. The re-calibrated risk equations demonstrated improved calibration, where predictions aligned well with the observed cumulative incidence across the whole study period. Whereas predictions from the risk equations without re-calibration consistently underpredicted risk of both ACS and stroke (Online Resource 1 [Figs S5 and S6], see ESM). When utilising risk equations for absolute risk prediction, adjusting the baseline risk of the population to account for differences between the derivation population and the population of interest can improve prediction accuracy [30, 42].

This study was only able to validate the risk equations representing patients receiving standard of care; therefore, the generalisability of the risk equations for those receiving semaglutide treatment remains unknown. The published risk equations used for comparison, Framingham and SMART-REACH, were identified pragmatically; a systematic literature review could have led to inclusion of additional risk equations which are relevant for the comparison. These risk equations were derived from studies that used different definitions for risk factors and outcomes than the SELECT trial, with many definitions for risk factors not available; therefore, these differences will likely introduce bias to direct comparisons, and these findings should be considered within the context of this limitation. Additionally, competing risks were handled differently between the compared risk equations; SELECT risk equations used cause-specific Cox models, SMART-REACH used Fine and Gray models, whilst Framingham did not handle competing risk. Furthermore, re-calibration was not performed in these comparisons; therefore, performance may be underestimated by differing magnitudes. However, the published risk equations showed limited ability to discriminate between low-risk and high-risk patients in the CPRD validation cohort, which cannot be explained by differing outcome definitions and modelling techniques alone, nor the lack of re-calibration. Furthermore, composite outcomes presented for the published risk equations needed to be separated into their component parts, which may introduce bias that should be noted when interpreting outcomes from the different risk equations. Finally, due to a high level of missingness in CPRD for two key predictors: hsCRP and uACR, the mean value from the SELECT trial was used for both. However, the impact of this was investigated, and results from sensitivity analyses show that the high level of missingness had limited impact on the validation results.

Conclusion

In the current study, the novel SELECT trial-derived risk equations for ACS and stroke were validated in an external real-world population from UK clinical practice, aligned with key SELECT trial eligibility criteria. Both of the SELECT trial-derived risk equations showed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, with recalibration, and demonstrated better predictive performance and ability to distinguish between low and high risk of secondary CV events when compared with published risk equations, without re-calibration. These findings enhance confidence in the validity of the SELECT trial–derived risk equations to predict secondary ACS and stroke in populations with overweight or obesity without diabetes.
