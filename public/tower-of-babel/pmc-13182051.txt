Lipid remodeling and phosphatidic acid signaling maintain the vigor of recalcitrant Panax notoginseng seeds under low temperature
Authors: Ting Luo, Chao-Lin Li, Wang-Shun Zhang, Min Huang, Na Ge, Qing-Yan Wang, Jin-Shan Jia, Jin-Yan Zhang, Jun-Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-temperature exposure during the early stage of seed imbibition markedly constrains seedling emergence in direct-seeded crops. Recalcitrant seeds are commonly accepted to be highly sensitive to low temperatures. Although Panax notoginseng seeds are characterized by recalcitrance, physiological and molecular responses to low temperature remain poorly defined.ResultsHerein, P. notoginseng seeds from three-year-old plants were treated at 0, 4, 10, and 25 °C for 24 or 48 h and analyzed by the method of integrated physiological biochemistry, transcriptomics, and lipidomics. Relative to other temperatures at 48 h, the 0 °C treatment significantly delayed germination, whereas neither 0–4 °C reduced the final germination rate. At 4 °C, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels exceeded those at 0 °C at 24 h and 48 h, accompanied by increased catalase (CAT) activity. Compared with the 25 °C treatment at 24 h and 48 h, both the 0 °C and 4 °C treatment suppressed phospholipase D (PLD) activity, with a significant inhibition at 0 °C treatment. PLD is a membrane-associated phospholipase that contributes to stress-induced phospholipid hydrolysis. Suppressed PLD activity at 0 °C and 4 °C suggests tighter restriction of membrane lipid catabolism, consistent with enhanced membrane integrity under low temperature. Transcriptome profiling showed that relative to the 25 °C treatment for 48 h, both the 0 °C and 4 °C treatment consistently enriched pathways for unsaturated fatty-acid biosynthesis and elongation, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, abscisic acid (ABA) pathway, and phosphatidylinositol signaling. Lipidomics further revealed temperature-specific remodeling: lysophosphatidylcholine, LPC (16:0) accumulated specifically at the 0 °C treatment, whereas phosphatidylcholine, PC (34:1), phosphatidic acid, PA (34:1), and the glycerolipids diacylglycerol (DG) and triacylglycerol (TG) accumulated at 4 °C treatment. Integrative analysis revealed that both PA and LPC are associated with transport and signaling genes. This study also identified the ABA core module centered on pyrabactin resistance 1-like proteins (PYLs) and protein phosphatase 2Cs (PP2Cs) whose module eigengene was negatively correlated with electrolyte conductivity and negatively correlated with phospholipase D activity. This inverse relationship suggests that ABA signaling helps maintain membrane integrity. It may reduce electrolyte leakage and limit PLD-driven lipid hydrolysis.ConclusionsIn conclusion, recalcitrant P. notoginseng seeds appear insensitive to low temperature at 0 °C and 4 °C. Changes in the membrane-lipid microenvironment initiate an integrated signaling mechanism that transduces signals via the core ABA pathway. This integrated mechanism ultimately converges on NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2 (NAC) transcription factors and dehydration-responsive element-binding protein (DREB) transcription factors to coordinate stress responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08673-w.

Background

Low temperatures significantly slow seed germination and reduce seedling vigor, thereby impairing seedling morphogenesis and constraining yields in direct-seeded crops [1, 2]. However, the effects of low temperature vary by species and physiological stage. Seeds are broadly classified as orthodox or recalcitrant according to their tolerance to desiccation and low temperature [3]. Orthodox seeds mature with low water content (typically 3–7% fresh weight), can endure prolonged storage at low temperatures, and resume germination upon rewarming [4]. Recalcitrant seeds disperse with high water content and remain metabolically active, making them sensitive to both desiccation and low temperatures. They are particularly vulnerable to freezing injury from intracellular ice-crystal formation [5]. Notably, temperature responses vary widely among recalcitrant species [6]. Accordingly, assessing cold tolerance requires an integrated evaluation of membrane integrity, lipid peroxidation, lipidomic profiles, and transcriptional responses of key genes.

Low temperatures primarily target cellular membranes, inducing a lipid phase transition from the liquid-crystalline to the gel phase. This transition reduces membrane fluidity and increases permeability and ion leakage [7]. Electrical conductivity (EC), an indicator of electrolyte leakage, serves as a key measure of membrane integrity. In orthodox seed Cold-tolerant Zea mays genotypes preserve membrane fluidity and stability by increasing membrane-lipid unsaturation, thereby exhibiting lower EC during low-temperature imbibition [8]. Damage to membrane systems, together with metabolic activation, intensifies oxidative stress and triggers bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9]. To counter low-temperature injury, seeds activate a coordinated defense network comprising antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), as well as osmolytes such as proline and soluble sugars. This network buffers transient ROS surges and preserves membrane structure and function [10]. For example, Oryza sativa enhances cold tolerance through the synergistic actions of sugars, proline, and antioxidant enzymes [11]. Likewise, in orthodox seed Triticum aestivum seeds treated with fatty-acid-ester growth regulators show reduced superoxide anion (O₂⁻) production rates and relative electrical conductivity, alongside increased SOD, CAT, and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities, indicating improved low-temperature tolerance [12].

The susceptibility of recalcitrant seeds to low temperature-induced ROS imbalance and lipid peroxidation, a consequence of their high water content, underlines the critical importance of both effective antioxidant systems and membrane stability. In recalcitrant seed of Baccaurea ramiflora, a 2-hour exposure to 2 °C reduced germination by 50% and increased electrolyte leakage [13]. Similarly, the recalcitrant seed of the Garcinia gardneriana lost all viability after 90 days of storage at 5 °C, concurrent with a rapid decline in SOD and APX activities [14]. By contrast, recalcitrant seeds with effective antioxidant defenses can rapidly scavenge ROS, thereby preserving membrane structure and function. Cold acclimation enhances SOD activity, malondialdehyde (MDA) and membrane integrity, consequently improving chilling tolerance in recalcitrant Araucaria angustifolia seed [15]. Despite progress in characterizing the physiology of low-temperature deterioration, the molecular progression from early membrane signaling to downstream transcriptional control requires further elucidation.

Enhanced membrane-lipid unsaturation and the remodeling of key lipids mediate sustained cold adaptation in orthodox seeds. These pivotal lipid classes include phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), diacylglycerol (DG), triacylglycerol (TG), as well as chloroplast galactolipids like monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) and digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG). Lipidomic studies in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and green pepper (Capsicum annuum) show that these cold-induced shifts in lipid abundance closely track the extent of membrane damage [16, 17]. Lipid-derived signals interface with Ca²⁺ signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling. These pathways collectively relay information to a core transcriptional module. This module comprises C-repeat-binding factor (CBF) regulators, the inducer of CBF expression (ICE), and cold-responsive (COR) regulators [18]. The ICE–CBF–COR pathway promotes germination by rebalancing the gibberellin (GA)/abscisic acid (ABA) ratio, thereby alleviating the ABA-dependent inhibitory module formed by pyrabactin resistance 1-like proteins (PYLs), protein phosphatase 2Cs (PP2Cs), SNF1-related protein kinase 2 (SnRK2), and ABSCISIC ACID-INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5) [19]. Notably, seed-expressed NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2 (NAC) transcription factors enhance cold tolerance by directly interacting with ABI5 or by binding CBF promoters, thereby safeguarding germination potential. In rice (Oryza sativa), OsNAC5 positively regulates cold tolerance during germination and seedling stages by directly activating OsABI5, whereas in apple (Malus domestica) MdNAC104 enhances cold tolerance by directly activating MdCBF1 and MdCBF3 [20, 21].

Panax notoginseng (Burkill) F.H. Chen, an herbaceous species in the family Araliaceae, is native to frost-free subtropical regions [22]. P. notoginseng seeds are recalcitrant and exhibit marked desiccation sensitivity [23]. Embryos are underdeveloped at dispersal and require a 45–60 day post-maturation (after-ripening) period to overcome dormancy [24]. In recalcitrant seeds, sensitivity to desiccation and low temperature are defining functional traits. However, research on P. notoginseng has focused primarily on the physiological, biochemical, and molecular bases of desiccation sensitivity [25], with comparatively few studies addressing low-temperature sensitivity. In contrast to many recalcitrant species that rely on nursery transplanting, P. notoginseng cultivation requires direct seeding right after harvest. Within 24–48 h of sowing, imbibing seeds in the principal production regions are directly exposed to highly variable spring field conditions [26]. This exposure to cold rainfall or low soil temperatures can induce imbibitional chilling injury, which is a primary constraint on seedling emergence and uniform stand establishment. Accordingly, three-year-old P. notoginseng seeds were subjected to 0, 4, 10, or 25 °C for 24 or 48 h, and quantified membrane integrity EC, MDA, antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, POD), osmolytes (proline, soluble sugars), phospholipase D (PLD) activity, and germination rate. The present study aims to elucidate early-response mechanisms linking ROS homeostasis to membrane stability under low-temperature stress by integrating these measurements with transcriptomic and lipidomic profiling. We anticipated that P. notoginseng seeds, originating from subtropic-regions might confer a tolerance to the above-zero low temperature for germination. This work advances fundamental understanding of low-temperature responses in recalcitrant seeds and informs cold-resilient, safe production of P. notoginseng.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and low-temperature treatments

Seeds of three-year-old Panax notoginseng were routinely harvested from the ‘Yi wan Shui’ standardized cultivation base (23°25′ N, 103°55′ E) operated by Wenshan Jingyuan Agricultural Development Co., Ltd. in Wenshan, Yunnan, China. Healthy, plump, and uniform P. notoginseng seeds were manually selected based on visual appearance after depulping and surface sterilization with 5% CuSO₄. Following this selection, the initial water content of these seeds was determined to be 62.87% ± 0.50% (mean ± SD) using the oven-drying method at 105 °C until constant weight. To prepare sufficient material, seeds were treated in breathable mesh bags at low temperatures of 0, 4, and 10 °C, with 25 °C as the control, for 0, 24, or 48 h. For each treatment, ten replicate bags were prepared, each containing 30 g of seeds. Following these treatments, the seeds were processed along two paths: (1) For germination assessment, seeds from all treatments were mixed with moist river sand at a 1:5 mass ratio and subjected to stratified cultivation in the dark at 15 ± 5 °C for 90 days. Germination percentage, mortality, embryo rate, and seedling length were recorded after stratification. (2) For molecular analyses, samples were collected after 24 and 48 h of treatment at 0 °C, 4 °C, and 25 °C for biochemical and transcriptomic studies, and after 48 h at the same temperatures for lipidomics analysis. All these samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

Assessment of physiological parameters

EC was measured according to the International Rules for Seed Testing [27]. For this assay, one biological replicate consisted of approximately 50 seeds. The seeds were immersed in 250 mL of deionized water and incubated at 25 °C for 24 h, with an equal volume of water serving as a blank control. After incubation, the conductivity of the leachate was measured at 25 °C. The mass-normalized electrolyte conductivity (EC₂₅) was then calculated using the formula: EC₂₅ = (EC leachate – EC blank) / seed fresh weight (µS·cm⁻¹·g⁻¹). Five independent biological replicates were performed per treatment. Embryo development was evaluated by longitudinally sectioning seeds and imaging them under a motorized stereomicroscope (SteREO Discovery V.12, ZEISS, Germany) after 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 days of stratification; embryo rate (%) was calculated as (embryo length / endosperm length) × 100%, based on 90 replicates per time point. For germination tests, stratified seeds with split coats were sterilized, sown in moist river sand, and kept at 15 ± 5 °C; Germination was recorded every 5 days, starting from the day after sowing, until no further germination was observed. The germination percentage was determined as (number of germinated seeds / total seeds) × 100%, with each treatment comprising three replicates, each containing 100 seeds.

Determination of biochemical parameters

Seeds from each temperature treatment were collected for biochemical assays. Five biological replicates were analyzed per treatment. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD, Kit No. G0101W48), peroxidase (POD, Kit No. G0107W), catalase (CAT, Kit No. G1015W), and phospholipase D (PLD, Kit No. G0925F) were measured using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The contents of malondialdehyde (MDA, Kit No. G0109W), proline (PRO, Kit No. G0111W), soluble sugars (Kit No. G0501F), and superoxide anion (O₂⁻, Kit No. G0129F) were quantified using the corresponding kits from the same manufacturer. All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions and absorbance was read using a microplate reader. Endogenous ABA and gibberellin A3 (GA₃) contents were quantified under CK-0 h, CK-48 h, T4-48 h and T0-48 h. Frozen seeds were ground in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 0.1–0.3 g of powder was extracted with 5 mL isopropanol–water–formic acid (80:19:1, v/v/v). Samples were homogenized for 2 min and ultrasonicated at 4 °C for 1 h. The residue was further extracted with dichloromethane. The combined supernatants were evaporated under nitrogen until the aqueous phase remained. The extract was reconstituted to 1 mL, diluted two-fold with methanol, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter prior to analysis.

Transcriptomic sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from P. notoginseng seeds subjected to temperature treatments (0, 4, and 25 °C for 24 or 48 h) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA quality was verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 7.0), and libraries were constructed by poly(A) enrichment, cDNA synthesis, and Illumina adapter ligation. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression was quantified as TPM. Differential expression analysis was conducted with edgeR [28] (|log₂FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05). Functional enrichment of DEGs was analyzed via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (FDR < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was also performed using GSEA software with the MSigDB database [29] to identify enriched KEGG pathways. Genes were ranked using the Signal2Noise metric, and enrichment significance was evaluated using default parameters (FDR < 0.05). For integrative analysis, the union of DEGs from these four key treatments (T0-48 h, T4-48 h, T25-0 h, T25-48 h) served as the transcriptomic input for O2PLS modeling.

Lipid extraction, LC–MS/MS acquisition, and data analysis

Approximately 30 mg of seed tissue per sample (three biological replicates per treatment: T25–0 h, T25–48 h, T4–48 h, T0–48 h was used for lipid extraction. Lipids were extracted via the methyl tert‑butyl ether (MTBE) method, followed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis on a Nexera LC‑30 A system coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) on an ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm), with gradients of acetonitrile–water and acetonitrile–isopropanol. with a gradient of acetonitrile–water and acetonitrile–isopropanol. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive ion mode (POS) and negative ion mode (NEG) with data‑dependent acquisition to achieve comprehensive lipid coverage. The data from the two ionization modes were processed and analyzed separately. Raw data were processed using LipidSearch for lipid identification and quantification. Differential lipids were selected based on orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis(OPLS-DA) variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1 and p < 0.05 (Student’s t‑test). A pooled quality‑control sample was injected at intervals to monitor system stability. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R.

O2PLS-based transcript–lipidomics integration

In order to integrate the transcriptomic and lipidomic data, we performed a two-way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) analysis [30]. Matched transcriptomic and lipidomic profiles were obtained from four conditions (T25–0 h, T25–48 h, T4–48 h, and T0–48 h), with three biological replicates per condition. O2PLS models were calculated in R using the OmicsPLS package [31]. The optimal number of components was determined using the alternative cross-validation procedure implemented in OmicsPLS [32]. The model with the smallest prediction error was selected for downstream analyses. Joint loadings were extracted from the fitted O2PLS models. Variables were ranked by the sum of squares of their joint-loading values, and the top 25 transcripts and top 25 lipids were selected for visualization.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Co-expression network analysis was performed in R (WGCNA v1.47) [33]. Genes with TPM > 5 were retained, and the TPM values were used for network construction. A soft-thresholding power (β = 14) (Fig. S6A) was selected to approximate a scale-free topology, and networks were constructed with blockwiseModules using TOMType = “unsigned”, mergeCutHeight = 0.2, and minModuleSize = 50, yielding 16 co-expression modules. Module–trait relationships were evaluated by correlating module eigengenes with two trait groups: physiological indices (relative EC, time to 50% germination (T50), PLD activity, superoxide anion radical (OFR), MDA, and soluble sugar content and lipid-composition indices summarized at the level of major lipid classes (phospholipids, lysophospholipids, galactolipids, neutral glycerolipids, and sphingolipids). Intramodular connectivity was calculated with softConnectivity, and highly connected genes were defined as candidate hub genes. For protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis, the top 100 genes were selected from the bisque4 module (MM. bisque4) based on intramodular connectivity. KEGG enrichment was performed for bisque4 module genes, and genes annotated to the top three enriched KEGG pathways were extracted from the bisque4 module gene set. The intersection of these two bisque4-derived gene sets was used to construct a protein–protein interaction network in STRING (combined score ≥ 0.4), and the network was visualized in Cytoscape v3.3.0 [34]. For each module, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the gene set employed for network construction as background. Enrichment significance was evaluated using a hypergeometric test with FDR < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1) was used for figure preparation, and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) was employed for statistical analyses. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with the significance level set at α = 0.05. In all tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physiological and developmental responses under low temperature

The electrical conductivity of P. notoginseng seeds exhibited a decline with prolonged treatment duration across all temperature conditions (Fig. 1A). Notably, EC at 0 °C was significantly lower than that at 25 °C after 48 h of treatment (p < 0.05). After 90 days of post-ripening, the germination percentage was consistent across treatments, ranging from 88% to 90%, with no statistically significant differences (p ≥ 0.05; Fig. 1C). However, the time required to achieve 50% germination indicated that seeds treated at 0 °C experienced a significant delay in the germination process after 48 h, differing significantly from the other groups (Fig. 1D). Seed mortality remained low throughout the post-ripening period (Fig. 1B), with no significant differences among treatments at 90 days. The embryo rate continued to increase and showed no significant differences among treatments at 50 days (Fig. 1E, F). Additionally, there was no significant difference in seedling length between the 0 °C and 25 °C treatments at 90 days (Fig. 1G, H). In summary, 0 °C treatment significantly reduces EC and delays the germination process. Nevertheless, it does not affect key physiological indicators such as germination percentage, embryo rate, seedling length.

Membrane damage and protective responses

In terms of oxidative stress, MDA content remained unchanged by temperature at any time point (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the OFR content did not differ significantly among the treatment groups at the initial treatment time point (0 h). Following 24 and 48 h of treatment, the OFR content was significantly higher at both 4 °C and 25 °C compared to 0 °C (Fig. 2B). Conversely, SOD activity peaked significantly at 0 °C after 24 h of treatment and was notably higher at 25 °C after 48 h compared to the other groups (Fig. 2C). CAT activity was significantly highest at both treatment time points at 4 °C (Fig. 2D). POD activity showed no difference after 0 h and 24 h of all treatments, but the activity at 4 °C was significantly higher than at 0 °C and 25 °C after 48 h (Fig. 2E).

Proline content increased significantly in the 25 °C treatment relative to the 0 °C treatment at 24 h. However, the 4 °C treatment exhibited significantly higher proline content than both the 0 °C and 25 °C treatments (Fig. 2F). At 24 h, soluble sugar content were significantly lower in the 0 °C treatment group than in the 4 °C and 25 °C groups, with no significant difference between them. By 48 h, the difference between the 0 °C and 4 °C groups was no longer significant, while both remained significantly higher than the 25 °C group (Fig. 2G). PLD activity showed a consistent temperature gradient at both 24 and 48 h time points, following 25 °C > 4 °C > 0 °C; the 0 °C treatment displayed the lowest PLD activity, which was significantly lower after both 24 and 48 h of treatment (Fig. 2H).

Transcriptome overview and functional enrichment of DEGs across temperatures

The proportion of clean reads in all samples was nearly 100% (Fig. S1A, B), and the mapped reads were predominantly located in exon regions (Fig. S1C). The TPM distribution patterns were consistent across samples (Fig. S1D, E), and correlation analysis confirmed strong concordance among biological replicates (Fig. S1F). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation of samples treated at different temperatures and times, with principal components 1 and 2 accounting for 70.0% of the variance (Fig. 3A). The T25-48 h versus T0-48 h comparison showed the most pronounced changes. 3059 genes were up-regulated and 4536 were down-regulated. In the T25-48 h versus T4-48 h comparison, 3400 genes were up-regulated and 3630 were down-regulated (Fig. 3B). The upset analysis indicated that the set of differentially expressed genes defined by T25-0 h is the largest and that multiple comparisons share a common core of differentially expressed genes (Fig. S2A). Based on the results presented above, subsequent functional enrichment and GSEA focused on T25-48 h vs. T0-48 h and T25-48 h vs. T4-48 h as the principal comparison pairs.

GO functional enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes related to membrane lipid metabolism, including “unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis” and “fatty acid metabolism and elongation” (Fig. 3C, E). KEGG pathway analysis also identified significant activation in pathways such as “Unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis,” “fatty acid metabolism and elongation,” “MAPK signaling pathway (plant),” “plant-pathogen interaction,” “stratum corneum/wax synthesis,” and “ABC transporter” (Fig. 3D, F). The GSEA results supported these findings. In the comparisons T25-48 h vs. T0-48 h and T25-48 h vs. T4-48 h, both “unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis (KO01040)” and “fatty acid metabolism (KO01212)” showed positive enrichment (NES > 0, FDR < 0.05), while “Zeatin nucleoside biosynthesis (KO00908)” showed negative enrichment (NES < 0) in the T4-48 h comparison (Fig. S3). Detailed KEGG enrichment analysis for other comparisons was shown in Fig. S2.

Lipidome overview and patterns of differential lipids under low temperature

A total of 2,248 lipid molecules were identified, 383 of which were annotated in the NEG mode. Lipidomic composition differed substantially between ionization modes. In POS mode, neutral lipids predominated, with TG (35.54%) and DG (20.24%) as the major components. In contrast, the NEG mode was enriched in phospholipids and chloroplast-associated lipids, mainly PC (13.32%), phosphatidic acid (PA, 12.01%), MGDG (5.74%), monohexosylceramide (Hex1Cer, 5.22%), and sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG, 4.70%) (Fig. S4A, B).

PCA revealed distinct separation of samples by group in both ionization modes. In POS mode, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 37.9% and 22.2% of the variance, respectively, while in NEG mode, they explained 59.5% and 13.0% (Fig. 4A, B). Analysis in the POS mode revealed numerous differential lipids across the three pairwise comparisons (T25–48 h vs. T0–48 h: 148 up, 14 down; T25-48 h vs. T4–48 h: 162 up, 8 down; T4–48 h vs. T0–48 h: 73 up, 79 down) (Fig. S4C). Subsequent Venn analysis identified a shared set of 43 core lipids in POS mode (Fig. 4C), which starkly contrasts with the single common lipid found in the NEG mode (Fig. 4D).

The heatmap of the top 30 differential lipids showed that, under the 4 °C treatment, several membrane lipids increased significantly. Integrated transcriptomic and lipidomic profiling elucidates core mechanisms of low-temperature adaptation

To investigate the relationship between gene expression and lipid metabolism under low-temperature conditions, we established O2PLS models for two comparisons: T25-48 h vs. T4-48 h and T25 -48 h vs. T0 -48 h. The optimal model, configured with zero orthogonal components (oX = oY = 0) and three joint components (a = 3) (Table S18), demonstrated high explanatory power. Furthermore, the high cumulative interpretation degrees (R²X and R²Y > 0.95) confirmed the model’s robustness (Fig. S5).

Joint-loading analysis revealed that variables distant from the origin exerted a major influence on the cross-omics covariation. In the T25°C–48 h vs. T4°C–48 h comparison, high-loading lipids were enriched in PC, PA, DG, TG, and sphingolipids (Fig. 5A), whereas high-loading transcripts included TGA2.2, MYB14, bHLH92, and RNS3 (Fig. 5B). For the T25 − 48 h vs. T0 − 48 h comparison, high-loading lipids were dominated by LPC, PA, PC, DG, TG, galactolipids (MGDG, DGDG), and sphingolipids (Fig. 5C). Concurrently, high-loading transcripts were associated with membrane transport, signal transduction, and cytoskeleton regulation, including the genes VTL1, SBT1.2, NPF6.3, KIN14I, and NIK2 (Fig. 5D).

WGCNA and protein interaction network analysis uncover core low-temperature response genes

In this study, we used weighted gene co-expression network analysis to construct gene co-expression networks. We applied a soft threshold of β = 14 to ensure a scale-free topology fitting index of approximately R² = 0.9 and stable average connectivity (Fig. S6A, 6A). This approach identified 16 gene co-expression modules, each comprising between 140 and 4800 genes (Fig. S6D). Many genes clustered in response to low-temperature, showing co-expression patterns (Fig. 6A). The module-sample association analysis revealed that the module correlation coefficients for the bisque4 module (MEbisque4) were significantly higher at T0-48 h and T4-48 h compared to T25-48 h (Fig. 6B). The module–module relationship heatmap further showed that MEbisque4 clustered closely with MEsaddlebrown and was negatively correlated with many other modules (Fig. 6C). Module–trait relationships were evaluated using Pearson correlation between module eigengenes and trait values. MEbisque4 was negatively correlated with EC (r = − 0.915, P = 6.47 × 10⁻⁹), positively correlated with T50 (r = 0.836, P = 2.35 × 10⁻⁶), and negatively correlated with PLD activity (r = − 0.872, P = 2.58 × 10⁻⁷). (Fig. 6D, Table S20). These results suggested that the activation of this module was linked to reduced membrane permeability, moderately delayed germination, and inhibited PLD activity.

Discussion

Membrane conservation at 0 °C and ROS-mediated defense activation at 4 °C

This study suggests that recalcitrant seeds adopt distinct strategies under subtly different low temperatures near 0 °C. At 0 °C, reduced EC and lower PLD activity support a stronger emphasis on membrane stabilization (Figs. 1A and 2H). Germination was delayed (Fig. 1D), consistent with suppressed metabolic activity under near-freezing conditions, and this shift may help maintain seed viability. A comparable chilling-associated slowdown of germination-related metabolism has been documented in rice (Oryza sativa) seed [35]. After 24 h and 48 h of treatment at 4℃, CAT activity was significantly higher than that at 0℃ and 25℃ (Fig. 2D). This suggests an early ROS burst in seeds, accompanied by a typical defense activation strategy. This is not passive damage, but an active response where ROS acts as an alarm signal to trigger the antioxidant system against stress [36]. This is consistent with the findings of Meng et al. (2023), who confirmed in wild-type cucumber seedlings that 4 ℃ enhances cold tolerance by inducing CAT activity [37]. The discovery of these two strategies deepens our understanding of the classic imbibitional chilling injury mechanism. It indicates that recalcitrant seeds possess subtle temperature sensing and resource allocation capabilities, and their response is far more complex than a simple damage-repair model.

Cold-induced membrane remodeling is driven by PA signaling and LPC accumulation

Low-temperature exposure at both 0 °C and 4 °C points to a coordinated cold-response program that links membrane lipid remodeling with stress signaling. (Fig. 3C-F). This pattern is consistent with established models of plant cold adaptation. In these models, increased membrane unsaturation is coupled with lipid and hormone-related signaling to support stress responses [38, 39]. Accordingly, phosphatidic acid PA acts as a key lipid messenger, playing a hub role in connecting the MAPK and ABA signaling pathways [40].

The lipidomic data indicate broad remodeling of membrane lipids under low temperature (Fig. 4E). This observation is consistent with the cross-species view that temperature regulates membrane lipid metabolism [41]. Notably, PLD activity decreased at both 0 °C and 4 °C, whereas total PA showed an increasing tendency. This phenomenon differs from the expected response of the typical PLD/PA signaling axis. PA accumulation in P. notoginseng seeds under low temperature may involve contributions from biosynthetic routes other than PLD. However, the current dataset does not allow direct attribution of PA origin to a specific pathway, and resolving this question requires pathway-level evidence. A review by Wu et al. (2022) indicated that PLD and phospholipase C-diacylglycerol kinase (PLC-DGK) pathways are the two major routes for PA biosynthesis in plants under cold stress [42]. The decreased PLD activity in this study may represent an active protective strategy adopted by recalcitrant seeds to prevent excessive membrane lipid hydrolysis. This mechanism is consistent with the finding that a specific PLDδ isoform regulates PA homeostasis to maintain cell viability in pear pollen tubes [43]. This suggests that the fine-tuned inhibition of PLD activity may be a conserved strategy for plants to maintain membrane integrity and cell survival under specific environmental adaptations.

The changing trend of chloroplast lipids further confirms the rule established by Spicher et al. (2016). Temperature changes from 10℃ to 38℃ drive adaptive adjustments in the unsaturation of chloroplast galactolipids, which is critical for membrane system remodeling [17]. Although 0℃ treatment maintained lower membrane leakage, the accompanying significant up-regulation of LPC (16:0) (Fig. 4F) suggests an active repair mechanism rather than mere physical inhibition. LPC is a key intermediate in the LPCAT-mediated deacylation-reacylation cycle, and its accumulation indicates rapid membrane lipid repair and fine-tuning by cells [44].

A synergistic signaling hub integrates lipid messengers (PA, LPC) with the ABA core module PYL4-PP2C

By integrating cross-omics associations revealed by O2PLS and phenotype-related modules identified by WGCNA, this study delineated a coherent regulatory network from “membrane lipid remodeling” to “hormone signaling” in the cold response of P. notoginseng seeds (Fig. 5). This integrative view supports the idea that membrane lipid remodeling is not only a structural adjustment but also an upstream layer that can shape early signal propagation under cold shock [45]. Membrane lipid signals represented by PA, LPC, and PC/DG/TG co-varied with genes implicated in transcriptional regulation, transport, and signal transduction. This finding constructs an early response module directly linking membrane lipid remodeling, signal transduction, and cellular regulation, strongly supporting the classic theory that PA and LPC act as second messengers [39].

Meanwhile, WGCNA analysis revealed that the core module “bisque4” integrates the ABA signaling core (PYL4-PP2C), MAPK and phosphatidylinositol signaling pathways, as well as NAC and DREB transcription factors into a co-expression unit. This agrees with peanut cold-tolerance models, where ABA coordinates cold-gene regulation and is coupled to membrane lipid metabolism and lipid signaling [46].

To complement the ABA-related module signature identified in bisque4, we quantified endogenous ABA and GA₃ contents under CK-0 h, CK-48 h, T4-48 h and T0-48 h, as shown in Fig. 7F–H. ABA and GA₃ contents were reduced under low temperature relative to CK-48 h, whereas the ABA-to-GA₃ ratio showed no significant difference among treatments. This pattern suggests that ABA signaling output can be strengthened without a rise in bulk ABA content. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that ABA signaling can be tuned by receptor–PP2C interaction states in seeds. ABA-independent PYL-like proteins can constitutively bind PP2Cs and modulate ABA signaling in seeds [47]. This interpretation is also consistent with recent work showing that constitutively active ABA receptors enhance signaling without elevated ABA, supporting a “signaling sensitivity” explanation for the bisque4 ABA core module [48].

Although the independent roles of the PYL-PP2C-SnRK2 signaling pathway, NAC, and DREB transcription factors in plant cold responses have been widely reported [49–51], this study is the first to observe their co-integration into a highly synergistic co-expression module (bisque4) during the early cold shock of P. notoginseng seeds. We therefore propose a model (Fig. 8). In the low-temperature response of P. notoginseng seeds, ABA signal perception, transduction, and downstream transcriptional reprogramming do not follow the traditional hierarchical signal transduction pattern but instead exhibit a parallel and synergistic mode. This difference in regulatory patterns may represent an efficient strategy for recalcitrant seeds to adapt to low temperatures. Nevertheless, the precise functional roles of pivotal hub components, including PYL4 and specific PLD isoforms, in transducing the cold signal require further validation via genetic or pharmacological approaches.

Conclusion

In the present study, we found the low-temperature insensitivity of recalcitrant Panax notoginseng seeds at 0 °C and 4 °C, and we therefore proposed a hypothesized model for the mechanism (Fig. 8). At 0 °C, seeds maintained membrane homeostasis by strongly suppressing PLD activity. This suppression was accompanied by LPC (16:0) accumulation and elevated LPCAT activity, indicating an active membrane repair and remodeling process. The lipid remodeling, together with the membrane-stabilizing effects of MGDG (65:2) and DGDG (30:3), collectively slowed metabolism and delayed germination. At 4 °C, seeds activated a defense-oriented response initiated by lipid remodeling between PA/PC and DG/TG, which further stimulated ROS production and activated antioxidant enzymes. These changes collectively activated core MAPK, ABA, and phosphoinositide signaling pathways, ultimately converging on downstream NAC and DREB transcription factors to regulate low-temperature adaptation. Our results uncovered an integrated early-stage mechanism wherein lipid second messengers (PA and LPC) act in parallel with hormonal (ABA) signaling and protein kinase (MAPK) signaling. The identified key candidates, including PLDα/LPCAT, PYL–PP2C–SnRK2, NAC, and DREB, provided a clear theoretical basis for genetic improvement and precise regulation of cold tolerance in direct-seeded P. notoginseng production.
