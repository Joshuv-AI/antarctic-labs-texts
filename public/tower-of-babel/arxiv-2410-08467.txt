Lattice fermions with solvable wide range interactions


Abstract
Exactly solvable (spinless) lattice fermions with wide range interactions are constructed explicitly based on exactly solvable stationary and reversible Markov chains 𝒦 R \mathcal{K}^{R} reported a few years earlier by Odake and myself. The reversibility of 𝒦 R \mathcal{K}^{R} with the stationary distribution π \pi leads to a positive classical Hamiltonian ℋ R \mathcal{H}^{R} . The exact solvability of ℋ R \mathcal{H}^{R} warrants that of a spinless lattice fermion c x c_{x} , c x † c_{x}^{\dagger} , ℋ f R = ∑ x , y ∈ 𝒳 c x † ​ ℋ R ​ ( x , y ) ​ c y \mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{x}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)c_{y} based on the principle advocated recently by myself. The reversible Markov chains 𝒦 R \mathcal{K}^{R} are constructed by convolutions of the orthogonality measures of the discrete orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme. Several explicit examples of the fermion systems with wide range interactions are presented.

Introduction

Here I report a simple construction of exactly solvable
(spinless) fermions with wide range interactions on a
one dimensional integer lattice.
Compared to fermion systems with the nearest neighbour interactions,
exactly solvable and wide range interactions are relatively hard to fathom.
The goal is achieved, following the general principle advocated in
[

by rewriting the stationary and reversible (detail balanced) Markov chain matrix
\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)

into a positive classical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)
by a similarity transformation in terms of
the square root of the stationary distribution
\pi(x)
.
The construction of many exactly solvable, stationary
and reversible Markov chain matrix
\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)

on one dimensional integer lattices
has been reported a few years earlier by Odake and myself
[
2
]
.
This reference will be cited as
I
in this paper.
Various convolutions of the orthogonality measures of the discrete orthogonal polynomials
of Askey scheme
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]

provide the desired forms of the reversible Markov chain matrix
\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)
.
The corresponding eigen polynomials are the Krawtchouk (K), Hahn (H),
q
-Hahn (
q

Meixner (M) and Charlier (C).

This paper is organised as follows. In section two, the general setting of the stationary and

In §

2
General setting; stationary and reversible Markov chains
\mathcal{K}^{R}

Let us start with a brief recapitulation of the general setting
of classical stationary Markov chains
\mathcal{K}

on a one dimensional integer lattice
\mathcal{X}
, finite or semi-infinite,

x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\ldots,N\},\quad N\in\mathbb{N},\quad x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

(2.1)

and points on
\mathcal{X}
are denoted by
x,y,z
for analytical treatments.
I use the convention that the transition probability matrix per
unit time interval
\mathcal{K}(x,y)
on
\mathcal{X}
means
the transition from an initial point
y
to a final point
x

with
\mathcal{K}(x,y)>0
and it satisfies the conservation of
the probability

\sum_{x\in{X}}\mathcal{K}(x,y)=1.

(2.2)

The positive
\mathcal{K}
means that all the points on
\mathcal{X}

are connected with each other by
\mathcal{K}

and this translates into wide range interactions.

Definition 2.1

Markov chain
\mathcal{K}^{R}
is reversible
(
detail balanced
)

if it has a reversible distribution
\pi
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)\pi(y)=\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x)\pi(x),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X};\quad\pi(x)>0,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)=1.

(2.3)

Taking
y
summation of the reversibility definition (
2.3
)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)\pi(y)=\pi(x)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x)=\pi(x).

(2.4)

leads to the following

Proposition 2.2

The reversible
\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)
has
a Perron-Frobenius eigenvector
\pi(x)
with the maximal and simple eigenvalue 1
and the range of spectrum

-1<\text{The moduli of the eigenvalues of }\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)\leq 1.

This is a consequence of the positivity,
i.e.
Perron-Frobenius theorem,
and the probability conservation (
2.2
).

Proposition 2.3

A positive Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}
, a real symmetric
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix,
is obtained by dividing the definition of the reversible Markov chain matrix
\mathcal{K}^{R}

(
2.3
) by
\sqrt{\pi(x)}\sqrt{\pi(y)}
,

\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}\,\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)\,\sqrt{\pi(y)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(y)}}\,\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x)\,\sqrt{\pi(x)}=\mathcal{H}^{R}(y,x),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.5)

It has the Perron-Frobenius eigenvector
\sqrt{\pi(x)}
,

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)\sqrt{\pi(y)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y)\pi(y)=\sqrt{\pi(x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x)=\sqrt{\pi(x)},

(2.6)

and its eigenvalues are all real,

-1<\text{The eigenvalues of }\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)\leq 1.

(2.7)

Those for the

The method is based on certain convolutions of the orthogonality measures of discrete orthogonal
polynomials of Askey scheme.
Normalised orthogonality measure, being positive, can always be a probability distribution.
Let us denote the normalised orthogonality measure with the explicit
N

\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda})>0,\qquad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda})=1,

(3.1)

\boldsymbol{\lambda}

3.1
Three types of convolutions

Here I present three types of convolutions among five reported in I
[
2
]
for simplicity and clarity;

\displaystyle\text{(i)}:

\displaystyle\ \ \mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{z=0}^{\min(x,y)}\!\pi(x-z,N-z,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(z,y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{(ii)}:

\displaystyle\ \ \mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{z=\max(0,x+y-N)}^{\min(x,y)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\pi(x-z,N-y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(z,y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\text{(iii)}:

\displaystyle\ \ \mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{z=\max(x,y)}^{N}\!\!\!\!\pi(x,z,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(z-y,N-y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}).

(3.4)

It is easy to verify the positivity and the conservation of the probability (
2.2
)
for each convolution.
The strategy is to find a good set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}
,

i=1,2,3
, such that the reversibility condition

\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(y,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})=\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}),

(3.5)

is satisfied. Obviously
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}
is a function of the parameters

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}
,
i=1,2
in
\mathcal{K}^{R}
.

The main result of
I

[
2
]
is the following

Theorem 3.1

(
Odake-Sasaki
)

The finite discrete orthogonal polynomials

\{\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\}
, whose orthogonality measure

\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})
provides the reversible distribution of

\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})
, constitute the left eigenvectors of

\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})=\kappa(n)\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}),\ -1<\kappa(n)\leq 1,\ x,n\in\mathcal{X},\ \kappa(0)=1.

(3.6)

The right eigenvectors are
\{\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(y,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(y,x;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=\kappa(n)\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}),\qquad x,n\in\mathcal{X}.

(3.7)

The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}
has the eigenvectors

\{\sqrt{\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})}\,\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\sqrt{\pi(y,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})}\,\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{x}}\mathcal{K}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\pi(y,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\,\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=\kappa(n)\sqrt{\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})}\,\check{P}_{n}(y,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}).

(3.8)

The normalisation constant of the polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x)

is determined by the universal normalisation condition

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(0,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=1,\qquad\quad\forall n\in\mathcal{X},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\check{P}_{m}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{m,n}}{d_{n}^{2}},\quad d_{n}>0,\quad m,n\in\mathcal{X}.

(3.10)

Of course, the constant
d_{n}
also depends on
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3}
but its dependence is suppressed for simplicity
of presentation.
It should be noted that, because of the context, the present definition of

is slightly different from previous one

[
1
,
6
]

The orthonormal eigenvectors of the classical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}
are
\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{R}\hat{\phi}_{n}=\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{n}\ \Leftrightarrow\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}d_{n}\sqrt{\pi(x,N,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})}\,\check{P}_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{3})\in\mathbb{R},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{m}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)=\delta_{m,n},\quad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\delta_{x,y}.

(3.12)

3.2
Fermion Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}

The fermion Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}
with wide range interactions is defined from the
classical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}
as
a bi-linear form of the lattice fermions
\{c_{x}\}
,
\{c_{x}^{\dagger}\}
on
\mathcal{X}

canonical anti-commutation relations,

\displaystyle\{c_{x}^{\dagger},c_{y}\}

\displaystyle=\delta_{x,y},\quad\{c_{x}^{\dagger},c_{y}^{\dagger}\}=0=\{c_{x},c_{y}\},\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}c_{x}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)c_{y},

(3.14)

in which the parameter dependence is suppressed for simplicity of presentation.

Theorem 3.2

The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}
is diagonalised by the introduction of the
the momentum space fermion operators

\{\hat{c}_{n}\}
,
\{\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\}
,
n\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)c_{x},\ \ \hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)c_{x}^{\dagger}\ \Leftrightarrow\ {c}_{x}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{c}_{n},\ \ {c}_{x}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{m}(x)\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger},

(3.15)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\Longrightarrow\ \{\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{n}\}=\delta_{m\,n},\ \{\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\}=0=\{\hat{c}_{m},\hat{c}_{n}\},

(3.16)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\Downarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}=\sum_{m,n,x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{\phi}_{m}(x)\mathcal{H}^{R}(x,y)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n}=\sum_{m,n,x\in\mathcal{X}}\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{m}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n}

\displaystyle\phantom{\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f}}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{X}}\kappa(n)\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n},

(3.17)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\Longrightarrow\ [\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f},\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}]=\kappa(n)\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger},\qquad[\mathcal{H}^{R}_{f},\hat{c}_{n}]=-\kappa(n)\hat{c}_{n}.

(3.18)
