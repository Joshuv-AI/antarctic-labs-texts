Cardiac Rehabilitation for All
Authors: Andrew Maiorana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Cardiac rehabilitation emerged in the 1960s as a program of gentle mobilization for patients recovering from myocardial infarction, challenging the prevailing practice of prolonged bed rest.1 Since then, both the scope and delivery of cardiac rehabilitation have evolved substantially. Early mobilization has been replaced by individually prescribed, progressive aerobic and resistance exercise integrated with comprehensive secondary prevention strategies. Concurrently, eligibility for cardiac rehabilitation has expanded beyond patients recovering from myocardial infarction to include those with a broad spectrum of cardiovascular conditions, including heart failure, congenital heart disease, and individuals recovering from cardiac procedures and surgery.

Despite this evolution, the evidence supporting cardiac rehabilitation remains largely derived from studies involving patients with myocardial infarction or those undergoing coronary revascularization.2 Comparatively, there is limited evidence regarding the effectiveness of cardiac rehabilitation at improving important clinical outcomes, including major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, among other patient populations who are routinely referred.

The study published in this issue of JACC: Asia3 reports a retrospective analysis of cardiac rehabilitation in 1,710 patients who underwent open thoracic aortic surgical repair between 2003 and 2015. Although patients in this group are accepted into some cardiac rehabilitation programs, evidence regarding clinical outcomes is lacking. Consequently, these patients are often excluded from cardiac rehabilitation when program capacity is constrained or because of historical concerns regarding the safety of exercise in patients with a history of aortic aneurysms. The study conducted by Yamazaki et al3 found that cardiac rehabilitation was associated with a reduced risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality at 5-year follow-up, a benefit that also tended to extend to former smokers in subgroup analysis, although this did not reach statistical significance.

The cardiac rehabilitation program evaluated in the study adopted a comprehensive model of care, integrating supervised exercise with education and counseling for patients who required additional support. The exercise intervention incorporated both aerobic and resistance training, consistent with current cardiac rehabilitation guidelines.4 The inclusion of resistance training is particularly noteworthy, as it was introduced within 1 month of commencing cardiac rehabilitation. This is clinically relevant given that sarcopenia affects more than half of patients undergoing aortic aneurysm surgery5 who commonly experience substantial losses in skeletal muscle mass and strength. These impairments are further exacerbated by the physiological stress of surgery and the prolonged periods of reduced physical activity during recovery. Importantly, the absence of acute disease exacerbations or emergency hospitalizations during, or within 24 hours following exercise sessions provides reassuring evidence that resistance training is well tolerated in patients recovering from thoracic aortic surgery.

Although these findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted in light of several important limitations. First, the retrospective study design is inherently susceptible to selection bias, including self-selection of patients who participated in cardiac rehabilitation. Second, the reported duration of cardiac rehabilitation (150 days) substantially exceeds the duration of conventional programs, which typically last 6-12 weeks.6 Although participants were required to remain enrolled in cardiac rehabilitation for at least 90 days to be included in the analysis, the study did not report adherence to the prescribed intervention or attendance rates throughout the program. Consequently, the extent to which patients engaged with the rehabilitation program over time remains unclear. Finally, although propensity score matching was used to reduce baseline differences between the cardiac rehabilitation and nonrehabilitation groups, this approach can account only for measured confounders and is unlikely to have fully eliminated residual confounding arising from unmeasured differences between groups. Accordingly, further studies, including randomized controlled trials, are required to confirm these results.

Cardiac rehabilitation is a fundamental component of multidisciplinary cardiovascular care. However, limitations in health care resources and reimbursement models often restrict access to patients who do not meet traditional eligibility criteria. The findings of this study add to the growing body of evidence supporting the benefits of cardiac rehabilitation in patient populations who have not historically been prioritized for referral. These results provide a compelling rationale for the routine inclusion of patients recovering from thoracic aortic aneurysm surgery in cardiac rehabilitation programs and reinforce the broader principle that equitable access to cardiac rehabilitation should be considered a standard of care for all eligible patients with cardiovascular disease.
