Correlations Between Serum Vitamin D, Synovial Succinic Acid Levels and Disease Activity in Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Xuwen Zha, Yue Liu, Ying Jiang, Shengting Ruan, Bo Yang, Fei Liu, Shan Wang, Shengqian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune synovitis characterized by progressive joint destruction. Vitamin D exerts immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects, and its insufficiency is highly prevalent in RA and closely related to exacerbated inflammatory status. Synovial succinate acts as a pro-inflammatory metabolic mediator that aggravates intra-articular inflammation. However, the interactive associations among vitamin D, synovial succinate, and RA disease activity remain poorly defined. This cross-sectional study aimed to elucidate their correlations in RA patients.MethodsA total of 120 RA patients were enrolled and stratified according to serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels (deficiency: < 20 µg/L). Demographic parameters, 28-joint Disease Activity Score (DAS-28), serum C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and synovial succinate levels were compared between groups. Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate correlations and independent determinants of disease activity.ResultsRA patients with vitamin D deficiency presented a significantly lower BMI, higher serum CRP and elevated synovial succinic acid levels compared with those without vitamin D deficiency, whereas no inter-group differences in DAS-28 scores and ESR were observed. Correlation analysis revealed that serum vitamin D levels were negatively correlated with CRP (P = 0.012), ESR (P = 0.049) and synovial succinic acid levels (P = 0.030), while no significant correlation was found between vitamin D and DAS-28 scores. Additionally, synovial succinic acid levels were positively correlated with DAS-28 scores (P = 0.002), CRP (P = 0.035) and ESR (P = 0.031), respectively. Multiple linear regression analysis validated that age, ESR and synovial succinic acid were independent positive influencing factors for increased DAS-28 scores in RA patients.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in RA patients, which is associated with elevated synovial succinate. Synovial succinate is significantly correlated with disease activity and serves as an independent influencing factor for DAS-28, together with age and ESR. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings confirm associative rather than causal relationships.Keywords: relationship, rheumatoid arthritis, succinic acid, disease activity, vitamin D

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) represents a prevalent chronic autoimmune disease pathologically defined by sustained synovial inflammation and hyperplasia. Such persistent inflammatory lesions progressively trigger irreversible cartilage deterioration and bony destruction, ultimately leading to joint dysfunction and disability.1 As a global public health challenge, RA affects approximately 1% of adults worldwide and imposes enormous socioeconomic burdens on individuals and healthcare systems.2 The multifactorial etiology of RA is collectively driven by genetic predisposition, aberrant immune activation, and exogenous environmental stimuli.3 Currently, the clinical management of RA adheres to a stratified step-up therapeutic regimen.4 In this framework, methotrexate serves as the first-line conventional disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (DMARD), whereas glucocorticoids, biological agents, and targeted synthetic DMARDs are administered as complementary alternatives for disease control. Nevertheless, nearly 20–30% of RA patients fail to achieve satisfactory clinical remission following standardized interventions, presenting with unabated inflammatory activity and continuous joint deterioration.5 Attainment of clinical remission in RA is modulated by multiple modifiable and non-modifiable factors, among which disease duration and compliance with standard therapy are well-acknowledged determinants. Even under comparable treatment conditions, considerable inter-patient variability in remission rates still exists, implying the involvement of additional regulatory mediators. This unmet clinical gap underscores the urgent necessity to decipher the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms of RA and develop innovative targeted therapeutic strategies.

Vitamin D, a lipid-soluble nutrient best recognized for its regulatory function in calcium and phosphate homeostasis, has been extensively validated to exert pleiotropic biological effects, encompassing anti-inflammation, immune modulation and antioxidation.6 Accumulating clinical and basic research has proven that vitamin D insufficiency is tightly correlated with the onset and progression of multiple immune-mediated inflammatory disorders, including RA.7 Consistent with this notion, multiple cohort studies have verified that RA patients exhibit a higher incidence of vitamin D deficiency, accompanied by significantly lower circulating levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the gold-standard biomarker for evaluating endogenous vitamin D reserves.8,9 Additionally, circulating 25(OH)D levels have been confirmed to be negatively associated with RA disease severity;10 correspondingly, vitamin D supplementation can effectively ameliorate partial inflammatory indicators in affected patients.11 Our previous research further corroborated that vitamin D deficiency exacerbates systemic inflammation, elevates disease activity, and increases atherogenic risk among RA patients complicated with cardiovascular diseases (CVD).12 All these lines of evidence indicate that vitamin D is closely involved in the development and progression of RA.

Innovative advances in immunometabolism have uncovered the critical regulatory role of metabolic intermediates in inflammatory responses. Succinate, a core metabolite of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, tends to accumulate abundantly in inflamed tissues and initiates pro-inflammatory signaling cascades by binding to its specific receptor SUCNR1.13 For RA, synovial fluid directly mirrors the metabolic and inflammatory status of the intra-articular microenvironment. A prior study revealed markedly increased succinate abundance in the synovial fluid of RA patients compared with osteoarthritis counterparts.14 Mechanistically, extracellular succinate can stimulate macrophage secretion of pro-inflammatory interleukin-1β (IL-1β) via a SUCNR1-dependent pathway, thereby aggravating arthritic pathological changes.15 Subsequent metabolomic analyses further identified succinate as a promising diagnostic biomarker to differentiate RA from other inflammatory arthropathies, such as gout and ankylosing spondylitis.14 Despite these groundbreaking findings, few studies have systematically clarified the associations between intra-synovial succinate levels and disease activity of RA.

Given the dual regulatory roles of vitamin D in immune homeostasis and succinate in local joint inflammation, exploring the potential crosstalk between these two factors may provide a novel mechanistic insight into RA pathogenesis. Existing evidence has demonstrated that vitamin D deficiency correlates with aggravated systemic inflammation,16 while succinate accumulation within synovial tissues fuels localized joint inflammation.15 A recent mechanistic study has provided preliminary evidence supporting the interplay between vitamin D signaling and succinate metabolism, revealing that vitamin D receptor (VDR) deficiency triggers extensive lysine hypersuccinylation and alters the activity of key tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes in white adipose tissue.17 However, this existing evidence is limited to adipose tissue contexts. Notably, no studies have yet investigated whether vitamin D modulates succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity or TCA cycle flux in synovial and immune cells, the primary effector cells that mediate synovial inflammation in RA. Collectively, although the independent proinflammatory roles of systemic vitamin D insufficiency and local synovial succinate overload are well documented, a major knowledge gap persists regarding their direct interaction in the RA joint microenvironment. It remains unclear whether circulating vitamin D status regulates synovial succinate accumulation via modulating TCA cycle and SDH function, and whether elevated intra-articular succinate is correlated with reduced serum 25(OH)D levels. Clarifying this potential correlation may help elucidate a novel pathogenic mechanism, whereby vitamin D deficiency exacerbates articular inflammation by reshaping metabolic profiles of the joint microenvironment. Accordingly, the present study aimed to analyze the correlation between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and synovial fluid succinate levels, and further explore their respective associations with clinical disease activity and severity in patients with RA.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

A total of 120 patients with RA were enrolled at The First People’s Hospital of Hefei between October 2024 and April 2026. The age range of the enrolled RA patients was 29 to 88 years, with a mean age of 62.4 years. Eligibility for study participation required meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of RA according to the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria; (3) disease duration ≥ 6 months at the time of enrollment, to ensure a well-established diagnosis and exclude patients with transient or undifferentiated arthritis; and (4) stable disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) therapy and/or corticosteroid treatment for at least 3 months prior to sample collection/procedure, with no changes in dosage or regimen during this period. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) use of TNF-α antagonists and/or IL-6 receptor inhibitors within the previous three months; (2) intake of vitamin D supplements within the previous three months; (3) presence of pregnancy, malignancies, infectious diseases, or an active infectious phase. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Hefei (approval number: 2024–333-01) and was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants gave written informed consent after receiving a thorough explanation of the study.

Demographic and Clinical Data Collection

Demographic characteristics, including age, height, weight, sex, and routine laboratory parameters, were obtained from medical records or patient self-reports. A specialist rheumatologist evaluated disease activity using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS-28). The DAS-28 calculation was based on the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), counts of tender and swollen joints among the 28 assessed (TJC28 and SJC28, respectively), patient-assessed general health (GH) measured on a 100-mm visual analogue scale (where 0 indicates best and 100 worst health), and the acute phase reactant level (ESR in mm/h). The specific formula used was: 0.56 × (TJC28) + 0.28 × (SJC28) + 0.014 × GH + 0.70 × ln(ESR).

Blood Sample Collection and Measurement of Vitamin D

Blood samples were obtained from participants between 7:00 and 8:00 a.m. Specimens were centrifuged at 1200g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used as serum samples. These samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum 25(OH)D levels were quantified using the commercially available Roche Elecsys Vitamin D total II electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on a Cobas E801 auto analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH).

Synovial Fluid Sample Collection and Measurement of Succinic Acid

All 120 enrolled patients with RA included in the present study presented with clinically detectable joint effusion and thus met the standard clinical indications for diagnostic or therapeutic arthrocentesis. Synovial fluid samples were successfully obtained from all 120 participants via standardized sterile arthrocentesis procedures, and synovial succinate measurement was performed in the entire study cohort rather than a subset. Fresh specimens were placed on ice immediately after collection. Within 30 minutes, each sample was centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to remove cellular debris and particulate matter. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted and stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80 °C until subsequent biochemical analysis. Succinic acid levels were measured using a specific liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) technique, specifically an Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (TQ-XS) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were accomplished with SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was adopted to examine data normality. Between-group comparisons relied on independent Student’s t-test for normally distributed parameters, whereas non-normal datasets were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Bivariate associations among synovial succinic acid, circulating vitamin D and DAS-28 disease activity were evaluated by Pearson correlation analysis. To reduce the risk of Type I error arising from multiple correlation analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied. Variables independently associated with DAS-28 were subsequently identified via multiple linear regression modelling. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Comparative Analysis of Demographic Characteristics, DAS-28 Scores, CRP, ESR, and Synovial Succinic Acid Levels in RA Patients with and without Vitamin D Deficiency

Vitamin D deficiency was defined as a serum 25(OH)D level below 20 µg/L, and its prevalence among RA patients was 46.7% (56/120). As shown in Table 1, no statistically significant differences were observed in age or gender between the deficiency and non-deficiency groups (P > 0.05). BMI was significantly lower in the deficiency group than in the non-deficiency group (t = 2.092, P = 0.039). No notable differences were found in DAS-28 scores or ESR between the two groups (P > 0.05). Serum CRP levels were significantly higher in the deficiency group compared with the non-deficiency group (t = −2.033, P = 0.046). In addition, synovial succinic acid levels were markedly elevated in the vitamin D deficiency group relative to the non-deficiency group (t = −3.173, P = 0.002).

Correlation of Serum Vitamin D Concentrations with DAS-28 Scores, CRP, ESR, and Synovial Succinic Acid Levels in Patients with RA

As illustrated in Figure 1A, no significant correlations were detected between serum vitamin D levels and DAS-28 scores in patients with RA (r = −0.119, P = 0.205). The serum vitamin D levels were negatively related to the serum CRP levels (r = −0.228, P = 0.012) and ESR (r = −0.181, P = 0.049) in patients with RA (Figure 1B and C). Additionally, a negative correlation was observed between the serum vitamin D levels and the synovial succinic acid levels in patients with RA (r = −0.198, P = 0.030; Figure 1D). After Bonferroni correction, only the negative correlation between serum vitamin D and CRP remained statistically significant (P = 0.012 < 0.05/4).

Correlation of Synovial Succinic Acid Levels with DAS-28 Scores, CRP, and ESR in Patients with RA

As depicted in Figure 2, synovial succinic acid levels were positively correlated with DAS-28 scores (r = 0.283, P = 0.002), CRP (r = 0.193, P = 0.035), and ESR (r = 0.198, P = 0.031) in patients with RA. After Bonferroni correction, only the positive correlation between synovial succinic acid and DAS-28 scores retained statistical significance (P = 0.002 < 0.05/3).

Associated Factors for DAS-28 Scores in Patients with RA: A Multiple Linear Regression Analysis

Multiple linear regression was performed to screen independent influencing factors of DAS-28 disease activity scores among patients with RA (Table 2). Regression modelling revealed that age, ESR, and synovial succinic acid concentration were independent positive contributors to elevated DAS-28 levels. To be specific, DAS-28 scores were positively associated with age (B = 0.198, t = 2.185, P = 0.031), ESR (B = 0.322, t = 2.686, P = 0.008), and synovial succinic acid (B = 0.222, t = 2.559, P = 0.012). The overall model test showed that the regression model was statistically significant (F = 5.232, P < 0.001), with an unadjusted R2 of 0.255 and an adjusted R2 of 0.206. Collectively, the model explained 20.6% of the variance in RA disease activity (DAS-28 scores), indicating a moderate explanatory effect of the included variables on disease activity.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate differences in demographic characteristics, disease activity indicators, and synovial succinic acid levels between RA patients with and without vitamin D deficiency, and to explore the correlations among serum vitamin D concentration, synovial succinic acid, and RA disease activity. Four key findings emerged. Firstly, RA patients with vitamin D deficiency exhibited significantly lower BMI, higher serum CRP, and elevated synovial succinic acid levels compared with those without vitamin D deficiency. Secondly, serum vitamin D levels were negatively correlated with CRP, ESR, and synovial succinic acid levels. Thirdly, synovial succinic acid levels were positively correlated with DAS-28 scores, CRP, and ESR. Fourthly, age, ESR, and synovial succinic acid were independent positive influencing factors for increased DAS-28 scores in RA patients.

Vitamin D deficiency is substantially more prevalent among patients with RA than in the general population, with published prevalence estimates varying widely from 40% to 85% depending on geographical location and individual risk characteristics. A prior meta-analysis pooling data from 24 independent studies encompassing 3489 RA patients calculated a pooled vitamin D deficiency prevalence of 54%, further confirming prominent geographical disparities across cohorts.9 Region-specific epidemiological data from China additionally documented a deficiency rate up to 68.5% in domestic RA populations.18 In line with these earlier epidemiological observations, the current work yielded an overall vitamin D deficiency prevalence of 46.7% (56/120) in enrolled RA subjects. These results further confirm the high burden of vitamin D insufficiency in RA populations, even though the observed prevalence in our cohort is slightly lower than the global pooled estimate and some regional figures. Differences in sample characteristics, geographic latitude, seasonal variation, dietary habits, and supplemental vitamin D intake may account for these discrepancies. Given the potential impact of vitamin D deficiency on bone metabolism, immune regulation, and disease activity in RA, routine screening and appropriate supplementation should be considered as part of comprehensive management in this patient population.

The present study revealed that among RA patients, vitamin D deficiency is associated with lower BMI, heightened systemic inflammation (reflected by CRP and ESR), and elevated synovial succinic acid levels. The negative correlations between serum vitamin D and both inflammatory markers and synovial succinate suggest a potential protective role of vitamin D in modulating joint metabolic inflammation. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanistic link between vitamin D status and RA pathogenesis, particularly through the lens of immunometabolism. The observed inverse correlation between vitamin D levels and CRP/ESR aligns well with existing literature. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation significantly reduces CRP levels in RA patients.19 Another systematic review confirmed that vitamin D supplementation improves DAS-28 scores and reduces both CRP and ESR, supporting the anti-inflammatory capacity of vitamin D in RA management.11 These immunomodulatory effects are mechanistically attributed to vitamin D’s ability to promote regulatory T cell differentiation, inhibit Th17 cell polarization, and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production including IL-17 and IL-1β.

More notably, our study identified a novel association between vitamin D deficiency and elevated synovial succinic acid levels, with a significant negative correlation between serum vitamin D and succinate. Succinate has emerged as a critical immunometabolite in RA pathogenesis. It has been demonstrated that succinate accumulates in the synovial fluid of RA patients and activates macrophages via its receptor GPR91, creating a feed-forward loop that potentiates IL-1β production.15 Furthermore, succinate has been identified as one of the most significantly upregulated metabolites in the synovial fluid of RA patients compared to other inflammatory arthritides, suggesting its diagnostic and pathogenic significance.14 A potential explanation for the link between vitamin D and succinate metabolism comes from recent evidence that vitamin D receptor (VDR) deficiency induces widespread lysine hypersuccinylation in white adipose tissue, with a notable impact on tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes including isocitrate dehydrogenase.17 This raises the intriguing possibility that vitamin D signaling regulates succinate homeostasis at the metabolic level, and its deficiency may promote succinate accumulation through dysregulated TCA cycle flux.

The finding that vitamin D-deficient RA patients exhibited significantly lower BMI warrants particularly cautious interpretation. Although population-based studies have consistently demonstrated an inverse association between BMI and vitamin D status, the reduced BMI observed in vitamin D-deficient RA patients appears counterintuitive to the well-documented obesity-hypovitamin D relationship in the general population. Existing RA-specific evidence also indicates that lower vitamin D levels are commonly correlated with increased BMI and metabolic dysfunction in this patient group.20 One highly speculative explanation for this counterintuitive finding involves rheumatoid cachexia, though we emphasize this interpretation remains unconfirmed in the present cohort due to the absence of detailed body composition data (e.g, lean mass, fat mass, and muscle mass quantification). Rheumatoid cachexia is defined as chronic inflammation-driven tissue wasting characterized by progressive muscle and fat depletion, which may theoretically reduce overall BMI in highly active RA. In line with this hypothetical mechanism, vitamin D-deficient patients in our cohort presented with higher CRP levels, which may reflect enhanced systemic inflammatory burden that could potentially contribute to cachexia-like metabolic alterations. Nevertheless, without objective body composition measurements, we cannot verify whether the lower BMI observed in our participants stems from inflammation-induced tissue loss or other confounding factors. An alternative plausible explanation is that patients with more active RA and aggravated vitamin D deficiency may experience reduced physical activity and limited outdoor sunlight exposure, which could concurrently lower BMI and exacerbate hypovitaminosis D. Collectively, the cross-sectional study design and the lack of body composition data restrict any causal or mechanistic conclusion. The bidirectional interplay among inflammation, physical activity, BMI, and vitamin D status remains complex, and the rheumatoid cachexia hypothesis requires further validation in future studies with precise body composition assessment.

The present study further reveals that synovial succinic acid levels are positively correlated with DAS-28 scores, CRP, and ESR in RA patients. Consistent with existing metabolomic findings, these correlational results support the notion that succinate is not merely a metabolic byproduct of joint inflammation, but a key immunometabolite closely associated with RA disease activity. The consistent positive correlations of synovial succinate with both composite clinical activity scores (DAS-28) and objective acute-phase markers (CRP and ESR) suggest that synovial succinate accumulation is statistically linked to heightened inflammatory burden in active RA. The correlation between succinate and DAS-28 is particularly informative, as DAS-28 comprehensively reflects RA disease activity by integrating both clinical and serological inflammatory indicators. These observational findings align with previous metabolomic studies that have identified succinate as a consistently upregulated metabolite in RA synovial fluid, with potential biomarker value for distinguishing inflammatory arthritis phenotypes.14 Extensive preclinical and mechanistic studies have established that succinate acts as a pro-inflammatory metabolite primarily through activating the GPR91 (SUCNR1) signaling axis in synovial fibroblasts, macrophages, and dendritic cells.21 Although the cross-sectional design of the present study cannot confirm causal or mechanistic pathways, existing literature provides plausible hypothetical mechanisms that may explain the observed clinical correlations. In hypoxic and inflamed RA synovial tissues, altered SDH activity is known to drive intracellular succinate accumulation in synovial stromal and immune cells.22 Based on prior experimental evidence, succinate buildup may potentially contribute to persistent synovial inflammation through multiple downstream regulatory pathways: it may stabilize HIF-1α to facilitate synovial angiogenesis and glycolytic metabolic reprogramming;23 activate macrophage GPR91 signaling to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β secretion;15 and enhance the migratory and invasive capacities of fibroblast-like synoviocytes, which may promote synovial hyperplasia and pannus formation.24 In addition, succinate may serve as a potential intermediate linking IL-1β/TGF-β1-mediated inflammatory and fibrotic responses, which have been reported to sustain chronic synovial inflammation in RA.25 Importantly, we emphasize that these downstream mechanistic cascades and self-sustaining inflammatory loop models are derived from published cellular and animal studies rather than verified in the present RA cohort. The current study only supports correlational associations between synovial succinate and disease activity, and future mechanistic investigations are required to validate whether a succinate-associated inflammatory amplification network operates in human RA synovial tissue.

From a clinical standpoint, these results indicate that detecting succinate in synovial fluid may act as a complementary biomarker for evaluating disease activity in RA. Although CRP and ESR are widely used systemic inflammatory markers, they fail to directly mirror the metabolic and inflammatory status within the joint cavity. In contrast, succinate is locally generated in inflamed articular tissues, and may offer additional insights into synovial metabolism as well as hypoxia-mediated inflammation.26 Future studies should explore whether serial measurements of synovial succinate (potentially via non-invasive imaging techniques or synovial fluid analysis) can track treatment response or predict flare in individual patients.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-center, cross-sectional investigation with a relatively modest sample size of 120 participants, which limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes the establishment of causal relationships between vitamin D status, synovial succinate accumulation, and RA disease activity. Longitudinal prospective studies with larger, multicenter cohorts are therefore required to validate the observed correlations and clarify potential causal mechanisms. Second, multiple correlation analyses were performed on a set of interrelated clinical and inflammatory variables, which increased the risk of Type I error. Although we applied Bonferroni correction to optimize statistical rigor in the revised analysis, some potential associations may be excluded due to the strict correction threshold. Third, although strict exclusion criteria were applied, unmeasured confounding factors including individual dietary patterns, seasonal sunlight exposure, physical activity levels, and long-term medication history may still interfere with vitamin D metabolism and intra-articular metabolic status, potentially introducing residual bias to the statistical results. Fourth, there exists inherent methodological confounding in the analyses of DAS-28, as ESR is a mandatory mathematical component of the DAS-28 scoring system. Accordingly, the observed correlation between synovial succinate and DAS-28 is partially attributed to structural statistical overlap rather than a purely independent biological association. This mechanical coupling may artificially strengthen the succinate-DAS-28 relationship and overestimate its biological relevance to RA disease activity. Therefore, this correlation should be interpreted with caution. Future studies adopting ESR-independent disease activity indices or optimized statistical adjustments are warranted to eliminate such structural confounding and validate the true biological association. Fifth, this study lacked an a priori statistical power calculation for prospective sample size determination. The total sample size of 120 patients is only statistically powered to identify small-to-moderate effect sizes. Importantly, several correlations between serum 25(OH)D, synovial succinate levels, and RA disease activity observed in this study were relatively weak, which are close to the minimum effect size threshold that can be reliably detected by a sample size of 120. Therefore, these marginal correlational results are subject to potential statistical instability and should be interpreted with caution. Future well-designed studies with larger independent cohorts and pre-specified a priori power calculations are warranted to further validate the subtle associations identified in the present study and strengthen the statistical robustness of the conclusions. Sixth, the adjusted R2 of the regression model was 0.206, demonstrating that the included variables only explained a moderate proportion of DAS-28 variance, which further indicates that additional unmeasured clinical and biological factors contribute to RA disease activity. This limited explanatory power also suggests that the predictive value of the current model is restricted, consistent with the modest effect sizes observed in our correlation analyses. Finally, this study failed to enroll patients with other inflammatory arthritis or healthy controls for comparative analysis, which restricts the evaluation of the specific diagnostic value of synovial succinate for RA. Collectively, these shortcomings highlight the need for further in vivo and in vitro mechanistic experiments combined with large-scale prospective clinical studies to deepen the understanding of the immunometabolic interaction between vitamin D and succinate in RA pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this single-center cross-sectional observational study reveals a high prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency in patients with RA, and demonstrates preliminary correlational relationships between low serum vitamin D status, elevated systemic inflammatory markers, and increased synovial succinate levels. Synovial succinate concentration was positively associated with RA disease activity and independently correlated with higher DAS-28 scores in our cohort. Importantly, the present observational findings generate a novel hypothesis for a potential clinical linkage between systemic vitamin D deficiency and intra-articular succinate accumulation in RA, suggesting that a putative vitamin D-succinate axis may plausibly modulate local joint inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation. We explicitly emphasize that this axis remains an unconfirmed hypothesis rather than an established mechanistic finding.

Collectively, our observational data suggest that synovial succinate may have preliminary potential as a candidate complementary biomarker for RA disease activity, though this hypothesis requires rigorous external validation. The observed association between synovial succinate and inflammatory activity indicates that succinate metabolism could serve as a local immunometabolic mediator in active RA. However, consistent with the cross-sectional study design, we stress that the biomarker utility of synovial succinate cannot be fully confirmed based on the current dataset. Large-scale, multicenter, prospective longitudinal studies are essential to validate its diagnostic and prognostic value before any clinical application can be considered.

Additionally, our findings highlight the clinical relevance of vitamin D status in RA populations. The high prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency and its observed correlation with systemic inflammation and synovial succinate accumulation tentatively support routine vitamin D screening in RA patients. Vitamin D supplementation for deficient individuals may be beneficial not only for bone protection but also for potentially regulating immunometabolic homeostasis, although anti-inflammatory efficacy remains speculative and requires further clinical evidence.

The present study primarily serves to generate hypotheses regarding the vitamin D–succinate regulatory axis and succinate-based biomarker potential in RA. Future mechanistic investigations and interventional trials are warranted to verify the causal interplay between vitamin D signaling and synovial succinate metabolism, and to determine whether vitamin D supplementation can reduce synovial succinate levels and mitigate RA inflammation. Further validations will clarify whether targeting the vitamin D-succinate axis represents a viable strategy for optimizing RA clinical management.
