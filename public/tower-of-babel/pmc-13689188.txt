Risk factors and prediction model of renal dysfunction in patients with inflammatory bowel disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yide Zhou, Kefang Sun, Xia Chen, Xian Shen, Junyin Chen, Liying Wang, Chunxiao Chen, Huogen Wang, Yidong Wan, Liping Yang, Lan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have identified the association between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and its renal complications, but specific risk factors and predictive models remain underexplored.AimsTo identify the risk factors for renal dysfunction in IBD patients and to develop a clinical model for the early detection of high-risk individuals.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 769 patients diagnosed with IBD from a tertiary hospital for model training and internal validation, and 298 patients from four other hospitals for external validation. A Cox regression model incorporating seven selected variables, identified through LASSO regression, was developed to determine independent risk factors, and its performance was subsequently validated in both internal and external cohorts.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified seven independent predictors of renal insufficiency [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2], including osteoporosis (HR: 1.77; P = 0.024), baseline eGFR (HR: 0.29; P < 0.001), baseline high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (HR: 0.52; P = 0.033), baseline C-reactive protein (CRP) (HR: 1.00; P = 0.09), biologic therapy (HR: 0.57; P = 0.011), enteral nutrition (HR: 1.86; P = 0.007), and intestinal surgery (HR: 1.64; P = 0.01). A predictive model was developed with a C-index of 0.719 for predicting renal insufficiency and 0.739 for predicting chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2).ConclusionsPredictors of renal dysfunction in IBD patients included osteoporosis, eGFR, HDL, CRP, biologic therapy, enteral nutrition, and intestinal surgery. Our predictive model can effectively identify IBD populations at high risk for renal dysfunction at an early stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05147-z.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by chronic, nonspecific inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, primarily manifesting as ulcerative colitis (UC) or Crohn’s disease (CD). Although IBD predominantly affects the gastrointestinal tract, it can also involve multiple extraintestinal organs. Common manifestations include joint disorders, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and skin lesions such as erythema nodosum and pyoderma gangrenosum [1]. Among these, renal complications are particularly concerning due to their severity and potential life-threatening outcome, sometimes posing a greater risk than the underlying intestinal disease itself [2].

Early studies have identified renal complications as extraintestinal manifestations of IBD, including kidney stones, secondary amyloidosis, proteinuria, and glomerulonephritis [3, 4]. These conditions can progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and, ultimately, kidney failure, which is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Recent meta-analytic evidence indicates an increased risk of CKD among patients with IBD, with a pooled odds ratio of 1.59 (95% CI 1.31–1.93) compared with non-IBD controls [5]. Studies have suggested that risk factors for CKD in hospitalized IBD patients include advanced age and longer disease duration [6], while population-based epidemiological studies have demonstrated that the relative hazard of CKD caused by IBD declines with increasing age [7]. Recent research has further identified intestinal surgery as an independent risk factor for renal dysfunction in IBD patients [8, 9]. However, most existing studies have not evaluated the association between laboratory markers or extraintestinal manifestations and the risk of CKD. Also, many studies have not adequately controlled for traditional risk factors for CKD, such as coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. As a result, the contribution of IBD-related factors to CKD risk remains insufficiently defined.

This study aimed to identify risk factors for renal dysfunction in IBD patients and to construct a clinical data-based model to enable early identification of high-risk individuals.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a retrospective cohort analysis. Data for the training and internal validation cohorts were collected from patients diagnosed with IBD (ICD-9: 555–556; ICD-10: K50, K51) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2014 and December 2021. Inclusion criteria were: age > 14 years with a diagnosis of CD, UC or IBD-unclassified. Exclusion criteria were: (1) baseline renal insufficiency (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) at first visit; (2) loss to follow-up exceeding 6 months; and (3) absence of renal function monitoring during follow-up.

To assess the robustness and generalizability of the model, we also included an external validation cohort from four additional tertiary hospitals. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to patients enrolled between January 2016 and March 2022. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2024 − 0674). The institutional review boards of each participating hospital granted a waiver of informed consent, as only de-identified data were used for academic research and model validation purposes.

Variables assessment and outcomes definition

Population characteristics were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record system. At enrollment, data collection included: demographic information (age and gender); disease classification (IBD subtype); body mass index (BMI); comorbidities; extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs); and IBD-specific laboratory parameters and endoscopic scores. Osteoporosis was identified based on diagnosis in the electronic medical record, ICD-10 coding (M80-M81), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) report, or fragility fracture. Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease was stratified as follows: grade 0 (0–2 points); grade 1 (3–6 points); grade 2 (7–15 points); and grade 3 (> 16 points) [10]. Endoscopic assessment of UC was quantified using the Mayo Endoscopic Score, with equivalent ordinal grading (0–3) reflecting progressive mucosal damage from normal pattern (grade 0) to ulcerations (grade 3) [11]. Serial measurements of serum creatinine and eGFR, as well as treatment details and surgical interventions, were recorded at baseline and during follow-up visits at 1–6 month intervals. The follow-up period ended at the first occurrence of renal insufficiency, CKD, or on December 31, 2024, whichever occurred first. The primary outcome was renal insufficiency, defined as an eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² on at least two consecutive measurements separated by ≥ 3 months. An eGFR threshold of 90 mL/min/1.73 m² was chosen to identify early renal function decline, which may precede overt chronic kidney disease and provide an opportunity for earlier risk stratification and intervention. Secondary outcomes included chronic kidney disease (CKD), defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² on at least two consecutive measurements separated by ≥ 3 months or a physician-documented diagnosis of CKD according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [12]. Transient, single-occurrence eGFR dips attributable to acute illness or dehydration were not counted as endpoint events.

Construction and evaluation of the model

Patients from the primary cohort were randomly assigned to the training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets using simple random sampling in R (version 4.2.0) with the “createDataPartition function” from the “caret” package and a fixed random seed (set.seed = 2025) to ensure reproducibility. Although stratified sampling by renal insufficiency status was not performed, event rates were comparable between the two sets (26.8% vs. 28.9%), indicating adequate balance following randomization. The training set was used to develop the predictive model for the 3-year risk of renal insufficiency and CKD, while the internal validation set was used to assess performance of the model. Patients from four additional hospitals were included in the external validation cohort to evaluate the performance in an independent population.

Model development followed a three-stage workflow. First, all variables with less than 20% missing data were entered into Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation on the training cohort; the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was selected using the 1-standard-error (1-SE) rule, and variables with non-zero coefficients at this λ were retained. Second, these LASSO-selected variables were incorporated into a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. Third, to derive the final parsimonious model, backward elimination was performed, retaining only variables with P < 0.1 in the multivariate Cox analysis.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the final model. First, renal events occurring within 30 days after intestinal surgery were excluded to minimize potential confounding from acute perioperative dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, or transient hemodynamic instability. Second, biologic therapies were stratified according to the predominant treatment received by each patient, including anti-tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF) agents, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab; Janus kinase inhibitors were not included in this stratified analysis because of the limited sample size (< 10 cases). Third, patients aged ≥ 65 years were excluded to minimize the potential influence of age-related renal decline. Fourth, to evaluate the robustness of the findings under stricter renal endpoints, the final Cox model was refitted using CKD, defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for > 3 months or physician-documented CKD, and sustained > 30% decline in eGFR from baseline during follow-up as alternative outcomes. Fifth, because dichotomization of continuous variables may lead to information loss, the final Cox model was refitted with baseline eGFR modeled as a continuous variable, while HDL and CRP were retained as continuous predictors. Restricted cubic spline analyses were further performed to explore potential non-linear associations between baseline eGFR, HDL, CRP, and renal insufficiency. Finally, because five imputed datasets may provide limited stability for standard error estimation, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using 20 imputed datasets to refit the final Cox model and assess the robustness of the pooled estimates.

To contextualize our IBD-specific model, we performed an exploratory comparison with two established CKD progression models: the Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE) and KDpredict [13, 14]. These models were selected because they represent widely validated renal failure risk stratification tools that clinicians might otherwise apply to IBD patients. The 4-variable KFRE uses age, sex, eGFR, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR); KDpredict adds diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Because measured UACR was unavailable in this retrospective cohort, we did not perform formal validation or recalibration of KFRE or KDpredict. Instead, we conducted scenario-based sensitivity analyses by assigning fixed UACR values of 10, 30, and 100 mg/g to all patients. These analyses were intended only to compare rank-based discriminative performance, as assessed by the C-index, under different UACR assumptions. Because the same fixed UACR value was assigned to all patients within each scenario, the comparator analyses should not be interpreted as assessments of calibration or absolute risk estimation. Therefore, the comparison with KFRE and KDpredict was considered exploratory and should not be overinterpreted as formal external validation of these models in patients with IBD.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using statistical methods appropriate to the distribution of each variable. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t tests. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported, and comparisons were made using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. Assuming that data were missing at random, 5 imputed datasets were generated using all candidate predictor variables. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, whereas binary variables were imputed using logistic regression, and the results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules to obtain the final parameter estimates and standard errors.

Significant independent risk factors identified through multivariate Cox regression with P < 0.1 were included in the final Cox model. Optimal cutoff values for these predictors were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the maximum Youden index used to define the best threshold. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to evaluate cumulative incidence between groups, and differences were compared using the log-rank test. Model calibration was assessed with calibration curves and Brier score. The concordance index (C-index) and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were used to evaluate model accuracy. All analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.2.0), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

We screened 1869 patients diagnosed with IBD for inclusion in the training and internal validation phase, and 769 patients were included in the final cohort. The study flow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The median age at the time of diagnosis was 31 years (IQR, 23–43 years), and 514 (66.8%) patients were male. The median follow-up duration was 180.5 weeks (IQR, 64.9–309.0). During follow-up, 211 cases of renal insufficiency and 66 of CKD were identified. The cumulative incidence of renal insufficiency at 1 and 3 years was 18.6% (95% CI, 15.8%–21.3%) and 28.5% (95% CI, 20.5%–36.2%). The numbers of patients at risk at 1, 2, and 3 years were 594, 527, and 430, respectively. The effective sample sizes for 1-, 2-, and 3-year risk prediction were 736 (95.7%), 705 (91.7%), and 641 (83.4%), respectively, supporting the reliability of the 3-year prediction horizon. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the occurrence of renal insufficiency within 3 years: eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m² (558 patients) and eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² (211 patients). Baseline characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. Variables with less than 20% missing data were eligible for inclusion in the LASSO regression model. Patients were randomly assigned to the training set and internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio.

A total of 520 patients from four tertiary hospitals were screened for inclusion in the external validation cohort, and 298 met the eligibility criteria. The median follow-up duration was 176.6 weeks (IQR, 104.8–238.9). The incidence of renal insufficiency and CKD was 23.15% (69/298) and 6.04% (18/298), respectively. The numbers of patients at risk at 1, 2, and 3 years were 254, 224, and 168, respectively, and the corresponding effective sample sizes for 1-, 2-, and 3-year risk prediction were 267 (89.6%), 244 (81.9%), and 201 (67.4%), respectively. Baseline characteristics of the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Ranking of feature importance

Using LASSO regression, 11 variables were selected based on the 1-SE rule (Supplementary Figure S1). Multivariate Cox regression analysis of the LASSO-selected variables identified the following as predictors of renal insufficiency within 3 years in patients with IBD: osteoporosis (HR, 1.763; 95% CI, 1.076–2.889; P = 0.024); baseline eGFR (HR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.21, 0.40; P < 0.001); baseline high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (HR, 0.522; 95% CI, 0.287–0.949; P = 0.033); baseline C-reactive protein (CRP) (HR, 1.004; 95% CI, 0.998–1.008; P = 0.09); biologic therapy (HR, 0.570; 95% CI, 0.369–0.878; P = 0.011); enteral nutrition (HR, 1.860; 95% CI, 1.182–2.926; P = 0.007); and intestinal surgery (HR, 1.640; 95% CI, 1.124–2.392; P = 0.010) (Table 2). Baseline CRP showed a borderline association with renal insufficiency and was retained in the final model because it met the prespecified P < 0.1 selection criterion and represents systemic inflammatory burden. Sensitivity analyses excluding perioperative renal events within 30 days after intestinal surgery, stratifying biologics by drug class, restricting the cohort to patients aged < 65 years, using 20 imputed datasets, using CKD as an alternative outcome, and applying sustained > 30% decline in eGFR from baseline as a stricter renal endpoint showed largely consistent results (Supplementary Tables S2–S7). In the CKD and sustained eGFR-decline analyses, osteoporosis, biologic therapy, lower baseline eGFR, and intestinal surgery remained robust predictors, whereas the associations for HDL, CRP, and enteral nutrition were attenuated.

ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive power of each variable for the incidence of renal insufficiency within 3 years. The optimal cutoff values identified were 110.25 mL/min/1.73 m² for baseline eGFR, 0.95 mmol/L for baseline HDL, and 7.69 mg/L for baseline CRP (Supplementary table S8). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in the incidence of renal insufficiency between groups stratified by these cutoff values. Similarly, Osteoporosis (P < 0.0001), biologic therapy (P < 0.0001), enteral nutrition (P = 0.0014), and intestinal surgery (P < 0.0001) were strongly associated with the incidence of renal insufficiency (Fig. 2).

To address potential information loss from dichotomizing baseline eGFR, we refitted the final Cox model with baseline eGFR modeled as a continuous variable while retaining HDL and CRP as continuous predictors. The results were consistent with the primary model: baseline eGFR remained independently associated with renal insufficiency (HR 0.95 per 1 mL/min/1.73 m² increase, 95% CI 0.94–0.96, P < 0.001), and the model maintained good discrimination (C-index = 0.775) (Supplementary Table S9). Predictive performance of the model

To assess the incremental value of the IBD-specific prediction model, we compared its performance with that of the KFRE model, the KDpredict model, and eGFR alone. In the internal validation cohort, the C-index for predicting renal insufficiency was 0.719 (95% CI: 0.65–0.77), which was significantly higher than that of the KFRE model (C-index 0.63, P = 0.01), KDpredict model (C-index 0.633, P = 0.001), and eGFR alone (C-index 0.652, P = 0.034). In the external validation cohort, the Cox model (C-index 0.719, 95% CI: 0.66–0.78) outperformed the KFRE model (C-index 0.628, P = 0.013), KDpredict model (C-index 0.607, P = 0.002), and eGFR alone (C-index 0.622, P = 0.002) in predicting renal insufficiency. Comparable findings were observed for the prediction of CKD. In the internal validation set, the C-index for predicting CKD was 0.69 (95% CI: 0.55–0.82), outperforming the other three models, although the results were not significant, likely due to limited statistical power. In the external validation cohort, the C-index of the Cox model was 0.739 (95% CI: 0.63–0.85), significantly higher than that of the KDpredict model (C-index 0.687, P = 0.006) and eGFR alone (C-index 0.658, P = 0.01). Additionally, the AUC for predicting renal insufficiency at 3 years was 0.718 (95% CI: 0.61–0.87) in the external validation set, while the AUC for CKD was 0.814 (95% CI: 0.69–0.91) in the external validation set, outperforming other models (Fig. 4). Scenario-based sensitivity analyses using fixed UACR assumptions of 10, 30, and 100 mg/g showed that the C-index values of KFRE and KDpredict remained essentially unchanged across assumptions and lower than that of the IBD-specific Cox model (Supplementary Table S11). These findings should be interpreted as an exploratory comparison of discrimination only, not as formal external validation or calibration assessment of KFRE or KDpredict in patients with IBD.

The calibration curve of the Cox model for predicting the 3-year incidence of renal insufficiency demonstrated good performance, with a Brier score of 0.103 in the internal validation set and 0.068 in the external validation cohort. Similarly, for predicting the 3-year incidence of CKD, our model achieved a Brier score of 0.059 in the internal validation set and 0.178 in the external validation cohort, indicating robust calibration in the internal validation set, and the higher Brier score in the external cohort suggests attenuated calibration when applied to independent centers for CKD prediction (Supplementary Figure S3). The decision curve analysis showed that the net benefit of the Cox model surpassed that of the conventional scoring systems throughout the range of threshold probabilities, indicating that our Cox model yielded a higher net benefit (Supplementary Figure S4). The risk of renal insufficiency for each patient was calculated based on the Cox model, and the cutoff value of 0.25 was determined by the Youden index to categorize patients into low-risk and high-risk subgroups. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significant divergence in the cumulative incidence of renal insufficiency between the two subgroups. For the risk of renal insufficiency, the high-risk subgroup exhibited a higher cumulative incidence in both the internal validation cohort (P < 0.0001) and the external validation cohort (P < 0.0001). Similarly, the high-risk subgroup showed an elevated risk of CKD progression in the internal validation cohort (P < 0.0001) and the external validation cohort (P = 0.0048) (Fig. 5). Together, our model outperformed other scoring systems, demonstrating superior discrimination and calibration in the external validation cohort.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, 280 patients (26.2%) developed renal insufficiency (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2), while 84 (7.8%) progressed to CKD during follow-up in five tertiary medical centers. These rates are higher than the 5-year risk of CKD in population-based studies of IBD (6.4%) but remain lower than the reported incidence of acute or chronic renal failure in hospitalized IBD patients (15.9%) from earlier studies [6, 9]. Our data suggest that renal dysfunction is common in IBD patients. Despite the availability of guidelines addressing renal comorbidities [15], the prevalence of IBD-associated renal impairment was high. Accurate estimation of renal comorbidity incidence is clinically important because early detection and management are associated with reduced mortality.

Renal complications in IBD have been reported extensively, but most prior studies come from Western populations and rely on case–control designs, limiting estimates of incidence and reducing generalizability to other regions. Our multicenter cohort study aims to address these gaps. By applying a time-to-event prognostic model, we estimated the incidence of renal insufficiency and CKD after IBD diagnosis and developed a validated prediction model demonstrating good discrimination (C-index: 0.719 for renal insufficiency and 0.739 for CKD in the external cohort). However, we also observed that the Brier score for CKD prediction increased substantially from 0.059 in the internal validation set to 0.178 in the external validation cohort, indicating attenuated calibration when the model was applied to independent centers. This increase may reflects heterogeneity in baseline CKD risk, follow-up intensity, and event ascertainment across institutions. Thus, while the model retains potential clinical utility, we emphasize the necessity of local recalibration before broad clinical implementation. Our model incorporates readily available clinical variables, including laboratory biomarkers (CRP, HDL, eGFR), extraintestinal manifestations (osteoporosis), and treatment-related factors (intestinal surgery, biologic therapy, enteral nutrition). These factors provide practical and objective indicators to support early risk stratification and guide surveillance strategies.

Early studies have identified advanced age at admission, UC, longer disease duration, and the presence of EIMs as risk factors for renal dysfunction [6]. We conducted a more detailed analysis of the relationship between EIMs and renal function, identifying an association between osteoporosis and renal dysfunction. Mechanistically, chronic systemic inflammation and impaired renal function may reduce the conversion of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to its active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, thereby aggravating osteoporosis through impaired calcium absorption and secondary hyperparathyroidism [16, 17]. However, osteoporosis in this cohort likely represents an integrated marker of frailty, cumulative inflammatory burden, nutritional deficiency, and prolonged corticosteroid exposure rather than an isolated pathological entity directly causing renal impairment. It should therefore be interpreted as a clinically recognizable risk indicator rather than a direct causal factor for renal dysfunction in patients with IBD.

Among the independent risk factors identified by the Cox model, two objective biomarkers – CRP and HDL at enrollment – were associated with renal comorbidities, suggesting a potential link between systemic inflammation, altered lipid metabolism, and the risk of renal dysfunction in patients with IBD. In contrast to HDL, CRP demonstrated only a borderline association with renal insufficiency and should therefore be interpreted as a supportive marker of systemic inflammatory burden rather than a robust individual predictor. IBD is characterized by chronic systemic inflammation, with elevated proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, which contribute to renal inflammation and damage to the glomeruli and renal tubules [18, 19]. Elevated CRP reflects this systemic inflammatory burden. In CKD, impaired glomerular filtration and tubular dysfunction can lead to accumulation of inflammatory mediators, further exacerbating systemic inflammation and renal injury in a self-perpetuating cycle [20]. Additionally, our study identified an association between lower HDL levels and lower eGFR in IBD patients, consistent with previous research. Low HDL is a feature of CKD-related dyslipidemia. Liao et al. found that elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL were both associated with an increased risk of CKD development [21]. This composite index may provide a more precise tool for assessing CKD risk in IBD patients.

Existing studies on the association between biologic therapy and renal dysfunction in IBD have reported mixed results. A large study based on the U.S. veterans cohort found that anti-TNF inhibitor use was independently associated with a significant decline in eGFR [22], whereas Rodriguez et al. reported no significant association between biologic therapy and CKD in a smaller, older CD cohort [8]. Sensitivity analyses stratified by biologic class demonstrated generally consistent protective associations across anti-TNF agents, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab, suggesting that the observed effect was not limited to a single therapeutic mechanism. These discrepancies may partly reflect differences in study populations, as white IBD patients are reported to have higher rates of colectomy, whereas Asian patients often demonstrate better therapeutic response and more sustained remission with biologic treatment [23]. However, this apparent benefit may reflect closer clinical monitoring, earlier corticosteroid withdrawal, and improved overall inflammatory control rather than a drug-specific renal protective effect. Given the retrospective nature of this study, we could not fully exclude the influence of treatment selection bias, differences in disease activity trajectories before biologic initiation, or reductions in cumulative corticosteroid exposure attributable to biologic therapy. Accordingly, class-specific studies using rigorous propensity-score methods or target trial emulation are warranted to better isolate the true renal effects of individual biologic agents.

Our study identified intestinal surgery as a risk factor for renal dysfunction in IBD patients, consistent with recent reports [8, 9]. To minimize confounding by acute postoperative kidney injury, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding renal events within 30 days after surgery, and the association remained robust. This finding likely reflects the long-term physiological consequences of bowel resection, including impaired fluid and electrolyte absorption, bile acid malabsorption, and nephrolithiasis [24]. Intestinal surgery may serve as a marker of cumulative disease severity and inflammatory burden, which themselves have been associated with adverse renal outcomes. Given the established link between bowel resection, nephrolithiasis, and CKD, residual confounding cannot be excluded because nephrolithiasis history was not systematically recorded across participating centers and therefore could not be adjusted for in our analyses [25, 26]. The exact temporal dynamics between new-onset surgery and renal events during follow-up remain imperfectly captured in this retrospective design and warrant prospective investigation with granular perioperative data.

Another observed association between enteral nutrition and renal dysfunction may be multifactorial. While high-protein formulations could theoretically increase renal load, enteral nutrition use may also serve as a surrogate marker for disease severity, malnutrition, or postoperative status, given that sicker patients are more likely to require nutritional support [27]. Due to the retrospective design, we were unable to capture formula composition, protein content, dose, or duration. Prospective studies with detailed nutritional phenotyping are needed to clarify this relationship.

The absence of baseline urinary findings is an important limitation of this retrospective study, including proteinuria, albuminuria, haematuria, and urinary sediment, which are powerful predictors of renal progression [28]. To prioritize generalizability in gastroenterology clinics where urinalysis is often unavailable, our model uses only routine variables (serum biochemistry, demographics, treatment history). While adding urinary biomarkers could improve accuracy, we recommend using the model as an initial screening tool, followed by confirmatory urinalysis and nephrology referral for high-risk patients. In addition, the absence of UACR data may have attenuated the performance of the comparator models in our cohort. These models were originally designed to predict kidney failure in patients with established moderate-to-severe CKD, whereas our model targeted earlier renal functional decline in a younger, inflammation-driven IBD population [14]. Thus, comparisons between our IBD-specific model and the KFRE or KDpredict models should be considered exploratory rather than definitive.

Several methodological constraints should be acknowledged. First, although data were collected from over 1,000 patients across five medical centers, the majority of participants were from the southern coastal regions of China, which may introduce population bias. The generalizability of our findings to other countries with different IBD phenotypes or treatment access, or to primary care settings remains uncertain. Second, the absence of a non-IBD control group limits our ability to determine whether identified risk factors are IBD-specific or reflect general chronic disease risks; therefore, the model should be viewed as a tool for intra-IBD risk stratification. Third, renal insufficiency defined as an eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² represents a sensitive but relatively nonspecific marker. It captures early reduction in renal functional reserve (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m², corresponding to KDIGO G2) rather than definitive chronic kidney disease. From a clinical surveillance perspective, identifying patients at this earlier stage allows timely intervention before irreversible damage occurs. To reduce the concern that the primary results were driven by transient eGFR fluctuations, we performed additional analyses using stricter renal outcomes, including CKD and sustained > 30% decline in eGFR from baseline. The key associations remained generally consistent, supporting the robustness of the model. Nevertheless, the primary outcome should be interpreted as early renal functional decline rather than definitive kidney disease. Fourth, as a retrospective study without protocol-mandated follow-up, eGFR measurement frequency was clinician-determined (usually once every 1–3 months). This may introduce informative visit bias, where sicker patients are monitored more frequently and thus more likely to have renal events detected. Fifth, significant baseline differences between the primary and external validation cohorts (age, IBD subtype, biologic exposure, enteral nutrition) reflect real-world heterogeneity. Despite this, the model maintained good discrimination in external validation, although further validation in diverse cohorts is essential. Finally, the primary multiple imputation procedure used five imputed datasets, which is generally considered minimal, an additional sensitivity analysis using 20 imputed datasets showed generally consistent results. Nevertheless, residual uncertainty related to missing data and imputation variability cannot be fully excluded.

In conclusion, this retrospective study developed and validated a preliminary model to predict 3-year renal outcomes in patients with IBD. Our findings suggest that patients with reduced renal functional reserve, metabolic disturbances, high inflammatory burden, or a history of intestinal surgery may be more prone to developing renal dysfunction. However, given the exploratory and retrospective nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective, multicenter studies are needed to confirm the predictive accuracy and generalizability of the model across diverse populations and clinical settings.
