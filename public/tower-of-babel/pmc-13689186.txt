From numbers to trajectories: a pragmatic hs-cTnT/NT-proBNP delta-check for 12-month risk stratification in chronic heart failure
Authors: Arif Albulushi, Gamal Aly, Hatem Al-Farhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are widely used in heart failure (HF), but outpatient interpretation often depends on isolated values rather than within-person change. We evaluated a pragmatic dual-biomarker delta-check framework for 12-month risk stratification in chronic HF.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of adults with chronic HF who had paired hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP measurements obtained during a clinically stable anchor visit and repeated within 3–6 months. The second paired measurement served as the index date. Patients were classified a priori into Green, Yellow, or Red trajectory tiers using combined relative and absolute biomarker changes, interpreted with a minimum clinical context bundle including rhythm, renal function, and clinical state. Meaningful adverse change was defined as hs-cTnT rise ≥ 20% and ≥ 5 ng/L, and NT-proBNP rise ≥ 30% and ≥ 500 pg/mL, or ≥ 300 pg/mL when anchor NT-proBNP was < 1,000 pg/mL. The primary endpoint was HF hospitalization or all-cause death within 12 months.ResultsAmong 520 patients, 229 (44%) were Green, 187 (36%) Yellow, and 104 (20%) Red. Anchor median NT-proBNP was 1,850 pg/mL [780–4,400], and hs-cTnT was 21 ng/L [13–36]. The primary endpoint occurred in 140 patients (26.9%). Event rates increased stepwise across tiers: Green 17.9%, Yellow 27.8%, and Red 45.2% (log-rank p < 0.001). In adjusted Cox models, Yellow showed an intermediate signal (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.0–2.0; p = 0.06), whereas Red remained independently associated with higher risk (HR 2.1, 95% CI 1.4–3.2; p < 0.001). In a sensitivity model excluding anchor hs-cTnT and anchor NT-proBNP, the associations were preserved: Yellow HR 1.5 (95% CI 1.1–2.2; p = 0.03) and Red HR 2.5 (95% CI 1.7–3.6; p < 0.001).ConclusionsA pragmatic hs-cTnT/NT-proBNP delta-check stratified 12-month risk in chronic HF. Concordant worsening across both biomarkers identified a high-risk trajectory, while isolated or borderline change was best interpreted as a lower-intensity review signal. Prospective testing is needed before this framework is used to guide outcome-modifying interventions.Keywords: Heart failure, High-sensitivity troponin, NT-proBNP, Biomarker trajectory, Risk stratification, Outcomes

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (HF) follow-up increasingly depends on structured clinical assessment, repeated laboratory surveillance, and timely adjustment of guideline-directed therapy. Contemporary HF guidelines emphasize longitudinal reassessment of symptoms, congestion, renal function, rhythm, and treatment tolerance during routine care [1].

Biomarkers are central to HF diagnosis and prognostic assessment, but outpatient interpretation remains variable. The American Heart Association scientific statement on HF biomarkers emphasized that natriuretic peptides and cardiac troponins are clinically useful, but should be interpreted in the context of the patient’s clinical state rather than as isolated values [2].

NT-proBNP reflects myocardial wall stress and hemodynamic burden, while hs-cTnT reflects ongoing myocardial injury and vulnerability. In chronic HF, both biomarkers provide prognostic information across HF phenotypes and clinical settings [3].

A key limitation of static biomarker interpretation is biological and analytical variation. Small numerical changes may not represent true clinical deterioration, particularly at lower concentrations or when renal function changes between tests [4].

Common clinical confounders also influence biomarker values. Atrial fibrillation may increase natriuretic peptide concentrations independently of overt congestion, while obesity may suppress natriuretic peptide levels and complicate interpretation, particularly in HFpEF [5, 6].

Serial biomarker change may provide clinically relevant information beyond isolated values. Serial troponin change has been associated with incident HF and cardiovascular mortality, and longer-term changes in high-sensitivity troponin have been linked to subsequent HF and death [7, 8].

In established chronic HF, combined interpretation of hs-cTnT, NT-proBNP, and renal function may improve prognostic assessment beyond any single marker alone [9].

Previous biomarker-guided HF trials have produced mixed results. GUIDE-IT did not show superiority of NT-proBNP-guided therapy over usual care in high-risk HFrEF, while TIME-CHF suggested that natriuretic peptide–guided intensification may have age-dependent effects [10, 11].

Earlier studies such as STARS-BNP and STOP-HF suggested that natriuretic peptide–based strategies may be useful in selected contexts, but translation into routine HF clinic workflows has remained inconsistent [12, 13]. These trials highlight a practical gap: biomarkers may be valuable, but clinicians need a simple decision architecture that distinguishes chronic elevation, isolated drift, and clinically meaningful worsening.

Structured early follow-up and treatment optimization can improve outcomes after acute HF, as shown in STRONG-HF [14]. A practical biomarker trajectory system may complement this model by helping identify which chronic HF outpatients require routine follow-up, lower-intensity review, or expedited clinician reassessment.

We therefore evaluated a pragmatic hs-cTnT/NT-proBNP delta-check framework in a real-world chronic HF cohort. We hypothesized that prespecified Green, Yellow, and Red trajectory tiers, interpreted with rhythm, renal function, and clinical state, would stratify 12-month risk of HF hospitalization or all-cause death.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed a single-center retrospective observational cohort study at the National Heart Center, The Royal Hospital, Muscat, Oman. Reporting followed the STROBE recommendations for observational studies [15].

Study population

Adults aged ≥ 18 years with a clinical diagnosis of chronic HF were eligible if they had paired hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP measurements obtained during outpatient follow-up and repeated within a prespecified 3–6-month interval.

HF phenotype was classified according to available clinical documentation and echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction as HFrEF, HFmrEF, or HFpEF. Systematic objective congestion verification, such as standardized lung ultrasound, invasive filling pressure assessment, or uniform congestion scoring, was not available for all patients.

Stable anchor definition

The first paired biomarker measurement was considered the “anchor” measurement. To improve biological interpretability, the anchor measurement was required to reflect clinical stability.

Clinical stability was defined from medical records as absence of: (i) intravenous diuresis or HF hospitalization within the surrounding clinical window; (ii) active infection or sepsis; (iii) uncontrolled tachyarrhythmia or recent cardioversion/ablation; and (iv) acute kidney injury or major renal step-down at the time of sampling.

Index date and follow-up

Because delta-tier assignment requires two biomarker measurements, the date of the second paired measurement was defined a priori as the index date. Outcomes were assessed from the index date for 12 months.

This design avoids immortal time bias because patients were not considered exposed to a biomarker trajectory until both biomarker measurements were available and the trajectory tier could be assigned.

Biomarker measurement and delta thresholds

hs-cTnT was reported in ng/L and NT-proBNP in pg/mL. Biomarker distributions were right-skewed and are reported as median [IQR].

Meaningful biomarker change was defined using a pragmatic combination of relative and absolute change. These thresholds were selected to reduce overinterpretation of small fluctuations, but they were not intended to represent assay-specific validated reference change value cutoffs. Therefore, they should be interpreted as conservative clinical decision thresholds requiring external validation [16].

For hs-cTnT, meaningful adverse change was defined as an increase of ≥ 20% from anchor and an absolute rise of ≥ 5 ng/L.

For NT-proBNP, meaningful adverse change was defined as an increase of ≥ 30% from anchor and an absolute rise of ≥ 500 pg/mL. For patients with anchor NT-proBNP < 1,000 pg/mL, an absolute rise of ≥ 300 pg/mL was accepted if the ≥ 30% relative increase criterion was also met.

Borderline adverse change was defined as hs-cTnT rise of 10–19% with an absolute rise of ≥ 3 ng/L, or NT-proBNP rise of 20–29% with an absolute rise of ≥ 300 pg/mL.

Delta-check tier definition

The delta-check framework was prespecified before outcome evaluation. Patients were classified into Green, Yellow, or Red tiers based on paired biomarker change between anchor and index measurements. After finalization of the numeric biomarker thresholds, all 520 patients were re-run through the tiering algorithm using paired biomarker change and the minimum clinical context bundle before outcome modelling. Outcome status was not used for tier assignment.

Tier assignment required a minimum clinical context bundle: rhythm and rate, renal function change, and clinical-state label. The clinical-state label categorized the patient as stable, post-discharge, or clinically decompensated at the time of index testing.

Green was assigned when there was no meaningful adverse delta in either biomarker and no contextual feature suggesting active decompensation.

Yellow was assigned when one biomarker met meaningful adverse delta criteria, when both biomarkers showed borderline adverse change, or when rhythm, renal function, or clinical state made the trajectory uncertain.

Red was assigned when both hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP showed meaningful adverse worsening after context review, or when concordant biomarker worsening occurred with clinical evidence of decompensation.

The context bundle was used as an interpretation rule. Isolated NT-proBNP rise during rapid atrial fibrillation or recent volume shift remained Yellow unless hs-cTnT also worsened. Isolated hs-cTnT rise during major renal deterioration remained Yellow unless NT-proBNP also worsened or clinical deterioration was present. Concordant meaningful worsening in both biomarkers was classified as Red unless a clearly transient non-HF cause was documented.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was a composite of HF hospitalization or all-cause death within 12 months from the index date.

HF hospitalization was defined as an unplanned admission with a primary HF diagnosis requiring intravenous diuretics and/or escalation of HF therapy. Death was ascertained from hospital records.

Covariates

The primary adjusted model included age, sex, LVEF category, atrial fibrillation, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hemoglobin, anchor hs-cTnT, and anchor NT-proBNP.

These covariates were selected because they are clinically relevant, associated with biomarker concentrations, and plausibly related to HF outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD or median [IQR], and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared descriptively across tiers.

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for the primary endpoint by delta-check tier and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for Yellow vs. Green and Red vs. Green.

The primary adjusted model included age, sex, LVEF category, atrial fibrillation, eGFR, hemoglobin, anchor hs-cTnT, and anchor NT-proBNP.

A sensitivity Cox model was performed excluding anchor hs-cTnT and anchor NT-proBNP, while retaining age, sex, LVEF category, atrial fibrillation, eGFR, and hemoglobin. This sensitivity analysis was performed to assess whether adjustment for baseline biomarker values attenuated the association between delta tier and outcome.

Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using log-minus-log inspection and Schoenfeld residual diagnostics where available.

Ethics

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health, Sultanate of Oman / The Royal Hospital, Muscat, Oman (IRB No. MoH/CSR/25/29649). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and de-identified analysis of routinely collected clinical data.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 520 patients met eligibility criteria and had paired hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP measurements within the prespecified 3–6-month interval.

The cohort reflected a chronic HF outpatient population with high baseline biomarker burden. Anchor median NT-proBNP was 1,850 pg/mL [780–4,400], and anchor median hs-cTnT was 21 ng/L [13–36]. Median follow-up from the index date was 12.0 months [11.4–12.0].

After reclassification using the final numeric delta thresholds and minimum context bundle, 229 patients (44%) were classified as Green, 187 (36%) as Yellow, and 104 (20%) as Red. Compared with Green patients, Red patients were older, more frequently had atrial fibrillation, had lower eGFR, and had higher anchor hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP concentrations (Table 1).

Primary endpoint

Over 12 months, the composite endpoint of HF hospitalization or all-cause death occurred in 140 of 520 patients (26.9%).

Event rates increased stepwise across tiers: 41 of 229 patients (17.9%) in Green, 52 of 187 patients (27.8%) in Yellow, and 47 of 104 patients (45.2%) in Red.

The absolute risk difference was + 9.9% for Yellow vs. Green and + 27.3% for Red vs. Green. The corresponding risk ratios were 1.55 for Yellow vs. Green and 2.53 for Red vs. Green (Table 2).

Cox regression

In unadjusted Cox analysis, Yellow was associated with higher risk compared with Green (HR 1.6, 95% CI 1.1–2.3; p = 0.01). Red was also associated with higher risk compared with Green (HR 2.7, 95% CI 1.9–3.9; p < 0.001).

After adjustment for age, sex, LVEF category, atrial fibrillation, eGFR, hemoglobin, anchor hs-cTnT, and anchor NT-proBNP, Red remained independently associated with the primary endpoint (HR 2.1, 95% CI 1.4–3.2; p < 0.001). Yellow showed an attenuated intermediate signal (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.0–2.0; p = 0.06).

In the sensitivity model excluding anchor hs-cTnT and anchor NT-proBNP, the direction and magnitude of association were preserved. Yellow vs. Green was associated with higher risk (HR 1.5, 95% CI 1.1–2.2; p = 0.03), while Red vs. Green remained strongly associated with the primary endpoint (HR 2.5, 95% CI 1.7–3.6; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this real-world chronic HF cohort, a pragmatic dual-biomarker delta-check using hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP stratified 12-month risk of HF hospitalization or all-cause death. Event rates increased in an orderly manner from Green to Yellow to Red, and the Red tier remained independently associated with outcomes after adjustment.

A key strength of the revised framework is improved transparency and auditability. Meaningful biomarker change is now defined by explicit relative and absolute thresholds rather than by subjective interpretation. However, these thresholds remain pragmatic clinical decision thresholds and require external validation before they can be considered reproducible across laboratories, assays, and HF populations.

This positioning is consistent with contemporary ESC HF guidance, which supports repeated clinical reassessment but does not define a single universal biomarker target for all patients [17, 18]. At the same time, hs-cTnT changes should remain anchored to clinical assessment for myocardial injury or ischemia rather than interpreted in isolation [19, 20]. Absolute natriuretic peptide thresholds have established diagnostic and short-term prognostic value [21, 22], and low-level troponin elevations also carry prognostic information in stable cardiovascular disease and chronic HF [23, 24]. The proposed delta-check should therefore be viewed as a pragmatic implementation layer, not a replacement for established HF risk scores [25, 26], invasive hemodynamic monitoring in selected patients [27], or evidence-based disease-modifying therapy such as ARNI and SGLT2 inhibition [28, 29].

The dual-biomarker structure is clinically important. hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP represent different but complementary risk domains: myocardial injury and hemodynamic stress. When both worsen together, the signal is more concerning than isolated drift in one marker.

The Yellow tier requires balanced interpretation. In this cohort, Yellow patients had higher unadjusted risk than Green patients, but the association attenuated after adjustment. For this reason, Yellow should not be treated as an urgent escalation tier. It is better framed as a lower-intensity review signal requiring confirmation, rhythm assessment, renal review, volume assessment, and symptom check.

This change improves the clinical realism of the pathway. Routing more than one-third of an outpatient HF cohort to urgent physician review would not be operationally practical in many HF clinics. A nurse-led or telehealth review model for Yellow patients is more feasible and better aligned with the strength of the observed signal (Fig. 2).

The context bundle is essential because biomarker change in chronic HF is not self-interpreting. A rise in NT-proBNP during rapid atrial fibrillation may carry a different meaning from a rise during progressive congestion. Similarly, hs-cTnT may increase with renal deterioration without representing acute coronary syndrome or new HF injury. This framework is therefore intended to support clinical interpretation, not replace it.

The high anchor NT-proBNP concentration in this cohort deserves careful discussion. A median NT-proBNP of approximately 1,850 pg/mL suggests that the cohort had a clinically meaningful burden of cardiac dysfunction and risk. However, elevated NT-proBNP is not identical to objectively verified congestion. This distinction is especially relevant in HFpEF, where natriuretic peptide values may be influenced by age, atrial fibrillation, renal function, obesity, pulmonary hypertension, and variable filling pressures.

This reinforces the rationale for using concordant within-patient biomarker change rather than fixed absolute NT-proBNP cutoffs alone. In HFpEF, trial-derived natriuretic peptide thresholds may enrich for risk but can also identify heterogeneous populations rather than a uniform congestive phenotype. Therefore, biomarker trajectories are likely most informative when interpreted alongside rhythm, renal function, obesity, clinical state, and objective evidence of congestion where available [30].

Because systematic objective congestion verification was not available for all patients, the findings should be interpreted as risk stratification among clinically diagnosed chronic HF patients. They should not be interpreted as proof that biomarker trajectory always reflects true congestion trajectory.

The findings also help explain why prior biomarker-guided care has been difficult to translate into routine practice. A single target value may be less useful than a practical trajectory system that distinguishes stability, isolated drift, and concordant worsening. The delta-check framework is not a competing HF risk score. It is a simple implementation layer that may help clinicians decide when routine follow-up is adequate and when earlier review is warranted.

Importantly, this study validates prognostic association, not clinical intervention. It does not prove that acting on the Red tier reduces hospitalization or death. It also does not establish that the Yellow tier should trigger medication escalation. Prospective studies are needed to test whether pathway-based action improves outcomes without increasing low-value care.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a clinically relevant chronic HF cohort, paired biomarker measurements within a prespecified 3–6-month interval, a stable anchor requirement, use of the second draw as the index date to avoid immortal time bias, and a clinically meaningful 12-month composite endpoint.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and may be affected by residual confounding and local practice patterns. Second, HF diagnosis and phenotype were based on clinical documentation and available echocardiography. Third, systematic objective congestion verification was not available for all patients. Fourth, although the delta thresholds are explicit, they are pragmatic clinical thresholds rather than formally validated assay-specific RCV cutoffs, and therefore require external validation across assays, laboratories, HF phenotypes, and care settings. Fifth, Yellow-tier interpretation remains less definitive than Red-tier interpretation and should be implemented as a lower-intensity review signal. Sixth, unmeasured changes between anchor and index sampling, including medication adjustment, adherence, diet, renal fluctuation, and outpatient congestion, may have influenced biomarker trajectories. Finally, this study does not prove that implementing the pathway improves outcomes.

Conclusions

A pragmatic hs-cTnT/NT-proBNP delta-check stratified 12-month risk in chronic HF using routine laboratory data. Concordant worsening across both biomarkers identified a high-risk Red trajectory, while isolated or borderline biomarker change identified an intermediate Yellow signal best suited to confirmation and clinical context review. The framework may support more transparent and consistent biomarker interpretation in HF clinics, but prospective validation is required before it is used as an outcome-modifying management pathway.
