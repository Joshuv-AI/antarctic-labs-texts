A supermassive black hole under the radar: repeating X-ray variability in a Seyfert galaxy
Matteo Imbrogno, Andrea Sacchi ⋆, Giovanni Miniutti, Francesco Tombesi, Gian Luca Israel, Enrico Piconcelli, Roberta Amato

Abstract
In the last few years, a few supermassive black holes (SMBHs) have shown short-term (of the order of hours) X-ray variability. Given the limited size of the sample, every new addition to this class of SMBHs can bring invaluable information. Within the context of an automated search for X-ray sources showing flux variability in the Chandra archive, we identified peculiar variability patterns in 2MASX J12571076+2724177 (J1257), a SMBH in the Coma cluster, during observations performed in 2020. We investigated the long-term evolution of the flux, together with the evolution of the spectral parameters throughout the Chandra and XMM-Newton observations, which cover a time span of approximately 20 years. We found that J1257 has repeatedly shown peculiar variability over the last 20 years, on typical timescales of ≃ 20 − 25 \simeq 20-25 ks. From our spectral analysis, we found hints of a softer-when-brighter behaviour and of two well-separated flux states. We suggest that J1257 might represent a new addition to the ever-growing size of relatively low mass SMBHs ( M ≃ 10 6 − 10 7 ​ M ⊙ M\simeq 10^{6}-10^{7}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot} ) showing extreme, possibly quasi-periodic X-ray variability on short time scales. The available dataset does not allow for a definitive classification of the nature of the variability. However, given the observed properties, it could either represent a quasi-periodic oscillation at particularly low frequency or be associated with quasi-periodic eruptions in an AGN with peculiar spectral properties.

Introduction

The study of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) has recently been revolutionised by the detection of different types of (extreme) X-ray variability phenomena. A few SMBHs have shown quasi-periodic oscillations
(
van der Klis 1989b
;
Ingram & Motta 2019
, QPOs; for the original definition and a more recent review, see)
in their X-ray flux
(
Smith et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
, see e.g.)
at frequencies as low as a few
10^{-4}
Hz.
González-Martín & Vaughan 2012
suggested that most of these detections were unreliable, and later
Zhang et al. 2023
reached a similar conclusion by analysing active galactic nuclei (AGN) data with (tentative) QPOs using Gaussian Processes, confirming the presence of QPOs only in the source RE J1034+396
(
Gierliński et al. 2008
)
. Thanks to its high significance and the high number of observations showing QPOs, RE J1034+396 has been extensively studied in the last few years
(
Xia et al. 2024
;
Xia et al. 2025
, see e.g.)
. However, it should be noted that the (re)analysis of
Zhang et al. 2023
does not include sources such as 2XMM J123103.2+110648
(
Lin et al. 2013
)
, whose QPO has a highly significant (
\sim 5\sigma
) detection. At the same time, after their work, other (highly) significant QPOs have been reported, such as Mrk 142
(
Zhong et al. 2024
)
and 1ES 1927+654
(
Masterson et al. 2025
)
. The latter is an AGN that exhibited extreme variability in the recent past
(
Ricci et al. 2021
;
Masterson et al. 2022
)
.

QPOs can be used as a tool to estimate the mass of the accreting SMBH
(
Zhou et al. 2015
;
Smith et al. 2018
;
Smith et al. 2021
, see e.g.)
, through an extension up to SMBH masses of the relation between QPO frequency and BH mass proposed initially for the case of Galactic BHs and candidate intermediate-mass BHs in ultraluminous X-ray sources

In recent years, a dozen SMBHs have shown X-ray quasi-periodic eruptions (QPEs), that is high amplitude soft X-ray flares typically lasting 1-10 h and recurring quasi-periodically every few-100 h and radiating X-ray luminosity of
\simeq 10^{42}-10^{43}\,\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
in the form of a thermal spectrum with typical
kT\simeq 100
-
200
eV
(
Miniutti et al. 2019
;
Giustini et al. 2020
;
Arcodia et al. 2021
;
Chakraborty et al. 2021
;
Quintin et al. 2023
;
Arcodia et al. 2024
;
Nicholl et al. 2024
;
Chakraborty et al. 2025
;
Arcodia et al. 2025
;
Hernández-García
et al. 2025
)
. A link with tidal disruption events
(
Komossa 2015
, TDEs; see)
is emerging from several lines of evidence ranging from host properties
(
Wevers et al. 2024
)
and X-ray long-term variability
(
Shu et al. 2018
;
Miniutti et al. 2023a
;
Chakraborty et al. 2021

to the detection of QPEs in optically-selected spectroscopically confirmed TDEs
(
Quintin et al. 2023
;
Nicholl et al. 2024
;
Chakraborty et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

The origin of QPEs is still debated, although models invoking the repeated interaction between the SMBH accretion disc and a secondary orbiting object within an extreme mass ratio inspiral (EMRI) system are gaining significant traction
(
Linial & Metzger 2023
;
Franchini et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Within this framework, QPEs might represent the electromagnetic counterpart or precursor of one of the most promising sources of gravitational waves to be observed with future space-based interferometric detectors
(
Kejriwal et al. 2024
;
Lui et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

In this paper, we report on the discovery of repeating X-ray variability in the Seyfert galaxy 2MASX J12571076+2724177 (J1257 hereafter). This source showed peculiar X-ray variability within the framework of the CATS@BAR project
(
Israel et al. 2016
, see also
Esposito et al. 2013a
;
Esposito et al. 2013b
;
Esposito et al. 2015
;
Sidoli et al. 2016
;
Sidoli et al. 2017
;
Bartlett et al. 2017
)
, aimed at the search for new X-ray pulsators and X-ray sources showing peculiar time variability in the
Chandra
archive. J1257 is located in the Coma cluster, at a redshift
z=0.02068

(
Ahn et al. 2012
)
. It is in the low-mass tail of the SMBH population, with a mass
\log M_{\mathrm{BH}}/M_{\odot}\sim 6.3
, as derived by
Liu et al. 2021
, who studied a sample of local AGN showing broad-H
\alpha
variability.

2.1
Chandra

We downloaded the
Chandra
J1257 observations from the
Chandra
Search & Retrieval
3
3

3

https://cda.cfa.harvard.edu/chaser/
(ChaSeR). For the data reduction and the extraction of source events, light curves, and energy spectra, we used the
Chandra
Interactive Analysis of Observations (
CIAO
) software v4.16.0
(
Fruscione et al. 2006
)
and v4.11.3 of the calibration database. We reprocessed the observations with the task
chandra_repro
. All observations were performed in
VFAINT
mode. Therefore, we set the
checkvfpha
flag to
yes
. For all the observations except ObsID 12887, we used a circular source extraction region of radius 4″, while to estimate the background contribution, we used a nearby circular region with a radius of 60″, free of other X-ray sources. During observation 12887, the source is off-axis and appears elliptical. Therefore, for this observation, we considered a 7″
\times
5″ elliptical source extraction region. For both timing and spectral analysis, we only considered events in the 0.5–7 keV band. We generated source and background energy spectra, together with spectral redistribution matrices and ancillary response files, with the task
specextract
and verified that the source contribution to the total spectrum was at least 95%. We discarded observation 23361 for spectral analysis due to its poor statistics (72 detected photons), but retained it for timing analysis to study the source variability. We then extract source events and background-corrected light curves for timing analysis using the tasks
dmcopy
and
dmextract
, respectively. Given the timescale of the observed variability, we did not barycenter the data.

2.2
XMM-Newton

For each
XMM-Newton
observation, we considered data coming from both EPIC pn
(
Strüder et al. 2001
)
and EPIC MOS
(
Turner et al. 2001
)
cameras. Due to the source falling in a chip gap or the presence of high background in one or two cameras, simultaneous data from all three cameras are available only for two observations (ObsIDs 0124710101 and 0652310801). In Table
2
we report the details of the analysed cameras for each observation. To prepare the raw
XMM-Newton
data for both timing and spectral analysis, we used
SAS

(
Gabriel et al. 2004
)
v21.0.0 with the latest
XMM-Newton
calibrations and applied standard data reduction procedures. EPIC pn and EPIC MOS data were reduced using the
epproc
and
emproc
tasks, respectively. We selected only the events with
\texttt{PATTERN}\leq 4

\texttt{PATTERN}\leq 12

tabgtigen
. For observations 0652310701, 0652310401, 0691610201, and 0691610301, given the higher background level, for the EPIC pn data we chose a threshold of 0.5, 0.5, 0.45, and 0.45 cts/s, respectively.

We considered events in the 0.3–10 keV band for both our timing and spectral analysis. We extracted source events from a circular region with a radius of 15″ centred at the
GAIA

The source always falls near a chip border or in a corner of the chip; therefore, for background extraction, we considered a nearby circular region with a radius of 40″ in the same CCD and free of other X-ray sources. To correct for the background and for the vignetting given by the off-axis position of the source, we produced background-subtracted light curves using the task
epiclccorr

3.1.1
Recurrent modulating behaviour

Figure 1:
Chandra
light curve (black points) in the 0.5–7 keV band of ObsIDs 22648, 22649, and 23182. The bin time is 1500 s. The best-fit sinusoidal is shown in red. The dotted line shows the model’s extrapolation during time intervals with no data.

In Fig.
1
we show the light curve in the 0.5–7 keV band of the
Chandra
ObsIDs 22648, 22649, and 23182, performed within 2 days in 2020. In the following, we will refer to these observations as dataset C1. A modulation on the timescale of a few tens of kiloseconds is clearly visible. To estimate the period of the modulation, we fitted the C1 light curve with a sinusoid function, plus a constant to model the mean flux level. We obtained a best-fit period of
P=25.4\pm 0.4
ks, with
\chi^{2}/\mathrm{dof}=147/64
. The overall bad quality of the fit is probably driven by the presence of a peak in the last 10 ks of the light curve. The peak appears to happen where one would expect a minimum of the modulation. As one can see from Fig.
4
and Fig.
14
, J1257 showed at least twice a flare-like event when the flux is low (see Section
3.1.2
). A similar event could have happened during the last 10 ks of the C1 light curve. We discuss in more detail the implications of this in Sect.
4.2
, where we consider the possibility that this source represents a QPE candidate. Here, we note that if we add a Gaussian component to model the flare, the fit significantly improves, with
\chi^{2}/\mathrm{dof}=75/61
.

Throughout 2020, J1257 flux is characterised by a modulating behaviour. Fig.

3.1.2
Flare-like behaviour

Figure 4:
Light curve of J1257 during the
XMM-Newton

The red (dashed purple) line shows the result of the fit of the hardness ratio with a constant outside (within) the high-flux phase (highlighted in blue). The corresponding shaded region shows the 1

J1257 showed (at least twice) a flaring-like behaviour, in which the count rate suddenly increased, that is ObsID 12887 and ObsID 0691610201. The light curve of ObsID 12887 is shown in Fig.
14
. Here, we focus on ObsID 0691610201, which has a higher statistic and allows for a better study of the energy-dependent emission. In the upper panel Fig.
4
, we present the
XMM-Newton
light curve from ObsID 0691610201 in the soft (0.3–1 keV, black points) and hard (1–10 keV, red points) band, together with the hardness ratio (hard/soft) in the lower panel. We tried different energy cuts. Based on the shape of the energy spectrum (see e.g. Figure
5
), we selected a soft band from 0.3 keV to 0.6 keV and a hard band from 1.5 keV to 10 keV or from 2 keV to 10 keV. These choices did not change our results. Therefore, we opted for the cut at 1 keV between the two bands to have a similar number of photons and similar statistics.

We identified a low-flux state and a high-flux state (highlighted in blue in the plots). After the first
\simeq 18
ks, during which the count rate has a mean value of
\simeq 0.05
counts/s, it rapidly increases of a factor
\sim 5
in about 5 ks. After a decrease in the following
5-6
ks, this high-flux state ends, but right after it, the count rate seems to rise again. Unfortunately, this rise coincides with the end of the observation. Therefore, we cannot verify whether J1257 is showing the same modulation we detected in the previously shown
Chandra
observations or a different kind of variability. It is interesting to note, however, that the flare during ObsID 0691610201 has a similar duration (
\sim 10
ks) and peak/out-of-peak flux ratio in the 0.5–7 keV band (
\sim 5
) of the flare detected at the end of ObsID 23182, shown in Fig.
1
. These similarities could suggest that the two flares are produced by the same mechanism.

To model the evolution of the hardness ratio, we considered the values outside (within) the high-flux state and fitted the hardness ratio with a constant. The result of our fit is shown by the red (purple) solid (dashed) line. The flux seems to get softer during the high-flux state, but the values of the hardness ratio outside (
HR_{\mathrm{low}}=0.69\pm 0.05
) and during (
HR_{\mathrm{high}}=0.55\pm 0.02
) this phase are consistent within 3
\sigma
. Therefore, we cannot claim any significant change in the hardness ratio during the observation. We studied the hardness ratio for all the
XMM-Newton
and
Chandra
observations reported in Table
2
. For the
XMM-Newton
observations, we used the same 0.3–1/1–10 keV bands, while for
Chandra
observations we used a soft 0.5–2 keV band and a hard 2–7 keV to met our requirement on an equal number of photons in both bands. As for ObsID 0691610201, we always found that a constant is sufficient to model the evolution of the hardness ratio.

3.2
X-ray spectral analysis

The X-ray spectra were analysed with the spectral fitting package
XSPEC

(
Arnaud 1996
)
version 12.12.1. The spectra were rebinned to have at least 1 count per energy bin, and the modified Cash statistic
(
Cash 1979
, W-stat;)
was adopted. All X-ray spectra were corrected for foreground interstellar absorption adopting the model
TBabs
, with
N_{\textup{H}}
fixed to the Galactic value
8.4\times 10^{19}
cm
-2

(
HI4PI Collaboration et al. 2016
)
, using abundances from
Wilms et al. 2000
, with the photoelectric absorption cross-sections from
Verner et al. 1996
. All reported uncertainties correspond to
1\sigma
, all reported fluxes and luminosities are corrected for absorption.

J1257 shows significant variability in its spectral behaviour: while a simple power-law model can reproduce satisfactorily the X-ray spectrum of the source in most observations, in some epochs the quality of the fit is significantly improved by the addition of a thermal component and/or a layer of intrinsic absorption (in excess of the Galactic one). We modelled the thermal component with a simple blackbody profile, with temperatures of about
kT\sim 110
eV. This thermal component is mostly detected in observations taken before 2012, as in the latest
Chandra
observations, the loss of effective area in the soft band
(
Plucinsky et al. 2018
;
Plucinsky et al. 2022
, below
\approx 1
keV; see)
prevents us from constraining its presence. The layer of intrinsic neutral absorption improves the quality of the fit in the most recent observations (from 2012 and in 2020), and it is characterised by a column density in the
(0.1-2)\times 10^{22}
cm
-2
range. The photon index
\Gamma
varies significantly too, spanning a wide range of values, from
\Gamma=0.8
to
\Gamma=2
.

This spectral evolution is associated with significant long-term variability in the source’s flux. As reported in Fig.
6
, the source’s flux varies by a factor of about 3 in time scales of days, both in the soft (0.3–2 keV) and hard (2–10 keV) band. We inspected the correlation between the spectral shape and the flux state of the source and, as highlighted in Fig.
7
, J1257 appears to exhibit two distinct behaviours. When the source’s flux is below
\approx 3\times 10^{-13}
erg/s/cm
2
, it shows the full range of spectral steepness described above. When instead the source flux is above the
\approx 3\times 10^{-13}
erg/s/cm
2
threshold, it seems to exhibit a softer-when-brighter behaviour. However, a more in-depth analysis of this feature is limited by the heterogeneity of the available dataset.

5
. An exemplary spectrum (rebinned for graphical purposes) is shown in Fig.

. Given the extreme variability in the spectral shape, it appears that the

4
Discussion

A crossmatch between the
Chandra
coordinates and the 2MASS catalogue
(
Skrutskie et al. 2006
)
confirms that we are indeed observing the central SMBH of the galaxy. It is interesting to note that the classification of J1257 is still debated: optical observations point towards a Seyfert-1 galaxy
(
Véron-Cetty & Véron 2010
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Negus et al. 2024
)
, while the analysis in the infrared band of WISE and SDSS data led
Toba et al. 2014
to classify J1257 as a Seyfert-2 galaxy, suggesting that J1257 could be a changing-look AGN.

Our analysis shows that J1257 possesses peculiar X-ray variability. A repeated modulation, and possibly a flare-like behaviour, is present on the timescales of
\sim
20 ks. Unfortunately, although J1257 has been observed multiple times by both
Chandra
and
XMM-Newton
, most observations do not last long enough for a definitive classification of this variability. The long-term light curve shows that the source flux can change up to a factor of 3 in a few days, while the spectral analysis reveals that J1257 shows hints of a softer-when-brighter behaviour.

In this Section, we discuss the most plausible origins of this repeated variability in light of our findings.

4.1
Quasi-periodic modulation candidate

Looking at Fig.
2
and
1
, it is tempting to classify the modulation as a QPO, or a quasi-periodic outflow
(
Pasham et al. 2024
, QPOut, see)
. The modulation seems to be present throughout 2020, as shown by the phase-folded light curve in Fig.
3
. With the available data, we cannot test the presence of quasi-periodicity in the absorption column density, a signature of QPOut. Therefore, we will only discuss the QPO scenario. Although J1257 has been observed multiple times by both
Chandra
and
XMM-Newton
, most observations do not last long enough to appreciate modulation on the timescales of 10--20 ks. Even in the best dataset (C1), we only detect a few cycles of the putative modulation, which is not enough for a firm detection. Therefore, we will not discuss more complicated models
6
6

6

For a list of possible models explaining QPOs in AGNs, see e.g. Table 1 of
Pasham et al. 2024
.
.

Assuming that the
\simeq 3.3\times 10^{-5}
Hz QPO is linked with a fundamental frequency at the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), the QPO is either related to the Keplerian orbital frequency
\nu_{\phi}
, the vertical epicyclic frequency
\nu_{\theta}
, or the Lense-Thirring frequency
\nu_{\mathrm{LT}}
. The three frequencies can be linked to the mass
M_{\mathrm{BH}}
, the radius of the ISCO
R_{\mathrm{ISCO}}
, and the dimensionless spin parameter
a_{*}
of J1257
(
Kato 1990
, see equations derived by)
. Considering a mass
\sim 10^{6.3}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, only Lense-Thirring can produce such a modulation. However, in this scenario it would be hard to reconcile the mass of the BH with the observed frequency of the modulation, compared to other SMBHs. By looking at Figure 5 of
Yan et al. 2024
, J1257 frequency of
\simeq 3.3\times 10^{-5}
Hz would be extremely low for a SMBH with a mass
\sim 10^{6.3}\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.

4.2
J1257 as a possible QPE candidate

The
Chandra
light curve in Fig.
1
comprises three main X-ray flares that are close to symmetric, separated by similar time intervals of
51.3
ks and
50.0
ks, and followed by a shorter-duration and higher amplitude burst towards the end of the third observation. The interpretation in terms of a sinusoidal modulation with period of
\simeq 25.4
ks in which some of the peaks fall within the gaps is not unique, and although the nature of the light curve prevents us from firmly assessing the significance of the putative periodicity, it is worth briefly exploring further possible interpretations. Here, we consider the possibility that the observed variability might be associated with QPEs.

Figure 8:
Chandra
light curve in the 0.5–7 keV band of ObsIDs 22648, 22649, and 23182, as shown in Fig.
1
. The upper and lower panels show in red the best-fitting model 1 and model 2, respectively (see text for details). The model extrapolation within the gaps is shown as a dotted line.

A model in which the sinusoidal modulation is replaced by three Gaussian functions, and the last shorter duration flare is also described with the same function representing QPEs, is shown in the upper panel of Fig.
8
(model 1). The double QPE structure seen in the third observation is possibly repeated in the first two as well, as hinted by the rapid rise in count rate following the main flares. In fact, adding two Gaussian functions with all parameters fixed to those of the last flare (except centroid) to account for the rise towards the end of the first and second observation returns a statistical result of
\chi^{2}/{\rm dof}=65/53
. Despite the excellent result, we refrain from showing the best-fitting model here because such a model appears too speculative at this stage. However, we point out that the X-ray light curve is at least consistent with the typical QPE one in which longer and shorter recurrence times (as well as stronger and weaker QPEs) alternate
(
Miniutti et al. 2023b
, for one representative example see)
.

On the other hand, and still within the framework of QPEs, the data can also be interpreted in a different way, which heavily relies on the analogy with the best-studied QPE source GSN 069. When QPEs are present in GSN 069, the quiescent emission (the emission in-between flares) exhibits a significant sinusoidal modulation at the same period as the average QPE recurrence time
(
Miniutti et al. 2023b
, see Figure 5 of)
. The situation might be similar in J1257 with the three main flares being part of the QPO-like modulation of the quiescent emission and the last burst being, in fact, a QPE (with others possibly confined within the gaps). Such a model (model 2), described by a sinusoidal modulation plus a Gaussian function, provides a good description of the overall light curve and is shown in the lower panel of Fig.
8
.

Therefore, an interpretation of the X-ray variability in terms of QPEs appears speculative - mostly due to the limited number of observed cycles - but plausible. It is then interesting to consider whether the derived timing parameters (recurrence time and QPE duration) are in line with the overall observed QPE population. Considering the broadest constraints that can be derived from either model, we infer
T_{\rm rec}=13.1
-
51.3
ks and
T_{\rm dur}=9.5
-
19.5
ks in J1257. These estimates allow us to place J1257 onto the
T_{\rm dur}
-
T_{\rm rec}
plane of the current QPE population,
(
Arcodia et al. 2025
, see, for example, Figure 8 of)
. The location of J1257 in the QPE
T_{\rm dur}
-
T_{\rm rec}
plane is shown in Fig
9
as a purple area and appears to be consistent with J1257 being part of the QPE family.

Figure 9:
Location of J1257 (purple region) on the
T_{\rm dur}
-
T_{\rm rec}
plane defined by the current QPE population (light blue regions). The grey area represents events with
T_{\rm dur}\geq T_{\rm rec}
that cannot be classified as well-isolated QPEs. We point out that QPEs with long recurrence time and short duration, which would populate the lower-right corner of the plane, are extremely difficult to detect, so that the clear correlation seen in the QPE timing plane could be affected, at least partly, by an observational bias. QPE data are taken from
Arcodia et al. 2025
.

However, from an X-ray spectral point of view, the X-ray variability in J1257 is distinct from that of QPEs. QPEs are thermal-like soft X-ray flares with typical temperatures of
kT\sim 100
-
200
eV - therefore contributing very marginally above 2 keV - which exhibit a clear counter-clockwise pattern in the
L-T
plane during the QPE evolution in all sources observed thus far
(
Miniutti et al. 2023b
, e.g. Fig. 18 in)
. On the other hand, the X-ray flaring activity in J1257 appears to be almost achromatic with only a slightly higher relative amplitude in the soft X-ray band, as shown in Fig.
3
. Hence, the X-ray flares in J1257 cannot be classified as bona-fide QPEs, despite tentative evidence from Fig.
9
. Besides the short-duration flare at the end of the C1 dataset, the variability properties of J1257 are in fact similar to those of 2XMM J123103.2+110648, which displays a clear
\simeq 3.8
h QPO which has been associated in the past with a long duty-cycle variation of QPEs
(
Webbe & Young 2023
)
, a suggestion recently challenged by results by
Cao et al. 2025
. We note that J1257 is an AGN with a standard X-ray corona producing hard X-rays, which is not the case in QPE sources, where the contribution above 2 keV is mostly marginal at best in both quiescence and QPE peak. The X-ray corona in J1257 might change the intrinsic X-ray spectrum of the (putative) QPEs by redistributing photons to higher energy through inverse Compton scattering. The quality of the C1 data set is not high enough for us to claim any significant lag between soft and hard bands that would corroborate this hypothesis.

Considering popular QPE models invoking the collision between a secondary object and the accretion disc in an EMRI system
(
Linial & Metzger 2023
;
Franchini et al. 2023
)
, the observed timescales are related to the EMRI orbit. In particular, if we assume as baseline the observed
25.4
ks timescale, this could correspond to the EMRI orbital period if QPEs are produced by impacts occurring once per orbit, or to half of it in the case of two impacts per orbit. For the estimated black hole mass (
\log M_{\rm BH}\simeq 6.3
), this translates into a semi-major axis in the range of
55
-
88
R
g
depending on the assumed number of collisions per orbit.

5
Conclusions

We have reported on the timing and spectral analysis of
Chandra
and
XMM-Newton
data of J1257, a Seyfert galaxy at
z=0.02
. Prompted by the detection of a repeated modulation at timescales of
\simeq
20 ks, we found that J1257 also shows long-term X-ray variability coupled with a complex spectral evolution in the last 20 years, reported here for the first time.
Whatever the origin, the results we showed in this paper suggest that J1257 is a new specimen of the growing class of SMBHs showing repeated X-ray variability. In particular, it could be either a QPO candidate at a particularly long period or a QPE candidate with an unusually hard spectrum, making J1257 a particularly interesting source. If confirmed, given the connection of these phenomena with EMRIs/IMRIs, J1257 could also represent a source of interest for future missions such as LISA, for a deeper comprehension of the SMBH phenomenology. Due to the limited number of observed cycles, the modulation’s statistical significance cannot be estimated robustly, and both the confirmation of its presence and the detailed characterisation of its spectral-timing properties must await longer, uninterrupted future X-ray observations.
