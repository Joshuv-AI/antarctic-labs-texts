Failure Rate After Shoulder Stabilization Procedures in the Military With Use of Knotless Suture Tape: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Scott M Feeley, Bobby G Yow, Benjamin W Hoyt, Caitlyn J Koo, Sean M Wade, Jonathan F Dickens, Robert A Waltz, John-Paul H Rue, Lance E LeClere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Biomechanical studies have demonstrated improved loading properties and tissue tear-through of suture tapes as compared with suture, suggesting that a tape construct in labral repair may reduce failure rates. However, comparative clinical studies are lacking.Purpose/Hypothesis:The purpose was to determine the rate of recurrent instability after arthroscopic stabilization and compare survivability and risk factors for failure in a persistently high-demand military population. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference between the failure rates of arthroscopic stabilization procedures when utilizing a suture tape construct as compared with suture for all instability patterns.Study Design:Case-control study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:The authors retrospectively reviewed all shoulder stabilization procedures performed by 3 fellowship-trained sports surgeons at a single high-volume military facility from 2010 to 2020. Given the purported benefits of tape constructs, the surgeons at this facility transitioned from knotted suture to knotless suture tape constructs in 2015. Patients were included who underwent arthroscopic labral repair with minimum 2-year follow-up. Descriptive data, prior instability events, surgical details, and revision procedures were recorded. Intraoperative details included size and location of the tear, concurrent lesions, number and type of anchors, and suture type used. Glenoid bone loss was measured using the circle method, and the Hills-Sachs index was used to evaluate for the presence of off-track lesions. The primary outcome was repair failure. Survival analysis and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed by instability pattern groups to identify factors most associated with failure.Results:A total of 448 procedures were identified, of which 163 anterior, 51 posterior, and 111 combined labral repairs were analyzed. Median follow-up was >6.5 years across groups. On univariate analysis for posterior instability, the use of a knotless suture tape construct was associated with a lower chance of failure when compared with knotted suture constructs (0% vs 20.6%; P = .044). On multivariate analysis accounting for other confounding factors, the use of knotless suture tape was associated with a lower odds of failure (anterior repair: odds ratio [OR], 0.58 [P = .042]; combined repair: OR, 0.22 [P = .025]), while glenoid bone loss >10% was associated with failure after anterior repairs (OR, 3.91; P = .038).Conclusion:In a young, at-risk military population undergoing primary arthroscopic labral repair, the use of knotless suture tape was associated with decreased failure regardless of instability pattern.Keywords: shoulder instability, suture tape, military, anterior, posterior, knotless

Shoulder instability occurs at a higher incidence in the military as compared with a civilian population, and posterior and combined instability patterns are becoming increasingly recognized.12,16,30,34,36,45 Regardless of pattern, recurrent instability can lead to extension of existing tears and critical progression of glenoid bone loss, which is a known risk factor for failure of arthroscopic repair.2,8,38 Owing to the high demands of the military profession combined with concern over worsening pathology from recurrent instability, surgeons treating military patients frequently recommend stabilization procedures after the first instability event. Despite this approach, 14% of military patients undergoing anterior shoulder stabilization procedures proceed to revision surgery, with similar rates of recurrent instability after arthroscopic anterior or posterior labral repair.4,12,41 However, there have been many advancements in the arthroscopic management of shoulder instability over the last decade, which may affect failure rates.

There are many known patient-specific risk factors for failure after stabilization procedures for shoulder instability, including younger age, participation in contact sports, glenoid bone loss, acromial morphology, glenoid version, and unaddressed near-track and off-track Hill-Sachs lesions.2,20,25,29,39,42,46 Additionally, patient positioning, open versus arthroscopic technique, and implant selection have been analyzed for their association with recurrent instability.7,10,44 One recent advance in fixation has been the development of high–tensile strength suture tape, which is wider and flatter than the historically used round and narrow high–tensile strength suture, distributing tensile forces more evenly over a broader area. In biomechanical studies, high–tensile strength suture tapes have demonstrated improved single and cyclical loads to failure and tissue tear-through as compared with high–tensile strength suture, suggesting that a tape construct may reduce failure rates at least at time zero.11,14,18,37 However, evidence comparing the influence of these implants on clinical outcomes after orthopaedic procedures is limited, particularly for labral repair.9,19,31

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to (1) determine the rate of recurrent instability at a minimum 2-year follow-up after arthroscopic shoulder stabilization procedures and (2) to compare survivability between suture and suture tape constructs and determine risk factors for failure in a persistently high-demand military population across patterns of shoulder instability. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference between the failure rates of arthroscopic stabilization procedures when utilizing a suture tape construct as compared with suture for all instability patterns.

Methods

After approval from the institutional review board, a retrospective study was performed of active duty servicemembers who underwent primary arthroscopic shoulder stabilization, as performed by 3 fellowship-trained sports surgeons at a single high-volume military facility from January 2010 to January 2020. Given the purported benefits of suture tape constructs, the surgeons at this facility transitioned to use of suture tape in 2015. Patients were included who underwent arthroscopic labral repair for documented history of shoulder instability (pain, apprehension, subluxation, or dislocation) and had examination or imaging consistent with glenohumeral labral pathology. Patients were excluded who underwent superior labral repair or had follow-up <2 years. Patients elected to undergo surgery after an informed discussion with the surgeon based on their symptoms, instability history, athletic and occupational demands, examination, and advanced imaging findings.

Patient demographics, prior instability events, details of surgery, and revision procedures were collected. Prior instability events were distinguished by subluxation and dislocation. Intraoperative details included size and location of the tear, concurrent lesions (humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament, anterior labroligamentous periosteal sleeve avulsion, rotator cuff tear, and glenolabral articular disruption), number of anchors, and suture type used. The primary outcome was repair failure, defined as recurrent shoulder instability events, including subjective subluxation and dislocation, recurrence of pain consistent with instability, functionally limiting apprehension, or a revision stabilization procedure. Pain with evidence of glenohumeral arthritis was not considered a failure without the presence of subjective symptoms or objective signs of instability.

Tear location was determined by review of the operative note with regard to anterior, posterior, or combined tears. When inconsistency in nomenclature occurred, anchor position was used to determine tear location, where anterior repair positions included the predominance of anchors from approximately 3 o'clock to 6:30, posterior repair positions included 5:30 to 9 o'clock, and combined tears included >1-hour extension into the posterior region from an anterior tear or vice versa. Clockface positions were defined with reference to a right shoulder, where 3 o'clock was defined as anterior.

Surgical Technique

All labral repairs were performed arthroscopically in the lateral decubitus position with a single-row repair. Before 2015, Arthrex 3.0-mm SutureTak or BioComposite SutureTak anchors (Arthrex) with single- or double-loaded high–tensile strength suture were utilized for labral repair at the surgeon's discretion (“suture” construct). Double-loaded suture anchors were generally placed at the 6-o’clock position on the glenoid when utilized. After 2015, 2.9-mm polyetheretherketone knotless PushLock anchors with 1.5-mm LabralTape (Arthrex; “suture tape” construct) were utilized for labral repair (Figure 1).

Imaging Analysis

Two reviewers assessed all preoperative 2-dimensional magnetic resonance imaging. Recorded data included the presence of Hill-Sachs lesions and the amount of glenoid bone loss (GBL) according to the best-fit circle method on sagittal sequences, defined as a ratio of the defect to the total diameter of a circle superimposed on the lower two-thirds of the glenoid. 13 When a repaired bony Bankart lesion was present that was >8% glenoid width, this was not considered glenoid bone loss given the expectation for bony healing at this size when repaired or incorporated into a labral repair. 27 The Hill-Sachs index was measured and compared with glenoid track measurements to evaluate for the presence of off-track lesions as previously described.28,43

Statistical Analysis

The cohort was grouped by instability pattern into isolated anterior, isolated posterior, or combined anterior and posterior instability for statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were generated for the study population, with continuous variables presented as mean and standard deviation when normally distributed or median and interquartile range when not. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Survival analysis and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with failure. Variables were eligible for entry into multivariate analysis if they were nearly significant or close to association with recurrence (P < .2). Regardless of association, age, sex, and suture tape versus suture were selected as independent factors. Statistical analysis was conducted in RStudio 1.2.1335 software (RStudio). P < .05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

In total, 448 arthroscopic stabilization procedures were identified: 212 anterior, 78 posterior, and 158 combined labral repairs.

Discussion

The chief finding of this study was that in young military patients undergoing primary arthroscopic labral repair, the use of knotless suture tape constructs was associated with lower rates of failure as compared with a knotted suture construct. On univariate analysis, there were no instances of failure in posterior instability with the use of suture tape, while a knotted high–tensile strength suture construct failed in 20.6% of cases. Recurrence rates for the anterior instability and combined instability groups were similar between suture and suture tape cohorts on univariate analysis; however, after accounting for associated risk factors, there was a 1.7-times lower odds of failure in anterior instability and a 4.5-times lower odds of failure in combined instability. In anterior instability, critical glenoid bone loss, defined as a defect >10% in this study, was associated with failure. The protective association with knotless suture tapes should be considered in all future studies evaluating risk factors for recurrence after shoulder stabilization procedures.

Anterior Instability

Failure Rate

The overall failure rate after anterior labral repair in this study was 17.8% at a long-term median follow-up of 8.1 years (IQR, 5.8-10.2). This failure rate is comparable to another study of military patients before the advent of suture tape availability, which demonstrated that 14% undergo revision surgery or fail to return to duty after 2 to 7 years. 41 Younger age in that study was a significant risk factor for failure, although the mean age was 28.0 years as compared with a median age and interquartile range within normal collegiate age in this study. The study also did not include recurrent instability events in its definition of failure. These factors and shorter follow-up duration may account for the comparable but small difference in failure rates found in the present study.

Edwin et al 9 reviewed outcomes for 67 patients undergoing primary arthroscopic Bankart repair with knotless 2.9-mm PushLock anchors with 1.5-mm LabralTape, similar to the implants used for suture tape constructs in this study. Only 3 of their 67 patients reported recurrent instability. However, they lacked a comparison cohort, and their follow-up time frame of 12 months was shorter than the long-term follow-up in the present study and may not have captured all cases of recurrence. Similar to the current study, Edwin et al reported a decrease in anchor utilization with suture tape, noting that <3 anchors were used in 76% of cases, where their standard practice before using suture tapes was to use 3 anchors for the same tightening of the capsulolabral complex.

Glenoid Bone Loss

Glenoid bone loss is a known risk factor for recurrent instability after arthroscopic Bankart repair.15,35 The threshold for critical bone loss is an ongoing area of investigation, with support for decreasing critical values to as low as 13.5% owing to associations with worse outcomes. 33 The prevalence of off-track lesions (2.2% for isolated anterior repairs) and glenoid bone loss was minimal in the current study, which is in agreement with other studies suggesting that glenoid bone loss is more prevalent in cases of recurrent instability. Our study adds support to decreasing critical thresholds for GBL in anterior instability patterns, demonstrating an increased rate of failure in cases of GBL >10% in highly active individuals (odds ratio, 3.91; 95% CI, 1.42-14.7; P = .038).

Posterior Instability

Posterior Anatomic Differences

The difference in rates of recurrent instability between fixation types was more pronounced in the isolated posterior instability subgroup. There were no instances of recurrent instability when suture tape was utilized, as compared with a 20.6% rate of recurrent instability with high–tensile strength suture. Anatomic differences between the posterior and anterior capsulolabral complexes may account for this impact. In a biomechanical study of knotless anterior and posterior Bankart repairs, there was no difference in failure load or stiffness between use of suture tape and high–tensile strength suture in anterior repairs. 22 While the authors did not perform the same comparison in posterior repairs, they did report that the intact posterior capsulolabral complex withstood higher loads to failure than the intact anterior complex. Another biomechanical study determined that the failure strains of the inferior and middle posterior capsule were significantly less than that anteriorly. 3 Additionally, the anterior capsular tissue was significantly thicker. This finding of thinning of the posterior capsular tissue as compared with anterior has been supported elsewhere.6,26 If posterior capsular tissue is indeed thinner, then the purported benefits of suture tape to increase loads to tissue pull-through may have increased benefit for posterior stabilization procedures. 11

Imaging Risk Factors: Glenoid Bone Loss and Retroversion

Posterior glenoid bone loss is a known risk factor for recurrent instability after posterior labral repair.1,42 The threshold for critical pGBL is an ongoing area of investigation, with support for decreasing critical values to as low as 11% for increased reoperation and failure rates. 1 The incidence of pGBL at these critical values was minimal in the current study at 7.1% of cases, which is in agreement with other studies that pGBL is more pronounced in cases of recurrent instability.1,42 However, in a similar patient population prospectively followed, an instability event has been shown to result in pGBL of 10% after the first time and 16.8% with recurrent instability. The mean values of 4.2% in reverse Bankart lesions and 4.6% when including larger reverse bony Bankart lesions in the present study are lower than critical thresholds previously reported, which may help to explain why pGBL was not associated with failure in the present study where there were importantly also no differences between fixation types.

Glenoid retroversion has been associated with failure of nonoperative treatment of posterior labral tears and recurrent instability after stabilization procedures.5,29,42 In a prospectively followed group at risk for shoulder instability, glenoid retroversion was identified as the most significant risk factor for posterior instability. 29 In patients with posterior instability, mean retroversion in that study was 17.6°, and an increase of 1° of retroversion correlated to a 17% increased risk. In the present study, the mean retroversion was 7.4° and did not differ between fixation types. Patients with critical values of pGBL have also been shown to have increased retroversion, which may help to explain the lack of association between version and failure in the present study since pGBL was minimal and version was closer to that of a normal population in our group. 42 Additionally, multivariate analysis was not able to be performed to further account for the possible confounding variable of suture tape use.

Combined Anterior and Posterior Instability

Incidence of Combined Instability

Among this cohort of 448 young military patients with a median age of 21.6 years treated with arthroscopic labral repair, 35.3% were confirmed to have a combined instability pattern. This finding supports the need for increased suspicion of combined labral pathology, especially in young and highly active individuals. Players who had labral tears on advanced imaging at the National Football League scouting combine were found to have combined tears in 35% of cases. 21 Javed et al 16 also previously found a higher proportion of combined labral tears in patients undergoing stabilization procedures who were involved in sports versus those who were not. Their rate of 35% was similar to the rate of 35.3% in our cohort. The occupational demands of our cohort are similar to these groups and are reflected in multiple studies of military cohorts reporting increased incidences of combined labral tears from 19% to 44% of patients treated with stabilization procedures.12,34,36

Failure Rate

Mazzocca et al 24 prospectively evaluated traumatic 270° labral tears excluding SLAP tears (superior labrum anterior and posterior) in 20 cases at short-term follow-up. The mean Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation score was 91, with failure and revision surgery occurring in 15% and 5% of cases, respectively. Recurrent instability was defined as any event causing pain that led to missed work or sporting activity. Despite evidence of repair restoring stability in their study, Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index scores indicated that patients remained protective of their shoulders. After 10 years, the authors reevaluated their cohort and had a failure rate of 14.2% in 21 cases, concluding that stability was maintained at long-term follow-up. Mean age at surgery was 27 years with a follow-up of 11.9 years in their final analysis. While there was a difference in follow-up time between use of suture tape and suture constructs in the present study, the long-term outcomes of Mazzocca et al suggest that stability restored in the midterm may persist in the long term for combined repairs. In contrast, our suture constructs continued to demonstrate risk of failure even after 8 to 10 years of follow-up. However, when controlling for follow-up duration on multivariate analysis, use of suture tape was still protective through the midterm.

Biomechanical Analysis

Wider suture tape for fixation is not the only implant-mediated approach to improve contact pressures and footprint coverage for healing tissue. The positioning and number of anchors and suture can also affect the healing and biomechanical properties of a repair. Kamath et al 17 demonstrated that a 2-anchor construct with double-loaded anchors was noninferior biomechanically to 3 single-loaded anchors for Bankart repair. However, multiple clinical studies have demonstrated increased failure rates with use of <3 single-loaded anchors. With the risk of suture arthropathy from implants and the potential for fracture from stress risers associated with multiple anchor sites, the use of double-loaded anchors or suture tape is an important consideration for decreasing these risks while providing adequate contact pressures for healing.32,40 In the current study, suture tape failure rates were not significantly different from suture constructs on univariate analysis in anterior and combined labral repairs. Yet, significantly fewer anchors and stitches were implanted in cases where suture tape was used, which may offset some of the aforementioned risks. On multivariate analysis, accounting for the number of stitches from double-loaded anchors revealed that suture tape constructs had a clinically and statistically significant decreased odds of failure as compared with suture.

While there may be some contribution to the failure rates reported in the present study attributed to a switch from a knotted to a knotless configuration, this impact was expected to be minimized on the basis of prior studies comparing these 2 techniques. Matache et al 23 found no significant difference in outcomes between knotted and knotless configurations for labral repair in their systematic review of level 2 and 3 studies. When examining posterior labral repairs, where the decreased odds of failure were highest in the present study, DeFoor et al 7 similarly found no difference in recurrent instability or revision surgery between knotted and knotless configurations in their systematic review. Based on these findings, the contributions of the suture tape may have a larger impact on the differences in failure rates between constructs in the present study.

The predominance of biomechanical studies examining implant use for labral repair have focused on anterior instability patterns. There may be differences in posterior capsule and labral quality in posterior instability relative to anterior instability that alter the effectiveness of either fixation construct and could account for the comparatively larger decrease in failure rates in the isolated posterior and combined instability groups in the present study relative to isolated anterior instability.

Limitations

There are several notable limitations to our study. The retrospective design is limited by inherent biases and relies on the accuracy and completeness of clinical documentation for determination of the primary outcome. The use of suture tape was clearly associated with a reduction in failure rate for posterior labral repairs on univariate analysis, whereas the failure rate was not statistically different for anterior and combined repairs until confounding factors were accounted for on multivariate analysis, such as the number of stitches per anchor. Thus, there may be fragility in the data for anterior and combined repairs that warrants further prospective study.

While all patients were highly active as military servicemembers getting treatment at a military training academy, level of sport activity and occupational demands were not analyzed. This may be a confounding factor affecting the failure rates for each group. Furthermore, while implant use was not randomized, there was a temporal shift to knotless suture tape constructs midway through the study period that should limit selection bias in patients who received knotless suture tape versus knotted suture constructs. However, there may be a selection bias for the patients who received double- versus single-loaded anchors that affected failure rates. The temporal shift in implant use did affect the follow-up duration for the fixation types, although this did not appear to influence the rate of recurrence in survival analysis through the midterm. Additionally, surgeon experience could have biased failure results toward the use of suture tape since the use of suture tape was performed later in each surgeon's career. Yet, each surgeon was >4 years into military practice when the switch in implants was made. The inclusion of bony Bankart lesions may have affected the results, as suture type might also have different effects for fixing a bony versus all soft tissue lesion.

The patients included in this retrospective review were treated by multiple surgeons at a single institution with similar techniques, postoperative protocols, and access to care. While some patients may have had instances of recurrent instability after leaving the military that were not captured, for those who remained in the military, the closed Military Health System allows for the ability to reliably follow patients longitudinally for tracking of any recurrent injury or subsequent surgery.

Conclusion

In a young, at-risk military population undergoing primary arthroscopic labral repair, the use of knotless suture tape was associated with decreased failure regardless of instability pattern.
