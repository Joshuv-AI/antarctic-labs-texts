Challenges in the Management of Hemorrhagic Shock in Patients with Bombay Blood Group in the ICU: What the H?

Dear Editor,

Bombay blood group is a rare blood group phenotype caused by an autosomal recessive condition. The patients' plasma contains anti-A, anti-B, and anti-H antibodies, rendering them unique so they can only be safely transfused with the blood of Bombay group. It is possible to arrange a group-specific compatible blood when there is no urgency, however, during emergencies it is extremely challenging. This immediate unavailability can pose morbidity and can even prove fatal. We report here a case of a patient with the Bombay blood group, who developed severe hemorrhagic shock in the ICU, and we faced significant logistical challenges in emergency management.

Bombay blood group (Oh or hh, i.e., H antigen deficiency) is a unique and rare blood group phenotype caused by autosomal recessive condition. First reported from India, its estimated prevalence is 0.0001%.1–3 In the ABO group, A and B antigen expression is determined by the presence of precursor H antigen on red blood cells. The A and B fucosyltransferase enzymes convert H antigen into either A or B antigens to create A and B blood groups respectively. Group O has inactive transferase and hence H antigen is not converted to either A or B. In Bombay phenotype, however, the H antigen itself is lacking. This may sometimes lead to Bombay blood group being misinterpreted as O group, with a potential for causing mismatched transfusion. The plasma of Bombay phenotype patients contains anti-A, anti-B, and anti-H antibodies, which will react with all blood groups, except the Bombay group.4 Therefore, patients with Bombay blood group can only be safely transfused blood of Bombay group.5 Also, platelet transfusion needs to be limited to the type A2, because the ABH antigens are expressed on platelets, and platelet units also contain some donor red blood cells (RBCs). They can however safely receive fresh frozen plasma and cryoprecipitate as any other patient.

A 61-year-old lady with Bombay blood group, underwent an uneventful radical hysterectomy for endometrial carcinoma. She had extrapulmonary tuberculosis, for which she was on Antitubercular medications for 2 months before surgery. Twenty days later, debridement of the wound was performed for persistent surgical site infection. She was transfused one unit of Bombay blood group blood during the procedure. Two days later, she was shifted to ICU with severe shock and diffuse intravascular coagulopathy (DIC). She was intubated, mechanically ventilated and put on intravenous antibiotics. After fluid boluses, norepinephrine infusion was commenced for hypotension. She continued to have severe diffuse bleeding from surgical site and vascular puncture sites. The wound site was packed and no further surgical intervention was planned. Her hemoglobin dropped to 4 g and the coagulation was deranged. One unit of packed red blood cells (PRBC) available from a single stand-by donor was immediately transfused. Fresh frozen plasma and platelets (type A2) were transfused cautiously under thromboelastography guidance. After this transfusion was over, no blood or donors or relatives with the HH group were immediately available. With great difficulty, four PRBC units were arranged and transfused from donors from other cities over 3 days, after which the Hb stabilized to 7 gm/dL. During this period of urgency, arranging donors was logistically quite challenging, causing significant delays in blood transfusion and potential risks associated with the delay. The patient later developed severe septic shock with multiorgan failure and eventually succumbed on day 12 of ICU admission.

We wish to highlight the logistical challenges in managing a Bombay blood group patient with hemorrhagic shock, when an urgent transfusion is required for resuscitation. This case highlights the pressing need for hospitals to have a well-maintained, regularly updated registry of donors of rare blood groups. In patients with Bombay blood group undergoing elective surgeries group-specific donors can be relatively easily arranged. Various methods to reduce the need for allogenic transfusion (i.e., acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH), preoperative blood donation (time permitting), and intraoperative cell salvage can also be used. However, in emergency surgeries or when unexpected bleeding occurs, it becomes extremely challenging to arrange blood of rare groups which causes significant risk to the patient.

To circumvent this problem, researchers have tried laboratory preparation of Oh red cells by using an enzyme α-1,2-L-fucosidase (FucOB) that removes the H antigen from the red cells and creates artificial Bombay group red cells. Researchers have also tried chemical coating of red cells using polyethylene glycol, which will hide the ABO antigens on the red cell surface. When the ABO antigens are not exposed the risk of ABO incompatibility will be mitigated. This can create truly universal donors as the red cells will be able to hide from the immune system. The studies while being promising, are still experimental.6 We suggest that the governmental and nongovernmental organizations maintain an active registry of hh type donors readily available to all blood banks, along with the contact details of the family members with the same blood type, who can then donate blood urgently during the time of crisis.
