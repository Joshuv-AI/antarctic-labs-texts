Systematic review and meta-analysis of the association of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio with periodontitis
Authors: Saeideh Ghasemi, Bardia Mortezagholi, Emad Movahed, Arshin Ghaedi, Aida Bazrgar, Samira Abdolalizadehil, Shokoufeh Khanzadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRecent evidence suggests the relationship between periodontitis and systemic inflammation, which complete blood count can assess (CBC)-derived biomarkers such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR). We conducted this meta-analysis to evaluate the levels of NLR and PLR of patients with periodontitis compared to those of healthy controls. MethodsWeb of Science, PubMed, ProQuest, Scopus, and Open Grey were searched for studies published before October 20, 2024, without any limitation on date and language; then, using the random-effects model, we reported a standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). In order to assess the quality of publications, we used the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS). Our study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023475214). ResultsOverall, 11 articles were included in the analysis. We found that patients with periodontitis had elevated levels of NLR compared to healthy controls (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI 0.08–0.52, p = 0.007) In the subgroup analysis according to race, patients with periodontitis had elevated levels of NLR compared to healthy controls in among East Asian patients (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI 0.15–0.55, p = 0.001), but not among Turkish (SMD = 0.15, 95% CI − 0.30–0.61, p = 0.50) and Indian (SMD = 0.38, 95% CI − 0.17–0.94, p = 0.18) patients. In addition, PLR level was not different among patients with periodontitis and healthy controls (SMD = 0.06, 95% CI − 0.71–0.83, p = 0.87). ConclusionsThe findings of our investigation, which indicate higher NLR levels in periodontitis patients, show that immune dysregulation plays a role in the etiology of the disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02175-x.

Introduction

Periodontitis is an inflammatory illness characterized by inflammation of periodontal soft tissues and gradual loss of alveolar bone and periodontal ligament [1–3]. Bleeding on probing was found in more than 85% of adults aged 35–64 in accordance with findings of the Fourth National Oral Epidemiological Investigation in China [4]. Periodontitis is described as the breakdown of periodontal tissue and its attachment to teeth due to uncontrolled inflammation in the gingiva over an extended and slow phase [1, 5, 6]. One of the most common chronic inflammatory disorders globally is severe periodontitis, which harms more than 700 million individuals [7]. A growing body of clinical and experimental findings also suggests a possible direct link between periodontitis and some systemic illnesses, including rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, Alzheimer's disease, atherosclerosis, and even cancers [8, 9]. There are two forms of periodontitis: aggressive periodontitis and chronic periodontitis. Aggressive periodontitis is distinguished by its rapid progression, lack of systemic involvement, and occurrence within family members. Conversely, chronic periodontitis generally exhibits a significant accumulation of plaque and calculus, which correlates with the degree of periodontal damage [10].

The initiation and progression of periodontitis depend on various etiologic and risk factors, two of the most prominent ones being the host immune response and local microbiota [11–14].

Recently, studies have shown that neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is a useful inflammatory biomarker in various illnesses. It is more accurate than lymphocyte or neutrophil count alone in predicting patient survival [5, 15]. In addition, NLR is simple, cheap, and straightforward to get. The NLR rises with illness progression, particularly in inflammatory disorders, and this rise is associated with the emergence of several diseases [16–21]. It is an emerging inflammatory marker derived from complete blood count, offering insights into various medical conditions [16]. It reflects the balance between innate (neutrophils) and adaptive (lymphocytes) immune responses, with normal values ranging from 1 to 2 in adults [22]. NLR has been associated with cardiovascular diseases, serving as a predictor of outcomes in stable coronary artery disease and acute coronary syndromes [16]. It also helps distinguish idiopathic orbital inflammation from infectious orbital diseases, with significantly lower NLR values observed in non-infectious cases [23]. NLR’s utility extends across multiple medical disciplines, including emergency medicine, intensive care, surgery, and oncology [22]. Elevated NLR (> 3.0) may indicate pathological states such as cancer, atherosclerosis, or infection, while values above 11 suggest critical illness or severe inflammation [22]. As an inexpensive and widely available marker, NLR offers valuable prognostic information in various clinical settings. Although the relationship between NLR and periodontitis development and progression has been studied in patients, the relationship between NLR and clinical prognosis remains contentious. In general, research findings differ due to differences in sample sizes or other confounding variables that may alter the connection. The inclusion of NLR as a parameter in our meta-analysis was motivated by its well-established significance as an inflammatory marker, which is especially important in the setting of periodontitis, which is known to have systemic inflammatory effects. Justifying the inclusion of NLR is critical because it has major clinical consequences, such as assisting in risk assessment, treatment planning, and assessing the efficacy of therapeutic therapies in periodontitis [24–26]. In addition, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), which can be easily calculated from a complete blood count, is another inflammatory biomarker and reflects two critically opposite inflammatory pathways. The association of this biomarker with several human disorders has been investigated in recent years. This meta-analysis aimed to assess the relationship of NLR and PLR with periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations were used while executing the meta-analysis. Supplementary material 1 shows our study’s PRISMA checklist. This study was registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with ID CRD42023475214. Two independent investigators systematically searched all relevant published papers published before October 20, 2024, in the online databases PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest, and Scopus. Checking these databases individually is important because they fulfill distinct roles in academic research. PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus are mainly geared toward accessing a wide array of peer-reviewed journal articles, while ProQuest encompasses a broader spectrum of resources, such as theses, dissertations, and other specialized academic content across various fields.

“Neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio,” “NLR,” “Platelet to lymphocyte ratio,” “PLR,” and “periodontitis” were the search terms. Furthermore, a free-text search was performed on the Open Grey—the grey literature database. Table 1 shows the exact search strategy of all databases. There were no language and date restrictions.

Exclusion criteria: (1) animal and/or experimental studies; (2) reviews, case reports, and conference abstracts; and (3) duplicate articles or missing biomarker data.

Selection of studies strategy

EndNote X7 (RRID: SCR_014001) was used to import the articles found using the search method. After removing duplicate papers, two authors (SGh and ShKh) independently assessed the publications’ titles and abstracts using predetermined inclusion criteria. Three writers (ShKh, SGh, and AGh) independently reviewed the full-text papers to see whether they satisfied the inclusion requirements. The data were then extracted from the chosen publications by two writers (AB and AGh). Any disagreements amongst the review writers were settled by consensus, with the involvement of a third review author (ShKh) where necessary.

Literature quality and data extraction

Two authors (ShKh and AGh) used the Newcastle–Ottawa quality evaluation scale (NOS) to assess the methodological quality of all included articles [27]. The following information was retrieved from each research: first author, publication year, study location, sample type, the number of participants, type of periodontitis, demographic information (age, gender, and BMI), potential confounding factors (smoking rate and medications), and mean with standard deviation (SD) of the NLR and PLR. Using the previously mentioned formula, data provided as a median and range were changed to mean with SD [28]. Further information was collected by emailing the corresponding author if the important original data were not accessible.

Evaluation of statistical associations

The NLR and PLR of periodontitis patients and healthy controls were compared in this meta-analysis. The mean and standard deviation of NLR and PLR were extracted from included studies and analyzed as continuous variables; then, SMDs (standardized mean differences) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to represent the results. Cochran’s Q-statistics and I2 statistics were used to assess heterogeneity. A value between 25 and 50% defined low heterogeneity, a value between 50 and 75% defined moderate heterogeneity, and a value above 75% defined high heterogeneity [29]. A random-effects model was used for heterogeneity, which was high and significant. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. We sequentially omitted a single article to assess our findings' stability (sensitivity analyses). Stata 11.2 (RRID: SCR_012763) was used to do statistical analyses (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

Evaluation of heterogeneity and publication bias

Meta-regression method and subgroup analysis were used to find the source of heterogeneity. A funnel plot was used to find evidence of publication bias.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) technique was used by one author (AGh) to evaluate the certainty of evidence.

As shown in Fig. 1, we found 1056 articles in the initial search, and among them, we included only 11 articles after multiple screening stages [30–40]. Among studies that initially appeared to meet inclusion criteria, two were excluded because they were review articles [41, 42], one because it was an animal study [43], and four studies because they lacked NLR or PLR data [44–47]. Differences in NLR Level between patients with periodontitis and healthy controls

Eleven studies with 2478 patients and 848 healthy controls were included in this meta-analysis [30–40]. We found that patients with periodontitis had elevated levels of NLR compared to healthy controls (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI 0.08–0.52, p = 0.007, I2 = 77.7%, p Q test < 0.001, Fig. 2). The GRADE approach determined that the evidence's certainty was low (Table 4). Sensitivity analysis showed that sequentially omitting studies did not change the overall estimate, so our result was stable (Fig. 3, Supplementary material 3). Meta-regression

In the meta-regression analysis, there was no significant effect of publication year (B = 0.14, adjusted R2 = 23.58, p = 0.08), the number of patients (B = 0.00001, adjusted R2-20.97, p = 0.96), mean age of cases (B = -0.03, adjusted R2 = 33.86, p = 0.06), NOS score (B = 0.28, adjusted R2 = − 0.25, p = 0.27), the percentage of male patients (B = − 0.002, adjusted R2 = − 19.20%, p = 0.81) or BMI (B = − 0.14, adjusted R2 = 69.94%, p = 0.11) on NLR. Therefore, they could not be the source of heterogeneity among studies reporting the NLR values.

In the meta-regression analysis of PLR, there was no significant effect of publication year (B = 0.31, adjusted R2 = 24.93, p = 0.15), the number of patients (B =− 0.001, adjusted R2 = 14.28, p = 0.23), mean age of cases (B = − 0.05, adjusted R2 = − 2.66, p = 0.4), NOS score (B = 0.34, adjusted R2 = − 17.80, p = 0.67), the percentage of male patients (B = − 0.03, adjusted R2 = − 3.24, p = 0.32), or BMI (B = − 0.27, adjusted R2 = 32.06, p = 0.26) on PLR. Therefore, they could not be the source of heterogeneity among studies reporting the PLR values.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we discovered that periodontitis patients had higher levels of NLR than healthy controls. Although the certainty of the evidence was low, the predetermined low rating of evidence quality from observational studies suggested by the GRADE system might result in an overly significant reduction in the perceived quality of evidence [46]. When we investigated the quality of the included studies, all had high scores based on the NOS scoring system, which can strengthen our results. In fact, a significant strength of this research is our systematic search, which was complemented by a manual evaluation of the references in the retrieved publications. In addition, we limited possible sources of reporting bias, like citation bias, language bias, publication bias, and time lag bias. We did not have language and time lag bias because we conducted our study without limitations on date and language. In addition, we did not have publication bias based on the funnel plot. In addition, we used PRISMA guidelines for our systematic review and included all published studies (Not preferred ones) so that we did not have citation bias.

The literature review and our findings indicate that NLR may be a useful biomarker in clinical practice for a variety of reasons, including: 1. Inflammation Assessment: The NLR is a straightforward hematological analysis that provides insight into the equilibrium between neutrophils, a subset of white blood cells closely linked to acute inflammatory responses, and lymphocytes, a vital component of the immune system. Elevated values of the NLR are suggestive of an augmented neutrophilic response to either infection or inflammation. In individuals diagnosed with periodontitis, an increased NLR signifies an augmented immune response, which may indicate the severity of their periodontal condition; 2. The relationship between periodontitis and many systemic health issues (like diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disorders, respiratory diseases, obesity, cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, metabolic syndrome, and cognitive impairment) has been established in the literature [48, 49]. Elevated NLR levels seen in individuals with periodontitis may function as an initial indicator of a systemic inflammatory response, which may lead to the development of these comorbidities; 3. Treatment Monitoring: NLR may also be used to track the effectiveness of periodontal therapy. A drop in NLR levels after therapy might suggest a good response and less inflammation in the oral cavity [30]; 4. Risk Assessment: Because increased NLR might signal a weakened immune response, it can aid in identifying individuals who may be at a greater risk of problems after periodontal operations.

Although the principal elements of periodontitis are the microbes that make up the pathogenic subgingival biofilm, the host's immune response determines the disease's progression and clinical outcome [24, 50]—the immune response results in the development of the periodontal inflammatory infiltrate and the activation of osteoclasts. Numerous studies have discussed the role of immunocytes in periodontitis and their vital and pleiotropic influences on the pathobiont regulation and activation or inhibition of osteoclastic activity [51–53].

It is essential to talk about how immune cells contribute to periodontitis. The importance of neutrophils in chronic periodontitis and other chronic inflammatory diseases such as psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, diabetes, cancer, and inflammatory bowel disease is now well understood [17, 54–56]. According to clinical data, neutrophils are responsible for significant inflammatory tissue destruction [57, 58], and their numbers are related to the severity of periodontitis [55, 56]. In addition, patients with chronic periodontitis have longer-lasting neutrophils in their oral tissues than those in healthy people’s oral tissues because of continuous pro-inflammatory stimuli [59–61]. Dysregulated, hyperactive, or abnormal neutrophils release connective tissue‐degrading metalloproteinases (like collagenase), contributing to bone resorption, or inflammatory and toxic chemicals (like reactive oxygen species) contributing to connective tissue damage [62]. Consistent with human research linking neutrophil activity to periodontal tissue destruction, animal studies have discovered that antibody-mediated neutrophil depletion alleviates experimental mouse periodontitis [63–65] while unconstrained neutrophil recruitment aggravates animal periodontitis [66, 67]. Aside from their usual antimicrobial role as first responders, neutrophils have tremendous adaptability, allowing them to participate in previously unknown tasks, such as controlling innate and adaptive immunological leukocytes [59, 68–70]. Neutrophils can release reactive oxygen species, antimicrobial peptides (like cathelicidin and alpha-defensins), and proteases (like cathepsin G, matrix metalloproteinase, and elastase). Moreover, recent findings have demonstrated that they can de novo biosynthesis of anti-inflammatory, pro-inflammatory, immunoregulatory cytokines, C–C and C–X–C chemokines, and fibrogenic and angiogenic factors [71, 72]. For instance, BlLYmphocyte Stimulator (BLyS) and A PRoliferation-Inducing TNF Ligand (APRIL), which have been demonstrated to be associated with B cell-dependent periodontal bone loss in mice and human periodontitis, are cytokines secreted by neutrophils that promote the survival of plasma and B cells [72, 73].

Neutrophils may attract CCR2 and CCR6-expressing Th17 cells, linked to autoimmune and inflammatory diseases such as human and animal periodontitis, by releasing CCL2 and CCL20 chemokines. Reciprocally, Th17-derived interleukin-17 stimulates the production of C–X–C chemokines (like interleukin-8 and C–X–C–L-8), which may cause the chemotactic recruitment of neutrophils to the periodontium, mainly via the action of fibroblasts [72, 73].

Innate immunity’s key players, neutrophils, enhance pro-inflammatory reactions—besides, lymphocytes, which are part of the adaptive immune system, control immunological responses. When the NLR is high, neutrophils' pro-inflammatory activity may surpass lymphocytes’ regulatory role, creating the conditions for uncontrolled peripheral inflammation to spread to sensitive tissue [8].

Limitations and directions for future research

There are a few issues with our research that need to be addressed. The small number of publications included in the analysis is the primary limitation of this research. Consequently, the strength of our findings may be compromised, and more studies are necessary to improve them. The studies we considered in our analysis also showed significant heterogeneity. Even if the random effect model was used to account for this, heterogeneity might still be a problem. This can be due to varying periodontitis types, center protocols, treatments, study designs, and study groups. In addition, the certainty of the evidence was low due to the limitations mentioned above.

Further testing of NLR and possible confounders in conjunction with other specific plasma contents and investigating the impact of interventions or specific treatments on NLR could move towards better predictive value. In addition, we suggest performing an updated meta-analysis on this topic when new articles are available. Furthermore, we found no article on the differences in NLR levels between aggressive and chronic periodontitis patients. This is an important issue for future research.

Conclusion

The findings of our investigation, which indicate higher NLR levels in periodontitis patients, show that immune dysregulation plays a role in the etiology of the disease. The mounting data over the last several decades have consistently supported the involvement of inflammation in the etiology of periodontitis. Based on the literature’s association of NLR with periodontitis, we conclude that NLR may be a parameter supporting the diagnosis. On the other hand, the presence of similar changes in many other diseases will prevent it from being a disease-specific biomarker. Along with a deepening understanding of periodontal host immunity and the cytokine network, more tissue-specific responses worthy of developing targeted therapies will be identified.
