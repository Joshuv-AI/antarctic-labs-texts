Contemporary Management of Coccydynia
Authors: Victor E. Staartjes, Daniel De Wilde, Aron Alakmeh, Omar Alsaleh, Adrian Elmi-Terander, Erik Edström, Gunnar Nilsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coccydynia is a painful condition of the coccyx that is frequently misdiagnosed and managed inconsistently. This review summarizes and grades the current evidence on diagnostic strategies and treatment options. We systematically searched the literature and included 42 studies covering conservative, interventional, and surgical management. Based on these data, we propose a current best framework for diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic management. Initial assessment should include detailed history and focused examination with palpation for localized coccygeal tenderness and symptom provocation. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs are recommended mainly to exclude serious pathology, while dynamic sitting-standing radiographs can be considered when mechanical pain is suspected and symptoms persist. Cross-sectional imaging with magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography (CT) should be reserved for trauma, red-flag features, suspected neoplasm or infection, or inconclusive basic imaging. First-line treatment should consist of education, ergonomic advice, offloading strategies, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or other simple analgesics, and physiotherapy, with extracorporeal shock wave therapy having the strongest support. In patients with persistent pain, image-guided diagnostic and therapeutic injections and radiofrequency procedures can provide substantial relief and help select candidates for more invasive treatment. Coccygectomy should be reserved for patients with chronic, function-limiting pain who have failed conservative and interventional care and show concordant findings on assessment, imaging, and diagnostic blocks, while modified incision strategies and minimally invasive techniques may be considered in selected cases.

INTRODUCTION

Coccydynia, or coccygodynia, describes pain localized to the coccyx that is typically exacerbated by sitting and transitions from sitting to standing. It is most often related to trauma, degenerative changes, hypermobility, or childbirth, but may also arise in association with pelvic floor dysfunction and other mechanical factors [1]. Neurogenic, infectious, and neoplastic causes, as well as referred pain from adjacent lumbosacral or pelvic structures, further broaden the differential diagnosis and may complicate recognition of the disease [2]. As a consequence, coccydynia remains frequently underappreciated in routine practice and is often subsumed under the umbrella of “chronic pelvic pain” or low back pain [3,4].

Although considered relatively rare, the true prevalence of coccydynia is probably underestimated due to underreporting, misdiagnosis, and therapeutic nihilism [5]. Prospective and long-term cohort data indicate that a subset of patients develop persistent, disabling symptoms that substantially affect health-related quality of life, work participation, and daily activities [6]. Delays between symptom onset, correct diagnosis, and definitive treatment can span years, during which patients often cycle through multiple providers and empirical treatments without a structured, stepwise strategy [7].

A broad spectrum of therapeutic options is currently used, ranging from simple conservative measures like nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), seating offloading, and various physiotherapy approaches, to image-guided injections, radiofrequency procedures, and ultimately coccygectomy. In most series, conservative management is recommended as firstline therapy—however, observational data suggest that up to half of patients continue to experience relevant pain despite multimodal nonsurgical treatment [8,9]. Interventional procedures such as ganglion impar block (GIB), coccygeal nerve block, and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) have gained popularity and are increasingly performed under fluoroscopic, computed tomography (CT), or ultrasound guidance, yet comparative data and long-term outcomes remain limited [10-12].

For patients with chronic, refractory coccydynia, coccygectomy continues to represent the definitive surgical option, with multiple cohort studies and case series reporting clinically meaningful and durable improvements in pain and function [6]. At the same time, concerns regarding wound infection, variable surgical techniques (midline versus paramedian or Z-plasty incisions), and alternative strategies such as minimally invasive coccygectomy or coccygeoplasty have led to considerable heterogeneity [13,14]. Prior reviews of coccydynia have mostly been narrative or focused on single modalities [15,16].

Given this fragmented and variably low-level evidence base, there is a clear need for an up-to-date, structured synthesis of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for coccydynia. Using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) Levels of Evidence as a framework [17], our evidence summary aims to provide an evidence-based overview of contemporary management, spanning clinical assessment and imaging through conservative, interventional, and surgical options. By grading the level of evidence (LOE) and strength of recommendation (SOR) for each modality, we seek to support clinicians in constructing a rational, stepwise treatment algorithm for patients with coccydynia and to highlight areas where further high-quality research is required.

METHODS

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE) and Embase on September 6, 2025, using a predefined search strategy and following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The search combined medical subject heading (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to coccydynia and its management. Specifically, the following query was used:

(“Coccygodynia”[MeSH] OR coccygodynia[tiab] OR coccydynia [tiab] OR “coccyx pain”[tiab] OR “coccygeal pain”[tiab] OR “tailbone pain”[tiab] OR coxalgia[tiab]) AND (“Therapeutics” [MeSH] OR “Pain Management”[MeSH] OR “Physical Therapy Modalities”[MeSH] OR “Injections”[MeSH] OR “Coccyx/surgery”[MeSH] OR “Surgical Procedures, Operative”[MeSH] OR management[tiab] OR treatment[tiab] OR therapy[tiab] OR intervention[tiab] OR conservative[tiab] OR nonsurgical[tiab] OR “non-surgical”[tiab] OR physiotherapy[tiab] OR “physical therapy”[tiab] OR injection[tiab] OR infiltration[tiab] OR “steroid injection”[tiab] OR “ganglion impar block”[tiab] OR radiofrequency[tiab] OR denervation[tiab] OR coccygectomy [tiab] OR surgery[tiab] OR surgical[tiab]).

All records retrieved through the search were screened in 2 stages by the first author. First, titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were reviewed. Original studies of the following designs were included: case series, cohort studies, comparative studies, and diagnostic accuracy studies. Studies had to report primary data on patients with coccydynia; reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, and nonhuman studies were excluded. Data extraction from the included studies comprised the first author, study location, study design, number of participants, the intervention or diagnostic test used, and the overall findings. LOE and SOR assessments were based on the OCEBM Levels of Evidence [17]. Risk of bias was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for nonrandomized studies and the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for randomized trials, respectively [18,19].

RESULTS

A total of 439 records were identified, of which 388 were excluded following title and abstract screening. The remaining 51 articles underwent full-text assessment, during which 13 were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria. An additional 4 studies were identified through citation search and manual search. In total, 42 studies were included in the final systematic review (Table 1). The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). An overview of the highest LOE and strength of recommendation for each diagnostic and therapeutic modality is provided in Table 2.

Of the included studies, among those assessed with the ROBINS-I tool, 11 (26%) demonstrated a serious risk of bias, while the remaining 26 studies (62%) were assessed as having a critical risk of bias. For the studies evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, 3 (7%) were deemed to have a high risk of bias, and 2 (5%) were assessed as having some concerns (Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

1. Diagnostic Options

Coccydynia refers to pain localized to the coccyx and may arise from a broad range of etiologies. Common causes include trauma, degenerative changes, hypermobility, and childbirth-related injury, and other mechanical factors [1]. Organic etiologies including neurogenic pain, arachnoid cysts, pilonidal sinus, and neoplasms, while less common, have also been described and should be considered during the diagnostic work-up [6]. A key distinction must be made between primary coccydynia and pseudococcydynia, the latter describing referred coccygeal pain originating from adjacent anatomical regions [2]. Furthermore, when no structural or organic cause can be identified, the condition is classified as idiopathic coccydynia, a diagnosis of exclusion [9].

The true prevalence of coccydynia is likely underestimated due to underreporting and misdiagnosis [3,5,20]. Notably, 28%–31% of patients in 2 included studies, reported receiving an incorrect diagnosis before the final diagnosis of coccydynia was established [9,20].

None of the included studies evaluated diagnostic processes as a primary objective. When diagnostics were discussed, authors typically summarized existing literature or described the workflow in relation to their inclusion criteria. As a result, no formal assessment of recommendation strength or evidence level could be performed for the diagnostic component of this review.

Among the studies that reported a diagnostic workflow, most described a similar approach [10,20-26]. First, clinicians obtained a detailed patient history, followed by a clinical examination focusing on local tenderness over the coccyx and surrounding sacrococcygeal area. When clinical findings supported a diagnosis of coccydynia, imaging was commonly used to identify the contributing pathology. Across studies, this included plain radiographs as well as lumbosacral or coccygeal magnetic resonance images, used either alone or in combination.

An exception was described in Shalaby et al., who predominantly relied on history and physical examination, and using anteroposterior and lateral radiographs only to characterize coccygeal morphology preoperatively [27,28]. This approach was, however, applied in an adolescent and pediatric cohort, which may explain the more conservative reliance on imaging.

A distinct diagnostic strategy was described by Milosevic et al. [29] and Jensen et al. [30], who emphasized bimanual palpation to assess local tenderness and instability. Rather than immediately proceeding to imaging, these studies used diagnostic steroid injections to confirm suspected coccydynia. In cases where instability was detected clinically, CT imaging was then performed to verify the diagnosis.

The subsequent paragraphs provide a more detailed examination of each diagnostic step and outline additional, more specialized tools used in the assessment of coccydynia. Where necessary, original sources cited within the included studies were consulted to supplement information not explicitly described in the primary articles.

1) Clinical examination

Many studies in our review used localized tenderness over the coccyx on palpation as the initial and most frequently used step in the diagnostic workflow of coccydynia [10,20,22,29-31]. Building on this, Sarmast et al. [9] used a form of provocative testing, applying pressure to the coccygeal region with a blunted needle to reproduce the patient’s pain. Authors have even gone further to suggest that local tenderness over the coccyx is highly suggestive of coccydynia, whereas absence of tenderness makes the diagnosis unlikely [13,32]. Collectively, these findings indicate that local tenderness is a useful initial marker of primary coccydynia, although not fully definitive, as some patients without tenderness may still show radiological abnormalities. Imaging should therefore follow clinical examination to confirm the diagnosis and identify the underlying pathology. In conclusion, although clinical examination was consistently reported as part of the diagnostic work-up, none of the studies included evaluated its accuracy in predicting coccydynia (LOE 5, SOR C).

2) Standard radiographs

Standard lateral and anteroposterior radiographs are an essential step of the diagnostic process and were mentioned in most studies, either alone or in combination with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Radiographs can help identify degenerative changes, spondylolisthesis, congenital anomalies, sacroiliac joint pathology, fractures, and dislocations [21]. However, Obeng-Gyasi et al. [33] noted an important limitation that because the coccyx is small and not weight-bearing, it is often overlooked on pelvic x-rays, contributing to underdiagnosis of coccygeal pathologies. Moreover, studies have reported that single-position (i.e., nondynamic) radiographs do not reliably differentiate individuals with coccydynia from healthy controls and therefore lack standalone diagnostic value [9,28].

Consistent with this, Finsen et al. [23] found among 171 operated patients with normal radiographs, that they were just as likely to benefit from surgery as those with abnormal findings, indicating that normal imaging should not be grounds for denying treatment. The authors concur with Wray et al. [34] that radiographs should only serve to exclude more serious pathology, rather than to confirm coccydynia.

Therefore, when standard radiographs are inconclusive or in the context of trauma, dynamic imaging, CT, or MRI should be used to provide more detailed diagnostic information [33]. Overall, the diagnostic value of standard radiographs remains questionable and no studies included assessed its value (LOE 5, SOR C).

3) Dynamic radiographs

The coccyx normally pivots between 5° to 25° during the transition from standing to sitting and returns to its baseline angle upon standing again. In patients with coccydynia, dynamic lateral radiographs may reveal coccygeal displacement, immobility (<5° of motion), or hypermobility (>25°) [35,36]. Such abnormalities in dynamic radiographs are detected in up to 70% of symptomatic patients, underscoring the diagnostic value of dynamic imaging (LOE 4, SOR C) [37].

Among the 4 studies [9,24,37,38] describing or using dynamic radiographs, Awwad et al. [38] highlighted its importance by demonstrating substantial variation in the Postacchini and Massobrio classification, a radiological system for categorizing coccygeal shape and angulation: type 1, a coccyx curved slightly forward; type 2, a coccyx whose curve points directly anteriorly; type 3, a coccyx sharply angulated forward; and type 4, subluxation at the sacrococcygeal or intercoccygeal joint. Specifically, in a cohort of 21 patients undergoing coccygectomy for refractory coccydynia, type 1 morphology predominated in standing images (66%), whereas type 4 morphology was most common in sitting images (71%) [38]. Overall, dynamic radiographs show strong potential diagnostic value, but current evidence is limited and higher-quality studies are needed (LOE 4, SOR C).

4) CT and MRI

Although dynamic radiographs remain the most established method for detecting the common mechanical causes of coccydynia, namely hypermobility and subluxation, advanced imaging techniques such as CT and MRI can also contribute to establishing the diagnosis, primarily by identifying less common or secondary causes [9,21]. MRI is commonly used to exclude tumors, abscesses, and other structural lesions [24], and studies such as that by Genc Perdecioglu and Yildiz [39] primarily used MRI to diagnose coccydynia. MRI provides detailed visualization of the sacrococcygeal joints and coccyx, helps rule out infection, bursitis, disc pathology, and referred lumbar pain [7,21], and can demonstrate signal changes corresponding that may indicate the pain’s origin [40]. Ogur et al. [41] even noted 2 trauma-suspected cases in which MRI clarified the differential diagnosis. Dynamic MRI has additionally been explored for assessing coccygeal motion [42]. Sarmast et al. [9] also noted that MRI and technetium-99m bone scans may demonstrate features suggestive of hypermobility.

CT imaging, while less sensitive to soft tissue, is superior to MRI for defining osseous anatomy and is recommended in the context of pelvic trauma or as an adjunct when neoplastic disease is suspected [9]. Studies such as Awwad et al. [24], have combined CT with sagittal reconstructions, MRI, and even dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry scanning to exclude alternative causes of coccygeal pain. Overall, advanced imaging, especially MRI, serves as an adjunct to radiographs and should be employed when initial imaging is inconclusive or when trauma, atypical features, or concerns for secondary pathologies are present [33]. To date, no study has specifically assessed the diagnostic performance of advanced imaging in identifying primary coccydynia (LOE 5, SOR C). Further radiologic research is therefore needed to define diagnostic value, optimal imaging indications, and the patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

5) Diagnostic and prognostic blocks

Diagnostic blocks are frequently used to confirm the coccyx as the source of pain and to identify candidates for interventional treatments such as steroid injections or RFA. Can et al. [11] defined a positive response as >50% pain reduction for at least one hour after an ultrasound-guided lidocaine injection, using this to verify coccygeal origin and guide treatment. Chen et al. [12] similarly used a prognostic block in patients undergoing RFA: 7 of 8 patients with a positive block achieved good or excellent outcomes, compared with only 1 of 4 without prior diagnostic testing (LOE 4, SOR C). Sagir et al. [10] also showed that fluoroscopy-guided GIBs, alone or followed by RFA, significantly reduced pain, reinforcing their value.

These findings are supported by other authors. Finsen et al. noted that temporary symptom relief after a small (1 mL) local anesthetic injection supported a coccydynia diagnosis [4,25,43]. Sarmast et al. likewise recommended fluoroscopy-guided anesthetic injections as a helpful diagnostic tool [9,36]. Milosevic et al. [29] and Jensen et al. [30] used CT-guided coccygeal steroid injections as combined diagnostic-prognostic blocks, with transient pain relief confirming the coccyx as the generator and identifying candidates for coccygectomy. Overall, diagnostic and prognostic blocks demonstrated that temporary pain relief following targeted anesthetic injection may be a reliable method for confirming the coccyx as the pain source and for selecting ideal candidates for interventional or surgical treatment (LOE 4, SOR C). Their therapeutic role is discussed in the following sections.

2. Conservative Therapeutic Options

Despite conservative therapy being widely regarded as the first-line approach for coccydynia, the available evidence remains very limited [8,15,44]. Numerous conservative treatment options, such as NSAIDs, offloading strategies like doughnut cushions, and physical therapy including pelvic floor strengthening exercises are commonly referenced in existing literature, yet robust data on the effectiveness of each individual modality remains scarce [8,10-12,15,21,22,25,31,39,44-48]. Furthermore, no consensus exists regarding which conservative treatment modality is most effective due to the limited number of comparative studies [15,39,46].

Importantly, the majority of available studies indicate that conservative management, even when combining multiple conservative treatment modalities, fails to provide adequate relief in a substantial subset of patients. Depending on the study design and follow-up interval, between 10% and 51% of individuals continue to experience persistent coccydynia despite conservative therapy [1,8].

1) NSAID and other pain medication

NSAIDs constitute the cornerstone of first-line management for coccydynia, aiming to reduce pain and inflammation in the coccygeal area. However, although NSAIDs are cited in almost all studies on coccydynia as a feasible conservative option with reported benefits in pain management, no study has yet investigated or quantified their isolated effect in patients with coccydynia (LOE 5, SOR C).

Moreover, even when combined with other conservative modalities, a substantial proportion of patients experience persistent symptoms, underscoring that while NSAIDs may provide symptomatic relief, they are insufficient as a definitive treatment option for many patients [8].

2) Offloading

Offloading was also frequently cited as part of conservative coccydynia management, typically implemented alongside NSAIDs and physical therapy [29,44,49]. Offloading refers to strategies aimed at reducing axial pressure on the coccyx during sitting. The approach is based on the fact that coccydynia is mainly triggered and/or intensified by direct mechanical coccygeal loading [1]. Various cushions are used in the management of coccydynia, most commonly doughnut, U-shaped, and wedge cushions. However, as with NSAIDs, offloading is described in the literature without any study having evaluated or quantified its independent effect in coccydynia patients (LOE 5, SOR C). Consequently, future research is needed to assess the efficacy of different offloading techniques and cushion designs.

3) Physiotherapy

Physiotherapy is among the most frequently cited conservative treatment modalities for coccydynia and, unlike many other approaches, is supported by several studies investigating and quantifying its impact on patient outcome. A recent systematic review by Blanco-Diaz et al. [16] on physiotherapy approaches for coccydynia summarizes different physiotherapy modalities, including extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT), various manual therapy techniques (intrarectal mobilization, external mobilization, McKenzie approach), kinesiotaping and pelvic floor rehabilitation. The authors report, that physiotherapy, especially ESWT, demonstrates clinically significant benefits in managing coccydynia. However, they conclude that the small sample sizes, substantial differences in outcome measurements, and the wide variation in physiotherapy techniques allow no conclusive statements regarding which approach is most effective [16]. A randomized controlled trial by Mohanty and Pattnaik [50] assigned patients with coccydynia to 3 groups: stretching of the piriformis and iliopsoas, the same stretching combined with Maitland thoracic mobilization, or conventional therapy with seat cushioning, sitz bath, and phonophoresis. At follow-up, both experimental groups showed significant improvements in pressure pain threshold and pain-free sitting duration compared with conventional treatment. Given the available evidence demonstrating symptom improvement across several physiotherapy approaches, physiotherapy (especially ESWT and stretching) should be recommended as an early treatment option (LOE 2b, SOR B) [51,52]. A study by Ahadi et al. [5]2 even demonstrated slightly superior long-term efficacy in ESWT compared with corticosteroid injection, suggesting that ESWT may be particularly suitable for patients refusing injections (LOE 2b, SOR B). Nevertheless, the scarcity of comparative studies and modality-specific cohort data prevents definitive statements about which technique is most effective.

4) Intrarectal manipulation

Intrarectal manipulation includes various massages, stretches, and mobilizations of the coccyx performed from within the rectum. These manual techniques are thought to relax the surrounding musculature and thereby reduce pain. Furthermore, manipulation may help mobilize stiff coccyx, which may in turn reduce irritation of the surrounding subcutaneous tissues [53,54]. A pilot study by Maigne and Chatellier [54] in 2001 compared different manual treatment techniques and demonstrated only limited and largely unsatisfactory results, with no clear benefit of manual therapy. The authors subsequently followed up this work with a randomized controlled trial in 2006 [55]. In this study, patients were divided into one group receiving 3 sessions of coccygeal manipulation and another group receiving low-power external physiotherapy. Intrarectal manipulation was guided by findings on dynamic radiographs. At 6 months post-treatment, 22% of patients in the manipulation group reported good outcomes, compared with 12% of patients in the physiotherapy group, as measured by patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). Overall, these findings suggest a mild benefit of intrarectal manipulation in selected patients with chronic coccydynia (LOE 2b, SOR B) [55].

3. Interventional Therapeutic Options

1) Nerve blockades and RFA

In patients with chronic coccydynia refractory to conservative treatment, several interventional procedures are suggested in literature, targeting different components of the pain pathway. These include nerve block as well as RFA techniques, aiming either at neuromodulation or at delivering corticosteroids in combination with local anesthetics to nociceptive structures in the sacrococcygeal region [10,21,39,45-47].

In general, the following interventional therapeutic options can be differentiated:

(1) Nerve blockades: GIB [10,39,45-47,56] and coccygeal nerve block (CnB) [6]

(2) RFA: conventional RFA [11,12,21] and pulsed RFA (PRF) [46,56]

(3) Caudal epidural steroid injection (CESI) [46]

RFA techniques can be further distinguished by their anatomical target, being applied either to the ganglion impar (GI-RFA and GI-PRF) or to the peripheral coccygeal nerves [12].

2) Nerve blockades

GIB refers to the block of the ganglion impar, a solitary retroperitoneally located ganglion providing sympathetic and visceral innervation to the coccyx and other structures [56]. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a needle is advanced to the ganglion, correct placement is confirmed using radiopaque contrast, and a mixture of local anesthetic (e.g., bupivacaine) and corticosteroid (e.g., triamcinolone acetate) is injected [46,56]. For GIB, multiple case series studies show an effective analgesic effect without or very low risk of minor complications, making it a valid interventional treatment option (LOE 4, SOR C) [10,21,45,47]. A study by Olgun et al. [45] furthermore was able to show that the contrast material distribution pattern did not affect outcomes (LOE 2b, SOR B) and a study by Kim et al. [47] showed cancer-related coccydynia to be a prognostic factor for good GIB outcome, making GIB particularly suited for cancer-related coccydynia (LOE 3, SOR B).

In contrast, CnB can be performed under ultrasound guidance, thereby avoiding radiation exposure and the potential adverse effects associated with contrast agents [11,48]. Similar to GIB, the technique involves advancing a needle toward the coccygeal nerve region and administering a mixture of local anesthetics and corticosteroids. Although no cohort study or case series specifically evaluating CnB exists, a recent randomized controlled trial by Genc Perdecioglu and Yildiz [39] demonstrated that CnB provides clinical outcomes comparable to GIB. This positions CnB as a valuable alternative to the more commonly used GIB, with the added advantage of eliminating patient radiation exposure (LOE 1b, SOR A).

3) Radiofrequency ablation

RFA can be applied in patients with coccydynia either to the ganglion impar (GI-RFA or GI-PRF) or to the peripheral sacrococcygeal nerves. Two main techniques are distinguished: conventional RFA, which produces continuous thermal lesions at approximately 90°C, and PRF, which delivers short bursts of energy at lower temperatures (around 42°C). The duration and number of treatment cycles may vary, with PRF typically administered in multiple cycles [46,56]. A case series by Chen et al. [12] demonstrated meaningful pain reduction after both conventional RFA and PRF of the peripheral sacrococcygeal nerves, supporting this approach as a viable option when conservative treatment has failed (LOE 4, SOR C).

A recent comparative study by Sir and Eksert [56] reported significantly longer pain relief and a lower recurrence risk with GI-PRF compared to GIB in chronic coccydynia (LOE 2b, SOR B). Additionally, Gazioğlu Türkyılmaz and Rumeli [46] showed that GI-PRF was particularly effective in patients with a history of trauma (LOE 4, SOR C), and that adjunctive CESI enhanced pain control and overall patient satisfaction (LOE 2b, SOR B).

4) Comparison of nerve blockades and RFA

Both RFA and nerve blockade techniques have demonstrated substantial pain reduction across multiple studies, with most reporting decreases of approximately 50%–80% in visual analogue scale (VAS) scores following treatment [10-12,21,46,56]. Comparative studies between interventional modalities are scarce. Sir and Eksert [56] were the only group to directly compare PRF with GIB, reporting that PRF provided longer-lasting pain relief and a lower recurrence rate in chronic coccydynia. Given the small sample size and the absence of corroborating studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Genc Perdecioglu and Yildiz [39] in turn, were the only authors to compare GIB with peripheral coccygeal nerve blockade and found no difference in clinical outcomes.

Literature on CESI is far more limited than for RFA or GIB. The only study specifically evaluating CESI in the context of coccydynia was conducted by Gazioğlu Türkyılmaz and Rumeli [46] who examined CESI as an adjuvant therapy to GI-PRF. They found that adding CESI to prior GI-PRF significantly improved pain control and increased patient satisfaction.

Both RFA and nerve blockade techniques have demonstrated substantial pain reduction across multiple studies, with most reporting decreases of approximately 50%–80% in VAS scores following treatment, making RFA, PRF and GIB effective interventional options that achieve notable pain improvement while maintaining very low complication rates, particularly when contrasted with surgical management [10-12,21,46,56]. Future studies are needed to determine whether any of these techniques is superior and to identify which patient subgroups benefit most from each intervention.

4. Surgical Therapeutic Options

Despite the mentioned variety in conservative and interventional treatment options, a considerable portion of patients continue to experience persistent coccydynia despite conservative therapy, necessitating surgical therapy [1,8]. At this stage, it is important to note that most included studies, particularly those addressing surgical interventions, exhibit a high risk of bias and that the resulting recommendations should therefore be interpreted with caution.

1) Coccygectomy

Among the available treatment options, coccygectomy represents the established surgical approach for patients with persistent coccydynia [6,7,9,13,14,20,23,24,26,30,38,41,57-62]. Although the optimal duration of nonoperative management remains undefined, Hochgatterer et al. [6] suggest considering surgery after 6 months of refractory symptoms. Ultimately, the decision to proceed with surgery should be made on an individual basis, taking symptom severity and treatment response into account.

Across the literature, reported success rates range from 60% to 90%, depending on etiology, surgical technique, and followup duration. The current evidence supports standard coccygectomy via a midline incision as a valid and well-studied option for patients with failed conservative treatment, offering superior and lasting pain relief, functional improvement, and quality-of-life gains compared with nonoperative therapy alone (LOE 3, SOR B) [60].

A major concern in coccygectomy is the high rate of postoperative wound infection, with reported incidences reaching up to 36% in some case series, though most studies describe rates in the range of 5%–15% [14,57]. Hence, enhanced prophylactic antibiotic protocols, different inpatient care protocols (regular wound cleaning and bandage replacement), new incision types (Z-flap, paramedian incision) and surgical strategies (minimally invasive coccygectomy, coccygeoplasty) have recently been introduced with the aim to reduce postoperative wound infection rates [14,33,49,59].

2) Incision strategies

The traditional incision type is the midline vertical incision, which however carries the mentioned high risk of infection, caused by the proximity to the anus and its location within or near the intergluteal cleft [63]. To address this complication, several authors have proposed alternatives such as Z-flap and paramedian incisions, designed to promote good wound healing and reduce exposure to anal flora [14,59]. While these techniques have been shown to be surgically feasible and demonstrated relatively low infection rates in a few case series (LOE 4, SOR C), bigger case series and comparative studies have to be conducted to confirm superiority of any technique [13,14,59].

3) Partial vs. complete coccygectomy

Complete coccygectomy is historically the common approach and is associated with high success rates [41]. Partial coccygectomy, which typically involves resecting only the most distal coccygeal segments, has been proposed as a less invasive alternative, especially in cases with localized pathology such as distal instability or spicule formation [41]. Comparative studies between the 2 approaches report similar outcome in postoperative pain relief between partial and complete resections [41,64]. Given the shorter operative time and lower procedural invasiveness, partial coccygectomy may be favored; however, both procedures remain valid and effective options (LOE 2b, SOR B) [41,64]. Importantly, current evidence is limited by a very small number of comparative studies with low patient numbers, underscoring the need for future research to better define outcomes and identify patient subgroups that may benefit preferentially from one strategy over the other.

4) Minimally invasive strategies

In line with the broader shift toward minimally invasive techniques in spine surgery, recent case reports have introduced minimally invasive coccygectomy approaches [33,65]. Obeng-Gyasi et al. [33] described a minimally invasive, exoscope-assisted approach and demonstrated its feasibility, proposing it as a potential alternative to the conventional procedure (LOE 4, SOR C). The small-incision technique aims to reduce postoperative recovery time and minimize infection risk. Given the limited evidence, further studies are required to evaluate safety, integration into standard workflows, and whether outcomes are equivalent or superior to those of traditional coccygectomy.

5) Coccygeoplasty

In cases of refractory coccydynia associated with coccygeal hypermobility or subluxation, coccygeoplasty has been proposed as minimally invasive alternative [49]. The technique involves percutaneous injection of polymethyl-methacrylate cement into the sacrococcygeal joint or affected segments to stabilize motion and reduce pain. Early results from a case series by Manfre et al. [49] demonstrated promising results with significant pain reduction and low complication rates (LOE 4, SOR C). However, evidence is still sparse, and long-term outcome is unclear.

5. Future Research

Current research on coccydynia is limited and the evidence available is restricted by medium to high risk of bias, mainly due to the limited availability of high-quality study designs such as prospective, comparative and randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs. Coccydynia has been a pathology of relatively low interest [66], and this gap in the literature may largely be attributed to the relatively small incidence of the condition [67]. It is important that future research focuses on high-quality study designs, including RCTs and prospective comparative studies, with the aim of evaluating and directly comparing the efficacy of the different diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, across all treatment categories, the adoption of validated PROMs and uniform reporting standards would substantially improve the comparability of future studies.

6. Conclusion and Current Best Recommendations for Diagnosis and Treatment

Based on the available literature, we recommend a stepwise current best framework for the diagnosis and management of coccydynia (Fig. 3). This approach should be understood as a recommendation to support evidence based, informed decision making in clinical practice, while clearly distinguishing it from a formal guideline. Furthermore, the moderate to high risk of bias observed across the included studies must be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Initial assessment should consist of a careful history and targeted physical examination with palpation for localized coccygeal tenderness and provocation of symptoms. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs are primarily useful to exclude fractures, dislocations, or other serious pathology, but normal findings do not preclude a good response to treatment, including surgery. When mechanical pain is suspected and symptoms persist, dynamic sitting–standing radiographs can be considered to detect hypermobility or luxation, while MRI or CT should be reserved for cases with trauma, red-flag symptoms, suspicion of neoplasm or infection, or inconclusive basic imaging.

First-line treatment should consist of education, ergonomic advice, offloading strategies, NSAIDs or other simple analgesics, and structured physiotherapy. Among conservative modalities, physiotherapy and particularly ESWT has the best supporting evidence and can be recommended with a moderate SOR.

In patients with persistent pain despite adequate conservative therapy, image-guided diagnostic and therapeutic injections should be offered. GIB and CnB can both provide substantial pain relief and serve as prognostic tests to select candidates for more invasive interventions. Radiofrequency techniques, either peripheral or targeting the ganglion impar, may be considered in specialized centers for chronic, injection-responsive coccydynia, with pulsed radiofrequency of the ganglion impar showing promising comparative results.

Coccygectomy should be reserved for patients with chronic, function-limiting coccydynia who have failed structured conservative and interventional treatment and in whom clinical findings, imaging, and response to diagnostic blocks are concordant. Standard midline coccygectomy remains the best-studied procedure, while modified incisions, minimally invasive approaches, partial resection, and coccygeoplasty may be considered in selected patients and high-volume centers to reduce wound complications or preserve anatomy, acknowledging that evidence for these newer techniques is still limited.
