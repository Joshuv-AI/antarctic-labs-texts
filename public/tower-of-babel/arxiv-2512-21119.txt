A Unified Truncation Method for Infinitely Many Solutions Without Symmetry
Anouar Bahrouni

Abstract
. This paper establishes the existence of infinitely many solutions for nonlinear problems without any symmetry, achieving three major advances. First, in the setting of semilinear elliptic PDEs, we introduce a refined variational-truncation method that yields infinite sequences of positive as well as negative solutions. Second—and most notably—we resolve a long-standing and difficult problem for non-variational elliptic PDEs with gradient dependence. By combining our truncation method with an iterative scheme, we prove, for the first time, the existence of infinitely many solutions for this class of PDEs. Third, we overcome a central difficulty for periodic Hamiltonian systems on the real line: we show that the multiplicity of solutions, constructed on a sequence of finite intervals, survives in the limit; in other words, no collapse occurs, and we obtain multiple distinct solutions on the whole real line. The core novelty lies in a carefully designed truncation methodology that systematically separates solutions and remains effective across variational and non-variational PDEs as well as infinite-dimensional dynamical systems. This unified perspective provides a robust and versatile tool for addressing multiplicity problems in the absence of symmetry. Keywords: Infinitely many solutions, lack of symmetry, variational and non-variational problems, periodic Hamiltonian systems, truncation methods, pseudomonotone operators. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35J20; 35J60; 35J70, 47J10; 46E35.



1.
Introduction

The question of finding multiple solutions to nonlinear differential equations and Hamiltonian systems is a central theme in modern analysis. When the underlying problem possesses symmetry—for instance, odd nonlinearities or invariant domains—the equivariant Lusternik–Schnirelmann theory for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetric sets can be applied to obtain infinitely many solutions; see
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
11
,
14
,
15
,
26
,
27
]
and the references therein.

However, in the absence of such symmetries, the problem becomes considerably more delicate. The classical arguments mentioned above no longer apply, and one must rely instead on finer, more constructive techniques to distinguish solutions. A detailed analysis of this issue was carried out in the pioneering works of Ambrosetti, Bahri, Berestycki, Ekeland, Ghoussoub, Lions, Rabinowitz, Struwe, and Tanaka; see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
17
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
, where new perturbation and variational methods were introduced. This question was emphasized in particular by Rabinowitz in his monograph on minimax methods
[
28
]
.

More recently, an alternative approach to symmetry breaking in elliptic problems was developed by Bolle, Ekeland, Ghoussoub, and Tehrani in
[
12
,
13
,
18
]
. The development of these ideas has continued over the last decades. In particular, Raymond, in his well-known paper
[
31
]
, proved the existence of infinitely many solutions using the truncation method, extending the techniques introduced in
[
23
,
24
]
.

Note that the above-mentioned methods are typically employed to establish the existence of infinitely many solutions for variational problems arising in various classes of partial differential equations. However, these approaches cannot be applied to non-variational problems or to periodic Hamiltonian systems, since the associated Hamiltonian functional is not defined on the entire space.

The goal of this paper is to fill this gap by developing a unified truncation strategy that applies in three distinct and progressively more challenging contexts:

(1)

semilinear elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) with variational structure, where we refine and simplify the argument of
[
31
]
, providing a more direct technique to distinguish the solutions;

(2)

non-variational elliptic PDEs with dependence on the gradient;

(3)

periodic Hamiltonian systems on the real line.

To enhance the clarity of the exposition, we introduce simplified model problems representing each of the three classes described above.

Our approach is rooted in a simple yet powerful idea: by strategically truncating the nonlinearity around a carefully chosen sequence of its zeros, we can isolate solutions within distinct energy ranges or spatial intervals. This method—whose origins go back to the work of Raymond
[
31
]
—is refined here to systematically produce a sequence of
pairwise distinct
solutions. We not only streamline its application to variational elliptic PDEs, but more importantly, extend its scope to two highly nontrivial settings in which classical variational tools are either unavailable or insufficient.

We first revisit the classical semilinear problem

-\Delta u=f(x,u)\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where the nonlinearity
f
is not assumed to be odd. Under mild growth conditions and assuming the existence of two interlacing sequences of zeros of
f
, we construct two infinite sequences of solutions, one positive and one negative. The proof is variational in nature, but the key ingredient is our refined truncation, which allows us to apply the direct method of the calculus of variations to suitably modified functionals and then to recover an infinite family of solutions by inductively “jumping” over intervals, ensuring that each new solution is distinct from the preceding ones; see Theorem
3.2
.

We then turn to the significantly more challenging non-variational problem

-\Delta u=f(x,u,\nabla u)\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

The presence of the gradient term
\nabla u
typically destroys the variational structure, rendering classical critical-point theory inapplicable. The available alternatives—such as the sub–super solution method, topological degree theory, fixed point theorems, and monotone operator theory—may yield multiple solutions; see
[
10
,
22
,
35
]
. However, the existence of infinitely many solutions in the non-variational framework remains largely open. To the best of our knowledge, only a few works
[
16
,
30
]
provide sets of solutions via bifurcation methods for certain eigenvalue-type problems.

Our work answers this question affirmatively. We overcome the lack of variational structure by reformulating the truncated problem using the iterative method employed in
[
19
]
.
More precisely, we fix the convection term in the equation, which makes the problem variational.
Then, we construct a sequence of recurrent variational equations, and by passing to the limit we obtain the existence of a solution to the truncated problems; see Theorem
4.3
. The true novelty lies in the synergy between this functional-analytic machinery and our refined truncation method. This combination not only guarantees the existence of solutions but allows us to construct two infinite and disjoint sequences of solutions—one positive and one negative—thus extending the infinite multiplicity theory for elliptic PDEs beyond the variational setting; see Theorem
4.2
.

The culmination of the paper concerns a long-standing challenge for periodic Hamiltonian systems:

-\ddot{u}(t)=V_{u}(t,u(t)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
V
is
T
-periodic in
t
. This type of problem has been extensively studied; see
[
9
,
20
,
21
,
25
,
29
]
and the references therein. In these works, the authors proved the existence of at least one homoclinic solution, even when the associated functional is not defined on the whole space
\mathbb{R}
, preventing a direct variational approach.

In
[
29
]
, Rabinowitz introduced a new idea: he studied the problem on the finite interval
[-kT,kT]
and, by letting
k\to\infty
, proved the existence of at least one homoclinic solution. To the best of our knowledge, the existence of multiple solutions for this problem remains open. The main difficulty is that although one can prove the existence of two solutions on each interval
[-kT,kT]
, these solutions may coincide as
k\to\infty
, causing a loss of multiplicity.

The main aim of this section is to fill this gap. To overcome the challenge, we develop a limiting procedure: we first solve an auxiliary problem on the expanding intervals
[-kT,kT]
with periodic boundary conditions, where variational methods are applicable. Using our unified truncation method, we prove the existence of a finite number of distinct solutions on each finite interval, with uniform control of their
C^{1}
-norms independent of
k
; see Theorem
5.3
.

The final and most delicate step is to pass to the limit
k\to\infty
using the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem. We show that the multiplicity produced on each finite interval persists in the limit, yielding a multiple distinct solutions on the entire real line. This overcomes the central difficulty of ensuring that the solutions do not “collapse” or become trivial during the limiting process; see Theorem
5.1
. Although a technical point prevents us from proving infinite multiplicity in this setting, we do establish the existence of multiple solutions, and we expect that the argument can be extended to yield infinitely many; see Remark
5.4
.

In summary, this paper introduces a unified truncation strategy for proving multiplicity results without symmetry, applicable to a broad range of nonlinear problems. Unlike classical approaches based on variational symmetry, genus theory, or bifurcation methods, our framework relies on a refined localization of the nonlinearity that allows one to systematically construct and distinguish solutions. Its effectiveness in the non-variational setting, where standard critical point theory fails, represents a substantial advance. Moreover, in the context of periodic Hamiltonian systems, the method resolves the fundamental issue of multiplicity persistence in the limit from finite intervals. We believe that this unified approach not only clarifies the underlying mechanism behind truncation-based multiplicity results but also opens new perspectives for treating nonsymmetric problems in nonlinear analysis and related fields.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall the necessary definitions and preliminary results. Sections 3, 4, and 5 are devoted to the formulation and resolution of the three problems described above.

3.
Problem I: Infinitely many solutions without symmetry for a variational problem

In this section, we address the first problem by proving the existence of infinitely many solutions for the following variational problem:

(
\mathcal{P}_{1}
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u&=f(x,u),&\text{in }\Omega,\\[3.00003pt]
u&=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 3
, is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Let us give the main assumptions for the nonlinear data:

(\mathrm{H}_{1})

f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function. Moreover, we assume that there exist
1\leq r<2^{\ast}
and some positive constant
c
such that

|f(x,t)|\leq c\big(1+|t|^{r-1}\big),\text{ for a.a. }x\in\Omega\text{ and all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(\mathrm{H}_{2})

There exist two sequences
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\eta_{n}<0,\quad\mu_{n}>0,\quad\text{and}\quad f(x,\eta_{n})=f(x,\mu_{n})=0\quad\text{for a.a. }x\in\Omega\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, we suppose that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist

\beta_{n}\in(\mu_{n},\mu_{n+1})
and
\gamma_{n}\in(\eta_{n+1},\eta_{n})
such that

F(x,\beta_{n})>0\ \mbox{and}\ F(x,\gamma_{n})>0,\ \text{ for a.a. }x\in\Omega,

where
F(x,u)=\int_{0}^{u}f(x,s)ds.

Remark 3.1
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(\mu_{n})
and
(\eta_{n})
are two increasing sequences.

Example
:

Let
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
a(x)>0
for a.a.
x\in\Omega
, and consider

f(x,t)=a(x)\sin t\qquad\text{or}\qquad g(x,t)=a(x)\cos t.

Then
f
is Carathéodory and satisfies
(\mathrm{H}_{1})
with

, since

|f(x,t)|\leq\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

For
(\mathrm{H}_{2})
, set

\mu_{n}=2n\pi,\qquad\eta_{n}=-2n\pi\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

so that
f(x,\mu_{n})=f(x,\eta_{n})=0
.
Choose

\beta_{n}=(2n+1)\pi\in(\mu_{n},\mu_{n+1}),\qquad\gamma_{n}=-(2n+1)\pi\in(\eta_{n+1},\eta_{n}).

For
f(x,t)=a(x)\sin t
we have
F(x,u)=a(x)(1-\cos u)
, hence

F(x,\beta_{n})=F(x,\gamma_{n})=2a(x)>0.

(The case
g(x,t)=a(x)\cos t
is analogous.)
Thus
(\mathrm{H}_{1})
–
(\mathrm{H}_{2})
are satisfied.

.

A function

is said to be a weak solution to equation
(

) if it satisfies

(3.1)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla w=\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)wdx,\ \forall\ w\in E.

The main result of this section reads as follows:

Assume that

4.
Problem II: Infinitely many solutions without symmetry for a non-variational problem

In this section, we address the second problem by proving the existence of infinitely many solutions to the following nonvariational problem without symmetry:

(
\mathcal{P}_{2}
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u&=f(x,u,\nabla u),&\text{in }\Omega,\\[3.00003pt]
u&=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 3
, is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.

We begin by stating the assumptions imposed on
f
:

(H
3
)

Let
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Carathéodory function such that the following hold:

There exist
c_{1}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
s\in[1,2^{*}[

such that

|f(x,t,\xi)|\leq c_{1}(1+|t|^{s-1}+|\xi|),

\text{for a.a.}\ x\in\Omega,\text{ for all }(t,\,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(\mathrm{H}_{4})

There exist two sequences
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\eta_{n}<0,\quad\mu_{n}>0,\quad\text{and}\quad f(x,\eta_{n},\xi)=f(x,\mu_{n},\xi)=0\quad\text{for a.a. }x\in\Omega\ \mbox{and for a.a}\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, we suppose that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist

\beta_{n}\in(\mu_{n},\mu_{n+1})
and
\gamma_{n}\in(\eta_{n+1},\eta_{n})
such that

F(x,\beta_{n},\xi)>0\ \mbox{and}\ F(x,\gamma_{n},\xi)>0,\ \text{ for a.a. }x\in\Omega\ \text{and}\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
F(x,u,\xi)=\int_{0}^{u}f(x,s,\xi)ds.

(\mathrm{H}_{5})

There exist two positive constants
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
such that

|f(x,t^{\prime},\xi)-f(x,t^{\prime\prime},\xi)|\leq L_{1}\,|t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}|,\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega,\;t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\;\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

and

|f(x,t,\xi^{\prime})-f(x,t,\xi^{\prime\prime})|\leq L_{2}\,|\xi^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime\prime}|,\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega,\;t\in\mathbb{R},\;\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

and moreover,

0<\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\,L_{2}}{\lambda_{1}-L_{1}}<1,

where
\lambda_{1}
denotes the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta
.

Remark 4.1
.

As in the first section, and without loss of generality, we may assume that
(\mu_{n})
and
(\eta_{n})
are two increasing sequences.

Example:

Let
a,b>0
be constants such that

a>b>0,\qquad a+b\leq c_{1},\qquad\pi(a+b)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4},\qquad\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\,b\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4}.

Define

f(x,t,\xi)=\sin(\pi t)\,\big(a+b\,\tanh(|\xi|)\big),\qquad x\in\Omega,\ t\in\mathbb{R},\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Then the following hold:

1. **(H_3):** Since
|\sin(\pi t)|\leq 1
and
|\tanh(|\xi|)|\leq 1
,

|f(x,t,\xi)|\leq a+b\leq c_{1}\leq c_{1}(1+|t|^{s-1}+|\xi|),

so the growth condition is satisfied.

2. **(H_4):** Set

\mu_{n}=n,\qquad\eta_{n}=-n.

Because

\displaystyle I_{0^{+}}^{k}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\min(\frac{1}{2},b_{1})\int_{-kT}^{kT}\left[\left|\dot{u}(t)\right|^{2}+|u(t)|^{2}\right]dt-2kTC_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\min(\frac{1}{2},b_{1})\|u\|_{E_{k}}^{2}-2kTC_{0},\ \forall u\in E_{k}.

Hence,
I_{0^{+}}^{k}
is coercive. On the other hand, exploiting the compactness
embedding and Fatou lemma, we conclude that
I_{0^{+}}^{k}
is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous. Then, by combining the last facts and using
the Weierstrass-Toneli theorem, we find
u_{0}^{k}\in E_{k}
such that

(5.8)

I_{0^{+}}^{k}(u_{0}^{k})=\inf\{I_{0^{+}}^{k}(u),\ u\in E_{k}\}.

It remains to show that
u_{0}^{k}
is nontrivial. To this end, using
(F_{2})
,
(K_{1})
and (
5.5
), we obtain

I_{0^{+}}^{k}\bigl(\beta_{0}\bigr)\leq 2kTb_{2}\beta_{0}^{2}-2kT\inf_{t\in[0,T]}F(t,\beta_{0})=2kT\beta_{0}^{2}(b_{2}-\frac{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}F(t,\beta_{0})}{\beta_{0}^{2}})<0,

which implies that

I_{0^{+}}^{k}\bigl(u_{0}^{k}\bigr)\leq I_{1^{+}}^{k}\bigl(\beta_{0}\bigr)<0.

Therefore
u_{0}^{k}\neq 0.

Claim
:
u_{0}^{k}\in(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
. We act with
w=(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}
in equation (
5.7
) and using
(K_{1})-(K_{2})
, we get

\displaystyle\min(b_{1},1)\!\int_{-kT}^{kT}\left(\bigl|\dot{(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}}\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}\bigr|^{2}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-kT}^{kT}\left[\dot{u_{0}^{k}}\,\dot{(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}}+K_{u}\bigl(t,u_{0}^{k}(t)\bigr)\,(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}(t)\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-kT}^{kT}f_{1^{+}}^{1}\!\left(t,u_{0}^{k}(t)\right)\,(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{0})^{-}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=0.

Using the truncation (
5.3
), we can conclude that

(5.9)

u_{0}^{k}(t)\geq\mu_{0},\ \forall t\in[-2kT,2kT].

Again, we act with
w=(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}
in (
5.7
), we
conclude that

\displaystyle\min(b_{1},1)\!\int_{-kT}^{kT}\left(\bigl|\dot{(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}}\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}\bigr|^{2}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-kT}^{kT}\left[\dot{u_{0}^{k}}\,\dot{(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}}+K_{u}\bigl(t,u_{0}^{k}(t)\bigr)\,(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}(t)\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-kT}^{kT}f_{1^{+}}^{1}\!\left(t,u_{0}^{k}(t)\right)\,(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=0.

which implies that
(u_{0}^{k}-\mu_{1})^{+}\equiv 0
, namely

(5.10)

u_{0}^{k}\in[0,\mu_{1}].

Consequently, in view of (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) , we get the proof of the claim.

By using the truncation (
5.3
), we can conclude that
u_{0}^{k}
is a positive bounded weak solution for problem (

P
4

) and
u_{0}^{k}\in(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
which finish the proof of Step
1
.

Step
2
: The existence of the second nontrivial solution
u_{1}^{k}
to problem (

P
4

) is established as follows.
We repeat the argument used in Step 1, now applied to equation (

⁢
A
P

H
1

) with
n=2
.
Proceeding in this way, we obtain a second solution
u_{1}^{k}\in(\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
.
Since
u_{1}^{k}\in(\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
, Remark
3.1
guarantees that

u_{1}^{k}\neq u_{0}^{k}
.
We emphasize that this distinction follows directly from our choice to pass from
n=0
in Step 1
to
n=2
in Step 2.
This “one-step shift” in our construction also explains the estimate in (
5.5
), which is valid for every

i\in\{1,\ldots,2n\}
.

Step

: Problem (

P
4

) admits
n
(
n\geq 1
) nontrivial weak solutions.
To prove this, we proceed by induction.
Assume that

u_{n-1}\in(\mu_{m},\mu_{m+1})
is a solution of
Problem (

).
Then we repeat the argument of Step 1, now applied to
equation (

⁢
A

) with

.
This yields the
n
-th solution, which lies in the interval

,
and the construction can be iterated indefinitely.

Furthermore, since
\mu_{n}>0
for every

, we conclude that

u_{i}>0
for all

.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and assume that inequality (
5.5
) holds.
The proof of the existence of multiple negative nontrivial solutions to problem (

P
3

) is analogous to that of the positive ones.
Therefore, we restrict our attention to the existence of
n
positive nontrivial weak solutions of problem (

P
3

).
To this end, we divide the proof into three steps:

Step
1
:
There exists a constant
M>0
such that

(5.11)

\|u_{i}^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{2kT}}\leq M,\qquad\forall\,i\in\{0,\ldots,n\},\ \forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast},

where each function
u_{i}^{k}
is the solution obtained in Theorem
5.3
.
Indeed, for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
we have

u_{i}^{k}\in(\mu_{m_{i}},\mu_{m_{i}+1})\quad\quad\text{for some }m_{i}\in\mathbb{N},

and
\mu_{m_{i}}
is independent of
k
.
Therefore, since the corresponding indices
m_{i}
belong to a finite set
I
(also independent of
k
), we can set

M:=\max_{m_{i}\in I}\mu_{m_{i}+1},

which immediately yields (
5.11
).

Step
2
:
For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the sequence
(u_{i}^{k})_{k}
converges to a function

u_{i}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
in
C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
.

Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We first show that the sequences

(\dot{u}_{i}^{k})_{k}
and
(\ddot{u}_{i}^{k})_{k}
are uniformly bounded.
Since
u_{i}^{k}
is a classical solution of problem (

P
4

), we have

\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}(t)=V_{u}\!\left(t,u_{i}^{k}(t)\right),\qquad t\in[-kT,kT].

Hence, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and
t\in[-kT,kT]
, one has

\bigl|\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}(t)\bigr|\leq\bigl|V_{u}(t,u_{i}^{k}(t))\bigr|\leq c,

for some constant
c>0
independent of
k
. Thus,

(5.12)

\|\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(-2kT,\,2kT)}\leq c.

Using the Mean Value Theorem, for every
t\in[-kT,kT]
there exists

\tau_{k}\in[t-1,t]
such that

\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(\tau_{k})=\int_{t-1}^{t}\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(s)\,ds=u_{i}^{k}(t)-u_{i}^{k}(t-1).

Combining Step 1 with (
5.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl|\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(t)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\tau_{k}}^{t}\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}(s)\,ds+\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(\tau_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t-1}^{t}\!\!|\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}(s)|\,ds+|u_{i}^{k}(t)-u_{i}^{k}(t-1)|

\displaystyle\leq c.

Therefore,

(5.13)

\|\dot{u}_{i}^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(-2kT,\,2kT)}\leq c,\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}.

We now prove the equicontinuity of
(u_{i}^{k})
and
(\dot{u}_{i}^{k})
.
For all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, we have

|\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(t)-\dot{u}_{i}^{k}(s)|=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\ddot{u}_{i}^{k}(\tau)\,d\tau\right|\leq c\,|t-s|.

Similarly,

|u_{i}^{k}(t)-u_{i}^{k}(s)|\leq c\,|t-s|.

Since
(u_{i}^{k})
and
(\dot{u}_{i}^{k})
are uniformly bounded in

L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and equicontinuous, the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem
implies the existence of a subsequence (still denoted
(u_{i}^{k})
) converging to
a limit function
u_{i}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
in

C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
.

Step
3
: For every
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
,
u_{i}
is a weak, positive, nontrivial solution of problem (

P
3

). To show this, we fix
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Since
(u_{i}^{k})
is a positive solution of problem (

P
4

), we have

(5.14)

\ddot{u_{i}^{k}}(t)=V_{u}(t,u_{i}^{k}(t)),\quad\text{for }t\in[-kT,kT],\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
. There exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k>k_{0}

(5.15)

\ddot{u_{i}^{k}}(t)=V_{u}(t,u_{i}^{k}(t)),\quad\text{for }t\in[a,b].

Integrating (
5.15
) from
a
to
t\in[a,b]
, we obtain

(5.16)

\dot{u_{i}^{k}}(t)-\dot{u_{i}^{k}}(a)=\int_{a}^{t}V_{u}(s,u_{i}^{k}(s))\,ds,\quad t\in[a,b].

Passing to the limit as
k\to+\infty
in (
5.16
), and using the uniform convergence
\dot{u_{i}^{k}}\to\dot{u_{i}}
and
u_{i}^{k}\to u_{i}
on
[a,b]
, we get

(5.17)

\dot{u_{i}}(t)-\dot{u_{i}}(a)=\int_{a}^{t}V_{u}(s,u_{i}(s))\,ds,\quad\forall t\in[a,b].

Since
a
and
b
are arbitrary, (
5.17
) shows that
u_{i}(t)
satisfies (

P
3

).

It remains to show that the obtained solutions are distinct. To achieve this goal, we use again the uniform convergence of
\dot{u}_{i}^{k}
to
\dot{u}_{i}
on any interval
[a,b]
. We can therefore conclude that

u_{i}\in(\mu_{m},\mu_{m+1})\quad\text{and}\quad u_{i+1}\in(\mu_{m+2},\mu_{m+3})

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
5.1
.
