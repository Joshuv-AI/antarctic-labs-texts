An intermittent ascending aorta clamping strategy to rescue an emergency management of unexpected massive hemorrhage in mediastinal tumor surgery: a case report
Authors: Yeke Zhu, Wenbin Teng, Yan Xiang, Yueying Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMediastinal tumors pose significant surgical and anesthetic challenges.Case presentationIn this paper, we discuss the management of a large mediastinal tumor complicated by sudden aortic injury. With the strategic application of an intermittent ascending aorta clamping technique combined with timely extracorporeal support, the aortic injury could be successfully managed. Additionally, complete tumor resection was possible without any deterioration of vital organ functions, which resulted in a successful outcome.ConclusionsOur experience with this case offers valuable insights for managing similar challenging surgical scenarios.

Background

Mediastinal tumors pose significant challenges for surgery and anesthesia because of their close proximity to the heart and major blood vessels and tendency for airway compression [1]. Surgical procedures for their removal are extremely complex and highly risky. One of the most severe complications of mediastinal tumors is aortic injury with consequent rapid and uncontrolled blood loss, similar to aortic dissection, which can also lead to rapid, massive hemorrhage with extremely high mortality [2]. Severe hemorrhage of this kind obscures the operative field view and impedes bleeding control. Several approaches for the treatment of traumatic aortic rupture have been investigated thus far, with early identification of the hemorrhage site, vascular repair under cardiopulmonary bypass, and cerebral protection being key [3–5]. ; however, studies on intraoperative strategies and anesthetic management in this area are limited. Here, we report a case of mediastinal tumor in a patient who underwent palliative surgical resection without preoperative cardiopulmonary bypass support. However, towards the completion of the tumor dissection, an aortic injury occurred, leading to massive hemorrhage. The emergency was successfully managed using an intermittent ascending aortic clamping technique. With this approach, crucial insights were obtained into the management of bleeding control in such situations.

Case Presentation

A 67-year-old woman (height, 160 cm; weight, 56 kg) was diagnosed with a mediastinal tumor. A biopsy performed at a local hospital confirmed a type B1 thymoma. Despite two cycles of chemotherapy (regimen: carboplatin, cyclophosphamide, and liposomal doxorubicin), the mediastinal tumor showed no reduction. The patient was then referred to our hospital for further surgical treatment. At the time of transfer to our hospital, she did not exhibit any common obvious symptoms such as chest tightness, asthma, cough, or muscle weakness. She had hypertension and had previously undergone surgical resection of right breast cancer. At presentation, the patient’s status was evaluated as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Class II and New York Heart Association (NYHA) class Ⅰ. Electrocardiogram (ECG) and cardiac ultrasound findings were unremarkable. Mediastinal CT showed presence of a large soft tissue mass, measuring approximately 8.4 × 7.4 cm, located within the retrosternal great vessel space, extending from the thoracic inlet to the level of the pericardium. Heterogeneous internal density and contrast enhancement were noted. Obliteration of the fat planes was observed between the mass and the adjacent local tissues. The lesion abutted and displaced the intrathoracic great vessels and compressed a part of the left thoracic cavity. (Fig. 1)

No abnormalities were detected on coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) and cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). On bronchoscopy, external pressure stenosis of the left main bronchus was detected. Laboratory tests showed a hemoglobin concentration of 114 g/L, platelet count of 121 × 109/L, and normal levels of liver and kidney function markers.

A preoperative multidisciplinary team discussion was held to plan the treatment strategy for this case. Since the contrast-enhanced mediastinal CT did not reveal any obvious vascular invasion, the thoracic surgeon recommended resection of the tumor to the greatest extent possible. Additionally, given the possibility of vascular encasement and invasion by the tumor, the cardiac and great vessel surgery team and the perfusionist were notified to be fully prepared and on standby on the day of surgery.

At the time of the operation, the patient was placed in a supine position on the operating table and it was confirmed that she did not experience chest tightness, shortness of breath, or other discomfort. The patient had initial non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) of 145/79 mmHg, a heart rate of 101 bpm, and a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 96%. Under monitoring of ECG, arterial blood pressure (ABP), and SpO2, anesthesia with tracheal intubation was induced with etomidate (0.25 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) and maintained with propofol (300 mg/h), remifentanil (0.2 µg/kg·min), and cis-atracurium (6 mg/h), and adjusted according to the results of bispectral index (BIS) monitoring. A double-lumen 8Fr catheter was inserted into the right femoral vein, and two large-bore intravenous access lines were established in the upper limbs for volume management in case of bleeding. The surgical field was disinfected, and the left groin field was prepared for establishment of extracorporeal circulation in an emergency.

Intraoperative assessment revealed that the tumor was dense and in close proximity to the surrounding vascular structures. A careful, progressive dissection was performed. Just as the procedure neared completion, profuse hemorrhage suddenly occurred, obscuring the surgical field. Arterial blood gas analysis showed adequate oxygen saturation; however, a critical drop in hemoglobin from 110 g/L to 72 g/L was noted. After 15 min, the hemoglobin dropped further to 55 g/L. Moreover, ABP had significantly reduced, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) dropped immediately from 32 mmHg to 16mmHg. The surgeon’s rapid assessment confirmed the possibility of aortic injury. The anesthesiologist actively maintained the blood volume and vital signs while constantly communicating with the cardiac and major vascular surgery team and the perfusionist. Because it was difficult to locate the bleeding site due to the rapid and massive hemorrhage, the thoracic surgeon and anesthesiologist decided to clamp the ascending and descending aorta (Fig. 2) for improved visualization. To prevent cerebral ischemia caused by prolonged clamping of the ascending aorta, clamping for 3 min was followed by a 2-minute release of the clamp to ensure cerebral perfusion. After three such cycles, the site of injury was precisely identified as the transition zone between the aortic arch and the descending aorta. Therefore, the thoracic surgeon shifted the aortic cross-clamp from the ascending aorta to a position proximal to the site of injury (Fig. 3). This restored the blood flow to the right brachiocephalic trunk, the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery, thereby ensuring perfusion to the brain and upper limbs.

At this point, extracorporeal circulation via the left femoral artery and vein was quickly established; in addition, a cell saver was employed to salvage blood. With a relatively clear view, the cardiovascular surgeon identified an irregular, 2-cm-long laceration requiring patch repair located. Consequently, the decision was taken to proceed with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) to ensure perfusion of the abdominal organs and lower limbs. Meanwhile, the anesthesiologist maintained spontaneous heartbeat through the use of vasoactive medications and volume management to ensure perfusion of the brain and upper extremities (Fig. 4). The anesthesiologist also coordinated the CPB suction flow rate to maintain adequate volume for the upper body. During this period, the mean arterial pressure in the upper limbs ranged from 70 to 100 mmHg, and the heart rate was between 110 and 150 beats per minute. Temperature was maintained at around 35 °C. An ice cap was promptly placed on the patient’s head during the initial phase of the rescue. CPB lasted for approximately 51 min, and the laceration in the descending aorta was successfully repaired with a prosthetic patch.

The operative procedure for the total dissection of the mediastinal tumor and involved lymph nodes lasted for three hours. The estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 2000 mL. The patient received 5.5 units of packed red blood cells, 520 mL of fresh frozen plasma, 17 units of platelets, and 1450 mL of autologous blood transfusion. Additionally, 400 mL of 20% albumin and 12,000 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution were administered, with a total urine output of 1000 mL. At the end of the operative procedure, thromboelastogram and activated clotting time (ACT) showed normal clotting parameters, and the patient’s pupils were equal in size and reactive to light. Subsequently, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). On postoperative day 6, the tracheal tube was successfully removed. The patient was discharged without complications after 20 days. Unfortunately, after more than two years of follow-up, the patient’s thymoma recurred. The family decided to forgo a second surgery and, instead, opted for a maintenance chemotherapy regimen.

Discussion

This report details a case of unexpected massive hemorrhage caused by aortic injury during mediastinal tumor resection, in which the occlusion and resuscitation strategy allowed for favorable postoperative recovery without impairment of organ function. Mediastinal tumors are manifested by a wide range of clinical presentations that vary with their size and location: while some tumors may be completely asymptomatic, others might cause severe cardiopulmonary issues. Generally, risk stratification is based on symptoms, the tumor-to-hemithorax ratio, and the presence of airway or vascular compression. In cases of respiratory or circulatory instability, anesthetic induction and resection of the mediastinal mass may be performed under the cover of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [6]. For this case, we convened a multidisciplinary team to discuss the complexity of the tumor resection, potential vascular injury, and coagulation challenges. Since the patient presented with no concerning preoperative symptoms or signs, the surgical team made the strategic decision to attempt resection of the tumor via median sternotomy while having the cardiac and major vascular surgery team and the perfusionist on standby.

Despite careful dissection of the tumor, sudden massive hemorrhage occurred, and the obscured surgical field made timely compression or clamping of the bleeding site impossible. This called for prompt decisions by the surgical team. Despite being the quickest way to stop bleeding, aortic clamping can trigger cerebral ischemia when the ascending aorta is obliterated. The immediate initiation of intermittent ascending aorta clamping for bleeding control was a crucial step in ensuring a clear visual field and locating the injury site. This approach requires the anesthesiologist to optimize the management of the patient’s vital signs while minimizing the risk of ischemia and hypoxia to critical organs such as the brain, heart, and spinal cord [7].

Unlike the case with aortic aneurysm surgery, in which extracorporeal circulation is established early, there is a rapid decrease in preload following aortic injury, which makes clamping of the ascending aorta possible. This, in turn, necessitates the use of epinephrine to maintain cardiac excitability and adequate blood pressure to ensure cerebral perfusion. The intermittent clamping-unclamping strategy was thus designed to maintain perfusion to the heart and brain within a limited ischemic time.

The surgical team, in close collaboration with anesthetists, effectively managed the aortic injury through the strategic use of ascending aortic occlusion and relaxation. This technique was pivotal in controlling the bleeding, locating the site of rupture, and facilitating the timely initiation of extracorporeal circulation, which ultimately enabled the successful repair of the injury.

Direct suture repair of the injured site was not feasible; therefore, extracorporeal circulation was employed to maintain perfusion to the abdominal organs and lower extremities. Additionally, vasoactive agents were administered to sustain cardiac autonomic rhythm and ensure perfusion to the upper limbs and brain. The intermittent aortic cross-clamping technique is consistent with the principle of ischemic preconditioning, a mechanism proven to protect myocardial and cerebral tissues from potential injury [8–11]. However, it is important to note that the two should not be conflated in this context. Remarkably, the patient regained consciousness postoperatively without any cerebral dysfunction, with no evidence of ischemia or infarction on cranial CT (Fig. 5).

The strategic regulation of temperature, i.e., the induction of mild hypothermia, has been validated for its neuroprotective effects in settings of cardiac arrest [12]. In the surgical context in the present case, the timely application of the cooling cap also served as an essential and indispensable component in protecting neurological function.

Post-injury management involved intraoperative adjustments to the surgical approach to ensure continued perfusion above the clamp, with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation supporting the rest of the body. Ongoing debates on the benefits and risks of distal aortic perfusion are particularly relevant to this phase of the surgery, given the high risk of complications such as paraplegia and renal failure associated with prolonged aortic clamping(>30 min) [4, 13]. In this case, there was an interval of approximately 30 min between the cross-clamping of the descending aorta and the establishment of full extracorporeal membrane oxygenation perfusion. Subsequently, cardiac and great vessel surgery exploration revealed a defect of approximately 2 × 2 cm. Since direct suture repair was not feasible, a decision was made to fashion a patch from a prosthetic vascular graft (Vascutek Limited) to repair the defect. The repair was successfully completed following a bypass period of approximately 50 min. The aortic cross-clamp was then released, restoring blood flow. Fortunately, no postoperative complications ensued.

Indeed, there are a few downsides in this case that need to be discussed. Since the patient did not exhibit any obvious symptoms of superior mediastinal mass syndrome such as cough, stridor, dyspnea, or dysphagia due to a mediastinal tumor [1], there was no need for any preferred position. Additionally, anesthesia induction proceeded smoothly. Due to these reasons, we overlooked the necessity of establishing extracorporeal circulation in advance. Although the injury site was promptly identified using a decisive alternating cross-clamping strategy, without any postoperative organ dysfunction, preoperative planning and preparation for extracorporeal circulation/extracorporeal membrane oxygenation could have made the surgery more manageable and safer.

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical importance of meticulous preoperative planning and decisive occlusion strategy in the management of mediastinal tumors complicated by acute aortic injury. Bleeding control and repair of the injury were successfully achieved with the effective use of ascending aortic occlusion and relaxation, coupled with strategic perioperative management. However, meticulous preoperative evaluation and preparation are crucial for such procedures. Preparing for CPB support in advance and considering an aortic cross-clamping strategy might be safer. This case delineates the key takeaways and areas for improvement in managing such scenarios, while also offering valuable insights for the management strategies of unexpected aortic injuries.
