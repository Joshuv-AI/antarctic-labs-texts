HIV inhibits Warburg metabolism in human macrophages infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Kevin Brown, Aaron Walsh, Anjali S Yennemadi, Dearbhla M Murphy, Sarah A Connolly, Grainne Jameson, Mary P O’Sullivan, Sharee A Basdeo, Seonadh O’Leary, Gina Leisching, Joseph Keane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB)-associated mortality remains disproportionately high among people living with HIV (PLWH), with macrophage dysfunction representing a key mechanism of impaired host defence against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection. Macrophage metabolic switching has emerged as a paradigm of host success, yet the influence of HIV on this ‘Warburg’ response in the context of Mtb infection has not been studied. Using the U1 chronically HIV-infected macrophage cell line model coupled with primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) exposed to HIV-1 gp120, we systematically characterized transcriptomic and immunometabolic perturbations during Mtb infection. Nanostring RNA analysis revealed that Mtb monoinfection upregulated glycolytic genes while suppressing oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) transcripts, consistent with a Warburg-type metabolic shift. Conversely, HIV infection downregulated glycolytic enzymes and enhanced OXPHOS. Coinfection studies demonstrated HIV-mediated suppression of Mtb-induced glycolytic reprogramming. Extracellular flux analysis demonstrated that gp120 exposure increased basal oxygen consumption rate while impairing spare respiratory capacity in Mtb-infected MDMs, effectively blocking the Warburg metabolic transition. Notably, gp120 attenuated Mtb-induced TNF-α secretion and impaired macrophage control of Mtb growth. This study reveals that HIV gp120 blocks the protective Warburg response to Mtb and highlights the potential of host-directed therapies that boost glycolysis or its downstream effectors (e.g. TNF-α) as adjunctive strategies in TB/HIV co-infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-50059-3.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV co-infection, Macrophage metabolism, Warburg effect, Oxidative phosphorylation, gp120, Host-directed therapySubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains the leading cause of death in people living with HIV (PLWH), with HIV infection conferring a substantially increased risk of developing active TB disease1. Macrophages play a critical role in early control of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), the causative agent of TB, orchestrating inflammatory responses and executing antimicrobial functions such as phagocytosis, phagosome maturation, and cytokine secretion. However, in the context of HIV, these essential macrophage functions are impaired—a phenomenon modelled in vitro by chronically HIV-infected macrophage cell lines (e.g., U1 cells) and exposure to the HIV envelope protein gp120, which circulates in PLWH even during antiretroviral therapy2–4.

Macrophage metabolic reprogramming is a sentinel feature of the host response to Mtb infection5, and we have recently published a transcriptomic analysis of aerobic glycolysis in Mtb-infected human alveolar macrophages6. Using similar cellular model systems, we have shown that this ‘Warburg’ response. Ie. increased glucose uptake and lactate production, even in the presence of sufficient oxygen, is limited by virulent Mtb infection7. Indeed, our in-vitro models of smoking and steroid use suggest that a diminished glycolytic response tracks with the inadequate innate immune response in these susceptible groups8,9. Despite knowing that HIV impairs macrophage effector functions, it remains unknown whether HIV co-infection influences the Warburg response to Mtb.

People living with HIV have a defective TNF response to Mtb infection in their pulmonary compartment, and we have seen that Mtb-infected macrophage TNF production is under metabolic control10,11, which suggests that there is an impaired metabolic response in HIV/TB macrophages. Notably, the HIV envelope protein gp120 circulates systemically in PLWH, including those on antiretroviral therapy. This has been shown to directly modulate myeloid cell function2,12, which makes adding exogenous gp120 to macrophages a relevant tool for modelling HIV-associated immune dysfunction in vitro.

This study therefore uses cellular models (U1 cells and gp120 treated cells) to explore human macrophage immunometabolism in Mtb/HIV co-infection. Despite the U1 chronic infection model reflecting aspects of HIV latency/persistence, we chose this cell type as a proof of concept, modelling HIV/Mtb co-infection. We also use the avirulent Mtb strain H37Ra, to model the successful immune response. After characterising the metabolic transcriptional signatures of chronically HIV-infected and Mtb/HIV co-infected human macrophages, we used flux analysis (Seahorse) to assess how HIV co-infection impairs Mtb-induced glycolytic reprogramming and/or Warburg metabolism. Using a cellular model, we assessed the effect of HIV gp120 treatment on mitochondrial function in Mtb-infected human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDM), and how gp120 perturbs downstream effector myeloid function.

Methods

Cell culture

U937 cells, a human myeloid leukemia cell line, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and chronically HIV-1-infected U1 cells (a subclone of U937) were purchased from the NIH AIDS Reagent Program. Cell lines were grown in RPMI completed with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and subcultured every 72 h. For experiments, U937 and U1 cells were matured from their monocytic to macrophage phenotype with 100 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P1585-1MG) for 48 h. PBMCs were isolated from commercially available buffy coats of healthy donors (obtained with informed consent from the Irish Blood Transfusion Services) using Lymphoprep (StemCell Technologies) density-gradient centrifugation. Following isolation, cells were washed and resuspended at a density of 2.5 × 10⁶ PBMCs/mL in RPMI medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% AB human serum (Sigma-Aldrich). The cell suspension was then plated onto non-treated tissue culture plates (Costar) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 7 days. Non-adherent cells were removed by washing every 2–3 days. MDM purity was assessed by flow cytometry and consistently exceeded 90%.

M. tuberculosis infection

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (H37Ra and H37Rv) acquired from the ATCC was grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth culture medium (Sparks Ltd), supplemented with 10% albumin-dextrose-catalase (ADC) (Sparks Ltd, Cat# 211887) in an upright Nunc T25 culture flask and maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in the CL 3 facility incubator and grown until log-phase. Gamma-Irradiated Mycobacterium tuberculosis (iH37Rv) was acquired from Bei Resources, NIAID, NIH. Macrophages were infected with a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) represented as ~ 30% infectivity and 1–5 bacilli/cell as previously described, including MOI checks with AFB staining13.

Macrophage stimulations

γ-irradiated iH37Rv stimulation

All bioenergetic flux Mtb stimulations were performed using γ-irradiated iH37Rv. After determination of MOI, MDM were stimulated with the desired volume of iH37Rv either 30–60 min before bioenergetic flux analysis, or during the bioenergetic flux run by direct injection from the cartridge port.

HIV gp120 stimulation of human macrophages

Recombinant HIV-1 IIIB Envelope Glycoprotein 120 (Baculovirus) (ImmunoDx, Cat# 1001) was acquired from the Centre for AIDS Reagents (CFAR), National Institute for Biological Standards and Control (NIBSC) (Repository Reference EVA607). Previously seeded human MDM in plastic tissue culture plates/Agilent Seahorse 24-well XF Cell Culture Microplates were treated for 24 h with HIV gp120 at either low (100 ng/mL) or high (5,000 ng/mL) dose and rested for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Low dose HIV gp120 was used to represent “ART-treated HIV infection”, as this concentration of HIV gp120 has been reported in BAL fluid of ART-treated PLWH2. Macrophage viability was confirmed by crystal violet staining post-infection (Fig.S1).

Gene expression and transcriptional analysis

Total RNA was harvested from human macrophages 24 h following H37Rv Mtb infection and isolated and purified by silica-membrane column centrifugation, using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN). RNA was assessed using the NanoString nCounter Metabolic Pathways panel. Metabolic mRNA signatures for four comparable conditions were characterised: “Control” (uninfected U937 macrophages), “TB” (Mtb-infected U937 macrophages), “HIV” (Mtb-uninfected U1 macrophages) and “TB/HIV” (Mtb-infected U1 macrophages). Data were analysed using the NanoString nSolver 4.0 software and Advanced Analysis module to generate raw mRNA counts for each condition, which were then normalised and expressed as log2FC values using the in-built housekeeping, normalisation, and QC processes. Data visualisations were generated by the NanoString nSolver 4.0 Advanced Analysis module and R programme (https://www.r-project.org) specifically pheatmap (v1.0.12).

Bioenergetic flux analysis

For the acute injection protocol, MDM culture medium in Agilent Seahorse 24-well XF Cell Culture Microplate was replaced with 500µL of supplemented warmed Seahorse XF RPMI Medium. For the Mtb treatment wells, the desired volume of iH37Rv (as determined by MOI) was placed in Port A and topped up to a final volume of 56µL with completed Seahorse XF RPMI Medium. Mito Stress tests were performed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After each run, MDMs in the microplate were fixed for normalisation using Crystal Violet Staining Solution (0.5%).

ELISA

Supernatants were assessed by ELISA for the detection of TNFα (Invitrogen, 88–734688), IL1β (Biolegend, 437004) and IL-10 (Biolegend, 430604) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessing M. tuberculosis growth using colony forming unit assays

MDMs were infected with H37Ra at an MOI 1–5 for 3 h. Cells were lysed in sterile 0.1% Triton-X for 10 min at room temperature for colony forming unit (CFU) enumeration on day 0 (3 h post-infection), on day 2 (48 h post-infection) and on day 5 (120 h post-infection). Sterile-filtered 7H9 Middlebrook broth supplemented with ADC was used to dilute lysates at 10− 1, 10 − 2, and 10− 3. These diluted lysates were then inoculated in triplicate onto 7H10 Middlebrook Agar supplemented with Oleic Albumin Dextrose Catalase (OADC) and cycloheximide (10 µg/ml). Plates were incubated in a humified non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 2–3 weeks before CFU were counted.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Statistically significant differences were determined using Student’s paired t-tests with two or one-tailed P-values. Differences between three or more groups were determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests. P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Mtb, HIV and Mtb/HIV Co-infection induced markedly distinct transcriptional profiles in human macrophages

Targeted transcriptomics on U1/U937 macrophages with and without Mtb infection depicted by the volcano plots (Fig. 1A) demonstrate the magnitude of gene expression changes in the “Mtb”, “HIV” and “Mtb/HIV” infected groups, respectively. Those genes found to be most markedly differentially expressed clustered into several important gene sets and are shown in Table S1. Those genes found to be most markedly differentially expressed between each of the conditions are shown in the heatmap in Fig. 1B, where clear clustering of the treatment conditions was observed, confirming that all treatment groups are transcriptionally distinct. Mtb generally induced genes within the “Cytokine and Chemokine Signalling”, “NFB” and “Tryptophan/Kynurenine Metabolism” gene sets, including the most upregulated genes IL-6, SOD2 and IDO1 vs. control (Fig. 2). HIV infection, on the other hand, was associated with reduced expression of genes within the gene sets “Cytokine and Chemokine Signalling”, “Tryptophan/Kynurenine Metabolism” and “Glycolysis”. Mtb/HIV co-infected macrophages exhibited a mixed transcriptional profile with impaired “Cytokine and Chemokine Signalling” induction, and differential gene expression profiles within “Glutamine Metabolism” and “Glycolysis” gene sets compared to Mtb-infected macrophages. Metabolic analysis was explored in further detail below.

Mtb/HIV co-infected human macrophages do not express a glycolytic transcriptional profile

Further analysis from the transcriptomic profile of the U1/U937 macrophages showed that “Mtb” and “HIV” infected groups induced markedly different glycolytic gene expression profiles (Table S2 and S3, Fig. 3A). HIV infection significantly reduced gene expression across nearly all glycolytic genes, including all HK isomers, PFKL, PKM, GPI and all glucose transporters (SLC2A1, SLC2A5 and SLC2A14) compared to control. Furthermore, HIV significantly reduced the expression of LDHA (which primarily catalyses the conversion of pyruvate to lactate), but significantly induced LDHB (catalyses lactate to pyruvate), suggesting that HIV-infected human macrophages exhibit a transcriptional profile consistent with reduced glycolysis. Changes in glycolytic gene expression were noted to be of greater significance and magnitude in HIV-infected macrophages compared to Mtb-infected macrophages (Fig. 3A).

Mtb/HIV co-infected macrophages exhibited a transcriptional profile consistent with reduced glycolytic gene expression (Fig. 3A). When compared with control macrophages (“Mtb/HIV vs. Ctrl”), Mtb/HIV co-infected macrophages mirrored the gene expression profile of HIV-infected macrophages, suggesting HIV induced strong and consistent changes in the glycolytic gene set (Fig. 3A,B). However, when controlling for chronic HIV infection (“Mtb/HIV vs. HIV”), Mtb appeared to induce a similar glycolytic profile to that observed in Mtb-infected macrophages (“Mtb”) (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that Mtb infection induced glycolysis in HIV-infected macrophages, but as HIV-induced changes in glycolytic gene expression were of greater magnitude, these changes were not observed in Mtb/HIV co-infected macrophages when compared to uninfected control.

HIV infection drives significant changes in the expression of the mitochondrial OXPHOS gene network but remains unchanged during Mtb/HIV Co-infection

Next, we assessed mitochondrial gene expression changes in the U1/U937 macrophage data set (Fig. 4). We assessed genes associated with complex I-V of the mitochondria and found that only HIV infection alone induced significant changes across all 5 complexes, whereas Mtb/HIV co-infection significantly reduced gene expression in mitochondrial complex II and V only. Comparison of transcriptional profiles between HIV and HIV/MTB showed no significant differences(Table S3, Fig. 4A). The heat map represented in Fig. 4B show uninfected control groups clustering together, however clustering was not uniform within treatment groups, suggesting that clonal selection and adaptation may have contributed to this transcriptional divergence. Additionally, examination of individual replicates suggests that this apparent induction may also be driven largely by a single biological replicate within the HIV group, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. A profile of unanimously reduced expression was evident from the treatment groups comparative bar chart in Fig. 4C. HIV induced a markedly different mitochondrial OXPHOS transcriptional profile vs. control. Of greatest interest was the almost universally increased expression of Complex I subunits, with similar expression patterns seen for Complexes IV and V (Fig. 4A). Reduced expression of Complex II was consistent with reduced SDH expression.

HIV gp120 stimulation disrupted early Warburg metabolism in Mtb-infected human MDM through the induction of oxidative metabolism

Low dose HIV gp120 was used to represent “ART-treated HIV infection”, as this concentration of HIV gp120 has been reported in BAL fluid of ART-treated PLWH2. HIV gp120-treated MDM were analysed on the Seahorse XFe24 Analyser using the Acute Injection protocol described in the methods section. Time of OCR and ECAR analysis was chosen as the time of maximum OCR and ECAR response in “Mtb” MDM, and is also indicated on the graph. MtbMDM (blue) exhibited immediate Warburg metabolism following Mtb injection, characterised by significantly increased ECAR (Fig. 5A,C) and significantly decreased OCR (Fig. 5A,C), compared to “Control” MDM (grey). “Lo HIV/Mtb” MDM (purple) exhibited a significantly lower ECAR response after Mtb injection, compared to “Mtb” MDM (Fig. 5B,C). Neither “Lo HIV/Mtb” nor “Hi HIV/Mtb” (red) exhibited any significant change in OCR compared to “Control”, and both exhibited significantly increased OCR compared to “TB” (Fig. 5A–C). Phenotypically, both “Lo HIV/Mtb” and “Hi HIV/Mtb” exhibited more energetic profiles, characterised by an increase in both ECAR and OCR (Figs. 5D), which contrasted with the Glycolytic profile expressed by “Mtb” MDM, characterised by an increased ECAR and decreased OCR. These results reinforce the notion that Mtb-infected MDM exhibit early Warburg metabolism5, and demonstrate how this Warburg metabolism is prevented by prior HIV gp120 treatment.

HIV gp120 treatment reprogrammed mitochondrial function in Mtb-infected human MDM

In light of our transcriptional data, it is plausible to suggest that Mtb-infected MDM mitochondrial function might be disturbed by HIV gp120 treatment. This hypothesis is supported by previous reports of HIV gp120-induced disturbances of mitochondrial function2,3.

Neither the “Lo gp120” nor “Hi gp120” treatment group significantly impaired any parameters of mitochondrial function (Fig.S2). Mitochondrial function was then investigated in Mtb/HIV gp120 co-infection. MDM were treated with HIV gp120 as before, and were then infected with iH37Rv Mtb 30–60 minutes before Mito Stress Test. Results show that ‘Mtb’ MDM and ‘Lo gp120/Mtb’ had no effect on ECAR (Fig. 6A) but significantly increased total ECAR compared to control (Fig. 6B). No significant difference was observed when comparing ECAR between groups. Regarding spare respiratory capacity (SRC), ‘Lo gp120/Mtb’ MDM showed significantly reduced SRC compared to both control and ‘Mtb’ MDM. ‘Hi gp120/Mtb’ MDM showed no significant difference in SRC compared to either control or ‘Mtb’ MDM (p = 0.08) (Fig. 6C).This finding indicates an exacerbated effect on SRC during co-stimulation with the low concentration of gp120.

HIV gp120 reduced TNF production in human MDM infected with Mtb

Considering Mtb-induced glycolytic reprogramming is an established paradigm in human macrophage host defence, that has been specifically linked to downstream effector functions including IL-1β secretion and bacillary killing, interruption of this early Warburg metabolism could be considered a mechanistic functional impairment of macrophage host defence. Consequently, effector functions were investigated in our gp120/MDM model assessment to strengthen the findings of impaired Mtb-induced macrophage metabolism described above.

Firstly, cytokines of known importance in Mtb infection, and/or those recognised to be under metabolic influence, were measured and included TNFα, IL-1β and IL-10. Macrophage supernatants were collected following bioenergetic analysis, and secreted cytokines were measured by sandwich ELISA. TNF-α secretion was significantly increased in ‘Mtb’ MDM compared to control MDM. ‘Lo gp120/Mtb’ MDM showed TNF-α levels comparable to ‘Mtb’ MDM. In ‘Hi gp120/Mtb’ MDM, TNF-α secretion was significantly reduced compared to ‘Mtb’ MDM and was not significantly different from control (Fig. 7A). For IL-1β and IL-10, no statistically significant differences were detected between any of the treatment groups at this time point. The numerical increases observed in some conditions did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the early measurement time point Macrophage TNFα secretion has been shown to peak 2–4 h following LPS stimulation14, however it is unlikely that macrophage IL-1β or IL-10 secretion would have increased significantly at this time point.

Reduced TNFα secretion from Mtb-infected human MDM is linked to impaired mitochondrial function

The data presented so far indicates that HIV gp120 treatment prevents Warburg metabolism in Mtb-infected MDM and, at high dose, restricts TNFα secretion. High gp120 reduces TNF-α (Fig. 7B), while low gp120 (ART-relevant dose) disrupts metabolism without TNF-α changes, suggesting metabolic defects precede cytokine failure. The TNF-α suppression by the high concentration of gp120 mirrors the effects of oligomycin/rotenone (Fig. 7B). These findings together with our recent work11 and others15 leads us to hypothesisethat mitochondrial dysfunction modulates TNFα secretion which may be relevant during HIV/Mtb co-infection.

To assess the functional consequences of HIV-mediated metabolic dysregulation, we evaluated bacterial burden in gp120-pretreated macrophages infected with M. tuberculosis. MDMs were pretreated with high (5,000 ng/mL) dose gp120 for 24 h, followed by infection with M. tuberculosis. Intracellular CFUs were quantified at 3 h, 2 days, and 5 days post-infection (Fig. 7C). By day 5, high-dose gp120 pretreatment resulted in a 1.8-fold increase in CFUs compared to untreated Mtb-infected controls.

Discussion

This study aimed to characterise the metabolic transcriptional profile of HIV-infected human macrophages, and to define specific metabolic pathway perturbations that might be important in corrupting macrophage immunity in Mtb infection thereby promoting TB disease.

Mtb/HIV co-infection induced a transcriptional profile similar to that observed in HIV-infected macrophages, suggesting that HIV infection inhibited Mtb-induced macrophage glycolysis. These findings are in keeping with observations of reduced glycolysis in HIV-infected macrophages16,17, and represent the first investigation of the glycolytic transcriptional profile in Mtb/HIV co-infected macrophages. Mtb-infected macrophages appeared to inhibit mitochondrial OXPHOS gene expression across all five ETC complexes. By contrast, HIV infection appeared to drive mitochondrial OXPHOS gene expression, with significantly induced expression observed in complexes I and IV. Inhibition of Complex I has been explored therapeutically through the use of Metformin as an adjuvant therapy in TB disease18,19. Considering this, and its central role in NAD+ recycling, this finding could be of particular relevance in Mtb/HIV co-infection.

The HIV envelope protein, gp120, has been shown to influence cellular metabolism in various human models. HIV gp120-treated S. pneumoniae-infected MDM exhibit reduced proton leak with maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, and impaired pneumococcal killing2. HIV gp120 treatment of human glioma cells induces glycolysis20 and HIV gp120-treated neurons exhibit reduced SRC compared to untreated control neurons in rat brains3. Persistent circulating HIV gp120 has been observed in the serum of PLWH who are taking virally suppressive ART21, and has also been detected in human BAL fluid2 and lung tissue22. Whether this circulating HIV gp120 might exert an inhibitory influence over the capacity of tissue-resident AM, or indeed migratory MDM, to effectively respond to Mtb during the critical early phases of infection has not been previously investigated, and might have potentially significant consequences for PLWH on effective ART.

The results presented here explore the early metabolic changes in human MDM in a Mtb/HIV co-infection model, and demonstrate how HIV gp120 stimulation of MDM corrupts immediate Mtb-induced glycolytic reprogramming, or Warburg metabolism. Our findings align with previous observations of HIV gp120-induced reduction in SRC3, and impaired mitochondrial kinetics in an HIV gp120/Pneumococcus co-infection model2. It has also been observed that Mtb-infected smoker’s AM have reduced SRC compared to non-smokers’ AM 8, and Mtb-infected cord blood macrophages trend towards reduced SRC compared to adult MDM23, suggesting that SRC is an important indicator of impaired macrophage metabolism in various other TB “at risk” groups. Together these results indicate that HIV gp120 treatment of MDM prevents early Mtb-induced Warburg metabolism and may impair their capacity to respond to insult. Such effects could reasonably be suggested to lead to long term immunometabolic paralysis of HIV gp120-exposed immune cells, corrupting their early anti-Mtb response. Interestingly, this result was true for both “Lo gp120/Mtb” and “Hi gp120/Mtb” treatment groups, suggesting the effect was not dose-dependent and that low dose HIV gp120 was sufficient to prevent glycolytic reprogramming in Mtb-infected MDM. Since the “Lo gp120/TB” treatment group models the HIV gp120 concentrations that have been documented in ART-suppressed HIV2,21, this finding is of particular interest when considering the continued risk of TB disease in PLWH who are on virally-suppressive therapy24,25.

Mtb-induced TNFα secretion from human macrophages is necessary for bacillary control26,27. Mtb/HIV co-infected human macrophages secrete lower concentrations of TNFα compared to Mtb-infected macrophages and this impairs Mtb-induced apoptosis28. The data presented here demonstrates how high dose HIV gp120 stimulation of MDM suppressed secretion of Mtb-induced TNFα, however a direct link between reduced TNFα secretion and impaired Warburg metabolism was not immediately apparent. Although not traditionally considered to be metabolically regulated, there have been a number of reports linking TNFα secretion to macrophage metabolism29. Our lab has shown previously that corrupted early macrophage metabolism in Mtb-infected neonatal cord blood macrophages resulted in a significant reduction in TNFα secretion when compared to adult MDM23. Supporting these findings are previous observations of reduced TNFα-associated inflammation in LPS-treated mice after treatment with metformin (a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor)30,31, and reduced TNFα secretion from Mtb lysate-stimulated human PBMC after treatment with metformin32. Intracellular NAD+:NADH ratios might therefore govern TNFα secretion capacity, and pharmacological bolstering of cellular NAD+ concentrations by the administration of nicotinamide might therefore represent an avenue for host-directed therapy exploration as a cheap and safe adjuvant for the treatment of Mtb/HIV co-infection33. Our CFU data underscore that gp120-mediated metabolic rewiring—specifically, suppression of Warburg metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction—compromises long-term macrophage control of M. tuberculosis. Overall, this data represents a step towards the understanding of immunometabolism in TB/HIV co-infection that can be further built upon with translational models.

Limitations

The U1 chronic infection model may reflect aspects of HIV latency/persistence therefore transcriptomic differences between U937 and U1 cells must be interpreted with the understanding that clonal selection and adaptation may contribute, alongside HIV infection effects. Not all experiments were completed with the H37Rv, however, the use of the H37Ra strain in our infection model helps isolate the specific contribution of macrophage-intrinsic immunometabolism to bacterial control. This excludes the immune evasion we reported with H37Rv, and allowed us to model the early stages of infection where macrophage metabolic responses are important7. We recognize that future studies employing virulent clinical Mtb strains within CL3-compatible metabolic assay systems will be essential to validate and extend our findings within a more physiologically relevant context of infection. Furthermore, our transcriptomic and functional flux data have revealed a compelling immunometabolic phenotype, but the underlying biochemical mechanisms, such as specific alterations in TCA cycle intermediates, NAD+/NADH ratios, or mitochondrial ROS production, should be examined through targeted metabolomics and stable isotope tracing experiments. Finally, the use of γ-irradiated Mtb (iH37Rv) in our flux assays, while necessary for containment, precludes observation of metabolic interactions with replicating bacilli.

This report links HIV gp120 to a corrupted immunometabolic response against Mtb. It provides the mechanistic hypotheses for more in-depth investigations into how HIV persistently rewires host metabolism, resulting in Mtb susceptible macrophages.
