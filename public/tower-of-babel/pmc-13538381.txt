A family of ultrathin non-van der waals kamiokite-type oxides for two-dimensional electronics
Authors: Run Shi, Yonghuang Wu, Liang Zhou, Wengui Jiang, Zonglin Li, Jiayuan Chen, Ning Cao, Chenghui Wu, Mingda Liu, Yinghui Sun, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-van der Waals (vdW) two-dimensional (2D) metal oxides provide an opportunity to enrich the current 2D material library and accelerate the adoption of 2D materials into future electronic devices. However, the exploration of 2D non-vdW oxides remains limited, constrained by synthetic challenges and insufficient fundamental understanding. Here, we report a 2D wide-bandgap non-vdW oxide family—A2Mo3O8 (A = Zn, Mn, Co, etc.), known as kamiokite-type oxides. A dual-controlled chemical vapor deposition strategy enables the in-plane growth of ultrathin A2Mo3O8 single crystals with thicknesses below 3 nm and domain sizes exceeding 100 μm, overcoming the intrinsic constraints of lattice isotropy while providing facile and precise control over product stoichiometry. Notably, 2D Zn2Mo3O8 exhibits a bandgap of 4.0 eV and a high dielectric constant (κ ~ 25). Benefiting from the good dielectric properties and high-quality interface of Zn2Mo3O8, MoS2 transistors with a Zn2Mo3O8 top-gate dielectric exhibit near-Boltzmann-limit subthreshold swing (~63.8 mV dec−1), an on/off ratio exceeding 107, and an ultralow hysteresis width of 1.6 mV MV−1 cm under ambient conditions. The A2Mo3O8 family offers a versatile materials platform for the development of high‑κ dielectrics and a broad range of functional components for next-generation high-performance 2D electronics.

Introduction

The advent of graphene in 2004 unveiled the remarkable world of two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) materials, revolutionizing our cognition on confined matter and its potential for next-generation technologies1–3. However, a fundamental constraint underlies the development of 2D materials: the vdW family is derived from a tiny fraction (<5%) of existing crystals, representing a profound selection barrier in the exploration of 2D systems4. The pursuit of 2D non-vdW materials, exemplified by MXenes and non-vdW metal oxides (MOs, like perovskites), has attracted increasing attention to transcend the inherent compositional and functional limitations of vdW materials. By contrast, 2D non-vdW materials provide access to a vastly expanded landscape of intrinsic properties, such as multiferroicity5,6, a broadly tunable band structure and carrier types7–9, as well as exceptional conductivity induced by strong interlayer coupling10. Therefore, by providing key functionalities absent in the vdW repertoire, non-vdW materials are expected to effectively broaden the application scope of 2D systems, spanning from high-performance electronics to energy storage/conversion applications.

In recent years, 2D non-vdW MOs have demonstrated potential for next-generation field-effect transistors (FETs), where they serve as high-mobility p-type semiconductors8,9,11 or high-κ gate dielectrics12,13. 2D non-vdW MOs with high crystallinity can form good interfaces with 2D semiconductors and evidently enhance transistor performance, revealing great potential in semiconductor technology. However, the practical application of 2D non-vdW MOs is fundamentally constrained by the challenges in their controllable bottom-up synthesis. The central challenge lies in overcoming the inherent isotropic growth behavior of non-vdW materials. Although some methods, such as the adoption of surface passivation14, space confinement15, and low-diffusion-barrier substrates (e.g., mica)16, have successfully promoted the in-plane growth of 2D non-vdW MO nanosheets during vapor deposition processes, the weak lattice anisotropy still makes it difficult to simultaneously obtain ultrathin thickness and large domain size. Moreover, the relevant research has so far been predominantly focused on single‑metal non-vdW MOs (e.g., Fe2O3, Bi2O3), while the exploration of multi‑metal ones (e.g., ternary MOs that contain two metal elements) remains scarce. Notably, the incorporation of additional metal cations offers a powerful lever for tailoring properties, such as band structure, carrier concentration and magnetic order, potentially unlocking functionalities beyond the reach of their single‑metal counterparts, such as colossal magnetoelectric effect17 and high-temperature superconductivity18. Yet, the stringent kinetic control required during synthesis becomes exponentially more complex for the growth of 2D multicomponent non‑vdW MOs15. The need for coordinated delivery and reaction of multiple precursors not only increases the complexity of the reaction system but also raises the risks of phase separation and non‑uniform distribution of cations in the final products, severely undermining the controllability and reproducibility of the synthesis process. Therefore, achieving the synthesis of ultrathin 2D multicomponent non‑vdW MOs and effectively promoting their 2D‑oriented growth remains a challenge.

In this work, we report a dual-controlled chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method to successfully synthesize a 2D non-vdW ternary MO family—A2Mo3O8 (A = Zn, Mn, Ni, Co, Fe), known as kamiokite-type oxides. This non-vdW oxide family shares a hexagonal structure, where A-O tetrahedra and octahedra form in-plane honeycomb networks, and exhibits inherent crystallographic polarity along the c-axis, with A-site metals strongly modulating magnetic order and lattice–spin interaction, enabling its wide applications in theoretical research and energy fields19–21. To overcome the synthetic challenges of multicomponent non-vdW MOs, a dual-controlled reaction system is developed, where S vapor and a MoO3-AOx eutectic mixture are utilized to synergistically modulate the size, composition, and evolution process of precursor droplets. This synergistic control effectively promotes the in-plane growth of 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets, i.e., a minimum thickness of 2.5 nm and a large domain size exceeding 100 μm at a thickness of 3.0 nm. Furthermore, the CVD strategy enables the precise control over the stoichiometry and chemical composition of products, thus providing a pathway for the selective synthesis of 2D MOs with varying oxygen stoichiometry (e.g., A2Mo3O8, AMoO4) and well-defined 2D oxide/sulfide heterostructures (e.g., A2Mo3O8/MoS2). As-prepared 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets also demonstrate a thickness dependence of the band structure, with significant bandgap broadening as thickness decreases. Among them, 2D Zn2Mo3O8 (ZMO) exhibits a wide bandgap of 4.0 eV and a high dielectric constant of ~25, making it a promising and competitive candidate for high-κ dielectrics in high-performance 2D nanoelectronics. Taking good of the high quality of the MoS2-ZMO interface, the fabricated MoS2 FETs with ZMO top-gate dielectric exhibit a high on/off current ratio ~107, near-ideal subthreshold swing (~63.8 mV dec−1) at ambient conditions, and negligible hysteresis width, i.e., ~0.8 mV MV−1 cm for vacuum conditions and ~1.6 mV MV−1 cm for ambient conditions.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and crystal structure of 2D A2Mo3O8

The crystal structure of A2Mo3O8, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, consists of alternating layers of A-O and Mo-O, belonging to the hexagonal crystal system and the space group of P63mc, with lattice parameters of a = b = 5.77 Å and c = 9.91 Å22. In this structure, all the Mo atoms occupy equivalent crystallographic sites and form Mo3O13 clusters through trimerization, ultimately forming a breathing kagome lattice23. Meanwhile, the A2+ ions partially occupy the tetrahedral and octahedral interstitial sites formed by oxygen atoms between the Mo-O layers, resulting in a honeycomb structure. It is noted that the A2Mo3O8 lattice belongs to a non-vdW structure, where all the crystal planes are connected by chemical bonds instead of vdW interactions. Because of the lack of anisotropy in the crystal structure, the direct synthesis of ultrathin A2Mo3O8 single crystals is quite challenging, like other non-vdW systems.

To synthesize A2Mo3O8, a MoO3-AOx eutectic mixture (Supplementary Fig. 2) is utilized as the precursor. In this system, Mo (VI) requires reduction to Mo (IV) in the target phase, necessitating an effective deoxygenation process under a reducing atmosphere. The deoxygenation of Mo precursor is usually accompanied by the formation of volatile suboxides (e.g., MoO3−δ)24. Furthermore, the eutectic nature of the mixture promotes the congruent release of both Mo and A species into the vapor phase25, providing a well‑mixed metal source for the growth of multi‑component oxide crystals. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a, there are two distinct CVD pathways for the growth of A2Mo3O8: a S-free route in pure argon (weakly reducing) and a S-assisted route. In the S-free pathway, the limited reducing atmosphere results in a high activation barrier for the growth reaction. As a result, large precursor droplets ([AOx][MoO3]y, Supplementary Fig. 3) condense on the substrate, within which the growth of A2Mo3O8 proceeds via a liquid-guided mechanism only at temperatures above 800 °C (Supplementary Fig. 4). The liquid-guided process promotes the three-dimensional growth of products, typically yielding crystals with considerable thickness (>30 nm) and limited domain size (<5 μm) as statistically corroborated in Fig. 1b. The non-uniform optical contrast and the coexistence of intermediate oxide phases further attest to the incomplete and inefficient reaction in the S-free environment (Supplementary Fig. 5). By contrast, the introduction of S vapor acts as a mild reducing agent, dramatically lowering the reaction energy barrier, enabling the effective deoxygenation and volatilization of precursors (Supplementary Fig. 6) at a significantly lower temperature (~650 °C). In the presence of S vapor, the size of precursor droplets is drastically reduced, ensuring the nucleation of ultrathin A2Mo3O8 nanocrystals. The reduced kinetic barrier and droplet size further facilitate the lateral expansion of these nuclei, either via coalescence with adjacent droplets or adsorption of surrounding vapor species (Supplementary Fig. 7), thereby promoting effective anisotropic 2D growth8. This mechanism yields large, well-faceted triangular nanosheets with thicknesses as low as ~2.5 nm and domain size of tens of micrometers (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, the moderate reducing capability of S is found to be critical for balancing precursor volatilization and redox kinetics, thereby ensuring good controllability and reproducibility for the in‑plane growth of 2D oxide nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. 9). By manipulating the redox equilibrium through S vapor concentration and amount of MoO3, we achieved the precise control over the stoichiometry and chemical composition of products, obtaining diverse 2D material systems and heterostructures (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 10). In the S-deficient condition, the evaporation of metal precursors is suppressed and the reducing atmosphere is inadequate, leading to the formation of AMoO4 flakes with high oxidation states and a minimal thickness of ~10 nm (Supplementary Fig. 11). As S concentration increases, the thermodynamic equilibrium shifts to form the A2Mo3O8 crystals. When S vapor concentration surges abruptly to supercritical levels, both vapor-phase and liquid-phase precursors participate in the formation of MoS2 crystals, resulting in the growth of A2Mo3O8/MoS2 heterostructures (Supplementary Fig. 12) or MoS2 single layers in S-rich environments. Notably, no signal of S can be detected in the CVD-grown A2Mo3O8 crystals (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating that a relatively high oxygen partial pressure is produced during the reaction, and thus it prevents the sulfidation reaction. In the S-deficient conditions, S vapor is primarily utilized to modulate the redox equilibrium rather than directly participating in the formation of S-containing compounds. In summary, the S-assisted system achieves a precise control of the reaction redox equilibrium, thereby effectively regulating material formation and composition, with promising prospects for diverse applications.

Fig. 1d displays five representative types of CVD-grown A2Mo3O8 crystals, including Zn2Mo3O8 (ZMO), Co2Mo3O8 (CMO), Fe2Mo3O8 (FMO), Ni2Mo3O8 (NMO), and Mn2Mo3O8 (MMO), obtained by utilizing different MoO3-AOx eutectic mixtures. The oxide eutectic precursor is chosen over chlorides to ensure controllable vapor concentration and composition (Supplementary Fig. 14), while the observed variations among A‑site systems are governed by differences in precursor volatility, reducibility, and eutectic temperature. Among them, ZMO exhibits the highest growth yield. Due to structural similarities, these A2Mo3O8 crystals exhibit analogous Raman spectra, consistent with previously reported data from their 3D counterparts26,27 and theoretical results28. Optical microscopy (OM) images reveal that all synthesized A2Mo3O8 samples adopt regular hexagonal or triangular morphologies with hexagonal crystal symmetry. Furthermore, the uniform optical contrast further confirms their flat surface morphology and 2D structure. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) image in Supplementary Fig. 7 verifies that the as-prepared samples demonstrate ultrathin thicknesses as low as 2.5 nm, corresponding to 5 structural layers (each structural layer contains one A-O layer and one Mo-O layer), and atomically smooth surfaces (root-mean-squared roughness ~0.1 nm). Additionally, the step edge (~0.5 nm in height) can be observed on some samples, suggesting a layer-by-layer growth mechanism, further supporting the formation of 2D structures (Supplementary Fig. 15). Notably, the CVD-synthesized 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets maintain a minimal thickness of 2.5−3.0 nm while achieving large domain sizes—up to 60 μm for ZMO and exceeding 100 μm for FMO (Supplementary Fig. 16). The enhanced growth of FMO likely stems from the oxygen-release process of Fe2O3 precursors, which moderates the increase of S vapor concentration and consequently broadens the optimal reaction window. Upon comparing with previously reported results of metal oxides grown by CVD (Fig. 1e), our approach enables synergistic control over both thickness and domain size, representing an advancement in anisotropic growth control for 2D non-vdW oxides5,8,13,29–34.

The substrate also plays a significant role in inducing the growth of 2D oxides. On c‑plane sapphire, A2Mo3O8 nanosheets exhibit straight edges aligned with the atomic steps of sapphire (Supplementary Fig. 17), verifying that the growth of 2D oxides follows a liquid-guided process and the atomic steps effectively confine the spreading of precursor droplets. On mica, triangular/hexagonal A2Mo3O8 flakes form with higher nucleation density compared with those grown on SiO2/Si or sapphire substrates (Supplementary Fig. 18), leading to the coalescence of adjacent domains that is a critical step toward the growth of a continuous film. Notably, on both sapphire and mica, the minimum thickness of as-prepared 2D oxides is about 3.0 nm, identical to that on SiO2/Si, underscoring the general applicability of our growth method to various substrates.

The scaling down of thickness to sub-10 nm fundamentally alters both the phononic and electronic structure of A2Mo3O8 single crystals. As shown in Fig. 1d, the Raman spectra of 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets demonstrate the strongest active Raman mode at ~443 cm−1, and this A1 vibration mode is attributed to the stretching of Mo atoms and bending of O atoms in the Mo3O13 cluster26. It is noted that the A1 mode undergoes a gradual softening as the thickness of ZMO nanosheets decreases from 12 nm to 5 nm (Fig. 3c). We consider that the structural flexibility of Mo3O13 clusters induced by the release of strong bonding interaction in the ultrathin ZMO nanosheets accounts for the thickness-dependent vibration frequency. Similar results can also be observed in other A2Mo3O8 single crystals (Supplementary Fig. 25). In addition, the bandgap is a fundamental parameter for determining the electrical properties of materials. Bulk A2Mo3O8 has been reported as a family of narrow-gap materials, for instance, bulk ZMO exhibits a direct bandgap of ~1.7 eV and bulk FMO possesses a bandgap of ~1.0 eV37. We utilized micro-area UV-Vis optical absorption spectroscopy combined with the Tauc-plot method to determine the optical bandgap of as-prepared 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets with varying thicknesses. As shown in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 26, 2D A2Mo3O8 single crystals generally exhibit a significantly increasing bandgap with the decrease of thickness, with the thinnest 2D ZMO nanosheets reaching a bandgap as high as 4.0 eV. The broadening of bandgap may be attributed to the release of strong interlayer interaction in the ultrathin A2Mo3O8 structures, and this phenomenon has been reported in other 2D non-vdW systems7. The thickness-dependent modulation of material properties provides a viable route for precisely tailoring the electrical and optical characteristics of 2D A2Mo3O8 systems.

We also measured the electrical properties of 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets by constructing simple two-terminal devices. The ultrathin ZMO nanosheets exhibit good in-plane insulating properties with a current of lower than 10−12 A (close to the measurement limitation of the instrument) within a wide voltage range from −5 V to +5 V (Fig. 3e), while other A2Mo3O8 samples with lower bandgaps demonstrate certain conductive capabilities (Supplementary Fig. 27). In addition, the electrical properties of all 2D A2Mo3O8 materials show no response to the gate voltage (using 300 nm SiO2 as the bottom gate dielectric), which should be attributed to the low concentration of free carriers in these materials. We further investigate the out-of-plane conductivity of 2D ZMO nanosheets by building graphene/ZMO/graphene stacks to avoid the disturbance from interfacial defects (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 28). Under an electric field of ~3 MV cm−1, the leakage current through the ZMO device with a 9-nm-thick channel remains at the pA level, corresponding to a current density below 10–3 A cm–2. Therefore, 2D ZMO nanosheets demonstrate superior out-of-plane insulation compared to some emerging 2D dielectric materials with analogous band gaps, e.g., Sr2Nb3O10 (3.8 eV)38 and Bi2SeO5 (3.9 eV)39, at comparable thicknesses and biases/field strengths. It is noted that the breakdown electric field of 2D ZMO flakes is abnormally increased from 5.14 to 6.93 MV cm−1 as the ZMO thickness decreases from 14 to 9 nm, which should be mainly attributed to the larger bandgap of thinner ZMO nanosheets. The combination of wide bandgap and low leakage positions ZMO as a potential candidate for gate insulators in 2D nanoelectronics.

The MoS2 FET exhibits a high on/off current ratio (Ids,on/Ids,off) of at least 107 within a narrow Vtg range from −1.0 to 1.0 V. The output curves in Fig. 5b exhibit good linearity at low drain-source voltage (Vds), indicating high-quality contacts between the source/drain electrodes and the channel, and the clear current saturation at higher Vds can be attributed to the pinch-off of the MoS2 channel. Taking good of the high quality of the MoS2-ZMO interface, the transfer curves of the MoS2 FETs based on the ZMO dielectric exhibit negligible hysteresis (hysteresis width ΔVH ~ 4 mV) under ambient conditions, as shown in Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 30. By contrast, under vacuum conditions, the measured ΔVH exhibits a significant reduction to below 2 mV and the on-state current of FETs is promoted. The improved electrical performance is attributed to the detachment of gaseous species on the exposed part of the MoS2 channels. Notably, the 2D FETs with ZMO retain their benchmark subthreshold performance, providing strong evidence for the defect-resistant stability engineered at the MoS2-ZMO interface. Furthermore, a typical MoS2 FET device demonstrates only marginal degradation in on/off ratio and even better SS after one-year storage (Supplementary Fig. 31), which also supports the long-term stability of the MoS2-ZMO interface. For a fair comparison, the measured ΔVH is normalized by the insulator field factor (ΔVg/t), where t represents the oxide thickness and ΔVg = Vg, max − Vg, min denotes the gate voltage sweep range. For the MoS2 FETs integrated with ZMO dielectric, the minimum normalized ΔVH reaches minimal values of ~0.8 mV MV−1 cm under vacuum conditions and ~1.6 mV MV−1 cm under ambient conditions, respectively. As summarized in Fig. 5e, our devices achieve state-of-the-art SS and normalized hysteresis values, outperforming most reported MoS2 transistors with high-κ dielectrics12,13,29–31,39,44–46, especially under ambient conditions.

Additionally, the band alignment between the gate dielectric and the channel material is a key factor governing transistor performance. As revealed by energy-band diagrams (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 32), the MoS2-ZMO interface exhibits a conduction band offset (CBO) of ~1.5 eV. This substantial offset effectively confines the charge carriers within the MoS2 channel and suppresses Schottky emission, enabling low-leakage and highly-reliable transistor operation38,47. Therefore, the MoS2 transistors with the sub-10-nm-thick ZMO dielectrics demonstrate a gate leakage current density of <10−5 A cm−2 under an electrical field of 1.5 MV cm−1 in the dielectric, several orders of magnitude below the low-power limit for complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) devices.

A benchmarking survey (Supplementary Table 1) demonstrates that our ZMO dielectric offers near‑thermionic SS, low Dit, narrow hysteresis, and good compatibility with ambient conditions in 2D FET devices, rivaling the best reported values for emerging oxide dielectrics. Additionally, the breakdown electric field and leakage current density are comparable to those of other wide-bandgap oxides with similar bandgaps. Beyond these metrics, ZMO-based devices exhibit reliable operation up to 523 K with stable switching characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 33), effective gate control in sub-100 nm channels (Supplementary Fig. 34), and functional down to ~5.0 nm thickness with respectable performance (Supplementary Fig. 35). While the thickness-dependent permittivity roll-off, the relatively moderate bandgap, and the increasing influence of native oxygen vacancies in ultrathin films present challenges for extreme EOT scaling, the overall figures of merit firmly position ZMO as a promising dielectric candidate for practical 2D device integration.

In summary, this work develops a dual-controlled CVD strategy for the synthesis of 2D ternary metal oxides A2Mo3O8 (A=Zn, Fe, Mn, Ni, Co) with a non-vdW lattice structure. By manipulating the vapor concentration of S and utilizing different MoO3-AOx eutectic mixtures, we effectively achieve the synergistic control over thickness (down to 2.5 nm) and domain size (>100 μm) of 2D A2Mo3O8 single crystals, overcoming the anisotropic growth limitations in non-vdW materials. This reaction system also enables a programmable control of redox equilibrium through S vapor, achieving on-demand synthesis of 2D materials with tunable oxygen stoichiometry and chemical composition—from tailored metal oxides to oxide/sulfide heterostructures. Among the A2Mo3O8 samples, the 2D Zn2Mo3O8 is discovered to have a wide band gap of ~4.0 eV and good dielectric properties, making it a competitive candidate for high-κ dielectrics in 2D nanodevices. Furthermore, both the crystal structure and band structure of 2D Zn2Mo3O8 are in good alignment with MoS2, enabling the ideal device performance of 2D FETs, like near-Boltzmann-limit SS and negligible hysteresis under even ambient conditions. Notably, we have demonstrated the growth environment compatibility of A2Mo3O8 with MoS2 and achieved the in-situ growth of A2Mo3O8/MoS2 heterostructures. Therefore, developing the growth techniques of wafer-scale A2Mo3O8 films, however, has not yet been realized, and could be a promising topic to advance the industrialization of existing 2D materials and devices.

Methods

Synthesis of 2D A2Mo3O8 single crystals

2D A2Mo3O8 single crystals were synthesized using a chemical vapor deposition system equipped with a 1-inch diameter quartz tube. Typically, MoO3 powder (0.5−2.0 mg, 99.9%, Aladdin) was covered by AOx powder (1.0−2.0 mg, including ZnO, MnO, Fe2O3, CoO, and NiO, 99.9%, Aladdin), and the mixture of oxides was loaded onto a quartz boat and placed in the center of the furnace as metal precursors. To enhance the evaporation of metal precursor and facilitate the transfer of samples, 1−5 μL of 10 mM ACl2 (including ZnCl2, MnCl2, FeCl2, CoCl2, and NiCl2) solution was dropped onto the quartz boat. Cleaned SiO2 (300 nm)/Si substrates were placed face-up downstream of the oxide mixture. Sulfur powder (0.04–0.2 g, 99.99%, Sigma Aldrich) was utilized as a reducing agent in another boat at the upstream of the oxide mixture, with the sulfur zone maintained at 90 °C–120 °C during the growth. The system was first heated up to 650 °C−700 °C at a heating rate of 8 °C/min and then naturally cooled down to room temperature. Throughout the whole growth process, 10 sccm argon flow was used as the carrier gas and the chamber of the quartz tube was maintained at atmospheric pressure.

Transfer of 2D A2Mo3O8 nanosheets

By using a hydrothermal method, the 2D A2Mo3O8 crystals were transferred onto other substrates, including Cu grids for TEM measurements, transparent sapphire substrate for ultraviolet–visible absorption spectroscopy, and the SiO2 (300 nm)/Si substrates with patterned microelectrodes for electrical measurements. A poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA 950k A4) solution was spin-coated onto the SiO2/Si substrates with 2D A2Mo3O8 as a supporting layer (2500 rpm for 50 s) and baked at 150 °C for 3 min. The PMMA-attached samples were then soaked in 80 °C deionized water for several hours to weaken the interaction between PMMA and the substrate. The PMMA film with 2D A2Mo3O8 was slowly picked up by a piece of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) sheet and then attached to the target substrate by using a low-dimensional materials transfer platform (Metatest, E1-G). Finally, the PMMA/A2Mo3O8/target substrate sample was baked at 150 °C for 3 min or at 60 °C for 20 min, and the PMMA was removed in acetone at 80 °C for 15 min.

Materials characterizations

The morphology, surface potential and thickness characteristics of samples were characterized using an optical microscope (OLYMPUS BX51M) and AFM (Bruker Multimode 8). The crystal structure and chemical composition were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-ARM300F2 and FEI, Titan ETEM G2). The Raman and SHG spectra were measured by a smart Raman confocal-micro-Raman module (Institute of Semiconductors, Chinese Academy of Sciences and WITec, alpha300 R). The optical band gap of the 2D nanosheets was measured using a micro-area UV–vis–NIR optical absorption spectroscopy (MStarter ABS, Metatest). The band offset was characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo).

Device fabrication and electrical measurements

For the fabrication of FET devices, the exfoliated 2D materials, including 1 L/2 L graphene and few-layer MoS2 flakes, and A2Mo3O8 flakes were sequentially transferred onto SiO2 (300 nm)/Si substrates. Subsequently, the Cr/Au (5/50 nm) electrodes were defined and deposited via standard electron beam lithography and electron beam evaporation. Electrical characterizations of FETs were conducted using semiconductor parameter analysers (Agilent B1500A and Keithley 4200A-SCS). All the electrical measurements were carried out under ambient conditions unless otherwise specified.
