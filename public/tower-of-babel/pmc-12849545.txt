Background

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, relapsing multisystem vasculitis that typically presents with recurrent oral aphthous ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and ocular involvement [1]. Vascular complications are relatively common in BD, with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) reported in up to one-quarter of patients. However, pulmonary embolism (PE) is an extremely rare manifestation despite the high frequency of thrombosis, mainly due to the strong adherence of thrombi to vessel walls, which prevents embolization. When it does occur, PE in BD is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [2–5]. The diagnosis of BD remains essentially clinical, often guided by the International Study Group (ISG) criteria, which require recurrent oral ulcers along with genital, ocular, skin, or vascular manifestations, or a positive pathergy test [6]. The management of vascular involvement also remains controversial: while immunosuppressive therapy is the cornerstone of treatment, the use of anticoagulants is debated because of the risk of fatal hemorrhage in patients with pulmonary artery aneurysms [7, 8].

The clinical significance of the present case lies in its atypical and life-threatening presentation. Our patient developed recurrent hemoptysis and bilateral PE as the first major manifestation of BD, in the absence of typical mucocutaneous features at the time of admission. This unusual course presented diagnostic challenges and raised important therapeutic considerations regarding the simultaneous use of immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapies. Reporting such cases is essential to increase clinical awareness, highlight the variability of pulmonary involvement in BD, and emphasize the importance of early recognition and individualized management strategies in young patients with unexplained PE.

This case report has been prepared according to the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines for case reports [9].

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man with no known past medical history was admitted to Golestan University of Medical Sciences Hospital (Gorgan, Iran) due to hemoptysis. The patient reported that approximately 40 days before admission, he had developed a productive cough with blood-streaked sputum, which had progressively worsened and culminated in nearly 200 mL of hemoptysis within the hours before hospitalization. He also reported recurrent episodes of fever, with documented temperatures reaching 38.5–39 °C, accompanied by chills over the past 11 months, which resulted in several hospital visits and admissions at different centers. During this period, he experienced additional systemic symptoms including anorexia, gradual weight loss, generalized weakness, night sweats, dyspnea, and right-sided pleuritic chest pain. He denied recent travel, contact with patients with suspected infection, gastrointestinal or urinary complaints, and psychiatric problems; however, he acknowledged consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. Family history was notable for pulmonary tuberculosis in his mother approximately ten years earlier.

On clinical examination, the patient appeared ill and febrile but remained alert and oriented. Pulmonary auscultation revealed decreased breath sounds on the right side with crackles in the lower lung field. Initial investigations, including complete blood count, coagulation profile, arterial blood gases, and chest radiography, were within normal limits. Due to persistent hemoptysis and recurrent fever, chest computed tomography (CT) and subsequently high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) were performed, both of which revealed no significant abnormalities. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was then carried out. Cytological and microbiological analyses of the BAL fluid were negative, and specific tests for tuberculosis, including acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear, Mycobacterium culture, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for M. tuberculosis, were all negative, significantly reducing the likelihood of active tuberculosis. In addition, autoimmune serologies, including C-ANCA, P-ANCA, and serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels, were all within normal limits. Subsequently, CT pulmonary angiography (CTA) was performed to evaluate possible vascular causes, which unexpectedly revealed bilateral pulmonary emboli (Fig. 1). The severity of radiological findings was disproportionate to the relatively mild clinical presentation, raising suspicion of an underlying vasculitis or systemic inflammatory disorder. Lower extremity Doppler ultrasonography was also performed and demonstrated no evidence of DVT, suggesting that the pulmonary emboli were likely due to in-situ thrombosis associated with vascular inflammation rather than embolic origin. Despite extensive infectious and rheumatologic work-up, no definitive etiology was identified at that stage, and the patient was started on intravenous heparin for anticoagulation.

CT pulmonary angiography demonstrating bilateral pulmonary emboli

Further evaluation by a rheumatologist revealed an old scar on the right side of the scrotum, a finding that the patient had not previously reported (Fig. 2). Additional history-taking also uncovered recurrent painful oral lesions, although they were not clinically typical aphthous ulcers (Fig. 3). Taken together, these findings raised suspicion of BD with vascular involvement. Immunologic investigations, including HLA-B51, ANA, C-ANCA, P-ANCA, anti-dsDNA, C3, and C4, were all negative, and the pathergy test was also negative. Ophthalmologic examination showed no evidence of ocular involvement.

Based on the constellation of clinical features, including recurrent hemoptysis, PE, genital scarring, and recurrent oral lesions, the diagnosis of BD with vascular involvement was established, and targeted therapy was initiated. The patient was initially treated with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, which resulted in rapid clinical improvement. However, due to the patient’s preference, physician discretion, and the better safety profile and more favorable long-term use of azathioprine in managing vascular Behçet’s disease, azathioprine was subsequently started as maintenance immunosuppressive therapy. Given the presence of pulmonary embolism and the absence of pulmonary artery aneurysm on imaging, a prophylactic course of anticoagulation with apixaban was added to prevent future thrombotic events, while the primary therapeutic focus remained on immunosuppression to control the underlying vasculitic process. During follow-up after discharge, the patient developed recurrent oral aphthous ulcers occurring approximately 4–5 times per year, along with folliculitis/pseudofolliculitis (Fig. 4) and a lesion consistent with erythema nodosum on the lower extremity (Fig. 5), further supporting the diagnosis of BD. Ocular involvement remained absent, and the pathergy test consistently yielded negative results.

Discussion and conclusions

BD is a chronic, relapsing vasculitis characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers together with skin and ocular manifestations. Vascular involvement is more frequent in young men and may include superficial and DVT, PE, pulmonary artery aneurysms, aortitis, and arterial thrombosis. Among these, PE is considered an uncommon but serious complication [3, 10, 11]. Pulmonary vascular involvement in BD can also manifest as pulmonary artery aneurysm, in-situ thrombosis, pulmonary hemorrhage, pulmonary infarction, and pleural effusion, all of which may present with nonspecific symptoms such as cough, fever, hemoptysis, pleuritic chest pain, and dyspnea [11, 12].

Pulmonary involvement in BD is an uncommon but clinically significant manifestation that can lead to life-threatening complications. Although pulmonary artery aneurysms are classically regarded as the hallmark pulmonary vascular lesion in Behçet’s disease, the spectrum of vascular involvement also includes in-situ pulmonary artery thrombosis and, more rarely, pulmonary embolism. Several reports have shown that in-situ thrombosis can occur even in the absence of pulmonary artery aneurysms [13, 14], and in some cases, it may represent the initial pulmonary presentation. Understanding this continuum, from local thrombosis to embolic phenomena, is essential for interpreting atypical respiratory manifestations in BD. Although venous thrombosis is frequently reported in BD, PE remains a rare presentation and is often under-recognized in clinical practice [15]. This rarity may delay diagnosis, particularly when classical mucocutaneous findings are absent or subtle at the time of presentation [16]. In our patient, recurrent hemoptysis and pleuritic chest pain were the predominant respiratory symptoms, which ultimately led to the detection of bilateral pulmonary emboli, a rare form of pulmonary involvement in BD. This atypical presentation, occurring in the absence of conventional thromboembolic risk factors, underscores the diagnostic challenge and highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for BD in young patients presenting with unexplained pulmonary embolism or hemoptysis, particularly in endemic regions such as the Middle East.

The diagnosis of BD is primarily clinical and relies on internationally accepted classification criteria. According to the International Study Group (ISG), recurrent oral aphthous ulceration, defined as at least three episodes within a 12-month period, is a diagnostic criterion. In addition, the presence of at least two of the following supports the classification: recurrent genital ulceration or scarring, ocular involvement such as uveitis or retinal vasculitis, characteristic skin lesions including erythema nodosum, papulopustular or pseudofollicular lesions, and a positive pathergy test. While the ISG criteria are valued for their specificity, alternative systems such as the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) offer higher sensitivity, making them useful in atypical cases [4, 17].

The choice of diagnostic modality is critical in patients with suspected PE, especially in the context of vasculitis. Although pulmonary angiography has traditionally been regarded as the gold standard, its invasive nature limits its use in many cases. Non-invasive imaging such as CTA has become the preferred tool due to its wide availability, high sensitivity, and acceptable specificity. In BD, CTA is particularly valuable because vascular obstruction may result from in-situ thrombosis rather than embolism, which complicates the interpretation of ventilation-perfusion scans [4, 7, 8, 18].

In our case, the diagnostic process was particularly challenging due to inconclusive laboratory findings. Autoimmune and inflammatory markers such as ANCA, ANA, ACE, and serologic tests, including AFB smear, culture, and PCR for M. tuberculosis, were all negative, making infectious etiologies, sarcoidosis, and other vasculitides less likely. Initial chest CT and subsequent HRCT also revealed no significant abnormalities, and bronchoscopy with BAL failed to identify an alternative cause for the patient’s hemoptysis. CTA later confirmed bilateral pulmonary emboli and additionally demonstrated mild pleural effusion and peripheral mass-like densities, findings that informed both diagnostic interpretation and therapeutic planning. Ultimately, the presence of a genital scar consistent with prior ulceration and a history of recurrent painful oral lesions emerged as decisive clinical clues, supporting the diagnosis of BD despite negative laboratory and immunologic markers. These subtle but characteristic mucocutaneous findings highlight the critical importance of thorough physical examination and repeated history-taking when evaluating atypical pulmonary presentations. Given the prominent pulmonary involvement, it was also important to differentiate the condition from Hughes–Stovin syndrome, as CTA not only helps identify pulmonary emboli but is also the key modality for detecting its characteristic pulmonary artery aneurysms [19, 20]. In our patient, the absence of any aneurysm or right ventricular thrombus on CTA made this diagnosis unlikely.

The management of vascular involvement in Behçet’s disease remains complex and often controversial, especially when pulmonary manifestations are present. Immunosuppressive therapy is universally recognized as the cornerstone of treatment, as vascular inflammation rather than embolic events underlie the majority of thrombotic complications in BD [5, 7, 21, 22]. Corticosteroids and steroid-sparing agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or interferon-α are commonly employed to suppress the underlying vasculitic process and prevent further vascular injury. In contrast, the role of anticoagulation remains debated. Although it may help prevent new thrombotic episodes, several reports and EULAR [23] recommendations caution against routine anticoagulation in BD, particularly when pulmonary artery aneurysms are present, due to the risk of fatal hemorrhage [6, 15]. In the present case, the therapeutic focus was on aggressive immunosuppression, which led to rapid improvement after high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by azathioprine maintenance. A prophylactic course of apixaban was added not as a primary therapy, but to reduce the risk of future thrombotic events, given the absence of pulmonary artery aneurysm on imaging. This individualized approach aligns with recent literature emphasizing that controlling vascular inflammation with immunosuppression is the key determinant of long-term outcomes, while anticoagulants should be used selectively and cautiously.

Our observations align with previous case reports describing PE as an unusual but severe manifestation of BD. Tajik Jalayeri et al. (2024) [24] reported a young male presenting with pleuritic chest pain and hemoptysis, later confirmed to have bilateral pulmonary emboli despite lacking traditional thromboembolic risk factors. Similarly, Tekantapeh et al. (2018) [21] described a patient with refractory BD who developed PE in the setting of DVT, highlighting the overlap of systemic and vascular manifestations. In both reports, as in our case, the diagnosis of BD was established only after careful clinical evaluation, and management required a combination of immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy. These cases collectively emphasize the importance of including BD in the differential diagnosis of unexplained PE in young patients, especially in endemic areas.

This case highlights several important clinical implications and provides valuable insights into the atypical vascular presentation of Behçet’s disease. BD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of young patients presenting with unexplained or disproportionate pulmonary embolism, particularly when routine risk factors are absent and no evidence of DVT is detected on Doppler ultrasonography. Such findings may indicate in-situ thrombosis secondary to vascular inflammation, a mechanism increasingly recognized in Behçet’s vasculitis. Our report further underscores the importance of repeated history-taking and meticulous physical examination, which ultimately revealed subtle mucocutaneous clues that were initially overlooked. This case also illustrates that timely initiation of immunosuppressive therapy remains the mainstay of treatment in vascular BD, effectively controlling inflammation and preventing progression, while anticoagulation should only be used selectively as an adjunct for secondary prophylaxis in the absence of pulmonary artery aneurysm. Overall, this case contributes a distinctive perspective by demonstrating that bilateral pulmonary embolism can be the first major clue leading to the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease, even before classical manifestations become evident. Recognizing this rare presentation is crucial for early diagnosis and for optimizing patient outcomes through individualized, immunosuppression-centered management.

Nevertheless, this case report has certain limitations. The diagnostic process was limited by the restricted availability of advanced laboratory facilities in our center, which necessitated the referral of samples to external laboratories and may have contributed to delays in evaluation. Furthermore, as a single case observation, the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with BD. Long-term follow-up data are also lacking, which would provide further insight into the natural course and treatment response of atypical pulmonary manifestations in BD.

In conclusion, our case illustrates that PE, although rare, can be a serious and even initial manifestation of BD, sometimes occurring before the appearance of classical mucocutaneous features. The diagnostic process may be challenging when laboratory and imaging findings are inconclusive; therefore, maintaining a high index of suspicion and relying on careful clinical evaluation are crucial. Early recognition of BD in young patients with unexplained PE is essential, as timely initiation of immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy can significantly improve outcomes and prevent potentially fatal complications.
