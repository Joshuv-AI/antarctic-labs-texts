Pan-cancer atlas analysis of FANCB and its potential mechanism in cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Youjie Fan, Jianhua Li, Haipeng Wang, Lei Yao, Xiaolei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) is a tumor with high malignancy and poor prognosis, and its morbidity and mortality have increased significantly in China. The current therapeutic methods have limited efficacy and are easy to be resistant to drugs, and the targeted and immunotherapy still need precise strategy support. FANCB, the core gene of Fanconi anemia (FA) pathway, is involved in DNA damage repair and genome stability maintenance, but its mechanism of action in solid tumors, especially cholangiocarcinoma, has not been clarified. In this study, pan-cancer analysis (TCGA, ICGC, GEO databases) found that FANCB was abnormally high in a variety of cancers and was significantly associated with poor prognosis (such as shortened OS/PFS). Pathway enrichment suggests that it may affect tumor progression by regulating DNA repair, immune response and fibrosis-related pathways. In this study, based on bioinformatics analysis, the pan-cancer map of FANCB and its potential mechanism in cholangiocarcinoma were mapped.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignant tumor of the biliary system with a poor prognosis [1]. It can be classified into intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [2–4]. Over the past three decades, the incidence and mortality rates of CCA in China have increased by 84% and 44% respectively [5]. The current treatment outcomes for CCA remains inadequate to meet clinical demands [6]. Surgical resection, as the sole potentially curative modality, is only applicable to a small cohort of patients with early-stage disease, while the postoperative recurrence rate remains considerably high [6].The gemcitabine-cisplatin combination therapy remains the standard first-line chemotherapy for advanced cholangiocarcinoma, yielding a median overall survival (mOS) of 11.7 months. Meanwhile, the emergence of drug resistance results in limited survival benefits. However, new hope for the precision treatment of CCA has been brought about by targeted therapy guided by molecular signature classification [7]. And the patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H) may derive benefits from such treatments according to the exploratory studies on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs, e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies) [8].

Tumor immune microenvironment (TME) plays a vital role in tumor initiation, progression, and treatment [9, 10]. The TME of CCA is characterized by a high degree of fibrosis. It contains abundant immune cells and fibroblasts, which interact through complex signaling pathways and form an environment favoring tumor growth [11]. For instance, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are a key component of TME. They interact closely with CCA and other stromal cells by releasing multiple pro-tumor factors, thus promoting tumor growth and immunosuppression [12]. Furthermore, TAMs also confer resistance to chemotherapy, which further accelerate tumor deterioration. Therefore, targeting TAMs may provide a new therapeutic approach for CCA. The application of ICIs in CCA has also attracted significant attention. Extensive immune cell infiltration is present in the TME of CCA, including lymphocytes expressing immune checkpoint receptors such as PD-1 and CTLA-4. It provides support for the efficacy of ICIs [13]. However, due to the high complexity and heterogeneity of the CCA TME, the efficacy of ICI monotherapy is limited. Identifying and targeting key factors in the TME through the therapeutic strategies combining targeting of different immune checkpoints will be more effective [14, 15]. In conclusion, a deep understanding of the CCA TME and its interactions with immune cells is crucial for developing more effective immunotherapeutic strategies.

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a rare hereditary disease caused by X-linked gene mutations or biallelic mutations in autosomal genes. It is related to diseases such as bone marrow failure, cancer, and developmental defects [16]. The development of the disease phenotype is generally attributed to defects in DNA repair and the consequent genomic instability [17]. FA core complex, consisting of multiple FA proteins (FANCA/B/C/E/F/G/L/M), is involved in cellular DNA damage repair and homologous recombination [18–20]. The protein encoded by FANCB is a key component of the FA core complex [21, 22]. Furthermore, FANCB can activate the downstream FANCD2/FANCI complex through ubiquitination modification, thereby recruiting nucleases (e.g., FAN1) and homologous recombination repair proteins (e.g., BRCA1/2) to complete DNA damage repair [23, 24]. However, the mechanism of FANCB in the initiation and progression of CCA and its role in the TME still require further investigation.

Materials and methods

Data download and processing

RNA sequencing data, somatic mutation profiles, phenotypic data, and relevant clinical datasets were retrieved from the Sangerbox database (http://vip.sangerbox.com/login.html) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). These databases encompass 33 cancer types. We also download cholangiocarcinoma Bulk RNA-seq dataset from the GEO database(number: GSE107943).

Expression analysis of FANCB

We assessed the expression levels of FANCB in 34 normal tissues, 21 tumor cell lines, and 34 tumor tissues using TCGA and the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx) cohort. The expression data were subjected to Log2 transformation and a two-group t-test was performed for significance between tumor and normal tissues (P < 0.05). Data analysis was conducted using R software, and box plots were generated with the R package “ggpubr”. Additionally, we analyzed the impact of FANCB expression on the overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in patients using the KM plotter database.

Analysis of FANCB mutations

Using the cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), we investigated the mutational profiles of FANCB across pan-cancer. Within the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas dataset, we selected mutations, structural variations, and inferred copy number alterations via the GISTIC module integrated in the platform. Following input of the gene symbol FANCB and submission of the query, we retrieved data regarding the types and frequencies of FANCB mutations across various cancer types, along with the prognostic impact of mutations in FANCB in patients with CCA.

TMB, MSI, MATH和LOH

The standardized TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset (N = 10,535, G = 60,499) were downloaded from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/), and the expression data for the gene ENSG00000181544 (FANCB) across all samples were further retrieved. Additionally, following sample screening the level 4 simple nucleotide variation datasets in TCGA were downloaded from GDC (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and processed using MuTect2 software [14].

The TMB for each tumor sample was calculated using the TMB function from the R package maftools (version 2.8.05). Subsequently, the TMB was integrated with gene expression data, and each expression value was further log 2(x + 0.001)-transformed. We excluded cancer types with fewer than 3 samples, resulting in expression data for 37 cancer types. In addition to TMB, we also downloaded data on MSI, mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH). The data were analyzed using the same methodology.

Functional enrichment analysis

The RNAseq data of TCGA_CHOL cohort on the HiSeq RNA platform were analyzed using LinkedOmics. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to evaluate the correlation between FANCB and its co-expressed genes. The Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted via LinkedOmics as well.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) enrichment analysis

GSEA was performed using HALLMARK pathways from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Pathway activity estimates were assigned to each gene by conducting Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) on each sample. The average gene expression levels for each sample were then calculated using the standard settings in the GSVA package.

Immune infiltration analysis

We explored the correlation between FANCB expression and the degree of immune cell infiltration using the TIMER 2.0 database. Additionally, we also analyzed stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, as well as their correlations with various tumor-infiltrating immune cells utilizing the Sangerbox website. To validate these findings, the CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to analyze immune cell infiltration.

FANCB survival analysis

Clinical information for each dataset was downloaded from the databases, and samples with incomplete survival time or survival status information were excluded. The predict() function was used to calculate the risk score for each patient, with the median value serving as the optimal cutoff to stratify patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. Subsequently, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the “survival” package in R to compare survival differences between the two groups.

Results

FANCB is upregulated in multiple cancer types

The uniformly standardized pan-cancer dataset: TCGA TARGET GTEx (PANCAN, N = 19131, G = 60499) were downloaded from the UCSC database. The expression data of the gene ENSG00000181544 (FANCB) were extracted from filtered samples based on their sources, encompassing normal solid tissues, primary solid tumors, primary tumors, normal tissues, primary blood-derived cancer-bone marrow, and primary blood-derived cancer-peripheral blood. Each expression value was further subjected to log2(x + 0.001) transformation. The expression data for 34 cancer types were finally obtained after excluding cancer types with fewer than 3 samples. The results showed that FANCB is upregulated in multiple cancer types, including bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), and cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) (Fig. 1A). Analysis of FANCB expression in TCGA dataset using the TIMER 2.0 database revealed that FANCB is upregulated in most cancer types (Fig. 1B), including BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), KIRC, kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), LUAD, LUSC, rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), THCA, and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Besides, Fig. 1C demonstrated that FANCB expression is significantly associated with the staging of adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), BRCA, KICH, KIRP, LIHC, and pan-kidney cohort (KIPAN). These results indicate that FANCB is upregulated in various cancer types and it may play a potential key role in cancer diagnosis.

Prognostic role of FANCB in pan-cancer

We analyzed the relationship between FANCB expression levels and tumor patient survival using the KM-plotter database. The results showed that high expression of FANCB was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in patients with BRCA (p = 0.049), ESCA (p = 0.0015), KIRC (p = 0.0028), KIRP (p = 6e-08), LUAD (p = 0.0088), LIHC (p = 9.2e-05), PDAC (p = 0.00061), SARC (p = 0.019), and UCEC (p = 0.0035). However, high expression of FANCB appeared to be a favorable prognostic factor in CESC (p = 0.0031), HNSC (p = 0.029), LUSC (p = 0.023), OV (p = 0.01), STAD (p = 0.038), and THYM (p = 0.016) (Fig. 2).

DFS is also one of the prognostic indicators for cancer patients. High expression of FANCB was associated with shorter DFS in patients with BRCA (p = 0.012), KIRP (p = 8.7e-07), LIHC (p = 0.00016), LUAD (p = 0.0016), PCPG (p = 0.012), PDAC (p = 0.00088), SARC (p = 0.029), THCA (p = 0.035), and UCEC (p = 0.042). Interestingly, in patients with CESC (p = 0.034), OV (p = 0.0094), STAD (p = 0.021), and TGCT (p = 0.007), high expression of FANCB may act as a protective factor, contributing to prolonged DFS (Fig. 3).

Relationship between FANCB expression and genomic heterogeneity

To investigated the relationship between FANCB expression and genomic heterogeneity, the correlations between FANCB expression and LOH, MSI, TMB and MATH in each tumor type were calculated and visualized respectively in Fig. 4. It was shown that FANCB expression was significantly positively correlated with LOH in 21 tumor types, including glioblastoma multiforme and lower-grade glioma (GBMLGG), lower-grade glioma (LGG), LUAD, COAD, BRCA, ESCA, stomach and esophageal carcinoma (STES), SARC, KIRP, KIPAN, prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), UCEC, HNSC, KIRC, LUSC, LIHC, mesothelioma (MESO), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), OV, uveal melanoma (UVM), and BLCA. As for the relationship between FANCB expression and MSI, a significant positive association was observed in 5 tumor types (LUAD, SARC, STAD, KIRC, and LUSC). In contrast, a significant negative association was detected in another 3 tumor types: GBMLGG, KIPAN, and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC). Furthermore, we found that the expression of FANCB was significantly positively correlated with TMB in 6 tumor types, including GBMLGG, LUAD, SARC, STAD, PRAD, and BLCA, while negatively correlated with KIRP. And the analysis of the correlation between FANCB expression and MATH demonstrated the significant correlations in 12 tumor types. Thereinto, 10 tumor types showed a significant positive correlation ( LUAD, COAD, BRCA, ESCA, STES, SARC, KIRP, STAD, LUSC, BLCA), and 2 tumor types showed a significant negative correlation (GBMLGG, LGG) (Fig. 4).

Evaluation of immune infiltration of FANCB

The stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores for each patient in each tumor type were calculated based on gene expression, and ultimately immune infiltration scores for 10,180 tumor samples across 44 tumor types were obtained. The significant correlations between FANCB and immune infiltration in each tumor type were identified according to Pearson correlation coefficient using the corr.test function from the R package Psych (Version 2.1.6). The significant correlations between FANCB expression and immune infiltration were observed in16 cancer types, among which 7 cancer types showed positive correlations, namely KIPAN (R = 0.28, P = 1.12e-7), KIRC (R = 0.20, P = 4.48e-06), GBMLGG (R = 0.14, P = 0.0001), THYM (R = 0.31, P = 0.0007), PAAD (R = 0.19, P = 0.007), and LIHC (R = 0.11, P = 0.035). The negative correlations were identified in 9 cancer types, including LUSC (R=-0.33, P = 9.58e-14), STES (R=-0.23, P = 1.17e-08), UCEC (R=-0.32, P = 1.22e-05), ESCA (R=-0.22, P = 0.002), TGCT (R=-0.24, P = 0.004), STAD (R=-0.14, P = 0.005), CESC (R=-0.14, P = 0.005), HNSC (R=-0.12, P = 0.008), and SKCM-P (R=-0.21, P = 0.0396). The relationship between FANCB and immune stimulating and inhibitory genes were further analyzed. It was found that FANCB showed positive correlation with most inhibitory genes, such as CD274, CTLA4, and TIGIT. This suggests that high FANCB expression enhances the inhibitory state of the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 6C).

Drug sensitivity analysis showed that high FANCB expression might be associated with potential sensitivity to gefitinib, lapatinib, ceritinib, and trametinib (Fig. 7A). We further analyzed the function of FANCB in CHOL using the LinkedOmics database. The top 50 positively and negatively correlated genes were shown in the Fig. 8A-C. Besides, the GO pathway enrichment analysis indicated that FANCB might be mainly involved in chromosome segregation, DNA replication, microtubule organizing center assembly, spindle formation, cell cycle checkpoint, double-stranded DNA break repair, negative regulation of cell cycle, cytokines, and DNA recombination (Fig. 8D). And fanconi anemia pathway, cell cycle, homologous recombination, DNA replication, oocyte meiosis, and mismatch repair were significantly enriched in KEGG pathway (Fig. 8E).

To validate the analytical results of FANCB in cholangiocarcinoma

Based on prior pan-cancer analysis, which indicated that high FANCB expression is associated with poor tumor prognosis, we focused specifically on its expression pattern in cholangiocarcinoma. We detected the expression of FANCB in human immortalized normal bile duct epithelial cells HiBEpiC and human bile duct cancer cell lines QBC939, HuCCT1, RBE, HCCC-9810. The results showed that FANCB was significantly overexpressed in these cancer cells. (Fig. 9A)

To validate these findings, we downloaded a cholangiocarcinoma dataset for in-depth analysis. First, differential expression analysis between normal and tumor tissues revealed significantly elevated FANCB expression in tumor tissues, as visually represented in volcano and heatmaps (Fig. 9B-C). Further investigation into the clinical prognostic impact of FANCB demonstrated a significant positive correlation between its expression level and the clinical parameter SUVmax(Fig. 9D-E). Elevated FANCB expression was accompanied by increased SUVmax values, suggesting its potential value as a prognostic predictor. To test this hypothesis, we performed survival analysis, which confirmed that high FANCB expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival, supporting its role as a potential prognostic biomarker.Subsequently, we employed GSVA analysis using the Hallmark database to explore FANCB-associated signaling pathways. Results showed significant enrichment in HALLMARK_MTORC1_SIGNALING, HALLMARK_MITOTIC_SPINDLE, HALLMARK_G2M_CHECKPOINT, and HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS pathways (Fig. 9G). These pathways are primarily involved in cell cycle regulation, including key biological processes such as cell growth, division, and metabolism. This suggests that high FANCB expression may contribute to cholangiocarcinoma pathogenesis through modulation of these pathways, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target.

Finally, to systematically characterize the immune microenvironment in cholangiocarcinoma, we analyzed immune cell infiltration using CIBERSORT(Fig. 9F). The results revealed substantial infiltration of immune cells including Macrophages M1, Dendritic cells, Mast cells, activated CD4 + memory T cells, and NK cells in tumor tissues, consistent with previous findings. This indicates that these immune cell types may represent potential targets for immunotherapy.

Discussion

The abnormally high expression of FANCB was observed in various types of cancer, which was associated with tumor stage. And we also found that the elevated expression of FANCB was related to poorer OS in patients with BRCA, CESC, ESCA, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, LIHC, and HNSC, as well as poorer DFS in BRCA, LIHC, SARC, TGCT, PDAC, and LUAD. Furthermore, mutation analysis showed that the expression of FANCB in LUAD, BRCA, BLCA, and SARC was positively correlated with TMB, MSI, and LOH. And in CHOL, the survival of patients was affected by FANCB mutation status, with the FANCB-mutant subgroup exhibiting significantly reduced PFS and DFS compared to the wild-type subgroup.

Based on these findings, we propose that FANCB holds significant research value in cholangiocarcinoma. Through differential expression analysis, survival analysis, and clinical correlation studies, we confirmed that high FANCB expression is significantly associated with poor patient prognosis, and its expression level shows a positive correlation with increased SUVmax values. PET/CT serves as a primary modality for evaluating potentially resectable esophageal cancer, with its parameter SUVmax (maximum standardized uptake value) effectively reflecting metabolic activity within tumors. Previous studies have established that most malignant tumors exhibit SUVmax values greater than 2.5 [25, 26], and multiple investigations, including a meta-analysis [27], have demonstrated that elevated SUVmax is associated with more aggressive tumor behavior and poorer survival outcomes [28, 29]. Our results further confirm that high FANCB expression correlates with increased tumor malignancy, suggesting its potential role as a driver of tumor progression.

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of FANCB, we performed GSVA enrichment analysis. The results revealed significant enrichment of FANCB in key signaling pathways including HALLMARK_MTORC1_SIGNALING, HALLMARK_MITOTIC_SPINDLE, HALLMARK_G2M_CHECKPOINT, and HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS.

The mTOR signaling pathway represents an evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine protein kinase cascade comprising two distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2, based on functional subunit differences [30]. mTORC1, through its downstream effectors p70S6K and 4E-BP1, regulates protein translation and ribosome biogenesis, playing a central role in energy metabolism and cell growth regulation [31, 32].

The G2/M checkpoint pathway constitutes a crucial regulatory mechanism for cell cycle progression, specifically governing the transition from G2 phase to mitosis. This checkpoint ensures complete and accurate DNA replication while repairing any DNA damage or replication errors before cells enter mitosis [33]. Dysregulation or functional impairment of the G2/M checkpoint pathway significantly contributes to cancer development and progression [34]. In normal cells, this pathway involves multiple key proteins and signaling cascades that coordinately monitor DNA integrity and cell cycle progression [35]. The G2/M checkpoint pathway has been implicated in various cancers including melanoma, breast cancer, gastric cancer, and pancreatic cancer. Mutations or functional alterations in genes within this pathway can disrupt its normal regulatory capacity [36–39].

E2F has emerged as a master transcriptional regulator of cell cycle-dependent gene expression. Defined by E2F target gene expression patterns, E2F activity is elevated in virtually all cancers, typically attributable to inactivation of its primary binding partner and key regulator RB (encoded by RB1), overexpression of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), or loss of CDK inhibitors [40, 41].

The TME consists of tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells, serving a pivotal function in tumor initiation and progression. To investigate the role of FANCB in regulating tumor immune infiltration, we calculated the scores of stromal, immune score, and ESTIMATE. Our results demonstrated that FANCB expression was positively correlated with these scores in CHOL, KIRP, GBMLGG, and KIRC, but showed a negative correlation with the scores in UCEC, CESC, and LUSC.

Furthermore, the analysis of the correlation between FANCB and various immune cell types across pan-cancer revealed that FANCB was positively correlated with NK cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, while negatively correlated with CD8 + T cells. Finally, we explored the function of FANCB in CCA and found that it was associated with multiple signaling pathways, such as the cell cycle, DNA replication, and DNA damage repair pathways.

In summary, our study elucidates the important role of FANCB in cholangiocarcinoma through multiple dimensions including clinical correlations, prognostic value, and molecular mechanisms. FANCB likely promotes tumor cell proliferation by regulating cell cycle-related pathways, thereby driving malignant progression, highlighting its potential value as a therapeutic target for translational investigation.This study provides a solid theoretical foundation for the role of FANCB in cholangiocarcinoma through systematic bioinformatic analysis, yet certain limitations remain. The current research does not include experimental validation of FANCB’s specific molecular mechanisms, which somewhat constrains the generalizability and translational potential of our findings. In future work, we plan to design and conduct both in vivo and in vitro functional experiments to further verify FANCB’s biological functions and underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

FANCB is highly expressed in various types of cancer. It is associated with tumor prognosis as well. Furthermore, high expression level of FANCB indicates a poor prognosis. FANCB can promote the establishment of a tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and is involved in multiple pathways in CCA.
