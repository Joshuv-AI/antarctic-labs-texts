Abstract
. Interface agents powered by generative AI models (referred to as “agents”) can automate actions based on user commands. An important aspect of developing agents is their user experience (i.e., agent experience). There is a growing need to provide scaffolds for a broader set of individuals beyond AI engineers to prototype agent experiences, since they can contribute valuable perspectives to designing agent experiences. In this work, we explore the affordances agent prototyping systems should offer by conducting a requirements elicitation study with 12 participants with varying experience with agents. We identify key activities in agent experience prototyping and the desired capabilities of agent prototyping systems. We instantiate those capabilities in the AgentBuilder design probe for agent prototyping. We conduct an in situ agent prototyping study with 14 participants using AgentBuilder to validate the design requirements and elicit insights on how developers prototype agents and what their needs are in this process.

1.
Introduction

Mei is an interaction designer.
She is hired by a coffee shop to deliver a new AI agent experience on their website to take customers’ commands in natural language and execute the tasks by navigating through the website.
As Mei brainstorms what the user experience with the agent should be like, she is not sure whether the agent should frequently ask for user input, perform the task and send a summary at the end, or something in between.
Mei wants to test these ideas, but she does not have a formal background in AI or software development. She wishes there was some kind of scaffold for her to easily prototype these experiences.

Mei wants to develop an
interface agent
, which is an AI that automates actions in user interfaces by understanding user habits, preferences, and interests
(
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
;
Lieberman 1997
)
.
In recent years, multimodal and vanilla large language models (i.e., (M)LLMs) have rapidly emerged to power interface agents
(
Liang et al. 2025a
;
Fourney et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
;
Iong et al. 2024
)
, which we refer to in this work as
agents
.
Agents are powered by generative AI models that can consider a user’s intent or instructions and complete a task on a UI environment (i.e., desktop
(
Fourney et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
, mobile
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, web
(
He et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Pan et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Iong et al. 2024
)
; specific applications such as Excel
(
Liang et al. 2025a
)
and code IDEs
(
Pu et al. 2025
)
; etc.), by calling upon external modules ranging from code executors
(
Fourney et al. 2024
)
to clicking on a UI element
(
Huq et al. 2025
;
Iong et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
)
.

Since developing agents typically requires AI engineering expertise and knowledge of specialized APIs automating application actions
(
Lieberman and Selker 2003
;
Lieberman 1997
)
, prototyping agents tailored to applications such as Mei’s has traditionally been limited to agent experts, such as machine learning researchers.
However, as illustrated by Mei’s experience, an important aspect of agent development is designing the user experience
(
Horvitz 1999
;
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
;
Schiaffino and Amandi 2004
)
, which we refer to as
agent experience
.
There is a growing need for a broader set of individuals like Mei, such as traditional software engineers, UX designers, product managers, or even end-users—who are not agent experts and lack the knowledge to program agents—to design and prototype agent experiences.
Prior studies suggest that, when supported by proper prototyping scaffolds
(
Subramonyam et al. 2025
;
Petridis et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2019
)
, these individuals can contribute valuable perspectives to developing well-designed agent experience, bridging design, machine learning, and engineering
(
Yang et al. 2020
;
Dove et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Kayacik et al. 2019
;
Subramonyam et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
.
Therefore, there needs to be scaffolds that enable a broader range of individuals to design and prototype agent experience. This is especially critical in the face of the increasing adoption of agents performing actions that result in real-world consequences (e.g., modifying user files, placing online orders)
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, where incorporating input from stakeholders beyond agent experts, like UX designers, could reduce the negative impacts of agents in practice.

In this work, we explore the affordances that an agent prototyping system should offer to effectively scaffold the design of agent experiences. We envision such a system as empowering not only agent experts, but also a broader group of people to prototype agent experiences—together whom we refer to as
developers
of agent experiences.
We provide an overview of the study in Figure
1
.
To this end, we first conduct a requirements elicitation study with 12 participants with various levels of experience with agents. We discover that prototyping agent experiences consists of two experimental and highly iterative phases of designing and inspecting the agent, which are further divided into five distinct
Activities
and six
Desired Capabilities
that an agent prototyping system should afford.
We instantiate the Activities and Desired Capabilities in a design probe, AgentBuilder, a graphical, no-code tool designed to support prototyping agent experiences. Using AgentBuilder, we run an
in situ
agent prototyping study with 14 participants to validate the design requirements and further understand the prototyping process and tooling needs of developers, including the need for mechanisms that help developers make sense of various forms of agent input, context, and outputs during execution, as well as to better understand the agent’s technical implementation. Based on these findings, we propose a set of design recommendations for agent prototyping systems.

In summary, the contributions of this work are:

•

A set of validated design requirements for agent prototyping systems
, articulated as five prototying activities and six capabilities that agent prototyping systems should support.

•

AgentBuilder, a design probe that instantiates the design requirements
, enabling individuals with varying levels of familiarity with agents to prototype agent experiences through a no-code interface.

•

Insights on the needs and process of agent prototyping
based on an
in situ
user study of AgentBuilder.

This work offers a basis for prototyping agent experiences, with the aim of informing future interfaces to support this activity.
Our work also serves as a step towards democratizing how AI can be designed and adapted to match user needs by empowering a range of individuals, from domain experts to non-programmers and end-users, to participate in its design
(
Esposito et al. 2023
;
Fischer 2021
)
.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Interface Agents

Building and designing “agents” has been studied extensively in HCI
(
Lieberman 1997
;
Lieberman and Selker 2003
)
. Such agents assist users with tasks at varying levels of autonomy, interacting with specific applications
(
Kozierok and Maes 1993
)
and interfaces like the web
(
Lieberman 1995
)
, and can learn from user preferences
(
Maes and Kozierok 1993
)
.
Among this work, interface agents were developed for specific applications, such as for email or calendar applications
(
Maes and Kozierok 1993
;
Kozierok and Maes 1993
)
by leveraging exposed application APIs
(
Lieberman and Selker 2003
;
Lieberman 1997
)
.
Early design considerations of interface agents included how to provide users with sufficient understanding on the agent’s actions, deciding when users should assume control over tasks, how to represent the agent in the UI, the modalities the agent can accept, and determining what types of support to provide to the user
(
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
;
Schiaffino and Amandi 2004
)
.
In mixed-initiative settings, researchers have enumerated multiple important design considerations, such as understanding the role of users in the mixed-initiative interface, deciding when users should provide input, adding agent controls, and determining the scope of services
(
Allen et al. 1999
;
Horvitz 1999
)
.

With the recent development of (M)LLMs, interface agents powered by these models can complete actions in specific applications (e.g., Excel
(
Liang et al. 2025a
)
) as well as more general environments like the websites
(
He et al. 2024
;
Huq et al. 2025
)
, mobile UIs
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, and computer operating systems
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
by semantically understanding user input.
One of the most prominent types of interface agents powered by generative AI is web agents—agents for web browsers that can perform UI actions such as clicking on buttons.
Much of the existing research on web agents focuses on developing new generative AI agentic approaches
(
He et al. 2024
;
Cai et al. 2025
)
, benchmarks
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Pan et al. 2024
;
Cai et al. 2025
)
, and evaluation environments
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Pan et al. 2024
;
Huq et al. 2025
)
to improve web agent performance.
Few works, however, consider the design of collaborative human-agent workflows.
To our knowledge, the only collaborative web agent is Cowpilot
(
Huq et al. 2025
)
, a Chrome extension that allows users to supervise and intervene in web agent tasks, providing a pause before all agent actions.

In this work, we extend the early and current literature on interface agent design considerations by understanding which aspects of user experience are accounted for in prototyping with (M)LLM-based agent experiences.

2.2.
Designing for User Experience in Generative AI

Designing for user experience is crucial in designing generative AI technologies, yet remains a significant challenge.

Yang et al. 2020
found the UX design challenges of AI come from two sources:
capability uncertainty
(i.e., the functionality and potential errors of an AI) and
output complexity
(i.e., the space of what the AI produces as a possible output).
With the UX design of generative AI experiences, similar challenges remain.
Capability uncertainty persists, since stakeholders of generative AI experiences are often unaware or have unrealistic expectations of generative AI capabilities
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Dolata et al. 2024
)
.
Further, the output complexity of generative AI is more pronounced, as the output space of generative AI can be any text, image, or more, depending on the model, creating challenges in managing or understanding generative AI outputs
(
Gero et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
.
In response to these new challenges with generative AI, researchers have proposed design guidelines for generative AI experiences, such as suggesting ideas for prompting and generating multiple outputs
(
Weisz et al. 2024
;
Subramonyam et al. 2024
)
.

Crucial to designing AI user experiences is prototyping, which requires a range of expertise in design, engineering, and machine learning
(
Yang et al. 2020
;
Dove et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Kayacik et al. 2019
)
.
Researchers have developed techniques to help prototype user experience in generative AI, allowing designers and developers to test existing components representing different aspects of the experience.
For example,
one promising approach is toolkits to develop speech UIs, such as Suede
(
Klemmer et al. 2000
)
and CSLU
(
Sutton and Cole 1997
)
, which enables designers to prototype dialogues by composing pre-defined components that were then tested with a Wizard-Of-Oz protocol. PromptInfuser
(
Petridis et al. 2024
)
is the only known tool that allows UX designers of generative AI to prototype both UI and prompts at the same time. Our work extends this line of research by exploring opportunities and directions for supporting UX prototyping in agents. Inspired by approaches that provide users with existing components, we envision combining these with prompt development techniques in agents, which we discuss in the next section.

2.3.
Developing Prompts for Generative AI Experiences

Developing agent experiences, like other generative AI applications (e.g., chatbots), largely involves writing natural language prompts that are embedded in the application
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Subramonyam et al. 2025
)
.
Prompt development is highly iterative and exploratory
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Dolata et al. 2024
)
, where developers prototype and refine their prompt through many interactions with the AI, which in turn drives the requirements and the content within the prompt
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Liang et al. 2025c
)
.
As a result, it is important for developers to decide what content to include in a prompt
(
Liang et al. 2025c
)
.
However, this task is a major challenge for developers
(
Subramonyam et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2025c
)
, especially in the beginning, when they are unaware of the AI’s capabilities for the task
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
In the specific case of prompting for user experience,
Subramonyam et al. 2025
found that the prototyping process of generative AI experiences required rapid exploration of prompts and consideration of UI components the AI would use to interact with a user.
To support the exploratory iteration in prompting, several tools enable natural language composition support to design, refine, and evaluate prompts for LLMs
(
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Arawjo et al. 2024
;
Joshi et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
.
Another common approach is node-based approaches for prompt prototyping (e.g.,
(
Wu et al. 2022
;
Arawjo et al. 2024
;
Hao et al. 2025
)
).
One such example is ChainForge
(
Arawjo et al. 2024
)
, a tool that enables rapid prototyping of prompting pipelines by creating nodes for user-defined data, metrics, and visualizations that users can chain together.

Meanwhile, agent development is a subset of prompt development that contains its own unique challenges.
However, this topic remains understudied.
To our knowledge, the only studies are
Epperson et al. 2025
’s and
Hao et al. 2025
’s formative studies on developers of multi-agent workflows.
While some challenges align with those of developing LLM prompts, like a lack of debugging support
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Liang et al. 2025c
)
, other challenges, such as wrangling long agent conversations and managing a large design space of how to decompose task, define agent interactions, assign agent responsibility, and configure individual agent behavior, are unique to this setting.
To assist with agent development, FlowForge
(
Hao et al. 2025
)
uses a node-based interface to scaffold the process of defining, viewing, and editing agentic workflows.

In this work, we extend the literature on prompting, including
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
’s study on non-AI expert prompting, by exploring how non-experts design prompts for agent experiences and how we can support such prompting needs using no-code scaffolds.

3.
Requirements Elicitation Study

To better understand prototyping agent experiences and to identify opportunities for tools to help, we conducted a requirements elicitation study with 12 participants at a large technology company.
We ask the following research questions:

RQ1:

What are the important activities that developers engage in while prototyping agent user experiences?

RQ2:

What capabilities should a system afford to support agent experience prototyping?

We recruited participants with a wide variety of experience, ranging from participants with relevant domain expertise (experts) to participants who had only passively used, seen, or had no familiarity with agents (non-experts).
We asked expert participants about their relevant experiences, probed non-expert participants for the agentic user experience using a mock agent, and elicited feedback while showing existing agent designs for all participants.
We were particularly interested in scenarios where already-built agents needed to be adapted to applications that require a specific user experience.

3.1.
Methodology

3.1.1.
Participants

To recruit study participants, we posted recruitment messages on public message boards at a large technology company for design and machine learning audiences, where 77 individuals expressed interest in the study by filling out a survey.
From this list, we invited 12 participants (5 women, 7 men) who covered varying familiarity with agents, spanning technology professionals with relevant domain expertise (
N=6
, including 2 machine learning engineers, 1 software engineer, 2 quality assurance engineers, and 1 product designer), current end-users (
N=5
), and individuals having no familiarity (
N=1
).

3.1.2.
Protocol

The user study contained two phases.
First, we sought to understand experiences with interface agents generally.
For participants with relevant expertise, we conducted a 30-minute interview, where they shared an experience developing an agent or a similar artifact.
They were prompted to discuss the different UX factors they considered, the process of developing the agent, and any challenges they faced.
For non-expert participants, to familiarize them with the interaction with an agent, we presented a mock interface agent and had them interact with it to complete 2 tasks (searching for and booking travel accommodations) and then probed for their experiences.
We did not collect data from non-expert participants in this part of the study.

Then, for all participants, we presented a slide deck showing examples of agent experience patterns from several existing agents, many of which appeared with a chat panel and within a webpage environment.
Each slide contained screenshots of examples that focused on a single part of the interaction, starting from when a user inputs a query, to when an agent executed the action on a UI.
After presenting each example, participants discussed what features they would like the agent to have across various scenarios.

Sessions were conducted remotely over a videoconferencing platform and lasted between 60 and 90 minutes (for experts and non-experts, respectively).
Sessions were recorded and transcribed for analysis.
Participants were compensated with a $24 meal voucher.

3.1.3.
Analysis

The study team qualitatively analyzed the interview transcripts, focusing on the various activities during agent prototyping through provisional coding
(
Saldaña 2009
)
, where the research team developed an initial list of codes through preparatory investigation activities such as synthesizing prior literature, results from previous studies, and the researcher’s prior experience. We refined the initial list during qualitative analysis
(
Saldaña 2009
)
that focused on achieving coder consensus, which is appropriate for research aiming to yield important concepts and themes
(
McDonald et al. 2019
)
.
These codes were modified by codes that emerged from interview transcripts as part of the provisional coding process, performed by the author who conducted the interview sessions.
We achieved code saturation after conducting 10 interviews.
The saturated codes were then collapsed, reorganized, and grouped into salient Activities using axial coding
(
Saldaña 2009
;
Corbin and Strauss 2015
)
.
The Activities were further organized into higher-order phases of agent prototyping using theoretical coding
(
Saldaña 2009
)
.
The codes, Activities, and phases of agent prototyping were then reviewed by one of the researchers who helped develop the initial set of codes, following the validation practice in settings with a single coder
(
Saldaña 2009
)
.

3.2.
Results

We find that the agent prototyping process involves two main phases:
Designing the Agent
and
Inspecting the Agent
. These phases are highly iterative and experimental, reflecting patterns found in machine learning workflows
(
Patel et al. 2008
;
Amershi et al. 2019a
)
, like developing LLM prompts
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
Within each phase, there are key
Activities
(labeled “A”) involved in agent prototyping (
RQ1
), which we explain in detail below with
thematic codes
that emerged from our data.
We also derived a set of
Desired Capabilities
(labeled “C”) for a system that could support agent prototyping (
RQ2
) by combining study findings with the literature on agent design, detailed in the corresponding Activities.

3.2.1.
Designing the Agent

Participants from different backgrounds and levels of technical expertise all expressed the need to prototype agents.
They noted that it was difficult to prototype designs due to the engineering challenges of building even a single agent (EP1-EP6), reflecting prior work
(
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Iong et al. 2024
)
.
For example, EP2 noted that
“to build [an agent] demo, you need to string a lot of things together…It’s integration pains with many components. Sometimes you do not have the components you need”
(EP2).
To support prototyping activities for users of varied skill levels, we derive the first Desired Capability:
C1. Use no-code interfaces
.

GUI-based prototyping tools for agent experiences could reduce the engineering challenges associated with prototyping agent experiences, as prompting and no-code interfaces have done for developing LLM applications
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Subramonyam et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Fiannaca et al. 2023
;
Mishra et al. 2025
)
.

We identified three main Activities in agent design: Design the scope and boundaries of the agent (
A1
), Design the agent’s information display (
A2
), and Design the interactions between the agent and user (
A3
).

A1. Design the scope and boundaries of the agent

When designing agent experiences, developers often first consider the high-level activities, actions, and inputs the agent is intended to handle.
This was important for designing
“guardrails”
(EP10) for the agent:
“You’re making this promise for your customers, so you have to respect [and] protect [them]”
(EP4).
We identified two common ways of scoping presented in interviews.
The first was selecting the
capabilities of the agent
—the types of tasks it is expected to execute—which could range from basic actions like
“search”
(EP8), to higher-risk ones like
“payments”
(EP8),
“delet[ing] photos”
(EP4), entering
“passwords”
(EP8, 12), and
“shar[ing] documents”
(EP4).
In addition to actions, scoping the agent’s capabilities also included the applications the agent could use, like having
“access right to [the] calendar”
(EP10).

Another important aspect of scope that developers would consider was the agent’s
knowledge about the user
.
This included
“personal details”
(EP6), what the user
“likes [and]…want[s]”
(EP9), and the activity history such as
“most frequently referenced [concepts]”
(EP5).
Developers used this information to
“build personalized experiences”
(EP2) and reduce user effort:
“[For] information about myself…I don’t want to repeat over and over…it’s tiresome”
(EP10).
Participants acknowledged that including a user’s context could expand an agent’s ability to complete tasks on their behalf, but posed risks to the user’s privacy, especially in settings where the agent interacts with the outside world.
For example, EP4 discussed how agents operating on messaging applications should have limited access to hidden chats.

From these needs, we derive:

C2. Help constrain the agent’s task space and knowledge of the user.

Prototyping tools should let developers set boundaries for the tasks an agent can perform, and the user information it has access to.
This could help constrain the large design space and put the potential risks that agents pose at the forefront of the design process
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.

A2. Design the agent’s information display

Developers also need to consider how the agent appears on the screen, including what
information
was displayed, such as exposing details of the tools an agent uses to complete a users’s task (e.g., clicking a UI element or invoking an application API),
“a small description of [the action]”
(EP3), or a summary of multiple actions.
The information should be designed at the correct granularity, as too much information could easily
“overwhelm [users] with unnecessary information”
(EP11).
For example, EP11 commented that the information presented was important for tasks such as decision-making:
“The user experience should be such that [the confirmation] gives me all the information I need as a user to make a decision”
(EP11).

In addition to what information to show about the agents’ activities, agent developers also need to decide on its
presentation
. This includes how it should appear when interacting with the user (e.g., in a chat window) and when executing tasks in an application interface
(
Pu et al. 2025
)
.
The UI components that dictate how an agent solicits user inputs also need to be considered:
“How is a user gonna input those parameters? Is it going to be a text box…[or] multiple text fields?”
(EP6).
Designing how an agent’s actions are represented to the user is also important. Participants discussed how actions could be
“invisible”
(EP9) or be revealed by highlighting the UI elements, e.g.,
“drawing a square around [the element] on the screen”
(EP1).
These findings are corroborated by several works which argue how presentation is an important consideration of designing agents
(
Schiaffino and Amandi 2004
;
Lieberman and Selker 2003
;
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
)
, as it can affect users’ perceptions and trust
(
Dehn and Van Mulken 2000
)
.

Therefore we derive:

C3. Define the UI of the agent in the chat and the environment.

Users should be able to control what
information
is shown and modify its
presentation
, i.e., in the chat and on the application interface.

A3. Design the interactions between the agent and user

Participants universally desired tools for supporting and constraining specific user interactions, which EP6 felt was important to design for:
“When you’re trying to turn [LLMs] into an experience that isn’t directly conversing with a model, there’s still a lot of boundaries that need to be established. I feel like the work of a designer…is, what are the boundaries of the interaction?”
(EP6).
One need pointed out by participants was to identify what
user inputs
could be needed during interactions, such as requesting confirmation for actions with impacts or
“prompting”
(EP3) the user through a
“conversational interaction”
(EP6) if it encountered errors.
This motivates the need to curate
interaction components
provided to the agent that enable it to interact with the user, and to establish
conditions
for the agent to invoke them:

“Sometimes, we as a human also make decisions about when to intervene [with] the agent”
(EP3).
Interaction components may be implemented as “tools” provided to the model
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, but are distinct from tools that ground actions into the UI because they mediate interactions with the user and support more collaborative workflows with agents.

In addition to designing
how
the user and agent communicate information during the task, another common theme was controlling proactivity, or
when
the agent initiated communication. EP2 described a
“slider of…autonomy”
(EP2), ranging from being supervised or collaborative to fully autonomous, depending on factors such as user trust in the system or how open-ended the task is:
“There are two paradigms: either you want… the model to handle everything, or you want to be the driver…and the model just does things”
(EP4).
It is challenging for developers to design the right level of autonomy of the agent, as
“everybody wants something different”
(EP10):
“There’s a challenge between being so obnoxious about [asking for input]…but at least it lets you know, ‘Hey, I’ve encountered an issue”’
(EP10).

We therefore derive the requirement:

C4. Provide components that enable the agent to invoke different user interactions.

Our findings echo early design principles for agents
(
Schiaffino and Amandi 2004
;
Horvitz 1999
;
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
)
, suggesting that developers should be provided affordances to coordinate the tools that the agent uses to design the experience with the agent, including which
interaction components
the agent calls and the
conditions
under which they are invoked.

3.2.2.
Inspecting the Agent

Even sophisticated agents are known exhibit error-prone behaviors in practice, such as performing the wrong action (EP1, EP3).
After prototyping agents, developers need to run the agent to validate the agent experience.

A4. Run the agent prototype

Our participants described the need to run prototypes in a test environment to
“get a realistic idea of what our models do”
(EP6) with the prompt.
Agents could be assessed through
programmatic testing
, e.g., in benchmarks using online, live environments
(
Xie et al. 2024
;
Koh et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
)
, or offline settings (i.e., a static set of screenshots)
(
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Experienced developers of agents with such evaluations used software tools, e.g.,
“command line instructions”
(EP5).
Participants also stressed the importance of
manual testing
(EP1, EP3, EP5) by
“send[ing the agent] straight to the device”
(EP5) and observing it execute actions.
For developing agents, both forms of evaluation were used:
“We start with manual [testing] but now we automate [with] tests…We have checkpoints that validate that if those checkpoints were reached [by the agent] or not”
(EP3).

Participants also described the need for
agent controls
such as
“pause”
(EP1, EP7),
“interrupt”
(EP1, EP2, EP4),
“cancel”
(EP7, EP8), or
“continue”
(EP8) commands.

A prototyping system for agent experience must:
C5. Provide an environment to run and control the agent.

Users should be able to run their agent prototype, just as LLM prompts need to be run for one to observe and understand the prompt prototype’s behavior
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
Such systems should at minimum allow for
manual testing
of the agent, emphasized in the development of generative AI experiences
(
Liang et al. 2025b
)
.
In addition, these systems should also enable agent global controls to steer the agent
(
Liang et al. 2025a
)
.

A5. Understand the agent’s runtime behavior

While prototyping agents, it is important to understand the agent’s runtime behavior, especially during debugging (EP3).
Participants with relevant domain expertise reasoned about both the agent’s
input context and outputs
as well as the
UI state
.
This introduced complexity compared to LLMs with text-only inputs and outputs.
Thus, the developers considered both visual representations of the UI—like screenshots
(
He et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
and
“recordings of the navigation that’s happening”
(EP1)—as well as textual representations—like HTML and accessibility trees
(
He et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
—since
“how [UI elements] look to [humans] is not exactly the same as how they look to the agent”
(EP1).
For UI support, EP5 used tools that gave an
“x-ray view into the [UI] hierarchy…when we want stuff as transparently as possible”
(EP5).

We derive that prototyping systems should:
C6. Help the user debug the agent’s runtime behavior.

Users should be able to have information on what the agent was observing and what output it generated to understand the behavior of the agent.
Literature also suggests providing sufficient information is essential for debugging prompts
(
Liang et al. 2025c
)
, which is known to be challenging activity in prompt prototyping
(
Epperson et al. 2025
;
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.

4.2.
Implementation

AgentBuilder is implemented as two TypeScript React applications. The
Prototyping
interface is hosted as a web application, and the
Execution
interface is a modified version of the CowPilot Chrome browser extension
(
Huq et al. 2025
)
.

The
Prototyping
interface uses the ReactFlow library for the graphical editor. To generate a prompt from the graph, the workflow is searched depth-first from the
Start
node to convert paths into a text representation. These are fed into an LLM which expands this into a detailed description of the workflow. To propagate edits to the exported prompt back into the workflow graph, the updated prompt and JSON representation of the workflow are input to an LLM, and it is prompted to update the JSON, which re-instantiates the workflow.

The
Execution
interface reuses CowPilot’s underlying agent and its agent controls. We added significant configurability to CowPilot—adding natural language descriptions of UI actions, making the UI action preview in the webpage configurable, and adding debugging agent outputs. We also added the Interaction Components, implemented as tool prompts.

RQ3:

How do developers prototype agents?

5.1.2.
Protocol

Before the study, participants completed a brief demographic survey.
Sessions began with a 30-minute onboarding sesesion, which included defining agents with examples, a demo of a baseline agent which accepts a user’s query and attempts to execute it without further interaction, and a tutorial on AgentBuilder.
Participants were asked to discuss the UX limitations of the baseline agent to encourage thinking about the agent user experience.

Next, participants were given 30 minutes for a prototyping exercise: to design an agent user experience using AgentBuilder for navigating a popular coffee chain’s website.
They were given a list of tasks that end-users of the website may want the agent to execute, including open-ended tasks (e.g., to discover menu options), specific tasks, and tasks with risks (e.g., to place an order).
Participants were instructed to improve the baseline agent’s user experience, such as how and when the agent should interact with a user.
Participants ran their agent prototypes on the website logged in with a real user account with associated payment information.
Participants were encouraged to think aloud as they worked on designing the agent and reflected on the user experience of their prototype. We occasionally reminded participants about available features in the probe, following approaches from similar studies
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
Given time constraints in the sessions, we pre-populated the
Agent Capabilities Prompt
and
User Information Prompt
.
Participants interacted with AgentBuilder by remotely controlling the researcher’s screen with the video conferencing software.

At the end of the study, the participant completed a 30-minute exit interview to reflect on their experience and any challenges faced in the process.

5.1.3.
Analysis

The author who conducted the interview studies analyzed the transcripts and recordings of the user study sessions
by close-coding on participant’s prototyping Activities from the requirements elicitation study (Section
3.2
) to validate the presence of the Activities.
Meanwhile, to understand the prototyping process (
RQ3
) and the needs developers have in this process (
RQ4
), the author performed provisional coding
(
Saldaña 2009
)
using the set of Desired Capabilities from the requirements elicitation study as well as open coding.

5.2.
Results

AgentBuilder enabled all participants—regardless of familiarity with agents, generative AI usage, prompt engineering experience, and job roles—to prototype agent experiences.
During the study, participants created between one and five agent prototypes, with an average of 2.5.
Below, we report the results of the two research questions. We refer back to the set of Activities (noted as
A
s) and Desired Capabilities (noted as
C
s) from Section
3.2
.

5.2.1.
Process of Agent Prototyping (RQ3)

Consistent with other forms of prompt prototyping
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
, all participants followed an iterative loop between the design and inspection phases of agent prototyping.
We validated the presence of all prototyping Activities identified in Section
3.2
(
A1
-
A5
). Below, we synthesize the typical steps of agent prototyping, and explain how the Activities manifest within them:

Step 1. Designing an initial prototype of the agent.

Participants started the prototyping process by designing a starter version of the information display (
A2
) and user interaction (
A3
) of the agent.
Participants often started with a rough goal to design their agent for an imagined user experience, which sometimes changed in later iterations.
For instance, P1 designed their agent for an imaginary user who wouldn’t initially trust it, whereas P14 designed a
“simple agent…[the] user’s ultimate goal is to order a coffee…so I don’t think it has to be very conversational”
(P14).

For user interaction, participants explored the interaction components available in the
Workflow
and/or wrote natural language instructions in
Prompt
to describe which actions the agent should take and when (e.g.,
Confirm with the user before spending more than ten dollars
(P7)), define the tone of the agent (e.g.,
Be very friendly
(P14)), as well as modify tool outputs (e.g., controlling the text content of agent responses).
Common processes to coordinate the agent’s interaction components included building a workflow, generating a textual prompt based on it, and manually refining the prompt; some also first wrote textual prompts, and then generated the workflow visualization.

When designing the information display of the agent, participants tried to anticipate what information the end-users might want to see about the agent’s actions.
Some participants designed their agents to reveal less information, such as P1, who intentionally designed their agent to not show UI action previews in the webage or any UI action information in the chat.
Other participants wanted to reveal more information during execution (e.g., showing description of actions or agent reasoning in the chat), so that users could better trust the agent.
P6 designed their agent to
“minimize information, but just giving the right information”
(P6),
Meanwhile, P10 was unsure how much information the agent should reveal about its UI actions and designed their prototype to display all possible information.
P12 and P14 both decided to reveal agent reasoning
“since for a developer it’s pretty useful”
(P12), but P14 removed it in the next iteration.

Step 2. Running and monitoring the agent.

Next, participants ran their initial prototype of the agent (
A4
).
This step usually started with composing a test query from the perspective of an end-user.
Participants formed their query around the given end-user tasks, and many used specific queries to target the agent’s knowledge of the user (
A1
), such as
“Can you place an order for my previously ordered drink”
(P11) or
“Order me a drink I like”
(P4).
Some participants crafted specific inputs to understand the user experience under specific conditions, such as when the user is unsure what they want to order:
“I don’t know what I want, can you suggest me something that[’s] cold?”
(P6)

Next, participants ran the agent on their queries to evaluate the effect of their design choices and brainstorm ways to improve their prototypes.
For example, P10 designed their first prototype to communicate
“maximal”
(P10) information about each UI action, but after execution found it was a poor experience as it produced
“way too much text”
(P10) in the
Chat
panel.
Meanwhile, P1 designed their agent to ask for lots of user input since they
“prefer knowing exactly what the agent is going to do”
(P1), but later felt the level of interaction
“is too much work…I take it back”
(P1).
Some participants ran the agent on specific queries to understand how it could handle edge cases.
For example, P4 ran a
“adversarial prompt”
(P4) to see if the agent would confuse a user’s favorite items as ones in their order history or would instead clarify what the user’s favorite order was.

During execution, participants closely monitored the agent by reviewing the agent’s messages and observing the agent’s actions in the webpage.
Participants who did not design the agent to reveal information about the agent’s UI actions (e.g., action name, action descriptions, reasoning, or UI action preview in the webpage) sometimes enabled
Debug Mode
during execution to view the agent’s reasoning live (
A6
).
For example, P4 designed their agent to not show the agent’s UI action preview in the webpage or any UI action information in the chat, which caused P4 to keep the
Debug Mode
activated for most of the study.

Step 3. Debugging the agent.

Participants monitored the agent until they observed that the agent encountered a defect, such as looping behavior (
A5
).
This would cause the participant to pause or stop the agent and then enable
Debug Mode
.
Most participants relied on the agent’s reasoning (P1, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11, P12, P13), while others referred to the
Detailed Debugging
interface (P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P9).
Outside of
Debug Mode
, some participants used alternative strategies like referring to the
Workflow
(P1, P8, P10, P11, P14),
Prompt
(P4, P10), or action descriptions in the
Chat
(P6).
P8 debugged their agent by comparing their workflow to the agent’s messages in the chat, identifying that the agent did not show a
Plan
.
Some participants also enabled the UI action previews on the webpage for further visibility of agent UI actions.
When asked whether understanding the agent was challenging, P2 replied:

“No…I always wanted show UI [actions] on…When I saw that, it was really easy to understand, ‘Ok, it’s clicking this”’
(P2)

Step 4. Iterating on the agent.

After identifying the defect and/or brainstorming ways to improve the agent experience, the participant located where in the
Workflow
or
Prompt
to update their prototype and made the change.
They evaluated the effect of their change by repeating steps 2 through 4 until they obtained the desired behavior on at least one example query.
For example, we observed participants updating the
Prompt
with further natural language instructions (
A3
), such as clarification (e.g., changing from
Ask for customizations for the order
to
Ask for customizations multiple times for the order
(P11)), constraints (e.g.,
Do not change items once on the Review Order screen
(P9)), context (e.g., P2 copied in the coffee shop’s menu for the agent to display as options to the user), and tool adjustments (e.g., P6 added instructions to change the format of the agent’s UI action descriptions).
For iterating the workflow, P8 realized their initially designed experience
“[felt] like an overkill”
(P8) for coffee ordering and simplified the number of
Workflow
nodes in their next iteration.

5.2.2.
Developer Needs in Agent Prototyping Systems (RQ4)

We validate all the Desired Capabilities identified in Section
3.2
by observing participants engaged with the affordances of AgentBuilder that instantiate these capabilities.
Below, we discuss our findings regarding each Desired Capabilities, focusing on how participants interacted with the corresponding affordances and the resulting implications for tooling in agent prototyping systems.

C1. Use no-code interfaces.

All participants utilized AgentBuilder’s no-code interfaces to prototype the agent’s behavior, but relied on the notations to varying degrees.
Some participants used the
Workflow
interface, whereas others relied on natural language by directly modifying the
Workflow Prompt
or
Other Instructions Prompt
.
For most participants, natural language prompts enabled them to customize nuances in the agents’ interactions with users, ranging from how it should handle user queries (e.g.,
Ask user maximum of 2 clarifying questions
(P14)), to the tone it should adopt with the user (e.g., being
energetic and kind
(P1)).
At the same time, the workflow notation provided helpful visualizations of agent behavior:
“[The workflow] had some value in the fact that I could see it visually…what’s going on step by step”
(P12).
It allowed participants to trace the workflow to reason about the agent’s actions (P1, P2, P4, P8, P10, P13, P14), helping them to identify conflicts in logic (P1, P4) and verify behavior before executing the agent (P13).
P14, a product manager, mentioned how workflow notations reflected their work practice:
“When I see my engineers…we [whiteboard] with the same building blocks before…coding. This is very helpful because you now see the idea of of the flow”
(P14).

However, despite the no-code interfaces, participants still wanted more support to
understand
what
about the agent could be modified
.
P8 commented on the
Prompt
and
Workflow
interfaces in AgentBuilder:

“They both feel dumbed down…
I feel like my agency has been taken…[when the agent] doesn’t work and then I get frustrated, but I only know these are the only options available.”
(P8)

Participants requested more granular scaffolds to modify the agent’s behavior (P6, P8, P9, P14), such as mechanisms to change the number of options that the agent provided in
Interact
nodes or reduce the wordiness of agent’s action descriptions and reasoning for
UI Actions
nodes.

C2. Help constrain the agent’s task space and knowledge of the user.

Although the
Agent Capabilities Prompt
and
User Information Prompt
in our study (Section
5.1
) were pre-defined, many participants still actively thought through and engaged with the agent’s task space and knowledge of the
user during the prototyping process.
This came in the form of running the agent on user queries with the intention to understand how the agent may account for personal information about the user, such as:
“Order me my favorite drink”
(P4).
By doing so, some participants were able to find out agent’s knowledge about user’s personal information that they previously overlooked.
This enabled several participants to reflect on how the agent handled sensitive user information and what about the experience should be improved.
For example, after observing the agent automatically inserting the user’s password to make a purchase, P9 said,
“The agent didn’t ask [for the password]… It just entered it for me… Definitely not the most comfortable thing”
(P9).

C3. Define the UI of the agent in the chat and the environment.

Most participants modified what information the agent displayed about UI actions in the chat and whether the agent revealed its UI action indicator on the webpage. These decisions were guided both by their expectations of end-user behavior and by their own needs as developers when inspecting the agent.
However, a tension exists between the anticipated information needs of end users and those of developers inspecting the agents. Participants envisioned that end users might prefer limited information about agent actions (e.g., only action names, no plans), yet as developers, they expressed a desire for a more complete picture of how the agent would approach the task (P5, P6, P9, P10, P11, P12).
For example, when P6 asked the agent to recommend items with oat milk, the agent navigated each menu category webpage one at a time.
Under the end-user–facing design, where the agent displayed only action names in the chat, P6 was unable to infer the agent’s overall strategy and, as a result, was unable to evaluate whether the agent was functioning correctly.
Even with more information (e.g., action description, reasoning, plans) displayed, participants found it difficult to make sense of those information as the agent was running. For example, participants like P9 further pointed out that the reasoning of the agent was only helpful for a post-hoc understanding of agent actions, but not while the agent was running:

“The reasoning…post-decision was helpful. In the moment, [the agent] is at the page. What is it doing? Is it thinking about what to get? Did it already pick and it’s waiting for the page to load?”
(P9)

Therefore, agent prototyping systems should offer mechanisms that
help developers make sense of the agent’s past, present, and anticipated behavior for a comprehensive understanding
.

C4. Provide components that enable the agent to invoke different user interactions.

When designing the agent, participants would explore the interaction components available to the agent in the
Workflow
, which allowed them to understand the
“capabilities of this entire agent”
(P12) and
“knowing what actions it can make”
(P13).
Some participants liked having the tool options available on the
Workflow
interface for them, since the design space is clearly defined with a finite set of options:

“That [the
Workflow
] helped me…come up with the phrases, words, and instructions in a much more concise and clear way because I know the limited amount of tools I had in place that I could use for the agent.”
(P5)

Others, however, found it challenging to compose an accurate prompt to define granular specifications of the agent’s behavior (P2, P5, P9, P12, P13).
For example, P12 prompted the agent to ask the user whether they would like to order anything else once an item was added to the cart. While the agent was able to send the appropriate message in the chat, it did not provide the expected UI component with dropdown menu options.
Furthermore, participants found it difficult to verify whether the interaction components would be delivered as they had designed them. Most participants were only able to verify whether the agent used the tools as expected via trial and error.
Alternatively, without execution, some participants leveraged the bidirectional translation between
Workflow
and
Prompt
to indirectly verify the interaction. For example, several participants first authored a preliminary
Workflow
and then used the tool’s
Generate Prompt
feature to produce a prompt as a starting point, which they subsequently reused or refined. Others, such as P10, adopted the opposite strategy of generating a workflow from a prompt and verifying that it aligned with the intended behavior.
None of these strategies were ideal, as they either required a full execution–iteration loop or were indirect. Consequently, participants expressed a desire for mechanisms that would
allow them to reliably specify, preview, and test interaction components across different scenarios
.

C5. Provide an environment to run and control the agent.

All participants ran and observed their agent at least once to understand the user experience with the agent.
A majority of participants used the
Stop
or
Pause
controls to intervene while the agent was running to focus on interesting behavior.
This usually occurred when the participant realized that the agent encountered an error and needed to read the agent’s reasoning that led to the error (P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P12, P13).
Only P9 used the
Pause
and
Resume
features from CowPilot
(
Huq et al. 2025
)
’s intended use, which was to guide the agent to a better trajectory.

While the agent was running, although participants were generally very attentive to the agent’s actions in the webpage and chat panel, many still lost track on whether and when the agent was running, encountering an error, stopped, or waiting for user input (P1, P3, P4, P5, P7, P9, P11).
This challenge was worsened when the participant designed the agent to not display action descriptions and/or reasoning in the chat or choose to hide the agent’s actions on the webpage.
For example, because P1 did not enable the UI action previews in the webpage, they failed to realize their agent added four croissants to the cart.

Even for those who designed the agent to display details about its UI actions,
each time the agent took an action, participants needed to consider both the webpage the agent was running on and the chat panel that contained the agent’s messages and its action reasoning,
creating difficulties in processing this information in real time (P3, P8, P9, P10, P12, P13, P14):
“The most difficult part was just trying to keep up with what’s going on the left [webpage] and what…the agent is thinking”
(P12).
As a result, many participants missed quick and subtle actions (e.g., adding items to the cart) and thus were unable to accurately recall the agent’s actions (P1, P3, P4, P6, P7, P10, P11).
To handle the volume of information, some participants had to pause the agent to process at their own pace (P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P14).
Others had to focus on one source of runtime information while ignoring others. For example, P10 ended up choosing to not read through the chat panel and only monitor the webpage.
Therefore, participants expressed a desire for execution interfaces that
support tracking of detailed runtime information and potential errors
, as well as present multiple sources of runtime data in a digestible format.

C6. Help user debug the agent’s runtime behavior

A majority of participants used AgentBuilder’s debug mode to understand the agent’s runtime behavior.
Most viewed only the agent’s reasoning (P1, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11, P12, P13), while several others further investigated the agent’s action inputs in the
Detailed Debugging
interface (P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P9).

At the same time, many found the agent input context and outputs in the
Debug Mode
, like the webpage accessibility tree and the text input from a given timestep, difficult to interpret (P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10). As a result, this information was insufficient for them to determine how the agent made certain decisions, making it challenging to debug the agent’s behavior.
For example, P10 mistook the accessibility tree text as agent reasoning and incorrectly recounted the agent’s previous actions.
Even though most participants relied mainly on the reasoning of the agent, the reasoning had issues like hallucinations (P1, P11) or insufficient detail (P6).
For example, when P1 asked their agent to order a large latte into their cart, the agent in actuality added a medium but said in the reasoning it added a large.
Other participants wanted greater insight into the agent’s internal mechanisms to support debugging, for example, understanding what information about the webpage the agent had access to. However, they were unable to synthesize the provided information across multiple modalities to derive a clear answer.
For example, several participants encountered the issue where only a small number of items appeared in the drop-down menu tool, but only one participant (P6) was able to figure out that it was due to constraints on the agent’s current viewport and the tool prompt.
Therefore, there is a need for scaffolds that
enable developers to make sense of and adjust how the agent makes decisions.

6.
Discussion

In this study, we investigate scaffolding approaches for prototyping user experiences in UI agents.
Specifically, our requirements elicitation study identifies a set of prototyping activities (
A1
–
A5
) and desired capabilities (
C1
–
C6
) for systems that support agent prototyping.
We develop a design probe, AgentBuilder, that instantiates a version of the identified design requirements.
Our design probe study further validates these design requirements and uncovers additional needs.

Similar to designing AI experiences
(
Yang et al. 2020
;
Dove et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Kayacik et al. 2019
)
, we find that the ability to prototype agents through no-code notations allowed participants with various backgrounds to explore and reason about designing agent experiences.
In other words, the prototyping activities, desired capabilities, and the resulting AgentBuilder speak to the trend of
broadening participation of prototyping AI
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Subramonyam et al. 2025
;
Petridis et al. 2024
;
Fischer 2021
;
Esposito et al. 2023
)
, specifically for agent experiences.

Below, we synthesize our insights on the challenges and opportunities of building user experience in agents, unique to other forms of generative AI (Section
6.1
).
We also propose the design implications for a system to scaffold prototyping agent experiences (Section
6.2
).

6.1.
Unique Challenges and Opportunities of Developing Prompts for Agents

Based on the findings of the
in situ
agent prototyping study, we identified several developer needs, some of which align with those documented in the literature on prompting generative AI, such as knowing the model’s capabilities
(
Liang et al. 2025b
)
and choosing effective prompt wordings
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
We also observe that prompting agents closely mirrors prompting LLMs
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
.
However, based on the study results, we note that it diverges in two main ways:

Making sense of several forms of agent inputs and outputs during execution

While prompting LLMs requires making sense of static text inputs and outputs, prototyping agents requires developers to make sense of many forms of agent input and outputs, as corroborated by the literature
(
Epperson et al. 2025
)
.
From this study, we learned this includes the agent’s actions in the webpage, messages in the chat, as well as the agent’s reasoning, text inputs, webpage screenshots, and webpage accessibility trees, but are insufficient
for developers to make sense of the agent’s past, present, and anticipated behavior for a comprehensive understanding
.
Further, these artifacts are updated each time the agent takes a new action, such as every click, scroll, or agent message, causing developers to need
support tracking of detailed runtime information and potential errors
. All together, developers need scaffolds to present multiple sources of runtime data in a digestible format.

While the designing the display of information of AI has been discussed within the human-AI
(
Amershi et al. 2019b
)
and early interface agent
(
Schiaffino and Amandi 2004
;
Horvitz 1999
;
Shneiderman and Maes 1997
)
experiences literature, future research should investigate ways for developers to better manage and make use of this information in the context of agents, such as visualizations to represent lengthy input contexts or prompts so that agent developers can understand the content of the prompt
(
Liang et al. 2025c
)
.

Developing a mental model of the agent for configuration and iteration

Effectively prototyping agents requires developing an understanding of how the agent is implemented.
While developers usually prompt LLMs by tracking and building from a single prompt, agent developers also need to
make sense of and adjust how the agent makes decisions
, potentially across multiple modalities, such as text instruction, the parts of the webpage visible to the agent, and the webpage accessibility tree.
There are also challenges in
understanding
what
about the agent could be modified
, including recalling the agent’s interaction components, knowing how to specify granular requirements on those components, and writing instructions to invoke them.
Furthermore, developers face challenges
to reliably specify, preview, and test interaction components across different scenarios.

In our design probe AgentBuilder, we offer the
Workflow
representation as a first step to address these challenges.

Workflow
offers reusable components that makes the
interaction components
the agent has access to explicit to the agent developer, while the flow-based diagrams can represent the temporal aspects of agent behavior (
conditions
).
With these abstractions, workflow representations help agent developers express the desired behavior of the prototype, reducing common barriers of not knowing what the system is capable of and what wording to use
(
Liang et al. 2025b
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Ko et al. 2004
)
.
However, we also learned that some participants found the workflow representation limits the flexibility of authoring prompts.
Future research could explore this direction to better integrate the workflow notation in agent prototyping, such as a visualization to localize fault to a specific part of the prompt during agent debugging.

6.2.
Design Implications for Agent Prototyping Systems

Make interaction components easy to understand, use, and customize

Interaction components are the building blocks for an agent.
Our
in situ
agent prototyping study provides insight into the potential benefits of using workflow notations for prototyping agent experiences by scaffolding a pre-defined set of interaction components, which allow for structured exploration and a constrained design space.
Following this, agent prototyping systems should include features that make interaction components and their prompts easy to understand.
This can be facilitated by using a limited number of abstractions and understandable tool names
(
Thayer et al. 2021
;
Bloch 2006
)
.
These systems should also include features to facilitate discovering (e.g., providing previews) and invoking these components (e.g., suggesting effective prompt wording)
(
Subramonyam et al. 2024
)
.
Furthermore, systems should provide affordances that reveal the agent’s interaction component prompts and allow agent developers to modify it.

Allow toggling between presenting different amounts of agent runtime information

Our study shows that the information needs of agent developers and end-users can conflict.
This is because for
“an engineering persona, all information might be interesting”
(P6) to understand the agent’s behavior, but this in turn may easily
“overwhelm [end-users] with unnecessary information”
(P11).
We find that in agent prototyping, agent developers need to switch between the developer’s mindset—where they need to inspect details about agent behaviors—and the end-user’s mindset to imagine the user experience.
However, we observed that providing agent developers with the amount of information intended for end-users caused them to be less effective at understanding and debugging the agent.
Thus, agent prototyping systems should consider providing different amounts of information about the agent that are designed for the needs of agent developers and end-users during the agent’s execution.
This could allow agent developers to experience the agent as an end user, while still having the appropriate amount of information to understand and debug the agent, which is a challenging activity in agent development
(
Liang et al. 2025c
;
Epperson et al. 2025
)
.

Enable reviewing and customizing the agent’s runtime

In our study, participants felt uncertain whether the agent was running, done, encountering an error, or waiting for user input.
Systems should therefore provide features to communicate the current status of the agent, such as status indicators, alerts, or update messages.
Another key challenge is controlling appropriate timing for the agent’s actions, echoing the principles of human-AI interaction
(
Horvitz 1999
;
Amershi et al. 2019b
)
.
At times, participants felt the agent was too slow and provided limited utility, but also felt the same timing was too fast when it outputted large amounts of detailed text about its behaviors. Systems should offer controls that allow the user to vary the agent’s execution speed with respect to different context factors, such as the length of the agent’s message.

6.3.
Limitations

Below, we discuss the limitations of this work and opportunities for future work.
For external validity, the results of both studies may not generalize to all agent developers and development contexts due to the small sample size and limited number of domains considered across the studies.
In addition, sampling bias could be introduced by recruiting all participants from the same large technology company.
While we reduced these threats by validating the results from the requirements elicitation study with the
in situ
agent prototyping study, the list of design requirements may not be fully exhaustive, especially in the face of the rapidly changing landscape of agents.
For internal validity, the results of the
in situ
agent prototyping study could be affected by the study participants using AgentBuilder by controlling the researcher’s computer, rather than through their own device, and by having an imposed 30-minute time limit to complete an agent prototyping task.
Therefore, the results of this work should be treated as hypotheses for future work to validate.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we explore how to prototype agent experiences.
We first outline the design requirements of agent prototyping systems based on interviews with 12 individuals who have a variety of prior experiences with agents.
The design requirements include an enumeration of 5 Activities in the agent prototyping process, from which we derive 6 Desired Capabilities that agent prototyping systems should support.
Next, we use these design requirements to inform the design of AgentBuilder, a design probe that enables the design, development, and execution of agent prototypes.
We use AgentBuilder as a probe to conduct an
in situ
study of agent prototyping with 14 individuals interested in agents.
Through this study, we validate the design requirements and obtain insights on how developers prototype agents and what their needs are in this process.
This includes the need for mechanisms that help developers make sense of various forms of agent input, context, and outputs during execution, as well as to better understand the agent’s technical implementation.
Based on these findings, we propose a set of
design recommendations for agent prototyping systems.

The user’s previous orders include a birthday cake pop, Caffè Misto, and a cold brew at Starbucks
