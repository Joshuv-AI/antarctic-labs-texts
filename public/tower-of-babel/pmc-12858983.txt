Engineering artificial biosynthetic pathway enables simultaneous production and in-situ bio-dyeing of indigoids for textiles
Authors: Qi Na, Xiaowang Zhang, Linna Long, Siyu Qi, Zhiyu Zhang, Ruoyu Zhi, Zhuotao Tan, Hanjie Ying, Chenjie Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe textile dyeing industry has shaped modern aesthetics through vibrant coloration technologies. Indigoid dyes embody this potential but are hindered by the hazardous chemo-synthesis and water-intensive chemo-dyeing processes. While biosynthesis and bio-dyeing efforts offer alternatives, disjointed synthesis-dyeing steps lead to unsustainable water and energy demands, and complex biosynthetic pathways cause low atom economy and inefficiency. Here, we report a one-pot one-step approach that unifies biosynthesis and bio-dyeing of indigoids to drastically reduce water usage in the dyeing process. By combining alcohol dehydrogenase with flavin-containing monooxygenase, we construct an artificial redox-neutral cascade biosynthetic pathway to produce bluish-violet indigoid dyes, which significantly enhances atom economy and achieves high production efficiency. Various natural and synthetic fabrics are dyed in-situ with the biosynthetic indigoids utilizing recombinant E. coli whole cells. Crucially, this system dyes synthetic nylon fibers with the historic purple hue.Subject terms: Metabolic engineering, Biocatalysis, Synthetic biology, Applied microbiology

Introduction

The textile dyeing industry has undergone rapid growth driven by advancements in synthetic chemistry, enriching human society with vibrant colors1. Bluish-violet indigoid dyes, long-renowned natural colorants, exemplify this legacy2,3. Indigo, one of the prominent indigoid dyes, which its blue hue can be traced back to the Indus Valley Civilization (~3000 BC) and was extracted from Indigofera tinctoria for dyeing cotton and linen fabrics4. Similarly, Tyrian purple, a rare 6,6′-dibromoindigo (6,6′-DBI)-based dye extracted from sea snails, symbolized the noble status and power of royalty within the Roman Empire5. At the end of the 19th century, BASF achieved the industrial chemical synthesis of indigo. The popularization of synthetic indigo and its fading effect on denim have spawned a global fashion trend, creating a nearly $100 billion/year market6. However, the widespread adoption of synthetic indigoid dyes has precipitated serious threats to human health and caused significant environmental burdens7. One is the toxic, carcinogenic, and hazardous chemical synthesis process, in which hydrogen cyanide, strong bases, and sodamide was employed8 (Fig. 1a). The other is the highly polluting and water-intensive dyeing process7 (Fig. 1a). In industry, large amounts of sodium dithionite need to be repeatedly added to reduce water-insoluble indigoid to its soluble leuco form, which enables the dyeing of fabrics with indigoid dyes9. This results in large quantities of corrosive sulfur-containing wastewater. It is estimated that a single medium-sized indigo dyeing factory can produce ~1000 tons of wastewater per day.

To mitigate the persistent environmental pollution and health hazard, recent biotechnological advances have pioneered eco-friendly alternatives, such as biosynthesis of indigoid dyes10,11 and enzymatic bio-dyeing systems8. A landmark example achieving sustainable and eco-friendly synthesis and dyeing of indigoid is the use of glucosyltransferase (UGT) and β-glucosidase (BGL)-dependent biochemical protection and deprotection strategies for indigo synthesis and dyeing8 (Fig. 1b). In engineered E. coli cells, fed tryptophan (Trp) was first converted to indoxyl, an unstable intermediate, by tryptophanase (TnaA) and flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO). Indoxyl was then stabilized via UGT-mediated glycosylation into indican and released by BGL for cotton dyeing. This method eliminated the use of sodium dithionite, presenting a sustainable alternative to the conventional chemical dyeing approach. Despite its promise, this method separates biosynthesis and dyeing, still consuming substantial water and energy due to BGL usage, which also suffers from limited enzymatic robustness and high production cost12. Furthermore, whether this system is applicable to other indigoid dyes (e.g., Tyrian purple analogs) still remains to be explored. The development of a simple, efficient, and universal biological strategy that integrates indigoid synthesis and dyeing to eliminate water usage during dyeing process is therefore of great interest.

Other research focused on using recombinant microbes or enzymes for biosynthesis of indigoid dyes, which made considerable efforts to create sustainable synthetic approaches13. However, the widely reported three-step biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 1c), which is composed of two-component halogenase (HL)-catalyzed selective halogenation of tryptophan (omitted for indigo biosynthesis), tryptophanase (TnaA)-mediated decomposition of substituted tryptophan, and oxygenase (OX)-driven hydroxylation of substituted indoles, still face critical limitations: (1) low atom economy due to the C-C bond cleavage reactions catalyzed by TnaA, which could generate byproducts including pyruvate and ammonia; (2) requires at least five enzymes (including two NAD(P)H regeneration enzymes) for the synthesis of substituted indigos complicating catalytic balance regulation and imposing metabolic burden on host cells; (3) inevitable pot replacement or temperature regulation that control expression of the tnaA gene for enhancing the selectivity of the pathway. Hence, the development of an easy-operation, atom economical, and efficient one-pot one-step strategy for biosynthesizing indigoids that eliminates process control steps is highly attractive.

Here, we demonstrate a one-pot one-step approach for simultaneous biosynthesis and bio-dyeing of indigoids, eliminating water-intensive dyeing processes (Fig. 1d). Our strategy integrates a highly atom-economical artificial biosynthetic pathway coupling NAD+-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH)-catalyzed aminoalcohol oxidation with NAD(P)H-dependent flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO)-catalyzed indole oxygenation. To eliminate exogenous cofactor dependence, we engineer a cofactor self-recycling redox-neutral cascade via a classic enzymatic hydrogen-borrowing mechanism. In recombinant E. coli whole cells co-expression of the ADH and FMO genes, indigoids (leuco form) including indigo and 6,6′-dibromoindigo (6,6′-DBI) are synthesized and directly fixed onto fabrics, enabling in-situ integration of biosynthesis and dyeing. This strategy can dye various natural and synthetic fibers, particularly when applying 6,6′-DBI to nylon fibers, achieving its inherent distinctive purple hue10.

Results

Design artificial biosynthesis pathway for 6-bromoindole

To design a proof-of-concept artificial biosynthetic pathway for indigoid dyes, retrosynthesis analysis was initially applied. Tyrian purple (primarily composed of 6,6′-DBI) was selected as the representative product due to its noble color and high commercial value. As shown in Fig. 2a, the 6,6′-DBI synthesis pathway was divided into two steps: the first involves the synthesis of 6-bromoindole (6-Br-indole), and the second involves the synthesis of 6,6′-DBI from 6-Br-indole. For the synthesis of 6-Br-indole, two routes including intermolecular (route 1) and intramolecular (route 2) oxidative condensation reactions were proposed inspired by classical metal-catalyzed hydrogen-borrowing (HB) reactions14 (Fig. 2a). In route 1, we suspected that alcohol dehydrogenase could catalyze the oxidation of diol substrate15, the resulting glyoxal could couple with substrate aniline forming 5-membered ring based on the aldimine condensation and C-C coupling. However, this condensation reaction usually needs high reaction temperature16. Thus, route 1 was excluded.

Route 2 begins from 2-amino-4-bromo-phenylethanol (1a), which can be synthesized from 4-bromo-2-nitrotoluene (Supplementary Fig. 1). Based on our previous study on the cascade catalysis of alcohol dehydrogenase and halogenase for the synthesis of halogenated indoles17, we hypothesized that it could undergo alcohol dehydrogenase- catalyzed oxidation followed by spontaneous intramolecular aldimine condensation and dehydration to generate 6-Br-indole. We selected twelve alcohol dehydrogenases belonging to short-18–21, medium-22–27, and long-chain28,29 families to verify the oxidation of the substrate and performed their phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 2b). Employing NAD+ or NADP+ as cofactors, the specific activity and conversion of each enzyme were tested in vitro (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2). The result showed that PpADH, YsADH, and HLADH exhibited better catalytic efficiency compared with other tested ADHs. The high enzyme activity (10.5 U mg−1) and conversion rate (45%) were achieved in the case of that using HLADH and NAD+ as co-substrates for catalyzing. To further confirm the formation of 6-Br-indole, GC-MS analysis was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 3). These spectra indicated that 6-Br-indole was generated during the oxidation of aminoalcohol. However, the conversion of this reaction was limited to 45% (Fig. 2c). To gain deeper mechanistic insights (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4), QM/MM and DFT calculations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanism. The results showed that DFT calculations yield a lower energy barrier from IM to 1c (16.5 kcal mol−1) compared to the barrier from IM to 1c’ obtained by QM/MM calculations (31.4 kcal mol−1) (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 5, 6), which indicates that 1a is first oxidized by HLADH, followed by spontaneous intramolecular aldimine condensation and dehydration to generate 6-Br-indole. The oxidation of 1a is apparently the limited step.

A conventional NADH oxidase (NOX)-coupled NAD+ regeneration system was then employed (Fig. 2f). The yield of 6-Br-indole increased to 96% in 8 h which is 2.1-fold higher compared with that of adding excess NAD+ (Supplementary Fig. 7). Residual substrate (4%) was attributed to partial amino group protonation at pH 7.0, hindering aldehyde-amine condensation30. Further Increasing the reaction pH to 9.0, >99% yield of 6-Br-indole was achieved in 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 8). The turnover frequency (TOF) reached 62,400 h−1, representing a 2-fold increase over the value at pH 7.0. This result motivated us to expand the substrate scope of this system. 2-amino-phenylethanol and its different substituent analogues (2a-21a) were tested (Fig. 2g). The results demonstrated that HLADH exhibited broad substrate scope toward substituted 2-amino-phenylethanols (-F, -Cl, -Br, -Me, -OMe) at various positions (4-, 5-, 6-, 7-), achieving yields ranging from 36% to 99% for corresponding substituted indole products (2d-21d). HLADH displayed high catalytic efficiency for substrates bearing substituents at the 5-, 6-, and 7-positions, with product yields reaching 76–99% under optimized reaction conditions. Moreover, the synthesis of indole (13d) achieved an excellent yield (>99%), consistent with our previous research31.

Construction and optimization of the HLADH-mFMO cascade

Next, we evaluated the biosynthesis of 6,6′-DBI from 6-Br-indole by using the NAD(P)H-dependent flavin-containing monooxygenases (FMOs) which were widely used for indigoid synthesis. Three FMOs including Methylophaga sp. strain SK1 FMO (mFMO)32, Corynebacterium glutamicum FMO (cFMO)33, Nitrincola lacisaponensis FMO (NiFMO)34 were selected for catalyzing 6-Br-indole to 6,6′-DBI. Using NADH or NADPH as cofactors, the specific activity and conversion of each enzyme towards 6-Br-indole was tested (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9). The result showed that mFMO exhibited the higher activity among these enzymes either using NADH or NADPH, but cFMO had no activity towards 6-Br-indole. We found that NiFMO showed similar activity when adding NADH or NADPH. The result of kinetic analysis of NiFMO also revealed that the catalytic activity with NADH is comparable to that of NADPH (Table 1). This observation contradicted the findings of Loncar et al.34, who reported NiFMO showed no activity towards NADH. Subsequently, glucose dehydrogenase (GDH)-catalyzed cofactor regeneration system was employed to further enhance the catalytic efficiency (Fig. 3a). As expected, the yield of 6,6′-DBI showed significant improvements compared with that adding excess amount of NADH or NADPH except for cFMO. The yields of NADP+-involved reactions tested in this study were all higher than those of NAD+-involved, suggesting that NADP+ may be a preferable cofactor for this specific biotransformation under the experimental conditions. mFMO was selected for the following experiment due to its higher yield (40.8%) among these FMOs when using NADP+ as cofactor.

Recently, enzymatic borrowing hydrogen via NAD(P)H self-regeneration has emerged as an efficient redox-neutral strategy for synthesizing value-added chemicals35–37. Inspired by this approach, we explored the potential of integrating ADH with FMO for 6,6′-DBI synthesis. Based on the above studies, NAD+ is the optimal cofactor for HLADH, whereas NADPH is preferred by FMO. Accordingly, we assessed the performance of NADP+ and NAD+ in the cascade system. The results indicated that the yields of 6,6′-DBI reached 15.2% and 42.1%, respectively (Fig. 3b). Kinetic analysis showed that the kcat/KM values of mFMO for NADH and NADPH were 3.4 and 9.2 mM−1 s−1, respectively. In contrast, the values for HLADH with NAD+ and NADP+ were only 0.8 and 0.004 mM−1 s−1, respectively (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 10). These kinetic data clearly demonstrate that HLADH exhibits very low catalytic activity toward NADP+. Hence, NAD+ is the more suitable cofactor for this cascade system. After further optimization of pH and temperature, the yield of 6,6′-DBI increased to 48.7% (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). The ratios of HLADH and mFMO were further optimized. A ratio of 1:2 was found to most effectively alleviate the inhibition caused by the accumulation of intermediate 1d, resulting in the 6,6′-DBI yield of 60.1% (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13).

Expansion of substrate scope

Having established the HLADH-mFMO-catalyzed 6,6′-DBI cascade synthesis reaction with NAD+ as a hydrogen-transfer mediator, we next applied the procedure for the synthesis of a variety of indigoids 2f-21f by using aminoalcohols (2a-21a) as substrates to evaluate the versatility of this HLADH-mFMO cascade catalytic system (Fig. 4, Method A). Directly using indoles as the substrates catalyzed by mFMO was also conducted for comparison (Fig. 4, Method B). In the dual-enzyme cascade reaction, substituent-free aminoalcohol 13a afforded 92% indigo yield. All 19 aminoalcohol derivatives were converted, with yields ranging from 2% to 83% for the indigoids. It was observed that not only substrates 2a-12a with electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs: -F, -Cl, -Br), but also substrates 14a-21a which contain electron-donating groups (EDGs: -Me, -OMe) exhibited enhanced compatibility under the optimized conditions. As shown in Fig. 4, the yields of HLADH-mFMO system are generally higher than those catalyzed by single mFMO, thus demonstrating the benefits of the dual-enzyme cascade catalytic system.

Substituent electronic effects exhibited pronounced structure-activity relationships in both enzymatic systems. The yield of mFMO-mediated C3-hydroxylation correlated with the electronegativity of EWGs (-F > -Cl > -Br), with stronger electron-withdrawing groups demonstrating enhanced reaction efficiency and higher product yields. Positional dependence was observed: C5/C6/C7-EWG derivatives achieved optimal yields (35–83%), while C4-substituted analogues suffered obvious yield inhibition (<30%). Conversely, EDGs (-Me/-OMe) universally suppressed activity, with 4,4′- and 5,5′-EDGs resulting in extreme yield reductions (2–14%), while 6,6′-/7,7′-EDG derivatives demonstrated comparatively higher yields (13–33%).

Finally, we used a red-green-blue (RGB) color model to quantitatively analyze the enzyme-synthesized dyes. The RGB values were acquired using Photoshop’s Color Picker38. The color and absorption maximum of indigo dyes depend on the type and position of substituents on the benzene ring (Supplementary Fig. 14). The combination of indigo derivatives with diverse substituents via the dual-enzyme cascade resulted in a broad spectrum of colors. Overall, our constructed catalytic route of dual enzyme cascade greatly enhanced the yield of substituted indigos, enriched the substrate scope, and characterized the color profiles of indigoids.

Production of 6,6′-DBI in E. coli

After demonstrating the in vitro redox-neutral cascade reaction with HLADH and mFMO, recombinant E. coli whole cells were engineered to convert 1a to 6,6′-DBI, which were more economical and stable than free enzymes (Fig. 5a). A single tailored recombinant whole-cell biocatalyst containing all required enzymes is preferred for cascade biocatalysis to avoid multi-strain cultivation, enzyme purification, and large amounts of external cofactor addition39. To balance cascade enzyme expression in a single recombinant E. coli strain, 12 variants coexpressing HLADH and mFMO were constructed (Fig. 5b), and their yields were evaluated for 6,6′-DBI production (Fig. 5c). The gene of TnaA was knocked out to avoid the generation of endogenic indigo40. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that all of the enzymes were produced in E. coli cells (Supplementary Fig. 15). All combinations were examined for the biotransformation of 2.5 mM 1a. With all the recombinant E. coli cells density at OD600 = 20, 6,6′-DBI was obtained in 0.2% − 55.1% yield (Fig. 5c). M1 was selected for further study due to its capability to achieve the yield (55.1%) of 6,6′-DBI (Fig. 5c). The growth and conversion efficiency of M1 were optimized (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b). The conversion (64.7%) was achieved after 12 h of cultivation at 18 °C with 0.1 mM IPTG. Furthermore, after optimizing the pH and temperature conditions in the whole-cell catalytic process, a maximum yield of 72.1% was achieved after 12 h at pH 8.0 and 30 °C (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 16c, d). Finally, a 6,6′-DBI titer of 367.5 mg L−1 was achieved in 200 mL shake-flask whole-cell fermentation (Fig. 5e). The space-time yield of 6,6′-DBI (15.31 mg L−1 h−1) achieved in this study demonstrates the effectiveness of the one-pot one-step biosynthetic strategy for 6,6′-DBI production in E. coli10,11,41 (Supplementary Table 1). The route also exhibited better environmental performance, with improved atom economy and a reduced E-factor (Supplementary Table 2).

In-situ bio-dyeing strategy for 6,6′-DBI

The synthesis and dyeing processes collectively constitute the complete cycle of textile dyeing42. To further demonstrate the practical applicability of this system, we develop an innovative strategy combining indigoid biosynthesis with in-situ bio-dyeing strategy (Bio-in-situ strategy), where fabrics were directly added in the indigoid biosynthesis development buffer, enabling effective fiber coloration through spontaneous utilization of leuco intermediates generated during biosynthesis (Fig. 6a).

Chemical and in-situ methods for dyeing fabrics with 6,6′-DBI.

a Scheme for 6,6′-DBI Bio-in-situ dyeing process. b Fabrics dyed using Bio-in-situ dyeing method under different dyeing time spent and fabrics dyed with Bio-Chem strategy. c SEM analysis on dyed nylon fabrics from in-situ dyeing and chemical dyeing strategy. Each experiment was independently repeated three times with similar results. d Nylon dyed with Bio-in-situ method and customized “NJTECH” pattern combined with embroidery technology.

To assess the feasibility, the recombinant whole cell that generating 6,6′-DBI was directly employed for dyeing diverse fabric substrates (Fig. 6a). When the substrate 1a was added to the whole-cell reaction system, multi-fabric strips are immediately introduced into the reaction solution. Dyeing efficiencies through K/S (absorption coefficient, K, and scattering coefficient, S, a value used to determine the depth of color of a dyed fabric) value43 measurement at 580 nm revealed optimal dyeing was achieved at 24 h (Supplementary Table 3). Among all tested fabric strips, natural wool and synthetic nylon exhibited satisfactory coloration efficacy under the catalytic conditions. For dyeing synthetic nylon, Bio-in-situ strategy reproduced the authentic “regal purple” of Tyrian purple, whereas the chemical dyeing strategy (Bio-Chem strategy) resulted in a bluish coloration (Fig. 6b). Moreover, we analyzed brightness indicator (L*), red to green indicator (a*), yellow to blue indicator (b*) and color difference (ΔE*ab) (Supplementary Table 4). Statistical analysis revealed high color difference between nylon substrates colored via Bio-Chem and Bio-in-situ strategies. Specifically, nylon substrates colored via Bio-in-situ strategy exhibited significantly higher a* values (indicating a redder hue), while those processed through chemical dyeing showed markedly lower b* values (suggesting enhanced blueness).

We wondered if this color distinction was due to a change in the particle size of 6,6′-DBI dyed on nylon. Previous study has demonstrated that pigment particle size critically influences chromatic performance on fabrics44. To validate this hypothesis, we performed SEM analysis on dyed nylon fabrics from both methods to compare particle size distributions. SEM analysis revealed significant differences in pigment particle morphology between the two dyeing approaches (Fig. 6c). The dyed nylon by Bio-in-situ strategy exhibited smaller 6,6′-DBI particles. In contrast, samples dyed by Bio-Chem strategy showed substantially larger particles. This structural divergence directly correlates with the observed chromatic differences. The smaller particles enhance light absorption at longer wavelengths (590 nm red shoulder), consistent with the characteristic Tyrian purple hue44. Conversely, the larger particles promote stronger scattering at shorter wavelengths, amplifying the 560 nm blue shoulder44. This contrast not only confirms the integrative advantage of combining synthesis and coloration in a single process through the Bio-in-situ strategy but also highlights its ability to achieve historically accurate chromatic reproduction on synthetic textiles, particularly for recreating the prestigious Tyrian purple aesthetics on nylon fabrics.

To further investigate the universality of Bio-in-situ method, we systematically evaluated six structurally diverse indigoid dyes (Supplementary Fig. 17). Experimental results demonstrated uniformly achieved robust dyeing outcomes. Building upon our prior work on scale-up of Tyrian purple biosynthesis, this approach enables feasible implementation of Bio-in-situ method with minimal infrastructure investment for producing commercially viable dyed materials. To demonstrate practical applicability, we strategically integrated the technique with embroidery technology for custom pattern development. Nylon via in-situ coloration was precision-stitched onto blank textile substrates using a computerized embroidery system, fabricating a proof-of-concept fabric sample featuring purple “NJTECH” letters (Fig. 6d). This can demonstrate the possible forms and aesthetics of in-situ dyeing.

Discussion

The indigoid dye industry has undergone a transformative development over the past 100 years thanks to advances in synthetic chemistry. However, the accompanying environmental pollution and health threats, including hazardous synthetic process and water-consuming dyeing process, cast a shadow on human society. Here, we addressed the challenges by proposing a one-pot one-step approach for simultaneous biosynthesis and bio-dyeing of indigoids. This strategy not only avoided the toxic and harmful chemical synthesis method, but also eliminated the usage of water in the dyeing process. We demonstrated an artificial biosynthetic pathway that couples ADH-catalyzed oxidation of aminoalcohols with FMO-catalyzed oxygenation of indoles for bioproducing indigoid dyes. Given the necessity of a cofactor regeneration system to promote the entire reaction, the classic enzymatic hydrogen-borrowing strategy was applied to further streamline this system45, thereby developing an efficient, atom-economical, and cofactor self-recycling redox-neutral cascade. Furthermore, we elucidated the potential of this simultaneous indigoid biosynthesis and bio-dyeing system by employing the recombinant E. coli whole cells for the biosynthesis of indigoids (leuco form) for textile dyeing, which succeeded in dyeing synthetic nylon with the historic Tyrian purple hue (6,6′-DBI) - a result challenging to obtain through conventional chemical dyeing processes. This strategy introduced an effective and integrable bio-dyeing alternative for sustainable textile dyeing.

Our study employed retrosynthetic analysis to design an artificial pathway for biosynthesis of indigoids. Starting from aminoalcohol substrates, utilizing HLADH to synthesize key indole intermediates, which are then coupled with mFMO to produce indigoid dyes. However, the reaction mechanism of HLADH-catalyzed conversion of aminoalcohols to indoles remains unresolved. Previous study has established that HLADH-catalyzed oxidation of aminoalcohols produces lactams30, while our experiments demonstrated that 2-amino-4-bromo-phenylethanol undergoes oxidization and dehydration to form 6-bromoindole. DFT energy calculations support the feasibility of this pathway. To streamline the enzymatic reaction and realize a redox-neutral cascade with self-sufficient cofactor recycling, the challenge lay in cofactor compatibility between HLADH (NAD+-dependent) and mFMO (NADPH-dependent). Through kinetic analysis exploring the cofactor preference of this enzymatic system, we revealed that both mFMO and NiFMO exhibit dual dependency on NADH and NADPH, despite previous explicit documentation that NiFMO lacks NADH utilization capability34. This unexpected bifunctional cofactor adaptability represents a significant advancement in understanding the cofactor specificity of bacterial-derived flavin-containing monooxygenases (FMOs), expanding our understanding of their catalytic versatility in redox-mediated biotransformation processes46,47. This result enabled the construction of the HLADH-mFMO cascade system, bypassing the need for external cofactor regeneration enzymes (e.g., NOX or GDH) and simplifying the reaction setup (two-pots changed to one-pot). Directed evolution of mFMO to enhance NADH preference could further improve catalytic efficiency. Moreover, we synthesized 20 indigoid derivatives with diverse substituents. Lee et al. reported the biosynthesis of Br- and Cl-substituted indigoids10, while we have expanded the substrate scope of mFMO to include F-, Me-, and OMe-substituted indigoids. This demonstrates the broad applicability of our newly developed redox-neutral enzymatic cascade for producing a wide range of indigoid dyes under mild and sustainable conditions. Furthermore, a unified qualitative analysis of the color–structure relationship (RGB values and UV–Vis absorption spectra) for 20 distinct mFMO-catalyzed indigoids provides guidance for subsequent research. While this enriches the color palette, the development of vivid hues38,48 (e.g., red and green) and multifunctional indigoids49 (e.g., carboxylated derivatives) remains an exciting frontier. Additionally, whole-cell biosynthesis in E. coli achieved a 72.1% yield and 15.31 mg L−1 h−1 space-time yield of Tyrian purple (6,6′-DBI).

Several reports have demonstrated the biosynthesis of indigoid via whole cell or multi-enzymes catalysis starting from various indoles50,51 or tryptophan48,52. The use of indole substrates faces a notable drawback due to their cytotoxicity. The tryptophan-based route entails an initial halogenation step. Biocatalytic halogenation offers high selectivity, mild reaction conditions, and environmental friendliness. However, its large-scale application is hindered by issues such as low reaction rates and narrow substrate scopedifficulties. In contrast, chemical halogenation is well-established, efficient, and cost-effective, yet suffers from poor selectivity, harsh reaction conditions, and generation of hazardous waste. HLADH-mFMO cascade strategy, employing halogenated aminoalcohol precursors as substrates, effectively circumvents the low reaction rates associated with halogenase enzymes. The higher atomic economy and lower E-factor, demonstrating superior sustainability and economic feasibility (Supplementary Table 2). Pham et al. reported an E. coli fermentation approach for synthesis of indigo starting from glucose, with titers reaching 235 mg mL−1 and 965 mg mL−1 at flask and 3 L bioreactor scales, respectively53. However, this strategy is limited to indigo. Developing glucose-based fermentation strategy for biosynthetic indigoids is a direction for future research. The enzyme FMO is widely used to catalyze the oxidization of indole to indigoid, while oxygen stress is always accompanied by the oxidation process54,55. The central challenge for future research lies in integrating enzyme engineering and process engineering strategies to precisely balance the oxygen transfer rate with oxidative stress.

Building upon our results on addressing 6,6′-DBI synthesis challenges, we have developed an innovative in-situ dyeing strategy that combines indigoid biosynthesis and textile coloration in a single process. The in-situ bio-dyeing strategy represents a substantial advance in sustainable textile manufacturing. Traditional indigo dyeing relies on sodium dithionite to solubilize pigments, generating sulfur-laden wastewater. In contrast, our approach immobilizes leucoindigo directly onto fabrics during biosynthesis, eliminating toxic reductants and minimizing water usage during the textile coloration process. This method achieves chromatic fidelity that is difficult to achieve with chemical means, particularly on synthetic nylon fibers. The reproduction of the characteristic Tyrian purple hue on nylon, a material traditionally resistant to indigoid dyes56, underscores the system’s ability to harmonize historical aesthetics with modern industrial demands. Mechanistic insights into particle size modulation further reveal that biosynthetic control over indigoid dye morphology enhances color depth and wavelength specificity.

In recent years, the field of biomaterials has attracted considerable research attention, with parallel advancements in biomaterial dyeing techniques. A notable study recently reported a two-step approach for simultaneous production and melanin-based dyeing of bacterial cellulose membranes, demonstrating significant application potential57. Our proposed one-step method for synthesizing indigo dyes and in-situ dyeing provides guidance for the dyeing of bacterial cellulose membranes. Future research directions should prioritize the establishment of integrated biomanufacturing platforms that combine biomaterial synthesis with biocatalytic coloration processes in a single bioreactor system. By merging synthetic biology with textile engineering, this work expands the scope of sustainable dye production. The dual achievements of high-efficiency biosynthesis and in-situ coloration address both environmental and economic challenges while revitalizing historical pigments for contemporary use. As industries increasingly prioritize circular economies, this integrated strategy provides a strategy for reconciling ecological responsibility with technological innovation.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

LB medium was prepared from 5 g L−1 yeast extract, 10 g L−1 peptone and 10 g L−1 NaCl. Indole 13d and substituted indole 1d-13d, 14d-21d were purchased from Aladdin. 2-aminophenethanol 13a and indigo 13f were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Oligomer synthesis and sequencing were purchased and done through Generay Biotech (Chuzhou, China). Enzymes involved in restriction reaction, ligation and PCR were purchased from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). Multifabric strips were purchased from SDC Corporation, UK. 1a-13a, 14a-21a were synthesized from substituted 2-nitrotoluene by two step chemical method (Supplementary Fig. 2) and they were identified by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). Characterization of substrates and products were shown in Supplementary Note 1.

Plasmids and strains construction

Primers, plasmids and strains in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Data 1, 2, respectively. The genes for 12 alcohol dehydrogenases, 3 flavin-containing monooxygenases, SmNOX and BmGDH were synthesized and cloned into the NdeI-XhoI site of pET-28a (+) after codon optimization by Generay Biotech (Chuzhou, China) (Supplementary Table 6). Plasmids used in this study were constructed in the following protocol. The fragments for target gene and vectors (pRSFDuet-1, pETDuet-1 and pACYCDuet-1(Novagen)) were amplified by PCR using primers to clone the target gene into the HindIII and XhoI sites of the vector, respectively. The gene fragments, the vector fragments, ddH2O and 2 × MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix from TransGen Biotech (Beijing, China) were added into 1.5 mL tube and incubated at 50 °C for 10 min. Then, the mixture was transformed into 100 μL competent cells using heat shock and plated on LB agar with appropriate antibiotics. The results were confirmed by sequencing. Strains were based on E. coli BL21 (DE3). The tnaA gene was knocked out of the genome to give the engineered strain E. coli BL21 (DE3) ΔtnaA. Briefly, the sequences of sgRNA for tnaA was obtained from NCBI and ligated to the plasmid pTargetF (Addgene), giving the modified pTargetF. The upstream and downstream homologous arms of target genes were amplified and merged, resulting in deletion fragments. The plasmid pEcCas (Addgene) was first transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) by chemical transformation and then used for the preparation of the electroporated competent cells. Next, the corresponding deletion fragment and the modified pTargetF were cotransformed into the above competent cells and then plated onto LB agar with appropriate antibiotics. The correct single colony was selected and confirmed by sequencing. The plasmids pTargetF and pEcCas were further removed by the corresponding culture conditions with appropriate antibiotics and sugar, finally giving the engineered strain E. coli BL21 (DE3) ΔtnaA. The plasmids for protein expression were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3), while the plasmids for whole-cell biotransformation were transformed into the engineered strain E. coli BL21 (DE3) ΔtnaA, giving the engineered strains M1-M12.

Expression of genes and purification of recombinant proteins

A single colony of recombinant E. coli BL21 (DE3) containing ADHs, FMOs, SmNOX and BmGDH was inoculated into LB medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Then, 20 mL of the seed cultures were inoculated into 400 mL of LB media and grown at 37 °C until the cell density reached an optical density (OD600) of roughly 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 25 °C for 20 h when the optical density (OD600) of engineered strain reached 0.6–0.8. Next, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 3,500 g for 10 min and washed with KPi buffer pH 7.0. The above pellets were resuspended with lysis buffer (50 mM KPi buffer pH 7.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 5% (w/v) glycerol) at a concentration of 0.1 g mL−1. The resuspended solution was disrupted by ultrasonication in ice-chilled water for 15 min (3 s turn on, 5 s turn off). The soluble fractions were collected by centrifugation of the cell lysate at 13,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The above supernatant was collected and used for target protein purification by NI-NTA agarose column. The collected protein was desalted to remove imidazole through the desalting column (50 mM KPi buffer pH 7.0 and 150 mM NaCl). After the desalting process, the purified protein was loaded into 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at 90 V for 15 min and 130 V for 60 min to verify the production of the recombinant proteins. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay. Finally, the purified protein was stocked at −80 °C for further study.

Enzymatic activity assay

To determine the catalytic activity of ADHs towards 1a (FMOs towards 1d), enzyme activity was determined by spectrophotometric assay at room temperature measuring the reduction of NAD(P)+ (oxidation of NAD(P)H) at 340 nm (ɛ = 6.22 mM−1 cm−1). The reaction mixture (3 mL) was composed of 5 μM the purified ADHs, 1 mM 1a and 0.5 mM NAD(P)+ (1 mM 1d and 0.5 mM NAD(P)H for FMOs activity assay). One unit of enzyme activity is defined as the amount of enzyme that catalyzes the reduction (or oxidation) of 1 μmol NAD(P)(H) per min under the assay conditions.

Kinetic assay of HLADH and mFMO

The kinetic parameters (Vmax, KM, kcat) were determined by assessing the initial reaction rates at different NAD(P)(H) concentrations at 30 °C in 50 mM KPi buffer, pH 7.0. The experimental procedure and method used for sample analysis were similar to the catalytic activity assay. The Michaelis-Menten equation was used to obtain the kinetic parameters.

Optimum conditions for the dehydrogenation reaction step catalyzed by HLADH

To determine the optimal conditions for the dehydrogenation of 1a catalyzed by HLADH, the conversion of 1a to 1d was utilized. The reactions were performed in 50 mM KPi pH 7.0 and 2.5 mM 1a, 0.5 mM NAD+ and 0.2 mg mL−1 purified HLADH and SmNOX were added at the beginning. KPi buffer (pH 5.5 − 8.0, 50 mM), Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0 − 9.0, 50 mM) and CHES-NaOH (pH 9.0 − 10.0, 50 mM) were used to determine the optimum pH. The optimal reaction temperature was determined by a range of temperatures from 20 °C to 45 °C with an interval of 5 °C.

In vitro reactions

To determine the feasibility of HLADH-mFMO cascade (in vitro), the one-pot one-step strategy was applied in vitro. In vitro reactions of HLADH-mFMO cascade were performed at a final volume of 1 mL in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. For the synthesis of 6,6′-DBI from 1a, the reactions were performed in 50 mM KPi pH 7.0. 2.5 mM 1a, 0.5 mM NAD+ and 0.4 mg mL−1 purified HLADH and mFMO were added to initiate the reactions. The reactions were stopped by incubating at 95 °C for 5 min, and the precipitated protein was removed by centrifugation before analysis via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Supplementary Table 7).

In vivo whole-cell biocatalysis

The cells used for the whole-cell reactions were harvested and washed by KPi buffer. For the twelve strains (M1-M12) containing different combinations of plasmids in the HLADH-mFMO cascade, the reactions were conducted in a 50 mL falcon tube with a final volume of 5 mL. Fresh cells were resuspended in the buffer containing 50 mM KPi pH 8.0, reaching a final OD600 of 20. 2.5 mM substrate 1a and 0.5 mM NAD+ were added to the above system to initiate the reaction at 30 °C and 250 rpm for 24 h. After 24 h, the samples were prepared by centrifugation for 5 min at 20,000 g before analysis via ultraviolet–visible light (UV–vis) spectroscopy.

HPLC analysis of reaction mixture

For quantification of 1a and 1d, 200 μL of the sample from reaction mixture was collected in Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm filters for HPLC analysis. Phenol was used as an internal standard for quantification, and ten-point standard curves were generated according to the concentrations of 1a and 1 d ranging from 0.01 to 2.5 mM. HPLC analysis was conducted as follows: HPLC-DAD, ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18, MeCN: H2O = 1: 1 for 30 min, flow rate: 0.4 mL min−1, injection volume: 10 µL. Analytes were detected by using a diode array detector (DAD) at a wavelength of 254 nm.

Quantitative analysis of 6,6′-DBI and indigoids

For extraction and quantification of 6,6′-DBI and indigoids, 400 μL of the reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was suspended in an 800 μL of dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) followed by vigorous vortexing. The mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 g, 10 min and the supernatants were diluted to half concentration with water. Absorbance of the samples was measured at the absorption maxima by ultraviolet–visible light (UV–vis) spectroscopy. Absorbances were converted into the concentrations of the 6,6′-DBI and indigoids from 0.005 to 0.25 mM yielding ten-point standard curves, where 6,6′-DBI and indigoids were dissolved in DMSO: H2O = 50%: 50% (v/v).

NMR analysis of 6,6′-DBI and indigoids

NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Ascend 400 MHz NMR spectrometer at 400 MHz (1H NMR) (Supplementary Figs. 18–57). Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm). Tetramethylsilane (TMS) was used as the reference substance for 1H and 13C chemical shifts.

Scale-up preparation of 6,6′-DBI

Similar to whole-cell biocatalysis section, 6,6′-DBI was scaled up to a 200 mL solution. After the reaction, the samples were prepared by centrifugation for 5 min at 20,000 g to collect the precipitates and subsequently washed by 50 mM KPi pH 7.0 (3 × 50 mL). The cleaned precipitate was lyophilized overnight to yield 6,6′-DBI crude extracts for further study.

Chemical dyeing procedure

The 6,6′-DBI cell dye was prepared by using the cells (OD600 = 20 in 200 mL of reaction mixture) collected from the reaction of the strain M1 in the consecutive one-pot one-step reaction system at a 1 L glass flask. After the reaction, the cells were collected and washed with water several times and freeze-dried. Dyeing solutions (distilled water 50 mL; tetrahydrofuran 7.5 mL, NaOH 0.5 g, Na2S2O4 0.5 g and chemical 6,6′-DBI 0.15 g or the produced cellular 6,6′-DBI dye) were prepared by heating with stirring on the 80 °C hot plate for 15 min. After the heating, NH4Cl 0.5 g was added to the dyeing solution and subsequent heating was repeated at 80 °C for 15 min. The fabrics (multifabric strips) were soaked into the solution and dyed using an automatic dyeing machine at 60 °C for 15 min. After the dyeing procedure, the fabrics were rinsed with 1% acetic acid solution and were dried under room temperature. Further repeated steps of washing with water and drying at room temperature were proceeded three times.

In-situ bio-dyeing strategy

The strain M1 was grown up to OD600 = 0.6–0.8 for IPTG induction in 400 mL of LB medium (containing 50 mg L−1 kanamycin) in a 1 L glass flask at 37 °C and 200 rpm, using 5 mL of seed culture of the same LB medium overnight grown under the same condition. After IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, the culture was incubated at 18 °C and 200 rpm for an additional 20 h. The collected cells were pelleted by centrifugation (4,000 g, 20 min, 4 °C) and washed with KPi solution for the following whole-cell reaction. The pelleted M1 cells were resuspended in 50 mM KPi, pH 8.0, and they were diluted down to OD600 = 20. The whole-cell reaction was proceeded by adding 2.5 mM 1a and 0.5 mM NAD+ at a final volume of 200 mL and the fabrics (multifabric strips) were soaked into the solution at the beginning of the whole-cell reaction. The dyeing process was conducted in a shaking incubator at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 24 h. After the dyeing procedure, the fabrics were washed with water and drying at room temperature proceeded three times.
