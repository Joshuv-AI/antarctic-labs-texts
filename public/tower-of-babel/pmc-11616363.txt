Predation on bacterial pathogens by predatory bacteria of sewage origin: three days prey-predator interactions
Authors: Zeinab Mohsenipour, Parya Arazi, Mikael Skurnik, Behnaz Jahanbin, Hamid Reza Abtahi, Maryam Edalatifard, Mohamad Mehdi Feizabadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPredatory bacteria are Gram-negative microorganisms that grow within and lyse their bacterial prey. As bacterial predators have potential applications in medicine and biotechnology, the aim of this study was to isolate and identify predators from environmental samples. Therefore, three environmental bacteria belonging to the genus Bdellovibrio were isolated and characterized.ResultsThe predator isolates tolerated pH range from 5 to 9 well, and were killed at pH ranges of 2 and 12. They survived best at 4 °C and 29 °C, tolerated to some extent -20 °C and even -80 °C, and were completely killed at 60° C. Furthermore, the host range analysis of the predator isolates was carried out on five Gram-negative (Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica) and two Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis). The environmental predator isolates inhibited Gram-negative preys more efficiently than Gram-positive. Predators inhibited S. enterica the most (70.48%) and P. aeruginosa the least (3.84%) among Gram-negative preys. The best inhibitory effect among Gram-positive preys was observed for S. aureus (29.83%).ConclusionsThe isolates showed broad-range predation on diverse preys under various pH and temperature conditions. Therefore, the predator isolates identified in here may be suitable choices for controlling the population of Gram-negative bacteria in different fields. Background

The increasing number of drug-resistant pathogenic bacteria has encouraged scientists to search for new solutions to tackle this threat. A variety strategies including, essential oils, combination therapies, phage therapy, and, more recently, predatory bacteria have been proposed [1, 2]. Predatory bacteria are a group of very small microorganisms that attack, invade and use other (preferentially Gram-negative) bacteria as their nutrition source, and in this process, they finally disintegrate them. These microorganisms have been isolated from ecosystems such as water environments, soil, and even human intestines [3–6]. The predatory bacteria have eighter obligate or facultative predation life cycles, depending on the environmental conditions [7]. The predators use periplasmic predation as their hunting mechanism is periplasmic predation, which means predatory bacteria attach to the outer membrane of the prey cell and then penetrate into the periplasmic space. Only single cell predator can enter the prey cell periplasm from where it starts feeding on the host cell. The predator grows longitudinally and eventually fills the whole periplasmic space, such as the two-cell complex called Bdelloplast. After a while, the predator is divided into several new cells and exits by destroying the prey cell [7, 8]. In addition, the production of predator metabolites is effective in prey cell digestion as well as in the use of its nutrients. For this reason, some studies also confirmed the ability of predatory bacteria against Gram-positive bacteria [9–11].

The newest phylogenetic studies have showed that predatory bacteria belong to two different phyla: Bdellovibrionota and Myxococcota; and Bdellovibrionota includes three classes: Bacteriovoracia, Bdellovibrionia, and Oligoflexia. Bacteriovoracia contains an order called Bacteriovoracales and a family called Bacteriovoracaceae with three important genera including Bacteriovorax, Halobacteriovorax, and Predibacter. Moreover, Bdellovibrionia includes an order called Bdellovibrionales and a family called Bdellovibrionaceae with the main known predatory genera Bdellovibrio, Vampirovibrio, Micavibrio named as BALOs (Bdellovibrio and Like Organisms). However, the new predatory bacteria, Vampirococcus, belongs to a different phylum Absconditabacteria [12–16]. Identifying the taxonomy of predatory bacteria helps to understand their physiological and ecological characteristics better. In this way, it can be highly effective in its optimal use for antimicrobial purposes as an alternative to antibiotics [17].

For these reasons, the present study was conducted to identify the phylogeny of predatory bacteria isolates, and also the predatory ability of important human pathogens, as well as their pH and temperature tolerance range.

Methods

Prey bacteria selection

Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as prey to isolate predatory bacteria from wastewater samples. Also, predation ability assessment of bacterial isolates was tested on some Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria including Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 17978, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 15442, E. coli ATCC 25922, Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis ATCC 13076, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 51299 [18]. In addition, the intraspecies predation potency was investigated against 25 clinical isolates of A. baumannii [19]. The clinical isolates were previously isolated from patients with acute respiratory disorders [20].

Predatory bacteria isolation and purification

The raw sewage sample was collected from Tehran wastewater treatment plant and transported to the laboratory. To enrich the sample, an overnight E. coli broth culture was prepared in nutrient broth medium. Then, one milliliter of microbial suspension with an opacity equal to 0.5 McFarland and 25 ml of raw sewage samples were added to the sterile Erlenmeyer flask and incubated for 24 h at 29 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. Subsequently, two series of centrifugation (2-16KL refrigerated centrifuge SIGMA, USA) were performed on the enriched sample: 500xg for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was centrifuged at 20000xg for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, the sediment was dissolved again in cold HM buffer (6 g Hepes powder, 3 ml MgCl2 (0.5 M), and 1 ml CaCl2 (0.5 M); pH = 7.4; all materials were from Merck, Germany) and filtered through the 0.45 μm pore membrane filter. The filtrate (possibly BALOs suspension) was stored at 4 °C [21].

To confirm the presence of BALOs in the suspension, a double-layer agar technique was performed. Briefly, a serial dilution of possible BALOs suspension (10–107) was prepared in HM buffer. Then, 100 μl of each dilution together with 350 μl of 0.5 McFarland E. coli as a prey were added to molten HM top agar with 0.7% (w/v) agar content. HM top agar was spread on the bottom agar 1.5%, and plates were incubated at 29 °C for 3–7 days until the lysis plaques appeared. Lysis plaques were collected from the top agar by a sterile scalpel and suspended in an appropriate volume of HM buffer. Then 500 μl of overnight culture suspension of E. coli with a turbidity equal to 0.5 McFarland was added as prey bacteria. The co-cultures were incubated for 48 h at 29 °C and 180 rpm. The double-layer agar was preformed to observe predator plaques and a single plaque was collected to repeat the described process. This purification steps were repeated three times resulting in three pure strains named as A, C and G. These strains were stored with 20% glycerol at −80 °C for the subsequent use [22].

Molecular identification of isolated predatory bacteria

According to the wide range of predatory bacteria, we focused on identifying BALO isolates in the main genus, Bdellovibrio. Therefore, predator isolates were molecularly confirmed by DNA extraction, PCR amplification of partial 16 s rRNA gene, and sequencing of the fragments.

DNA extraction and BALOs PCR

At first, predator cells were passed through the 0.45 μm pore membrane filter, centrifuged at 27000xg for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was used for DNA extraction by standard phenol–chloroform extraction protocol. The DNA concentrations of the samples were measured with NanoDrop™ 2000 (Thermofisher Scientific, USA) [23]. PCR (Polymerase chain Reaction) amplification was performed using the 16S rDNA-specific primer pair 63F and 707R (Table 1) optimized for Bdellovibrionaceae [21]. PCR mixture in all reactions were adjusted a final volume of 25 μl containing 12.5 μl of 2 × PCR Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 1 μl of template DNA (25 ng/μl), 1 μl of each forward and reverse primers (0.2 µM), and proper volume of PCR grade double-distilled water (DDW) to 25 μl [21]. Bdellovibrionaceae PCR program was: 94 °C-5 min, 30 cycles (94 °C-1 min, 56 °C-1 min, 72 °C-1 min), and 72 °C-5 min for final extension [24]. For the negative control, the template DNA was omitted and replaced with DDW, and as positive control, genomic DNA of Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus HD100 (ATCC15356) was used.

Partial 16srRNA gene sequencing and phylogeny analysis

All the PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, and then amplicons were sent for sequencing to Microsynth AG company (Swiss). The sequencing results were analyzed with NCBI BLAST search and MEGAX program [25]. Phylogenetic assignment of each predator strain was registered, and the accession numbers in the GenBank database are: PP948523 (Bdellovibrio sp. A), PP939827 (Bdellovibrio sp. C), and PP940008 (Bdellovibrio sp. G).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of the predator isolates was carried out by the negative-staining as Evans et al. described [26]. About 20 μl of co-culture predator of each isolate and A. baumannii as prey were placed on the carbon-formvar-coated grids. Then, the extra suspension was removed from the grids, and staining was performed with uranyl acetate 1% for 1 min. Extra dye was removed, and they were studied by Carl Zeiss AG TEM when the grids were dry.

pH and temperature stability assays

According to the similarities between bacteriophages and predatory bacteria, pH and temperature stability assays were performed as per Kaur Litt and Jaroni protocol [24] with some modifications. In both tests, pure and highly active predator suspension were used. For this purpose, a co-culture of predator and prey was prepared, and after 48 h incubation at 29 °C, the predator was purified by filtering through the 0.45 μm membrane filter. The cell concentration of this predator’s suspension was determined by double-layer agar technique. Subsequently, the pure predator cells underwent exposure to pH and temperature treatments. pH stability test of the predator isolates was carried out by incubating each strain in HM buffers with five different pH values [2, 5, 7, 9, 12] for 1 h, 5 h, and 24 h. For temperature stability testing, predator isolate suspension was incubated at five different temperatures (−80 °C, −20 °C, 4 °C, 29 °C, and 60 °C) for 1 h, 5 h, and 24 h. The starting predator cell concentration for each test was adjusted to 109 PFU (plaque forming unit)/ml, and the predator cell concentration after the treatments was determined by double-layer agar technique [24].

The Predator host-range assays

Prey range assessments

The predation range of predatory isolates was investigated by co-culturing each isolate with five Gram-negative and two Gram-positive bacteria as shown in section “ Prey bacteria selection”. The prey ranges for each predator isolate were determined by measuring the colony forming units (CFU) of each prey bacteria in the shaken co-cultures during the 72 h incubation at 29 °C [27]. Briefly, 200 μl of pure predator isolates suspension and 600 μl of 0.5 McFarland of prey bacterial suspension were added into 20 ml HM buffer. The prey CFU/ml of the co-culture was determined immediately at time point 0 h. The co-culture was then incubated with shaking at 180 rpm at 29 °C, and the prey CFU/ml was measured from samples withdrawn at 3 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h time points.

Predation assessments by the spot test

The ability of the predator isolates to use 25 clinical isolates of A. baumannii as preys was determined using the spot-test as described previously with some modifications [29]. For further study purposes with predatory isolates, A. baumannii was used as the prey of choice for the intraspecific test. Briefly, 250 μl of 0.5 McFarland prey culture was added to molten 0.7% HM top agar and spread on 1.5% HM bottom agar. After solidification of the top agar, 10 μl of the pure predator isolate suspension was applied on the top agar. Plates were incubated at 29 °C for 3–7 days until the lysis plaques became distinct. As described by Ezzedine et al. [30], the predation ability was classified into four types according to the number and diameters of lysis plaques: (-) No predation, ( +) few plaques, (+ +) moderate number of plaques, and (+ + +) multiple and big plaques.

Data and statistical analysis

All tests were performed in triplicate, and data sets were analyzed by GraphPad-Prism version 9. The results of pH and temperature resistance assay, and also, prey range assessment was explored by Two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to compare means. The test was followed by Tukey post-hoc analysis to determine the significance of the effect of variables including incubation time, predator type, and pH or temperature values on PFU of predators after treatment as an indicator of predator survival ability. In the similar way, the tests were used to determine effect of the variables including incubation time, predator isolates and prey on the prey CFU change as an index of predation ability. Also, intraspecies predation differences between clinical strains of A. baumannii were examined by the chi-squared test.

Results

Isolation and identification of predatory bacteria

Co-culture of prey bacteria and wastewater sample clarification confirmed the presence of possible predatory bacteria in the sample (Figure A-a). In total, ten putative BALOs were isolated from the sewage samples. Three of them, which made a large lysis halo on the double-layer agar, were chosen for additional characterization (Figure A-b). After triple purification, the DNA of the isolates named A, C, and G, was used as template in 16S-rDNA PCR with the primer pair 63F and 707R. For all isolates a PCR product of expected size was obtained as preliminary evidence of Bdellovibrio spp. isolation (Fig. 1).

Based on the 16srRNA sequencing and BLAST search, predatory isolates A, C, and G were related to the genus Bdellovibrio (Fig. 2). Predator isolates A (PP948523), C (PP939827), and G (PP940008) showed maximum identity with Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus strain SAT19-05–28 (MW158316.1), Bdellovibrio svalbardensis (OR698905.1), and Bdellovibrio sp. Strain GF2 (MK415060.1) with the identity percentage 93.31%, 96.79%, and 94.51% respectively. The nearest type strains of Bdellovibrio with these isolates were Bdellovibrio svalbardensis strain PAP01 (NZ_JANRMI010000008.) with 96.79% of identity for isolate C, and Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus strain 109 J (NZ_CP007656.1) with identity 93.29% for isolate A, and 94.51% with isolate G.

pH and temperature stability assays

The survival ability of the predatory isolates was investigated for the first time, in a wide range of temperatures and pH. Data analysis showed that the pH and temperature values, as well as, treatment time have a significant effect (P-value ≤ 0.05) on the survival ability of the predator isolates (Figs. 4 and 5). The survival rates after the treatments were examined using the double-layer predatory test with E. coli as a prey (Figs. 4 and 5). While the predators tolerated pH values of 7 and 9 very well, at pH 5 they started to be less optimal, and at pH 2 and 12, almost all isolates were killed after 24 h incubation (Fig. 4). At pH 5 the predator survival decreased over time, and at pH 12, 20–50% of the predators survived for 5 h.

The temperature tolerance of the predators was determined by incubating them for 1 h, 5 h, and 24 h at five temperatures: −80 °C, −20 °C, 4 °C, 29 °C, and 60 °C (Fig. 5). Freezing the predators to −80 °C without cryoprotectants was not tolerated well as by 24 h survival rate was below 5%. The predators survived a little better when frozen to −20 °C with a survival rate of 10–20% by 24 h. The predators survived best at 4 °C with a survival rate of 92–95% by 24 h, followed by 29 °C with a survival rate of 81–86% by 24 h. The predators were rapidly killed at 60 °C (Fig. 5).

Five Gram-negative (A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, S. enterica) and two Gram-positive (S. aureus, and E. faecalis) strains of different species were used to test the predatory range of the isolates (Fig. 6 and Table 2). Predatory efficiency was determined by decreasing the CFU of prey along the treatment time. Analyzing the results for each tested prey showed a significant change (p-value ≤ 0.05) in CFU depending on the predator type and treatment time (Fig. 6).

In double-layer agar plates, predators formed lysis plaque only on Gram-negative preys, and no lysis could be detected on Gram-positive preys. However, in liquid cultures some CFU/mL values showed reduction also for Gram-positive preys due to the presence of predatory bacteria. However, slightly positive predatory efficiency values were reached only for isolate G for both Gram-positive preys and isolate C for S. aureus, other values were negative (Table 2). The only significant reduction (p-value ≤ 0.05) in the prey CFU was observed for isolate G in co-culture with S. aureus (Fig. 6 and Table 2). These results indicate that the predator isolates may not use Gram-positive bacteria as preys.

The predatory efficiencies were calculated at 3 h time point, as CFU/mL reduction percentages for the preys with each predator were calculated using the formula in section “ Prey range assessments”. The formula removes the effect of normal prey growth from the predator’s ability to reduce the prey CFU allowing to reach more accurate results of predation efficiency. With Gram-negative bacteria, the most efficient predation was observed for isolates C and A on S. enterica (70.48% and 61.53% reduction, respectively). Table 2 confirms that all the predatory isolates efficiently reduced the numbers of S. enterica, E. coli, K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii and less efficiently P. aeruginosa (except isolate A). Changes in CFU over time for each prey in the treatment with predator isolates are shown in Fig. 6. Also, the significance of prey reduction compared to control at the same time point (3 h, 24 h, 48 ​​h and 72 h) was characterized based on P values. Although prey reduction in a 3 h period is considered as the predator efficiency, the significant reduction at all test time points also confirmed that S. enterica is the most preferable prey for predator isolates (Fig. 6-g).

Clinical A. baumannii strains as preys

The ability of predator isolates to use 25 clinical isolates of A. baumannii as preys was assessed by spot tests. Clinical A. baumannii isolates were selected to find out whether this notorious opportunistic pathogen could be targeted by the predators in future. The spot test results were scored based on the number and diameter of the lysis plaques (Table 3). Almost all 25 A. baumannii strains were susceptible to the three predatory isolates. Based on the chi-square test results, there was no significant difference in the inhibitory ability of different predator isolates on different prey strains.

Discussion

The increasing number of infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens has become a global threat. For this reason, the World Health Organization (WHO) regularly publishes a list of pathogens that require immediate attention due to antibiotic resistance [1, 2]. From that list, A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, S. enterica, S. aureus, and E. faecalis were investigated in our research as potential prey organisms of the predator isolates.

As important criteria for different applications of microorganisms are their ability to tolerate different temperatures and pH ranges. Here we examined the predation ability of the environmental predatory isolates after subjecting them for different times to a wide pH and temperature ranges (Figs. 5 and 6). The results showed that the predators survived best at pH 7 and 4 °C. These conditions are therefore likely the optimum for the storage of the isolates. In general, the pH range of 5–9 was well tolerated by the predator isolates even up to 24 h of treatment. However, pH 9 was much more favorable, which can be justified considering the isolation of predatory isolates from raw wastewater. The predators tolerated low temperatures better, and were rapidly killed at 60 °C. This is consistent with the fact that BALOs predation ability is higher at 29 °C compared to 37 °C [22]. It should be noted that the number of viable predator isolates decreased at 24 h time point in all pH and temperature treatments, even at optimal values. This may be attributed to the dependence of predator survival on the presence of prey in the environment. For this reason, the guidelines recommend storing the predators with their preys at different temperature conditions [22].

The results of Table 2 show that the predators were not efficient on the tested Gram-positive bacteria (except isolate G). Also, the graph observed for Gram-positive differed from that of the predatory cycle on gram-negative bacteria. The CFU-fold change was consistent with the control group, except for the decrease observed at 24 h.

The pattern of prey CFU changes over time pertaining the function of the predation cycle. Contrary to the classic characterization of BALOs, recent studies confirm the ability of some predators to inhibit the growth of Gram-positive bacteria [31–33]. Iebba et al. described the epibiotic predation for Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus, when a predator encounters S. aureus in the environment. The change in predation strategy means the release of hydrolytic enzymes such as proteinase K, which has been proven to exist in B. bacteriovorus [34], in order to destroy the Gram-positive prey and the predator’s survival [31]. Also, the absence of lysis plaques with a slight reduction in the number of S. aureus and E. fecalis by B. bacteriovorus was reported in the study of Waso et al. [32].

The observed difference in the Gram-positive predation diagram is due to metabolites released from the predatory bacteria, which reduces preys without going through a normal predatory cycle. On the other hand, 24 h is an optimum period for metabolites accumulation and lysis in some Gram-positive preys. After the decreasing predator’s population, due to the inability to enter the prey and division, and the removal of metabolites from the environment, the opportunity to reproduce again is provided for the surviving prey bacteria. In addition, cell lysate in the co-culture environment is a food source that supports prey re-growth. These phenomena are why both types of Gram-positive and Gram-negative preys, increase prey bacteria in the treatment group after 48 h, unlike the control group.

Similar to the results of the present study, the effectiveness of predatory bacteria on various Gram-negative preys has been confirmed in other studies [31, 32, 35–39]. In the present study, E. coli and S. enterica were the most favorite prey for the predator isolates, but P. aeruginosa was inhibited very slightly as reported by others [32, 35, 38].

The differences observed in the CFU reduction graph of Gram-negative prey are due to differences in the type of bacteria, but most of them showed a similar graph corresponding to the predatory cycle. In the first three hours of co-culture incubation, a decrease in prey CFU was observed, which was corresponding to the time of attachment and completion of a predator reproduction cycle and, finally prey lysis. Subsequently, up to 24 h, the growth of the prey bacteria increased. A similar algorithm for CFU reduction of Gram-negative prey has been reported in other studies [33, 36, 39].

This pattern can be explained by the predation-derived waste model [40]. According to this, the nutrients released from the lysed prey, which are resistant to the predator's action, cause the population of prey bacteria to multiply. In addition, this period provides the susceptible prey with the opportunity to escape getting lysed by the predator. However, since predation is a dynamic process after the population of susceptible prey had increased, predatory bacteria re-invaded this population and caused a decrease in prey CFU within 48 h of incubation. Therefore, the prey debris was regenerated, and an opportunity was provided for the growth of bacteria within 72 h. This conclusion is compatible with the mathematical model that Baker et al. described for B. bacteriovorus s in HM buffer [40].

The findings of the intraspecies predatory test on 25 clinical strains of A. baumannii confirmed no significant functional difference for different strains of the same prey, regardless of their different characteristics. Various characteristics of these strains, especially different antibiotic resistance, were shown in our previous study [19]. In other words, our study confirmed the findings of other researches that the predation process is not influenced by the metabolic activity of the prey [35, 41].

Conclusion

In this study, we isolated three predatory bacteria strains from raw sewage, and identified them by sequencing a partial 16srRNA gene which confirmed that the isolates belong to Bdellovibrio genus. Also, our result confirmed the predator isolates tolerated the pH range 5–9 with optimum at pH 7. While the predators survived best at 4 °C and then 29 °C, and were killed at 60 °C, to some they tolerated extent freezing conditions. The predator survival seemed to depend on the presence of the prey, since without the prey even at optimum pH and temperature, the number of predators decreased after 24 h. Although, the isolated predators targeted a wide range of Gram-negative prey, they showed poor inhibition against the tested Gram-positive prey. Also, the CFU reduction algorithm of Gram-positive and Gram-negative prey is different, confirming the different predation behavior of the predators with them. The world of predatory bacteria still has many unanswered questions: prey physiology, microbial metabolites, interaction with different prey, and many similar things that are very important to understand. For example, the results of the present study clarified the importance of a more detailed understanding of the metabolism of predatory bacteria against Gram-negative and Gram-positive prey. Therefore, answering such questions can pave the way for better use of this group of microorganisms for various medical and industrial purposes.
