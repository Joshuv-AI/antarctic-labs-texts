Climate change and health system preparedness: A scoping review of adaptation plans in Central America and the Caribbean
Authors: Lidia Bonilla-Zarrazaga, Ashley Arriaga, Sofia Castro-Vargas, José Boue, Sebastian Bauhoff, Ana Mylena Aguilar-Rivera, Pamela Gongora-Salazar, Patrick Fahr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEvidence on how health is incorporated into climate adaptation planning in Central America and the Caribbean remains limited. We conducted a scoping review following PRISMA-ScR guidelines to characterise how climate-health adaptation is reflected across planning documents from 20 countries in this region and the extent to which key dimensions are addressed. Official documents published between 2000 and 2025 were retrieved from the UNFCCC repository and government websites. A total of 147 national and seven cross-border planning documents were reviewed using a structured framework covering six dimensions: objectives, actions, disproportionately affected populations, costing and funding, monitoring and evaluation, and cross-border collaboration. Climate-health objectives and actions are widely included, followed by references to disproportionately affected populations, whereas only four countries reached the highest level of detail in costing and funding information, suggesting an area where future planning could support implementation and policy translation.Keywords: Climate-health adaptation, Health systems, Climate change, Adaptation planning, Central America and the Caribbean, Health policy

Introduction

Climate change has emerged as a systemic determinant of population health. It increases exposure to extreme weather events, shifts epidemiological profiles, and places unprecedented stress on health system capacity.1 Climate change is expected to cause around 14.5–15.6 million deaths by 2050 in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with a cumulative economic burden exceeding US$ 20.8 trillion over the same period.2

Recent evidence suggests that, as global warming increases from 1.5 °C to 2.0 °C, many tropical and subtropical regions, including Central America and the Caribbean, will experience a substantial rise in cooling-degree days (a measure of heat exposure above a base temperature of 18 °C).3 Such climatic anomalies, defined as deviations from long-term average climate conditions, are already exacerbating the region’s burden of climate-sensitive diseases, contributing to excess mortality, and placing growing pressure on health systems.4, 5, 6 In parallel, changes in rainfall patterns, reflecting both natural variability and climate change, have been associated with a 20–30% increase in dengue transmission in Latin America and a similar increase in Zika transmission in the Caribbean.7

To address these challenges, the international community has emphasised the need to integrate health into climate adaptation action.8 The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) process, developed under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), supports Least Developed Countries (LDCs) in assessing and planning their longer-term climate adaptation needs.9 Health National Adaptation Plans (HNAPs) are recommended to guide public health adaptation strategies.10 Complementary instruments include Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), which outline climate commitments, and National Communications (NCs), which report on mitigation and adaptation progress. These mechanisms support national planning and facilitate access to climate finance and implementation support.11,12 Given the transboundary nature of climate and health, cross-border collaboration is also critical for public health adaptation, by enhancing coordination, stakeholder engagement, and the identification of shared priorities.13,14

These efforts are further supported by the World Health Organization (WHO) through the Quality Criteria for HNAPs, which guide countries in defining climate-health adaptation measures. This framework emphasises resourcing, monitoring, evaluation, and reporting to support implementation. It also provides recommendations on comprehensive coverage of climate-sensitive health risks and adaptation actions, government commitment, cross-sectoral coordination, and the needs of populations disproportionately affected by climate-related health risks.10 Complementing this, WHO’s Operational Framework for Climate-Resilient Health Systems identifies key aspects of climate-resilient health systems, including elements relevant to adaptation planning.15

Although WHO’s Global Survey indicated that by 2021 more than half of responding countries reported having a national health and climate change plan in place,16 evidence on climate-health adaptation planning in Central America and the Caribbean remains limited. Existing studies in LMICs primarily examine broader regional trends,17 group countries within broader LMICs categories,12 assess other regions,5,11,18 or include selected countries from Central America and the Caribbean under the umbrella of Latin America.19 This may obscure the region’s distinct environmental contexts and vulnerabilities, including high exposure to tropical cyclones, flooding, and drought in Central America20; and to sea-level rise, coastal hazards, and extreme weather events in the Caribbean.21 Additionally, there is a lack of visibility on government plans that may exist but are not submitted to international platforms. This may reflect reporting burdens, challenges in reconciling context-specific national planning with standardised global reporting, and the difficulty of identifying adaptation-related actions that may exist across sectors but are not explicitly included or framed within climate change adaptation plans.22 Understanding whether and to what extent adaptation plans address health vulnerabilities in this region could inform effective action and policy development.

This scoping review aims to assess the existence of climate-health adaptation planning across 20 countries in Central America and the Caribbean and to characterise the extent to which planning documents address key dimensions. It contributes to filling this knowledge gap by identifying and mapping the extent and specificity of content across UNFCCC instruments (NAPs, HNAPs, NDCs, and NCs), as well as additional national and regional cross-border efforts that are often overlooked. The analysis considers recommendations from WHO’s Quality Criteria for HNAPs and WHO Operational Framework for Climate-Resilient Health Systems.

Methods

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist23 and the methodological framework for scoping reviews proposed by Arksey and O’Malley.24 Data charting criteria were informed by recommendations from WHO frameworks, and a structured charting framework was developed using an approach adapted from Paz-Soldán et al.,5 as detailed in section 2.3. This framework was intended as a mapping tool, rather than a quality assessment of the planning documents.

Search strategy and selection criteria

The search strategy was designed to identify official policy and planning documents, which are often not indexed in academic databases and are dispersed across government websites and institutional repositories. Accordingly, non-journal sources were used as the primary sources for identifying official adaptation planning documents relevant to the study objectives, consistent with scoping review methodology.25,26 All searches were conducted between June and July 2025.

Searches were executed in two phases. In the first phase (the National phase), the UNFCCC repository served as the primary source for retrieving HNAPs, NAPs, NDCs, and NCs from all the countries included in the review. To identify additional national-level climate and health planning documents, supplementary searches were conducted on official government websites. However, as many of these websites were outdated or had incomplete document coverage, the search strategy was expanded to include Google Boolean searches combining climate- and health-related terms with each country’s official government domain (for example, “climate change” AND “health” AND “adaptation” site:.gov.tt). This approach was more effective for identifying dispersed national documents. Searches were conducted in the official language of each country. For Haiti, artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used to replicate the Boolean search strategy in French, aligned with searches conducted in English and Spanish, and to support the identification of potentially relevant national documents. All documents identified through this process were subsequently screened and assessed by LB, using the same eligibility criteria used for other countries.

The second phase (the Regional phase) involved retrieving documents from multi-country health and climate change initiatives spanning Central America and the Caribbean. The strategy used in the National phase was replicated, substituting country names with the names of regional organisations and their acronyms (for example, “Caribbean Public Health Agency”, “CARPHA”) to identify relevant cross-border documents. Searches were conducted in English and Spanish.

Both phases covered the period 2000–2025. For each country and organisation, up to 50 search results were screened. When searches yielded fewer than 50 results, all available results were reviewed. The limit of 50 search results was applied as a pragmatic and consistent approach across countries to ensure feasibility and comparability. The number of hits reviewed, documents retrieved, publication years, and relevant notes were systematically recorded in a structured Excel log file for each phase and an Appendix. The full Boolean search strings are reported in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Selection of sources of evidence

The eligibility criteria for this scoping review were guided by the Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework, as recommended for scoping review methodology (see Supplementary Tables S3 and S4).27

The Central American countries considered for the study were Belize, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama. The Caribbean countries considered were Antigua and Barbuda, Bahamas, Barbados, Cuba, Dominica, Dominican Republic, Grenada, Haiti, Jamaica, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and Trinidad and Tobago. Non-sovereign territories and overseas departments or dependencies (e.g., British, Dutch, or French territories) were excluded, as the focus was on sovereign states.

The review targeted publicly accessible official national and multi-country regional documents addressing the intersection of climate change and health in countries geographically situated in Central America and the Caribbean. Official documents were defined as those issued by national governments or documents reflecting a formal governmental commitment. When the same strategy was found more than once, but for different periods, only the most recent one was included in the review. If a strategy covered a period that had already ended (e.g., 2020–2023), but no subsequent strategy had been issued, it was retained for inclusion. Subnational, project-specific documents, or grey literature not endorsed by governments or multilateral organisations, were excluded. Documents from Haiti were translated into English for review and selection.

Screening was conducted in two stages. The initial screening was performed by a single reviewer (LB) based on the title, table of contents, and executive summary of each document. Screening was not conducted in duplicate. Where eligibility was uncertain, a second stage involved discussion among the review team, with inclusion decided by consensus. While dual independent screening is recommended for scoping reviews, a single-reviewer screening approach combined with a validation procedure may be considered a pragmatic alternative.28

Data charting

The data charting process followed a structured charting framework adapted from Paz-Soldán et al. (2023). Their approach assesses the extent to which adaptation planning documents integrate climate-sensitive health risks and specify intersectoral health-related actions. The data charting criteria of this study were informed by the WHO Quality Criteria for HNAPs (2021) and the WHO Operational Framework for Building Climate-Resilient and Low Carbon Health Systems (2023). These frameworks provide recognized and comprehensive guidance for structuring climate-health adaptation planning, facilitating comparison across national and multi-country documents. Nonetheless, their application does not guarantee effective outcomes without considering country-specific capacities, priorities, and constraints.29

The criteria were developed to assess whether adaptation planning documents include climate-health related objectives and actions, costing and funding, monitoring and evaluation (M&E) mechanisms, the needs of populations disproportionately affected by climate-related health risks, and multi-country regional collaboration. Populations disproportionately affected refer to those facing greater impacts due to pre-existing health inequities, shaped by structural, social, and economic determinants rather than being inherent in specific groups.10 While the criteria do not replicate either WHO framework verbatim (e.g., the WHO Operational Framework comprises ten pillars), they are anchored in their conceptual and substantive content. For example, the charting of actions encompasses planning related to service delivery, health workforce, and system capacity (e.g., provision of essential medical products and technologies), to capture key operational components of climate-resilient health systems. The charting criteria therefore cover six dimensions reflecting key components of policy planning, allowing for a more streamlined and consistent assessment across heterogeneous documents.

An Excel-based data charting form for each phase was developed by LB and reviewed by PG and PF to refine its structure and content before being shared with the broader reviewer team. This form included a predefined rubric ranging from 0 (no mention) to 3 (comprehensive and explicit inclusion), as presented in Table 1, with minor adaptation for the Regional phase where Multi-country regional collaboration was not included to avoid redundancy. For the purposes of this study, “climate-health content” was defined as the presence of explicit sections, subsections, or mentions of the intersection between climate change and health. Data charting followed two steps. In both the national and regional phases, the reviewers were divided into two groups: one for the initial charting and the other for validation. Each reviewer was responsible for a subset of countries in the National phase, or documents in the Regional phase. The first group assigned a level to each document using the predefined rubric and documented their rationale with written notes, direct quotations, and page references from the source documents, consistent with Paz-Soldán et al. (2023). To ensure consistency in interpretation of the rubric and data charting, the reviewers held weekly meetings to discuss any unclear criteria, classification challenges and resolve interpretive discrepancies. Subsequently, the second group of reviewers validated the assigned levels, verifying the accuracy and consistency of the rationale and the documents content. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved among the reviewers. This validation served as a mechanism to enhance the credibility of the charting approach and reduce potential bias. In the National phase, initial data charting was conducted by LB, AA, SC, and JB, each responsible for documents from approximately four to five different countries, and validation was conducted by PG, PF, SB, and AMA. In the Regional phase, initial data charting was conducted by LB, AA, and SC and validation was conducted by PG, PF, and JB.

The purpose of the data charting framework was not to appraise document quality, but to systematically characterise the extent and specificity with which key climate-health adaptation dimensions were addressed across diverse planning documents. The associated rubric captures the extent to which climate-health adaptation content is explicitly documented. The results should therefore be interpreted as indicators of how climate-health considerations are articulated in these documents, rather than as measures of the overall quality, coherence, or implementation readiness of national or regional adaptation policies.

Because this review focused on assessing the existence and extent of climate-health content across national planning instruments rather than the average quality of individual documents, multiple documents were charted for each country in the National phase. Accordingly, the data charting form retained the highest level of inclusion observed for each criterion across all documents. This approach allows to minimise the risk of underestimating the presence of key elements due to existing fragmentation of adaptation planning across multiple policy documents, ensuring that countries receive full credit for relevant content, even if it was mentioned in only one document. To reduce potential bias due to the number of documents reviewed per country, a sensitivity analysis was conducted comparing the results with those obtained when only UNFCCC documents were included. In the Regional phase, data charting was conducted at the document level, and participating countries were recorded in an Excel log file.

Inter-rater reliability

An inter-rater reliability assessment was conducted to measure the consistency of data charting across reviewers for each dimension in the National phase and Regional phase.30 In the National phase, agreement was evaluated based on the final levels of inclusion assigned to each country and in the Regional phase it was based on the level of inclusion assigned to each document reviewed, using the predefined 0–3 rubric. For example, Costa Rica (n = 8 documents) inter-rater agreement was calculated using its final aggregated score per dimension, rather than at the document level. As the analysis was conducted on aggregated country-level scores, inter-rater reliability reflects agreement at this level of synthesis.

Agreement was quantified using the weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient with quadratic weights. Results were interpreted using established benchmarks which classify agreement as poor (<0), slight (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (0.81–1.00).31

Synthesis of results

Data analysis was facilitated by constructing a charting framework for each phase. For the National phase, a summary charting framework was used, in which the highest level of inclusion per dimension per country was retained to ensure that relevant content was not underrepresented due to document fragmentation. Quantitative information (e.g., number of documents retrieved by type, number of countries with a level of inclusion of 3) was summarized using simple frequency counts and tabulations (see Supplementary Table S5). In parallel, a thematic analysis was conducted to identify common patterns and gaps across the documents that received a level of inclusion 3. This included examining the climate-health risks and health system building blocks addressed in the objectives and actions; the approaches proposed for cost and funding and M&E; the actions planned for populations disproportionately affected by climate-related health risks; and the strategies outlined for multi-country regional collaboration.

Critical appraisal of the included documents was not conducted, in line with the objectives and scope of this scoping review. The aim of the study was to characterise the existence and extent to which national adaptation planning addresses the specific health challenges posed by climate change, rather than to evaluate the methodological quality or rigor of the documents themselves.

For the Regional phase, the analysis was conducted at the document level rather than the organisation or regional level (see Supplementary Table S6), as there was some overlap of countries among documents found. Each document was individually reviewed and charted across dimensions, with levels of inclusion directly recorded in the charting framework without aggregation.

Results

General findings

Inter-rater reliability was high across dimensions. In the National phase, weighted kappa ranged from 0.72 to 0.92 (substantial to almost perfect agreement) (Supplementary Table S28). The highest agreement was for actions (0.92), and costing and funding (0.86), both corresponding to almost perfect. Substantial agreement was observed for M&E (0.79), objectives (0.78), multi-country regional collaboration (0.78), and disproportionately affected populations (0.72). No dimensions showed fair or moderate agreement, indicating strong consistency across the data charting framework. These kappa values reflect agreement on aggregated country-level charting rather than document-level charting and therefore may not fully capture disagreements at the document level. In the Regional phase, agreement was perfect across all dimensions (1.00) (Supplementary Table S29), however, these estimates were based on only seven documents and should therefore be interpreted cautiously rather than as confirmatory evidence of reliability.

In the National phase, 270 documents were identified from UNFCCC and government websites. Of these, 154 were reviewed based on their title, table of contents, and executive summary. After review, seven documents were excluded for lacking climate-health content, leaving 147 eligible documents for charting (Fig. 1).

National phase findings

Only ten of the 20 countries included have a NAP. All have submitted NDCs and NCs, and none has submitted an HNAP to the UNFCCC. Beyond UNFCCC documents, 97 additional documents were reviewed, comprising primarily documents from climate sector (n = 54), followed by national level (n = 24), health sector (n = 11), and from other sectors (e.g., gender) (n = 8).

As a sensitivity check, we compared results using only UNFCC documents (see section 2.3). Differences were minor, with improvements to the highest inclusion level in 17 out of 120 country–dimension combinations (14.2%). When all plans were considered, Costa Rica, Jamaica, Nicaragua, and Panama improved to the highest level in objectives; Belize, El Salvador, Nicaragua, and the Dominican Republic in actions. For populations disproportionately affected, Belize and Panama advanced to the highest level; Barbados was the only case in costing and funding; Honduras, Haiti, Jamaica, and Panama advanced in M&E; and Barbados and the Dominican Republic advanced in multi-country regional collaboration. For this reason, and to ensure a more complete representation of national planning efforts, results below include UNFCCC and additional national documents.

To further examine the potential influence of document availability across countries, a count of documents reaching level 3 for each dimension and country are presented in Supplementary Table S7. In total, 27 documents reached level 3 in the objectives dimension, 13 in actions, 21 in populations disproportionately affected, six in cost and funding, seven in M&E, and two in multi-country regional collaboration. The occurrence of level 3 was not limited to countries with larger document portfolios. For instance, Guatemala (12 documents reviewed) and the Bahamas (four documents reviewed) each achieved level 3 in three dimensions. Similarly, Jamaica (11 documents reviewed) and Cuba (four documents reviewed) reached level 2 in two dimensions. Supplementary Tables S8–S27 present the data charting for each country.

The highest levels of inclusion were more frequent in objectives and actions, with 15 and 13 countries, respectively. Seven countries reached this level for populations disproportionately affected, while four did so for costing and funding, and five for M&E. Only two countries reached the highest level in Multi-country regional collaboration. Countries with the highest overall levels of inclusion across dimensions were Panama, the Dominican Republic, Barbados, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras, and Jamaica. Among countries with the highest level of inclusion in objectives, top themes were public awareness-raising and emergency preparedness; and the most frequently identified climate-sensitive risks were vector-borne diseases, health systems functioning, and healthcare infrastructure. Other objectives include epidemiological surveillance, health information systems, integration of climate change into health planning, and institutional coordination for climate-health risk management. Corresponding actions include epidemiological surveillance and early warning systems, monitoring systems for climate-sensitive diseases, infrastructure improvements and equipment provision. Other reported actions are capacity building for health personnel, public awareness campaigns, digitalisation and health information systems. Activities also encompass disease control, including vector control in some cases, as well as health risk management in facilities, inter-institutional coordination, and emergency preparedness measures.

Climate-health disease actions most frequently included vector-borne diseases and health impacts of extreme climate events, with water-borne diseases and other water-related health impacts also frequently reflected. These were generally tied to surveillance and early warning systems; dengue and other arboviral diseases were cited in relation to surveillance and control measures but were often embedded within broader health system interventions rather than disease-specific interventions in the planning documents. Actions for water-borne diseases included water quality monitoring, wastewater management, and safe water and sanitation practices. Heat-related illnesses were also reflected in reported actions, although less frequently. Specific interventions included heat response planning, forecasting, and monitoring for heat waves.

Costing and funding content was heterogeneous. Some documents itemised activities and budget requirements without specifying funding sources or financing gaps, while other reported aggregate costs and gaps without linking them to specific activities. In several cases, costing was intersectoral, limiting attribution to health-specific actions. M&E content was mostly in documents separate from adaptation plans, health or climate strategies, and was framed at broader climate policy rather than climate-health specifically. Only Costa Rica included explicit M&E in its NAP.

Populations disproportionately affected by climate-related health risks were defined mainly by gender, geographic location, and poverty. Indigenous populations were explicitly mentioned in 37 of the 147 reviewed documents (25%), covering ten countries, whereas Afro-descendant populations were identified only in 18 documents (12%) across seven countries. Only seven documents (5%) specified adaptation actions (rubric level 3) for Indigenous populations and five (3%) for Afro-descendant populations. Explicit ethnic references were less frequent in the Caribbean, where countries commonly described their populations as predominantly Afro-descendant and used broader social and demographic categories to describe vulnerability.

Regarding multi-country collaboration, only two countries reached the highest level; however, this does not imply the absence of collaboration, as collaborative efforts may not be explicitly integrated within national planning documents.

Subregional differences were observed in both the number and type of documents available, with fewer documents per country in the Caribbean. Differences were also identified in the levels of inclusion across dimensions. While both subregions showed relatively higher levels in objectives and actions, Central American countries demonstrated a higher proportion of level 3 across most dimensions, particularly for climate-health objectives (100% vs. 62%) and monitoring and evaluation (43% vs. 15%). Both subregions showed similar patterns in the inclusion of disproportionately affected populations (43% vs. 31%), and lower levels in costing and funding (29% vs. 15%). A notable contrast emerged in the inclusion of multi-country regional collaboration, as the Caribbean reached level 3 in 15% of cases compared with none in Central America.

Regional phase findings

Seven documents were considered for data charting in the Regional phase. Documents focused on Central America were most frequent. Five were issued by the Sistema de la Integración Centroamericana (SICA) and its subsidiary bodies, the Consejo de Ministros de Salud de Centroamérica (COMISCA), and the Comisión Centroamericana de Ambiente y Desarrollo (CCAD). SICA includes Belize, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Panama, and the Dominican Republic. Of these five documents, two focused primarily on climate policy, one addressed agri-environmental issues with health relevance, one constituted a health policy, and one focused on food security and nutrition. For the Caribbean, only one regional document was identified, issued by the Pan-American Health Organization (PAHO), specifically addressing climate change and health. However, it does not specify the list of countries formally committed to the plan.

The remaining document was issued by the Secretaría General Iberoamericana, including countries from Central America and the Caribbean (Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama), along with others from Ibero-America. This broader regional action plan references both health and climate change, but addressed them in separate strategic axes, without explicit linkage between climate change and health actions.

As in the National phase, the most detailed dimensions were objectives and actions, followed by disproportionately affected populations, and M&E. Costing and funding remained least developed. Documents with the highest level in objectives emphasised intersectoral approaches, alongside commitments to climate-risk surveillance, early-warning systems, and public awareness. Actions outlined in documents with the highest level focused on operational measures, particularly strengthening surveillance and early-warning capacities. Content on disproportionately affected populations incorporated measures for Indigenous peoples, migrants, women, children, and other high-risk groups. However, the inclusion of Indigenous peoples was inconsistent and not always linked to specific actions. Explicit references to Afro-descendant populations were limited. No regional document reached the highest level in costing and funding or M&E, mirroring the limitations observed in national documents. Discussion

This study documents how climate-health challenges are reflected across planning instruments from Central America and the Caribbean. However, key aspects to plan the operationalisation of objectives appear to be less integrated. Attention to disproportionately affected populations is present but limited. Costing and funding, M&E, and multi-country collaboration are the least integrated dimensions. Importantly, these findings reflect the extent and specificity of documented climate-health planning content, rather than the feasibility, coherence, or implementation of adaptation measures.

Awareness of the challenges of climate-health adaptation aligns with previous studies from LMICs, including those conducted in Latin American countries.5,12 Similarly, lower specificity of actions across planning documents compared to the inclusion of objectives, may reflect the complexity of translating climate-health objectives into operational planning. Moving from objectives to actions requires data, diagnostics, and intersectoral coordination, further complicated in long-term planning contexts.32,33 While not directly assessed, these barriers may reflect institutional fragmentation and weak intersectoral coordination, limiting collaboration between health, environmental, and other sectors.34 Evidence also shows fragmented and limited climate-health data in the region.35,36

These challenges may be more pronounced for populations disproportionately affected by climate-related health risks, particularly Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations. These groups were largely absent in planning documents, in contrast to the more consistent integration of gender perspectives. Their disproportionate risks and limited visibility in planning may reflect barriers to public policy inclusion, predominance of top-down policy design, and limited disaggregated data.37,38 At the same time, in some Caribbean countries, the limited explicit reference to Afro-descendant populations may reflect their demographic predominance, reducing the likelihood that they are identified as a distinct vulnerable group in policy documents. These findings should also be interpreted in light of the charting framework used, which captured the inclusion of population groups and related actions but not the processes through which vulnerability is defined or certain groups become underrepresented. Future research could explore how these considerations shape climate-health planning in the region.

A similar lack of specificity is observed in climate-health diseases, which are predominantly framed through health system functions, suggesting that climate-health integration occurs at the system level rather than through disease-specific plans. Difficulties in defining actions extend to costing and funding, possibly explaining the absence of standalone HNAPs, with climate-health adaptation embedded in broader policy documents.39 This limitation is relevant, as clearly specified and costed actions are increasingly required by international climate finance mechanisms.

These patterns are not unique to the region. Global evidence shows that, although many countries have climate-health plans, implementation challenges persist, with insufficient financing identified.16 Studies from Latin America and the Caribbean report limited operational detail in adaptation funding, roles, and indicators.5 Beyond the region, monitoring systems remain weak due to data, capacity, and resource constraints,40 and priorities are acknowledged without being translated into coherent action.41

The Regional analysis shows that Central America has more policy documents, reflecting the structure of regional mechanisms such as SICA. However, despite fewer Caribbean documents identified, regional cooperation is well-established through institutions like the Caribbean Public Health Agency (CARPHA) and the Caribbean Community (CARICOM).42 This suggests that collaboration may be underestimated when assessed solely through documented instruments, consistent with policy-practice gaps.14 These findings do not suggest that regional approaches consistently yield more comprehensive planning than national frameworks, as strengths and limitations mirror national patterns.

This scoping review is the first to expand the analysis of adaptation planning measures to include official, government issued national documents other than NAPs, NDCs, or NCs. This approach strengthens the analysis of countries’ planning strategies, which may be overlooked when only the UNFCCC repository is analysed. Results show coherence between national and international commitments across Central America and the Caribbean. However, the content of internationally submitted documents still lacks important aspects, such as costing and monitoring mechanisms, that could hinder access to external funding.43

Within the scope of documented planning instruments, these findings help to clarify where countries in Central America and the Caribbean stand in climate-health adaptation planning. Based on these the results, three recommendations follow. First, countries could prioritise developing vulnerability assessments to identify and prioritise adaptation actions, and to better position planning efforts to demonstrate alignment with criteria required by international climate finance mechanisms. These could consider disproportionately affected populations, ensuring territorial rights and the inclusion of these groups in decision-making processes.37 Particular attention could be given to Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations, which were rarely referenced.37 Second, countries could strengthen cross-sectoral and cross-border collaboration in both planning and implementation of climate-health adaptation efforts. Evidence suggests that collaborative networks do not emerge spontaneously, as they entail transaction costs and collective action challenges.44 Addressing institutional fragmentation, particularly between Ministries of Health, Environment, and Finance, may reduce silos and support the development and execution of more comprehensive health adaptation plans. Regional collaboration may also strengthen countries’ ability to access international climate finance, share technical expertise, and achieve economies of scale in adaptation investments.

Third, countries could consider increasing international visibility of their climate-health adaptation planning efforts. Developing HNAPs while improving the specification and costing of adaptation actions may help countries present clearer planning priorities, investment needs and M&E frameworks, which are increasingly required by international climate finance mechanisms such as the Green Climate Fund and the Adaptation Fund. Similarly, estimating climate-health costs and financing needs is relevant within the emerging Loss and Damage agenda, as it may help inform resource allocation and international support. Existing adaptable frameworks such as CHEERS or SCALE-up45,46 could support assessment of health impacts, effectiveness, and climate risk management across scales. This may also strengthen the planning basis for longer-term policy commitments. In line with these recommendations, regional bodies like SICA, CARPHA and PAHO could support coordination, technical exchange, and accountability for climate-health adaptation efforts across countries.

Despite its contributions, this study has limitations. Google-based search strategies are less reproducible than database-based searches due to variation by user, location, and time. For this study, this approach was necessary to identify policy documents not indexed in academic databases. However, searches were fully documented to maximize transparency, following PRISMA 202047 and PRISMA-S48 standards. Nevertheless, full reproducibility remains inherently limited due to the dynamic nature of online content and search engine results over time.49 Language barriers in Haiti may also have constrained document identification and interpretation.

Bias may arise from uneven document availability across countries, as those with more retrieved documents may had greater opportunity to meet criteria. However, the sensitivity analysis suggested limited impact on the inclusion patterns. Additional limitations relate to the inter-rater reliability assessment as validation was not fully blinded, potentially inflating agreement estimates. Reliability in the National phase was assessed at the aggregated country level, which may not fully capture disagreement across documents.

The charting framework itself also presents a limitation. The criteria used to assess climate-health adaptation content were developed for the purposes of this study and have not been formally validated. While informed by established WHO frameworks and adapted from a published methodological approach, the framework should be understood as a structured mapping tool rather than a validated instrument. Accordingly, this study assesses the extent to which climate-health content is articulated in planning documents, rather than the quality, coherence, implementation, or implementation readiness of adaptation policies, and does not formally evaluate intersectoral coordination.

Further limitations relate to scope and depth. The exclusion of non-sovereign territories limits regional completeness and may affect the interpretation of regional patterns, particularly given the strong epidemiological and institutional linkages in the Caribbean. The analysis also excluded subnational plans, other sectoral documents, and intersectoral coordination mechanisms, and it did not assess gaps between policy and implementation capacity. The assessment of disproportionately affected populations was limited to their identification and actions, without exploring structural determinants, constraining equity analysis. Heterogeneity and blurred boundaries between mitigation, adaptation, and sectoral roles also complicated the analysis. Future research should examine implementation gaps, formally assess intersectoral coordination, and incorporate broader regional coverage and more in-depth equity analyses, including non-sovereign territories.

Conclusions

Climate-health adaptation needs in Central America and the Caribbean are acknowledged across national and regional planning documents. However, costing, funding, and monitoring are not fully integrated in policy documents. Although attention to disproportionately affected populations is present, further analysis is required to ensure structural determinants of vulnerability are integrated. These gaps may reflect constraints in data availability and institutional coordination, limiting the translation of policy objectives into comprehensive climate–health adaptation plans. Multi-country regional collaboration occurs primarily through international bodies rather than within national plans. Strengthening these dimensions may support effective protection of population health across the region.
