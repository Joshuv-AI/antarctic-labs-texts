Disorder-mediated non-equilibrium photocurrent redistribution enables homeostatic synaptic conditioning in AgBiS2 heterostructure
Authors: Hyun Woo Kim, Neul Ha, Hyun Min Kwon, Chan Kim, Jin Hyeon Kim, Min Sung Kim, Minkyun Son, Hanbyeol Oh, Sung Yeol Lim, Hui Chan Ahn, Sunil V Barma, Jeong Ho Cho, Wooseok Yang, Sae Byeok Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisorder-induced energetic landscape in semiconductors is typically regarded as a parasitic source of non-radiative energetic losses, yet emerging theory suggests that controlled cationic disorder in multicomponent chalcogenides can define reproducible routes for achieving exceptional optical and electrical properties. Here, we establish cationic disorder-engineered AgBiS2 heterostructures as optically addressable synaptic elements, in which precisely reconfigurable trap-state population serves as a scalable analogue memory variable. By coupling AgBiS2 with an optically complementary narrow-bandgap fused-ring organic semiconductor (Y6), nonequilibrium photocarrier redistribution across disorder-induced states becomes selectively driven by excitation wavelength, enabling bidirectional and homeostatic plasticity in a single device. Near-infrared excitation populates disorder-mediated states to yield >10-fold conductance enhancement with large analogue hysteresis characteristic of accelerated long-term potentiation (LTP), while short-wavelength excitation depopulates these states to selectively accelerate long-term depression (LTD). Wavelength-dependent trap occupation dynamics, resolved through ultrafast transient spectroscopy, validate this disorder-mediated memory volatility mechanism. Neuromorphic simulations show that such spectrally segmented LTP/LTD enables color-conditioned learning with intrinsic negative-feedback stabilization. These results redefine ionic disorder from an unavoidable defect to a functional design parameter for excitability control, offering a materials platform for spectrally programmable neuromorphic hardware.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electronic properties and materials, Electronic materials, Electronic devices

Introduction

The exponential growth of artificial intelligence, from its inception in the 1950s to contemporary systems based on large language models, has been accompanied by an equally dramatic increase in computational demands and energy consumption1–3. As data scales continue to expand, conventional von Neumann architectures face a fundamental bottleneck, as the energy-intensive data shuttling between physically separated memory and processing units becomes increasingly unsustainable4. This architectural limitation has sparked urgent interest in neuromorphic computing, which promises to overcome the memory wall through in-memory processing inspired by biological neural networks, where synaptic connections enable both computation and storage within the same physical location. Among various neuromorphic implementations, artificial synapses represent the most critical component for mimicking biological learning and memory functions through tunable synaptic weights. Among various approaches toward emulating synaptic functions based on ionic, electronic and optical stimulus5–13, photonic and optoelectronic synapses have attracted intensive research interest as transformative building blocks for neuromorphic computing. Recent advances in photonic integrated circuits have demonstrated sub-nanosecond operation speeds and ultra-low energy consumption, with devices achieving femtojoule-level switching energies14–16. The integration of novel materials and architectures, from phase-change materials to organic semiconductors, has enabled photonically reconfigurable synaptic weights and bidirectional plasticity essential for neuromorphic learning. These developments position photonic synapses as critical components for next-generation AI accelerators requiring massive parallelism and energy-efficient computation16–19.

In addition to energy efficiency and bandwidth, however, homeostatic synaptic control is a critical yet often overlooked requirement for scalable neuromorphic systems. Biological neural circuits maintain stable activity by continuously adjusting the balance between synaptic potentiation and depression. Without such homeostasis, networks undergo runaway excitation, quiescence, or adversary reconfiguration20,21. In artificial hardware, however, long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) are frequently implemented with highly correlated or even identical physical mechanisms, such as simple reversing of the polarity or amplitude of electrical pulses, which makes it difficult to independently tune their strength, threshold, and timescale. This coupling becomes particularly problematic in spike-based neuromorphic architectures, where small device-level imbalances between LTP and LTD accumulate across many learning events, leading to weight saturation, drift, or loss of sparsity22. In principle, homeostatic regulation can be implemented algorithmically at the network level through weight-bounding or normalization rules. However, such software-based approaches require additional computational overhead for every weight update cycle, which becomes increasingly costly as network size scales. In contrast, hardware-native homeostasis, where the physical mechanism itself inherently provides balanced regulation, enables automatic equilibration without algorithmic intervention, offering significant advantages for massively parallel systems23. To achieve such hardware-native robustness homeostasis at the device level, material systems are required in which potentiation and depression can be accessed through distinct, preferably orthogonal, physical knobs, rather than through minor variations of the same stimulus. In this sense, together with the desire for solid-state, CMOS-compatible operation, it remains a challenge to find new photoactive material platforms enabling wavelength-selective, bidirectional fine modulation of LTP and LTD24–28. Specifically, a photoactive material system with fundamentally different characteristics from conventional single-component absorbers, such as a heterostructure architecture in which distinct spectral absorption bands can be engineered to produce opposing carrier dynamics through strategic band alignment and interface trap engineering.

AgBiS2 has emerged as a highly promising ternary chalcogenide for next-generation photovoltaics29–33. A recent discovery of controlled cation disorders, which refer to the random distribution of cations across crystallographic sites30, has demonstrated that this materials class can attain significant enhancement in optical absorption coefficient, the highest amongst any other photovoltaic absorber31, across the entire visible spectrum, which enables low-cost. solution-processed, environmentally friendly, and high-performance ultrathin solar cells and photoelectrochemical water splitting application31–33. However, similarly to the conventional solution-processed inorganic semiconductors34–36, they suffer from critical drawbacks from prevailing trap-limited carrier transport through disordered domains as well as multi-crystalline grain boundaries, which even leads to persistent photoconductivity (PPC) behaviors37–39. This behavior prevents efficient charge extraction in solar cells, as trapped carriers continue conducting in the dark, ultimately degrading device performance. From a neuromorphic perspective, however, the same PPC that degrades photovoltaic performance can be a virtue rather than a defect. The exceptionally long-lived trap states naturally store charges and thus stabilize conductance states without continuous power supply, providing a built-in mechanism for non-volatile synaptic weights. For photonic synapses, which are expected to operate under continuous optical stimulation and spike-like transient inputs, this persistence is particularly attractive because it can support temporal integration and long-term plasticity at the device level9. At the same time, scalable neuromorphic hardware also demands homeostatic synaptic control, in which LTP and LTD remain balanced to prevent runaway plasticity and weight saturation. This creates a specific materials requirement in which disorder-mediated PPC and trap occupancy can be regulated in a wavelength-selective manner, such that different optical colors can access opposing carrier dynamics and thereby implement balanced synaptic updates. Taken together, these considerations point to cation-disordered AgBiS2-based heterostructures capable of disorder-mediated nonequilibrium photocurrent redistribution as a promising platform for homeostatic synaptic conditioning in photonic neuromorphic hardware.

Here, we demonstrate an approach that transforms the apparent limitation of disorder-mediated PPC in AgBiS2 into a synaptic conditioning mechanism for homeostatic learning of photonic neuromorphic hardware. We first use a dithiocarbamate (DTC)-based molecular precursor ink to fabricate AgBiS2 thin films with homogeneous cation disorder, which yields an exceptionally high absorption coefficient (~7 × 105 cm⁻¹) and densely populated sub-gap trap states located ~0.3 eV below the conduction band edge. These disorder-induced traps provide the long-lived charge storage necessary for non-volatile synaptic weights. We then stack the narrow-bandgap non-fullerene acceptor Y6, featuring a thiadiazolo–indacenodithienothiophene fused-ring core, on top of AgBiS2 to form an optically complementary heterostructure that allows wavelength-selective control of trap occupancy. Near-infrared light at 830 nm selectively excites Y6 and injects carriers into AgBiS2 trap states stabilized by the disordered potential, producing up to 1320% conductance potentiation and large analog hysteresis, i.e. optical long-term potentiation (LTP). In contrast, short-wavelength illumination at 405 nm predominantly excites AgBiS2 and drives trap depopulation, enabling efficient optical long-term depression (LTD). Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy directly reveals long-lived trap-associated bleaching across 520–1550 nm and allows us to extract wavelength-dependent trap filling and emptying rates that quantitatively reproduce the electrical plasticity, thereby linking microscopic disorder-mediated carrier dynamics to macroscopic synaptic behavior. Finally, neuromorphic simulations using experimentally measured conductance states demonstrate that this wavelength-dependent LTP/LTD control supports color-dependent homeostatic synaptic conditioning, where blue-light-induced LTD counteracts NIR-driven LTP to prevent weight saturation and promote routes for synaptic scaling in neural networks. Together, these results establish cation-disordered AgBiS2/Y6 heterostructures as a materials platform in which disorder-mediated nonequilibrium photocurrent redistribution is intentionally exploited to regulate synaptic weights in the optical domain, enabling spectrally addressable and CMOS-compatible neuromorphic hardware.

Results

Materials characterization

In conventional optoelectronic synapses, synaptic weight modulation is typically achieved through either spectral illumination or electrical gating, primarily by using optical power intensity variations rather than spectral selectivity. Our approach exploits the intrinsically strong PPC characteristics of AgBiS2, a property detrimental to its photovoltaic applications, as the foundation for synaptic memory retention. By stacking Y6 atop AgBiS2, a heterostructure that combines the PPC of AgBiS2 with the spectrally selective photoexcitation of Y6 is created, enabling wavelength-dependent synaptic operations across the visible-to-NIR range.

Figure 1 presents the material selection rationale and fundamental characterization of the AgBiS2/Y6 heterostructure system. Figure 1a shows a schematic for a wavelength-selective photonic synapse that processes visible to near-infrared light to achieve differential synaptic plasticity. The device exploits spectral differences to enable both LTP and LTD through wavelength-dependent signal processing. This spectral selectivity enables color-selective acceleration of electrical potentiation or depression operations, effectively implementing software-defined homeostatic conditioning of synapses in hardware. The schematic in Fig. 1b demonstrates how wavelength-dependent acceleration of potentiation and depression can achieve a homeostatic regulation of neural networks, with respect to individual synaptic weight (Σωij) updates. Figure 1c shows the molecular and crystal structures of photoactive materials. AgBiS2 adopts a cubic rock-salt structure with Ag, Bi, and S atoms forming a three-dimensional network, while Y6 features a characteristic non-fullerene acceptor architecture with extended π-conjugation. Structural confirmation of the synthesized AgBiS2 film is provided by X-ray diffraction analysis in Supplementary Fig. 1. The exceptional optical properties of AgBiS2 are strongly dependent on cation disorder within its crystal structure, as demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. As previous studies have shown that homogeneously disordered cation arrangements significantly enhance optical absorption40, the cation disorder was controlled through DTC ligand coordination chemistry during synthesis to maximize photonic responses. As increasing the relative ratio of metal cations and DTC in the precursor solutions from 12 (DTC12) to 60 (DTC60), it progressively enhances the absorption coefficient across the entire visible spectrum, with DTC60 achieving the highest absorption values. This ligand-mediated control of cation distribution enables optimization of the AgBiS2 optical properties for maximum photosensitivity.

Figure 1d presents the normalized UV-vis absorption spectra revealing the complementary optical properties of our material system. Y6 exhibits strong absorption centered around 830 nm with minimal absorption below 600 nm, while AgBiS2 demonstrates broadband absorption across the visible spectrum with peak absorption in the blue-green region extending from 400-600 nm. The heterostructure combining both layers (AgBiS2/Y6) displays combined absorption characteristics covering the entire visible to near-infrared range from 400-1200 nm. Notably, 830 nm illumination selectively excites Y6 without directly photoexciting AgBiS2, while 405 nm illumination predominantly activates AgBiS2. This spectral differentiation forms the basis for wavelength-selective synaptic operations. The strong PPC characteristic of AgBiS2 single-component film, depending on the DTC compositions, is demonstrated in Fig. 1e through time-resolved photocurrent measurements under pulsed illumination of pulse width 1 s at 24 Hz. The time-resolved photocurrent response was measured using a lateral Au/PMMA/AgBiS2/Glass structured photodetector and a schematic illustration of the device addressed in Supplementary Fig. 3. Upon light exposure, the photocurrent for AgBiS2 rapidly increases to ~200 nA for DTC60-AgBiS₂ and 55 nA for DTC12-AgBiS2 and exhibits slow decay behaviors over multiple on-off cycles. Besides the common bi-exponential time constant of τ₁ for the decays in both films, DTC60-AgBiS₂ film shows a noticeable long-term decay component with time constant of τ₂. This PPC, highlighted by the sustained current level during dark periods, confirms that photogenerated carriers remain within the energetic landscape of film for extended durations, which is a property that can be leveraged for synaptic memory functions. Detailed surface morphology and thickness profiles characterization of individual layers and the heterostructure are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4, confirming uniform film formation with controlled thickness across the device area. Figure 1f schematically summarizes the energy landscapes of Y6 and disorder-engineered AgBiS2, measured through ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopy, optical absorption spectroscopy and ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy. The trap states in AgBiS2 are relevant to PPC in terms of energy level alignments. Specifically, trap states capture photogenerated carriers and prevent their immediate recombination, leading to persistent photoconductivity. While such long-lived states are detrimental for photovoltaic applications, they become advantageous for neuromorphic computing by enabling long-term charge storage essential for synaptic memory retention. Detailed explorations of such behaviors are discussed in the later sections. This combination of strong PPC characteristics, tunable cation disorder for optimized absorption, complementary spectral responses, and scalable solution processing establishes AgBiS2/Y6 as a promising material platform for spectrally reconfigurable neuromorphic synapses capable of hardware implementation of color-selective synaptic conditionings.

Wavelength-dependent hysteresis behaviors

Figure 2a illustrates the device architecture of reconfigurable photonic synapse (RPS), featuring a bottom-gate/bottom-contact configuration with the AgBiS2/Y6 heterostructure channel sandwiched between gold source-drain electrodes on a Si/SiO2 substrate. The vertical stacking of AgBiS2 and Y6 layers creates the foundation for wavelength-selective charge generation and trapping dynamics. Figure 2b outlines the fabrication process flow consisting of five key steps: (1) positive photoresist patterning to define the channel region, (2) reactive ion etching to create the device isolation, (3) negative photoresist application for channel tranch, (4) lift-off process to form the top layer, and (5) final metallization to complete the source-drain electrodes. This photolithographic approach enables precise control over device geometry and scalable fabrication.

To demonstrate the wavelength-selective synaptic behavior, we first fabricated control devices with Y6-only active layer (Supplementary Fig. 5) and compared them with our incorporating the AgBiS2/Y6 heterostructure. The control experiments revealed that Y6 alone exhibits clean, reversible transistor behavior with negligible hysteresis across all tested wavelengths (405, 450, 655, 830, and 1060 nm) and power densities (0–1000 μW), confirming the absence of persistent trap states required for memory operations. Figure 2c–e demonstrate the wavelength-dependent transfer characteristics and their evolution with optical power. At 405 nm illumination (Fig. 2c), the transfer curves show minimal change with increasing optical power, with all curves closely overlapping and maintaining similar threshold voltages. The small modulation of the channel current, despite strong AgBiS2 absorption, indicates that under this transistor geometry and bias condition, direct excitation of AgBiS2 does not efficiently alter the channel conductance, even though PPC is present in AgBiS2 films. In contrast, as shown below, Y6-assisted carrier injection into AgBiS2 under NIR excitation is required to significantly reconfigure trap occupancy and produce large, non-volatile shifts in the transfer characteristics. At 655 nm illumination (Fig. 2d), an intermediate wavelength where Y6 exhibits partial absorption, the transfer curves begin to show moderate leftward shifts with increasing optical power, suggesting the onset of charge transfer processes. Most dramatically, at 830 nm illumination (Fig. 2e), which corresponds to Y6’s peak absorption, the transfer curves exhibit substantial negative shifts proportional to optical power, with threshold voltage changing from approximately +10 V in darkness to −8 V at 1000 μW. This progressive enhancement of hysteresis with increasing Y6 absorption clearly demonstrates that the magnitude of charge trapping, and consequently the hysteresis loop area, scales directly with the efficiency of photogeneration in the Y6 layer. The stark contrast between wavelengths confirms that memory operation requires the specific pathway of photoexcitation in Y6 followed by charge transfer to AgBiS2 trap states, rather than direct optical excitation of either material alone.

Beyond threshold modulation, dual-sweep measurements reveal the mechanism of wavelength-selective charge storage. Figure 2f plots threshold voltage (VTH) evolution with optical power for four representative wavelengths. For each wavelength, both forward sweep (VG:−20→+60V) and reverse sweep (VG:+60→−20V) threshold voltages are extracted and plotted as a function of optical power. 830 nm forward sweep induced VTH progressively shifting from +10V to −8V, while reverse sweep VTH (and poorly absorbed wavelengths like 405 nm) remain nearly constant around +10V regardless of power. A clear asymmetry emerges that the forward sweep shows dramatic wavelength-dependent VTH shifts, particularly at 830 nm where VTH moves from +10V (dark) to −8V (1000 μW), while the reverse sweep maintains nearly constant values around +10V across all wavelengths and powers. This asymmetry directly manifests the field-dependent photocurrent redistribution behavior. Photogenerated carriers in Y6 transfer to AgBiS2 trap states during the forward sweep, acting as effective photogating that electrostatically shifts the channel potential and enables earlier transistor turn-on. During the reverse sweep, these trapped charges persist due to deep trap states, preventing VTH from returning to its original position. The magnitude of forward-reverse separation defines the hysteresis window, which grows substantially only at 830 nm, where Y6 absorption is maximal, while remaining negligible at poorly absorbed wavelengths (405 nm, 1000 nm) despite identical electrical and thermal conditions.

Figure 2g quantifies this wavelength selectivity by plotting absolute hysteresis loop area, which was obtained by the integrated difference between forward and reverse transfer curves, as a function of optical power. At 830 nm, the hysteresis area grows exponentially from near-zero in darkness to approximately 174 μA·V at 1000μW, indicating substantial charge storage capacity. In stark contrast, 405 nm and 1000 nm maintain minimal hysteresis (<10μA⋅V) across the entire power range, while 655 nm shows intermediate response (~40μA⋅V) consistent with partial Y6 absorption. This selective growth establishes that memory operation is controlled by wavelength-dependent photogeneration in Y6, not by non-selective heating or substrate effects. To visualize the complete operational parameter space, Fig. 2h presents a normalized heatmap of hysteresis area across wavelength (405−1000nm) and optical power (0−1000μW) axes. Each cell is normalized to the maximum value, revealing a distinct “hotspot” at NIR wavelengths that intensifies with increasing power. The map clearly delineates three regimes. Negligible response at short wavelengths where AgBiS2 absorption dominates but fails to produce persistent trapping, strong response at 750–900 nm where efficient Y6 photogeneration drives trap filling, and minimal response beyond Y6’s absorption edge. Importantly, intermediate wavelengths like 655 nm exhibit proportional responses (normalized value ~0.25-0.3), demonstrating that the device encodes continuous analog memory states through wavelength tuning, which is a key capability for implementing multi-level synaptic weights in neuromorphic applications.

Color-addressed synaptic plasticity

The wavelength-dependent hysteresis observed in static transfer characteristics translates directly into biomimetic synaptic plasticity functions essential for neuromorphic computing. Figure 3 systematically demonstrates excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC), paired-pulse facilitation (PPF), long-term potentiation (LTP), and critically, wavelength-selective long-term depression (LTD), establishing complete bidirectional synaptic modulation through spectral control. Figure 3a–c reveal the fundamental distinction in temporal photoresponse characteristics across different wavelengths by monitoring drain current decay following 20 ms electrical pulses (Vp=20V) in different wavelength illumination conditions. At 405 nm stimulation (Fig. 3a), the device exhibits typical phototransistor behavior where photocurrent rises during illumination and rapidly decays to baseline after light cessation, showing minimal persistent photoconductivity regardless of power level. Similarly, 1060 nm illumination (Fig. 3b) produces only a transient photoresponse with complete recovery to dark current levels. In stark contrast, 830 nm illumination (Fig. 3c) generates pronounced persistent photoconductivity, with current remaining elevated in the blue-shaded region even after illumination ceases. The magnitude of this persistence scales with optical power. Higher intensities (500−1000μW) maintain elevated conductance states for extended periods, while lower powers show proportionally reduced persistence. It is noted that this behavior is consistent with the transfer measurements in Fig. 2. Although AgBiS2 itself exhibits PPC in isolation, in the full transistor geometry, the dominant, persistent modulation of channel conductance arises when Y6 is optically excited and injects carriers into AgBiS2. Short-wavelength (405 nm) or long-wavelength (1060 nm) excitation that does not efficiently excite Y6 mainly produces transient responses without appreciable long-term conductance change. This wavelength-selective PPC establishes 830 nm as the optimal accelerating wavelength for potentiation of synaptic weights.

Figure 3d demonstrates long-term potentiation across multiple wavelengths under identical stimulation conditions under continuous illumination (100 μW) with 50 consecutive gate pulses (VG=20V,20ms,2.5Hz). VG drives photogenerated carriers into trap states in the AgBiS2 layer. The EPSC evolution clearly differentiates wavelength-dependent plasticity: 830 nm produces the strongest potentiation with conductance increasing approximately 13-fold, 655 nm achieves moderate 9-fold enhancement, while 405, 450, and 1060 nm wavelengths generate minimal potentiation despite identical electrical and optical parameters. This spectral selectivity directly correlates with Y6 absorption spectrum, confirming that LTP magnitude scales with photogeneration efficiency in the Y6 layer. Figure 3e examines paired-pulse facilitation (PPF), a fundamental short-term plasticity mechanism. PPF ratio, calculated as the second pulse response divided by the first (A2/A1), varies systematically with wavelength. The characteristic PPF decay with increasing inter-pulse interval reflects the temporal dynamics of charge accumulation, with 830 nm showing enhanced facilitation compared to weakly absorbed wavelengths, consistent with Y6-mediated carrier injection driving synaptic enhancement. Figure 3f explores power-dependent LTP dynamics at 830 nm with 50 electrical pulses (VP=20V), revealing how optical intensity modulates potentiation strength. Increasing power from 10 to 1000 μW progressively enhances both the potentiation rate and saturation level, with maximum conductance enhancement reaching nearly 40-fold at 1000 μW. This power tunability provides analog control over synaptic weight updates, enabling fine-grained plasticity modulation essential for neuromorphic learning algorithms. Importantly, the upper panel demonstrates complete reversibility of the potentiated state: after LTP induction, application of negative gate voltage (VE=−20V) successfully extracts the trapped carriers and returns the conductance to its initial baseline level. This electrical erasure capability confirms that the charge storage mechanism is fully reversible, validating that both potentiation and depression can be achieved in a controlled manner, a critical requirement for implementing reconfigurable synaptic weights in neuromorphic systems.

Figure 3g reveals wavelength-selective optical erasure without any electrical bias. After LTP conditioning with 830 nm light with ~12.2 nA conductance, different wavelengths are applied during the erasure phase. The magnified view (Fig. 3h) clearly resolves the differential decay dynamics: The 405 nm illumination produces the most rapid conductance decay, while 830 nm shows minimal depression despite identical power. This inverted wavelength dependence, where short wavelengths that do not contribute to LTP efficiently promotes accelerated LTD, establishes bidirectional optical control. The wavelength-dependent depression acceleration rates follow: 405 nm > 450 nm > 655 nm ≈ 830 nm ≈ 1060 nm, demonstrating that wavelength-dependent LTD conditioning enables complementary synaptic operations. Figure 3i compares depression dynamics under different wavelengths with electrical contribution (VE=−10V) showing normalized decay profiles that highlight the dramatic acceleration achieved with 405 nm versus longer wavelengths. The differential erasure rates enable wavelength-encoded temporal dynamics, where the choice of optical wavelength determines not just the direction but also the kinetics of synaptic plasticity.

These results establish a complete wavelength-addressable synaptic system where 830 nm serves as the primary wavelength for potentiation acceleration through Y6 absorption and charge transfer to AgBiS2, while 405 nm acts as the selective wavelength for promoting accelerated depression. Supplementary Figs. 8–9 provide additional characterization of power-dependent LTD dynamics and gate-voltage modulation of retention times, further confirming the multi-dimensional control achievable in this heterostructure platform. This bidirectional optical control mirrors the biological paradigm of memory consolidation versus active forgetting: NIR-driven potentiation stabilizes synaptic weights analogous to consolidation processes that strengthen memory traces, whereas blue-light-driven depression selectively weakens connections analogous to active forgetting mechanisms that remove irrelevant information to maintain network homeostasis.

Mechanistic insights on disorder-mediated photoexcited dynamics

To elucidate the wavelength-selective charge transfer mechanism underlying our synaptic device operation, we performed femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) measurements on the AgBiS2/Y6 heterostructure under different excitation conditions. The system is excited by pump pulses at either 830 nm (selectively exciting Y6) or 405 nm (predominantly exciting AgBiS2), while a broadband probe pulse (400-1600 nm) monitors the transient absorption changes. Upon 830 nm excitation, photogenerated carriers in Y6 can transfer to trap states in AgBiS2, creating a trapped state population. The probe pulse detects these dynamics through excited state absorption (ESA) from trapped carriers and ground state bleaching (GSB) signals. Critically, the trapped carriers exhibit distinct spectral signatures that allow us to track the charge transfer and trapping processes in real-time.

Figures 4a and 4b present pseudo-color plots of temporal spectral comparing the “trapped” states under different excitation wavelengths. Figure 4a shows fs-TAS maps upon 830 nm pumping with electrically filled trap states. The blue regions in the visible spectrum (500-700 nm) indicate GSB, exhibiting signal patterns similar to Y6-only samples. Notably, signals near 680 nm remain stable up to 1000 ps, consistent with the long-lived PPC observed in device measurements. Figure 4b further reveals completely different dynamics under 405 nm pumping with electrically filled traps. While GSB signals are observed before 700 nm, unlike 830 nm pumping, there is no broad GSB present. Signals in the visible region generally follow the transient absorption characteristics of AgBiS2. On the other hand, Fig. 4c presents results from electrically detrapped samples pumped at 830 nm. The visible region shows some tendency to follow AgBiS2 signals, but critically, the NIR region exhibits GSB signal recovery unlike Fig. 4b, 4c, suggesting that empty trap states are being refilled with carriers. Figure 4e compares kinetics at wavelengths showing similar signals. At the 520 nm probe, the 830 nm trapped state exhibits the most prominent GSB signal. At 680 nm, the trapped state GSB exceeds that of Y6 alone. At 1550 nm, the trapped state displays combined GSB and ESA signals with distinct trends, and these different kinetic rates enable quantification of trap-state carrier populations. Wavelength-selective charge dynamics and synaptic operation mechanism

Based on the transient photophysical analysis, Fig. 5 elucidates the fundamental charge transfer mechanisms underlying wavelength-selective synaptic behavior through three distinct pathways that explain both the fs-TAS observations and device-level plasticity. Figure 5a illustrates the depopulation route when the trap states are filled and subsequently excited with 830 nm light. Under combined optical and electrical stimulation, photogenerated electrons in Y6 are initially driven by the applied electric field from Y6 LUMO to AgBiS2 conduction band, where they diffuse into trap states. Both light (for carrier generation) and gate voltage (for carrier transfer and trapping) are essential for synaptic weight modulation. Upon 830 nm pump excitation, Y6 selectively absorbs photons, generating electron-hole pairs within its active layer. Two recombination pathways then compete: (1) direct electron-hole recombination within Y6, and critically, (2) recombination between photogenerated holes in Y6 HOMO and trapped electrons in AgBiS2. In the trapped state (Fig. 5a), this second pathway, where photogenerated holes in the Y6 HOMO recombine with trapped electrons in AgBiS2, leads to depopulation of trap states and partial photobleaching of the trapped population. This recombination process occurs on a timescale of ~10 ps, as extracted from fs-TAS kinetic analysis (τ1, Figures S11 and Table S2). As a result, once the traps are strongly filled and the device has reached a potentiated state, further 830 nm illumination does not indefinitely increase the conductance and only weakly contributes to erasure, consistent with the saturation behavior and limited LTD observed under 830 nm in Fig. 3.

Wavelength-selective charge transfer pathways.

a Trap depopulation under 830 nm excitation with pre-filled states via hole-mediated recombination, (b) trap repopulation under 830 nm excitation with empty states via electron transfer from Y6, and (c) selective erasure under 405 nm excitation where minimal Y6 absorption prevents refilling while AgBiS₂ excitation enables sustained depopulation.

In contrast, when the device is first electrically detrapped (Fig. 5b), 830 nm excitation primarily drives electron transfer from Y6 into empty AgBiS2 traps, enabling robust potentiation. Figure 5b demonstrates the repopulation route following complete trap emptying. By applying reverse bias, trapped electrons are electrically extracted back to Y6 LUMO, creating a depopulated trap-state condition (“detrapped state”). When 830 nm excitation is subsequently applied to this detrapped state, photogenerated electron-hole pairs in Y6 follow different dynamics: while holes still undergo intra-Y6 recombination, the absence of trapped electrons eliminates the inter-layer recombination pathway. Consequently, photogenerated electrons can efficiently transfer to and repopulate the empty AgBiS2 trap states, resulting in rebleaching of GSB features. This interfacial charge transfer and trap filling process occurs on a timescale of ~4 ns (τ3, Figures S12 and Table S3), enabling 830 nm to accelerate the potentiation of synaptic weights when starting from an depopulated state and explaining the device’s ability to undergo multiple cycles. Figure 5c further reveals the selective erasure mechanism under 405 nm excitation. Starting from a filled trap-state (identical to Fig. 5a initial condition), 405 nm pump light is applied. Crucially, Y6 exhibits negligible absorption at 405 nm, preventing electron-hole pair generation in the Y6 layer. Instead, 405 nm primarily excites AgBiS2, generating carriers within the inorganic semiconductor. Without photogenerated electrons from Y6 to repopulate the traps, only depopulation processes occur through intra-AgBiS2 recombination. The absence of trap repopulation pathway thus results in sustained depopulation. This asymmetric response explains why 405 nm serves as an effective wavelength for accelerated depression. It can depopulate traps through AgBiS2 excitation without triggering the Y6-mediated repopulation mechanism, accelerating depopulation significantly faster than dark conditions as observed in the photocurrent decay characteristics of Supplementary Fig. 10.

To establish a direct correlation between microscopic charge transfer dynamics and macroscopic device behavior, we performed fluence-dependent fs-TAS measurements at 405 nm, 830 nm, and the intermediate wavelength of 655 nm (Supplementary Figs. S17–S19). For 405 nm excitation, increasing fluence results in broadened and intensified ESA signals with complete disappearance of GSB features in both trapped and detrapped states. This indicates significantly enhanced intra-AgBiS2 recombination rates at higher carrier densities, leading to more pronounced trap depopulation. Importantly, no rebleaching is observed regardless of fluence or initial trap state, confirming the complete absence of Y6-mediated carrier injection at this wavelength. These results support the exclusive role of 405 nm as an LTD wavelength.

For 830 nm excitation, on the other hand, fluence-dependent behavior differs between trapped and detrapped states. As fluence increases for trapped states, GSB signals nearly vanish as enhanced Y6-generated hole population increases recombination with trapped electrons, accelerating depopulation. Under detrapped conditions, photo-rebleaching appears at longer time delays, indicating charge refilling into empty traps. As fluence increases, both ESA and GSB signals intensify. This result indicates that, while recombination increases, the amount of charge refilling into trap states also increases proportionally due to higher carrier generation. Fluence-dependent photophysical behavior agrees with the observation that higher optical power produces both larger hysteresis in transfer characteristics and stronger LTP in PSC measurements. We further examined 655 nm, an intermediate wavelength at the Y6 absorption edge. At low fluence under detrapped conditions, ESA-dominant behavior is observed with a weak GSB signal emerging at longer time delays, indicating modest charge refilling. As fluence increases, both ESA and GSB signals increase with more pronounced rebleaching at extended time delays. Compared to 830 nm, the refilling signal is significantly weaker due to lower Y6 absorption efficiency at 655 nm. Notably, rebleaching is observed in both trapped and detrapped states at 655 nm, in contrast to 830 nm, where rebleaching occurs only in the detrapped state. This difference arises because weaker Y6 absorption at 655 nm results in incomplete trap filling, leaving partial charge transfer pathways available even in nominally “trapped” conditions. These observations confirm the intermediate synaptic character of 655 nm, consistent with PSC measurements showing weaker LTP than 830 nm due to partial carrier injection efficiency.

Neuromorphic simulation with wavelength-dependent synaptic weights

To validate the practical applicability of wavelength-selective synaptic plasticity for neuromorphic computing, we performed MNIST handwritten digit recognition simulations using experimentally measured conductance values as synaptic weights in an artificial neural network. Figure 6a illustrates the neural network architecture and wavelength-dependent learning framework. The network consists of three layers: an input layer with 784 neurons (corresponding to 28 × 28-pixel MNIST images), a hidden layer with 200 neurons for feature extraction, and an output layer with 10 neurons representing digits 0-9. Each pixel intensity (I1 to I784) from the flattened MNIST image is fed to the input neurons. The synaptic connections between layers are weighted by conductance values experimentally measured from our RPS devices under different wavelength conditions. The key innovation lies in the wavelength-dependent weight update mechanism during backpropagation. Instead of using conventional gradient descent with fixed learning rates, we implement “color-selective learning” where the weight update magnitude is modulated by the wavelength-dependent LTP/LTD characteristics measured from our devices. Specifically, weights are updated according to: ΔW=η×δ×G(λ), where η is the base learning rate, δ is the error gradient, and G(λ) represents the wavelength-dependent conductance modulation extracted from our experimental data. This approach directly maps the physical synaptic plasticity to the computational weight updates, creating a hardware-aware training algorithm.

Neural network simulation for homeostatic traning/recognition processes.

a Neural network architecture with color-selective learning framework using experimentally measured conductance values as synaptic weights. b Recognition rate evolution over 120 training epochs for different wavelengths (830 nm, 655 nm, 405 nm and dark). c Reconstructed weight matrices at different training stages (30, 60, 90, and 120 epochs) showing wavelength-dependent pattern recognition development. d Homeostatic plasticity simulation over 1000 epochs. Four scenarios demonstrate: runaway (orange, LTP only), ΔH1 (cyan), ΔH2 (green), and quiescence (blue). Shaded regions indicate target homeostatic ranges. e Pattern evolution across extended training for the four plasticity scenarios. Runaway condition shows learning followed by saturation failure (red noise). Homeostatic conditions maintain stable pattern recognition throughout 1000 epochs with different quality stability. Quiescence condition exhibits progressive pattern degradation back to noise. Extended analyses for other wavelength combinations in Supplementary information.

Figure 6b demonstrates the wavelength-dependent learning dynamics through recognition rate evolution over 120 training epochs. As predicted by LTP behavior, the 830 nm condition exhibits the fastest convergence, reaching 90% accuracy within 40 epochs and plateauing at ~95% after 60 epochs. This accelerated learning directly results from the strong LTP characteristics at 830 nm, which enable larger weight updates during the initial training phase. The 655 nm condition shows intermediate performance, requiring ~60 epochs to reach 90% accuracy, consistent with its moderate potentiation capability. The 405 nm and dark condition display the slowest learning, taking over 80 epochs to achieve comparable accuracy. This wavelength-dependent learning speed hierarchy precisely mirrors the LTP magnitude differences observed in device measurements, validating that optical wavelength can control not just synaptic weight values but also learning dynamics. Figure 6c visualizes the evolution of pattern recognition through reconstructed weight matrices at different training stages. The leftmost column shows the initial randomized weights appearing as noise. After training with different wavelengths (columns 2–4), distinct digit patterns emerge with varying clarity and convergence speeds. Under 405 nm and dark training (top row), the recognized pattern remains partially obscured even after full training, reflecting weak synaptic updates. The 655 nm condition (middle row) produces clearer digit recognition with moderate feature definition. Most remarkably, 830 nm training (bottom row) yields sharp, well-defined digit representations with high contrast between active and inactive pixels, demonstrating superior feature extraction enabled by strong synaptic potentiation. The progression from noisy initialization to clear digit recognition illustrates how wavelength-selective plasticity translates to computational performance. The varying degrees of pattern clarity directly correlate with the wavelength-dependent conductance modulation ranges: 830 nm provides the widest dynamic range ( > 13 times conductance change), enabling fine-grained weight adjustments that capture subtle digit features, while 405 nm limited modulation restricts the network’s ability to develop discriminative representations.

Despite the successful demonstration of the learning capability as many of recent literatures show, excessive learning without homeostatic regulation in hardware neural network can lead to catastrophic failure modes. Unchecked synaptic potentiation causes runaway excitation and weight saturation, while unopposed depression results in network quiescence, which is the failure modes well-documented in both biological and artificial neural systems 20–22. To experimentally validate wavelength-decoupled homeostatic control, we implemented bidirectional plasticity simulations combining LTP and LTD using various spectra. In our homeostatic framework, wavelength-orthogonal LTP and LTD act as complementary regulatory mechanisms that maintain synaptic weights within a predefined target region. When weights exceed the upper boundary of the homeostatic region, LTD is selectively activated to suppress further potentiation; conversely, when weights fall below the lower boundary, LTP is triggered to prevent further depression. This boundary-triggered bidirectional control creates a self-stabilizing system where synaptic strengths naturally oscillate within the target range without external supervision. Figure 6d presents four distinct plasticity scenarios over 1000 training epochs, revealing the critical importance of balanced LTP/LTD control. The runaway condition (LTP only) exhibits rapid initial learning but catastrophic weight saturation, reaching w≈1.0 by epoch 60 and remaining trapped at this maximum value throughout subsequent training. The quiescence condition (LTD only) shows the opposite failure mode: weights initially potentiate to w≈0.82 at epoch 60 but progressively decay toward zero, returning to w≈0.0 by epoch 1000 as continuous depression erases learned patterns. In stark contrast, the two homeostatic scenarios demonstrate stable weight maintenance through balanced wavelength-orthogonal LTP/LTD. The ΔH1 condition (broad homeostatic region within 0.55-0.8) maintains weights within this target range through large-amplitude oscillations (σ=0.067), while ΔH2 condition (narrow region within 0.3-0.4) achieves tighter regulation (σ=0.027) with smaller oscillations around the target value, both maintaining learning stability over the entire 1000-epoch duration. Finally, Fig. 6e visualizes the computational consequences of these plasticity modes through MNIST digit “5” reconstruction across extended training. The runaway row demonstrates catastrophic failure of unregulated potentiation: digit recognition successfully achieved by epoch 60 subsequently degrades into uniform saturation by epoch 200 as all synaptic weights saturate at maximum values, destroying pattern discriminability. The quiescence row exhibits inverse degradation: successful learning at epoch 60 followed by progressive pattern loss, with the digit representation fading back to background noise by epoch 200 as LTD-driven weight decay erases the learned features. Both homeostatic conditions, however, maintain clear digit recognition throughout all 1000 epochs. Notably, ΔH1 condition exhibits visible pattern quality fluctuations, alternating between sharper and slightly blurred digit representations, reflecting its larger weight oscillations. In contrast, ΔH2 produces consistently sharp, stable digit recognition across all epochs, demonstrating that tighter homeostatic regulation not only prevents catastrophic failure but also improves pattern fidelity by minimizing synaptic weight fluctuations during inference.

These demonstrations establish that wavelength-orthogonal LTP/LTD control provides a viable pathway toward robust homeostatic plasticity unachievable with conventional electrically-coupled synaptic devices. Critically, the comparison between ΔH1 and ΔH2 reveals that narrower homeostatic regions provide superior computational precision—the 2.5× reduction in oscillation amplitude directly translates to more stable and accurate pattern recognition. The ability to independently tune homeostatic region width (ΔH) through wavelength selection thus represents a hardware-level parameter for balancing learning stability against computational precision—a fundamental requirement for scalable neuromorphic systems operating over extended timescales without external intervention.

Discussion

Based on photophysical and device physical investigations, we have shown that controlled ionic disorders in multicomponent chalcogenides can be directly harnessed as a functional design parameter for photonic neuromorphic hardware. By using DTC-based precursor chemistry to realize cation-disordered AgBiS2 thin films with strong PPC and integrating them with an optically complementary narrow-bandgap molecular semiconductor Y6, an all-solid-state synaptic heterostructure is demonstrated in which disorder-mediated nonequilibrium photocurrent is redistributed in a wavelength-dependent manner. Near-infrared excitation selectively fills disorder-induced states in AgBiS2 and yields large, non-volatile conductance potentiation, while short-wavelength excitation predominantly depopulates these states, enabling purely optical, bidirectional writing and erasing of synaptic weights. Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy provided direct spectroscopic access to the disorder-induced states and their kinetics, revealing long-lived bleaching features and wavelength-dependent filling and depopulation rates that quantitatively reproduce the observed electrical hysteresis and plasticity. This kinetic analysis bridges microscopic disorder-mediated carrier dynamics with macroscopic synaptic behavior, clarifying how the balance between Y6-driven charge transfer and intra-AgBiS2 recombination determines whether optical excitation results in long-term potentiation or depression. Finally, we demonstrated that experimentally measured conductance states from this heterostructure can be directly used as synaptic weights in neuromorphic simulations. The wavelength-dependent plasticity translates into color-tunable learning dynamics, with NIR light enabling fastest convergence and blue light providing slowest training. Beyond learning speed, wavelength-orthogonal LTP/LTD enables homeostatic weight regulation where NIR-driven potentiation and blue-light-induced depression act as complementary control mechanisms, maintaining stable pattern recognition over extended operation (1000 epochs) while preventing catastrophic runaway saturation or quiescence degradation, which are inherent failure modes of single-mechanism synaptic devices. These results establish engineered disorders in heterostructures as a materials platform in which nonequilibrium photocurrent redistribution is deliberately exploited for synaptic regulation in the optical domain, offering a general strategy to repurpose PPC-exhibiting semiconductors toward spectrally addressable, CMOS-compatible neuromorphic hardware.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used as received without additional purification: silver chloride powder (AgCl, 99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich), bismuth(III) chloride powder (BiCl3, 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), carbon disulfide (CS₂, ≥99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), n-butylamine (CH3(CH2)3NH2, 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), pyridine (C₅H₅N, anhydrous, 99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich), Y6 (BTP-4F, Sigma-Aldrich), dichloromethane (DCM, anhydrous, ≥99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich), and photoresist (AZ5214-E, MicroChem).

Preparation of AgBiS2 molecular ink

All dithiocarbamate (DTC) solution preparations were conducted in a fume hood. N-butyl DTC solution was prepared by reacting n-butylamine with carbon disulfide (CS2). Pyridine (1.831 mL) was mixed with n-butylamine (1.78 mL) in a glass vial and stirred in an ice bath for 15 min. CS2 (1.138 mL) was injected into the vial under stirring. The exothermic reaction between n-butylamine and CS2 changed the solution color from transparent to yellow. The DTC solution was stirred for 5 min after CS₂ injection, removed from the ice bath, and allowed to warm naturally to room temperature for 5 min. The DTC solution was used to dissolve AgCl and BiCl3 in an N2-filled glove box at a metal concentration of 0.2 M for both Ag-DTC and Bi-DTC solutions. After overnight stirring, the fully dissolved Ag-DTC solution was transparent yellow, while the Bi-DTC solution was transparent orange with no precipitates. The solutions were mixed overnight in a 1:1 volume ratio to obtain a homogeneous Ag-Bi-DTC solution with stoichiometric Ag:Bi ratio.

Device fabrication and characterizations of AgBiS2 photodetector

AgBiS2 films were spin-coated onto cleaned soda-lime glass substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed directly at 250 °C for 5 min. Each layer repeated three times, followed by a post annealing process at 250 °C for 20 min. A poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) layer was deposited on top of the AgBiS2 film by spin-coating the PMMA solution (concentration: 5 mg mL−1 in chlorobenzene) at 4000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. A 50 nm thick top Au electrode with a lateral configuration was deposited via thermal evaporation. All electrical measurements were performed using a vacuum chamber probe station system equipped with an optical fiber-guided halogen illuminator (FOK-100 W, Fiber Optic Korea) and a Keithley 4200 SCS source meter at ≈10−2 torr.

Device fabrication and characterization of AgBiS2 heterostructure transistor

Heavily doped silicon substrates with 300 nm thermally grown SiO2 were used as gate electrode and dielectric. Substrates were cleaned by sequential sonication in acetone, isopropanol, and deionized water, followed by O₂ plasma treatment (100 W, 40 min). AgBiS2 ink was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at 250 °C for 5 min on a hotplate. A second layer was deposited under identical conditions and annealed at 250 °C for 20 min to ensure complete conversion to the crystalline phase. For patterning, photoresist (AZ5214-E) was spin-coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s and exposed to UV light (365 nm, 10 s). After development in conventional developer for 45 s, the exposed AgBiS2 was etched using reactive ion etching (RIE) with O₂ plasma (flow rate: 10 sccm, power: 60 W, time: 20 min). Residual photoresist was removed by acetone. Y6 ink was spin-coated at 2500 rpm for 60 s into the trenches. Gold source-drain electrodes (40 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation through a shadow mask, defining channel dimensions of width (W) = 1000 μm and length (L) = 50 μm. The electrical properties were measured using a Keithley 4200 A SCS unit (Tektronix) and a 405, 450, 655, 830, 1060 nm laser (MLL-III-450-1060L, CNILaser) at room temperature under vacuum conditions.

Structural characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in θ-2θ configuration, scanning from 20° to 50° with a step size of 0.02°. Absorption coefficients and optical constants were determined using spectroscopic ellipsometry (SENResearch) with measurements across the 300–1700 nm wavelength range. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed in tapping mode to examine the surface morphology and film uniformity of the AgBiS2. Film thickness and surface uniformity were measured using a surface profilometer (Bruker DXT-A).

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS)

Transient spectroscopic analyses of films and solutions were carried about by using an ultrafast pump-probe spectrometer (HELIOS-TA, Ultrafast Systems, Inc.) consisting of a 6 W 2 kHz repetition rate femtosecond Yb:KGW amplifier laser (HYPERION, Ultrafast Systems, Inc.) and an optical parametric amplifier system (APPOLO-Y, Ultrafast Systems, Inc.) in the Cryogenic Femtosecond Transient Kerr-Gate Spectro-microscopy Facility (NFEC-2025-03-305124). Pump pulses at 405 nm and 830 nm with a power density of ∼100 μJ cm−2 were produced using an optical parametric and a variable neutral density filter, whereas a broadband white-light continuum probe pulse was generated by focusing a small portion of the 1030 nm amplifier output into the window of either sapphire for a visible range or YAG for a near infrared (NIR) range. For the measurement in NIR range, pump and probe pulses were polarized to observe the oscillatory transient features from molecular motions.
