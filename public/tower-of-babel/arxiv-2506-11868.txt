A “trembling hand perfect” equilibrium for a certain class of mean field games Thanks: The author is grateful to be supported by National Science Foundation through NSF Grant DMS-2045027.
P. Jameson Graber

Abstract
. We study a particular class of mean field games whose solutions can be formally connected to a scalar transport equation on the Wasserstein space of measures. For this class, we construct some interesting explicit examples of non-uniqueness of Nash equilibria. We then address the selection problem of finding rational criteria by which to choose one equilibrium over others. We show that when the theory of entropy solutions is used, we can obtain explicit error estimates for the “vanishing noise limit,” where the error is measured in a certain norm that measures the distance between two functions over the set of empirical measures.

1.
Introduction

Mean field game theory was introduced by Lasry and Lions
[
LL07
]
and independently by Caines Huang and Malhamé
[
HMC06
,
HCM07
]
as a way to model the contiuum limit of
N
-player Nash games as
N\to\infty
.
An overview of the theory can be found in the following monographs, surveys, and lecture notes:
[
CD17a
,
CD17b
,
BFY13
,
G+14
,
Car10
,
ACD+21
]
.
Briefly, a mean field Nash equilibrium is understood conceptually as a population distribution in which no individual has any incentive (as measured by a cost function) to change behavior.
In Section
1.1
below we will give a precise mathematical description of a mean field game, at least in one particular framework.

Most of the literature on mean field games has been occupied with the problem of proving there exists a unique solution, i.e. a unique mean field Nash equilibrium, and then characterizing it as the limit of either open-loop or closed-loop equilibria for
N
-player games (see e.g.
[
CDLL19
]
), or else as an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium (i.e. an “almost equilibrium”) to the
N
-player game.
However, there is an increasing number of results showing non-uniqueness of the equilibrium for various types of mean field games
[
BF19
,
BZ20
,
Cir16
,
CT19
,
CP19
]
, which has led to an interest in “selection principles”
[
CD22a
,
DT20
]
.

The concept of a selection principle is essential to the justification of Nash equilibrium as a concept, since without uniqueness, each player is left without a rational way of selecting a strategy
[
HS88
]
.
The idea introduced by Selten
[
Sel75
]
, which is now often called “trembling hand perfect equilibrium,” is to refine the concept of equilibrium to include only those strategy distributions that are robust in the face of a “perturbed” game in which all players use mixed strategies and employ every strategy with at least probability
\varepsilon
.
Drawing inspiration from this idea, we will offer in this study a possible selection principle based on the same principle of “vanishing noise.”
See
[
Gra25
]
for further discussion in a broader context.
It is important to stress that, although the purpose of a selection principle is to uniquely determine an equilibrium, selection principles are themselves not unique.
Each selection principle can be viewed as a modeling choice, which may have certain advantages and disadvantages in various contexts.

The idea of selecting the vanishing noise limit as a weak solution is common in the mathematical theory of PDE.
In the context of Hamilton-Jacobi equations, for example, it gives rise to the viscosity solutions
[
CL83
]
, whereas for nonlinear balance laws one can arrive at entropy solutions
[
Kru70
,
Kru67
]
.
The concept of entropy solutions has recently been used by Cecchin and Delarue to define weak solutions to master equations for potential mean field games
[
CD22b
]
.
Indeed, the connection between mean field games and entropy solutions to classical nonlinear transport equations seems to be a common theme in recent studies of selection principles
[
CP19
,
CD22b
,
DT20
,
GM24
]
.
This theme is a source of inspiration for the main results of this paper.

In this study, we will examine only a specific class of mean field games, which is taken from a recent article by the author and Meszaros
[
GM24
]
.
This class is conveniently simple in one sense, since the Nash equilibrium is determined entirely by a single real number.
When taken as a function of the initial distribution of states and the time horizon, this real number turns out to satisfy a nonlinear transport equation on the space of probability measures, which can be viewed as a simplified (or even “factorized”
[
GM24
]
) master equation.
The study of uniqueness of mean field equilibria is equivalent to studying whether or not characteristics of the transport equation cross each other.

In Section
2
we will construct some explicit examples where uniqueness of the equilibrium fails, both for
N
-player games and for the mean field game.
Then in Section
3
we will offer a selection principle based on a particular stochastic perturbation of the mean field game, letting the noise vanish.
Our principle will be based on the theory of entropy solutions for a sequence of discretized versions of our master equation.
The main result is an error estimate in a certain norm, which measures the distance between two functions over the set of empirical measures; the statement is found in Section
3.1
, and its proof is given in Section
4
.
Finally, in Section
5
, we provide some concluding remarks and open questions.

1.1.
The setting

Consider the following class of mean field games.
For
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we will define a value function

(1.1)

u(t,x)=\inf\mathinner{\left\{\int_{0}^{t}L(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\dif s+g\mathinner{\left(\sigma_{0}(\mu_{0}),x(0)\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}\mathinner{\left([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right)},\ x(t)=x\right\}}

for a given flow of measures
(\mu_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
where
\mu_{t}=m
is given.
Here the cost depends on
(\mu_{s})
only through the final distribution
\mu_{0}
, via the function

(1.2)

\sigma_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}.

Here
{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the set of all Borel probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite second moments.

Nash equilibrium is defined as follows.
Starting from a distribution
m
, all players choose optimal curves that cause an evolution of the population (backwards in time) so that at time
0
the players are distributed according to some new measure
m^{\prime}
.
If
m^{\prime}=\mu_{0}
, the game is said to be in equilibrium.
Formally, this is equivalent to finding a solution to the usual PDE system

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+H(x,Du)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu+\operatorname{div}\mathinner{\left(D_{p}H(x,Du)\mu\right)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u(0,x)

\displaystyle=g\mathinner{\left(\sigma_{0}(\mu_{0}),x\right)},

\displaystyle\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=m.

The coupling occurs only at time
0
, which is simpler than most mean field games.
There is a further simplification in that the equilibrium is entirely characterized by the scalar quantity
\sigma=\sigma_{0}(\mu_{0})
.

Throughout this manuscript we will assume that the optimal control problem in (
1.1
) is always uniquely solvable.
In particular, let us define

(1.4)

v(t,\sigma,x)=\inf\mathinner{\left\{\int_{0}^{t}L(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\dif s+g\mathinner{\left(\sigma,x(0)\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}\mathinner{\left([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right)},\ x(t)=x\right\}}.

Then we assume
v
is a
classical
solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(1.5)

\partial_{t}v+H(x,Dv)=0,\quad v(0,\sigma,x)=g(\sigma,x).

There is a unique optimal trajectory that solves (
1.4
) given by solving

(1.6)

\dot{x}(s)=D_{p}H(x(s),Dv(s,\sigma,x(s))),\quad x(t)=x.

We denote the solution by
x(s,t,\sigma,x)
.
Then we can see that the mean field Nash equilibrium condition is given by

(1.7)

\sigma=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m\right)}.

Here
f_{\sharp}m
denotes the push-forward of
m
through a Borel measurable function
f
, i.e.
f_{\sharp}m(E)=m(f^{-1}(E))
.
If
\sigma
is a solution of (
1.7
), then (
1.3
) is solved by setting
u(t,x)=v(t,\sigma,x)
and
\mu_{s}=x(s,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m
.
Conversely, if
(u,\mu)
is a solution of (
1.3
), then (
1.7
) is solved by setting
\sigma=\sigma_{0}(\mu_{0})
.

Mean field games are supposed to be the limits of
N
-player games as
N\to\infty
.
Let us now define the
N
-player game.
Let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
be given.
Denote by
\bm{x}_{-i}
the vector
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N})
, and denote by
(\bm{x}_{-i},y_{i})
the vector
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N})
.
For
i=1,\ldots,N
define a cost

(1.8)

J_{i}(\bm{x}(\cdot))=\int_{0}^{t}L(x_{i}(s),\dot{x}_{i}(s))\dif s+g\mathinner{\left(\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x(0,t,\sigma_{j},x_{j})}\right)},x_{i}(0)\right)}

over all
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
curves
\bm{x}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,t]\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
such that
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{x}
.
The curve
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\cdot)
is a Nash equilibrium (in open loop controls) provided

(1.9)

J_{i}\mathinner{\left(\widehat{\bm{x}}(\cdot)\right)}=\inf_{x_{i}(\cdot)}J_{i}\mathinner{\left(\widehat{\bm{x}}_{-i}(\cdot),x_{i}(\cdot)\right)}\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,N.

The
N
-player Nash system is given by

(1.10)

\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x(0,t,\sigma_{j},x_{j})}\right)},\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

If system (
1.10
) has a solution, then a Nash equilibrium
\bm{x}(\cdot)
is given by setting
x_{i}(s)=x(s,t,\sigma_{i},x_{i})
for each
i
.
Conversely, if
\bm{x}(\cdot)
is a Nash equilibrium, then
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x_{j}(0)}\right)}
is a solution of (
1.10
).

The purpose of this study is to examine the uniqueness of solutions of both the
N
-player game and the mean field game.
If there is more than one equilibrium, then we are interested in the following question:
is there a selection principle that rationally distinguishes between equilibria, and if so, do we get convergence of selected equilibria to the mean field limit?

1.2.
Notations and basic definitions

The Wasserstein space
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moment, i.e.,

\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\;\middle|\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\,\dif\mu(x)<\infty\right\}.

The Wasserstein distance of order 2 between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\,\dif\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e., the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.
The integral on the right-hand side is called the (quadratic) transportation cost.
The set of optimal couplings, denoted
\Pi_{0}(\mu,\nu)
, is the set of all
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
that minimize the transportation cost; this set is always nonempty
[
Vil08
]
.

Let
u\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
We say that
u
is
continuously differentiable
provided there exists a continuous function
D_{m}u\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

(1.11)

u(\nu)-u(\mu)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}D_{m}u(\mu,x)\cdot(y-x)\dif\pi(x,y)+o\mathinner{\left(W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\right)}

for some (hence for all)
\pi\in\Pi_{0}(\mu,\nu)
.
See e.g.
[
CD17a
,
GT19
]
for a discussion of Wasserstein derivatives and conditions for smoothness.
A simple example is
u(\mu)=\int\phi\dif\mu
where
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function, in which case
D_{m}u(\mu,x)=D\phi(x)
.

A vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
will be denoted
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{d})
.
A vector in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
will be denoted by
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, where each
x_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be written
x_{j}=(x_{j}^{1},\ldots,x_{j}^{d})
.
For
\bm{x}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
, the
n
point empirical measure corresponding to
\bm{x}
is
\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}
, where
\delta_{x}
is a Dirac mass at the point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

For a continuous function
u\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the discretization to
n
point empirical measures is defined by

(1.12)

u_{N}(\bm{x})=u(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}}).

If
u
is continuously differentiable, then so is
u_{N}
, with

(1.13)

D_{x_{j}}u_{N}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{N}D_{m}u(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}},x_{j}).

See, e.g.
[
CD17a
, Proposition 5.39]
.

2.
Uniqueness and non-uniqueness

2.1.
Conditions leading to uniqueness of the mean field equilibrium

As discussed in
[
GM24
]
, the equilibrium condition (
1.7
) has at most one solution under a suitable assumption of monotonicity.
In particular, if the function

(2.1)

F(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sigma-\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m\right)}

is strictly monotone, there is at most one Nash equilibrium.
To give a sufficient condition that guarantees uniqueness, we start by setting

(2.2)

b(t,\sigma,x)=D_{p}H(x,Dv(t,\sigma,x)).

Assumption 2.1
.

\sigma_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Borel measurable and bounded, taking values in some interval
(\sigma_{*},\sigma^{*})
.

Assumption 2.2

(Regularity of
\sigma_{0}
)
.

\sigma_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuously differentiable, and
D_{m}\sigma_{0}
is bounded.
Moreover, for each
N
,
\sigma_{0,N}(\bm{x})=\sigma_{0}(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}})
is twice continuously differentiable with Hölder continuous second derivatives.

We do not always insist on Assumption
2.2
, especially in discussing uniqueness and non-uniqueness, since regularity is not as essential as monotonicity.
Some particularly interesting examples of non-uniqueness arise when
\sigma_{0}
is discontinuous; see Section
2.3
.

Assumption 2.3

(Regularity of
b
)
.

b\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathinner{\left[0,\infty\right)}\times(\sigma_{*},\sigma^{*})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, written
b(t,\sigma,x)
, is a continuous vector field, continuously differentiable with respect to variables
(\sigma,x)
such that
b
is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz with respect to
x
, i.e. there exist constants
M_{b}
and
L_{b}
such that

(2.3)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert b(t,\sigma,x)\right\rvert}\leq M_{b},\ \ \mathinner{\!\left\lvert D_{x}b(t,\sigma,x)\right\rvert}\leq L_{b}\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ \forall t\geq 0,\ \forall\sigma\in(\sigma_{*},\sigma^{*}).

We also assume
b
is Hölder continuous with respect to
t
on bounded intervals
[0,T]
, uniformly with respect to the variables
(\sigma,x)
.
Finally, let
B(t,\sigma,x)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}b(t,u,x)\dif u
.
Then there exists a constant
H_{B}
such that

(2.4)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert D_{xx}^{2}B(t,\sigma,x)\right\rvert}\leq H_{B}\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ \forall t\geq 0,\ \forall\sigma\in(\sigma_{*},\sigma^{*}).

Remark 2.4
.

It follows from (
2.3
) that
D_{x}B
is also bounded.
A sufficient condition implying (
2.4
) is a bound on
D_{xx}^{2}b
.

Unlike Assumption
2.2
, Assumption
2.3
is rather crucial in this study.
It guarantees that, given a parameter
\sigma
and time horizon
t
, the optimal trajectory
x(s,t,\sigma,x)
is uniquely defined and continuous in all of its arguments.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
v=(v^{1},\ldots,v^{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We will write
v\geq 0
and say that
v
is non-Negative provided
v^{j}\geq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
.
If
v^{j}>0
for all
j
then we write
v>0
and say it is positive.
We say
v
is non-positive, resp. negative, if
-v
is non-Negative, resp. positive.

Assumption 2.6

(Monotonicity of
\sigma_{0}
)
.

\sigma_{0}
is “increasing in all directions,” i.e.

(2.5)

D_{m}\sigma_{0}(m,x)\geq 0\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad\forall m\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

cf. Definition
2.5
.

Assumption 2.7

(Monotonicity of
b
)
.

Each component of
b
is strictly increasing with respect to
\sigma
, i.e.
\partial_{\sigma}b>0
in the sense of Definition
2.5
.

Assumption 2.8
.

The vector field
b(t,x,u)
is
diagonal
in the sense that the
j
th component function can be written
b^{j}(t,x^{j},u)
where
x^{j}
is the
j
th component of
x
.
In particular,
D_{x}b(t,x,u)
is a diagonal matrix.

Assumption
2.7
can be thought of as a monotonicity assumption on
b
. However, it only guarantees uniqueness when accompanied by Assumption
2.6
.

A simple example that satisfies Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.6
,
2.7
, and
2.8
is
d=1
,
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}
and
g(\sigma,x)=\sigma x
so that
b(t,\sigma,x)=\sigma t
, and
\sigma_{0}(m)=\int\phi\dif m
where
\phi
is a smooth, bounded, increasing function.
More generally, we have the following:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert p\right\rvert}^{2}
.
Suppose
g(\sigma,x)
satisfies the following:

(1)

D_{xx}^{2}g(\sigma,x)
is diagonal, i.e.
D_{x^{j}x^{k}}^{2}g(\sigma,x)=0
for
j\neq k
;

(2)

D_{x^{j}x^{j}}^{2}g(\sigma,x)\geq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
(hence
g
is convex with respect to
x
);

(3)

D_{xx}^{2}g(\sigma,x)
and
D_{xxx}^{3}g(\sigma,x)
are bounded;

(4)

D_{x^{j}\sigma}^{2}g(\sigma,x)>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
.

Then
b
satisfies all the conditions specified in Assumptions
2.3
,
2.7
, and
2.8
.

Let
\sigma_{0}(m)=\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\dif m(x_{1})\cdots\dif m(x_{n})
where
\phi
is a smooth, bounded function that is increasing in all variables, i.e.
D_{x_{j}}\phi\geq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
(see Definition
2.5
).
Then
\sigma_{0}
satisfies the conditions specified by Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.6
.

Proof.

In this case we have the Hopf-Lax formula

(2.6)

v(t,\sigma,x)=\min\mathinner{\left\{\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}}{2t}+g(\sigma,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\}}.

One computes that the optimal velocity is given by

(2.7)

b(t,\sigma,x)=D_{x}v(t,\sigma,x)=\frac{x-\mathinner{\left(I+tD_{x}g(\sigma,\cdot)\right)}^{-1}(x)}{t}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frac{x-y(t,\sigma,x)}{t}.

By implicit differentiation and the conditions given on
g
, one sees that
y
is smooth and has derivatives given by

(2.8)

\begin{split}\partial_{x^{j}}y^{k}&=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{1+tD_{y^{k}y^{k}}^{2}g(\sigma,y)},\\
\partial_{\sigma}y^{k}&=\frac{-tD_{y^{k}\sigma}^{2}g(\sigma,y)}{1+tD_{y^{k}y^{k}}^{2}g(\sigma,y)}.\end{split}

From this we deduce that
b=D_{y}g(\sigma,y)
satisfies the conditions specified in Assumptions
2.3
,
2.7
, and
2.8
.

As for
\sigma_{0}
, we note that
\sigma_{0}
is smooth with Wasserstein gradient given by

(2.9)

D_{m}\sigma_{0}(m,x)\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N-1}}D_{x_{j}}\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},x,x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})\dif m(x_{1})\cdots\dif m(x_{j-1})\dif m(x_{j+1})\cdots\dif m(x_{n}).

Since
\phi
is increasing in all variables, so is
\sigma_{0}
.
Therefore Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.6
are satisfied.
∎

The assumption that
b
should be increasing with respect to
\sigma
can be interpreted as saying that optimal velocity is an increasing function of the coordination parameter
\sigma
.
Assumption
2.8
is introduced for technical reasons, as we will see presently.

Under these assumptions, it is possible to show that
\partial_{\sigma}x(0,t,\sigma,x)<0
(in the sense that every component is negative).
Indeed, by implicitly differentiating the ODE (
1.6
), one computes

(2.10)

\partial_{\sigma}x(s,t,\sigma,x)=-\int_{s}^{t}\exp\mathinner{\left(-\int_{s}^{\tau}D_{x}b(\tau^{\prime},x_{j}(\tau^{\prime},t,x,\sigma),\sigma)\dif\tau^{\prime}\right)}\partial_{\sigma}b(\tau,x(\tau,t,x,\sigma),\sigma)\dif\tau,

and for
s<t
this is negative by Assumption
2.6
.
Therefore, with
F
defined as in (
2.1
), we see that

(2.11)

F^{\prime}(\sigma)=1-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}D_{m}\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m,x(0,t,\sigma,x)\right)}\partial_{\sigma}x(0,t,\sigma,x)\dif m(x)\geq 1,

hence
F
necessarily has precisely one zero in the range of
\sigma_{0}
.
Therefore Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.6
,
2.7
, and
2.8
give us unconditional uniqueness for the equilibrium, in the sense that it holds no matter the size of
t
.

Throughout the rest of this manuscript, we will always take Assumption
2.3
, concerning the regularity of the optimal vector field
b
.
Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, concerning boundedness and regularity of
\sigma_{0}
, are usually in force when we seek to prove general results; however, they can often be relaxed in the context of finding explicit Nash equilibria.
On the other hand, Assumption
2.6
, on the monotonicity of
\sigma_{0}
, as well as Assumptions
2.7
and
2.8
on the structure of
b
, are only in force when proving uniqueness of a mean field equilibrium.
Otherwise, these assumptions may be dispensed with.

Let us remark that the monotonicity conditions presented here have no necessary connection to other more commonly used conditions, such as the Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition or displacement monotonicity.
That is, Assumptions
2.6
and
2.7
do not imply that the coupling function
g(\sigma_{0}(m),x)
must be monotone in a Lasry-Lions or displacement sense, and vice versa.
This is made clear by the examples found in
[
GM24
,
GM23
]
.
Conceptually, the monotonicity conditions presented here are comparable to the classical conditions leading to global in time smoothness to solutions of nonlinear conservation laws; see e.g.
[
Daf16
, Theorem 6.1.1]
.

In the remainder of this section, we examine examples of non-uniqueness, which is our motivation for studying selection principles.
In deriving examples, we also notice that the
N
-player game can have equilibria that do not converge to mean field equilibria as
N\to\infty
.
This phenomenon has been noticed elsewhere; see for instance
[
Lac16
,
CR20
]
, and see
[
CF18
,
NMT20
,
CP19
,
Lac20
]
for related results.

2.2.
Uniqueness for the mean field does not imply uniqueness for
N
players

Since Assumption
2.6
guarantees uniqueness for the mean field game, one could reasonably ask whether it guarantees uniqueness for the corresponding
N
-player game.
Unfortunately, the answer is no.
In this section we give a simple example to prove this.

Consider the case
d=1
,
H(p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}
,
g(\sigma,x)=x\sigma
, and
\sigma_{0}(m)=\int\phi\dif m
for a function
\phi
that is at least
piecewise
smooth.
We will assume
\phi^{\prime}>0
, so that the monotonicity from Assumption
2.6
holds; in particular, the mean field equilibrium is unique.
Indeed, the mean field equilibrium condition is now precisely

(2.12)

\sigma=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\phi(x-\sigma t)\dif m(x),

and one can check directly that there is a unique solution by virtue of the fact that
\phi
is non-decreasing.
To simplify even further, we will assume
m
is a Dirac mass at zero, so the mean field equilibrium condition is nothing more than

(2.13)

\sigma=\phi(-\sigma t),

which clearly has a unique solution.
On the other hand, for the
N
-player game, condition (
1.10
) becomes

(2.14)

\sigma_{i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\neq i}\phi(-\sigma_{k}t),

where for simplicity we just assume that all players start at
x_{i}=0
, which is the most natural choice given that they should converge on the Dirac mass at zero in the mean field limit.
If
\sigma
is a mean field equilibrium, then one can take
\sigma_{i}=\sigma
for all
i
to get an
N
-player equilibrium.
However, it is in general not the only equilibrium.

Let us suppose that
\phi
is an odd function, i.e.
\phi(x)=-\phi(-x)
, such that
\phi(x)/x
is unbounded on
(0,\infty)
.
(For example, one may take
\phi(x)=x^{\alpha}
for
x>0
where
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
.)
Then there exists a sequence
b_{N}>0
such that
\phi(tb_{N})=(N-1)b_{N}
for all
N
.
Suppose that
N
is even.
For
k
an odd number, we take
\sigma_{k}=b_{N}
, whereas for
k
even we take
\sigma_{k}=-b_{N}
.
Since
\phi
is an odd function, we have that

(2.15)

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi(-t\sigma_{k})=0,

the even terms canceling with the odd terms.
It follows that

(2.16)

\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\neq i}\phi(-t\sigma_{k})=-\frac{1}{N-1}\phi(-t\sigma_{i})=\frac{1}{N-1}\phi(t\sigma_{i}).

Since
\phi(tb_{N})=(N-1)b_{N}
and
\phi
is odd, we deduce that
\frac{1}{N-1}\phi(t\sigma_{i})=\sigma_{i}
for all
i
, hence

(2.17)

\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\neq i}\phi(-t\sigma_{k})=\sigma_{i},

as desired.

This example can be used to show that
N
-player equilibria need not converge to mean field equilibria.
If
\phi(x)/x
is unbounded as
x\to\infty
, this means
b_{N}
can be chosen such that
b_{N}\to\infty
.
Thus the final measure is
\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-tb_{N}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{tb_{N}}
, which diverges.
The issue here is a lack of compactness: the mass escapes to infinity.
Alternatively, if
\phi(x)\to\infty
as
x\to M
for some
M\in(0,\infty)
, we can choose
b_{N}\to M
and get a final measure that converges to
\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-tM}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{tM}
, which is not a mean field equilibrium.
The issue in that case is that
\phi
is singular.
Note that both of these examples violate the boundedness Assumption
2.1
.
Below we will give examples that need not violate this assumption, though they will assume singular data.

2.3.
Non-uniqueness for both kinds of equilibrium

In this section we give examples for which both mean field and
N
-player games can have multiple equilibria.
First, let us show at least one generic condition under which it is possible to derive an
N
-player equilibrium from a mean field equilibrium.

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose
\sigma
is a solution of (
1.7
).
Let
m
be a probability measure with compact support and let
\bm{x}^{N}=(x_{1}^{N},\ldots,x_{N}^{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
be a sequence such that each
x_{j}^{N}
is in the support of
m
and
W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}^{N}},m\right)}\to 0
.
Define
m_{0}=x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m
.
Assume the function
\sigma_{0}
is constant in a neighborhood of
m_{0}
.
Then there exists
N_{0}
sufficiently large so that if
N\geq N_{0}
, then
\sigma_{i}=\sigma
for all
i
gives a solution to (
1.10
).

Proof.

Let
y_{i}^{N}=x(0,t,\sigma,x_{i}^{N})
.
Since
x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)
is Lipschitz, it follows that
W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{j}^{N}},m_{0}\right)}\to 0
, and also that
m_{0}
has compact support.
Notice that each
y_{j}^{N}
is in the support of
m_{0}
.
It follows that

(2.18)

W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{j}^{N}},\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{y_{j}^{N}}\right)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y_{j}-y_{i}\right\rvert}^{2}\leq\frac{D^{2}}{N},

where
D
is the diameter of the support of
m_{0}
.
Let
U
be a neighborhood of
m_{0}
such that
\sigma_{0}
is constant on
U
.
It follows that there exists
N_{0}
such that if
N\geq N_{0}
, we have
\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{y_{j}^{N}}\in U
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, which implies

(2.19)

\sigma=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{y_{j}^{N}}\right)}=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x(0,t,\sigma,x_{j}^{N})}\right)}.

Thus
\sigma_{i}=\sigma
solves system (
1.10
).
∎

It is straightforward to find examples of non-uniqueness.
Let us consider the simple case where
d=1
,
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}
, and
g(\sigma,x)=\sigma x
.
Then optimal trajectories are lines with
x(0,t,\sigma,x)=x-\sigma t
.
The mean field Nash equilibrium condition becomes

(2.20)

\sigma=\sigma_{0}((\tau_{-\sigma t})_{\sharp}m),

where
\tau_{y}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=x+y
.
We will furthermore specify that
\sigma_{0}(m)
depends only on the barycenter of
m
, i.e.

(2.21)

\sigma_{0}(m)=f\mathinner{\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)\right)}.

Hence the Nash equilibrium condition becomes

(2.22)

\sigma=f\mathinner{\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)-\sigma t\right)}.

As explained in
[
GM24
]
, solutions of (
2.22
) are closely related to solutions of the Burgers equation

(2.23)

\partial_{t}\sigma+\sigma\partial_{\xi}\sigma=0,\quad\sigma(0,\xi)=f(\xi),

where the variable
\xi
stands in for
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)
.
We deduce the existence of multiple equilibria in the well-known cases where the Burgers equation has crossing characteristics, namely whenever
f
is not a monotone increasing function.
To give a concrete example, let us consider

(2.24)

f(\xi)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}~\xi<0,\\
0,&\text{if}~\xi\geq 0.\end{cases}

If
0\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)<t
, then
\sigma=0
and
\sigma=1
are both solutions of (
2.22
).

Moreover, if
0<\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)<t
, then by Proposition
2.10
we see that the corresponding
N
-player game can be solved with either
\sigma_{i}=0
for all
i
or
\sigma_{i}=1
for all
i
, for
N
sufficiently large.
There are thus two distinct equilibria for the
N
-player game, each of which converges to one of the two mean field equilibria.
However, there may be other equilibria for the
N
-player game, which do not converge to either of the mean field equilibria.
We now present one such example.

Proposition 2.11
.

Assume that
x(0,t,\sigma,x)=x-\sigma t
and
\sigma_{0}
is given by (
2.21
) and (
2.24
).
Fix
t>0
.
Let
a,b>0
be given.
For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
j=1,\ldots,N
define

(2.25)

\begin{split}J_{N}&\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\lceil\frac{(N-1)b}{a+b}\right\rceil,\\
x_{j}^{N}&\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}a+t&\text{if}~j\leq J_{N},\\
-b&\text{if}~j>J_{N}\end{cases}\end{split}

where
\left\lceil x\right\rceil
means the smallest integer greater than or equal to
x
.
Then for
N
large, there are at least three solutions to the
N
-player Nash equilibrium condition (
1.10
), which are given by

(1)

\sigma_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
;

(2)

\sigma_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,N
; and

(3)

\sigma_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,J_{N}
and
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i=J_{N}+1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

Set
\lambda=\frac{b}{a+b}
.
Let
m=\lambda\delta_{a+t}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{-b}
, where each
\delta_{x}
is a Dirac mass at
x
.
Then we have

(2.26)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}^{N}}=\frac{J_{N}}{N}\delta_{a+t}+\mathinner{\left(1-\frac{J_{N}}{N}\right)}\delta_{-b}\to m

as
N\to\infty
.
We also have

(2.27)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif m(x)=\lambda(a+t)-(1-\lambda)b=t\lambda\in(0,t),

so Proposition
2.10
implies that there are at least two equilibria for
N
large:
\sigma_{i}=0
for all
i
or
\sigma_{i}=1
for all
i
(this can easily be checked directly).

Now set
\sigma_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,J_{N}
and
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i=J_{N}+1,\ldots,N
.
We will now show this is also an equilibrium.
First rewrite (
1.10
) as

(2.28)

\sigma_{i}=f\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)\right)}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}~\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)<0,\\
0,&\text{if}~\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)\geq 0\end{cases}

using (
2.21
) and (
2.24
).
To see that (
2.28
) holds true, note that
\sum_{j=1}^{N}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)=\sum_{j=1}^{J_{N}}(a+t-t)+\sum_{j=J_{N}+1}^{N}(-b)=J_{N}a-(N-J_{N})b
.
We deduce that

(2.29)

\begin{split}\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)&=\begin{cases}(J_{N}-1)a-(N-J_{N})b,&\text{if}~i\leq J_{N},\\
J_{N}a-(N-J_{N}-1)b,&\text{if}~i>J_{N}\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}(a+b)(J_{N}-1)-(N-1)b,&\text{if}~i\leq J_{N},\\
(a+b)J_{N}-(N-1)b,&\text{if}~i>J_{N}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Since
J_{N}=\left\lceil\frac{(N-1)b}{a+b}\right\rceil
, we see that
\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)<0
for
i\leq J_{N}
and
\sum_{j\neq i}(x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t)\geq 0
for
i>J_{N}
, so (
2.28
) holds.
Thus we have an equilibrium.
∎

The first two equilibria given by Proposition
2.11
naturally give final measures that converge to mean field equilibrium measures.
However, the third equilibrium does not.
In this case the final measure is given by

(2.30)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}-\sigma_{j}t}=\frac{J_{N}}{N}\delta_{a}+\mathinner{\left(1-\frac{J_{N}}{N}\right)}\delta_{-b}\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\lambda\delta_{a}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{-b}.

Yet the two mean field equilibria correspond to the measures

(2.31)

\lambda\delta_{a+t}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{-b}\quad(\text{for}\ \sigma=0)\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda\delta_{a}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{-b-t}\quad(\text{for}\ \sigma=1).

Thus the limit of the third equilibrium is a sort of mixture of two mean field equilibria.
The interpretation is that the players at point
a+t
expect the parameter
\sigma
to be
1
, hence they should move to the left by
\sigma t=t
, whereas the players at point
-b
expect
\sigma=0
, hence they should not move.

Notice that the barycenter of the anomalous limit
\lambda\delta_{a}+(1-\lambda)\delta_{-b}
is precisely zero, which is the point of discontinuity of the function
f
.
The existence of the first two equilibria in Proposition
2.11
is not a consequence of the discontinuity, but merely of the fact that
f
is decreasing; this is the same phenomenon as when characteristics of the Burgers equation cross.
It is this third, anomalous equilibrium that emerges as a result of the discontinuity.
When
f
is continuous, the results of Lacker
[
Lac16
]
prove that equilibria for
N
-player games must converge to (weak) mean field equilibria.

Let us generalize Proposition
2.11
to measures whose support consists of more than two points, and to more general optimal trajectories
x(s,t,\sigma,x)
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
d=1
, and let
b(t,\sigma,x)
satisfy Assumptions
2.3
and
2.7
(Assumption
2.8
holds trivially as
d=1
).

Fix
t>0
.
Let
\tilde{\mu}
and
\tilde{\nu}
be probability measures of compact support such that

(2.32)

\operatorname{supp}\tilde{\mu}\subset\mathinner{\left(-\infty,0\right]},\quad\operatorname{supp}\tilde{\nu}\subset\mathinner{\left[c,\infty\right)}

for some
c>0
, and such that
\int x\dif\tilde{\mu}(x)<0
.
Assume also that
\sigma_{0}
is given by (
2.21
) and (
2.24
).
Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
be chosen such that

(2.33)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\dif\mathinner{\left((1-\lambda)\tilde{\mu}+\lambda\tilde{\nu}\right)}=0.

Set
\mu=x(t,0,0,\cdot)_{\sharp}\tilde{\mu}
,
\nu=x(t,0,1,\cdot)_{\sharp}\tilde{\nu}
, and
m=(1-\lambda)\mu+\lambda\nu
.
Then there exists an infinite sequence
N_{k}
such that for
N=N_{k}
, there exists a discrete measure
\mu^{N_{k}}_{\bm{x}_{k}}
such that
W_{2}(\mu^{N_{k}}_{\bm{x}_{k}},m)\to 0
and such that
there are at least three solutions to the
N
-player Nash equilibrium condition (
1.10
), which are given by

(1)

\sigma_{i}=0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
;

(2)

\sigma_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,N
; and

(3)

\sigma_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,J
and
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i=J+1,\ldots,N

where
1<J<N
satisfies
J\approx\lambda N
.

Proof.

Define

(2.34)

\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int x\dif(\tilde{\nu}-\tilde{\mu})(x)\geq c.

For a given,
N
we will choose
J
such that

(2.35)

0\leq J-\lambda N<\frac{c}{\delta}.

In other words, we choose
J=\left\lceil\lambda N\right\rceil
, and we must have
N
such that
\left\lceil\lambda N\right\rceil-\lambda N<\frac{c}{\delta}
.
Note that there are infinitely many
N
for which this holds.

Let
\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{y_{j}}
be the optimal discretization with
J
points of
\tilde{\nu}
, given in Section
A
; likewise, let
\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}\delta_{y_{j}}
be the optimal discretization with
N-J
points of
\tilde{\mu}
.
For
j=1,\ldots,J
we define
x_{j}=x(t,0,1,y_{j})
so that
y_{j}=x(0,t,1,x_{j})
; likewise, for
j=J+1,\ldots,N
we define
x_{j}=x(t,0,0,y_{j})
so that
y_{j}=x(0,t,0,x_{j})
.

Our first objective is to show that
W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}},m\right)}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.
We have that

(2.36)

\begin{split}W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}},\nu\right)}&\leq\operatorname{Lip}(x(t,0,1,\cdot))W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{y_{j}},\tilde{\nu}\right)},\\
W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}},\mu\right)}&\leq\operatorname{Lip}(x(t,0,0,\cdot))W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}},\tilde{\mu}\right)},\end{split}

and by the results of Section
A
these go to zero as
N\to\infty
.

Now we claim that
\mu
and
\nu
have disjoint supports.
To prove this, note that Assumption
2.3
allows us to show that
x(s,t,\sigma,x)
is increasing in the
x
-variable; indeed, it is straightforward to prove that

(2.37)

\partial_{x}x(s,t,\sigma,x)=\exp\mathinner{\left(\int_{t}^{s}\partial_{x}b(\tau,\sigma,x(\tau,t,\sigma,x))\dif\tau\right)}>0.

It follows from (
2.32
) that

(2.38)

\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset\mathinner{\left(-\infty,x(t,0,0,0)\right]},\quad\operatorname{supp}\nu\subset\mathinner{\left[x(t,0,1,c),\infty\right)}.

By (
2.10
) with Assumptions
2.3
and
2.7
it follows that
\partial_{\sigma}x(t,0,\sigma,x)>0
, and so in particular

(2.39)

x(t,0,1,c)>x(t,0,0,c)>x(t,0,0,0).

Thus
\operatorname{supp}\mu\cap\operatorname{supp}\nu=\emptyset
.

Now if
T_{\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a transport map such that
(T_{\nu})_{\sharp}\nu=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}
and
T_{\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a transport map such that
(T_{\mu})_{\sharp}\nu=\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}
, then we can construct a transport map
T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
T=T_{\nu}
on
\operatorname{supp}\nu
and
T=T_{\mu}
on
\operatorname{supp}\mu
.
Then
T_{\sharp}m=\frac{\lambda}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}
and

(2.40)

\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert T(x)-x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif m(x)=\lambda\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert T_{\nu}(x)-x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\nu(x)+(1-\lambda)\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert T_{\mu}(x)-x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\mu(x).

Since
T_{\mu}
and
T_{\nu}
are arbitrary, it follows that

(2.41)

W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{\lambda}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}},m\right)}^{2}\leq\lambda W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}},\nu\right)}+(1-\lambda)W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}},\mu\right)}^{2},

which becomes arbitrarily small as
N\to\infty
.
Finally, we construct a transport plan from
\frac{\lambda}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}
to
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}
as follows.
Noting that
\frac{\lambda}{J}\leq\frac{1}{N}
and
\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\geq\frac{1}{N}
, we let
\pi
be a discrete measure such that

(2.42)

\begin{split}\pi(\{x_{j},x_{j}\})&=\frac{\lambda}{J},\quad j=1,\ldots,J,\\
\pi(\{x_{j},x_{j}\})&=\frac{1}{N},\quad j=J+1,\ldots,N,\\
\pi(\{x_{j},x_{k}\})&=\frac{J-\lambda N}{NJ(N-J)},\quad j=J+1,\ldots,N,\ k=1,\ldots,J.\end{split}

This is a probability measure that couples
\frac{\lambda}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}
to
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}
, and so

(2.43)

\begin{split}W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{\lambda}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{N-J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{x_{j}},\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}\right)}^{2}&\leq\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\pi(x,y)\\
&=\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x_{j}-x_{k}\right\rvert}^{2}\frac{J-\lambda N}{NJ(N-J)}\\
&\leq\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{J}2(x_{j}^{2}+x_{k})^{2}\frac{J-\lambda N}{NJ(N-J)}\\
&=\frac{2(J-\lambda N)}{N(N-J)}\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}+\frac{2(J-\lambda N)}{NJ}\sum_{k=1}^{J}x_{k}^{2}.\end{split}

Since
J-\lambda N\leq 1
,
\frac{1}{N-J}\sum_{j=J+1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}\to\int x^{2}\dif\mu(x)
and
\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}x_{j}^{2}\to\int x^{2}\dif\nu(x)
as
N\to\infty
, it follows that the right-hand side of (
2.43
) goes to zero as
N\to\infty
.
We conclude that
W_{2}\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}},m\right)}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Now we prove that the three equilibria defined in the Proposition are indeed solutions to (
1.10
).
Equivalently, we need to show

(2.44)

\sigma_{i}=f\mathinner{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}x(0,t,\sigma_{j},x_{j})\right)}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}~\sum_{j\neq i}x(0,t,\sigma_{j},x_{j})<0,\\
0,&\text{if}~\sum_{j\neq i}x(0,t,\sigma_{j},x_{j})\geq 0.\end{cases}

To see that the constant
\sigma_{i}=0
is an equilibrium, recall that, by (

\begin{split}\int x\dif\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,0,\cdot)\sharp m\right)}(x)&=\int x(0,t,0,x)\dif m(x)\\

2.4.
A selection principle for the
N
-player game

In light of the discussion of this section, we will propose a modest “selection principle” for the
N
-player game, which is that
we should at least eliminate those equilibria that do not converge to a mean field equilibrium
.
Generically, we expect all
N
-player equilibria to converge to a mean field equilibrium
[
Lac16
]
, which we can see here by a direct argument.
Suppose
\sigma_{0}
is continuous and bounded, and let
m
be a probability measure with compact support, with empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}^{N}}
converging to
m
.
Suppose
\sigma_{1}^{N},\ldots,\sigma_{N}^{N}
is an
N
-player equilibrium, i.e. a solution to (
1.10
).
Notice that

(2.54)

\sigma_{i}^{N}=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\cdot,\cdot)_{\sharp}\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{\sigma^{N}_{j},x_{j}^{N}}\right)}.

If we pass to a subsequence on which
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\sigma_{j}^{N},x_{j}^{N}}
has a cluster point, which we call
\mu
, then on that subsequence we get, following the proof of Proposition
2.10
,

(2.55)

\sigma_{i}^{N}\to\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\cdot,\cdot)_{\sharp}\mu\right)},

uniformly in
i
.
But this implies that
\mu=\delta_{\sigma}\times m
where
\sigma
satisfies

(2.56)

\sigma=\sigma_{0}\mathinner{\left(x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m\right)},

i.e. the
N
-player equilibrium converges to a mean field equilibrium.

The examples where this fails are examples where
\sigma_{0}
fails to satisfy either boundedness or continuity.
The way in which they fail can be interpreted as introducing an asymmetry: two segments of the population are distinguished and choose complementary strategies to form an equilibrium.
Therefore, our selection principle for
N
-player equilibrium is really an insistence on complete symmetry, such that the forming of “factions” is not allowed.

3.
The “trembling hand”

The purpose of this section is to investigate one method of selecting from among multiple mean field equilibria.
The inspiration for our approach is the notion introduced by Selten
[
HS88
,
Sel75
]
, which is now commonly called “trembling hand perfect equilibrium.”
To understand this concept, we first need to define an
\varepsilon
-perturbation of a game.
For a finite game (i.e. finite number of strategies), this can be defined by changing the game so that every player must use mixed strategies, assigning probabilities of order
\varepsilon
to every possible strategy.
A trembling hand perfect equilibrium can be defined as a strategy distribution that continues to be an equilibrium for all
\varepsilon
-perturbed games with
\varepsilon
small enough.
This definition is not suitable for differential games, in which the space of strategies is infinite and even infinite-dimensional.
We propose instead to define
\varepsilon
-perturbations of the game as follows.
As we have done throughout this article, we assume that each player always has a unique best response, in the form of a feedback control, to each possible population behavior.
To perturb the game, we posit that each player’s trajectory will be driven by this feedback control plus
\varepsilon
times a Brownian motion.
In other words, each player intends to play rationally in a deterministic setting, but their actual behavior is stochastically perturbed.
We will admit a certain mean field Nash equilibrium as a “trembling hand perfect” equilibrium if it is robust with respect to this perturbation.

Let us make this notion mathematically precise.
Recall that
b(t,\sigma,x)=D_{p}H(x,Dv(t,\sigma,x))
is the optimal response vector field.
Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.7
, and
2.8
are in force.
Let us introduce an additional assumption, which will make it easier to prove the main result of this section:

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists
R>0
such that

(3.1)

\operatorname{supp}\sigma_{0}\subset\mathinner{\left\{m\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif m(x)\leq R^{2}\right\}}.

If we start with a
\sigma_{0}
that satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, we can modify it to satisfy
3.1
by using a cut-off function.
To wit, let
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function supported in
[-R^{2},R^{2}]
, and define
\tilde{\sigma}_{0}(m)=\phi\mathinner{\left(\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif m(x)\right)}\sigma_{0}(m)
.
Then
\tilde{\sigma}_{0}
satisfies all three Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
3.1
.

The mean field equilibrium condition (
1.7
) can be expressed as the following system of equations:

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(s)=b(s,\sigma,x(s)),&x(t)=x\\
\sigma=\sigma_{0}(x(0,t,\sigma,\cdot)_{\sharp}m).&\end{cases}

In order to introduce noise, we face a difficulty: the space of probability measures is infinite-dimensional.
Thus it is not clear how to define a noise that acts on all players both independently and all at once. (See, however, some work in this direction by Delarue
[
Del19
]
.)
Our solution will be to discretize, considering only distributions that are empirical measures:

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{i}(s)=b(s,\sigma,x_{i}(s)),&x_{i}(t)=x_{i}\\
\sigma=\sigma_{0,N}\mathinner{\left(\bm{x}(0)\right)}&\end{cases}

where we recall that
\sigma_{0,N}(\bm{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}
.
One could choose to think of (
3.3
) as an
N
-player game in which player
i
reacts to the measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{j}}
, which includes herself, instead of
\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{x_{j}}
as in (
1.10
).
This forces all players to calculate the same parameter
\sigma
, thus following the selection principle from Section
2.4
.
However, for our purposes, (
3.3
) is nothing more than a special case of the mean field equilibrium, in which the population distribution is an empirical measure characterized by the vector
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
.

To add noise, let
(W(s))_{s\geq-t}
be a standard Brownian motion in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
adapted to a filtration
({\mathcal{F}}_{s})_{s\geq-t}
.
We set

(3.4)

x_{i}(s)=\bar{x}_{i}(-s),\quad\sigma(s)=\bar{\sigma}(-s)

where
\bar{\bm{x}},\bar{\sigma}
solve the system

(3.5)

\displaystyle\dif\bar{x}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=-b(-s,\bar{\sigma}(s),\bar{x}_{i}(s))\,\dif s+\varepsilon^{N}\,\dif W_{i}(s),\quad\bar{x}_{i}(-t)=x_{i},

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle=E\mathinner{\left[\sigma_{0,N}\mathinner{\left(\bm{x}(0)\right)}\middle|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}\right]},\quad-t\leq s\leq 0.

The interpretation is as follows.
Instead of trajectories
x_{i}(s)
following precisely the optimal dynamics for a given
\sigma
, their trajectory is perturbed by a Brownian motion. Then, instead of
\sigma
being a constant determined by (
3.3
), it is a martingale, which is obtained at each time by conditioning
\sigma_{0,N}(\bm{x}(0))
on the knowledge player
i
has up to the present time. We have reversed time with the substitutions (
3.4
); this was necessary only because the PDEs studied in Section
3.1
have forward-in-time solutions. The parameter
\varepsilon>0
is a small parameter, such that
\varepsilon^{N}
measures the strength of the noise. We note that as
N\to\infty
, this strength decreases exponentially. We will see later precisely what role
\varepsilon
plays in the selection of equilibria.

The noise introduced here is not the same thing as an
N
-player game with idiosyncratic noise. In an
N
-player game with noise, each player selects a strategy that optimizes the expected value of the objective functional over all stochastic curves. In this setting, by contrast, the noise represents a stochastic deviation from what would otherwise be a deterministic optimal velocity. It represents the idea that, although the parameter
\sigma
might be given and the corresponding optimal trajectory
x_{i}(s)
definitely computable, player
i
will not adhere to the optimal trajectory with absolute certainty. This is the “trembling hand.”
(It is also not to be confused with common noise, since, although it affects the whole population distribution at once, it does so in a way that affects each Dirac mass independently.)

For each
t
and for each
\bm{x}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
, let
\sigma=\sigma(t,\bm{x})
be an equilibrium solving (
3.3
) and let
\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t,\bm{x})
be a solution of (
3.5
).
For the purposes of this study, we will say that
\sigma
is a trembling hand perfect equilibrium if the limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\sigma
holds in some suitable sense (see Theorem
3.3
).
However, it needs to be emphasized that such a definition is far from unique; there are in fact many ways of selecting from among multiple mean field Nash equilibria.
In particular, we have chosen to perturb the path of the empirical measure by making it a martingale. If we instead allow other sorts of stochastic perturbations, then we can get any number of selection principles, as in
[
LS23
]
.
See
[
Gra25
]
for a discussion.

Our main contribution is to explicitly account for the dependence on

Let us introduce a seminorm on measurable functions

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathinner{\left(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}})\right\rvert}^{N}\dif\bm{x}\right)}.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\sigma_{N}
and
\sigma_{N,\varepsilon}
be as in Theorem
3.3
.
Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathinner{\left[0,\infty\right)}\times{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
and

be measurable functions satisfying

We prove Corollary

The interpretation of this main result is as follows.

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t,m)
gives the solution to the perturbed game (
3.5
) for any discrete measure
m=\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}}
with
N
and
\bm{x}
arbitrary. It has a limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in sense of Corollary
3.4
.
This new function,

, can be interpreted as a weak solution to the mean field equilibrium condition (
1.7
).
It is in general not clear whether or not, for an arbitrary discrete measure

,

is in fact a Nash equilibrium; see
[
GM24
]
for examples showing the answer can sometimes be “no.”
Nevertheless, in terms of rational response,

represents the parameter that players should anticipate if they expect all players to interpret this parameter as a martingale with vanishingly small variance.
In this sense, it can be interpreted as a “selection” of a mean field equilibrium, even if it is sometimes a fictitious one.

Remark 3.5
.

It seems natural to conjecture that, under Assumptions
2.3
,
2.7
and
2.8
on the vector field

give Nash equilibria for a.e.
\bm{x}
, i.e. (

.
The intuition behind this conjecture is that Assumptions

generalize the condition for one-dimensional conservation laws that the flux function should be strictly convex.
One then expects that shocks do not meet to form rarefaction waves, which is what would cause (

This will be a topic for future research.

4.
Proof of Theorem

The proof of Theorem

[

The main we point we need to emphasize is how the estimates depend on the dimension of the space, which in turn corresponds to the number of points in the empirical measure.
Our estimates will thus hinge on a few basic observations from vector calculus.
Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a vector field, and suppose
\bm{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
is given by
\bm{F}(\bm{x})=(F(x_{1}),\ldots,F(x_{N}))
.
Define

(4.1)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\operatorname{esssup}_{x}|F(x)|,

where as usual
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
It follows that

(4.2)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{F}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\sqrt{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

Now consider the derivative
DF(x)
at a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We define the norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert DF(x)\right\rvert}
by

(4.3)

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert DF(x)\right\rvert}=\sup_{|u|=1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert DF(x)u\right\rvert}.

We then define

(4.4)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert DF\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\operatorname{esssup}_{x}|DF(x)|.

Since
D\bm{F}(\bm{x})
is diagonal, one sees that

(4.5)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\bm{F}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert DF\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

Similarly, we define

(4.6)

\begin{split}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert D^{2}F(x)\right\rvert}&=\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u\right\rvert}=1,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert v\right\rvert}=1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert D^{2}F(x)[u,v]\right\rvert},\\
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D^{2}F\right\rVert}_{\infty}&=\operatorname{esssup}_{x}|D^{2}F(x)|\end{split}

and observe that

(4.7)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D^{2}\bm{F}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D^{2}F\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

On the other hand, if we consider the divergence operator, we see that
\operatorname{div}\bm{F}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{div}F(x_{j})
, and thus

(4.8)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\operatorname{div}\bm{F}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=N\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\operatorname{div}F\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

It is also worth noting that the optimal estimate for the divergence in terms of the derivative is given by

(4.9)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\operatorname{div}F\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq d\mathinner{\!\left\lVert DF\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

Let us now recall the definitions of
\bm{b}
,
B
, and
\bm{B}
from (

).
Since
\bm{B}
is the flux function from (
3.11

) written as a balance law, the proof of Theorem

will depend on estimates of

and its derivatives, which we collect here: from Assumption

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\partial_{\sigma}\bm{B}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq M_{b}\sqrt{N},

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\partial_{\sigma}D_{\bm{x}}\bm{B}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{\bm{x}}\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq L_{b},

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\partial_{\sigma}\operatorname{div}_{\bm{x}}\bm{B}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\operatorname{div}_{\bm{x}}\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq L_{b}Nd,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{\bm{xx}}^{2}\bm{B}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq H_{B},

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D_{\bm{x}}\operatorname{div}_{\bm{x}}\bm{B}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

Lemma 4.1

Proof.

The proof essentially follows the argument found in

4.3.
Homogenizing the error estimates with respect to
N

Our goal in this section is to prove Corollary
3.4
, which is interpreted as an error estimate that is uniform with respect to
N
, i.e. with respect to all possible empirical measures.
We make use of the following seminorm.
For
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
measurable, we define

(4.51)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathinner{\left(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f_{N}(\bm{x})\right\rvert}^{N}\dif\bm{x}\right)}^{1/N}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathinner{\left(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}})\right\rvert}^{N}\dif\bm{x}\right)}^{1/N}.

It is straightforward to check that (
4.51
) defines a seminorm on the space of measurable functions.

Before proceeding to the proof of Corollary
3.4
, it will be useful to point out some properties of the seminorm defined in (
4.51
).
First, let us consider
f=1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}
, where
\delta_{0}
denotes the Dirac mass at the origin, so that

(4.52)

B_{R}(\delta_{0})=\mathinner{\left\{\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathinner{\left(\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\mu(x)\right)}^{1/2}\leq R\right\}}.

Thus

(4.53)

f(\mu)=1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}(\mu)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}~\mathinner{\left(\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\mu(x)\right)}^{1/2}\leq R,\\
0&\text{if}~\mathinner{\left(\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\dif\mu(x)\right)}^{1/2}>R.\end{cases}

It is instructive to compute
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert 1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}\right\rVert}
.
Observe that

(4.54)

f_{N}(\bm{x})=1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}(\mu^{N}_{\bm{x}})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}~\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\bm{x}\right\rvert}\leq\sqrt{N}R,\\
0&\text{if}~\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\bm{x}\right\rvert}>\sqrt{N}R\end{cases}=1_{B_{\sqrt{N}R}(0)}(\bm{x}).

Thus
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert 1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}\right\rVert}
essentially comes down to the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
denote
n

B_{1}
a ball of radius 1.
Then

(4.55)

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/2}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(B_{1})^{1/n}=\sqrt{2\pi e}.

Proof.

Define
A_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=n^{1/2}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(B_{1})^{1/n}

Recall the well-known formula

(4.56)

{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(B_{1})=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\mathinner{\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}}.

Thus
A_{n}=(n\pi)^{1/2}\Gamma\mathinner{\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}^{-1/n}
.

By a change of variables, we have

(4.57)

\Gamma\mathinner{\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n/2}e^{-t}\dif t=n^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathinner{\left(t^{1/2}e^{-t}\right)}^{n}\dif t.

The function
t^{1/2}e^{-t}
is bounded on
\mathinner{\left[0,\infty\right)}
, and a simple calculation shows that its maximum is
(2e)^{-1/2}
.
We deduce that

(4.58)

n^{-1/2}\Gamma\mathinner{\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}^{1/n}=n^{1/n}\mathinner{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathinner{\left(t^{1/2}e^{-t}\right)}^{n}\dif t\right)}^{1/n}\to(2e)^{-1/2},

from which the claim follows.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert 1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}\right\rVert}=R^{d}(2\pi e/d)^{d/2}
.
Thus, if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathscr{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is any bounded measurable function whose support is contained in the set
B_{R}(\delta_{0})

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}\leq R^{d}(2\pi e/d)^{d/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{u}
, where
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{u}=\sup_{m}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f(m)\right\rvert}
.

Proof.

Let
f=1_{B_{R}(\delta_{0})}
.
From Lemma
4.3
we have

(4.59)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\left(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f_{N}(\bm{x})\right\rvert}^{N}\dif\bm{x}\right)}^{1/Nd}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{L}}^{Nd}(B_{N^{1/2}R}(0))^{1/Nd}

\displaystyle=N^{1/2}R{\mathcal{L}}^{Nd}(B_{1}(0))^{1/Nd}

\displaystyle\to R(2\pi e/d)^{1/2},

and the claim follows by taking both sides to the

The claim for general bounded

with support in

follows easily.
∎

Thus convergence in the seminorm is strictly weaker than uniform convergence for sequences of functions with bounded support.

Let

By the maximum principle,

.
It follows from Theorem

\mathinner{\left(\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sigma_{N}(t,\bm{x})-\sigma_{N,\varepsilon}(t,\bm{x})\right\rvert}^{N}\dif\bm{x}\right)}^{1/N}\\

5.
Conclusion

In this paper we have used a simple class of mean field games to explore the issue of non-uniqueness for Nash equilibrium.
We have constructed explicit examples where uniqueness can fail, thus demonstrating the need for a selection principle.
The selection principle we have proposed relies on the classical theory of entropy solution, and it selects an equilibrium whenever the given initial measure is discrete, i.e. an empirical measure.
To this we add the caveat that the selection is only defined for “almost every” empirical measure, i.e. for
m=\mu_{\bm{x}}^{N}
for a.e.
\bm{x}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
, for every
N
.
This selection can be interpreted as a “vanishing noise” selection because of Theorem
3.3
, and Corollary
3.4
shows that the error between the selected equilibrium and the perturbed equilibrium from (
3.5
) can be made uniform with respect to
N
.

We are left, however, with some open questions.
Our method does not directly provide a selection principle for arbitrary initial measures.
Since any measure in the Wasserstein space can be approximated by empirical measures, one might expect that a selection principle for empirical measures to extend more generally.
However, our error estimates rely on
L^{1}
estimates in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{N}
, which do not extend naturally to the whole Wasserstein space.
Thus, a major open question is whether and how a vanishing noise limit could be established for measures that are not discrete.
A second open question would be to prove the conjecture mentioned in Remark
3.5
, concerning whether entropy solutions necessarily give genuine equilibria.
Finally, concerning the
N
-player game, we know that System (
1.10
) is associated with a system of PDEs.
While the
N
-player equilibria are expected to converge to mean field equilibria, it is not clear whether one can prove a similar result for entropy solutions to the PDE system.

To conclude, it is worth remarking that we have seen rather deep mathematical questions arise from a seemingly very simple class of mean field games.
The questions left open are quite difficult in full generality and will perhaps be best approached using new families of special cases.

(3)

(4)
