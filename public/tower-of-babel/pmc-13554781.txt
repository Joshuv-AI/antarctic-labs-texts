Mitral Regurgitation Phenotype, Coronary Physiology, and Outcomes After Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Julian Alexander A. Huibonhoa, Haw-Ruey Hsieh, Ching-Wei Lee, Yi-Lin Tsai, Su-Chan Chen, Tai-Wei Chen, Chun-Chin Chang, Chiao-Po Hsu, Shih-Hsien Sung
Published: 2026

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is a major cause of heart failure (HF) and cardiovascular morbidity.1 Transcatheter mitral valve edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has become an established treatment for severe MR in patients at elevated surgical risk.2, 3, 4 However, outcomes after M-TEER remain heterogeneous and are influenced by the underlying MR phenotype.5,6 Functional MR (FMR) reflects ventricular remodeling and myocardial dysfunction, whereas degenerative MR (DMR) is primarily caused by intrinsic valvular pathology. In addition, coronary artery disease is common among patients undergoing M-TEER, yet the prognostic significance of lesion-level ischemia in this population remains uncertain.7,8 We investigated the association between angiography-derived virtual fractional flow reserve (vFFR),9 MR phenotype, and clinical outcomes after M-TEER.

We retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients undergoing successful M-TEER at Taipei Veterans General Hospital between April 2016 and January 2025. Patients with analyzable preprocedural coronary angiography suitable for 3-vessel vFFR assessment and longitudinal follow-up were included. MR etiology was classified as DMR or FMR according to contemporary echocardiographic criteria. Physiologically significant coronary disease was defined as vFFR <0.80 in at least one major epicardial coronary artery. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital.

Ninety patients were included, comprising 38 patients with FMR and 52 patients with DMR. Compared with patients with DMR, those with FMR demonstrated significantly greater ventricular remodeling and HF burden, including lower left ventricular ejection fraction (48.0% [32-57] vs 61% [55-65], P < 0.001), higher NT-proBNP levels (4,122 [1,759-14,455] vs 729 [242-2,545] pg/mL, P < 0.001), larger ventricular volumes, and worse right ventricular function. The prevalence of physiologically significant coronary disease did not differ significantly between groups, with at least one vFFR-positive lesion identified in 23.6% (9/38) of patients with FMR and 34.6% (18/52) of patients with DMR (P = 0.352). Similarly, the rates of coronary revascularization performed before or after M-TEER did not differ significantly between the FMR and DMR groups (7.9% [3/38] vs 11.5% [6/52], respectively; P = 0.728).

At the 1-year follow-up, both groups demonstrated evidence of reverse remodeling following M-TEER, including reductions in left ventricular volumes and pulmonary artery systolic pressure. Nevertheless, patients with FMR continued to exhibit larger ventricular dimensions and lower systolic function than patients with DMR.

During a median follow-up of 934 days (IQR: 477-1,519), adverse clinical outcomes occurred more frequently among patients with FMR. All-cause mortality occurred in 21.1% (8/38) of patients with FMR and 7.7% (4/52) of patients with DMR (P = 0.086), whereas HF hospitalization occurred in 15.8% (6/38) and 3.8% (2/52), respectively (P = 0.054) (Figure 1). The composite endpoint of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization was significantly more common in patients with FMR than in patients with DMR (28.9% [11/38] vs 9.6% [5/52], P = 0.025). In univariable Cox analysis, FMR phenotype was associated with a more than 3-fold increase in risk for the composite endpoint (HR: 3.08; 95% CI: 1.07-8.86; P = 0.037). Higher Society of Thoracic Surgeons score was also associated with adverse outcomes (HR: 1.07 per point increase; 95% CI: 1.03-1.11; P = 0.001). In contrast, vFFR-positive lesions were not associated with the composite endpoint (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.22-2.16; P = 0.527). After multivariable adjustment, Society of Thoracic Surgeons score remained independently associated with adverse outcomes (HR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.02-1.10; P = 0.002), whereas the association between FMR and the composite endpoint was attenuated and no longer reached statistical significance (HR: 2.54; 95% CI: 0.87-7.42; P = 0.087).

Several important findings emerged from this study. First, MR phenotype was strongly associated with outcomes after M-TEER. Patients with FMR experienced significantly higher rates of death or HF hospitalization despite successful transcatheter correction of MR. Second, patients with FMR exhibited a substantially greater burden of ventricular remodeling and neurohormonal activation at baseline, suggesting that underlying myocardial disease rather than valvular dysfunction alone contributes to long-term prognosis. Third, angiography-derived coronary physiology demonstrated limited prognostic value in this cohort.

The poorer outcomes observed in FMR are biologically plausible. Unlike DMR, which primarily reflects leaflet pathology, FMR represents a manifestation of adverse ventricular remodeling and chronic HF. Consequently, correction of MR may improve hemodynamics without fully reversing the underlying myocardial disease process.10 This concept is supported by the observation that both MR phenotypes experienced reverse remodeling after M-TEER, yet patients with FMR continued to demonstrate greater ventricular dysfunction and a higher incidence of HF-related events. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that successful MR reduction does not eliminate the adverse prognostic impact of the underlying myocardial substrate in FMR. In patients with DMR, MR is often the primary driver of symptoms and ventricular volume overload; therefore, correction of the valvular lesion may substantially normalize cardiac loading conditions and improve long-term outcomes. In contrast, FMR frequently represents a downstream manifestation of diffuse myocardial disease, including ischemic cardiomyopathy, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, myocardial fibrosis, and adverse ventricular remodeling. Consequently, although M-TEER effectively reduces regurgitant volume and improves hemodynamic status, the underlying disease process often persists. This concept may explain why patients with FMR in the present study demonstrated evidence of reverse remodeling yet continued to experience a higher incidence of HF-related events. From a clinical perspective, these observations highlight the importance of comprehensive management beyond procedural success alone. Optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy, careful surveillance for recurrent HF, and individualized management of ventricular dysfunction may remain critical determinants of long-term outcomes in patients with FMR undergoing M-TEER.

The absence of a significant association between vFFR-positive lesions and clinical outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. Limited statistical power, modest event numbers, and nonstandardized revascularization strategies may have attenuated detectable associations. Moreover, outcomes after M-TEER may be driven predominantly by ventricular substrate and HF severity rather than by focal epicardial ischemia alone. Therefore, although coronary physiological assessment may provide complementary information regarding ischemic burden, its incremental prognostic value appears modest compared with MR phenotype.

In conclusion, although both MR phenotypes exhibited favorable cardiac remodeling after M-TEER, residual ventricular dysfunction and HF vulnerability remained more pronounced in patients with FMR. Clinical outcomes after M-TEER appear to be driven primarily by MR phenotype and the underlying ventricular substrate rather than by lesion-level vFFR.
