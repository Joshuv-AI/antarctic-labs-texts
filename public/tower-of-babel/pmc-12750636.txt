Pathogen profiles in CSF from patients with acute infectious meningitis or encephalitis: a multicenter study based on multiplex PCR and mNGS in China, 2018–2019
Authors: Changcheng Wu, Xinlei Sun, Wei Zhang, Yang Yang, Peihua Niu, Jing Chen, Xuejie Zhang, Roujian Lu, Wenling Wang, Zhihai Chen, Wenjie Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute meningitis or encephalitis (AME) is mainly caused by viral and bacterial infections, leading to severe sequelae and even death. However, there is limited systematic research on the spectrum of pathogens causing AME in China. Here, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from 229 acute meningitis or encephalitis patients (AMEP) excluding Japanese encephalitis virus infections collected from multicenter of China between 2018 and 2019 were analyzed. The pathogen profile was comprehensively characterized using both 43-pathogens multiplex PCR (MPCR) and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS). MPCR revealed that 73 (31.88%) cases had at least one potential pathogen. mNGS were conducted on 22 randomly selected AMEPs, and the results showed that mNGS reported all the pathogens detected by MPCR. Interestingly, mNGS successfully identified multiple pathogens in cases that were undiagnosed by MPCR. The most frequently detected viruses by MPCR were Human herpesvirus (HHV), Adenovirus, Enterovirus/Rhinovirus (Ev/Rv), and Bocavirus. The most abundant bacteria were Bordetella pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae, and Listeria monocytogenes. Coronavirus and other respiratory viruses could also induce AME. Ev/Rv were significantly enriched in the adolescent cases, and tick-borne encephalitis cases were concentrated in northeastern China. This study systematically characterized the pathogen spectrum and distribution of AME in China. Additionally, it revealed that combining MPCR and mNGS significantly improves the diagnostic rate of pathogens in AME. These findings will provide important support for the diagnosis, treatment, and development of drugs and vaccines for AME.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12204-7.Keywords: Acute meningitis, Encephalitis, Metagenomic next generation sequencing, Multiplex PCR, Cerebrospinal fluid

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) infection is an acute or chronic inflammatory disease caused by pathogens such as viruses [1], bacteria [2], or fungi [3]. Major types include encephalitis, meningitis, and myelitis. This infection poses a serious threat to human health and can lead to death or severe sequelae. There are reported cases of CNS infections worldwide. Noticeably, there is currently a lack of systematic research on the pathogenic spectrum of CNS infections in the Chinese region. Recent longitudinal monitoring results for patients with acute meningitis or encephalitis in China over the past 10 years have shown that AME is more susceptible in children, and viral pathogens are one of the main causes of mortality in affected children [4]. Only four pathogens were tested, and the results showed significant differences in the age and geographic distribution characteristics of the infected cases. Additionally, community-acquired bacterial meningitis (CABM) has spread globally, particularly affecting children [5–7].

Traditional microbiological detection methods have limitations in diagnosing the etiology of CNS infections, especially in detecting rare pathogens. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing technology has the characteristics of high throughput and high resolution, allowing for the detection of all pathogenic microorganisms including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites [8–10]. mNGS is considered an important and highly sensitive method for pathogen detection, contributing to the definitive diagnosis of infectious diseases and personalized patient treatment. mNGS has advantages in diagnosing mixed infections as well as detect potential pathogens in negative cerebrospinal fluid cultures [11, 12]. Recently, large-scale AME cohort studies have been reported in China. Liu et al. screened 94 infection cases and found that mNGS significantly improved the pathogen detection rate compared to traditional detection methods. In addition, they identified at least 27 bacterial species, 10 viral species, and 4 fungal species as potential major pathogens causing the aforementioned cases [13]. Wang et al. analyzed 428 central nervous system infections patients in China, and found mNGS could identify pathogens from 34.6% samples [14]. The most reported pathogens were Varicella-zoster virus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Herpes simplex virus, and Epstein-Barr virus. Xing et al. analyzed 213 cases of central nervous system infections and found that the pathogen detection sensitivity of mNGS was close to 80% [15]. However, most studies have not conducted detailed analysis between pathogen detection results and infection population characteristics. Further analysis is needed regarding the target population and geographic distribution characteristics of acute encephalitis infection pathogens in China.

Cerebrospinal fluid is advantageous for AME pathogen detection due to its direct reflection of central nervous system infection status and easy, quick sample collection through procedures like lumbar puncture, enhancing diagnostic accuracy with high specificity. This study collected CSF samples from 229 AMEPs in multiple regions of China from 2018 to 2019. Based on multiplex PCR diagnosis and mNGS, a comprehensive characterization of the pathogenic microbial spectrum in AME was performed, revealing both common and atypical viral and bacterial pathogens. We also compared the diagnostic results between mNGS and MPCR. Based on information such as age and geographic location of the infection cases, we identified several pathogens with infection group or geographic distribution specificity.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by The Third People’s Hospital of Shenzhen (Approval No. 2016-001), and Beijing Ditan Hospital Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2018-027-03). All cases were informed in advance and authorized to use their clinical samples for this project.

Study design and participants

From October 2018 to October 2019 in China, 239 cerebrospinal fluid samples from 229 AMEPs were collected from the northern, eastern, and southern China. Case definitions of suspected AME were created prior to study initiation and clinically based: having acute onset of fever, headache, or vomiting, accompanied with at least one of neural paralysis, disturbance of consciousness, meningeal irritation sign or changes in mental status. All cases have been ruled out for Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) infection based on PCR and IgM tests. Most of the cases under study had a previous history of antibiotic treatment, with variations in both the dosages administered and the drugs used.

Multiplex PCR diagnosis

After extracting RNA/DNA from the sample, both the MeningoFinder and RespiFinder 2SMART assay (Shanghai GeneoDx Biotech Co., Ltd. NO. PF-1200 and SMR01) were performed for initial screening of pathogens. The MeningoFinder 2SMART assay, utilizing single-tube multiplex PCR technology, enables the identification of 22 common pathogens responsible for CNS infection including 12 virus and 8 bacteria as well as two fungi. The RespiFinder 2SMART assay can detect 22 respiratory pathogens, including 16 RNA viruses and 2 DNA viruses, as well as four bacteria. A detailed list of the targeted pathogens is provided in Table S1. After configuring the target gene amplification system according to the instructions, the PCR reaction program is set: 50 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 2 min, and then cycling for 40 times at 94 °C for 20 s − 55 °C for 20 s − 72 °C for 35 s. At the same time, prepare the fluorescence PCR detection system and place it in the LightCycler480 II real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. The reaction conditions are as follows: 5 °C for 2 min, followed by 10 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s − 55 °C for 15 s − 72 °C for 15 s; then 23 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s − 50 °C for 15 s − 72 °C for 15 s; and finally, 95 °C for 2 min − 40 °C for 90 s, followed by a melting program (40–90 °C) with fluorescence detection at a rate of 1 °C per second, and a cooling step at 37 °C for 1 s.

Metagenomic sequencing

To ensure the comparability of the test results, the samples used for MPCR and mNGS were from the same specimen. Viral nucleic acid (DNA and RNA) was extracted using the QIAamp MinElute Virus Spin Kit, following the instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed using the Superscript™ III from Invitrogen (Thermo Scientific, USA). 1.8× Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter) were added to the reaction product and mixed by pipetting. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min. After centrifugation, the centrifuge tube was placed on a magnetic stand and allowed to stand for 2–5 min until the liquid became clear. The supernatant was discarded. The centrifuge tube was kept on the magnetic stand, and 200 µL of freshly prepared 80% ethanol was added. The mixture was allowed to stand for 30 s, and then the supernatant was discarded. Add 20 µL of nucleic acid-free water to elute the DNA and incubate at room temperature for 5 min. After centrifugation, place the centrifuge tube on a magnetic stand and allow it to sit for 2–5 min until the liquid becomes clear. Carefully remove and retain 18 µL of the supernatant as the DNA. Using Qubit to determine the concentration of DNA and dilute the DNA to 0.2 ng/µL. Take 5 µL of DNA, add 10 µL of TD buffer, and mix well. Add 5 µL of ATM, mix well, and centrifuge. Place it in the PCR machine and react at 55℃ for 5 min. When the temperature drops to 10℃, add 5 µL of NT buffer, mix well, centrifuge, and incubate at room temperature for 5 min. Add 5 µL of label 1 adapter (i5) and 5 µL of label 2 adapter (i7) respectively. Add 15 µL of NPM to the centrifuge tube, mix well, and centrifuge briefly. Place it in the PCR machine with the following reaction conditions: 72℃/3 min; 95℃/10 s − 55℃/30 s − 72℃/30 s, a total of 12 cycles; 72℃/5 min; store at 10℃. Purify the PCR product using 0.8× AMPure XP magnetic beads, mix well, and incubate at room temperature for 8 min. Place the centrifuge tube on a magnetic stand, let it stand for 3 min, carefully aspirate and remove the supernatant once it clarifies. Add 200 µL of freshly prepared 80% ethanol, let it stand for 30 s, discard the supernatant. Air-dry at room temperature for about 3 min. When the magnetic beads on the tube walls appear sandy, add 15 µL of RSB buffer, vortex to mix. Let it stand at room temperature for 8 min, then place it on the magnetic stand for 3 min. Once the solution clarifies, carefully aspirate 12 µL of the upper clear liquid into an EP tube. Sequencing was performed in Illumina Novaseq platform.

mNGS data analysis

Fastp v0.23.2 [16] was used to filter out low-quality reads from raw sequencing data. BWA v0.7.17 [17] is applied to map the remaining reads to the human reference genome (hg38) and filter out mapped reads. Kraken2 [18] and Braken [19] are used for pathogen identification on the remaining reads. The identification criteria for suspected pathogenic microorganisms are as follows: Read per million (RPM) no less than one, with a minimum support of five species-level unique reads. All interpreting physicians were double-blinded.

Statistical analysis

If the same pathogen is detected multiple times in cases with multiple samplings, it is only counted once. All statistical analyses were performed using R v4.0 software. Enrichment of pathogens were done using Fisher’s Exact Test. P value less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. Significance levels are: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001.

Results

Pathogen profiles in CSF of AMEPs detected by multiplex PCR assays

A total of 229 cases suspected to have AME not resulting by Japanese encephalitis were included. Among them, there were 136 males (59.39%), 82 females (35.81%), and 11 cases (4.80%) with missing data. 153 patients (66.81%) were children aged less than 18 years, 62 patients (27.08%) were adults aged 18 or above, and 14 cases (6.11%) with missing data. There was a higher proportion of patients from hospitals in eastern China (42.79%). The proportion of patients collected during the summer and autumn seasons was higher (Table 1). A total of 239 CSF samples collected from 229 patients were tested for 43 predefined pathogens using MPCR. Additionally, 23 cerebrospinal fluid samples, randomly selected from 22 patients, were subjected to mNGS analysis (Fig. 1A).

MPCR detected at least one potential pathogen in 73 out of 229 tested patients, resulting in an overall positivity rate of 31.88%. Among the patients, only one pathogen was detected in 46 cases (20.09%). There were 14 patients (6.11%) with viral and bacterial co-infections. Six patients (2.62%) were simultaneously infected with two or more viruses, while three patients (1.31%) had co-infections of viruses and other pathogens. Additionally, two patients (0.87%) were coinfected with bacteria and other pathogens. Moreover, two patients (0.87%) had simultaneous infections with two or more bacteria (Fig. 1B). MPCR detection revealed that viruses were the most prevalent potential pathogens, with the four mostly detected pathogens being Human herpesvirus (including Herpes simplex virus type 1&2, Varicella zoster virus, Cytomegalovirus, Epstein Barr virus, Human herpesvirus 6&7), Adenovirus, Enterovirus/Rhinovirus, and Bocavirus. Among bacterial infections, B. pertussis, H. influenzae, and L. monocytogenes were the most frequently observed (Fig. 1C).

Notably, a multitude of uncommon pathogens associated with AME have been identified. The implementation of the 22-respiratory pathogen MPCR screening strategy in this study help to discover that various respiratory pathogens, including Coronaviruses, may play a significant role in the development of AME. Additionally, bacterial-induced AM, including those triggered by B. pertussis, H. influenzae, and L. monocytogenes, also contribute significantly to the overall cases. These findings indicate that when diagnosing and treating AMEPs, healthcare providers should consider other potential pathogens in addition to the more common ones such as Japanese encephalitis virus, Adenovirus, Enterovirus/Rhinovirus, and Herpes simplex virus.

mNGS significantly expanded the spectrum of pathogen detection compared to MPCR

The pathogens in CSF of 22 randomly selected AMEPs were verified using mNGS. The MPCR identified 19 pathogens in 50% cases (11/22) (Fig. 2A). Whereas mNGS successfully detected 34 pathogens in 86.36% cases (19/22), encompassing all the pathogenic microorganisms identified by MPCR (Fig. 2B). Besides, mNGS reported 15 additional pathogens, including Human herpesvirus (1), Coronavirus (1), Tick borne encephalitis (4), Monodnaviria (2; Human papillomavirus and Human parvovirus), Ev/Rv (1), Anelloviridae (2), Encephalomyocarditis virus (2), and Human immunodeficiency virus (2) (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, mNGS identified 9 viral pathogens in 8 AMEPs, whereas none of these pathogens were detected by MPCR. Additionally, mNGS revealed additional pathogens in 6 out of 11 AMEPs that tested positive via MPCR (Fig. 2D). Notably, the additional pathogen findings from mNGS indicate the need for further improvement in the pathogen coverage of MPCR. For instance, MPCR failed to detect 4 cases of tick-borne encephalitis virus infection. Therefore, integrating mNGS results in the future can enhance the range of pathogens covered by MPCR. In conclusion, the combined application of mNGS and MPCR significantly enhances the diagnostic effectiveness of identifying pathogens in cases of AME, resulting in an overall pathogen detection rate of 86.36% (19/22). Therefore, for samples with negative MPCR test results, mNGS can be conducted to determine the causative pathogen.

The prevalent pathogens and their distribution patterns of AME in China

A comprehensive analysis of all pathogenic tests in the AMEPs showed that the most common viruses were Human herpesvirus, Adenovirus, and Enterovirus/Rhinovirus (Fig. 3A). Enteroviruses/Rhinovirus is susceptible to children (P = 0.02, Fisher’s Exact test; Table 2). Tick-borne encephalitis is predominantly prevalent in northern China (p = 0.01; Table 2), with all reported cases originating from Inner Mongolia. Coronavirus is likely susceptible to the northern China (P = 0.06; Table 2). Adenovirus is more prevalent in eastern than in the northern China (P = 0.05; Table S2). There was no significant difference in seasonal distribution of viral infection (Table S3). The most common pathogens of bacterial infection are B. pertussis, H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae (Fig. 3B). B. pertussis is mainly distributed in the southern and eastern China (P = 0.02; Table 3 and S2) and H. influenzae could be mainly distributed in the northern China (P = 0.06). There is no significant difference in the seasonal distribution of B. pertussis (Table S4). Interestingly, six AMEPs infected with M. pneumoniae were all from eastern China, implying that the eastern China may face potential epidemic trends (P = 0.05; Table 3).

Various pathogens co-infected widely in the AMEPs

There is currently a limited amount of research on cases of AME with multiple pathogen co-infection. In this study, we identified common viruses known to cause AM, as well as bacteria commonly associated with bacterial meningitis, from a large pool of cases. The most frequently coinfection was observed among Adenovirus, B. pertussis, Bocavirus, H. influenzae and L. monocytogenes (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we discovered that bacteria, which were less frequently reported in previous cases of pertussis, were also prevalent among the AMEPs included in this study. Furthermore, a majority of patients with pertussis infection were also co-infected with various pathogens, including Adenovirus, Bocavirus, and Ev/Rv. Further research is warranted to determine whether pertussis can independently trigger AME. Moreover, four patients were concurrently infected with two common bacterial meningitis pathogens: L. monocytogenes and H. influenzae. Although co-infection of multiple pathogens was observed, significant associations were found only between Ev/Rvs and HSV-1 infections (Fig. 3D). There was no preference for co-infection among the majority of pathogens. It is worth noting that while this study noticed a common occurrence of co-infections in AMEPs, it did not investigate whether concurrent infections of multiple pathogens were related to the progression and prognosis of AME.

Discussion

Exploring the pathogens associated with AME to devise specialized detection strategies

This study employed multiple novel detection techniques to systematically analyze the potential pathogens associated with AMEPs in China. Our findings revealed a high prevalence of viruses in AMEPs. Notably, this study, for the first time, incorporated 22 common pathogens that can cause respiratory infections into the MPCR detection, thereby providing evidence for the widespread presence of respiratory pathogens in the cerebrospinal fluid of AMEPs. Based on the pathogen spectrum established in this study, we advocate for an expanded range of AME pathogen detection, with a specific focus on including respiratory disease-related pathogens that may contribute to AME. This study preliminarily observed that common respiratory pathogens detected by RespiFinder, including Coronaviruses, Adenoviruses, Enteroviruses, and Boca viruses, may also potentially trigger encephalitis or meningitis. Although RespiFinder and MeningoFinder are both based on pathogen nucleic acid multiplex PCR testing and have been used in previous studies [20–22], further investigation is still needed to determine whether RespiFinder may lead to potential detection bias in CSF samples. Besides, several multiplex pathogen detection kits have been applied for diagnosing central nervous system infection pathogens, such as the FilmArray® Meningitis/Encephalitis panel [23]. Preliminary comparative studies indicate that these kits still require substantial improvements in performance [24]. Future efforts should focus on optimizing target selection based on regional pathogen profiles and enhancing detection sensitivity and specificity through methods like improved probe design.

Despite employing the MPCR detection strategy, which encompasses 43 common pathogens associated with central nervous system and respiratory infections, the diagnostic rate of AME only reached 31.88%. The utilization of mNGS technology has significantly enhanced the identification rate of pathogens, including the novel Coronavirus, while also considerably reducing diagnostic time. To further enhance the pathogen diagnostic rate of AME and assess the effectiveness of mNGS in the clinical diagnosis of AM, this study performed supplementary mNGS testing on 22 randomly selected samples. In comparison to the MPCR results, we observed that mNGS exhibited nearly 100% diagnostic sensitivity and substantially improved the diagnostic rate of negative samples. Moreover, mNGS enabled the identification of diverse pathogens, including tick-borne encephalitis viruses. Strikingly, several pathogens only detected by mNGS were within the MPCR target range (Table S1). The limited presence of pathogenic microorganisms in CSF could lead to diminished genomic integrity, potentially impacting the amplification of the target region in multiplex polymerase chain reaction. The unbiased advantage of mNGS detection, which is not limited by specific regions, once again demonstrates the high sensitivity.

These findings highlight the importance of promoting the application of mNGS technology in diagnosing AME. Therefore, the integration of pathogen targeting from MPCR and the high throughput and unbiased advantages of mNGS make tNGS a crucial area for the advancement of pathogen monitoring technology in the future. Unfortunately, due to the limitations of cerebrospinal fluid sample collection and experimental costs, this study only conducted mNGS testing on a limited number of samples. We used a completely random selection strategy in choosing samples for metagenomic sequencing to avoid bias in the results. However, it should be noted that the results of this study might underestimate the pathogen diversity in AMEPs in China.

Characteristics of pathogens spectrum in AMEPs in China

It is worth noting that a large number of bacteria in the CSF of AMEPs were identified. S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and L. monocytogenes are prevalent pathogens in the surveillance of bacterial meningitis in CABM Children in China [7] and adults in France [25]. A study conducted in the Netherlands adults also identified S. pneumoniae and L. monocytogenes as primary pathogens [5]. Notably, AME caused by pathogens like pertussis was not observed in the aforementioned case series. Furthermore, Wang et al. reported a higher incidence of Escherichia coli in infants under one year old, accounting for 91.6% of the CABM population. However, our study observed a relatively low detection rate of E. coli, which may be attributed to the limited inclusion of infants in the sample. Wang et al. reported that S. pneumoniae is one of the major pathogens causing AME in Children in China. In this study, we do identify three cases of AME resulted by S. pneumoniae. All of them were children consisting with the previous studies. Previous studies have consistently shown the association of Klebsiella pneumoniae with bacterial meningitis [7, 26]. Nonetheless, K. pneumoniae was not detected in our study, potentially attributed to factors such as prior treatment with antibiotics. It is crucial to closely monitor the rise in the positivity rate associated with Listeria and Pertussis-induced AME. However, further research is needed to determine whether these detected bacteria are the main pathogens causing AME. The incidence of bacterial meningitis is higher in infants, which may be attributed to their incomplete development of humoral and cellular immunity, phagocytosis, and an immature blood-brain barrier.

Pertussis mainly observed the neonatal population, potentially resulting in AME [27, 28], which can lead to seizures in severe cases. The brain imaging of pertussis-positive AMEPs indicates enhanced cytotoxic edema, for which interventions such as azithromycin are employed [27]. Recently, an increasing number of pertussis-related AMEPs have been reported, underscoring the imperative need for sustained improvement in vaccine coverage. This study has identified several other prevalent respiratory pathogens that could potentially act as inducers of AME. It may be because the cases included in this study are all severe meningitis cases, most of which are cases where general broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment has failed to some extent enriching the less common bacterial meningitis. Recently, Guo et al. reported that pertussis has made a comeback in China over the past decade, showing a high epidemic trend [29]. Pertussis continues to pose significant public health challenges in China, with a record 15,275 cases reported in January 2024. The trend of Chinese pertussis strains toward higher drug resistance and virulence underscores the urgency of strengthening international monitoring and public health measures [30]. In addition, China’s current immunization strategy does not recommend pertussis vaccination for individuals over two years old, which may be a significant reason for the occurrence of pertussis encephalitis cases in adults (3/14). Based on the results of this study, we believe that enhanced pertussis detection should be emphasized in patients with AME. A recent analysis of a Chinese cohort of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) patients indicated that age under 30 years may be a risk factor for TB meningitis [31]. However, Wang et al. reported in a multicenter cohort study in China that only 3 out of 6,892 cases tested positive for TB [4]. Therefore, further research is needed to determine whether TB should be included in multiplex PCR testing for the diagnosis of encephalitis. Meanwhile, it is anticipated that high-throughput detection technologies such as mNGS or target-NGS will cover a broader range of pathogens, including TB, in the future.

Recently, AMEPs caused by Coronaviruses (HCoV-OC43/NL63/HKU-1/SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2) have been reported [32–39]. Particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, a significant number of confirmed AMEPs, supported by comprehensive laboratory molecular detection and imaging evidence, have been widely documented [34, 36]. The virus can invade the nervous system through various pathways, including synaptic transmission between neurons, entry through the olfactory nerve, and migration across the blood-brain barrier via infection of endothelial cells or leukocytes. Current research efforts aim to further investigate the specific molecular mechanisms, diagnosis, and treatment strategies for AME resulting from Coronavirus infection. A small number of case report studies have reported meningitis caused by the co-infection of SARS-CoV-2 and N. meningitidis, highlighting the significance of early administration of antibiotics [32]. Moreover, a recent multicenter study conducted in Iran has confirmed that patients with bacterial meningitis can be simultaneously infected with two serotypes of S. pneumoniae [40]. Viral infections, due to the burden they place on the host’s immune system, may potentially reduce the ability to fend off other pathogen infections, resulting in the co-infection of both viruses and multiple pathogens. For instance, patients with compromised or diminished immune function, such as those with long-term HIV carriage, are more susceptible to bacterial meningitis [41, 42]. According to our results, meningitis cases in the China exhibit co-infections of common viruses and bacteria related to bacterial meningitis. Co-infections of Adenovirus, B. pertussis, Bocavirus, H. influenzae, and L. monocytogenes were the most frequently observed. Additionally, we found that some bacteria less reported in previous pertussis cases were also prevalent among AMEPs. It is worth noting that while this study observed frequent co-infections of multiple pathogens in AMEPs, it did not investigate whether such co-infections were related to the progression and prognosis of AME.

Additionally, multiple sampling from individual cases suggests that the collection time may affect the pathogen detection rate. There were nine patients with multiple CSF samples. No pathogen was detected in CSF samples of case 1 on the first day, while H. influenzae was detected on the fifth day (Table 4). H. influenzae and L. monocytogenes were detected in the CSF samples of case 2 on the first day, but only H. influenzae was detected on the seventh day. Pathogenic pathogens were detected in CSF samples of patients 3, 4 and 5 on the first day, but no pathogens were detected in subsequent samples (Table 4). Notably, the cases included in this study were not comprehensively representative, especially in terms of their regional and temporal distribution. Additionally, not all detected pathogens underwent timely isolation and cultivation. The lack of whole-genome sequencing hindered an in-depth investigation into factors such as serotypes, drug resistance, variations, and circulation trends.

Due to factors such as host immune suppression after infection, some detected microbes may not be the main pathogens that cause AME. Future investigations should incorporate additional clinical cases to further substantiate these findings. The rising incidence of AME caused by atypical pathogens necessitates the prioritization of clinical diagnostic and treatment strategies. Unfortunately, we were not allowed to access all the clinical data, biochemical indicators, ICU admission rates, and prognostic information of the participating cases, so this study could not further analyze the association between the infecting pathogens and the clinical symptoms of the cases. Conclusion

AME remains a significant global health challenge, and timely and accurate diagnosis is crucial for reducing the neurological damage. We employed multiplex PCR and metagenomic sequencing techniques to detect pathogens in the cerebrospinal fluid of over 229 patients with AME across multiple centers. Our results revealed significant potential for expediting the diagnosis of AME pathogens using both detection strategies. Analysis of the identified pathogen spectrum confirmed notable differences in the spatial distribution and susceptible populations affected by AME pathogens in China. The findings of this study will provide important insights for the prevention of AME, particularly in terms of developing new vaccines, establishing early treatment protocols, and enhancing precision diagnostics.
