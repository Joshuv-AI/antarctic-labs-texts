Introduction

Primary intraosseous hemangioma is a rare, benign entity commonly presenting after the fourth to fifth decade of life as a painless bony outgrowth resulting in facial asymmetry.1 In the skull and face, the most common sites of involvement include the parietal bone, mandible, and the zygomatic bone.2 Due to its extreme rarity, we herein describe the radiographic findings, clinical course, and histopathology of a case of primary intraosseous cavernous hemangioma involving the zygoma.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old previously healthy female presented with a 1-year history of a growing painful left periorbital mass, without any history of trauma. Examination revealed an elevated and palpable lesion of the left zygoma. Visual acuity, ocular motility, anterior segment, and funduscopic examination were normal. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a circumscribed, expansile left lateral orbital rim and wall lesion with internal trabeculation involving the anteromedial zygoma and frontal process of the zygoma with minimal encroachment or mass effect into the orbit (Fig. 1A). She underwent en bloc resection of the lesion with adequate normal bone margin through a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthal and upper gingival sulcus incisions. The lesion did not extend past any of the natural, developmental suture lines of the zygoma bone. The bone and subsequent defect were primarily reconstructed using a porous polyethylene implant (Medpor, Stryker corp.), anchoring the implant with two separate titanium plates. (Fig. 1B). Macroscopic examination showed a bulging purple red mass surrounded by bone measuring 2.1 × 1.8 × 1.6 cm (Fig. 2A). Histopathology examination demonstrated a lesion with thin-walled vascular channels lined by a monolayer of endothelial cells interspersed among bony trabeculae (Fig. 2B) most consistent with intraosseous cavernous hemangioma. Immunohistostaining demonstrated endothelium positive for CD31 (Fig. 2C, arrow). At 3 months follow up, the patient had excellent functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Discussion

Intraosseous hemangiomas of the zygoma are extremely rare benign entities, accounting for 0.2% of all bone tumors.3 The first case was reported in 1950 by Schoenfield with approximately 50 cases reported in the literature to date.2, 3, 4, 5 They are typically encountered in male adults in the fourth and fifth decades of life with left sided predominance.3,6,7

Though the pathogenesis of these lesions remains unclear, it has been hypothesized that intraosseous hemangiomas are secondary to congenital KRIT1/CCM1 gene frameshift mutations or follow trauma from differentiation and proliferation of undifferentiated mesenchymal tissue.1,4 Our patient had no history of trauma to the face, and no pertinent family history.

Patients generally present with painless progressive facial asymmetry with an enlarging bony mass.3 When symptomatic, the most common complaint is pain, followed by ocular findings secondary to mass effect including diplopia, proptosis, ptosis, or entrapment leading to extraocular motility deficits.2

CT scan remains the diagnostic imaging modality of choice for diagnosing intraosseous hemangioma due to its superior characterization of cortical and trabecular detail.2 The classic characteristic appearance of hemangioma on CT is a “honeycomb” or “sunburst” pattern with fine, radiating and reticulated lines or trabeculae.3 Nonetheless, radiographic appearance of intraosseous hemangioma is variable, and it can mimic the appearance of other bony tumors such as osteosarcoma, fibrous dysplasia, central giant cell granuloma, ameloblastoma, odontogenic myxoma, multiple myeloma, dentigerous cyst, and aneurysmal bone cyst.6 Our patient presented with imaging findings, including internal trabeculation, consistent with a hemangioma.

Histologically, hemangiomas can be of the capillary, cavernous, and mixed type.1 Almost all intraosseous hemangiomas of the facial skeleton are of the cavernous type being composed of large thin-walled vessels and sinusoids lined with a single layer of endothelium.8,9 This reported hemangioma is of the cavernous type, but is unique in that, to our knowledge, it is the first reported case of zygomatic hemangiomas demonstrating a strong positivity for CD31; a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed by endothelial cells and a variety of hematopoietic cells that is thought to be a sensitive and specific marker for vascular differentiation.10 While most published zygomatic intraosseous hemangioma cases11,12 confirm the diagnosis histologically based on vascular spaces lined by endothelial cells, few reports explicitly describe endothelial marker immunohistochemistry. In our case, strong CD31 positivity provided supportive evidence of endothelial differentiation, serving as a useful adjunct to routine histopathologic evaluation.

Management of primary intraosseous hemangiomas of the zygoma depends on both ophthalmic symptoms and cosmetic considerations, and varies according to the location and extent of the lesion.2 Asymptomatic patients can receive regular medical observation, but complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice as it is generally felt to be curative.1,6 A combined transconjunctival, lateral canthal, and upper gingival sulcus approach was selected to achieve adequate exposure of the lateral orbital rim and zygomatic body while permitting en bloc resection with clear margins and minimal external scarring. Since the entire body of the zygoma appeared involved, this approach that was thought to have the highest likelihood of complete excision. The lateral canthal incision allowed for release of both upper and lower lateral canthal tendons and exposure of the entire lateral rim (to above the front-zygomatic suture line), the anterior half of the zygomatic arch, as well as the inner and outer aspects of the lateral walls since bone cuts would be needed in each of these places. The inferior trans-conjunctival incision allowed exposure of the entire inferior rim where another cut would be required medially and exposure of the face of the maxilla and anterior zygoma face-tumor face could be enhanced. Finally, the gingival sulcus incision allowed a wide exposure of the maxillary face and tumor when combined with the trans-conjunctival exposure. This type of wide exposure from multiple angles allowed a clear view and good access for the planned bone cuts and removal of the lesion as one solid piece. The same exposure facilitated the shaping, placement, and fixation of the reconstructive implant. Combining these approaches allowed for complete excision with only a 2 cm visible skin incision at the lateral canthus which has blended nicely. Compared with isolated transconjunctival or transcutaneous approaches, which may limit access to the inferior zygoma, and coronal approaches, which carry greater morbidity, this combined approach provided sufficient exposure for complete excision and reconstruction in this case. Biopsy of the lesion to exclude malignancy is not necessarily advised as it creates risks associated with bleeding without definitively managing the process. Risk associated with bleeding can be mitigated with preoperative embolization.3,8 In unresectable or residual tumors, radiotherapy has been reported as an alternative approach.1 Our patient underwent an uncomplicated en bloc resection of the lesion with adequate normal bone margin with no recurrence of the tumor at 3 months follow-up.

The reconstruction method is potentially more controversial, but lesions greater than 1cm generally require reconstruction.2 Depending on the size of the defect and surgeon preferences, autologous or heterologous materials can be used. Techniques may include hydroxyapatite associated with titanium mesh for reconstruction, Medpor implants, and bone grafting.8 In this patient, a fixated Medpor implant yielded good intraoperative appearance and postoperative aesthetic outcome.

Published reports of intraosseous cavernous hemangioma of the zygoma emphasize the rarity of this entity, its typical presentation as a painless, expansile swelling with characteristic trabecular patterns on CT or MRI, and the importance of surgical excision as definitive treatment. Powers et al. reviewed 49 cases in the English literature, documenting clinical features and management outcomes.11 Moore et al. described the CT/MR characteristics and the critical need to avoid routine bone biopsy due to hemorrhage risk.13 Recent reports, including Habimana et al. (2024)14 and Hekmat & Aghmasheh (2025),15 further illustrate imaging and histopathologic confirmation of these lesions. Few case reports specifically detail immunohistochemical staining such as CD31 positivity, making the present case's explicit documentation of this marker a useful diagnostic adjunct.

In conclusion, primary intraosseous cavernous hemangioma of the zygoma is a rare benign orbital bone tumor commonly presenting as a painless bony outgrowth resulting in facial asymmetry with generally good prognosis. Treatment of choice is complete excision with reconstruction to prevent recurrence and improve aesthetic results.
