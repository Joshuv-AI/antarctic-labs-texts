SRaFTE: Super - Resolution and Future Time Extrapolation for Time-Dependent PDEs Conference: SC25; 2025; St. Louis, MO
Hardeep Bassi, Yuanran Zhu, Erika Ye, Pu Ren, Alec Dektor, Harish S. Bhat, Chao Yang

Abstract
. We present SRaFTE (Super-Resolution and Future Time Extrapolation), a two-phase learning framework that couples coarse grid solvers with neural operators to super-resolve and forecast fine grid dynamics for time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs). In our construction, Phase 1 learns a super-resolution map from coarse to fine solutions while Phase 2 embeds this operator in a predictor–corrector loop with the coarse solver, forming an operator composition that serves as a surrogate fine grid propagator for future-time extrapolation. We benchmark SRaFTE on three canonical two-dimensional PDEs of increasing dynamical complexity: the heat equation, the wave equation, and the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. Compared to well-known benchmarks, SRaFTE provides reliable super-resolution in Phase 1 and delivers consistently accurate long-term forecasts in Phase 2 across all three examples for new test data. Our results suggest that coupling a learned super-resolution operator with a coarse grid solver provides an effective and efficient means of modeling high-resolution spatiotemporal dynamics, particularly when the dynamics of the PDEs at the coarse and fine resolutions exhibit pronounced scale separation.



1.
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) form the mathematical backbone for a variety of problems in the physical sciences and engineering. Reliable numerical solutions, therefore, underpin progress in areas as diverse as climate forecasting, photonics, and materials design. A persistent obstacle in being able to generate solutions to these PDEs, however, is the scale at which their dynamics evolve
(
Lei et al. 2023
;
Xie 2024
)
. This is closely tied to what is known as
resolution
; important physical effects for a system of interest often emerge only after one refines the resolution of the computational grid to be fine enough. Performing such a refinement, however, proves to be costly even with modern day supercomputing power.

A natural response to this computational bottleneck has been the emergence of machine learning (ML) based accelerations for PDE solvers
(
Brunton and Kutz 2024
)
, which can be broadly grouped into two complementary strands. The first strand projects the full high-dimensional dynamics onto a reduced set of variables, yielding a lower-order evolution equation that often includes an unresolved closure term representing the missing subgrid-scale physics. A neural network is then trained to approximate this term, thereby retaining the structure of the governing equations while supplying the missing physics that simplified models lack
(
Girimaji 2024
;
Duraisamy 2021
;
Brunton and Kutz 2022
;
Bhat et al. 2022
)
. The second strand adopts a fully equation-free, data-driven approach, opting to learn the complete propagator of the PDE and any refinements directly from training snapshots; our work falls within this category
(
Long et al. 2018
;
Cao and Zhang 2023
;
Lu et al. 2019
)
. While this approach affords equation-agnostic training and inference, the absence of explicit physical constraints and equations of motion can limit its fidelity and robustness outside the training regime for the systems studied.

Here we introduce a two-phase operator learning framework—
Super-Resolution and Future-Time Extrapolation
(SRaFTE)—that couples super-resolution (SR) and long-range forecasting. Our two-phase procedure separately incorporates both super-resolution and time propagation. In particular, Phase 1 trains a neural operator to map each coarse snapshot to its fine grid counterpart on a short time interval. Phase 2 then fine-tunes the same network inside a predictor–corrector loop: each step advances a projected version of the fine grid dynamics on the coarse grid with a known numerical solver and then applies the neural operator to lift it back to the fine resolution space. Since the coarse integrator already respects the governing equations and the network is applied only once as a corrective lift, we expect that the cumulative errors for time extrapolation should grow more slowly than schemes that continually apply a learned model or merely upsample the coarse solution
(
Ing 2007
;
Pasini et al. 2024
;
Chattopadhyay and Hassanzadeh 2023
)
.
SRaFTE also has similarities to the equation-free multiscale computation (EFMC) paradigm of Kevrekidis
(
Kevrekidis and Samaey 2009
;
Samaey et al. 2006
)
, where forward propagation alternates between lifting, short fine-scale propagation, and projection to accelerate long-time integration. Accordingly, SRaFTE can be interpreted as the neural network analogue of an EFMC-like scheme: the learned operator plays the role of the lift, while the coarse solver provides the deterministic macroscale advance.

Scope and expectations.

This paper is a proof of concept for coupling a coarse solver with a learned super-resolution lift. We do
not
claim to replace high-fidelity solvers or to obtain significant computational savings for the equations of motion considered here. Our aim is to quantify when and where the SRaFTE framework improves over current data-driven state-of-the-art baseline methods for super-resolution and time extrapolation.
Thus, to demonstrate this clearly, we consider three canonical PDEs: the 2D versions of the Navier-Stokes, heat, and wave equations. Our experiments show that using SRaFTE with standard SR models can recover accurate fine-resolution dynamics within Phase 1 and retain acceptable agreement beyond the training horizon in Phase 2.

2.
SRaFTE: Phase 1 and Phase 2

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded periodic domain and let
X
be a Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
.
Define the corresponding coarse domain as
\Omega_{c}
, and the spatial projection operator
P
as

P:X(\Omega)\longrightarrow X(\Omega_{c}),\qquad u_{c}=P[u_{f}].

The fine field
u_{f}
and its coarse projection
u_{c}
evolve according to

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{f}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{f}\,u_{f},

\displaystyle u_{f}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{f,0},

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{c}\,u_{c},

\displaystyle u_{c}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{c,0},

where
\mathcal{L}_{f}
and
\mathcal{L}_{c}
denote the fine and coarse generators, respectively.
For a timestep
\Delta t
, the corresponding one-step evolution operators are given by
\mathcal{P}_{f}(\Delta t)
and
\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Delta t)
for the fine and coarse grid, respectively.
Thus, for time steps
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,

u_{f}^{\,n+1}=\mathcal{P}_{f}(\Delta t)\,u_{f}^{\,n}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u_{c}^{\,n+1}=\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Delta t)\,u_{c}^{\,n}.

In practice, we take
X=L^{1}(\Omega)
, the space of absolutely integrable functions on
\Omega
, for training, and observe indistinguishable results when repeating the experiments with
X=L^{2}(\Omega)
, the space of square-integrable functions on
\Omega
.

SRaFTE can be broken down into two separate learning phases. Phase 1 can be understood as the standard super-resolution task
(
Ren et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2021
;
Fukami and Taira 2024
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
: given
K
pairs of coarse and fine grid snapshots at matching times,
\{(u_{c}^{(i)},u_{f}^{(i)})\}_{i=0}^{K}
, we learn the neural operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}:u_{c}\to u_{f}
by minimizing the loss function,

(3)

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{P1}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=0}^{K}\|u_{f}^{(i)}-\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\bigl[u_{c}^{(i)}\bigr]\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

The specific choice of neural operator is arbitrary at the moment and our particular choices will be detailed later.

For future time extrapolation, such a neural operator is insufficient. In particular, we note that

\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Delta t)\bigr)[u_{c}(t)]\neq\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{f}(\Delta t)\circ\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\bigr)[u_{c}(t)]].

Consequently, chaining
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
with
\mathcal{P}_{c}
naively would inject scale-separated discrepancies at every step, which is then recycled by the next update. These errors would then accrue for each step, which causes the predicted fine solution to drift away from the ground truth very quickly. Curbing this compounding error is therefore the central objective of Phase 2, where we fine-tune the model from Phase 1 to be a single-step propagator (SSP). Concretely, we employ the weights obtained from (
3
) as an initialization and fine-tune the model to be applied in the operator composition

(4)

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Delta t)\circ P

as a
surrogate
fine grid propagator.

For Phase 2 training, we now use paired snapshots of
exclusively
fine grid dynamics
\{(u_{f}^{(i)},u_{f}^{(i+1)})\}_{i=0}^{K+1}
as the dataset. Crucially, this
is not
a new dataset: the Phase 2 pairs are generated by simply time-shifting the fine targets from Phase 1, pairing each snapshot with its immediate successor. By (
4
), we see that the input fine snapshot is projected into the coarse grid, advanced in time, and then lifted back to the fine grid via the neural operator. Consequently, the loss function we seek to minimize is

(5)

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{P2}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=0}^{K}\bigl\lVert\mathcal{P}_{f}(\Delta t)\,u_{f}^{(i)}-\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)\,[u_{f}^{(i)}]\bigr\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

For every
n\geq K
, we advance

\hat{u}_{f}^{\,n+1}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)\,\hat{u}_{f}^{\,n},

so that after
m
additional steps, (i.e. at horizon
T=m\Delta t
), the fine-grid forecast is

\hat{u}_{f}(K\Delta t+T)=\bigl[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)\bigr]^{m}u_{f}^{K}.

In principle, the surrogate propagator delivers forecasts at arbitrarily long horizons while adding minimal computational overhead: the projection
P
is fixed and a forward pass through the trained
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
is inexpensive, so each step costs little more than a coarse grid update. In practice, however, errors still accumulate at each step as a result of numerical and model errors. However, since the composition of operators incorporates the true coarse propagator, which preserves macro-scale characteristics of the underlying PDE, we expect that it will be more stable in future time extrapolation when compared to autoregressive models that do not incorporate true propagators
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Chattopadhyay and Hassanzadeh 2023
;
Darman et al. 2025
)
.

As practical remarks and observations, we note that:

(1)

The same network architecture is used in both stages; only the loss function changes.

(2)

No backward passes through the coarse solver are ever used: once parameterized, the coarse solver is completely fixed, and gradients from backpropagation only flow through the neural operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
, which is responsible for super-resolution only.

(3)

Skipping Phase 1 pretraining markedly degrades Phase 2 performance.

2.1.
On approximate space-time separability and short memory

SRaFTE couples a
temporal
update on the coarse grid with an
instantaneous
learned spatial lift to the fine grid. This architecture is most effective when a short-memory (or “instantaneous closure”) condition holds: conditioned on the present coarse field
u_{c}(t)
, the subgrid content is well approximated by a
function
of
u_{c}(t)
alone,

u_{f}(t)\approx\Phi\big(u_{c}(t)\big),

so the one-step surrogate propagator

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{P}_{c}(\Delta t)\circ P

is a reasonable ansatz for the fine solver. In that sense, SRaFTE exploits an
approximate separation
of temporal and spatial correlation: the coarse solver
\mathcal{P}_{c}
carries the time advance while
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
provides an instantaneous spatial correction.

Linear intuition (heat / wave).

For linear, constant-coefficient PDEs with periodic BCs (e.g.,
\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u+\alpha f
or
\partial_{tt}u=c^{2}\Delta u
), a Fourier projection onto low (
|k|\leq k_{c}
) and high (
|k|>k_{c}
) modes commutes with the generator. Each wavenumber evolves independently in time:

\widehat{u}_{k}(t)=e^{\lambda_{k}t}\widehat{u}_{k}(0)+\text{(forced response)},\qquad\lambda_{k}=\begin{cases}-\nu|k|^{2}&\text{(heat)},\\
\pm i\,c|k|&\text{(wave)}.\end{cases}

Thus, the
temporal
evolution is multiplicative and modewise, while the
spatial
content is set by which modes are present. For the heat equation, high modes decay on a time scale
\tau_{k}\sim(\nu|k|^{2})^{-1}
; when
k_{c}
is chosen so that
\tau_{k_{c}}
is significantly smaller than the resolved time scale, the unresolved content is quickly chained to the resolved part, justifying an instantaneous lift. For the wave equation, although the dynamics are nondissipative, the
(k,\omega)
spectrum concentrates along the dispersion ridge
\omega=c|k|
, so phase is primarily governed by
\mathcal{P}_{c}
and the lift mainly corrects spatial aliasing.

Nonlinear intuition (2D NSE)

Writing
\omega=\omega_{<}+\omega_{>}
for low/high modes, we take
\mathbf{P}_{<}
and
\mathbf{P}_{>}
to be orthogonal Fourier projectors at a cutoff
k_{c}
:
for any solution on a grid
f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\hat{f}_{k}e^{ikx}
,

\mathbf{P}_{<}f:=\sum_{|k|\leq k_{c}}\hat{f}_{k}e^{ik\cdot x},\qquad\mathbf{P}_{>}f:=f-\mathbf{P}_{<}f,

so that
\mathbf{P}_{<}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{<}
,
\mathbf{P}_{>}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{>}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{<}\mathbf{P}_{>}=0
. Both of these operators reduce a fine grid solution to its coarse counterpart, but the earlier spatial decimation projector
P
is a practical grid-level downsampler (not necessarily orthogonal or spectral) used to form
u_{c}=Pu_{f}
, whereas the present projectors
\mathbf{P}_{<}
and
\mathbf{P}_{>}
are the ideal orthogonal Fourier projector at a cutoff
k_{c}
. This can coincide with
P
only when
P
implements spectral truncation in conjunction with spatial decimation.
Note that
\mathbf{P}_{<},\mathbf{P}_{>}
are used only for the discussion in this section. Using these operators yields

\partial_{t}\omega_{>}=\nu\Delta\omega_{>}+\mathbf{P}_{>}\,B(\omega_{<},\omega_{<})+\mathbf{P}_{>}\,B(\omega_{<},\omega_{>})+\mathbf{P}_{>}\,B(\omega_{>},\omega_{>}),

where
B
is the advection operator in 2D vorticity form, and the corresponding Mori–Zwanzig identity expresses the resolved dynamics as

\partial_{t}\omega_{<}=\mathbf{P}_{<}\mathcal{L}\,\omega_{<}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s;\omega_{<}(s))\,ds+\eta(t),

with a memory kernel
K
and noise
\eta
. When viscosity and the cutoff
k_{c}
yield a spectral gap, high modes decay on a fast time
\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\sim(\nu k_{c}^{2})^{-1}
and the memory integral is well-approximated by a Markovian (instantaneous) functional,

\int_{0}^{t}K(\cdot)\,ds\approx\Phi(\omega_{<}(t)).

SRaFTE’s Phase 1 learns an analogous operator to
\Phi
from the dataset
(u_{c},u_{f})
; Phase 2 wraps it around the coarse integrator so that the
time
advance is done by
\mathcal{P}_{c}
while the
space
correction is refreshed each step by
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
.

When does this help (or fail)?

The short-memory condition is more likely to be satisfied for (i) diffusive systems and (ii) linear dispersive systems with constant coefficients, as well as (iii) moderately nonlinear, viscous flows where high modes relax quickly relative to resolved dynamics. The short-memory condition can degrade (and Phase 2 may drift) when (a) the Reynolds number is so high that subgrid time scales are not fast compared to resolved scales, (b) strong nonlocal interactions create long memory, or (c) the downsampling ratio is so aggressive that crucial interaction bands move entirely into the unresolved set. In such cases the lift can be augmented with a short history window
[u_{c}(t-\ell\Delta t),\dots,u_{c}(t)]
or a small subgrid state.

3.
Neural operators architectures

3.1.
Models tested in SRaFTE

SRaFTE imposes no
a priori
constraints on the architecture of the neural operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
. In principle, any neural operator framework developed for super-resolution tasks can be adapted into the two-phase SRaFTE pipeline, which is used first to learn and super-resolve the coarse-scale time dynamics, and then to form an effective one-step propagator for predicting future fine-scale dynamics. In this section, we present several neural operator architectures that can be integrated into SRaFTE. We will test these architectures in Section
5
.

3.1.1.
U-Net

The U-Net
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
)
is a classical encoder-decoder architecture widely used in computer vision for image super-resolution. In our setting, the numerical solution of a PDE at each grid point can be interpreted as a grayscale image, allowing the U-Net to be directly employed to learn the PDE super-resolution map
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
.

3.1.2.
Fourier U-Net (FUnet)

FUnet, a new architecture introduced in this work, augments the U-Net with a spectral convolutional layer. Specifically, between the encoder and decoder in the bottleneck, we add a spectral convolutional block. The FUnet lifts the features
z\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{0}}
from the encoder and computes their discrete Fourier transform:
\widehat{z}=\mathcal{F}[z]
. Then only the lowest
R
modes are retained and transformed back into the time domain. This added Fourier layer is combined with the local convolutional block to produce a latent representation, which is subsequently decoded and upsampled to the target resolution in the decoder using pixel-shuffle layers with skip connections from the encoder
(
Shi et al. 2016
)
.

3.1.3.
Enhanced Deep Super-Resolution (EDSR)

The image super-resolution baseline EDSR
(
Lim et al. 2017
)
is adopted here with minimal modifications. Like the U-Net, the RGB channels used in image processing are replaced by field channels representing the temporal trajectories of the PDE solution. The low-resolution input is first processed by 16 residual blocks, followed by a two-stage pixel-shuffle to achieve the desired resolution increase. A final convolution layer then produces the high-resolution output predictions.

3.1.4.
Fourier neural operator (FNO)

The FNO was originally proposed in
(
Li et al. 2021
)
to learn a one-step propagator for PDEs. However, as a general neural operator framework, it can also be adapted as a modeling ansatz for a super-resolution operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
. Specifically, it can be trained to take the numerical solution of a PDE on a coarse grid as input and return the corresponding fine grid solution. This super-resolution variant of FNO (referred to as FNO-SR) can then be used in Phase 2 to form an effective propagator for PDE dynamics. Note that this usage of FNO differs from applying FNO to directly approximate the one-step propagator, which will be referred to as the autoregressive FNO (FNO-AR) in the following discussion.

3.2.
Canonical benchmarks

3.2.1.
Upsampling

Upsampling refers to increasing the spatial sampling rate by estimating values on a fine grid from a coarse one, typically via interpolation kernels such as nearest neighbor, bilinear, or bicubic. For this work, we adopt bicubic interpolation as a simple baseline for assessing the performance of SRaFTE in fine-scale dynamics reconstruction and prediction. In Phase 1, it maps the coarse grid PDE solution at each time step to a fine grid solution by evaluating the standard cubic convolution kernel in all spatial directions. In Phase 2, the PDE is first solved on the coarse grid, and the resulting coarse-grained solution at each time step is then upsampled using the same interpolation. Although simplistic, this baseline is helpful for quantifying how much of SRaFTE’s advantage stems from learning versus mere smooth spatial upsampling.

3.2.2.
FNO-AR

As mentioned above, the FNO architecture can also be used to directly approximate the single-step propagator for PDEs. After training, the resulting neural operator can be iterated autoregressively to predict the future dynamics of the PDEs. Given fine grid pairs
\bigl(u_{f}(t),\,u_{f}(t+\Delta t)\bigr)
, the network
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
is optimized to satisfy
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\bigl[u_{f}(t)\bigr]\approx u_{f}(t+\Delta t)
. To forecast for arbitrarily long horizons, we iterate the learned map autoregressively,

u_{f}^{n+1}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\bigl[u_{f}^{n}\bigr]
for

n=K,K+1,\dots
,
forming another benchmark.

4.
Datasets and evaluation metrics

We now introduce the PDEs studied using SRaFTE, briefly describing the generation of the training datasets for each example and the evaluation metrics used to assess the predicted dynamics. More details are provided in Appendices
B
and
C
.

2D heat equation

Consider the 2D heat equation
\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u+\alpha f
with parameters
\nu=10^{-3}
,
\alpha=10^{-2}
, and forcing term
f(x)=\sin(2\pi x)\sin(2\pi y)
. The equation is solved using a Fourier spectral method on the square domain
[0,1]^{2}
using periodic boundary conditions (PBC) and the resulting solution is sampled on both coarse and fine spatial grids to construct the training datasets.

2D wave equation

As an example of an energy-conserving PDE, we consider the linear wave equation
\partial_{tt}u=c^{2}\Delta u
with
c=0.5
on the square domain
[0,1]^{2}
under periodic boundary conditions. The equation is solved using an explicit finite-difference scheme on both coarse and fine spatial grids to generate the training datasets.

Navier–Stokes equations (NSE)

As an example of a nonlinear PDE, we consider the 2D incompressible NSE in vorticity form
\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=\nu\Delta\omega+f
,
\nabla\cdot u=0
on
[0,1]^{2}
with
\nu=10^{-4}
, periodic BCs, and forcing term
f(x)=0.025\bigl[\sin 2\pi(x{+}y)+\cos 2\pi(x{+}y)\bigr]
. The NSE is solved using a Fourier spectral method, and the solution is sampled on both coarse and fine grids to construct the training datasets.
To close the system, we introduce a streamfunction
\psi
and set

u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi:=(\partial_{y}\psi,-\partial_{x}\psi)
with
-\Delta\psi=\omega
.

Training data

For all three examples, we generate 1000 trajectories using random initial conditions detailed in Appendix
B
to form the training datasets. The neural operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
is trained on a paired dataset of coarse-fine dynamics with an 80/20 training/validation split. Specifically, the following coarse-to-fine grid mappings are used:
32\times 32\to 128\times 128
(heat equation),
16\times 16\to 128\times 128
(wave equation) and
64\times 64\to 256\times 256
(NSE). These ratios are chosen after experimenting with a range of downsampling factors, which clearly demonstrate the benefits of super-resolution and future-time extrapolation, particularly in regimes with pronounced multiscale structure. For an example of performance shift based on resolution, see Figure
5
.

In Phase 2, the same neural operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
is used to construct an effective propagator
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{f}(\Delta t;\theta)
, which is retrained using the fine grid data from Phase 1 over a slightly shifted time window
t\in[0,1+\Delta t]
and subsequently used for dynamics extrapolation. For the heat equation, extrapolation is performed until physical time
t=2
, when the system reaches a steady state. For the wave equation and NSE, the PDEs are evolved up to physical time
t=10
to probe strongly out-of-distribution (OOD) dynamics.

Metrics

To assess the generalizability of the learned super-resolution operator and effective propagator for PDE dynamics prediction, we evaluate the model-predicted dynamics in both phases using a new set of solutions generated from initial conditions randomly sampled from the same distribution. Specifically, we test 20 new trajectories and report the mean and standard deviation across three independent training runs. For Phase 1, we calculate the relative
L^{2}
error across all predictions, while for Phase 2, we compute each of the metrics described below.

Why multiple metrics?

As suggested in
(
Ren et al. 2025
)
, simple pixel-wise losses alone can be misleading for scientific data, as lower pixel-wise error does not guarantee physically faithful solutions. Thus, it is necessary to use different metrics to evaluate the performance of our models, as no single scalar score can capture every facet of fidelity that matters for high-resolution scientific data. Below we characterize the key features of each metric.

\bm{\mathcal{E}_{L^{2}}}
:

Relative
L^{2}
is
scale-agnostic
but
location-sensitive
: any pixel-wise mismatch is penalized,
so large-amplitude errors anywhere in the field dominate. In machine learning contexts, this is one of the most canonical error metrics.

SSIM
:

Structural similarity
rewards
local pixel-wise texture and contrast agreement. Two fields can be close in
L^{2}
yet very different perceptually; SSIM detects such structural artifacts. By definition,
\text{SSIM}\in[-1,1]
, with
1
indicating perfect structural fidelity/reconstruction.

\bm{\text{SpecMSE}}:

Spectral MSE
measures only the
distribution
of energy across Fourier modes. It flags aliasing or over-smoothing that may or may not inflate
L^{2}
or SSIM.

\bm{r}
:

Pearson correlation
gauges the
linear association
between the predicted and reference fields. Because it is computed after centering each field,
r
is
scale-invariant
: two fields that differ only by a global multiplicative factor still achieve
r=1
. Thus,
r
complements the other metrics by highlighting how well the spatial patterns vary, irrespective of absolute amplitude.

errors averaged over all 20 new test trajectories. These results clearly show that the super-resolution models consistently outperform the naive bicubic upsampling baseline by
1
–
3
orders of magnitude, confirming the utility of the ML-based super-resolution approach for dynamics reconstruction.

For the three examples considered, the heat equation exhibits the simplest dynamics because it is a linear parabolic PDE with constant coefficients, corresponding to a gradient flow that smooths and decouples Fourier modes via pure exponential decay. As a result, all tested super-resolution models successfully capture the main features of the flow and produce quantitatively more accurate predictions compared to the other two PDEs. The dynamics become more complex for the wave equation, and are further complicated in the Navier–Stokes equation due to nonlinearity. Consequently, as shown in Figure
2
and Table
1
, the discrepancy between super-resolution models and baseline bicubic interpolation increases from the heat equation to the wave equation and then to the NSE. Among all tested SRaFTE models, no significant difference is observed in the Phase 1 results between FUnet, EDSR, and FNO-SR; these three models consistently yield more accurate predictions of the PDE solutions than the purely convolutional based U-Net.

To gauge the scale-robustness of Phase 1, we repeat the NS experiment at two additional coarse-fine pair resolutions,
32\times 32\to 128\times 128
and
128\times 128\to 512\times 512
for all models and baselines. The resulting resolution sweep, presenting in Figure
5
tracks the mean and standard deviation of the relative
L^{2}
error for every model as fine grid resolution increases. Across all three resolutions, we see that the FUnet remains the most accurate and shows the flattest error growth. Interestingly, the FNO-SR curve bends downward as we move from the lowest target resolution to the highest, indicating that the model becomes more accurate. We believe this is because the FNO is inherently biased toward low- and mid-frequency content because each spectral layer retains only a prescribed band of modes
(
Qin et al. 2024
;
Khodakarami et al. 2025
)
. When the fine grid is enlarged while the down-sampling ratio remains fixed, most dynamically important structures now fall deeper inside that retained band, so the proportion of energy that the network must extrapolate beyond its spectral cut-off is reduced.

Taken together, these observations indicate that models in the SRaFTE framework learn super-resolution operators that outperform existing baselines in Phase 1.

5.2.
Future dynamics prediction (Phase 2 results)

For the prediction of future dynamics in each PDE example, we use the trained effective propagator as the numerical scheme to approximate the fine-scale solution. Table
2
summarizes the averaged statistics over the 20 unseen test trajectories for each SRaFTE model. For the heat, wave, and NS examples, we extrapolate from time
t=1
to
t=2,10,10
, respectively.
Figure
4
presents the dynamics extrapolation results for a test run with a new initial condition that was randomly sampled. Taken together, the results in Table
2
and Figure
4
lead to the following conclusions:

(i) Numerical advantages for a nonlinear PDE

From Figure
4
and the metric values in Table
2
, SRaFTE shows clear numerical advantages over baseline methods in predicting the dynamics of the NSE, a nonlinear PDE. This trend is consistent with Phase 1 reconstruction results. The main reason is the rapid accumulation of prediction errors in a nonlinear system
(
Chattopadhyay and Hassanzadeh 2023
)
. As shown in Figure
3
, for an unseen test trajectory, the FNO-AR approach quickly accumulates approximation errors during the dynamics extrapolation phase, leading to large deviations from the true trajectory after only a short time. This effect is also evident in Figure
4
. These results demonstrate that, compared with the baselines, the effective propagator learned in SRaFTE provides a more accurate approximation of the fine-scale dynamics solver for nonlinear PDEs.

(ii) Composition propagator vs. equation-free propagator

Comparing the numerical results of FNO-SR and FNO-AR, we can assess the effectiveness of learning an effective propagator via the composition operator (
4
). FNO-AR uses the FNO architecture from its original formulation
(
Li et al. 2021
)
to learn an equation-free fine propagator; in contrast, FNO-SR learns an effective fine propagator that consists of the composition of a projection, a coarse propagator, and a learned super-resolution operator. From Figure
4
and the metrics in Table
2
, we observe that FNO-AR is more accurate for the heat equation, whereas FNO-SR performs better for more complex systems such as the non-dissipative wave equation and the nonlinear NSE across all metrics. This demonstrates that the strategy of learning an effective propagator is more advantageous for systems with no clear scale separation. In such cases, the composition operator (
4
) serves as a better ansatz for the fine-scale dynamics solver. This trend is consistent with the numerical performance of the other SRaFTE models discussed above.

(iii) SSIM and SpecMSE as discriminative metrics

Relative
L_{2}
error tends to conflate large-scale shape alignment with fine-scale features, so model predictions that
visually
differ can have similar
L_{2}
error. Thus we employ two additional metrics, SpecMSE and SSIM. SSIM compares the structure difference between the predicted flow and the ground truth; hence it is able to track the human-level perceived fidelity of the predictions much more closely. SpecMSE compares the error in the energy spectra of the predicted PDE solutions, a physically relevant metric. Thus, when relative
L^{2}
errors are similar (e.g. for the wave equation), SSIM and SpecMSE offer more discriminative, task-relevant diagnostics to identify the best performing model. This is particularly pronounced in the Phase 2 wave equation results of Table
2
.

Learning and preserving high-frequency content in scientific machine learning is nontrivial. Many neural operators exhibit an inherent low-frequency bias: optimization and architectural priors favor smooth, large-scale modes while underrepresenting small-scale, oscillatory features
(
Qin et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Khodakarami et al. 2025
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2025
;
Chattopadhyay and Hassanzadeh 2023
;
Subramanian et al. 2023
)
. In FNO-style models this bias is explicit through Fourier-mode truncation, whereas in purely convolutional-based models, it arises from pooling/upsampling and implicit smoothing. Pixel-wise losses further de-emphasize high-
k
components that carry less energy, and coarse-to-fine projection can introduce aliasing, so a network may achieve low relative
L^{2}
yet miss the energy cascade that determines fine structures. In Phase 2, delegating large-scale transport to the coarse PDE step lets the learned lift concentrate its capacity on subgrid corrections; SSIM and SpecMSE quantify its success by rewarding structural fidelity and spectral alignment.

In summary, the numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the SRaFTE framework in PDE dynamics reconstruction and prediction. When integrated with modern super-resolution architectures, it enables the reconstruction of fine-scale numerical solutions of PDEs from the coarse grid solutions. Moreover, the composition operator serves as a natural ansatz for constructing an effective propagator to predict future fine-scale dynamics. In the NSE example, it substantially outperforms baseline approaches such as FNO-AR.

6.
Conclusion

We presented SRaFTE, a two-phase learning approach that combines neural super-resolution with an inexpensive coarse solver to improve long-horizon forecasts of time-dependent PDEs without the full cost of fine grid simulation. On three benchmark systems of escalating complexity—heat, wave, and Navier–Stokes equations —we showed that SRaFTE reproduces spatial detail in Phase 1 and delivers stable approximations in Phase 2, with the largest benefits over baseline methods observed in the multiscale NS case. Although Phase 2 predictions remain only approximate, the improvements across a variety of metrics and visual quality highlight the value of embedding learned resolution enhancement inside a predictor-corrector loop.

The main goal for future work is to scale these methods to realistic 3D systems. For our 2D Navier-Stokes example, we streamed data from disk (
\sim 7
GB for the coarse dataset and
\sim 122
GB for the fine dataset) and were able to train on a single 80GB A100 GPU. End-to-end training of the largest model (Phase 1 + Phase 2) took
\sim 4
GPU hours. For a 3D system at the same per-axis resolution, the dataset size grows in proportion to the number of grid cells to
\approx 31
TB. Per-step memory and computation scale with the total number of grid points
V=N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}
. For spectral layers, the FFT contributes a
V\log V
factor. Relative to 2D at the same resolution, for the same number of training instances and epochs, the current approach would require
\approx 1000
GPU hours. To make this cost tractable, we would use a smaller batch size/minibatching, mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and multi-GPU training. Parameter counts grow only modestly (
\approx 1.5
–
3\times
for convolutional; typically
<10\times
for spectral, with similar per-axis mode cutoffs), and memory is dominated by activations rather than dataset size or weights. Hence, we do envision that future work on SRaFTE methods will enable scaling to 3D PDEs.

Appendix A
Model summaries

Unless otherwise stated, models operate on stacks of coarse/fine grain frames treated as channels (denoted
C_{in}=100
) and use
GELU
activations.

FUnet.

A shallow encoder–decoder U-Net with skip connections with a spectrally augmented bottleneck. The encoder lifts channels (to 128), applies two local convolutions, and downsamples once via max-pooling. The bottleneck inserts a single
spectral
layer: 2D FFT of the latent,
low-frequency truncation
, learned complex mixing on the kept modes, then inverse FFT. The decoder upsamples with pixel-shuffle and ends with a small convolutional head back to
C_{\text{out}}
. In total, this means that local details from the convolutions and global spectral context from the Fourier bottleneck provide a mixture of information for reconstruction.

U-Net.

A two-level U-Net made entirely of local convolutions with skip connections. Decoding uses transpose convolutions back to the coarse resolution; a final bilinear resize performs the upsampling to the fine grid. No spectral information is used in this architecture.

EDSR.

A residual CNN with three stages: a shallow convolutional head, a
body
of residual blocks, and a pixel-shuffle tail. We use a moderate feature width of 64 and
\mathbf{16}
residual blocks with residual scaling (0.1). The network stays at coarse resolution until the pixel-shuffle tail upsamples to the target resolution.

FNO-SR.
Each spectral block combines: (i) a truncated 2D FFT that
retains only low frequencies
(we keep
16{\times}16
modes) with learned complex mixing on the kept coefficients, and (ii) a parallel
1{\times}1
pointwise path; the two are summed and passed through a nonlinearity. We stack
3
such spectral blocks with a light
1{\times}1
lift/head (with width 64).

FNO-AR.
The autoregressive FNO uses the same spectral block idea as FNO-SR but run at the fine resolution and trained as a single-step propagator. We use
4
spectral blocks (width 64, retaining the lowest
16{\times}16
modes ) wrapped by a
1{\times}1
lift and a
1{\times}1
convolutional head.

Model training.
The choice of all parameters for each model were done through standard hyperparameter sweeps. Training for all models used the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
with cosine-annealing learning rate scheduler with a default learning rate of
\epsilon=5e^{-4}
. For Phase 1, we trained all models for 5000 epochs, and for Phase 2, we trained all models for 2500 epochs.

Appendix C
Metrics

To assess the performance of all models and baselines, we adopt the following metrics:

Relative
L^{2}
error.
For a predicted
\tilde{u}
and the corresponding true
u
on the same
N_{f}\times N_{f}
grid, the
relative
L^{2}
error
is

(6)

\mathcal{E}(t)=\frac{\|\,\tilde{u}(\cdot,\cdot,t)-u(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{2}}{\|\,u(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{2}+{\varepsilon}},\qquad\varepsilon=10^{-12},

where the Euclidean norm is taken over all spatial grid points. In the following results, unless otherwise indicated, we report the mean over every time snapshot
t_{m}
.

Structural similarity index measure (SSIM).
The single-scale SSIM between a prediction and the reference is

\text{SSIM}(t)=\frac{(2\mu_{u}\mu_{\tilde{u}}+C_{1})\,(2\sigma_{u\tilde{u}}+C_{2})}{(\mu_{u}^{2}+\mu_{\tilde{u}}^{2}+C_{1})\,(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{\tilde{u}}^{2}+C_{2})},

where
\mu_{u}=\mathbb{E}[u]
and
\mu_{\tilde{u}}=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{u}]
are local pixel-sample means,
\sigma_{u}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[(u-\mu_{u})^{2}]
and
\sigma_{\tilde{u}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{u}-\mu_{\tilde{u}})^{2}]
are local pixel-sample variances,
\sigma_{u\tilde{u}}=\mathbb{E}[(u-\mu_{u})(\tilde{u}-\mu_{\tilde{u}})]
is the local pixel-sample covariance, and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are small constants to stabilize division.

Spectral mean-squared error (Spectral MSE).
Denote the 2D Fourier amplitudes by

\widehat{u}_{k,\ell}=\mathrm{DFT}[u]_{k,\ell},\widehat{\tilde{u}}_{k,\ell}=\mathrm{DFT}[\tilde{u}]_{k,\ell}.

The spectral MSE is
\text{SpecMSE}(t)=\frac{1}{N_{f}^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell}\bigl|\,|\widehat{\tilde{u}}_{k,\ell}|-|\widehat{u}_{k,\ell}|\bigr|^{2}
. Physically, this is the mean-squared difference between spectral magnitudes. It penalizes mismatches in the distribution of energy over wavenumbers.

Pearson linear correlation
r
.
If we flatten each field into a vector of length
N_{f}^{2}
, the Pearson coefficient
r
is given as

r(t)=\frac{\sum_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u})\,(\tilde{u}_{i}-\bar{\tilde{u}})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}(\tilde{u}_{i}-\bar{\tilde{u}})^{2}}},

where bars denote sample means. Higher values indicate higher linear correlation.
