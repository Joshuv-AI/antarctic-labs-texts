Effectiveness of Natural Deep Eutectic Solvents (NADES) in Extracting Bioactive Compounds from Salvia sclarea: HPLC-DAD Analysis, Antioxidant and Enzymes Inhibition Potential Evaluation
Authors: Mohammed Mansour Quradha, Mehmet Emin Duru, Selcuk Kucukaydin, Abdulkader Moqbel Farhan Qahtan, Rasool Khan, Sallah A. Al Hashedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current study aimed to investigate the efficacy of green solvents to extract bioactive compounds from S. sclarea and evaluate their antioxidant and enzyme inhibition potential. Three natural deep eutectic solvent (NADES) formulations were synthesized: NADES-1 (choline chloride: citric acid 1:2), NADES-2 (choline chloride: glucose 1:2), and NADES-3 (choline chloride: glycerol 1:2). The HPLC-DAD analysis of NADES extracts showed that rosmarinic acid was found in high concentrations in NADES-2 (44.56 ± 0.28 μg/g), NADES-3 (29.49 ± 0.31 μg/g), and NADES-1 (25.63 ± 0.35 μg/g), respectively, while trans-cinnamic acid was detected exclusively in NADES-1. Similarly, chlorogenic acid and p-hydroxy benzoic acid were detected only in NADES-2, whereas ellagic acid was unique to NADES-3. In vitro antioxidant activity, assessed via different assays including (1) the β-carotene-linoleic acid test, (2) the DPPH test, (3) the ABTS test, (4) the CUPRAC test, and (5) the metal chelating test, demonstrated significant antioxidant activity across all NADES extracts, with NADES-2 exhibiting superior efficacy, exceeding the performance of reference standards (BHA and α-tocopherol). Furthermore, NADES-2 exhibited considerable inhibitory effects against AChE (IC50 = 107.7 ± 0.95 μg/mL) and BChE (IC50 = 96.81 ± 0.87 μg/mL). Conversely, each of the NADES extracts showed minimal inhibition of urease (<21%), indicating limited relevance for urease-related pathologies. These findings underscore the capacity of NADES as sustainable, high-efficiency solvents for the targeted extraction of bioactive phytochemicals, with NADES-2 emerging as a particularly promising candidate for antioxidant and cholinesterase-inhibitory applications. Further studies are recommended to elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of NADES selectivity and to expand their utility in large-scale extraction and isolation of bioactive compounds from natural sources.

Introduction

Bioactive compounds obtained from natural sources have long been documented for their substantial contributions to drug development and discovery, mainly due to their broad spectrum of biological activities and vast structural diversity, and they frequently inspire synthetic drug design. These compounds often possess complex molecular architectures that are challenging to replicate synthetically, providing invaluable leads for food, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical innovation. The intricate interactions of bioactive compounds extracted from natural sources with biological systems not only offer direct therapeutic potential but also serve as templates for the design of synthetic analogues with enhanced properties. , Despite the evolution of synthetic chemistry, natural products remain indispensable sources of novel bioactive compounds and structural leads. The ongoing exploration of biodiversity promises further breakthroughs in medicine by unveiling new molecular entities. , The initial step in obtaining these compounds for applications ranging from pharmaceuticals to cosmetics involves their extraction from natural sources. Traditionally, the extraction of these bioactive compounds has relied on conventional solvents, which, while effective, present several disadvantages. These include toxic waste generation, environmental degradation through air pollution, and health risks such as potential carcinogenic effects and respiratory issues. Moreover, the economic burden of solvent disposal and the inefficiency in extraction processes, leading to the loss of valuable compounds and extended extraction times, are notable concerns. − In response to these challenges, researchers are seeking alternative methods to address these problems by shifting toward the use of green solvents, which are sourced from renewable materials, thereby diminishing reliance on nonrenewable fossil fuels. These solvents exhibit reduced toxicity and flammability, enhancing workplace safety and providing a more selective extraction of specific bioactive compounds, which can lead to higher purity and yield of desired products. − Among these, deep eutectic solvents (DESs) and natural deep eutectic solvents (NADESs) have emerged as particularly promising alternatives. These solvents are formed through the combination of a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD), offering environmental benefits due to their biodegradability and lower toxicity. ,, Studies have shown that DES and NADES can achieve higher extraction efficiencies for various bioactive compounds, including phenolics, compared to conventional solvents such as water and ethanol, owing to their unique ability to solubilize both polar and nonpolar substances. , Furthermore, Tan et al. have demonstrated the efficacy of DES as novel additives in mobile phases for chromatographic separations, underscoring their versatility in analytical chemistry. Green extraction using NADES is becoming highly relevant to drug discovery, food sciences, and technology due to its response to green chemistry principles, providing a sustainable and effective alternative to conventional methods. NADES are composed of naturally occurring biocompatible and typically food-grade compounds such as sugars, urea, and choline derivatives, opposing the need for nontoxic, biodegradable, and sustainable solvents in the isolation and extraction of bioactive molecules from natural sources. Their nontoxic and biodegradable nature addresses the growing demand for sustainable solvents in the isolation of bioactive compounds from food matrices and natural sources. The green extraction approach is significant because it tends to yield higher extraction yields and improved stability of sensitive food-derived bioactives such as polyphenols and essential oils. Furthermore, NADES exhibit remarkable solvation versatility, capable of dissolving both polar and nonpolar compounds. This property facilitates the selective recovery of functional ingredients from complex food systems, thereby supporting the development of novel functional foods, clean-label formulations, and safer food additives. Their contribution to food and pharmaceutical applications is especially interesting as they, furthermore, NADES can also serve as therapeutic deep eutectic solvents by creating a eutectic mixture with an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) or, even in certain instances, being the API itself. In addition to their role in drug discovery, NADES technologies are increasingly being integrated into food processing pipelines to enhance product quality, safety, and environmental sustainability. Salvia sclarea, a medicinal species within the genus Salvia of the Lamiaceae family, is widely recognized for its aromatic properties. This plant is indigenous to Central Asia, North Africa, and the Northern Mediterranean. , Previous studies have highlighted its different therapeutic properties, including antiinflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, antidiabetic, sedative, and antitumor activities. Additionally, the essential oil derived from S. sclarea has been employed for its antiseptic, antidepressant, carminative, aphrodisiac, and antispasmodic activities. The present study aims to document the green solvent techniques for the extraction of bioactive compounds from S. sclarea. This research employs three types of natural deep eutectic solvents (NADESs) and evaluates their antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiurease activities.

Materials and Methods

Collection of Plants

The Salvia sclarea (aerial parts) was systematically collected from a site near Muğla-Menteşe, specifically along the Yerkesik road at an altitude of 60 m. The taxonomic verification of the plant was conducted by Dr. Hasan Yıldırım from Ege University in İzmir, Turkey. Following identification, a voucher specimen was archived at the Natural Products Laboratory within the Faculty of Science at Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University, bearing the identification number TSP1012. Postcollection, the plant material was air-dried under shade, crushed, and prepared for the extraction process.

Crude Extraction Using Green Solvent

Extraction was performed according to the described method reported previously, ,, with tiny modifications. Briefly, 3 g of plant material was extracted with the prepared NADES (composition shown in Table ; HBA: HBD molar 1:2), followed by the addition of 20 mL of distilled water to reduce viscosity. The mixture was heated at 40 °C and stirred for 10 h. The sample was transferred to a Falcon tube (50 mL) and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min. The sediments were removed, and the supernatant was filtered through 0.45 μm cellulose paper. After filtration, the water was evaporated using a rotary evaporator, freeze-dried, and stored at 30 °C for further analysis.

Determination of Phenolic Composition

High-performance liquid chromatography coupled with a photodiode array detector (HPLC-DAD) was used to detect and identify phenolic compounds. The NADES extracts were dissolved in a solution of 80:20 water–methanol and passed through a 0.20 μm disposable LC filter disk to remove suspended particles prior to injection into an Intersil ODS-3 reverse-phase C18 column for detection and separation. The flow rate of the solvent was kept at 1.0 mL/min, and the injection volume of the sample was 20 μL. 0.5% acetic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.5% acetic acid in methanol (mobile phase B) were used as the mobile phases. Gradient elution was programmed as follows: 0–10% B (0–0.01 min), 10–20% B (0.01–5 min), 20–30% B (5–15 min), 30–50% B (15–25 min), 50–65% B (25–30 min), 65–75% B (30–40 min), 75–90% B (40–50 min), and 90–10% B (50–55 min). Detection was carried out at a wavelength of 280 nm. Phenolic compounds were identified based on comparison of their retention times and UV spectra with commercial standards. Quantitation was performed with the help of a calibration curve prepared by injection of the standard compounds in known concentrations. Twenty-six standard phenolic compounds were screened, namely: gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, 3-hydroxybenzoic acid, syringic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, ellagic acid, rosmarinic acid, transcinnamic acid, catechin, pyrocatechol, 6,7-dihydroxycoumarin, vanillin, taxifolin, coumarin, rutin, myricetin, quercetin, luteolin, hesperetin, kaempferol, apigenin, and chrysin. Quantification was presented as μg/g of dry weight.

Biological Activities

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant potential of NADES extracts was evaluated using different well-known methods, including (1) the β-carotene-linoleic acid assay, (2) the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhyrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay, (3) the 2,2’-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical cation decolonization assay, (4) the cupric ion reducing antioxidant capacity (CUPRAC) assay, and (5) the metal chelating assay.

β-Carotene-Linoleic Acid Assay

The antioxidant capacity of the NADES extract obtained from S. sclarea was evaluated using the β-carotene/linoleic acid bleaching technique Marco with slight modifications as cited by Tel-Çayan and Duru. In this technique, an emulsion is formed by mixing 0.5 mg of β-carotene with 1 mL of chloroform, 25 μL of linoleic acid, and 200 mg of Tween 40. Then, under reduced pressure, the chloroform is removed, and finally 100 mL of saturated distilled water is added with vigorous agitation to form an emulsion. For the assay, samples of this emulsion were placed in each test tube with different concentrations of the given test sample. The initial absorption at 470 nm was measured immediately after the addition of this emulsion (t = 0). The samples were then placed in a 50 °C oven for a 2-h incubation time and measured again for absorption. In this case, butylated hydroxyanisole and alpha-tocopherols were used as the standard antioxidant samples, with a tube containing ethanol used as a negative control. DPPH Free Radical Scavenging Assay

The free radical scavenging capacity of the NADES extract obtained from S. sclarea was determined using the stable 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhyrazyl­(DPPH) radical, following the procedure of Blois. , Briefly, 1 mL of extract at various concentrations was mixed with 4 mL of a 0.1 mM methanolic DPPH solution. After incubation for 30 min in the dark at room temperature, the absorbance is measured at 517 nm. Lower absorbance indicates higher radical scavenging activity. ABTS•+ Cation Radical Scavenging Assay

Scavenging of the 2,2’-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cation ABTS •+ was done using the method of Re et al., with some modifications. The ABTS •+ chromophore was produced by the reaction of a 7 mM ABTS aqueous solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate and left to stand for 12 h in the dark. This stock solution was diluted with ethanol before analysis to an absorbance of 0.703 ± 0.025 at 734 nm. Extract activity was determined by decolorization of this preformed radical by the addition of the antioxidant. The control used was ethanol, while BHA and α-tocopherol were the standards. Scavenging activity is expressed as IC50 (μg/mL) calculated from a plot of percent inhibition against concentration. ABTS•+−scavengingeffect%=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

Where A control is the initial concentration of ABTS•+ and A sample is the absorbance of the remaining concentration of ABTS•+ in the presence of the extract and positive control.

Cupric Reducing Antioxidant Capacity (CUPRAC)

The Cupric Reducing Antioxidant Capacity (CUPRAC) was measured following the method reported by Apak et al., with minor adjustments. In summary, 50 μL of 10 mM neocuproine and 60 μL of ammonium acetate buffer (1 M, pH 7.0) were mixed in each well of a 96-well plate. Then, 40 μL of NADES extract at different concentrations was added to make a final reaction volume of 200 μL. After incubation for 1 h, the absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader, with a reagent blank as the reference. Butylated hydroxylanisole (BHA) and α-tocopherol were used as standard antioxidant controls. The A 0.5 represents the concentration of extract providing 0.5 absorbance units under the CUPRAC assay conditions.

Metal Chelating Assay

Ion (Fe2+) chelating capability of the NADES extract was measured by its effectiveness in competing with ferene­(3-(2-pyridyl)-5,6-diphenyl-1,2,4,triazine-4“,4”’-disulfonic acid) for ferrous ions, by modification of a previously described method by Decker and Welch. In brief, 80 μL of sample solution [in ethanol] at various concentrations was mixed with 40 μL of 0.2 mM FeCl2. The reaction was initiated with 80 μL of 0.5 mM ferene. Following rapid agitation and incubation for 10 min at room temperature, the absorbance of the ferene-Fe2+ complex that formed was read at 593 nm. Chelation was calculated using the formula: Metal chelating activity(%)=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

where A control is the initial concentration devoid of the sample, and A sample is the absorbance in the presence of the chelator; EDTA was used as an antioxidant standard for comparison of the activity.

Anticholinesterase Activity

The anticholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, and acetylcholinesterase activities of NADES extract(s) were assayed spectrophotometrically, adopting Ellman’s method with minor modifications. The reference compound used was galantamine. IC50 values were computed using a computer program from the plot of anticholinesterase activity. Percentage inhibitory activity (%) was calculated against the sample concentration (μg/mL).

Antiurease Activity

Urease inhibitory activity of all NADES extracts was assessed by measuring the production of ammonia using the indophenol method, as reported earlier, with the assistance of a microplate reader. For this assay, a mixture of 25 μL of enzyme urease solution (jack bean), 50 μL of 100 mM urea, and 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 8.2, was mixed and incubated at 30 °C for 15 min after adding 10 μL of the extract. 70 μL of alkali reagent and 45 μL of phenol reagent were added to each well. After 50 min of incubation, the absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a microplate reader. Thiourea was employed as the reference compound, and the activity was expressed as IC50 values and percentage inhibitions at a concentration of 200 μg/mL.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Differences among extraction treatments were evaluated using one-way ANOVA. When significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s HSD posthoc test was applied to determine pairwise significance. Statistical assumptions (normality and homogeneity of variance) were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively; when required, data were log-transformed prior to ANOVA. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v22.0 software.

Results

Three types of natural deep eutectic solvents (NADESs) were synthesized as NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3. Each of the NADES extracts was subjected to examination and comparison of their HPLC phenolic profile, as well as their potential biological activities.

Phenolic Composition

The presence of phenolic compounds, extracted using NADES as environmentally friendly solvents, was assessed utilizing High-Performance Liquid Chromatography with Diode-Array Detection (HPLC-DAD). Twenty-six phenolic standards were employed to identify these compounds, with the findings detailed in Table . The analysis revealed that rosmarinic acid was present in notably high concentrations in each NADES extracts, namely 44.56 ± 0.28 μg/g, 29.49 ± 0.31 μg/g, and 25.63 ± 0.35 μg/g in NADES-2, NADES-3, and NADES-1 extracts, respectively. Chlorogenic acid and p-hydroxybenzoic acid were exclusively detected in the NADES-2 extract, whereas ellagic acid was found only in the NADES-3 extract. Trans-cinnamic acid was unique to the NADES-1 (choline chloride: citric acid (1:2) extract. Caffeic acid, syringic acid, coumarin, and luteolin were present in both the NADES-2 and NADES-3 extracts, while 3-hydroxybenzoic acid was identified in both NADES-1 and NADES-3. Gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, catechin, rutin, myricetin, quercetin, and apigenin were detected across all three NADES extracts. Based on the result trends obtained from the HPLC analysis, it is possible to identify that there is selectivity exhibited by the NADES systems toward various classes of phenolic compounds. Phenolic acids were the most abundant compounds identified in all the extracts, with rosmarinic acid being the major one, especially in the case of the NADES-2 system. The use of acidic components in the NADES solvents ensures effective solubilization of highly polar hydroxycinnamates due to hydrogen bonding. The identification of flavonoids including quercetin, apigenin, luteolin, and rutin in all of the extracts points toward the fact that polarity plays a significant part in the extraction of flavonoids.

DPPH Assay

The free radical scavenging capacity of various green extracts prepared from S. sclarea via NADES was evaluated, and the results are presented in Table and Figure . In general, the results exhibited the efficacy of green extracts to scavenge free radicals. Accordingly, NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3 extracts demonstrated considerable antioxidant activity, with IC50 values of 22.95 ± 0.65 μg/mL, 17.39 ± 0.46 μg/mL, and 18.05 ± 0.33 μg/mL, respectively. Comparative studies showed that both NADES-2 and NADES-3 surpassed the efficacy of both reference antioxidants, BHA (IC50 = 19.70 ± 0.25 μg/mL) and α-tocopherol (IC50 = 38.70 ± 0.32 μg/mL). NADES-1 also presented greater activity than α-tocopherol; however, it was relatively lower than that of BHA. These findings highlight the variable antioxidant capacities of the tested NADES formulations, with NADES-2 and NADES-3 emerging as particularly potent radical scavengers.

Discussion

The current study was designed to demonstrate the efficacy of green solvents for the extraction of bioactive compounds from S. sclarea. Three different natural deep eutectic solvents (NADESs) were prepared, namely: NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3, and applied to extract phenolic compounds from S. sclarea, followed by evaluation of their biological potential. In our previous studies, one type of natural deep eutectic solvent (citric acid:glucose) was prepared and applied to extract bioactive compounds from S. sclarea and S. triloba. The results indicated the significance of green solvents in obtaining phenolic compounds compared to conventional solvents. Furthermore, the green extract exhibited considerable biological activity properties. This led us to further explore the efficiency of other types of green solvents in the extraction of bioactive compounds. As mentioned above, the three types of green solvents, including NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3, exhibited significant effectiveness in extracting phenolic compounds. Generally, all three NADES extracted quite a broad range of phenolics, which were dominated by rosmarinic acid. NADES-2 yielded the highest content of rosmarinic acid, which may be due to its higher polarity and stronger hydrogen-bonding capacity; these properties favor the solubilization of highly hydroxylated phenolic compounds. , Each of the different solvents expressed its specific selectivity. NADES-2 uniquely extracted chlorogenic and p-hydroxybenzoic acid, suggesting its suitability for midpolarity phenolics. NADES-3 allowed for the extraction of ellagic acid, which may suggest compatibility with either larger or more rigid polyphenols. Accordingly, the least polar NADES-1 selectively extracted only transcinnamic acid. On the other hand, all NADES provided similar recoveries regarding core phenolics such as gallic acid, catechin, quercetin, and apigenin, confirming their wide solvating capability. Overall, the phenolic yield from all NADES was comparable to, or even higher than those obtained when using conventional solvent (methanol) as reported previously. This efficiency arises from their polar nature and the extensive hydrogen-bond networks that enhance interactions with phenolic hydroxyl groups and promote matrix penetration. − As extensively documented in prior research, phenolic compounds demonstrate significant antioxidant capacity, a property critical to their role in mitigating oxidative stress. Furthermore, these phytochemicals have been shown to exhibit diverse therapeutic properties, including antiinflammatory, antidiabetic, antiaging, and antiproliferative effects. Therefore, the current study adds to the literature on the extraction efficiencies of NADES as green solvents to obtain bioactive compounds from natural sources instead of conventional solvents and, subsequently, develops green chemistry applications in phytocompound extraction.

Antioxidants are crucial in the prevention of the risk of chronic pathologies such as cancer and cardiovascular diseases, which are associated with oxidative stress resulting from free radical activity in biological systems. , These reactive species induce cellular damage and apoptosis, necessitating protective mechanisms mediated by antioxidant compounds that scavenge radicals and inhibit oxidative cascades. , In this study, the antioxidant activity of S. sclarea extracts (NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3) was determined using a number of assays, including β-carotene-linoleic acid oxidation inhibition, DPPH• scavenging, ABTS•+ quenching, metal chelation, and CUPRAC assays. Overall, the data illustrate that the formulation of NADES used in the preparation contributes significantly to the antioxidant activity of the extract, wherein NADES-2 has consistently been identified as the most powerful among the studied solvents in all conducted tests. In the β-carotene-linoleic acid assay, which models the inhibition of lipid peroxidation in an emulsified system, NADES-2 had superior activity (IC50= 6.52 μg/mL), followed by NADES-3 and NADES-1. This order of activities could be related to particular physicochemical properties of NADES-2, such as polarity, viscosity, and a hydrogen-bonding network, being more effective in the extraction of nonpolar or moderately polar antioxidant compounds present in S. sclarea. These compounds, probably composed of terpenoids and phenolic diterpenes, which are usual for the Salvia genus, could donate hydrogen atoms to intercept the free radical chain reaction during the process of lipid oxidation. , This was further confirmed by free radical scavenging activity assays. Indeed, the IC50 values of NADES-2 and NADES-3 extracts were not only better than that of the natural standard α-tocopherol but also surpassed or equaled those of the synthetic antioxidant BHA in both DPPH and ABTS+ assays, which estimate the capacities of hydrogen atom transfer and electron transfer, respectively. The promising activity in the ABTS+ assay-as low as IC50 = 10.40 μg/mL for NADES-2-further highlighted the hydrophilic and lipophilic nature of free radicals scavenged by these NADES extracts. The CUPRAC assay, based on the reduction of copper­(II) to copper­(I), provided consistent evidence showing an identical order of potency: NADES-2 > NADES-3 > NADES-1. The results obtained from DPPH, ABTS+, and CUPRAC assays all showed strong correlation with each other since the main mechanism of such antioxidants was through redox reactions, which are characteristic of phenolic antioxidants. Furthermore, the remarkable activity observed in the metal chelating assay is an important dimension of the antioxidant profile. The ability of the NADES-2 extract to chelate ferrous ions, with an IC50 = 38.57 μg/mL, is important since this prevents the initiation of Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions that generate very active hydroxyl radicals. This implies that NADES extracts, in particular NADES-2, contain compounds capable of secondary antioxidant action through deactivation of pro-oxidant transition metals that complements their direct free radical scavenging capacity.

The consistent predominance of NADES-2 and, to a lesser extent, NADES-3 through all five different methodological frameworks underlines one of the key advantages of NADES technology: being able to tune the properties of a solvent in such a way as to selectively enhance the extraction of certain classes of bioactive compounds.

The results of the current study for antioxidant activity were more effective than those utilizing conventional solvents and validate previous studies on S. sclarea , and Salvia species such as S. fruticosa, S. virgata Jacq., S. verticillata, and S. officinalis L. indicating high antioxidant activities. Phenolic compounds existed in high levels in all extracts; thus, the observed antioxidant activity in this study may be attributed to the occurrence of phenolic compounds as antioxidant compounds such as gallic acid, quercetin, and rosmarinic acid.

The current study examined the cholinesterase inhibitory potentials of extracts prepared using three different NADES. The findings revealed a promising difference in the cholinesterase inhibitory activities of green solvent extracts, namely AChE and BChE, which are established therapeutic targets in the management of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease. The most salient finding is superior inhibitory activity exhibited by NADES-2 against both enzymes, with IC50 values of 107.7 ± 0.95 μg/mL for AChE and 96.81 ± 0.87 μg/mL for BChE. This dual-inhibitory profile represents a very important feature from the point of view of the pharmaceutical industry. AChE is the main enzyme responsible for terminating cholinergic neurotransmission, while BChE becomes more important in the later stages of Alzheimer’s disease. The sharp difference in activity observed for NADES-2 when compared with the other two solvents NADES-1 and NADES-3, which exhibited IC50 200 μg/mL, reminds us of a very valid dogma in natural products chemistry, the extractive medium is not inert; it can actively influence the pharmacological profile of the final preparation. The findings for NADES-1 and NADES-3 against cholinesterase enzymes are in line with results obtained utilizing conventional solvent (methanol); however, NADES-2 demonstrated significantly greater inhibition of both acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) than the methanol extract. These findings highlight the potential efficacy of NADES-2 in extracting compounds capable of inhibiting AChE and BChE enzymes.

From the perspective of green chemistry, these results strongly support NADES as a design tool rather than just a green alternative, due to the fact that variation in the different nature of its constituents-for example, choline chloride, organic acids, and sugars-allows the fine-tuning of the properties of a solvent. This allows for the directed extraction of targeted bioactivities, beyond the simple replacement of toxic solvents such as methanol or DMSO, and instead enters the realm of functionalizing the process of extraction itself.

In terms of urease inhibition, all three NADES extracts were quite weak, with inhibition ranging from 13.42 ± 0.47% (NADES-3) to 21.46 ± 0.33% (NADES-1), and IC50 values always greater than 200 μg/mL. This shows that NADES extracts are weak urease inhibitors at the given concentration. This result is in line with a previous study we have reported, which found that both the green extract and the conventional extract of S. sclarea exhibited weak urease inhibition. The weak urease inhibitory activity in all NADES extracts suggests that S. sclarea may not be suitable for applications against urease-associated pathologies, such as peptic ulcers or bacterial infections caused by the action of urease-producing bacteria such as Helicobacter pylori.

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first investigation to demonstrate the efficacy of NADESs in extracting phenolic compounds from S. sclarea and evaluating their biological activities.

Conclusion

The current study, for the first time, has clearly illustrated the efficiency of natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) as a green solvent for phenolic compound extraction from S. sclarea, with increased biological properties. Three different NADES (NADES-1, NADES-2, and NADES-3) efficiently extracted a large set of phenolic components, with rosmarinic acid identified as the most dominant compound in all NADES solutions. It is worth noting that NADES-2 performed best with respect to efficiency, presenting a high amount of rosmarinic acid, which specifically targeted midpolar components, thereby showcasing the selective properties of NADES solvents. The biological evaluation showed that the antioxidant activity of S. sclarea extracts was impacted to a significant level by the components of the NADES used. NADES-2 exhibited the highest degree of overall antioxidant activity in a variety of complementary methods such as β-carotene/linoleic acid, DPPH • , ABTS •+ , CUPRAC, and metal chelating activity, which even exceeded that of standard antioxidants in many instances. Additionally, NADES-2 showed significant dual inhibitory activity against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), which might be useful in the preparation of a neuroprotective drug against cholinergic dysfunction. On the contrary, the urease inhibitory activity of all NADES extracts was weak, which hinders the use of S. sclarea extracts in urease-related therapeutic applications. In conclusion, this research has proven that NADES are more than environmentally friendly substitutes for common solvents; they are a versatile tool for selective phytochemical enhancement. The results of this study emphasize the potential of NADES-mediated extraction as a cutting-edge green chemistry approach for producing valuable biologically active molecules from medicinal plants. This research establishes a solid basis for future research on the development of NADES as tools for various industrial applications, such as food, pharmaceutical, or nutraceutical, with the goal of scaling up the NADES-mediated plant extraction process.
