Diffusion-Weighted Imaging–Based Virtual MR Elastography of the Liver: Beyond Mechanical Waves
Authors: Takashi Ota, Denis Le Bihan, Masatoshi Hori, Atsushi Nakamoto, Hideyuki Fukui, Hiromitsu Onishi, Noriyuki Tomiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is widely recognized as the most accurate, non-invasive imaging technique for evaluating liver fibrosis. However, MRE requires dedicated hardware, including an external vibration device, which limits its availability. Virtual MRE (VMRE) has recently emerged as a promising alternative technique in which tissue stiffness is estimated using diffusion-weighted imaging data without the need for external mechanical vibrations. VMRE is based on the hypothesis that tissue elasticity and water diffusivity share common mechanical features at the microstructural level. By calculating the shift in the apparent diffusion coefficient from the optimized b-values (200 and 1,500 s/mm2), the virtual stiffness can be derived and calibrated against the MRE-derived stiffness. Studies in cohorts with chronic viral hepatitis have demonstrated strong correlations between VMRE and MRE findings, with both techniques showing good agreement in liver fibrosis staging. Although the utility of VMRE in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was previously considered debatable because of the confounding effects of steatosis, recent evidence suggests that VMRE performed using an optimized fat-correction method combined with MASLD-specific b-values (200 and 1,200 s/mm2) can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to that of MRE. Beyond liver fibrosis assessment, the combination of VMRE and MRE has shown potential for distinguishing hepatocellular carcinoma from metastatic liver cancers. This article provides a comprehensive overview of VMRE, including its technical basis and current clinical applications in chronic liver diseases and focal liver lesions.

INTRODUCTION

In patients with chronic liver disease, precise staging of liver fibrosis is crucial for predicting disease progression, formulating treatment strategies, and assessing prognosis [1]. Traditionally, liver biopsy has been regarded as the gold standard for evaluating liver fibrosis. However, the limitations of liver biopsy, including invasiveness, sampling errors, and inter-observer variability, make it unsuitable for screening or monitoring disease progression over time [23]. These challenges have driven the increase in the use of non-invasive imaging techniques for assessing liver fibrosis [1]. These techniques, which include magnetic resonance elastography (MRE), ultrasound elastography, and serum biomarker measurements, have demonstrated efficacy in monitoring therapeutic responses and disease progression [145].

Against this background, virtual MRE (VMRE) has recently gained substantial attention. This technique employs data derived from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to estimate tissue stiffness, thereby eliminating the need for external vibration [6]. This article aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of VMRE, including its technical basis and current clinical applications for liver diseases.

MRE AND DWI

MRE, which was first reported by Muthupillai et al. in 1995 [7], is a technique used to evaluate tissue stiffness by analyzing the propagation of mechanical waves through tissues. In this technique, an active driver located outside the MR imaging room generates mechanical waves that are transmitted to the liver through the chest wall. A passive driver fixed to the chest wall transmits continuous acoustic vibrations to the liver. The shear-wave velocity is subsequently measured to calculate the quantitative stiffness values (µMRE) [8]. The shear-wave velocity correlates directly with tissue stiffness, allowing the generation of elastogram images and quantification of µMRE in kilopascals (kPa) (Fig. 1) [8]. In clinical practice, the main application of µMRE measurements is in the diagnosis and staging of liver fibrosis [8]. Meta-analyses have consistently reported that MRE shows high diagnostic accuracy in detecting liver fibrosis and classifying its stage [59]. Thus, MRE is widely recognized as the most accurate, non-invasive MR technique for evaluating liver fibrosis.

DWI is a technique based on visualization of the Brownian motion of water molecules within biological tissues (Fig. 2) [10]. Le Bihan et al. [1113] established the principles of DWI in the 1980s and subsequently proposed the intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) theory in 1986 to separate and evaluate the Brownian motion of water molecules from perfusion in microcapillaries [111213]. The DWI technique employs motion-probing gradients to visualize the microscopic movement of water molecules through changes in the signal [14]. Clinically, multiple b-values are used to acquire images and calculate the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) from the signal intensity decay [15]. The ADC value reflects the restriction of water-molecule movement due to microstructural organization and is influenced by the cell density, cell-extracellular matrix interactions, and capillary perfusion [16]. The clinical applications of DWI in the liver are multifaceted and primarily include detection and characterization of focal liver lesions (FLLs). DWI improves the conspicuities of FLLs, and malignant FLLs generally exhibit low ADC values, facilitating the distinction between benign and malignant FLLs [17]. In addition, multiple studies have reported that IVIM parameters and ADC values decrease with the progression of liver fibrosis [18]. However, the diagnostic accuracy of DWI/IVIM measurements for liver fibrosis staging is moderate and has been demonstrated to be inferior to that of MRE in terms of precision and variability [9].

TECHNICAL OVERVIEW OF VMRE

The concept of VMRE is based on the hypothesis that tissue elasticity and water diffusivity share common mechanical features at the microstructural level [6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that virtual stiffness (µdiff) can be estimated from DWI data by calibrating the shifted ADC (sADC), which is calculated from specific b-value combinations (b-values of 200 and 1,500 s/mm2), against µMRE (Fig. 1) [619]. Le Bihan et al. [6] and Kromrey et al. [19] demonstrated a strong correlation between µdiff measurements derived from DWI and µMRE derived from MRE, which reported good agreement in fibrosis staging.

The theoretical foundation of VMRE is based on the distinction between Gaussian and non-Gaussian diffusion [6]. In free (i.e., Gaussian) diffusion, water molecules move randomly without restriction, and the logarithm of the diffusion signal decays linearly with increasing b-values. However, in biological tissues (e.g., liver parenchyma), water molecules interact with microstructural elements, and the resultant restricted diffusion deviates from the Gaussian model, particularly at high b-values (Fig. 2) [1020]. This non-Gaussian diffusion component carries essential information regarding the tissue microstructure, including the density and arrangement of cellular and extracellular components, which also contribute to tissue stiffness (Fig. 3) [2122].

Conventional ADC calculations typically employ b-values ranging from 0 to 600–1,000 s/mm2, which predominantly reflect Gaussian diffusion and are substantially influenced by microcapillary perfusion (IVIM effect) at low b-values [2324]. To overcome these limitations and enhance sensitivity to tissue microstructures, VMRE utilizes sADC values calculated from specifically optimized key b-values of 200 and 1,500 s/mm2 [6]. The selection of b = 200 s/mm2 as the lower key b-value effectively excludes the confounding effects of capillary perfusion, which predominantly affects the signal at b-values below 200 s/mm2. Conversely, the higher key b-value of 1,500 s/mm2 maximizes the sensitivity to non-Gaussian diffusion effects, thereby capturing microstructural information correlated with tissue elastic properties (Fig. 3) [62526].

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS FOR CHRONIC LIVER DISEASE

Chronic Liver Disease

Le Bihan et al. [6] first explored whether DWI could provide quantitative estimates of liver tissue stiffness without requiring external mechanical vibrations in a chronic liver disease cohort. Their study included 15 patients with chronic liver disease (chronic hepatitis C [CHC]: 8, chronic hepatitis B [CHB]: 3, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [MASLD]: 2, and others: 2). All patients underwent MRE (60 Hz) and DWI (b = 0, 200, 400, 600, 1,000, 1,200, and 1,500 s/mm2). The normal ADC (b = 0 and 1,000 s/mm2) and µMRE (kPa) showed a correlation coefficient of 0.62, indicating relatively weak correlation. In contrast, sADC (b = 200 and 1,500 s/mm2) showed a stronger correlation coefficient of 0.90 with µMRE. Furthermore, among various b-value combinations, the combination of b = 200 and 1,500 s/mm2 showed the strongest correlation with µMRE. On the basis of these results, Le Bihan et al. [6] developed formulas for calculating µdiff from sADC.

µdiff (kPa) = -9.8 ln(S200/S1,500) + 14.0

µdiff (kPa) = -12,740 sADC (mm2/s) + 14.0

Using these formulas and previously reported cut-off values for liver fibrosis stages (F0–1: <2.5 kPa, F2: ≥2.5 kPa, F3: ≥3.0 kPa, and F4: ≥4.5 kPa) [27], µdiff could completely match the fibrosis stages determined by µMRE (Fig. 4).

Kromrey et al. [19] compared and evaluated the degree of agreement between µdiff and µMRE in a larger cohort with 74 patients (CHC: 41, CHB: 16, MASLD: 2, alcohol-related liver disease: 4, and others: 11). In their study, sADC and µMRE showed a strong correlation (correlation coefficient = 0.81). Although exact concordance between µdiff and µMRE in fibrosis staging was achieved in only approximately 55% of cases, agreement improved to approximately 85% when fibrosis was dichotomized into low-grade (F0–F1) and significant (F2–F4) categories, indicating that VMRE performs adequately at the clinically decisive threshold of F2. The area under the curve (AUC) for distinguishing fibrosis stages ≤F2 from ≥F3 by using µMRE values was 0.79, indicating good diagnostic performance. Furthermore, combining serum biochemical markers, such as the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) and fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, increased the AUC to 0.83. VMRE measurements showed a high correlation with MRE stiffness measurements, making them a promising alternative for non-invasive fibrosis staging.

Using histological fibrosis staging as the reference standard, Yang et al. [28] evaluated the diagnostic performance of µdiff in comparison with serum biochemical markers such as the APRI, FIB-4 index, fibrosis quotient, and the gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase to platelet ratio. Their study included a total of 145 patients with CHB. Among µdiff and serum markers, µdiff showed the strongest correlation with liver fibrosis stage (ρ = 0.52). The AUC for detecting ≥F3 fibrosis was 0.817 for µdiff alone. Combining µdiff with serum markers further improved the AUC. Thus, Yang et al. [28] concluded that VMRE may serve as an alternative for estimating the liver fibrosis stage in facilities without MRE equipment.

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease

Previously, the focus was on Asian cohorts, primarily consisting of patients with chronic viral liver diseases such as CHC and CHB. Hanniman et al. [29] compared VMRE corrected for the confounding effects of unsuppressed fat signals to MRE in 49 patients with MASLD. When the liver contains excessive fat, as in MASLD, residual fat signals cause ADC to appear lower, preventing an accurate reflection of true water diffusion. Therefore, the proton density fat fraction was used to calculate the fat-corrected ADC, which removed the influence of fat. Significant differences in µMRE were observed across fibrosis stages; however, fat-corrected sADC showed no significant differences across fibrosis stages. Furthermore, neither µdiff nor fat-corrected µdiff correlated with µMRE. Thus, Hanniman et al. [29] concluded that VMRE alone is difficult to recommend as an alternative for assessing fibrosis in MASLD. However, the significant difference in body habitus between the North American MASLD patient cohort and the Asian patient cohorts from previous studies may represent a technical limitation of DWI. Specifically, the North American MASLD cohort had a mean body weight of 96 kg, whereas the Asian cohort had a mean body weight of 58 kg, indicating a substantial difference in body habitus between the two populations [61929]. In patients with a larger body habitus, DWI quality is degraded by several factors, including incomplete shimming across the liver, suboptimal respiratory gating, and a lower signal-to-noise ratio. These issues represent technical limitations for the application of VMRE in patients with MASLD [30]. Moreover, errors may have occurred in the fat-correction method used in this study [30]. Volniansky et al. [31] prospectively compared VMRE and MRE in 43 patients with MASLD and 11 healthy volunteers. VMRE showed no association with liver fibrosis in the MASLD cohort and did not correlate with MRE results. Applying the fat correction proposed by Le Bihan et al. [30] did not improve the diagnostic performance. VMRE was suggested to be susceptible to the effects of steatosis. Thus, the utility of VMRE in patients with MASLD remains debatable. However, a recent study by Yuan et al. [32] provided new insights into the application of VMRE in MASLD: The authors enrolled 463 patients with MASLD and developed a novel fat-correction method for sADC based on linear regression modeling in a training cohort of 361 patients. In a validation cohort of 102 patients with biopsy confirmation, fat-corrected VMRE achieved an AUC of 0.848 for the detection of significant fibrosis (≥F2), demonstrating diagnostic performance comparable to that of MRE. Notably, among the various b-value combinations evaluated, the combination of b = 200 and 1,200 s/mm2 was identified as the most optimal for sADC calculation in the MASLD cohort, which differs from the b-value combinations previously reported for chronic viral liver diseases. These findings suggest that with appropriate fat correction and optimization of b-values tailored to MASLD, VMRE may serve as a clinically viable alternative to MRE for assessing liver fibrosis in patients with MASLD.

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS FOR FLLs

In clinical practice, benign FLLs such as hemangiomas can be distinguished from malignant FLLs using DWI and ADC maps [33]. Moreover, most malignant FLLs can be distinguished using conventional liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, without the use of MRE [34]. Notably, the role of MRE in FLL characterization has not been firmly established; the available evidence is largely limited to small studies, and MRE is not currently incorporated into standard diagnostic algorithms for FLLs. Accordingly, the use of VMRE for characterization of FLLs should be regarded as an exploratory approach. Against this background, a limited number of studies have examined whether stiffness-related information can provide supplementary value when conventional imaging yields equivocal findings [35].

Ota et al. [26] evaluated the usefulness of VMRE in characterizing FLLs beyond assessing liver fibrosis. In their study, 54 patients with 56 tumors (31 hepatocellular carcinomas [HCCs] and 25 metastatic liver cancers [MLCs]) were enrolled, and a linear discriminant model combining VMRE and MRE was constructed to distinguish HCC from MLC. For HCC, sADC and µMRE showed a moderate correlation (r = 0.68), whereas for MLC, sADC and µMRE showed a strong correlation (r = 0.71). To distinguish HCC from MLC, a predictive equation (Classifier: C) was constructed using discriminant analysis with parameters derived from the sADC and µMRE.

C = -25.15 × sADC (10-3 mm2/s) -1.72 × µMRE (kPa) + 36.46

Using this formula, HCCs were distinguished from MLCs with high diagnostic capability (AUC = 0.96). By setting the cut-off value of the formula to zero, HCCs and MLCs could be distinguished with 100% sensitivity, 92% specificity, and 96.4% accuracy (Figs. 7, 8).

Notably, however, this model combined VMRE with MRE rather than employing VMRE as a standalone alternative. In addition, this study did not assess the added value of VMRE over standard liver MRI. Furthermore, differential diagnosis of MLC and intrahepatic cholangiocellular carcinoma (ICC), which is problematic because of the similar imaging characteristics of these lesions, was beyond the scope of this study [36]. Therefore, large-scale studies that include cases of ICC and directly compare VMRE with conventional liver MRI are required before the clinical role of VMRE in FLL characterization can be defined.

CONCLUSIONS

VMRE has the potential to serve as an alternative to MRE for non-invasive assessment of liver parenchymal stiffness, particularly in settings where MRE hardware is unavailable or an abbreviated MRI protocol is preferred. However, VMRE has several limitations that require consideration. Since VMRE assessments have still not achieved complete concordance with MRE findings in fibrosis staging, they should be interpreted with caution in individual cases. This is especially relevant for patients with MASLD, since sADC values in these patients are substantially influenced by hepatic steatosis and require refined fat-correction algorithms. Recent evidence suggests that with an optimized fat-correction method and the use of b-values tailored to MASLD, VMRE can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to that of MRE. Thus, disease-specific protocol optimization is essential for successful application of VMRE in MASLD. The clinical application of VMRE also requires high-quality baseline DWI data, necessitating strategies to mitigate motion and susceptibility artifacts, as well as MRI systems equipped with high-performance gradients capable of acquiring DWI data at a b-value of 1,500 s/mm2. Finally, the existing evidence is largely derived from single-center studies with small cohorts, and prospective multicenter validation across diverse populations and scanner platforms is essential to firmly establish the clinical role of VMRE.
