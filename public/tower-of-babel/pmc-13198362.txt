Effects of post-farrowing feed delivery schedule and sow parity on piglet performance and survivability1
Authors: Aryeri S Bardales-Castellanos, Andres F Bolivar-Sierra, Hyatt Frobose, Casey Bradley, Crystal L Levesque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-wean mortality (PWM) in commercial pig herds typically ranges from 10% to 20%, with most deaths occurring within 72 hours of birth. Crushing by the sow is a primary cause of piglet deaths. Early in lactation, piglets spend more time near the sow for warmth which is also the period of sow recovery from farrowing, which may increase the risk of crushing. Consequently, sow feed delivery schedule (ie number of feed deliveries per day) during this period may influence piglet survival by altering sow activity patterns and slowing recovery from farrowing. This study evaluated the effects of post-farrow feed delivery schedule and sow parity on sow lactation performance and piglet PWM. A total of 261 sows (Parity 1 to 9) in 8 rooms of approximately 32 sows/room) were assigned to one of three lactation feed delivery schedules beginning at farrowing: six meals per day (Feed-6), two meals per day (Feed-2), or two meals per day for the first five days post-farrow followed by six meals per day until weaning (Feed-2/6). Sow and litter performance to wean were recorded, as well as sow pre-farrow hemoglobin concentration and piglet mortality causes. Data was analyzed using SAS (Version 9.4) in a randomized incomplete block design with farrowing room as the blocking factor and sows as the experimental unit. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using PROC MIXED with feed delivery schedule, parity group and their interaction as fixed effects, and farrowing room included as a random effect. Outcomes that did not meet distributional assumptions, including feeder bowl coverage score, were analyzed using PROC NPAR1WAY. Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05. There was no interaction between feed delivery schedule and parity. Total lactation feed intake was greater (P < 0.04) in Feed-2 sows than in Feed-6 and Feed-2/6 sows. Litter performance did not differ among feed delivery schedules; litter size at wean, wean weight, average daily gain, and PWM were comparable across all groups. In contrast, sow parity had a marked effect; older parity sows (parity 4+) had greater total feed intake (P < 0.03) than parity 1 sows and higher PWM due to crushing (P = 0.003) and stillborn piglets (P < 0.001) compared to parity 1, 2 and 3 sows. Pre-farrow hemoglobin concentration was lower (P < 0.05) in 3 and 5+ parity sows than in young sows. These findings indicate that sow age/parity, rather than post-farrowing feed delivery schedule, was associated with piglet mortality outcomes and highlights the importance of incorporating sow parity into management strategies to improve piglet survival rates.

Introduction

Pre-wean mortality (PWM) remains a major challenge in pig production and has been reported to range from 5 to 35%, representing a significant impact on profitability and herd efficiency (Edwards and Baxter 2015; Mainau et al. 2015). Piglets are most susceptible to death during the first 5 days of life, particularly within the first 72 hours after birth (Nicolaisen et al. 2019). Advancements in genetics and management practices have increased litter size, but challenges to piglet survival to weaning have also increased (Knap et al. 2023). Several factors contribute to PWM, including sow and piglet health, management practices, and the environmental conditions (Romero et al. 2022). The early postpartum period (first 5 days) is specifically critical for piglet survival because they spend substantial time in close proximity to the sow while nursing and to maintain body temperature, even when supplemental heat is provided (Nicolaisen et al. 2019). Nicolaisen et al. (2019) evaluated the behavior of sows and their litters in different farrowing spaces (ie single pen vs. group housing). Regardless of housing style, in the first 3 days after farrowing, sows were noted to change body posture 2 to 3 times per hour and to spend less than 5% of the day in a standing position. Dourmad (1993) also notes that the daily number of standing events and the duration of each standing event was lower in the first week of lactation compared to the following 3 weeks. The reduced activity and extended time lying immediately after farrowing may represent a period of sow recovery from farrowing. Wischner et al. (2009) reported a positive relationship between the activity events of sows in the first 24 h after farrowing and the number of crushed piglets. Alonso-Spilsbury et al. (2007) state that crushing by the sow is a leading cause of PWM.

Adequate sow nutrient intake and piglet protection during lactation are important to overall farm productivity; sows must meet their own nutritional demands while ensuring adequate milk production to nourish their growing litter (Van den Bosch et al. 2023). To support sufficient sow nutrient intake, current commercial practices employ various sow feeding strategies during the transition period from gestation to farrowing and lactation. These include set amounts in the morning, multiple meals throughout the day, or ad libitum feeding (Neil 1996; Gourley et al. 2020) that can be accomplished using electronic sow feeding systems that provide flexibility for feed delivery strategy to the sow (Aparicio et al. 2024) and to stimulate sow feed intake. Lactation sow feed intake is directed related to litter growth rate (Hawe et al. 2020). Research suggests more frequent feeding before farrowing improves piglet survival compared to restricted feeding schedules (Gourley et al. 2020). However, there is limited information on how the frequency of post-farrow feed delivery schedule influences piglet survival, particularly in the first five days postpartum. Feed delivery is a routine management event that can increase sow activity around the feeder and within the stall area, and it may also influence how frequently sows change position during early lactation. It was hypothesized that reducing the number of daily feed deliveries during the first 5 days after farrowing would be associated with improved piglet survival through weaning, without negatively affecting sow lactation performance.

In addition to feeding management, sow parity is associated with variation in sow condition and piglet outcomes across commercial herds. In an extensive review, Corrales-Hernández et al. (2025) report that peak sow reproductive performance (based on litter growth, colostrum production, return to estrus, among others) occurs in parity 2 to 3. Recently, sow blood hemoglobin has been proposed as an indicator of sow health where pre-farrow hemoglobin (<10 g/dL) was associated with increased stillborns and sow removal from the herd (McClellan et al. 2024a). Numerous others have reported that sow hemoglobin status declines with advancing parity (Castevens et al. 2020; McClellan et al. 2024b) and thus hemoglobin level before farrowing may be relevant to subsequent litter outcomes.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of post-farrow feed delivery schedule and sow parity on sow lactation performance, litter growth, and piglet PWM in a commercial setting.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted at the Rolling Meadows commercial sow farm in Jones, Michigan. The protocol for this experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at South Dakota State University (IACUC # 2104-020A).

Study location, housing, and environment

The experiment was conducted at a commercial sow farm in Jones, Michigan, from September to October 2022. Lactating females were housed in individual farrowing stalls across eight mechanically ventilated rooms. Target room temperature set points were 18 to 20°C. A piglet microenvironment was achieved using supplemental heat via heat lamps and heat mats set at 35 to 32°C. Room temperature and ventilation were monitored using daily and adjusted by barn staff as needed. Water was available ad libitum. Each stall included a shelf feeder with a hopper and a nipple waterer.

Animals and experimental design

A total of 261 sows (parity 1 to 9; Camborough L1050, PIC, Hendersonville, TN) across 8 farrowing rooms with approximately 32 sows/room and 4,200 piglets (Camborough L1050 x Compart Duroc) were used in a randomized incomplete block design across eight farrow rooms (blocks) of the sow farm. At day 113 ± 2 of gestation, females were measured for backfat and then assigned to a parity group (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5+) within room and allotted to one of 3 lactation feed delivery schedules of 6 daily allotments (Feed-6), feed in 2 daily allotments (Feed-2), and feed in 2 daily allotments up to day 5 after farrowing then 6 daily allotments until wean (Feed-2/6) (Table 1). Daily feed allotment was distributed across each feed delivery as outlined in Table 1. All sows received a lactation diet formulated to meet or exceed nutritional requirements based on expected litter size and historical sow lactation feed intake (NRC 2012; Table 2). Sows were placed on the respective feed schedule the morning after farrowing was completed (post-farrowing day 1; Fig. 1). Feed samples were collected routinely throughout the trial from a subsample of feeders in each room. A subsample of the composite feed sample was sent for proximate analysis to a commercial analytical lab (University of Missouri Experiment Station Chemical Laboratory, Columbia, Missouri). Feed allowance followed a ramp-up curve beginning with the standard post-farrowing day 1 allotment (5 kg) and increasing incrementally daily based on feed pan coverage (e.g., bowl score of less than 2 for 2 consecutive days) until reaching the target maximum daily allowance on day 5 of lactation, based on historical barn intake curves. Total feed intake was the total feed delivered in lactation for each sow based on records from the electronic feed system (Gestal®, Jyga Technologies, Inc. St. Lambert, Qc, Canada).

Feeding system, feed delivery measurement, and calibration placeholder

Feed was delivered using an automated electronic feed system (Gestal Quattro®, Jyga Technologies, Inc., St. Lambert, Quebec, Canada) that records feed delivered according to programmed feed delivery events. Total feed intake for each sow was calculated as the total feed delivered during lactation based on records from the electronic feeding system. Feed delivered is reported as feed delivered or feed disappearance because feed refusals were not directly measured. The feeders dispensed feed volumetrically and were calibrated to convert volumetric delivery to grams of feed delivered. Calibration was performed on at least one feeder per diet used. During calibration, the person calibrating entered the calibration data into the feeder software, which generated a ratio linking the feeder revolution counter to feed output, expressed as grams of feed dispensed per degree of auger revolution. This calibration approach typically results in an absolute error of less than 2%. Feeders were calibrated weekly during the trial.

A feeder bowl coverage score was assigned once daily throughout lactation by trained research staff (Fig. 2). Scoring was performed at the same time for each day for all sows to standardize the assessment relative to the daily feed delivery schedule. Bowl coverage was visually estimated as the proportion of the feeder bowl surface covered with feed and scored on a 1 to 5 scale, where score 1: indicated approximately 0% - 10% of the bowl coverage, score 2: 10–29% coverage, 3 indicated 30–49% coverage, score 4: 50–69% coverage, and score 5 indicated 70–100% bowl coverage. A target bowl coverage score of 2 was used as an operational threshold consistent with standard farm practice to maintain feed availability while minimizing accumulation of residual feed. Bowl coverage was used as an indicator of residual feed in the bowl and was not intended to quantify feed refusals or feed wastage.

Sow and piglet performance

Sow backfat thickness was measured on day 113 ± 2 of gestation and on the day of weaning (day 21 ± 1 of lactation) at the 10th rib approximately 10 cm from the dorsal midline on the right side using a dorsal fat meter (Renco Lean Meter digital back fat indicator, Renco Corporation, Minneapolis, Minnesota). Backfat change during lactation was calculated as the difference between the pre-farrowing and weaning measurements. Hemoglobin concentration was measured on day 113 ± 2 of gestation as a prefarrow enrollment descriptor using a small drop of blood collected from an ear vein with a 20 ga needle and ear prick, then read on a HemoCue Hb 201+ analyzer (HemoCue America, Brea, California; McClellan et al. 2024c).

Complete litters were weighed within 24 h of birth and the day before weaning. Pig mortality and removal were recorded, and the cause of death determined by examination of external lesions according to the methodology of Kielland et al. (2018). Piglets were classified as laid on (crushing by the sow; presence of fractures of major bones such as the spine, lacerations, or extensive bruising), low viability (low body weight, weakness, starvation, or lethargy), euthanized due to compromised health status (evidence consistent with abdominal rupture or herniation), rupture (evidence of abdominal rupture or hernia based on external examination) or other (deaths that did not fit the predefined categories or could not be confidently classified). Piglets meeting predefined welfare criteria were humanely euthanized by trained farm personnel in accordance with the “American Association of Swine Veterinarians and National Pork Board On-farm Euthanasia of Swine: Recommendations for the Producer” (2016). Euthanasia was performed using manual blunt force trauma and was applied only to piglets with no reasonable chance of recovery. Criteria included piglets with no prospect for improvement or those that failed to respond to intensive care after 2 days unless a veterinarian advised otherwise, and severely injured or non-ambulatory piglets. A piglet was considered non-ambulatory if it could not rise, or if it could stand only with support but was unable to bear weight on 2 legs (Fig. 3). All piglets received 200 mg iron (GleptoForte™ Injection, Ceva Santé Animale S.A., Libourne, France) in conjunction with piglet processing (ie tail docking and castration) which occurred on a single day for each room after farrowing was complete for all sows in a given room. As such, age of litters at processing ranged from 2 to 5 days.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (Version 9.4, SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC). Sow BW, lactation length, total and average daily feed delivered, sow backfat, prefarrow Hb, litter characteristics at birth, PWM (% of born alive), and dead category (% of total dead) were analyzed using a randomized incomplete block design with sow as the experimental unit and room as the incomplete block. Distributional properties of continuous outcomes were evaluated using PROC UNIVARIATE and by examining residual diagnostics from fitted models. When outcomes met distributional assumptions, data were analyzed using PROC MIXED. Fixed effects included feed delivery schedule (Feed-6, Feed-2, and Feed-2/6), parity group, and the feed delivery schedule × parity group interaction. Farrowing room was included as a random effect. Lactation length was used as a covariate in the model to evaluate litter wean weight. Least squares means were generated and separated using the Tukey adjustment when the overall fixed effect was significant.

Feeder bowl coverage score was analyzed as an ordinal outcome. Daily bowl coverage scores were compared among feed delivery schedules within each lactation day using PROC NPAR1WAY, with feed delivery schedule as the classification variable and exact tests requested when applicable. For dead to day 5, total dead, and mortality category, count data was analyzed using a nonparametric test (Pearson Chi-square, PROC NPAR1WAY). Values of P or χ2 ≤ 0.05 were considered significant, and trends were noted from 0.05 to 0.1.

Results

There was no interaction between sow feed delivery schedule and parity; therefore, all data presented in tables and described below are specific to the main effects of feed delivery and parity. A total of 8 sows were removed from data analysis due to poor farrowing outcomes (eg prolapse, total born less than 10, stillborn percentage > 40%).

Sow growth and lactation feed intake

Total feed intake and daily feed intake were affected by the feed delivery schedule (P = 0.007), where sows under the Feed-2 schedule had greater total feed intake compared to sows on the Feed-2/6 and Feed-6 schedule (Table 3). As a result, daily feed intake was also greatest (P = 0.005) in Feed-2 sows compared to all other groups. Pre-farrow sow backfat was similar across feed delivery schedules. At weaning, sows in Feed-6 had less BF thickness than those in the other feed delivery groups (P = 0.004). Pre-farrow blood Hb taken at sow enrollment tended to be greater (P = 0.070) in Feed-2 and Feed-2/6 assigned sows than those in Feed-6.

Piglet mortality by category

Sow feed delivery schedule had no influence on the number of piglets in each death category (Table 7). Similarly, the percentage of total dead pigs for each category was not impacted by sow feed delivery schedule. Laid on piglets represented greater than 55% of all mortalities. Parity significantly influenced piglet deaths by crushing (Table 8) based on count (χ2 < 0.001) and percentage of total deads where parity 5+ sows (P = 0.033) had greater percentage laid on piglets compared to parity 1 sows with parity 2, 3 and 4 intermediates. Parity did not influence other piglet death reasons.

Discussion

Prewean mortality serves as a valuable metric for the overall efficiency of pig production and is closely linked to animal welfare and economics of pig production (Muns et al. 2015). Despite ongoing efforts, the issue of PWM remains a persistent challenge. Selection for increased productivity has contributed to larger litters, and these changes have inadvertently increased the proportions of piglets at risk of illness or death in early life, such as piglets < 1 kg (Feldpausch et al. 2019). Recognition of this issue has spurred research focused on strategies to improve survival rates of piglets (Tucker et al. 2021). The current study evaluated whether the frequency of feed delivery events to lactating sows during the first five days following parturition, together with parity, was associated with sow lactation performance, piglet growth and PWM.

Overall, sow daily feed delivery schedule had no impact on piglet survivability or litter growth. Despite the 500–600 g/day more daily feed delivery in Feed-2 sows, all sows had average daily feed delivery (8.2 ± 0.35 kg) that was above the 6.0 kg/d target suggested for optimal reproductive outcomes by Cabezón et al. (2017) and Seo et al. (2023). The average daily feed delivered in this work was at least 30% above that target and suggests total sow nutrient intake across all feed delivery schedules did not limit piglet growth potential and thus did not increase risk of weak piglets leading to PWM. However, Hawe et al. (2020) evaluated the influence of sow lactation feed allowance (up to 7 kg/d vs up to 11 kg/d) on growth and suckling behavior of piglets < 1.7 kg at birth and reported no impact of sow feed allowance on PWM. The greater feed delivery in Feed-2 sows can, in part, explain the lesser back fat loss and greater backfat at weaning in this group. Lactation feed intake is directly related to litter growth (Schinkel et al. 2010; Cabezón et al. 2017) and litter growth There are few studies evaluating the influence of feed delivery schedule in the early lactation period; although Ampode et al. (2024) reported sows subjected to a feeding regimen of five times a day, exhibited a significantly higher reduction of back fat thickness at weaning than sows delivered feed hourly which is similar to the pattern observed for Feed-6 sows in this work.

The feed bowl score is a crude method to estimate left over feed and in the context of this study was the option available thus there was cautious interpretation of feed bowl coverage. The greater feed delivery in Feed-2 sows may in part be explained by the higher number of days that their bowl score was 3 or greater (7 of 19 days) compared to Feed-6, which had a bowl score of 2 or less for 13 of 19 days, and Feed-2/6 sows (8 of 19 days). The bowl score trends reported herein may suggest a relationship between feed intake patterns and daily feed delivery schedule where lesser daily feed deliveries may increase feed wastage or leftover feed in the feeder. The consistency of bowl scores across feed delivery schedules in week 3 indicates that feed intake normalized as lactation progressed and that, as feed allotment increases later in lactation, bowl scores (i.e., leftover feed) also increase.

Sow parity had a greater influence on PWM than the sow lactation feed delivery schedule in this study. Older parity sows have been associated with a number of physical characteristics that may in part explain this greater PWM. Sows with higher parity numbers tend to have larger litters thus more piglets compete for resources like milk and space and weaker piglets might be more susceptible to death (Kielland et al. 2018; Ju et al. 2021). Older sows are reported to have reduced mobility or stamina, frequently exhibit movement disorders and reduced physical condition, impacting their maternal behaviors making it harder to properly care for and nurse all piglets in litters of more than 14 piglets (Baxter et al. 2013). This reduced stamina may be related to muscle weakness due to aging making it difficult for older sows to maneuver around the piglets. Reduced muscle stamina may lead to accidental crushing or inability to reach all piglets for nursing (Barnett et al. 2001; Baxter et al. 2023). Chronic foot or leg issues are common in older breeding sows which can also contribute to reduced stamina (Supakorn et al. 2018; Henningsen et al. 2024). Udder health can deteriorate with age. For example, mastitis or other udder problems might reduce milk production or quality, impacting piglet growth and survival (Sărăndan et al. 2009). In the context of this study, there was no difference in litter size at birth or weaning indicating that differences in PWM cannot be explained by litter size. The lower total litter and individual piglet gain in the older sows indicates milk output may have been reduced in these sows which may have contributed to weaker piglets and hence piglet mortality. This likely lesser milk output was in spite of greater feed intake in older parity sows compared to the young sows. The increase in total and daily feed intake with parity are in line with Gianluppi et al. (2021) and Estrada et al. (2024). There is a negative relationship between sow lactation feed intake and backfat and BW loss of sows (Eissen et al. 2003). Considering the relatively low backfat loss and lower litter ADG, the greater nutrient intake in older sows was most likely directed to maternal body stores rather than milk production.

Differences in sow Hb status prior to farrowing may contribute to parity associated differences in piglet outcomes, particularly stillborn incidence. Sow Hb status declined with parity and was lowest in parity 4+ sows at <10 g/dL, a common cutoff for anemia (Bhattarai et al. 2019; McClellan et al. 2024a). The decline with age is consistent with a number of other studies (Castevens et al. 2020; Boulbria et al. 2021; McClellan et al. 2024b). Low Hb status has been associated with increased risk of stillborn piglets (Bhattarai et al. 2019; Schoos et al. 2023; McClellan et al. 2024b) which is consistent with the greater stillborn piglets in older parity sows in this work. To our knowledge this is the first study linking anemic older parity sows with higher incidences of crushing and may be related to anemic sows being more lethargic and fatigued, a known outcome of anemia. In humans, anemia leads to lethargy due to insufficient oxygen delivery to tissues, causing fatigue and weakness, particularly in women (Handelman and Levin 2008). Because hemoglobin was measured prior to implementation of feeding schedule treatments in the present study, these findings should be interpreted as baseline associations with parity rather than treatment effects.

Overall, reduced stamina and lower baseline Hb in older sows provides plausible explanations for the greater stillborn incidence and greater mortality observed with increasing parity particularly combined with the lack of difference in litter size across parity groups. Perinatal asphyxia is the most common cause of stillbirth and diminished viability and liveliness, both of which increase the risk of postnatal death. Dystocia is also recognized as a contributing factor to stillbirth and can result in an increased incidence of piglets with impaired viability, rendering them more susceptible to crushing or death by starvation (Hoai Nam and Sukon 2022). While perinatal asphyxia and dystocia are important factors related to stillborn and weak piglets, these variables are not specific to older parity sows. Another potential variable that contributes to mothering ability in older sows is the stress of repeated breeding cycles or negative experiences in previous farrowing that can lead to aggressive or neglectful behavior in older sows (Verdon et al. 2016; Brajon et al. 2021). This suggests that strategies aimed at improving muscle strength, health, and overall robustness or stamina of older sows could play a key role in lowering PWM.

In conclusion, the present study indicates that parity played a more significant role in piglet PWM than the frequency of the feed delivery schedule. In particular, the higher PWM due to crushing during the initial stages of lactation in parity 5+ sows compared to younger sows highlights the critical influence of sow age. Comprehensive strategies to enhance care and attention to piglets born to older sows (ie parity 5+) with large litters (ie 15+ piglets) may help avert mortality resulting from crushing, trauma, and low viability.
