Comparative effectiveness of ivermectin versus permethrin for the treatment of scabies: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: David Chinaecherem Innocent, Rejoicing Chijindum Innocent, Precious Ebube Anyakorah, Promise Somtochukwu Chukwuemeka, Chiamaka Eugene, Juliet Chinaza Anuwe, Olivia Udoka Udoh, Boris Chichebem Nwachukwu, Sophia Ifechidere Obani, Ikechukwu Nosike Simplicius Dozie, Uchechukwu Madukaku Chukwuocha, Dickson Ifeanyi Okwara, Jude Eguolo Moroh, Tamunowengifiri Charles George, Allen-Adebayo Blessing, Jaiyeoba-Ojigho Jennifer Efe

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScabies is a highly contagious parasitic skin disease and a recognised neglected tropical disease with substantial global burden. Permethrin and ivermectin are widely used treatments, yet uncertainty remains regarding their comparative effectiveness.AimTo systematically evaluate and compare the effectiveness of ivermectin versus permethrin for the treatment of scabies in humans.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Studies comparing ivermectin with permethrin were identified through searches of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Risk of bias was assessed using the updated Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2). A random-effects meta-analysis was performed using RevMan version 5.4.1 Only peer-reviewed RCTs involving human participants with clinically diagnosed scabies were included.ResultsSeven studies involving 1,216 participants were included. Compared with permethrin, ivermectin was associated with a lower probability of clinical cure at final follow-up (RR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.86–0.99; I² = 61%) and a higher risk of treatment failure (RR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.06–2.20; I² = 0%).ConclusionPermethrin demonstrates a modest advantage over ivermectin in achieving clinical cure and reducing treatment failure. Ivermectin remains a useful alternative where topical therapy is unsuitable, but optimised dosing regimens are essential.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Evidence report that scabies remains a highly contagious ectoparasitic skin disease caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, characterized by intense pruritus and a rash that often leads to secondary bacterial infections and significant impairment in quality of life [1]. Globally, scabies affects an estimated 130 million people at any one time, with higher prevalence observed in resource-limited settings, overcrowded environments, and tropical climates [2]. Recognizing its public health burden, the World Health Organization (WHO) added scabies to its list of neglected tropical diseases, emphasizing the need for effective control and management strategies [2].

The primary goal of scabies treatment is complete eradication of the mite to relieve symptoms, prevent transmission, and reduce complications and this is achieved through acaricidal agents known as scabicides, which exert parasiticidal effects by disrupting critical biological functions of the mite [3]. Two widely used scabicides include permethrin, a topical synthetic pyrethroid that interferes with sodium channels leading to arthropod paralysis, and ivermectin, a macrocyclic lactone that enhances inhibitory neurotransmission in invertebrate nerve and muscle cells [3]. Permethrin 5% cream is recommended as a first-line topical therapy globally and is typically applied once, with a potential repeat application after one week [3]. Ivermectin, commonly administered orally at a dose of 200 µg/kg, may require repeated dosing to cover the full mite lifecycle and is especially useful in mass drug administration settings or when topical application is impractical [3].

Despite their widespread use, the comparative effectiveness of these agents remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Early randomized control trials (RCTs) have indicated that a single application of permethrin may yield superior early cure rates compared to a single dose of oral ivermectin, though repeated ivermectin dosing approaches parity with permethrin effectiveness [4]. In the trial by Usha [4], permethrin achieved near-complete cure at the primary follow-up, while a second dose of ivermectin significantly increased its effectiveness. Similarly, Ranjkesh et al. [5] reported that two doses of ivermectin achieved comparable outcomes to permethrin in treating uncomplicated scabies. Other comparative studies have demonstrated no significant difference between topical permethrin and oral ivermectin when assessed at specified follow-up intervals, suggesting similar overall effectiveness [6].

Systematic evidence syntheses also reveal mixed findings with high-quality systematic reviews, including a Cochrane review, found little to no difference in efficacy between permethrin and systemic or topical ivermectin, although the certainty of evidence was judged to be low to moderate due to limited and heterogeneous trial data [7]. Another recent systematic effort demonstrated that repeated doses of ivermectin may lower treatment failure compared with single doses, underscoring the need to consider dosing regimens in comparative evaluations [2]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that topical ivermectin formulations, such as 1% cream, may offer advantages in specific populations like pediatric patients, further complicating direct comparisons [8].

Despite extensive clinical use and several RCTs, existing literature is limited by variability in study designs, treatment regimens, follow-up durations, and outcome definitions, which impairs precise comparison and evidence synthesis. Unlike the 2018 Cochrane review, which included older trials with heterogeneous methodologies and outdated treatment protocols, our analysis prioritises contemporary evidence reflective of current clinical practice, including updated ivermectin dosing strategies and topical formulations. This approach aligns with recommendations for evidence updates that emphasise temporal relevance alongside methodological qualityFurthermore, most previous meta-analyses have not fully explored subgroup effects such as topical versus systemic administration, age-specific responses, or implications of repeated dosing. Therefore, a rigorous systematic review and meta-analysis focusing specifically on comparative effectiveness of ivermectin versus permethrin across clinical trials is warranted. The aim of this updated systematic review and meta-analysis is to systematically evaluate and quantitatively synthesize evidence on the comparative effectiveness of ivermectin and permethrin for the treatment of scabies in humans.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251274906. This meta-analysis followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA) [9].

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted to identify relevant primary studies comparing the effectiveness of ivermectin and permethrin for the treatment of scabies in humans (Table 1). Seven electronic databases were searched from inception to the most recent update prior to manuscript submission: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov. These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of biomedical, clinical, and public health literature [10].

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) and free-text keywords related to scabies, ivermectin, and permethrin. Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”) were applied to maximise sensitivity while maintaining specificity. Truncation and phrase searching were used where appropriate. In addition, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible articles that may not have been captured in the electronic search [11]. No date restrictions were applied during the search process.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were formulated using the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, comparison(s), Outcome), which is recommended for effectiveness-focused systematic reviews [10].

Population: Human participants of any age diagnosed clinically or parasitologically with scabies.

Intervention: Ivermectin administered orally or topically at any dose or regimen.

Comparison(s): Topical permethrin at any concentration or application schedule.

Outcomes: Primary outcomes included clinical cure or treatment success at follow-up. Secondary outcomes included symptom resolution, recurrence, and adverse events.

Study design: RCTs and prospective comparative studies.

Studies were excluded if they were non-comparative, case report, editorials, conference abstracts without full data, published before 2010, animal studies, or mass drug administration studies without a direct comparison arm. Only peer-reviewed studies published in English were included to ensure methodological robustness and interpretability. Reviews and meta-analyses were excluded but screened for relevant primary studies.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into Zotero reference management software, where automatic and manual de-duplication was performed. De-duplicated records were then exported into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for screening. Title and abstract screening were conducted systematically by the authors to identify potentially eligible studies, followed by full-text review against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study selection process was conducted with methodological rigour, and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. The entire screening and selection process was recorded and summarised using a PRISMA flow diagram, in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines [9].

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out by all the authors using a pre-piloted Microsoft Excel data extraction form after eligible studies were transferred from Zotero. The following variables were extracted: author(s), year of publication, country, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, ivermectin regimen, permethrin regimen, follow-up duration, outcome definitions, cure rates, recurrence rates, and reported adverse events. Where required, corresponding authors were consulted for clarification of missing or unclear data.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias of the included RCTs were assessed by all the authors using the updated Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [12]. This tool evaluates bias across five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomisation process, (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias, leading to an overall judgement for each study.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A quantitative synthesis was performed using a random-effects meta-analysis, recognising the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, particularly in dosing regimens, follow-up periods, and study populations. Effect estimates for dichotomous outcomes were pooled using risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [13]. The results were represented in Forest Plots showing the pooled estimates. In the included trials where more than two arms were present, only the arms directly relevant to the comparison of interest (ivermectin versus permethrin) were included in the quantitative synthesis. Where multiple ivermectin arms were reported (e.g., oral and topical ivermectin), the ivermectin arms were pooled into a single intervention group following the guidance provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [12]. This approach avoids double-counting of the comparator group and preserves the independence of observations in the meta-analysis.

Results

Overview of the search process

Following a comprehensive search across multiple electronic databases, a total of 1,783 records were initially identified. After removal of 230 duplicate records, 1,553 unique studies remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 1,127 records were excluded for irrelevance. Subsequently, 426 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, leading to the exclusion of 418 studies due to reasons including not addressing scabies, lack of comparative design, absence of the interventions of interest, being systematic reviews, or non-English publication. Ultimately, 7 studies met the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in both the systematic review and meta-analysis [5, 14–19]. Clinical cure at final follow-up

Evidence from Fig. 2 below presents the forest plot of the random-effects meta-analysis evaluating clinical cure at final follow-up among participants treated with ivermectin compared with permethrin. Seven studies contributed data to this outcome, comprising 606 participants in the ivermectin group and 610 in the permethrin group [5, 14–19]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the probability of clinical cure with ivermectin compared with permethrin (RR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, p = 0.03). This indicates that, at final follow-up, participants treated with ivermectin were approximately 7% less likely to achieve clinical cure compared with those receiving permethrin.

Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 61%, χ² = 15.20, p = 0.02), suggesting meaningful variability in effect estimates across studies, likely attributable to differences in dosing regimens, formulations (oral versus topical ivermectin), and follow-up duration. Despite this heterogeneity, the overall effect remained statistically significant, favouring permethrin. These findings indicate that permethrin may provide a modest but clinically relevant advantage over ivermectin in achieving clinical cure of scabies at final follow-up (Fig. 2).

Treatment failure / non-cure

Figure 3 illustrates the random-effects meta-analysis assessing treatment failure (non-cure) among participants treated with ivermectin compared with permethrin. Five studies contributed data to this outcome, including 468 participants in the ivermectin group and 471 in the permethrin group [5, 14, 16–18]. The pooled analysis showed a significantly higher risk of treatment failure with ivermectin compared with permethrin (risk ratio = 1.52, 95% CI 1.06–2.20, p = 0.02). This finding indicates that participants receiving ivermectin were approximately 52% more likely to experience non-cure at follow-up than those treated with permethrin, favouring permethrin for this outcome. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0%, χ² = 0.27, p = 0.99), suggesting a high level of consistency across the included studies. The lack of heterogeneity strengthens confidence in the pooled estimate and implies that the observed effect is robust across different study settings and populations. Overall, these results support permethrin as a more reliable option than ivermectin in reducing treatment failure in scabies management (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The main purpose of this meta-analysis was to synthesise evidence comparing the effectiveness of ivermectin and permethrin for the treatment of human scabies, focusing on clinical cure and treatment failure. Our findings must be interpreted alongside the broader literature, including previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses, to understand how they align with or diverge from the existing body of evidence. A Cochrane review of ivermectin and permethrin for treating scabies, which included a larger set of trials, reported no substantial difference in the efficacy of topical permethrin compared to systemic or topical ivermectin across multiple contexts and time points of follow-up, although evidence certainty was mostly low to moderate due to poor reporting and methodological limitations in many studies [1]. This aligns with several of our findings suggesting that while permethrin may have a modest early advantage in some settings, both agents are broadly effective for parasite elimination and symptom resolution. Specifically, the Cochrane review found that one dose of systemic ivermectin and one application of permethrin lotion led to similar cure rates by week two and by four weeks, supporting the concept that differences between the agents diminish over time [1]. The moderate-certainty evidence from that review that topical ivermectin and permethrin creams yield similar clearance rates at four weeks is consistent with our analysis showing comparable clinical effectiveness across several RCTs.

Other narrative reviews and comparative studies echo this theme as Dressler’s systematic review of scabies treatments reported that topical permethrin and systemic ivermectin did not differ substantially in efficacy, although individual trials varied in outcome definitions, dosing regimens, and population structures [20]. This ‘no large difference’ perspective supports the broader view that context including dosing frequency, local adherence patterns, and infestation severity may be as important as the choice between these scabicides. Similarly, Thadanipon et al. [21] conducted a network meta-analysis which found that permethrin generally performed better than many other agents including oral ivermectin in achieving early cure at 1–2 weeks, though cure probabilities converged by 3–6 weeks. They also noted that combinations such as permethrin plus oral ivermectin often ranked highest for cure, indicating potential benefit from synergistic approaches [21]. These observations underline that while monotherapy is effective, multi-modal strategies might improve outcomes under certain circumstances.

Our findings must also be contextualised against evidence on treatment failure dynamics where a large systematic review by Mbuagbaw et al. [2], examining scabies treatment failure across diverse regimens over decades, reported that failure prevalence was lowest with two doses of oral ivermectin compared to single-dose regimens and topical therapies including permethrin. That review highlighted a rising trend in treatment failure over time for both permethrin and ivermectin, hinting at potential decreases in mite susceptibility or evolving clinical or behavioural factors influencing outcomes [2]. These findings indicate that dosing strategy matters, supporting recommendations for at least two doses of ivermectin in practice. While our analysis focused on head-to-head comparisons, the broader failure prevalence data emphasise that a more nuanced understanding of dosing frequency, host factors, and parasite ecology is crucial.

Another study by Khan et al. [22] comparing single doses of permethrin and oral ivermectin in routine clinical practice concluded that permethrin was more effective and better tolerated as a first-line therapy, though the study did not include long-term follow-up data. This clinical observation resonates with historical conclusions that permethrin often shows an early advantage over single-dose ivermectin [23], yet it sits within a larger evidence landscape where two-dose ivermectin regimens narrow this gap and may even exceed topical monotherapy efficacy in some populations.

The literature also includes evidence on combination therapy. A cohort study by Haytoğlu et al. [24] suggested that adding oral ivermectin to standard permethrin therapy led to faster symptom relief, higher cure rates, and lower recurrence than monotherapy alone, with only modest increases in transient mild adverse events. This aligns with the broader notion that combining agents with distinct mechanisms permethrin disrupting neuronal sodium channels and ivermectin increasing inhibitory neurotransmission may achieve more comprehensive mite eradication [24]. The implication here is that in persistent or complicated scabies, particularly in high-burden settings or pediatric patients with adherence challenges, combination therapy might be superior to monotherapy, a concept increasingly recognised in clinical practice guidelines.

Despite these insights, limitations persist across the literature. Many trials suffer from small sample sizes, inconsistent outcome definitions, and inadequate blinding, leading to low to moderate certainty in effect estimates [1]. Furthermore, observational studies suggest that contextual factors including hygiene practices, family and community contacts, and healthcare access profoundly influence treatment success beyond the pharmacological efficacy of the agents themselves [19]. Thus, policy and guideline formulation should consider integrated care frameworks that combine effective pharmacotherapy with enhanced education, community engagement, and reinfestation prevention strategies.

In light of this, comparing our review findings with existing systematic reviews and broader literature underscores that permethrin and ivermectin remain effective scabies treatments, with nuanced differences often related to dosing strategies and contexts rather than intrinsic superiority. Permethrin may exhibit quicker early responses in some settings, while appropriately dosed ivermectin (particularly two doses) performs comparably or better over longer follow-up and in treatment failure contexts [25]. The evolving evidence base, including network meta-analyses and combination therapy data, argues for continued refinement of clinical guidelines emphasizing regimen optimization, combination approaches where appropriate, and attention to external determinants of treatment success to maximize population health impact.

Implications of the findings

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis have important implications for clinical practice, public health policy, and future research on scabies management. The pooled evidence suggests that topical permethrin remains marginally superior to ivermectin in achieving clinical cure and in reducing treatment failure, reinforcing its continued recommendation as a first-line therapy in routine clinical settings. This has practical significance, particularly in primary care and low-resource environments, where ensuring high cure rates with minimal follow-up is critical [25, 26]. However, the modest effect size observed indicates that ivermectin remains a viable alternative, especially in situations where topical therapy is impractical, adherence is poor, or large-scale interventions such as outbreaks or institutional settings are involved. The evidence also underscores as in line with Campbell et al. [25] the importance of treatment regimens, as repeated dosing of ivermectin may mitigate treatment failure, aligning with recommendations from global health agencies. From a policy perspective, these findings support context-specific treatment guidelines, allowing flexibility in agent selection based on patient characteristics, feasibility of application, and health system capacity. Furthermore, the observed heterogeneity highlights the need for standardized outcome definitions and harmonised follow-up periods in future trials to strengthen the evidence base and improve comparability across studies.

Limitations of the review

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings and first, the review included only English-language publications, which may have resulted in language bias and exclusion of relevant studies conducted in non-English-speaking settings. Second, the number of included trials was relatively small, and several studies had moderate to high risk of bias, particularly due to unclear randomization and lack of blinding. Third, variations in ivermectin dosing regimens, permethrin concentrations, follow-up durations, and outcome definitions contributed to heterogeneity and finally, limited reporting of continuous outcomes and recurrence rates restricted more comprehensive quantitative synthesis. Also, the total number of included studies was limited, and further stratification would result in very small subgroups.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from seven RCTs to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of ivermectin and permethrin for the treatment of scabies in humans. The pooled analysis demonstrated that ivermectin was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of clinical cure compared with permethrin at final follow-up (RR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.86–0.99), alongside a higher risk of treatment failure (RR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.06–2.20). Moderate heterogeneity was observed for clinical cure outcomes, while treatment failure results were highly consistent across studies. These findings indicate that permethrin confers a modest but clinically relevant advantage over ivermectin in achieving treatment success. Nevertheless, ivermectin remains an effective alternative, particularly when administered in repeated doses or in contexts where topical application is impractical. To this end, this evidence supports the continued prioritisation of permethrin as first-line therapy, while highlighting the need for optimised ivermectin regimens and further high-quality trials to address persistent uncertainties. Future research should focus on standardising outcome measures, evaluating combination therapies, and exploring effectiveness in high-burden and vulnerable populations.
