HyLnc: a hybrid deep learning and feature-based approach for long non-coding RNA prediction
Authors: Amrit Venkatesan, Prashasti Sinha, Jolly Basak, Ranjit Prasad Bahadur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLong non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play important roles in gene regulation, development and disease, yet accurate identification of lncRNAs from transcriptomic data remains a major computational challenge. Existing methods often rely either on handcrafted sequence features or deep learning approaches, each with their inherent limitations in capturing the full complexity of RNA sequences. In this study, we proposed HyLnc, a computational framework that integrates transformer-based contextual embeddings with biologically meaningful sequence features for improved lncRNA prediction. A custom BERT-based model was first pre-trained on a large corpus of metazoan RNA sequences using a masked language modelling strategy to learn contextual nucleotide dependencies. The model was subsequently fine-tuned on curated datasets of lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts and 256-dimensional deep sequence embeddings were extracted. Parallelly, 348 handcrafted features, including ORF characteristics, untranslated region (UTR) properties, nucleotide composition and Fickett scores, were computed. A multi-stage feature selection strategy was applied to identify the most informative features, resulting in optimized hybrid feature sets. Multiple machine learning classifiers were evaluated, with the RF model achieving the best performance. The proposed framework attained an accuracy of 91.30%, F1-score of 91.23% and MCC of 82.60 on an independent validation dataset, outperforming several existing lncRNA prediction tools. Thus, HyLnc demonstrates that integrating deep contextual representations with biologically interpretable features enhances lncRNA prediction. This approach provides a robust and scalable solution for large-scale transcriptome annotation and can be extended to other sequence-based prediction.

Introduction

High-throughput sequencing techniques reveal a substantial proportion of RNA molecules transcribed from eukaryotic genomes that do not encode proteins [1]. Among these transcripts, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as an important class of regulatory RNAs, which typically are longer than 200 nucleotides and lack protein-coding potential [2]. Increasing evidence suggests that lncRNAs participate in diverse biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodelling, epigenetic regulation and post-transcriptional control of gene expression [3,4]. Several studies have demonstrated that lncRNAs are involved in cellular differentiation, developmental processes, immune regulation and disease progression [3,5,6]. Aberrant expression of lncRNAs is associated with numerous pathological conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic disorders [7,8]. Thus, accurate identification and annotation of lncRNAs from transcriptomic datasets has become an essential step towards understanding the regulatory complexity of the genome. High-throughput RNA sequencing experiments routinely generate thousands of transcript candidates, making accurate discrimination between lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts essential for reliable transcriptome annotation [9]. Misclassification can result in false-positive lncRNA annotations or miss physiologically significant regulatory transcripts, influencing downstream analyses such as differential expression, co-expression network creation and disease-associated transcriptome studies. Therefore, accurate computational identification of lncRNAs becomes indispensable for large-scale transcriptome analysis and for understanding disease-associated regulatory mechanisms. Despite rapid advances in transcriptome sequencing, distinguishing lncRNAs from protein-coding transcripts remains challenging. Experimental approaches such as ribosome profiling, mass spectrometry and functional validation provide reliable evidence of coding potential but are labour-intensive, costly and difficult to apply at transcriptome scale. Consequently, computational prediction methods become indispensable for high-throughput lncRNA identification.

Early computational methods relied primarily on handcrafted sequence features to estimate coding potential. These features include the length of the open reading frame (ORF), codon usage bias, nucleotide composition and evolutionary conservation patterns. For instance, statistical metrics such as the Fickett score evaluate the coding potential of sequences based on nucleotide positional frequencies and compositional characteristics [10]. Several machine learning-based predictors have been developed using such features to classify coding and non-coding transcripts. Although these approaches demonstrate reasonable accuracy, their predictive performance often depends heavily on manually designed features that may not fully capture complex sequence dependencies present in RNA molecules. The emergence of machine learning and deep learning techniques has significantly improved the ability to model biological sequence data. Specifically, neural network-based models have been widely applied for sequence prediction due to their ability to automatically learn hierarchical representations from raw data [11]. Recently, transformer-based architectures, originally developed for natural language processing, have shown remarkable success in modelling sequential data [12]. Among these, the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) architecture has gained significant attention due to its ability to learn contextual representations using a masked language modelling framework [13]. In this approach, the model predicts masked tokens in a sequence by utilizing both left and right contextual information, enabling it to capture long-range dependencies and semantic relationships within sequences [14]. Although BERT was originally designed for natural language processing, its transformer-based model is well suited for biological sequence analysis since nucleotide sequences can be treated as symbolic sequences similar to linguistic tokens. By pre-training BERT models on large set of biological sequences, it becomes possible to learn rich contextual representations that capture intrinsic patterns such as nucleotide dependencies, sequence motifs and structural relationships [15]. These contextual embeddings can be fine-tuned for downstream tasks such as prediction of RNA, functional annotation and the prediction of regulatory elements in genome.

Many existing models either rely solely on deep learning-based embeddings [16–19] or exclusively on handcrafted sequence features [20,21]. Models that depend only on handcrafted features may fail to capture contextual relationships across long nucleotide sequences, while purely deep learning-based approaches may overlook biologically interpretable features such as ORF identification or its translatability [22]. Recent studies have increasingly explored hybrid computational frameworks that combine deep learning with handcrafted biological features to improve lncRNA prediction [14,23]. For example, comparative benchmarking studies have demonstrated that integrating biologically meaningful sequence descriptors with learned representations often yields superior performance compared to either strategy alone. Hybrid models such as BioDeepFuse [24] integrate convolutional neural networks with engineered sequence descriptors, whereas other approaches combine transformer-derived embeddings with conventional coding potential features to enhance classification performance. However, existing hybrid models [24,25] rely on generic pretrained language models, incorporating a relatively limited set of biologically meaningful features or lack systematic feature optimization, leaving scope for further methodological improvement. Recent transformer-based approaches such as LncRNA-BERT have demonstrated that RNA language models can effectively distinguish coding and non-coding transcripts [26]. Additionally, redundant or highly correlated features may reduce model interpretability and computational efficiency. Therefore, integrative computational frameworks that combine deep contextual representations with biologically meaningful sequence features while incorporating systematic feature selection remain necessary for improving prediction accuracy, robustness and biological interpretability.

In this study, we have developed a hybrid computational framework for lncRNA identification (HyLnc) that integrates RNA-specific contextual embeddings learned from a custom BERT-based language model with a comprehensive set of biologically interpretable sequence features. Unlike previously reported hybrid frameworks that primarily combine generic deep learning representations with a limited set of handcrafted descriptors [24–26], HyLnc integrates an RNA-specific custom BERT-based model with comprehensive features including ORF, UTR, sequence composition and coding potential using a systematic multi-stage feature selection strategy to improve both prediction accuracy and biological interpretability. The custom BERT-based model was pre-trained on a large collection of metazoan RNA sequences encompassing multiple RNA classes to learn generalized contextual representations before being adapted for lncRNA prediction. The pre-trained model is further fine-tuned using curated datasets containing experimentally annotated lncRNA and protein-coding transcripts. In parallel, biologically relevant sequence features such as ORF characteristics, untranslated regions, nucleotide compositions and Fickett scores are extracted to complement the contextual embeddings. A systematic feature selection pipeline combining correlation analysis, mutual information scoring and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression is employed to identify the most informative features and eliminate redundant variables [27]. The selected feature sets are subsequently used to train multiple machine learning classifiers including Decision Tree (DT), k-Nearest Neighbour (kNN), Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting (LGB) and XGBoost (XGB) [28–32]. The resulting models are evaluated on independent validation datasets to assess their predictive accuracy and robustness. Among the tested classifiers, the RF classifier demonstrated the best performance within the HyLnc framework, achieving an accuracy of 91.30%, F1-score of 91.23% and MCC of 82.60. In addition to classification performance, the developed framework is further applied to RNA-Seq datasets GSE213610, GSE181029 and GSE124439 associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) to identify potential tissue-specific novel lncRNA candidates [33–36]. This application demonstrates the utility of HyLnc for disease-associated lncRNA discovery. The model is implemented as a web server, ‘HyLnc’, which is freely accessible at http://www.csb.iitkgp.ac.in/applications/HyLnc/index.php.

Methods

Dataset curation and pre-processing

RNA sequences used for model development were collected from publicly available and curated databases, including GENCODE, RefSeq and RNAcentral [37–39]. Pre-training was performed on a diverse dataset of 428,823 metazoan RNA sequences, including protein-coding transcripts (mRNA) and multiple classes of non-coding RNAs, along with uncharacterized sequences. This diversity enables the model to learn generalized sequence representations. For the downstream classification, a labelled dataset consisting of lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts was constructed. After removing redundant and duplicate sequences separately from the positive and the negative classes using CD-HIT [40] with a sequence identity threshold of 80%, we obtained 14,455 lncRNA sequences and 13,876 protein-coding sequences for model fine-tuning and feature extraction. The dataset represents experimentally annotated transcripts obtained from curated genomic repositories. All sequences were formatted in FASTA format and processed using custom scripts to ensure consistency in sequence representation prior to model training.

Custom BERT-based architecture and pre-training

Our custom BERT-based architecture extends the standard transformer encoder with several RNA-oriented design choices. Unlike conventional BERT models, the proposed architecture incorporates CNN-based nucleotide embeddings [41], sinusoidal positional encoding [42] chunk-based processing of long nucleotide sequences and aggregation of chunk-level representations to efficiently capture both local sequence motifs and long-range contextual dependencies. RNA sequences were treated as symbolic tokens analogous to words in natural language processing and were subsequently transformed into contextual representations for downstream classification. The convolutional embedding module consisted of four embedding layers with a hidden dimension of 512. Two convolutional kernels of size 6 and 9 were employed to capture nucleotide motifs at different receptive fields. The resulting convolution-derived embeddings were combined with sinusoidal positional encodings before being supplied to the transformer encoder. The transformer encoder comprised of four attention heads and a dropout rate of 0.25 was applied during training to reduce overfitting. The transformer architecture consists of a stack of transformer encoder layers, multi-head self-attention layers for contextual representation learning and feedforward neural network layers for feature transformation. To process long RNA transcripts that exceed the maximum input length of conventional transformer models, each sequence was partitioned into fixed-length chunks of 4000 nucleotides. Individual chunks were processed independently by the transformer encoder and their contextual representations were subsequently aggregated to generate a sequence-level embedding for downstream prediction. This strategy enabled efficient modelling of long biological sequences while preserving contextual information.

During the pre-training phase, the model was trained using masked language modelling (MLM), where randomly selected nucleotides within the sequence were masked and the model was trained to predict these masked tokens based on both the preceding and succeeding context [42,43]. Approximately 15% of the nucleotides in each sequence were masked during training. The model was pretrained on the compiled metazoan RNA corpus for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of 2. These training settings enabled the model to learn generalized RNA-specific contextual representations, capturing nucleotide dependencies, sequence motifs and contextual relationships across diverse RNA classes.

Fine-tuning of the pre-trained model

Following pre-training, the model was fine-tuned on the labelled lncRNA prediction dataset containing lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts. Fine-tuning enables the pretrained model to adapt its parameters to the specific prediction while retaining the general sequence knowledge learned during pre-training. The fine-tuning process was performed for 100 epochs, during which the learned weights of the model were updated using the labelled sequences. These transformer embeddings from fine-tuning capture specific sequence-level contextual information, which serve as deep feature representation for downstream classification.

Feature extraction

We used two categories of features, including BERT-based contextual embeddings and handcrafted biological features. From the fine-tuned transformer model, 256 deep contextual features were extracted for each RNA sequence. These features represent high-level sequence representations learned by the neural network and capture long-range nucleotide dependencies. To complement the contextual embeddings with biologically interpretable attributes, a set of 348 handcrafted sequence features was calculated. These features were derived from sequence composition and ORF characteristics. Each RNA sequence was scanned in six possible reading frames to identify potential ORFs. Candidate ORFs were defined as nucleotide segments beginning with the start codon ‘AUG’ and terminating at one of the canonical stop codons ‘UAA’, ‘UAG’ or ‘UGA’. Among all detected ORFs, the longest one was selected as the representative ORF for the sequence. The region between the transcription start site and the start codon was defined as the 5’ untranslated region (5’ UTR). The regions between the termination codon and transcription end site were defined as the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR).

Further, coding potential were computed using sequence composition statistics. One important metric used was the Fickett score [10], which estimates coding potential using nucleotide frequency and positional bias within a sequence. This score helps to differentiate coding transcripts from non-coding transcripts based on statistical properties of nucleotide distribution. Additional sequence-derived descriptors include nucleotide composition metrics, ORF coverage and the features based on sequence length. Combining the contextual embeddings and handcrafted descriptors produced a total of 604 features per sequence.

Feature selection strategy

To remove redundant and irrelevant features while retaining the most informative predictors, a multi-stage feature selection strategy was applied. Initially, pairwise feature correlations were computed using a correlation matrix. To minimize multicollinearity, features exhibiting correlation coefficients greater than 0.8 were considered redundant and removed. The remaining features were ranked using mutual information, which measures the dependency between each feature and the target class label [44–46]. Features with higher mutual information scores were retained for subsequent analysis. Finally, LASSO regression was applied using a regularization parameter (α) of 1 × 10−7 and features with non-zero regression coefficients were retained as the optimal feature subset for downstream model training [47].

Training, validation and benchmarking

Using the selected feature subsets, multiple machine learning classifiers, including DT, kNN, RF, LGB and XGB, were trained to distinguish lncRNAs from protein-coding transcripts. Each classifier was trained using the selected feature sets and optimized using cross-validation. The labelled dataset was randomly divided into training and test sets using 80:20 ratio, and a random seed of 5 was used to ensure reproducibility throughout model training and evaluation. Hyperparameters were optimized using 10-fold cross-validation on the training dataset. Model performance was assessed using standard metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. To assess robustness and generalizability, the trained models were evaluated on an independent validation dataset. The validation dataset consisted of 10,000 experimentally annotated RNA sequences, including 5000 lncRNAs and 5000 protein-coding transcripts retrieved from the Ensembl database (https://asia.ensembl.org). The performance of the developed model was further compared with seven previously reported lncRNA prediction tools. Each method was evaluated using the same validation dataset to ensure a fair comparison. Metrics, including prediction accuracy, ROC-AUC score and misclassification rate, were used to assess the relative performance of the models.

Comparative sequence feature analysis of method-specific predictions

To investigate sequence characteristics associated with method-specific prediction patterns, a comparative feature analysis was performed using the independent validation dataset. The benchmark methods were grouped into four categories: ML-based predictors (CPC2, LGC and PLEK), traditional CNN-based predictors (lncRNA_Mdeep and NCResNet), hybrid model-based predictors (IRSOM) and transformer-based predictors (lncRNA-BERT). For each benchmark category, two complementary analyses were performed. First, transcripts correctly classified exclusively by HyLnc were compared with transcripts correctly classified by both HyLnc and the corresponding benchmark method. Second, transcripts correctly classified by the benchmark method but not by HyLnc were compared with transcripts correctly classified by both methods. Thus, the first analysis characterized sequence features associated with HyLnc-exclusive predictions, whereas the second characterized sequence features associated with benchmark-exclusive predictions. For each comparison, the complete set of sequence features represented within the five optimized HyLnc feature sets was evaluated. The distributions of individual features between the benchmark method-specific and shared true-positive groups were compared using the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test [48]. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Features with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly different. For significant features, the median values in the two comparison groups were calculated to determine the direction of the observed difference. This complementary analysis was used to determine whether transcripts uniquely identified by HyLnc and those uniquely identified by existing methods exhibit distinct sequence characteristics within the feature space considered by HyLnc.

Prediction of neurodegenerative disorder‑specific lncRNAs

We have analysed RNA-Seq data from three independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets: GSE213610 for AD, GSE181029 for PD and GSE124439 for ALS. The AD dataset included 32 samples representing familial AD (FAD), sporadic AD (SAD) and non-AD individuals, derived from CA3 hippocampal neurons and hippocampal neural precursor cells (hpNPCs) [33–36,49]. Within the hpNPC group, eight samples were available for each disease subtype along with four controls, whereas the CA3 neuron group contains four samples for each subtype and four controls. The PD dataset contains 36 samples obtained from neural progenitors and terminally differentiated neurons, with each cell type represented by nine PD and nine control samples. The ALS dataset initially contains 176 samples categorized as ‘ALS Spectrum MND’, ‘Non-neurological Controls’ and ‘Other neurological disorders’. Samples belonging to the ‘Other neurological disorders’ group were excluded, leaving 162 samples for downstream analysis. Each sample included in the selected GEO datasets represents an independent biological replicate obtained from the original studies [34–36]. Accordingly, the disease and control groups comprised biological replicates as defined by the original experimental design. No additional biological replicates were generated beyond those available in the source datasets. Raw sequencing reads for all datasets were retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) [50] and converted to FASTQ format [51] using fasterq-dump. Read quality was assessed using FastQC, and adapter sequences were removed with Cutadapt 3.5 [52]. The cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38p.14) using STAR v2.7.10 [53], generating Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files [54] for all samples.

Transcript assembly and quantification were performed using StringTie (v3.0.3) [55] with GENCODE (v47) [38] human transcript annotations as the reference. StringTie enables the identification of both annotated and previously uncharacterized transcripts. The assembled transcripts were subsequently compared against the human genome primary assembly using gffcompare [56] to assign class codes, reflecting their genomic context. For this study, only transcripts assigned class codes ‘u’, ‘x’ and ‘i’, corresponding to intergenic, antisense and intronic transcripts, respectively, were retained for further analysis. Transcript abundance was quantified across all samples using the prepDE module implemented in StringTie, and expression levels were normalized as Transcripts Per Million (TPM) [57]. Transcripts with TPM greater than 1 in the majority of disease samples and TPM less than 0.1 in control samples were selected for downstream filtering. The selected transcripts with length greater than 200 nucleotides were aligned against the human protein-coding sequences retrieved from UniProt [58] using blastx [59] to eliminate potential protein-coding transcripts. The remaining non-coding transcripts were then aligned against known lncRNA sequences obtained from RNAcentral [39] using blastn with an e-value threshold of 1 × 10−6 to identify novel transcripts. The resulting unannotated sequences were finally classified using HyLnc.

Results

Training performance of the custom BERT-based model architecture

The customized BERT-based model developed in this study is trained using masked language modelling on a large corpus of metazoan RNA sequences. A schematic representation of the developed pipeline is shown in Figure 1. During pre-training, the model is trained for 50 epochs with a learning rate of ~3.01 × 10−11, followed by fine-tuning for 100 epochs at a learning rate of ~2.20 × 10−10. Learning rates, training and validation losses across epochs for pre-training and fine-tuning are represented in Figure 2. Both training and validation losses exhibit a consistent and gradual decrease in pre-training, indicating a stable convergence of the model and effective learning of general sequence representations. Around the later stages of pre-training (approximately epoch 47), a sharper reduction in the learning rate is observed as a consequence of the learning rate scheduling strategy employed during optimization. These facilitate fine adjustment of model parameters during the final stages of training, leading to a corresponding decrease in training and validation losses. The pre-training duration of 50 epochs is selected based on convergence of the masked language modelling loss, whereas fine-tuning for 100 epochs ensures stable convergence of the downstream classification task without evidence of overfitting. The loss values are significantly lower in fine-tuning and stabilized rapidly, suggesting that the pre-trained model already captured meaningful contextual features and only required minimal adjustment for the downstream classification. This behaviour highlights a key advantage of the transformer-based approach in its ability to learn transferable representations from large unlabelled datasets, thereby reducing the dependence on extensive supervised training. The low validation loss during fine-tuning further indicates the model does not suffer from any overfitting. Feature extraction and contribution of hybrid representations

For each RNA sequence, a total of 604 features are extracted, of which 256 are deep contextual features derived from the customized BERT-based model and 348 are handcrafted biological features, which are listed in Table 1. The handcrafted features include sequence-level descriptors, ORF-based attributes and UTR-derived features such as k-mer composition, AU content and Fickett score. These features capture biologically interpretable properties related to coding potential, while the BERT-derived embeddings encode higher-order contextual dependencies within nucleotide sequences. While deep learning features capture long-range sequence dependencies and hidden motifs, handcrafted features provide complementary biological signals such as ORF structure and compositional bias. This hybrid representation ensures that both data-driven learning and biological interpretability are incorporated into the model. Feature selection using correlation filtering, mutual information and LASSO regression reduces redundancy and identifies the most informative features. Retained features represent complementary sources of information. While transformer-derived embeddings primarily capture contextual nucleotide dependencies and hidden sequence patterns, handcrafted features encode biologically interpretable properties associated with coding potential. Their integration enables HyLnc to exploit both data-derived representations and biologically meaningful sequence characteristics for robust lncRNA prediction. The contribution of ORF- and UTR-derived features is supported by their biological roles in transcript function. Protein-coding transcripts generally contain longer and more coding-compatible ORFs, whereas lncRNAs typically exhibit lower coding potential [60]. Similarly, the 5’ UTR and the 3’ UTR regulate translation initiation, mRNA stability and post-transcription in protein-coding transcripts, while lncRNAs often exhibit distinct UTR organization and sequence composition. Consequently, these biologically meaningful features provide complementary information that enhances the discrimination of lncRNAs from protein-coding transcripts when integrated with contextual sequence embeddings. Effect of different feature sets on prediction performance

Feature set 1 is a fully hybrid representation. This integrates ORF, sequence, 3’ UTR, 5’ UTR and BERT-derived features, achieving the highest overall performance. Among all the classifiers, RF performed best with an accuracy of 91%, F1-score of 0.91 and MCC of 0.81 (Table 3). The high specificity (0.92) and sensitivity (0.90) indicate that the model effectively distinguishes between lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts with minimal bias towards either class. The ROC (Figure 3(a)) further confirms the strong performance of RF using feature set 1 to discriminate lncRNAs from protein‑coding transcripts, and also suggests that the hybrid model captures both contextual relationships and biologically relevant constraints effectively. The superior performance of this feature set demonstrates that lncRNA prediction benefits from a holistic representation combining structural, compositional and contextual information. Each feature type contributes complementary information, leading to optimal prediction performance. Feature set 2 includes ORF-derived features combined with deep contextual embeddings. Using this feature set, RF achieved an accuracy of 89% with F1-score of 0.89. Although, specificity remains high (~0.91), sensitivity dropped compared to feature set 1, indicating a reduced ability to correctly identify lncRNAs. The ROC curve remains well above the diagonal baseline, but shows a slightly reduced steepness compared to feature set 1 (Figure 3(b)). This result suggests that ORF features and contextual embeddings together provide strong predictive power, but the absence of sequence and UTR features leads to a loss of discriminative information. In particular, UTR regions may contain regulatory signals that help to differentiate non-coding transcripts, which are not captured in this reduced feature set.

Feature set 3 excludes 5’ UTR while retaining ORF, sequence, and 3’ UTR features. Using this feature set, RF achieved an accuracy of 89% and F1-score of 0.90 (Table 3), which are comparable with the performance of feature set 2. The ROC shows better performance compared to feature set 2, but slightly lesser than feature set 1 (Figure 3(c)). The comparable performance of feature set 1 and 3 indicates that 3’ UTR features alone are insufficient to fully compensate for the absence of 5’ UTR information. This suggests that both the UTR regions contribute distinct and complementary signals in distinguishing coding and non-coding transcripts.

Feature set 4 retains all the features except 3’ UTR. RF achieved an accuracy of 90% and F1-score of 0.91 (Table 3), which are comparable with the performance of feature set 1. The improved performance of feature set 4 compared to feature set 3 suggests that 5’ UTR features contribute more significantly than 3’ UTR features in distinguishing lncRNAs from protein-coding transcripts. This may be attributed to the role of 5’ UTRs in translation initiation, which differs substantially between coding and non-coding transcripts in terms of presence of structural motifs, sequence conservation and sequence length [61,62]. The ROC of feature set 4 (Figure 3(d)) shows better performance than feature set 3 and comparable with the performance of feature set 1.

Feature set 5 represents the most reduced configuration, containing only sequence-level features and BERT-based embeddings. RF achieved an accuracy of 80% (Table 3), significantly lower than the other feature sets. Both sensitivity and MCC are notably reduced, indicating weaker prediction performance. This result demonstrates that deep contextual embeddings alone are insufficient for accurate prediction of lncRNAs. Although the BERT-based model captures sequence dependencies, it does not explicitly encode biological information such as ORF structure and coding potential. This highlights the importance of integrating domain specific biological features with deep learning representations. The ROC shown in Figure 3(e) is closer to the diagonal compared to the other feature sets, indicating reduced performance. Among all the feature sets (Supplementary Table S1), the RF classifier has overall superior performance in terms of performance metrics. Therefore, RF classifier is implemented in HyLnc for prediction of lncRNAs and protein coding sequences.

Classifier comparison and model selection

Across all the feature sets, RF consistently outperformed other classifiers, achieving the highest accuracy, F1-score and MCC values. Meanwhile, XGB and LGB also show strong performance but are slightly less consistent across feature sets. The performance of DT and kNN is comparatively poor, likely due to their limited ability to model complex feature interactions. The robustness of RF can be attributed to its ensemble nature, which effectively captures non-linear relationships and reduces overfitting [28]. This makes it particularly suitable for high-dimensional hybrid feature spaces. Based on these results, RF is selected as the final classifier in the HyLnc framework. To further evaluate model robustness, the RF classifier is assessed using 10-fold cross-validation for each feature set. The model exhibits consistent performance across all folds with mean accuracies of 89.4 ± 0.7% (Feature set 1), 87.9 ± 0.6% (Feature set 2), 89.0 ± 0.5% (Feature set 3), 89.3 ± 0.7% (Feature set 4) and 84.8 ± 0.8% (Feature set 5). The corresponding mean F1-scores are 0.898 ± 0.006, 0.887 ± 0.007, 0.894 ± 0.005, 0.900 ± 0.007 and 0.854 ± 0.005, while the mean MCC values are 0.788 ± 0.013, 0.761 ± 0.012, 0.783 ± 0.008, 0.788 ± 0.010 and 0.694 ± 0.012, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). These results demonstrate that the proposed framework exhibits stable predictive performance and good generalization across different validation partitions.

Benchmarking against existing lncRNA prediction tools

The performance of the proposed HyLnc framework is systematically compared with seven widely used lncRNA prediction tools, namely CPC2, LGC, lncRNA_Mdeep, NCResNet, PLEK, IRSOM and lncRNA-BERT [25,26,63–67], summarized in Table 4 and illustrated in Figure 4. Among all the evaluated methods, HyLnc achieves the highest accuracy (91.30%), MCC (82.60) and sensitivity (90.56%), while maintaining a high specificity (92.04%). Among the other classifiers, only LncRNA-BERT achieves a slightly higher F1-score (91.89%) than HyLnc (91.23%). These results demonstrate that HyLnc provides better discrimination between lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts, with a particularly favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity. Although lncRNA_Mdeep (95.52%) and LncRNA-BERT (97.28%) achieve slightly higher specificity than HyLnc, both the methods exhibit lower sensitivity, 85.64% and 87.32%, respectively. This indicates that a larger proportion of true lncRNAs are incorrectly classified as protein-coding transcripts. Similarly, CPC2 achieves higher specificity (92.08%) but lower sensitivity (87.52%) compared with HyLnc. Since lncRNA discovery primarily aims to minimize false-negative predictions while maintaining acceptable false-positive rates, the higher sensitivity achieved by HyLnc is advantageous for large-scale transcriptome annotation. Comparative sequence feature analysis of method-specific predictions

Across all pairwise comparisons, multiple sequence features exhibit statistically significant differences between method-specific and shared true-positive transcripts (Supplementary Tables S3-S5 and S6-S8). For the HyLnc-exclusive analysis, significant differences are observed in features related to ORF organization, UTR characteristics, nucleotide composition and coding potential. This indicates that transcripts uniquely identified by HyLnc possess sequence characteristics that distinguish them from transcripts commonly identified by both HyLnc and existing prediction methods. Importantly, no single sequence feature is consistently associated with HyLnc-exclusive predictions across all benchmark categories, suggesting that the unique predictions arise from combinations of complementary sequence characteristics rather than a single discriminative property. To further investigate the complementary prediction by existing methods, we perform an analogous analysis of transcripts correctly classified by benchmark methods but not by HyLnc. Across the ML-based, traditional CNN-based, hybrid and transformer-based benchmark categories, benchmark-exclusive transcripts exhibit significant differences in multiple sequence characteristics relative to transcripts correctly classified by both the HyLnc and the respective benchmark methods. These differences involve features related to sequence length, nucleotide composition, coding potential, ORF characteristics and UTR-derived properties, although the significant features varied among benchmark categories.

Interestingly, the benchmark-exclusive groups generally exhibit a larger number of statistically significant feature differences than the corresponding HyLnc-exclusive groups. This observation should not be interpreted as evidence that HyLnc is unable to capture these features, because the analysis evaluates differences in feature distributions between prediction groups rather than whether a feature is represented in the model. Indeed, the discriminative features identified in the benchmark-exclusive analysis are represented within the five optimized feature sets incorporated into HyLnc. Thus, the results indicate that benchmark-exclusive transcripts exhibit distinct patterns and distributions within the sequence-feature space considered by HyLnc, rather than demonstrating these features are missed by the model. Taken together, these complementary analyses demonstrate the broader applicability and generalizability of HyLnc, as it identifies a distinct yet partially overlapping set of transcripts relative to existing prediction methods. The HyLnc-exclusive transcripts are characterized by a diverse combination of sequence properties, suggesting that HyLnc can capture a broader range of transcript characteristics. The transcript identifiers uniquely detected by HyLnc are provided in Supplementary Table S3, while the complete feature-level analyses for HyLnc-exclusive predictions are provided in Supplementary Tables S4-S5. Similarly, the transcript identifiers for benchmark-exclusive predictions and their complete feature-level analyses are shown in Supplementary Tables S6-S8.

Prediction of lncRNAs related to neurodegenerative disorders

To demonstrate the practical applicability of HyLnc, RNA-Seq datasets associated with AD, PD and ALS are analysed for the identification of novel lncRNA transcripts. Since the prediction model is trained on a further diverse metazoan RNA corpus, the framework is not restricted to human transcriptomes. Raw sequencing data are processed through a transcript discovery pipeline involving read pre-processing, genome alignment, transcript assembly and annotation-guided filtering. Transcripts corresponding to intergenic, antisense and intronic regions are extracted as candidate novel lncRNAs. These sequences are subsequently filtered based on expression, coding potential and similarity to known lncRNA databases. Finally, the filtered sequences are classified using HyLnc to obtain high-confidence tissue-specific novel lncRNA candidates. The predicted high-confidence lncRNA candidate sequences for AD, PD and ALS datasets are provided in Supplementary Tables S9-S15. All the predicted novel candidate lncRNA transcripts are consistently present in all diseased samples and not in the control samples. No novel lncRNA candidate sequences are identified for terminally differentiated neurons in the PD dataset. A summary of the predicted lncRNA candidates is presented in Table 5. Across all datasets, the predicted transcripts exceed the conventional 200‑nucleotide threshold for lncRNAs and exhibit substantial variability in transcript length, reflecting the structural diversity of lncRNAs [2]. The average GC content ranged from 43.79% to 55.76% across datasets, indicating sequence compositions consistent with previously reported lncRNA characteristics [68]. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that integrating transformer-derived contextual embeddings with biologically interpretable sequence features can improve prediction of lncRNA. Unlike conventional lncRNA prediction approaches that rely either on handcrafted descriptors or deep learning alone, HyLnc combines both strategies to capture complementary biological and contextual information. The superior performance of the hybrid framework highlights that accurate discrimination between lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts requires not only recognition of sequence composition and ORF-related properties, but also modelling of long-range nucleotide dependencies embedded within transcript sequences.

The contextual embeddings generated by the custom BERT-based framework likely contribute to the ability of the model to recognize hidden sequence patterns and higher-order dependencies. Transformer-based models are highly effective in biological sequence modelling because of their self-attention mechanisms and contextual learning capabilities. However, purely embedding-based methods often suffer from limited biological interpretability [61,62]. At the same time, handcrafted features such as ORF length, ORF coverage, nucleotide composition and Fickett score provide biologically meaningful indicators of coding potential [23,24,69]. The improved performance observed for feature set 1 compared with reduced feature combinations demonstrates that both contextual and biological features contribute distinct predictive information. The reduction in performance observed in feature set 5 indicates that contextual embeddings alone are insufficient for robust lncRNA predictions.

Among all evaluated classifiers, RF consistently produces the best overall performance across all the feature sets. This may be attributed to its ensemble-based learning technique, which efficiently captures non-linear relationships within high-dimensional hybrid feature space while minimizing overfitting. The high MCC and balanced sensitivity-specificity profile further suggest that HyLnc achieves reliable classification without strong bias towards either coding or non-coding transcripts. HyLnc demonstrates better performance across the benchmarked methods, with a favourable balance between sensitivity, specificity, accuracy and MCC. Although LncRNA-BERT and lncRNA_Mdeep achieve marginally higher specificity, HyLnc provides a favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity, resulting in the highest accuracy and MCC among the evaluated methods. This balanced performance of HyLnc is particularly important for transcriptome-wide lncRNA discovery, where minimizing false-negative predictions while maintaining low false-positive rates is essential for reliable downstream biological analyses.

A major strength of this study is the application of HyLnc to neurodegenerative disorder-associated transcriptomic datasets. The identification of high-confidence tissue-specific novel lncRNA candidates in AD, PD and ALS suggests that the framework can support large-scale discovery of disease-associated regulatory transcripts. Since lncRNAs are increasingly implicated in neural development, synaptic regulation and protein aggregation pathways, the predicted candidates may provide important leads for future functional and biomarker studies. Since the disease-associated lncRNA analysis is performed using publicly available RNA-Seq datasets, experimental validation and biological validation of the predicted candidates remain important directions for future investigation. The complementary sequence-feature analysis provides additional insight into the relationship between HyLnc and existing prediction methods. Transcripts uniquely identified by HyLnc exhibit significant differences across multiple sequence characteristics, supporting the contribution of complementary contextual and biologically interpretable representations. Conversely, transcripts exclusively identified by existing methods also display distinct sequence-feature distributions relative to transcripts jointly identified by HyLnc and the respective benchmark methods. Interestingly, the benchmark-exclusive transcripts show a broader range of statistically significant feature differences. The results highlight that HyLnc captures a broad and heterogeneous region of the lncRNA sequence space, while also showing some differences from existing predictors in sensitivity to specific combinations and distributions of sequence characteristics. However, this does not indicate that HyLnc fails to represent these features as the significant characteristics, which are included within the feature space of its five optimized feature sets. This complementary prediction behaviour provides a more appropriate interpretation of the marginal differences observed in the overall benchmarking performance. Thus, HyLnc presents a scalable and biologically informed framework, demonstrating how hybrid deep learning approaches can improve non-coding RNA discovery in complex diseases.

Conclusion

In this study, HyLnc, a hybrid computational framework is developed for accurate identification of long non-coding RNAs by integrating transformer-based sequence embeddings with biologically meaningful features. The proposed approach combines deep contextual representations learned from a custom BERT-based model with handcrafted descriptors derived from ORF characteristics, untranslated regions and nucleotide composition. The results demonstrate that this hybrid strategy significantly improves prediction performance compared to individual feature types. The integration of deep learning and biological features provides complementary information, enabling more effective discrimination between lncRNAs and protein-coding transcripts. Among the evaluated classifiers, RF consistently achieves the best performance, highlighting its robustness in handling high-dimensional hybrid feature space. Benchmarking against the existing lncRNA prediction tools demonstrates better performance of HyLnc, which achieves the highest accuracy, sensitivity and MCC among the evaluated methods while maintaining a favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity. Among the other classifiers, only LncRNA-BERT achieves a slightly better F1-score compared to HyLnc. The integration of contextual embeddings with biologically interpretable sequence features provides complementary predictive information for lncRNA classification. Therefore, HyLnc offers a robust and scalable framework for lncRNA identification and can be extended to other sequence-based prediction. This approach contributes to improved transcriptome annotation and enhances our understanding of the functional landscape of non-coding RNAs.
