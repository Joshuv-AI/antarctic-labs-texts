Intrathecal pemetrexed improves survival outcomes in previously treated EGFR-mutant advanced non-small-cell lung cancer with leptomeningeal metastases
Authors: Liqun Li, Zhe Huang, Yangqian Chen, Hongzhi Ma, Xiaoquan Chen, Huan Yan, Haoyue Qin, Yuda Zhang, Xing Zhang, Wenjuan Jiang, Zhan Wang, Lin Zhang, Fanxu Zeng, Zhiguo Zhou, Xingxiang Pu, Nong Yang, Liang Zeng, Yongchang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study assessed the impact of intrathecal pemetrexed (IP) in managing leptomeningeal metastases (LM) in previously treated patients with EGFR-mutant advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsWe analyzed the clinical and survival outcomes of 50 patients with LM who received 50 mg IP after disease progression with epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) treatment between September 2021 and September 2023 at the Hunan Cancer Hospital. Treatment response was evaluated based on improvement in neurological symptoms/signs and Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scores. We also evaluated the overall survival (OS), intracranial progression-free survival (I-PFS), and extracranial progression-free survival (E-PFS). Next generation sequencing (NGS) was employed to explore the underlying mechanisms of LM after EGFR-TKIs resistance.ResultsIP treatment was associated with a 64 % clinical response rate, median I-PFS of 5.3 months (95 % confidence intervals [CI], 1.4–9.2), E-PFS of 8.0 months (95 % CI, 2.2–13.8), and OS of 12.0 months (95 % CI, 9.6–14.4). Compared with non-responders, responders to IP demonstrated significantly prolonged I-PFS (11.2 months vs. 1.0 month; hazard ratio [HR]: 0.15, 95 % CI: 0.06–0.39), E-PFS (11.2 months vs. 3.0 months; HR: 0.24, 95 % CI: 0.10–0.57), and OS (15.5 months vs. 3.8 months; HR: 0.22, 95 % CI: 0.08–0.58) (all P < 0.001). Adverse events(AEs) primarily consisted of myelosuppression (54 %) and hepatic damage (24 %), but were manageable and did not result in any deaths. EGFR mutations were consistent between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and primary tumors in 100 % of cases. CSF also exhibited genetic variations such as EGFR mutations/amplifications and alterations in TP53, CDKN2A, MET, and CDK6.ConclusionIP represents a potentially viable treatment option for managing LM in patients with EGFR-TKI-resistant EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC. Clinical responses as measured by the improvement of neurological symptoms/signs and KPS scores were associated with favorable survival outcomes.Keywords: Intrathecal pemetrexed, NSCLC, Leptomeningeal metastases, EGFR-TKI resistance, Cerebrospinal fluid NGS, Circulating tumor DNA

Introduction

Leptomeningeal metastases (LMs), alternatively referred to as leptomeningeal carcinomatosis or leptomeningeal disease, frequently manifest in patients with advanced cancers, affecting approximately 3–5% of individuals with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and 9.4 % of those harboring epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations [1]. Consequently, patients who develop LMs experience a relatively poor prognosis, with a median survival of around 3 months [2]. While targeted therapy has demonstrated notable success in treating NSCLC with actionable driver mutations, their effectiveness is limited, particularly for first-generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), owing to their inadequate penetration to the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [3,4]. Nevertheless, third-generation agents, such as osimertinib, have exhibited remarkable BBB penetration and address the clinical challenge from first-generation EGFR-TKIs. Administering standard or double dosages of osimertinib has extended the median survival to approximately 7–11 months in individuals with EGFR-mutated LMs [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, challenges persist in the treatment of patients whose disease had progressed on TKIs, or have refractory LM (rLM). Currently, there are only a few effective therapeutic options for rLM and this raises an urgent need to conduct studies exploring alternative treatment options to improve survival outcomes in this patient population.

Intrathecal chemotherapy is a method that bypasses the BBB to deliver antitumor drugs directly into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and is widely employed in clinical practice for treating cancers involving the brain or spinal cord or has a high risk of spreading in these areas such as leukemia and lymphoma [9]. While intrathecal administration of chemotherapeutic agents such as methotrexate, cytarabine, and thiotepa have been investigated in NSCLC, limited efficacy has been observed [9,10]. Pemetrexed is an extensively used, intravenously administered chemotherapy in NSCLC treatment, which has demonstrated notable effectiveness in controlling brain metastases or LM [11,12]. However, the concentration of intravenous pemetrexed in the CSF is below 2 % [13]. Given the proven efficacy of intrathecal chemotherapy in treating LMs, there is a rationale to explore the potential of pemetrexed administered intrathecally. A phase I/II study reported that administration of 50 mg intrathecal pemetrexed (IP) resulted in an 84.6 % response rate in patients with EGFR-TKI-resistant EGFR-mutant NSCLC, with minimal side effects [14]. Despite these promising results, the available studies are limited, and further research is necessary to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of IP in LM treatment.

This study aims to assess the effectiveness of IP in treating LM among patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC after experiencing disease progression with EGFR-TKIs. Moreover, we explored the molecular profile of LM using DNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) of CSF obtained after EGFR-TKI resistance to understand the molecular mechanisms associated with LM emergence.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective observational study included patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC diagnosed with LM with or without brain metastasis (BM) and received EGFR-TKI treatment between September 2021 and September 2023 at the Hunan Provincial Tumor Hospital.

The inclusion criteria were: 1) advanced NSCLC treated with EGFR-TKIs; 2) confirmed presence of LM; and 3) received IP treatment. Patients with LM at initial diagnosis of NSCLC or before receiving EGFR-TKI treatment were excluded.

Given the difficulty in diagnosing LM, we defined LM according to 1) typical clinical symptoms, 2) cytological positivity of CSF samples, or 3) the detection of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) by DNA-based NGS of CSF samples [15,16]. Considering the lack of standardized definition and treatment guidelines for rLM, we defined it as: 1) the diagnosis of LM during EGFR-TKI treatment or the progression of known LM during EGFR-TKI treatment among patients with actionable EGFR mutations; 2) the progression of LM after developing resistance to third-generation EGFR-TKI for patients with EGFR T790M mutation-positive NSCLC; and 3) the progression of LM after developing resistance to first-, second-, or third-generation EGFR-TKIs for patients without EGFR T790M mutation. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Hunan Cancer Hospital. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Data collection

Patient data were retrieved from an electronic medical records database, including demographic and clinical characteristics, tumor characteristics, treatment history, and clinical outcomes. The demographic and clinical characteristics included age, sex, smoking status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance score (ECOG PS), Karnofsky performance score (KPS), and Glasgow coma scale (GCS) at the time of LM diagnosis or when LM progression. is detected. Tumor characteristics included lung cancer histology, disease stage before LM diagnosis, and the detection of specific EGFR mutations. Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results, CSF cytology, CSF NGS, and genetic tests of tissue or blood were also collected for outcome evaluation, along with dates of LM diagnosis, death or last follow-up. Recorded treatments encompassed EGFR-TKIs, chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy, and intrathecal chemotherapy. The determination of the LM diagnosis date was based on the first positive CSF cytology, NGS results, or the initial neuroimaging evidence indicative of LM.

Intracranial progression-free survival (I-PFS) was defined as the duration from the start of IP to the worsening of neurological symptoms, radiological confirmation of brain progression, or patient death. Extracranial progression-free survival (E-PFS) was considered from the start of IP until the progression of extracranial lesion or death. Overall survival (OS) was the time from the start of IP to death or last follow-up. The data cut-off date was September 15, 2023.

Molecular testing

Genetic testing was performed on all baseline tissue samples or pleural effusion specimens from each patient. Some underwent additional biopsies or had their plasma CSF tested. To determine the presence of somatic mutations in tumor tissue or pleural effusion samples, methods such as the amplification-refractory mutation system (ARMS) assay, droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), or DNA-based NGS were employed. Before receiving intrathecal treatment, CSF samples were either discarded or processed for NGS analysis. The gene panels used for DNA-NGS were sourced from two companies: 168 cancer-related genes from Burning Rock Biotech (Guangzhou, China), and 338 cancer-related genes from Geneplus-Beijing Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Protocol for IP

IP was administered to most patients via lumbar puncture, with two patients receiving it through implantable drug delivery systems (IDDS) in the subarachnoid space. Currently, there is no established standard protocol for IP administration. We administered a dose of 50 mg IP based on a previous study [16]. The treatment schedule was initially given on day 1 and 5 for the first week, followed by once every three weeks during the four-week consolidation phase, and then monthly during maintenance until patient refusal, worsening of neurological symptoms, progression of brain metastasis, or death. Patients concurrently received folic acid (400 μg orally daily) and vitamin B12 (1000 μg intramuscularly every 9 weeks), starting 5–7 days before the first pemetrexed administration to mitigate side effects. Pemetrexed lyophilized powder (100 mg; Eli Lilly and Company) was prepared in a 0.9 % saline solution to a concentration of 10 mg/ml. On the day of the treatment, 5 ml was drawn using a sterile syringe for injection either by lumbar puncture or IDDS. To reduce the risk of chemical myelitis, a co-injection of dexamethasone (5 mg) was administered with each IP. Prior to the IP, about 15 ml of CSF was either discarded or utilized for testing. Patients also received individualized systemic and supportive care.

Treatment evaluation

The Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) group for LM suggests three metrics for evaluating treatment response: neurological examination, CSF cytology or flow cytometry for hematological malignancies, and MRI of the neuro-axis with contrast [15]. However, these criteria present challenges. Neurological examinations may not clearly distinguish between LM progression and other neurological declines. CSF cytology often lacks sensitivity, potentially failing to represent accurate changes in meningeal disease. Additionally, MRI can be inconclusive, given that most LM lesions are immeasurable with atypical MRI findings observed in 20–30 % of patients with LM [17,18]. A recent study indicated that the RANO criteria might not be applicable to current clinical trials [19]. In contrast, two previous studies have utilized a clinical response criterion based on the improvement of neurological symptoms/signs and KPS score adjustments [20,21]. This approach delineates five possible outcomes: complete response (CR), obvious response (OR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). A detailed description of these five outcomes is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Effective response is defined as achieving a treatment outcome of CR, OR or PR; whereas non-effective response is defined as a treatment outcome of either SD or PD. The Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines version 1.1 were adopted to assess the response of extracranial disease to treatment. Adverse events (AEs) were classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 4.03).

Statistical analysis

The SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp), R language version 4.3.1 and GraphPad Prism version 9 were used for statistical analysis. The continuous variables were expressed as median (range). Categorical variables were described as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Survival data were analyzed by Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were utilized to identify risk factors for survival. All tests were two-sided, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 789 patients with advanced NSCLC treated with EGFR-TKIs, 79 patients were suspected or confirmed with newly developed or progression of LM during EGFR-TKI treatment, with 29 patients who refused to receive IP. Finally, 50 patients were included and analyzed in this study (Fig. 1). Among them, 40 % (n = 20) were males, the median age was 54 (range: 31–68) years, and all were confirmed to have lung adenocarcinoma. Regarding the EGFR mutations, 15 patients (30 %) had EGFR exon 19 deletion, and 18 patients (36 %) harbored EGFR L858R mutations (Table 1 and Fig. 5). There were 9 patients with LM only, and 41 patients with LM and BM. There was no statistical difference in clinical features and treatment history between patients with and without BM (Supplementary Table 2). The main clinical symptoms included headache and dizziness (92 %; 46/50), weakness (76 %; 38/50), and nausea and vomiting (54 %; 27/50) (Supplementary Table 3). The median interval from NSCLC diagnosis until the development of LM was 25.1 months (range: 0–87.3). The treatment history of the cohort is displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1 and detailed in Supplementary Table 4.

Treatment response and survival

The median time from the initial IP administration until death or last follow-up was 8.9 months (range: 0.3–20.3). According to clinical response criterion based on the improvement of neurological symptoms/signs and KPS score, our study found a 64 % response rate (32/50), where seven patients showed a CR (median OS: unreached), OR was noted in 14 patients (median OS: 13.0 months [range: 9.2–16.8 months]), PR was noted in 11 patients (median OS: 12.0 months [range: 4.8–19.2 months]), 10 patients had SD (median OS: 5.3 months [range: 3.3–7.3 months]), and eight had PD (median OS: 2.2 months [range: 0.0–5.1 months]), and there was no significant difference in the clinical response rate between patients with only LM and those with both BM and LM (77.8 % vs 61.0 %, P = 0.200; Table 2). At the end of follow-up, 27 (54.0 %) patients died, with no patients lost to follow-up. In the overall cohort, the median I-PFS was 5.3 months (95 % confidence intervals [CI], 1.4–9.2 months), the median E-PFS was 8.0 months (95 % CI, 2.2–13.8 months), and the median OS was 12.0 months (95 % CI, 9.6–14.4months) (Fig. 2). The survival rates at six months and one year were 68 % and 34 %, respectively. Patients who responded to IP treatment were more likely to have significantly longer I-PFS, E-PFS and OS (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3). Moreover, responders to IP treatment were more likely to have changes in carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels in the CSF (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Patients who received rechallenged with EGFR-TKI experienced significantly longer I-PFS (P = 0.020), E-PFS (P = 0.009), and a trend of longer OS (P = 0.05) as compared to those who received chemotherapy as systemic therapy (Supplementary Figs. 2A–C). However, OS were comparable for those who developed LM regardless of EGFR-TKI generation, administration of brain radiation, or KPS status (Supplementary Figs. 2D–F). Univariate and multivariate analysis was also applied to identify potential factors related to OS; however, no significant factor was identified (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We also explored and compared the OS between the patients in our cohort who received IP treatment and the 29 patients who were excluded from the study due to their refusal to receive IP treatment. Compared with patients who did not receive IP, patients who received IP treatment had significantly longer OS (12.0 months vs 6.4 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.47 [95 % CI: 0.24–0.91]; P = 0.006; Supplementary Fig. 4). However, it should be noted that the treatment history and clinical features of these two cohorts might differ.

Safety and AEs

No fatal adverse effects related to IP treatment was observed. A total of 32 (64 %) patients experienced any grade AEs. The most common AE was myelosuppression, occurring in 54 % (n = 27), followed by hepatic function impairment in 24 % (n = 12). The majority of AEs were mild, classified as grade 1 or 2. Nonetheless, a significant portion of the patients, 24 % (n = 12), suffered from serious AEs with grade 3 or higher AEs. IP treatment was discontinued in five patients due to AEs. Notably, two of these patients were receiving a combination of systemic chemotherapy and IP, and it's plausible that the severe myelosuppression they experienced was more closely related to systemic chemotherapy rather than IP alone (Table 3).

CSF NGS analysis

A total of 37 patients who received IP submitted their CSF for NGS analysis. The analysis revealed a robust consistency in EGFR driver mutations between CSF and primary tumors, showing a 100 % concordance rate. This underscores the potential of CSF gene testing as a reliable diagnostic tool for LM. The three most prevalent alterations detected from CSF samples of the cohort included EGFR mutations (100 %), TP53 mutations (64.9 %), and CDKN2A mutations (29.7 %).

For the next analysis, patients were categorized into two cohorts: Cohort 1 comprised patients diagnosed with LM after receiving third-generation EGFR-TKI and Cohort 2 included patients diagnosed with LM after receiving first/second-generation EGFR-TKI. A comparison of the CSF and lung tumor specimens from each cohort revealed distinct genetic mutations. CSF samples from Cohort 1 were detected with various concomitant mutations in genes such as TP53 (65.2 %; 15/23), EGFR amplification (43.5 %; 10/23), CDKN2A deletion (30.4 %; 7/23), SMAD4 deletion (21.7 %; 5/23) and BRCA2 (21.7 %; 5/23) (Fig. 5A). In contrast, CSF samples from Cohort 2 were detected with TP53 (65.2 %; 8/14), EGFR amplification (28.6 %; 4/14), CDKN2A deletion (28.6 %; 4/14), CDK6 amplification (21.4 %; 3/14), PDCD1LG2 deletion (14.3 %; 2/14), and FGFR1 deletion (7.1 %; 1/14) (Fig. 5B). This suggests diverse resistance pathways associated with different EGFR-TKI treatments (Fig. 5). We have also attempted to compare the somatic gene mutation profile between patients who achieved response and those who were non-responders to IP treatment. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows that these two groups have very heterogeneous genetic profiles, and specific mutations associated with response cannot be identified due to the limited sample size.

Furthermore, two patients from Cohort 2 submitted CSF samples for NGS testing pre- and post-IP treatment. In patient #1, the allele fraction of EGFR L858R, TP53 missense mutation, SMO missense mutation, and MAP2K2 missense mutation increased from baseline. Additionally, new genetic alterations emerged, including BRCA2 deletion, PIK3CG amplification, MLH1 mutation, RB1 deletion, PTEN deletion, and TMEM127 missense mutation. At the time of LM diagnosis, MET amplification was detected in the CSF, and the patient exhibited neurological improvement following EGFR-TKI and MET-TKI combination therapy (Supplementary Fig. 6A). In patient #33, CSF samples obtained at disease progression from IP treatment showed the absence of MET amplification and FGFR1 amplification but revealed the emergence of TP53 missense mutation, SMO amplification, EGFR amplification, and PTEN deletion (Supplementary Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that IP resistance mechanisms may involve an increase in original mutant allele fractions and the emergence of new mutations such as BRCA2 and PTEN deletions, along with other gene mutations, warranting further research.

Discussion

This study revealed that 64 % of patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC with LM had the potential to benefit from IP treatment, and these benefits were accompanied by manageable side effects. Patients who demonstrated an effective response, as assessed through the improvement of neurological symptoms/signs and KPS scores, tended to experience longer survival outcomes. These findings serve as valuable evidence supporting the utility of intrathecal prophylaxis in the management of LM in patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

IP treatment was associated with a median I-PFS of 5.3 months, median E-PFS of 8.0 months, and OS of 12.0 months. These results compare favorably with the 6.1-month median survival reported in studies of TKI rechallenge in similar patient populations [22], as well as the 3- to 8-month median OS reported in studies utilizing traditional intrathecal drugs [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Notably, the median OS in our study exceeded that of a previous study that reported a median OS of 9.0 months [20], and it is important to highlight the lower dosage of intrathecal prophylaxis utilized in other studies. For instance, the administration of 10 mg intrathecal prophylactic dose of pemetrexed resulted in a 3.8-month median OS, while treatment with 30 mg intrathecal prophylactic dose of pemetrexed via Ommaya Reservoir was associated with a median OS of 9.5 months, suggesting a potential dose-dependent efficacy of pemetrexed [27,28]. In comparison, Zhou et al. reported a median OS of 10.1 months and a median I-PFS of 8.0 months with intracranial chemotherapy combined with systemic treatment, which was slightly shorter than the outcomes observed in our study [29]. Another study demonstrated a median OS of 20 months, but the study had a shorter median follow-up and included patients with various mutations [30]. Another study by Hata et al. directly showed that central nervous system collapse represented a poor prognosis, especially among patients with EGFR T790M mutation and LM [31]. Thus, our study contributes clinical evidence supporting the need to further explore the utility of IP in the treatment of NSCLC with LMs, while emphasizing the need for additional studies to evaluate the optimal IP dosage.

Except for the prognostic profile associated with a particular treatment regimen reported by various studies, the response of the disease to the treatment regimen is also an important consideration for an overall evaluation of the drug's benefit. In our study, KPS score was used to evaluate the patient's functional impairment status, which helped us in classifying patients into subgroups for further analysis, which is in alignment with other studies. Modesto et al. observed an accelerated decline in KPS score in patients from 12 months before death [32]. Moreover, Schodel et al. used KPS score to guide the surgical resection of BM and further improved OS [33].

Regarding the safety of IP treatment, our study aligns with existing literature indicating that myelosuppression and elevation of hepatic aminotransferases were the most frequent AEs associated with pemetrexed chemotherapy [34]. However, myelosuppression observed in our study was manageable, with seven patients recovering and resuming IP therapy. Similarly, liver function returned to normalized levels after the temporary suspension of IP treatment. While other studies on intrathecal prophylaxis have reported AE rates between 35 % and 45 % [20,[27], [28], [29], [30]], our study noted a higher AE incidence of 64 %, possibly attributable to the use of a higher dose of pemetrexed (50 mg) as intrathecal prophylaxis in addition to concurrent systemic treatments. Although AEs were common among the study cohort, the majority were low-grade, and no deaths were directly linked to them. Thus, our study demonstrated that IP treatment is safe with manageable toxicity profile.

Diagnosing LM remains challenging, primarily due to the low sensitivity (as low as 50 %) of CSF cytology, which is traditionally considered as the gold standard. Recognizing this limitation, our study advocates for genetic testing as a preferred approach over routine CSF cytology. This coincided with a study by Zhao et al., which emphasized the advantages of using NGS in analyzing CSF circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [24]. CSF NGS had an impressive sensitivity rate of 100 %, surpassing that of traditional CSF cytology and MRI diagnostics [24]. Therefore, CSF NGS was employed to provide additional diagnostic clarity in cases where neuroimaging and CSF cytology failed to confirm LM. Plasma ctDNA is increasingly acknowledged as a valuable diagnostic tool for cancer, as it is typically absent in benign or non-cancerous conditions [35]. Similarly, CSF ctDNA can prove particularly helpful in diagnosing LM when CSF cytology and neuroimaging consistently yield negative results. In our research, patients who submitted specimens for CSF NGS testing exhibited detectable ctDNA harboring cancer-related genetic mutations, thereby aiding the diagnosis of LM in the absence of positive neuroimaging or cytology. Aligned with the clinical practice guidelines from the European Society for Medical Oncology–European Association of Neuro-Oncology (ESMO-EANO), we recommend that patients with confirmed or probable LM be considered for clinical trials and receive targeted therapies for managing their condition. NGS also provide insights for the exploration of EGFR-TKIs resistance, Ma analyzed 81patients with EGFR T790M mutation and proposed that dynamic cfDNA sequenced by NGS could be helpful for understanding drug resistance mechanisms [36].

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the study, along with the inclusion of a limited number of patients, may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, the absence of a specified systemic regimen in our study introduces the possibility of bias. Third, the unavailability of CSF and plasma samples that were collected at specified time points limits our ability to dynamically monitor the treatment process.

In conclusion, IP emerges as a potentially viable option for the treatment of LM in patients with EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC. Notably, our study observed that patients who exhibited clinical response with IP, based on the improvement of neurological symptoms/signs and KPS scores, were more likely to experience favorable survival outcomes. These findings highlight the potential benefits of incorporating IP into the therapeutic approach for managing LM in patients with NSCLC.
