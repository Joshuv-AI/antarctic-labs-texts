On a Diophantine Inequality with Primes Yielding Square-Free Sums with Given Numbers
Temenoujka P. Peneva, Tatiana L. Todorova

Abstract
. Let α ∈ ℝ ∖ ℚ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q} and β ∈ ℝ \beta\in\mathbb{R} be given. Suppose that a 1 , … , a s a_{1},\ldots,a_{s} are distinct positive integers that do not contain a reduced residue system modulo p 2 p^{2} for any prime p p . 1.
Introduction

Let
r\geq 2
be an integer. A natural number
n
is called
r
-free if it is not divisible by the
r
th power of any prime
p
. In particular, 2-free numbers are also known as square-free numbers.

Define
\mu_{r}(n)
as the characteristic
function of the sequence of
r
-free numbers, i.e.
\mu_{r}(n)
takes the value
1
if
n
is
r
-free, and
0
otherwise. If
\mu
denotes the Möbius function, it is easy to verify that

\mu_{r}(n)=\sum\limits_{d^{r}|n}\mu(d).

Let
s\geq 2
be an integer and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
be distinct positive integers. The frequency of occurrence of systems of
r
-free numbers was first studied in 1936 by Pillai
[
10
]
for
r=2
, who established an asymptotic formula, with an error term
O(x/\log x)
, for the number of systems of square-free numbers
n+a_{1},n+a_{2},\ldots
,
n+a_{s}
not exceeding
x
. This result was later generalized by Mirsky
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
for any
r\geq 2
, who proved that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum\limits_{n\leq x}\mu_{r}(n+a_{1})\ldots\mu_{r}(n+a_{s})=x\prod\limits_{p}\left(1-\frac{\nu(p^{r})}{p^{r}}\right)+O\left(x^{\frac{2}{r+1}+\varepsilon}\right),

(1)

where
\nu(p^{r})
is the number of distinct residue classes modulo
p^{r}
, represented by the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
.

Changa
[
1
]
considered the case where
n
is restricted to the set of prime numbers and obtained that for any
A>0
,

\sum\limits_{p\leq x}\mu_{r}(p+a_{1})\ldots\mu_{r}(p+a_{s})=\pi(x)\prod\limits_{p}\left(1-\frac{\nu^{*}(p^{r})}{\varphi(p^{r})}\right)+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}\right),

(2)

where
\varphi
denotes the Euler function, and
\nu^{*}(p^{r})
is the number of distinct residue classes modulo
p^{r}
that are co-prime with
p
, represented by the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
.

Observe that the infinite product in (1) (respectively, (2)) remains positive as long as, for any prime
p
, the numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{s}
do not contain a complete (respectively, reduced) residue system modulo
p^{r}
.

A more general problem was considered by Hablizel
[
2
]
.
For fixed
r_{1},\ldots,

r_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

2\leq r_{1}\leq\ldots\leq r_{s}
, he derived the asymptotic formula

\sum\limits_{p\leq x}\mu_{r_{1}}(p+a_{1})\ldots\mu_{r_{s}}(p+a_{s})=\frac{x}{\log x}\prod\limits_{p}\left(1-\frac{D^{*}(p)}{\varphi(p^{r_{s}})}\right)+o\left(\frac{x}{\log x}\right),

where
D^{*}(p)
is a computable function of the prime
p
, depending on the choice of the numbers
a_{i}
and
r_{i}
.

Next, suppose that
\alpha
is an irrational number and
\beta
is any real number. A fundamental question in number theory concerns the validity of the Diophantine inequality

||\alpha p+\beta||<p^{-\theta}

(3)

for infinitely many primes
p
, where, as usual,
||y||
denotes the distance from
y
to the nearest integer.

In 1947, I. M. Vinogradov
[
15
]
first demonstrated that if
0<\theta<1/5
, then there exist infinitely many primes
p
such that (
3
) holds. Subsequent research extended the range of the exponent
\theta
, with the most recent result,
0<\theta<1/3
, established by Matomäki
[
6
]
.

A natural variation of this problem involves restricting
p
in inequality (
3
) to a specific subset of prime numbers (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). In this paper, we take the set of primes
p
for which
p+a_{1},\ldots,p+a_{s}
are square-free.

We shall prove the following

Theorem
.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number and
\beta
be a real number. Suppose
s\geq 2
is an integer, and let
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{s}
be positive integers that do not contain a reduced residue system modulo
p^{2}
for any prime
p
. Then, for any
\theta<1/10
, there
exist infinitely many primes
p
satisfying inequality (
3
) such that all the numbers
p+a_{1},\ldots,p+a_{s}
are
square-free.

Notation.
Let
x
be a sufficiently large integer. Define

\Delta=\Delta(x)=x^{-\theta},\quad\,K=\Delta^{-1}\log^{2}x,\quad\mbox{where }\theta<\frac{1}{10}.

(4)

Throughout this paper,
p
denotes a prime number. Instead of writing
m\equiv n\pmod{k}

we use the shorthand notation
m\equiv n\,(k)
. For real
y
, we write
||y||
for the distance from
y
to the
nearest integer,
e(y)=\exp^{2\pi iy}
. As usual,
\mu(n)
,
\varphi(n)
,
\Lambda(n)
, and
\tau_{k}(n)
denote the Möbius function, the Euler function, the von Mangoldt function, and the
k
th divisor function, respectively;
\tau(n)=\tau_{2}(n)
. The function
\nu_{p}(n)
is defined such that
\nu_{p}(n)=k
if
p^{k}|n
but
p^{k+1}\nmid n
.

If
X
and
Y
are positive numbers, the notation
X\asymp Y
signifies that
X\ll Y\ll X
. Furthermore,
n\sim X
indicates that
n
runs through some subinterval of
(X,2X]
, although the precise endpoints may vary depending on the context. The symbol
\varepsilon
denotes an arbitrarily small positive number, whose value may change from one occurrence to another. This convention allows for the use of inequalities such as
X^{\varepsilon}\log X\ll X^{\varepsilon}
.

2.
Auxiliary results

Before launching the proof of Theorem 1, we prepare the ground with some auxiliary results for the reader’s convenience.

The first two statements correspond to Lemmas 8 and 9 of Mennema [8]. They provide average bounds for the divisor function over square-free numbers.

Lemma 1
.

There exists
C_{1}>1
such that for all integer
k\geq 2

and for all real
x\geq 1
,

\sum\limits_{n\leq x}\mu^{2}(n)\tau_{k}(n)\leq C_{1}^{k}x(\log x)^{k-1}.

Lemma 2
.

There exists
C_{2}>1
such that for all integer
k\geq 2

and for all real
x\geq 1
,

\sum\limits_{d>x}\frac{\mu^{2}(d)\tau_{k}(d)}{d^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{2}^{k}(2k-2+\log x)^{k-1}}{x}.

Let
n,\varpi\in\mathbb{N}
. Following the notation of Mennema
[
7
, §3]
, we write

\mu(n)=\mu_{\varpi}(n)\widetilde{\mu}(n),

(5)

where

\mu_{\varpi}(n)=\mu\Bigg(\prod\limits_{p|\varpi}p^{\nu_{p}(n)}\Bigg),\qquad\widetilde{\mu}(n)=\mu\Bigg(\prod\limits_{p\nmid\varpi}p^{\nu_{p}(n)}\Bigg).

(6)

The following two lemmas are Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 3.4 from Mennema
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
n,m,\varpi\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
n\equiv m\pmod{\varpi^{2}}
, and let the function
\mu_{\varpi}
be as in (
6
). Then

\mu_{\varpi}(n)=\mu_{\varpi}(m)
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
n,\varpi\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\widetilde{\mu}
be as in (
6
). Then

\widetilde{\mu}^{2}(n)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{2}|n\\
(d,\,\varpi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

From this point onward, we put
\varpi
to be the constant

\varpi=\prod\limits_{p\leq(a_{s}-a_{1})^{1/2}}p.

(7)

The following lemma is Lemma 3.5 from
[
7
]
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
n,a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
be positive integers, and let
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{s}
. If
d_{i}^{2}|n+a_{i}
,
d_{j}^{2}|n+a_{j}
, and
(d_{i}d_{j},\,\varpi)=1
, then
(d_{i},\,d_{j})=1
for all
i\neq j
.

The proof of our Theorem will depend on estimates of exponential sums. The following statement is a direct consequence of Lemma 4 in
[
4
, Chapter 6, §2]
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
X\geq 1
and
\alpha
be real numbers,
a
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
d\geq 1
. Then

\left|\mathop{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\,(d)\end{subarray}}e(\alpha n)\right|\ll\min\left\{\frac{X}{d},\frac{1}{||\alpha d||}\right\}.

Furthermore, suppose that
\alpha
is a real number with a rational approximation
a/q
satisfying

\left|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{q^{2}},\quad\mbox{where}\;(a,q)=1\;\mbox{and}\;q\geq 1.

(8)

The following lemma is a well-known estimate of Vaughan
[
14
, Chapter 2, §2.1]
.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose that
X,\,Y\geq 1
are real numbers, and that
\alpha
is a real number satisfying (
8
). Then

\sum_{n\leq X}\min\left\{\frac{XY}{n},\frac{1}{||\alpha n||}\right\}\ll XY\left(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{Y}+\frac{q}{XY}\right)\log(2Xq).

The next lemma is a consequence of Matomäki’s result
[
5
, Lemma 8]
.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
x,\,M,\,J\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\varepsilon>0
,

\sum\limits_{m\sim M}\tau_{\mu}(m)\sum\limits_{j\sim J}\tau_{\zeta}(j)\min\left\{\frac{x}{m^{2}j},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha m^{2}j||}\right\}\\
\ll x^{\varepsilon}\left(MJ+\frac{x}{M^{3/2}}+\frac{x}{Mq^{1/2}}+\frac{x^{1/2}q^{1/2}}{M}\right).

The following statement is
[
12

.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that
x,\,M,\,J\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\mu,\,\zeta\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum\limits_{m\sim M}\tau_{\mu}(m)\sum\limits_{j\sim J}\tau_{\zeta}(j)\min\left\{\frac{x}{m^{4}j},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha m^{4}j||}\right\}\\

3.
Proof of the Theorem

We start by observing that there exists a periodic function
\chi
with period 1 such that

\begin{gathered}0<\chi(t)\leq 1\;\mbox{ for }\;-\Delta<t<\Delta,\\
\chi(t)=0\;\mbox{ for }\;\Delta\leq t\leq 1-\Delta,\end{gathered}

and
\chi(t)
admits a Fourier expansion

\chi(t)=\Delta+\Delta\sum\limits_{|k|>0}g(k)e(kt),

(9)

where the Fourier coefficients satisfy

g(k)\ll 1\;\mbox{ for all }\;k\neq 0,\qquad\Delta\sum\limits_{|k|>K}|g(k)|\ll x^{-1}.

(10)

The existence of such a function is a consequence of a lemma of Vinogradov (see
[
4
, Chapter 1, §2]
).

Consider the sum

\Gamma(x)=\sum\limits_{p\sim x}\chi(\alpha p+\beta)\mu^{2}(p+a_{1})\ldots\mu^{2}(p+a_{s}).

To prove our theorem, it suffices to determine the
constant
\theta
such that
there exists a sequence of positive integers
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}x_{j}=\infty\qquad

(11)

and

\Gamma(x_{j})\geq\frac{C\Delta(x_{j})\,x_{j}}{\log x_{j}},\,\quad j=1,2,3,\ldots

(12)

with some absolute constant
C>0
.

Applying the Fourier expansion (
9
) and the inequalities (
10
), we obtain

\Gamma(x)=\Delta\left(\Gamma_{1}(x)+\Gamma_{2}(x)\right)+O(1),

(13)

where

\Gamma_{1}(x)=\sum\limits_{p\sim x}\mu^{2}(p+a_{1})\ldots\mu^{2}(p+a_{s}),

and

\Gamma_{2}(x)=\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{p\sim x}\,\mu^{2}(p+a_{1})\ldots\mu^{2}(p+a_{s})e(\alpha kp),\,

(14)

with
c(k):=g(k)e(\beta k)
, satisfying

c(k)\ll 1\;\mbox{ for all }\;k\neq 0.

(15)

Consider the sum
\Gamma_{1}(x)
. Changa’s asymptotic formula (
2
) yields that for any
A>0
,

\Gamma_{1}(x)=\mathfrak{S}\left(\pi(2x)-\pi(x)\right)+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}\right),

where

\mathfrak{S}=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{\nu^{*}(p^{2})}{p(p-1)}\right)

is the infinite product in (
2
) for
r=2
. Observe that
\mathfrak{S}>0
, since for the given
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
, every factor in
\mathfrak{S}
is positive, and the factor corresponding to
p
is at least
1-s/(p(p-1))
for all sufficiently large values of
p
.
According to Rosser and Schoenfeld’s classic estimate
[
11
, Corollary 3]
, for
x\geq 20.5
,

\pi(2x)-\pi(x)>\frac{3x}{5\log x}.

Thus, it follows that

\Gamma_{1}(x)>\frac{\mathfrak{S}x}{2\log x},

(16)

for sufficiently large
x
.

The estimate of
\Gamma_{2}(x)
is postponed until Section 4.

Now, let
\{q_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence
of values of
q
that satisfy (
8
). In view of (
4
) and the estimate of
\Gamma_{2}(x)
obtained in Section
4.5
, we define a sequence
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
by setting

x_{j}=q_{j}^{20/7},\quad j=1,2,\ldots\,.

Condition (
11
) is clearly satisfied. Furthermore, for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
and any
A>0
, we have

\Gamma_{2}(x_{j})\ll x_{j}^{9/10+\varepsilon}K\ll\frac{x_{j}}{(\log x_{j})^{A}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots\,.

(17)

Using (
13
), (
16
) and (
17
), we deduce the estimate (
12
)
with some absolute constant
C<\mathfrak{S}/2
, thus completing the proof of the Theorem.

4.
The estimation of the sum
\Gamma_{2}

In this section, we estimate the sum
\Gamma_{2}(x)
, as defined in
(
14
)
.

4.1.
Preparation

We begin by adapting Mennema’s argument from
[
7
, §3]
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

f(n)=\mu_{\varpi}^{2}(n+a_{1})\ldots\mu_{\varpi}^{2}(n+a_{s}),

(18)

where
\mu_{\varpi}
and
\varpi
are given in (
6
) and (
7
), respectively.
Obviously,
f(n)=1
if
(n+a_{i},\varpi^{2})
is square-free for all
i
, and
f(n)=0
otherwise. In all cases,

0\leq f(n)\leq 1\quad\mbox{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(19)

Now, using (
5
), (
18
), and Lemma
4
, we can express

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{p\sim x}\,f(p)\,\tilde{\mu}^{2}(p+a_{1})\ldots\tilde{\mu}^{2}(p+a_{s})e(\alpha kp)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\sim x\end{subarray}}f(p)e(\alpha kp)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}^{2}|p+a_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi)=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s}).

Next, we decompose the sum over
p
into sums over residue classes modulo
\varpi^{2}
. Noting that, by Lemma
3
,
p\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})
implies
f(p)=f(t)
, and changing the order of summation, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{1\leq t\leq\varpi^{2}}f(t)\!\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\sim x\\
p\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\end{subarray}}e(\alpha kp)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}^{2}|p+a_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi)=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{1\leq t\leq\varpi^{2}}f(t)\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{i}\leq X_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi)=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\!\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\sim x\\
p\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\\
p\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}e(\alpha kp),

with

X_{i}:=(2x+a_{i})^{1/2},\qquad X_{i}\asymp x^{1/2},\qquad i=1,\ldots,s.

Note that if there exists an index
i
such that
d_{i}
and
a_{i}
share a common prime factor
p^{\prime}
, then the condition
p\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})
implies that
p^{\prime}
divides
p
, which further leads to
p^{\prime}=p
and
p^{\prime}\sim x
. However, this contradicts
p^{\prime}\leq d_{i}\ll x^{1/2}
. Consequently, we may assume
(d_{i},a_{i})=1
, and hence
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1
for
i=1,\ldots,s
. Following the same line of reasoning, we conclude
(t,\varpi)=1
. By using (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\varpi^{2}\max\limits_{1\leq t\leq\varpi^{2}\atop{(t,\varpi)=1}}\left|\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{i}\leq X_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\!\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\sim x\\
p\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\\
p\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}e(\alpha kp)\right|.

By partial summation, we have

\Gamma_{2}(x)\ll(\log x)^{-1}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<y\leq 2x\\
1\leq t\leq\varpi^{2}\\
(t,\varpi)=1\end{subarray}}\big|\Gamma_{3}(y)\big|+O(Kx^{1/2}\log x),

(20)

where

\Gamma_{3}(y)=\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{i}\leq X_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\\
n\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)e(\alpha kn).

It is evident that the conditions imposed on the summation over
d_{i}
’s imply
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}

\in[1,X_{1}\ldots X_{s}]
. Thus, we decompose this interval as:

[1,X_{1}\ldots X_{s}]=\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{3},

where the intervals are defined as:

\mathcal{I}_{1}=(y^{1/2},X_{1}\ldots X_{s}],\qquad\mathcal{I}_{2}=(y^{1/5},y^{1/2}],\qquad\mathcal{I}_{3}=[1,y^{1/5}].

Consequently, we express the sum
\Gamma_{3}(y)
as the sum of three components:

\Gamma_{3}(y)=\mathcal{U}_{1}+\mathcal{U}_{2}+\mathcal{U}_{3},

(21)

with each
\mathcal{U}_{i}
defined by

\mathcal{U}_{i}=\sum\limits_{0<|k|\leq K}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{i}\leq X_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\end{subarray}}\!\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\\
n\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\!\Lambda(n)e(\alpha kn).

(22)

The remainder of this paper is devoted to estimating these quantities.

4.2.
Estimating
\mathcal{U}_{1}

To estimate
\mathcal{U}_{1}
, we follow Mennema’s approach from
[
7
, §3]
.

First, we impose the ordering constraint
d_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d_{s}
, which introduces an additional factor of
s!
in our estimate. For integers
r
satisfying
2\leq r\leq s
, we define

\alpha_{r}=\frac{s-r+2}{s-r+1},\qquad A_{r}=y^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{r}}}=y^{\frac{s-r+1}{2s-2r+3}},

and the corresponding set

\begin{split}&\mathcal{D}_{r}=\bigg\{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}\;:\end{split}\quad\;\begin{split}&d_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d_{s},\\
&d_{r}\ldots d_{s}\leq A_{r},\end{split}\quad\;\begin{split}&(d_{i},\varpi)=1\mbox{ for all }i\;\\
&d_{r-1}\ldots d_{s}>A_{r-1}\end{split}\bigg\}.

Notice that, according to Lemma
5
, we have
(d_{i},d_{j})=1
whenever
i\neq j
. Since
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}>y^{1/2}
, there exists an
r
such that
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{s})\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
(see
[
7
, Remark 3.7]
).
On writing

\delta=d_{r-1}\ldots d_{s},

we deduce
A_{r-1}<\delta\leq A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}
and
(\delta,\varpi)=1
(see
[
7
, p. 20]
). Therefore, the contribution of
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{s})\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
to the sum
\mathcal{U}_{1}
is

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{0<k\leq K}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{r-1}<\delta\leq A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}\\
(\delta,\,\varpi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(\delta)\tau_{s-r+2}(\delta)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=r-1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d_{i}\leq X_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
d_{i}^{2}|n+a_{i}\\
i=1,\ldots,r-2\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}K\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{r-1}<\delta\leq A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}\\
(\delta,\,\varpi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(\delta)\tau_{s-r+2}(\delta)\max\limits_{1\leq a\leq\delta^{2}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv a\,(\delta^{2})\end{subarray}}\tau(n+a_{1})\ldots\tau(n+a_{r-2})

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}K\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{r-1}<\delta\leq A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}\\
(\delta,\,\varpi)=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(\delta)\tau_{s-r+2}(\delta)\left(\frac{y}{\delta^{2}}+1\right),

where we have used (
15
) and the well-known estimate

\tau_{k}(n)\ll_{k,\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}.

(23)

Applying Lemmas
1
and
2
, we deduce

\mathcal{U}_{1}\ll y^{\varepsilon}K\sum_{r=2}^{s}\left(A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}(\log A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}})^{s}+\frac{y(2s+\log A_{r-1})^{s}}{A_{r-1}}\right).

For
2\leq r\leq s
, it is easy to verify that

A_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}=y^{\frac{s-r+2}{2s-2r+3}}\leq y^{2/3},\qquad\frac{y}{A_{r-1}}<\frac{y}{A_{r}}=y^{\frac{s-r+2}{2s-2r+3}}\leq y^{2/3}.

Thus, we conclude that

\mathcal{U}_{1}\ll y^{2/3+\varepsilon}K.

(24)

4.3.
Estimating
\mathcal{U}_{2}

We estimate the sum
\mathcal{U}_{2}
, as defined in (
22
), fairly straightforwardly. Setting
\tilde{d}=d\varpi
and applying (
15
) and (
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{2}

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{0<k\leq K}\;\;\sum\limits_{\varpi y^{1/5}<\tilde{d}\leq\varpi y^{1/2}}\tau_{s+1}(\tilde{d})\max\limits_{1\leq a\leq\tilde{d}^{2}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv a\,(\tilde{d}^{2})\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}K\sum\limits_{\varpi y^{1/5}\leq\tilde{d}\leq\varpi y^{1/2}}\left(\frac{y}{{\tilde{d}}^{2}}+1\right)

\displaystyle\ll y^{4/5+\varepsilon}K.

(25)

4.4.
Estimating
\mathcal{U}_{3}

By a dyadic decomposition of the summation ranges, we write
\mathcal{U}_{3}
, defined in (
22
), as a sum of
O(\log^{s+1}x)
sums of the type

W=\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\sim D_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\in\mathcal{I}_{3}\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\sim y\\
n\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})\\
n\equiv-a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)e(\alpha kn),

where

1\leq K_{0}\ll K,\qquad 1\leq D_{i}\ll X_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,s.

(26)

Define

D=D_{1}\ldots D_{s}.

The conditions
d_{i}\sim D_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,s
imply that
D\leq d_{1}\dots d_{s}\leq 2^{s}D
. Since
d_{1}\dots d_{s}\in\mathcal{I}_{3}
, we deduce

1\leq D\leq y^{1/5}.

(27)

With this inequality in mind, we proceed to replace the condition
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\in\mathcal{I}_{3}
with
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\asymp D
in the sum over
d_{i}
’s.

Applying Heath-Brown’s identity
[
3
]
with parameters

u=2^{-7}y^{\frac{1}{5}},\quad v=2^{7}y^{\frac{1}{3}},\quad w=y^{\frac{2}{5}},

we decompose the sum
W
as a linear combination of
O(\log^{6}x)

sums of type I and type II. The type I sums are

W_{1}=\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\sim D_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\asymp D\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\sum_{m\sim M}a(m)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\sim L\\
m\ell\equiv t(\varpi^{2})\\
m\ell\equiv-a_{i}(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}\!a(m)e(\alpha m\ell k),

and

W^{\prime}_{1}=\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\sim D_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\asymp D\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\sum_{m\sim M}a(m)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\sim L\\
m\ell\equiv t(\varpi^{2})\\
m\ell\equiv-a_{i}(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}e(\alpha m\ell k)\log\ell,

where

ML\asymp y,\quad L\geq w,\quad a(m)\ll y^{\varepsilon}.

(28)

The type II sums are

W_{2}=\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}c(k)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\sim D_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\asymp D\end{subarray}}\mu(d_{1})\ldots\mu(d_{s})\sum_{\ell\sim L}b(\ell)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\sim M\\
m\ell\equiv t(\varpi^{2})\\
m\ell\equiv-a_{i}(d_{i}^{2})\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}a(m)b(\ell)e(\alpha m\ell k),

where

ML\asymp y,\quad u\leq L\leq v,\quad a(m),\;b(\ell)\ll y^{\varepsilon}.

(29)

Recalling (
27
), in Sections
4.4.1
and
4.4.2
we establish bounds for the sums
W_{1}
,
W_{1}^{\prime}
and
W_{2}
, and hence for
W
, under the
assumptions
y^{3/20}\leq D\leq y^{1/5}

and
1\leq D\leq y^{3/20}
, respectively.

4.4.1.
Estimate of
W
in the case
y^{3/20}\leq D\leq y^{1/5}

Let us denote
\tilde{d}=\varpi\,d_{1}\ldots d_{s}
. Since
(t,\varpi)=1
,
(d_{i},\,\varpi a_{i})=1
, and
(d_{i},d_{j})=1
for
i\neq j
, the Chinese Remainder Theorem implies that the system of congruences
n\equiv t\,(\varpi^{2})
,
n\equiv a_{i}\,(d_{i}^{2}),\,i=1,...,s
, has a unique solution
a
modulo
\tilde{d}^{2}
, with
(a,\tilde{d})=1
. Thus, using estimates (
15
), (
23
), and (
28
), we obtain

{W}_{1}\ll y^{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\;\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\tau_{s+1}(\tilde{d})\;\sum\limits_{m\sim M}\;\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq\tilde{d}^{2}\\
(a,\tilde{d})=1\end{subarray}}\left|\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\sim L\\
m\ell\equiv a(\tilde{d}^{2})\end{subarray}}e(\alpha m\ell k)\right|.

It is clear that if
(m,\tilde{d})>1
, then for any integer
a
satisfying
(a,\tilde{d})=1
, the congruence
m\ell\equiv a\,(\tilde{d}^{2})
admits no solution for
\ell
. Conversely, if
(m,\tilde{d})=1
, the congruence
m\ell\equiv a\,(\tilde{d}^{2})
has a unique solution for
\ell
modulo
\tilde{d}^{2}
. Applying Lemma
6
, we obtain

W_{1}\ll y^{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\;\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\;\sim\varpi D}\;\sum\limits_{m\sim M}\min\bigg\{\frac{yK_{0}}{mk\tilde{d}^{2}},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha mk\tilde{d}^{2}||}\bigg\}.

(30)

By setting
h=mk
and employing (
26
) and (
28
), along with Lemma
8
, we derive

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{h\sim MK_{0}}\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\min\bigg\{\frac{yK_{0}}{h\tilde{d}^{2}},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha h\tilde{d}^{2}||}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}\left(y^{4/5}K+\frac{y^{17/20}K}{q^{1/2}}+y^{7/20}K^{1/2}q^{1/2}\right).

(31)

We get the same estimate for
W_{1}^{\prime}
by partial summation.

To estimate
W_{2}
, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality repeatedly, and appeal to (
15
), (
23
), and (
29
) to deduce

W_{2}^{2}\ll y^{\varepsilon}\left(y^{9/5}K^{2}+W_{21}\right),

(32)

where

\displaystyle W_{21}

\displaystyle\ll MDK\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\sim D_{i}\\
(d_{i},\varpi a_{i})=1\\
i=1,\ldots,s\\
d_{1}\ldots d_{s}\asymp D\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\sim L\\
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\end{subarray}}b(\ell_{1})b(\ell_{2})\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\sim M\\
m\ell_{j}\equiv t(\varpi^{2})\\
m\ell_{j}\equiv-a_{i}(d_{i}^{2})\\
j=1,2\\
i=1,\ldots,s\end{subarray}}e(\alpha m(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})k)

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}MDK\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\tau_{s+1}(\tilde{d})\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\sim L\\
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\end{subarray}}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq\tilde{d}^{2}\\
(a,\tilde{d})=1\end{subarray}}\left|\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\sim M\\
m\ell_{j}\equiv a(\tilde{d}^{2})\\
j=1,2\end{subarray}}e(\alpha m(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})k)\right|,

by reasoning analogously to the estimation of the sum
W_{1}
. From
\ell_{1}\not=\ell_{2}
and
m\ell_{j}\equiv a(\tilde{d}^{2})
for
j=1,2
, we have
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}+z\tilde{d}^{2}
.
By setting
h=zk
, we have

\displaystyle W_{21}

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}MDK\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\sum\limits_{\ell_{2}\sim L}\sum\limits_{z\leq L/\tilde{d}^{2}}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq\tilde{d}^{2}\\
(a,\tilde{d})=1\end{subarray}}\left|\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\sim M\\
m\ell_{2}\equiv a(\tilde{d}^{2})\end{subarray}}e(\alpha mt\tilde{d}^{2}k)\right|.

As discussed previously, the congruence
m\ell_{2}\equiv a\,(\tilde{d}^{2})
either admits no solutions for
m
or exactly one solution for
m
modulo
\tilde{d}^{2}
. Consequently, by using Lemma
6
, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{21}

\displaystyle\ll y^{\varepsilon}MLDK\sum\limits_{k\sim K_{0}}\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\sum\limits_{z\leq L/\tilde{d}^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{yK_{0}}{\tilde{d}^{4}tk},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha\tilde{d}^{4}tk||}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll y^{1+\varepsilon}DK\sum\limits_{h\ll\frac{LK_{0}}{\varpi^{2}D^{2}}}\sum\limits_{\tilde{d}\sim\varpi D}\min\left\{\frac{yK_{0}}{\tilde{d}^{4}h},\,\frac{1}{||\alpha{\tilde{d}}^{4}h||}\right\}.

(33)

Applying Lemma
9
and appealing to (
26
), (
29
), and (
32
), we get

W_{2}\ll y^{\varepsilon}\left(y^{9/10}K+\frac{y^{17/20}K}{q^{1/16}}+y^{63/80}K^{15/16}q^{1/16}\right).

(34)

From (
31
) and (
34
), we obtain that for
y^{3/20}\leq D\ll y^{1/5}
,

W\ll y^{\varepsilon}\left(y^{9/10}K+y^{63/80}K^{15/16}q^{1/16}+\frac{y^{17/20}K}{q^{1/16}}+y^{7/20}K^{1/2}q^{1/2}\right).

(35)

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{3}\ll y^{\varepsilon}
