Abstract
. Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) are critical for enterprise operations, yet existing language models struggle with SOP understanding and cross-domain generalization. Current methods fail because joint training cannot differentiate between reasoning capabilities that SOP requires: terminology precision, sequential ordering, and constraint reasoning. We propose FM SO.P, solving these challenges through two novelties. First, we introduce progressive task mixtures that build capabilities by stages across three task types with cumulative data: concept disambiguation for terminology precision, action sequence understanding for procedural correctness, and scenario-aware graph reasoning for conditional logic. Second, we propose an automatic multi-agent evaluation system consisting of three agents that adaptively generate rubrics, stratified test sets, and rubric scoring, adapting to domains (e.g., temporal constraints for DMV, regulatory compliance for banking). Evaluated on SOPBench across seven domains (Bank, DMV, Healthcare, Market, University, Library, Hotel), FM SO.P achieves 48.3% pass rate with our 32B model and 34.3% with our opensource 7B model, matching Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct baseline (34.4%) with 10× fewer parameters.

2.
Method

2.1.
Problem Formulation and Motivation

2.1.1.
Formal Problem Definition

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{N}\}
denote a collection of domain-specific SOPs, where each SOP
d_{i}
consists of a structured document containing procedural knowledge. An SOP understanding task can be formalized as a mapping function:

(1)

f_{\theta}:(\mathcal{C},Q)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}

where
\mathcal{C}
represents contextual information (SOP documents, user state, constraints),
Q
denotes a query or problem scenario,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space of valid procedural responses, and
\theta
represents model parameters.

The challenge lies in three interconnected dimensions:

(1)

Concept Disambiguation:
Given domain terminology
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{K}\}
, distinguish semantically similar but contextually distinct terms. Formally, for terms
t_{i},t_{j}
with high similarity, we require:

(2)

P(t_{i}|C_{1})\neq P(t_{j}|C_{2})\text{ where }C_{1}\neq C_{2}

(2)

Sequential Dependency Understanding:
For a procedural workflow
W=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle
, verify ordering constraints:

(3)

\forall i<j:\text{precond}(a_{j})\subseteq\text{effect}(a_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\text{effect}(a_{i})

where
\text{precond}(a)
and
\text{effect}(a)
denote preconditions and effects of action
a
.

(3)

Contextual Constraint Reasoning:
Given a context
c\in\mathcal{C}
and an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, evaluate conditional validity:

(4)

\text{valid}(a|c)=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\text{constraint}_{i}(a,c)

2.1.2.
Limitations of Existing Methods

Existing methods suffer from three limitations that prevent effective SOP understanding.

First, joint training fails to recognize that procedural understanding requires different types of reasoning capabilities. Distinguishing between samples that require basic concept recognition, sequential reasoning, and contextual reasoning, is essential, yet they treat all samples identically without recognizing that these represent fundamentally different task types.

Second, they lack explicit contrastive signals showing what models should not do. For a correct procedure
W^{+}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle
, models never observe systematically constructed incorrect variants
W^{-}
that differ minimally from correct workflows yet violate essential procedural constraints, preventing them from learning to distinguish subtle procedural violations across different reasoning task types.

Third, existing evaluation relies on fixed metrics that apply uniform criteria across domains. Such metrics cannot capture domain-specific requirements, e.g., temporal constraint satisfaction in DMV domains, policy compliance verification in banking, or patient safety validation in healthcare, which makes adaptive rubrics necessary that depend on domain characteristics rather than fixed weighted combinations of surface-level similarity metrics.

2.2.
Progressive Task Mixture Framework

2.2.1.
Three-Stage Architecture

Our framework structures training through three progressive stages with cumulative data. At stage
k
, the model trains on:

(5)

\mathcal{D}^{(k)}_{\text{train}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{D}_{i}

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}
contains concept disambiguation pairs,
\mathcal{D}_{2}
adds action sequence pairs, and
\mathcal{D}_{3}
incorporates graph reasoning pairs.

Difference from Curriculum Learning
: Unlike curriculum learning ordering that samples by difficulty within single task types, our progressive mixture introduces fundamentally different task types at each stage with cumulative data where all previous types remain in training.

2.2.2.
Stage 1: Concept Disambiguation Task

SOP understanding requires distinguishing domain-specific terminology where lexical similarity does not imply semantic equivalence. For example, in banking SOPs, “refund” and “reimbursement” may trigger entirely different workflows.

For each concept term
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we generate positive-negative question-answer pairs. Given a query
q
containing term
t
and context
c\in\mathcal{C}
, we construct

(6)

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\left\{(q,c,a^{+},\{a_{j}^{-}\}_{j=1}^{m})\right\},

where
a^{+}=f_{\text{correct}}(q,c,t)
is the correct answer using term
t
in context
c
, and negative answers
a_{j}^{-}
are generated by substituting semantically similar but contextually inappropriate terms.

Learning Objective.

We optimize a contrastive loss

(7)

\mathcal{L}_{1}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(q,c,a^{+},\{a_{j}^{-}\})\sim\mathcal{P}_{1}}\left[\log\frac{e^{s^{+}/\tau}}{e^{s^{+}/\tau}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{s_{j}^{-}/\tau}}\right],

where
s^{+}=s_{\theta}(q,c,a^{+})
,
s^{-}=s_{\theta}(q,c,a^{-})
,
s_{\theta}(q,c,a)
represents the model’s scoring function for answer
a
given query
q
and context
c
, and
\tau
is a temperature parameter.

2.2.3.
Stage 2: Action Sequence Understanding Task

Procedural correctness requires understanding sequential dependencies and ordering constraints that cannot be captured by concept-level understanding alone. This stage introduces a new task type focused on sequential reasoning.

For a correct workflow
W^{+}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle
, we systematically generate negative samples through error injection operators:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\text{order}}(W^{+})

\displaystyle=\{W^{\prime}|\exists i<j:W^{\prime}=\langle\ldots,a_{j},\ldots,a_{i},\ldots\rangle\},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\text{omit}}(W^{+})

\displaystyle=\{W^{\prime}|W^{\prime}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle,i\in[m]\},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\text{insert}}(W^{+})

\displaystyle=\{W^{\prime}|W^{\prime}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{i},a_{\text{spurious}},a_{i+1},\ldots\rangle\}.

The complete negative set is

(11)

\mathcal{W}^{-}=\mathcal{O}_{\text{order}}(W^{+})\cup\mathcal{O}_{\text{omit}}(W^{+})\cup\mathcal{O}_{\text{insert}}(W^{+}).

The loss is similar to Eq.
7
, while
s^{+}=S_{\theta}(c,W^{+})
and
s^{-}=S_{\theta}(c,W^{-})
,
W^{-}\in\mathcal{W}^{-}
, compute a sequence-level score for workflow
W
given context
c
.

Figure 3.
Automatic Multi-Agent Evaluation System. Agent 1 analyzes SOP corpus to generate domain-adaptive rubrics
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D})=\{(r_{i},w_{i})\}
with dimensions and weights. Agent 2 creates stratified test set
\mathcal{B}
across complexity levels, question types, and rubric coverage. Agent 3 computes multi-dimensional scores
\mathbf{e}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{|\mathcal{D}|}]^{\top}
and aggregates as
e_{\text{final}}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot e_{i}
, adapting to domain requirements.

2.2.4.
Stage 3: Scenario-Aware Graph Reasoning Task

Real-world SOP requires reasoning over complex dependencies where actions may have conditional applicability based on context states, temporal constraints, and cross-procedural dependencies. This stage introduces a graph-structured reasoning task type.

We represent each SOP scenario as a Directed Action Graph (DAG):

(12)

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\phi),

where
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
are action nodes,
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
are directed dependency edges indicating precondition relationships, and
\phi:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow 2^{\Phi}
maps each action node to its set of required preconditions from the precondition space
\Phi
. An edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
indicates that action
v_{i}
must be completed before
v_{j}
can be executed.

Similar to Eq.
11
, We generate negative graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\text{cycle}}^{-}
,
\mathcal{G}_{\text{precond}}^{-}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\text{edge}}^{-}
through constraint violations, where
\mathcal{G}_{\text{cycle}}^{-}
introduces cyclic dependencies violating execution order,
\mathcal{G}_{\text{precond}}^{-}
removes required preconditions for critical actions, and
\mathcal{G}_{\text{edge}}^{-}
adds or removes dependency edges creating invalid SOPs. The loss is also similar to Eq.
7
while
s^{+}=R_{\theta}(c,\mathcal{G}^{+})
and
s^{-}=R_{\theta}(c,\mathcal{G}^{-})
combine textual context and graph representations.
R_{\theta}(c,\mathcal{G})
represents the model’s scoring function for graph
\mathcal{G}
given context
c
, implemented through structured prompting that presents the graph’s nodes, edges, and constraints to the language model.

Final Training Objective.

The complete training loss optimizes

(13)

\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\left[\alpha_{1}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\theta;\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\text{train}})+\alpha_{2}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\theta;\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{\text{train}})+\alpha_{3}\mathcal{L}_{3}(\theta;\mathcal{D}^{(3)}_{\text{train}})\right],

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}>0
are loss weights satisfying
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=1
.

2.3.
Automatic Multi-Agent Evaluation System

After our progressive task mixture framework for training,
we now solve the evaluation challenge: how to evaluate SOP understanding
across diverse domains with varying requirements. Traditional evaluation faces a fundamental limitation: fixed metrics
apply uniform criteria across all domains, failing to capture that banking
SOPs require regulatory compliance assessment while healthcare SOPs need
patient safety verification. We propose a three-agent automatic evaluation system that generates
adaptive rubrics, stratified test sets, and rubric
scoring. This enables scalable evaluation
that automatically adapts to different domains.

2.3.1.
Agent 1: Adaptive Rubric Generation

The first agent analyzes the SOP corpus
\mathcal{D}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}\}
to generate a domain-adaptive rubric
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D})=\{(r_{i},w_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each tuple consists of a rubric dimension
r_{i}
and its normalized weight
w_{i}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
.

The prompt instructs the agent to identify rubrics (e.g., policy compliance, temporal constraint validation), determining their relative importance through domain characteristic analysis, and specifying concrete scoring criteria for each rubric. This generation ensures that rubrics adapt to domain requirements. For instance, banking SOPs emphasize regulatory compliance and audit trails, while healthcare SOPs prioritize patient safety protocols and privacy protection.

2.3.2.
Agent 2: Test Set Generation

The second agent generates a stratified test set
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{B}_{l}
from the SOP corpus
\mathcal{D}
and rubric
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D})
, where each subset
\mathcal{B}_{l}
contains test questions at complexity level
l
. We stratify test generation across three dimensions: (1) complexity levels ranging from basic factual recall to multi-step reasoning, (2) question types including factual, sequential, conditional, and multi-hop questions, and (3) rubric dimension coverage ensuring that each dimension
r_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
is tested by at least
n_{\min}
questions.

2.3.3.
Agent 3: Rubric Scoring

The third agent evaluates model outputs against reference answers using the adaptive rubric. For each test question
(q,a^{*})\in\mathcal{B}
and model output
\hat{a}
, the agent computes an evaluation vector
\mathbf{e}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}]^{\top}
where each component
e_{i}
measures performance on rubric dimension
r_{i}
by comparing
\hat{a}
against
a^{*}
.

The reference answers
a^{*}
are generated using a separate LLM that is independent from all agents in our framework. We acknowledge that without human annotation, this method is necessary for automatic evaluation at scale. The generated reference answers serve as pseudo ground truth for our evaluation system. We emphasize that the primary validation of our framework comes from experimental results on publicly available benchmarks (§
3.2
), where we compare against SOTA baselines using standardized test sets. Our automatic evaluation system should be viewed as a functional component that enables scalable, domain-adaptive evaluation, complementing rather than replacing benchmark-based validation.

The final weighted score aggregates across all dimensions:

(14)

e_{\text{final}}=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}w_{i}\cdot e_{i}.

3.
Experiments

3.1.
Experimental Setup

3.1.1.
Datasets and Domains

We evaluate our framework on SOPBench
(
21
)
, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating language agents on SOPs. SOPBench covers seven diverse domains: Banking (Bank), Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV), Healthcare (Health), Marketplace (Market), University Administration (Univ), Library Services (Lib), and Hotel Management (Hotel), with verified test cases spanning multiple complexity levels and constraint types.

For each domain, we construct training data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}\cup\mathcal{D}_{3}
following the three-stage task mixture design: concept disambiguation task pairs (
\mathcal{D}_{1}
), action sequence task pairs with procedural errors (
\mathcal{D}_{2}
), and graph-structured scenario reasoning task pairs (
\mathcal{D}_{3}
).

3.1.2.
Implementation Details

We implement FM SO.P based on Qwen-2.5 series models: Qwen-2.5-7B, Qwen-2.5-14B, and Qwen-2.5-32B. Training is conducted using the unified objective in Eq.
13
with loss weights
\alpha_{1}=1/3
,
\alpha_{2}=1/3
,
\alpha_{3}=1/3
. We use AdamW optimizer with learning rate
1\times 10^{-4}
, batch size 256, and train for 12 epochs. Temperature parameter
\tau=1
for all contrastive losses. All experiments are conducted on
8\times
NVIDIA H100-80GB GPUs with mixed-precision training (bfloat16).

3.1.3.
Baseline Methods

We compare FM SO.P with baseline models from three categories, following the SOPBench evaluation protocol. The first category includes
proprietary non-reasoning models
: GPT-4.1 (FC), GPT-4o (FC), Claude-3-7-Sonnet (FC), GPT-4.1-mini (FC), Claude-3-5-Sonnet (FC), GPT-4o-mini (FC), Gemini-2.0-Flash (FC), and Gemini-1.5-Pro (FC), where FC indicates function-calling capability. The second category consists of
reasoning models
that use explicit reasoning mechanisms such as ReAct or extended thinking processes, including o4-mini-high (FC), Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking (ReAct), Deepseek-R1 (ReAct), and Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (FC). The third category includes
open-source models
representing state-of-the-art alternatives with various parameter scales: Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (ReAct), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (ReAct), Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct (ReAct), Qwen-2.5-14B-Instruct (ReAct), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (ReAct), and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct (ReAct). For fair comparison, we apply our FM SO.P training framework to the same base architectures used in open-source baselines (Qwen-2.5-7B, Qwen-2.5-14B, and Qwen-2.5-32B).

3.1.4.
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate model performance using two main metrics.
Pass rate
measures the percentage of test cases where the model output correctly solves the SOP task according to ground truth action sequences, providing a standardized measure for comparing different models. This metric captures whether models can generate procedurally correct, complete, and properly ordered action sequences that satisfy all task requirements. We also compute
Borda count

(
8
;
30
;
12
)
based on group performance comparisons. Across all test cases, we determine which method produces better outputs, and the Borda count aggregates these win rates to produce a group ranking that accounts for relative performance rather than absolute scores alone. This metric is particularly valuable when comparing methods with different inference strategies or when performance distributions overlap significantly.

3.2.
Main Results

3.2.1.
Overall Performance Comparison

Table
1
shows comparison between FM SO.P and baseline models across all seven domains. FM SO.P (Qwen-2.5-7B) achieves 34.33% overall pass rate, representing a +23.03% improvement over the base Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct model (11.30%). The 14B variant achieves 39.67% (+7.78% over its 31.89% base), and most notably, the 32B variant reaches 48.30% (+8.65% over its 39.65% base), establishing new state-of-the-art performance among open-source models. Our results demonstrate the effectiveness of progressive task mixture for SOP understanding, with consistent improvements scaling across model sizes.

Comparing the parameter efficiency, FM SO.P (Qwen-2.5-7B) matches Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct performance (34.33% vs 34.44%) with 10
\times
fewer parameters. FM SO.P (Qwen-2.5-32B) largely outperforms Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (48.30% vs 34.44%/41.20%, +13.86%/7.10%) while using 2.25/2.2
\times
fewer parameters, and achieves competitive performance with reasoning models like Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (53.27%) without inference-time reasoning mechanisms. This parameter efficiency validates that task mixture design can compensate for scale disadvantages.

3.3.2.
Impact of Negative Sample Ratio.

Fig.
5
shows performance across varying positive-to-negative ratios (1:4, 1:8, 1:20) for our three training stages. For the 7B model, Stage 1 (concept disambiguation) performs best at 1:4 ratio, Stage 2 (action sequence understanding) maintains best at 1:4, while Stage 3 (graph reasoning) achieves highest performance at 1:8. The 14B model at Stage 3 shows similar pattern with 1:8 as the best ratio. This reflects increasing representation complexity across training stages. We also have domain-specific analysis to show how different task types benefit from varying negative ratios. For example, banking domain shows Stage 1 highest at 1:4 (17.2%), with significant drops at 1:20 (8.2%). For detailed domain results, see Appendix.

Generally, for task mixture design, best contrastive learning ratios are task-dependent, not universal. The common practice of using fixed negative ratios across all training stages is not the best strategy. Our results demonstrate that task mixture should use adaptive negative sampling, adjusting ratios based on the representation complexity of each stage’s learning objective. The best ratios also provide insight into why FM SO.P succeeds. By using 1:4 for Stages 1-2 and 1:8 for Stage 3, we optimize each stage’s contrastive learning effectiveness.

Certain rubrics show large improvements with increased model size, e.g., authentication verification (DMV: 58.7% → 69.2%, +10.5%), resource availability assessment (Library: 60.6% → 73.4%, +12.8%), alternative solutions (Online Market: 19.0% → 32.8%, +13.8%), and loyalty program integration (Hotel: 7.5% → 30.0%, +22.5%). These dimensions involve multi-step reasoning over resource states and eligibility conditions that become more robust with scale. From these increase, Our 7B models are good at core SOP capabilities (privacy, authentication, basic constraint reasoning). Our 14B models provide balanced performance across most rubrics, offering a sweet spot for general-purpose SOP understanding. Our 32B models are good for complex scenarios (generating alternative solutions, managing tiered service programs, coordinating multi-step interactions).

SOP Understanding.

Standard Operating Procedures encode critical organizational knowledge, yet existing language models struggle with procedural reasoning
(

27
;

(

(

However, these benchmarks rely on fixed evaluation metrics that apply uniform criteria, failing to capture domain-specific requirements (e.g., temporal constraints for DMV vs. regulatory compliance for banking). Moreover, no prior work systematically solves how to train models for SOP through specific task design.

Progressive Training and Task Mixture.

Curriculum learning
(

)
orders training samples by difficulty using predefined metrics. Recent methods include GPT-4-scored stratification

\to
sequence
\to
graph) with cumulative data, optimizing across multiple task-specific objectives that build hierarchically rather than ordering by difficulty alone.

5.
Conclusion

We propose FM SO.P, a framework for cross-domain SOP understanding through progressive task mixture and automatic evaluation. Our three-stage task mixture trains models through contrastive pair generation across different task types with increasing complexity: from terminology precision (concept disambiguation) to sequential correctness (action sequence understanding) to conditional reasoning (graph reasoning). Our multi-agent evaluation system generates adaptive rubrics capturing domain-specific requirements, enabling scalable evaluation. Our method achieves large improvements across seven domains, with FM SO.P (7B) improving from 11.30% to 34.33% (+23.03%), matching 72B baseline with 10× fewer parameters. The 14B and 32B variants reach 39.67% and 48.30%, establishing new state-of-the-art among open-source models.

Our method demonstrates that progressive task mixture effectively teaches complex procedural reasoning, with potential extending to any domain requiring procedural workflows and contextual constraints. The ability to match large-model performance with significantly fewer parameters makes enterprise-scale SOP automation practical, enabling automatic compliance verification, workflow optimization, and intelligent process assistance.
