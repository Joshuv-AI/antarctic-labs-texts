Interpretable glucose forecasting for type 2 diabetes across traditional, deep, and large language models
Authors: Rawan Alredaini, Maysoon Abulkhair, Hind Almisbahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent chronic condition characterized by elevated blood glucose levels resulting from insulin resistance or inadequate insulin secretion. Accurate prediction of future glucose levels is essential for minimizing complications and enabling proactive management. While machine learning and deep learning models have been extensively applied in this domain, the potential of large language models (LLMs) remains underexplored, with no prior studies systematically comparing them to conventional approaches using real patient data. In this study, we evaluate three model types: traditional (XGBoost, Random Forest), deep learning (GRU, LSTM, Transformer, Ensemble), and finetuned LLMs (GPT-4.1, MiniGPT, LLaMA-1B, LLaMA-7B), for predicting glucose levels 30, 60, and 90 minutes ahead using hybrid inputs of six static features and 20 prior CGM readings. GPT-4.1 achieved the best performance at 30 and 60 minutes, while LLaMA-7B excelled at 90 minutes. Among conventional models, LSTM showed the best performance. Beyond forecasting, interpretability was a central focus. We used explainable AI (XAI) techniques to interpret LSTM results, while GPT-4.1 explained its predictions directly using natural language, without additional training. Notably, the study revealed an alignment between the two explanation techniques, with both highlighting recent glucose readings as key predictors across all forecasting horizons.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Endocrinology

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a globally prevalent metabolic disorder defined by persistent hyperglycemia caused by insulin resistance and/or insufficient insulin secretion1. Unlike type 1 diabetes, which usually manifests in childhood as an autoimmune disease, T2DM tends to develop gradually in adulthood and is strongly associated with lifestyle factors such as obesity and physical inactivity. When poorly managed, persistent hyperglycemia can cause a range of complications, including cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, impaired wound healing, and other long-term conditions that significantly decrease quality of life and place a burden on healthcare systems2. Emerging therapeutic strategies for these complications, such as glucose-responsive nanozyme hydrogels for diabetic wounds, rely heavily on the regulation of the local glucose microenvironment, further underscoring the need for precise glycemic forecasting3. In addition to these clinical consequences, T2DM is accompanied by low-grade chronic inflammation and complex immunometabolic changes4, with macrophage-mediated inflammation of adipose tissue and altered glutamine metabolism that link obesity with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes5. Maintaining blood glucose within a target range is essential to reduce the risk of these complications. Consequently, the ability to anticipate future glucose trends has become a key component of diabetes management, allowing timely adjustments in medication, nutrition, or physical activity6. This need has been supported by the growing use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems, which provide detailed real-time glucose measurements at regular intervals7. Compared to traditional finger-stick testing, CGM is more user-friendly and informative, providing insight into glucose fluctuations during meals, exercise, and sleep. As a result, CGM data are now integral to both clinical care and predictive modeling for short- and mid-term glycemic control2. During the past decade, various computational approaches have been applied to CGM data for glucose forecasting. These range from classical statistical models to machine learning algorithms such as random forests and gradient boosting, as well as more complex deep learning architectures8. Although many of these models achieve promising accuracy, a persistent challenge remains: most fail to offer transparency regarding how predictions are generated. This lack of interpretability limits their clinical adoption, especially in scenarios where treatment decisions must be supported by explainable evidence. In many cases, even highly accurate models function as “black boxes,” producing results without revealing the underlying rationale, which is an issue that reduces clinician trust and hinders integration into care pathways9. To address this gap, large language models (LLMs) have recently emerged as potential tools to enhance model interpretability. Originally developed for processing natural language, LLMs have demonstrated an impressive ability to identify complex patterns and articulate reasoning in a human-readable format. These capabilities position them as promising candidates for generating transparent, context-aware predictions in medical applications, including blood glucose prediction10. This raises a critical question: To what extent can large language models (LLMs) outperform traditional machine learning and deep learning approaches in predicting blood glucose levels for people with type 2 diabetes, and how effectively can they provide interpretable explanations to enhance user understanding and trust?

To address this question, our study makes the following key contributions:

Comprehensive evaluation of modeling approaches:We present a systematic comparison between traditional machine learning models, deep learning architectures, and large language models (LLMs) to predict blood glucose levels over short and medium time frames in patients with type 2 diabetes. Using a consistent experimental setup, we compare performance across 30-, 60-, and 90-minute prediction horizons in a fair and balanced manner.

Comprehensive evaluation of modeling approaches:We present a systematic comparison between traditional machine learning models, deep learning architectures, and large language models (LLMs) to predict blood glucose levels over short and medium time frames in patients with type 2 diabetes. Using a consistent experimental setup, we compare performance across 30-, 60-, and 90-minute prediction horizons in a fair and balanced manner.

Design of a hybrid preprocessing pipeline:We introduce a custom preprocessing method to design and implement a preprocessing pipeline that integrates continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data with static patient characteristics, ensuring data completeness and allowing personalized glucose forecasting. This includes a smoothing step to handle missing values and produces a hybrid format that can be used by both neural networks and language-based models. By embedding patient-specific information as static data, the preprocessing pipeline not only ensures data completeness, but also enhances personalization of glucose forecasting based on individual patient profiles.

In summary, this study not only compares large language models with traditional and deep learning approaches, but also explores a text-based formulation for glucose forecasting. In this formulation, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) sequences are represented as textual tokens, while static patient descriptors are embedded within the same natural-language prompt. This unified representation enables general-purpose LLMs, such as GPT-4.1 and LLaMA, to reason simultaneously over both the temporal dynamics and the patient-specific context, supporting personalized forecasts and language-based explanations from a single input format. By bridging the methodological gap between existing domain-specific numerical models, such as TimeGPT and Time-LLM, and general language-based systems, the proposed framework demonstrates that a unified text-driven paradigm can achieve competitive predictive performance while improving interpretability and clinical relevance in this data set.

Together, these contributions advance both the predictive capacity and interpretability of AI models for diabetes care, thereby supporting transparent and personalized clinical decision-making.

Related works

Recent years have seen growing interest in predicting blood glucose levels using a range of computational techniques, from traditional machine learning to deep learning, and more recently, large language models (LLMs). These studies differ in focus, with some targeting type 1 diabetes and others type 2 and in the kinds of data they use, whether continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) alone or combined with other clinical input. Previous work can be broadly grouped into three main areas: CGM-based prediction using traditional and deep learning models, the use of SHAP to interpret predictions, and early efforts to apply LLMs to prediction and explanation in diabetes care. Across these lines of research, several limitations remain unaddressed.

Blood glucose prediction using classical and deep learning approaches

Type 2 Diabetes (T2DM)

To facilitate comparison between modeling strategies, the following studies are grouped according to the predominant approach they used: traditional machine learning, deep learning, or hybrid frameworks. It is important to note that some studies incorporated multiple methodologies, but are presented here based on their primary modeling emphasis.

Among traditional approaches, Fu et al.11 evaluated five different machine learning algorithms: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Logistic regression using a data set of 273 patients characterized by structured variables such as body mass index (BMI) and laboratory test results. Of these, XGBoost produced the highest performance, reaching an accuracy of 78.18% and an AUC of 0.68, underscoring its utility for long-term glycemic control forecasting11. In a separate personalized context, Anjum et al.12 applied XGBoost along with Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) and Prophet to predict glucose levels from CGM data sampled at 15-minute intervals. Their approach yielded notably strong predictive power, with a determination coefficient () of 0.96 and a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 0.04, indicating its viability for AI-assisted individualized dietary planning12.

Turning to deep learning models, Han et al.13 introduced Glu-Ensemble, an integrated neural network framework designed to enhance prediction reliability. Tested on CGM time series, this model consistently outperformed conventional baselines based on evaluation metrics such as root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute errors (MAE) and error grid analysis13. Similarly, Lee et al.14 presented a Transformer-based model that aimed to detect hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic episodes in a cohort of 104 hospitalized patients with T2DM. By incorporating synthetic data generated by GAN to augment training, their system achieved a MAPE of 17.89 and an F1 score of 0.67 for predictions 60-minutes ahead14. In another inpatient-focused study, Kim et al. assessed multiple recurrent neural network (RNN) architectures, including simple RNN, GRU, and LSTM using CGM data from 20 patients. The GRU variant yielded the best results, reporting a RMSE of 21.5 mg/dL and a MAPE of 11.1%, demonstrating its ability to manage the high variability observed in clinical glucose levels15. Regarding hybrid and optimization strategies, Yang et al.16 proposed a domain adaptation method that takes advantage of patient clustering to enhance model personalization when CGM data are sparse. Their framework utilized multi-level clustering to segment patients and transferred knowledge between groups to construct individualized predictive models. When evaluated in a large dataset of 908 subjects, the model achieved a RMSE of 14.96 mg/dL for 30-minute forecasts, with more than 94% of its predictions residing within clinically acceptable ranges16. Deng et al.17 proposed a solution to data scarcity by using transfer learning alongside artificially generated data. When applied to a data set of 40 patients, their model demonstrated strong performance, exceeding 95% accuracy and reaching 90% sensitivity to predict glucose levels half an hour in advance17. In a related optimization effort, Kim et al.18 proposed an ensemble deep learning system that combines Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) models, optimized by genetic algorithms. Evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, the system achieved precision of 96.8%, sensitivity of 95.2% and RMSE of 4.9 mg/dL, demonstrating strong short-term prediction performance18.

Type 1 Diabetes (T1DM)

Following the same categorization strategy used for T2DM, the studies in the following are organized by their main modeling approach. Although several works used combinations of methods, each is positioned within the category that best reflects its core methodological contribution.

Among traditional approaches, Chu et al.19 evaluated 15 machine learning models in the DiaTrend dataset for glucose prediction in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost and the Bagging Regressor, demonstrated superior performance in the detection of hyperglycemia, achieving lower RMSE values and improved sensitivity to critical fluctuations19. Similarly, Alfian et al.20 compared Deep Neural Network (DNN), SVR, RF and XGBoost on CGM data from seven patients, reporting the highest accuracy for XGBoost, with MAPE values between 7.5 and 17.7% and scores reaching 0.92 across various prediction horizons20.

Within hybrid and personalized modeling strategies, Shuvo and Islam21 designed a multitask deep learning framework that combines stacked LSTM layers with dense layers adapted by gender and individual profiles. Training in the same data set, their model delivered RMSEs of 16.06, 30.89, and 40.51 mg/dL for 30-, 60 and 90 minute horizons, respectively, with more than 94% of the predictions falling within clinically acceptable ranges21. Bosoni et al.22 assessed generic, hybrid, and personalized neural network models using CGM data from 194 patients in the WISDM study. While performance at 30 minutes was similar across models (RMSE 19.4–19.6 mg/dL), the generic model showed a slight advantage at 60 minutes (RMSE = 34.9 mg/dL), indicating that both individualized and generalized models can be effective when trained on large-scale datasets22. In addition, Moon et al.23 proposed a hybrid model combining Bidirectional LSTM and Transformer with Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (BiT-MAML) for personalized glucose prediction in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Using the OhioT1DM data set and a Leave-One-Patient-Out Cross-Validation protocol (LOPO-CV), their model achieved superior performance compared to the baselines of LSTM and Edge-LSTM, with an RMSE of 24.89 mg/dL in the 30-minute horizon and improved consistency between patients, highlighting its ability to address the heterogeneity between patients23.

Regarding deep learning paradigms, Meriem et al.24 designed an LSTM model using the D1NAMO dataset, reporting root mean squared errors of 14.87 mg/dL for 30 minutes and 24.72 mg/dL for glucose predictions of 60-minutes24. Dylag25 applied a recursive regression framework using models such as linear regression, Hidden Markov Model (HMM), and LSTM on 11-hour CGM data from the CITY dataset. LSTM outperformed the others, achieving a 60-minute RMSE of 28.55 mg/dL25. Cappon et al. developed a bidirectional LSTM model tailored for individualized glucose prediction, leveraging both CGM data and inputs from wearable sensors. Using the OhioT1DM dataset for training, the model achieved root mean square errors (RMSE) of 20.20 mg/dL in 30 minutes and 34.19 mg/dL in 60 minutes26. Despite the broad application of traditional and deep learning models in glucose forecasting, prior research has seldom included direct comparisons between these approaches and emerging large language models (LLMs), which will be presented in detail later in this study. Although some studies have utilized CGM data and static patient characteristics such as age, BMI, and duration of diabetes, few have thoroughly investigated how each contributes to prediction accuracy at different forecast intervals. This study aims to address this gap by systematically comparing the performance of traditional machine learning models, deep learning architectures, and large language models (LLMs) in predicting blood glucose levels at 30-, 60-, and 90-minute intervals for people with type 2 diabetes.

The use of SHAP for interpreting predictions

To enhance the interpretability of our glucose prediction models and gain clearer insight into how individual inputs influence model predictions, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP provides a unified framework for feature attribution by assigning to each input variable a numerical contribution to the model’s output for a given instance. Based on cooperative game theory, SHAP is both model-agnostic and versatile, making it suitable for explaining predictions across a wide range of modeling approaches (Annuzzi et al.27).

Several recent studies have used SHAP to explain how glucose prediction models work and whether they align with known physiological patterns. For example, Prendin et al.28 applied SHAP analysis to evaluate and contrast a baseline LSTM model with its personalized version. They found that only the personalized model gave meaningful explanations: carbohydrate intake had a positive effect on predicted glucose, insulin had a negative effect, and recent glucose readings were the most important. In related work, Annuzzi et al.29 applied SHAP to interpret glucose predictions made at 15 and 60 minutes after food intake. Their analysis showed that glucose levels measured before meals played the most significant role in influencing the predictions of the model, while insulin and carbohydrate intake had less impact in the short term, possibly due to the delay in their physiological effects. In a follow-up study, Annuzzi et al.27 examined how different macronutrients influence glucose prediction outcomes. Their findings showed that glycemic load and carbohydrate intake had the strongest effect at the 60-minute mark, whereas fat intake played a more significant role in forecasts extending to 120 minutes.

Cappon et al.26 applied SHAP to interpret a bidirectional LSTM model trained on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data. Their analysis confirmed that the most recent CGM values had the greatest impact on the prediction of the model, emphasizing the relevance of the short-term glucose dynamics. Duckworth et al.30 integrated SHAP into a real-time risk prediction system to detect episodes of hypo and hyperglycemia. The system prioritized features such as current glucose level, time of day, and recent sharp declines in readings. Importantly, SHAP allowed for the delivery of customized individualized explanations to each patient. Pustozerov et al.31 used SHAP to investigate postprandial glucose responses in individuals with gestational diabetes. Their results identified carbohydrate intake and the time elapsed since the last meal as the most influential factors that affect glucose levels. Although SHAP is widely used to improve model transparency in glucose forecasting, most studies focus on a single prediction interval, often 15 or 60 minutes, and do not investigate how feature contributions vary across different horizons. To our knowledge, only one prior study evaluated three intervals, but no studies have systematically examined how static patient characteristics and temporal CGM values contribute to predicted glucose levels. In contrast, our study applies SHAP across three intervals (30, 60 and 90 minutes), allowing us to analyze both the temporal evolution of the contributions of the CGM features and the influence of the static characteristics of the patient on the predictions of the model.

Large language models in glucose forecasting and explanation

While large language models (LLMs) were initially created for language processing tasks, they have recently shown promise in time-series applications, such as predicting blood glucose levels. Their ability to model complex temporal dynamics, along with their ability to produce clear, human-friendly explanations, makes them a powerful asset for improving interpretability and supporting clinical decision-making.

Predicting glucose values using LLMs

Several recent studies have been adapting these models to tackle glucose prediction by adjusting transformer architectures to work with numerical time-series data. A standout example is the Time-LLM framework introduced by Lara-Abelenda et al.,32 which takes pre-trained models like GPT and BERT and repurposes them to analyze continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data. When applied to records from 100 individuals with type 1 diabetes, these personalized models showed impressive predictive capabilities, achieving mean absolute errors (MAEs) as low as 15.7 mg/dL for 60-minute forecasts32. In a similar vein, Rancati et al.33 evaluated the performance of TimeGPT, a foundational model trained on a variety of time-series datasets. Using CGM and insulin data from 15 pediatric patients, TimeGPT outperformed both traditional methods and deep learning benchmarks for 30- and 60-minute predictions, with MAEs of 15.59 and 18.45 mg/dL, respectively, remarkably without any fine-tuning33.

Although domain-specific frameworks such as Time-LLM and TimeGPT32, 33 successfully adapt transformer-based architectures for glucose forecasting, their design remains focused on specialized numerical time-series processing. These models operate primarily as numeric forecasters rather than large language models in the classical sense: they do not make use of natural-language conditioning and typically do not integrate patient-level contextual information directly within a single input representation. In contrast, the present study explores a language-native formulation that reframes the prediction of CGM as a natural-language reasoning task. Instead of treating glucose readings as pure numerical sequences, we represent the 20-point CGM history as textual tokens and embed static patient attributes directly within the same prompt. This unified, text-based representation allows general-purpose LLMs, such as GPT-4.1 and LLaMA, to reason simultaneously over temporal dynamics and patient-specific context while producing both structured numeric forecasts and human-interpretable explanations from a shared input.

Interpreting CGM data using LLMs

In parallel, other works have investigated the use of LLMs to interpret CGM data through natural language. Healey and Kohane34 proposed LLM-CGM, a benchmark designed to evaluate how well LLMs like GPT-4 can answer patient-centered questions about historical glucose trends. Their benchmark included 30 structured queries, and GPT-4 demonstrated strong performance in summarizing data points such as average glucose, time in range, and hypoglycemic events, highlighting the potential of conversational AI in diabetes self-management34. Building on this idea, Healey et al.35 applied GPT-4 to generate narrative clinical summaries of 14-day CGM records. The generated reports were evaluated by clinicians based on accuracy, safety, and clarity. While the output was generally well-received, limitations were noted, such as underreporting nocturnal hypoglycemia and overstating mild hyperglycemia, raising concerns about clinical reliability without further refinement35. Both studies used GPT-4 only as a prompt-based model without fine-tuning. Methodology

This study used a unified methodological framework to investigate the effectiveness of various modeling strategies for the prediction of short- to mid-term blood glucose. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were trained and evaluated using the same hybrid input format, consistent preprocessing steps, and standardized metrics. The subsections below detail the dataset, preprocessing pipeline, model configurations, evaluation criteria, and interpretability techniques employed in this work. Data collection and preprocessing

Data collection

For this work, we relied on the publicly accessible ShanghaiT2DM dataset, first introduced by Zhao et al.36, which contains real-world clinical and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data collected from 100 patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) in China. Glucose measurements were recorded every 15 minutes over monitoring periods ranging from 3 to 14 consecutive days per patient. From the original data set, we selected a subset of six clinically relevant static features: age, gender, weight, body mass index (BMI), diabetes duration, and smoking years. These were combined with sequences of the 20 most recent CGM values, representing a 5-hour window, to create hybrid inputs for short- to mid-term glucose forecasting. Therefore, each input sample consisted of 29 features: 20 sequential CGM readings and 6 static attributes, along with 3 target glucose values that correspond to future time points at 30, 60, and 90 minutes. These horizons were chosen because they are widely adopted in the glucose prediction literature, reflect clinically significant intervals for timely interventions, and balance the trade-off between prediction accuracy and practical utility. Accordingly, we paired them with a 5-hour (20-sample) context window to capture the recent CGM dynamics that are most relevant for 30–90 minute forecasts while keeping the input length manageable and consistent across all model families.

Data preprocessing and windowing

The ShanghaiT2DM data set was processed through a reproducible pipeline as illustrated in Fig. 3 that transforms raw CGM sequences and static patient descriptors into standardized input–output pairs for supervised learning, highlighting the end-to-end transformation from raw data to structured windows. The pipeline includes static-feature cleaning, rare missing-value imputation, non-overlapping temporal windowing with label generation, feature integration, dataset assembly, feature scaling/encoding, and the evaluation split. Overview of the data preprocessing pipeline. The diagram illustrates the five-step transformation of raw CGM sequences and static patient information into structured input-output pairs, as described in the preprocessing section.

Static feature selection and cleaning. For each patient, we extracted six clinically relevant descriptors, such as age, gender, weight, BMI, diabetes duration, and smoking years, from the metadata file. Non-numeric entries (gender) were converted to numeric and consistency checks were applied across patients.

Missing-value imputation in CGM. Sensor dropout was rare and occurred in a single patient only (13 missing readings across the entire data set), representing a very small fraction of all CGM values. For these short, isolated gaps, each missing value was replaced by the average of its nearest valid neighbors (one before and one after) within the same sequence. This simple local-mean strategy preserves the local temporal dynamics of the glucose trace and allows patient data to be retained in the analysis despite these few missing readings. Ensuring data completeness through such preprocessing is essential for maintaining model robustness, as rigorous data cleaning pipelines have been shown to be fundamental to the performance of deep learning frameworks in complex biological prediction tasks37.

Temporal windowing and label generation. Each CGM series was divided into non-overlapping windows of 20 consecutive readings (15-minute sampling; 5-hour context). This 5-hour context was chosen to align with the short- and mid-term prediction horizons (30, 60, and 90 minutes) and to focus the models on the segment of the CGM trace that is most informative for near-term forecasting. For each window, we extracted three future targets at 30, 60, and 90 minutes after the last reading (20 inputs + 3 targets). Windows were constructed from the preceding 20 CGM readings only; the three targets lay strictly outside the window at +30/+60/+90 minutes (15-minute sampling offsets 2/4/6). Windows were instantiated only when all three future targets existed, ensuring that no look-ahead information was introduced into the inputs.

Feature integration. We add the six static patient descriptors to the 23 glucose-related values, producing a unified 29-element record per window as illustrated in Fig. 2. For modeling, inputs (X) comprised 20 historical CGM readings plus six static attributes (26 features), and outputs (y) comprised three future glucose targets (30/60/90 minutes).

Data sliding window: each sample combines 20 CGM readings (blue) with six static features (green) as inputs, and three future glucose values at 30, 60, and 90 minutes (orange) as outputs, repeated across patients for model training. The CGM readings were taken every 15 minutes, so the difference between and corresponds to 15 minutes.

Dataset assembly. Aggregation of all patients yielded 5,534 supervised windows. The Per-patient windows were concatenated into a single pooled data set for modeling. Residual tail points that did not complete a full 20-step context were discarded to avoid partial windows.

Feature scaling and encoding. Continuous predictors, which are the 20 CGM readings and the numeric static attributes (age, BMI, weight, smoking history, diabetes duration), were standardized (z-score) using StandardScaler. The categorical gender variable was one-hot encoded. The target values were kept in mg/dL.

Split protocol and scope. Data were split at the sample/window level in the pooled window data set (80% training, 20% test) using a fixed random seed. All preprocessing transformers (scaling/encoding) were fitted on the training partition only and applied unchanged to the validation/test partitions. This protocol evaluates in-cohort performance and does not assess patient-independent generalization. In this version, no cross-validation or patient-level separation was performed.

All models were trained using the previously defined hybrid input format. Full configurations are listed in Tables 5, 6 and 8. In terms of hyperparameter policy and selection, we did not perform an exhaustive hyperparameter search; instead, each model was trained with pre-specified configurations motivated by previous work and pilot runs; full settings are provided in Tables 5, 6, 7 and 8. Traditional machine learning models

XGBoost: In our study, we used Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), which is a well-known ensemble method based on decision trees, to forecast future glucose levels. This model has gained popularity in clinical applications due to its ability to handle structured data efficiently12, 38. We structured each input by combining 20 recent CGM values with six patient features, allowing the model to learn from both time patterns and individual health characteristics. To make multi-step predictions (30, 60, and 90 minutes ahead), we implemented the model using the MultiOutputRegressor from scikit-learn.

Random Forest: We also used Random Forest (RF), a widely used ensemble learning method that aggregates the results of multiple decision trees to improve prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting risks38. Thanks to its resilience to noisy data and its ability to capture non-linear relationships, RF has been frequently used in clinical prediction tasks. For consistency, we trained the model using the same hybrid input format as in the XGBoost setup: a flattened vector that combined 20 CGM values and six demographic features. Glucose levels at 30, 60 and 90 minutes were predicted using scikit-learn’s MultiOutputRegressor.

Deep learning models

LSTM Model: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, a specialized type of recurrent neural network (RNN), are particularly well-suited to model temporal sequences that exhibit both short- and long-range dependencies39. In this study, we designed an LSTM-based architecture with two parallel branches to integrate sequential glucose fluctuations and patient-specific static attributes. The temporal branch processes the 20 most recent CGM readings, reshaped and fed into two stacked Bidirectional LSTM layers. Each layer is followed by batch normalization and dropout to ensure stable learning and minimize overfitting. Currently, the static branch receives six demographic and clinical features, which are passed through dense layers activated by ReLU, along with normalization and dropout. The outputs from both branches are then combined and passed through fully connected layers to generate glucose predictions at 30-, 60-, and 90-minute intervals.

Time2Vec-Enhanced LSTM Model: To enhance the sensitivity of the model to periodic trends and temporally structured glucose variations, we modified the LSTM baseline architecture by integrating a Time2Vec embedding layer. This layer, designed to encode time in a learnable and interpretable format, improves the model’s ability to detect recurring fluctuations and time-dependent changes in CGM data40. In our design as shown in Fig. 4, the Time2Vec module was inserted prior to the LSTM layers within the sequential branch, enabling the network to explicitly incorporate temporal context into its learning process. Aside from this addition, the remainder of the architecture, including the parallel static input stream and the fusion mechanism, remained consistent with the original LSTM configuration.

Simplified architecture of the Time2Vec–LSTM model. The temporal branch encodes CGM sequences using a Time2Vec embedding and stacked BiLSTM blocks with normalization and dropout, while the static branch processes demographic and clinical features through dense layers, and both are fused to predict glucose at 30, 60, and 90 minutes.

Transformer-Based Model: Transformer-based architectures have gained prominence in time-series forecasting due to their self-attention mechanism, which enables effective modeling of both local and long-range temporal dependencies41. In this study, we designed a two-branch architecture to incorporate both time-series CGM data and patient-specific static features. The sequence branch processes the 20 most recent glucose readings by first mapping them to a higher-dimensional space and adding positional information to preserve their temporal order. These enriched representations are then passed through several Transformer encoder layers, followed by a global average pooling operation that produces a fixed-length summary of the sequence. In parallel, static features, such as age and BMI, are treated as individual input units and passed through a self-attention mechanism along with a feed-forward network and pooling. The outputs of both branches are then combined and fed into a series of dense layers, with regularization applied through dropout and normalization, to produce glucose predictions at 30, 60, and 90 minutes. The model is illustrated in Fig. 5. For clarity, Table 7 provides a side-by-side comparison of the Time2Vec-enhanced LSTM and Transformer-based model architectures.

Two-branch Transformer model for glucose forecasting. The sequence branch encodes 20 CGM readings using embedding, positional encoding, and stacked Transformer blocks, while the static branch processes patient features with self-attention and feed-forward layers. Both outputs are concatenated and passed through dense layers to predict glucose levels at 30, 60, and 90 minutes.

GRU: Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), a simplified variant of recurrent neural networks, are well-regarded for their ability to capture temporal relationships in sequential data with reduced computational complexity42. In our study, we designed a GRU-based architecture that integrates both temporal and static inputs through a dual-branch framework. The temporal branch takes the 20 most recent CGM readings, reshapes them for sequential processing, and passes them through two Bidirectional GRU layers with progressively smaller hidden dimensions. To enhance learning stability and prevent overfitting, each GRU layer is followed by batch normalization and dropout. In parallel, the static branch processes six demographic and clinical features through two fully connected layers with ReLU activations, also regularized with normalization and dropout. The representations from both branches are then merged and passed through a set of dense layers to generate glucose predictions at the designated future time points.

Ensemble Model To make glucose predictions more consistent and reliable, we combined two distinct model types, which are LSTM and Transformer, into a single ensemble. Each model was trained on the same input: 20 recent glucose readings from CGM devices and six fixed patient characteristics. Rather than relying on just one architecture, we averaged the predictions from both models at each target point (30, 60, and 90 minutes ahead). This allowed us to take advantage of the strength of the LSTM in capturing recent trends, while also benefiting from the Transformer’s ability to recognize longer-term patterns in the data.

Large Language Models (LLMs)

Beyond conventional machine learning and deep learning techniques, this study also explored the potential of large language models (LLMs) to predict blood glucose levels over short to intermediate time frames. Four transformer-based models were assessed: GPT-4.1, MiniGPT, LLaMA-1B, and LLaMA-7B.

Data preprocessing for LLMs:Although large language models (LLMs) were originally developed for natural language understanding and generation, in this study they were adapted to the glucose prediction task by reformulating it as a text-based reasoning problem. Each training instance was converted into a natural-language prompt that jointly represented the temporal sequence of 20 CGM readings and six static patient attributes (age, BMI, weight, smoking history, diabetes duration, and gender). This unified text representation allowed the model to reason over both sequential and contextual information within a single input. To accommodate architectural differences across model families, the prompt structures were formatted accordingly. GPT-4.1 and MiniGPT followed OpenAI’s role-based schema comprising system, user, and assistant messages, while LLaMA-1B and LLaMA-7B adopted an instruction–response format using user and assistant tags. Examples 1 and 2 illustrate these prompt configurations, reflecting how model-specific training protocols influence input formatting. All processed data were stored as JSONL prompt–completion pairs, where completion consisted only of three numeric glucose values in mg/dL, in a fixed order corresponding to the 30-, 60- and 90-minute horizons. We did not include a separate variant in which the same LLMs operate on non-textual numerical-token sequences; instead, the present work focuses on the text-based formulation as a first step toward assessing the feasibility and interpretability of LLMs for CGM forecasting in a setting where both temporal dynamics and static patient characteristics are jointly encoded. For LLaMA-based models, the datasets were manually divided into 90% training and 10% validation splits to ensure adequate data for fine-tuning, while GPT models handled the data split internally. This standardized preprocessing pipeline ensured methodological consistency and reproducibility across all LLM variants.

Prompting, Inference, and Evaluation: GPT-4.1 and MiniGPT:The fine-tuned GPT-4.1 and MiniGPT models were accessed through the OpenAI API and evaluated within the Google Colab environment. For each test case, a natural language prompt was programmatically generated by embedding the 20 most recent CGM readings along with the six static features of the patient into a structured textual format based on OpenAI’s role-based convention (system, user, assistant), and explicitly instructing the model to output only three glucose values (30, 60, and 90 minute horizons) in mg/dL, in fixed order and separated by commas, without additional text. The model output therefore consisted of a short and constrained text string containing the three predicted glucose values in this predefined format. Numerical values were extracted using regular expressions and a simple validation step checked that exactly three numeric tokens were present in the expected order before casting them to 32-bit floating point numbers. Under this setup and deterministic decoding (temperature set to 0), all predictions used for the evaluation adhered to this three-value numeric schema.

LLaMA-1B and LLaMA-7B: Evaluation of the LLaMA-1B and LLaMA-7B models was carried out using the Hugging Face Transformers library in Google Colab. For each input instance, the 20-value CGM sequence and six static patient features were formatted as a textual instruction following the LLaMA-Instruct style, using user and assistant tags, and the model was explicitly instructed to return only three glucose values (30, 60, and 90 minute horizons) in mg/dL, separated by commas, and without additional explanatory text. All numeric values were rounded to one decimal place for consistency and clarity. As in the GPT-based models, the raw output consisted of a short constrained text string containing the three predicted glucose values for the 30-, 60- and 90-minute horizons, in a predefined order and expressed in mg/dL. The predicted values were extracted using pattern-matching techniques, and a validation step ensured that exactly three numeric tokens were present before they were cast into the float32 format for evaluation.

Quantitative comparison of interpretability techniques:For each test window, we derive two feature orderings: (i) a SHAP-based ordering and (ii) a GPT-based ordering. On the SHAP side, the features were sorted in descending order of their absolute SHAP values per-sample using dense ranks. On the GPT side, we did not attempt to score free-form adjectives. Instead, GPT-4.1 was prompted to produce a semi-structured explanation in which it first briefly justified the prediction in natural language and then returned an explicitly ordered list of input features (e.g., a numbered list from most to least influential). A rule-based parser automatically reads this list, maps each item to the corresponding feature in our design matrix, and assigns ranks according to its position in the list (rank 1 for the first item, rank 2 for the second, etc.), while features that did not appear in the list were assigned the worst rank 1. All GPT-based ranks were therefore generated by the same script for all samples, without any manual per-window editing or interpretation of qualitative adjectives.

We used Spearman’s rank correlation as the primary measure of agreement between GPT’s a priori feature ordering and SHAP-based importances, because it captures monotonic rank agreement without assuming linearity or interval-scaled importance scores. To obtain a robust and more conservative estimate of bonds, which is particularly relevant when the values per sample are equal or nearly indistinguishable, we complemented Spearman with Kendall’s , which quantifies the concordance through the balance of concordant versus discordant pairs with an explicit correction for bonds. To quantify statistical uncertainty around the per sample rank agreement distributions, we estimated 95% nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals for the mean and median Spearman by resampling per sample values with replacement (B replicates), which avoids parametric assumptions that are often unsuitable for rank based data. We also reported two complementary evaluation protocols: (i) intersection, which is calculated only on overlapping features and therefore reflects a “fair” agreement that is not affected by non-overlapping, and (ii) union, which expands the set and assigns the worst rank 1 to missing features on either side, which yields a stricter assessment penalizing non-overlapping. Together, Spearman plus Kendall’s plus bootstrap confidence intervals, under both intersection and union protocols, provide a balanced and transparent characterization of global and local agreement: point estimates of rank concordance (with and without robustness of the tie), accompanied by uncertainty quantification, and an explicit account of differences in feature coverage.

Evaluation metrics

To assess how well each model performed, we used four standard regression metrics: RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and . Each of these offers a different perspective on the prediction performance across the three forecasting horizons (30, 60, and 90 minutes). RMSE, by squaring the individual errors before averaging, places more weight on larger deviations, making it useful for identifying predictions with significant discrepancies. MAE, on the other hand, provides a more balanced view by averaging the absolute differences between predicted and actual values, using the same units as the target variable. MAPE translates those errors into percentages, allowing results to be interpreted regardless of the scale of the data. Lastly, shows how much of the variation in actual glucose levels can be explained by the predictions of the model. The R2 values closer to 1 suggest a better fit of the model. These metrics were chosen because they offer a well-rounded assessment when used together, capturing both the magnitude of prediction errors and the overall consistency between the predicted and observed values.

Model interpretability

SHAP-based model interpretation

To better understand how the LSTM model makes its predictions and to quantify the influence of each input feature, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) using the KernelExplainer from the SHAP Python library. The LSTM model was treated as a black box by defining a custom wrapper function that accepts a 27-dimensional input vector—composed of 20 sequential CGM readings and 7 static patient features (BMI, Age, Weight, Smoking years, Diabetes duration, Gender, and Patient ID)—and returns predicted glucose levels at 30, 60, and 90 minutes ahead.

To approximate the baseline behavior of the model, a reference data set (background) was created by randomly selecting 50 samples from the test set. This background data represents the expected model output in the absence of any specific feature information, providing a foundation for calculating feature attributions.

The SHAP values were then calculated for a subset of 200 test samples. For each prediction, 1,000 random coalitions of features were sampled to estimate the marginal contribution of each input characteristic. This process produced three SHAP value matrices, each with dimensions (200, 27), corresponding to the forecast targets of 30 minutes, 60 minutes, and 90-minutes. Each element in these matrices represents how much a particular feature increased or decreased the predicted glucose level for a specific sample.

To visualize and interpret these attributions, we generated several plots: Figures 6, 7 and 8 which will be presented later in the paper. SHAP summary plots were used to highlight the importance of global characteristics by grading features according to the magnitude and direction of their impact in all samples. In addition, we created heatmaps that show the average absolute SHAP values for each of the 20 CGM time steps (from t–19 to t–0), allowing us to assess how the importance of glucose readings changes with the prediction horizon. Finally, for selected individual cases, waterfall plots were used to break down the contribution of each feature to a single prediction, offering detailed local-level explanations that complement global insights.

GPT-4.1 interpretation

Although no additional fine-tuning was performed to optimize GPT-4.1 for interpretability, we leveraged its inherent reasoning capabilities to evaluate how it explains its own glucose predictions. The model was trained solely on structured glucose data, comprising sequential CGM values and static patient features without specialized supervision for explanation or feature attribution tasks. Despite this, GPT-4.1 was able to generate coherent and clinically meaningful interpretations in natural language.

Results of interpretability using SHAP

SHAP analysis revealed that the LSTM model is primarily based on recent CGM readings, especially for shorter prediction horizons. For example, in the 30-minute forecast as shown in Fig. 6, the most influential inputs were CGM values at t–0 through t–3, which are the most recent CGM readings, indicating strong short-term autoregressive behavior. As the prediction window extended to 60 minutes, the model began to incorporate slightly earlier time points, such as t–5 and t–6. By 90 minutes, the SHAP attributions became more widely distributed, with moderate contributions emerging from t–10 and beyond. As the prediction window extended, the dependence of the model changed to earlier CGM readings, indicating an increasing dependence on longer-term trends to address the added uncertainty associated with time.

The SHAP summary plots in Fig. 6, which use a blue-to-red gradient to denote input values, showed a consistent pattern: higher glucose levels (highlighted in red) tended to push the predicted values upward. This aligns with physiological expectations, lending credibility to the model’s behavior. To enhance interpretability, we standardized the color scale across all SHAP heatmaps in Fig. 7, enabling direct visual comparison between prediction horizons. This revealed a consistent pattern: the most recent glucose readings (e.g., t-0 to t-3) held the highest importance across all forecasts. The unified scale further highlighted how the model’s reliance on earlier time steps increased with longer prediction horizons, reinforcing its need for a richer temporal context.Static patient characteristics, such as age, BMI, diabetes duration, and smoking history, showed limited influence across all horizons. This was clearly seen in the SHAP waterfall plot in Fig. 8 for a typical test case, where the CGM values were the main drivers of the prediction, while the static features made minimal contributions.

Together, both global and local SHAP results provide a coherent and interpretable explanation of how the LSTM model forms its predictions, strengthening the transparency and clinical relevance of the model. These findings are visualized in three figures: Figs. 6 and 7 illustrate global explanations using SHAP summary plots and heatmaps across the three prediction horizons (30, 60 and 90 minutes), while Fig. 8 presents a representative SHAP waterfall plot for a single 30-minute prediction case.

Results of interpretability using GPT-4.1

To assess the interpretability and internal reasoning patterns of large language models in glucose forecasting, we conducted a series of structured evaluations using GPT-4.1. The model received prompts containing patient-specific static features along with 20 consecutive CGM readings sampled at 15-minute intervals The model was prompted to generate glucose forecasts at 30, 60, and 90 minutes into the future, followed by an open-ended question requesting an explanation of how those predictions were formed and which features had the greatest influence.

Across various input scenarios, including stable, rising, falling glucose trajectories and stable glucose trajectories, GPT-4.1 consistently employed a structured and logical line of reasoning. It frequently highlighted the final three to five CGM readings as the most influential elements shaping its predictions. These values were often interpreted as indicative of short-term trends, which the model appeared to extrapolate forward. Although static attributes such as BMI and duration of diabetes were occasionally mentioned, they were generally treated as contextual factors rather than primary drivers of the forecast. In instances where the predicted values diverged from the true outcomes, GPT-4.1 tended to attribute the discrepancy to physiological events not captured in the observed data, such as an impending meal or delayed insulin action.

What sets GPT-4.1 apart is its ability to express these rationales in a fluent, human-like language. Without requiring any additional interpretability tools, the model transparently communicated the reasoning behind its predictions. In particular, its repeated emphasis on recent CGM readings aligns closely with the findings of the SHAP analysis applied to the LSTM model, which are detailed in the following section. Interpretability comparison between SHAP and GPT-4.1

To explore the interpretability of the model from multiple perspectives, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to assess the attributions of features in LSTM models and prompted GPT-4.1 to generate justifications in natural-language for its predictions. Although these methods rely on fundamentally different mechanisms, one symbolic and the other generative, they consistently highlighted the same insight: the most recent CGM readings, particularly the last 3–5 values, played the most influential role in forecasting glucose levels. This alignment among explanation modalities strengthens confidence in the underlying patterns learned by the models and underscores their clinical relevance. Beyond this qualitative convergence, we quantified the rank agreement between GPT and SHAP using Spearman’s under both intersection and union protocols, Kendall’s , and per-sample analyzes with 95% nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals. Across the 30/60/90 minute horizons, all three analyzes showed consistently high concordance, indicating that the recency-driven pattern identified by both methods is quantitatively consistent with the model’s attributions. However, clinical validation of explanations remains outside the scope of this work.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the performance and interpretability of traditional machine learning, deep learning, and large language models (LLMs) for short- and mid-term glucose forecasting in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Within this in-cohort design, LLMs generally achieved the strongest performance, with GPT-4.1 showing the clearest gains at 30 and 60 minutes and LLaMA-7B performing best at 90 minutes.

We interpret performance using effect-size tables (absolute and percent changes versus an LSTM baseline) and a rank-based summary. Together, they indicate a consistent short- and mid-horizon advantage for GPT-4.1 and a long-horizon strength for LLaMA-7B.

Two consistent patterns emerge. First, near-term forecasts benefit most from models that emphasize very recent CGM dynamics, where both SHAP attributions and GPT rationales repeatedly prioritize the last three to five readings. This agreement across explanation modalities matches the improvements at 30–60 minutes and is physiologically plausible, because short-horizon forecasts are largely driven by the recent CGM trend, which are the current level and the short-term slope (change in mg/dL over the last 3–5 readings). Second, at longer horizons we observe lower () and smaller performance gaps between models, which is consistent with the outcomes being less determined by recent CGM history. In this horizon, LLaMA-7B performs best at 90 minutes. Note that () can drop even when MAE/RMSE improve, because () reflects explained variance, whereas MAE/RMSE measure absolute error.

In terms of conventional models, the LSTM remains the strongest baseline across horizons; however, its improvements are smaller than those of the top LLMs at 30–60 minutes and attenuate at 90 minutes. In practical terms, this gap highlights the added value of large, pretrained sequence models for structured clinical time series. To place our results in context, we compared them with prior benchmarks. Our top models, which are GPT-4.1 at 30–60 minutes and LLaMA-7B at 90 minutes, outperformed previously reported performance at the same horizons.

Although cross-paper comparisons warrant caution given differences in datasets and experimental setups, combined effect-size and rank-based views indicate that LLMs offer a meaningful advance for glucose forecasting. At the same time, LLM-based predictors also present practical constraints compared to more conventional models such as LSTM and Random Forest. Fine-tuning and deploying GPT-4.1 and LLaMA-7B required substantially greater computational resources and longer training and inference times than the classical and deep-learning baselines, in line with recent reviews describing transformer-based LLMs in healthcare as computationally expensive and resource-intensive48, 49. In addition, current LLM-based systems often depend on proprietary APIs or large open-weight checkpoints, which introduces further considerations related to cost, data governance, and regulatory compliance, and their behavior can be sensitive to prompt design and deployment choices, complicating auditing and oversight in clinical workflows49, 50. Taken together, these factors suggest that, despite their strong performance in our setting, LLMs are best regarded as complementary to rather than straightforward replacements for established forecasting models, particularly in routine or low-resource care settings. Beyond predictive performance, interpretability is central because it determines whether model behavior aligns with clinically plausible mechanisms and can be trusted in practice. In this study, we applied SHAP and GPT 4.1 and consistently found that the most recent CGM readings, particularly the last three to five values, exert the strongest influence on model output across all forecasting horizons. This convergence between two fundamentally different interpretability approaches not only reinforces confidence in the models’ internal logic but also reveals a physiologically plausible reliance on short-term glucose trends. Notably, this pattern aligns with previous findings in the literature: Cappon et al.26 observed a similar emphasis on recent CGM values using a bidirectional LSTM model, and Prendin et al.28 reported that the CGM data were more influential than other contextual factors such as insulin or carbohydrate intake. We also provided a quantitative comparison of the two interpretability techniques using rank based statistics. On the intersection of overlapping features, Spearman’s rank correlation showed consistently high global concordance across the 30, 60, and 90 minute horizons. Under the stricter union protocol, which penalizes lack of overlap by assigning the worst rank plus one, correlations remained high although lower, indicating robustness when coverage differs. Kendall’s on the intersection corroborated these findings with an estimate that is robust to ties, confirming that agreement is not driven by a small number of inversions or ties. At the local level, per sample analyzes summarized the distribution of Spearman rho values with 95 percent nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals, revealing strong alignment for most windows and a modest attenuation at longer horizons. Taken together, these results indicate the method level rank concordance between SHAP and GPT across horizons, providing a quantitative cross check that complements the qualitative convergence observed in their explanations. However, this study has several limitations. First, the data set includes 100 patients from a single population. Although windowing with no overlap yields 5,534 supervised instances, these windows still arise from a limited number of unique patients, constraining external validity. In future work, we will expand to larger and demographically diverse cohorts and perform multicenter external validation to assess generalization to unseen patients more rigorously.

Second, data were split at the sample or window level across the pooled data set, and preprocessing transformers were fitted on the training split and applied unchanged to validation and test sets. Consequently, the reported metrics primarily reflect within patient performance and should not be interpreted as patient independent generalization. Looking ahead, we will adopt patient level group splitting such as group based cross validation or leave one patient out, report both within patient and cross patient performance, compute bootstrap confidence intervals for all metrics across horizons, and apply paired nonparametric tests such as the Wilcoxon signed rank test. In addition, by constraining all models to a 5-hour history window to ensure a controlled comparison, we may under-utilize architectures that are designed to exploit longer temporal contexts, and future work should explore longer histories and day-scale inputs for such models.

Third, we report aggregate metrics, namely MAE, RMSE, , and MAPE, and we also presented practical effect sizes as absolute changes in mg per dL and relative changes % versus an LSTM baseline, together with a rank based summary across metrics and horizons to convey magnitude and consistency. However, all comparisons remain descriptive under a window level split since we did not compute confidence intervals or conduct hypothesis tests, and differences between models should not be interpreted as supported by inferential statistics. Tests such as the paired t test or the Wilcoxon signed rank test were not performed, as they are difficult to implement in our setting given the substantial computational and financial cost of repeated fine tuning of large language models. Given that this study is a comparative analysis across three model families, we also refrained from testing only the machine learning and deep learning models, since excluding the large language models would produce an incomplete and less informative comparison. In addition, we did not compute time-alignment measures such as Time Gain, which quantify delays between entire predicted and reference glucose profiles, for two reasons. First, all models in this study were configured to produce discrete point forecasts on three fixed horizons (30, 60, and 90 minutes) rather than continuous glucose trajectories, so our evaluation focuses on value-based errors at these clinically significant time points. Second, the data set was reformulated into non-overlapping windows that were randomly shuffled into train/validation/test splits at the window level, and the original continuous timestamps for test windows were not preserved, which prevents reconstructing full patient-level CGM profiles and computing a well-defined Time Gain ex post. Extending the analysis to profile-based temporal-alignment metrics, including Time Gain, is therefore left for future work, particularly in the context of sequence-to-sequence architectures that generate full predicted trajectories under preserved chronological ordering.

Fourth, in interpretability, the GPT-based rationales complement the quantitative attributions of SHAP, although they were not reviewed by clinical experts and therefore should not be treated as clinically validated justifications. In addition, the GPT-based feature orderings used in our agreement analysis are themselves generated by the same model whose predictions they explain and should not be viewed as ground truth. Our rank-concordance results between SHAP and GPT therefore quantify methodological agreement between two explanation techniques under a constrained, list-based format, rather than providing a definitive measure of objective feature importance. From a generative point of view, LLMs are also known to hallucinate free-text content. In this work, we mitigated this risk for the numeric forecasts by constraining the output to a three-value schema and enforcing automatic format validation, and we used deterministic decoding so that repeated queries with the same input produced identical predictions, and all predictions included in the evaluation adhered to this three-value schema. However, a broader qualitative characterization of hallucination modes, especially in unconfined narrative explanations, remains an important topic for future research. Formal clinical validation lies outside the present study and is an appropriate next step, together with incorporating richer contextual covariates and evaluating additional explanation methods that may offer complementary perspectives.

Fifth, while we reformulated the LLM-based predictors as a text-based reasoning task, we did not perform a direct comparison against alternative implementations in which the same models ingest purely numerical-token or embedding-based representations of the CGM sequences. As a result, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating that the text-based formulation is viable and competitive in this setting, rather than as establishing that it is superior to numerical encoding. Future work should include such head-to-head comparisons to more fully characterize the trade-offs between text-based and numerical-token representations for LLMs in time-series forecasting. Finally, the models relied only on structured input consisting of CGM measurements and basic demographics, and did not incorporate contextual factors such as meal timing and composition, insulin dosing or physical activity, which are known to influence glucose dynamics. Future work should integrate these variables to better capture short term perturbations and improve external validity. Furthermore, while our analysis found that basic static features (e.g., BMI, age) had limited influence, future models could benefit from incorporating genotypic static features, such as ICAM-1 variants, which have been shown to stratify patients based on inflammatory susceptibility and disease risk51.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive comparison of large language models (LLMs), traditional machine learning, and deep learning techniques for the prediction of short- and mid-term blood glucose in people with type 2 diabetes. Across the evaluated time frames, LLMs, which are GPT-4.1 and LLaMA-7B, generally achieved the strongest performance, particularly in shorter horizons, illustrating their ability to handle structured time-series data in a clinically relevant setting. Beyond performance, the study emphasized the importance of interpretability as a key requirement for reliable clinical AI. By combining SHAP with the natural-language reasoning of GPT-4.1, we were able to examine both numerical and textual explanations for the predictions. This dual perspective shed light on how recent glucose readings influence forecasts and helped make the reasoning behind model outputs more transparent to potential clinical users. Taken together, these findings highlight the potential of LLMs as effective tools for transparent and personalized decision support in diabetes management.

However, more extensive validation is necessary with a wider range of patient populations. Future studies should also look at adding more contextual factors, such as meals and insulin use, and assess other interpretability methods. Beyond glucose forecasting itself, similar AI-based approaches are increasingly being explored in other aspects of diabetes care, including early detection and management of complications such as diabetic kidney disease52, suggesting that interpretable forecasting models of the kind studied here could eventually be integrated into broader AI-assisted care pathways. Together, these efforts could improve both the reliability and practical utility of AI-driven glucose forecasting systems.
