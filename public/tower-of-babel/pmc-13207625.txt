Assessing the degree of cortical dislamination through electrical pattern analysis
Authors: Ana Aquiles, Laura Pinedo, Mirelta Regalado, Hiram Luna-Munguia, Fernando Peña-Ortega, Luis Concha, Román Rossi-Pool
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFocal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is a leading cause of pharmacoresistant epilepsy in pediatric populations, yet its role in epileptogenesis remains incompletely understood. Recent findings suggest that hyperexcitability may originate in non-dysplastic peripheral areas rather than the malformation itself. However, given the significant variability across FCD cases, clarifying whether cytoarchitectural disorganization drives activity changes remains challenging. Using the carmustine-induced animal model, we investigated how varying degrees of cortical malformation influence neural dynamics. Local field potentials were recorded via multielectrode arrays during spontaneous activity and external perturbation. We introduced a novel metric to quantify spatial heterogeneity in signal organization and assessed its association with excitation-inhibition balance. Results show that alterations in signal structure correlate with the extent and distribution of cortical abnormalities, highlighting the functional relevance of cytoarchitectural variability. This work advances understanding of FCD-related network dysfunction and offers analytical tools with potential translational value for pre-surgical evaluation.

Introduction

Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is the leading cause of surgical intervention for pharmacoresistant epilepsy in pediatric patients.1,2 The occurrence of FCD in cases of focal epilepsy is roughly 5–25%. Yet, despite this prevalence, the relationship between FCD and epilepsy remains incompletely understood,1 as not every cortical malformation progresses to epilepsy.3 It has been suggested that hyperexcitation in pediatric FCD type IIa may stem from the presence of balloon cells,4 which are primarily identified by their unique morphology. However, balloon cells do not trigger epileptic activity despite their highly abnormal appearance; rather, dysmorphic neurons and immature neurons—cytological hallmarks of FCD IIa—are the primary contributors to epileptic discharges.5 The combination of animal models and advanced analytical techniques has brought us closer to understanding the mechanisms of cortical dysplasias. Groundbreaking discoveries by Koh and collaborators,6 have challenged traditional views of cortical dysplasia, demonstrating that functional impairments may originate not within the primary malformation itself, but rather in surrounding non-malformed neurons. Their findings reveal that hyperexcitability often emerges from these ostensibly intact regions, while neurons within the malformed zones can remain hypoexcitable. The balance between excitation and inhibition is a fundamental concept to understand neural circuit deformation and brain dynamics alteration. These principles have been increasingly explored to understand the emergence of epilepsy and other neurological conditions across various spatial and temporal scales,7,8 including during the pre-ictal activity, which has been widely regarded as a critical window into the mechanisms leading to seizure onset.9,10 Yet, despite growing interest in early electrical changes, the extent to which the degree of cortical delamination caused by cortical dysplasia contributes to functional alterations remains underexplored. In this work, we propose that spontaneous activity and, particularly, the local balance between excitation and inhibition, may play a key role in mediating how varying severities of malformation disrupt neural dynamics. To address the latter, we employed the carmustine model to simulate the cytoarchitecture of cortical dysplasia type IIa-like, as previously reported,11,12,13,14,15,16,17 this model is notable for its broad range of malformation severity, making it an effective representation of the morphological challenges associated with this condition. Importantly, since this model does not exhibit functional changes such as seizure activity,15 we opted to measure spontaneous electrical activity using (MEA), and the Mg2+ Free model18,19,20,21,22 to mimic the electrical currents evoked during epileptiform activity. Here, we decomposed the local field potential (LFP) spectrum to assess potential differences in signal structure based on cortical localization, and categorized those differences using frequency covariation. To quantify this categorization, we developed a metric that captures the heterogeneity of electrode groupings within each MEA recording. We then evaluated the aperiodic exponent in relation to an excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) balance metric,8,23,24 aiming to uncover a potential link with the degree of functional spatial sparsity in the categorization. We describe the flow of information between different classes of electrodes during spontaneous activity, which varies depending on the level of dyslamination. We also identified a common fluctuation band that spans various activity states—spontaneous, magnesium-free, and recovery. Finally, we monitored its information flow during temporal evolution in relation to functional electrode categorization. Through this work, we aim to deepen the understanding of dysfunction in cortical dysplasia while accounting for the variability inherent in these malformations, while also offering analytical approaches that may inspire and support the study of complex biological time series in other systems characterized by structural and functional variability.

Results

Neural recordings from medial motor cortices during spontaneous activity, perturbation (Mg2+-Free) and recovery

To describe the electrical activity differences between healthy tissue and with cortical disarrangement (i.e., Eulaminated and Dyslaminated, respectively), we evaluated M1 cortex in an animal model of cortical maldevelopment,15 which reproduces morphological features from FCD IIa,11,13,14 such as loss of cortical lamination and some other cell malformations (Figure 1). Also, we incorporated a model that induces epileptogenic conditions. The Mg2+-Free model, previously reported and described,18,25,26 was employed to assess how the absence of Mg2+ ions affects current dynamics and how these changes relate to cortical disorganization. We compare signal differences in approximately the medial motor cortex (see Methods 10), where the MEA recorded activity spanning layer II-III to V (Figure 2). We first qualitatively examined the logarithmic power spectra (Figure 3A). Visual inspection of raw traces of untreated conditions (Figure 3B) across different states revealed noticeable differences in spontaneous and evoked activities. It is evident from the spectrograms, even without formal analysis, that the primary distinctions between the conditions become apparent during spontaneous activity. Remarkably, these differences are much more pronounced during this resting period than during the other conditions. Nevertheless, it is also noteworthy that the appearance of both signals evolved differently during the perturbation challenge and continued during the recovery phase. Categorization of electrodes and effects on the aperiodic exponent

Following the idea that a normal anatomical cortical array can lead to a functional structure, as shown by Dura-Bernal,27 based on real and simulated spike train data, we first focused on the description of spontaneous activity (Figures 2, 3, and 4). We emphasized the categorization of electrodes within the MEA, using the distinct signal shapes associated with electrode depth (Figures 2 and 4A). We covary the frequency domain (60 points, from 0.5 to 100 Hz) over time for each electrode, and noted that the cumulative variance captured by the first modes (components) was greater in the Eulaminated condition than in the Dyslaminated one (Figure 4B). To further explore this result, we projected the first two modes on a comodulogram constructed from the spectrogram, ordered by electrode position in the MEA (Figure 4C), where we observed differences in frequency range and weighted magnitude across Modes 1 and 2. Mode 1 shows that the initial electrodes exhibit stable frequency and weighting values, while later electrodes display an upward trend in these values. Additionally, the Dyslaminated projection identifies several electrodes that display distinct behavior compared to the others. In module 2, variations in the electrode spectrum are also observed. This observation motivated us to look for groups in the space projection of the first two components. We obtained a variation of groups and dispersion along the MEA architecture, which led us to develop a metric to describe the degree of sparsity of the clusters. We refer to this metric as ϵ, (see Methods 10), with values ranging from 0 to 1, where lower ϵ values indicate higher sparseness and less structured clustering, and higher values indicate the opposite (Figure 4D). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in ϵ between the Eulaminated and Dyslaminated arrays, providing a measure to quantify the degree of disorder. Furthermore, based on previous results, these findings also highlight the range and variety of disorders, along with the histological validation of the same model.15,17 Consequently, we suggest a hypothetical link between the ϵ metric and cytoarchitecture, as illustrated in Figure 4D, bottom. As depicted in our initial observations (Figure 3A), we next examined the aperiodic component found in each electrode of the MEAs to assess the observed variations. In Figure 3E, it is evident that the most extreme cases along the disorder gradient exhibit distinct power-law scale decay, based on the mean and variance of all measured electrodes. Interestingly, when evaluating the distribution of total aperiodic exponent values (Figure 4F), a bimodal function emerges, with a clear decline around the 0.6 value (sensitivity: 0.71, specificity: 0.53, and 95% CI for Accuracy: [0.612, 0.691]). Based on this observation, we classified the aperiodic exponents using 0.6 as a threshold and re-evaluated the data using the same four representative examples shown in Figure 4D to investigate whether the previously identified clusters were preserved. This analysis revealed that Dyslaminated arrays predominantly exhibit aperiodic exponent values higher than 0.6, whereas Eulaminated arrays show a balanced distribution of values both below and above this threshold. To assess how well these values are related to the actual level of disorder described by ϵ, we performed a traditional linear regression between the bimodal coefficient of each individual array distribution and its ϵ value (Figure 5A). However, we found no significant correlation between them. Here, it is analyzed as an additional axis and not as an effect of the ϵ value. Nevertheless, as shown in both Figures 5A and 5B, a tendency toward a bimodal distribution is apparent, where there exist both a high and a low aperiodic values. Following the literature,23,24 we propose a second hypothetical approach to interpret this tendency: Higher aperiodic exponent values could reflect an inhibitory predominance I ≫ E, whereas the lower values could correspond to an excitatory predominance E ≫ I. Despite the presence of electrodes that do not fit the previously mentioned categorization in Figure 4, it is evident that in dyslaminated cortices, there is an imbalance between the two distributions. In contrast, in eulaminated cortices, the highest and lowest values are evenly distributed in terms of the number of electrodes, which may play a significant role in the dynamics during an activity disturbance. We also conduct a comparison between groups similar to what is shown in Figure 4F, focusing on aperiodic exponential values during the subsequent activities, Mg2+-Free and Recovery (Figure 5C). The most pronounced perturbations in signal distributions for both eulaminated and dyslaminated groups occur during two critical periods: The initial 15 min of 0 Mg2+ exposure and the recovery phase. The first 15 min represent a transient hyperexcitable state, where the network is maximally sensitive to the loss of Mg2+ blockade of NMDARs. This aligns with epileptiform activity models, where acute hyperexcitability, driven by unregulated Ca2+ influx and synchronous neuronal firing, precedes compensatory homeostatic adaptations. Prolonged absence of extracellular Mg2+ further alters voltage dynamics, leading to hypersynchronized circuit activity, as reflected in the similarity of aperiodic exponent distributions between groups during this phase. However, the late recovery phase reveals a striking divergence between groups. The eulaminated (control) group demonstrates a robust return to baseline aperiodic activity, with increased density in the 1.0–1.2 range, indicative of effective homeostatic compensation. In contrast, the dyslaminated group fails to restore this peak, suggesting impaired recovery dynamics. This divergence likely stems from disrupted laminar connectivity in dyslaminated networks, which may hinder the coordination of inhibitory and excitatory adjustments required for stabilization.

These findings contribute to the critical role of structural integrity in network resilience. The 1.0–1.2 aperiodic range appears to represent a stable operational state for neuronal populations, potentially linked to optimal information processing or metabolic efficiency. The inability of dyslaminated networks to return to this range suggests persistent dysfunction, possibly due to altered inhibitory-excitatory balance or fragmented local circuitry.

Functional changes in typical bands during perturbation and recovery

To assess functional differences associated with perturbation induced by a magnesium-free medium and the subsequent recovery period, we analyzed a shared range of frequency fluctuations. This choice was motivated by established evidence that human seizure discharges often exhibit coupling between low and high frequencies.9,28 Our goal was to identify the frequency range showing the greatest variation in our recordings, drawing inspiration from Zhang, W. et al.,29 who reported a consistent fluctuation band by analyzing temporal covariation in the power spectrum. We adapted their approach, considering that no specific frequency band has been universally defined for evoked epileptic activity. Our analysis revealed that the highest covariation occurred in the lower frequency range (0.5–30 Hz), and this effect was more pronounced in Dyslaminated cortices. We demonstrated this through the projection of the first two components of spectral covariation onto a weighted spectrogram and by projecting both modes onto a weighted power spectrum (Figures 6A and 6B). Notably, covariance during baseline was consistently greater in Eulaminated cortices than in Dyslaminated ones (Figure 6C, upper panel). In contrast, during the Mg2+-Free perturbation, cumulative variance between the two groups converged (Figure 6C, lower panel). This pattern is further illustrated in the representative logarithmic power spectra (Figure 6B, right), where baseline bandwidths maintain divergent decay slopes, while the Mg2+-Free condition shows spectral convergence. This confirms our initial qualitative observations from Figure 3B, now filtered for the frequencies showing the highest covariance. To validate that the outcome of this bandwidth investigation preserves the ripple activity traits of the Mg2+-Free model, we mapped the bandwidth threshold onto three electrodes chosen at random from different deepest MEA positions (upper, middle, and lower). As illustrated in Figure 6D, the observed activity changes persist under this constraint. To track the evolution of activity in relation to the previously defined levels of disorder, we analyze the transfer of information between the electrode categories identified, using the common band variation (Figure 7). Starting with baseline recordings (Figure 7A), we clarify that Dyslaminated samples conveyed lower information values compared to Eulaminated ones. It appears that the sparsity category transmits less information compared to the other, which may correspond to the hyper and hypoexcitable regions previously suggested by Koh, H.6 but this time interpreted in terms of information content. However, as the ϵ values increased, a hierarchical pattern began to emerge in the flow of information,30 progressing from deeper electrode categories to more superficial ones. This observation seems to align with the known hierarchical organization of the motor cortical circuit,31 particularly in the zone where we attempted to record Figure 2. We also investigated whether the ϵ values could reflect changes in information sharing along the cortical motor circuit. To this end, we assessed information dominance based on the depth position of the electrodes. Electrodes were grouped into three layers: upper (A), middle (B), and lower (C) (Figure 7C). Using the same procedure applied for transfer flow, we extracted all possible directional connections between these layers. Analysis of the directionality asymmetry32 allowed us to identify the dominant directions of information transfer between each partition. However, due to high variability in the results, the correlation remained low (ρ = 0.24, p = 0.25). Nevertheless, the eulaminate cortex exhibited consistent directional dominance in certain pathways, such as A → C, B → C, and C → A, a stability that may reflect its normal functional organization.

We next asked which component of the signal carries the highest amount of information. Following the approach of Kayser, C.,33 where they found that information amounts are distributed differently in the distinct components of an LFP signal, we analyzed the mean weighted spectrogram across the three activity paradigms (baseline/spontaneous activity, Mg2+-Free and Recovery) and evaluated the information content of three signal components: phase, energy, and instantaneous frequency (Figure 7B, lower panel). These values reflect the average trend across all MEAs within the common LFP bandwidth, without distinguishing between levels of delamination, as the observed variability was insufficient for clear separation. Our results show that phase carries the highest amount of information across conditions, while instantaneous frequency contributes the least. Furthermore, phase and energy follow similar trends over time. In Eulaminate cortices, both components begin with high information values during baseline activity but decrease during Mg2+-Free perturbation and do not return to baseline levels during recovery. In contrast, Dyslaminated cortices also begin with high values, but their information content remains more stable over time. Additionally, the trends of phase and energy appear to evolve similarly over time in both conditions; on one hand, Eulaminated phases and energy begin with high information values, but as perturbation continues, they decrease and never return to their original levels, even during the recovery state. On the other hand, the Dyslaminated values also initiate at high information levels, but their trend shows minimal change over time. However, instant frequency shows the least informative values in both situations. Additionally, the pattern seen suggests that the Dyslaminated cortices seem to enhance their information content as time goes on and are likely to show higher values compared to the Eulaminated, during spontaneous activity and in the absence of Mg2+, but again appear similar in the recovery phase, as with the other two components.

Temporal evolution of signal components

To better understand the temporal evolution of these components, we focused on the most informative component, the phase, and the least informative, the instantaneous frequency. Using mutual information computed between electrodes across the full shared LFP bandwidth and a sliding window approach, we quantified the dissimilarity between each instantaneous state and its subsequent state within each window (see Methods 10). Dimensionality reduction was then applied to these temporal dissimilarity matrices to track the evolution of each component across different activity paradigms (Figure 7D). The left panel presents a representative example from the extremes of the ϵ distribution. Each point corresponds to a time window, with color indicating temporal progression (red = early, blue = late). The ellipses represent the low-dimensional projections of the dynamics: red for frequency and blue for phase. For each multidimensional scaling (MDS) projection, an ellipse was fitted to quantify the spread of dynamics; larger ellipses indicate greater variability and a higher capacity to transform the underlying interaction dynamics. In the right panel, boxplots beneath these projections reveal that frequency dynamics were more temporally constrained, with slightly higher mean values observed in eulaminate cortices than in dyslaminated ones, except during the recovery phase. This observation aligns with the reduced information content shown in Figure 7B, supporting the interpretation that lower information corresponds to a diminished capacity for dynamic change. In contrast, phase dynamics exhibited greater variability in dyslaminated cortices across all activity paradigms, whereas eulaminate cortices showed increased variability only during the Mg2+-Free condition. This highlights a key role for phase in modulating neural interactions under perturbed conditions. As with frequency, the average ellipse sizes for phase were generally larger in eulaminate cortices, except during recovery, where dyslaminated cortices showed increased variability that corresponded to elevated phase information content (Figure 7B). These findings offer valuable insights for future analytical decompositions and are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of phase locking across frequency bands.10,33 Building upon the prior decomposition analysis, we re-examined the information transfer depicted in Figure 7A, this time focusing on distinct phases of activity while maintaining a consistent bandwidth. Figure 7E illustrates the same extreme cases from Figure 7A, now under Mg2+-Free conditions (left) and during the recovery phase (right). In dyslaminated cortices, average information transfer shows a slight increase; notably, during the first 15 min of Mg2+-Free exposure, the transfer flux remains aligned with baseline levels. However, this trend reverses over the subsequent 30 min. In contrast, eulaminate cortical activity displays a progressive decline in information transfer relative to spontaneous activity, with an early disruption of hierarchical organization evident from the onset of Mg2+-Free exposure. During recovery, the dyslaminated cortex rapidly re-establishes its original transfer pattern within the first 15 min, whereas the eulaminate cortex requires 30 min to partially restore hierarchical flow and fails to fully recover it. Collectively, these findings suggest that a certain degree of structural disorganization differentially recovers after activity perturbations. This disorganization appears to affect not only the excitatory-inhibitory balance but also the overall efficiency and stability of information transmission within the network. These insights may have broader implications for understanding how cortical circuits respond to and recover from functional disruptions, and how structural variability modulates their adaptive capacity.

Discussion

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for cortical dysplasia, which is strongly associated with pharmacoresistant epilepsy, particularly in pediatric populations.1,2 However, the functional variability inherent to cortical dysplasia often compromises the accurate delineation of the epileptogenic zone, underscoring the critical need to characterize the signal features that define and distinguish pathological cortical territories.34 This clinical challenge motivated our investigation of the carmustine model across early stages of neurodevelopment11. Although this model produces embryonic-onset, regionally diffuse, and heterogeneous malformations.15 This approach aligns with a central goal of translational neuroscience: improving the detection of pathological network states under resting-state conditions, with direct implications for presurgical planning. To deepen this analysis, we decomposed LFP spectrograms, recognizing that specific spectral components may reflect underlying cognitive and pathological states. In line with EEG based approaches9,35,36 this analysis revealed functional signatures potentially tied to local cytoarchitecture. Notably, we observed patterns consistent with previously described neuron-type-specific signal features.37 By mapping activity classifications using our proposed metric ϵ, we characterized spatial heterogeneity in functional groups, distinguishing disorganized (Dyslaminated) from more structured (Eulaminated) cortical regions. We further explored the aperiodic exponent of LFP signals to assess its relationship with these functional classifications. Interestingly, a bimodal distribution emerged, with higher divergence in Dyslaminated regions and greater uniformity in Eulaminated areas. This dual pattern may reflect distinct (E/I) regimes, as previously had been suggested,38,39 echoing previous findings from both cognitive and epileptic contexts.7,8,23,24,40 Under Mg2+-Free perturbation, this distinction faded, suggesting that strong ionic shifts may transiently override intrinsic network heterogeneity. After recovery, the absence of the bimodal pattern implies a homogenization of network activity, masking the functional distinctions evident during spontaneous states, distinctions likely rooted in cytoarchitectural organization.

These observations, together with the differences in inferred baseline information transmission between electrode categories, point to a possible alteration of the E/I balance at baseline.41 Furthermore, the extent of cortical delamination may play a role in shaping signal propagation, especially when the network is challenged by external perturbations such as Mg2+-Free medium.

In response to external perturbations, our analysis revealed that the most informative features for electrode categorization, signal aperiodicity, and inter-category transfer entropy consistently emerged from spontaneous activity. This highlights the importance of intrinsic dynamics in uncovering structural-functional relationships and supports the broader applicability of our methodology. Thus, we propose that the key signal features in both healthy and diseased states could be assessed using this approach. This methodology may be adapted to study functional inhomogeneities across various experimental paradigms, including healthy brain states and different neurological conditions, potentially providing a simplified method for pre-surgical evaluation.

Limitations of the study

This study presents several limitations inherent to the use of an ex vivo model of cortical malformation. A primary constraint is the inability to directly validate the functional findings with the histological analysis of the exact recorded regions. This is due to the use of multielectrode array (MEA) recordings in 400 μm-thick cortical slices, which often sustain substantial structural damage after prolonged recording sessions, rendering subsequent histological processing unfeasible. Nevertheless, our histological validation was performed using tissue from animals in the same cohort. Thus, all links between ϵ and cytoarchitectural disorder should be interpreted as hypothesis-driven and population-level rather than slice-specific. However, it should be noted that the carmustine model is well characterized at the cytological level, and the morphological features observed in our slices were consistent with those extensively described in the literature, including disrupted cortical layering, heterotopic neurons, and altered cellular organization that closely mirror the cytoarchitectural hallmarks of FCD. While the model effectively replicates these key structural features, it does not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity observed in human cases. Therefore, future studies applying similar analytical frameworks to human tissue, particularly samples obtained from epilepsy surgeries, will be essential for strengthening translational relevance and assessing the generalization of the present findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Protocols followed in this study were approved by the ethics committee of the Institute of Neurobiology, UNAM (Protocol 111-A) and carried out in accordance with federal regulatory laws for animal experimentation (NOM-062-ZOO-1999). Pregnant Sprague-Dawley rats were intraperitoneally injected with BCNU following as previously reported methods,11,15 with 20 mg/kg diluted in 5% sucrose) on gestational day E15, for the Dyslaminated group, n = 3. For the Eulaminated group, n = 2 dams were injected with saline solution, at the same gestational day. All animals were kept in a room with a 12-h light/dark cycle and had unlimited access to food and water. For the Eulaminated group, five offspring from the dams were used for electrical recording n = 5 and three for histological analysis n = 3. For the Dyslaminated group n = 7 animals were employed for electrophysiology and n = 5 for histological validation.

Method details

Slice preparation

To conduct Multielectrode Array (MEA) recordings, we obtained 400 μm-thick coronal slices of the primary motor cortex (M1;bregma +0.20 mm) from male rats. Rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with a cold sucrose-based artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing the following (in mM): 3 KCl, 2.5 MgCl2, 25 NaHCO3, 238 sucrose, and 30 D-glucose, as also employed previously.42,43 The solution was maintained at pH 7.4 with constant carbogen bubbling. The brain was then removed and placed in an ice-cold carbogenated solution (95% O2/5% CO2). Coronal slices were cut using a vibratome (HM-650-V, Thermo Fisher, or VT1000S, Leica) and transferred to a slice recovery and maintenance solution (in mM): 119 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1.5 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 25 NaHCO3, and 30 D-glucose. This solution was maintained at pH 7.4, oxygenated, and incubated at 34 °C for at least 90 min before use. Each recording was performed using slices from a different animal. Mg2+-Free solution composition was the following (in mM): 120 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1.5 CaCl2, 26 NaHCO3, and 30 D-glucose.

Electrophysiological recordings

Recordings were obtained using a 60-electrode MEA (8 × 8 grid, 60pMEA200/30iR-Ti, Multichannel Systems), perfused from above and below with circulating aCSF at 34°C, bubbled with carbogen. Electrodes targeted the primary motor cortex, verified via bregma distance and stereotaxic microscope. Negative pressure was applied to ensure adhesion of the slice. Recordings were acquired at 25 kHz for 15 min. Baseline activity was recorded, followed by Mg2+-Free aCSF recordings (3 samples of 15 min), and then recovery activity, with normal aCSF (2 samples of 15 min).

Immunofluorescence

Consecutive coronal brain slices obtained from animals used in this study were collected and processed to evaluate neuronal morphology in both experimental groups: Eulaminated (n = 1) and Dyslaminated (n = 3). Brain slices were placed in a cryoprotective solution and subsequently embedded in a cryomatrix. Tissues were rapidly frozen using absolute ethanol cooled with liquid nitrogen and stored at −20 °C until sectioning.

Coronal sections of 20 μm thickness were cut using a cryostat. Sections were preserved in 1× PBS (w/v) and then washed five times for 10 min each in 1× PBS containing 0.3% (w/v) Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich). Blocking was performed for 45 min at room temperature using a blocking solution composed of 1× PBS, 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA).

Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody NeuN (ab104224) diluted 1:350 in blocking solution. After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescently conjugated secondary Alexa 488 (Invitrogen a32723), a goat anti-mouse antibody, diluted 1:500 in blocking solution for 4 h at 4 °C. Sections were then coverslipped with microscope cover glass.

Immunofluorescence imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 780 DUO).

Preprocessing of electrophysiological data

Raw MEA recordings were decompressed and extracted from MC Rack software using McsPyDataTools We then applied a low-pass filter using a 5th-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 100 Hz. Electrode traces were downsampled to 1 kHz. Power-line noise was eliminated using a notch filter with a quality factor of 90. We then reorganized the index of each electrode to recreate the original architecture of the 60-electrode MEA (8 × 8 grid), and kept the reference electrode off for all further analyses. Quality control was performed by comparison of the logarithmic power spectrum and revisited during dimensionality reduction of the LFP. Sample sizes after quality control were: Eulaminated n = 3; Dyslaminated n = 5.

LFP spectrograms

Spectrograms were computed using 1-s sliding windows with 75% overlap. Power spectra over 0.5–100 Hz were computed using 6 tapers and multi-taper decomposition. The number of frequency points was reduced to 60, and power was normalized using baseline mean power.

Dimensionality reduction and electrode clustering

To characterize how the spectrogram varies along electrodes for different frequencies f as a function of time t, we performed PCA on the spectrograms computed as described above. The number of significant dimensions spans up to 60 bins (0.5–100 Hz). PCA was performed from the covariance matrix of every electrode spectrogram over the time windows t and frequencies f as follows:(Equation 1)Cij=1TF∑t=1T∑f=1Fspeci,f(t)−spec¯ispecj,f(t)−spec¯jwhere T is the number of time windows, F is the number of frequency bins, speci,f(t) is the spectrogram of electrode i at time window t and frequency f, and spec¯i is the mean spectrogram of electrode i.

Level of disarrangement metric (ϵ)

We selected the principal components carrying the largest fraction of variance and projected them back into the original electrode space. Unsupervised clustering was then performed using mean-shift clustering from the Scikit-learn Python library,44 with an estimated bandwidth function (quantile = 0.45, n_samples = 80).

To quantify the level of disarray in each MEA array, we used the projection of the electrode clustering result and calculated the sparseness of clusters across the array:(Equation 2)ϵ=1C∑i=1Lncluster,inelectrode,iwhere C is the total number of clusters found, L is the number of lines in the array (L = 8), ncluster,i is the number of clusters cohabiting on line i, and nelectrode,i is the total number of electrodes on line i (ranging from 6 to 8 depending on array geometry). Differences in ϵ between groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Common band determination

Inspired by the methodology of Zhang W. et al.,29 we aimed to identify a common oscillatory band across the computed spectrograms using basal activity, and to verify whether that oscillation persisted during Mg2+-Free and recovery conditions. For this purpose, PCA was performed from the covariance matrix of each time window over all electrodes e and frequencies f:(Equation 3)Ci,j=1F⋅1E∑e=0E∑f=1FSpeci,e,f−Spec¯iSpecj,e,f−Spec¯jwhere F is the total number of frequencies, E is the number of electrodes, Speci,e,f is the spectrogram at time window i, electrode e, and frequency f, and Spec¯i is the mean spectrogram at time window i.

We projected the first 4 principal components to evaluate the variance carried within each (see Supplementary 4). By computing the mean over extreme examples (Dyslaminated, Eulaminated) in baseline activity, we identified the frequency range where the first component varies most, and then evaluated how that range persisted across other recordings and conditions.

Aperiodic exponent fitting and bimodality

Following the literature,8 we evaluate the aperiodic exponent in every electrode at baseline activity, Mg2+Free and recovery by fitting the Lorentzian function:L=b−log(k+Fx)Where b is the broadband offset, x is the exponent and k the knee parameter, that according to Donoghue, T.8 and Miller, K.45 and also applied by Gao, R.,38 allows a control of the bend in the aperiodic component. F corresponds to the frequency vector in input frequencies. Knowing that when k = 0 the Lorentzian fits a line in log-log space.

To perform the tendency of the power log, we applied a Gaussian filter using Scipy46 with a σ = 5 and also measured the variance respecting the logarithmic scale to approach the level of variation of spectrograms along all our electrodes shown in Figures 1 and 2.

Transfer entropy along electrode clusters

We computed transfer entropy during baseline, Mg2+-Free, and recovery conditions from the common LFP band identified for each cluster, where Xt denotes the LFP from cluster n and Yt from cluster m. We used a finite set of lags L = {τ1, τ2, …, τk} (200 lags, range [−100, 100]). For each lag, the lagged versions of the time series are defined as:(Equation 4)Xt(τ)=Xt+τ,Yt(τ)=Yt+τ

We then computed the mutual information (MI) between Xt(τ) and Yt:(Equation 5)MIXY(τ)=IXt(τ);Yt,MIYX(τ)=IYt(τ);Xtwhere I denotes Shannon mutual information and H Shannon entropy:(Equation 6)I(A;B)=H(A)+H(B)−H(A,B)with A and B representing different cluster sets. To compute directionality, we identified the lag that maximizes mutual information:(Equation 7)XY=argmaxτMIXY(τ)

The same logic was applied in the reverse direction YX. When more than two electrode clusters were found, all possible pairwise combinations were used to evaluate all directional interactions.

Electrode depth segmentation of the MEA

To investigate the relationship between electrode depth and information transfer, we reconstructed the spatial arrangement of the MEA. The array pattern was defined according to the experimental layout, with electrodes positioned in a hexagonal grid. For each electrode, x and y coordinates were computed based on grid spacing, and the vertical (y) coordinate was used to estimate relative cortical depth. Depth values were normalized to the interval [0, 1], where 0 corresponds to the deepest electrodes and 1 to the most superficial.

Following normalization, electrodes were sorted by depth and divided into three layers (upper, middle, lower), each containing approximately one-third of the electrodes, yielding three partitions (A, B and C) used for subsequent analyses of directional information transfer.

Multi-set directionality computation

The directionality analysis was extended to multiple signal sets by evaluating all pairwise interactions. Each pair of sets was analyzed independently following the same time-lagged MI and permutation-testing procedure described in Section 10.7.

Directionality asymmetry index

To summarize net directional influence between two signal sets, the Directionality Asymmetry Index (DAI) was computed as:(Equation 8)DAI=TEX→Y−TEY→XTEX→Y+TEY→Xwhere TEX→Y and TEY→X denote the total information transfer in each direction. The DAI ranges from −1 to 1, indicating dominant directional influence or balanced bidirectional interactions.

MI of LFP components (magnitude, phase, and instantaneous frequency) during time evolution

Following the assumption the information carried in an LFP signal is distributed differently as Kayser et al.33 has described. We decompose our LFP in the common band evaluated (0.5–30 Hz) using a discrete Hilbert Transform,46 to obtain the phase, envelope (energy) and instantaneous frequency of every electrode. MI between every pair of electrodes was then computed using a sliding window of 15 s with 75% overlap:(Equation 9)I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)where X and Y represent the signals of two different electrodes across the MEA. For each electrode, the maximum MI value across all pairwise interactions was retained, and this was computed for every condition: Baseline, Mg2+-Free (3 samples), and Recovery (2 samples). This metric does not inform directionality or causation; it was used here as a measure of the total information carried under each condition.

Pairwise dissimilarity evaluation and multidimensional scaling

We computed temporal dissimilarity matrices Dj, previously obtained from MI temporal sliding windows of every LFP component, where each entry Dabj quantifies the Euclidean distance between the a-th entity at time j and the b-th entity at time j + 1, decomposing the system’s temporal evolution into interpretable pairwise dissimilarities:(Equation 10)Dabj=∑k=1dxa,kj−xb,kj+12where k represents the feature/dimension index in each sample vector, with a = 1, …, m and b = 1, …, n. MDS was then applied to the reduced vectors Dabj to assess how activity differed from baseline across Mg2+-Free and recovery conditions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using SciPy46 and Scikit-learn,44 unless otherwise stated. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied throughout.

Quality control

Signal quality was verified at two stages. First, the logarithmic power spectrum of each electrode was inspected visually and compared across recordings. Second, electrode traces deviating beyond ±2 standard deviations in the principal components identified during dimensionality reduction (Section 4.5.3) were flagged and excluded from further analysis. Sample sizes after quality control: Eulaminated n = 3; Dyslaminated n = 5.

Aperiodic exponent thresholding and bimodality

To identify a data-driven boundary separating the two groups, kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied independently to each distribution using a Gaussian kernel. The summed density function was inspected for local minima, and the intersection of the two KDEs was computed as the point where the signed difference between densities changed sign. Both approaches converged on a threshold of 0.6, consistent with the visual valley observed in the KDE plot.

Classification performance at this threshold was quantified using sensitivity (true positive rate for Dyslaminated), specificity (true negative rate for Eulaminated), overall accuracy, and balanced accuracy (reported to account for group size imbalance).

To assess whether classification accuracy was disproportionately influenced by individual observations, a leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was performed for each group: at each iteration, a single observation was removed and all metrics were recomputed. The standard deviation of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity across LOO iterations served as an index of sample susceptibility.

Bootstrap resampling (n = 1, 000 iterations, with replacement) was used to derive 95% confidence intervals around the accuracy estimate. The degree of bimodality was quantified using the bimodal coefficient statistic.47 A simple ordinary least-squares (OLS) linear regression was then fitted between the bimodality coefficient and ϵ at baseline to characterize the relationship between spectral heterogeneity and spatial disarrangement.

Multidimensional scaling and condition comparison

Temporal dissimilarity matrices were subjected to classical (metric) MDS to obtain low-dimensional embeddings of electrode-activity trajectories across conditions (Baseline, Mg2+-Free, Recovery). The first two MDS dimensions were retained, and a 95% confidence ellipse was fitted to each condition’s point cloud to characterize trajectory dispersion. Differences in spread between conditions and LFP components (magnitude, phase, instantaneous frequency) were evaluated using an independent-samples Student’s t test applied to the ellipse semi-axis lengths.

Group comparison of the disarrangement metric (ϵ)

Differences in ϵ between Eulaminated (n = 3) and Dyslaminated (n = 5) preparations were assessed using the two-sample Mann–Whitney U test, a non-parametric rank-based test chosen because of the small and unequal group sizes and the absence of normality assumptions.

Permutation test for correlation between ϵ and the directionality asymmetry index

To assess whether the DAI was significantly associated with ϵ, we employed a non-parametric permutation test. The observed Pearson correlation coefficient r between the two variables was first computed. A null distribution was generated by randomly permuting the DAI values 10,000 times while keeping ϵ fixed, and recomputing r for each permutation. The p-value was defined as:(Equation 11)p=1N∑k=1N1|rk∗|≥|robs|,N=10,000

The observed correlation was r = 0.25(p = 0.23), indicating no statistically significant association between the two measures under the conditions studied.

Permutation-based significance testing of mutual information

To determine whether the observed MI at a given lag exceeded chance levels, a permutation null distribution was constructed independently for each lag τ. Specifically, N = 1, 000 surrogate time series Yt(n) were generated by randomly shuffling the original Yt (and equivalently Xt for the reverse direction). MI was recomputed for each surrogate, yielding a null distribution. Only lag values for which the observed MI exceeded the 95th percentile of the null distribution (p < 0.05, one-tailed) were retained for subsequent directionality analyses.

Comparison of directional information flow

For each combination of cluster pair, experimental group, and activity condition, the distribution of mean MI values in the forward direction was compared with that in the reverse direction using a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test. This non-parametric test was selected because (i) normality of mean MI distributions could not be assumed given our sample sizes, and (ii) the number of electrodes per direction within each stratum was unequal.

Multiple comparison correction

Statistical tests were performed across all combinations of cluster pairs, experimental groups, and activity conditions, yielding K = 18 simultaneous comparisons. p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni procedure. Statistical significance was declared at α = 0.05 after correction, with the annotation scheme: p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p ≥ 0.05 (ns).

Effect size

To quantify the magnitude of directional asymmetry independently of sample size, the rank-biserial correlation coefficient r was computed as an effect size measure for the Mann–Whitney test:(Equation 12)r=1−2Un1n2

Effect sizes were interpreted as: |r| < 0.3 (small), 0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5 (medium), and |r| ≥ 0.5 (large). Positive values of r indicate higher mean MI in the forward direction; negative values indicate dominance of the reverse direction.
