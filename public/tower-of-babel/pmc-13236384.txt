One case of facial salivary gland secretory carcinoma and a literature review
Authors: Zhou Qiao, Wang Yu, Liu Jianying, Zhang Mengsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:To explore the clinical and pathological characteristics, as well as the immunophenotypes, of facial salivary gland secretory carcinoma and to improve the understanding of this disease.Method:Clinical data of a case of facial salivary gland secretory carcinoma occurring on the face were summarized. HE and immunohistochemical staining were performed to observe its morphological and immunophenotypic characteristics, and relevant literature was reviewed.Result:The patient was an elderly male. Facial Color Doppler ultrasound showed that a slightly hypoechoic solid mass was observed between the fat layer and muscle layer of the left cheek. Under the microscope, the tumor cells exhibited eosinophilic staining and displayed cystic and papillary infiltrative growth, with papillary structures lined by spiculated cells. In some areas, the tumor cells appeared solid and microcystic. The tumor was encapsulated and presented a cystic appearance. It grew in a lobulated and nest-like pattern, with fibrous capsules separating the lobules, and exhibited papillary, microcystic, and tubular growth patterns. The nuclei were round or oval, arranged in an orderly manner, with vacuolated cytoplasm, fine chromatin, and centrally located nucleoli. Immunohistochemistry showed S100 (+), MUC4 (+), and DOG1 (−). The molecular test results showed that NTRK3 gene translocation was positive.Conclusion:Facial salivary gland secretory carcinoma (SC) is a rare, low-grade malignant salivary gland tumor, mainly occurring in the parotid gland. ETV6-NTRK3 gene translocation is the most reliable basis for diagnosing SC. Combining morphological diagnosis with gene testing is not difficult.

Introduction

Secretory carcinoma of salivary glands (SCSG) is a low-grade malignant tumor of the salivary glands, characterized by a distinctive ETV6 gene rearrangement. It was first reported as an independent entity in 2010[1]. This tumor often presents as microcystic, tubular, or solid structures morphologically, with cells rich in eosinophilic secretions. It shares similar immunophenotypes and genetic bases with breast secretory carcinoma[2], and was officially included in the 2017 WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumors. Recent studies have shown that SCSG generally has a good prognosis, but some cases may undergo high-grade transformation, necrosis, or distant metastasis[3–6].

The vast majority of salivary gland secretory carcinomas harbor a t(12;15) (p13;q25) translocation, leading to the formation of the ETV6-NTRK3 fusion gene. This gene further activates downstream signaling pathways, promoting tumorigenesis[1,3]. However, recent studies have revealed that, in addition to the common ETV6-NTRK3 gene fusion, alternative fusions such as ETV6-RET and ETV6-MET, as well as a few variants without classical fusions, can also occur in this type of tumor[7–12]. This type of disease predominantly occurs in middle-aged patients (median age 45–50 years), with males slightly more affected. It is most commonly found in the parotid gland, followed by minor salivary glands in the oral cavity and the submandibular gland[3,4]. Intraoral lesions are more common in the lips, cheeks, and palate, with primary involvement of the tongue being extremely rare[3,5,15]. The immunophenotype is often positive for S100, mammaglobin, CK7, and MUC4, and negative for DOG-1, which aids in differentiation from adenoid cystic carcinoma (AciCC) and other similar conditions[16–18]. Although most cases of this tumor exhibit indolent growth with a high 5-year survival rate, tumor staging, neural invasion, or high-grade components can significantly affect prognosis[4,5].

This article reports a rare case of secretory carcinoma in the facial region and reviews the progress in its diagnosis and treatment based on the latest literature, aiming to enhance the understanding of rare tumors in this region.

Method

All the work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria.

Case presentation/results

Clinical data

The patient is a 37–year-old male. On 25 December 2024, due to the discovery of a left cheek mass that had been present for 3 months, a facial lesion excision surgery was performed at Wuhan Asia General Hospital. Laboratory tests, including platelet count and coagulation function, showed no abnormalities. Past history: He has a history of hypertension and has been treated with oral Azilsartan kamedoxomil. He has no history of diabetes, heart disease, etc. Physical examination: Body temperature: 36.2°C, pulse rate: 68 beats per minute, respiration rate: 18 beats per minute, and blood pressure: 150/101 mmHg. A subcutaneous mass of approximately 3 × 3 cm in size can be palpated on the left cheek, with a soft texture, no tenderness, poor mobility, and no redness, swelling, or ulceration of the skin. Auxiliary examination: On 27 November 2024, a facial ultrasound was performed at an external hospital, showing a solid, slightly hypoechoic mass between the fat layer and muscle layer of the left cheek. After completing the examination in our hospital, a facial lesion resection surgery was performed under general anesthesia on 27 December 2024, and a pathological examination was conducted after the surgery.

Follow-up

The patient in this case did not receive any other treatment after surgery. Through regular telephone follow-up for 1 year, the patient’s condition was good, and there was no recurrence or metastasis observed during regular follow-up examinations.

Discussion

The clinical and imaging characteristics of salivary gland tumors often lack specificity, necessitating precise differentiation through pathology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and molecular testing[19]. In the 2022 update of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, the classification of secretory carcinomas in the salivary glands was further refined, introducing microsecretory carcinoma as an emerging subtype and emphasizing the central role of molecular testing in diagnosis[20]. SCSG is often confused with various salivary gland tumors, with key differentiation points including histomorphological diversity, IHC markers, and genetic characteristics[16–18,20]. Recent studies have shown that high-grade transformation cases can increase the risk of lymphatic metastasis, making molecular testing crucial to avoid misdiagnosis[20]. The following focuses on the differentiation between SCSG and common salivary gland tumors. (1) AciCC: Both present as slowly progressing painless masses, but AciCC cells are rich in blue–purple proenzyme granules and exhibit more morphological diversity, including solid, follicular, or papillary cystic types[21]. On IHC, AciCC often shows positive staining for CK7, ANO1, and SOX10, while S100, STAT5A, and mammaglobin are mostly negative. Additionally, AciCC lacks the ETV6-NTRK3 fusion[22]. (2) Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC): MEC is composed of mucous cells, epidermal cells, and intermediate cells, often presenting as cribriform or cystic structures with mucus secretion[23]. Unlike SCSG, MEC does not show significant vacuolization or eosinophilic secretions accumulation in the background. IHC demonstrates positive staining for CK7, CK19, and P63, while S100 and mammaglobin are negative. In genetics, MEC is typically characterized by t(11;19) (q21;p13), leading to a CRTC1-MAML2 fusion, with a minority accompanied by NTRK3 mutations, but without ETV6-NTRK3 fusion[20]. (3) Salivary duct carcinoma (SDC): SDC is a highly invasive tumor, often accompanied by comedo necrosis, duct-like or cribriform structures, and marked cellular pleomorphism[24]. SDC has a poor prognosis and is prone to distant metastasis. Its IHC expression is S100 negative and androgen receptor positive. There is no ETV6 fusion at the molecular level, but there may be HER2 amplification. (4) Intra-ductal carcinoma (IC): IC is characterized by being confined within salivary gland ducts and surrounded by myoepithelial cells, which are positive for P63, Calponin, CK14, and SMA[20]. (5) Polymorphous adenocarcinoma (PADK): The histological characteristics of PADK include extensive invasive growth of the tumor, accompanied by a lobular pattern, with single cells surrounding nerves presenting a target-like appearance. Tubular, fascicular, and papillary structures can also be seen[25]. Immunohistochemical features include positive P63 and SOX10 and negative P40[20].

As mentioned earlier, salivary gland secretory carcinoma is more likely to occur in the parotid gland, and it is relatively rare to occur in small salivary glands in the oral cavity. Cases originating from the face are extremely rare. Since the official inclusion of this disease in the WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumors in 2017, no reports of this type of tumor occurring on the face have been found through a literature search. The high rarity of this case prevented us from considering the possibility of this type of tumor before surgery. We initially tended to make a diagnosis of papillary cystadenocarcinoma, but after a series of examinations and multiple consultations, it was determined to be secretory carcinoma. This diagnosis and treatment experience remind us to be highly vigilant about the possibility of rare salivary gland tumors, such as secretory carcinoma, in differential diagnosis.

At present, the treatment strategy for salivary gland secretory carcinoma is mainly surgical, with complete tumor resection to achieve curative effects. For cases with high-risk characteristics, such as incomplete resection during surgery, shortest distance between the resection margin and the tumor being less than 5 mm, nerve or vascular involvement, late stage (T3 or T4), and positive regional lymph nodes, postoperative radiation therapy is recommended. Research has shown that such comprehensive plans can significantly improve patient survival[24]. Although SCSG is mostly a low-grade malignancy[26], there are still some patients who can transform into high-grade malignancy, and histological examination shows that the prognosis is particularly poor for tumors with necrotic areas[24]. Therefore, all cases require continuous monitoring after surgery, including imaging examinations and clinical follow-up.

In recent years, targeted therapy has become an important complementary treatment for invasive SCSG[13], especially inhibitors targeting the ETV6-NTRK3 fusion gene (such as erlotinib or entrectinib), which can be used for systemic therapy of recurrent or metastatic cases. In addition, in some preliminary studies and clinical observations, immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 drugs) have been explored for use in some patients with salivary gland malignancies, providing potential treatment options for cases with certain subtypes or specific molecular features[27].

This article reports, for the first time, a case of salivary gland secretory carcinoma that occurred on the face. This case, from the initial diagnosis of papillary cystadenocarcinoma to the final pathological diagnosis of salivary gland secretory carcinoma, fully demonstrates the complex process of achieving an accurate diagnosis of facial masses. It once again emphasizes the decisive role of pathology, especially IHC and genetic testing techniques, in the differential diagnosis of salivary gland tumors. It is also hoped that this case can improve the understanding of clinicians and pathologists regarding rare tumors in this area and accumulate experience for the diagnosis and treatment of related cases.
