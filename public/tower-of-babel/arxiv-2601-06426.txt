NC-Bench: An LLM Benchmark for Evaluating Conversational Competence
Robert J. Moore, Sungeun An, Farhan Ahmed, Jay Pankaj Gala

Abstract
The Natural Conversation Benchmark ( NC-Bench ) introduce a new approach to evaluating the general conversational competence of large language models (LLMs). Unlike prior benchmarks that focus on the content of model behavior, NC-Bench focuses on the form and structure of natural conversation. Grounded in the IBM Natural Conversation Framework (NCF), NC-Bench comprises three distinct sets. The Basic Conversation Competence set evaluates fundamental sequence management practices, such as answering inquiries, repairing responses, and closing conversational pairs. The RAG set applies the same sequence management patterns as the first set but incorporates retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). The Complex Request set extends the evaluation to complex requests involving more intricate sequence management patterns. Each benchmark tests a model’s ability to produce contextually appropriate conversational actions in response to characteristic interaction patterns. Initial evaluations across 6 open-source models and 14 interaction patterns show that models perform well on basic answering tasks, struggle more with repair tasks (especially repeat), have mixed performance on closing sequences, and find complex multi-turn requests most challenging, with Qwen models excelling on the Basic set and Granite models on the RAG set and the Complex Request set. By operationalizing fundamental principles of human conversation, NC-Bench provides a lightweight, extensible, and theory-grounded framework for assessing and improving the conversational abilities of LLMs beyond topical or task-specific benchmarks.

Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) increasingly serve as the foundation for conversational agents, evaluating their conversational competence has become a central challenge in user experience and AI design. Unlike traditional NLU-based agents (e.g., Siri, Alexa, Cortana, Watson), which followed predefined intent–response structures, LLM-based systems generate open-ended, contextually adaptive responses through natural language generation. While this flexibility enables more natural interactions, it also makes it more challenging to evaluate whether their responses are coherent, contextually appropriate, or aligned with natural conversation.

The term “conversation” is used to refer to many different kinds of interaction, from emails to social media posts to instant messaging to telephone calls
9
. By
conversational competence
we mean the ability to engage in natural conversation. Natural conversation is the standard way that people interact through language, especially face to face. Natural conversation, or “naturally occurring talk-in-interaction,” is documented in the field of Conversation Analysis and operationalized in the IBM Natural Conversation Framework

While prior benchmarks have assessed language models on tasks such as reasoning, factual QA, and instruction-following

, few focus on conversation as social interaction. Natural conversation involves more than information exchange; it requires repairing answers, closing sequences, and many other practices that coordinate mutual understanding between participants

. Measuring such behaviors is essential for determining whether an LLM can engage in genuinely conversational interaction rather than simply producing plausible text.

To address this gap, we introduce the Natural Conversation Benchmark (
NC-Bench
), a benchmark designed to evaluate the conversational competence of large language models. Our benchmark operationalizes “conversation competence” as the model’s ability to perform and coordinate a range of conversational actions across multiple dialogue scenarios. These include managing turn exchanges (e.g., eliciting information, redoing the prior request), responding appropriately to conversation signals (e.g., “Got it,” “never mind”), and maintaining conversation patterns in the face of a document context. NC-Bench
consists of a structured set of conversation prompts derived from the IBM Natural Conversation Framework (NCF)

Each test case presents the model with a conversational scenario designed to probe specific interaction patterns. The model’s responses are then classified into predefined categories and evaluated based on whether the assigned labels represent acceptable answers.

Our benchmark serves several purposes. First, it provides a standardized way to evaluate and compare LLMs on their ability to handle natural conversational patterns. Second, it offers insight into how various design choices (e.g., training data composition or prompting strategy) affect the emergence of conversational competence. By identifying specific gaps or patterns that models fail to complete correctly, it enables developers and conversational UX designers to diagnose strengths and weaknesses and to direct targeted improvement efforts. By measuring how well models understand and respond in realistic conversations,
NC-Bench
helps identify and refine models suited for downstream tasks such as customer support, tutoring, and other dialogue-based applications. To support further research, we open source

Related Work

The Natural Conversation benchmark (
NC-Bench
) differs from other benchmarks that analyze characteristics of model behavior such as engagingness, truthfulness, offensiveness, harmfulness or discrimination because they focus on the form of the interaction rather than on its content. They are more similar to the MT-Bench benchmarks that analyze coherence across multiple turns
14
. However, unlike MT-Bench, the
NC-Bench
tests generic conversation practices, like repeating a prior turn or closing a sequence, rather than domain-specific skills, like solving math problems or completing writing tasks. Such generic practices are applicable to conversations about any topic because they are part of the infrastructure of communication independently of what is being communicated. Another similar approach to
NC-Bench
is that of the new MT-RAG benchmarks, which evaluate retrieval augmented generation (RAG) and multi-turn question answering
5
. MT-RAG focuses on the faithfulness, appropriateness, naturalness and completeness of models’ answers. However,
NC-Bench
differs from MT-RAG in that it focuses on the range of models’ general conversational abilities, such as answering, repairing, closing, prefacing, eliciting, telling, rather than on the range of question types, such as comparison, explanation, factoid. Although RAG patterns are not the focus of
NC-Bench
, it tests models for action type across both RAG and non-RAG patterns to see if information passages change the behavior of models. In addition to having different but complementary goals, MT-RAG and
NC-Bench
employ different methods for testing multi-turn patterns: the former uses turn-by-turn testing, while the latter uses transcript continuation.

3
The Natural Conversation Benchmarks (NC-Bench)

NC-Bench
aims to answer the question: How well can generative AI converse like humans do? In other words, the benchmarks begin to measure the general conversational competence of LLMs. They do this by testing models’ ability to generate an appropriate type of conversational action, or dialogue act, in response to a particular sequence of actions. The sequences of conversational actions, or patterns, are adapted from conversation science, specifically the model of sequence organization in the field of conversation analysis
(
12
)
and the pattern library of IBM Natural Conversation Framework
(
10
)
. Models are tested by generating the next line in a transcript.
NC-Bench
is a lightweight method that is easily extensible to more conversation patterns.

The overall construction process includes four stages: 1) Select Pattern, 2) Create Example, 3) Generate (Prompt Models), 4) Judge (Classify Output) and Evaluate (Score Output).

3.1
Natural Conversation Patterns

To evaluate the conversational competence of LLMs, a generic conversational interaction pattern is selected, consisting of a sequence of actions or dialogue acts that characterize natural human conversation. Grounding our benchmark approach in the Natural Conversation Framework (NCF) provides extensibility
10
. Not only does the NCF contain over 120 patterns in its library, but its adaptation of the literature and methods of Conversation Analysis mean that additional patterns can be added systematically. Table
1
summarizes patterns included in the benchmark.
NC-Bench
includes three different sets: basic conversation competence, basic conversation competence with RAG, and complex requests. Patterns and examples can be found in Appendix
C
.

3.1.1
Basic Conversation Competence

The basic conversation competence capture practices of sequence management:
answering inquiries
,
repairing answers
, and
closing pair sequences
. This set uses ordinary conversational use cases and does not include passages for retrieval augmented generation (RAG).

Answering inquiries
involves three patterns:

•

Inquiry
tests whether the model can produce an appropriate and relevant answer to a user’s initial information-seeking inquiry.

•

Incremental Request
tests whether the model can maintain topical continuity by providing a suitable response when the user extends a prior inquiry (e.g., “What about…”, “How about…”).

•

Self-Correction
tests whether the model can revise its prior answer appropriately when the user corrects their earlier question (e.g., “No, I meant…”). Instead of simply doing a new request, the self-correction is further marked as a redo of the first request.

Repairing answers
involves four patterns.

•

Definition Request
tests whether the model can provide a clear explanation of the meaning of a word or phrase it previously used, in response to a user signal of non-understanding (e.g., “What do you mean by blew up?”, “What is a FHO?”, “Define keto”).

•

Paraphrase Request
tests whether the model can reword all or part of its previous utterance without changing its meaning, in response to a user request for clarification (e.g., “What do you mean?”, “I don’t understand”, “Can you say that a different way?”).

•

Repeat Request
tests whether the model can accurately repeat all or part of its previous utterance when the user indicates a problem hearing the prior turn (e.g., “What did you say?”, “Say again”, “I didn’t hear you”).

•

Example Request
tests whether the model can provide a concrete instance or illustration of a general concept it previously mentioned, in response to a user request (e.g., “Can you give an example?”, “Like what?”).

Closing the sequence
involves two patterns:

•

Sequence Closer
tests whether the model can appropriately recognize and produce signals that a conversational sequence or activity is possibly complete and the conversation is ready to move on. Such closers include acknowledgments (e.g., “okay,” “got it”), appreciations (e.g., “thank you,” “thanks”), laughter tokens (e.g., “haha,” “lol”), positive assessments (e.g., “great,” “excellent”), or negative assessments (e.g., “too bad,” “oh well”).

•

Sequence Abort
tests whether the model can recognize and respond appropriately when a user cancels or retracts a previous statement, indicating that it is no longer relevant and no response is required (e.g., “never mind,” “forget it”).

3.1.2
Retrieval Augmented Generation

The Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) set includes more or less the same set of generic conversation patterns as the Basic Conversation Competence set but with information-seeking use cases and a passage for grounding. Determining the faithfulness of the models’ responses to the passage are not the primary goal. Instead, the goal is to determine if the model can maintain the conversation pattern in the face of a document context. Within this setting, the Inquiry pattern is divided into three contextual variations:

•

Inquiry (Grounded)
tests whether the model can provide an appropriate and relevant answer when the user’s question is supported by the provided document context.

•

Inquiry (Ungrounded)
tests whether the model can appropriately recognize when a user’s question is NOT supported by the document and respond without producing unsupported or fabricated information.

•

Incremental Request/Self-Correction
combines two patterns that share a similar structure: basically a second answer by the agent either in response to additional details or to the correction of a prior detail.

3.1.3
Complex Request

Sequence management patterns involving complex requests. Such requests require the agent to elicit details from the fictional user (for example, slot filling). Other patterns involve preliminaries to the inquiry-answer pair (i.e., pre-expansions).

•

Preliminary
tests whether the model can appropriately handle user utterances that provide preliminary context before making a request. This includes two scenarios:

1.

Preliminary-Screen: The user first checks whether the assistant can discuss a topic or provide a certain type of help (e.g., “Do you work here?”).

2.

Preliminary-Detail: The user provides relevant details in advance that help contextualize the upcoming request (e.g., “Oh great! You do pour over coffee!”).

•

Recommendation
tests whether the model can generate appropriate recommendations or suggestions based on user requests and the details provided. It includes three variations:

1.

Recommendation-Compact: The user provides all relevant details in a single turn.

2.

Recommendation-Expanded: The user provides partial information, and the assistant must elicit the remaining details across multiple turns before giving a complete recommendation.

3.

Recommendation-Incremental: The user provides some details and may then extend the request or ask for additional options.

•

Detail Request
tests whether the model can elicit additional information required to fulfill a user request or answer a question when the initial user input is incomplete. The key focus is on the assistant’s ability to identify missing information and ask for it rather than directly providing the final answer or recommendation. This pattern includes three variations:

1.

Detail Request-All: The user provides no information in the initial user turn,

2.

Detail Request-Partial: The user provides partial information in the initial user turn,

3.

Detail Request-Expanded: The user has already provided some information in prior turns.

•

Expansion
tests whether the model can appropriately expand a conversational sequence during a request. This pattern has two main variations:

1.

Expansion-Choices: Evaluates whether the model can provide options or choices when the user explicitly asks for them. For example, when a guest asks for restaurant recommendations, the assistant should be able to respond with available options: “We have tall, grande, and venti sizes” or “We sell gas, charcoal, and wood-burning grills.”

2.

Expansion-Repair: Evaluates whether the model can provide clarification or elaboration when the user signals a need for repair. For example, if a customer asks, “What do you mean by sweetener?” the assistant should respond with a clarification that resolves the user’s confusion.

This pattern assesses the model’s ability to maintain a coherent, informative conversation while expanding on the previous turn based on the user’s explicit requests or repair signals.

3.2
Create Example

After defining the general conversation patterns, the next step involves generating specific instances, or examples, that operationalize each pattern within a concrete domain or use case. Each example represents a realistic conversational exchange that instantiates the underlying structure of the pattern while varying the topical content, wording, and situational context. For each pattern, at least twenty examples were created to ensure sufficient diversity and coverage across linguistic and situational variations. Finally, the last assistant turn in each example was removed so that the remaining dialogue could serve as an input prompt for model evaluation.

Examples were created by adapting from existing datasets and manual authoring:

•

DailyDialogue
6
: Examples adapted from the DailyDialog corpus were used to represent ordinary everyday conversational situations in the Basic Conversation Competence set.

•

Wikipedia
7
: Passages from Wikipedia were served as external reference documents for the RAG set.

•

Synthetic business scenarios: 20 realistic business scenarios were manually generated, including hotel recommendations, travel advice and booking, opening an account, flights, and life insurance.

3.3
Generation

For each example of a selected conversational pattern, we construct a corresponding prompt by combining the example transcript with an instruction to continue the conversation. The model is then asked to generate the next turn, completing the final action that was previously removed from the pattern. Example prompts are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

3.4
Evaluation

We evaluate model outputs using a LLM as a judge. Specifically, the judge LLM classifies each model response according to the type of conversation action it represents. For each test, the judge is provided with a tailored instruction that defines the set of possible action classes (see the prompts in Appendix
A.2
). Once classified, the output is scored using a set of predefined rules that specify which action types are acceptable for the given test. Each response receives a binary score (0 or 1) based on these rules. The scores across all tests in a set are then aggregated to compute the model’s final evaluation score.

4.2
Analysis

Overall, the models performed well on most benchmarks. All test types were completed correctly by at least some models, at least some of the time (across the 20 examples). In many cases, many models completed the tests correctly most or all of the time, which helps confirm their validity.

The answering tasks—inquiry, incremental request and self-correction—were the easiest for all models no doubt because question answering is a major task that models are trained on and aligned for. It therefore also serves as a kind of reality check: if a model does poorly on question answering, there may be something wrong with it or with the testing procedure. The inquiry task consists of simply generating an answer to an inquiry (one pair), whereas the other answering patterns involve two QA pairs (i.e., multi-turn). The incremental request involves generating a second answer to an inquiry that is incrementally different from the first, responding to turns like “what about X?” or “how about Y?” Similarly, the self-correction pattern involves generating a second answer in response to the user’s correction of his or her own prior turn, that is, “no, I mean Y.” In all three patterns, the model should generate the action “answer,” although “non answer” (I don’t know) or a repair request are also correct.

Unlike the answering tasks, the repairing tasks involve redoing all or part of a prior answer. They include requests for a repeat, paraphrase, example or definition. These were more challenging for the models. Although most models were quite good at keeping paraphrases, examples and definitions straight, all of them struggled at simply repeating a prior response. For example, the Granite models consistently paraphrase the prior turn instead of repeating it in more or less the same words. The models may be fine-tuned not to repeat what they said previously, which is not so useful in text-based interaction, due to the chat history, but critical in voice interfaces, in which typically there is no chat history. While the repairing tests do not project new answers, they still project answer-like responses that are tied to the agent’s prior response. And these repairs can resemble summarization, a task that LLMs are often trained on.

The closing task, on the other hand, requires not answering or doing anything like answering. Both the sequence closer and sequence abort tasks require the model to stop answering and to transition to another topic or activity. The former acknowledges the prior answer as adequate, e.g., with “okay,” “got it,” “thank you,” etc., while the latter aborts or cancels the inquiry sequence, with “never mind” or “forget it.” While some models performed perfectly on these closing tasks, many struggled. Models that underperformed tended to elaborate their prior answer, giving additional unrequested information. This could perhaps reflect the practice of fine-tuning models to be “helpful” by giving more than a minimal answer.

The Retrieval Augmented Generation, or RAG, test set differs from the Basic set in that the use cases are all information-seeking rather than action-seeking and include information passages in which to ground the responses and discourage hallucination. Despite these differences, the conversation patterns in the Basic set performed much the same way in the RAG set. All models performed perfectly on the inquiry and correction tasks, using the information passage to construct their answer. Similarly, the models performed slightly better on the repair tasks, except for repeat, which was equally bad. The increased scores no doubt reflect the fact that the RAG passages provide answers, definitions, paraphrases and examples for the models to use, making it somewhat easier than fabricating those in the ordinary conversation of the Basic test set. And the models similarly showed mixed success with the closing tasks.

A new element with the RAG set was “grounding” or limiting the model’s answer to information provided in the accompanying passage instead of other information from pre-training or entirely hallucinated answers. So in the ungrounded inquiry task, models should always respond with a version of “I don’t know” because none of the answers are contained in the RAG passages. However, we see that all of the models struggled with this by frequently providing answers nonetheless.

Finally, we see comparable performance of the patterns in the Basic set when adapted to the use cases of the Complex Request set. While the Basic set consists of ordinary conversation use cases and the RAG set of information-seeking ones, the Complex Request set consists of use cases involving business processes. Each use case involves a request for service with a required set of details that must be elicited before the request can be granted (also known as the “slot-filling” pattern). In this set, the models performed well on the definition and paraphrase repair tasks and poorly on repeat, as expected. However, they performed somewhat less well on the example task than with the Basic and RAG sets. This seems due to the fact that it is harder even for humans to fabricate examples of entities in particular business processes, like financial planning or construction contracting, than for ordinary conversation or than simply selecting them from a given RAG passage. And in the Complex Request set, like Basic and RAG, the models showed mixed performance on the closing tasks.

What is unique about the Complex Request set is management of the required details. Fulfilling Complex Requests was harder for the models than answering inquiries. The performance on the preliminary, recommendation, detail request, expansion and correction tasks were more mixed than performance on the inquiry tasks. Overall the models were good at eliciting request-relevant details although not always the details specified in the instruction prompt (i.e., the “grounded” ones). And in the case of the self-correction task, models tended to continue requesting known details instead of fulfilling the corrected request.

5
Conclusions

Taken as a whole, model performance is impressive. NC-Bench shows that many basic conversation patterns are known to the models. No doubt natural conversation patterns are learned by the models in pre-training, along with many other kinds of language patterns. The poor and mixed performance on some tasks may be the unintended result of prompting at the system level, configuring parameters, and fine-tuning or aligning with additional data. In short, the natural conversation patterns appear to be in the models, but how to shape generation to optimize their performance and increase their conversational competence is still an open area of investigation and design.

NC-Bench provides a foundation for systematic evaluation of the conversational competence of LLMs. By specifying particular patterns, such as requesting a paraphrase or aborting a sequence, NC-Bench enables the identification of specific gaps in model ability, such its inability to repeat its own prior turn. Such gaps in conversational ability may then be correctable with system prompting or with fine-tuning. While NC-Bench includes multiple basic conversation patterns, focusing on adjacency pairs, there are still more to add. Future work will add the storytelling sequence type, used for long answers, narratives, instruction giving, etc.
10
. Future patterns may also include those for embodied conversation, for example, deictic references to physical artifacts (“look at this” or “it is there”). By open-sourcing NC-Bench, we hope to achieve a comprehensive set of patterns through community contribution.

B.2
Label Definitions

•

Answer: The preferred response to a question, inquiry or request, which provides the requested information or fulfills the request or provides a recommendation; for example, giving business hours in response to a request for a bakery’s business hours, cancelling an account, or recommending a hotel to stay at.

•

NonAnswer: Failure to provide an answer to an inquiry or to fulfill a request for action either by giving an excuse or refusing to do so; for example, ”I’m afraid I cannot close your account” or ”We have no models that fit your preferences” or ”I’m sorry, I don’t know the answer to your question.”

•

Repeat: When AGENT’s last turn (line (placeholder)) is a repeat of AGENT’s prior turn (previous turn) using the same words, for example, to resolve USER’s trouble in hearing. It may be a full or partial repeat of the misheard turn.

•

RepeatRequest: A request that the other speaker repeat all or part of what they previously said, in order to resolve a trouble in hearing; for example, ”what did you say?” or ”say again” or ”I didn’t hear you.”

•

Paraphrase: When AGENT’s last turn (line (placeholder)) is a paraphrase of AGENT’s prior turn (previous turn) using different words, for example, to resolve USER’s trouble in understanding. It may be a full or partial paraphrase of the misunderstood turn.

•

ParaphraseRequest: A request that the other speaker reword all or part of what they previously said, without changing its meaning, in order to resolve a trouble in understanding; for example, ”what do you mean?” or ”I don’t understand” or ”can you say that a different way?”

•

Definition: When AGENT’s last turn (line (placeholder)) is a definition of a keyword or phrase in AGENT’s prior turn (previous turn), for example, to resolve USER’s trouble in understanding that keyword or phrase; for example, ”artificial intelligence means technology that enables computers to simulate human reasoning or problem-solving” or ”Chantilly is a kind of sweetened whipped cream.”

•

DefinitionRequest: A request for the intended meaning of a word or phrase previously produced by another speaker, in order to resolve a trouble in understanding; for example, ”what do you mean by blew up?” or ”what is a FHO?” or ”define keto.”

•

Example: When AGENT’s last turn (line (placeholder)) provides an example of a keyword or phrase in AGENT’s prior turn (previous turn), for instance, to resolve USER’s trouble in understanding that keyword or phrase; for example, an ”Old Fashioned” is an example of a ”classic cocktail.”

•

ExampleRequest: A request for a particular instance of a general idea expressed in a previous turn; for example, ”can you give an example?” or ”like what?”

•

Appreciation: A display of gratitude for something the other person said or did; for example, ”thank you” or ”that is much appreciated.”

•

GratitudeReceipt: An acknowledgement that one has been thanked or appreciated. It usually takes the form of a simple common phrase; for example, ”you’re welcome” or ”no problem.”

•

Assessment: A characterization of something as good or bad, positive or negative. Sometimes it follows an assessment by the other person; for example, ”great,” ”like it,” ”love it,” ”cool,” ”wow,” ”that sucks,” ”oh no.”

•

PreClosing: A signal to end the current topic or the conversation itself. This may be an intention to leave, reference a future conversation, or check if the other person has more topics to talk about; for example, ”anything else?” or ”got to go” or ”is that all?”

•

Apology: An expression of regret for inconvenience, offense or harm one caused; for example, ”I’m sorry” or ”sorry.”

•

SequenceAbort: A signal to cancel or retract something previously said, indicating that it is no longer relevant and that a response to it is no longer due; for example, ”never mind” or ”forget it.”

•

Silence: An empty response or the indication of a ”silence” or ”pause”; for example, ”AGENT: ”, ”AGENT: (silence)”, ”AGENT: …….”, ”AGENT:\n\n”, or ”AGENT: [empty].”

•

NonVerbal: An indication of a facial expression or bodily gesture in words or an emoticon; for example, ((smiles)) or ((nods)).

•

NewTopic: Changing the topic of conversation, proposing to change topics, discontinuing the current topic, or proposing an alternative course of action; for example, ”let’s talk about something else,” ”on a different note,” or ”by the way.”

•

DetailRequestGrounded: Asking for additional information that is required for fulfilling a request or answering an inquiry. The detail request is grounded if it asks about one of the particular details: (placeholder).

•

DetailRequestUngrounded: Asking about any other details that are not specified in DetailRequestGrounded.

•

Affirmation: A response that positively affirms a prior yes/no question by the USER; for example, ”yes,” ”sure,” ”yeah,” or any equivalent affirmative words.

•

Acknowledgment: A simple indication that what the other person said was heard or understood; for example, ”okay,” ”all right,” ”I see,” ”got it,” ”oh,” ”I know.”

•

HelpOffer: Offering to help USER generally or with a specific type of request; for example, ”how can I help you?” or ”I can help you with that,” or ”would you like me to look up the business hours?”

•

ChoiceGiving: Giving choices to USER for answering a detail request; for example, ”We have tall, grande and venti sizes” or ”We sell gas, charcoal and wood burning grills.”

•

OOC: A stance that is not in the voice of the AGENT, but in that of another character, such as an analyst or AI personality; for example, ”(Note:),” ”In this improved conversation,” or ”Or, you could say:” (Chain-of-Thought).

•

Other: An utterance that does not fit any of the above classes.
