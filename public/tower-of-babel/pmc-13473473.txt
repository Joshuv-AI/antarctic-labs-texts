Real-world use of submaximal doses of long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide in patients with obesity: a prospective observational study
Authors: Zuzana Miertová, Patrik Lecký, Boris Focko, Tomáš Bolek, Martin Jozef Péč, Jakub Jurica, Norbert Nagy, Peter Galajda, Marián Mokáň, Matej Samoš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a global health problem with numerous metabolic and mechanical complications. In previous studies, a long-acting glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) semaglutide has been identified as one of the most promising medications for treating obesity. We carried out a prospective observational study investigating the effect of submaximal doses of semaglutide in 56 adult patients with obesity (mean age 49 ± 12 years, 42 female and 14 male). We evaluated the effects on body weight, waist circumference, height/waist ratio, and BMI during 3-month follow-up. 30 patients in our group also reached a 6-month follow-up. Our patients achieved a weight loss of 6.45 ± 0.31% (p < 0.01) in 3 months of semaglutide therapy, and in the subgroup of 30 patients where semaglutide was administered for 6 months, weight loss was 11.35 ± 0.47% (p < 0.01). Regarding waist circumference, patients achieved a 7 cm decrease in waist circumference in 3 months, and an additional 6 cm at 6 months, respectively (p < 0.01). The mean height/waist ratio decreased from 0.71 ± 0.08 to 0.67 ± 0.09 after 3 months of treatment (p < 0.01) and to 0.63 ± 0.09 (p < 0.01) after 6-month of semaglutide treatment. Mean BMI decreased from 40.3 ± 6.7 to 37.5 ± 6.83 kg/m2 (p < 0.01) after 3 months of treatment and to 35.5 ± 7.73 kg/m2 in the subgroup with 6 months of therapy (p < 0.01). Our study showed a significant decrease in body weight, waist circumference, height/waist ratio, and BMI in patients with obesity treated with submaximal doses of semaglutide.Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist, Metabolic health, Weight loss, Weight management, Anti-obesity medicationSubject terms: Endocrine system and metabolic diseases, Outcomes research

Introduction

Obesity is a complex metabolic disorder characterized by excessive fat tissue accumulation in the body. In addition, approximately 1/8 of the world’s population suffers from obesity; according to WHO statistics from 2022, 43% of adults aged 18 years and older are overweight, and 16% of adults meet the criteria for obesity1. It is predicted that the prevalence of obesity will grow in the following decade. Obesity is associated with a high mortality rate, with at least 2.8 million people dying each year as a result of being overweight or having obesity, and an estimated 35.8 million (2.3%) of global DALYs are caused by being overweight or having obesity2.

Pathogenesis of obesity is far more complex than fat accumulation and involves interactions among many cell types. In obesity, we see hypertrophied adipocytes with a dysregulated adipokine secretion profile, increased recruitment of inflammatory cells, and impaired metabolic homeostasis that eventually results in the development of systemic insulin resistance, a phenotype typically encountered in type 2 diabetes3. Studies have shown that improper interactions between satiety hormones and the central nervous system’s satiety centers in the hypothalamic area lead to increased caloric intake, creating and maintaining excess adipose tissue. Pathologic adaptive reactions occur after weight loss is achieved with lifestyle changes. For example, restriction of caloric intake results in a decrease in anorexigenic hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and an increase in orexigenic hormones such as ghrelin4,5, and GLP-1 secretion seems to be significantly lowered in patients with obesity6. Therefore, specific agents tackling imbalances in satiety hormones are required to treat obesity effectively. Although effective management of obesity may require multidisciplinary treatment, including non-pharmacologic approaches, pharmacotherapy, and surgical and endoscopic procedures, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) are one of the most promising therapeutic agents. GLP-1 RA have several effects on various organ systems, combining central and peripheral actions that promote satiety, decrease hunger, decrease appetite, and ultimately lead to reduced food intake4. Long-acting GLP-1 RA semaglutide has demonstrated one of the most significant effects on weight loss of all obesity medications, with reductions of approximately 15% of initial weight at 68 weeks when administrated in dose of 2.4 mg once a week7. Nevertheless, this high-dose regimen might be connected with a higher risk of side effects, leading to early therapy discontinuation and lower drug compliance. As there is no post-marketing study evaluating the effect of low-dose (submaximal dose) semaglutide on obesity, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of submaximal doses of a long-acting GLP-1 RA semaglutide in patients with obesity. The primary goals of our study were to establish the effect of submaximally dosed semaglutide on body weight, waist circumference, height/waist ratio, and body mass index (BMI) after 3 months of submaximally dosed semaglutide therapy.

Methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a pilot prospective observational study investigating the effect of semaglutide in patients with obesity. The study was carried out as part of the project „Long-term strategic research of prevention, intervention and mechanisms of obesity and its comorbidities “(Operational Programme Integrated Infrastructure ITMS 2014 + : 313011V344). The study was conducted in line with all Ethical Principles – formal approval of the study was obtained from Local Ethical Comitte, and all the enrolled patients signed a written informed consent with study participation. Additionally, all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Patients were eligible for study if they met the inclusion and exclusion criteria reported in (Table 1).

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The study was conducted at the Obesity Clinic of the Department of Internal Medicine I, Jessenius Faculty of Medicine in Martin and Teaching Hospital in Martin, Slovakia. The study enrollment period took 3 months (from 15th June 2024 until 15th September 2024). The sample size was determined by study enrollment period duration (all consecutive patients treated for obesity at the Obesity Clinic fulfilling study inclusion and not meeting study exclusion criteria during this enrollment period were included). Due to the pilot/preliminary and hypothesis generating character of this study, no specific sample size calculation was performed.

All patients were treated with semaglutide at an initial dose of 0.25 mg s.c. weekly, which was up-titrated over 3 months. After achieving the desired weight loss (approx. 2–5 kg per month), the dose of semaglutide was not increased further. Patients received submaximal doses of semaglutide ranging from 0.25 to 1.5 mg once a week administrated subcutaneously. No other pharmacologic antiobesity therapy was administrated in studied patients. Patients were instructed on lifestyle changes following current guidelines8, a reduced-calorie diet (500 kcal/day deficit relative to estimated energy expenditure) and increased physical activity (150 min of aerobic activity/week and 2–3 strength training sessions per week) was recommended. During the study, the patients went through regular monthly check-ups in the cardio-obesity out-patient clinic. These examinations included measuring anthropometric parameters (height, weight, waist, and hip circumference), calculating height/waist ratio, BMI, and basic laboratory examination. Patients were monitored monthly for the side effects of semaglutide, including diarrhea, nausea, constipation, abdominal pain, acute pancreatitis, fatigue, and headache.

End-points and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint of the study was the reduction of body weight, waist circumference, height/waist ratio, and body mass index (BMI) after 3-months of submaximally dosed semaglutide therapy. The secondary endpoint was the effect of submaximally dosed semaglutide on these parameters after 6-months of therapy. These end-points were analyzed using the paired samples T-Test. To control for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Linear correlations were performed with Pearson test, while Spearman test was used for rank correlations. Multivariable regression analysis was performed (with gender, age, baseline BMI, baseline lipid levels, glycemia, hemoglobin levels, presence of arterial hypertension and dyslipidemia tested as a possible independent predictors of weight loss). We used the statistical software Jamovi 2.4.14 to perform the statistical analysis.

Effect of submaximally dosed semaglutide on primary outcome

In our group of 56 patients, where submaximal doses of semaglutide were administered for 3 months, the patients achieved a mean weight loss of 8 kg (Fig. 1). Mean weight before starting treatment was 117 ± 25.8 kg and was reduced to 109 ± 24.5 kg (p < 0.01), representing a mean weight loss of 6,45 ± 0.31% after 3 months of semaglutide administration. At 3 months, a total of 8 patients (14.3%) achieved a weight loss of more than 10% of their pre-treatment weight. A total of 28 patients (50%) achieved a weight loss of 5–10% of their pre-treatment weight, and the rest of our study group (20 patients total, 35.72%) lost less than 5% of their pre-treatment weight. Looking on waist circumference, a mean decrease in waist circumference of 7 cm in three months was observed. Mean waist circumference before starting treatment was 121 ± 15.4 cm and the mean waist circumference after 3 months of treatment with semaglutide was 114 ± 15.3 cm (p < 0.01). The height/waist ratio decreased by 0.041 (0.71 ± 0.08 versus 0.67 ± 0.09, p < 0.01) and BMI decreased by 2.8 (40.3 ± 6.7 versus 37.5 ± 6.83, p < 0.01) after 3 months of submaximal semaglutide therapy (Fig. 1). No patient discontinued the therapy due to side effects.

Effect of submaximally dosed semaglutide on secondary outcome

In the subgroup of 30 patients where submaximal doses of semaglutide were administered for 6 months, the weight changed from 117 ± 25.8 to 104 ± 29.1 kg (p < 0 0.01), representing a mean weight loss 11.35 ± 0.47% of pre-treatment weight (Fig. 2). At 6 months, a total of 19 patients (63.3%) achieved a weight loss of more than 10% of their pre-treatment weight.

Effect of submaximal doses of semaglutide on body weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference and height/waist ratio after 6 months of therapy.

A total of 8 patients (26.7%) achieved a weight loss between 5 and 10% of the weight before treatment, and only 3 patients (10%) achieved a weight loss of less than 5% of their pretreatment weight (ranging from 3.45 to 4.89%). Looking on the waist circumference, it decreased by an additional 6 cm after 6 months of semaglutide. In the subgroup of patients who reached 6 months of semaglutide therapy, the height/waist ratio decreased by an additional 0.04, and BMI decreased by an additional 2 kg/m2. The final BMI in the subgroup with 6 months of treatment with semaglutide was 35.5 ± 7.73 kg/m2 (p < 0 0.01). Effect of gender and other variables on weight loss outcomes

As mentioned univariable and multivariable regression models were applied to analyze the possible effects of gender and other factors, namely age, baseline BMI, baseline lipid levels, glycemia, hemoglobin levels, presence of hypertension and dyslipidemia on weight loss outcomes.

Looking on the gender effect on weight loss outcomes (Fig. 4), in unadjusted analysis, women revealed more significant weight change after 3 months of therapy (women −6.78 ± 3.89, men −8.64 ± 1.91, p = 0.023), while no significant changes were found after six months of therapy (women 13.18 ± 5.26, men −11.66 ± 2.82, p = 0.334). Nevertheless, these differences were not significant in adjusted analysis (adjusted difference after 3 months for men versus women β = −2.18/−5.25, 0.89/, p = 0.158; R2 = 0.275; adjusted difference after 6 months for men versus women β = 0.95/−6.34, 8.23/, p = 0.784; R2 = 0.318).

Discussion

To date, several studies investigating the effect of semaglutide administration on body weight in patients without diabetes have been published. The largest studies published so far are STEP studies – The Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity, including STEP 1 to STEP 8. The breakthrough study was STEP 1, which showed significant weight loss in non-diabetic patients with obesity with a weekly dosing of 2.4 mg of semaglutide subcutaneously9. Similar results were shown in studies with a longer follow-up period10,11. Additionally, the efficacy of semaglutide was compared to the efficacy of liraglutide in the STEP 8 study. The results of this study showed a significant weight loss in the semaglutide group, and a modest reduction in the group treated with liraglutide12. Besides STEP studies, a retrospective cohort study assessing the efficacy of semaglutide at doses 1.7 and 2.4 mg weekly on weight loss showed significant weight reduction after 3 and 6 months13. Nevertheless, although effective, high semaglutide dosing (2.4 mg/weekly) might increase the risk of adverse gastrointestinal (GI) events (treatment side effects), subsequently leading to premature treatment discontinuation. For example, in the already mentioned STEP 1 trial9, 4.5% of semaglutide-treated patients discontinued treatment due to GI adverse events. In our study, only submaximal doses of semaglutide ranging from 0.25 to 1.5 mg weekly were used, with comparable efficacy on weight reduction, and no need for treatment discontinuation during the first months of semaglutide therapy. To our knowledge, there are only two other studies investigating the effect of submaximal doses of semaglutide in patients without diabetes. These studies were designed as phase 2 clinical studies. In one study, semaglutide was administered daily in doses of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, or 0.4 mg, initiated at 0.05 mg per day and incrementally escalated every 4 weeks. All of the groups showed a significant (dose dependent) weight reduction after 52 weeks of therapy14. Another study showed significant weight loss in patients treated with a weekly dose of 1 mg semaglutide for 12 weeks15. Nevertheless, post-marketing data regarding the use of submaximally dosed semaglutide for the treatment of obesity are still missing. As mentioned, in our study, submaximally dosed semaglutide achieved significant reduction in both primary and secondary end-points, with very good treatment tolerance (no patient needed treatment discontinuation due to side effects). Looking on the possible differences with previously published marketing studies, the mean starting BMI (39.3 kg/m2 and 33.8 vs. 40.3 kg/m2, respectively)14,15 and overall semaglutide weight loss efficacy reported in these studies were slightly lower than that observed in our post-marketing study. In another postmarketing study, Oral et al.16 examined the anti-obesity effect of liraglutide (short-acting GLP-1 RA) in 568 patients (from whom 487 were women) and found a 19 ± 9.06 kg weight reduction after 24 weeks of therapy (representing a 18.55 ± 4.63% weight reduction), and a weight reduction ≥ 5% was detected in 63.2% of treated patients. In this study, the treatment was, in overall, well tolerated with nausea being the most frequent side effect. However, 70% of patients reported at least one episode of side effects, which was more common in the phase of dose up-titration. Right now, it is difficult to explain these differences. One can speculate that, in our study, a close clinical follow-up of patients with monthly clinical controls was implemented and this could lead to additional “psychological” treatment effect. Although it is quite common in daily practice to reduce the dose of semaglutide if the maximal dose is not tolerated; our study shows first post-marketing data with the treatment regimen up-titrating the semaglutide dose only if the previous dose was not clinically effective. With this dosing regimen, we have demonstrated good treatment efficacy (which was similar to the efficacy reported with maximal semaglutide dosing in marketing studies) with an excellent treatment tolerance. We believe that this adds novel data for clinicians dealing with obesity. In the light of these data, the attending physician might, in theory, consider to use sub-maximal treatment regimen also in those patients with high risk of treatment intolerance, avoiding side effects with early discontinuation of the therapy due to these side effects. However, further research will be needed for final conclusions.

It is recognized and also recommended to lose 10 to 15% or more of pre-treatment weight in people with complications of overweight and obesity, such as prediabetes, hypertension, and obstructive sleep apnea, to reduce the disease burden17. In our study, at 3 months, 46.43% of our group of patients achieved a weight loss of 5% or more of their pretreatment weight. In our subgroup of patients treated with semaglutide for 6 months, 90% achieved a weight loss of 5% or more of their pretreatment weight. The 10% margin of weight loss was reached by 14.28% of our patients after 3 months and 63.33% after 6 months. These results show a significant benefit in most patients with real-world use of semaglutide.

In our study, age, baseline BMI, baseline lipid levels, glycemia and hemoglobin levels did not impact significantly weight loss outcomes in a multivariable regression model. This corresponds with the previously mentioned study with liraglutide16, where no significant impact of gender, age, diabetes mellitus and hypertension on weight loss was seen. Based on these observations, it seems that GLP-1 RA achieve consistent weight loss across different patient populations; this might be another learning point with a value for a daily clinical practice.

Our study had limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the results. The first limitation would be the sample size, especially the size of our subgroup, which reached a six month follow-up and consisted of 30 patients. Considering the observational nature of this study, we did not compare weight loss, waist circumference, height/waist ratio, and BMI changes between our group of patients receiving semaglutide and controls with only lifestyle changes. Thus, the weight loss may be associated with our patient’s lifestyle changes. Another limitation is the generalizability of our study since most of our patients were female (42 female and 14 male), and all of our patients were of the caucasian race. Finally, the investigated time frame is relatively short and studies with longer treatment duration and follow-up would be needed to confirm our results.

Conclusion

Treatment with semaglutide in submaximal doses resulted in significant weight loss after three months of treatment, with further weight loss after six months without additional dose adjustment. Semaglutide treatment was well tolerated without severe adverse effects. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer periods of follow-up are required to support the effectiveness and safety of submaximally dosed semaglutide for weight reduction in a real-world setting.
