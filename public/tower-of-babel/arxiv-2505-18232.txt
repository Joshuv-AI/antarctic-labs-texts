ELDeR: Getting Efficient LLMs through Data-Driven Regularized Layer-wise Pruning
Mingkuan Feng, Jinyang Wu, Siyuan Liu, Shuai Zhang, Hongjian Fang, Ruihan Jin, Feihu Che, Pengpeng Shao, Zhengqi Wen, Jianhua Tao

Abstract
The deployment of Large language models (LLMs) in many fields is largely hindered by their high computational and memory costs. Recent studies suggest that LLMs exhibit sparsity, which can be used for pruning. Previous pruning methods typically follow a prune-then-finetune paradigm. Since the pruned parts still contain valuable information, statically removing them without updating the remaining parameters often results in irreversible performance degradation, requiring costly recovery fine-tuning (RFT) to maintain performance. To address this, we propose a novel paradigm: first apply regularization, then prune. Based on this paradigm, we propose ELDeR: Getting E fficient L LMs through D ata-Driv e n R egularized Layer-wise Pruning. We multiply the output of each transformer layer by an initial weight, then we iteratively learn the weights of each transformer layer by using a small amount of data in a simple way. After that, we apply regularization to the difference between the output and input of the layers with smaller weights, forcing the information to be transferred to the remaining layers. Compared with direct pruning, ELDeR reduces the information loss caused by direct parameter removal, thus better preserving the model’s language modeling ability. Experimental results show that ELDeR achieves superior performance compared with powerful layer-wise structured pruning methods, while greatly reducing RFT computational costs. Since ELDeR is a layer-wise pruning method, its end-to-end acceleration effect is obvious, making it a promising technique for efficient LLMs.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have made remarkable progress in various tasks and fields, showcasing their strong capabilities
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. As models grow larger, the increasing number of parameters results in substantial computational and memory demands, posing major challenges to the practical deployment of LLMs. There is an urgent need for techniques that can compress model size without compromising performance.

The main methods for reducing model size include pruning
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, quantization
[
8
,
9
]
, distillation
[
10
]
, and low-rank decomposition
[
11
]
. This study primarily concentrates on pruning, an effective and widely adaptable technique that is orthogonal to other model compression methods. Pruning methods are typically divided into two main types: unstructured pruning
[
12
,
13
]
and structured pruning
[
14
,
15
]
. In contrast to unstructured pruning, structured pruning enables recovery fine-tuning (RFT) for specific downstream tasks without the need for specialized hardware. It can also achieve significant end-to-end acceleration without the need for specially designed hardware
[
16
]
.

Despite advancements, existing structured pruning methods have some limitations. They all follow the paradigm of first prune channels or layers based on a designed metric, and then performing RFT. However, they overlook that important information may also exist in the pruned parts
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, and directly removing them without updating the remaining parameters often results an irreversible decline in model performance. Therefore, obtaining satisfactory performance with pruned models typically requires a large amount of data for RFT
[
14
]
, increasing computational overhead. Moreover, high sparsity can cause the model’s performance to collapse, limiting the acceleration effect.

To address these limitations, we present a new pruning paradigm that first apply regularization and followed by pruning. The regularization process dynamically transfers crucial information from the layers to be pruned to the remaining layers, thus minimizing the information loss resulting from direct parameter removal. This paradigm can maintain good model performance without RFT. Based on this paradigm, we propose ELDeR, a simple and effective data-driven regularized layer-wise pruning method. The comparison of the ELDeR with existing layer-wise pruning approaches is shown in Figure
1
. Specifically, the process of ELDeR is divided into the following steps: (1) select a small amount of data for learning layer weights and regularization, (2) iteratively learn the layer weights, (3) apply regularization to the difference between the outputs and inputs of the layers to be pruned, (4) prune.
First
, we randomly choose a small subset of data from commonly used benchmark datasets. Since the selected data is small, the computational costs in learning the layer weights and regularization process can be greatly minimized.
Second
, we iteratively learn the weights of each layer by adding their
\ell_{1}
-norm in the loss function.
Third
, we apply regularization (
\ell_{1}
-norm or
\ell_{2}
-norm) to the difference between the outputs and inputs of the layers with smaller weights, forcing important information to be transferred to the remaining layers which significantly reduces the performance decline caused by parameter removal. Thus the model can maintain good performance even at high pruning ratios, thereby providing significant end-to-end acceleration.
Finally
, we prune the layers with smaller weights. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ELDeR substantially outperforms current layer-wise pruning methods in terms of perplexity and accuracy across different pruning ratios, while also significantly improving end-to-end acceleration. The main contributions of ELDeR are summarized as follows:

•

Propose A New Paradigm:
ELDeR reduces information loss caused by direct parameter removal by sequentially learning layer weights, applying regularization (
\ell_{1}
-norm or
\ell_{2}
-norm), and performing pruning. This approach helps preserve model performance without requiring for RFT.

•

High Performance:
ELDeR outperforms previous layer-wise structured pruning methods in both generation and zero-shot tasks. The pruned model demonstrates a considerable acceleration compared to the dense model.

•

Low Overhead:
By using only a small amount of data for learning layer weights and regularization, ELDeR significantly reduces the overhead of RFT.

Related Works

2.1
Pruning Methods

The process of removing unnecessary weights, known as pruning, has proven to be an effective method for reducing the complexity of deep neural networks over the past few decades
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. Pruning methods can generally be divided into two categories: unstructured pruning
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
and structured pruning
[
5
,
27
,
28
]
.

Unstructured pruning methods eliminate individual weights according to their significance. For instance, Magnitude
[
22
,
29
]
assumes larger weights are more important, Wanda
[
13
]
uses the product of the layer weights and activations as the criterion, and SparseGPT
[
12
]
uses the weight squared divided by the inverse Hessian matrix. Their main advantage is flexibility. However, their drawbacks include the need for specialized hardware to accelerate
[
30
]
, the requirement for extreme sparsity to achieve considerable acceleration
[
31
]
, and are not suitable for RFT on downstream tasks.

Structured pruning methods, including channel-wise pruning
[
15
]
and layer-wise pruning
[
32
]
. Channel-wise pruning methods typically create a metric to assess the importance of channels in the parameter matrixs of LLMs, and then remove the less important ones. Layer-wise pruning methods treat entire layers as the basic units for pruning. For instance, SLEB
[
16
]
, iteratively remove entire transformer layers by evaluating their impact on the model’s final loss, ShortGPT
[
33
]
thinks high similarity between layers means redundancy, LaCo
[
27
]
uses layer collapse to prune, Shortened LLaMA
[
34
]
prune layers in one-shot based on their importance. However, these methods have a major limitation: even less important channels or layers may still contain valuable information, and directly pruning them often results in significant performance degradation. To overcome this, we explore the possibility of "shifting" important information from the pruned parts to the remaining parts, which could greatly maintain model performance.

2.2
Regularization

Regularization is commonly used in machine learning
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
, including preventing model overfitting
[
38
]
and feature selection
[
39
]
. The
\ell_{1}
-norm typically promotes sparsity by driving certain coefficients to zero, while the
\ell_{2}
-norm encourages smoother solutions
[
40
]
. Both can significantly affect the distribution pattern of the data
[
22
,
41
]
. Inspired by this, we propose a new pruning paradigm, in which the regularization process could transfer important information from the pruned parameter space to the remaining parts of the model, thus improving performance.

4.9
Impact of Regularization

We keep other settings consistent with Section
4.2
. After learning the layer weights iteratively, we consider two scenarios: (1) without regularization and (2) with regularization, followed by pruning. The perplexity and average accuracy under (1) and (2) on LLaMA2-7B are shown in Table
5
. The model exhibits lower perplexity and higher average accuracy with regularization. Since perplexity is the exponential form of cross-entropy loss, lower perplexity corresponds to a lower cross-entropy loss. A smaller loss function indicates better model performance indicating that the regularization process mitigates the impact of pruning on the overall model.

According to previous work
[
59
]
, the output and input of each layer in LLMs exhibit high similarity. This inherent similarity enables us to apply regularization on the difference between the input and output of certain layers using only a small portion of data. We compute the cosine similarity according to Equation
6
on the LLaMA2-7B model between the input representations
\mathbf{X_{\text{in}}^{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n\times d}
and the output representations
\mathbf{X_{\text{out}}^{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n\times d}
, where
b
denotes the batch size,
n
the number of tokens, and
d
is the hidden size of the model. The computation is performed on layers [3, 6, 9, 11, 13, 20, 23, 29], which are selected using the iterative method described in Section
4.8
, both before and after regularization.

\text{CosSim}(\mathbf{X_{\text{in}}^{i}},\mathbf{X_{\text{out}}^{i}})=\frac{1}{bn}\sum_{k=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{X_{\text{in}}^{i}}[k,j,:],\ \mathbf{X_{\text{out}}^{i}}[k,j,:]\right\rangle}{\left\|\mathbf{X_{\text{in}}^{i}}[k,j,:]\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{X_{\text{out}}^{i}}[k,j,:]\right\|_{2}}

(6)

As shown in Figure
7
, the input-output similarity of these layers is already high before regularization and increases even further after regularization. The increased similarity in the layers with regularization indicates that the input undergoes less change after passing through these layers, suggesting that less information is retained in them. In the Appendix
F
, we illustrate the changes in similarity between the input and output of the layers that were not regularized, before and after regularization. The results in Figure
8
of Appendix
F
show that the similarity in these layers decreases, meaning that, after regularization, the input undergoes greater changes when passing through these layers than before. This demonstrates that the regularization process weakens the influence of the regularized layers and enhances the influence of the unregularized parts, suggesting that regularization process may facilitate the transfer of information from the regularized layers to the rest of the model.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new pruning paradigm: first apply regularization, then prune. Unlike previous paradigm that first prune and then apply RFT, ELDeR transfers critical information from the pruned parameter space to the remaining parts during regularization, effectively mitigating the irreversible performance degradation caused by information loss. ELDeR demonstrates superior performance in generation and zero-shot tasks, significantly outperforming existing methods. For instance, ELDeR surpasses the powerful ShotGPT by 20% in perplexity on LLaMA2-7B. On OPT-13B, under 25% sparsity, the average accuracy drops by only 1%, while achieving 1.35× speedup compared to the dense model. Moreover, ELDeR is finetuning-free, substantially reducing computational costs and the reliance on RFT. The new paradigm may provide insights for structured pruning in LLMs.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Step 1: Expressing
\ell_{1}
-norm Using Elements.

The objective function in the unconstrained problem is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the vector
x
, which is defined as:

||x||_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|

(7)

This function aims to minimize the sum of the absolute values of the components of
x
.

Step 2: Reformulating the Constrained Problem

The constrained optimization problem introduces an auxiliary variable
y
, where for each element
i
:

x_{i}\geq-y_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i}\leq y_{i}\quad

(8)

This implies that
y_{i}\geq|x_{i}|
, meaning each element of
y
serves as an upper bound for the absolute value of the corresponding element in
x
. Consequently, minimizing
|x|_{1}
is equivalent to minimizing the sum of the elements in
y
. Thus, the objective function is defined as:

\mathbf{1}^{T}y

(9)

Thus, minimizing
\mathbf{1}^{T}y
is equivalent to minimizing the sum of the absolute values of
x
, which is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
x
.

This transformation allows the optimization problem to be solved without directly involving the absolute value function, resulting in an equivalent constrained optimization problem that can be addressed via backpropagation. Thus, the proof of the Proposition
3.1
is complete.

Similarity Changes in Unregularized Layers

We plot the input-output similarity of the unregularized layers in LLaMA2-7B, as described in Section
4.9
, in Figure
8
. According to the results shown below, the similarity of all layers except for the first one is already high before regularization is applied. After applying regularization, the similarity in these unregularized layers decreases, indicating that the transformations undergone by the inputs in these layers become more substantial. This suggests that more information is being captured in these layers compared to before, implying that the regularization process may cause information to shift from the regularized layers to the unregularized ones.

Appendix G
Future Work and Limitations

ELDeR has primarily been evaluated on autoregressive language models and its applicability to other architectures or tasks remains unexplored. In future work, we plan to explore the application of ELDeR to other model architectures, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs).
