The effect of Lippia Citriodora (LC) extract on cardiometabolic risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sha Xu, Zhenghui Huang, Ke Wang, Lin Li, Xin Zhao, Ying Liu, Han Ding, Qian Zhang, Minwen Cai, Yi Yu, Yang Xu, Xiao Yuan, Zhihong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic disorders are a growing public health concern worldwide. Lippia citriodora (LC), a polyphenol-rich medicinal plant, has been suggested to offer metabolic benefits due to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-modulating properties. However, the clinical evidence regarding its efficacy remains inconsistent. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of LC extract supplementation on key cardiometabolic risk factors in adults.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted through June 2025 in original databases. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of LC extract (alone or combined with Hibiscus sabdariffa) on anthropometric indices, lipid profiles, glycemic markers, and blood pressure were included. Data synthesis was performed using a random-effects model.ResultsEleven RCTs with diverse populations (503 participants) and intervention durations (3–12 weeks) were included. LC supplementation significantly reduced body weight (WMD: –2.09 kg), BMI (WMD: –0.70 kg/m²), fat mass (WMD: –0.62%), total cholesterol (WMD: –9.06 mg/dL), systolic blood pressure (WMD: –8.59 mmHg), and diastolic blood pressure (WMD: –3.73 mmHg) as well as increased levels of HDL-C (WMD: +2.10 mg/dL). No significant effects were found for glycemic markers and other lipid profiles. Subgroup analyses suggested enhanced benefits in shorter interventions (≤8 weeks), individuals with metabolic syndrome, and combined formulations. Heterogeneity was generally low across outcomes.ConclusionsLC extract may offer modest but clinically relevant improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, particularly in weight, lipid profile, and blood pressure. These findings support the potential of LC as an adjunctive intervention, though larger and longer-term trials are warranted to confirm its efficacy.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic disorders, such as obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, represent some of the most significant contributors to global morbidity and mortality, placing an ever-increasing strain on healthcare systems [1, 2]. The worldwide surge in sedentary behavior, suboptimal dietary patterns, and aging populations has fueled the escalating prevalence of these interrelated conditions [3]. Because cardiometabolic syndrome arises from multiple interacting biological and lifestyle factors, its prevention and management require comprehensive approaches that integrate lifestyle modification, pharmacologic therapy, and targeted nutritional interventions [4].

In this context, growing attention has been directed toward plant-derived bioactive compounds for their potential role in mitigating cardiometabolic risk [5]. Among the numerous botanicals studied, Lippia citriodora (LC), commonly known as lemon verbena, has attracted particular interest due to its broad spectrum of pharmacological properties [6]. Native to South America and now widely cultivated throughout the Mediterranean region, LC contains abundant polyphenolic constituents, most notably verbascoside, isoverbascoside, and luteolin derivatives, that exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-modulating activities [7, 8]. Evidence from experimental and preclinical studies suggests that LC may enhance lipid metabolism, regulate glucose balance, reduce adiposity, and improve vascular tone through mechanisms involving AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, inhibition of adipogenesis, and improved endothelial function [9–11].

Despite encouraging mechanistic and animal data, human clinical trials investigating LC supplementation have produced mixed findings. Randomized controlled studies assessing its effects on anthropometric parameters, lipid profile, blood glucose, and blood pressure have reported variable outcomes: while several observed favorable shifts in body composition and lipid metabolism, others found minimal or statistically nonsignificant changes [9–13]. Differences in trial design, dosing regimens, intervention duration, and participant characteristics likely contribute to these discrepancies.

Previous reviews have broadly examined the cardiometabolic benefits of polyphenol-rich botanicals such as green tea, curcumin, and mixed herbal formulations [5, 14–16]. However, none have quantitatively summarized the clinical effects of LC itself. Prior narrative discussions have mainly described its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms without integrating data across RCTs [5, 15]. Therefore, the present meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive quantitative synthesis focused exclusively on LC and its impact on diverse cardiometabolic risk factors. Although prior reviews have explored the health effects of various botanical supplements on metabolic outcomes, a rigorous, quantitative synthesis focusing specifically on LC and its influence on cardiometabolic risk factors has not yet been undertaken. Given the increasing incorporation of LC extracts into nutraceutical formulations and functional foods, alongside the lack of consensus regarding their clinical efficacy, an evidence-based evaluation is warranted to inform both clinical practice and public health recommendations.

Accordingly, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantify the overall effects of Lippia citriodora extract supplementation on principal cardiometabolic risk indicators, including anthropometric measures, lipid metabolism, glucose regulation, and blood pressure. In addition, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to identify possible effect modifiers and assess the robustness of the pooled results. This work seeks to bridge existing knowledge gaps and clarify the therapeutic potential of LC in supporting cardiometabolic health.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [17]. A comprehensive literature search was performed from database inception to June 2025 in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. The following search strategy was used with appropriate adaptations for each database: (“Lippia citriodora” OR “lemon verbena” OR “Metabolaid” OR “verbascoside”) AND (“cardiometabolic” OR “metabolic syndrome” OR “obesity” OR “overweight” OR “body weight” OR “blood pressure” OR “hypertension” OR “lipid profile” OR “cholesterol” OR “triglyceride” OR “HDL” OR “LDL” OR “glucose” OR “HbA1c”) AND (“randomized controlled trial” OR “clinical trial”). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine the intervention and outcome terms. The search covered all records published up to June 2025 without language or publication date restrictions. In addition, the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened. To minimize publication bias, grey literature sources, including ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), and conference proceedings indexed in Scopus and Web of Science, were also reviewed for potentially eligible unpublished or ongoing studies.

Eligible studies were RCTs evaluating Lippia citriodora supplementation in adult populations. Both parallel and crossover designs were included if they reported baseline and post-intervention data for at least one cardiometabolic outcome. Non-randomized, quasi-experimental, single-arm, or observational studies were excluded. Trials were eligible if the control arm received a placebo or no active treatment.

Regarding combination interventions, studies in which Lippia citriodora was administered together with Hibiscus sabdariffa were included as a separate predefined subgroup, since this formulation (commercially known as Metabolaid®) has been widely used in clinical research and allows comparison of single versus combined botanical effects. However, studies combining Lippia citriodora with other bioactive ingredients (e.g., vitamins, minerals, probiotics, or additional plant extracts) were excluded to ensure analytical specificity for LC-related outcomes.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened all titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extracted included author, publication year, country, participants’ health status, age, sample size per group, intervention details (type, dose, duration), and baseline and post-intervention means and standard deviations (SDs) for reported outcomes. When necessary, corresponding authors were contacted for missing data. Data extraction was performed using a pre-designed standardized form and cross-checked by the second reviewer. The longest follow-up period was used for analysis.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) for randomized trials [18]. Domains evaluated included randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Two reviewers independently rated each domain as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was appraised using the GRADE approach [19].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA version 14.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA). Continuous outcomes were pooled as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Missing SDs were estimated from reported standard errors, confidence intervals, or p-values using standard formulas [20, 21]. A random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was employed to account for between-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics, with I² interpreted as low (<25%), moderate (25–50%), substantial (50–75%), or considerable (>75%) [22]. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted based on: Intervention duration (≤8 weeks vs. >8 weeks), intervention type (Lippia citriodora alone vs. combined with Hibiscus sabdariffa), and participant metabolic status (metabolic syndrome vs. healthy). In addition, a sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach was conducted to assess the influence of each individual study on the overall effect size. These analyses were intended to identify possible effect modifiers and evaluate the robustness of pooled outcomes. Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plot visualization and Egger’s regression test, with trim-and-fill methods applied if asymmetry was suspected [23]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Study selection

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 1098 records were identified through database searching. After removing duplicates, 842 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 823 were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Nineteen full-text articles were then assessed in detail, and eight were excluded due to reasons such as insufficient data (n = 1), incompatible study design (n = 2), irrelevant outcomes (n = 3), absence of an appropriate control group (n = 1), or combining LC with other interventions (n = 1). All studies were rated as low risk for random sequence generation, and allocation concealment was commonly assessed as low or unclear. However, blinding of participants and study personnel was often rated high or unclear, likely due to the distinct sensory characteristics (taste, color, or aroma) of plant-based preparations. In contrast, outcome assessor blinding was generally adequate, with most studies rated as low risk, though a few lacked sufficient methodological details. Incomplete outcome data and selective reporting were commonly judged as unclear, reflecting limited reporting in trial protocols or registries. Only one study, Herranz-López et al. [9], was assessed as high overall risk of bias, primarily due to inadequate blinding procedures and missing outcome data. Subgroups analysis

Based on the subgroup analysis results from Supplementary Table 1, several statistically significant effects of LC supplementation on metabolic health outcomes were observed. Specifically, body weight was significantly reduced in interventions lasting ≤8 weeks (WMD: –2.15 kg; 95% CI: –4.01 to –0.28), in studies using a combination of LC and Hibiscus sabdariffa (WMD: –2.25 kg; 95% CI: –3.93 to –0.57), and among participants with metabolic syndrome (WMD: –2.21 kg; 95% CI: –4.12 to –0.30). BMI and fat mass also showed meaningful reductions in interventions of ≤8 weeks, with the combined intervention, and in participants with metabolic syndrome. In terms of lipid profiles, TC was significantly reduced in interventions longer than 8 weeks (WMD: –18.72 mg/dL; 95% CI: –35.26 to –2.18) and in studies involving healthy participants (WMD: –14.23 mg/dL; 95% CI: –26.31 to –2.15). Regarding blood pressure outcomes, SBP showed significant reductions in both short-term (≤8 weeks; WMD: –10.88 mmHg; 95% CI: –18.72 to –3.04) and longer-term (>8 weeks; WMD: –4.88 mmHg; 95% CI: –8.52 to –1.23) interventions. SBP also decreased significantly in healthy individuals (WMD: –9.55 mmHg; 95% CI: –15.96 to –3.13). Additionally, DBP was significantly reduced among participants with metabolic syndrome (WMD: –3.69 mmHg; 95% CI: –6.52 to –0.86) with short-term intervention.

Importantly, all significant subgroup effects occurred under conditions of low or negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0%), indicating robust and consistent findings across studies.

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out method was applied to assess the influence of each individual study on the pooled effect size. The findings remained robust after sequential elimination of studies (Supplemental Figs. 1–4).

Publication bias

A visual review of the funnel plots indicated no apparent asymmetry, suggesting a low likelihood of publication bias concerning the effects of LC Extract interventions across the evaluated outcomes. This visual observation was further corroborated by Egger’s regression test, which revealed no statistically significant evidence of publication bias for any of the parameters analyzed. The P-values were as follows: weight (P = 0.211), BMI (P = 0.180), Fat mass (P = 0.523), LDL cholesterol (P = 0.327), HDL cholesterol (P = 0.851), TC (P = 0.216), TG (P = 1.00), FBS (P = 0.621), HbA1c (P = 1.00), SBP (P = 0.652), and DBP (P = 0.881). Supplementary Figures 5–8 present the corresponding funnel plots. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method revealed no missing studies, further supporting the robustness of the publication bias analysis. However, it should be noted that for some outcomes fewer than ten studies were included; therefore, the results of Egger’s regression test should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we examined RCTs assessing the impact of LC extract on a range of cardiometabolic risk factors. Pooled results indicate meaningful improvements in anthropometric measures, TC, and HDL concentrations, as well as both SBP and DBP, whereas glycemic indices showed no significant changes. These findings expand current understanding of LC’s potential metabolic effects; below we place the results in the context of existing literature and consider plausible mechanisms.

Across included trials, LC supplementation did not produce statistically significant reductions in FBS or HbA1c compared with control conditions. The weighted mean difference for FBS was –1.71 mg/dL and for HbA1c –0.02%. These null effects are consistent with several previous RCTs. For example, Herranz-López et al. [9] reported no changes in FBS or insulin sensitivity after an 8-week LC regimen in overweight adults, and Marhuenda et al. observed no HbA1c improvement after 12 weeks in a prehypertensive and hypertensive cohort [12]. Short intervention durations in many trials may partly explain the absence of HbA1c change, since HbA1c reflects longer-term glycemic control. Moreover, baseline glycemic measures across studies were generally normal to borderline, leaving limited room for measurable improvement.

Mechanistically, LC is rich in phenolic constituents such as verbascoside and isoverbascoside, compounds that have shown insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical models [24]. Proposed pathways include activation of AMPK and suppression of proinflammatory cytokines, both of which can influence glucose homeostasis [24, 25]. Nevertheless, translating these molecular effects into clinically meaningful glycemic benefits, particularly in populations without overt hyperglycemia, remains uncertain. Low between-study heterogeneity for glycemic outcomes further underscores the consistency of the null result. It remains plausible that LC might exert stronger glycemic effects in individuals with established insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes, populations underrepresented among the included trials.

The meta-analysis found that LC supplementation was associated with statistically significant reductions in body weight, BMI, and percent body fat, with minimal heterogeneity. These clinical findings align with prior trials reporting favorable changes in body composition following LC intake, particularly when administered alongside lifestyle modifications or combined with other botanicals [9, 12, 13]. The observed effects may be attributable to LC’s polyphenolic profile, which has been shown in vitro and in animal studies to inhibit adipogenesis, stimulate lipolysis, and attenuate oxidative stress and inflammation in adipose tissue [11, 26]. Verbascoside, a prominent LC constituent, has been reported to downregulate adipogenic transcription factors such as PPARγ and C/EBPα, potentially limiting adipocyte differentiation [27]. Additional preclinical evidence suggests LC can increase energy expenditure and enhance mitochondrial function, mechanisms that would support reductions in fat mass and weight [28]. Dose ranges in the included trials typically fell between 100 and 300 mg/day and largely enrolled adults with overweight or obese populations that may be more responsive to metabolic interventions.

Furthermore, LC supplementation produced selective improvements in lipid metrics: TC decreased, and HDL-C increased significantly, while changes in LDL-C and TG were not statistically meaningful. These outcomes concord with several clinical reports, Serna et al. [29] and Herranz-López et al. [9] among them, that observed HDL increases and modest TC reductions after LC interventions. Conversely, some trials reported no lipid benefits, a discrepancy possibly explained by short treatment durations or heterogeneous baseline lipid statuses [12]. Mechanistically, LC’s phenylpropanoid compounds, especially verbascoside, have demonstrated hypolipidemic actions in preclinical work through modulation of hepatic lipid metabolism, inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase activity, and enhancement of reverse cholesterol transport [8, 28, 30]. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of LC may also indirectly improve lipid handling by reducing systemic oxidative stress and inflammation, both contributors to dyslipidemia [31]. The overall pattern, improvement in HDL and TC but not LDL or TG, suggests a targeted lipid effect rather than broad-spectrum lipid lowering. Heterogeneity for lipid outcomes ranged from low to moderate, supporting reasonable consistency across studies.

Supplementation with LC was associated with statistically significant reductions in both SBP and DBP. These results mirror earlier clinical observations of LC’s antihypertensive potential [9, 10, 32]. For example, Boix-Castejón et al. reported a meaningful systolic pressure decline after six weeks in prehypertensive subjects [32]. Putative mechanisms include enhanced endothelial function, greater nitric oxide bioavailability, reduced oxidative stress, and attenuation of vasoconstrictive signaling [33, 34]. Specific LC constituents, verbascoside and luteolin derivatives, have been shown in animal and cell models to inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) activity and to decrease arterial stiffness [35]. Moreover, LC may also exert mild diuretic effects, contributing to lower plasma volume and reduced blood pressure [36]. Moderate heterogeneity in DBP (I² ≈ 40.7%) could reflect differences in baseline blood pressure, population characteristics, or trial methodologies.

Although the pooled reduction in triglycerides was small and not statistically significant, the direction of effect was consistent across studies, suggesting a potential modest improvement in lipid metabolism. In contrast, the mean reduction of approximately 8.6 mmHg in SBP and 3.7 mmHg in DBP is considered clinically meaningful, as epidemiological evidence indicates that each 5 mmHg decrease in SBP is associated with about a 10% reduction in risk of major cardiovascular events [37]. Therefore, the blood pressure-lowering effects of LC extract, even over short intervention periods, may have tangible clinical implications.

The biological plausibility of LC’s cardiometabolic effects is supported by multiple mechanistic studies. Verbascoside, the principal phenylpropanoid glycoside in LC, exerts potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities through inhibition of the NF-κB pathway and activation of AMPK signaling, thereby improving lipid and glucose metabolism [8, 25, 28, 30]. These actions can reduce oxidative stress, downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), and enhance endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, which collectively contribute to improved vascular function and reduced blood pressure. Additionally, LC polyphenols have been shown to inhibit adipogenesis by suppressing PPARγ and C/EBPα expression, stimulate mitochondrial activity, and modulate gut microbiota composition, all of which may synergistically attenuate adiposity and metabolic dysfunction [27]. Such multimodal mechanisms provide strong biological support for the observed clinical improvements in anthropometric and cardiovascular parameters.

Regarding safety, none of the included randomized trials reported serious or treatment-related adverse events associated with LC supplementation. Mild gastrointestinal discomfort was rarely noted and occurred with similar frequency in placebo groups. Overall, LC extract appears to be well tolerated and safe at the doses (400–1800 mg/day) and durations (up to 12 weeks) investigated. These findings align with previous reports describing the favorable safety profile of LC and its polyphenolic constituents, supporting its use as a generally recognized safe (GRAS) nutraceutical ingredient.

Subgroup analyses provided additional nuance. Reductions in body weight, BMI, and fat mass were most pronounced in shorter trials (≤8 weeks), studies combining LC with Hibiscus sabdariffa, and among participants with metabolic syndrome. Total cholesterol improvements were more evident in longer interventions (>8 weeks) and in healthy cohorts, while blood pressure benefits tended to be larger in shorter-duration studies and in metabolically at-risk populations. These patterns suggest that intervention length, baseline metabolic status, and whether LC was administered alone or as part of a combination product are important effect modifiers. The amplified response observed in metabolic syndrome populations may be driven by higher baseline inflammation and oxidative stress, pathways particularly amenable to LC’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions [13]. Co-administration with Hibiscus sabdariffa, a plant with established antihypertensive and lipid-lowering properties, may yield synergistic benefits beyond those of LC monotherapy [38].

This work is, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive meta-analysis synthesizing LC extract effects across a broad set of cardiometabolic endpoints. Strengths include consistent findings across many outcomes, generally low-to-moderate heterogeneity, and the application of rigorous statistical methods, including sensitivity and publication-bias assessments. Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, most included trials were conducted with relatively small sample sizes, limiting statistical power and precision of effect estimates. Second, the duration of interventions was generally short (ranging from 3 to 12 weeks), precluding firm conclusions about long-term efficacy or sustainability of effects. Third, none of the included studies systematically evaluated dose–response relationships, making it difficult to determine optimal supplementation levels. Also, the lack of registration of this study protocol in PROSPERO is another limitation of our study. Finally, most studies were performed in specific geographic regions (mainly Europe and Asia), which may restrict generalizability to other populations. Future well-designed, longer-term RCTs with diverse participants and varying doses of LC extract are warranted to confirm and expand upon these results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, supplementation with LC extract may confer modest but meaningful improvements in several cardiometabolic risk factors, particularly by reducing body weight, BMI, fat mass, TC, and blood pressure. However, no significant effects were observed on glucose metabolism parameters. These results support the potential of LC as a complementary approach in cardiometabolic risk management, especially in at risk populations and short-duration interventions. Future long-term RCTs are warranted to confirm these findings, explore optimal dosing supplements, and assess safety in broader populations.
