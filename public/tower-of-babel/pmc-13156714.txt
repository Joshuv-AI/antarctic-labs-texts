Childhood maltreatment and intimate partner violence victimisation: what explains the link?
Authors: Jasmin Wertz, Leah S Richmond-Rakerd
Published: 2026

Despite overall declines in violent crime in many countries, intimate partner violence remains relatively high. A third of women worldwide will become a victim of intimate partner violence in their lifetime,1 with a lower, yet substantial, number of men also affected. The significant harm and suffering caused by intimate partner violence2 has made its reduction a public-health and policy priority for governments and international organisations.

For many individuals, intimate partner violence is not their first exposure to victimisation. Indeed, there is a well-established link between exposure to childhood maltreatment and intimate partner victimisation. For example, children exposed (vs. unexposed) to physical abuse have 2–4 times increased risk of becoming victims of intimate partner violence involving physical violence and injury.3,4 This finding could be interpreted pessimistically, as indicating a deterministic ‘cycle of violence.’ However, it also implies that there is a high-risk group—children exposed to maltreatment—for whom targeted interventions could be particularly important. For such efforts to be effective, it is important to understand why maltreated children are at greater risk of experiencing intimate partner violence as adults.

One possibility is that childhood maltreatment causally increases vulnerability to future victimisation. For example, being victimised as a child may shape expectations of the world, including about partners' behaviours in relationships5; contribute to economic and social disadvantages that increase exposure to risky environments6; or lead to the development of mental health problems or personality traits that leave people vulnerable to further abuse.7 Alternatively, there may be a non-causal link, whereby child characteristics such as behaviour difficulties or familial background factors such as parental mental illness increase individuals’ vulnerability to experience both childhood maltreatment and intimate partner violence. In this issue of the journal, Pezzoli et al.8 aim to disentangle causal effects of childhood maltreatment on subsequent intimate partner violence victimisation from non-causal associations arising from shared risk.

Using data from a large UK twin study, Pezzoli et al.8 tested pathways from childhood maltreatment to intimate partner violence in early adulthood, focusing on mental health and personality mediators. Analysing twins made it possible to control for shared background risk, including child characteristics (indicated by genetic influences in this design) and family background (indicated by shared environmental influences). This approach sharpens inferences about associations causally instigated by childhood maltreatment. Conceptually, it is comparable to a discordant twin design, testing whether the co-twin who experienced (more) maltreatment is at greater risk of intimate partner violence, and to what extent this link is explained by twin differences in mental health and personality. Although experiences of childhood maltreatment are often shared by siblings, one child may be more targeted or affected than another. Leveraging this within-family variation, Pezzoli et al.8 show that even after accounting for shared background risk, childhood maltreatment predicts intimate partner victimisation in early adulthood (supporting a causal link), and that it does so largely via difficulties with regulating temper, anger, and aggression, as well as low life satisfaction. Interestingly, a number of other mediators were tested but ultimately not found to be significant contributors, including anxiety and risk-taking.

There are a number of strengths of Pezzoli et al.’s8 analysis, including a comprehensive set of mediators whose selection was shaped by lived-experience involvement. There are also some limitations, including the use of a cross-sectional design relying on retrospective recall of maltreatment and the lack of information about intimate partner violence perpetration (in addition to victimisation). Notwithstanding these limitations and the overall small effects, a number of implications can be drawn from the findings. First, although exposure to childhood maltreatment increases the risk of experiencing intimate partner violence, this risk operates through modifiable pathways. This implies that re-victimisation is not inevitable, with scope for intervention even after maltreatment has occurred. Second, the mental health problems that survivors of childhood maltreatment may come into contact with health services for—such as anxiety disorders9—might differ from those that mediate the link with intimate partner violence. Focusing on aspects of functioning that may receive less attention in routine clinical care, such as interpersonal dynamics, may be helpful to address the factors implicated in re-victimisation risk. Third, the findings suggest the value of integrating relationship-focused interventions or components into support for survivors of childhood maltreatment. Previous research shows that dating violence prevention programmes for at-risk adolescents are effective in reducing their risk for becoming victimised.10 Such approaches could therefore meaningfully complement existing clinical and social supports, to disrupt paths of re-victimisation.
