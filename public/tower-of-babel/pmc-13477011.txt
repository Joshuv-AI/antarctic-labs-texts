Cost-effectiveness of multiarterial versus single-arterial grafting in coronary artery bypass surgery
Authors: Niyi O. Odewade, Rami Bikdash, Ezra S. Brooks, Phoebe Otchere, Boateng Kubi, Marle Sabatino, Sameer Hirji, Thoralf M. Sundt, Asishana A. Osho, Ashraf A. Sabe, Antionia Kreso, Louis L. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the cost-effectiveness of multiarterial grafting (MAG) compared with single artery grafting (SAG) using institutional outcomes and a linked perioperative-chronic Markov model.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of 3583 adults undergoing isolated coronary artery bypass grafting between 2008 and 2022 at 2 tertiary hospital centers. Operative and perioperative data were obtained from the institutional Society of Thoracic Surgeons database. Propensity score overlap weighting was used to balance baseline covariates. A 2-phase (30-day perioperative and 10-year chronic) Markov state-transition model, informed by weighted institutional outcomes and supplemented with national Society of Thoracic Surgeons and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services data, was used to estimate long-term costs and quality-adjusted life years.ResultsAmong 3583 patients at 2 academic centers, MAG was associated with similar operative mortality and morbidity compared with SAG. In a linked perioperative-chronic Markov model, MAG yielded greater quality-adjusted survival (+0.241 quality-adjusted life-years) and lower 10-year discounted costs (–$7755 per patient). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that MAG was not consistently cost-effective in the perioperative period but was robustly dominant in the chronic phase, with long-term results stable across a wide range of cost assumptions.ConclusionsMAG was associated with comparable perioperative outcomes but improved quality-adjusted survival and lower long-term costs compared with SAG. Broader adoption of MAG could reduce health care expenditures while improving population health by addressing barriers to MAG use, particularly in women, minority patients, and socially vulnerable populations.

Central Message

Multiarterial grafting improves long-term quality-adjusted survival and reduces 10-year costs compared with single-arterial grafting, despite similar perioperative outcomes.

Perspective

Although multiarterial grafting improves long-term outcomes after CABG, its adoption remains limited. In a linked perioperative–chronic cost-effectiveness analysis, multiarterial grafting improved quality-adjusted survival while reducing long-term costs compared with single-arterial grafting, supporting broader use of arterial revascularization in value-based cardiac surgery.

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the cornerstone of revascularization for patients with multivessel coronary artery disease. Although technical refinements have improved perioperative safety, long-term outcomes after CABG are increasingly recognized to depend on conduit choice. Accumulating observational and randomized evidence suggests that multiarterial grafting (MAG) improves graft patency, reduces major adverse cardiovascular events, and enhances survival compared with the more commonly performed single-arterial grafting (SAG).1, 2, 3, 4 Despite these advantages, the adoption of MAG remains limited in contemporary practice, accounting for approximately 16% of CABG procedures in the United States on the basis of contemporary Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) data.5

Several factors contribute to the underuse of MAG, including perceived technical complexity, longer operative and cardiopulmonary bypass times, and concerns about perioperative morbidity, such as sternal wound complications.6,7 At the same time, surgical decision-making is increasingly occurring in the context of resource use.8 However, there are limited data specifically evaluating the economic implications of grafting strategy. Furthermore, although the clinical outcomes of MAG and SAG have been extensively studied, few analyses have integrated both perioperative and long-term cost and quality-of-life outcomes.

Understanding whether the survival and quality-adjusted life year (QALY) benefits of MAG justify potential increases in perioperative resource use is essential for informing patient counseling, guideline development, and institutional policy. To address this gap, we conducted a comprehensive comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness analysis of MAG versus SAG using real-world data from 2 academic medical centers. Leveraging institutional STS registry data, propensity-score overlap weighting, and a linked perioperative-chronic Markov model, we evaluated operative outcomes, long-term survival, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) for MAG compared with SAG. We aimed to determine whether MAG is associated with superior clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness compared with SAG in contemporary CABG practice.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults undergoing isolated CABG between 2008 and 2022 at 2 tertiary centers. Operative, perioperative, and follow-up data were extracted from our institutional STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database (institutional review board no. 2025P001690, approved on July 2, 2025; individual consent not required). Patients undergoing concomitant valve surgery or other major cardiac procedures as well as patients with missing or “unknown” values for select covariates (eg, chronic lung disease, previous myocardial infarction, liver disease) were excluded. Alternative revascularization strategies, such as percutaneous coronary intervention, were also excluded. The analytic sample included patients who underwent SAG or MAG. The analytic workflow comprised (1) cohort assembly and baseline characterization; (2) confounding control using propensity-score overlap weighting; (3) weighted analyses of operative outcomes (time-to-event and binary/continuous endpoints); and (4) a linked, perioperative to chronic, state-transition cost-effectiveness model with deterministic sensitivity analyses using weighted institutional data complemented by national data, resources, and literature.

Exposure

The exposure of interest was grafting strategy at index CABG: SAG (left internal mammary artery [LIMA] plus vein grafts) versus MAG (LIMA + right internal mammary artery or radial).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was operative mortality, defined as death within 30 days of surgery or during the index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes included major morbidity, using STS definitions for postoperative stroke, acute renal failure, prolonged ventilation, deep sternal wound infection, as well as hospital readmission, intensive care unit (ICU) readmission, reintubation, and crossclamp time.

Covariates and Risk Adjustment

Covariates were chosen a priori on the basis of clinical relevance to operative risk and graft selection and were aligned with risk-adjustment variables conventionally used in the STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database. This included age, sex, left ventricular ejection fraction, diabetes, dialysis dependence, number of diseased vessels, and previous myocardial infarction. Continuous covariates were modeled as numeric variables; categorical and binary variables were recoded as factors. The propensity score model included age, sex, body mass index, race (White, Black, Asian, other), left ventricular ejection fraction, dialysis, diabetes, number of diseased vessels, and previous myocardial infarction.

Handling of Missing Data

We did not perform imputation of missing values. Variables with minimal missingness (<5%) were retained in analyses without further adjustment, because the proportion of missing observations was unlikely to introduce meaningful bias. Variables with greater or potentially nonignorable rates of missingness (eg, New York Heart Association class, history of stroke) were excluded from propensity score models and adjusted analyses to avoid bias related to imputation assumptions. For each analysis, we used a complete-case approach, restricting the sample to patients with available data on all covariates included in the respective model.

Statistical Analysis

Propensity score overlap weights were estimated using logistic regression with the estimand set to the average treatment effect in the overlap population. Overlap weights were applied to all subsequent models using survey-weighted estimators. Balance was assessed using P values (P > .05) and Love plots.

Weighted Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used for time-to-event analyses. Logistic regression estimated overlap-weighted odds ratios (ORs) for binary outcomes, and weighted Gaussian regression estimated mean differences for continuous outcomes. Results were summarized in forest plots and tabular form. Two-sided tests were performed with α = 0.05.

Costing and Utilities

The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the US health care system, with all costs reported in 2025 US dollars. Costs were not adjusted for regional variation and are intended to represent average US-based estimates. Costs for the index CABG procedure, complications, readmission, ICU admission, and reoperation were obtained from multiple complementary sources, including STS-derived literature, Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services data, and published cost analyses in contemporary US cohorts. When estimates were drawn from earlier years, values were standardized to 2025 dollars using the Consumer Price Index. Where multiple cost estimates were available, we prioritized high-quality, US-based studies and registry data to maximize relevance and generalizability. These cost estimates represent bundled procedural costs and do not explicitly incorporate operating room duration, as a microcosting approach was not used.

Health state utilities, which represent patient’s quality of life on a scale from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health), were derived from previously published quality-of-life studies in CABG and cardiovascular surgery populations using validated instruments such as the EQ-5D and Short Form (36) Health Survey. These utility values were assigned to each health state in the model to reflect differences in quality of life associated with complications, readmission, or reoperation. Quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were calculated by multiplying the utility value by the time spent in each health state, consistent with standard methods used in cost-effectiveness analyses. Because costs and utilities were not directly collected from our institutional cohort, we emphasize that absolute values should be interpreted cautiously. However, both strategies (MAG and SAG) were evaluated using the same standardized costing framework, minimizing bias in incremental comparisons. Furthermore, we conducted one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses across wide, literature-supported ranges for each parameter, demonstrating that the direction and magnitude of the ICER remained stable.

State-Transition Modeling

We developed a 2-phase Markov model using the R statistical package “heemod” (R, version 4.3.2):•Perioperative phase (30 days): States included CABG, ICU readmission, reoperation, readmission, complication, discharge, and death (absorbing). Mutually exclusive transitions were enforced using a clinical severity hierarchy (death > reoperation > ICU > readmission > complication > discharge).•Chronic phase (10 annual cycles): States included event-free survival, complication, ICU readmission (1-year tunnel), readmission (1-year tunnel), reoperation (1-year tunnel, with persistent post-reoperation state), and death.

Survivors from the perioperative model were used to initialize the chronic phase. Perioperative transition probabilities were derived directly from the weighted institutional cohort. For the chronic phase, transition probabilities were estimated by taking reported annual event rates (eg, mortality and readmission) from published CABG studies and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services analyses and converting them into per-cycle probabilities for the model. STS data were used as a reference for typical event rates in contemporary CABG practice.21, 22, 23 Costs and utilities were applied by health state. Costs and QALYs were discounted at 3% annually. ICERs were calculated for MAG versus SAG.

Sensitivity Analyses

One-way, sensitivity analyses varied each perioperative and chronic cost parameter by positive or negative 20%, with ICER recalculated after each change. Willingness-to-pay thresholds of $50,000/QALY were applied to interpret results.

Software

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the packages heemod, WeightIt, survey, survival, survminer, cobalt, broom, ggplot2, dplyr, and DiagrammeR.

Results

Study Population

Among 3583 patients undergoing isolated CABG between 2008 and 2022, 654 (18.3%) received MAG and 2929 (81.7%) received SAG. Among patients undergoing MAG, 180 (27.5%) received bilateral internal mammary artery grafting (BIMA), 166 (25.4%) received LIMA plus radial artery grafting, and 23 (3.5%) received ≥3 arterial conduits (BIMA plus radial artery). In unadjusted analyses, patients receiving MAG were younger (mean age, 60.9 vs 67.4 years; P < .001), less often female (10.6% vs 18.4%; P < .001), and had lower prevalence of diabetes (32.1% vs 48.7%; P < .001), hypertension (85.3% vs 90.0%; P < .001), previous myocardial infarction (25.7% vs 34.3%; P < .001), and chronic lung disease (8.3% vs 13.6%; P < .001) (Table 2). Operative Mortality and Major Morbidity

In unadjusted analyses, MAG was associated with significantly lower odds of any complication (OR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.49-0.73; P < .001) and 30-day readmission (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.45-0.94; P = .026). Differences in deep sternal wound infection, ICU readmission, renal failure, stroke, prolonged ventilation, and 30-day mortality were not statistically significant (Table 1). Cox proportional hazards modeling for 30-day readmission did not meet significance, although the direction of effect was consistent (hazard ratio, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.51-1.05; P = .09).

In adjusted analyses using overlap weighting, the incidence of 30-day operative mortality was low and not significantly different (MAG vs SAG: OR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.09-5.25; P = .70) (Figure E1, Tables 1 and E2). Weighted odds of STS-defined morbidity end points were also similar. MAG was associated with numerically lower odds of any complication (OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.68-1.04; P = .11), deep sternal wound infection (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.10-4.28), and readmission (OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.58-1.28; P = .45), but none reached significance. Odds of ICU readmission (OR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.55-2.63), prolonged ventilation (OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 0.75-2.57), renal failure (OR, 1.64; 95% CI, 0.57-4.72), and stroke (OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 0.81-2.41) were also comparable (Figure E2). Mean crossclamp time did not differ significantly (mean difference, 1.0 minutes; 95% CI, −1.6 to 3.6). Predicted probabilities were directionally consistent with the primary odds ratio analyses (Table E3).

Readmission and Survival

Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves showed lower 30-day readmission for MAG compared with SAG (log-rank P = .03), although Cox regression did not achieve statistical significance. In adjusted overlap-weighted analyses, no significant difference in 30-day readmission was observed (hazard ratio, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.58-1.28) (Figure E1). Survival curves were overlapping, with no early separation evident.

Markov Projections of Mortality and Event-Free Survival

In the state-transition model linking perioperative to chronic outcomes, MAG was associated with lower long-term mortality and greater event-free survival over 10 years (Figure E3). By year 10, approximately 10% to 15% more patients remained event-free after MAG compared with SAG.

Discussion

Interpretation

In this comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness analysis of MAG vs SAG for CABG, we found that MAG was associated with sustained improvements in survival and event-free status while also reducing long-term costs. Although perioperative costs for MAG were slightly greater, reflecting longer operative time and resource use, these differences were offset by fewer downstream readmissions and reoperations over a 10-year horizon. In the linked perioperative and chronic state-transition model, MAG accrued greater quality-adjusted survival (+0.241 QALYs) while costing $7755 less per patient compared with SAG, yielding an ICER of −$32,141 per QALY. These results indicate that MAG is a dominant strategy—more effective and less costly—within the US health care system perspective.

The projections of mortality and event-free survival provide further clinical context. By year 10, approximately 10% to 15% more patients remained event-free after MAG compared with SAG. This survival advantage, coupled with reduced complication burden, translated into both greater utility values and lesser downstream costs.

Sensitivity analyses highlight important distinctions between the perioperative and chronic phases. In the perioperative (30-day) period, ICER estimates were sensitive to changes in the index CABG cost and other short-term parameters, with scenarios in which MAG was not cost-effective compared with SAG. In contrast, the chronic (1-10 year) phase consistently demonstrated robust cost-effectiveness, with long-term reoperation and ICU costs exerting the greatest influence but never overturning the conclusion that MAG is cost-effective at a $50,000 per QALY threshold. Together, these findings indicate that although the short-term economic profile of MAG is vulnerable to variation in operative and perioperative costs, the long-term survival and cost advantages render MAG a highly favorable strategy overall.

Comparison With Previous Studies

The survival benefit of multiarterial revascularization has been demonstrated in multiple observational studies and meta-analyses, yet previous analyses have not systematically examined its economic implications. Previous registry studies have shown reductions in late reintervention, recurrent angina, and mortality with MAG, but adoption has remained limited as the result of perceptions of greater technical complexity and perioperative morbidity. Our findings align with and extend these observations by demonstrating that the clinical benefits of MAG translate into economic advantages.

Specifically, our data directly address the perception that MAG is technically more challenging, time-consuming, and therefore less desirable in routine practice. In our analysis, perioperative outcomes—including mortality, stroke, renal failure, and prolonged ventilation—were comparable between groups, and crossclamp times differed by only 1 minute on average. These results suggest that in experienced centers, the incremental technical demands of MAG do not translate into measurable increases in morbidity or operative time. When coupled with long-term survival and cost savings, this finding undermines the notion that MAG's complexity outweighs its value and instead supports investment in training and broader institutional adoption.

Economic evaluations of CABG strategies remain relatively limited. Among Medicare beneficiaries, the mean hospitalization cost for an isolated CABG was approximately $32,200 (±$23,100).6 Commercial and self-pay hospital prices can be substantially greater—with regional median rates ranging from $35,624 to $84,080.7 However, those studies did not differentiate between MAG and SAG. More recent analyses of cardiovascular interventions—including transcatheter aortic valve replacement and hybrid revascularization—have reported ICERs ranging from $20,000 to $50,000 per QALY, values well within the traditional US willingness-to-pay threshold.8,21,22 In contrast, our study found MAG to be cost-saving, a stronger position on the cost-effectiveness plane. This suggests that MAG may represent one of the most high-value strategies in contemporary surgical revascularization.

Utility values from previous quality-of-life studies provide further validation of our findings. For example, published utilities for event-free states (0.80-0.85) and complication states (0.60-0.70) are consistent with the parameters used in our model.24,25 The magnitude of QALY gain observed here (+0.241) is clinically meaningful, roughly equivalent to 3 months of perfect health over a decade, and aligns with QALY improvements reported for other life-prolonging cardiovascular therapies.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this analysis was performed using data from 2 tertiary referral centers, which may not be generalizable to community hospitals or low-volume centers, where surgical expertise and resource availability differ. In addition, the number of MAG cases in this study (n = 654 over 14 years) reflects a moderate-volume experience across 2 centers, which may influence outcomes, given the known volume-outcome relationship in arterial grafting, particularly for BIMA use. As such, these findings may not be fully generalizable to centers with different levels of technical expertise. Also, operative technique (on-pump vs off-pump CABG) was not explicitly modeled, and differences related to off-pump CABG were not assessed. Furthermore, we were unable to directly assess completeness of revascularization, as intended graft number was not captured preoperatively in the dataset. As such, differences between MAG and SAG may in part reflect variation in completeness of revascularization rather than grafting strategy alone.

Second, missing data were handled using a complete-case approach, which can introduce bias. In this study, most missing data were attributable to incomplete New York Heart Association classification (Table E4). Nonetheless, variables with substantial missingness (such as New York Heart Association class) were excluded from adjusted analyses. The main variables used in propensity score modeling were largely complete.

Third, although the use of overlap weighting balanced observed baseline covariates, unmeasured confounding may remain, particularly regarding surgeon experience, conduit-selection practices, the total graft number, and completeness of revascularization. In addition, patients with dialysis-dependent renal failure were included in the cohort; although adjusted for in the propensity model, their outcomes may be driven predominantly by underlying comorbidity rather than grafting strategy. Fourth, although institutional data provided robust estimates for perioperative readmission and death, transition probabilities for some health states (eg, ICU readmission, chronic complications) were supplemented from the literature, introducing potential bias.

Fifth, cost estimates were derived from published sources rather than direct hospital charge data. Although these sources represent peer-reviewed, nationally relevant estimates, variation in payor mix and regional variation in health care costs may affect absolute values and generalizability. In addition, operating room time was not explicitly modeled as a cost driver, and variation in operative duration may not be fully captured within bundled cost estimates. Importantly, sensitivity analyses across wide ranges of cost parameters supported the long-term robustness of our findings. However, perioperative (30-day) sensitivity analyses demonstrated instability of ICER estimates because quality-adjusted survival differences in this short horizon were negligible, making the ratio highly sensitive to small variations in cost inputs. This underscores that short-term cost-effectiveness results should be interpreted cautiously and that meaningful differences emerge primarily in the chronic phase.

Finally, as with any Markov model, results depend on assumptions of transition structure and cycle length; although these assumptions were informed by literature and expert consensus, real-world prospective validation is necessary. Nevertheless, we used a standard state-transition (Markov) modeling framework; although this general approach is well established in health-economic evaluation and has been applied in CABG studies, our specific linked perioperative-chronic model is novel and has not been externally validated in an identical prior publication.26,27 Furthermore, not all external cost and outcome references were perfectly contemporaneous with every year of the study period. We therefore prioritized the most clinically relevant US-based sources available, inflation-adjusted older cost estimates to 2025 US dollars. Several chronic-phase cost inputs, for example, were estimated from published ranges and modeled assumptions rather than directly observed patient-level costs, which may affect the precision of absolute cost estimates. In all regards, uncertainty was tested with a wide ranged sensitivity analysis of 20%. Future work, nonetheless, should include prospective, multicenter studies that directly measure hospital-level costs, patient-reported utilities, and long-term QALYs. Such trials would provide definitive evidence to complement institutional and modeled estimates and would strengthen the case for policy-level changes.

Implications for Practice and Policy

These findings have significant implications for both surgical practice and health policy. Despite consistent evidence of superior long-term survival, MAG remains underused in the United States, with fewer than 10% of patients who undergo CABG receiving more than 1 arterial conduit. Our study provides economic justification for broader implementation, showing that MAG is not only clinically advantageous but also cost-saving relative to SAG. These results directly address one of the primary barriers to MAG adoption: the perception that its technical complexity and operative costs outweigh its benefits.

Policy and practice initiatives could accelerate uptake of MAG. Bundled payment programs and value-based reimbursement schemes may consider incentivizing the use of arterial grafting, given its long-term cost savings and survival benefit. Training programs could prioritize surgeon proficiency in multiarterial techniques, as operator experience has been shown to be a major determinant of MAG use. In addition, ongoing evaluation of disparities in MAG use—by sex, race, and social vulnerability—may help identify inequities in access to this high-value therapy and inform targeted interventions.

Conclusions

In this comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness analysis, MAG during CABG was associated with improved long-term survival, greater event-free life-years, and lower 10-year costs compared with SAG. From the perspective of the US health care system, MAG represented a dominant strategy, simultaneously reducing expenditures and improving outcomes. If implemented nationally among the estimated 40% to 50% of patients scheduled to undergo CABG who are eligible for multiarterial revascularization, MAG could save approximately $465 million to $582 million annually while improving quality-adjusted survival. These findings provide strong clinical and economic justification for broader adoption of MAG in surgical revascularization and underscore the need for prospective, multicenter validation to inform guideline recommendations and policy initiatives.
