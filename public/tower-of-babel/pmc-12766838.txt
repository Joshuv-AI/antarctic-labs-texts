Optimising regional organisation of endovascular thrombectomy for ischaemic stroke reusing a simulation model
Authors: Willemijn J Maas, Durk-Jouke van der Zee, Maarten M H Lahr, Bob Roozenbeek, Diederik W J Dippel, Henk Kerkhoff, Thomas Monks, Erik Buskens, Maarten Uyttenboogaart, The CONTRAST investigators and the MR CLEAN registry investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study assessed whether a previously developed Monte Carlo simulation model can be reused for evaluating various strategies to minimise time-to-treatment in southwest Netherlands for endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in patients who had an ischaemic stroke.DesignReuse of a previously developed simulation model to simulate various strategies in another region, using prospectively collected data from stroke centres and retrospective data from emergency medical services.ParticipantsData from 509 patients who had an ischaemic stroke (≥18 years) treated with EVT (2014–2018) were used.InterventionsInput for the simulation model reuse included distributions of observed time delays along the acute stroke pathway. Validation of the baseline models was based on face validity and statistical measures (patient data vs model output) using the Assessment of the Validation Status of Health Economic decision models tool. We simulated strategies for a subregion: interhospital patient transfer by helicopter, transport of the neurointerventionalist to the primary stroke centre (‘drive-the-doctor’), interhospital patient transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre (TSC) outside the region and prehospital triage using the Rapid Arterial Occlusion Evaluation (RACE) scale.Primary outcomeOnset-to-groin time was the outcome.ResultsReuse of the original simulation model was obtained by minimal effort, implying limited adaptation. Compared with the baseline model, interhospital patient transfer by helicopter or to a TSC outside the region and prehospital routing using the RACE scale reduced mean onset-to-groin time by 16, 13 and 39 min, respectively (95% CrI for all: equal to the point estimate). ‘Drive the doctor’ reduced mean onset-to-groin time by 27 (car), 49 (ambulance) or 58 min (helicopter), each with a 95% CrI equal to the point estimate.ConclusionsThe original simulation model can be applied to different regions in the Netherlands. Strategies tested within the subregion resulted in promising results of ‘drive the doctor’ and prehospital patient routing using the RACE scale.

Introduction

The benefits of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) for ischaemic stroke have been clearly established and EVT has been implemented as standard treatment for acute large-vessel occlusions (LVOs). Fast treatment with EVT is paramount in achieving good outcomes and disability-free survival, with per-hour delay resulting in a reduction in favourable functional outcome of 5%–6%.1 2 There is an ongoing debate about the optimal regional organisation of EVT. While some studies argue that direct transfer towards a comprehensive or thrombectomy-capable stroke centre (‘mothership’ strategy) is superior to the ‘drip and ship’ strategy (interhospital transfer from a primary centre to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre),3 a recent clinical trial failed to confirm this superiority.4 Importantly, such clinical trials only provide answers for regions with highly comparable characteristics, and thus, results remain difficult to generalise to other regions. Simulation modelling is an alternative approach to identify and assess optimisation efforts for region-specific organisation of stroke care. This allows for comparing adaptation and enhancement of workflow management, pathway set-up and alternative organisational strategies, prior to implementation.58

Typically, simulation modelling uses patient-level data to accurately represent regional stroke care systems and their performance. In addition, simulation modelling is efficient, allowing for simultaneous testing of various improvement strategies. So far, simulation models of acute stroke care systems have only been developed and used to test strategies in specific regions. For example, simulation models have been developed both for intravenous thrombolysis and EVT treatment, specifically in one region, the northern region of the Netherlands.5 9 Generalised models for reuse will provide cost advantage and time savings compared with building entirely new models, important in the rapidly developing world of stroke.1012 A central question, therefore, is whether a previously developed simulation model, such as the one developed in Northern Netherlands, can be reused and applied in other regions, acknowledging similarities as well as differences in stroke care systems across regions. Ideally, such reuse could further lower thresholds for adopting simulation models to optimise regional stroke care systems.

Therefore, the objectives of this study are (1) to test whether a previously developed simulation model can be reused and externally validated in the southwestern region of the Netherlands, considering needs for adjusting model structure and inputs and (2) to assess improvement strategies potential for the current organisation in the southwestern region of the Netherlands, and estimate the impact of several hypothetical improvement strategies for reducing onset-to-groin puncture (OTG) time.

Patients and methods

Model reuse

A previously developed Monte Carlo simulation model for patients treated with EVT in the northern region of the Netherlands, targeting opportunities to reduce onset-to-groin times by expediting workflow, was considered for reuse in the southwestern region of the Netherlands (figure 1).5 The underlying conceptual models (online supplemental figure S1) used for both regions, covering both a strategy with direct patient routing to the thrombectomy-capable centre and a strategy requiring interhospital transfer, were validated by stroke experts participating in the national Collaboration for New Treatments of Acute Stroke (CONTRAST) consortium.13 To adjust the simulation model to region-specific characteristics, distributions capturing patient delays along the acute stroke pathway (ie, time intervals between stroke symptom onset to start of EVT) and patient logistics (triage and routing patterns) were adapted, based on prospective patient-level data. The main output of the model was the onset-to-groin time and proposed strategies were implemented as simulation model extensions. Detailed information on the simulation modelling methodology and model set-up is provided in online supplemental material.

Setting

The region covered the area of southwest Netherlands, including two thrombectomy-capable stroke centres and eight primary stroke centres, with a total population of approximately 2.1 million inhabitants (figure 1A,B). Population densities differ per municipality between 89 and 4472 per km2.14 Before June 2016, the region was served by one thrombectomy-capable stroke centre and eight primary stroke centres, as one thrombectomy-capable stroke centre did not yet perform EVT during that period. Initially, two simulation models were built representing the southwest Netherlands region, making a distinction between patients directly transferred to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre and those put on interhospital transfer. Next, considering potential for improvements, a third, tailored simulation model including patients with interhospital transfer only was developed for the underserved subregion within the southwest Netherlands, that is, Zeeland, allowing for strategies to be evaluated.

The Zeeland subregion (figure 1C) is served by two primary stroke centres and faces relatively long interhospital transfers to the nearest thrombectomy-capable stroke centre (at distances ranging from 98 to 123 km). The total population of this region is approximately 300 000 with population densities per municipality ranging between 89 and 1297 per km2.14 Importantly, the subregion hosts more than 12 million tourist overnight stays per year, where these tourists might also suffer a stroke and definitely add to traffic congestion.15

Participants and model input data

We used patient-level data from the MR CLEAN Registry as input for the simulation model. The MR CLEAN Registry was a nationwide observational study to collect data of all patients who underwent EVT for ischaemic stroke in the Netherlands between April 2014 and December 2018.16 From the MR CLEAN Registry, we selected data from 648 patients who were treated with EVT in the study region, that is, all patients treated with EVT in the southwest Netherlands during the registry period. To obtain a complete overview of the acute stroke pathway for patients treated with EVT, data from local emergency medical services (EMS) were retrospectively collected and linked to the MR CLEAN Registry data.17 Patients were excluded from analyses in case of pre-stroke functional dependence (defined as a pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale >2) and when OTG exceeded 390 min, as selection for EVT based on CT-perfusion parameters beyond 6 hours since the moment of last seen well was not yet implemented during the study period. All collected time variables were used to create input distributions for the model. In case of missing data, no imputation was executed due to individual variable use for simulation model input, that is, distributions were created per time delay variable.

We collected the following time variables for all patients: symptom onset or last seen well, time of emergency call, EMS arrival at the stroke onset location, EMS departure to primary stroke centre or thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, EMS arrival at primary stroke centre or thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, non-contrast CT (NCCT), start intravenous thrombolysis at primary stroke centre or thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, CT angiography (CTA), arrival at angiography suite and groin puncture. For patients who were transferred from a primary stroke centre to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, we additionally collected the following time variables: transfer notification (second emergency call), EMS arrival at primary stroke centre, EMS departure from primary stroke centre to thrombectomy-capable stroke centre and EMS arrival at the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre. Based on these recorded time points, we derived time-delay variables representing intervals in the acute stroke workflow, which were subsequently used as input parameters (and pertaining parameter uncertainty in terms of 95% CrI) in the simulation model.

Patients presenting in-hospital at either a primary stroke centre or a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre were routed through the emergency department via one of three diagnostic routes: (1) NCCT followed by intravenous thrombolysis and then CTA; (2) NCCT followed by CTA and then intravenous thrombolysis; or (3) NCCT followed by CTA only, for patients ineligible for intravenous thrombolysis. For patients transferred from a primary stroke centre to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, additional imaging—such as NCCT and/or CTA—may be performed on arrival.

Distributions based on time-delay variables, which were calculated from the collected time variables, were used as input for the simulation models. For each time-delay variable, a probability distribution with corresponding parameters was implemented in the simulation model, allowing time delays to be assigned to individual simulated patients based on stochastic sampling. Distributions were obtained using ExpertFitTM.18 Details are presented as online supplemental material. The model is built and run in Plant Simulation software.19

Model validation

Face validity of the newly developed models was ascertained by discussing the model structure with regional stroke experts. In addition, models were assessed by comparing the mean, median, SD, minimum and maximum time values of the collected patient data and the simulation models representing the existing set-up of regional stroke services (baseline). Validation of the model in terms of the conceptual model, input values, the computerised model and operational validation was performed according to the Assessment of the Validation Status of Health Economic decision models (AdViSHE) tool20 (online supplemental materials). Ease of model reuse was considered by scrutinising changes of the new simulation model structure for the southwest Netherlands region vs the existing model of the northern Netherlands region.

Calibration was not required, as the model was designed to replicate observed time distributions rather than estimate unknown parameters.

Hypothetical organisational strategies

Based on the opinion of local stroke experts and the regional spread of primary stroke centres and thrombectomy-capable stroke centres, hypothetical strategies were identified and tested for the Zeeland subregion. These included: (1) expediting interhospital transfer of the patient by the use of helicopter EMS, (2) transport of the neurointerventionalist to the primary stroke centre (‘drive the doctor’),21 22 (3) routing patients to an alternative but existing thrombectomy-capable stroke centre formally located outside the southwest Netherlands region and (4) using prehospital triage for direct transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre for suspected LVO patients. To model these scenarios, the baseline model for the Zeeland region, assuming patient interhospital transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre elsewhere in the southwest Netherlands region, was adjusted for each parameter pertaining to the selected scenario.

Interhospital transfer by helicopter

To model interhospital transfer by helicopter EMS, time delays were estimated by expert opinion from the ANWB Medical Air Assistance, operating helicopters for medical purposes in the Netherlands.23 Time lapses from notifying the helicopter to patient arrival at the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre were analysed and incorporated to replace interhospital transfer by ground EMS. Time variables identified were: time from alarm to take off (4 min), flight time from station to primary stroke centre (20 min with uncertainty range of ±10%), loading up the patient who had a stroke into the helicopter and take off (5 min), flight time from primary stroke centre to thrombectomy-capable stroke centre (18 min with uncertainty range of ±10%) and time for disconnecting and unloading the patient from the helicopter (3 min). Due to bad weather conditions, in 15% of the calls, it is not possible to use helicopters for interhospital transfer, and ground EMS is used instead. Accordingly, this was reflected in the model.

‘Drive the doctor’ strategy

For modelling the ‘drive the doctor’ model, earlier analyses from a northern Netherlands study were used.22 Implementation of the ‘drive the doctor’ model boiled down to the introduction of a new thrombectomy-capable stroke centre for Zeeland, ie, a suitable primary stroke centre, based on availability of resources, supporting staff and expected treatment volumes. That centre may be upgraded for EVT performance by a mobile interventionalist, that is, an interventionalist from the existing thrombectomy-capable stroke centres in the southwest Netherlands region being on call. For Zeeland, one primary stroke centre is hypothetically upgraded.

Components analysed for hypothetical implementation of the ‘drive the doctor’model were: (1) mobile interventionalist dispatch time from the core thrombectomy-capable stroke centre and (2) the mobile interventionalist to primary stroke centre transport time.22 Previous studies recognise mobile interventionalists’ notification moment after LVO confirmation, which is modelled as the moment directly after CTA.2428 For the mobile interventionalist to the primary stroke centre transport time, the time for travelling to the primary stroke centre was constructed assuming that the interventionalist is stationed nearby or at the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre.21 Possible transport modality options for the region were car (private vehicle), ground EMS and helicopter. Mobile interventionalist to the primary stroke centre transport time for transport by car was generated using a web-based route planner (https://www.anwb.nl/verkeer/routeplanner).29 For modelling ground emergency medical centre data collected at regional EMS was used. For modelling helicopter times, expert opinion of the ANWB Medical Air Assistance was used.23 15% of the time, ground EMS was used in the helicopter scenario due to bad weather conditions. To model mobile interventionalists’ path as realistically as possible, a time delay from notification moment to departure was added (5–10 min, ie, 10 min when mobile interventionalist views the CTA through telemedicine as preparation for EVT). Likewise, mobile the interventionalists time from primary stroke centre arrival to angiography suite arrival was estimated at 5 min.

Parallel to the mobile interventionalists’ path, and following LVO confirmation, the patient preparation time after CTA to the actual start of the EVT on the angiography suite in the primary stroke centre was adjusted accordingly. Because this time interval could not be based on real patient-level data (as it is a hypothetical situation), it was assumed to be comparable to the preparation time observed for LVO patients with direct transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre. The time variable, CTA to angiography suite from all thrombectomy-capable stroke centres (involving patients with direct transfer) in the MR CLEAN Registry was used to assess time intervals as input for the simulation model.

Patient routing to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre outside the region

To model interhospital patient routing to a more nearby thrombectomy-capable stroke centre that is officially located outside the southwest Netherlands region (figure 1C), the transport time of the interhospital transfer was adjusted. Due to unobserved transport times to this thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, the transport time for ground EMS was based on the time for transport by car, collected by the web-based route planner,29 and adjusted for presumed higher ambulance speeds by reducing transport times by 37% (calculated by comparing route planner car times and EMS time variables that were collected for the southwest Netherlands region).1 In addition, workflow times of this thrombectomy-capable stroke centre are unknown and therefore we used time variables obtained from all thrombectomy-capable stroke centres in the MR CLEAN Registry for patients with interhospital transfer to develop distributions capturing in-hospital patient delays.

Prehospital routing based on Rapid Arterial Occlusion Evaluation scale

Based on results of the Prehospital Stroke Treatment Organization (PRESTO) study,30 the implementation of the prehospital Rapid Arterial Occlusion Evaluation (RACE) scale31 was modelled for prehospital triage and subsequent routing decisions in the Zeeland subregion. When LVO was suspected based on the RACE scale score >4, patients were routed directly to the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre. PRESTO showed a negative predictive value of a RACE scale score >4 of 95%, which means that only 5% of LVO patients will not directly be transported to the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre. This latter percentage is therefore still modelled as patients with interhospital transfer. For patients with suspected LVO based on a RACE scale score >4, and thus directly routed to the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, distributions for prehospital transport and in-hospital delays were adjusted and interhospital transfer was deleted. Prehospital transport was adjusted to times from the onset location to the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre, based on postal codes, and adjusted time of the ground EMS (reduced by 37%). In-hospital delays for the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre are modelled according to the model with direct patient transfer developed for the southwest Netherlands region.

Outcome measures and statistics

For each hypothetical pathway enhancement, we calculated the clinical benefits in terms of reduction in onset-to-groin time compared with the validated baseline model of the (sub)region. As clinical outcomes are strongly time-dependent (‘time is brain’), we focused on time-based metrics rather than reporting estimated probabilities of functional independence or mortality.32 Significance testing or providing CIs was deemed relevant, as the goal is to assess the potential gain expected based on 100 000 simulated patients (100 000 iterations) rather than to test a hypothesis in an empirical experiment. To capture uncertainty, 95% credibility intervals (CrI) were calculated. The number of 100 000 simulated patients was selected based on high demands on model accuracy in estimating patient onset-to-groin time, as measured by relative confidence credibility interval half-width <1%.

Model validation

For the simulation model with direct transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre a mean (SD) onset-to-groin time of 183.9 min (81.2) was found compared with a mean onset-to-groin time of 185.5 min (80.3) based on observed patient data. The simulation model with interhospital transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre for the whole southwestern Netherlands region was validated with a mean onset-to-groin time of 221.1 min (71.4) compared with the mean onset-to-groin time of 219.2 min (67.0) for observed patient data. In addition, the simulation model of the Zeeland subregion with interhospital transfer to a thrombectomy-capable stroke centre was validated with a mean onset-to-groin time of 233.9 min (53.3) compared with a mean onset-to-groin time of 230.6 min (57.5) for the observed patient data. The median, minimum and maximum time values were also comparable between the simulated and observed data. The results of the AdViSHE assessment have been added to online supplemental material.

Modelling results of strategies

Interhospital transport by helicopter can be expected to reduce onset-to-groin time by 16 min (table 2). Hypothetical implementation of the ‘drive the doctor’ model would reduce onset-to-groin time by 27, 49 and 58 min, respectively, for transport modality use of car, ground EMS and helicopter. Routing LVO patients to another thrombectomy-capable stroke centre outside the region would reduce onset-to-groin time by 13 min, while using the RACE scale as prehospital triage and subsequent routing LVO patients would reduce onset-to-groin time by 39 min.

Discussion

A previously developed simulation model for EVT in the northern region of the Netherlands was successfully reused in the southwestern region of the Netherlands, implying minimal efforts in identifying promising strategies. Using the ‘drive the doctor’ model and prehospital patient routing based on the RACE scale score >4 may reduce the onset-to-groin time between 27 and 58 min in the Zeeland subregion.

Reuse of the northern Netherlands simulation models for the new region was straightforward due to a comparable set-up and operation of acute stroke systems for both regions. Similarities in guidelines for stroke treatment found on a national scale may serve as an important explanation. Moreover, data collection was related to similar periods in time (2014–2017 vs 2014–2018), allowing for an equal playing field concerning the adoption of innovations in stroke treatment and organisation. Clearly, differences in stroke system set-up may be greater in an international setting, due to, for example, geography and local health infrastructure. Furthermore, innovations in the rapidly developing acute stroke care may render model reuse less straightforward, for example, when alternative approaches in diagnostic work-up, such as MRI or use of prehospital triage tools, are implemented in current practice. While such differences do exist and will emerge, many similarities in stroke systems will indeed remain, making simulation model re-use a pragmatic and efficient approach for optimisation of regional stroke systems.

Furthermore, our modelling has demonstrated how to test several hypothetical strategies deemed suitable for the Zeeland subregion. The ‘drive the doctor’ model showed promising results for all three transport modalities studied, indicating improvements of onset-to-groin times by around 30–60 min. Follow-up research has to clarify whether helicopter use may be cost-effective for the region.33 This may also apply to interhospital patient transfer by helicopter, which has yet only resulted in a 16-minute time gain. Prehospital routing using the RACE scale showed a promising 40-minute time gain. However, based on a positive predictive value of 42% for the RACE scale score >4, an estimated 58% of non-LVO patients would be routed directly to the thrombectomy-capable stroke centre. This would possibly cause a delay in administering intravenous thrombolysis for these patients, which in turn implies a worse prognosis and therefore should be further investigated.31 For routing eligible patients to another thrombectomy-capable stroke centre outside the region, limited time gains of 13 min may be expected. The precision of findings for this thrombectomy-capable stroke centre may be at some debate, due to missing data on in-hospital workflow.

Despite these promising results for the Zeeland region, thorough validation is required for each setting—national or international—before re-using our model, as regional differences in distances, protocols and system organisation may significantly affect its applicability.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, the reused simulation model was only developed for LVO patients treated with EVT. For a more realistic model of acute stroke systems, the developed simulation models should be extended to include other patients who have had a stroke, that is, non-LVO patients, haemorrhage patients and stroke mimics. The effect of certain organisational strategies on these patients could be examined as well, such as possible delayed intravenous thrombolysis for non-LVO patients, being an effect of prehospital routing.

Another limitation is the small sample size of the Zeeland subregion, for which hypothetical strategies were tested. More patients might be included to improve model robustness and enhance the realism of model findings. Our 100 000 individuals simulation model does indeed provide a clear estimate of time gains, yet with these numbers, uncertainty for individual patients’ improvements is lost. Still, for policy purposes, the simulations do provide clear guidance.

Conclusion

Our model, which is designed to test strategies to optimise EVT organisation, can be applied to different regions in the Netherlands. For the Zeeland subregion of the Netherlands, strategies tested resulted in promising results of ‘drive the doctor’ and prehospital patient routing using the RACE scale.
