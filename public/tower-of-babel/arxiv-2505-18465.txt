BiomechGPT: Towards a Biomechanically Fluent Multimodal Foundation Model for Clinically Relevant Motion Tasks
Ruize Yang, Ann Kennedy, R. James Cotton

Abstract
Advances in markerless motion capture are expanding access to biomechanical movement analysis, making it feasible to obtain high-quality movement data from outpatient clinics, inpatient hospitals, therapy, and even home. Expanding access to movement data in these diverse contexts makes the challenge of performing downstream analytics all the more acute. Creating separate bespoke analysis code for all the tasks end users might want is both intractable and does not take advantage of the common features of human movement underlying them all. Recent studies have shown that fine-tuning language models to accept tokenized movement as an additional modality enables successful descriptive captioning of movement. Here, we explore whether such a multimodal motion-language model can answer detailed, clinically meaningful questions about movement. We collected over 30 hours of biomechanics from nearly 500 participants, many with movement impairments from a variety of etiologies, performing a range of movements used in clinical outcomes assessments. After tokenizing these movement trajectories, we created a multimodal dataset of motion-related questions and answers spanning a range of tasks. We developed BiomechGPT, a multimodal biomechanics-language model, on this dataset. Our results show that BiomechGPT demonstrates high performance across a range of tasks such as activity recognition, identifying movement impairments, diagnosis, scoring clinical outcomes, and measuring walking. BiomechGPT provides an important step towards a foundation model for rehabilitation movement data.



1
Introduction

How someone moves provides a huge amount of information about their health status. Recent advances in markerless motion capture make it substantially easier to capture biomechanics in more clinically accessible ways, such as from multiview or even smartphone video
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. A remaining barrier preventing accessible biomechanics from having its full potential clinical impact is analyzing the resulting data. While easier access to movement data empowers clinicians and scientists to measure more things, there is a nearly unlimited range of movements that people perform, and many questions that might be asked about that movement, from measuring specific biomechanical parameters to high-level tasks like producing a differential diagnosis underlying movement impairments.

Developing a specialized analysis for each possible application is highly time-consuming. Here, we explore an alternative approach: training a multimodal large language model (MLLM) that is fluent in human biomechanics in addition to natural language and trained to solve these clinically meaningful movement tasks. We call this model BiomechGPT.

Movement-fluent MLLMs have been demonstrated in recent works like MotionGPT and MotionLLM
[
4
,
5
]
. These first train a vector quantized variational autoencoder
[
6
]
on motion sequences to convert them to a sequence of discrete tokens. Then, they extend the vocabulary of the language model to include this novel modality and train it to consume and output these tokens, allowing the model to both caption and generate motion sequences.

Our work differs from MotionGPT and MotionLLM in several ways. First, prior works tokenize movement sequences that are represented with the skinned multi-person linear (SMPL) model
[
7
]
. However, this model has a kinematic chain that is not biomechanically accurate
[
8
]
and does not reflect how clinicians talk about movement. Instead, we parameterize movements using a biomechanical model
[
9
,
10
]
. Second, language outputs describing movement in prior works are evaluated with standard linguistic measures such as BLEU
[
11
]
. In contrast, we define a set of clinically meaningful, natural language motion assessment tasks, and benchmark the performance of BiomechGPT on these tasks. To support this project, we curated a large dataset of 34 hours of data from 467 participants. Most have movement impairments from various etiologies, including using a lower limb prosthesis or a history of neurologic injuries. Participants performed a variety of movements, with a heavy emphasis on clinical outcome assessments for mobility. We annotated this dataset with the activity being performed, the presence of a gait impairment, the etiology of this gait impairment, the use of an assistive device, performance on clinical outcome assessments, and a history of falls. In this way, our work tests whether these motion-language models can solve fine-grained clinical tasks and, additionally, whether they can generalize to populations with movement impairments. Finally, we explicitly test for and identify evidence of positive transfer learning as we expand the set of motion tasks that BiomechGPT is trained on.

Our work shows that a multimodal biomechanics-language model trained on a large dataset of movements parameterized with a biomechanical model can solve these detailed tasks (Figure
1
). This opens a promising avenue for the use of LLMs in motion science and rehabilitation, comparable to other successes in multimodal language models in medicine
[
12
,
13
]
. In particular, it is now possible to train a model that, in addition to being exposed to the broad information about medical history from imaging, genomics, and text data, can also be trained on how people move. This offers an exciting new way for clinicians and researchers to interact with biomechanical data, such as through a chat interface (Figure
1
).

In short, our contributions are:

•

We trained a VQ-VAE on motion sequences of biomechanical data from clinical participants, and created a large multimodal biomechanics-language dataset of a set of clinically-meaningful question-and-answer tasks.

•

We train BiomechGPT on this dataset and rigorously evaluate it on these tasks, and show it performs well across a range of them, including activity recognition, identifying movement impairments and likely etiologies, classifying a history of falls, and measuring walking parameters and performance on clinical outcomes assessments.

•

We demonstrate positive transfer learning when including more tasks in the training corpus.

More recent works fine-tune pretrained large language models (LLM). [

]
. This was made more efficient and performant in MotionLLM by using QLoRA

Recently, MotionGPT-2 extended MotionGPT to include additional tokens for understanding hand movements
[

. They tokenized motion sequences and trained a LLM to perform chain-of-thought reasoning and predict the MDS-UPDRS gait score, with the chain of thought prompts generated from kinematic measurements using another language model.

All these above works parameterized movement using the SMPL model

3
Approach

3.1
Dataset Curation

Our dataset contains biomechanical trajectories from a wide range of participants performing different activities. Many of the performed activities are commonly used clinical outcome assessments for mobility, including overground walking, the timed-up-and-go test, the L-test, the four-square-step-test, and the functional gait assessments, such as which also involve sitting in a chair. Many participants had gait impairments, such as from a lower-limb amputation and the use of a prosthetic, or from neurologic conditions such as a stroke. Participants were recorded with multiple cameras and processed with markerless motion capture algorithms to generate the kinematic trajectories. All data collection was approved by our Institutional Review Board.

We used a 90/10 training and validation split. This was performed at the level of participants with a consistent split when training the VQ-VAE and when training BiomechGPT.

Biomechanical Reconstruction.

We acquired synchronized multiview video while participants performed the above activities, and fit our markerless motion capture data similarly to
[
24
]
. We used a biomechanical model that was modified from LocoMujoco
[
10
]
, which was derived from the OpenSim model
[
25
]
. We modified this to include a 3-degree-of-freedom neck joint. The fitting process results in a trajectory for each trial
\mathbf{\tau}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{q}_{0},\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\mathbf{q}_{1},\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{1}...\mathbf{q}_{T},\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{T}\right\}
.
\beta
corresponds to the body shape and is not used in this work.
\mathbf{q}
is a 41-element vector where the first 3 elements are the global position in Euclidean space, the next 4 correspond to a quaternion representation of the pelvis orientation in 3-D space, and the remaining 34 elements are the body joint angles.
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
is the change in these parameters, which is a 40-element vector as the change in the quaternion is represented as a 3-element rotational vector.

3.2
Motion Tokenization

We base our motion tokenizer on the VQ
-
VAE architecture used by
[
5
]
and
[
26
]
, using a 1D
-
convolutional encoder–decoder to convert 34
-
dimensional joint
-
angle trajectories from our biomechanical model into discrete tokens. These tokens are taken from a codebook of
K
vectors, each of dimension
D
, that is learned during VQ-VAE training. The encoder downsamples an input sequence of length
L
by a factor
l
, producing a latent sequence of length
L/l
. During encoding, at each timestep, this latent vector is replaced by the closest codebook vector by Euclidean distance. A symmetric 1D
-
convolutional decoder then reconstructs the original motion from the quantized embeddings. Training minimizes a combination of reconstruction loss and a commitment loss
L=L_{recon}+\beta_{commit}*L_{commit}
. In training, we used
l
=4,
K
=512,
D
=512,
\beta_{commit}=0.02
. We also follow common practice and learn the codebook with an exponential moving average update and resetting, to encourage greater entropy of codebook vector usage. Tokenizer training takes about three hours on one A6000 GPU. Once trained, the tokenizer is fixed, and is used to convert each trial in the dataset into a sequence of motion tokens for use in the multimodal dataset. Our tokenizer obtained an average reconstruction error per joint of 5.78 degree measured by RMSE.

To eliminate spatial information as a source of variance, our encoder discards the position and velocity information of the pelvis. We also zeroed out the wrist flexion, deviation, supination because they were less pertinent for our downstream tasks, and the metatarsal joint in the foot as it was less accurately tracked.

Clinical Annotation and Multimodal Dataset Creation.

To train and evaluate our MLLM, we created a dataset of multimodal question-and-answer tasks. Tasks fall into two categories: discrete classification tasks, in which the model is asked to identify an action or detect presence of an impairment, and regression tasks in which the model is prompted to produce a numerical output based on some aspect of the subject’s movement.

Classification tasks.
For each participant, we knew the activity being performed, whether they had a gait impairment, and if so the etiology. We also knew whether they were using an assistive device. For our lower-limb prosthesis users, we also asked whether they had a history of falls. Using this available information, we generated a set of motion assessment tasks, taking the form of prompt-answer pairs such as:

Q: <motion_start>... <motion_end> What is the most likely diagnosis for this gait impairment? A: Stroke.
"

See the supplementary text for a full list of the text prompts used. We then tokenized each biomechanical trajectory, and generated multimodal training examples for all applicable tasks. For example, we did not create a most likely diagnosis prompt for someone without a gait impairment, or a fall history for someone where we did not collect that data. This resulted in about 27k pairs of prompts and answers.

Regression tasks.
In addition to the above labels, some clinical assessments of movement use continuously valued measurements of some aspect of subject movement, such as the time to complete a Timed Up and Go (TUG) or For Squared Step Test (FSST). Additionally, in many trials, participants walked on an instrumented walkway that measured their walking speed and cadence. Therefore, we created additional prompts to produce measures of time, speed, or cadence for these tasks, following the approach used for the classification tasks, and incorporated them into our dataset.

3.3
Multimodal Finetuning

Our training protocol closely followed that of MotionLLM
[
4
]
. We used Gemma 3 as our base model
[
27
]
and fit a quantized low-rank adapter (QLoRA) to perform supervised fine tuning
[
21
]
, using the Huggingface Transformers library with 4bit quantization
[
28
]
. Prior to setting up the adapter, we extended the language model vocabulary with additional elements to handle the tokenized motion elements. We used a rank and alpha of 32 for QLoRA with a dropout of 0.1, other than in an experiment with a rank and alpha of 16 and a dropout of 0.005. We trained for 1.2 epochs, as early experiments showed evidence of overfitting much beyond one epoch. Our learning rate was 0.0002. We used a batch size of 10 and maximum sequence length of 1024 when training a 4B parameter base model on an 80GB A100 (taking 2 hours for QLoRA SFT) and a batch size of 6 for our experiments on a 1B parameter base model, which fit on a 40GB A100 (and which took 1 hour for QLoRA SFT).

One pertinent difference between our work and MotionLLM is that MotionLLM fine-tunes a base-pretrained model with separate adapters for motion captioning and motion generation. However, we wanted to train a single model on a variety of tasks, to permit the possibility of positive transfer learning across tasks. To this end, we used an instruction-tuned model as our base and used the standard Gemma chat template to formulate these prompts:

We did not train on any multi-turn conversations or use any reinforcement learning from human feedback. Since our prompts contain the newly added motion tokens but are otherwise short, we chose to include tokens in the prompt as part of the loss computation, helping the model to learn these new elements. Training BiomechGPT took about one hour on an A100 for the Gemma-1B base model and four hours for the Gemma-4B base model.

4
Results

We trained several variations of BiomechGPT and measured the cross-validated performance for classification (Table
2
) and regression tasks (Table
3
). Each model variant was trained 3 times, with each training run drawing different samples from the annotation templates for each trial to produce unique annotations for our multimodal dataset, although with the same tokenized trajectories.

Our classification tasks included activity classification, movement impairment (binary classifier), diagnosis for people with movement impairments, and a history of falls for lower limb prosthesis users. Regression tasks included the cadence and average walking speed for trials on an instrumented walkway, and the manually measured time for the timed-up-and-go and the four-square-step test. Next, we describe the performance of these models in more detail.

Assistive Device Classification.

Classification of the assistive device being used yielded an F1-score of 0.76 across five categories, which dropped to 0.72 for the 1B model (
Table

2
). The most common error was for BiomechGPT to declare no assistive device was used even when one was present (

Compared to other tasks, this task showed a particularly large gap between the performance evaluated on the training data and the test scores, perhaps suggesting a high individual variability in movement patterns associated with device use. Performance may also have been adversely impacted by us zeroing out the wrist and forearm angles, features that would more directly indicate when a hand-held device such as a cane or walker is in use.

Fall Prediction in Lower Limb Prosthesis Users.

We also tested the ability of our model to infer the history of falls in lower-limb prosthesis users (
Fig

3
). This population report fairly high rates of falls, thus our dataset was fairly balanced, although still small due to the relatively low number of lower-limb prosthesis users in the population. Our 4B model had an average F1 score of 0.83, which dropped to 0.72 for the 1B model. However, the test set only included two participants on this task, and the test performance frequently exceeded the training performance, so the test participants may have movement patterns that are easier to classify than the general population.

Comparison to XGBoost.

We developed a non-LLM baseline for our tasks, adapting an approach that used the frequency of motion tokens to classify drug treatments in an animal study
[
29
]
. For each trial, we tokenized movement using our VQ-VAE, then computed the normalized histogram of tokens appearing in that trial, yielding a fixed-length vector. We then used these vectors of token usage to train an XGBoost classifier
[
30
]
separately for each classification task. XGBoost parameters were chosen via randomized search to maximize F1-score, with 3-fold cross-validation on the training set. We also used this classifier to compute chance performance by shuffling the labels on all trials before training.
Table

2
shows that our LLM outperforms this baseline for all the classification tasks, and greatly exceeded chance performance.

We note that stronger non-LLM baselines can be created, as the normalized histogram approach discards sequence information that is available to the LLM. However, the ability of a single LLM to learn multiple tasks and outperform standard methods supports our hypothesis that a large biomechanics-language multimodal model is a promising generalist approach to biomechanics analysis.

4.2
Regression Tasks

Our training prompts included four tasks with numerical outputs. These are tasks where the objective answer is apparent from the biomechanics, rather than other outcomes assessments that rely on a clinician’s subjective impressions. Two tasks derive from biomechanical analyses of walking, specifically estimating average walking speed and cadence; the other two involve clinical outcome assessments, specifically the time to completion in the TUG and FSST. To evaluate model performance on each task, we extracted the numerical output from BiomechGPT-generated responses to task prompts, and performed linear regression of these values against the ground truth measurements, reporting the Pearson’s correlation in

We note that the cross-entropy loss used in SFT is not designed to optimize this type of numerical output; it is therefore striking that BiomechGPT can perform fairly well on these tasks without additional strategies like reinforcement learning.

4.3
Positive Transfer Learning and Model Variations

Positive Transfer Learning.

To determine whether BiomechGPT exhibited positive transfer learning between tasks, we also quantified the classification performance as we changed the size of the set of tasks. We found that removal of a subset of tasks, like fall prediction, diagnosis classification, and the regression tasks, degraded performance on impairment classification, reducing the average F1 score from 0.88 to 0.84. Performance on diagnosis classification reduced more strikingly when other tasks were removed, from an F1 score of 0.73 to 0.68. This demonstrates positive transfer learning in BiomechGPT, where the inclusion of additional tasks improves the performance on a set of base tasks. This also suggests that continuing to more densely annotate our biomechanical reconstruction dataset will improve performance on all tasks, through better learning of the shared features of human movement.

LLM Model Variations.

Table
2
and Table
3
show that model performance scaled modestly with base model size, with a 1B parameter base model showing high but generally reduced performance compared to a 4B parameter base model. We also tested reducing the rank of our QLoRA adapter from 32 to 16 in our 1B parameter model, and did not observe a clear impact on performance.

VQ-VAE Variations.

We explored several hyperparameters in both our motion tokenizer and in training BiomechGPT. For example, we varied the number of motion tokens from 512 to 1024, or reduced the downsampling from every 4 frames to every 16 frames. While our results did vary somewhat, this seemed to be primarily within the noise of refitting rather than showing a systematic influence of tokenization hyperparameters. However, the reconstruction error of 5.6 degrees in our tokenizer suggests that it might fail to encode some subtle motions differences that could ultimately be required for some downstream tasks, suggesting that performance gains may be attainable with further optimization of the motion representation.

5
Discussion

Our work shows that recent advances in motion-language modeling can be translated to clinical movement analysis with promising results. We advance prior work in two ways. First, we measure and tokenize movements using a biomechanical model, which parametrizes movement in a manner consistent with clinical applications. Second, prior work verified the plausibility of motion captioning using standard linguistic metrics, whereas we rigorously validated the performance of BiomechGPT on a set of clinically meaningful tasks. This included cross-validation at the level of participants during training of both our VQ-VAE and the language model. Our results suggest that multimodal language models fluent in human biomechanics can provide a powerful tool for enhancing the analysis of movement data. Such an approach could be applied to data collected both in clinical settings and in the home, facilitated by accessible methods like markerless motion capture
[
24
,
3
]

Most AI studies of human movement have focused on able-bodied participants. While other clinical biomechanics datasets exist, these typically involve only 10-20 people from a homogeneous clinical background. Here, we use 34 hours of data from 465 participants with a variety of clinical backgrounds. While this dataset enabled us to create the rich suite of tasks necessary to train BiomechGPT, it is still small compared to many studies training language models; some of our tasks only had a few participants in the test set, limiting statistical interpretation. Nonetheless, our results in the context of the current dataset size highlight the potential of this approach, and suggest that performance of models like BiomechGPT is likely to improve as we expand both the number of participants in the dataset, the range of actions they perform, and tasks we delineate and train on.

Our existing dataset can also be expanded with denser annotations to provide a richer understanding of subject movement. Future iterations of the dataset could include additional clinical information, or even performance on clinical assessments at future time points to develop predictive models of patient recovery trajectories. We can also include multiple motion sequences in prompts to compare performance on different trials. Another potential direction is to expand the training dataset to include multi-turn prompts for open ended question and answering, and to support movement generation; the latter is feasible to implement due to our tokenization of movement data inputs.

Our findings of positive transfer learning on this dataset suggest that additional tasks and data will further bolster model performance. Thus growing the motion dataset, alongside other improvements, might lead to a future foundation model of clinically meaningful movement that can generalize outside of the training support, and perhaps even incorporate knowledge from its broad language pretraining. This type of emergent cross-modal medical understanding has been seen in vision-language models
[
13
]
.

5.1
Limitations

This method has many limitations, some of which are readily addressable. For example, our current training set does not include many clinically relevant motor tasks, primarily due to the need to annotate and curate appropriate data. A related limitation is the accuracy and reliability of model output, which varies across tasks in the dataset. We anticipate that model accuracy on many tasks will improve with more data, which we are actively collecting. An equally important future step is to train BiomechGPT to indicate the uncertainty of its assessments. This could be accomplished by training the model to produce a differential diagnosis or report confidence of its diagnosis, and using reinforcement learning to penalize confident but inaccurate statements.

We also plan to extend BiomechGPT’s movement vocabulary by developing multiple VQ-VAEs that map different movement representations into a shared latent space. This would allow us to expand the set of movement BiomechGPT is familiar with beyond those performed in clinical settings.

As with many language models, BiomechGPT is susceptible to hallucinations; this was quite apparent when we prompted the model with questions outside the scope of our training data. The model currently lacks the capacity to explain its outputs, which is important for trusthworthiness in clinical practice. This may be addressed by expanding the model to produce differential diagnoses and confidence estimates; we further hypothesize that in the future, reasoning models with access to high-performance biomechanical simulations could improve the capacity of BiomechGPT to give transparent interpretations of its predictions.

Notably, we did not include arm and hand biomechanics in this work, though these features can be captured with markerless motion capture
[
31
]
and MotionGPT2 has demonstrated the use of tokenized hands for better arm and hand movement understanding
[
22
]
. This is an important future direction, as the high range of motion and open-ended nature of arm and hand movements make them challenging to quantify, presenting a critical gap in computational assessment of rehabilitation.

5.2
Potential broader impact

For our goal of clinical access to motion analysis, we anticipate these methods primarily producing societal benefits. Furthermore, our focus on a population that includes both able-bodied individuals and individuals with movement impairments is an important step towards AI Fairness for people with disabilities. Nonetheless, all technologies have the potential for misuse. One concern is the premature use of this technology for clinical decision making, and we strongly emphasize that these tools can be challenging to use in the wild and caution is required at all stages. Furthermore, there are many movement patterns and diagnoses not represented in our current dataset. Another concern is that this technology could allow inferring private information about health status from video observations without consent.

6
Conclusion

Building on recent work in motion captioning, we train BiomechGPT on a large multimodal dataset containing paired language annotations with tokenized biomechanical movements from a variety of people with different clinical histories. We show that BiomechGPT performs well on a variety of clinically meaningful tasks, including recognizing activities, identifying movement impairments, inferring likely etiology, measuring gait cadence and walking speed, and scoring clinical outcome assessments. We also find it outperforms XGBoost trained on the classification tasks, and find evidence of positive transfer learning as we increase the set of tasks.
