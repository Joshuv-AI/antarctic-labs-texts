A previously uncharacterized Fur-family metalloregulator integrates iron- and manganese-sensing to control virulence gene regulatory networks in Brucella
Authors: Gastón E Amato, Tadeo Pascua, Lila Y Ramis, Magalí G Bialer, Angeles Zorreguieta, Rodrigo Sieira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracellular survival of bacterial pathogens relies on specific adaptations to the stress found within the eukaryotic host cell environment. These processes depend on the activity of transcriptional regulatory networks that ensure proper spatiotemporal expression of all genes participating in the subversion of eukaryotic cellular functions. Such transcriptional networks often involve complex regulatory mechanisms, including both activators and repressors as well as functional redundancy among transcription factors (TFs), making the discovery of novel virulence regulators challenging. In this study, we dissected a bacterial gene expression network in order to identify new TFs impacting the virulence of Brucella, a widespread intracellular pathogen. By combining in silico and experimental approaches, we identified TF-binding sites resembling the ferric-uptake regulator (Fur) binding motif adjacent to binding sites of VjbR, the main activator of Brucella virulence. Genetic and molecular analyses revealed that Fur4, a previously uncharacterized Fur family regulator, is responsible for iron (Fe)- and manganese (Mn)-dependent repression of different loci including VjbR target virulence genes such as the operon encoding the VirB Type-IV Secretion System. Our results showed that Fur4 acts in concert with the Mn-uptake regulator Mur as well as other Mn/Fe-responsive TF, affecting bacterial gene expression both in vitro and during intracellular trafficking of Brucella within macrophage cells. These findings reveal a novel metal-dependent regulatory mechanism modulating the expression of Brucella virulence determinants, enhancing our understanding of how pathogens integrate environmental signals to fine-tune gene expression during intracellular infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29103-1.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology

Introduction

As a result of continuous coevolution with their hosts, intracellular bacterial pathogens have developed sophisticated adaptations to overcome the stress found in the eukaryotic cell environment. Such adaptations include strategies that enable them to evade host bactericidal activities and promote the formation of intracellular niches that support bacterial replication while regulating the level of damage inflicted to the eukaryotic host. This balance allows for the establishment of chronic infections, ensuring survival and spread of the pathogen. Brucella, the genus of α-proteobacteria responsible for a zoonosis known as brucellosis1, employs an intracellular adaptation strategy that involves subverting the endocytic pathway to evade phagolysosomal-mediated degradation, hijacking the secretory pathway to create its replicative niche, and interacting with multivesicular bodies and components of the autophagic pathway2. These latter interactions culminate in the completion of the Brucella intracellular life cycle with the egress of bacteria from the host cell, colonization of neighboring cells, and bacterial dissemination3–5.

Intracellular adaptation processes involve the activity of transcriptional regulatory networks necessary for the proper spatiotemporal expression of all genes participating in the subversion of eukaryotic cellular functions or in the adjustment of bacterial physiology to the harsh intracellular environmental conditions found within the host. Following internalization into phagocytic cells, Brucella is enclosed within the so-called Brucella-containing vacuole (BCV), a membrane-bound organelle that matures from early to late endosome-like compartments6,7. This transition comprises limited and controlled fusion events with lysosomes, through which BCVs rapidly undergo intraluminal acidification, reaching pH values of 4.55,8. While this acidification process kills a significant portion of intracellular brucellae, paradoxically, low pH serves as a key environmental signal that triggers the expression of essential virulence genes necessary for Brucella to avoid clearance by macrophages9,10. One such virulence factor is the Type-IV Secretion System (T4SS) encoded by the virB operon11,12. In Brucella, the VirB T4SS is responsible for the translocation of effector molecules that interfere with the host secretory pathway and promote the biogenesis of the bacterial replicative niche.

Recent studies showed that low-nutrient acidic conditions induce the phosphorylation of the two-component system regulator BvrR13, enabling it to bind to the vjbR promoter and induce the expression of VjbR14, a LuxR-type TF that plays a crucial role in Brucella virulence as the main transcriptional activator of the virB genes15. It was observed that the virB promoter is rapidly induced following internalization and subsequently repressed upon bacterial replication16. Among the transcriptional activators directly involved in regulation of the virB genes, Integration Host Factor (IHF) and the histidine-utilization repressor HutC were shown to act as positive regulators necessary for full virB promoter activity within macrophages16,17. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying the subsequent shutdown of intracellular virB expression have remained elusive so far. The fact that VjbR is a LuxR-family protein naturally raises the question whether quorum sensing (QS) plays a role in the temporal pattern of virB expression. However, several lines of evidence argue against this notion. First, VjbR is a strikingly atypical ‘orphan’ LuxR homolog that binds DNA and activates transcription in the absence of QS signaling molecules15,18,19. Moreover, VjbR is required for virB expression in the context of single bacteria enclosed within individual BCVs. Furthermore, although additional QS components were identified (i.e., a second LuxR-type TF19,20, an acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL)-degrading acylase21, and intrinsic production of low levels of AHLs by unknown mechanisms22), the current evidence does not support a canonical mechanism of population-density sensing for virB regulation, and QS in Brucella remains far from being completely understood.

In addition to the known role of VjbR as a transcriptional activator of the virB genes, early transcriptomic and proteomic studies revealed that in bacteria grown in rich medium, this regulator also affects the expression of hundreds of additional genes23,24. A further comprehensive ChIP-seq and RNA-seq analysis performed under nutrient-poor, acidic conditions mimicking the intraphagosomal environment revealed that VjbR binds to 129 intergenic regions in the B. abortus 2308 genome and modulates the expression of 47 genes associated with the requirements of Brucella for evading lysosome-mediated degradation and adapting its energy metabolism25. However, only a subset of these VjbR-binding sites was functional, since many of the detected VjbR-binding events showed no transcriptional effects on neighbouring genes under these conditions. Moreover, some genomic regions matching the VjbR-binding motif lacked VjbR ChIP-seq peaks. Furthermore, VjbR also interacted with high affinity to DNA sequences showing no match with the VjbR-binding motif. Based on these observations, we speculated that VjbR may interplay with additional TFs through different mechanisms, including repressors that compete with VjbR for binding to specific promoters and combinatorial interactions with transcriptional activators that assist VjbR in binding to genomic regions lacking conserved VjbR-binding sequences.

In this work, we combined in silico and experimental approaches to find TFs that might interfere with the VjbR regulon. This led us to identify a conserved DNA sequence similar to binding sites of the Ferric-uptake regulator (Fur), whose presence correlated with an observed repression of the activity of several VjbR target promoters in response to iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn). Using genetic and molecular tools, we identified two transcriptional regulators belonging to the Fur family that were responsible for the observed metal-dependent control of promoter activity. Taken together, our results provide evidence of a transcriptional regulatory network where VjbR-mediated induction of gene expression is counteracted by the activity of TFs that respond to changes in the concentration of specific transition metals.

Results

Determination of a conserved DNA motif adjacent to genomic VjbR-bound sequences

Under conditions mimicking an acidic, nutrient-poor intracellular environment, the VjbR regulon exhibits features suggesting possible interactions with additional TFs25. Such regulatory elements may be required to repress or activate the expression of different subsets of VjbR target genes in response to specific environmental signals. This idea prompted us to search for conserved sequences adjacent to either VjbR-bound regions lacking VjbR-binding motifs, or to promoter sequences matching the VjbR-binding motif but showing no VjbR binding, which may serve as operator sites for putative transcriptional activators or repressors, respectively (see Supplementary File S1 for the rationale of the in silico analysis). This approach led us to identify a DNA sequence motif (Fig. 1A) enriched in a subset of VjbR target promoter sequences. Using motif comparison tools and databases of known prokaryotic TF-binding sites, we found that the identified motif resembles binding site sequences recognized by the ferric uptake regulator (Fur), with the closest match being the Neisseria gonorrhoeae Fur homolog (p-value = 2.41e-03). Fur is an Fe-responsive repressor of iron transport systems and the canonical member of the Fur family of metalloregulators26. A scan of the B. abortus 2308 genome with the Fur-like binding site motif identified a total of 285 occurrences (p-value cutoff of 1.0e-4), 57.2% of which were located in intergenic sequences. Since 88% of the Brucella genome consists of coding sequences, this latter observation suggested that the identified motif is enriched in promoter regions and therefore may serve as an actual binding site for Fur-like TFs. Of note, like other α-proteobacteria of the orders Rhizobiales and Rhodobacterales, Brucella lacks the prototypical Fur homolog27, where iron sensing and homeostasis is proposed to be relegated to other TFs. Accordingly, we speculated that the Fur-like binding site motif might be recognized by one of the four other Fur paralogs encoded in the Brucella genome (i.e., the manganese-uptake regulator Mur28; the zinc-uptake regulator Zur29; the heme-responsive regulator Irr30; or an uncharacterized fourth Fur homolog encoded by bab1_0393, which we designated Fur4).

To study the functionality of the Fur-like binding site and its potential impact on the VjbR regulon, we constructed single-copy chromosomal lacZ transcriptional fusions of three candidate promoters selected from the 46 intergenic regions that bind VjbR and match the identified motif. The chosen promoter regions correspond to genes showing direct VjbR-mediated regulation of expression, including virB1, the first gene of the virB operon; bab2_0131, a gene encoding a phosphatidic acid phosphatase 2 (PAP2) family protein likely involved in recycling lipid carriers during peptidoglycan biosynthesis; and bab2_0738, which encodes a putative K+/H+ antiporter with homology to NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductases. β-Galactosidase levels were then measured to assess promoter activity in the wild-type strain B. abortus 2308 under low-nutrient acidic conditions mimicking the intraphagosomal environment25. The assays were performed with or without the addition of Fe, the metal to which different Fur family regulators can directly or indirectly respond to. As shown in Fig. 1B, in the presence of Fe, we observed a significant reduction in promoter activity of the three tested promoters, suggesting a correlation between motif occurrence and metal-dependent modulation of promoter activity.

Mur and/or Fur4 are involved in metal-dependent repression of specific VjbR target promoters

To identify the TF responsible for the observed Fe-dependent repression of gene expression, we assessed virB1 and bab2_0131 promoter activity in the wild-type strain or in different mutant contexts. First, we studied isogenic mutants for two TFs known for their involvement in regulation of iron homeostasis (i.e., the Fur family regulator Irr and RirA, an iron-responsive Rrf2-family regulator of Rhizobiales). However, in both cases, no differences in promoter activity were observed compared to the wild-type strain (data not shown). Subsequently, we analyzed the behavior of a mutant of the well-studied regulator Mur, a TF known to respond to Mn and also to Fe31,32. Since preliminary results indicated that Fur4 also responds to both of these transition metals (R.M. Roop II, personal communication), we included a fur4 mutant in our analysis as well. As shown in Fig. 2A and 2B, while little or no effect was observed in the Δmur strain in the absence of metals, deletion of fur4 resulted in a slight increase in virB1 promoter activity and a more pronounced increase in bab2_0131, suggesting that this Fur family regulator participates in transcriptional repression of these genes. However, Fe- and Mn-dependent repression of the activity of both the virB1 and bab2_0131 promoters persisted in the fur4 mutant (Fig. 2A and 2B), suggesting that an additional Fe- and Mn-responsive TF may act redundantly with Fur4 to downregulate transcription of these genes. To address this possibility, we examined the effect of both transition metals in a Δfur4 Δmur double mutant context. As shown in Fig. 2A, the double deletion resulted in an increase in virB1 promoter activity, together with abrogation of repression by Fe and Mn, suggesting that Mur and Fur4 play redundant roles in metal-dependent repression of virB expression. In the bab2_0131 promoter, however, the double mutant displayed a profile of promoter activity similar to that of the single Δfur4 mutant strain (Fig. 2B), suggesting that this promoter is under the control of both Fur4 and an additional, yet unidentified Fe- and Mn-responsive transcriptional repressor.

Taken together, the above results point to a regulatory mechanism where Fur4 and Mur, or other metal-responsive TF, play overlapping roles in antagonizing VjbR-mediated activation of promoters containing Fur-like binding site motifs. Since the fur4 promoter region was previously predicted to contain a Mur-binding site33 and also matches the predicted Fur-like binding site-like motif (p-value cutoff of 1.0e-3), we analyzed the fur4 promoter to further characterize this Fur homolog and assess whether it is also subject to dual regulation by redundant metal-responsive TFs. As shown in Fig. 2C, while deletion of fur4 had no noticeable effect on fur4 promoter activity, the Δmur mutant strain displayed an increase in fur4 expression, albeit still showing strong repression by Fe and Mn. Furthermore, similar to what was observed in the virB1 promoter, both Fe- and Mn-mediated repression of fur4 expression were abrogated in the Δfur4 Δmur double mutant strain (Fig. 2C), demonstrating that Mur and Fur4 also play redundant roles in regulation of fur4 promoter activity.

Mur and/or Fur4 directly interact with VjbR target promoters

To determine whether the observed transcriptional regulation by Fur4 and/or Mur is a result of direct interactions with the aforementioned promoters, we performed DNase I footprinting assays using different concentrations of the TFs in the presence of Mn, one of the two transition metals that enable both Fur4 and Mur to bind to DNA. As shown in Fig. 3A and 4A, in the virB1 promoter, our in vitro assays showed that Fur4 interacted with two regions centered at positions -193 and -45 relative to the start of the virB1 coding sequence, which were designated as binding sites I and II, respectively. These designations were based on the apparent hierarchy of binding affinities, inferred from the occupancy patterns at different Fur4 concentrations (Fig. 3A). As depicted in Fig. 4A, the 48-bp long Fur4-binding site I overlaps with the binding sites of IHF, HutC and MdrA34, suggesting a mechanism of repression of virB expression where Fur4 competes with three different transcriptional activators for the binding to the virB promoter. On the other hand, the shorter 28-bp long Fur4-binding site II is located 5 bp upstream of the virB1 transcription start site(TSS)16, overlapping the -10 element of the promoter (Fig. 4A). Therefore, these observations support a model in which Fur4 represses virB expression not only by competing with transcriptional activators but also by blocking the access of the RNA polymerase (RNAP) holoenzyme to the promoter in response to increasing levels of Fe and/or Mn. In addition to Fur4, we also observed direct interaction of Mur with the virB1 promoter, in this case with three different binding sites centered at 223, 129 and 52 bp upstream of the start of the coding sequence, designated as sites I, II and III, respectively, following the same criteria as described above (Fig. 3B and 4A). As shown in Fig. 3B, the Mur-binding site I is extensive, encompassing a 108-long sequence that displayed a 41-bp central core region fully protected from DNase I digestion at 150 nM Mur, whereas its 30- and 37-bp flanking sequences showed complete protection only at higher concentrations of the TF (300 nM). These observations suggest initial Mur binding to the site I core region followed by lower-affinity binding events to flanking sequences at increasing concentrations of the regulator, consistent with that observed in other members of the Fur family35. As depicted in Fig. 4A, the Mur-binding site I core region overlapped with the entire HutC-binding site and with significant portions of the MdrA- and IHF-binding regions. At higher Mur concentrations, the full Mur-protected region overlapped with all three TFs’ binding sites. Thus, similarly to that observed with the Fur4-binding site I, Mur could also repress virB expression through competition with the aforementioned transcriptional activators. Of note, the experimentally-determined Mur-binding site I core region encompassed the sequence matching the predicted Fur binding site-like motif in the virB1 promoter (Fig. 3B and 4A), supporting the applicability of our approach to identify TFs that impact the regulon of VjbR. In a further similarity to Fur4, the Mur-binding site III was also located in close proximity to the virB1 TSS, in this case encompassing both the -10 and -35 elements of the virB promoter. Furthermore, the Mur-binding site II almost entirely overlapped the VjbR-binding site (Fig. 4A). Therefore, taken together, our observations point to a robust mechanism of repression of virB gene expression, where two different Fur-family TFs redundantly compete with many direct transcriptional activators and also block the access of RNAP to the promoter.

In bab2_0131, the DNase I footprinting assays showed that Fur4 interacted with two operator sequences centered at positions -232 and -291 bp relative to the start of the coding sequence, designated as Fur4-binding sites I and II, respectively (Fig. 3C and 4B). As shown in Fig. 3C, the Fur4-binding site I region encompassed a 50-bp-long sequence. Similarly to what was observed with Mur in the virB1 promoter, the bab1_0131 Fur4-binding site I displayed a 16-bp central core region fully protected from DNase I digestion and two 19- and 15-bp flanking sequences with noticeable but not complete protection (Fig. 3C). On the other hand, the distal Fur4-binding site II was 22 bp long (Fig. 3C). Importantly, similarly to that observed in the virB1 promoter Mur-binding site I, the experimentally-determined Fur4-binding site I encompassed the predicted Fur-like binding site-like motif in the bab2_0131 promoter (Fig. 3C and 4B), providing further support for the suitability of our in silico approach. To study the possible regulatory mechanism by which Fur4 counteracted VjbR-mediated transcriptional activation of bab2_0131, we used DNase I footprinting to determine the precise location of the VjbR-binding site in this promoter region, which led to the identification of a 40-bp VjbR-protected sequence centered at position -217 (Fig. 3D). As depicted in Fig. 4B, the proximal Fur4-binding site overlapped 30 bp of the VjbR-protected region, supporting a model of regulation by direct competition, in which metal-bound Fur4 displaces VjbR from its binding site, thus repressing bab2_0131 gene expression.

To gain further insight on dual Fur4- and Mur-mediated regulation of gene expression, we also analyzed the TF-binding site arrangements of the fur4 promoter. In this case, our DNase I footprinting analyses revealed that Fur4 interacted with a 25-bp long binding site centered at position -277 relative to the start of the coding sequence (Fig. 3E and 4C). In contrast, Mur exhibited three binding sites centered at positions -193, -238, and -98 (Fig. 3F), designated as I, II, and III, respectively, also based on their apparent hierarchy of binding affinities. Similarly to what was observed in virB1, in the fur4 promoter, it was Mur, not Fur4, that displayed a binding site (site II) encompassing the Fur-like binding motif. Moreover, the Mur-binding site with higher apparent binding affinity (site I) completely encompassed the Mur-box previously predicted in silico by Rodionov et al. (2006) (Fig. 4C). Although the precise location of the bab1_0393 TSS is still unknown, previous RNA-seq sequence data suggest that this fur4 homolog has a long 5’-untranslated region (see Supplementary Fig. S2). Based on the positions of the Fur4- and Mur-binding sites I and II relative to the potential TSS position, our observations point to a mechanism of repression of fur4 expression through blocking the access of the RNAP holoenzyme in response to increased levels of Fe and/or Mn. Similarly to that observed in the virB1 and bab2_0131 promoters, the latter results indicate that the fur4 promoter is also under the direct control of two Fe- and Mn-responsive TFs, reinforcing the notion that a robust metal-dependent repression of gene expression is of importance for the biology of Brucella.

Determination of Fur4- and Mur-binding sequence consensuses

Experimentally determined DNase I-protected regions from this and other studies were examined for conserved Fur4- and Mur-binding motifs using the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) algorithm36. As shown in Fig. 5A, this approach led to the identification of an 11-bp Fur4-box motif whose consensus sequence (TTATAAADCAA; D=A/G/T) closely resembled the inverted repeats of the heptameric (7-1-7) palindromic Fur-family peroxide stress regulator PerR-box of Bacillus subtilis (TTATAATNATTATAA)37. Moreover, comparison of the obtained Fur4-box logo with databases of known binding sites of prokaryotic TFs yielded a first match with significant similarity to the binding site of a Fur family regulator, the Pseudomonas protegens Zur ortholog (E-value 1.55e-1). Notably, in the bab2_0131 promoter Fur4-binding site I, the Fur4-box motif aligned with the center of the DNase I-protected region and overlapped the in silico-predicted Fur-binding site-like motif (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In other Fur4-binding sites, however, the Fur4-box-matched sequences were located near the edges of the DNase I-protected regions (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

Within the experimentally determined Mur-binding sites, our approach led to the identification of a 20-bp Mur-box motif including a 15-bp palindromic structure similar to the heptameric (7-1-7) symmetry of the binding sites of B. subtilis Fur family transcriptional regulators (Fig. 5B). The palindromic sequence derived from the Mur-box logo corresponds precisely to the central region of the Mur-binding site previously determined by DNase I footprinting in the Brucella mntH promoter28. Moreover, comparison of this Mur-box logo with databases of known binding sites of prokaryotic TFs revealed significant similarity (E-value 3.38e-2) to the Salmonella enterica Fur-binding site. Furthermore, manual examination of the Mur-box motif also revealed a remarkable similarity to the palindromic Rhizobiales Mur-box predicted by Rodionov et al. (2006)33. Indeed, the MEME algorithm placed the most significant Brucella Mur-box hit precisely at the previously predicted location of the Mur-binding site in the fur4 promoter33 (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Of note, all hits determined by MEME located the Mur-box near the center of the Mur DNase I-protected regions, suggesting high resolution and predictive performance (Supplementary Fig. S3A-B).

Fur4 and Mur modulate intracellular virulence gene expression of Brucella

Thus far, we have demonstrated that in bacteria incubated under low-nutrient, acidic conditions, Mur and/or Fur4 counteract VjbR-mediated activation of specific target genes. To assess the potential role of these Fur family TFs in the life cycle of Brucella, we analyzed intracellular survival and replication of the isogenic ∆mur and ∆fur4 single mutants, as well as the ∆fur4 ∆mur double mutant strain in J774 macrophages. To this end, we first performed cell infection assays using bacteria grown in a nutrient-rich medium (TSB). Under these conditions, all mutant strains were able to replicate intracellularly at 24 and 48 h p.i. (Supplementary Fig. S4). Given that the addition of Fe or Mn is required to fully evaluate the regulatory phenotypes of the mur and fur4 mutants (Fig. 2), we further examined their role in intracellular survival of Brucella by infecting J774 macrophages with bacteria pre-treated with (iron-replete) or without (iron-limiting) Fe under nutrient-poor acidic conditions. As shown in Fig. 6, under iron-limiting conditions, all strains exhibited an intracellular behavior similar to that of bacteria grown in TSB. Under iron-replete conditions, however, the intracellular survival of the wild-type strain and both single mutants was severely compromised at 24 h p.i., whereas the double deletion mutant was unaffected by Fe and efficiently replicated intracellularly (Fig. 6). Of note, all strains displayed similar CFU counts at 5 h p.i. regardless of prior incubation with Fe, suggesting that the observed phenotypes were not due to reduced bacterial internalization into J774 macrophages but to defects in overcoming host cell defense mechanisms. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the Δmur, Δfur4 and wild-type phenotypes may reflect a direct involvement of Fur4 and Mur in the control of the expression of virulence genes necessary for intracellular survival such as the virB operon, identified in this study as a direct target of both Fur family TFs. According to this assumption, under iron-replete conditions, the intracellular induction of virB expression may have been impaired due to Fe-dependent repression of promoter activity by either Fur4, Mur, or both, leading to a reduced bacterial survival of the wild-type strain and both single-deletion mutants.

To examine the potential role of Fur4 and Mur in regulation of intracellular virB expression, wild-type and ∆fur4 ∆mur deletion mutant strains harboring a plasmid with a virB1 promoter-GFP transcriptional fusion were cultured under standard laboratory growth conditions (TSB at 37 °C with agitation) and used to infect J774 macrophages. Subsequently, at different times p.i., the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the GFP signal derived from individual intracellular bacteria was quantified in fluorescence microscopy images (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B, following internalization, strain B. abortus 2308 exhibited an increase in virB1 promoter activity that peaked at 15 h p.i. and then dropped abruptly, maintaining basal expression levels until 48 h p.i. This pattern was consistent with the wild-type intracellular expression profile previously observed using plasmid-based transcriptional fusions to lacZ16, supporting the approach used in this study to measure promoter activity from intracellular bacteria. As shown in Fig. 7B, the ∆fur4 ∆mur strain exhibited a markedly altered pattern of virB expression since, unlike the sharp peak observed in the wild-type strain, it displayed a gradual, delayed increase in virB1 promoter activity that peaked at 20 h p.i. and remained high by 24 h p.i., eventually decreasing to wild-type levels at 48 h p.i. These results indicate that Fur4 and Mur affect the induction dynamics of virB expression during the first stages of the intracellular infection, but most importantly, the ∆fur4 ∆mur double mutant strain lost the ability to rapidly turn off virB1 promoter activity after induction, demonstrating the involvement of Fur4 and Mur in regulation of intracellular expression of an essential virulence determinant of Brucella.

Discussion

The discovery and functional characterization of T4SS, its effectors, and other virulence factors of Brucella have greatly enhanced our understanding of how this bacterium subverts host cell vacuolar trafficking and establishes the intracellular replication niche2. However, there are still gaps in our knowledge of the mechanisms that coordinate the precise spatiotemporal expression of the virulence genes involved in such processes. Here, we aimed to investigate the possible interplay between VjbR and additional TFs participating in the control of the Brucella virulence gene expression network. Using in silico, genetic and molecular approaches, we identified Fur4 and Mur as components of a regulatory network that overlaps with the VjbR regulon, counteracting VjbR-mediated activation of specific target genes in response to Fe or Mn. Moreover, our findings revealed that these two TFs are involved in downregulation of intracellular Brucella virulence gene expression.

This study was prompted by the discovery of a conserved Fur-like binding site motif in genomic regions that lacked conserved VjbR-binding sequences but still exhibited VjbR binding in a prior ChIP-seq analysis25 (see Supplementary File S1). Originally, we hypothesized that this subset of VjbR target promoters would reveal binding sites for transcriptional activators that cooperate with VjbR to enhance its binding to low-affinity operator sites. However, our approach uncovered transcriptional repressors instead. We attribute this unexpected result to the fact that the previous VjbR ChIP-seq study was conducted without the addition of Fe or Mn, conditions under which Fur4 and Mur were not activated to bind to DNA and counteract VjbR-mediated induction of gene expression. Thus, despite the initial premise of our strategy, here we serendipitously found a conserved repressor-binding motif that was statistically enriched in a smaller subset of sequences compared to the complete list of VjbR binding regions identified by ChIP-seq.

The Fur-like binding site motif identified in this work has proven highly effective in predicting genes regulated by metal-dependent TFs, as all four tested candidate promoters were downregulated in response to Mn and/or Fe. Moreover, DNase I footprinting analysis further validated this motif by revealing several matching binding site sequences for either Fur4 or Mur (Fig. 3). When considering the Fur4- and Mur-boxes determined from experimentally-defined binding site sequences, they exhibited striking similarity to the previously reported B. subtilis PerR- and Rhizobiales Mur-binding motifs, respectively (Fig. 5). Of note, among the 170 Fur4-box and 482 Mur-box motif occurrences detected in the B. abortus 2308 genome (p-value cutoff of 1.0e-4), 45% and 20%, respectively, were intergenic, significantly exceeding the 12% expected by chance and suggesting they may be functional. Therefore, although the actual number and identity of direct Mur and Fur4 target genes remain to be determined by further genome-wide analyses, our observations suggest that the Mur and Fur4 regulons may affect the expression of a high number of targets. Accordingly, we are currently assessing the utility of the newly identified Fur4- and Mur-boxes to uncover novel genes regulated by these TFs and to explore the overlap between metal homeostasis and virulence gene regulatory networks in Brucella.

Among the regulators we uncovered as TFs counteracting VjbR-mediated activation, Mur has a well-defined role in metal homeostasis of Brucella. Specifically, Mur acts as a transcriptional repressor of mntH, which encodes a high-affinity Mn transporter necessary for intracellular survival and virulence in mice38. In contrast, the Fur homolog Fur4 encoded by bab1_0393 had not been characterized previously. Given several puzzling aspects of the structure and function of Fur4, categorizing this TF as one of the known canonical Fur-type regulators poses a significant challenge. Among the four Brucella Fur paralogs, the predicted Fur4 protein product shares the highest similarity (46% identity) to the heme-binding iron-responsive regulator Irr39. Moreover, Fur4 displays even higher similarity to Irr homologs from species of other bacterial genera such as Bradyrhizobium japonicum and Rhizobium leguminosarum, with identities of 48% and 49%, respectively. However, despite these sequence similarities, Fur4 and Irr exhibit functional differences regarding their responsiveness to Mn and Fe. While Fur4 represses transcription in response to either of these transition metals (Fig. 2), Mn stabilizes Irr binding to its operator site and, conversely, Fe induces heme-mediated dissociation from DNA and subsequent degradation of Irr40,41. In terms of structure, Fur4 and Irr differ in the amino acid sequences of their predicted DNA-binding α-helix. While rhizobial Irr homologs, as well as Irr of Brucella, share a highly conserved SLATVYN motif42, Fur4 exhibits an SVATVYN motif identical to that of the B. subtilis PerR homolog, which is consistent with the similarity observed between the B. abortus Fur4-box and B. subtilis PerR-box (Fig. 5). In view of the resemblance between Fur4 and PerR in both DNA-sequence binding motifs and responsiveness to Mn and Fe, we initially speculated that Fur4 may function in Brucella as a peroxide stress regulator. However, attempts by our group and others (R.M. Roop II, personal communication) to demonstrate its possible role as a hydrogen peroxide-sensing TF were unsuccessful. Therefore, while it cannot be ruled out that Fur4 senses a yet unknown signal, the evidence collected thus far suggests that its function is circumscribed to sensing and transducing changes in the concentration of Mn and Fe, redundantly with Mur.

Recent studies have revealed that Brucella actively modulates cellular iron levels in infected macrophages to prevent the production of reactive oxygen species and avoid oxidative stress-induced damage43. This is in part achieved by the secretion of the ferritin-like protein Dps, which reduces the host cell labile Fe pool within the first 12 hours p.i. Moreover, innate immunity mechanisms of mammalian hosts further restrict the access of Brucella to transition metals44. For instance, the natural resistance-associated macrophage protein (NRAMP1) translocates Fe and Mn divalent cations out of the intraphagosomal lumen, depriving pathogens of these essential micronutrients45. Following trafficking of Brucella through the endocytic pathway, once the replicative niche is established within an endoplasmic reticulum-like compartment, the bacterium gains access to the nutrients necessary for bacterial growth. Different experimental approaches from this and previous studies showed that after reaching the maximum intracellular virB expression level, the Brucella virB genes undergo an abrupt shutdown that takes place concomitantly with the onset of bacterial replication10,16. Here, we demonstrate that two Mn- and Fe-responsive TFs mediate downregulation of intracellular virB expression after the peak of virB1 promoter activation. Collectively, our findings support a model where an Mn- and Fe-dependent regulatory mechanism modulates Brucella virulence gene expression in response to fluctuations in the availability of metals during the transition from a nutrient-deprived acidified phagolysosomal-like environment to replication-permissive conditions. While this hypothesis is in line with the observation that pre-loading bacteria with Fe impaired intracellular survival of the wild-type, mur and fur4 single mutant strains, while not affecting the double mutant (Fig. 6), a limitation of our study is that we did not measure intracellular virB promoter activity in the presence of metals. Therefore, further work will be required to demonstrate that Fur4 and Mur sense changes of Fe and/or Mn concentrations during intracellular trafficking of Brucella.

Besides virulence-related loci, evidence of Fur4-mediated downregulation of fur4 and bab2_0131 in in vitro-cultured bacteria (Fig. 2) suggests that expression of these genes may also be controlled by Fur4 intracellularly. This would imply that the Brucella intracellular adaptation process requires that Fur4 function and PAP2-mediated maintainment of peptidoglycan and cell envelope integrity are turned off when they are no longer required within the replicative niche. Furthermore, the identification of Fur4-binding site motifs in promoter regions of many additional VjbR targets reinforces the idea that Fur4 plays a leading role in intracellular regulation of a broader spectrum of gene functions.

Notably, the three Fur4 target promoters analyzed so far are also controlled by a second Mn- and Fe-responsive regulator (i.e., Mur in the virB1 and fur4 promoters; and, in the case of bab2_0131, a hypothetical TF that we still could not identify despite attempts with different molecular and genetic approaches). In line with these observations, the occurrence of Fur-binding-site-like, Mur, and Fur4 boxes determined in this work across the B. abortus 2308 genome suggests that the list of Mn- and Fe-responsive promoters with double regulation may be longer. Taken together, the findings of this work raise the question of whether functional redundancy is a required trait in Brucella for Mn- and Fe-dependent downregulation of intracellular gene expression. In this regard, other bacteria have been shown to have redundant paralogs of the Fur family, including PerRA and PerRB of Leptospira interrogans46, the three PerR paralogs of Bacillus licheniformis47, IrrA and IrrB of Rhizobium leguminosarum48, and FurA1-3 of Mycobacterium smegmatis49. Some of these paralogs are proposed to have different sensitivities to stress signals such as H2O2 or to different concentrations of metals perceived within the host, leading to fine-tuning regulation of the expression of different cohorts of virulence genes. Likewise, dual regulation by Fur4 and an additional Mn- and Fe-responsive TF could provide a way to fine-tune intracellular gene expression in response to changing concentrations of Mn and Fe, and/or Mn/Fe ratios throughout the intracellular trafficking of Brucella within the host. Moreover, dual regulation might also reflect the importance of a robust intracellular transcriptional response to fluctuations in Mn and Fe during the infection process of Brucella.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and media

Brucella abortus 2308 wild-type or the isogenic deletion mutants, B. abortus Δmur, B. abortus Δfur4, and B. abortus Δfur4 Δmur strains were cultured in Tryptic soy broth (TSB) 5 μg/ml nalidixic acid, or incubated in MM1 medium (33 mM KH2PO4, 60.3 mM K2HPO4, and 0.1% yeast extract) pH 5.5 in the presence of 5 mM urocanic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in a rotary shaker (250 r.p.m.), with or without the addition of 100 μM FeCl3 or 100 μM MnCl2. E. coli S17 strains were cultured in LB broth at 37°C in a rotary shaker at 250 rpm. Kanamycin was added (50 μg/ml) for culture of strains harbouring pK18mob-lacZ or pPROBE’-PvirB-gfp[ASV]. All manipulations of Brucella strains were performed at the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory facility at Fundación Instituto Leloir.

Construction of plasmids

All the primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Promoter regions from loci bab2_0131, bab2_0738, fur4 and virB1 were amplified by PCR using primers pbab2_0131fw and pbab2_0131rv, pbab2_0738fw and pbab2_0738rv, promfur4_fw and promfur4_rv, and pvu144 and pvirdown, respectively, with Pfu DNA polymerase (Productos Bio-Lógicos) and B. abortus 2308 genomic DNA as template. The resulting PCR products were cloned in plasmid pGEM-T easy (Promega), generating plasmids pGEM-T-Pbab2_0131, pGEM-T-Pbab2_0738, pGEM-T-Pfur4, and pGEM-T-PvirB.

In order to generate transcriptional fusions to lacZ, inserts from the aforementioned plasmids were excised with BamHI, EcoRI, or both restriction enzymes, and cloned in the same restriction sites of plasmid pK18mob-lacZ, thus generating plasmids pK18mob-Pbab2_0131-lacZ, pK18mob-Pbab2_0738-lacZ, and pK18mob-Pfur4-lacZ, respectively.

Plasmid pPROBE’-PvirB-gfp[ASV] was generated by subcloning a 450 pb EcoRI fragment from pBluescript-PvirB 16 to pPROBE’-gfp[ASV]50 in the same restriction sites.

To generate plasmid pK18mob-sacB-Δfur4, two PCRs were carried out using Pfu DNA polymerase (Productos Bio-Lógicos), genomic DNA of B. abortus 2308 as the template, and primers mut_fur4_fwA and mut_fur4_rvA or mut_fur4_fwB and mut_fur4_rvB. Both PCR products, corresponding to two 500-bp flanking regions of the fur4 open reading frame (ORF), were annealed and used as templates for a PCR performed with primers mut_fur4_fwA and mut_fur4_rvB. The product was digested with BamHI and cloned into the kanamycin-resistant plasmid pK18mob-sacB. Equivalenty, plasmid pK18mob-sacB-Δmur was created using primers Fw_mur_1 and Rv_Mur_com1 or Fw_mur_com2 and Rv_mur_2 to generate flanking regions to mur ORF, followed by a PCR with primers Fw_mur_1 and Rv_mur_2 to generate the desired insert. The product was digested with BamHI and cloned into plasmid pK18mob-sacB.

Plasmid pDEST565-His-GST-Mur was generated as follows. First, a PCR was carried out using Pfu DNA polymerase (Productos Bio-Lógicos), genomic DNA of B. abortus 2308 as the template, and primers Fw_mur_prot and Rv_mur_prot. The resulting product was used as template for a second PCR, using primers fw_gateway and rv_gateway. As a result, a PCR product consisting of mur ORF, flanqued by attB sites and a TEV protease restriction site was obtained. This product was cloned into plasmid pDEST565 (Addgene plasmid 11520, donated by Dominic Esposito) following standard Gateway cloning procedures, thus obtaining pDEST565-His-GST-Mur. The same protocol was followed to generate pDEST565-His-GST-Fur4, using primers Fw_fur4_prot and Rv_fur4_prot in the first PCR reaction. All restriction enzymes and T4 ligase were purchased from Promega.

Construction of mutants

B. abortus ∆mur and B. abortus ∆fur4 were obtained by transfer of plasmids pK18mob-sacB-∆mur or pK18mob-sacB-∆fur4 to B. abortus 2308 wild-type by biparental conjugation with E. coli S17. Kanamicyn-resistant colonies were selected as single-homologous recombinants. Single colonies were cultured overnight in rich medium (TSB) without antibiotics and plated on TSB agar containing 10% sucrose, in order to select bacteria in which plasmid pK18mob-sacB-∆mur or pK18mob-sacB-∆fur4 was excised by single homologous recombination. Single colonies were plated on TSB agar or TSB agar supplemented with kanamicyn. Kanamicyn-sensitive colonies were screened by PCR for deletion of mur (using primers Fw_mur_1 and Rv_mur_2) or fur4 (using primers mut_fur4_fwA and mut_fur4_rvB). B. abortus ∆fur4 ∆mur strain was obtained following the same procedure by transfering plasmid pK18mob-sacB-∆mur to B. abortus ∆fur4.

Construction of strains containing single chromosomal transcriptional fusions

Plasmid pK18mob-PvirB-lacZ, pK18mob-Pbab2_0131-lacZ, pK18mob-Pbab2_0738-lacZ, or pK18mob-Pfur4-lacZ was transferred by biparental conjugation into strain B. abortus 2308, B. abortus ∆mur, B. abortus ∆fur4, or B. abortus ∆fur4 ∆mur. Kanamycin-resistant colonies were selected as single-homologous recombinants.

β-Galactosidase activity measurements

Bacteria were cultured in TSB, harvested during the exponential phase of growth (OD600 = 0.5), washed once with PBS and resuspended in MM1 medium pH 5.5 in the presence of 5 mM urocanic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) with or without the addition of 100 μM FeCl3 or 100 μM MnCl2. After 24 h, β-Gal. activity was determined in whole bacterial cells as described previously16, and it was expressed in Miller units by the following formula: [A420/(volume x OD600 x t(min))] x 100.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

Recombinant Mur and Fur4 proteins were prepared as follows. Plasmid pDEST565-His-GST-Mur or pDEST565-His-GST-Fur4 was transfered to E. coli BL21 (DE3). Bacteria were grown at 37°C in LB medium until exponential phase (OD600 = 0.8). Subsequently, the cultures were induced with 0.1 mM IPTG and incubated over-night at 20°C. Bacteria were then harvested, suspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.6], 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], 0.5 M NaCl), and disrupted by sonication. After centrifugation, supernatant was loaded into a Hi-Trap nickel-chelating column (Amersham Biosciences). After a wash with buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.6], 0.5 M NaCl), the column was eluted with a linear gradient of buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.6], 0.5 M NaCl, 0.5 M imidazole). The eluates were analyzed by 12.5% SDS-PAGE, and the fractions containing His-GST-Mur or His-GST-Fur4 (purity near 75%) were pooled and dialyzed over-night against buffer A at 4 ºC. In order to remove His-GST tags, 1 mM DTT was added and samples were treated with TEV protease (mass ratio 1:30 between TEV and protein samples) for 3 h at room temperature. Dialysis against buffer A was repeated to remove DTT from samples. After dialysis, the samples were reloaded onto a HiTrap nickel-chelating column, and the previously described purification procedure was repeated to isolate Mur and Fur4 proteins (purity ~90%). Recombinant VjbR protein was prepared as described previously18.

DNase I footprinting

6-FAM labeled probes were obtained by PCR using Taq Pegasus DNA polymerase (Productos Bio-Lógicos) with primer T7-FAM and pvu144 (for PvirB probe), pbab2_0131fwshort (for Pbab2_0131 probe), or promfur4_fw (for Pfur4 probe). Plasmids pGEM-T-PvirB, pGEM-T-Pbab2_0131, or pGEM-T-Pfur4 were used as templates, respectively. Probes were then purified in native polyacrylamide gels. 6-FAM labeled probes were incubated with recombinant Mur, Fur4 or VjbR proteins at the indicated concentrations in DNA-binding buffer [15 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM DTT, 10 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA), 30 mM KCl] with adjusted NaCl concentrations of 40 mM for VjbR, 10 mM for Mur and 90 mM for Fur4 reactions. For Mur and Fur4, incubations were performed in the presence of 100 μM MnCl2. After incubation of the binding reaction mixtures for 20 min, MgCl2 and CaCl2 concentrations were adjusted to 1.5 and 0.5 mM, respectively, and each reaction mixture was incubated with RQ1 DNase I (Promega) for 4 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped by the addition of stop solution [EGTA 30mM (pH 8.0)]. Digested products were extracted by phenol-chloroform, ethanol precipitated and resuspended in H2O. DNA sequencing reactions were carried out using the Thermo Sequenase Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), primer T7-FAM and the corresponding plasmids pGEM-T-PvirB, pGEM-T-Pbab2_0131, or pGEM-T-Pfur4 as template. 6-FAM labeled DNA fragments were analysed by the Fragment Analysis service provided by Macrogen. Electropherograms were visualized using PeakScanner 2.0 software.

Cell infection assays

Murine macrophage-like J774 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM L-glutamine. Cells seeded in 24-well plates (105 cells per well) were inoculated with 1 ml of cell culture medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM L-glutamine without antibiotics) containing bacteria at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 (for intracelular replication experiments) or 700 (for intracelular activity of the virB1 promoter assays). Multiwell plates were centrifuged for 10 min at 141 g at room temperature and placed in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 ºC. After 30 min, cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) and incubated with cell culture medium containing gentamicin (100 μg/ml) and streptomycin (50 μg/ml) to eliminate extracellular bacteria.

Intracelular replication experiments

At different times p.i., cells were washed three times with PBS and treated for 10 min with 1 ml of 0.1% Triton X-100 in deonized sterile water. The number of intracellular viable bacteria was determined as CFU/ml by serial dilution and plating on tryptic soy agar supplemented with antibiotics as needed.

Intracellular activity of the virB1 promoter

At different times p.i., cells were washed three times with PBS and treated for 15 min with 1 ml of paraformaldehyde 4% (w/v) solution in PBS at 37 ºC. Fixed cells in 12 mm coverslips were then washed three times with PBS and stained with DAPI (Invitrogen) prior to mounting on microscope slides with BacLight mounting oil (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were then observed using a Zeiss Axio Observer 3 Inverted Microscope, using software Zen 2.3 for image acquisition. For each biological replicate, GFP fluorescence intensity was measured at all time points using fixed exposure time and gain parameters. Images were analyzed with software ImageJ to determine mean flourescence intensity (MFI) of 100 individual bacteria for each time point and strain. The background fluorescence for each image was determined by calculating the mean pixel intensity in cell-free regions, which was then subtracted from the individual bacteria fluorescence measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software Inc). Ratio paired t-test, one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Mann-Whitney U test were used as indicated. All analyses were two-tailed with α = 0.05 as a threshold for significance. For t-test and ANOVA data normality was assesed with D’Agostino-Pearson test. Where statistical significance is indicated with an asterisk, the p-value is provided in the legend.
