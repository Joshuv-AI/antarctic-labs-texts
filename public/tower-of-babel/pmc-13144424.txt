Interventions Targeting the Beef Feedlot Environment to Control Antimicrobial Resistance: A Mathematical Modelling Study
Authors: Wendy Anne Beauvais, Getahun Ejeta Agga, Terrance Arthur, John William Schmidt, Rachel Jennings, Renata Ivanek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionTo address concerns about livestock as a source of antimicrobial‐resistant bacteria and genes, there have been many recent efforts worldwide to study prescribing practices, optimal antibiotic use, and alternatives to antibiotic use. However, there is empirical evidence supporting the persistence of antimicrobial resistance genes at high densities in cattle pens for at least 2 years after cattle were removed.MethodsWe describe a mathematical modelling framework to predict and explore the dynamics of antimicrobial‐resistant enteric bacteria in food‐producing animals and their immediate environments. Using the difference equation based compartmental modelling framework, we algebraically derive a formula for the relative rate of growth of antimicrobial‐resistant enteric bacteria in the environment (R AMR ).ResultsWe demonstrate that R AMR > 1 (i.e., growth) of tetracycline‐resistant Escherichia coli in feedlot environments can occur under a range of plausible conditions, even in the absence of antimicrobial use in the feedlot cattle. Our model can reproduce data observed under field conditions showing rapid growth of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in the environment despite no antimicrobials being used. Finally, we demonstrate that generic hygiene measures such as scraping pen floors are likely to reduce the density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in the farm environment considerably, especially in cold climates.ConclusionsFarm environments such as beef cattle feedlots may be conducive to persistence or even growth of antimicrobial resistant bacteria under a wide range of plausible conditions, even in the absence of antimicrobial use. The system may be quite resilient, and even stringent cleaning will likely not be sufficient to eliminate resistant bacteria from the environment in some climates, especially where freeze–thaw cycles are uncommon.

Introduction

To address concerns about livestock as a source of antimicrobial‐resistant bacteria and genes, there have been many recent efforts worldwide to study prescribing practices, optimal antibiotic use and alternatives to antibiotic use. Further, there have been policies introduced in many countries such as the Veterinary Feed Directive (VFD) implemented in the USA (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2021) that have significantly restricted the use of medically important antimicrobials such as tetracyclines in the feed and water of food producing animals. Upon implementation of the VFD, the use of medically important antimicrobials administered in the feed and water of livestock for the purpose of growth promotion was prohibited in the USA. The aim of such policies is to reduce antimicrobial selection pressure on bacteria in livestock, to ultimately reduce the likelihood of new antimicrobial resistant strains arising, and possibly decrease the prevalence of existing antimicrobial resistant strains.

However, it is not certain what impact current or more stringent antibiotic use policies will have on the presence of tetracycline‐resistant bacteria in farm environments. There is empirical evidence supporting the persistence of antimicrobial resistance genes at high densities in cattle pens for at least 2 years after cattle were removed (Agga et al. 2019). Additionally, tetracycline‐resistant Escherichia coli can be found in very high densities in animals not being treated with tetracyclines (Agga et al. 2016; Tamminen et al. 2011; Khachatryan et al. 2004). We hypothesize that relevant explanations could include: (i) bacterial population dynamics for example, environmental conditions conducive to prolonged bacterial survival rates combined with a relatively low fitness cost; (ii) residual effects of prior use of tetracyclines in the animals (before arrival at the farm); (iii) indirect exposure to tetracyclines due to use in nearby animals; (iv) tetracyclines naturally produced by other bacteria in the environment; (iv) co‐selection pressure via use of heavy metals or antibiotics other than tetracyclines (Agga et al. 2021); and/or (v) the presence of an environmental niche which confers a relative selection advantage to resistant bacteria, even in the absence of tetracyclines through an unknown mechanism. Tetracyclines also have a relatively long half‐life in the environment (Arikan et al. 2006), which may extend their residual effect long after the treatment has ended. One study found that the half‐life of oxytetracycline in manure was 56 days (Arikan et al. 2006).

The feedlot pen environment remains a relatively under‐explored target for interventions to reduce the presence of antimicrobial resistance although there is accumulating evidence of the role of the environment in the persistence of antimicrobial resistant pathogens (Jimenez et al. 2023). Although previous studies of E. coli O157:H7 transmission on cattle farms have identified environmental conditions including temperature as important drivers of incidence in animals (Wang et al. 2014; Gautam et al. 2011, 2015), previous mathematical models of tetracycline resistant E. coli in cattle have so far focused on processes within the cattle and not the environment (Volkova et al. 2012, 2013, 2017; Cazer et al. 2017; Baker et al. 2016, 2022; Arya et al. 2021). A recent review of mathematical modelling studies identified environmental modelling studies as an under‐studied area (Niewiadomska et al. 2019). There is therefore a need to develop a theoretical framework to quantify the role of the environmental reservoir in driving antimicrobial resistance, particularly on farms, and to explore potential interventions to address antimicrobial resistance, in addition to reducing antimicrobial use.

The objectives of this study were to use tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in feedlot beef cattle as an example to: (i) elucidate the role of the pen environment in the persistence of antimicrobial‐resistant enteric bacteria on livestock farms; and (ii) explore the hypothesis that generic hygiene measures such as scraping manure from pens more regularly can contribute to reducing the presence of antimicrobial‐resistant enteric bacteria on livestock farms.

Materials and Methods

Overview

In part I of the study, we constructed a compartmental model based on ordinary differential equations to explore the dynamics of tetracycline‐resistant and tetracycline‐sensitive generic E. coli within the ingesta of a single pen of feedlot beef cattle and in the pen environment. For simplification, we modelled tetracycline resistance in E. coli as being conferred by a single resistance gene, although in reality there are many different tetracycline‐resistance genes (Roberts and Schwarz 2016). In part II, we used difference equations to calculate parameter cut‐off values for increase versus decrease of the density of resistant E. coli in the pen environment. In part III, we used difference equations and fitted the parameter values to data from the control pens of no tetracycline use in a previously published trial of experimental feedlot cattle (Agga et al. 2016) in order to predict the effectiveness of manure management to reduce tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in the pen environment. The model was developed and analysed in R software version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2020) using the “smfsb” package version 1.3 (Wilkinson 2018).

Ethical Approval Statement

No ethical approval was required for this work because all work was conducted in silico using secondary data.

Part I

Model Structure and Equations

A model framework was developed to investigate the dynamics of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli (B r ) and tetracycline‐sensitive E. coli (B s ) in a single beef cattle feedlot pen from day 0 (immediately prior to the introduction of feedlot cattle) until day 126 post‐introduction of cattle, in a closed pen population, to replicate the control pens in a published intervention trial (Agga et al. 2016). In both the published study, and in our model, the cattle did not receive tetracycline either in‐feed or as injection during the study/simulation period. As shown in Figure 1, the differential equation model consists of two pen environment compartments, each consisting of generic E. coli (colony forming units per gram [CFU/g]) that either carry a genetic element conferring tetracycline resistance (B r,e ) or do not carry any such genetic element (B s,e ). The pen environment is assumed to include feed, water, faeces, urine, and any other pen surface materials. E. coli within the feedlot cattle intestinal tract is also modelled as resistant to tetracycline (B r,c ) or tetracycline‐sensitive (B s,c ). We assume homogenous mixing of all animals and the E. coli in the environment; thus, the E. coli in the intestinal tract of all cattle is modelled as a single compartment. The model is deterministic that is, the effects of chance in transmission events and the role of stochastic fade‐out are assumed to be negligible. These model limitations were considered acceptable considering the animals in a pen are in very close contact and E. coli is known to be ubiquitous in the cattle intestinal tracts and in beef cattle feedlot pens.

Parameter Ranges and Initial Conditions

For the purposes of model sensitivity analysis, each parameter was assigned a Uniform uncertainty distribution with the ranges given in Table 2. The ranges were based on published scientific literature where possible and transformed to hourly rates. See also Appendix S1 named params_enviro_120221.xlsx.

Sensitivity of R AMR to Different Parameters

In order to determine the sensitivity of R AMR to different parameters, we calculated R AMR using Equation (5), according to different combinations of parameter values. For each parameter in turn, we fixed the parameter value at each decile of its uncertainty distribution (minimum, 10th, 20th…90th, maximum), and at each decile we generated 1000 combinations of values for the remaining parameters by sampling randomly from their distribution (using the “runif” function in R). The resulting values of R AMR were plotted against normalised parameter values using violin plots for comparison. See also Appendix S1 named Part2_WB_130421.pdf.

Part III

Dataset and Data Cleaning

In order to parameterize the model, a dataset from a controlled trial of feedlot beef cattle (Agga et al. 2016) was used (See Appendix S1 named experimental_data_enviro_WB_180520.xlsx and experimental_data_full_pens_WB_180520.xlsx). Briefly, five control pens of 30 cattle were followed from the day that the cattle were newly introduced to the feedlot, to 126 days post‐introduction. These cattle were not given any tetracycline during the trial (and any cattle that required antimicrobial treatment were removed from the pens), although some cattle had received antimicrobial treatment prior to the trial. Additionally, five empty pens of no cattle were followed over the same period for comparison. Samples of pen surface and cattle faeces were collected at five time‐points throughout the time‐period, with the first environmental sampling occurring immediately prior to cattle being introduced to the pens.

The trial data were used as follows. Firstly, the minimum limits of detection (LOD) and the limits of enumeration (LOE) were assigned based on two different tests that were performed, one that was quantitative and another with a lower limit of detection that was binary, as in the original publication (Agga et al. 2016). Counts below the LOD were imputed by randomly sampling a log10‐uniform distribution between the zero and the LOD. Counts between the LOD and the LOE were imputed by randomly sampling a log10‐uniform distribution between the LOD and LOE. The imputation was repeated three times to check that the mean counts at each sampling point remained constant. Secondly, faecal counts were in (Agga et al. 2016) expressed in units of log10 CFU/swab. In order to convert the counts to units of CFU/g, in the current study we repeated the collection of 20 faecal swabs using identical methods and calculated the mean weight in grams of faeces from each of the swabs (estimated as 0.37 g of faeces per faecal swab). Finally, we estimated the mean of the microbial counts at each time‐point, combining all five pens in each group (empty and populated), because the dynamics were similar in each pen.

Effects of Temperature and Nutrients

The model described in Part I was adapted to incorporate the effects of temperature and nutrients on E. coli dynamics as follows. The maximum specific growth rate was assumed to be linearly associated with ambient temperature above 4°C as has previously been modelled, using gradient, intercept and temperature parameters (table 2; Arikan et al. 2006). Daily mean temperature was recorded during the trial in (Agga et al. 2016) using methods that have been reported previously (Shulski et al. 2018). See also Appendix S1 named Weather_data_cleaned_150921_WB.xlsx.

We model pen environment contamination as a combination of two processes. The first process is the contamination determined by the parameter K e , which describes the maximum CFU/g that can be sustained in the environment for a given amount of nutrients defined by the frequency of scraping (or lack thereof). Specifically, K e is a constant that determines, through the logistic function (1 − f r )g e B r,e (1‐(∑B e ))/K e , to what density the resistant (B r,e ) (or non‐resistant (B s,e )) bacteria in the environment can grow. We scale K e at the level of the pen by calculating k e = (10 Ke )E, where E is the size of the pen environment. When scraping is performed, it removes nutrients for bacterial growth, which we reflect in a lower value of K e . For simplicity, this approach ignores K e fluctuations corresponding to the discrete events of faeces removal (through scraping) and instead we set K e to a constant value reflecting an average of K e values between scraping events.

In Part III, we introduce the second process, which defines environmental contamination through the impact of parameter N (CFU/pen/h). Specifically, N describes the addition of faeces to the environment due to faeces excreted by animals in the pen. In other words, N reflects how faeces accumulate in the pen and organic matter increases over time.

The two processes are combined in k e3 (Table 2). Calculated as k e3 = (10 Ke )E + Nt, k e3 describes the change in the pen carrying capacity over time t (in hours) due to both the limit in the logistic growth (i.e., K e impacted by hygiene) and hourly addition of faeces to the environment (i.e., N). See also Appendix S1 named Part3_equations_new_dates_130221.pdf.

At the start of the trial replicated in this model, animals were 5.6 months old and weighed 181.3 kg (Agga et al. 2016). Assuming a daily gain of 1.13 kg (2.5 lb), over the 126 day trial they each gained approximately 1.13*126 = 142.9 kg. Daily manure production per beef animal varies with ration, animal sex and animal weight, but in general, the amount of manure produced per day is approximately 6% of the animal's body weight (Araji and Stodick 1990). Thus, as animals in the trial nearly doubled in body weight, so did the manure they produced per day. To account for that, we replaced the linear k e3 carrying capacity with a quadratic k e3_slow = 10 Ke E + (3 N)/(2 T)t 2, where T = 3024 (i.e., maximum value of time in our model). k e3_slow cumulatively adds the same amount of faeces into the environment as k e3 but it does so at an accelerated rate, slow at the start of the trial when animals had a low weight and fast towards the end reflecting doubling of the added manure per day as animals doubled in weight. Additionally, we tested the impact of k e3_fast , modelled as a non‐scaled version of k e3_slow . k e3_fast = 10 Ke E + Nt 2 doubles the amount of faeces added to the environment every day, resulting in considerably more faeces added over 126 days than k e3 . Because of this, k e3_fast is not a fair comparison to k e3 , but it was considered to assess the impact of an extreme increase in N per unit time.

Model Fitting and Assessment of Model Validity

To improve the efficiency of the fitting process, the ordinary differential equations model was converted into a difference equation model with a time step of 1‐h. The model was also run with a smaller time‐step of 0.1 h to ensure that the model results were stable.

Model parameters were fit to populated pen data and empty pen data simultaneously using LSS (Least Sum of Squares method) using sequential Monte Carlo—Approximate Bayesian Computation (SMC‐ABC) (Wilkinson 2018) (See Appendix S1 named Basic_model_fitting_procedure.pdf and Full_model_fitting_procedure_WB.pdf). Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) is a simulation‐based algorithm which repeatedly randomly samples a set of parameter values and accepts or rejects them based on a criterion (in this case LSS). SMC‐ABC is more efficient than simple rejection ABC. Uniform priors were assigned for all parameters (with minimum and maximum values shown in Table 2). The algorithm was set to complete 10 steps of 20,000 iterations each. On completion of each step, the top 10% best fit iterations were weighted and passed on to the next step. The perturbation parameter in the SMC‐ABC algorithm was randomly drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 0.005, initially. Subsequently, we explored a range of larger values of standard deviation to ensure that the parameter space was fully explored. We fixed the starting conditions (density of bacteria in each of four compartments in Figure 1) to the data at the first time‐point of the field study, and fit the model using the remaining four time‐points (4 compartments × 4 time‐points for both empty and populated pens).

We conducted validation as follows. First, we used the fitted parameter values to plot predicted specific maximum growth rates of E. coli against a range of ambient temperatures and compared the results to observed values from the field study (Agga et al. 2016). Finally, we used the fitted parameterized model to predict the dynamics of E. coli at a range of constant temperatures and compared the findings to observations from the field study (Agga et al. 2016; See Appendix S1 named Figures_230122.pdf).

Results

Model Exploration

The model predicted positive initial relative growth of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli populations in the feedlot environment (R AMR > 1) under a wide range of plausible parameter values (Figure 2). However, the model predictions of R AMR ranged widely from a rapid decline to an increase of up to approximately 2.5 times per hour, depending on parameter values. The density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in the environment was predicted to increase most frequently when there was a high carrying capacity for E. coli in the environment (K e ) or a low initial density of non‐resistant E. coli in the environment. The predicted value of R AMR was particularly sensitive to the maximum specific growth rate of E. coli in the environment (g e ).

Model Fitting and Validity

The fitted values converged after 20,000 iterations (Table 2). The ABC‐SMC fitting algorithm was run three times. The replicated results were similar, confirming that the fitting process had converged. The fitted model accurately reproduced the dynamics of tetracycline‐resistant and tetracycline‐sensitive E. coli observed under field conditions in feedlot cattle and their pen environment at 5 time points (Days 0, 14, 36, 84 and 126 post‐arrival of the cattle onto the farm) in a controlled trial (Table 2, Figure 3a,b) (Agga et al. 2016). However, the model tended to overestimate the growth of E. coli in empty pens (Figure 3b), suggesting that the model did not fully account for some effects of cattle on the environmental conditions, although the model accounted for a linear increase in nutrients deposited into the environment via faeces (via N in k e3 ). As shown in Figure 3c, the fitted model predicts that the specific maximum growth rate increases by 0.068 per 1°C increase in ambient temperature over 4°C, which is significantly higher than the value reported by Wang et al. (2014), based on E. coli O157:H7 growth rates in controlled laboratory studies. Figure 3d shows that according to the fitted model, the density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in each compartment rapidly approaches the carrying capacity at 30°C, but rapidly decays at 0°C.

Sub‐sets of models fit without temperature‐dependence or nutrient‐dependence gave slightly poorer fit to the observed field data but yielded similar conclusions about the effectiveness of hygiene measures to reduce levels of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli .

Simulation of Hygiene Interventions

We simulated hygiene interventions by changing the carrying capacity (K e ) parameter in the environment. The model predicted efficacy of hygiene interventions to reduce the density of resistant E. coli in both the pen environment and the cattle ingesta (Figure 4). When the carrying capacity at time zero was reduced from the fitted value of 6.8 log10 CFU/g to 4 log10 CFU/g, the density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli at day 126 was reduced from approximately 5 log10 CFU/g in the environment to approximately 2.5 log10 CFU/g. When the carrying capacity was reduced further to 1 log10 CFU/g, the density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli at day 126 was reduced to < 1 CFU/g. The trend in density of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in cattle ingesta was similar to that in the environment. To interpret this, note that the ingested E. coli grows to the carrying capacity in cattle (K c ). Thus, the density of resistant E. coli in cattle is affected by K e because with better hygiene and fewer bacteria in the environment, fewer bacteria are ingested; thus, it takes longer for the bacteria in the cattle gut to reach K c . Reducing the baseline maximum specific growth rate (g e ) had a negligible impact on the model predictions. Replacing k e3 with k e3_slow had no impact on the predicted efficacy of cleaning while k e3_fast had a considerable impact, as expected, in that it gradually overpowered the positive impact of maximum scraping (K e = 1) resulting in a gradual increase in the predicted contamination to over 1 log10 CFU/g under this scenario (see Appendix S1 named Fig 4_ke3fast and Fig 4_ke3slow).

Predicted impact of manure management interventions that reduce carrying capacity in log10 CFU/g (K e ) on dynamics of tetracycline‐resistant E. coli in beef cattle feedlot pens. Increased pen scraping is simulated assuming K e = 4. Maximum pen scraping is simulated assuming K e = 1.

Discussion

The developed model reproduced field data showing rapid growth of tetracycline‐resistant Escherichia coli in the environment despite no antimicrobials being used (Agga et al. 2016). Modelling findings support that generic hygiene measures, such as regular scraping of pens, can reduce the density of AMR bacteria in the farm environment. However, these measures alone are unlikely to reduce the density in the farm environment to negligible levels except in very cold temperatures, because E. coli populations survive very well in the pen environment at a range of ambient temperatures, and tetracycline resistance genes confer a relatively low fitness cost. Nevertheless, in the absence of other options for reducing antimicrobial use, increased hygiene measures may be considered to reduce antimicrobial resistance while having negligible impacts on animal welfare. Pen scraping may confer additional benefits to farmers by reducing hide contamination and other disease problems, including foot disease. Also, it helps with dust suppression in hot and dry conditions. However, pen scraping can be less practical in very wet conditions. Additionally, scraping has practical and financial drawbacks, including considerable labor and equipment costs (Park et al., n.d.) and pen surface loss and the need for resurfacing. Thus, implementing frequent scraping would require considerable additional effort and significant expense to resurface pens and improve drainage and would benefit from the development of novel, more cost‐effective technological approaches for frequent removal of faeces to make pen scraping consistently feasible.

Whilst acknowledging the limitations of the model, we have demonstrated that a relatively simple modelling framework can reproduce observed field data showing an increase in resistant bacteria in the environment, despite no antibiotic use. Furthermore, the model suggests that the increase could be driven by population dynamics of generic E. coli , i.e., under environmental conditions that support rapid growth of generic E. coli , tetracycline‐resistant E. coli can persist or even grow rapidly. These findings are timely considering the enhanced efforts to curtail antibiotic use in farm animals as they offer an explanation for the reported variability in effectiveness or lack of effectiveness at least in the short term, of antibiotic use restrictions on the observed antimicrobial resistant bacteria in cattle (Davies and Wales 2019). Manure management practices (e.g., cleaning frequency and method) as well as ambient conditions, or even better, the soil surface temperature, should be recorded in future studies to allow us to characterise and ultimately control their effect on antibiotic resistance in open cattle operations. This framework could be expanded and adapted to other bacteria and farming systems. For example, in its present form the model developed here can sufficiently represent a number of heifer‐raising operations that raise replacement cows for dairy farms and which are characterised by similar animal age categories, stocking density and management practices considered in the model developed here.

Another important output of this study was a formula for the metric R AMR which could be used in both modelling and field studies to quantify the propensity of interventions to control AMR in the system and to compare effectiveness across systems. Importantly, it can capture the effect of interventions on the infection's initial capacity for spread. We used R AMR to assess the impact of model parameters on the initial relative increase of resistant E. coli . However, this metric can also be calculated for any other two successive time points in the model, which may be of value in characterisation of the effect of time‐varying factors on the antibiotic resistant bacteria in the farm environment and therefore also in livestock kept in that environment, including weather and management conditions that affect the dynamics of antimicrobial resistance on livestock operations. However, the metric will only inform about the initial relative increase of resistant E. coli (between two successive time points) not in the long term.

Our model structure makes a number of simplifying assumptions, and the findings should be applied with caution, particularly to contexts outside of the single feedlot trial which was used to parameterize the model. We model the carrying capacity K e per gram of faeces in the environment as a constant to describe the density of bacteria that can be sustained in the environment for a given level of hygiene. Because this approach ignores the real‐world fluctuations of K e due to the discrete nature of scraping events, the model predictions could be over‐ or under‐estimated depending on how long ago the scraping event has happened. Additionally, it should be noted that the values for K e under the two scraping scenarios are arbitrary and were chosen to assess the impact of moderately and extremely effective hygiene interventions. Each pen environment was considered as a single homogenous compartment that included feed, water and the pen environment whereas we only used pen environment data for fitting the model. In the trial dataset that we used for fitting our model, generic E. coli (defined as an E. coli strain regardless of antibiotic resistance or virulence factor) counts and tetracycline‐resistant E. coli counts were lower in feed and water than the pen floor, but there was a similar trend of increasing counts over time in feed, water and pen samples (Agga et al. 2016). Previous studies have highlighted that the distribution of generic E. coli may be heterogenous (Ayscue et al. 2009). However, since the pens in the trial that we used were relatively small (30 animals per pen), homogenous and similar in design, and the cattle were also similar in terms of age and size, we assumed that heterogeneities in spatial distribution of E. coli would have a negligible impact on the model predictions. Thus, our model is applicable to feedlots which have uniform pen design and homogenous age‐groups of cattle.

We also modelled the dynamics of generic E. coli within the cattle ingesta as a homogenous compartment. Studies of E. coli O157:H7 have shown animal‐to‐animal variation and temporal variation in shedding rates (Matthews et al. 2006), however, generic E. coli includes a wide variety of strains and so we expect individual variation in shedding to have a negligible impact on the model predictions.

In our model, resistance to tetracyclines was conferred by a single gene, which is a simplification of reality. The model could be expanded in future work to explore the impacts of multiple genes.

Feedlot cattle typically grow rapidly for the duration of time they are on the feedlot. We assumed a linear accumulation of faeces—and thus nutrient availability for bacterial growth—which was modelled as an increased carrying capacity (k e3 ) in occupied pens over time. This is a simplification as cattle grow over the time period and so faecal output per time unit would increase. Replacing the linear increase in k e3 by a non‐linear function k e3_slow that increases more slowly at first and then accelerates more rapidly, reflecting increasing addition of faeces by growing animals, showed that this assumption had no impact model findings. We assumed a linear association between temperature and growth rate at temperatures above 4°C. Previous studies have demonstrated a loglinear relationship (Gonthier et al. 2001), however the optimal growth rates of all strains of E. coli that were studied (> 38°C) exceeds the maximum daily temperature during this study (26.3°C), and so the linear model is considered to be adequate. We did not model an association between temperature and decay rate (μ e ) which could have obtained a better fit to data, but at the expense of model parsimony. In some settings (e.g., where temperature oscillates above and below freezing temperatures frequently), this may be more important to include. Further, the model did not explicitly account for moisture levels, which could vary with precipitation and temperature; the model was fit using ambient temperatures, rather than the temperature of the pen floor which could be significantly warmer and fluctuate less due to the higher heat capacity of the pen floor and accumulation of manure. This could explain discrepancies between model predictions and data for empty pens (where the pen environment might be colder because of the absence of animals and manure; Figure 3b), the fitted relationship between temperature and growth rate when compared to laboratory data under controlled conditions (Figure 3c), and the high sensitivity of model predictions to ambient temperature (Figure 3d). Daily oscillations in temperatures were not included due to a lack of data for the study location, but previous modelling studies have shown that these may have an impact on bacterial dynamics (Semenov et al. 2007). It is plausible that repeated freezing and thawing cycles may increase the mortality rate of the bacteria in the environment more than a single freeze thaw cycle.

In addition to the simplifying assumptions of the model structure, we only used a single dataset for fitting and validation. All pens were on the same farm and studied over the same time period, although we used data from a large number of pens, including empty pens. The model fit and the predictive value of the model may be improved by fitting the model to a variety of datasets. For example, there was a cold period which coincided with the days when cattle were newly arrived and adjusting to a new feed regime. This could be an alternative explanation for changes in bacterial dynamics over this time period. We also cannot rule out the presence of residual tetracyclines in the environment, or the effect of the use of antimicrobials prior to the cattle being introduced to the feedlot on Day 0. Finally, moisture levels in the pen environment were not included in the model and are an important predictor of E. coli growth rates (Berry and Miller 2005) that could account for some of the dynamics, including the difference between empty and occupied pens. For these reasons, application of our model could have resulted in an overestimation of the influences of temperature and/or increase in manure throughout the trial.

While our model predicted efficacy of hypothetical hygiene interventions to reduce the density of resistant E. coli in both the pen environment and the cattle ingesta (Figure 4), we acknowledge plausible alternative explanations, including that a decreased intake of E. coli with a stable gut niche could result in an increased growth rate in the gut. However, experimental research (Gautam et al. 2015) for E. coli O157:H7 in cattle supports that the infection spread and the basic reproduction number increase with an increase in the level of environmental contamination, providing support that our model prediction is feasible.

In conclusion, farm environments such as beef cattle feedlots may be conducive to persistence or even growth of antimicrobial resistant bacteria under a wide range of plausible conditions, even in the absence of antimicrobial use. The system may be quite resilient, and even stringent cleaning will likely not be sufficient to eliminate resistant bacteria from the environment. However, under colder temperatures, the possibility of reducing antimicrobial resistant E. coli becomes much more likely. Interventions targeting cleaning of the environment, particularly removal of manure from pens by scraping, may be effective at reducing the density of resistant bacteria, especially during cold temperatures.
