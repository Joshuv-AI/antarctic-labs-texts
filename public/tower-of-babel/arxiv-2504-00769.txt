Abstract
It is a challenging problem that solving the multivariate linear model (MLM) 𝑨 ​ 𝒙 = 𝒃 \bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b} with the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm approximation method such that ‖ 𝑨 ​ 𝒙 − 𝒃 ‖ 1 {\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{1} , the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm of the residual error vector (REV), is minimized. In this work, our contributions lie in two aspects: firstly, the equivalence theorem for the structure of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm optimal solution to the MLM is proposed and proved; secondly, a unified algorithmic framework for solving the MLM with ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm optimization is proposed and six novel algorithms (L1-GPRS, L1-TNIPM, L1-HP, L1-IST, L1-ADM, L1-POB) are designed. There are three significant characteristics in the algorithms discussed: they are implemented with simple matrix operations which do not depend on specific optimization solvers; they are described with algorithmic pseudo-codes and implemented with Python and Octave/MATLAB which means easy usage; and the high accuracy and efficiency of our six new algorithms can be achieved successfully in the scenarios with different levels of data redundancy. We hope that the unified theoretic and algorithmic framework with source code released on GitHub could motivate the applications of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm optimization for parameter estimation of MLM arising in science, technology, engineering, mathematics, economics, and so on. Keywords : Multivariate linear model (MLM); Parameter estimation; ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm optimization; Residual error vector (REV); Equivalence theorem; Algorithmic framework

Introduction

It is a key issue that how to solve the
multivariate linear model
(MLM)
accurately, efficiently and robustly in science, technology, economics, management, and so on
[
1
,
2
]
. The MLM is also named by overdetermined system of linear algebraic equations and
generalized linear model
(GLM). Formally, the MLM can be expressed by

\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}

(1)

where
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{n\times 1}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{n}\times{1}}
is the unknown
n
-dim parameter vector,
\bm{A}=(a_{ij})_{m\times n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{n}}
is the coefficient matrix such that
m\geq n
, and
\bm{b}=(b_{i})_{m\times 1}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}
is the
m
-dim observation vector.

Although the form of MLM (
1
) arising in various applications is simple, it is not well-defined
[
3
]
due to the noise existing in the matrix
\bm{A}
and/or in the vector
\bm{b}
.

1
1

1

Conceptually, the MLM (
1
) is called well-defined or consistent if there exists at least one solution
\bm{x}
such that the equation (
1
) holds on strictly in the sense of algebra. However, it is not our topic in this study.

Usually, the vector

\bm{r(x)}=\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}

(2)

is called the
residual error vector
(REV). The non-negative function

\mathcal{C}_{p}(\bm{x})={\left\lVert\bm{r}(\bm{x})\right\rVert}_{p}^{p},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{n}\times{1}},p\geq 1

(3)

is called the cost function for the MLM, in which
{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{p}
is the

\ell^{p}
-norm defined by

{\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert}_{p}=\sqrt[p]{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|w_{i}\right|^{p}},\quad\forall\ \bm{w}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}.

(4)

In order to reduce the impact of the noise arising in
\bm{A}
and/or
\bm{b}
, it is necessary to solve the optimization problem

{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\mathcal{C}_{p}(\bm{x})

(5)

with the given integer
p\geq 1
. The most popular choice for the cost function is

\mathcal{C}_{2}(\bm{x})={\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}=(\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b})^{\textsf{T}}(\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}),

(6)

which is a smooth function. This choice leads to the family of least square methods, which includes the classic
least squares
(LS)
[
4
,
5
]
and its variants such as
data least squares
(DLS),
total least squares
and
scaled total least squares
(STLS)
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
The advantages of the family of least square methods include the following aspects:

•

firstly the differentiability of
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\bm{x})
in (
6
) leads to a closed form of optimal solution to (
5
) derived by the orthogonality principle in Hilbert space;

•

secondly, the geometric interpretations for the TLS is intuitive, and

•

finally the statistical and topological interpretation for the STLS are clear.

However, there are still some disadvantages for the family of least square methods:

i)

when the distribution of the noise in
\bm{A}
and/or
\bm{b}
is not the normal distribution, the performance of the estimation can not be guaranteed;

ii)

if there are some outliers, the parameter estimation obtained by the family of least square methods will be useless.

In order to avoid the impact of outliers, the RANSAC method was proposed by Fischler and Bolles in 1981
[
11
]
, which is widely used in computer vision. Generally, the time computational complexity of RANSAC method and its variations is high due to the process of random sampling if the ratio of outliers is high.
For the purpose of seeking precise and robust solution to (
5
) effectively by dealing with the dark sides i) and ii) simultaneously, it is a good choice to take the
\ell^{1}
-norm instead of the
\ell^{2}
-norm. In this case, the cost function

\mathcal{C}_{1}(\bm{x})={\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{b}\right\rVert}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_{j}-b_{i}\right|

(7)

is not differentiable, which leads to the following challenging optimization issue

{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{n}\times{1}}}\mathcal{C}_{1}(\bm{x}).

(
\rm{P}_{1}
)

The solution to the problem (

P
1

) is known as solving the MLM in the sense of
\ell^{1}
-norm minimization. The solution is called the
minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximate solution
to the MLM. Simultaneously, the problem (

P
1

) is called the
minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation problem

[
3
]
.

Wagner
[
12
]
in 1959 highlighted the correlation between linear programming and
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation problem. In 1966, Barrodale et al.
[
13
]
introduced a primal algorithm that capitalizes on the unique structure of the linear programming formulation for (

P
1

), and later presented an enhanced version in
[
14
]
. Converting the
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation problem into a linear programming problem offers a refined mathematical representation, albeit at the expense of increasing the complexity problem-solving (see section
2.4
).

In 1967, Usow
[
15
]
introduced a descent method for calculating the optimal
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation. This method involves locating the lowest vertex of a convex polytope that represents the set of all possible
\ell^{1}
-norm approximations. In 2002, Cadzow et al.
[
3
]
proposed an improved
\ell^{1}
-norm perturbation algorithm based on brute-force methods for solving for the minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximate solution (see section
2.3.3
). Although the structures for their iterative algorithms are similar, these algorithms are limited due to the high computational complexity when the dimension
n
is large.

Bartels et al. in 1978 presented a projection descent method by minimizing piecewise differentiable cost functions. This method achieved the minimization of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\bm{x})
in a finite number of steps. In 1991, Yang and Xue
[
16
]
proposed an active set algorithm by converting the (

P
1

) into a piecewise linear programming problem via establishing an active equation index set and solving it iteratively. In 1993, Wang and Shen
[
17
]
proposed an interval algorithm to solve the minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation problem. However, the structure of the interval algorithm is complex and the existence of the optimal solution interval should be verified repeated. Unfortunately, the interval algorithm is not attractive until now.

Yao
[
18
]
proposed an effective numerical method for problem (

P
1

) based on the minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm residual vector in 2007. By converting the problem (

P
1

) to a constrained
\ell^{1}
-norm optimization problem, Yao obtained the minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximate solution through a compatible system of linear equations with the assumption
\rank(\bm{A})=n
. However, Yao’s method does not hold when
\rank(\bm{A})<n
.

In summary, there is a lack of good method and ready-to-use algorithms for solving the
\ell^{1}
-norm optimization problem (

P
1

) with the general condition
\rank(\bm{A})\leq n
, in which the ”good” means high precision, high robustness, and low or acceptable computational complexity.
In this study, our contributions for solving (

P
1

) include the following aspects:

i)

a unified theoretic framework is given by establishing and proving the equivalence theorem, which states the new sufficient and necessary condition for solving the minimum
\ell^{1}
-norm approximate solution with the basis pursuit method and the Moore-Penrose inverse;

ii)

a unified algorithmic framework is proposed via REV, which covers all of the available algorithms for (

P
1

);

iii)

six new algorithms are designed, which just relies on fundamental matrix operations and do not depend on specific optimization solvers;

iv)

the performance of different algorithms are evaluated with numerical simulations; and

v)

all of the algorithms designed are described with pseudo-code in the sense of computer science and the Python/Octave implementations are released on the GitHub, which not only reduces the difficulty of understanding the mathematical principles but also increases the potential usability and popularity in the sense of engineering applications.

Conclusions

Solving the problem (

P
1

) accurately and efficiently is a challenging task, especially in real-time applications such as the SLAM in computer vision and autonomous driving. The key theoretical contribution of this work is the equivalence theorem for the structure of
\ell_{1}
approximation solution to the MLM
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
: the optimal solution
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is sum of the traditional LS solution
\bm{A}^{\dagger}\bm{b}
and the correction term
\bm{A}^{\dagger}{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}
specified by the ML1-REV.

The simulations show that our
\ell^{1}
-norm approximation algorithms based on existing
\ell_{1}
-norm optimization algorithms to minimizing the residual vector, namely the L1-POB, L1-GPRS, L1-HP, …, have the following advantages in solving the problem (

P
1

):

1)

Both the L1-POB and the L1-GPRS have low time complexity. Given the computational platform, the ratio of the running time for the L1-HP algorithm and L1-RES algorithm is approximately
1/5
if there is no noise. By comparison, the ratio of the running time for the L1-POB algorithm and the L1-RES algorithm is about
1/9
if the noise is sparse;

2)

The accuracy of the solutions obtained by our algorithms is comparable to accuracy of the L1-RES algorithm in various scenarios;

3)

The implementations are simple and they does not depend on
any built-in mathematical programming solvers;

4)

The L1-POB and L1-GPSR algorithms can not only find the solution with high accuracy but also with high efficiency when compared with the L1-RES algorithm
in the scenarios with different levels of data redundancy.

Among the evaluated algorithms, the L1-POB algorithm distinguishes itself with its low time complexity, which implies that it is suitable for real-time computation in data-constrained scenarios. In the sense of accuracy, all of the
\ell_{1}
optimization algorithm for solving the problem (

P
1

) exhibit a relative error within
3\%
. Particularly, the L1-HP algorithm could offer better accuracy in applications. In the case of high data redundancy, both the L1-LP algorithm and the L1-RES algorithm demonstrate high accuracy exceptionally. By relaxing the constraints, the space of the feasible solutions to the MLM can be expanded, which allows a broader range of the potential solutions.

It is still a challenging problem that how to solve the MLM with high accuracy, high efficiency and high robustness via the
\ell^{1}
-norm optimization in computational mathematics. We believe that our explorations and algorithms could offer new directions in addressing the problem (

P
1

) in the future.

For the convenience of usage, all of the algorithms discussed in this paper are implemented with the popular programming languages Python and Octave/Octave, and the source code has been released on GitHub. We hole that this study could speed up the propagation and adoption of the
\ell_{1}
-norm optimization in various applications where the MLM appears naturally.

A.1
Linear Programming Method

Farebrother
[
34
]
pointed out that the unconstrained
\ell^{1}
-norm optimization problem
\min\limits_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}\right\rVert}_{1}
is equivalent to

\min\limits_{\bm{r}^{+},\bm{r}^{-}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\bm{1}^{\textsf{T}}_{m}(\bm{r}^{+}+\bm{r}^{-}),\ \mathrm{s.t.}\begin{cases}\bm{r}^{+}-\bm{r}^{-}=\bm{r}\\
\bm{r}^{+},\bm{r}^{-}\succcurlyeq 0\end{cases}

(52)

Let

\bm{t}=\bm{1}_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{1}_{m}\\
\bm{1}_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}^{+}\\
\bm{r}^{-}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{D},-\bm{D}\end{bmatrix}

(53)

and substitute (
53
) into (
52
), we immediately have the equivalent description
[
23
]

\min\limits_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{2m}\times{1}}}\bm{t}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\beta},\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\begin{cases}\bm{\Phi}\bm{\beta}=\bm{w}\\
\bm{\beta}\succcurlyeq\bm{0}\end{cases}

(
\rm{LP}_{2}
)

where
\bm{D}
and
\bm{w}
are derived from (
33
) and (
34
). Suppose the solution to the problem (

LP
2

) is

{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{opt}}=[{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}^{+},{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}^{-}]^{\textsf{T}},

(54)

then the solution to the problem (
BP
) must be

{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}={\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}^{+}-{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}^{-}={\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{opt}}(1:m)-{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{opt}}(m+1:2m).

(55)

A.2
Gradient Projection Method

The first
Gradient Projection
(GP) method is the GPSR method
[
28
]
. By segregating the positive coefficients
\bm{r}^{+}
and the negative coefficients
\bm{r}^{-}
in
\bm{r}
, the equation (

QP
λ

) can be reformulated into the form of standard quadratic programming, namely

\min\limits_{\bm{z}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{2m}\times{1}}}Q(\bm{z})=\bm{\gamma}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{z}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{B}\bm{z},\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{z}\succcurlyeq 0

(56)

where

\bm{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}^{+}\\
\bm{r}^{-}\end{bmatrix},\bm{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{w}\\
\lambda+\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{w}\end{bmatrix},\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{D}&-\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{D}\\
-\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{D}&\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{D}\end{bmatrix}

(57)

Note that the matrix
\bm{B}
defined in (
57
) is symmetric.
The gradient of the quadratic form
Q(\bm{z})
is

\nabla_{\bm{z}}Q(\bm{z})=\bm{\gamma}+\bm{B}\bm{z}.

(58)

Thus the iterative scheme based on the steepest-descent can be
designed by (
58
), viz.

\bm{z}_{k+1}=\bm{z}_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla Q(\bm{z}_{k})

(59)

where the optimal step size
\alpha_{k}
can be solved by a standard line-search process such as the Armijo rule
[
35
]
. Under appropriate convergence criteria, we can obtain the solution to (
56
) according to (
59
) by simple iterations.

The second GP method, known as the TNIPM method
[
29
]
, can be obtained by transforming the equation (

QP
λ

) into the following constrained quadratic programming problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{r}-\bm{w}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i},

(60)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\ -u_{i}\leq r_{i}\leq u_{i},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,m.

By constructing the following
logarithmic barrier
function for the bound constrain

\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{u})=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(u_{i}+r_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(u_{i}-r_{i})

(61)

over the domain

\Omega=\left\{(\bm{r},\bm{u})\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}\times\mathbbm{R}^{m}:\left|r_{i}\right|\leq u_{i},i=1,2,\cdots,m\right\}

(62)

and substituting (
61
) into (
60
), we can find the unique optimal solution
(\bm{r}^{*}(c),\bm{u}^{*}(c))
to the convex function
[
29
]

F_{c}(\bm{r},\bm{u})=c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{r}-\bm{w}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\right)+\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{u})

(63)

where
c\in[0,+\infty)
. The optimal search direction
[\Delta\bm{r},\Delta\bm{u}]^{\textsf{T}}\in\Omega
for (
63
) can be determined by

\nabla^{2}F_{c}(\bm{r},\bm{u})\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{r}\\
\Delta\bm{u}\end{bmatrix}=-\nabla F_{c}(\bm{r},\bm{u})\in\mathbbm{R}^{{2m}\times{1}}

(64)

with the Newton’s method, in which the
\nabla^{2}F_{c}(\bm{r},\bm{u})
in (
64
) is the Hessian matrix of
F_{c}(\bm{r},\bm{u})
. The optimal step

{\tau}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\arg\min_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}F_{c}(\bm{r}+\tau\Delta\bm{r},\bm{u}+\tau\Delta\bm{u})

(65)

specified by (
65
)
can be computed with a backtracking line search. In
[
29
]
, the search direction mentioned in (

) is accelerated by the
preconditioned conjugate gradients
(PCG) algorithm
[
36
]
to efficiently approximate the Hessian matrix.

A.3

The algorithm starts with an initial value
\bm{r}^{(0)}=\bm{0}
and operates iteratively, calculating the solutions
\bm{r}^{(k)}
at each step
k=1,2,\cdots
. Throughout the computation, the active set

\bm{c}^{(k)}=\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}(\bm{w}-\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k)})
is the vector of residual correlations
[
5
]
. Let

(\cdot)_{\lambda}=(\cdot)[\mathscr{L}]

be the update direction on the sparse support, then
\bm{d}^{(k)}_{\lambda}
is the solution to the following linear system

\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}_{\lambda}\bm{D}_{\lambda}\bm{d}^{(k)}_{\lambda}=\textrm{sign}(\bm{c}^{(k)}_{\lambda}).

(67)

Thus we can solve (
67
) to obtain the
\bm{d}^{(k)}_{\lambda}=\bm{d}^{(k)}[\mathscr{L}]
, which consists of the non-zero elements in
\bm{d}^{(k)}
. Along the direction indicated by
\bm{d}^{(k)}
, there are two scenarios when an update on
\bm{r}
may lead to a break-point
[
32
]
:
inserting an element into the set
\mathscr{L}
or removing an element from the set
\mathscr{L}
.
Let

\gamma_{+}^{(k)}=\min\limits_{i\in\mathscr{L}^{c}}\left\{\min\left(\frac{\lambda-c^{(k)}_{i}}{1-\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}_{i}\bm{D}_{\lambda}\bm{d}_{\lambda}^{(k)}},\frac{\lambda+c^{(k)}_{i}}{1+\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}_{i}\bm{D}_{\lambda}\bm{d}_{\lambda}^{(k)}}\right)\right\}_{+}

(68)

and

\gamma_{-}^{(k)}=\min\limits_{i\in\mathscr{L}}\left\{{-r^{(k)}_{i}}\big/{d^{(k)}_{i}}\right\}_{+}

(69)

where
\min\left\{\cdot\right\}_{+}
means that the minimum is taken over only positive arguments. The Homotopy algorithm proceeds to the next break-point and updates the sparse support set
\mathscr{L}
iteratively by the following formula

\bm{r}^{(k+1)}=\bm{r}^{(k)}+\min\left\{\gamma_{+}^{(k)},\gamma_{-}^{(k)}\right\}\cdot\bm{d}^{(k)},\quad k=1,2,\cdots

(70)

The iteration terminates when
{\left\lVert\bm{r}^{(k+1)}-\bm{r}^{(k)}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{re}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}^{(k)}\right\rVert}_{2}
for the given relative error
{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{re}}
according to the Cauchy’s convergence criteria.

A.4
Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Method

The IST method
[

38
]
are proposed to solve the (

composite objective function

{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\arg\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}f(\bm{r})+\lambda g(\bm{r})

(71)

where
f(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{r}-\bm{w}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}
and
g(\bm{r})={\left\lVert\bm{r}\right\rVert}_{1}
. The updating rule of IST to minimize (
71
) can be calculated by taking a second-order approximation of
f
. Mathematically, we have
[
38
]

\displaystyle\bm{r}^{(k+1)}=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\left\{f(\bm{r}^{(k)})+(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{(k)})^{\textsf{T}}\cdot\nabla f(\bm{r}^{(k)})\right.

(72)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{(k)}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\cdot\nabla^{2}f(\bm{r}^{(k)})+\lambda g(\bm{r})\right\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\left\{(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{(k)})^{\textsf{T}}\cdot\nabla f(\bm{r}^{(k)})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{(k)}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\lambda g(\bm{r})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{u}^{(k)}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{k}}g(\bm{r})\right\}

where

\bm{u}^{(k)}=\bm{r}^{(k)}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\nabla f(\bm{r}^{(k)}),\quad\alpha_{k}\in\mathbbm{R}^{+}.

(73)

Note that
g(\bm{r})
is a separable function. As a result, a closed-form solution for
\bm{r}^{(k+1)}
can be obtained for each component

\displaystyle r^{(k+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{r_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}}\left\{\frac{(r_{i}-u^{(k)}_{i})^{2}}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{k}}\left|r_{i}\right|\right\}

(74)

\displaystyle=\textrm{soft}\left(u^{(k)}_{i},\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{k}}\right)

where

\textrm{soft}(u,a)=\textrm{sign}(u)\cdot\max\left\{\left|u\right|-a,0\right\}

(75)

is the
soft-thresholding
or
shrinkage
function
[
39
]
. The coefficient
\alpha_{k}
used in (
72
),(
73
) and (
74
) is adopted to approximate the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f
and it can be calculated with the
Barzilai-Borwein
spectral approach
[
38
]
.

A.5
Alternating Direction Method

With the help of the auxiliary variable
\bm{u}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}
, the problem (

BP
ϵ

) can be converted to the following equivalent form
[
33
]

\min\limits_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}},\bm{u}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}\right\rVert}_{1},\ \mathrm{s.t.}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bm{D}\bm{r}-\bm{w}=\bm{u},\\
&{\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq\epsilon.\end{aligned}\right.

(76)

Moreover, we have an augmented Lagrangian subproblem of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{m}\times{1}},\bm{u}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{r}\right\rVert_{1}-\bm{y}^{\textsf{T}}(\bm{D}\bm{r}+\bm{u}-\bm{w})+

(77)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{r}+\bm{u}-\bm{w}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\ {\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq\epsilon

where
\bm{y}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}
is a multiplier and
\mu>0
is a penalty parameter. Applying inexact alternating minimization to (
77
) yields the following iterative scheme
[
33
]

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{u}^{(k+1)}&=\mathscr{P}_{\bm{B}_{\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\bm{y}^{(k)}}{\mu}-\left(\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k)}-\bm{w}\right)\right),\\
\bm{r}^{(k+1)}&=\textrm{soft}\left(\bm{r}^{(k)}-\tau\bm{g}^{(k)},\frac{\tau}{\mu}\right),\\
\bm{y}^{(k+1)}&=\bm{y}^{(k)}-\zeta\mu\left(\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k+1)}+\bm{u}^{(k+1)}-\bm{w}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(78)

where
\tau>0
is a proximal parameter,
\zeta>0
is a constant and

\bm{g}^{(k)}=\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\left(\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k)}+\bm{u}^{(k+1)}-\bm{w}-\frac{\bm{y}^{(k)}}{\mu}\right).

(79)

Note that
\mathscr{P}_{B(\epsilon)}:\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}\to B(\epsilon)
is the projection onto the closed ball

B(\epsilon)=\left\{\bm{d}\in\mathbbm{R}^{{(m-n)}\times{1}}:{\left\lVert\bm{d}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq\epsilon\right\}
with radius
\epsilon
.
It should be pointed out that, when
\epsilon=0
, (
79
) can rewritten by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{r}^{(k+1)}&=\textrm{soft}\left(\bm{r}^{(k)}-\tau\bm{D}^{\textsf{T}}\left(\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k)}-\bm{w}-\frac{\bm{y}^{(k)}}{\mu}\right),\frac{\tau}{\mu}\right),\\
\bm{y}^{(k+1)}&=\bm{y}^{(k)}-\zeta\mu\left(\bm{D}\bm{r}^{(k+1)}-\bm{w}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(80)

which induces a simple iterative algorithm for solving the problem (
BP
).
