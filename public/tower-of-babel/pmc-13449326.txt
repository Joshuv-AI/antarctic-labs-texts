ETV4 as a potential target gene for alleviating proliferation metastasis of HCC
Authors: Yuan Yuan, Qinglin Tan, Maolin Zhu, Long Chen, Di Sun, Yun Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) develops and progresses via the RAF/MERK/ERK signaling pathway. Sorafenib, a first-line therapy for advanced HCC, inhibits the RAF gene. However, RAF dimerization and ERK activation caused by RAF inhibition in HCC, and this paradoxical RAF/MERK/ERK activation is one of the reasons for Sorafenib resistance in liver cell carcinoma. ETV4 is an ERK downstream gene, hence inhibiting it may help to reduce treatment resistance in HCC. The human hepatoma cell line HepG2.2.15 was used as an HCC tumor cell model, while the HL-7702 cell line was used as a human normal hepatocytes cell model. In order to find out the function of ETV4, Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to knock down ETV4 gene. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), occupied 75%-85% of primary liver cancer, is the third primary cause of cancer-related fatalities globally, projected to exceed one million persons yearly by 2025 [1]. Owing to its exceedingly minimal 5-year survival rate, HCC has greatly exacerbated the worldwide illness burden [2]. It is the RAF/MERK/ERK signaling pathway that has been implicated in tumorigenesis and the progression of hepatocarcinoma [3, 4]. Sorafenib, as a first-line therapy for advanced HCC, exerts its effects by inhibiting RAF gene [5].

ETV4 has been linked to carcinogenesis and is significantly expressed in several human malignancies, including endometrial cancer [6], non-small cell lung cancer [7], breast cancer [8], melanoma [9], prostate cancer [10], bladder cancer [11], and hepatocellular carcinoma [12]. Building upon the FGF19/FGFR4 signaling axis, previous research indicates that this pathway mediates the transcriptional upregulation of ETV4. The elevated ETV4 then facilitates hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis by directly promoting the expression of downstream targets, notably PD-L1 to suppress anti-tumor immunity and CCL2 to recruit protumorigenic macrophages, thereby remodeling the tumor microenvironment [13]. Another study revealed a distinct oncogenic mechanism for ETV4 in HCC, demonstrating that it transcriptionally activates key components of the hepatic TNF-α signaling pathway. ETV4 induces a pro-inflammatory cascade that fosters chronic inflammation, cell survival, and proliferation in the liver, hence expediting tumorigenesis and establishing a milieu favorable to hepatocarcinogenesis [14]. However, the specific molecular pathways and mechanisms through which ETV4 governs the differentiation, metastasis, and invasion of HCC remain incompletely defined and a subject of active investigation. Existing research reports ETV4 is expressed through the extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) axis and positively related to higher tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage, microvascular invasion, and poor prognosis in human HCC [13]. ETV4 is also associated with tumor growth and metastasis, especially in Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [15]. ETV4 is an upstream target gene of Matrix metalloproteases (MMPs), which works as a critical role in ETV4-driven tumors. Within the MMPs family, two key genes are MM1 and MMP9. MMP1 contributes to the invasive and migratory capabilities of malignant tumors through EMT and participates in the degradation of stromal collagen [16, 17]. While MMP9 cleaves a variety of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins to regulate ECM remodeling and has the ability to proteolytically hydrolyze a wide range of structural and signaling proteins, it is very important in tumorigenesis, such as EMT, cell migration, and new blood vessel formation [18, 19]. RAF gene is an upstream ERK gene. In summary, emerging evidence suggests a hypothesis wherein the RAF/MEK/ERK pathway may inhibit hepatocellular carcinoma progression by modulating MMP expression via ETV4 (Fig. 1), a specific mechanism that has not been directly reported. Furthermore, despite ETV4’s established role in promoting resistance to sorafenib and cisplatin [20], it remains unknown whether this resistance operates through the proposed RAF/MEK/ERK/ETV4/MMPs axis. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap. Furthermore, since sorafenib resistance in HCC frequently involves compensatory ERK activation post-RAF inhibition, we also seek to explore ETV4’s role in this resistance mechanism. To achieve these aims, we performed ETV4 knockdown in HepG2.2.15 cells via siRNA transfection, in order to uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms governing HCC proliferation and metastasis.

Methodology

Cell culture and gene knockdown

Hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2.2.15 and human healthy hepatocyte cell line HL-7702 (both purchased from a reagent company rather than directly from the patient) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing penicillin-streptomycin at 37℃ in 5% carbon dioxide. The study used to divide into four cell groups: the tumor cell group without any addition was the control group; tumor cells transfected with no relevant vector sequences were negative control group, named as siRNA-NC group; the silencing target ETV4 in tumor cells was the siRNA-ETV4 group; and the healthy liver cell group was normal group.

Cultured Hep2.2.15 cell in the 6 well plates (cell density 2 × 105/well). Following the manufacturer’s instructions for ETV4 siRNA, 2 ml per well was applied to each group, and the cultures were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide. 48 h later, the cells were subjected to further biochemical assays as required for each experiment.

RNA preparation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total proteins were extracted by using RNA Trizol reagent. And then PrimeScript RT reagent Kit was used to synthesize cDNA. Expression levels of each target were measured using the StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to obtain relative mRNA expression levels. Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion assays

Cells (number as 5 × 103 cells per well) were seeded onto five 96-well plates after siRNA transfection, and cell viability was assessed by MTT at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days. Microplate-Reader determined the absorbance of each well of every day at the wavelength of 570 nm. The MTT experiment specifies the detection time point, and the number of repetitions is supplied in the Supplementary Materials. Cells were transplanted into the upper chamber of 24-well transwell plates in serum-free medium. 20% FBS complete medium was added to the Bottom chamber. After 24 h of incubation, cells migrate from the upper-surface to the under-surface of the membrane. Fixed with formalin, stained with crystal violet, the number of cells finally photographed under a microscope.

Cell western blotting

All cells in each group were added working reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then measured on the flow cytometer. Proteins from cultured cells were extracted with RIPA Lysis Buffer, and the same amount of proteins was measured by the Bicinchoninic acid method (BCA). Protein blots were analyzed by gel electrophoresis and electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, then blocked with 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered Saline-Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated with antibodies specific such as ETV4 (1:2000), ERK (1:1000), MMP1 (1:2000), MMP9 (1:2000), pERK (1:1000), GAPDH (1:50000). GAPDH was used as an internal control for Western blotting. The blot was then incubated with a secondary antibody and visualized by chemiluminescence, then scanned using the Imaging System. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used for Western blot analysis: rabbit monoclonal anti-ERK (Cat# ET1601-29, Huabio); rabbit monoclonal anti-ETV4 (Cat# A15667, Abclonal); rabbit monoclonal anti-MMP1 (Cat# 383245, Zen-Bio); rabbit monoclonal anti-MMP9 (Cat# 502095, Zen-Bio); rabbit monoclonal anti-phospho-ERK (Cat# 301245, Zen-Bio); mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (Cat# AC033, Abclonal); biotinylated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Cat# AS033, Abclonal); and biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Cat# AS014, Abclonal). All antibodies were commercially sourced. Antibodies were diluted in the indicated ratios: anti-ETV4 (1:2000), anti-ERK (1:1000), anti-MMP1 (1:2000), anti-MMP9 (1:2000), anti-phospho-ERK (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:50000).

Animals and xenograft model

Selected BALB/c nude-mouse of specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade, with 3–4 weeks old, were housing at ambient temperature (22 ± 2) °C, humidity 50%~60%, alternating light and dark every 12 h, free to eat and drink. HepG2.2.15 cells transfected with short hairpin RNA (shRNA) were used to establish nude mouse xenograft model. Accordingly, three experimental groups were established: an untreated blank group (Control group), a negative control group harboring the empty shRNA vector (shRNA-NC group), and a positive group in which ETV4 was knocked down (shRNA-ETV4 group). A 100-µL cell suspension from each group was inoculated subcutaneously at a site approximately 1 cm from the axilla of nude mice. Tumor engraftment was observed every three days, and tumor volumes were measured. On day 15 post-inoculation, the mice were euthanized and dissected.

Tissue western blotting

Tumor tissues (50 mg) were collected and lysed in lysis buffer, followed by thorough homogenization using a tissue grinder. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected as the extracted protein lysates. The remaining steps were performed as described for the cell experiments. Immunohistochemistry.

(1) Deparaffinization and rehydration: Sections were treated with deparaffinization solution I and II for 5 min each, followed by rehydration in graded ethanol (absolute ethanol I–III, 95% ethanol, and 75% ethanol) for 5 min each, and finally rinsed with distilled water for 1 min. (2) Antigen retrieval: Sections were heated in EDTA buffer using a microwave (medium-high power for 8 min, held for 7 min, then low power for 8 min) and cooled to room temperature. (3) Blocking: Sections were incubated with blocking serum at 37 °C for 30 min. Excess serum was discarded without washing. (4) Primary antibody incubation: Sections were incubated with primary antibody in a humidified chamber overnight at 4 °C, then washed three times with PBS (5 min each). (5) Secondary antibody incubation: Sections were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 50 min at room temperature in the dark, then washed three times with PBS (5 min each). (6) Chromogenic detection: DAB substrate was applied for 1–3 min at room temperature until a brownish-yellow color developed, and the reaction was stopped by rinsing with water. (7) Counterstaining: Sections were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 30–60 s, rinsed with distilled water, immersed in bluing solution for 1 min, and washed with water. (8) Dehydration, clearing, and mounting: Sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol (75%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in three changes of xylene (2 min each), and mounted with neutral balsam. The following antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry: Ki-67 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, dilution ratio: 1:200); E-cadherin Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, dilution ratio: 1:200); Vimentin Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, dilution ratio: 1:200).

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed by using computer software SPSS, and the drawings were made by Graph Pad Prism. Data obeyed Normal Distribution and therefore used Nonparametric Test. Differences between each group were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). LSD and Bonferroni test were used in Post Hoc Multiple Comparisons after ANOVA. P values 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ETV4 knockdown did not alter EKR protein expression, as evidenced by Western blot analysis; nevertheless, p-ERK, MMP1, and MMP9 protein expression were dramatically impacted by ETV4 gene silencing (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The online GSCA database shows that ETV4 is overexpressed in a various human cancer, especially in human HCC tissue, as evidenced by the aforementioned study. In our study, low expression of ETV4 limited tumorigenesis and metastasis in HCC, which indicates that ETV4 is expected to be a biological marker for clinical targeted therapies to alleviate ERK-activated HCC in the future.

ETV4 is a crucial transcription factor that directly binds to and stimulates the production of many MMP genes, including MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-7, and MMP-9. This regulatory axis is frequently upregulated by oncogenic pathways like RAS-MAPK [21]. ETV4 enhances MMP synthesis, hence promoting extracellular matrix disintegration and enabling cancer cell invasion, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Consequently, the ETV4-MMP relationship is a central mechanism in tumor progression and a potential therapeutic target [22–24]. In Yue team study [25], the RAF/MERK/ERK signaling pathway promotes survival by inhibiting apoptotic, such as apoptotic BCL-2 gene family. Additionally, approximately 50% of HCC patients exhibit upregulation of ERK expression [26]. Previous studies [27–30] have reported that ERK is activated via phosphorylation to generate p-ERK, which subsequently activates the downstream ERK/MEK signaling pathway. In the present experiment, silencing of ETV4 led to reduced p-ERK protein expression, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation through suppression of the RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathway. Meanwhile, MMP1 and MMP9 expression in RNA level and protein level were both decreased, which shed light on that MMP1 and MMP9 gene were downstream gene of ETV4. It was revealed by ChIP-qPCR analysis that the enrichment of ETV4 at the MMP1-EBS3 and MMP9-EBS4 regions in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Therefore, the evidence of ERK/ETV4/MMPs axis is complete. In Kim study [31], their team demonstrated that CIC-ETV4-MMP1 axis was a pathway in HCC progression and verified MMP1 was downstream target of ETV4 gene. Our findings in vivo and in vitro experiments indicate that the silencing of ETV4 inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells. In cytometric analysis, the apoptosis rate was statistically higher in the knockdown ETV4 group compared with the control group, implying that silencing ETV4 was beneficial in suppression HCC cells. According to the cell cycle results, the siRNA ETV4 group’s cells took more time in the G2/M stage and less time in the G0/G1 stage. This suggested that tumor cells with low ETV4 expression were imprisoned in the cytokinesis phase because they lacked the necessary ingredients for division. This also proved that silencing ETV4 is beneficial for inhibiting HCC cells. In animal experiments, ETV4 silencing suppressed tumor growth and modulated related protein expression in a manner consistent with that seen in cell experiments. Ki67 is a nuclear protein that reflects the proliferative activity of tumor cells [32]. E-Cadherin is a calcium-dependent transmembrane glycoprotein specifically expressed in epithelial cells, whereas Vimentin is an intermediate filament protein, both E-Cadherin and Vimentin serve as markers of EMT [16, 33]. In the immunohistochemical analysis, silencing of ETV4 led to a significant reduction in Ki67 protein expression, indicating marked inhibition of tumor tissue growth. This finding is consistent with the observed suppression of tumor growth rate in the ETV4 knockdown group in the tumor-bearing experiment. Furthermore, the elevated E-Cadherin expression and reduced Vimentin expression in the ETV4 knockdown group suggest that the conversion of epithelial cells into migratory mesenchymal cells was impeded, thereby diminishing the metastatic potential of ETV4-silenced tumors. This result aligns with the decreased tumor invasive capacity observed in the cell experiments. In Xie’s research [13], they overexpressed ETV4 and found HCC metastasis in some extend and concluded that ETV4 could be targeted in a combinational therapeutic strategy to inhibit HCC metastasis.

Sorafenib, utilized as a first-line agent for HCC, exerts its effects by multifaceted tyrosine kinase inhibition, hence impeding tumor proliferation by targeting neoplasms and angiogenesis [34]. It is the RAF/MERK/ERK pro-survival signaling pathway plays a key part in cell growth and survival, as well as regulates cell differentiation [35]. Our investigation indicated that ETV4 can alter ERK expression; therefore, combination-target treatment using the genetic silencing of ETV4 presents significant research potential. Additionally, targeting both upstream activators and downstream targets of ETV4 to block both intrinsic and extrinsic drivers of tumor progression may prove a powerful therapeutic strategy for treating HCC.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that ETV4 links the RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathway to MMPs, positioning it as a potential tumor suppressor gene in hepatocellular carcinoma. Furthermore, ETV4 represents a promising biomarker and therapeutic modulator. Nevertheless, its additional roles and underlying mechanisms warrant further clinical validation.

Conclusion

ETV4 is a key gene connecting the RAF/MER/ERK pathway and the MMP family in tumor growth and development, especially in HCC. This study provides functional and mechanistic evidence suggesting that ETV4 knockdown limits the progression of HCC. This effect is mediated by the reduction of tumor cell proliferation and migration, which may occur, at least in part, through the modulation of the ERK/ETV4/MMPs axis.
