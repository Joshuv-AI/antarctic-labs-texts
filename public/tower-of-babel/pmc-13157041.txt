FAST imaging of extrapericardial cardiac tamponade caused by mediastinal hematoma: A case report of blunt chest trauma
Authors: Shohei Matsukubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost sternal fractures can be conservatively treated. Herein, we report a rare case of extrapericardial cardiac tamponade due to mediastinal hematoma secondary to sternal fracture and internal thoracic artery injury. A 54-year-old man was hospitalized for blunt chest trauma after an accident. He was in shock on arrival, and initial echocardiography revealed extrapericardial cardiac tamponade caused by an epicardial hematoma. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed extravasation from the sternal fracture and cardiac compression due to mediastinal hematoma. Emergency surgery was performed for hematoma removal, the internal thoracic artery on the sternal fracture surface was identified as the bleeding source, and hemostasis was performed. The postoperative course was good. Extrapericardial cardiac tamponade due to mediastinal hematoma secondary to sternal fracture and internal thoracic artery injury due to blunt chest trauma is a rare presentation requiring extreme caution. Surgical intervention should be considered despite the lack of treatment consensus.Keywords: Cardiac tamponade, Echocardiography, Hematoma, Hemostasis, Thoracic injuries

Introduction

Most sternal fractures are followed up on without invasive treatment. Klei et al. reported that sternal features occurred in only 2% of all trauma cases and that over 95% of all sternal fractures were conservatively treated [[1], [2]]. Fokin et al. reported that inpatients with sternal fracture accounted for 2.1% of all trauma cases and that although sternal fracture alone was not fatal in most patients, many had complex injuries, with rib and vertebral fracture rates of 56.7% and 38.2%, respectively [3]. In particular, the mortality rate increased to 12.9% in older patients with complex injuries compared to 1.9% in younger patients.

Sternal fractures are occasionally accompanied by dorsal-sternal hematomas; however, Rashid et al. reported that the hematoma size ranged from a few mm to <20 mm in many cases and resolved with conservative treatment [4].

We report a rare case of blunt chest trauma caused by a traffic accident, resulting in a sternal fracture and extrapericardial cardiac tamponade (ECT) with hematoma, which was observed via the focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) procedure.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old man with a history of alcoholic liver disease and high blood pressure was injured in a self-inflicted traffic accident. He was not trapped in the car, and the airbag was activated; however, he was not wearing a seatbelt.

Physical findings on arrival were as follows: pulse rate, 166 beats/min; blood pressure, 108/89 mmHg; arterial O₂ saturation, 95% with 5 L/min O₂ by mask; respiratory rate, 32 breaths/min; and Glasgow Coma Scale, 4-5-6. Physical examination of the body surface revealed no overt bruises or subcutaneous hematomas. Initial chest X-ray showed mediastinal widening; however, retrospective review demonstrated subtle superior mediastinal widening with a positive cervicothoracic sign, suggesting an anterior mediastinal process consistent with the subsequently confirmed mediastinal hematoma (Fig. 1).

Chest X-ray image at initial treatment.

On FAST, signs suggesting hematoma were observed between the lateral side of the endocardium and sternum (Fig. 2, Video 1). Based on the signs of hemorrhagic shock and FAST findings, extrapericardial tamponade and associated obstructive shock due to impaired cardiac expansion secondary to pericardial cardiac hematoma were suspected.

Pericardial cardiac tamponade imaging on FAST at initial treatment. (A) Hematoma, (B) right ventricle, (C) left ventricle.

A detailed examination using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a sternal fracture and mediastinal hematoma with a maximum thickness of approximately 30 mm. Extravasation suggesting sternal bleeding was also observed (Fig. 3). The initial diagnosis was obstructive shock due to mediastinal hematoma; hemorrhagic shock; right internal thoracic artery injury; right hemopneumothorax; fractures of the 1st, 2nd, 4th, 5th, and 6th left ribs; fractures of the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th right ribs; right forehead contusion, and left knee contusion.

Extravasation image from sternal and periosteal fractures. 1: Extravasation and fractures 2: Hematoma.

Priority was given to relieve obstructive shock due to ECT secondary to mediastinal hematoma, and emergency exploratory thoracotomy was performed. The time interval from the accident to hospital arrival was 43 minutes, and the time interval from the initial medical examination and diagnosis to entry to the operating room was approximately 120 minutes.

Midline sternotomy was performed, and multiple sternal fractures with active bleeding were observed. Hemostasis was performed to address pulsatile bleeding from the right internal thoracic artery. Pericardial examination revealed a normal amount of intrapericardial fluid with a serous appearance; therefore, pericardial incision was not performed.

The total surgical time was 94 minutes. Intraoperatively, the patient received transfusions of 2960 mL of blood products (1120 mL erythrocytes, 1440 mL fresh frozen plasma, and 400 mL platelets). The infusion and blood loss volumes were 2000 and 1200 mL, respectively.

The patient recovered without postoperative complications and was transferred on postoperative day 22 for further treatment of alcohol withdrawal delirium.

Discussion

Although Fokin et al. noted a high mortality rate in cases of blunt thoracic trauma accompanied by injuries other than sternal fracture, they also stated that factors such as respirator usage, ICU admission rate, length of ICU and hospital stays, and mortality rates did not differ between patients with and without sternal fracture-associated mediastinal hematoma [3]. Consequently, they concluded that sternal fracture with hematoma formation did not necessarily increase clinical severity. However, the present case illustrates that ECT may occur due to hematoma secondary to a sternal fracture.

Cases of ECT due to hematoma associated with a sternal fracture are rare [[4], [5], [6]]. Endocardial tamponade caused by blunt chest trauma leads to obstructive shock, and it is clear from the physical findings, vital signs, and FAST results that these features are similar to those observed with ECT in the present case. However, the potential contribution of hemorrhagic shock could be considered due to an intraoperative blood loss of 1200 mL.

FAST images in the present case revealed that the impaired ventricular relaxation was secondary to an extrapericardial hematoma (Fig. 2). To the best of our knowledge, this is the second report of echocardiographic imaging revealing extrapericardial tamponade, after Coleman et al., highlighting the rarity of this clinical presentation [5].

Studies have demonstrated the utility of both FAST and CT in detecting ECT associated with sternal fractures. Arabi et al. reported a patient with a sternal fracture caused by fall-related trauma who could not be saved due to a mediastinal hematoma that resulted in extrapericardial tamponade; CT was effective in the diagnosis and clarification of the need for prompt therapeutic intervention [7].

Treatment guidelines for mediastinal hematoma associated with a sternal fracture are unclear. In a retrospective review of embolization and surgical hemostasis for the treatment of internal thoracic artery injury associated with sternal fracture, Whigham et al. advocated the effectiveness of TAE based on a success rate of 91.6% [8].

We considered internal thoracic artery embolization with TAE. However, because hemostasis achieved with internal thoracic artery occlusion would not relieve existing ECT by the mediastinal hematoma, TAE was not employed. Hematoma drainage via subxiphoid puncture was also considered but abandoned because it was not a curative procedure and our institution did not have experience with the drainage of subxiphoid hematoma associated with ECT. Coleman et al. and Kao et al. recommended rapid surgical hematoma removal and therapeutic intervention of the underlying lesion [[4], [6]]. Further case reports are warranted to evaluate their utility.

Conclusions

ECT due to blunt trauma is rare. Diagnosis with FAST is possible. Contrast-enhanced CT should be considered for a detailed examination of the bleeding pathology. Despite the lack of consensus on treatment, surgical intervention should be considered for hematoma removal and the control of the bleeding source in patients with shock due to ECT.
