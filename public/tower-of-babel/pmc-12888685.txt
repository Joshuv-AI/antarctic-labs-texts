Development and application of ActA-based competitive ELISA for the specific diagnosis of ovine listeriosis
Authors: Chao Chen, Beiru Wang, Mengdi Zhao, Fanzeng Meng, Suojun Jiang, Xin’an Jiao, Yuelan Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundListeriosis is a severe zoonotic disease caused by Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes), which poses a significant threat to both livestock and public health. Efficient detection of L. monocytogenes infections in humans and various animal species requires the development of a specific and sensitive method for clinical diagnosis.ResultsIn this study, a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (cELISA) was developed for the detection of anti-ActA antibodies in serum, based on a horseradish peroxidase-labeled monoclonal antibody 3G11 (HRP-MAb 3G11). The assay parameters, including the coating antigen concentration, HRP-MAb 3G11 titer, serum dilution factor, blocking buffer, and blocking incubation time, were optimized for the detection of ActA-specific antibodies. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified 27.50% as the optimal cutoff value for the percentage of inhibition (PI), yielding a diagnostic sensitivity of 100% and a diagnostic specificity of 93.33%. This assay exhibited no cross-reactivity with positive sera specific for other common pathogens, demonstrated stable reproducibility, and achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 95.7% compared with a commercial indirect LLO-based ELISA kit. The cELISA kit was used to detect 930 ovine serum samples, revealing an overall seropositivity rate of 24.84%.ConclusionsThe cELISA kit developed in this study provides a specific, sensitive, and repeatable serological method for the rapid detection of L. monocytogenes infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05217-6.

Introduction

Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is a Gram-positive opportunistic pathogen and a causative agent of zoonotic listeriosis [1, 2]. Hosts are typically infected through the consumption of contaminated food, resulting in asymptomatic cases in the majority of instances; however, severe bacterial infections may also occur, known as listeriosis [3]. Ruminants such as sheep and cattle are highly susceptible to L. monocytogenes infections, with meningitis and abortion as the primary clinical symptoms, leading to substantial economic losses in the livestock industry [4]. Additionally, the long-term latency of L. monocytogenes in asymptomatic carriers poses a threat to food safety and public health as the pathogen enters the food chain through contaminated dairy products [5]. Therefore, strengthening the monitoring of animal listeriosis at the farm level is crucial for preventing infected livestock from entering the food production chain, which is of great importance for listeriosis prevention.

Common approaches for detecting listeriosis include cultural isolation of pathogens (bacterial isolation and identification), PCR-based methods, and serological assays [6]. Cultural isolation of pathogens is regarded as the “gold standard” for diagnosing listeriosis because of its high accuracy. However, the process is labor-intensive and time-consuming, as it typically requires more than five days for the detection and confirmation of the pathogen [7, 8]. PCR-based techniques detect L. monocytogenes by targeting specific DNA or RNA sequences, providing high sensitivity and reliable results. Nevertheless, quantitative PCR requires specialized instruments and skilled personnel, and the sensitivity of conventional or multiplex PCR may be compromised by inhibitors present in the samples [9, 10]. Serological methods, characterized by their high-throughput capability and ability to reveal host exposure history, are particularly valuable for flock-level risk assessment and epidemiological investigations [11]. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is a method based on immunological reactions that combines the specificity of antigen-antibody interactions with the catalytic efficiency of enzymes acting on substrates. Compared with sandwich and indirect ELISA, competitive ELISA (cELISA) reduces washing and incubation times by enabling direct competition between enzyme-labeled antibodies and serum antibodies, thereby enabling rapid sample analysis [9]. Moreover, cELISA provides an efficient approach for pathogen surveillance, allowing for the rapid identification of asymptomatic carriers and supporting large-scale screening efforts.

Actin polymerization protein (ActA), a Listeria-derived surface protein, promotes actin polymerization and bacterial motility to facilitate gut colonization and evasion of autophagy [12, 13]. ActA plays a key role in L. monocytogenes persistence within the host and transmission from the host back to the environment [14]. As a novel adjuvant, the fusion of ActA with tumor antigens or its administration as a mixture can increase immunogenicity, thereby facilitating specific tumor immunotherapy [15]. ActA elicits specific cellular and humoral immune responses, leading to the production of high titers of antigen-specific antibodies [16, 17]. Owing to its strong immunogenicity, ActA also represents a promising diagnostic target for the detection of L. monocytogenes infection.

In this study, we established a competitive ELISA method for detecting antibodies against ActA and assessed the sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability of the cELISA. The cELISA kit demonstrated comparable agreement and sensitivity to the commercial ELISA kit, while requiring less time, thereby showing promising potential for the clinical detection of listeriosis in sheep.

Materials and methods

Antigen, antibody and serum samples

The 6⨯His-tagged ActA antigen and horseradish-peroxidase labeled monoclonal antibody (HRP-MAb) 3G11 against ActA were preserved in our laboratory. The E. coli strain BL21 (DE3) transformed with the expression vector pET-30a-ActA and the hybridoma cell line producing MAb 3G11 were constructed in a previous study, and the antigen and antibody were prepared following the protocol previously described [18].

Positive sera specific to Escherichia coli; Salmonella Enteritidis; Salmonella Gallinarum; Salmonella Pullorum; Campylobacter jejuni; Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Listeria ivanovii were maintained in our laboratory. Twenty-three positive sera against L. monocytogenes and 35 negative sera were collected from experimentally immunized ovines and uninfected controls in previous animal experiments [19]. The serological status of these samples was determined by integrating the results of L. monocytogenes isolation from organs (spleen and liver) together with the levels of anti-L. monocytogenes IgG measured using an indirect ELISA [19]. A total of 930 ovine serum samples were collected from 14 ovine farms in Jiangsu Province and stored at -20 ℃.

Establishment and optimization of the cELISA

The optimal concentration of coating antigen and dilution of HRP-MAb 3G11 were determined using a checkerboard titration via cELISA. Different concentrations of ActA antigen (1, 2, and 3 µg/mL, 100 µL) were coated on 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher, USA) at 4 ℃ overnight. After three washes with PBST (PBS buffer containing 0.05% Tween-20), the plates were blocked with 300 µL of PBS containing 2% BSA (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) per well at 37 °C for 3 h. Following another PBST wash, 50 µL of the diluted controls and HRP-MAb 3G11 were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The positive and negative controls were diluted 1∶10, while the HRP-MAb 3G11 was diluted 1∶16,000, 1∶20,000, 1∶24,000, 1∶28,000, and 1∶32,000, and then mixed in equal volumes. After thorough washing, 100 µL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to each well for color development. The plates were then incubated in the dark for 5 min, after which the enzymatic reaction was terminated by the addition of 50 µL of stop solution (2 M sulfuric acid). Optical densities at 450 nm (OD450) were measured with a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA) and the percentage of inhibition (PI) was calculated via the formula: PI = [1 - (OD450 of test serum / OD450 of blank control)] × 100%. The optimal working concentrations of the coating antigen and enzyme-labeled antibody were determined on the basis of the condition that yielded the highest P/N value (PI of positive control / PI of negative control).

Furthermore, the optimal conditions of the cELISA including the serum dilution factor (1:8, 1:10, 1:12, 1:16, and 1:20), blocking buffer (2% BSA, 5% BSA, 2% skim milk, and 5% skim milk), and the times of blocking (1.5 h, 2 h, 2.5 h, and 3 h), incubation (0.5 h, 1 h, 1.5 h, and 2 h) and chromogenic reaction (3 min, 5 min, and 7 min) were screened by checkerboard titration. The P/N ratios under each condition were compared, and the optimal conditions yielded the highest P/N ratios.

Determination of the cELISA cutoff value

The establishment of a cutoff value facilitates accurate differentiation between negative and positive samples, thereby improving the accuracy of the method. Under the optimized cELISA conditions described above, 23 positive and 35 negative ovine serum samples were tested, and the PI values for each serum sample were calculated. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated and analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0, with the area under the curve (AUC) reported at a 95% confidence interval (CI). The sensitivity and specificity corresponding to each PI value were used to calculate the Youden index, defined as: Youden index = [sensitivity + specificity – 1]. The PI value corresponding to the maximum Youden index was determined to be the optimal cutoff point.

Assessment of cross-reactivity, diagnostic sensitivity and specificity

A panel of specific sera against Escherichia coli; Salmonella Enteritidis; Salmonella Gallinarum; Salmonella Pullorum; Campylobacter jejuni; Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Listeria ivanovii were used to assess the cross-reactivity of the cELISA. Additionally, 23 positive serum samples from experimentally infected ovines and 30 negative ovine serum samples were used to evaluate the diagnostic sensitivity (DSe) and diagnostic specificity (DSp) of the cELISA.

Estimation of repeatability and reproducibility

The repeatability of the competitive ELISA was assessed by evaluating intra-assay variation, inter-assay variation, and variation among different operators. For the intra-batch evaluation, 8 ovine serum samples were tested using three plates coated with the antigen from the same batch. For the inter-batch evaluation, 12 ovine serum samples were analyzed using antigen-coated plates prepared from three independent batches. Additionally, 24 ovine serum samples were independently tested by three different operators using the standardized cELISA protocol. The percentage of inhibition (PI), standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated to assess the assay’s repeatability and consistency within and between batches.

Assembly and storage stability analysis of the cELISA kit

Based on the optimized parameters described above, we assembled an ActA-based cELISA kit, which consisted of the following components: one antigen-coated plate, positive control serum (800 µL), negative control serum (800 µL), blank control (0.3 g BSA), HRP-MAb 3G11 (450 µL), antibody diluent (30 mL, PBS), PBST buffer (60 mL, 20×), TMB substrate (22 mL), stop solution (12 mL, 2 M sulfuric acid), and one instruction manual. The HRP-MAb 3G11 was diluted 1:50 with an HRP-Protector (Candor, Germany), while the positive and negative control sera were diluted at 1:2:17 with an HRP-Protector and 2% BSA in PBS, supplemented with 1% ProClin 300 (Supelco, USA) and aseptically aliquoted. The assembled kit was stored at 4 °C for preservation and subsequent use.

To evaluate the storage stability of the kit, the first assessment was performed on the first day of storage, followed by monthly evaluations up to the tenth month. During each test, the OD450 values of the positive control, negative control, and blank control were recorded, and their coefficients of variation (CVs) were calculated to assess the kit’s stability.

Comparison of the cELISA kit with a commercial kit

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the cELISA, 47 ovine serum samples were tested in parallel via cELISA and a commercial Listeria Sheep Test Kit (Diatheva) for comparative analysis. The coincidence rate (CR) was calculated using the formula: CR (%) = [(total number of sera - number of inconsistent sera) / number of total sera] × 100%.

Clinical applicability study

A total of 930 serum samples collected from 14 ovine farms were tested by the cELISA kit. The results were analyzed to assess the prevalence of L. monocytogenes infection among the sampled population.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed via GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, which is a statistical tool for evaluating the discriminatory power of a diagnostic test. Repeatability was assessed using CV (CV = SD / mean), where a CV value less than 15% was considered an acceptable repeatability level for the assay.

Results

Establishment of the cELISA

Considering the P/N value and reagent usage, the optimal antigen coating concentration for cELISA was determined to be 2 µg/mL, while the optimal dilution for the HRP-MAb 3G11 was 1:32,000, corresponding to a concentration of 36.2 ng/mL (Fig. 1A). The PI and P/N values of positive and negative sera at different dilution factors were compared (Fig. 1B), and a 1:10 dilution was determined to be the optimal dilution factor for serum samples. Based on the P/N values, other optimized conditions were as follows: optimal blocking buffer: 2% skim milk, optimal blocking time: 3 h, optimal incubation time: 1 h, and optimal substrate incubation time: 3 min (Fig. 1C-F).

Cutoff values of positive and negative serum

The ROC curve generated from the detection results of different samples (Fig. 2A) presented an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.0, with P < 0.0001, indicating that the method effectively distinguishes between negative and positive samples. The ROC analysis identified a PI cutoff of 27.5%, achieving 100% sensitivity and 93.3% specificity at the maximum Youden index (Fig. 2B).

Sensitivity and specificity analysis of the cELISA

Among the 53 tested samples, the results for all but two negative sera (Table 1) were consistent with their expected status. The overall concordance rate was 96.2%, with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 93.3%. The PI values for positive sera specific to Escherichia coli; Salmonella Enteritidis; Salmonella Gallinarum; Salmonella Pullorum; Campylobacter jejuni; Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Listeria ivanovii were all below the threshold values (Fig. 3). These results showed that the cELISA exhibited no cross-reactivity with positive sera of other common pathogens.

The repeatability and reproducibility of the cELISA

The repeatability and reproducibility of the cELISA were evaluated on the basis of intra-batch, inter-batch, and inter-operator variations. The intra-assay CVs for PI ranged from 2.55% to 6.80% with a median value of 4.74%; the inter-assay CVs ranged from 1.22% to 14.44% with a median value of 8.77%; and the inter-operator CVs ranged from 1.00% to 9.70% with a median value of 7.52% (Table 2). These data indicate that the cELISA method has good reproducibility.

Storage stability of the cELISA kit

The assembled cELISA kit was stored at 4 ℃ and used to detect the negative, positive, and blank controls monthly from day 60 to day 300. The OD450 values of the controls (Fig. 4A) were recorded, and the inter-batch and intra-batch CVs (Table 3) were calculated to evaluate the storage stability of the kit. The inter-batch CVs for the positive, negative, and blank controls were 0.40%–11.50%, 0.40%–9.80%, and 0.40%–7.60%, respectively, while the intra-batch CVs were 13.70%–14.50%, 13.50%–14.90%, and 13.40%–14.80%, respectively. In addition, the PI values at different time points (Fig. 4B) were calculated and compared, and no significant differences were observed. Although the detection variability increased with prolonged storage, the CVs value remained below 15%, indicating that the kit exhibited good storage stability.

Detection results of ovine serum samples

A total of 930 serum samples collected from 14 ovine farms in Jiangsu Province were tested with the cELISA kit. The seropositivity between 14 ovine farms ranged from 0% to 51.30% and the overall positive rate was 24.84% (231/930) (Fig. 5). On Farm C, despite the high seropositivity rate (51.30%), the concordance with bacterial isolation from ovine body swabs was low (isolation rate: 5.84%, 9/154) (Supplemental Table 1). The low isolation rate may be attributable to the difficulty of recovering bacteria during different stages of infection in ovine. Moreover, the use of body swabs for sampling might have further reduced the detection rate. Altogether, these results indicate a relatively high prevalence of infection among ovines in this region. This highlights the importance of implementing effective prevention and control measures to mitigate the spread of infection on ovine farms.

Discussion

L. monocytogenes is an opportunistic pathogen that infects ruminants and humans, causing listeriosis with high morbidity and mortality. In animals, listeriosis causes a reduction in herd productivity, directly impacting the import and export trade of livestock products, and resulting in significant economic losses [20, 21]. Additionally, animal products carrying L. monocytogenes enter the food supply chain, resulting in human cases of listeriosis upon consumption. Therefore, the detection of infected animals at the farm stage is crucial for preventing the spread and transmission of the disease. Serological assays offer a practical approach for large-scale screening of listeriosis owing to their operational simplicity and high-throughput capability [22]. Among the virulence determinants of L. monocytogenes, factors such as LLO, ActA, InlA, and InlB play pivotal roles in infection and pathogenesis, and their strong immunogenicity makes them particularly suitable targets for ELISA-based detection [9, 23]. Multiple indirect ELISA assays have been developed based on specific L. monocytogenes antigens, for example, listeriolysin O (LLO) [23–25], rPI-PLC [26], p60 [27], LapB [23], InlA and InlB [28]. Additionally, sandwich ELISA based on InlG [29] was developed to detect L. monocytogenes in the slaughter environment. However, the high conservation of PI-PLC may lead to cross-reactivity with other Listeria species, and the high homology of LLO to other genera, such as the SLO of Streptococcus pyogenes and the PFO of Clostridium perfringens, leading to false-positive and reducing detection specificity [26]. The actin polymerization factor ActA, a surface-expressed virulence factor of L. monocytogenes with strong immunogenicity [30], shows no cross-reactivity with other Listeria species and other genera [31, 32]. Previous studies have generated ActA-specific antibodies, including a single-chain antibody isolated by Paoli et al. and highly specific polyclonal antibodies reported by Lathrop et al., both of which demonstrated potential for ActA-based immunoassays targeting L. monocytogenes [33, 34]. However, reports on immunoassays using ActA as a diagnostic target are limited to indirect ELISA [33]. Several immunoassay platforms have been developed for the detection of L. monocytogenes, and most of these assays focus on detecting bacterial antigens rather than host antibodies [6]. In this study, we developed a competitive ELISA targeting the L. monocytogenes ActA protein to enable specific serological detection of ovine infections. The assay showed excellent diagnostic performance, with ROC analysis identifying an optimal cutoff of 27.5% and yielding a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI: 90.00–100.00), a specificity of 93.3% (95% CI: 85.18–100.00), an AUC of 1, and P < 0.0001. Previously, Boerlin et al. reported two indirect ELISAs based on LLO and InlA, which achieved sensitivities of 82% and 100% and specificities of 92% and 90%, respectively [23]. Compared with these indirect ELISAs, the ActA-based cELISA developed here provides a distinct serological strategy and exhibits comparable as well as improved diagnostic accuracy, underscoring its potential utility for the effective detection of ovine listeriosis. Antigenic cross-reactivity test results indicated that the cELISA did not cross-react with positive serum against common bacteria, and was capable of detecting anti-sera to L. monocytogenes. The inter-batch, intra-batch, and inter-operator CVs were all below 10%, indicating that the assay showed consistent performance under the tested conditions [29]. Moreover, the entire detection process can be completed within 2.5 h, reducing the detection time by approximately 40% compared to the Listeria Sheep Test Kit (Diatheva) and significantly improving testing efficiency. This cELISA also has the potential to be applied to detect L. monocytogenes infection in other mammals and poultry, but further studies are needed to establish species-specific cutoff values. Furthermore, this method may be limited in detecting early infections due to low antibody levels; however, combining it with other assays, such as PCR, can overcome this drawback [24].

To further evaluate the efficacy of the method, 930 ovine serum samples from 14 farms in Jiangsu Province were detected, revealing that seropositivity ranged from 0 to 51.3%. The prevalence of L. monocytogenes varies significantly among ovine farms, and is influenced by both environmental factors and host variations, indicating distinct regional epidemiological patterns. Farms with a history of L. monocytogenes infection show markedly higher seropositivity rates than healthy farms, likely due to bacterial shedding from infected animals causing secondary infections [35]. This suggests potential L. monocytogenes contamination and control measures to prevent infected livestock from entering the food supply chain, which is crucial for reducing economic losses and safeguarding public health.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a highly effective competitive ELISA kit employing an HRP-labeled ActA monoclonal antibody. The assay demonstrated outstanding performance characteristics, including exceptional sensitivity, high specificity, rapid processing time (completion within 2.5 h), and user-friendly operation. The kit enables efficient high-throughput screening of serum samples while maintaining process stability and delivering reliable, easily interpretable results, making it suitable for rapid screening of listeriosis in the livestock industry, thereby providing technical support for effective prevention and control of listeriosis.
