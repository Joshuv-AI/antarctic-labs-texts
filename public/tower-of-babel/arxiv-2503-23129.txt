Scattering of transient waves by an interface with time-modulated jump conditions
\firstname Michaël \lastname Darche \CDRorcid 0000-0001-5731-4551, \firstname Raphaël \lastname Assier, \firstname Sébastien \lastname Guenneau, \firstname Bruno \lastname Lombard \IsCorresp, \firstname Marie \lastname Touboul

Abstract
Time modulation of the physical parameters offers interesting new possibilities for wave control. Examples include amplification of waves, harmonic generation and non-reciprocity, without resorting to non-linear mechanisms. Most of the recent studies focus on the time-modulation of the bulk physical properties. However, as the temporal modulation of these properties is difficult to achieve experimentally, we will concentrate here on the special case of an interface with time-varying jump conditions, which is simpler to implement. This work is focused on wave propagation in a one-dimensional medium containing one modulated interface. Properties of the scattered waves are investigated theoretically: energy balance, generation of harmonics, impedance matching and non-reciprocity. A fourth-order numerical method is also developed to simulate transient scattering. Numerical experiments are conducted to validate the numerical scheme and to illustrate the theoretical findings.

{altabstract}
La modulation temporelle des propriétés d’un milieu de propagation offre de nouvelles possibilités intéressantes pour le contrôle des ondes. Les exemples incluent l’amplification des ondes, la génération d’harmoniques et la non-réciprocité, sans avoir recours à des mécanismes non linéaires.
La plupart des études récentes se concentrent sur la modulation temporelle des propriétés physiques volumiques d’un milieu de propagation. Cependant, cette modulation étant difficile à réaliser expérimentalement, on se concentre ici sur le cas particulier d’une interface avec des conditions de saut variables en temps, plus simple à mettre en œuvre. Ce travail se concentre sur la propagation des ondes dans un milieu monodimensionnel contenant une interface modulée. On étudie théoriquement de nombreuses propriétés des ondes diffusées : bilan d’énergie, génération d’harmoniques, adaptation d’impédance et non-réciprocité. Une méthode numérique d’ordre 4 est développée pour simuler les ondes transitoires diffractées. Des expériences numériques sont menées pour valider le schéma numérique et illustrer les résultats théoriques attendus.

2
Physical modeling

2.1
Problem statement

Let us consider a one-dimensional linear elastic medium with density
\rho(x)
and Young’s modulus
E(x)=\rho(x)\,c^{2}(x)
, where
c
is the sound speed (the analysis is however relevant to other wave physics problems such as acoustics or s-polarization and p-polarization in electromagnetism, see Remark 1). These parameters may be discontinuous at
x=x_{0}
. The conservation of momentum and the Hooke’s law are formulated as follows :

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho(x)\,\partial_{t}u(x,t))=\partial_{x}\sigma(x,t)+F(x,t),

(1a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma(x,t)=E(x)\,\partial_{x}u(x,t),

(1b)

with
u
being the displacement field,
\sigma
the stress field, and
F
a body force with compact support that does not contain
x_{0}
. The velocity field is denoted by
v=\partial_{t}u
. For any function
g(x)
, we define the jump and mean operators
\left\llbracket.\right\rrbracket_{x_{0}}
and
{\langle\!\langle}.{\rangle\!\rangle}_{x_{0}}
as

\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket_{x_{0}}=g^{+}(x_{0})-g^{-}(x_{0}),\qquad{\langle\!\langle}g{\rangle\!\rangle}_{x_{0}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{+}(x_{0})+g^{-}(x_{0})\right),

(2)

where

g^{\pm}(x_{0})=\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}g(x_{0}\pm\eta).

(3)

To simplify the notations, the indices and arguments denoting space and time will be omitted when no risk of ambiguity occurs ; in particular,
x_{0}
is omitted in the jump and mean operators from now on.
As can be seen from direct computations, for any two functions
g
and
h
, these operators satisfy the property

\left\llbracket g\,h\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket\,{\langle\!\langle}h{\rangle\!\rangle}+{\langle\!\langle}g{\rangle\!\rangle}\,\left\llbracket h\right\rrbracket.

(4)

Introducing interface parameters of stiffness
{K}(t)>0
, compliance
{C}(t)=1/{K}(t)
, inertia
{M}(t)\geq 0
and dissipation
{Q}_{C,M}(t)\geq 0
, the imperfect interface is modelled by the time-dependent jump conditions :

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket v(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket=\partial_{t}\left({C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle}\right)+{Q}_{C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle},

(5a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket\sigma(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket=\partial_{t}\left({M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle}\right)+{Q}_{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle}.

(5b)

Five remarks arise from (
5b
) :

—

The modulated jump conditions are assumed to result from an external stimulus. Such a modulation of
\left\llbracket v\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket
can be obtained by piezoelectric components
[
47
]
and time-modulated membranes
[
60
]
, respectively ;

—

As seen further in Section
2.2
, the compliance
{C}(t)
and the inertia
{M}(t)
must be
inside
the time derivative to ensure a sound energy balance ;

—

The terms in (
5a
) are reminiscent of the Maxwell model of viscoelasticity in the case of a static dissipation parameter
{Q}_{C}(t)={Q}_{C}
;

—

When
{C}=0
,
{M}=0
,
{Q}_{C}=0
and
{Q}_{M}=0
, the conditions of perfect bonding are recovered ;

—

If
{Q}_{C}=0
, then (
5a
) can be recast in the more usual form with displacement :

\displaystyle\left\llbracket u(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket={C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle};

{Remark}

The problem studied in this article is generic to various wave physics problems. For instance, the case of acoustics is obtained by changing
u
into the acoustic pressure,
E
into the inverse of mass density and
\rho
into the compressibility. Similarly, the case of s-polarization (resp. p-polarization) in electromagnetism can be obtained by changing
u
into the transverse electric (resp. magnetic) field,
\rho
into the permittivity (resp. permeability) and
E
into the inverse of the permeability (resp. inverse of the permittivity).

2.2
Energy balance

We take the product of the momentum equation (
1a
) by
v=\partial_{t}u
and integrate over
(-\infty,x_{0})
. Integrating by parts and using (
1b
) give

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}\rho\,v\,\partial_{t}v\,dx&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}v\,\partial_{x}\sigma\,dx+\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}F\,v\,dx,\\[10.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}\sigma\,\partial_{x}v\,dx+[v\,\sigma]_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}+\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}F\,v\,dx,\\[10.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}\frac{1}{E}\,\sigma\,\partial_{t}\sigma\,dx+v^{-}(x_{0},t)\,\sigma^{-}(x_{0},t)+\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}F\,v\,dx.\end{array}

(6)

Similarly, integrating over
(x_{0},+\infty)
yields

\int_{x_{0}}^{+\infty}\rho\,v\,\partial_{t}v\,dx=-\int_{x_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{E}\,\sigma\,\partial_{t}\sigma\,dx-v^{+}(x_{0},t)\,\sigma^{+}(x_{0},t)+\int^{+\infty}_{x_{0}}F\,v\,dx,

(7)

where we have used the fact that
F
is compactly supported and that the fields vanish at infinity. Summing (
6
) and (
7
) leads to

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\rho\,v^{2}+\frac{1}{E}\,\sigma^{2}\right)\,dx\right)=-\left\llbracket v\,\sigma\right\rrbracket+\int_{\mathbb{R}}F\,v\,dx.

(8)

The property (
4
) and the jump conditions (
5b
) lead to

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\left\llbracket v\,\sigma\right\rrbracket&=&\displaystyle\left\llbracket v\right\rrbracket{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}+{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket,\\[8.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left({C}^{\prime}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}+{C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\partial_{t}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}+{Q}_{C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}\right){\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}+\left({M}^{\prime}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}+{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\partial_{t}v{\rangle\!\rangle}+{Q}_{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}\right){\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle},\\[8.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left({C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left({C}^{\prime}(t){\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+{M}^{\prime}(t){\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}\right)+{Q}_{C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+{Q}_{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}.\end{array}

(9)

It leads to the following energy balance.

{Proposition}

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}={\mathcal{E}}_{b}+{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
be the total energy, with the bulk energy

{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\rho\,v^{2}+\frac{1}{E}\,\sigma^{2}\right)\,dx

(10)

and the interface energy

{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{2}{M}\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}{C}\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}.

(11)

Introducing the power of external forces due to body forcing

{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F\,v\,dx,

(12)

one has

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}}_{m}(t)={\mathcal{P}}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\left({M}^{\prime}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+{C}^{\prime}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}\right)-\left({Q}_{C}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}\sigma{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}+{Q}_{M}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}v{\rangle\!\rangle}^{2}\right).

(13)

Five remarks arise from Proposition
13
and positivity of
{M}
,
{C}
,
{Q}_{C}
and
{Q}_{M}
:

—

both
{\mathcal{E}}_{b}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
are positive, hence
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}\geq 0
;

—

in
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
one recognizes the kinetic energy and the potential energy of a spring-mass system when static parameters are considered ;

—

if there are no body force (
F=0
), no time-modulation (both
{M}
and
{C}
are constant in time) and no dissipation term in the jump conditions (
{{Q}_{C}}
=0 and
{{Q}_{M}}
=0), then the total mechanical energy is conserved ;

—

if no body force is applied (
F=0
) and both
{M}
and
{C}
are constant in time, but
{{Q}_{C}}
or
{{Q}_{M}}
is non-zero, then the total mechanical energy is dissipated ;

—

if
F=0
but either
{M}
or
{C}
varies with time, then the total mechanical energy may vary, due to the power of external forces required to modify
{M}
and
{C}
. The sign of the right-hand-side in (
13
) is arbitrary, so that
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
may increase or decrease.

2.3
Some additional properties

The latter remark about the amplification of energy raises the question of possible parametric amplification. Under suitable assumptions, the next Proposition states that such an amplification is not possible, and that the solution remains bounded. The proof is given in Appendix
C
.

{Proposition}

Let us assume :

(i)

a sinusoidal modulation of the interface compliance
{C}(t)
and the dissipation term
{Q}_{C}(t)
;

(ii)

no jump of stress (ie
{M}(t)=0
and
{Q}_{M}(t)=0
) ;

(iii)

a body force in (
1a
) in the form
F(x,t)=\delta(x-x_{s})\,S(t)
, where
S
is bounded.

Then the scattered fields
v
and
\sigma
remain bounded for all
t
.

This result is counter-intuitive, since modulated systems often exhibit resonance (e.g. a modulated spring-mass system). An interpretation is that the energy may be evacuated on both sides of the domain, preventing from an unbounded increase. A similar conclusion holds by interchanging the roles of
{C}
and
{M}
. The case where both interface compliance and interface inertia are modulated remains open but this limitation seems, a priori, purely technical. Indeed, our numerical experiments suggest that the same result will hold in this case.

{Proposition}

When only
{Q}_{C}(t)\neq 0
,
i.e

{M}(t)=0
,
{C}(t)=0
and
{Q}_{M}(t)=0
, the limits between which the solutions evolve are determined analytically (Appendix
B
) and correspond to the static cases with the extreme values of
{Q}_{C}(t)
. The limits for the case with only
{Q}_{M}(t)\neq 0
can be obtained using the same reasoning.

2.4
Generation of harmonics

The interaction of a single-frequency wave with a modulated interface generates an infinite number of harmonics. Here we shortly describe how to compute them. For simplicity, constant physical parameters are considered around the interface at
x_{0}=0
. In this part, a sinusoidal modulation for
{C}
,
{M}
,
{Q}_{C}
and
{Q}_{M}
is chosen :

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{C}(t)={C}_{0}\left(1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(\Omega t)\right),\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle{M}(t)={M}_{0}\left(1+\varepsilon_{M}\,\sin(\Omega t)\right),\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle{Q}_{C}(t)={Q}_{C_{0}}\left(1+\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}\,\sin(\Omega t)\right),\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle{Q}_{M}(t)={Q}_{M_{0}}\left(1+\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}\,\sin(\Omega t)\right),\end{array}

(14)

with
\Omega=2\,\pi\,f_{m}
, where
f_{m}
is the modulation frequency, and
{C}_{0}\geq 0
,
{M}_{0}\geq 0
and
-1<\varepsilon_{X}<1
, with
X=C,M,Q_{C},Q_{M}
.
A harmonic incident wave
v^{\operatorname{in}}
and
\sigma^{\operatorname{in}}
of the form

\displaystyle v^{\operatorname{in}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\omega(t-x/c)};\qquad\sigma^{\operatorname{in}}(x,t)=-\rho c\,e^{i\omega(t-x/c)}

impacts the interface from the left. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle v^{\operatorname{tot}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}v^{\operatorname{in}}(x,t)+v^{\operatorname{ref}}(x,t)&\operatorname{for}&x<0\\
v^{\operatorname{trans}}(x,t)&\operatorname{for}&x>0,\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\sigma^{\operatorname{tot}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sigma^{\operatorname{in}}(x,t)+\sigma^{\operatorname{ref}}(x,t)&\operatorname{for}&x<0\\
\sigma^{\operatorname{trans}}(x,t)&\operatorname{for}&x>0,\end{array}\right.

for some reflected and transmitted wave fields
v^{\operatorname{ref}}
,
\sigma^{\operatorname{ref}}
,
v^{\operatorname{trans}}
and
\sigma^{\operatorname{trans}}
that also satisfy the governing wave equation. Floquet theorem states that these fields write

\displaystyle{}v^{\operatorname{ref}}(x,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}R_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t+x/c)},

\displaystyle\sigma^{\operatorname{ref}}(x,t)=\rho c\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}R_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t+x/c)},

(17)

\displaystyle v^{\operatorname{trans}}(x,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t-x/c)},

\displaystyle\sigma^{\operatorname{trans}}(x,t)=-\rho c\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t-x/c)},

(18)

for some reflection and transmission coefficients
R_{k}
and
T_{k}
to be determined, and where we denote
\omega_{k}\equiv\omega+k\Omega
. One introduces the auxiliary quantities
\Psi_{k}=T_{k}-R_{k}
and
\Phi_{k}=T_{k}+R_{k}
.

2.4.1
Dealing with the first jump equation

Using (
) and (
), together with the definition of the jump operator, we find that

\displaystyle\llbracket v^{\operatorname{tot}}\rrbracket=e^{i\omega t}\left(-1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{k}e^{ik\Omega t}\right)

and

\displaystyle{\langle\!\langle}\sigma^{\text{tot}}{\rangle\!\rangle}=-\frac{\rho\,c}{2}e^{i\omega t}\left(1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{k}e^{ik\Omega t}\right).

Similarly, though it is slightly longer, we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\langle\!\langle}{C}(t)\,\sigma^{\operatorname{tot}}{\rangle\!\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\omega t}\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{-1}e^{-i\Omega t}-\frac{i\mathcal{C}}{2}\omega-\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{1}e^{+i\Omega t}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{i\omega t}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{k}\Psi_{k-1}-\frac{i\mathcal{C}}{2}\omega_{k}\Psi_{k}+\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{k}\Psi_{k+1}\right)e^{ik\Omega t},

where we have introduced the reduced quantity
\mathcal{C}=\rho\,c\,{C}_{0}
. Therefore the first jump condition (
5a
), after dividing through by
e^{i\omega t}
and rearranging slightly becomes

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}\omega_{k}-i\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4}\Psi_{k-1}+\left(1+\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{C}}{2}+i\frac{\mathcal{C}}{2}\omega_{k}\right)\Psi_{k}-\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}\omega_{k}-i\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4}\Psi_{k+1}\right)e^{ik\Omega t}\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\hskip 19.91684pt=\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}\omega_{-1}-i\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4}\,e^{-i\Omega t}+\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{C}}{2}-i\frac{\mathcal{C}\omega}{2}\right)-\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}\omega_{1}-i\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4}\,e^{+i\Omega t},\end{array}

(19)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{C}=\rho\,c\,{Q}_{C_{0}}
.
Since the
e^{ik\Omega t}
functions are linearly independent, we obtain this system in matrix form :

\mathbb{A}(\mathcal{C},\varepsilon_{C},\mathcal{Q}_{C},\varepsilon_{Q_{C}},\omega,\Omega)\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}={\bf V}(\mathcal{C},\varepsilon_{C},\mathcal{Q}_{C},\varepsilon_{Q_{C}},\omega,\Omega),

(20)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}\equiv\left(\ldots\,\Psi_{-N},\,\ldots\,\Psi_{-1},\,\Psi_{0},\,\Psi_{1},\,\ldots\,\Psi_{N},\,\ldots\right)^{\top},\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle{\bf V}(\mathcal{C},\varepsilon_{C},\mathcal{Q}_{C},\varepsilon_{{Q}_{C}},\omega,\Omega)=\left(\ldots\,0\,\ldots\,\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{-1}-i\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4},\,1-\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{C}}{2}-i\frac{\mathcal{C}}{2}\omega,\,-\frac{\mathcal{C}\varepsilon_{C}}{4}\omega_{1}+i\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{C}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}}{4},\,\ldots\,0\,\ldots\right)^{\top},\end{array}

(21)

and
\mathbb{A}
is a tri-banded antidiagonal matrix. Now we can truncate this system so that
\mathbb{A}
becomes a

(2N+1)\times(2N+1)
matrix, and the truncated vector

\boldsymbol{\Psi}
can be recovered by simple inversion.

2.4.2
Dealing with the second jump equation

One obtains directly

\displaystyle\llbracket\sigma^{\operatorname{tot}}\rrbracket=\rho c\,e^{i\omega t}\left(1-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Phi_{k}e^{ik\Omega t}\right)

and

\displaystyle{\langle\!\langle}v^{\text{tot}}{\rangle\!\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\omega t}\left(1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Phi_{k}e^{ik\Omega t}\right).

Similarly, we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\langle\!\langle}{M}(t){v^{\operatorname{tot}}}{\rangle\!\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\omega t}\left(-\frac{{M}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{M}}{4}\omega_{-1}e^{-i\Omega t}+\frac{i{M}_{0}}{2}\omega+\frac{{M}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{M}}{4}\omega_{1}e^{+i\Omega t}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{i\omega t}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{{M}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{M}}{4}\omega_{k}\Phi_{k-1}+\frac{i{M}_{0}}{2}\omega_{k}\Phi_{k}-\frac{{M}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{M}}{4}\omega_{k}\Phi_{k+1}\right)e^{ik\Omega t}.

Upon introducing
\mathcal{M}={M}_{0}/(\rho c)
and
\mathcal{Q}_{M}={Q}_{M_{0}}/(\rho c)
, the second jump condition (
5b
) leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}\,\varepsilon_{M}\omega_{k}-i\mathcal{Q}_{M}\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}}{4}\Phi_{k-1}+\left(1+\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}{2}+i\frac{\mathcal{M}}{2}\omega_{k}\right)\Phi_{k}-\frac{\mathcal{M}\,\varepsilon_{M}\omega_{k}-i\mathcal{Q}_{M}\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}}{4}\Phi_{k+1}\right)e^{ik\Omega t}\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\hskip 19.91684pt=\frac{\mathcal{M}\,\varepsilon_{M}\omega_{-1}-i\mathcal{Q}_{M}\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}}{4}\,e^{-i\Omega t}+\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}{2}-i\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega}{2}\right)-\frac{\mathcal{M}\,\varepsilon_{M}\omega_{1}-i\mathcal{Q}_{M}\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}}{4}\,e^{+i\Omega t}.\end{array}

(22)

We end up with a system of the form

\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{M},\varepsilon_{M},\mathcal{Q}_{M},\varepsilon_{Q_{M}},\omega,\Omega)\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}={\bf W}(\mathcal{M},\varepsilon_{M},\mathcal{Q}_{M},\varepsilon_{Q_{M}},\omega,\Omega).

(23)

The matrix
\mathbb{B}
and the vector
{\bf W}
are obtained by replacing
(\mathcal{C},\varepsilon_{C},\mathcal{Q}_{C},\varepsilon_{Q_{C}},\omega,\Omega)
by
(\mathcal{M},\varepsilon_{M},\mathcal{Q}_{M},\varepsilon_{Q_{M}},\omega,\Omega)
in
\mathbb{A}
and
{\bf V}
. The vector
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is

(\cdots,\Phi_{-N},\cdots,\Phi_{-1},\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{N},\cdots)^{\top}.

As before, this system can be solved for any truncation by simple inversion.

2.4.3
Final reflection and transmission coefficients

Once
\Psi_{k}
and
\Phi_{k}
are computed, the reflection and transmission coefficients are given by :

R_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{k}-\Psi_{k}),\qquad T_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\Psi_{k}+\Phi_{k}).

(24)

Two limit-cases are highlighted :

—

Without modulation, the only non-zero coefficients are
R_{0}
and
T_{0}
. These coefficients are given in Appendix
A
;

—

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{C}
,
\varepsilon_{M}=\varepsilon_{C}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{C}=\mathcal{Q}_{M}
and
\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}=\varepsilon_{Q_{M}}
, then the two matrix systems (
20
) and (
23
) are exactly the same, and so
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, which leads to
R_{k}=0
for all
k
. This property of
impedance matching
is summarized in the following proposition.

{Proposition}

Let us consider a homogeneous medium of impedance
Z=\rho\,c
. Under the following equalities

{M}_{0}=Z^{2}\,{C}_{0},\qquad{Q}_{M_{0}}=Z^{2}\,{Q}_{C_{0}},\qquad\varepsilon_{C}=\varepsilon_{M},\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathcal{Q}_{C}}=\varepsilon_{\mathcal{Q}_{M}},

(25)

then no reflected wave is generated at the modulated interface.

Proposition
2.4.3
generalizes the impedance matching condition already obtained with static jump conditions and no dissipation in
[
66
]
.

{Remark}

Proposition
2.4.3
can be extended to any periodic modulation, while respecting
{M}(t)=Z^{2}{C}(t)
and
{Q}_{M}(t)=Z^{2}{Q}_{C}(t)
, using the linearity of the equations and the fact that periodic functions can be decomposed into Fourier series.

3
Numerical modeling

3.1
Numerical scheme

First-order formulation

A velocity-stress formulation of the evolution equations is used. We introduce the compact notations

\begin{array}[]{lll}{\bf U}(x,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v(x,t)\\
\sigma(x,t)\end{array}\right),&{\bf F}(x,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{F(x,t)}{\rho(x)}\\
0\end{array}\right),&\\[12.0pt]
{\bf A}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\\
-E(x)&0\end{array}\right),&{\bf B}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\displaystyle{C}(t)\\
{M}(t)&0\end{array}\right),&{\bf E}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\displaystyle{Q}_{C}(t)\\
{Q}_{M}(t)&0\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(26)

where
{\bf A}
and
{\bf E}
can be discontinuous at the interface.
From (
1b
) and from (
5b
), one obtains the boundary-value problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf A}\,\partial_{x}{\bf U}={\bf F},\qquad x\neq x_{0},

(27a)

\displaystyle\left\llbracket{\bf U}(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket=\partial_{t}\left({\bf B}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}{\bf U}(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle}\right)+{\bf E}(t)\,{\langle\!\langle}{\bf U}(\cdot,t){\rangle\!\rangle}.

(27b)

It is assumed that the body force is zero at the interface :
{\bf F}(x_{0},t)={\bf 0}
, which is not restrictive.

ADER-4 scheme

A uniform grid with space step
\Delta x
and time step
\Delta t
is introduced, to determine an approximation
{\bf U}_{j}^{n}
of
{\bf U}(x_{j}=j\,\Delta x,t_{n}=n\,\Delta t)
. Integration of (
27a
) is done by a fourth-order ADER scheme. This explicit two-step finite-difference scheme is fourth-order accurate in space and time, using a stencil of 2 neighboring points on the right and on the left. It is stable under the usual CFL condition

\zeta=\max(c(x))\,\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\leq 1,

(28)

where
c(x)=\sqrt{\frac{E(x)}{\rho(x)}}
is the sound speed.
The reader is referred to
[
69
]
for implementation details.

3.2
Immersed interface method for time-modulated jump conditions

Discretization of the modulated jump conditions (
27b
) is done by implementing the Explicit Simplified Interface Method (ESIM). The philosophy of this numerical method is as follows : based on the jump conditions and the interface geometry, smooth extensions of the solution
{\bf U}
on either side of the interface are constructed at each time step (Figure
1
). Evaluation of these extensions leads to modified values
{\bf U}^{\star}_{j}
of the solution. At a point
x_{j}
on one side of the interface, the integration scheme (ADER 4 here) then uses the usual values
{\bf U}_{j}^{n}
if
x_{j}
is on the same side, and
{\bf U}_{j}^{\star}
if
x_{j}
is on the other side of the interface. This method has been applied to various interface problems, notably to constant imperfect interfaces in 1D
[
67
]
and 2D
[
68
]
.

Figure 1:
Scheme of the principle of ESIM at an interface with a discontinuous solution
{\bf U}(x,t)
(blue line) and its smooth extensions
{\bf U}^{\star}(x,t)
(red dotted lines).

An important ingredient of ESIM is to determine the jump conditions satisfied by the solution and its successive spatial derivatives. As the jump conditions here vary as a function of time, an important modification has to be made to the method, as detailed now. From (
2
) and (
27b
), we obtain

{\bf U}^{+}-\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{t}({\bf B\,U}^{+})-\frac{1}{2}\,{\bf E}\,{\bf U}^{+}={\bf U}^{-}+\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{t}({\bf B\,U}^{-})+\frac{1}{2}\,{\bf E}\,{\bf U}^{-},

(29)

where the superscripts
\pm
refer to the limit values, as in (
3
). Using (
27a
), we then have

\left({\bf I}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}{\bf B}+{\bf E}\right)\right)\,{\bf U}^{+}+\frac{1}{2}{\bf B\,A}^{+}\,\partial_{x}{\bf U}^{+}=\left({\bf I}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}{\bf B}+{\bf E}\right)\right)\,{\bf U}^{-}-{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf B\,A}^{-}\,\partial_{x}{\bf U}^{-},

(30)

where
{\bf I}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix. Successive time-derivatives of (
30
) up to an order
k
yields the matrix relation

{\bf C}_{k}^{+}(t)\,{\bf U}_{k}^{+}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k+1}^{+}={\bf C}_{k}^{-}(t)\,{\bf U}_{k}^{-}+{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k+1}^{-},

(31)

with the
2\,(k+1)
vectors of limit values

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{\bf U}_{k}^{\pm}=\left({\bf U}^{\pm},\,\partial_{x}{\bf U}^{\pm},\cdots,\,\partial^{k}_{x}{\bf U}^{\pm}\right)^{\top},\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k+1}^{\pm}=\left({\bf 0},\,{\bf 0},\cdots,\,{\bf 0},\,\mp\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2}\,{\bf B}\left({\bf A}^{\pm}\right)^{k+1}\,\partial_{x}^{k+1}{\bf U}^{\pm}\right)^{\top},\end{array}

(32)

where
{\bf C}_{k}^{\pm}
are
(2(k+1))\times(2(k+1))
time-dependent matrices,
{\bf 0}
is the
2\times 2
zero matrix, and
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k+1}^{\pm}
are
2(k+1)
vectors. The matrices
{\bf C}_{k}^{\pm}
depend on the bulk parameters (through the matrices
{\bf A}^{\pm}
) and the successive time derivatives of the jump conditions (through the matrix
{\bf B}(t)
and
{\bf E}(t)
in (
26
)). Moreover, the matrices
{\bf C}^{\pm}
have a structure of block lower diagonal matrix ; the
2\times 2
blocks below the diagonal depend on the successive time derivatives of
{C}(t)
,
{M}(t)
,
{Q}_{C}(t)
and
{Q}_{M}(t)
.

Neglecting the
(k+1)
-th spatial derivatives (
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k+1}^{\pm}={\bf 0}_{2(k+1)}
) and inverting (
31
) yields

\begin{array}[]{lll}{\bf U}_{k}^{+}&=&\left({\bf C}_{k}^{+}(t)\right)^{-1}\,{\bf C}_{k}^{-}(t)\,{\bf U}_{k}^{-},\\[8.0pt]
&:=&{\bf D}_{k}(t)\,{\bf U}_{k}^{-}.\end{array}

(33)

The transfer matrices
{\bf D}_{k}(t)
of order
k
(
33
) are the building-block of the ESIM. These are used to calculate extrapolation matrices, which are used to determine the modified values
{\bf U}^{\star}_{i}
at each time step. Once computed, the rest of the method follows the usual lines. The reader is referred e.g. to
[
70
]
for practical details. Four remarks can be made concerning the implementation and the properties of the ESIM :

—

The transfer matrices
{\bf D}_{k}
in (
33
) depend on time. Consequently, the extrapolation matrices must be recalculated at each time step. This is an important difference with the cases previously dealt with by ESIM, where the extrapolation matrices were calculated once during a preprocessing step ;

—

The calculation of
{\bf D}_{k}
is tedious, especially for large values of
k
. A naive idea to simplify this calculation is to
freeze
the jump conditions at the instant under consideration, so that
\partial_{t}{\bf B}={\bf 0}
in (
30
), and
{\bf D}_{k}(t)
depends only on
{\bf B}
and
{\bf E}
and not on their successive derivatives. This is a bad idea : the time evolution of the modulated interface conditions is then poorly described, leading to inaccurate numerical results ;

—

The problem under study has similarities with other time-varying interface problems. But here we are in a
kinematic
case, where the jump conditions are known and imposed, unlike
dynamic
cases such as
[
71
]
, where the jump conditions are unknown and depend on the solution in a non-trivial way ;

—

The optimal choice of the derivation order
k
is important. In the case of static jump conditions (see e.g.
[
67
]
), a numerical analysis of the ESIM has been carried out, and we will reuse these results here. In the limit case where the interface disappears (
{C}_{0}=0
,
{M}_{0}=0
,
{Q}_{C_{0}}=0
and
{Q}_{M_{0}}=0
),
{\bf U}_{j}^{\star}={\bf U}_{j}^{n}
is needed to recover the scheme in homogeneous medium. This consistency property is obtained if
k
is odd and
k\geq 2s-1
, where
s
is the width of the stencil. Additionally, the local truncation error of a
r
-th order accurate scheme is maintained at the interface if
k\geq r
. Putting together these two conditions yields

k=\max\left(r,2s-1\right)\,\mbox{and $k$ odd}.

(34)

Here
r=4
and
s=2
, so that
k=5
is the optimal value, which will be used in the numerical experiments. We will examine numerically the convergence properties using this value in Section
3.4
since we have no proof that it still holds in the current modulated case.

5
Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the interaction of waves with an interface whose jump conditions are modulated in time.
These jump conditions can be experimentally implemented by considering e.g. membranes, spring-mass systems or vibrating plates whose thickness is small compared to the wavelength.
Numerical methods for time-domain simulations have been developed. Numerical experiments have demonstrated and validated theoretically predicted properties : amplification of the energy, harmonic generation, impedance matching, and non-reciprocity.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.3
(only one sinusoidally modulated interface parameter, the other parameter being zero), we proved that no parametric amplification resonance can occur. That said, we were not able to conclude on this when these assumptions were relaxed. A more complete theoretical study of the energy evolution remains to be carried out, especially in order to better understand the high-frequency regime.

While mentioning resonance, it is worth noting here that we have only considered non-resonant jump conditions. A relaxation of this assumption could be envisaged, and would allow, for example, to study the case of Helmholtz resonators whose physical or geometrical properties vary with time
[
32
,
63
]
.

Another natural follow-up to this work is to consider a set of modulated interfaces, in phase or not, and periodically distributed in space. In the case where the wavelength is large compared with the spacing between interfaces, asymptotic homogenization can be applied. The work carried out in
[
72
]
for the case of non-linear but static interfaces is currently generalized to the case of modulated interfaces. This work, currently under finalization, will be the subject of a forthcoming publication. It could also be interesting to study the effective properties induced by a network of time-modulated resonators, as considered in
[
73
,
74
]
.

Here we prove Proposition
2.3
. The assumptions are :

—

\rho_{0}\,c_{0}=\rho_{1}\,c_{1}=Z
;

—

source point at
x_{s}<x_{0}
, with a bounded source function

;

—

T
-periodic modulation of the compliance and of the corresponding dissipation parameter (
38

\phi_{X}(t+T)=\phi_{X}(t)=\varphi_{X}(\Omega t)
,

, and

;

—

continuity of stress (
{M}_{0}=0
and
{Q}_{M}=0
). Similar conclusions hold with a modulated inertia and continuity of velocity (
{C}_{0}=0
and
{Q}_{C}=0
).

The mean of a function
w(t)
over
T
is denoted by

\overline{w}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}w(t)\,dt.

(59)

We need the following Lemmas.

Lemma 1

Consider the Ordinary Differential Equation

y^{\prime}(t)=\alpha(t)\,y+\beta(t),

(60)

where
\alpha(t)
is a
T
-periodic function, and
\beta(t)
is bounded. If
\overline{\alpha}\leq 0
, then
y(t)
is bounded.

Proof C.1
.

The integrating factor method yields

y(t)=e^{A(t)}\left(y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A(s)}\,\beta(s)\,ds\right),\qquad\mbox{with }A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)\,ds.

(61)

If
\overline{\alpha}\leq 0
, then
e^{A(t)}
does not grow exponentially and
y(t)
is bounded.

Lemma 2

Let the function

\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})=1+\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{b}}\left(\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}-1\right)

(62)

defined on the domain
{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}/\,|\mathfrak{a}|<1,\,|\mathfrak{b}|<1,\,\mathfrak{b}\neq 0\}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})>0
for all
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})\in{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof C.2
.

One introduces

\mathfrak{h}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})=-\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{b}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\right).

For
(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})\in{\mathcal{D}}
, one has

1-\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\in(0,1),\quad\left|\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{b}}\right|<\frac{1}{|\mathfrak{b}|},

so that

|\mathfrak{h}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})|<\frac{1}{|\mathfrak{b}|}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}\right):=H(\mathfrak{b}).

The real function
H
satisfies

H(\mathfrak{b})=\frac{|\mathfrak{b}|}{2}+o(\mathfrak{b})\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits_{\mathfrak{b}\rightarrow 0}0,\quad H(\mathfrak{b})\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits_{\mathfrak{b}\rightarrow\pm 1}1.

For
\mathfrak{b}>0
, its derivative writes

H^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b})=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}}{\mathfrak{b}^{2}\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{b}^{2}}}>0,

so that
H
is increasing from 0 (at
\mathfrak{b}=0
) to 1 (at
\mathfrak{b}=1
). Lastly,
H
is a continuous even function. For all
\mathfrak{b}\in(-1,1)
, one deduces that

0<H(\mathfrak{b})<1.

It follows
|\mathfrak{h}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})|<1
, and hence

\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})=1+\mathfrak{h}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b})>1-1=0,

which concludes the proof.

Following the same lines as in Appendix
B
, it can be proven that
y(t)=v^{+}(t)
satisfies the Ordinary Differential Equation

y^{\prime}=a(t)\,y+b(t),

(63)

with

a(t)=-\frac{1}{Z}\,\frac{2+Z\,\left({C}^{\prime}(t)+{Q}_{C}(t)\right)}{{C}(t)},\qquad b(t)=-\frac{1}{Z}\,\frac{S(t)}{{C}(t)}.

(64)

The function
a(t)
is
T
-periodic, and
b(t)
is bounded. If
\overline{a}\leq 0
, then the conclusion of Lemma
1
holds and
y(t)
is bounded.
Let us consider again the modulation
\varphi_{X}(t)=\sin(t)
, with
X=C,Q_{C}
. From (
64
), one obtains

a(t)=-\frac{1}{Z\,{C}_{0}}\,f(t),

(65)

with

f(t)=\frac{2+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}+\gamma\,\cos(\Omega t)+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}\sin(\Omega t)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(\Omega t)},\qquad\gamma=Z\,{C}_{0}\,\varepsilon_{C}\,\Omega.

(66)

The mean value of
f
writes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\overline{f}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{2+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}+\gamma\,\cos(\Omega t)+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}\sin(\Omega t)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(\Omega t)}\,dt,\\[10.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left((2+Z{Q}_{C_{0}})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{ds}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}+\gamma\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos(s)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}\,ds\,+\,Z{Q}_{C_{0}}\varepsilon_{Q_{C}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(s)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}\,ds\right).\end{array}

(67)

Classical change of variables, and integration of a periodic function over one period, gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{ds}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}=\frac{2\,\pi}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{C}^{2}}},\qquad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos(s)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}\,ds=0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(s)}{1+\varepsilon_{C}\,\sin(s)}\,ds=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon_{C}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{C}^{2}}}\right),\end{array}

(68)

Straightforward calculations give

\overline{f}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{C}^{2}}}\left(2+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}\,\mathfrak{g}(\varepsilon_{C},\varepsilon_{Q_{C}})\right),

(69)

where
\mathfrak{g}(.,.)
has been introduced in (
62
). Equation (
65
) and Lemma
2
yield

\overline{a}=-\frac{1}{Z\,{C}_{0}}\,\frac{2+Z{Q}_{C_{0}}\,\mathfrak{g}(\varepsilon_{C},\varepsilon_{Q_{C}})}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{C}^{2}}}<0.

(70)

Lemma
1
implies that the solution of (
63
) is bounded, which concludes the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
