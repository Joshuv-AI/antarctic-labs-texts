Construction of Solutions with Extraordinary Gradient Amplification and Localization for Schrödinger Equations


Abstract
. This paper constructs solutions to linear and nonlinear Schrödinger-type equations in two and three spatial dimensions that exhibit prescribed, extraordinary gradient amplification and localization. For any finite time interval [ 0 , T ] [0,T] , any prescribed collection of n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} distinct points on ∂ D \partial D , where D D is the compact support of the anisotropic coefficients, lower-order terms, or nonlinearities, and any amplitude threshold ℳ > 0 \mathcal{M}>0 , we show that one can design smooth initial and/or boundary data such that the spatial gradients of the resulting solutions exceed ℳ \mathcal{M} in neighborhoods of these points outside D D for almost every t ∈ [ 0 , T ] t\in[0,T] . Moreover, the ratio between the local C 1 , 1 2 C^{1,\frac{1}{2}} -norm of the solution near each prescribed point outside D D and the C 1 , 1 2 C^{1,\frac{1}{2}} -norm inside D D is bounded from below by ℳ / 2 \mathcal{M}/2 for almost every t ∈ [ 0 , T ] t\in[0,T] . We further prove that the spatial measure of the regions where the gradient magnitude exceeds ℳ \mathcal{M} tends to zero as ℳ → ∞ \mathcal{M}\to\infty , demonstrating that the amplification phenomenon is highly localized. This effect arises from the structure of the Schrödinger-type equation combined with carefully designed input profiles. From a physical perspective, the results provide a deterministic analogue of localization phenomena observed in quantum scattering and Anderson localization. In addition, the observed trade-off between extreme spatial localization and large gradient amplification is fully consistent with the spirit of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: while the latter is traditionally formulated in a global L 2 L^{2} space–frequency framework, our results offer a complementary deterministic manifestation at the level of localized spatial gradients in Schrödinger dynamics. Keywords: Schrödinger equation; localized gradient amplification; localized surface resonance; customized Anderson localization; quantum wave scattering; Heisenberg uncertainty principle 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q55,35B65,35P25,35B40,47A75,35B30,81Q10

1.2.
Existing studies and motivation

We begin by recalling several classical results on singularity formation in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

iu_{t}=\Delta u+\mu|u|^{p-1}u,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0,\qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x).

Blow-up refers to the existence of a finite time
T>0
such that a suitable norm of the solution
u(t)
becomes unbounded as
t\to T
. In the
L^{2}
-supercritical case
p>1+4/d
, Glassey
[
11
]
showed that for the focusing equation
(\mu=-1)
, smooth initial data with sufficient decay at infinity and satisfying certain energy and momentum conditions lead to finite-time blow-up. In the
L^{2}
-subcritical case
1<p<1+4/d
, global well-posedness holds for the defocusing equation
(\mu=1)
for all finite-energy data, whereas in the focusing case
(\mu=-1)
global existence is guaranteed below specific mass-energy thresholds
[
10
]
. In the
L^{2}
-critical case
p=1+4/d
, Merle
[
16
]
demonstrated that solutions can blow up at any prescribed finite set of points, with the blow-up profile concentrating around these locations while vanishing elsewhere. Further refinements of multi-point blow-up, including log–log type concentration, are given in
[
8
]
. These results highlight the remarkable flexibility of singularity formation in the critical regime, where spatial localization can be prescribed precisely. For general background on the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, see
[
20
,
19
,
4
]
.

In the present work, we establish an analogous, yet fundamentally different,
phenomenon for certain linear and nonlinear Schrödinger equations, where
solutions remain local
H^{3}
-regular over the entire interval
[0,T]
but
nevertheless develop sharply localized regions of high spatial gradient at
arbitrarily selected points. This controlled formation of high-gradient regions
draws conceptual inspiration from localization phenomena extensively studied in
both mathematical and physical Schrödinger theory. In systems with random or
quasi-periodic potentials, Anderson localization
[
2
,
6
]

demonstrates that eigenfunctions may concentrate strongly around specific
spatial locations even in the absence of nonlinear effects. Such behavior plays
a fundamental role in quantum scattering, wave propagation in disordered media,
and the spectral analysis of Schrödinger operators. More generally, classical
quantum scattering theory shows that potential landscapes, boundary conditions,
and geometric trapping can generate highly localized wave functions,
resonances, or tunneling effects
[
21
,
24
,
17
]
, which
govern transport and energy distribution in quantum systems.

From a physical perspective, the localization mechanism uncovered in this work
is also consistent with the qualitative content of the Heisenberg uncertainty
principle
[
12
]
. The uncertainty
principle asserts a fundamental trade-off between spatial localization and
frequency (or momentum) concentration, typically formulated in a global

L^{2}
-based phase-space framework. In our setting, although no probabilistic
or Fourier-analytic uncertainty inequality is invoked, an analogous trade-off
emerges at the level of deterministic PDE dynamics: as the spatial gradients of
the solution become arbitrarily large near prescribed points, the regions in
which this amplification occurs necessarily shrink in measure. Thus, extreme
spatial localization is accompanied by increasingly large gradients, reflecting
a complementary manifestation of uncertainty-type behavior within
Schrödinger-type evolutions. Our results therefore provide a rigorous and
deterministic illustration of how localization and high-frequency effects can
coexist in regular solutions, bridging ideas from PDE analysis, quantum
scattering, and foundational principles of quantum mechanics.

1.3.
Main contributions and technical discussions

Our work constructs novel solutions for a broad class of Schrödinger-type equations defined by their behavior inside and outside a bounded domain
D
. Specifically, we consider Schrödinger equations that reduce to the standard Schrödinger equation outside
D
, while inside
D
they are governed by variable-coefficient operators and may include arbitrary polynomial nonlinearities. These solutions with localized gradient amplification behavior exhibit the following precise characteristics:

(i)

For any prescribed
\mathcal{M}>0
and any prescribed number

of distinct points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
on
\partial D
, we construct inputs (specifically, initial and/or boundary inputs) that yield solutions
u
and
U
to problems (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), respectively. In the specified regions near these points, the
L^{\infty}
-norms of
\nabla u
and
\nabla U
exhibit localized gradient amplification bounded below by
\mathcal{M}
for almost every time
t
in the interval
[0,T]
, where
T
can be chosen arbitrarily in advance. Moreover, the amplification is quantitatively contrasted with the behavior inside
D
: the ratio between the local
C^{1,1/2}
-norm of the solution in each amplification region outside
D
and the
C^{1,1/2}
-norm of the solution restricted to
\overline{D}
is bounded from below by

for almost every

. This demonstrates that the observed amplification is not merely absolute, but also relative to the interior regularity of the solution. For the nonlinear problem (

(ii)

Due to the well-posedness of problems (

) and (

), the measure of the localized gradient amplification regions—where the gradient of solutions is bounded below by

—vanishes for almost every

as

and

. Stronger localized gradient amplification corresponds to smaller spatial support of these regions. This quantitative decay property of the measure with respect to

contrasts with classical blowup, where the singular set typically has positive measure at blowup time. Importantly, under the specified smooth and finely tuned inputs—even with the weaker regularity coefficients specified in Items 3 and 5 of (

.

This localized gradient amplification behavior is realized through a careful combination of spectral theory for transmission eigenfunctions of the interior transmission problem
[
5
]
and Herglotz wave function approximations. By analyzing the spectral properties of these eigenfunctions, we identify key structures that allow for the selective amplification of gradients near pre-specified points. Herglotz approximations are then employed to explicitly construct smooth initial and/or boundary inputs that excite these structures, effectively transferring the amplification to the solutions of both the linear problem (
1.1
) and the nonlinear problem (
1.2
). The resulting inputs, detailed in (

. The floor function is written as
\lfloor x\rfloor
.

For an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an integer
k\geq 0
, and
0<\gamma\leq 1
, the Hölder space
C^{k,\gamma}(\bar{U})
consists of all functions
u\in C^{k}(\bar{U})
for which the norm

\|u\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(\bar{U})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\sup_{\bar{U}}|D^{\alpha}u|+\sum_{|\alpha|=k}[D^{\alpha}u]_{C^{\gamma}(\bar{U})}

is finite, where
[v]_{C^{\gamma}(\bar{U})}
denotes the
\gamma
-Hölder seminorm:

[v]_{C^{\gamma}(\bar{U})}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in U\\
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})|}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{\gamma}}.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and let
X
be a Banach space. The Bochner–Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(0,T;X)
consists of all functions
u\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
such that the first weak derivative
u^{\prime}
exists and belongs to
L^{p}(0,T;X)
. The norm on
W^{1,p}(0,T;X)
is defined as

\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(0,T;X)}=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|_{X}^{p}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}^{p}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{if }1\leq p<\infty,\\
\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\|u(t)\|_{X}+\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}\right)&\text{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Similarly, the Bochner Sobolev space
W^{2,p}(0,T;X)
consists of all functions
u\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
such that both the first and second weak derivatives
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
exist and belong to
L^{p}(0,T;X)
. The norm on
W^{2,p}(0,T;X)
is given by

\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(0,T;X)}=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|_{X}^{p}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}^{p}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\|u^{\prime\prime}(t)\|_{X}^{p}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{if }1\leq p<\infty,\\
\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\|u(t)\|_{X}+\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}+\|u^{\prime\prime}(t)\|_{X}\right)&\text{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Equipped with these norms, both
W^{1,p}(0,T;X)
and
W^{2,p}(0,T;X)
are Banach spaces.

Step 1: a priori estimates for problem (

Introduce the notation

Taking the complex conjugate of (

Note that

\displaystyle A[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U};t]

for all

Proceeding as before, we derive

\displaystyle=A[\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{1};0]-2\Re\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}F_{1}^{\prime}(x,s)\,\overline{\partial_{s}\mathcal{U}_{1}(x,s)}\,dx\,ds

depends only on

,
l_{0}
,
D
,
\|\mathbf{A}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}
,

\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
, and
\|\alpha_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
.

Proof.

Properties 1 follows immediately from the compact support in
D
of
\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}_{2}(\cdot,t)
,
c(\cdot,t)
,
\alpha_{k}(\cdot,t)
for
t\in[0,T]
,
k=2,\dots,l_{0}
combined with the expressions defining
\hat{c}
,
\hat{N}(\mathscr{U})
and
F_{2}
.

For properties 2, note that
\alpha_{k}
are smooth, so all their derivatives are bounded in
D
. It remains to show
\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(D)
for all multi-indices
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\leqslant 3
. By the Sobolev embedding theorem, (
3.17
), and (
3.18
), we have

(5.4)

\|\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leqslant C\|\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{H^{2}(D)}\leqslant C\|H_{g}\|_{H^{5}(D)}\leqslant C\varepsilon\leqslant C,\quad\forall|\alpha|\leqslant 3,\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

where the final inequality holds since
\varepsilon\leqslant 1
(See Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.2
). Consequently,

\|\hat{c}(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{2,\infty}(D)}\leqslant C\left(\|c_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{W^{2,\infty}(D)}+\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\|u_{0}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(D)}^{k-1}\right)\leqslant C\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{l_{0}-1}\varepsilon^{k}\right)\leqslant C,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
.

For the source term, using the Banach algebra property of
H^{3}(D)
and
(
3.17
), (
5.3
), and since
\varepsilon\leq 1
,
we obtain

(5.5)

\|F_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{3}(D)}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l_{0}}\|H_{g}\|_{H^{5}(D)}^{k}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l_{0}}\varepsilon^{k}\leq C\varepsilon,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

We now establish the well-posedness of problem (
5.1
) via Banach’s fixed point theorem, combined with an estimate for the nonlinear term
F_{2}
. This approach yields an explicit characterization of the solution’s existence time
T
in terms of the parameter
\varepsilon
. We show that
T
admits a uniform positive lower bound independent of
\varepsilon
and, moreover, becomes arbitrarily large for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, we prove that the Hölder norm of the solution is bounded by
C\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for some constant
C>0
.

Theorem 5.2
.

There exists a time
T>0

(
given below by (
5.21
)
)
, depending on
\varepsilon
,
d
,
l_{0}
,
D
,
\|\mathbf{A}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}
,
\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
, and
\|\alpha_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
, such that problem (
5.1
) admits a unique solution
\mathscr{U}
satisfying:

\mathscr{U}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

with the following properties:

(1)

Uniform bounds for
T
:

T\geqslant T_{\mathrm{lower}},

where
T_{\mathrm{lower}}
is given by (
5.22
) and independent of
\varepsilon
.

(2)

Hölder regularity:

\mathscr{U}\in L^{\infty}\big([0,T];C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big).

(3)

Smallness estimate:

\|\mathscr{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leqslant C_{6}\sqrt{\varepsilon},

where the constant
C_{6}
depends only on
d
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in four steps.

Step 1: Setup of fixed-point framework.

Define the Banach space

X:=\left\{{u}\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\right)\right\}

with norm

\|{u}\|:=\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\operatorname{ess}\sup}\|{u}(t)\|_{H^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}.

Introduce the stronger norm

|||{u}|||:=\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\operatorname{ess}\sup}\|{u}(t)\|_{H^{3}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)},

and the closed subset

X_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{{u}\in X\mid\ |||{u}|||\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon},{u}(0)=0\right\}.

For
\mathscr{V}\in X_{\varepsilon}
, define
\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}]
as the solution to the linear initial-value problem

(5.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\mathscr{U}+\hat{L}_{2}\mathscr{U}=F_{2}-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})&&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],\\
&\mathscr{U}(\mathbf{x},0)=0&&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{aligned}\right.

Step 2: A priori estimate for the problem (
5.6
).

For problem (
5.6
), we claim that we have the estimate

(5.7)

|||\mathscr{U}|||\leqslant C_{4}(T)T\varepsilon,

where constant
C_{4}(T)
depends continuously on
T
,
d
,
l_{0}
,
D
,
\|\mathbf{A}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}
,
\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
, and
\|\alpha_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,\infty}(D))}
, but not on
\varepsilon
. Moreover,
C_{4}(T)
is monotonically increasing in
T
with
C_{4}(0)<\infty
.

For any multi-index
\beta
with
0\leq|\beta|\leq 3
, apply
\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta}

to (
5.6
) to obtain

(5.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}+\hat{L}_{2}\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}+[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta},\hat{L}_{2}]\mathscr{U}=\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}F_{2}-\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})&&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],\\
&\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}(\mathbf{x},0)=0&&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{aligned}\right.

where
[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta},\hat{L}_{2}]=\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta}\hat{L}_{2}-\hat{L}_{2}\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta}
denotes the commutator,
which is a differential operator of order at most 2 in spatial derivatives. Multiply the first line in (
5.8
) with
\overline{\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}}
, use integration by parts one obtains,

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}\overline{\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}}d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a^{ij}_{2}(t)\partial_{i}\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}\overline{\partial_{j}\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta},\hat{L}_{2}]\mathscr{U}\overline{\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}F_{2}-\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}))\overline{\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}}d\mathbf{x}.

Then taking conjugate in (
5.9
), subtracting, taking the imaginary part, using integration by parts and Cauchy Schwartz inequality yields

(5.10)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}||\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(||\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{U}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\beta},\hat{L}_{2}]\mathscr{U}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}F_{2}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\partial^{\beta}_{\mathrm{x}}\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(||\mathscr{U}(t)||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||F_{2}(t)||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)

Summing over
\beta
with
0\leqslant|\beta|\leqslant 3
and applying Gronwall’s inequality, we obtain that

(5.11)

||\mathscr{U}(t)||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}||F_{2}(s)||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})||^{2}_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}ds

for a.e.
0\leqslant t\leqslant T
.

Next, we estimate
||\hat{N}(\mathscr{V})||_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. By (
5.2
),

\operatorname{supp}\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}(t))\subset D
. Since
\mathscr{V}\in X_{\varepsilon}
, then
||\mathscr{V}(t)||_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. By (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), we have
||u_{0}(t)||_{H^{3}(D)}\leqslant C\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Combine the above results and
\varepsilon<1
, we calculate

(5.12)

\displaystyle||\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}(t))||_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=||\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}(t))||_{H^{3}(D)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\sum_{i=2}^{k}||\mathscr{V}(t)||^{i}_{H^{3}(D)}||u_{0}(t)||^{k-i}_{H^{3}(D)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\sum_{i=2}^{k}\varepsilon^{\frac{i}{2}}\varepsilon^{k-i}\leqslant C\varepsilon,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. Combining estimates (
5.5
), (
5.11
), and (
5.12
), we obtain the desired bound (
5.7
). By the Hahn–Banach theorem, together with the preceding estimates (see, for example, Theorem 3.2 in
[
18
]
), we conclude that the linear problem (
5.6
) admits a unique solution
\mathscr{U}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Step 3: Contraction mapping argument and well-posedness of problem (
5.1
).

By properly choosing
T
, we show that

(5.13)

\mathscr{F}:X_{\varepsilon}\to X_{\varepsilon}

and

(5.14)

\|\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{1}]-\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{2}]\|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}\|,\ \forall\mathscr{V}_{1},\mathscr{V}_{2}\in X_{\varepsilon}.

To ensure that (
5.13
) holds, we select
T
such that, according to (
5.7
),

|||\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{V})|||=|||\mathscr{U}|||\leqslant C_{4}(T)T\varepsilon\leqslant\sqrt{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all }\mathscr{V}\in X_{\varepsilon}.

This condition is equivalent to

(5.15)

C_{4}(T)T\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.

For (
5.14
), let
\mathscr{U}_{1}=\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{1}]
,
\mathscr{U}_{2}=\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{2}]
, and
\mathscr{W}=\mathscr{U}_{1}-\mathscr{U}_{2}
. Then
\mathscr{W}
satisfies

(5.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\mathscr{W}+\hat{L}_{2}\mathscr{W}=-\big(\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{1})-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{2})\big)&&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],\\
&\mathscr{W}(\mathbf{x},0)=0&&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{aligned}\right.

Repeating the procedure to obtain estimate (
5.10
), for problem (
5.16
), we have, for any multiindex
\gamma
with
0\leqslant|\gamma|\leqslant 1
,

(5.17)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}||\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{W}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(||\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}\mathscr{W}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma},\hat{L}_{2}]\mathscr{W}(t)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}(\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{1})-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{2}))||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(||\mathscr{U}(t)||^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}(\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{1})-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{2}))||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right),

where
[\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma},\hat{L}_{2}]=\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}\hat{L}_{2}-\hat{L}_{2}\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}
denotes the commutator,
which is a differential operator of order 0 in spatial derivatives. By using (
5.2
), the fact that
\mathscr{V}_{1},\mathscr{V}_{2}\in X_{\varepsilon}
implies
\|\mathscr{V}_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for
i=1,2
and Lemma
5.1
, we calculate the term
||\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}(\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{1})-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{2}))||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
as follows,

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}(\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{1})-\hat{N}(\mathscr{V}_{2}))|^{2}d\mathrm{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2|\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}|^{2}|\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}\big(\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\alpha_{k}\sum_{i=2}^{k}\binom{k}{i}u_{0}^{k-i}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\mathscr{V}^{i-1-j}_{1}\mathscr{V}^{j}_{2}\big)|^{2}

\displaystyle+2|\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathscr{V}_{1}-\partial^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathscr{V}_{2}|^{2}|\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\alpha_{k}\sum_{i=2}^{k}\binom{k}{i}u_{0}^{k-i}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\mathscr{V}^{i-1-j}_{1}\mathscr{V}^{j}_{2}|^{2}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant C||\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}||^{2}_{H^{1}(D)}\sum_{k=2}^{l_{0}}\sum_{i=2}^{k}\varepsilon^{2k-2i}\varepsilon^{i-1}\leqslant C\varepsilon||\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Using (
5.18
) and summing over
\gamma
with
0\leqslant|\gamma|\leqslant 1
to (
5.17
), then applying Gronwall’s inequality and using the definition of
\|\cdot\|
yields

(5.19)

||\mathscr{W}||^{2}\leqslant C_{5}(T)\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}||\mathscr{V}_{1}(t)-\mathscr{V}_{2}(t)||^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}dt\leqslant\varepsilon C_{5}(T)T||\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}||^{2}

where
C_{5}(T)
has the same dependencies as
C_{4}(T)
and is monotonically increasing in
T
with
C_{5}(0)<\infty
. To ensure
\|\mathscr{W}\|=\|\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{1}]-\mathscr{F}[\mathscr{V}_{2}]\|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|\mathscr{V}_{1}-\mathscr{V}_{2}\|
, we require by (
5.19
),

(5.20)

C_{5}(T)T\leqslant\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}}.

Set the lifespan time

(5.21)

T_{\mathrm{lifespan}}:=\max\{T\mid C_{4}(T)T\leq\varepsilon^{-1/2}\ \text{and}\ C_{5}(T)T\leq(4\varepsilon)^{-1}\}.

For such

the map
\mathscr{F}
is a contraction on
X_{\varepsilon}
, and
Banach’s fixed point theorem gives a unique solution
\mathscr{U}\in X_{\varepsilon}

to (
5.1
). Sobolev embedding provides the Hölder regularity.

Step 4: Uniform lower bounds for

of problem (
5.1
).

Define

(5.22)

T_{\mathrm{lower}}:=\max\{T|C_{4}(T)T\leqslant 1\ \text{and}\ C_{5}(T)T\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\}.

Since
\varepsilon<1
, we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}>1
and
\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}>\frac{1}{4}
. As
C_{4}(T)T
and
C_{5}(T)T
are continuous and increasing, it follows that
T_{\mathrm{lifespan}}\geqslant T_{\mathrm{lower}}
, with

independent of
\varepsilon
which implies that properties 3 holds.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The argument in the proof of Theorem
5.2
shares the same spirit as that of Theorem 3 in Section 12.2 of
[
7
]
. To ensure that the mapping
\mathscr{F}
preserves the space
X_{\varepsilon}
and satisfies the contraction property, the time
T
is chosen to satisfy conditions (
5.15
) and (
5.20
). Notably, as
\varepsilon
decreases, the existence time
T
for problem (
1.2
) increases, thereby generalizing the conclusion of Theorem 3 in Section 12.2 of
[
7
]
in terms of the existence time
T
. In our setting,
T
can be chosen arbitrarily large, whereas Theorem 3 in Section 12.2 of
[
7
]
generally guarantees only a short-time existence. This enhancement stems from three key structural features: (i) the problem (
5.1
) has trivial initial data; (ii) the polynomial nonlinearity
\hat{N}
has compact support in
D
and an explicit form; and (iii) the source term
F_{2}
has an explicit expression, with its

-norm being small in terms of
\varepsilon
. Combined with the careful construction of the closed set

and the properties of the function

, these features enable the existence of a solution to problem (
5.1
) over an arbitrarily large time interval

.

We now proceed to prove Theorem
