A comprehensive look at Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV): from pathogenesis to prevention
Authors: Omid Salahi Ardekani, Parisa Hasannezhad, Yasaman Goudarzi, Arash Letafati, Zahra Taghiabadi, Mahshid Bahari, Negar Nayerain Jazi, Ali Qaraee Najafabadi, Somayeh Shatizadeh Malekshahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a formidable respiratory pathogen that particularly affects infants and children. RSV presents with diverse clinical severity, most often causing mild upper airway symptoms but sometimes progressing to severe and potentially fatal lower respiratory tract disease. This virus has a major global public health impact and places a substantial strain on healthcare systems. RSV exhibits distinct seasonal trends that vary across geographic locations and climate zones, and recognizing these patterns and their determinants is essential for policymakers to schedule RSV immunization programs effectively. Increasing attention has been directed toward the evolving landscape of RSV vaccines, particularly regarding their potential to prevent infection and reduce disease severity, especially among vulnerable populations. Until now, three RSV vaccines, Arexvy, Abrysvo, and mRESVIA, have received approval from the FDA. In addition, monoclonal antibodies such as Nirsevimab and Clesrovimab prevent severe RSV disease and are recommended for infants. Given the significant global impact of RSV, this narrative review addresses its multifaceted aspects, including epidemiology and global impact, clinical manifestations, therapeutic strategies, vaccine development, and its emerging potential as an oncolytic agent.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus, Epidemiology, Clinical manifestations, Antiviral treatments, Vaccines, Oncolytic virus

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a widespread and contagious virus that affects people of all ages [1]. However, RSV tends to cause more serious disease in infants, older adults, and people with compromised immune systems [2]. RSV affects the upper and lower respiratory tracts, causing respiratory infections, and may also be responsible for hospitalizing infants [3]. First, RSV was discovered in 1956 by Dr. Morris and colleagues [4]. They isolated the virus from a chimpanzee with a respiratory illness with coryza symptoms and named it Chimpanzee Coryza Agent (CCA) [4]. People who cared for chimpanzees also developed mild respiratory symptoms, less severe than those observed in animals. This was the first discovery of RSV as a distinct virus causing respiratory infections. Subsequent studies recognized the importance of RSV in causing respiratory infections in humans, especially infants and young children. The Long and Schneider strains were the first and native RSV strains isolated from the bronchopulmonary lavage of a child with bronchopneumonia and a patient with croup, respectively [5]. It is estimated that RSV affected approximately 33 million children under the age of 5 worldwide in 2019. Of these, around 3.6 million required hospitalization, and 101,400 deaths were reported [6]. Additionally, it is important to consider health inequality data, which suggests that global mortality rates are likely higher than official statistics indicate. Although RSV is the second leading cause of death in the first year of life worldwide, it can also infect frail older adults with chronic conditions [7, 8]. Among immunocompetent infants and adults, the infection usually follows a benign and self-limited course, presenting predominantly with mild symptoms affecting the upper respiratory tract. However, disease progression to the lower respiratory tract may occur in vulnerable populations including infants, young children, immunocompromised individuals, and older adults, resulting in clinical manifestations such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia [9, 10]. Studies have shown that RSV infects a significant proportion, 90 to 95%, of infants in their initial two years of life [11]. Additionally, contracting this infection in childhood can be associated with potentially health-threatening problems in the future. Some studies established an association between RSV infection during childhood and the development of asthma in adulthood [12]. Moreover, individuals with preexisting lung or heart conditions, older adults, and immunocompromised patients particularly those with hematologic cancers receiving active chemotherapy are at a heightened risk of developing severe RSV infection [13, 14]. A cohort study of older adults hospitalized with RSV cases revealed that the incidence of RSV in patients with chronic diseases such as pneumonia, chronic lung disease, heart failure, and asthma was 10.6%, 11.4%, 5.4%, and 7.2%, respectively [15]. Given the significant health challenges in caring for high-risk patients, the treatment of RSV focuses primarily on supportive care and preventive measures [3]. Additionally, Three RSV vaccines have been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [16]. These Three RSV vaccines, namely RSVPreF3 (Arexvy, GSK) and RSVpreF (Abrysvo, Pfizer), mRESVIA (mRNA-1345, Moderna) have recently been granted authorization for use in individuals aged 60 years and older [16].

This narrative review aims to comprehensively investigate the genome structure, epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical symptoms, and diagnostics of the RSV. The ultimate goal is to improve our understanding of the virus, develop more effective strategies to manage global outbreaks, and reduce mortality among high-risk individuals. Furthermore, we highlight RSV studies supporting the development of preventive measures, including vaccines and antiviral therapies, as well as research into the virus’s potential as an oncolytic agent.

Methods

For this narrative review, relevant references were retrieved from PubMed and Google Scholar with no date restrictions. Only English-language articles were considered. The search concepts included respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) structure and morphology, genome, epidemiology, transmission, risk factors, tropism, clinical manifestations, laboratory diagnosis, immune response, vaccines, treatment, control measures, and RSV oncolytic virus. Articles were included if they provided relevant information on the aforementioned factors.

Structure and morphology

Respiratory syncytial virus is a pleomorphic and lipoprotein-enveloped virus, classified within the Mononegavirales order, the Orthopneumovirus genus, and the Pneumoviridaefamily [17, 18]. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of RSV-infected cells revealed that the virus was 0.5–12 μm long and 130 nm in diameter [19]. The virion structure comprises a left-handed helical nucleocapsid, enclosed within an envelope derived from the host cell [20, 21]. There are also two main glycoproteins on the surface of RSV, the attachment glycoprotein (G) and the fusion glycoprotein (F), which play central roles in facilitating virus binding to and fusion with the host cell membrane, respectively [22].

Genome organization

The RSV genome is a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA molecule spanning 15,222 nucleotides and is encapsulated within the helical nucleocapsid [2, 21]. The RNA genome of RSV comprises 10 distinct coding regions that encode 11 viral proteins [23]. As depicted in (Fig. 1), From 3’ to 5’, it comprises genes for non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2), nucleocapsid (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), and a small hydrophobic protein (SH), glycoproteins (G), and fusion proteins (F), M2 proteins, and large polymerase proteins (L). Each gene is transcribed into an individual mRNA with a 5′ cap and a 3′ poly(A) tail for protein translation. The M2 gene produces two proteins, M2-1 and M2-2, from slightly overlapping ORFs [24]. In RSV transcription, the production of ten subgenomic mRNAs happens sequentially. The activity of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is regulated by cis-acting gene start (GS) and gene end (GE) sequences that flank each gene. The GS sequences, which are highly conserved in RSV and exhibit only minor variations, play a crucial role in directing the initiation of mRNA synthesis [25].

Viral proteins and their function

To induce a respiratory infection, the RSV particle must bind to the host’s receptors, enter the host cell, fuse into the host cell, and initiate infection. RSV then enters a multiplication phase, transcribes RNA, and translates its proteins. Each of these proteins contributes to a specific aspect of the pathogenic process [24]. Now, we will discuss the specific functional roles of RSV proteins in detail.

Attachment glycoprotein (G)

RSV’s surface attachment glycoprotein, or G protein, plays a crucial role in viral binding to host cells, RSV infection, and host immune disturbance [26]. This protein is a heavily glycosylated type II membrane protein consisting of 292 to 319 amino acids [27]. RSV-G exists in two forms: transmembrane-bound (tG) in full length and secretory or soluble form (sG), which is 65–74 aa shorter [22, 23]. The structure of the transmembrane RSV-G is composed of three main segments, including the cytoplasmic domain and transmembrane domain, as well as the ectodomain [28]. The ectodomain is composed of the mucin-like domains (variable gene regions), the conserved central domain (CCD), and the heparin-binding site [29]. CCD encompasses a CX3C chemokine motif that binds to the surface receptor CX3CR1 [7]. CX3CR1, expressed on the respiratory epithelium and immune cells such as T cells and NK cells, functions as the receptor for the human CX3CL1 chemokine, also known as fractalkine [7, 30]. An in vitro investigation of CX3CR1 interaction with RSV revealed that RSV’s G protein could inhibit transcription of genes associated with human ciliated cells [31]. Moreover, another in vitro study using a human alveolar cell line infected with RSV demonstrated that the motif located between amino acids 182 and 186 in the central region plays a crucial role, not only in attachment but also in evading immune responses [30]. Upon lung infection, the immune system releases the soluble chemokine fractalkine, which induces chemotaxis and attracts immune cells expressing the CX3CR1 receptor, guiding them toward the inflamed site [32]. The virus utilizes an immune evasion mechanism in which RSV competes with the host’s CX3CL1 chemokine for binding to CX3CR1 receptors on immune cells, such as T cells. This competition is facilitated by mimicry of the CX3CR1, resulting in reduced chemotaxis of CX3CR1 + T cells and NK cells [23]. It has been reported that RSV-G can hinder the secretion of type 1 interferon [33]. RSV-G impedes the secretion of type 1 IFN by interacting with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on the epithelial and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). Moreover, it reduces IFN-γ production by T cells [7]. RSV-G plays a pivotal role in the engagement and activation of TLR2 and TLR6 signaling in airway epithelial cells [34]. Also, research has shown that RSV-G can inhibit certain immune responses, such as the induction of IFN-β via TLR4 [35].

Furthermore, administration of RSV-G vaccination demonstrated the capacity to elicit peripheral Th2 cell memory responses, leading to increased lung inflammation and an eosinophilic reaction [30]. RSV-G also contributes to stimulating the host’s neutralizing antibody response during RSV infection. In a research study examining an RSV vaccine incorporating RSV-G and RSV-F in a mouse model, Brakel et al. observed neutralizing antibody secretion after vaccine administration [36]. Moreover, based on epitope variation in the G protein, RSV is categorized into two antigenic groups, A and B [37].

Fusion proteins (F)

Fusion glycoprotein (F) is a trimer comprising 574 aa. It serves as one of the envelope proteins that facilitate the virus’s attachment to the host cell and enable the incorporation of the viral genome into the host cell [7, 38]. The fusion protein triggers the formation of syncytia, a pathological manifestation of RSV infection in which infected cells fuse with neighboring cells, leading to the formation of multinucleated giant cells [37, 39]. RSV-F, which exhibits genetic stability, is present in two forms: prefusion (F0) and post-fusion forms [40]. Before the fusion of the virus with the host, an inactive fusion precursor existed on the viral surface, which includes theta (Ø) antigenic site [13, 38]. Nucleolin, a human phosphoprotein, serves as a critical cellular receptor for RSV. Specifically, the RSV-F protein binds to the RNA-binding domain (RBD1,2) of nucleolin [41]. Upon binding RSV-F to its potential receptors on the host cell, which include nucleolin, TLR-4, EGFR, ICAM-1, IGFR-1, and C-type lectin, the prefusion form undergoes structural alterations, transitioning to a post-fusion state. Post-fusion, it facilitates host cell penetration by forming a fusion pore [42]. Research findings indicate that the highest concentration of neutralizing antibodies is directed toward the theta antigenic segment of the prefusion form [13].

Small hydrophobic protein (SH)

The small hydrophobic protein functions as a pentameric ion channel and is the third glycoprotein in the viral envelope. Its presence contributes to bolstering the virus’s virulence [22]. Beyond its potential role in viral fusion, which creates pentameric transmembrane channels within the host cell membrane, this protein can disrupt the host’s immune responses by suppressing TNF-α signaling and dampening inflammation. Torrey et al. demonstrated that the ectodomain expressed on the infected cell’s surface, which emerges following RSV infection, can trigger the host to produce specific antibodies, making it a potential vaccine candidate for older adults [43].

Nucleocapsid proteins

The RSV nucleocapsid is associated with proteins such as nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), large polymerase (L), and matrix protein (M), most of them play roles in transcription [37]. The genome is enclosed by the nucleoprotein, with each RSV nucleoprotein shielding 7 RNA nucleotides. This results in the formation of a helical capsid comprising more than 2000 nucleoproteins [24]. RSV-N constitutes the primary focus of the CD8 + T-cell response within the immune system. Given its status as one of the most conserved RSV proteins, Pierre-Louis Hervé et al. have proposed it as a candidate for vaccine development [44]. The phosphoprotein (P) acts as a cofactor within the RdRp, collaborating with the large polymerase protein (L). Collectively, they generate ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) responsible for RNA synthesis and viral replication [24, 39]. RSV replication is error-prone, lacking a proofreading mechanism, leading to rapid emergence of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and subsequent mutations that aid in evading antiviral treatments [39].

Non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2)

Nonstructural proteins NS1 and NS2 exist in homo- and heteromeric forms and consist of 139 and 124 amino acids, respectively [45]. These two proteins are not present in the mature RSV virion but are expressed by infected cells; hence, they are called nonstructural proteins [46]. These proteins are often observed in infected host mitochondria [45]. The specific structural regions of NS1 modulate immune responses, including blocking type 1 IFN responses, hindering dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and elevating inflammatory responses [47]. Moreover, NS2 was capable of binding to and inhibiting the ubiquitination of inactive variants of RIG-I (retinoic acid-inducible gene I) and MDA5 (melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5), thereby halting subsequent signaling and the production of type I IFN [48]. In addition, research indicates that RSV nonstructural proteins may influence dendritic cell maturation, suppress Th17 differentiation, and facilitate Th2 polarization [33, 49].

Matrix protein (M)

The matrix protein is a phosphorylated, non-glycosylated protein that serves as a structural component in virion formation by bridging the envelope and the nucleocapsid [50]. Matrix protein plays multiple roles in the assembly and budding of new virions and RNPs into the host cell’s cytoplasm [50]. The M protein facilitates virus assembly by interacting with the cytoplasmic tail of the fusion protein (FCT) and with protein P, which binds to ribonucleoprotein (RNP), thereby facilitating the transport of RNP to the budding site [51]. Moreover, this protein is located in the nucleus of RSV-infected cells and may play a role in suppressing host cell transcription [52].

M2 protein (M2-1, M2-2)

The regulatory M2 proteins are a vital element of the viral replication machinery [39]. M2-1 is a tetrameric soluble protein and participates in transcription alongside RNA polymerase and the P protein [24]. The M2 protein acts as a regulator of both transcription and replication. Research findings suggest that the absence of M2-2 results in an upsurge in RNA transcription and antigenic expression, while concurrently decreasing genome replication [53]. M2-2 is a 10 kDa protein found in the nucleus and cytoplasmic granules and serves as a polymerase cofactor, facilitating the replication of the RSV genome during infection. Moreover, Scudero et al. indicated that M2-2 reduces eiF2α (α subunit of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2) phosphorylation, blocking translation and stress granule formation [54].

Epidemiology

Global prevalence and incidence rate

RSV is represented as a significant global health concern, particularly affecting infants and young children with acute lower respiratory infection [55]. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in 2019 on the worldwide prevalence of RSV revealed that approximately 33 million children under the age of 5 contracted RSV infection [6]. Of these cases, approximately 3.6 million children required hospitalization, which unfortunately resulted in the deaths of about 101,400 individuals. In infants under 6 months of age, RSV was responsible for an estimated 6.6 million episodes of acute lower respiratory tract infection, leading to about 1.4 million hospitalizations. Approximately 13,300 in-hospital deaths were reported, with total RSV-attributable deaths reaching nearly 45,700 in this age group [6]. Over 95% of RSV-related acute lower respiratory infection cases and more than 97% of RSV-associated mortality occurred in low- and middle-income countries [6]. While RSV is often associated with pediatric populations, epidemiological research has revealed that it is a major cause of respiratory illnesses among the elderly with comorbidities [37]. Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses in developed countries indicates that RSV accounted for 4.66% of symptomatic respiratory infections annually and 7.80% seasonally among older adults, with an 8.18% case fatality rate. In high-risk adults, RSV accounted for 7.03% of infections annually and 7.69% seasonally, with a case-fatality rate of 9.88% [56]. Table 1 presents the global distribution of RSV.

Seasonal patterns of RSV outbreaks

RSV is a seasonal pathogen whose epidemiological patterns vary according to geographic region and climatic conditions [57]. Furthermore, outbreaks can occur in annual (one-year) or biennial (two-year) cycles [58]. RSV shows clear seasonal patterns, emerging in winter months (November and March) in the Northern Hemisphere and peaking in January and February, while in the Southern Hemisphere, infections are most frequent from June to September [59]. The increased prevalence of RSV during this period is often attributed to a greater tendency for indoor human gatherings during cold weather. In temperate regions, the virus typically peaks during the cold season and lasts a few months. As well, RSV seasonality can be influenced by meteorological factors like temperature, humidity, and rainfall. Studies show that in temperate and subtropical regions, RSV activity tends to rise with lower temperatures and higher humidity, while in tropical and some subtropical areas, these associations are less consistent [60].

In contrast, in tropical areas, infections often coincide with the rainy season and can persist much longer. For instance, epidemics in Mozambique and Malaysia may last up to 10 months, whereas in Spain or the UK, the virus season is shorter [61]. Across Asia, in temperate countries such as China and Japan, infection rates rise sharply in winter. In contrast, in tropical nations like Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia, virus circulation is prolonged and largely follows the rainy season. In areas such as Singapore and Malaysia, transmission is influenced by rainfall and temperature, but patterns are less predictable than in temperate zones [61].

From 2010 to 2019, RSV was detected in 3.1% of over 14 million respiratory samples across 32 American states [62]. Epidemics generally moved from south to north. Seasonal peaks occurred in May–August in South America, September–November in the Caribbean, and in Central America, RSV activity typically lags behind influenza, while in the Andes, RSV peaks occur earlier than influenza. At higher latitudes, RSV outbreaks were shorter and occurred earlier. RSV circulation was influenced by climate and geography, with negative correlations observed for lower temperatures and precipitation, and positive correlations seen with decreasing longitude, barometric pressure, lower elevation, and more westerly locations [62]. However, in Europe’s temperate climate, RSV circulation peaks from October through February [63]. A study conducted across 13 European countries demonstrated consistent winter epidemics in all settings, with a noticeable west-to-east gradient in seasonal onset [64]. Eastern European countries experienced the start of RSV activity approximately four weeks later than Western part of Europe. The analysis also showed that lower temperatures and higher relative humidity were linked to increased RSV circulation, with an observed lag of about 14 days [64]. Recent estimates suggest that among healthy infants in Europe, approximately one in 56 is hospitalized as a result of RSV infection [65]. A meta-analysis from Africa reported RSV test positivity rates ranging between 9% and 30% across various subregions. In tropical and subtropical African settings, seasonal patterns differed: RSV activity peaked in autumn in Egypt, whereas in Mozambique, transmission increased toward the end of the rainy season [66]. Another systematic review found that RSV prevalence peaked during the winter months across all countries in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, with the exception of Pakistan [67].

RSV after COVID-19 pandemic

After the emergence of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic in late 2019, stringent personal hygiene practices and government-imposed social restrictions led to a change in the prevalence and seasonal patterns of other respiratory viruses, such as RSV and influenza [68–70]. In addition, there have been reports of off-season RSV outbreaks in Australia and European countries such as France and Iceland. This has been attributed to the adoption of unprecedented public health strategies to combat the COVID-19 pandemic [71, 72].

In the USA, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, RSV represented 5.4% of all detected respiratory pathogens between December 2019 and March 2020. Following the implementation of public health and social measures, the RSV positivity rate sharply declined to 0.03% from December 2020 to March 2021 [73]. Moreover, across Europe, nations like Austria experienced a substantial drop in RSV infections beginning in March 2020, aligning with the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic [73]. In 2020 and 2021, Italy and Belgium experienced a remarkable decrease in RSV prevalence rates. Belgium experienced an over 99% decline in reported RSV cases [74, 75]. In addition, during the 2020/2021 season, Spain, Germany, and Finland all exhibited a substantial decrease in RSV prevalence. In Spain, during the 2020–2021 respiratory virus surveillance season, RSV displayed an unusual pattern: circulation was almost nonexistent throughout winter, followed by a resurgence in late spring [72, 76, 77]. Australia observed a remarkable 98% reduction in reported infection cases compared to the previous five-year period. Similarly, Asian countries, such as Japan, South Korea, and Iran, showed a decline in RSV incidence during the 2020/2021 [73, 78–80]. A study conducted among children in Iran from March to May 2021 revealed no evidence of concurrent transmission of RSV and the COVID-19 outbreak [81]. However, epidemiological studies in China have yielded different results. One study reported a decline in both the number of respiratory virus tests and the corresponding positive cases of RSV and other respiratory viruses, with the most pronounced decrease occurring in February 2020 [82]. In another study conducted researchers found that, compared with previous years, the RSV positivity rate from December 2020 to February 2021 remained relatively high [83].

In general, scientists have suggested that the reduction in RSV prevalence is due to two main factors: personal hygiene practices and viral interference [84]. In the middle and late 2021, as hygiene and social restrictions were eased, there was a sudden, significant resurgence of RSV outbreaks. This led to the reporting of new waves of RSV infections in various communities. This unexpected off-season resurgence led to the hospitalization of numerous people in countries such as the United States, France, Israel, and Japan [73]. During the 2020 winter in Australia, RSV activity declined markedly. However, atypical off-season outbreaks emerged in spring 2020 in New South Wales and Western Australia [85]. As social restrictions were eased, a delayed resurgence of RSV was documented in the England, Switzerland, and Japan in the first half of 2021 [86–88]. The RSV wave off-season occurred in southern Italy in August 2021 and subsequently in Finland in September 2021 [89, 90]. A study conducted in Iran revealed an upsurge in the number of RSV cases and hospitalizations in a children in the southern region in April 2022 [91]. Scientists have provided several explanations to rationalize this delayed RSV wave. These reasons include, in particular, the easing of restrictions in 2021, a decline in population immunity to RSV, and an increase in cross-border migration [71, 91]. For instance, in Malaysia, RSV cases increased markedly after restrictions were lifted in 2022, with the positivity rate rising to 36.3%, exceeding levels observed before the COVID-19 pandemic [92].

Modes of transmission and risk factors

RSV is extremely contagious, and the incubation period lasts between 2 and 8 days [3]. RSV can be transmitted via multiple routes (Fig. 2). RSV is transmitted primarily through contact and nasopharyngeal droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks, or even breathes [121, 122]. Therefore, in this context, the WHO has also recommended preventive measures against RSV based on respiratory droplets and contact [122]. Studies of the nosocomial spread of RSV have identified RNA and even viable RSV viruses in airborne aerosols in healthcare facilities [123]. The virus can also persist on various surfaces for several hours, and people can become infected with RSV by touching contaminated surfaces and then touching their mouths, noses, or eyes. It is worth noting that RSV remains viable on surfaces for up to 6 h, on items such as gloves that provide personal protection for about 90 min, and on a person’s skin for about 20 min [124]. The R0 of RSV, or its transmission rate, ranges from 1 to 5, but is generally estimated at 3. This means that each person infected with RSV can potentially infect three other people. The circulating antigenic strain in a given year can also influence RSV transmissibility [2].

RSV transmission and clinical manifestations

The RSV-A strain may have higher transmissibility than RSV-B according to articles, suggesting that the strain circulating in a given year may influence transmission rates [2, 18]. To implement effective prevention strategies, it is important to understand how RSV is transmitted, particularly in settings where vulnerable individuals are at risk. Interestingly, multiple risk factors increase the likelihood of RSV infection or worsen its severity [125]. A systematic investigation along with a meta-analysis that focused on risk factors influencing RSV infections in children under 5 years of age found that factors such as preterm infants, underweight neonates, male gender, and siblings increase the risk of RSV infection. Furthermore, maternal smoking during pregnancy, a history of atopic skin diseases, lack of breastfeeding, and exposure to crowded environments may increase the likelihood of RSV infection [126].

Tropism

The main cellular targets of RSV are the ciliated columnar epithelial cells that form a continuous lining of the entire respiratory tract. RSV enters the respiratory tract through virus-laden droplets expelled by an infected individual. After a brief replication in the epithelial cells of the upper respiratory tract, the virus invades the terminal lung tissues. The virus can infect a variety of cells in the lower respiratory tract, including pneumocytes, epithelial basal cells, and dendritic cells, thereby causing respiratory symptoms [127–129]. Numerous studies have documented the presence of RSV RNA in various bodily fluids and tissues, underscoring the need for further comprehensive studies in this area [29, 130]. RSV uses different cell receptors to enter target cells. The two important receptors of the virus are CX3CR1 and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs). The interaction between the G glycoprotein and these receptors initiates virus attachment to the cell surface, facilitating subsequent cell entry [39, 131]. CX3CR1 is the important viral receptor, which can be expressed on the surface of airway epithelial cells [131, 132]. The interaction between the G protein and CX3CR1 led to the suppression of interferon type I (IFN-I) responses, while triggering the initiation of Th2-dependent responses [33, 131]. HSPGs serve as the main attachment receptors that facilitate RSV entry in immortalized cell lines under in vitro conditions [131]. The mode of entry, whether via plasma membrane fusion or macropinocytosis, varies depending on the cell type. Current findings assume that the interaction between the F protein and attachment factors, or alternative cell receptors, triggers conformational changes. This, in turn, facilitates the fusion of the viral envelope with the plasma membrane, ultimately leading to the release of ribonucleoprotein (RNP) into the host cell [29].

Clinical manifestation

Following RSV entry into the body through direct or indirect contact and the subsequent completion of an incubation period, the virus manifests its initial symptoms [129, 133]. The spectrum of disease symptoms can vary greatly depending on factors such as the patient’s age and individual immune status [134]. In healthy adults, the disease usually causes mild respiratory symptoms such as fever, cough, wheezing, nasal congestion, runny nose, and occasional shortness of breath, which are similar to other viral infections. More severe disease occurs when the lower respiratory tract is affected, especially in infants and children, leading to bronchiolitis and pneumonia. These complications are important risk factors for hospitalization and possible admission to intensive care units [14, 135]. Individuals at increased risk of severe manifestations of RSV-induced illness include infants, young children, the older adults, and immunocompromised individuals [39].

Clinical presentation in infants and young children

Studies have shown that approximately 95% of people in the population develop RSV in the first two years of their life [136]. In most infants, the disease presents with mild respiratory symptoms. The RSV infection could lead to acute bronchiolitis. Bronchiolitis is characterized by predominant symptoms such as wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath, decreased food intake, cyanosis, and fatigue [136, 137]. Important risk factors for the progression of a severe form of the disease and subsequent hospitalization in infants include chronic cardiopulmonary disease, immune system weakness, premature birth, exposure to tobacco smoke, and malnutrition [135, 138]. Seizures, severe neurological impairment, and death due to encephalitis have been documented in certain pediatric cases [130]. Research has revealed that children affected by RSV are at significantly increased risk of developing chronic respiratory conditions, including asthma, wheezing, and allergies, compared to the general population [139].

Clinical presentation in elderly and immunocompromised individuals

In the older population, the influence of RSV is similar to that of non-pandemic influenza [124]. The incidence of RSV is significantly higher in immunocompromised patients, particularly among those who have undergone lung and bone marrow transplantation [140]. Elderly individuals with preexisting cardiac and pulmonary conditions, along with immunocompromised patients, are at the greatest risk of RSV infection–associated pneumonia and mortality [141]. The likelihood of developing a severe form of the disease is increased in infants under 6 months, the elderly, those with underlying medical conditions, those exposed to RSV for the first time, and men [127, 142, 143]. Laboratory diagnosis

Clinical manifestations resulting from respiratory viruses are similar, making it impossible to differentiate RSV based on clinical symptoms alone. Prompt identification of the causative agents of respiratory illnesses has enabled the effective use of targeted antiviral drugs. This approach ensures appropriate patient treatment and mitigates the need for broad-spectrum antibiotics, thereby avoiding associated complications [144–146]. Therefore, the use of laboratory methods remains the only approach to detecting RSV in respiratory samples from patients. The nasopharyngeal aspirate is the optimal specimen for viral diagnosis, and is typically collected from hospitalized individuals [147]. Presently, nasopharyngeal swabs are widely used in laboratories as the preferred sampling method. Flocked swabs are particularly recommended because they capture epithelial cells, thereby increasing viral load [148]. In general, the RSV titer detected in respiratory samples from children tends to be several times higher than in adults and the elderly [144]. In clinical practice, RSV in clinical specimens is commonly identified using several laboratory approaches, including cell culture, molecular techniques such as reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), and antigen detection assays, including rapid antigen detection tests (RADTs) and direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) assays. In addition, a new generation of diagnostic tools, known as rapid molecular methods, is currently used to enable faster, more accurate viral detection [134].

Cell culture

Before the development of RT-PCR, cell culture was the predominant standard for RSV detection for many years. cell culture has several limitations, including the need for careful specimen handling to preserve viral viability, slow turnaround times (typically 2–5 days), higher costs and labor demands, and lower sensitivity compared with nucleic acid amplification tests. Despite the increasing use of newer diagnostic methods, cell culture remains valuable for monitoring selected viral strains for genetic and antigenic variations [134, 144]. Cell culture vials are also used to amplify and concentrate RSV, enabling testing by enzyme-based or fluorescent antibody detection [39]. One notable vial in this context is the R-mix, which includes the A549 and mink lung cell lines, facilitating the concentration of RSV for performing DFA tests [39, 149]. The most important cell lines for RSV replication include Hep-2, A549, and RhMK (rhesus monkey kidney). Additionally, in certain instances, the H358 and MRC-5 cell lines also play a pivotal role [39]. After introducing the unidentified sample into the cultured cells and incubating, morphological changes become evident. Of particular note are the formation of syncytia and cell death, which together indicate a viral infection [150, 151].

Molecular tests

Real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

Currently, RT-PCR is considered the gold standard test for RSV diagnosis, due to its exceptional specificity and sensitivity. For this purpose, it is routinely used in reference laboratories [144]. In this assay, RSV RNA is amplified using a series of primers targeting conserved regions of the N, F, G, and L genes. The result of this amplification is the determination of viral RNA presence or absence and the quantification of viral load, indicated by the cycle threshold (CT) [152, 153]. Quantitative RSV RT-PCR serves as an indicator of disease severity and the effectiveness of antiviral treatments by measuring viral load. Furthermore, owing to its increased sensitivity, this method enables the detection of even low RSV viral loads in adults and older individuals [137]. Disadvantages of this approach include longer testing time, the need for expensive equipment, and the need for qualified personnel. Furthermore, the method cannot distinguish between a resolved infection and an ongoing viral infection, as people who have recently recovered can still shed the virus for a longer period [154].

Rapid molecular tests

Rapid molecular tests represent a novel category of molecular diagnostic methods, classified as point-of-care tests [39, 154]. Several FDA approved rapid molecular tests for RSV detection includes Alere→ RSV test, cobasⓇ LiatⓇ Influenza A/B & RSV nucleic acid test, Cepheid XpertⓇ Flu/RSV XC and XpertⓇ Xpress Flu/RSV assays [155–157]. The recommended clinical specimen for these assays is a nasal or nasopharyngeal swab, which should be used directly for testing or first eluted in viral transport medium (VTM) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [154]. RSV rapid molecular tests provide fast, accurate results, enhancing clinical management of patients.

Antigen detection immunoassays

Antigen detection immunoassays are classified into two distinct categories: the traditional set, encompassing enzyme/optical immunoassays and immunochromatographic assays, and the contemporary category, comprising novel rapid antigen detection assays [134]. Historically, EIA (Enzyme Immunoassay) exhibited the highest sensitivity and specificity and was the predominant choice for RSV diagnosis. The sensitivity and specificity of previous immunoassay tests have varied depending on factors such as manufacturer, virus strain, patient age, and timing of testing relative to RSV seasonality [144]. Various RSV glycoprotein antigens and antibodies against RSV were detected using different EIA assays [158]. Novel immunoassays belong to the realm of point-of-care tests, including RSV rapid antigen tests and automated immunoassays such as BD Veritor → (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) and Quidel Sofia Ⓡ (Quidel Corp., San Diego, CA, USA) for reading the rapid test strip signals, standing out as significant advancements in this category [154].

Immune response

Innate immunity

Innate immune responses are the first line of defense against RSV. Several elements of innate immunity are important for controlling RSV infection, including PRR receptors (TLR, RLR, and NLR), cytokines and chemokines, and innate immune cells [159]. Several pattern recognition receptors, such as TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, TLR6, TLR7, RIG-I, and NOD2, are involved in the initial recognition of RSV, which ultimately leads to the production of antiviral (IFN type I, II, and III) and other cytokines through different signaling pathways [159, 160]. IFN type I (IFN-α, IFN-β), type II (IFN-γ), and type III(IFN-λ) are among the cytokines rapidly produced in response to RSV infection. IFN type I induces antiviral responses, recruits monocytes, and develops TH1 responses. IFN-λ is also important in controlling RSV infection and in activating immune cells [161]. The most important innate immune cells that contribute to infection include epithelial cells, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), dendritic cells, eosinophils, and NK cells. Epithelial cells act as a physical barrier against pathogens and secrete cytokines and mucins to clear pathogens. cytokines produced by epithelial cells (CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, CCL7, CXCL10, CXCL11, IL-8, and IL-15) also contribute to inducing inflammation and antiviral responses. Secretion of IL-8 by epithelial cells recruits neutrophils to the respiratory tract [162].

Neutrophils have several antimicrobial effects, including limiting virus proliferation through phagocytosis of infected cells, limiting virus spread through NETosis, and inactivating the virus by secreting antimicrobial peptides [163]. Furthermore, the release of various cytokines and chemokines recruits additional immune cells, including macrophages, eosinophils, basophils, DCs, and T helper cells. However, neutrophil influx is believed to play an important role in the pathogenesis of RSV infection [164]. Alveolar macrophages are the other primary innate immune cells that are important for virus clearance and lung homeostasis [161]. In addition to alveolar macrophages, monocyte-derived macrophages function as APCs and recruit other innate and adaptive immune components to these inflammatory areas [161]. Eosinophils are also recruited to the respiratory tract during RSV infection, and their activity increases in bronchiolitis. It is therefore believed that complications such as wheezing are caused by eosinophils releasing mediators [165].

NK cells are other immune cells that are activated by cytokines (such as IL-12 and IFN-β) released in the airway inflammatory environment. NK cells develop T cell responses via IFN-γ production [166]. Also, two subpopulations of dendritic cells (DCs), including classical dendritic cells (cDCs) and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), migrate to the respiratory tract in response to RSV infection [167]. Both DCs recognize and present viral antigens to T cells and induce adaptive immune responses, whereas pDCs secrete high amounts of type I IFNs through TLR receptors for the clearance of RSV compared to cDCs. cDCs secrete IL-4 and shift TH responses to TH2, improving RSV immunopathology [167]. DCs are professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and it is assumed that they serve as a bridge between the innate and adaptive immune responses [168].

Adaptive immunity

Adaptive immunity is the second line of defense with specific responses to each pathogen, which is important for the clearance of viruses, and is divided into humoral and cellular immunity [169].

Humoral immunity

The humoral arm of the adaptive immune system plays a crucial role in neutralizing RSV and preventing its spread [169]. RSV infection stimulates the production of serum and secreted antibodies, which protect against re-infection. Antibodies are produced against some RSV proteins, but F and G proteins are the main targets of neutralizing antibodies [169]. It was shown that the majority of these neutralizing Abs were produced against the prefusion conformation of the F protein (pre-F). Hence, pre-F is an appropriate antigen for the development of RSV vaccines [170, 171]. Neutralizing antibodies are crucial for preventing viral spread and reducing illness severity. In addition, neutralizing antibodies can promote clearance of RSV-infected cells through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent phagocytosis (ADP) [42].

Cellular immunity

Cellular immunity includes cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) and T helper cell responses, which are important for virus clearance. CTLs destroy virus-infected cells, and the stronger the CTL responses, the greater the pulmonary damage [172]. So CTLs have a role in both RSV clearance and pathogenesis. Also, TH1 cells activated by DC- and MQ-derived cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α, are essential for the clearance of the virus. However, in severe disease, the number of CTLs in the respiratory tract is greater than that of TH1 cells, and the amount of IFN-γ produced by CTLs is also higher [172, 173]. Cytokines produced in the inflammatory environment also induce TH2 responses, which lead to increased mucus secretion, eosinophil recruitment, and allergic mechanisms. T regulatory and B regulatory cells have regulatory effects and control the responses of T cell subsets by secreting IL10, which is important in preventing immune damage [173]. γδ T cells are also among the first immune cells that react to a virus, and their importance in an efficient antiviral response has been shown in γδ T cell-deficient mice, which have impaired responses in primary and secondary infections [174]. γδ T cells have an imperative role in early detection of RSV infection and development of adaptive immune response through production of cytokines and chemokines [174]. Thus, pulmonary cytolytic γδ T cells can limit viral replication in the early stages of RSV infection and reduce the intensity and duration of RSV replication. However, the accumulation of γδ T cells in the lungs during RSV infection may contribute to the pathogenesis of acute RSV disease and the development of wheezing after bronchiolitis [174, 175].

Despite several immune responses against RSV, the virus has mechanisms of evasion; for example, RSV has two non-structural proteins, NS1 and NS2, which inhibit IFN-α production and, in this way, affect CTL responses [176]. The soluble form of protein G also acts as a decoy, binds neutralizing antibodies to prevent neutralization of virions, and, by binding to DCs and lymphocytes, reduces their function [46]. F protein binds to TLR4 and may have an immunomodulatory role. Also, the N protein of the virus disrupts T-cell immunological synapses and can limit the immune response. Thus, RSV evades the immune responses [46, 159].

Immunopathology

The pathogenesis of RSV disease results from both virus-mediated and host immune responses. RSV first infects upper respiratory epithelial cells and then spreads to the lower respiratory tract by cell-to-cell transmission or by the migration of infected macrophages, where it replicates in bronchial epithelial cells [2, 177]. Viral replication in bronchial epithelial cells leads to cell necrosis and cilia loss. Infection in these areas also increases infiltration of immune cells, especially CTLs and neutrophils, and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6), thereby activating the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway and resulting in increased plasma leakage, edema, and mucus secretion [2]. Increased mucus and edema can cause airway obstruction, and, following the absorption of trapped air, atelectasis occurs. Also, the release of the inflammatory cell mediators can cause epithelial cell damage [2, 178].

Vaccines

After the virus was isolated in 1956, RSV vaccine research began. In the 1960s, a formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine (FI-RSV) was developed. However, during trials, the FI-RSV vaccine sensitized children to the virus, and re-infection resulted in exacerbation of the disease in the immune group, resulting in the death of two children [179]. It is supposed that the reason was a weak induction of neutralizing antibodies and a shift in the cellular immune response toward TH2 [179]. Currently, promising clinically developed vaccines are classified into six categories: live attenuated vaccines, chimeric RSV vaccines, subunit vaccines, vector-based vaccines, mRNA-based vaccines, and particle-based vaccines (Table 2). Extensive research ultimately led to the use of a stabilized prefusion (PreF) RSV F protein, culminating in the development of several clinically validated safe and efficacious vaccines. Currently, there are three FDA-approved vaccine for RSV. Two of them are two recombinant protein vaccines namely Arexvy and Abrysvo and one them is mRNA vaccine (mRESVIA) [180]. These vaccines can be used for individuals aged 60 years and above. In addition, Abrysvo is approved for maternal vaccination, with the goal of providing protection to infants. Following evidence from adult studies, Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) advises a one-time vaccination with any of the three FDA-licensed RSV vaccines for adults [181, 182].

On May 3, 2023, the FDA approved the first-ever RSV vaccine, Arexvy, and on June 7, 2023, the European Union approved it [183]. Arexvy® (GSK), an RSV Prefusion F vaccine (RSVpreF3) with AS01E adjuvant, has earned the distinction of being the world’s first FDA-approved vaccine specifically designed for individuals aged 60 and above, targeting RSV (subtypes A and B) [184]. On May 31, 2023, the FDA approved Abrysvo (manufactured by Pfizer), a second RSV vaccine intended for individuals aged 60 years and above. Moreover, on May 18, 2023, an FDA advisory panel endorsed the data, affirming the effectiveness and safety of a potential maternal RSV vaccine. This vaccine is designed for administration to pregnant women, aiming to safeguard infants up to 6 months of age [183]. This vaccine is the exclusive RSV vaccine endorsed for use during pregnancy, ensuring protection for infants against RSV-related lower respiratory tract infections. The recommended time to administer the vaccine is during weeks 32 through 36 of pregnancy [185]. The trial’s findings indicate that while this candidate maternal RSV vaccine carried a higher risk of preterm birth, it also reduced the risks of RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease in infants compared to a placebo [186].

mRESVIA® (mRNA-1345), developed by Moderna, is a single-dose intramuscular vaccine approved by the FDA in 2024 for adults ≥ 60 years. mRESVIA® is an mRNA vaccine that encodes the pre-fusion form of the membrane-anchored RSV F glycoprotein. It provides protection against severe RSV-induced lower respiratory tract disease and is effective against both RSV subtypes, including in older adults with pre-existing medical conditions [187, 188]. Based on the systematic review, Arexvy lowered RSV-related hospitalizations in the elderly by 60–65% (95% CI: 56–66%) [189]. Abrysvo showed 58–63% efficacy in older adults and 68–72% protection of infants when administered maternally. mRESVIA provided 55–58% protection against RSV illness. All vaccines produced a 5–7-fold increase in neutralizing antibody titers, with effects persisting for up to 12 months [189]. In general, FDA-approved RSV vaccines provide significant protection against RSV in older adults and high-risk populations, with all three vaccines eliciting durable immune responses and a favorable safety profile. While results are promising, data on long-term effectiveness and applicability to underserved regions remain limited, highlighting the need for ongoing real-world monitoring and comparative studies to guide global immunization strategies [189].

Treatment

Treatment of RSV generally includes supportive measures, antiviral medications, and immune-based prophylaxis [208]. The main option for treatment the RSV is supportive care, which includes antipyretics, hydration, saline nasal drops, oxygen therapy for hypoxia, and ventilation for respiratory failure [209]. As well, antiviral drugs like ribavirin can be used for this purpose. Ribavirin is a broad-spectrum guanosine analog that interferes with the replication of both DNA and RNA viruses. Ribavirin can be administered via aerosol, orally, or intravenously (IV); however, only the aerosolized formulation is officially approved by FDA for treating RSV infection [39, 210]. Aerosolized therapy is approved for pediatric pneumonia but not for adults. However, its use is limited due to cost, administration difficulties, adverse effects, and teratogenicity concerns [211, 212]. The oral and IV forms of ribavirin are mainly used in immunocompromised populations. Research has reported possible reductions in RSV-associated complications and mortality among transplant patients, along with improvements in respiratory function. Despite these findings, poor oral absorption and the potential for acute hemolytic reactions with IV therapy pose significant limitations [212].

A systematic review and meta-analysis included 788 patients diagnosed with RSV infection, of whom 14.3% had isolated lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) [213]. Among 445 patients receiving ribavirin, 195 (43.8%) were treated with an aerosolized formulation. Overall meta-analysis indicated no significant difference in mortality between patients receiving aerosolized or oral ribavirin and those receiving supportive care (risk ratio [RR] 0.63; 95% CI 0.28–1.42). In subgroup analyses, mortality was significantly lower in patients with hematological conditions (RR 0.32; 95% CI 0.14–0.71), whereas no significant difference was observed in lung transplant recipients (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.31–2.56). Oral ribavirin was associated with higher rates of viral clearance compared to supportive care (RR 2.60; 95% CI 1.35–4.99). Among 119 patients monitored for adverse events, 17 were reported, none of which were severe in nature [213]. A Cochrane review examined twelve trials, all involving infants under six months of age [214]. In four studies including 158 participants, mortality was slightly lower in those receiving ribavirin (5.8%) compared to placebo (9.7%), although the difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.58; 95% CI 0.18–1.85). In three trials with 116 infants, the risk of worsening respiratory status was 7.1% with ribavirin versus 18.3% with placebo (OR 0.37; 95% CI 0.12–1.18). Data from three studies of 104 ventilated infants indicated that ribavirin shortened hospital stays by an average of 1.9 days (95% CI − 4.6 to + 0.9) and reduced time on mechanical ventilation by 1.8 days (95% CI − 3.4 to − 0.2). No meaningful differences were observed in long-term lung function or in the incidence of recurrent wheezing after RSV infection between treatment and control groups [214]. However, concerns regarding its therapeutic benefits, safety profile, and the necessity for dedicated administration devices have curtailed its widespread adoption.

Monoclonal antibodies are among the most important preventive measures for populations at risk of RSV infection. They provide an additional layer of protection against severe disease for children [215]. RespiGam® is one of the first treatments for RSV, a combination of human intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) containing neutralizing antibodies that can prevent acute lower respiratory tract infection. RespiGam® was voluntarily withdrawn following the introduction of palivizumab, and it is no longer available as a treatment option for severely ill immunocompromised adults with RSV infection [216].

Palivizumab (Synagis), a recombinant human monoclonal antibody that inhibits fusion, viral infection, and prevents viral invasion of host cells, was approved. Palivizumab is prescribed for high-risk infants and is indicated to be safe and well-tolerated [217]. Motavizumab (MEDI-524) is a second-generation human anti-RSV monoclonal antibody derived from palivizumab, with a longer half-life and greater neutralization activity than palivizumab [218]. In a Phase 2 trial, infants were randomized to receive monthly intramuscular injections of either palivizumab or motavizumab. The results showed similar outcomes in terms of antidrug antibody formation and overall rates of adverse effects. In a phase 3 randomized, double-blind, palivizumab-controlled trial, motavizumab recipients had numerous adverse effects. Hence, despite encouraging prior results, the FDA refused to approve the drug’s commercialization [219]. MEDI-557 is a next-generation motavizumab that has a longer half-life. A randomized, double-blind, single-dose phase 1 trial was ongoing to assess the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of this mAb in healthy adults [129]. However, more data is not available. Nirsevimab (MEDI8897) is a recombinant human IgG1 mAb with a longer serum half-life than palivizumab, which binds to the RSV F protein and prevents lower respiratory tract infection. The nirsevimab-assisted clinical trials for the prevention of RSV infections created a great design model. Healthy preterm and term infants from the north and south of the globe were selected by the investigators, who then divided infants into subgroups and assessed the results of pharmacokinetic and antidrug antibody tests [220]. Nirsevimab is a long-acting injectable monoclonal antibody that provides protection against RSV after a single dose, typically covering an entire RSV season. In August 2023, the CDC recommended its use for all infants in the United States younger than 8 months who are born during or entering their first RSV season [221, 222]. Suptavumab (REGN-2222) is a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that targets the pre-F form of the RSV fusion protein. A phase 3 trial in infants revealed that all RSV subtype B isolates carried two amino acid mutations within the epitopes. This resulted in the loss of suptavumab’s neutralizing activity, leading to the discontinuation of the drug’s clinical development [129]. Also, several nucleoprotein inhibitors, non-nucleoside inhibitors, and fusion inhibitors are being developed, which are listed in Table 3 [223, 224].

Control measures

Currently, the primary strategies for preventing RSV infection include proper hygiene, breastfeeding, and passive immunization with palivizumab [250]. To prevent the spread of RSV, avoid close contact with sick people, wash hands frequently, clean surfaces and other objects that may be contaminated with RSV, and isolate the RSV patient to prevent transmission [178]. In healthcare facilities, standard respiratory infection control practices like isolating cases, maintaining high hand hygiene standards, cohort nursing, and using personal protective equipment have proven somewhat effective in limiting RSV outbreaks. Additionally, the monoclonal antibody palivizumab has been used for high-risk patients, such as preterm infants, during hospital RSV outbreaks to reduce severe complications [123].

RSV as an oncolytic virus

Cancer ranks among the leading global causes of death. Current methods of treating cancer include surgery intervention, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [251]. However, due to the varying success of existing treatments, the need for appropriate treatment options is increasing. Therefore, medicine has long sought more efficient cancer treatment, and oncolytic virus (OV) therapy has attracted researchers as an innovative anticancer approach [252]. As well, combination of OVs with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and other approaches may help reduce each therapy’s shortcomings and enhance antitumor efficacy [253]. OVs under current investigation can be categorized into two main groups: wild-type (naturally occurring) and genetically modified viruses. These engineered viruses are designed to selectively infect, replicate within, and destroy malignant cells while leaving healthy tissues unharmed [254]. Moreover, OVs not only damage cancer cells but also induce a systemic immune response against the tumor and are expected to be effective in treating metastatic tumors [255]. After the tumor cell destruction, tumor antigens are released and taken up by DCs. Then, DCs enter the lymph nodes, present tumor antigens to naive T cells, and induce differentiation into tumor-specific CD4 + and CD8 + T cells [256]. Thus, OVs can trigger innate and adaptive immune responses to combat cancer cells. OVs can promote anti-angiogenesis by decreasing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels. This reduction leads to diminished blood flow within the tumor, ultimately causing tumor cells to undergo apoptosis and necrosis [257].

Nemours preclinical and clinical studies with several OVs have been conducted to date, and four OV products in total have received approval for use in tumor treatment. Latvia approved Rigvir in 2004 for melanoma, the first OV cancer treatment globally. China followed in 2005 with Oncorine for adenovirus-based therapy. In 2015, the FDA approved talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) for recurrent melanoma, which was subsequently approved in Europe and Canada. Most recently, in June 2021, Japan conditionally approved Delytact (G47Δ), an oncolytic herpes simplex virus, for malignant glioma [258–260].

Evidence of the RSV strain A2 oncolytic activity has previously been studied in a variety of tumor tissues, including the skin (A431), breast, hepatocellular carcinoma cell (HCC), prostate cancer cells, which include PC-3 and lymph node carcinoma of the prostate (LNCaP). Additionally, its application has been studied in the TC-1 cell line, which serves as a relevant model for Human Papillomavirus (HPV)-related cervical cancer [261]. According to the studies, RSV induces apoptosis in PC-3 human prostate cancer cells in both cultured medium and a xenograft model, which is caused by downregulation of NF-kB. When assessed in vitro using prostate cancer models, the androgen-independent and highly metastatic PC-3 human prostate cancer cells exhibited a dramatic increase in RSV infectivity relative to the non-tumorigenic RWPE-1 human prostate cells [262]. Further research demonstrated that RSV possesses oncolytic activity, selectively promoting apoptosis in androgen-sensitive and androgen-refractory prostate cancer cells. This selectivity is attributed to the virus exploiting a deficiency in antiviral signaling within infected cells, namely the IFN-mediated JAK/STAT or NF-κB activation pathways [263]. Within skin cancer cells, RSV suppresses cellular proliferation and initiates apoptosis. Therefore, RSV can play a role in regulating the growth of skin cancer cells and inducing their apoptosis [264]. In addition, in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines, RSV infection decreased the growth and migration of BNL-HCC, Hep3B, Huh-7, and SNU-739 cells [265]. Nevertheless, more studies are needed to identify the mechanism mediated by RSV. According to the Samadi et al. investigation, RSV-A2 showed oncolytic and inhibitory effects in murine TC-1 cancer cell lines as a model for HPV-related cervical cancers. They found that RSV-A2 oncolytic activity was through inducing apoptosis and autophagy, activating caspase-3, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inhibiting the cell cycle [261]. Furthermore, RSV may enhance the therapeutic effect of OV therapy by inducing syncytia formation. The formation of syncytia can increase viral spread in the tumor microenvironment and induce the killing of adjacent uninfected tumor cells [252]. Nevertheless, further investigation is warranted to explore this concept.

Overall, based on previous studies, RSV may have promising oncolytic properties, with its unique ability to form syncytia. However, challenges such as immune evasion, differences in in vivo and in vitro tropism, and potential side effects remain to be addressed, and further investigation, especially animal studies, is needed to support the claim that RSV is a valuable tool in the pursuit of improved cancer treatments. With continued investigation and development, RSV may play a pivotal role in the future of oncolytic virus-based therapies, contributing to more effective and targeted cancer treatments.

Conclusion

Overall, RSV is emerging as a significant respiratory pathogen with a widespread impact, particularly on vulnerable populations such as infants, young children, and immunocompromised individuals. Its global prevalence underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of the virus and its multiple dimensions. The clinical manifestations of RSV infections, ranging from mild respiratory symptoms to severe distress, highlight the urgency of developing effective preventive and therapeutic measures. As discussed in this article, ongoing research into antiviral treatments, supportive care, and the promising vaccination landscape represents a concerted effort to mitigate the burden of RSV-related illnesses. Moreover, the intriguing potential of RSV as an oncolytic virus makes its role in healthcare even more complex. As we navigate the complex RSV landscape, from prevalence and clinical impact to therapeutic interventions and breakthrough potentials, this comprehensive investigation highlights the importance of a holistic approach. By fostering a deeper understanding of RSV, we can pave the way for innovative strategies, informed public health initiatives, and advancements in medical science to better manage and, ultimately, prevent the impact of this formidable respiratory pathogen.
