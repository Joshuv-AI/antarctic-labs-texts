Alcohol use among adolescents in eight sub-Saharan African countries: evidence from the Global School-based student health survey (2012–2017) using the socio-ecological model
Authors: Nuworza Kugbey, Stephen Manortey, Emmanuel Dziwornu, Frank Kyei-Arthur, Millicent Ofori Boateng, Sandra Boatemaa Kushitor, Edward Kofi Sutherland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlcohol use is a major global public health issue due to associated negative physical, mental, and social health outcomes among adolescents. This study examined the current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problems and associated correlates among 16,331 adolescents from eight selected sub-Saharan African countries.MethodsA secondary analysis of the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) datasets from eight cross-sectional surveys was conducted. The STATA statistical software package (Version 17) was used for the data analysis. Chi-square and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted with statistical significance set at p-value < 0.05.ResultsWe found that the overall prevalence of current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol-related problems among adolescents was 12.56%, 8.32%, and 4.74%, respectively. Seychelles recorded the highest rates of current alcohol use (47.21%) and lifetime drunkenness (42.17%), while Tanzania reported the lowest for both (2.89% and 1.41%, respectively). The highest prevalence of alcohol-related problems was observed in Namibia (13.76%), with the lowest again in Tanzania (1.89%). Risk factors associated with alcohol use behaviours included male gender, older adolescent age (15–18 years), mental health challenges (anxiety, loneliness, suicidal behaviours), and psychosocial issues (truancy, bullying, fighting, physical attacks, lack of close friends, hunger, cigarette and marijuana use). However, peer support and positive parenting emerged as protective factors.ConclusionThis study highlights the urgency of addressing alcohol use behaviours among adolescents, taking into cognizance the prevalence and country-level variations. Multilevel vulnerability and resilience factors consistent with the socio-ecological model were identified, and they should be incorporated into school-based public health interventions to reduce the risk and burden of alcohol use among adolescents in SSA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07555-z.

Introduction

Several psychosocial and physical challenges characterise adolescence as a transitional period. During this period, adolescents experiment with several risky behaviours, including sexual debut and substance use. These experimentations put adolescents at risk for developing several health and psychosocial challenges that negatively affect their growth and smooth transition to adulthood [1–4]. One of the well documented risky behaviours among adolescents worldwide is substance use, especially among school-going ones who are faced with several personal, school-environmental, and psychosocial stressors [4, 5]. One of the most experimented substances among adolescents is alcohol [6]. For instance, a study by Kyei-Gyamfi et al. [7] on substance use among adolescents in Ghana found alcohol as the main substance used by adolescents. Alcohol use across all ages (especially during adolescence) is a major problem as its negative consequences are enormous. The negative consequences of alcohol use include poor academic achievement, violence, contentious relationships, increased risk of unintentional injuries and mortality, and alcohol use disorders [8–11]. For example, adolescents who are exposed to alcohol at an early age suffer from several problems, including social, academic, economic, physical, and psychological issues [12, 13].

Evidence from previous studies indicates that alcohol use contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality among youth aged 10 to 24 years, accounting for four of the ten leading causes of disability in this age group [14, 15]. However, it was observed in the studies that significant differences exist between male and female adolescents in their rate of alcohol use, as alcohol ranked highest among males than females [6, 14–16]. Kyei-Gyamfi’s [16] study in Ghana found that more male adolescents (7.1%) consumed alcohol than female adolescents (6.2%).

Globally, the epidemiology of adolescents’ alcohol use is pervasive. For instance, a study in the US in 2019 showed that over 7 million youth between 12 and 20 years reported alcohol use beyond “just a few sips” over one month [17]. Past research findings have shown differing patterns and frequency of adolescents’ use of alcohol. A systematic review examining alcohol use among adolescents in India reported that lifetime alcohol use ranged from 3.9% to 69.8%, while past-year consumption ranged from 10.6% to 32.9%, with notable variations observed across gender and age groups [18]. A study conducted in Thailand showed 60.5% and 53.0% rates for lifetime and past-year alcohol use among adolescents [19]. Similar findings were reported among school-going Italian adolescents [20].

On the continent of Africa, several cross-sectional studies showed differences in alcohol consumption in adolescents. Findings from previous studies conducted in various African countries have reported that the prevalence of alcohol use among adolescents ranges from approximately 10% to 44% [5, 21–23]. Interestingly, a study conducted in Morocco reported close to 10% prevalence of alcohol use among adolescents [24]. It is interesting to note that an Islamic country with strict alcohol regulations still records substantial alcohol consumption use among adolescents. Also, a study by Olashore et al. [25] in Botswana found that 25.1% of adolescents consumed alcohol in the past 12 months preceding the survey. Similarly, Jaguga et al.‘s [26] study in Kenya found that lifetime alcohol use among adolescents was 42%.

In addressing adolescents’ alcohol use and its associated problems, there is a need to unravel the factors that influence their alcohol consumption to inform appropriate and relevant programmes. Factors ranging from individual characteristics (e.g., age, sex and place of residence) [16, 25, 27–29], psychosocial problems (e.g., loneliness, suicidal ideation and attempts) [5], school-level issues (e.g., class) [27, 30], peer influence [25, 31], and parental [25, 27, 29, 30] are implicated in alcohol consumption among adolescents. The findings from these studies provide the basis for assessing their roles in adolescents’ alcohol use and its associated consequences.

Though there are studies on alcohol use in sub-Saharan African countries, few multi-country studies on this topic have been conducted [6, 32, 33]. Conducting such studies will provide a comprehensive understanding of alcohol use and enhance the generalization of the findings. To bridge this knowledge gap, this study examined alcohol use among adolescents in eight sub-Saharan African countries. These eight countries were chosen because of data availability and completeness. The latest dataset was used for each country per records on the WHO/CDC website.

The socio-ecological model [34] underpinned the selection of the key correlates in this study. The model postulates that human behaviour is influenced by personal characteristics, relationships, community, and society-level factors. Therefore, to understand a particular behaviour holistically, there is a need to examine factors from all these levels of influence. This study, therefore, analysed the potential determinants of adolescents’ alcohol consumption across demographic characteristics, psychosocial factors, mental health factors, and parenting factors. This study examined (1) the overall prevalence of current alcohol use, lifetime drunkenness, and alcohol problems among in-school adolescents in SSA, (2) the country-specific prevalence of current alcohol use, lifetime drunkenness, and alcohol problems among in-school adolescents in SSA, and finally, (3) the associated vulnerability and resilience factors of current alcohol use, lifetime drunkenness, and alcohol problems among in-school adolescents in SSA.

Methods

Data source

Data was obtained from the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) of eight (8) countries in SSA between 2012 and 2017 (Benin-2016, Ghana-2012, Liberia-2017, Mauritus-2017, Mozambique-2015, Namibia-2013, Seychelles-2015 and Tanzania-2014). The GSHS is sponsored by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to collect data on health behaviours and their associated factors in adolescents in school across several low-income and middle-income countries worldwide. The variables considered in the data collected include issues concerning adolescents’ health and wellbeing as well as academic outcomes. Table 1 presents the sample sizes from each selected country.

Study variables

Outcome variables: For this study, three alcohol-related outcomes were considered: “current alcohol use,” “lifetime drunkenness,” and “alcohol problem.” Single-item questions were used to measure each of the three outcome variables. For example, “current alcohol use” was measured with the item “During the past 30 days, on how many days did you have at least one drink containing alcohol? A 6-point Likert response format was used with responses ranging from 0 days, 1–2 days, 3–5 days, 6–9 days, 10–19 days, 20–29 days, to all 30 days. The responses were recoded as no day = 0 and one or more days = 1. Lifetime drunkenness was measured with the item “During your life, how many times did you drink so much alcohol that you were really drunk?“. A 4-point Likert response format was used with responses ranging from 0 times, 1–2 times, 3–9 times, to 10 or more times. The responses were recoded as 0 times = 0 and one or more times = 1. Lifetime alcohol problem was measured with the item “During your life, how many times have you got into trouble with your family or friends, missed school, or got into fights as a result of drinking alcohol?“. A 4-point Likert response format was used with responses ranging from 0 times, 1–2 times, 3–9 times, to 10 or more times. The responses were recoded as 0 times = 0 and one or more times = 1. The recoding of the variables was based on similar recoding used in previous studies among adolescents [5, 18, 21, 22, 30].

Explanatory variables: The explanatory variables in this study were divided into four: socio-demographic factors (age and sex), psychosocial factors (truancy, bullying, physical attack, fighting, close friends, peer support, smoking cigarette, hunger, and marijuana), mental health factors (anxiety, loneliness, suicidal ideation, plan and attempt), and parenting factors (monitoring, bonding, understanding and intrusion of privacy). The socio-ecological model underpins the choice of these explanatory variables [34]. Details of the explanatory variables and items used to measure them are presented in Table 2.

Data analysis

The data collected were analyzed using the STATA statistical software package (StataCorp.2007. Stata Statistical Software. Release 17. StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). The prevalence of alcohol use behaviours was done using percentages with graphical illustrations. The chi-square test was used to determine the association between the explanatory variables and all three categorical outcome variables. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the explanatory variables’ association with the outcome variables to address the set objectives. A binary logistic regression was used because all three outcome variables were dichotomous. Both unadjusted and adjusted odd ratios (OR) were presented, and complex sampling weights were applied to all the analyses. In estimating the adjusted odds ratios, all explanatory variables were controlled for simultaneously within a single model to assess the independent association between each explanatory variable and the outcome variables.

Results

Socio-demographic profiles of the participants

In the total sample, there were slightly more females (50.75%) than males, and the majority (66.35%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. The results showed that in Benin, there were more male adolescents (55.56%) than females, and the majority (84.47%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Ghana, there were more male adolescents (55.39%) than females, and the majority (77.47%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Liberia, there were more male adolescents (54.93%) than females, and the majority (84.89%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Mauritius, there were more female adolescents (56.05%) than males, and the majority (57.01%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Mozambique, there were more male adolescents (53.29%) than females, and the majority (82.78%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Namibia, there were more female adolescents (53.45%) than males, and the majority (73.75%) of the adolescents were aged 15–18 years. In Seychelles, there were more female adolescents (57.96%) than males, and the majority (62.63%) of the adolescents were aged 11–14 years. In Tanzania, there were more female adolescents (52.29%) than males, and the majority (52.73%) of the adolescents were aged 11–14 years.

Adolescents’ alcohol use behaviours in SSA

The overall prevalence of current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problems was 12.56% (95 CI = 12.05%-13.08%), 8.32% (95 CI = 7.90% − 8.76%), and 4.74% (95 CI = 4.42% − 5.08%), respectively. For current alcohol use, adolescents in Seychelles reported the highest prevalence of 47.21% (95 CI = 44.86% − 49.61%), whereas adolescents in Tanzania reported the lowest prevalence of 2.89% (95 CI = 2.28% − 3.58%). For lifetime drunkenness, adolescents in Seychelles reported the highest prevalence of 42.17% (95 CI = 39.82% − 44.52%), whereas adolescents in Tanzania reported the lowest prevalence of 1.41% (95 CI = 1.00% − 1.92%). For lifetime alcohol problem, adolescents in Namibia reported the highest prevalence of 13.76% (95 CI = 12.56% − 15.05%), whereas adolescents in Tanzania reported the lowest prevalence of 1.89% (95 CI = 1.41% − 2.47%). Correlates of alcohol use behaviours among adolescents in SSA

Current alcohol use

Results from Table 4 showed that the sex of adolescents significantly predicted current alcohol use (AOR = 1.40, 95%CI = 1.22–1.60), where being a male participant had higher odds (1.40) for current alcohol use after controlling for all other covariates in the model. The age of the adolescents significantly predicted current alcohol use (AOR = 2.77, 95%CI = 2.32–3.29), with adolescents between 15 and 18 years reporting 2.77 higher odds for current alcohol use compared to adolescents between 11 and 14 years. Truancy among adolescents predicted higher odds of current alcohol use (AOR = 1.46, 1.26–1.68). Being bullied significantly predicted higher odds of alcohol use among adolescents (AOR = 1.60, 95%CI = 1.39–1.85).

Age significantly predicted drunkenness (AOR = 3.21, 95%CI = 2.59–3.99), with adolescents between 15 and 18 years reporting 3.21 higher odds for drunkenness compared to adolescents between 11 and 14 years. Truancy significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness among the adolescents (AOR = 1.65, 1.41–1.93). Being bullied significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness among adolescents (AOR = 1.57, 95%CI = 1.33–1.84).

Being physically attacked significantly predicted lower odds for drunkenness (AOR = 0.72, 95%CI = 0.61-0.85). Being involved in a physical fight significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness (AOR = 1.23, 95%CI = 1.03–1.46). Hunger significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness (AOR = 1.27, 95%CI = 1.01–1.59). Smoking cigarettes significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness (AOR = 4.07, 95%CI = 3.02–5.48). Smoking marijuana significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness among adolescents (AOR = 8.93, 95%CI = 5.55–14.36).

Anxiety significantly predicted higher odds of drunkenness (AOR = 1.83, 95%CI = 1.48–2.25). Discussion

Prevalence of current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problem

Findings from the analysis showed overall prevalence rates of 12.56% (95%CI = 12.05%-13.08%), 8.32% (95%CI = 7.90% − 8.76%), and 4.74% (95%CI = 4.42% − 5.08%) for current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problems respectively. The overall prevalence rate of current alcohol use among school-going adolescents means that more than 1 in every 10 adolescents who are in school in SSA currently use alcohol. Similarly, almost 1 in every 10 adolescents and every 1 out of 20 adolescents reported drunkenness and alcohol problems, respectively. This pattern of alcohol use behaviours is a major challenge that requires coordinated efforts on the continent to address the menace of alcohol use due to the adverse outcomes associated with alcohol use. The overall current alcohol use behaviours are within the ranges of individual findings of alcohol use in SSA [5, 21–23] and other parts of the world [19]. Since alcohol has been noted as an experimental drug across the world, especially among adolescents, it is not surprising that a substantial number of school-going adolescents in SSA currently use alcohol.

Furthermore, significant variations were observed in alcohol use among the selected countries for this study. These variations in the prevalence rates across the countries in SSA can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, one of the key factors found to influence alcohol use is religious affiliations, as Islam-dominated countries have strict regulations on access and use of alcohol, unlike countries with liberal religious orientations or African traditions practices with alcohol being an integral part of their rituals. For example, some studies have reported that religious norms and commitments regarding alcohol are significantly associated with alcohol use among adolescents [35, 36].

Christianity and Islam are the main religions of people living in Ghana, Liberia, and Tanzania, while Islam is the main religion of people living in Mauritius. Also, Christianity is the main religion in Namibia and Seychelles, while Christianity, Islam, and Traditional religions are the main religions of persons living in Benin and Mozambique. Generally, Islam forbids the consumption of alcohol, while Christianity supports the consumption of wine in moderation, and it is used in its religious practices [37]. However, traditional religion encourages the consumption of alcohol, and it is often used in their practices and ceremonies [38]. Therefore, it is not surprising that alcohol use is relatively high in Benin, Namibia, and Seychelles. Also, Pengpid and Peltzer’s [39] study in Seychelles reported that alcohol drinking is socially tolerated in Seychelles.

Secondly, the variations in the regulatory frameworks around advertisement, access, and use of alcohol products could account for these variations. For example, it has been argued by some scholars that weak enforcement of laws and regulations prohibiting alcohol access and use, especially among the youth, are major contributors to high alcohol mis/use among the youth in SSA [5, 21, 40]. In Tanzania and Ghana, there are records of a high density of alcohol selling outlets and advertisements, and social spaces or events that promote the consumption of alcohol [41–43]. Through music festivals and other social activities, alcohol companies promote their products using quantity deals, low prices, and giveaways to young people. In Benin City, Nigeria, about 6000 participants who attended a Guinness show drank alcohol daily, while some participants made impulse purchases [44].

Risk factors for alcohol use behaviours

Several risk factors have been identified for alcohol use behaviours among adolescents who are in school in SSA. It was observed from this study that socio-demographic characteristics such as being a male and being a late adolescent (15–18 years) are significant risk factors for alcohol use behaviours among adolescents who are in school. These findings are consistent with previous evidence among adolescents across the world on their health risk behaviours, such as substance use [27, 28, 45]. Male adolescents are found to be prone to risky behaviours compared to females. Several explanations are proffered regarding gender differences in health risk behaviours among adolescents. One of the evolutionary perspectives is that males are prone to risk-taking compared to females due to their nature and, thus, are high on sensation seeking [46, 47]. Culture could also underlie the significant sex differences in alcohol use behaviours as most cultures in SSA associate certain health risk behaviours, including alcohol use, with masculinity [48, 49].

Some key mental health such as anxiety, loneliness, suicidal plans, and attempts, are significant risk factors for at least one of the three alcohol-related behaviours (current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problem). Experience of mental health challenges can predispose adolescents to substance use, including alcohol, as substance use is seen as a coping strategy to deal with stressful situations. The literature is replete with evidence of the link between mental health factors and alcohol use, with some studies suggesting a bidirectional relationship between alcohol use and mental health problems [5, 21, 22, 24, 32, 50].

Furthermore, psychosocial risk factors such as truancy, being bullied, fighting, having no close friends, hunger, being attacked physically, cigarette smoking, and smoking marijuana increased the risk for at least one of the alcohol behaviours (current alcohol use, drunkenness, and alcohol problem). For example, food insecurity (hunger) has been reported to create behavioural vulnerabilities, such as impulsivity, poor social skills, and hyperactivity, in early adulthood [51–53]. Adolescents who are food insecure may have impulse control and susceptibility to maladaptive behaviours, including substance misuse. This finding calls for policymakers to ensure that household members, including adolescents, are food secured to help reduce their behavioural vulnerabilities.

Evidence from the adolescent health literature highlights the interconnectedness of behavioural risk factors, with studies indicating that adolescents who engage in truancy are also more likely to participate in other risky behaviours, including physical fighting, alcohol consumption, marijuana use, and risky sexual practices [19, 23, 54]. It is important to note that polysubstance use among adolescents is very pervasive, as adolescents who use tobacco and marijuana are more likely to use alcohol and other substances [19, 30, 55].

The results showed that being physically attacked was associated with lower odds of current alcohol use but higher odds of lifetime alcohol trouble. Individuals who have experienced physical attacks/assaults may abstain from drinking as a coping strategy. Nevertheless, individuals who use alcohol may engage in problematic behaviours, which can increase their risk of alcohol-related issues [56]. These findings underline the need for policymakers to support adolescents who consume alcohol, helping them better cope with daily life issues, including the trauma of being physically attacked.

The study found that loneliness was associated with higher odds of drunkenness among adolescents. The finding of this study supports previous studies that found an association between loneliness and increased use of alcohol [57–59]. A plausible explanation is that when people are lonely, they may use alcohol to cope with their loneliness. Also, consistent with previous studies [60, 61], this study found that anxiety was associated with higher odds of drunkenness among adolescents. Adolescents who experience anxiety are more vulnerable to problematic alcohol use, such as drunkenness [62]. Furthermore, supporting previous studies [63], this study found an association between suicidal ideation and drunkenness among adolescents. The association between these mental health issues (anxiety, loneliness, and suicidal ideation) and drunkenness indicates that adolescents with these mental health issues are vulnerable and require interventions, such as counselling and therapy, to enhance their health and wellbeing.

Protective factors against alcohol use behaviours

Evidence from this study showed that peer support and parenting variables are key protective factors against alcohol use behaviours, such as drunkenness. For example, parental supervision and bonding are key protective factors against alcohol use behaviours, such as drunkenness. Parental supervision may ensure that adolescents do not engage in deviant behaviours, such as drunkenness. This finding supports previous studies, which found that parental supervision leads to lower use of alcohol [64, 65]. Also, adolescents with strong bonds with their parents may avoid engaging in deviant behaviour that could jeopardise their relationship. Hence, they may abstain from alcohol use.

However, peer support, parental understanding, and parental monitoring of privacy were significantly associated with lower odds of alcohol use behaviours in the unadjusted models, indicating their potential relevance for consideration in the development of intervention strategies targeting in-school adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa. The impact of parental involvement on adolescents’ health and educational outcomes has been reported by several studies [22, 32, 33, 40, 66–68]. Parental bonding can provide a safe haven for adolescents to express their challenges and be addressed adequately rather than resorting to alcohol and other substances to deal with their problems [69, 70].

Implications for public mental health practice and research

There is a need for comprehensive alcohol education programs in schools across the selected countries in SSA. These programs should focus on increasing awareness about the risks associated with alcohol use, addressing peer pressure, and building life skills to resist alcohol consumption. School health coordinators of the country-specific educational ministries or departments at the school level can implement such programmes. Stricter regulations on the marketing and sales of alcoholic beverages targeting adolescents should be enforced. There is a need for longitudinal research and data collection to examine the trends in alcohol use behaviours among school-going adolescents in SSA to determine whether interventions and public health policies regarding alcohol use behaviours at national and regional levels are yielding the desired results. Such research could document the contexts of drinking, the duration of the drinking occasion, and the consequences experienced.

Limitations

One of the major limitations of this study is the variations in the study period across the countries which could bias the findings. Since major events in each country could characterise the periods, the variations seen might not reflect any systemic problems but transient occurrences. Additionally, since alcohol use is not acceptable among adolescents in several countries, there could be social desirability issues that might influence the estimated prevalence rates of alcohol use behaviours. Although used in many public health researches, single-item questions lack psychometric soundness in adequately measuring the key constructs under investigation and thus, should be considered in the interpretation of the findings. Another key limitation is the number of missing data on some of the study variables as this could influence the rates of alcohol use behaviours reported across the eight countries. This is because cases with incomplete dataset for the variables of interests were dropped in the analysis. Finally, the pooling of data from the eight countries with significant variations may limit the interpretations of data in terms of the macro-system level factors that could influence alcohol use behaviours among adolescents in SSA.

Conclusion

The findings of relatively high alcohol use behaviours among in-school adolescents in SSA pose a significant public health challenge to the continent. The in-country variations in the prevalence rates of alcohol use behaviours among adolescents in the selected SSA countries underscore the need for tailored interventions that will take into account specific contextual factors that influence their alcohol use behaviours. The identification of significant risk and protective factors for alcohol use behaviours among adolescents provides valuable information in designing health promotion interventions that could be tailored to the needs of adolescents. Thus, the complex influences of both personal characteristics (e.g., age, sex, anxiety, smoking cigarettes, among others), relationships (e.g., parental supervision, parental bonding, and having no close friends), and community and society (e.g., being bullied, being physically attacked, being involved in a physical fight, and truancy)-level factors in influencing adolescents’ alcohol use behaviours emphasised the importance of the socio-ecological model in addressing health risk behaviours among adolescents.
