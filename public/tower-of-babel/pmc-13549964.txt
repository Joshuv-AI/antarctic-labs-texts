Unlocking bipolar electrode-electrolyte solid-solid conversion in aqueous zinc-halogen batteries for high Zn utilization across various cell formats
Authors: Ao Liu, Sheng Chen, Hongyu Qin, Mangwei Cui, Youfa Liu, Shubing Wei, Kun Zhang, Yan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing demand for high-energy-density batteries pushes practical aqueous Zn-based batteries. However, high Zn utilization achieved in coin symmetric cells is unable to translate to full cells, especially large pouch cells, due to the poor reversibility of Zn and capacity output of high-loading positive electrodes. Here, solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms via chelation at electrodes-electrolyte interfaces effectively suppress parasitic reactions being widely prevalent under traditional solid-liquid conversion mechanisms. Consequently, high Zn utilization ( ≥ 78.3%) is unified across coin symmetric, coin full and pouch full cells. The Zn | |I2 pouch cell (10 cm×10 cm) demonstrates a specific energy of 116.8 Wh kg−1 (based on total mass) and over 850 cycles (85.1% capacity retention) at 10 mA cm−2. Furthermore, solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms exhibit universality across halogen positive electrodes, advancing practical aqueous Zn | |halogen batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

Aqueous Zn-based batteries (AZBs) offer inherent safety and fast-charging capabilities. However, the practical application of AZBs is still hindered by the critical challenge of low specific energy1–4, which is inextricably linked to the low Zn utilization at the negative electrode side (Supplementary Fig. S1)5,6. Although previous studies have achieved long-term stability of Zn | |Zn coin symmetric cells at high depth-of-discharge of Zn (DODZn, DODZn = 68.1–91.5%), the realization of high DODZn full cells, especially large pouch full cells, still remains a big challenge (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table S1)7–15. Part of the reason is that in a full cell configuration, the Zn negative electrode is unable to continuously replenish Zn2+ from the counter electrode as in symmetric cells, leading to inferior cyclability of full cells at high DODZn4,16. Even worse, as the battery size increases, side-reactions such as Zn dendrite formation, corrosion and hydrogen evolution become more prominent in pouch full cells, further exacerbating the problem17.

Another contributing factor originates from the positive electrode side. For the intercalation-type positive electrodes based on the Zn2+/H+ insertion/extraction mechanism, irreversible phase transitions tend to occur during the battery cycling18,19, which severely impede ion diffusion within high-mass-loading electrodes, leading to a non-linear correlation between the areal capacity and the material loading (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, conversion-type positive electrodes (e.g., halogens) do not rely on the Zn2+ de/intercalation into the host lattice. The composite architecture comprising by the active materials and porous conductive carbon offers negligible ion transport barriers, effectively lowers the charge transfer resistance of full cells even under high-mass-loading, allowing the areal capacity to increase proportionally with the material loading (Fig. 1c). This feature lays a solid groundwork for developing full cells with high DODZn, particularly in large pouch cells20–26. However, halide anions generated during the discharge process exacerbate the dissolution of positive electrode materials, meanwhile the resulted poly-halide anions further shuttle through the electrolyte and corrode the Zn negative electrode (Fig. 1d).

Although previous studies have reported effective routes for protecting the Zn negative electrode and mitigating the poly-halide shuttle effect, these strategies are still confined to solid-liquid conversion mechanisms, which cannot fundamentally solve the associated inherently severe parasitic reactions. Consequently, the DODZn in current aqueous Zn | |halogen cells typically fall below 30%, and their overall specific energy is generally under 20 Wh kg−1 27–29. Compared with the solid-liquid conversion mechanism, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism can effectively suppress the rampant and active side-reactions at the electrode/electrolyte interface, greatly improving the battery stability. The achievement of solid-solid interfacial conversion requires that Zn2+ and halide anions remain in a non-free ionic status during the cycling. That is to say, at the negative electrode side, Zn2+ are captured from the electrolyte to form solid-phase intermediates that are subsequently reduced operando to metallic Zn, meanwhile promoting rapid Zn2+ diffusion on the electrode surface to prevent the formation of depletion zones and inhibit Zn dendrite growth. At the positive electrode side, halide ions generated from the halogen reduction are trapped within the solid matrices and re-oxidized to halogen during charging. Chelating agents, which undergo reversible circular (water-insoluble) and linear (water-soluble) structural transformation during the complexation/decomplexation process30, serve as a type of electrolyte additives to implement solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms at both electrodes.

Results

High Zn utilization in the triad of batteries by triethylenetetramine (TETA)

Herein, TETA is introduced as a chelating agent to enable solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms at electrode interfaces, thereby delivering stable cycling performance under high DODZn across three types of batteries mentioned above. Zn negative electrode with 100 μm thickness achieves stable cycling stability under harsh conditions (DODZn = 68.3% (40 mAh cm−2), 40 mA cm−2; DODZn = 85.6% (50 mAh cm−2), 50 mA cm−2) in TETA-ZnSO4 electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. S3a, b). Even under a more practical condition of 20 μm-thickness Zn, Zn | |Zn coin symmetric cells can still operate for over 400 h at 10 mA cm−2 and 100 h at 40 mA cm−2 under DODZn = 85.6% (10 mAh cm−2) (Supplementary Fig. S3c and Fig. 2a), which are competitive with recently reported results (Supplementary Fig. S4). Subsequent electrochemical measurements are all tested with a Zn foil thickness of 20 μm unless specified.

The Zn | |I2 coin full cell exhibits a stable cycling performance for 110 cycles under high DODZn of 88% and high current density of 20 mA cm−2 (Fig. 2b), delivering a specific energy of 180 Wh kg−1 (based on the mass of negative electrode and positive electrode). Moreover, when DODZn = 66.5% (7.8 mAh cm−2) is achieved, it shows a capacity retention of 87.5% after 3200 cycles, corresponding to a cumulative capacity as high as 24.96 Ah (Supplementary Fig. S5). Notably, in a scaled-up configuration, the 10 × 10 cm2 Zn | |I2 pouch cell achieves stable cycling over 845 cycles under DODZn = 78.3% and 10 mA cm−2, delivering a specific energy of 52.3 Wh kg−1 (based on the total mass of the cell) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table S2). By multilayer stacking of electrodes, the capacity of the pouch cell is further extended to 5.57 Ah with the 50 μm Zn negative electrode, meanwhile its specific energy is still maintained at 50.9 Wh kg−1 (Supplementary Table S3). The cell retains a high-capacity retention of 80.4% even under 200 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The above results fully demonstrate that TETA realizes the unified high DODZn across coin symmetric cells, coin full cells and pouch full cells. Importantly, pouch full cells exhibit advantages in terms of cyclability, capacity and overall specific energy, which are competitive with recently reported results (Fig. 2d, e)28,31–37.

Chelation and solvation structures of TETA

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are used to analyze the structure of TETA in the electrolyte. In the MD simulations, the concentration parameters for each substance are set according to the actual concentrations in the pouch cell. For detailed parameter specifications, please refer to Tables 1 and 2. As revealed by the simulations, TETA shows a chain-like conformation in the unchelated status and spontaneously transforms to a circular structure by combining Zn atom through N atoms TETA shows a chain-like conformation in the unchelated status and spontaneously transforms to a circular structure by combining Zn atom through N atoms (Fig. 3a, b-i, c and Supplementary Data 1). In a fully charged state under the pouch cell configuration, i.e., high I− concentration at the positive electrode/electrolyte interface, I− replaces one of the coordinated H2O molecules in the chelate and forms TETA-Zn-I ternary chelate (Fig. 3b-ii, c). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations of solvation free energies of different Zn2+ complexes reveal that, compared with the traditionally solvated ion Zn2+–(H2O)6, TETA-Zn binary chelate exhibits a lower solvation free energy. When I− participated to form the TETA-Zn-I chelate, the solvation energy of the ternary chelate further decreases to –636.5 kcal−1, confirming the good thermodynamic stability of the solvated chelate. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S7, upon Zn2+ chelation, the formation of the chelate ring induces steric shielding that shifts 13C signals upfield, while the simultaneous inductive effect results in the deshielding of protons, shifting 2H signals downfield. Subsequently, the introduction of I− triggers an electron cloud rearrangement due to steric hindrance, causing a reverse trend where 13C signals shift back downfield and 2H signals shift upfield, confirming the participation of iodine in the coordination sphere. Consistent with our theoretical calculations, the NMR results explicitly confirm the formation of a cyclic TETA-Zn chelate. Furthermore, the spectral data verifies that I− actively participate in the coordination sphere of this cyclic structure. As a result, the activation energy for Zn2+ desolvation decreases markedly from 22.8 to 11.3 kJ mol−1 upon TETA participation in the Zn2+ solvation structure (Supplementary Figs. S8, S9). This reduction arises from the strong Zn2+-TETA coordination, which reconstructs the primary solvation sheath, lowers the number of coordinated water molecules, and thus facilitates Zn2+ desolvation.

The solvation structure of the newly formed chelate is then verified by multiple spectroscopic characterization techniques. In the Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR), the stretching vibration peaks corresponding to SO42− and O–H in TETA-ZnSO4 electrolyte exhibits a shift compared with those in the blank ZnSO4 electrolyte, suggesting the introduction of TETA alters the solvation structure of coordination environment of Zn2+ (Supplementary Fig. S12). The characteristic peak of SO42⁻ in the Raman spectra shifts from 978.2 cm−1 to 984.7 cm−1, further confirming that TETA modifies the primary solvation shell of Zn2+. That is, the chelation between TETA and Zn2+ restricts the combination between Zn2+ and SO4⁻ (Supplementary Fig. S13). A similar result is observed in the 1H NMR spectra. Upon TETA addition, the strong interaction between TETA and Zn2+ reduces the amount of free H2O in the primary solvation shell of hydrated Zn2+, disrupts the hydrogen-bond network among H2O molecules, driving the 1H chemical shifts toward the low-field region (Supplementary Fig. S14). Driven by its robust chelating capability, trace TETA effectively remodels the bulk solvation structure by liberating water molecules and reorganizing hydrogen bond networks, while concurrent Zeta potential shifts validate the specific interfacial adsorption of TETA-Zn species and the subsequent coordination of iodide anions (Supplementary Fig. S15).

The thermodynamics of iodine reactions with different solvation structures are further elucidated by DFT calculations (Fig. 3e). In the typical ZnSO4 electrolyte, the conversion from I⁻ to I3⁻ shows a significant negative Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of − 7.37 kJ mol⁻1, which promotes the formation of abundant I3⁻, resulting in a severe shuttle effect that compromises the battery performance20,38,39. In comparison, for the ternary TETA-Zn-I chelate formed with I⁻, the ΔG value for this conversion is nearly neutral ( − 0.17 kJ mol⁻1), indicating that the spontaneous I to I3⁻ process is effectively suppressed. Consequently, this resulting chelation structure simultaneously mitigates the shuttle effect of the I2 positive electrode and the corrosion of the Zn negative electrode, thereby ensuring the realization of high-performance aqueous Zn | |I2 batteries.

Solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms at electrode/electrolyte interfaces

Concentration polarization at the negative electrode/electrolyte interface plays a crucial role in the formation of Zn dendrites40. Especially under high current density, the mismatch between Zn2+ transport kinetics and charge transfer rate leads to local Zn2+ depletion at the interface, thereby inducing and accelerating dendrite growth41–44. Hence, increasing the Zn2+ diffusion rate is key to mitigating concentration polarization and suppressing dendrite formation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is used to compare Zn2+ transport kinetics in different electrolytes (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S16). During the plating process, the Zn2+ diffusion relaxation time in the TETA-ZnSO4 binary chelate system (τ = 12 s) is remarkably shorter than that in the blank ZnSO4 electrolyte (τ = 90 s), which is likely attributed to the resulting interfacial reaction pathway provided by the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism.

To unveil this proposed interfacial reaction pathway, operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) technology is employed to monitor the interfacial evolution of Zn negative electrode during the charge/discharge process, using TETA-Zn chelate as negative electrode, active carbon (AC) as positive electrode and LiI/Li2SO4 mixed aqueous solution as electrolyte, respectively (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. S17). Then, the redox behavior of TETA-Zn chelate is verified by assembling a TETA-Zn | |Ti cell in a Na2SO4 electrolyte (pure TETA-Zn chelate is synthesized by incorporating TETA into ZnSO4 electrolyte). The CV curve exhibits distinct redox peaks similar to a conventional Zn negative electrode, confirming that solid-solid interfacial conversion is both kinetically feasible and mechanistically sound (Supplementary Fig. S18). According to the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism, the Zn negative electrode undergoes a reduction from TETA-Zn to metallic Zn during charging and shows reverse reaction during discharging. The characteristic peaks attributed to the binary chelate (8.53°, 16.4° and 18.5°) gradually weaken as charging progresses, meanwhile the characteristic peaks of Zn (36.5°, 38.8° and 40.6°) correspondingly increase. The above analysis indicates that the Zn plating in this work should proceed via the observed solid-solid conversion mechanism10. In this process, Zn2+ in the electrolyte is coordinated with TETA molecules to form a circular TETA-Zn binary chelate, which deposits as metallic Zn on the negative electrode surface through the solid-solid interfacial conversion, aligning well with the theoretical calculations mentioned above. In detail, TETA coordinates Zn2+ via its N atoms to form circular chelates. During the plating process, the chelate ring opens to release Zn2+ and deposits as metallic Zn, regenerating the linear TETA molecules and re-chelating Zn2+ in the electrolyte, continuing the plating process (Fig. 4c).

Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis reveals a significant Zn signal in the TETA-Zn binary chelate powder (Supplementary Fig. S19). Subsequently, the TETA-Zn binary chelate powder is immersed into a saturated Na2SO4 electrolyte and subjected to electrochemical plating in a Cu | |Pt electrode system. The plated Cu foil surface exhibits a silvery metallic luster (Supplementary Fig. S20), which further supports the feasibility of the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism.

While operando transmission electron microscopy (TEM) would be helpful for directly visualizing the dynamic process, its application in AZBs is challenging due to the low saturated vapor pressure of water, which poses a risk to the equipment. Despite this limitation, the ex-situ TEM characterization provides valuable insights into the solid-solid interfacial conversion process of the TETA-Zn chelate, offering evidence consistent with the proposed mechanism Prior to charging, TETA-Zn chelate shows a large lattice spacing of 0.93 nm (Supplementary Fig. S21). Such a distinctively large spacing shows the species as a coordination complex formed between Zn2+ and TETA, effectively ruling out the presence of common inorganic zinc compounds (ZnN: 0.26 nm; ZnO: 0.281 nm)45,46. Upon charging, dark regions emerge within the powder matrix in the TEM images, suggesting the nucleation of metallic Zn. This is decisively corroborated by the observed lattice spacing of 0.241 nm, which corresponds to the characteristic plane of metallic Zn (002)47, providing strong evidence for the operando generation of Zn metal.

Compared to the conventional solid-liquid conversion mechanism of Zn plating, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism exhibits a higher nucleation overpotential, lower corrosion current, and a more negative hydrogen evolution potential (Supplementary Figs. S22–S24), effectively enhancing the stability of Zn negative electrode in the electrolyte and improving the uniformity of Zn plating. Normalized chronoamperometry (Supplementary Fig. S25) and surface migration measurements (Supplementary Fig. S26) indicate that this solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism does not fundamentally alter the nucleation process of Zn2+. Instead, according to the Scharifker-Hills model, the interfacial layer provides abundant nucleation sites that are progressively activated. These uniformly distributed Zn nuclei guide the subsequent growth of Zn metal and significantly restrict the 2D diffusion of Zn2+. Consequently, the crystal growth exhibits a dense and planar morphology, leading to uniform Zn plating32,37,48. Meanwhile, operando pH test demonstrates that compared to the blank ZnSO4 electrolyte, the TETA-ZnSO4 electrolyte effectively stabilizes the pH at the Zn negative electrode interface during cycling, preventing the massive formation of zinc hydroxide sulfate caused by localized pH elevation10,49,50 (Supplementary Fig. S27). Even after 30 days of immersion in the electrolyte, only trace amounts of zinc hydroxide sulfate formed on the Zn electrode surface in the TETA-ZnSO4 electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. S28), and the symmetric cells using this electrolyte maintain stable interfacial impedance after 3 days of rest (Supplementary Fig. S29).

Benefiting from the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism, the cycling stability and Coulombic efficiency of Zn | |Cu half-cells are significantly improved (Supplementary Fig. S30). Moreover, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism effectively suppresses the dendrite growth and the formation of pits caused by uneven stripping (Supplementary Figs. S31–S34), markedly enhancing the cycling performance of Zn negative electrodes under high DODZn conditions. In addition, XRD tests demonstrate that the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism promotes the preferred orientation of Zn plating, improving the uniformity and surface smoothness of the Zn negative electrode (Supplementary Figs. S35, S36). Hence, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism at the negative electrode side unifies the high DODZn in coin symmetric cells, coin full cells and pouch full cells, significantly alleviating the cycling lifetime limitations under high DODZn that restrict the development of high-energy-density AZBs.

As mentioned above, TETA forms the TETA-Zn-I ternary chelate upon the introduction of I− at positive electrode (Fig. 3b-ii). Herein, the formation of the ternary chelate is confirmed by mixing ZnI2 solution with TETA, isolating and purifying the resulting powder, and characterizing it through multiple techniques including EDS, Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Figure. S37, S38). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy shows that after adding TETA into a ZnI2 solution that containing I3⁻ and resting for 24 h, the characteristic absorption peaks of I3⁻ completely disappeared (Fig. 4d), indicating that the TETA-Zn ternary chelate significantly suppresses I3⁻ formation. Moreover, the generation of I3⁻ during the charge/discharge process is qualitatively analyzed by dynamic response testing (DRT). As shown in Fig. 4e, the impedance peak intensity associated with the I3⁻ shuttle effect is significantly reduced in the TETA-ZnSO4 electrolyte compared to the cell using a blank ZnSO4 electrolyte, further confirming the strong inhibitory effect of TETA on I3⁻51,52. Operando UV-Vis spectroscopy reveals that for the battery using the bare ZnI2 positive electrode, significant absorption peaks of I3─ ions (located at approximately 280 nm and 350 nm) emerge in the electrolyte during the discharge process (Supplementary Fig. S39a). In stark contrast, the signal for I3─ ions in the electrolyte of the TETA-ZnI2 positive electrode remains considerably weak (Supplementary Fig. S39b). The absence of a substantial I3─concentration in the TETA-ZnI2 electrolyte suggests that the interfacial solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism effectively suppresses the dissolution and shuttle effect of iodine species. Consequently, this leads to the stable cycling performance of Zn-I2 batteries under high areal capacity conditions. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests verify the electrochemical activity of the TETA-Zn-I ternary chelate as a positive electrode material, showing highly reversible redox peaks at 1.26 V and 1.17 V (Fig. 4f), thus confirming the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism at the positive electrode side (Fig. 4g). The STEM-EDS elemental mapping images offer evidence for the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S40a–c, the iodine signal is uniformly distributed throughout the TETA-Zn framework, confirming the formation of the solid-state TETA-Zn-I species. Upon the completion of the conversion reaction (Supplementary Fig. S40d–f), although the iodine signal is significantly diminished—indicating the extraction of iodine from the lattice—the morphology of the framework remains remarkably intact. The observed reduction in Zn content results from the breakdown of the TETA-Zn-I lattice, as the removal of iodine disrupts the essential Zn-TETA-I cooperative coordination, leading to some release of Zn2+, which are in turn captured by the TETA ligands in the electrolyte. This maintenance of morphological integrity and framework stability provides compelling evidence that the redox process proceeds via an operando solid-solid phase transition, effectively circumventing the dissolution-loss issues prevalent in traditional conversion-type positive electrodes.

Benefiting from the synergistic regulation of both negative electrode and positive electrode interfaces by solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms, Zn | |I2 batteries demonstrate stable electrochemical performance, maintaining low cell polarization (Supplementary Fig. S41) and good rate capability even under high areal capacity conditions (Supplementary Fig. S42). It is important to highlight that in AZBs, severe self-discharge caused by the spontaneous corrosion of Zn negative electrode coupled with side-reactions at the positive electrode remains a critical bottleneck for practical applications53. The solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms not only significantly enhance the interfacial stability of Zn negative electrode but also suppress the formation of I3⁻, thereby inhibiting the I3⁻ shuttle effect and minimizing the self-discharge rate of Zn | |I2 full cells to a good low level. A 10 mAh coin cell exhibits only 1.9% self-discharge after resting for 72 h, far lower than the 22.33% observed in blank ZnSO4 electrolyte cells (Supplementary Fig. S43). The Zn | |I2 battery based on the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms exhibits a self-discharge rate that is competitive with recently reported advanced AZBs (Supplementary Fig. S44). Even when the battery capacity is scaled up 100-fold to 1 Ah, the Zn | |I2 pouch cell maintains favorable anti-self-discharge performance, retaining 93.1% of its capacity after 72 h of rest (Supplementary Fig. S45).

Extending to a broader range of battery systems

Beyond the I2 positive electrode, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism is extended to a broader range of battery systems with various conversion-type halogen positive electrodes. To systematically verify the universality of the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism, two-electron Zn | |Br2 and Zn | |Cl2 batteries as well as four-electron Zn | |Br2 batteries and Zn | |I2 batteries, are assembled by utilizing Br2 and Cl2 positive electrode or activating multi-electron transfer in Br2 and I2 positive electrode, further enhancing the overall specific energy of the batteries (Supplementary Fig. S46)54–60.

Specifically, by mixing ZnBr2 solution with TETA, followed by separation and purification, a TETA-Zn-Br ternary chelate compound is prepared. The stable coordination between Br⁻ and the metal chelate is confirmed by EDS, Raman spectroscopy and XPS (Supplementary Figs. S47, S48). CV curves (Fig. 5a) shows that the TETA-Zn-Br exhibits electrochemical reactivity and distinct solid-solid interfacial conversion characteristics similar to those of the TETA-Zn-I chelate (Fig. 5b). A 0.6 Ah Zn | |Br2 pouch cell with 286 stable cycles at DODZn = 66.7% is achieved based on the solid-solid mechanism (Fig. 5c), delivering a specific energy of 73.2 Wh kg−1 (based on the total cell mass) (Supplementary Table S4). Furthermore, we evaluated the stability of the redox-active TETA-Zn-X (I and Br) chelates by employing them as positive electrodes. Even in a standard 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte, the positive electrode demonstrated good cycling stability based on the solid-solid interfacial conversion reaction. Specifically, the iodine-based species retained a specific capacity of 93.7 mAh g−1 after 10000 cycles. Meanwhile, the unmodified bromine-based species exhibited a high discharge voltage of approximately 1.7 V and sustained cycling for 400 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S49).

Notably, the solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanism also demonstrates certain advantages in four-electron conversion systems. Zn | |I2 and Zn | |Br2 batteries based on four-electron conversion exhibit average discharge voltages of 1.55 V and 1.85 V, respectively, and theoretical specific capacities of 422 mAh g⁻1 (I2) and 710 mAh g⁻1 (Br2)52–55. By further optimizing the electrolyte composition (3 M ZnSO4 + 1 M ZnCl2), four-electron conversion reactions of I⁻/I0/I⁺ and Br⁻/Br0/Br+ in pouch cells are realized (Fig. 5d, f). Among them, the four-electron Zn | |Br2 pouch cell displays a high average discharge plateau of 1.85 V and a capacity of 0.9 Ah, achieving improved Zn utilization and specific energy relative to previously reported Zn | |Br2 batteries (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. S50, S51 and Supplementary Table S5). While the four-electron Zn | |I2 pouch cell realizes 96 stable cycles at 1.6 Ah capacity and 78% Zn utilization, with a specific energy of 116.8 Wh kg−1 (based on the total cell mass) (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Table S6). Even when the cell capacity is scaled up to 3.92 Ah, a distinct voltage plateau of four-electron conversion is still observed (Supplementary Figure. S52). Rigorous tests confirm that the Zn | |I2 pouch cell maintains safety (Supplementary Figure. S53), retaining an open-circuit voltage (OCV) above 1.1 V and an internal resistance below 200 mΩ, thereby validating the robustness, safety, and practical viability of the assembled AZBs under extreme conditions.

Discussion

In summary, we propose solid-solid interfacial conversion mechanisms between negative electrode/positive electrode and electrolyte interfaces utilizing chelating agent as electrolyte additive, which unify high DODZn in coin symmetric, coin full and pouch full cells and result in the competitive specific energy and capacity records for Zn | |halogen pouch full batteries in current AZBs (116.8 Wh kg−1, 5.57 Ah). The proposed solid-solid interfacial conversion reaction mechanisms at the electrode/electrolyte interfaces pave the way for high specific energy AZBs under practical conditions and are expected to be extendable to other metal ion battery systems.

Methods

Preparation of electrolytes

ZnSO4·7H2O (ACS, Aladdin) and C6H18N4 (AR, Aladdin) are used as received. ZnSO4·7H2O (2 mol L−1) and C6H18N4 (10 mmol L−1) are first dissolved in deionized water and used for the two-electron reaction (I−/I0 or Br−/Br0).

ZnCl2 (ACS, Aladdin) are used as received. ZnSO4·7H2O (3 mol L−1), ZnCl2 (1 mol L−1) and C6H18N4 (10 mmol L−1) are first dissolved in deionized water and used for the four-electron reaction (I−/I0/I+ or Br−/Br0/Br+).

The electrolyte preparation was conducted in an ambient atmosphere at 25 ± 2 °C. All electrolytes were prepared immediately prior to use.

Preparation of I2@AC and tetrapropylammonium tribromide (TPABr3) positive electrodes

I2 (ACS, Aladdin), active carbon (AC) (AR, Xian Feng) are used as received. I2@AC is prepared via a typical melt-diffusion method, with the following steps: First, AC and I2 are weighed with a 1:3 ratio and ground for 30 min to ensure thorough mixing. The homogeneous mixture is then transferred into a quartz glass tube, sealed and subjected to thermal treatment at 90 °C for 4 h. After cooling, the product is collected, transferred into a quartz reagent bottle, sealed and stored in the dark for preservation. The I2 content is determined to be 68% (Supplementary Fig. S54).

HBr (ACS, Aladdin), KBr (ACS, Aladdin), NaBrO3 (ACS, Aladdin) and C12H28BrN (ACS, Aladdin) are used as received. The TPABr3 positive electrode is prepared via a simple co-precipitation method. First, 10 mL of 120 mM Br3− solution is obtained by mixing 3 mL of 2 mol L−1 HBr, 3 mL of 2 mol L−1 KBr and 4 mL of 0.1 mol L−1 NaBrO3, followed by stirring in the dark for 10 min. The resulting Br3− solution shows a deep orange color. Next, 1 mL of deionized water and 1 mL of 1 mol L−1 tetrapropylammonium bromide (TPABr) are added to 3 mL of the 120 mmol L−1 Br3− solution, allowing Br3− to react with TPABr to form TPABr3. After washing with deionized water, centrifugation and vacuum drying, the bromine positive electrode active material is obtained. Finally, the product is transferred to a quartz reagent bottle, sealed and stored in the dark for preservation.

For the I2@AC or TPABr3 positive electrode with low mass loading, a wet preparation method is adopted. Specifically, I2@AC or TPABr3, Ketjen black (EC-300J), and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) 5130 are uniformly mixed at a mass ratio of 8:1:1. Then, N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) is added at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:4, and the slurry is magnetically stirred at 500 rpm for 12 h. Subsequently, the slurry is uniformly coated onto the Ti foil using a doctor blade. The coated foil is then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 12 h, followed by cutting into Φ12 mm discs for further use. The I2@AC or TPABr3 loading is 2 ~ 3 mg cm−2.

For the I2@AC or TPABr3 positive electrode with high mass loading, a dry preparation method is adopted. Specifically, I2@AC or TPABr3 and Ketjen black (EC-300J) are first mixed at a mass ratio of 9:0.5, followed by the addition of 5 wt.% polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). After thorough mixing, the blend is transferred to a preheated mortar and ground repeatedly for 5 min. During this process, the PTFE undergoes fibrillation and uniformly encapsulates the active materials. The resulting fibrous positive electrode mixture is repeatedly rolled using a roller press to form a freestanding film. This film is then laminated onto a 100-mesh Ti grid (Olegeeino, Ti grid were used as received) and cut into appropriately sized pieces for subsequent use. The areal capacity of the positive electrode can be adjusted by controlling the thickness of the freestanding film.

Preparation of NaV3O8 and MnO2 positive electrodes

Typically, 2.0 g of V2O5 (99%, Aladdin) powder is added into 30 mL of 2 mol L−1 NaCl (99%, Aladdin) solution under continuous stirring. After 96 h of stirring at ambient temperature, the solution exhibits a distinct color change from yellow to brown. The resulting products are then rinsed several times with deionized water and ethanol to remove byproducts. Finally, the NaV3O8 powders can be obtained by vacuum drying at 80 °C.

MnO2 are obtained from Canrd Technology Co. Ltd. and used as received.

Similarly, for NaV3O8 or MnO2 positive electrode with low mass loading, a wet preparation method is employed except that NaV3O8 or MnO2 as the active material, while for high mass loading, a dry preparation method is employed except that NaV3O8 or MnO2 as active material.

Preparation of TETA-ZnSO4, TETA-ZnI2 and TETA-ZnBr2 electrodes

Specifically, 10 mM TETA is added to 2 mol L−1 ZnSO4, ZnI2 or ZnBr2 solution and stirred magnetically at 500 rpm for 1 h at room temperature. The resulting solution is then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 30 min to collect the chelate precipitate. The precipitate is washed three times with deionized water and ethanol, with each wash followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the purified chelate is vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h to obtain the powder product.

TETA-ZnSO4/TETA-ZnI2/TETA-ZnBr2, Ketjen Black and PTFE are mixed in an 8:1:1 ratio and pressed into a self-supporting film as the electrode. The as-prepared freestanding electrode is then laminated onto a 300-mesh Ti grid for subsequent use.

Materials characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is performed using a Rigaku Ultima IV. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) are conducted on a Gemini SEM360. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is carried out using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha. Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) is performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iN10. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is recorded on a Bruker 600 MHz instrument. Raman spectroscopy is conducted using a WITec alphaR300. Three-dimensional optical profilometry is performed on a UP-Lambda system. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectra are collected using a Thermo Fisher EVOLUTION One spectrometer. Operando pH measurements are conducted using a PHSJ-6L meter (Shanghai Leici). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is carried out on a HITACHI STA200.

Electrochemical measurements

All galvanostatic measurements are carried out on a NEWARE battery testing system. Electrochemical tests, including cyclic voltammetry (CV), Tafel plots, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and chronoamperometry (CA), are conducted on a CHI760e electrochemical workstation. CV curves for Zn | |TETA-ZnI2 batteries (2 e⁻) are recorded in the voltage range of 0.8–1.6 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1. For the 4 e⁻ process, the range is extended to 0.5–1.9 V at the same scan rate. Tafel plots for the Zn negative electrode in various electrolytes are obtained using a three-electrode system (Zn foil as working electrode, Pt foil as counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl as reference electrode) in the voltage range of – 0.7 to – 1.2 V at 1 mV s−1. EIS measurements are performed with an AC voltage of 10 mV for Zn | |Zn symmetric cells, Zn | |I2 cells, Zn | |MnO2 cells, and Zn | |NaV3O8 cells, covering a frequency range of 100 000 to 0.1 Hz.

For Zn | |Zn symmetric cells, Zn foil (Olegeeino, used as received) with a thickness of either 20 μm or 100 μm serves as the electrode, and CR-2032 coin cells are assembled using glass fiber (GF-F) as the separator, Zn electrodes as the working electrode, and Zn as the counter electrode, with 100 μL of electrolyte added per cell.

For Zn | |I2 coin cells under limited Zn supply (20 μm, 11.7 mAh cm−2), the areal mass loading of the I2 electrode is approximately 50 mg cm−2, and the voltage range is set to 0.5–1.6 V, and CR-2032 coin cells are assembled using glass fiber (GF-F) as the separator, I2 electrodes as the working electrode, and Zn as the counter electrode, with 100 μL of electrolyte added per cell.

For Zn | |I2 pouch cells, the negative electrode, positive electrode, and separator are Zn foil (20 μm, 10 × 10 cm2), I2 positive electrode (10 × 10 cm2), and glass fiber (Olegeeino, GF/F, 10.5 × 10.5 cm2).

For Zn | |Br2 pouch cells, Zn foil (20 μm, 10 × 10 cm2), a TPABr3 positive electrode (10 × 10 cm2), and glass fiber (Olegeeino, GF/F, 10.5 × 10.5 cm2) serve as the negative electrode, positive electrode, and separator, respectively. CV patterns for Zn | |TETA-ZnBr2 batteries (2 e⁻) are recorded from 1 to 2 V at 1 mV s−1, while those for the 4 e⁻ process are obtained from 0.8 to 2.2 V at the same scan rate.

Pouch cells are tested under specific pressure conditions (80 kPa) using a multichannel battery tester (Neware CT-4008T) at 25 °C in an environmental chamber. All cells undergo a pre-test rest period, which is essential for TETA–Zn2+ interactions to ensure sufficient adsorption onto the Zn negative electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. S55). This step is a prerequisite for the subsequent interfacial solid–solid conversion.

To guarantee experimental reproducibility, all measurements were performed on at least three independently prepared batteries for each condition.

Operando XRD test

AC and PTFE are evenly mixed in a 9:1 ratio and pressed into a self-supporting film to serve as the positive electrode. TETA-ZnSO4, Ketjen Black, and PTFE are mixed in an 8:1:1 ratio and pressed into a self-supporting film as the negative electrode. The battery is assembled using a Φ19 mm glass fiber separator, Li2SO4 (1 mol L−1)/LiI (0.2 mol L−1) electrolyte, and Ti mesh as the current collector.

The Ti mesh, positive electrode, separator, negative electrode and another Ti mesh are stacked sequentially and placed into a quartz glass disc, followed by the addition of 200 μL electrolyte. Polyimide tape is used to package the battery, ensuring that the negative electrode surface remains flat and wrinkle-free. Operando XRD measurements are then performed after resting the battery for 2 h.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Simulations of the three Zn2+-based electrolyte systems are performed using the Gromacs program suite with a hybrid force field combining General Amber Force Field (GAFF), Merz ion parameters, and OPC3 water model. Specifically, H2O, I− and Zn2+ are described using OPC3 and Merz parameters, respectively. The force field parameters for SO42− are generated via the mSeminario method with RESP charges, while TETA is parameterized using GAFF with AM1-BCC atomic charges. All topology files are generated directly using the AuToFF web server.

Initial simulation boxes with dimensions of 50×50×50 nm3, containing the electrolyte components, are created using the Packmol program. Each electrolyte system contains 150 cations. The structures are first energy-minimized and then annealed from 0 to 298.15 K over 1 ns (with a time step of 1 ps) to reach an equilibrium state. The temperature is maintained at 298.15 K using the velocity-rescale thermostat with a relaxation constant of 1 ps. The pressure is maintained at 1.01325 × 105 Pa using Berendsen’s barostat with an isothermal compressibility constant of 4.5 × 10−5. Periodic boundary conditions are applied in all directions. Electrostatic interactions and van der Waals forces are treated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff distance of 15 Å.

Following the energy minimization and equilibration steps, a 10 ns MD simulation is performed, with trajectories saved every 1 ps. The results are analyzed using the Gromacs tool suites, the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) program, and additional researcher-written scripts. From the MD trajectories, various Zn2+ solvation structures are selected for subsequent theoretical calculations of solvation free energy.

The geometries of TETA, I−, H2O, and their Zn2+ complexes are optimized using density functional theory (DFT) at the PBE0/def2-SVP level. The DFT-D3 dispersion correction is applied to describe dispersion interactions. To further account for the solvation effect of H2O, the SMD (Solvation Model Based on Density) implicit solvent model is employed in all calculations. Vibrational frequency analysis is performed on the optimized structures using the same method to obtain zero-point energy and free energy corrections. The thermodynamic correction terms and Gibbs free energies at 298.15 K are then derived using the Shermo program. To achieve higher accuracy in electronic energy—which critically influences the Gibbs free energy—single-point calculations are performed on the optimized structures at the PBE0/def2-TZVP level.
