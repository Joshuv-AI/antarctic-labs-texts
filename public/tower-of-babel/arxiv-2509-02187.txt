Divisibility by p p for Markoff-like Surfaces
Matthew de Courcy-Ireland, Matthew Litman, Yuma Mizuno

Abstract
. We study orbits in a family of Markoff-like surfaces with extra off-diagonal terms over prime fields 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} . It is shown that, for a typical surface of this form, every non-trivial orbit has size divisible by p p . This extends a theorem of W.Y. Chen from the Markoff surface itself to others in this family. The proof closely follows and elaborates on a recent argument of D.E. Martin. We expect that there is just one orbit generically. For some special parameters, we prove that there are at least two or four orbits. Cayley’s cubic surface plays a role in parametrising the exceptional cases and dictating the number of solutions mod p p .

1.3.
Graphs

Orbits can be thought of as connected components in a graph where a vertex

is adjacent to
m_{i}x
for each of the moves (

i=1,2,3

The following proposition shows that there are no bigons in these graphs.
If there are two different edges between vertices

is a fixed point for both the corresponding moves.
This fact is important for the proof of Theorem

However, for the statement, one can take any triples

Proposition 1.3
.

If
m_{i}x=m_{j}y
where
i\neq j
and
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}
, then

.

Proof.

Suppose

and

.
Since each move changes only one coordinate, from

and

agree in the second and third coordinates.
Similarly from

2.
Divisibility of orbit sizes

In this section, we prove the main result Theorem
1.1

[

For simplicity, let us first give a proof assuming that

in a given orbit. It is also helpful to imagine that

As we will argue later, these assumptions can be removed as long as

It is also worth noting that, if

Proof.

With

and taking the indices cyclically, we can write (

If no

vanishes, then dividing both sides by

gives

(12)

\sum_{i\bmod 3}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i-1}x_{i+1}}+\frac{a_{i}}{x_{i}}\Big)=s.

We have

\sum_{i\bmod 3}\frac{a_{i}}{x_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\bmod 3}\Big(\frac{a_{j-1}}{x_{j-1}}+\frac{a_{j+1}}{x_{j+1}}\Big)

since the second sum
\sum_{j\bmod 3}
amounts to summing
a_{i}/x_{i}
with each index counted twice (once as
i-1+1
and again as

).

This motivates the definition, for

, of three functions

(13)

\Delta_{i}(x)\coloneqq\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i-1}x_{i+1}}+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{a_{i-1}}{x_{i-1}}+\frac{a_{i+1}}{x_{i+1}}\Big).

From (
1

12
), they satisfy

(14)

\sum_{i\bmod 3}\Delta_{i}(x)=s.

From (
2
), it follows that

(15)

\Delta_{i}(x)+\Delta_{i}(m_{i}x)=s

for each index
i
.
If
x=m_{i}x
is fixed by a move, then for that index

\Delta_{i}(x)=\frac{s}{2}.

If a particular coordinate vanishes, say
x_{i}=0
, then (
13
) can be taken as a definition of the corresponding
\Delta_{i}(x)
.
The same formula for the other two
\Delta_{i\pm 1}(x)
would involve division by
0
. However, as we argue in the next sections, the definition can be extended so that (
14
) and (
15
) continue to hold.
See (
22
) and (
23
) for a suitable extension.

Assuming this extension is possible, here is how to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Let

be the number of points

in an orbit

By (

sV=\sum_{x\in O}s=\sum_{x\in O}\sum_{i\bmod 3}\Delta_{i}(x)=\sum_{i}\sum_{x\in O}\Delta_{i}(x).

Since

is an orbit, we have

for each
x\in O
.
It is a union of pairs

together, perhaps, with some singletons

.
By (

\sum_{x\in O}\Delta_{i}(x)=\sum_{\{x,m_{i}x\}}s+\sum_{x=m_{i}x}\frac{s}{2}=\frac{sV}{2}.

Combining these steps, we get the same sum three times

∎
