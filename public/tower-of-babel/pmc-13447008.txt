Molecular heterogeneity and prognostic biomarkers in primary meningeal melanocytic tumors
Authors: Rosina Paterra, Monica Patanè, Anna Stroppa, Gianluca Marucci, Serena Ammendola, Sofia Asioli, Valeria Barresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary meningeal melanocytic tumors (PMMTs) are rare neoplasms currently stratified by the World Health Organization (WHO) into three grades of malignancy based exclusively on histopathological criteria, while the prognostic significance of molecular alterations remains poorly defined. This study aimed to investigate the prognostic relevance of histopathological and molecular features in PMMTs and to assess the diagnostic utility of immunohistochemical and molecular markers for distinguishing PMMTs from malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumors (MMNSTs). Thirty‐six primary central nervous system (CNS) melanocytic tumors, including 24 PMMTs (10 grade 1, 12 grade 2, and 2 grade 3) and 12 MMNSTs, were analyzed through integrated histopathological, immunohistochemical, genetic, and epigenetic characterization. Among grade 2 PMMTs, descriptively defined CNS‐invasive otherwise benign melanocytomas defined a clinically favorable subgroup, whereas SF3B1 mutations and chromosome 8q gains were associated with higher recurrence rates and shorter recurrence‐free survival. Losses of chromosome 17 or 21q were observed exclusively in MMNSTs, supporting their potential diagnostic utility in distinguishing MMNSTs from PMMTs. These findings demonstrate biological heterogeneity among intermediate‐grade PMMTs and identify molecular markers associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Integrated histopathological and molecular characterization may enhance prognostic stratification and diagnostic accuracy in primary CNS melanocytic tumors.

INTRODUCTION

Primary meningeal melanocytic tumors (PMMTs) are rare neoplasms, comprising less than 0.05% of all primary tumors of the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. In the current World Health Organization (WHO) classification, PMMTs are stratified into: (1) melanocytomas, CNS WHO grade 1, defined by a mitotic index <0.5 mitoses/mm2 and the absence of necrosis or invasion of CNS parenchyma; (2) intermediate‐grade melanocytomas, CNS WHO grade 2, characterized by CNS parenchymal invasion and/or a mitotic index of 0.5–1.4 mitoses/mm2; and (3) melanomas, CNS WHO grade 3, defined by the presence of necrosis and/or a mitotic index ≥1.5 mitoses/mm2 [2]. Given the higher recurrence risk and disease‐specific mortality associated with melanomas [3], adjuvant radiotherapy is recommended, irrespective of the extent of surgical resection achieved [4]. By contrast, melanocytomas are generally treated with radiotherapy after subtotal resection [4]. However, the small number of available studies with sufficiently long follow‐up has hampered a robust assessment of the prognostic value of this grading system. In addition, the grading criteria were only standardized in the most recent WHO (2021) classification [2], complicating direct comparisons across historical cohorts. The underrepresentation of intermediate‐grade melanocytomas in published series has further limited reliable estimation of recurrence risk in this subgroup. Chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs)—including loss of chromosome 3 or 6q, gain of chromosomes 6p or 8q—and mutations in SF3B1 have been detected predominantly in grade 3 tumors, suggesting an adverse prognostic impact [5, 6, 7]. Nonetheless, the influence of these genetic alterations on recurrence‐free survival (RFS) has not been systematically investigated, thereby limiting their current applicability for postoperative risk assessment.

PMMTs exhibit morphological and immunohistochemical overlap with malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumors (MMNSTs) [8], which makes this differential diagnosis challenging based only on histopathology [9]. In this context, the potential utility of somatic mutation analysis was suggested, as the majority of MMNSTs [10] are characterized by PRKAR1A mutations and the immunohistochemical loss of the encoded protein, whereas PMMTs more commonly harbor mutations in GNAQ, GNA11, and SF3B1 [6, 7, 9, 11]. However, a case report of an MMNST harboring concomitant mutations in both PRKAR1A and GNAQ indicates that GNAQ mutations are not specific to PMMTs, as previously believed [12]. The limited available studies on DNA methylation profiling of PMMTs and MMNSTs suggest that these neoplasms display distinct methylation signatures that may aid in their differential diagnosis, although validation in larger, independent cohorts is required.

This study aimed to investigate the prognostic significance of genetic alterations in a histologically and epigenetically characterized cohort of PMMTs and to assess the diagnostic utility of immunohistochemical and molecular markers for distinguishing these neoplasms from MMNSTs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cases

All cases with a histopathological diagnosis of circumscribed PMMT or MMNST, and available paraffin‐embedded tissue blocks in the Units of Anatomic Pathology of Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta (Milan, Italy) and the University of Verona (Verona, Italy) were included.

In detail, the study cohort comprised 23 tumors diagnosed as PMMTs (9 WHO grade 1, 11 WHO grade 2, and 3 WHO grade 3) and 13 tumors diagnosed as MMNSTs.

Ethical issues

This study was approved by Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 4 (protocol n. CET 03/26, BST 01/26, 2026/04/08) and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histological revision

We reviewed histological slides from all cases to assess the number of mitoses per mm2, CNS invasion, and necrosis, and to determine histopathological grading according to WHO 2021 criteria in PMMTs [2].

Clinical data

Information on tumor localization, extent of surgical resection, and RFS was retrieved from clinical records and surgical reports. Patients lost to follow‐up were censored at their last documented clinical encounter.

Tumor sites were classified into two groups: (1) intracranial, (2) spinal.

Immunohistochemistry

All samples were immunostained using antibodies against PRAME (clone EP461, Cell Marque, Millipore Sigma, CA, USA; dilution 1:400), BAP1 (clone C4, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA; dilution 1:100), PRKAR1A (polyclonal, Sigma Aldrich, MA, USA; dilution 1:100) using an automated immunostainer.

Sections of testis were used as a positive control for PRAME immunoreactions.

Endothelial cells served as internal positive controls for BAP1 immunostaining.

PRKAR1A expression was interpreted as retained when diffuse cytoplasmic staining was present in tumor cells and as lost when tumor cells lacked cytoplasmic staining in the presence of positive internal controls (endothelial cells).

Next‐generation sequencing

All cases were analyzed using the QIAseq Targeted Human Comprehensive Cancer Panel (DHS‐3501Z, Qiagen, Milan, Italy), targeting 275 cancer‐related genes for the detection of somatic single nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions. FFPE tissue sections (5 μm) were obtained from representative tumor blocks. Tumor‐rich areas were manually microdissected under pathologist supervision to ensure adequate neoplastic cellularity (>30%). Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentration and amplifiable DNA quality were assessed using the QIAseq DNA QuantiMIZE Assay Kit (Qiagen), and only samples meeting the recommended quality thresholds were processed further. In cases with moderate to heavy melanin pigmentation, additional measures were implemented to mitigate melanin‐associated PCR inhibition.

Samples failing initial library preparation or quality control metrics were reprocessed or excluded. Libraries were prepared using QIAseq Targeted DNA Library Kits with QIAseq 12‐Index I adapters, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library concentration and fragment size distribution were evaluated using the QIAseq Library Quant Assay. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired‐end reads.

Bioinformatic analysis was conducted using CLC Genomics Workbench and QIAGEN Clinical Insight (QCI) Interpret software (Qiagen). Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), and variant calling was performed using validated pipelines. Variants were filtered based on quality metrics, read depth, and allele frequency, and annotated using QCI Interpret. Only variants meeting established quality criteria were retained for downstream analysis.

DNA methylation analysis

Methylation analysis was performed in all cases, using the Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC v2.0 BeadChip targeting ~930 K unique methylation sites on 500 ng of DNA extracted from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue sections in enriched tumor areas (tumor purity >90%). Raw DNA methylation files (.idat) were imported and processed using the minfi package in R. Cases were classified using the Heidelberg methylation classifier v. 12.8 (accessible at “https://epignostix.com/”) and the Bethesda NCI CNS tumor classifier v2.0 (accessible at https://methylscape.ccr.cancer.gov/). For both classifiers, calibrated scores ≥0.90 were considered a confident match, scores between 0.30 and 0.89 were considered suggestive, and scores <0.30 were regarded as no match.

DNA methylation arrays were used to assess CNV. Generation of the copy number profiles was based on conumee2 [13].

Multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification

CNVs involving chromosomes 1p, 3, 6, and 8 were assessed by multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA; SALSA MLPA Probemix P027, MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) in cases with an integrated diagnosis of PMMTs in which DNA methylation profiling was unsuccessful.

Results obtained by ABI Prism 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Life Technologies) were analyzed with the Coffalyser.Net Software (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands).

Statistical analyses

We used the Fisher exact test or chi‐square test to analyze the correlations between the clinical (sex of the patients, site of the tumor), histological, and molecular parameters and tumor recurrence in PMMTs. RFS was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with the date of primary surgery as the entry point and the detection of a recurrent tumor as the endpoint. The Mantel‐Cox log‐rank test was used to compare RFS distributions according to clinical, histological, or molecular parameters. Given the rarity of PMMTs and the limited sample size, statistical analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. A probability (p) value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc 12.1.4.0 statistical software (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium).

RESULTS

Cases

Clinical, histological, and molecular data are summarized in Figure 1.

The integration of histopathological and molecular data led to the reclassification of three cases. In detail, in two tumors histologically classified as MMNSTs (cases 10 and 22 in Figure 1), the integrated diagnosis was melanocytoma, CNS WHO grade 1 (case 10), or intermediate‐grade melanocytoma, CNS WHO grade 2 (case 22). In one case with a histological diagnosis of melanoma, the integrated diagnosis was MMNST (case 25). Therefore, the cohort finally included 24 PMMTs (10 grade 1, 12 grade 2, and 2 grade 3) and 12 MMNSTs following integrated histopathological and molecular classification.

Clinical and histological features of PMMTs

PMMTs were resected from 10 male and 14 female patients (mean age 55 ± 14 years; age range: 25–84 years). Eight tumors originated in the intracranial meninges, whereas 16 originated in the spinal compartment. Surgical resection was complete in 13 cases and partial in 11.

Four cases were classified as intermediate‐grade melanocytomas based on CNS invasion in the absence of other histopathological criteria qualifying for CNS WHO grade 2. For descriptive purposes, we refer to these tumors as “CNS‐invasive otherwise benign” melanocytomas, although this term is not included in the current WHO classification (cases 11, 12, 14, and 15 in Figure 1).

Postoperative radiotherapy was administered to seven patients with PMMTs, including six with intermediate‐grade melanocytomas and one with a melanoma. Among the treated intermediate‐grade melanocytomas, five were mitotically active, and one was CNS‐invasive otherwise benign.

Tumor recurrence was observed in eight patients, six with intermediate‐grade melanocytomas and two with melanomas (Figure 1). The median follow‐up was 47 months (range, 13–150 months) in non‐recurrent patients. The median time to recurrence was 30 months (range, 17–72 months) in recurrent patients. All treated mitotically active melanocytomas developed recurrence during follow‐up, whereas the treated CNS‐invasive otherwise benign melanocytoma remained recurrence‐free. Recurrence was significantly associated with CNS WHO grade (p = 0.0052) (Table 1). Among CNS WHO grade 2 tumors, none of the CNS‐invasive, otherwise histologically benign lesions recurred (p = 0.0006). Moreover, RFS was comparable between patients with CNS WHO grade 1 melanocytomas and those with CNS WHO grade 2 CNS‐invasive otherwise benign tumors, and was longer in both groups than in patients with CNS WHO grade 2 tumors characterized by a high mitotic index or those with CNS WHO grade 3 tumors (p = 0.0029) (Figure 2; Table 1). No statistically significant differences in recurrence risk or RFS were observed according to patient sex, tumor site or extent of surgical resection (Table 1).

Clinical and histological features of MMNSTs

MMNSTs were resected from six males and six females (mean age 50 ± 17 years; age range: 19–77 years).

Three cases were located intracranially, namely two in the Meckel cave and one in the cavernous sinus, whereas 9 originated in the spinal compartment (Figure 1). Surgical resection was complete in 11 cases. Mitotic index was <0.5/mm2 in six cases, between 0.5 and 1.4/mm2 in five cases, and ≥1.5/mm2 in one case. One case exhibited necrosis, and none was CNS‐invasive (Figure 1). During follow‐up, tumor recurrence occurred in 2 of 12 patients with MMNSTs, none of whom had received adjuvant radiotherapy.

Immunohistochemical markers

PRAME immunohistochemical expression was negative in all analyzed cases.

PRKAR1A immunostaining could not be evaluated in three cases, including a melanocytoma CNS WHO grade 1 (case 8) and two MMNSTs (cases 29 and 36), which showed absent immunostaining in the endothelial cells serving as an internal positive control. All other PMMTs retained the expression of this protein, whereas PRKAR1A expression was lost in eight of the remaining 10 MMNSTs (Figure 3).

PRKAR1A immunohistochemical expression in an MMNST. PRKAR1A immunohistochemical expression is lost in tumor cells and retained in non‐neoplastic cells.

BAP1 expression could not be evaluated in two cases: a melanocytoma CNS WHO grade 1 (case 8) and one MMNST (case 31), both of which showed absent immunostaining in the endothelial cells, serving as an internal positive control. BAP1 immunostaining was retained in all cases except one (case 15), an intermediate‐grade melanocytoma that did not recur.

Gene mutations

In nine cases, including four PMMTs and five MMNSTs, low sequencing coverage prevented obtaining conclusive results from the NGS analysis (Figure 1). This limitation was likely attributable to the high melanin content in these samples.

PMMTs harbored mutually exclusive mutations in GNAQ and GNA11 in 11 and 6 cases, respectively (Figure 1; details in Table S1). SF3B1 mutations were detected in three intermediate‐grade melanocytomas and two melanomas, all of which recurred (p = 0.001). Three intermediate‐grade melanocytomas harbored BAP1 mutations, with no significant association with recurrence (Table 1). Finally, one melanoma harbored a pTERT mutation, and none of the cases harbored PRKAR1A mutations. Patients with tumors harboring an SF3B1 mutation had a significantly shorter RFS compared to patients with wild‐type tumors (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4).

RFS is significantly shorter in patients with PMMTs harboring SF3B1 mutations than in those with wild‐type tumors.

MMNSTs harbored PRKAR1A mutations in 6 out of 7 cases, all of which lacked the expression of the corresponding protein in neoplastic cells. One case lacking PRKAR1A mutations lacked immunohistochemical PRKAR1A expression in tumor cells. No cases exhibited mutations in GNAQ, GNA11, BAP1, SF3B1, or pTERT.

DNA methylation analysis

DNA fragmentation hindered the acquisition of conclusive results in nine cases in this study, including seven PMMTs and two MMNSTs. Results of the analysis are detailed in Table S1.

Using the Heidelberg classifier, DNA methylation profiles either matched or suggested the methylation class (MC) “melanocytoma” in 13 cases, and matched the MC “MMNST” in two cases. In addition, the profiles suggested the MC “schwannoma” in six cases, with variable calibrated scores.

Using the Bethesda classifier, DNA methylation profiles either matched or suggested the MC “melanocytoma” in 14 cases, and matched or suggested the MC “schwannoma” in nine cases.

One tumor was assigned to the superfamily “cranial and spinal nerve tumors” by the Heidelberg classifier and to the superfamily “mesenchymal tumors” by the Bethesda classifier, without a definitive match to any specific MC in either classifier. Four tumors did not match any MC using the Heidelberg classifier, although in the Bethesda classifier, their DNA methylation profiles matched the MC “control tissue reactive tumor microenvironment”, the MC “melanocytoma”, or suggested the MC “melanoma metastasis” or the MC “schwannoma”.

CNV

Chromosomal CNVs were inferred from DNA methylation array data in 27 tumors, including 17 PMMTs and 10 MMNSTs.

CNVs involving both chromosome arms were reported as whole‐chromosome gains or losses, whereas alterations confined to a single chromosome arm were reported using standard cytogenetic nomenclature (e.g., 17p or 21q).

In five of seven PMMTs with inconclusive DNA methylation results, alterations involving chromosomes 1p, 3, 6, and 8 were further assessed using multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA), increasing the number of evaluable PMMTs for these chromosomal alterations to 22. Further analyses could not be performed in the remaining two PMMTs caused by insufficient residual tissue. In PMMTs, gains of chromosome 6p represented the most frequent CNV, identified in 9 of 22 cases, followed by gains of chromosome 8q, detected in 8 cases. Among the five SF3B1‐mutant PMMTs, four also harbored chromosome 8q gain. Conversely, chromosome 8q gain was identified in four additional PMMTs lacking SF3B1 mutations. Losses of chromosomes 17p and 21 were each observed in a single case. The presence of CNVs was significantly associated with WHO tumor grade (p = 0.0032), with a higher prevalence in grade 2 and 3 tumors than in grade 1 lesions. Furthermore, CNVs were significantly more frequent in grade 2 melanocytomas with a high mitotic index than in otherwise benign CNS‐invasive tumors (p = 0.0026) (Figure 5).

CNV profiles obtained by DNA methylation arrays in a CNS‐invasive otherwise benign melanocytoma (A), in a mitotically active intermediate grade melanocytoma (B), and in an MMNST (C). Gains are indicated in red, losses in blue. CNS‐invasive otherwise benign showed a flat CNV, whereas the mitotically active intermediate grade melanocytoma harbored losses in 1p, 6q, 9p, and gains in 6p, and 8q. The MMNST showed a complex profile characterized by multiple CNVs, including the loss of chromosomes 17 and 21q.

Gains of chromosomes 6p and 8q were significantly associated with tumor recurrence (p = 0.001; p = 0.008), whereas no significant association was observed between losses of chromosomes 1p or 3 and recurrence (Table 1). In addition, gains of chromosomes 6p (p = 0.0139) and 8q (p = 0.0204) were significantly correlated with shorter RFS (Figures 6 and 7).

RFS is significantly shorter in patients with PMMTs harboring 6p gains than in those harboring tumors without 6p gains.

RFS is significantly shorter in patients with PMMTs harboring 8q gains than in those harboring tumors without 8q gains.

In MMNSTs, losses of chromosomes 17 and 21q represented the most frequent CNVs, each occurring in 7 of 10 cases (Figure 1). Loss of chromosome 1 was identified in 4 cases, and loss of 22q in 3. Notably, all 10 MMNSTs successfully analyzed for CNVs exhibited loss of either chromosome 17 or 21q (Figures 1 and 5).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the molecular profiles of 36 primary CNS melanocytic tumors and evaluated their association with clinical outcomes. By integrating histopathological and molecular findings, 24 tumors were classified as PMMTs and 12 as MMNSTs. Our analyses highlighted biological heterogeneity among CNS WHO grade 2 PMMTs, suggesting that the descriptively defined CNS‐invasive, otherwise benign melanocytomas may represent a more favorable prognostic subgroup, whereas SF3B1 mutations and gains of chromosomes 6p and 8q were associated with increased recurrence risk and shorter RFS. Furthermore, MMNSTs exhibited distinct chromosomal aberrations, with losses of chromosome 17 or 21q identified in all evaluable cases, supporting their diagnostic utility in distinguishing MMNSTs from PMMTs.

The current WHO grading system for PMMTs is based solely on histopathological parameters, namely mitotic index, CNS invasion, and necrosis [2]. Notably, the mitotic thresholds defining each grade were standardized only in the 2021 WHO classification. Consequently, the prognostic contribution of each grading parameter remains incompletely defined. Our data corroborate the prognostic relevance of the current grading system, while suggesting potentially divergent prognostic implications for CNS invasion and mitotic activity. None of the four CNS‐invasive, otherwise histologically benign, intermediate‐grade melanocytomas recurred during follow‐up, and these tumors showed RFS comparable to that of WHO grade 1 melanocytomas. Furthermore, these neoplasms exhibited a significantly lower burden of CNVs and lacked SF3B1 mutations, suggesting that they may represent a biologically distinct subset compared with mitotically active intermediate‐grade melanocytomas. These findings raise the possibility that, in this context, CNS invasion may reflect a local growth pattern rather than intrinsic biological aggressiveness.

SF3B1 mutations have previously been reported more frequently in leptomeningeal melanomas than in melanocytomas [14, 15]. However, follow‐up information was available for only a limited subset of reported cases [14, 15], and the absence of standardized histopathological grading criteria in earlier studies has hindered assessment of their prognostic significance, particularly in intermediate‐grade melanocytomas. In the present cohort, all three SF3B1‐mutant intermediate‐grade melanocytomas recurred, and patients harboring SF3B1‐mutant PMMTs had significantly shorter RFS than those with wild‐type tumors, suggesting the potential prognostic relevance of this alteration. Notably, all but one recurrent intermediate‐grade melanocytomas also harbored gains of 8q, which were associated with shorter RFS. These results are consistent with findings in uveal melanoma, where chromosome 8q gain is a well‐established adverse prognostic marker [16, 17]. Nonetheless, given the limited number of recurrence events, the prognostic significance of chromosome 8q gain should be interpreted cautiously. Collectively, these data suggest that PMMTs may encompass a biological spectrum, with more aggressive tumors characterized by the accumulation of genomic alterations. Although most SF3B1‐mutant PMMTs also exhibited chromosome 8q gain, chromosome 8q gain was not restricted to SF3B1‐mutant tumors. PMMTs exhibit morphological and immunohistochemical overlap with MMNSTs. This distinction is clinically relevant because MMNSTs may exhibit low mitotic activity [17] and mimic benign melanocytomas. Our findings support the utility of integrating histological, genetic, and epigenetic findings for the differential diagnosis. Consistent with previous studies [10], PRKAR1A mutations and/or the immunohistochemical loss of the corresponding protein were identified in almost all MMNSTs, supporting their diagnostic utility for this differential diagnosis. PRAME immunohistochemical expression was absent in all MMNSTs and PMMTs. Although PRAME has recently been proposed as a potential adjunctive marker in the differential diagnosis of primary CNS melanocytic tumors [18], the inclusion of only two melanomas, both of which were PRAME negative, precludes definitive conclusions regarding its utility in distinguishing primary leptomeningeal melanoma from melanocytoma or MMNST. Therefore, the diagnostic value of PRAME in this setting requires further validation in larger cohorts. Notably, all evaluable MMNSTs demonstrated loss of chromosome 17 or 21q, whereas these alterations were absent in PMMTs, in line with previous studies [5], supporting their potential utility in this differential diagnosis. Finally, our findings confirm previous evidence that PMMTs and MMNSTs are epigenetically distinct [5, 19, 20]. However, the utility of this analysis may be limited by high melanin content, which precludes successful profiling in a substantial proportion of cases. Moreover, some tumors did not match any methylation class using either the Heidelberg or Bethesda classifiers. Interestingly, only two cases matched the MMNST methylation class using the Heidelberg classifier, and none did so using the Bethesda classifier, while most MMNSTs instead showed methylation profiles compatible with or suggestive of schwannoma. This epigenetic overlap has been reported previously [5, 20] and is consistent with the current concept that both tumors arise from the Schwann cell lineage, although the biological basis and diagnostic implications of this methylation overlap warrant further investigation. Although methylation profiling did not distinguish PMMTs by grade, the derived CNV profiles provided clinically relevant information.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, the relatively small cohort size, the inclusion of only two melanomas, which precludes definitive conclusions regarding CNS WHO grade 3 PMMTs, the relatively limited median follow‐up of non‐recurrent patients for these slow‐growing tumors, and technical challenges related to melanin content that limited successful molecular and epigenetic analyses in a subset of cases. In addition, the limited number of recurrence events precluded robust multivariable survival modeling. Given the limited number of recurrence events relative to the number of clinicopathological and molecular variables evaluated, the statistical analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, and the identified prognostic associations require validation in larger independent cohorts. Postoperative radiotherapy was preferentially administered to patients with higher‐grade or mitotically active tumors and therefore represents a potential source of treatment‐related confounding. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate biological heterogeneity among intermediate‐grade PMMTs, with distinct genetic alterations associated with increased recurrence risk. SF3B1 mutations and chromosome 8q gains identify PMMTs at increased risk of recurrence, whereas CNS invasion in the absence of other adverse histological features may define a more favorable subgroup. Integrated histopathological, genetic, and epigenetic characterization of primary CNS melanocytic tumors improves both prognostic stratification and diagnostic accuracy, particularly in distinguishing PMMTs from MMNSTs. Further validation in larger independent cohorts will be necessary to confirm these findings and refine their clinical applicability.
