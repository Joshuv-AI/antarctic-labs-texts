A stabilized finite element method for a flow problem arising from 4D flow magnetic resonance imaging
Gabriel R. Barrenechea Cristian Cárcamo Abner H. Poza

Abstract
In this work we propose, analyze, and validate a stabilized finite element method for a flow problem arising from the assessment of 4D Flow Magnetic Resonance Imaging quality. Starting from the Navier-Stokes equation and splitting its velocity as the MRI-observed one (considered a datum) plus an “observation error”, a modified Navier-Stokes problem is derived. This procedure allows us to estimate the quality of the measured velocity fields, while also providing an alternative approach to pressure reconstruction, thereby avoiding invasive procedures. Since equal-order approximations have become a popular choice for problems linked to pressure recovery from MRI images, we design a stabilized finite element method allowing equal-order interpolations for velocity and pressure. In the linearized version of the resulting model, we prove stability and (optimal order) error estimates and test the method with a variety of numerical experiments testing both the linearized case and the more realistic nonlinear one.



Keywords
: 4D Flow MRI, Navier-Stokes equations, Stabilized finite element methods.

1
Introduction

A precise characterization of cardiovascular hemodynamics is fundamental for the early diagnosis and risk stratification in pathologies such as aortic aneurysms, valvular diseases and congenital heart defects
[
30
,
24
]
. In this scenario, 4D flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D flow MRI) has emerged as the non-invasive gold-standard technique to quantify in vivo the cardiovascular blood flow
[
23
]
. Unlike ultrasound or conventional 2D resonance techniques
[
20
,
35
]
, 4D flow MRI enables the acquisition of three-dimensional velocity fields resolved in time, opening the door to the calculation of hemodynamics biomarkers such as
helicity, oscillatory shear index, pressure, turbulent kinetic energy, vorticity, wall shear stress, among other quantities of interest that are necessary to characterize the physiological or pathological state of the cardiovascular system
[
12
]
. Nevertheless, the clinic use of this technology presents a longstanding obstacle: the data acquisition time. Encoding velocity in three spatial directions throughout the cardiac cycle requires the acquisition of a large amount of frequency-domain data (
k-
space).
As a result, the scanning time increases which may lead to the introduction of artifacts, thereby degrading the image quality and the reliability of subsequent numerical quantification
[
11
]
.

To lower the processing time, the scientific community has moved away from the classical Nyquist-Shanon sampling, which traditionally required fully sampling
k-
space to avoid aliasing
[
38
]
, to aggressive undersampling techniques that dramatically reduce acquisition time
[
30
]
. This implies that just a small fraction of the data is captured reducing the scanning time. However, the application of aggressive undersampling also means being careful, because otherwise we could obtain aliasing. The shift from Nyquist-Shannon to aggressive undersampling has been made possible by modern reconstruction strategies, which recover high-fidelity images from highly sparse
k-
space data, thereby enabling clinically feasible 4D flow MRI acquisition. In the past decade, two techniques have dominated the methodological landscape: Parallel Imaging (SENSE and GRAPPA)
[
34
,
22
,
40
,
37
]
and Compressed Sensing
[
27
,
29
,
31
,
32
]
. These methods assume that flow signal is sparse in some transformation domain, and reconstruct the image solving an optimization problem
[
14
,
34
]
.

Total variation regularization is a sparsity-promoting prior commonly used in compressed sensing MRI and 4D flow MRI reconstructions
[
27
,
28
]
.
Seminal studies have demonstrated that regularization based on the total variation and its generalized variants enable robust reconstructions with significant acceleration factors
[
14
]
. However, most of the algorithms of standard 4D flow data reconstruction process, ignore the physical nature of the subject of study, which is a moving incompressible fluid, and thus for example, violate fundamental physical principles, such as mass conservation. This gap introduces systematic errors in the computation of spatial derivatives, which suggests that purely phenomenological reconstruction pressure have reached a ceiling in their numerical precision. In order to obtain reliable approximations of hemodynamic biomarkers, including pressure differences, some approaches have been developed over the past decades.
Despite the careful handling and processing of 4D flow MRI data by trained technicians, the acquired measurements inevitably contain noise. This noise may now originate from limitations in instrument calibration as well as from involuntary patient motion during image acquisition.

In light of these considerations, we assume throughout this work that the available 4D flow MRI data consist of noisy velocity measurements, while the underlying physical velocity field
{\boldsymbol{u}}
satisfies the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. Decomposing the fluid velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}
into the reconstruction
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}
of the MRI-obtained data and an unknown noise component
{\boldsymbol{w}}
,
we establish, under suitable assumptions, that
{\boldsymbol{w}}

satisfies a modified version of the Navier-Stokes equations, whose source terms depend, among other factors, on
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}
. The unknowns in this differential problem are the noise velocity

{\boldsymbol{w}}
and the fluid pressure
p
, and in this way we obtain an alternative procedure for pressure reconstruction (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
21
]
).
Due to the nonlinear nature of the problem and dependence on time intrinsic to the pulsatile flow, the computational costs might be prohibitively expensive.
As a consequence of the above discussion, it has become usual to use low-order, equal order finite element pairs for velocity and pressure in the fluid solution (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
). This choice violates the standard
inf-sup condition that is advocated to prove stability (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
), but it can be extremely efficient if it is supplemented with appropriate stabilization terms.

Stabilized finite element methods appeared as a need to both enhance the stability for convection-dominated problems, and also to render velocity-pressure spaces that are not inf-sup stable
(in particular, equal-order spaces) usable in practice. Since the mid 1980’s there has been a vast amount of work devoted to this technique, and a full literature review is outside the scope
of the present work. Herein, we briefly mention that, for fluid problems, the methods can be divided into residual (such as PSPG/SDFEM
[
25
,
39
]
, GLS
[
18
,
41
,
13
,
33
]
, Unusual FEM
[
19
,
6
]
, just to name a few,
see
[
8
]
for a review), and non-residual (such as CIP
[
16
]
, or LPS
[
9
,
7
,
3
]
, see
[
36
,
26
]
for reviews).

In this work we propose a stabilized finite element method for a flow problem arising from 4D flow MRI. The design of the stabilizing term is inspired from the one analyzed in
[
5
]
, (and thus it can be aligned with the family of Unusual Stabilized finite element methods) as this
type of stabilization has provided very accurate stable numerical results, e.g., in
[
2
]
. The method’s presentation and analysis are presented for a linearized version of the problem, where we prove
well-posedness and optimal error estimates. Additionally, we
present numerical experiments for the steady-state Navier-Stokes equation,
showcasing the ability of the method to
recover pressure and velocities from piecewise constant data, as it would be natural in the 4D flow MRI context.
A related approach can be found in
[
21
]
, where a finite element scheme is studied, and one of the main contributions of the present work is the analysis of the optimal convergence of the proposed scheme.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows. The remaining of this introduction is devoted to presenting some preliminary notation used throughout the manuscript.
In Section

we present the derivation of the perturbed problem starting from the Navier-Stokes equation, which is then analyzed in Section

.
The stabilized finite element method for the linearized problem in presented in Section
4
, and its well-posedness and optimal order error estimates in velocity and pressure
are proven in Section
5
. Finally, in Section
6
we present a series of numerical experiments both validating the error estimates, and in more realistic cases.
Some conclusions and future work are drawn in Section
7
.

2
From the 4D flow MRI’s image to the model problem

We consider the dynamics of blood flow in human circulatory system modeled by
the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in the vessel lumen
\Omega
: Find the perfect velocity and pressure measurements
({\boldsymbol{u}},p)
, such that

(\textrm{NS})\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\rho\,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}-\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla p&=\,\boldsymbol{0}&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],\\
\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}&=\,0&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}&=\,\boldsymbol{0}&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T],\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)&=\,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}&\text{in }\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}
is an initial datum,

\mu>0
is the kinematic viscosity,
\rho>0
is the density,
and
T
denotes the final time.

As it was mentioned in the introduction, the main goal in this paper is to assess how
good the measurements made using MRI are. For this purpose, we denote by
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}
the reconstruction
of the 4D flow measurements (e.g., the interpolation of nodal values of the velocity), and we assume
that there is an additive noise
{\boldsymbol{w}}
in the velocity measurements, that satisfies the following properties:

(H1)

{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}+{\boldsymbol{w}}
in
\Omega
.

(H2)

\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}=-\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}=g
in
\Omega
.

(H3)

{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Using (H1)-(H3), we can see that a variational formulation of
(\textrm{NS})
is given by:

Find

({\boldsymbol{w}}(t),p(t))\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle\small\rho\,({\boldsymbol{w}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\mu\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})+

\displaystyle\,\rho\,(\nabla({\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}+{\boldsymbol{w}}){\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{v}})-(p,\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}})+(q,\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\rho\,(({\boldsymbol{u}}_{m})_{t},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\mu\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})-\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{v}})+(g,q),

(2.1)

for all
({\boldsymbol{v}},q)\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
.

Now, if we use a
semi-implicit Euler scheme with fixed time step
\tau
,
we have the following time discretization: Given
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
for
k=1,2,\dots,
find
({\boldsymbol{w}}^{k},p^{k})\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle\,\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}^{k},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\mu\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}^{k},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k}){\boldsymbol{w}}^{k},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}^{k}){\boldsymbol{w}}^{k-1},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}^{k}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k},{\boldsymbol{v}})-(p^{k},\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\,+(q,\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}^{k})=\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}^{k-1},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k-1},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\mu(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})-\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}^{k},{\boldsymbol{v}})+(g,q),

(2.2)

where
\sigma{\,:=\,}\dfrac{\rho}{\tau}
, for all
({\boldsymbol{v}},q)\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
.

The time scheme (
2.2
) is, in fact, the weak formulation of a
linear modified Oseen equation. So, in the next section we will introduce a model problem that
is an idealization of (
2.2
) and for which we will propose a stabilized finite element scheme.

3
Model problem and preliminary results

The singularly perturbed Oseen problem that will study in this work consists on finding the velocity
{\boldsymbol{w}}
and the pressure
p
such that satisfying
equation system

(\textrm{P})\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{w}}-\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{w}}+\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{w}}+\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}})(\boldsymbol{a}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m})+\nabla p&=\,{\boldsymbol{f}}&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}&=\,g&\text{in }\Omega,\\
{\boldsymbol{w}}&=\,\boldsymbol{0}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

where

{\boldsymbol{f}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
is the body force vector,
g\in L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
,

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{a}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
are given convective velocities field. In addition,
\boldsymbol{a}
is a given solenoidal datum,
and

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}
is the velocity field reconstructed from MRI-acquired data in
\Omega
. The concrete definition for each
case will be specified in Section
4
(cf. Remark
2
).

The standard variational formulation of
(\textrm{P})
reads:

Find

({\boldsymbol{w}},p)\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{a}({\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+b({\boldsymbol{v}},p)=

\displaystyle\,({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}})\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{H},

(3.3)

\displaystyle b({\boldsymbol{w}},q)=

\displaystyle\,{-(g,q)}\quad\forall q\in Q,

(3.4)

where
\tilde{a}:\boldsymbol{H}\times\boldsymbol{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
b:\boldsymbol{H}\times Q\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
are the bilinear forms defined by

\tilde{a}({\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}})=\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\mu\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}})(\boldsymbol{a}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}),{\boldsymbol{v}})\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{H},

(3.5)

and

b({\boldsymbol{v}},q)=-(q,\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}})\qquad\forall({\boldsymbol{v}},q)\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q.

(3.6)

The following results will be needed throughout the paper.

Lemma 1
.

For all
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{H}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
, we get

((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}})\boldsymbol{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{v}})=-((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})\boldsymbol{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{w}})-((\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}).

(3.7)

Proof.

See
[
10
]
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
and that

\sigma>4\rho\,\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}.

(3.8)

Then (
3.3
)-(
3.4
) has a unique solution
({\boldsymbol{w}},p)\in\boldsymbol{H}\times Q
.

Proof.

Using the assumption (
3.8
), Lemma
1
and that
\boldsymbol{a}
and is
divergence–free, for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{H}
we get

\displaystyle\tilde{a}({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})=

\displaystyle\,\sigma\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})\boldsymbol{a},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\sigma\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\dfrac{\rho}{2}\,((\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\left(\sigma-\rho\,\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}-\dfrac{\rho}{2}\,\|\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\right)\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\left(\sigma-2\rho\,\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\right)\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,{\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big\{\sigma\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\Big\},}

and thus
\tilde{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
is elliptic in
\boldsymbol{H}
.
On the other hand, it is well-known in the literature (for details, see
[
10
]
), that the bilinear form

satisfies the inf-sup condition:

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{H}}\dfrac{b({\boldsymbol{v}},q)}{\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\beta\,\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall q\in Q,

where
\beta
is a positive constant. Then the classical theory of Babuška-Brezzi can be applied to (
3.3
)-(
3.4
) and the existence and uniqueness of weak solution
({\boldsymbol{w}},p)
is guaranteed.
∎

The assumption (

) may appear as restrictive, but it is consistent with the CFL condition

4
The stabilized method

To propose the discrete scheme, in this work we will consider the following assumptions:

•

There is a shape-regular initial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
of
\overline{\Omega}

that allows the reconstruction of a velocity data
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{H}
(which will be specified later).

•

When the initial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
is refined, this defines
a shape-regular family
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
of partitions of
\overline{\Omega}

with elements
T
, corresponding to triangles in
d=2
and tetrahedra in
d=3
, respectively, of diameter
h_{T}
, and
h:=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}
.

•

There is an approximation of
\boldsymbol{a}
, denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}_{h}
, that belongs to the finite element space
\boldsymbol{H}_{h}
defined below.

Remark 2
.

The function
\boldsymbol{a}_{h}
may correspond to an approximation of the velocity noise in the previous step of a nonlinear scheme such as Newton or Picard iteration to solve the Navier-Stokes equation. For this reason in Section
5.2
we will consider that the convective term
\boldsymbol{a}
has the same regularity as the velocity noise
{\boldsymbol{w}}
and that his approximation
\boldsymbol{a}_{h}
is in the same space as
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}
.

Since the objective is to propose a discrete scheme of finite elements with spaces of the same order of approximation, we introduce the following finite element subspaces of
\boldsymbol{H}
and
Q

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{h}:=

\displaystyle\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in C(\overline{\Omega})^{d}\;:\;{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)^{d},\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}\cap\boldsymbol{H},

\displaystyle Q_{h}:=

\displaystyle\left\{q\in C(\overline{\Omega})\;:\;q|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T),\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}\cap Q,

with
k\geq 1
, where
\mathbb{P}_{k}
stands for the space of polynomials of total degree smaller than or equal to

Using the above-defined finite element spaces
the discrete stabilized scheme to approximate (

)-(
3.4
) is given by:
Find

({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h})\in\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

B_{\mathrm{stab}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})=F_{\mathrm{stab}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\qquad\forall({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\in\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h},

(4.9)

where
B_{\mathrm{stab}}:(\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h})\times(\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the bilinear form defined by

B_{\mathrm{stab}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h}){\,:=\,}\,A({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})+S_{\mathrm{conv}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+S_{\mathrm{press}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h}).

(4.10)

Here, the bilinear form
A(\cdot,\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h}){\,:=\,}

\displaystyle\,\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+\mu\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+

\displaystyle\,\lambda\,(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})-(p_{h},\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+(q_{h},\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}),

(4.11)

where
\lambda
is a positive parameter. Moreover, the modified convective term is

S_{\mathrm{conv}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}){\,:=\,}\,\rho\,((\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})(\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})+\dfrac{\rho}{2}\,\left((\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{h})\,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right),

(4.12)

and the pressure stabilization term is defined by

\small S_{\mathrm{press}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h};{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h}){\,:=\,}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Big(\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}-\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h}),\,-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\Big)_{T},

(4.13)

where

is the linear operator defined by
{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p_{h}){\,:=\,}\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}+\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})(\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m})+\nabla p_{h}
and the stabilization parameter is given by

(4.14)

where

Finally,

(4.15)

for all
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\in\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h}
.

5
Stability and error estimates

5.1
Well-posedness

We start recalling that, as a consequence of the standard inverse inequality
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Lemma 12.1]
), there exists
C_{\mathrm{inv}}>0
, independent of
h
,
such that

h_{T}\,\|\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{0,T}\leq\,C_{\mathrm{inv}}\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}|_{1,T},\\

(5.18)

for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{h}

In the product spaces
\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h}
, we consider the norm

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:=\left\{\sigma\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\lambda\,\|\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\|{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\|^{2}_{0,T}\right\}^{1/2},

for all
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})\in\boldsymbol{H}_{h}\times Q_{h}
.

The next result guarantees the well-posedness of the stabilized finite element scheme (

Lemma 2
.

\displaystyle\,C\rho\,h^{k}\,\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{k+1,\Omega}\,\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2,\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

(5.55)

On the other hand, to bound
I_{7}
, we need to decompose it in the follow way:

\displaystyle I_{7}=

\displaystyle\,\small\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})-\mu\,\Delta({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\small\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

\displaystyle\,\small-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\mu\,\Delta({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,I_{9}+I_{10}.

(5.56)

Before that we continue bounding these new terms, we need to note that using (
3.8
), (
4.16
)-(
4.17
)
and Lemma
5.18
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\left\|-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\right\|_{0,T}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,C\,\Bigg\{\sigma+\mu+\sigma^{-1}\rho^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}\Bigg\}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}.

(5.57)

Next, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
3.8
) and (
5.57
), we have

\displaystyle I_{9}=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\sigma\,({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h})+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,C\,\Bigg\{\sigma^{1/2}+\mu^{1/2}+\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

(5.58)

To estimate
I_{10}
, we use the interpolation operator
\boldsymbol{I}_{h}{\boldsymbol{w}}
and the definition of
\eta^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
and
e^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{h}
given in (
5.34
) join to (
5.57
) with Lemma
5.18
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\small I_{10}=

\displaystyle\,-

\displaystyle\small\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\mu\,\Delta({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\small\,C\,\Bigg\{\sigma^{1/2}+\mu^{1/2}+\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\Bigg\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mu h_{T}^{2}\,\Big[\|\Delta\eta^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\|^{2}_{0,T}+\|\Delta e^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,T}\Big]\Bigg\}^{1/2}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\small\,C\,\Bigg\{\sigma^{1/2}+\mu^{1/2}+\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\Bigg\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\Big[\mu h_{T}^{2}\,\|\eta^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\|^{2}_{2,T}+\mu\,|{\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}|^{2}_{1,T}

\displaystyle\small+\mu\,|\eta^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}|^{2}_{1,T}\Big]\Bigg\}^{1/2}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\small\,C\,\Bigg\{\sigma^{1/2}+\mu^{1/2}+\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\Bigg\{\mu^{1/2}h^{k}\,\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{k+1,\Omega}+{\left|\kern-0.96873pt\left|\kern-0.96873pt\left|({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-0.96873pt\right|\kern-0.96873pt\right|}\Bigg\}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

We may now to bound the last term on (
5.53
), using Cauchy-Schwarz and Hölder inequalities (with
L^{4},L^{4},L^{2}
), (
5.57
) and we obtain

\displaystyle I_{8}=

\displaystyle\,\small\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\Bigg(\rho\,(\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}})(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}),-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\Bigg)_{T}

(5.60)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\small\rho\,\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}\|_{0,4,T}^{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{0,4,T}^{2}\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\|-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\|_{0,T}^{2}\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\small\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\,\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}\|_{0,4,T}^{4}\right\}^{1/4}\,\,\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{0,4,T}^{4}\right\}^{1/4}\,\left\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\,\|-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\|_{0,T}^{2}\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\small C\,\rho\,\sigma^{-1/2}\,\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\Bigg\{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\tau_{T}\|-\sigma\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+\mu\,\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}+{\mathcal{L}}_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},0)\|_{0,T}^{2}\Bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\small C\,h^{k}\,\Bigg\{\rho+\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\mu^{1/2}+\sigma^{-1}\rho^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{a}_{h}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{m}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{k+1,\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

(5.61)

Thus, from (
5.53
)-(
5.56
) and (
5.58
)-(
5.61
), we get

I_{4}\leq\,C\,\Bigg\{C_{2}\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+h^{k}\,M_{4}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

(5.62)

Next, connecting (
5.52
) and (
5.62
) with (
5.49
), we can deduce that

\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq\,C\,\Bigg\{M_{3}\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+M_{4}\,h^{k}\,\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{2,\Omega}\Bigg\}\,\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\|_{1,\Omega}.

(5.63)

Finally, using estimate (
5.48
) in (
5.63
) with Theorem
2
and Hölder inequality (
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
) the result follow.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have presented and analyzed a stabilized finite element method for the modified (linearized) Navier-Stokes equation
arising from 4D MRI data. The method was proven stable and optimally convergent, and the numerical experiments show that this
scheme can be used successfully to recover the pressure and correct the velocity data errors. Despite this, there are several open questions
that deserve further investigation. For example, the extension to time-dependent problems is of practical interest. For such problems,
the choice of stabilization will be of paramount importance. This, as the search for pressure-robust and/or divergence-free discretizations of (P) will be the topic
of future research.
