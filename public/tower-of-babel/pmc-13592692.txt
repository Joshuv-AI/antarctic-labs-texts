Biogenic Synthesis and Nematicidal Potency of Highly Stable Ag and Fe Nanoparticles From Krascheninnikovia ceratoides and Elaeagnus angustifolia
Authors: Muhammad Ismail, Khursheed Ali, Fatima Aijaz Malik, Salma Javed, Manzoor Hussain, Iftikhar Ali, Hina Siddiqui, Douglas Law
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTManaging root‐knot nematodes, particularly Meloidogyne incognita, is a significant difficulty in sustainable agriculture because of the environmental toxicity of synthetic chemical nematicides. This study uses leaf extracts of Krascheninnikovia ceratoides (KC) and Elaeagnus angustifolia (EA) to synthesize silver (KC‐AgNPs) and iron (EA‐FeNPs) nanoparticles in a fast and environmentally friendly manner. The products were characterized by XRD, SEM, FTIR, and UV–visible spectroscopy, and their UV–visible spectral profiles were monitored for 30 days and under pH and temperature stress. XRD‐derived mean crystallite sizes were approximately 56 nm for KC‐AgNPs and 31 nm for EA‐FeNPs. SEM micrographs revealed quasi‐spherical and cubic morphologies for AgNPs, but FeNPs had faceted, polycrystalline tube architectures. FTIR supported the presence of plant‐derived surface functional groups but did not identify individual phytochemicals. At a final nanoparticle concentration of 1.000 mg/L, the KC‐AgNP and EA‐FeNP preparations produced 98% and 70% mortality, respectively, in second‐stage juveniles of M. incognita after 72 h. Since matched plant‐extract‐only controls were not included, the observed activity is attributed to the complete biogenic nanoparticle preparations rather than exclusively to the metallic cores. These findings indicate that biogenic AgNPs and FeNPs derived from K. ceratoides and E. angustifolia are stable and effective alternatives to conventional synthetic pesticides.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is a multidisciplinary scientific frontier that investigates materials at the nanoscale, which typically ranges from 1 to 100 nm. At this scale, quantum mechanical processes take precedence, giving nanomaterials distinct physicochemical, optical, and magnetic properties that differ significantly from their bulk counterparts. The ability to modify matter at the atomic and molecular levels has sparked a revolution in a variety of industries, including microchip technology, precision medicine, and environmental cleanup. Nanoparticles (NPs) have a high surface‐to‐volume ratio, which provides a broad platform for surface functionalization, making them attractive candidates for targeted delivery and catalytic applications [1, 2, 3]. Silver (AgNPs) and iron (FeNPs) have emerged as key materials due to their extensive antibacterial, catalytic, and magnetic properties [4]. Traditionally, these NPs have been generated using physical procedures (e.g., laser ablation, ball milling) or chemical processes (e.g., chemical reduction with sodium borohydride or hydrazine). While effective, these “top‐down” and “bottom‐up” procedures frequently require high energy consumption, specialized equipment, and the use of dangerous reagents that endanger human health and ecological systems [2, 5].

The “Green Synthesis” technique, which uses plant extracts, fungus, or bacteria as biological factories, has become the new paradigm [6]. Plant‐mediated synthesis is particularly favorable because it is a one‐step, cost‐effective approach that does not require high pressure or harmful stabilizing chemicals. Secondary metabolites, such as polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids, serve two functions in this “bioreduction” process: they reduce metal ions to zero‐valent or oxide states and then cap the NPs to avoid oxidation and undesired agglomeration [7, 8]. Krascheninnikovia ceratoides (L.) Gueldenst. (KC), a hardy shrub found in dry places, and Elaeagnus angustifolia L. (EA), noted for its therapeutic and antioxidant characteristics, are potential biological resources for nanotechnology. K. ceratoides contains phytochemicals that have developed to tolerate environmental stress, perhaps providing novel stabilizing ligands for AgNPs. E. angustifolia is known for its bioactive chemicals, which help reduce iron salts and create stable FeNPs. Several studies have used plant‐based methods to create iron oxide nanoparticles. Kumar et al. (2014) used Passiflora tripartita fruit extract for catalytic applications, while Venkateswarlu et al. (2013) used Syzygium cumini seed extracts to create magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles [9, 10]. The use of biogenic materials in agriculture is a vital response to the world's food security dilemma. The root‐knot nematode, Meloidogyne incognita, is a major agricultural pest that causes billions of dollars in annual crop losses by forming galls on plant roots and inhibiting nutrient uptake. The overuse of synthetic chemical nematicides has resulted in soil deterioration, groundwater contamination, and the rise of resistant nematode populations. Nanopesticides offer a revolutionary approach by providing targeted and long‐lasting toxicity to pests while minimizing environmental impact. Recent breakthroughs in pesticide chemistry have centered on generating new target‐specific pest‐control compounds, reflecting the critical need for safer and more effective alternatives to conventional agrochemicals [11, 12].

The objectives were to screen the two aqueous plant fractions against silver and iron precursors, characterize the successful nanoparticle preparations by XRD, SEM, FTIR, and UV–visible spectroscopy, assess their UV–visible spectral response during storage and under pH and temperature stress, and compare their in vitro activity against M. incognita juveniles. This study intends to provide a viable, low‐cost, and environmentally friendly framework for large‐scale production of metallic nanoparticles, establishing them as a cornerstone of next‐generation integrated pest management.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

The studies employed analytical‐grade solvents and reagents (from E. Merck and Fisher Scientific). Methanol, n‐hexane, dichloromethane, and acetone were all commercial grade. The solvents used in the experiment were distilled before use.

Plant Collection, Extraction, and Fractionation

Leaves of Krascheninnikovia ceratoides (L.) Gueldenst. (KC) were collected on 28 Jun 2024 from Nagar Proper (36.2333°N, 74.6500°E), Nagar District, Pakistan, and authenticated by Dr. Sujjad Haider, Department of Environmental Sciences, Karakoram International University (KIU). Voucher specimen KC‐75/24 was deposited in the KIU herbarium. Leaves of Elaeagnus angustifolia L. (EA) were collected on 22 Aug 2024 from Konodas (35.9242°N, 74.3681°E), Gilgit, Pakistan; authenticated by Prof. Dr. Sherwali Khan; and deposited as voucher EA‐112/24 in the KIU herbarium. The leaves were cleaned, air‐dried for five days, and milled. KC powder (275 g) was macerated three times with 2 L of 95% methanol per extraction, and EA powder (320 g) was macerated three times with 3 L of 95% methanol per extraction. Each extraction lasted overnight, and the extracts for each species were combined and filtered. Methanol was removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to yield 75 g (KC) and 83 g (EA) of crude extract. Each crude extract was suspended in 1 L of water and successively partitioned with n‐hexane (3 × 1 L) and dichloromethane (3 × 1 L); the residual aqueous fractions were retained for nanoparticle synthesis.

Preliminary Plant–Metal Screening and Nanoparticle Synthesis

Both aqueous plant fractions were preliminarily tested with both metal precursors. Under the conditions used, KC produced an isolable product with AgNO3, whereas EA did not; EA produced an isolable product with FeCl3, whereas KC did not. The unsuccessful reciprocal combinations showed no persistent reaction signature or recoverable nanoparticle product and were not characterized further. This outcome is specific to the tested conditions and does not exclude formation after optimization of precursor concentration, pH, temperature, or extract‐to‐precursor ratio. For KC‐AgNP synthesis, 90 mL of aqueous 1 mM AgNO3 was combined with 10 mL of KC aqueous fraction (9:1, v/v) under low‐light conditions. For EA‐FeNP synthesis, 90 mL of 10 mM FeCl3 was combined with 10 mL of EA aqueous fraction (90:1, v/v). The Fe reaction was magnetically stirred for 24 h. A yellow‐to‐dark‐brown color change was used as a preliminary indication of reaction progress; nanoparticle formation was evaluated by the analytical methods described below rather than by color alone.

Nanoparticle Characterization

The KC‐AgNP and EA‐FeNP preparations were examined by FTIR (AABB MB3000 spectrophotometer), powder XRD (Rigaku D/Max 2500 VBZ+/PC diffractometer), SEM (Hitachi S‐4700), and UV–visible spectroscopy (BMS/UV‐1602, 200–1100 nm). XRD was equipped with a Cu‐Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 30 mA. The diffraction data were recorded in the 2θ range of 10° to 80° with a step size of 0.02° and a continuous scan rate of 2°/min at room temperature. To prevent surface charging in SEM, the dried specimen was sputter‐coated with a thin layer of gold (approx. 5–10 nm). Micrographs were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5–15 kV and a working distance of 8–12 mm using the secondary electron detector. SEM size distributions were obtained from 200 measured particle features per preparation (n = 1). FTIR was used to assign functional‐group classes associated with surface‐bound plant constituents.

Nematicidal Activity

A Pakistani population of Meloidogyne incognita was maintained on eggplant (Solanum melongena L.) in a glasshouse at the National Nematological Research Center, University of Karachi. A 10 mg/mL stock suspension of each dried nanoparticle preparation was prepared using 5% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as solvent and diluted to final nanoparticle concentrations of 1.000, 0.500, and 0.125 mg/L. Carbofuran was tested as the positive control at the same nominal concentrations, and 5% DMSO without nanoparticles was used as the solvent/negative control. Each treatment was evaluated in three independent replicate wells (n = 3), with 100 freshly hatched second‐stage juveniles (J2 ) per replicate in a final assay volume of 200 µL. Mortality was assessed after 72 h using a stereomicroscope at 4× magnification. Juveniles that remained immobile after transfer to distilled water were scored as dead [13]. Matched KC‐ and EA‐extract‐only controls and metal‐precursor‐only controls were not included; accordingly, the assay cannot separate effects of the metal‐containing particles from effects of residual plant‐derived constituents in the nanoparticle preparations.

Statistical Analysis

Mortality was calculated for each replicate and is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent replicates, each comprising 100 s‐stage juveniles (J 2). Treatment and concentration effects were analyzed using two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) for mean separation at p ≤ 0.05. Prior to analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated and found to be satisfied. No data transformation, outlier removal, or exclusion of observations was performed. Probit regression analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.1 (PROC PROBIT) to estimate LC50 values with their 95% confidence intervals. Goodness‐of‐fit was assessed using the chi‐square (χ 2) test. SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for ANOVA and post‐hoc analyses.

Results and Discussion

Biogenic Synthesis of AgNPs and FeNPs

Preliminary cross‐screening of both aqueous plant fractions with both metal precursors produced two successful combinations under the tested conditions: KC with AgNO3 and EA with FeCl3. The reciprocal combinations (KC with FeCl3 and EA with AgNO3) did not yield detectable, isolable nanoparticle products and were not advanced to characterization. Plant secondary metabolites can contribute reducing and surface‐capping functions during nanoparticle synthesis [6, 7, 14, 15]. The different outcomes for KC and EA may reflect differences in extract composition and reaction conditions, but this explanation remains a hypothesis because constituent‐level chemical profiling was not performed.

FTIR Analysis and Phytochemical Capping

FTIR spectroscopy was used to assess functional groups associated with the nanoparticle preparations (Figure 1). Broad bands in the high‐wavenumber region are consistent with O–H stretching, whereas bands in the mid‐infrared region may include C═O, C═C, C─O, and C─N vibrations from plant‐derived organic matter [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. These assignments indicate classes of surface‐associated functional groups; they do not identify specific phenols, flavonoids, proteins, or other individual compounds in the absence of complementary chromatographic and mass‐spectrometric analyses.

The annotated peaks indicate transmission minima corresponding to vibrational modes of functional groups present on the nanoparticle surfaces, likely originating from plant‐derived capping and stabilizing agents during synthesis.

The presence of functional groups in the nanoparticle spectra indicates that biomolecules from K. ceratoides and E. angustifolia remained on the surfaces following synthesis. These surface‐bound phytochemicals are important because they can stabilize nanoparticles through electrostatic and steric interactions [17, 18]. Slight changes in FTIR absorption bands after nanoparticle manufacturing support the link between metallic cores and plant‐derived functional groups. These data add credence to the theory that the generated nanoparticles were capped with phytochemicals but do not demonstrate which molecules were involved or quantify which aided in their colloidal stability throughout storage, temperature, and pH stress.

XRD Analysis and Crystallite Size Determination

Powder XRD (Figure 2 and Table 1) indicated the presence of crystalline components in both preparations. The KC‐AgNP pattern showed a major peak at 2θ = 35.70°, with a calculated crystallite size of 55.86 nm using the Debye–Scherrer equation (FWHM = 0.149°). The reflections at 2θ = 28.44° and 40.58° in the EA‐FeNP pattern corresponded to the crystallite sizes of 31.18 and 31.55 nm (average 31.36 nm). These values are related to coherent diffraction‐domain sizes, but not necessarily to full particle dimensions. Polycrystalline Fe3O4 has been described under glucose‐mediated synthesis circumstances [8], and plant‐mediated iron syntheses often produce oxidized iron phases rather than solely zero‐valent iron [19, 20]. However, the phase of the present material cannot be determined from the peak position alone.

The XRD data show that the two nanoparticle systems have considerable differences in crystal‐domain size and structural organization. The AgNPs had larger crystalline domains, while the FeNPs had smaller crystallites. Such variation could be attributed to differences in reduction rate, nucleation density, and capping strength between the two plant extracts [6, 7]. Metal‐ion reduction and nanoparticle stabilization via phytochemicals such as phenolics, flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, proteins, and other functional biomolecules may occur during plant‐mediated nanoparticle synthesis. These biomolecules can influence nucleation, crystal growth, aggregation, and final crystallite size. Both spectra exhibit well‐defined diffraction peaks, demonstrating the crystalline structure of the biosynthesized nanoparticles. However, the diffraction profiles differed dramatically between the two systems. The AgNPs produced a sharper and more intense diffraction signal, indicating increased crystallinity and larger ordered crystalline domains. In contrast, FeNPs showed bigger diffraction peaks, which are typically associated with smaller crystallite sizes and greater nanoscale structural dispersion. These structural features may influence nanoparticle stability, surface reactivity, and biological performance. Crystallite diameters of 56 nm for AgNPs and 31 nm for FeNPs show effective nanoparticle synthesis using K. ceratoides and E. angustifolia extracts. XRD provides useful information on crystal structure, crystallite size, and phase organization; however, it does not predict external particle morphology. As a result, it acquired additional support from microscopic techniques like SEM. Nonetheless, the XRD data reveal that both plant extracts effectively increased the production of crystalline nanoparticles.

SEM Morphology and Correlation With XRD Findings

SEM investigation revealed the shape, surface topology, and aggregation behavior of the produced KC‐AgNPs (Figure 3) and EA‐FeNPs (Figure 4). KC‐AgNPs ranged from quasi‐spherical to irregular particles, with individual grains measuring 40–120 nm. A significant fraction of these particles were detected in the 50–60 nm region, which is consistent with the XRD‐derived crystallite size of 55.86 nm. This significant correlation between SEM particle size and XRD crystallite size implies that many AgNPs are single‐crystalline or made up of only a few crystalline domains.

In contrast, EA‐FeNPs exhibited bigger faceted, polygonal, clustered, and flower‐like aggregates. SEM images showed aggregate sizes ranging from about 150 to 400 nm. However, higher magnification images revealed rough and granular surface textures made up of smaller nanoscale subunits. These subunits are consistent with the XRD‐derived crystallite size of around 31.36 nm. The distinction between the larger SEM‐observed FeNP aggregates and the smaller XRD crystallite size reveals that the FeNPs are polycrystalline assemblies made up of many smaller crystalline domains. This shape is comparable with prior studies on green‐synthesized iron‐based nanoparticles, which show that fast nucleation followed by aggregation can result in bigger secondary structures from smaller crystallites [19, 20]. Magnetic interactions, surface oxidation, and the structure of plant‐derived capping molecules may all increase FeNP aggregation. The AgNPs had a better correlation between crystallite size and particle size, indicating improved particle growth and stability by K. ceratoides phytochemicals.

UV–Visible Stability Analysis

UV–visible spectra were monitored during storage and after exposure to different temperatures and pH values (Figure 5). Changes in absorbance intensity, peak position, or broadening can be consistent with aggregation, sedimentation, oxidation, or changes in particle size distribution [21, 22]. The KC‐AgNP spectra retained a more similar overall profile over one month, whereas EA‐FeNPs showed larger intensity changes compatible with sedimentation or partial aggregation. Temperature‐ and pH‐dependent measurements likewise retained recognizable spectral profiles across the tested conditions. These data show relative UV–visible spectral persistence. Without complementary dynamic light scattering or zeta‐potential studies, they should not be construed as a thorough quantitative assessment of colloidal stability. Soil pH varies greatly within soil types and treatment practices [23]. The UV–visible data, therefore, support the relative spectral persistence of both preparations, with greater changes in EA‐FeNP absorbance intensity. A plant‐derived surface layer may have limited rapid aggregation [7, 14, 15], but this mechanism was not measured directly.

Nematicidal Activity Against Meloidogyne incognita

The KC‐AgNP and EA‐FeNP preparations and carbofuran were evaluated against M. incognita J2 (Table 2). Root‐knot nematodes are major plant parasites [24, 25], and environmental concerns surrounding conventional nematicides motivate evaluation of alternative control agents [26]. Green‐synthesized nanoparticles have shown activity against plant‐parasitic nematodes in previous studies [27, 28, 29]. As shown in Table 2, mortality increased with nanoparticle concentration. KC‐AgNPs produced 98.0% ± 1.0%, 82.0% ± 1.5%, and 47.0% ± 1.0% mortality at final nanoparticle concentrations of 1.000, 0.500, and 0.125 mg/L, respectively. EA‐FeNPs produced 70.0% ± 1.0%, 60.0% ± 0.5%, and 40.0% ± 2.5% mortality at the same concentrations. Carbofuran produced 100% mortality at all three tested concentrations. The observed natural mortality in the control treatment was 0.0% ± 0.0%, confirming that no mortality occurred in the absence of the test treatments. Since extract‐only controls were not tested, these values describe the activity of the complete biogenic nanoparticle preparations and do not establish that the metal‐containing cores alone caused mortality.

The goodness‐of‐fit statistics indicated that the probit models adequately described the mortality responses for KC‐AgNPs (χ 2 = 1.86, df = 1, p = 0.76) and EA‐FeNPs (χ 2 = 3.01, df = 1, p = 0.55). The non‐significant goodness‐of‐fit tests (p > 0.05) indicated no significant lack of fit between the observed and expected responses, confirming that the probit models provided an adequate fit to the data. A probit model was not fitted for carbofuran because complete mortality (100%) was observed at all tested concentrations, precluding reliable estimation of the LC50 and goodness‐of‐fit statistics.

The greater mortality observed for KC‐AgNPs may reflect differences in particle properties, silver‐ion release, oxidative stress, membrane interactions, or plant‐derived surface constituents [16, 17, 18, 30, 31]. Previous studies have reported nematicidal activity of green‐synthesized nanoparticles [27, 28, 29]. EA‐FeNPs were less active at the tested concentrations, and their larger secondary aggregates may reduce accessible surface area; iron oxidation state and aggregation can also influence reactivity [19, 20, 32]. These mechanisms were not measured directly. Most importantly, the absence of matched KC‐ and EA‐extract‐only controls prevents separation of nanoparticle‐core effects from effects of capping or co‐isolated plant constituents. Future experiments should include solvent, precursor‐only, extract‐only, and nanoparticle treatments prepared at matched extract‐equivalent concentrations, together with GC‐MS or LC‐MS profiling where compound attribution is intended.

Conclusion

Under the tested conditions, KC yielded an Ag‐containing nanoparticle preparation, and EA yielded an Fe‐based nanoparticle preparation, whereas the reciprocal plant–metal combinations did not produce detectable, isolable products. The preparations contained crystalline domains, exhibited distinct SEM morphologies, and retained recognizable UV–visible spectral profiles during storage and pH/temperature stress. KC‐AgNPs produced greater 72 h mortality of M. incognita J2 than EA‐FeNPs at the tested final nanoparticle concentrations, while carbofuran produced complete mortality. These results are promising but preliminary. Since extract‐only controls and constituent‐level chemical profiling were not included, the mortality cannot be assigned exclusively to the metallic cores or to particular phytochemicals. Confirmatory studies must validate the probit estimates, incorporate suitable matched controls, evaluate batch reproducibility, characterize particle phases and isolate constituents, and assess non‐target toxicity, soil dynamics, formulation stability, and field efficacy prior to recommending agricultural application.
