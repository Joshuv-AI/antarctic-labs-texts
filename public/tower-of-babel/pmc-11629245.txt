Upcycling date juice waste: Impact of date press cake on sponge cake quality
Authors: Golsa Karambakhsh, Mohammad Taghi Golmakani, Parisa Houshmandi, Asgar Farahnaky, Mahsa Majzoobi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Date press cake (DPC) is the main waste stream of date fruit juice extraction. The primary aim of this research was to explore the potential of DPC as a novel food component, using sponge cake as a case study, to upcycle waste from date fruit processing. Thus, different concentrations (0 %, 10 %, 20 %, 30 %, and 40 %) and particle sizes (210 and 500 μm) of DPC were tested on the quality characteristics during storage for 1, 7, and 14 days. While the protein content remained constant, a decrease in particle size was found to increase the fat content from 4.48 % to 5.37 % and decrease the fiber content from 14.35 % to 9.13 %. In addition, the consistency and density of the cake batter increased while increasing the concentrations and decreasing the DPC particle sizes. The height, volume, cohesiveness, and springiness of the cake decreased, and its density, hardness, gumminess, and chewiness increased, by decreasing the DPC particle sizes, the increase in its levels and the storage days.

Introduction

Date fruit (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is a monocotyledonous plant of the palm family, which is a product of arid and semi-arid regions of the world. This fruit is the most important product of the Persian Gulf region in the Middle East. In recent years, date fruit cultivation has expanded in America, sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, and southern Europe (Farag et al., 2023). Botanically, it is composed of a seed, fibrous endocarp, fleshy mesocarp, and pericarp (fruit skin), and the shape, size, and weight of the fruit are dependent on the variety and growing conditions. Date fruit reaches full maturity by passing through the Kimri, Khalal, Rutab, and Tamer stages (Ranasinghe et al., 2022).

Date syrup, date liquid sugar, date juice, date paste, date powder, date jam, date vinegar, fermentation products (like date alcohol, organic acids, wine, baker's yeast, antibiotics, and single-cell protein), enzymes, etc. are the products of date processing, while low-quality dates, date seeds and the date press cake (DPC) are among the by-products of date processing (Muñoz-Tebar et al., 2023). These date fruit by-products have health benefits due to their chemical composition such as dietary fibers, vitamins, minerals, pectin, organic acids, sugars, pigments, antioxidants, flavonoids, phenolic, antibacterial, or antifungal compounds (Alqahtani et al., 2022; Ranasinghe et al., 2022). Therefore, using these by-products as natural ingredients in applied formulas will be beneficial. Based on the extraction method of date extract, date flesh with residual sugar with or without seed is called DPC, which has dietary fiber and the highest amount of protein, while date seed has the highest concentration of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity and the most amount of fat (Sheir, 2022).

Bakery products are one of the most consumed food items among people around the world, so they can be used as a suitable carrier to transfer food fibers to the human body (Quiles et al., 2018). Among these products, cakes are one of the most popular products (Majzoobi et al., 2013), which are one of the high-calorie products since they have a large amount of carbohydrates and fats and a low fiber content (Alqahtani et al., 2022). Therefore, these characteristics make their consumption unsuitable for a healthy diet. Adding DPC to the cake can improve the dietary fiber content of this product and provide various health benefits to the consumer (Usman et al., 2020).

The primary aim of this research was to explore the potential of DPC as a significant waste stream from the date fruit juicing industry, employing an upcycling strategy to reduce food waste and develop a novel healthier food product with sponge cake serving as a case study. The results of this research may be suitable for expanding the application of DPC in value-added food products and reducing waste from the date extraction processing.

Materials and methods

Materials

DPC with coarse particles was produced by a major date fruit producer; Minoo Date Sugar Company, Shiraz Industrial Town, Shiraz, Iran. Wheat flour (moisture 10.75 % ± 0.18, ash 0.50 % ± 0.17, protein 9.78 % ± 0.93, fat 1.44 % ± 0.08, carbohydrate 77.52 % ± 1.35, fiber 0.52 % ± 0.02, wet gluten 28.64 % ± 0.02, dry gluten 9.79 % ± 0.11) was purchased from Sepidan Flour Company, Zarghan, Iran. Liquid lecithin was purchased from Golestan Golpoodr Company, Agh Qola Industrial Town, Gorgan, Iran. Fresh semi-skimmed milk (2 %), fresh eggs, sugar, sunflower oil, vanilla, and baking powder were purchased from a local store.

Methods

Preparation of DPC with different particle sizes

The DPC was grounded with a laboratory bench grinder (Moulinex, model 320, France). Then, sieved through 120, 70, and 35 mesh sieves (ASTME: 11 model, R-U Media company, Iran) to obtain 210 and 500 μm particles and then packed and sealed in polyethylene bags and stored at 4 °C for further experiments.

Chemical composition of DPC

Moisture, ash, fat, protein, and fiber were measured according to the AOAC standard (AOAC, 2000). The total carbohydrate content was obtained by difference. A conversion factor of 6.25 was used for protein content.

Color measurement

To evaluate the color and determine the color parameters including lightness (L⁎), redness-greenness (a⁎), and business-yellowness (b⁎) for DPC, cake batter and cakes, high-quality photos were taken under similar conditions using a digital camera (12/1 megapixel, PowerShot SX270 HS model, Canon company, Malaysia) and Photoshop CS5 software were used. After placing the samples in the case and taking photos with a digital camera, the photos were checked by Photoshop software and by selecting an area fixed from the center of each sample, L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ were measured for each area. The total color difference (∆E∗) was calculated by Eq. (1). The untreated sample was taken as standard (Afshari-Jouybari and Farahnaky, 2011).

∆E∗ = [(L∗sample - L⁎standard) 2 + (a∗sample - a⁎standard)2 + (b∗sample - b⁎standard)2]1/2 (1)

where L⁎standard = 59.43 ± 5.00, a⁎standard = 16.50 ± 1.58, and b⁎standard = 35.13 ± 2.85.

To determine whether the total color difference could be detected by the human eye, the following values were used:

∆E∗ < 1; The color differences are not easily discernible to the human eye.

1 < ∆E∗ < 3; The color differences are not appreciated by the human eye.

∆E∗ > 3; The color differences are obvious to the human eye.

Batter preparation

The ingredients for the control batter were 160 g cake flour (wheat flour), 130 g sugar, 90 g fresh whole eggs, 100 g fresh cow milk, 50 g sunflower oil, 5 g baking powder, 1 g vanilla, and 0.8 g lecithin. The same formulation was used to prepare other samples except that the cake flour was replaced with 10 %, 20 %, 30 %, and 40 % DPC. The mixture of flour and baking powder was sieved three times for aeration. Sugar, eggs, and vanilla were mixed using a kitchen cake mixer (Kenwood, model HM320, UK) at medium speed for 2 min to obtain a creamy white foam. Milk was added to the foam and mixed for 2 min. Then, the mixture of flour and baking powder was added gradually and mixed well. In the end, sunflower oil and lecithin were added to the above mixture. A part of the produced batter was separated to perform tests related to the cake batter.

Batter consistency

To measure the consistency of the cake batter, a Bostwick consistometer was used. The reservoir of the device was filled with 100 g of batter at ambient temperature (20 ± 0.5 °C), left for 2 min, and then, the distance moved by the batter (cm) for 30 s was measured (Majzoobi et al., 2012).

Batter density

Batter density was assessed by measuring the weight of a specific volume of cake batter. Initially, the weight of a calibrated 50 mL measuring cylinder was recorded. Subsequently, the weight of the same cylinder filled with 20 mL of batter was obtained. The density was then calculated using the following equation (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020):(2)Batter densityg/mL=W2−W120where W2 is weight of the cylinder with batter (g) and W1 is weight of the empty cylinder (g).

Batter microscopic properties

To observe the air bubbles in the batter, a light microscope (Olympus Company, CH-2 model, UK) was used. A small amount of the batter was placed on the glass slide of the microscope. Then, the sample was observed with a 4× microscopic lens (Rodríguez-García et al., 2012). DinoCapture 2.0, Version 1.4.2.D software was used to take micrographs.

Cake preparation

Cake batter (150 g) was poured into non-stick cake molds (4 × 5 × 12 cm3) and placed in an electric oven at 180 °C for 30 min until the crust turned golden. After baking, and cooling for 1 h at room temperature, the cakes were removed from the mold. The cake samples were packed and sealed in polyethylene bags and stored at room temperature for further tests.

Cake height, volume, and density

The height of the cake was measured using an INOX caliper (with a measurement accuracy of 0.001, Bulgaria) (Majzoobi et al., 2012). The rapeseed displacement method was employed to measure the volume and density of various cake samples (AACC, 2000, 10–10).

Cake texture

The texture of fresh and stored samples (days 1, 7, and 14) was evaluated using a Texture Analyzer (Stable Microsystems, model TA-TX2, UK) by performing TPA (Texture profile analyzer) test. Cubic pieces of different cake treatments were prepared in dimensions of 2 × 2 × 2 cm3. The two-bite compression test was performed at a pretest speed of 5 mm s−1, a test speed of 1 mm s−1, a post-test speed of 5 mm s−1, a time interval of 10 s, and a strain deformation of 25 % using a stainless-steel cylindrical probe with a diameter of 40 mm, and various parameters such as hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, gumminess, and chewiness were measured from the TPA curve obtained (Aydogdu et al., 2018). For each sample, all measurements were performed at 21 °C in at least 6 repetitions.

Statistical analysis

A completely randomized design was employed for the statistical analysis of data obtained from various tests, with each test repeated at least three times. Averages and standard deviations were calculated using Microsoft Office Excel. To assess significant differences among the samples, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Duncan's multiple range test (P < 0.05) using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software version 16 (SPSS, Inc., New Jersey, USA).

Results and discussion

DPC properties

Chemical composition of DPC

The chemical composition of DPC with particle sizes of 500 μm and 210 μm is presented in Table 1. It was found that the fiber was in the highest quantity (14.35 % and 9.13 %) followed by protein, fat, and ash in both samples. Thus, the DPC is prominently a rich source of fiber to enrich low fiber foods (e.g., cakes). The moisture, ash, protein, and carbohydrate contents remained consistent with reducing the particle size. However, decreasing the particle size led to a significant decrease in fiber content. Considering that DPC is date flesh with residual sugar with or without seeds, therefore it is expected that the compounds such as fiber in DPC come from the fleshy mesocarp part of the date fruit, which is connected to the seed through a fibrous endocarp and the seed particles. During grinding to reduce particle size, parts of the fleshy mesocarp and attached seed fragments may not have been effectively reduced in size and were likely removed during sieving, leading to a lower fiber content in the smaller particle size DPC. Previous studies on different types of DPC have shown that it contained 87.7–95.2 % dry matter, 3.62–13.00 % protein, 9.13–33.81 % fiber, 1.30–5.37 % fat, and 1.68–4.80 % ash (Alqahtani, 2024; Oladzad et al., 2021; Subhash et al., 2024). These studies also confirmed that DPC is a rich source of fiber. However, variations in processing techniques to produce DPC, date varieties, and environmental factors influencing date fruit composition can lead to the differences in the content of different components of DPC (Oladzad et al., 2021).

Color measurement of DPC

The main pigments of date flesh and seeds are carotenoids and anthocyanins (Majzoobi et al., 2019). The results of measuring the color parameters (L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎) and color difference (∆E∗) of DPC are presented in Table 1. It is shown that the L⁎ and b⁎ values increase by decreasing the particle size. By decreasing the DPC particle size, the ∆E∗ values increased and were higher than 3, implying that their color was obvious to the human eye. The significant increase in a⁎ value from 13.00 to 15.50 may be attributed to improved light reflection from the smaller and smoother surface of the finer particles. Additionally, the sample with larger particles contains more dark pigments from seeds and skin, contributing to the lower L⁎ value (Haris et al., 2023). Although color parameters vary across date fruit processing waste, including pomace, the brown color of this by-product should be considered both a potential drawback and an advantage for value addition and new product development (Haris et al., 2023; Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020).

Batter properties

Batter consistency

The Bostwick number is negatively correlated with batter consistency; as the Bostwick number decreases, the consistency of the batter increases (Majzoobi et al., 2013). The Bostwick number of the batter (Table 2) shows that by increasing the DPC concentration and decreasing its particle size, the Bostwick number significantly decreased and as a result, the consistency increased. Fiber and proteins (hydrocolloids) in DPC can absorb water and interact with proteins, starch, and other hydrocolloids in flour, forming a network that traps free water. This interaction affects the movement of particles in the batter, thereby increasing its consistency. Maintaining the appropriate batter consistency is essential for retaining the air bubbles created during mixing and the CO2 produced by the baking powder. If the batter consistency is too low, air bubbles will rise to the surface and escape. Conversely, a batter with excessively high consistency may not trap enough air bubbles during mixing, hindering expansion during baking. Batter density

In general, the density of the batter is influenced by its composition and the amount of air bubbles present (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020). The results indicated that increasing DPC concentration from 0 % to 40 % significantly increased batter density. However, at each concentration, reducing the particle size from 500 to 210 μm did not result in any significant change in batter density (Table 2). The increase in batter density with higher DPC concentration can be attributed to the increased fiber and protein content in DPC, which enhances the water holding capacity (WHC) and subsequently raises the batter density. Reducing the particle size of DPC resulted in fewer air bubbles being trapped during batter mixing, leading to increased batter consistency and density (Aydogdu et al., 2018; Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020).

Batter color

DPC has natural dark pigments as discussed in 3.1.2 that can affect the color of the final product (Haris et al., 2023). By decreasing DPC particle size and increasing its concentration, the L⁎ and b⁎ values decreased significantly, which resulted in the darker and less yellow batter samples (Table 2). By increasing the DPC concentration, the a⁎ increased significantly while particle size reduction had no significant effect on the a⁎ value.

Batter microscopic properties

The distribution of air bubbles in the batter is crucial, as an increased number of air bubbles enhances the mobility and mixing of the ingredients in the batter formulation (Majzoobi et al., 2012). According to Fig. 1, the number of air bubbles decreased by increasing the DPC concentration and reducing the particle size from 500 to 210 μm. Cake properties

Cake height, volume, and density

The results presented in Table 3 demonstrate that increasing the concentration of DPC, reducing its particle size, and extending storage from 1 to 14 days led to a significant reduction in the height and volume of the cake samples, while simultaneously increasing their density. By increasing the concentration and decreasing the particle size of DPC, the consistency and density of the batter increased, but the height and volume of the cake samples reduced. The excessive increase in batter consistency decreases the number of air bubbles trapped during the mixing of the batter and prevents the proper expansion of the cake batter during baking and creating the desired height and volume in the product (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020). Conversely, compounds in DPC, such as hydrocolloids, sugars, and fats, may interact with the starch in the cake flour, influencing its gelatinization temperature. These compounds can delay starch gelatinization due to competition for free water. This delay can result in the batter setting later during baking, leading to the premature release of air bubbles and gases before the batter has fully set, resulting in reduced cake volume and porosity (Majzoobi et al., 2012). Additionally, the fiber and proteins from DPC can interfere with the formation of the protein network in the cake batter, further weakening the cake structure and reducing its volume (Aydogdu et al., 2018). Reducing cake volume by addition of different fiber sources (apple, orange, and carrot pomace, oat, pea, apple, and lemon fibers, and wheat germ and bran) in products such as cake, bread, and cookies Cake color

Color is a key criterion for consumers' acceptance of a product with a new formulation. The appearance of the cakes is given in Fig. 2. By increasing the DPC concentration and the storage days, the L⁎ value decreased and the a⁎ value increased. As a result, the samples became darker and their redness increased. Reducing the particle size showed no significant effects on L⁎ and a⁎ values. The color of the cake crust is affected by Maillard reactions and caramelization during baking. In addition, the interaction between ingredients during different processes can also affect these reactions. The increased darkness of the crust can be attributed to the enhancement of Maillard and caramelization reactions, as the addition of DPC elevated the sugar and protein content responsible for these reactions. Additionally, the natural dark pigments present in DPC contribute to the overall darkness of the cake. Furthermore, the impact of DPC on water activity and, consequently, the rate of the Maillard reaction may also play a role in the development of a darker color (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020; Kırbaş et al., 2019).

Fig. 3 shows the color of the crumbs of the cake samples. Compared to the control sample, the L⁎ and b⁎ values of the samples containing DPC decreased significantly and the a⁎ value increased by increasing the DPC concentration, the decrease in the particle size, and increasing the storage days. Since the temperature of the cake crumb is not high enough to facilitate Maillard and caramelization reactions, its color is predominantly influenced by the ingredients in the formulation. Consequently, the darker color of DPC, attributed to its pigments, is a key factor in reducing the L⁎ and b⁎ values while increasing the a⁎ value of the cake crumb as DPC concentration rises (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020; Kırbaş et al., 2019). Furthermore, the compounds in DPC, such as hydrocolloids, sugars, and fats, may interact with the starch in cake flour, elevating the gelatinization temperature and causing starch gelatinization to occur later. As a result, a significant number of air bubbles and gases that contribute to the cake's volume may be lost before the starch gelatinizes, leading to a reduction in cake volume (Majzoobi et al., 2012). Additionally, the increased DPC concentration elevates the levels of hydrocolloids and fiber, which weakens the gluten network responsible for retaining gases and creating volume in the cake. This weakening effect contributes to a decrease in cake volume and ultimately results in a cake with a dense texture (Aydogdu et al., 2018; Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020), further enhancing the darkness of the cake crumb.

Similar findings have been reported for other food products, such as bakery items, cookies, and biscuits, enriched with various waste streams. Research on products like cakes, bread, biscuits, and cookies has consistently shown a reduction in L⁎ and b⁎ values, alongside an increase in a⁎ value, with the increasing concentration of different sources of fiber, including date seed powder, olive stone powder, oat, pea, apple, and lemon fibers, grape pomace and peels, apple, orange, and carrot pomace as well as wheat germ (Aydogdu et al., 2018; Iuga & Mironeasa, 2020; Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020; Kırbaş et al., 2019; Majzoobi et al., 2012; Subhash et al., 2024).

Cake texture

The results of the hardness of different cake samples (Table 3) showed that by reducing the particle size of DPC, increasing its concentration and storage time, the hardness of the cake samples increased significantly. This is mostly related to the staling process of the cakes during storage (Jahanbakhshi & Ansari, 2020), which can be further enhanced by the reduced volume and porosity of the cakes as explained before.

Increasing the hardness of the cake samples by increasing the concentration of different fiber sources (defatted apple seed cakes, grape pomace, date press cake, date pomace, oat, pea, apple, and lemon fibers, and wheat germ and bran) in products such as cakes, cookies, biscuit, and bread (Aydogdu et al., 2018; Iuga & Mironeasa, 2020; Majzoobi et al., 2012; Majzoobi et al., 2013; Purić et al., 2020; Sheir, 2022; Subhash et al., 2024) has also been reported.

According to Table 3, by increasing the DPC concentration, reducing its particle size, and increasing the storage days, the cohesiveness and springiness of the cake samples decreased significantly, while the gumminess and chewiness of the cake samples increased significantly.

Conclusion

Incorporating DPC into sponge cake formulations presents a viable strategy for upcycling food waste, while simultaneously enhancing the nutritional quality of the product. This approach not only contributes to reducing food waste in the date fruit industry but also offers a novel method to increase the dietary fiber content in baked goods, promoting healthier eating habits. DPC, being a rich source of fiber, can effectively enhance the nutritional profile of foods including sponge cake, which typically has low fiber content. The findings showed that increasing DPC concentration affected the physical properties of the cakes by reducing their height and volume while increasing density. However, it served as a natural colorant, imparting a pleasant chocolate hue to the cakes. Cakes containing a moderate level of DPC (i.e., 20 %, regardless of particle size) can be considered a good source of protein (about 10–11 %) and a source of fiber (about 3–4 %).

The results of this study showed that the addition of DPC to the cake formulation is feasible as an environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and healthy alternative for producing value-added cakes, although more studies are needed to improve the physicochemical characteristics of cakes containing DPC during the storage period.
