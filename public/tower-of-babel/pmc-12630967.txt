Inverse relationship between dietary index for gut microbiota and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome among the general adult population
Authors: Yixiao Zhao, Xiaolin Zhao, Jianping Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is defined as the complex interaction of metabolic diseases such as metabolic syndrome (MS), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD). A study indicates that the CKM syndrome affects over 25% of the U.S. population, contributing to an increasing burden of disease on a global scale. The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) is an indicator used to assess the impact of diet on the health of the gut microbiota. The DI-GM has been demonstrated to be closely associated with a variety of diseases. However, it remains unclear whether the DI-GM can serve as a predictor for the CKM syndrome. This study aims to explore the association between the DI-GM and CKM syndrome. This study employed a cross-sectional research design utilizing publicly available data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) for the years 2007–2016. After excluding participants with missing demographic data and age < 20, those with missing DI-GM and CKM data, and those with missing values for covariates, a total of 10,786 participants were selected from the NHANES database. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated DI-GM and CKM syndrome, adjusting for demographics and socioeconomic factors. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression assessed dose-response relationships. Subgroup analyses further explored the relationship between DI-GM and gender, age, marital status, race, education level, drinking, smoking, poverty-income ratio (PIR) and dietary inflammatory index (DII). Sensitivity analysis using random forest for missing data were performed. In this study, participants with CKM syndrome were significantly older than those without CKM syndrome (CKM: 48.81 ± 16.66 years vs. non-CKM: 34.28 ± 12.64 years, P < 0.001). The results from multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated a consistent negative association between DI-GM and CKM syndrome across the unadjusted model (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.95, P < 0.001), Model 1 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92, P < 0.001), Model 2 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.84–0.93, P < 0.001), and Model 3 (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99, P < 0.001).After adjusting for all confounders, participants with DI-GM ≥ 6 exhibited a 23% lower odds of CKM compared to those with DI-GM ≤ 3 (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.59–0.99, P < 0.05). RCS analysis indicated a linear negative relationship between DI-GM and CKM (P = 0.071). Subgroup analyses suggested that the protective association between DI-GM and CKM prevalence was more pronounced in females (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.95), non-Hispanic Whites (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96), participants with education above high school (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.98), and high-income individuals (PIR ≥ 3) (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.97). Significant heterogeneity (interaction P-value < 0.05) was observed in race (P = 0.027), education level (P = 0.008), and smoking (P = 0.017). Smoking has a significant effect on the association between DI-GM and CKM syndrome (P < 0.05), suggesting that smoking may weaken the protective effect of DI-GM against CKM. No significant interaction between DI-GM and DII (P = 0.451). All sensitivity analyses supported the inverse relationship between DI-GM and CKM syndrome. Our findings suggest that the DI-GM score was significantly negatively correlated with CKM syndrome. Specific subgroups (such as non-Hispanic Whites, individuals with higher education levels, and smokers) may modify the association between the DI-GM and CKM syndrome. Introduction

CKM is a new medical concept proposed by the American Heart Association in recent years, which is defined as the complex interaction of metabolic diseases such as MS, CKD and CVD1–3. Emerging epidemiological evidence demonstrates that CKM syndrome-induced multiorgan dysfunction substantially elevates susceptibility to major adverse cardiovascular events, with its global prevalence exhibiting an alarming upward trajectory that constitutes a significant public health burden4,5. Notably, a landmark review has recently established CKM syndrome as the second leading contributor to mortality in oncological populations, surpassing even tumor recurrence risks in certain cohorts6,7. The introduction of this syndrome reflects the deeper academic understanding of the interconnections between metabolic disorders, renal injury and cardiovascular events, and also provides a theoretical basis for multidisciplinary collaborative management. Consequently, identifying evidence-based strategies for CKM syndrome prevention and intervention represents a critical priority in contemporary clinical research.

The pathogenesis of CKM syndrome is intricate, commencing with adipose tissue dysfunction. It triggers metabolic disturbances and vascular endothelial damage via core pathways like inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that exacerbates cardiac, renal, and metabolic impairments8–10. Emerging research has elucidated the pivotal role of the gut microbiota in CKM syndrome pathogenesis. Gut dysbiosis, characterized by microbial imbalance and loss of functional diversity, is strongly associated with the development of metabolic disorders, sustained inflammatory states, and elevated cardiovascular risk11–16.

Diet is one of the most important modifiable factors affecting the composition and function of the gut microbiota. Kase et al.17 proposed the concept of the dietary index for gut microbiota(DI-GM)in April 2024, which was designed to quantify the effect of diet on the gut microbiota. DI-GM directly correlates diet quality with microbiome function, and it provides a new perspective for studying the relationship between diet and health18. In recent years, it has been used in the study of multiple metabolic-related diseases19–23.

Studies have shown that dietary patterns rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and fermented foods promote the growth of beneficial gut microorganisms, whereas diets high in fat, sugar, and salt may lead to an imbalance in the gut microbiota, which in turn increases the risk of CVD, MS, and CKD24. Although studies have shown links between DI-GM and other chronic diseases, the exact correlation with CKM syndrome has not yet been fully validated.

This study seeks to investigate the association between DI-GM and CKM syndrome based on data from the 2007–2020 NHANES. NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey covering a wide range of health and nutrition indicators25. The results of this study will help to deepen the understanding of the diet-gut microbiota-CKM syndrome relationship and provide a scientific basis for the development of diet-based strategies for the prevention and treatment of CKM syndrome.

Materials and methods

Research design and population selection

The National Centre for Health Statistics administers the NHANES, a nationwide study that evaluates the nutrition and health of American adults living outside of institutions using a stratified multistage sampling method. The NCHS Research Ethics Review Board authorized the project. The NHANES data are publicly available, and all participants provided written informed consent. The information for this inquiry came from the official website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm).

It was a cross-sectional research project that used publicly accessible data from NHANES (2007–2016). A total of 50,588 participants participated in NHANES 2007–2016. We excluded participants who lacked demographic data and age < 20 (n = 21,444), those with missing DI-GM and CKM data (n = 16,550), participants without covariates values (n = 1,808). Ultimately, 10,786 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Definition of the DI-GM

The DI-GM is based on a large number of research literature to construct a tool for assessing the relationship between diet and gut microbiota that effectively identifies food components that are beneficial or detrimental to gut microbiota. DI-GM consists of 14 food items or nutrients: beneficial components include fermented dairy products, chickpeas, soybeans, whole grains, fiber, cranberries, avocados, broccoli, coffee, and green tea; detrimental components include refined grains, red meat, processed meats, and high-fat diets(≥ 40% of energy from fat). Each component is scored as 0 or 1 based on gender-specific median intake levels, and the scores are summed to derive the overall DI-GM score. DI-GM is assessed based on whether the intake of each food group aligns with recommended guidelines. 1 point is awarded if the intake meets or exceeds the recommended amount (e.g., daily vegetable intake ≥ 400 g). 0 points is given if the intake falls below the recommendation. The total DI-GM score is the sum of scores across 14 food groups, resulting in a range of 0–14 points. DI-GM was calculated using two 24-hour dietary recall data. The first recall was obtained at a mobile examination center, and the second recall was conducted via telephone interview. Since the NHANES 24-hour recall data do not include specific tea consumption, DI-GM scores range from 0 to 13. More detailed information about the composition and calculation of DI-GM can be found in STable 1.

Definition of CKM syndrome

In October 2023, the American Heart Association introduced a new concept — CKM syndrome. It defines this syndrome as a systemic disease characterized by complex pathophysiological interactions between MS, CKD, and subclinical or clinical CVD. CKM syndrome leads to multi-organ dysfunction and high-risk adverse cardiovascular outcomes1,26,27. MS is diagnosed based on the NCEP-ATP III criteria, which require the presence of at least three of the following five factors: Central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men or ≥ 88 cm in women), Elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL), low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women), hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg), and hyperglycemia (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or use of antidiabetic medications)28. CKD was identified when the estimated glomerular filtration rate fell below 60mL/min/1.73m2, or when the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio exceeded 30 mg/g29,30. The eGFR calculation was performed using the CKD-EPI equation as recommended by current clinical standards. Self-reported CKD was excluded from this definition to minimize misclassification bias. Clinical CVD includes a history of myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure, stroke, or coronary heart disease, while subclinical CVD is defined by a 10-year CVD risk of ≥ 20%.

CKM syndrome has been categorized into five distinct stages. Stage 0 indicates all normal conditions; Stage 1 includes only obesity or prediabetes; Stage 2 involves diabetes mellitus or hypertension or CKD with medium-high risk; Stage 3 includes very high risk of CKD or predicted risk of CVD within 10 years; and Stage 4 is marked by clinical manifestations of CVD. For the purpose of this study, non-CKM was defined as stage 0 and CKM was defined as stages 1–4.

Demographic characteristics and other covariates

All covariates were also obtained from the NHANES database from 2007 to 2016.A number of demographic factors were selected in this study, such as sex, age, race, marital status, and education level. Additionally, smoking status, drinking status, poverty-income ratio (PIR) and DII were also included. Race was classified as Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other racial. Educational level was classified into three categories: less than high school, high school or equivalent, above high school. Marital status was divided into three categories: married or living with partner, never married, widowed/divorced/separated. The PIR reflected family income relative to the federal poverty line, adjusting for inflation and family size. Smoking status was categorized into three groups: never smoked, former smoker and current smoker. Drinking status was classified into two groups: drinker (had at least 12 alcohol drinks/1 year) and non-drinker.

The DII evaluates the inflammatory potential of an overall diet based on the pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory properties of various food components, including vitamins and minerals31. A higher DII score(≥ 0) indicates a pro-inflammatory diet, while a lower score (<0) reflects an anti-inflammatory diet. Furthermore, higher DII scores suggest harmful dietary habits, whereas lower DII scores are indicative of healthier dietary practices. More detailed information about the calculation of DII can be found in Supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

To generate nationally representative estimates accounting for NHANES’ complex survey design, all analyses incorporated sampling weights, stratification variables, and primary sampling units (PSUs). Specifically, we applied the standard subsample weight (WTSAF2YR) to Subsample A and derived a decennial integrated weight (WTSAF10YR = WTSAF2YR / 5) for the combined 2007–2016 cycles per CDC specifications. The sampling structure was formalized using R survey package (v4.4.3) with design parameters: id = SDMVPSU, strata = SDMVSTRA, weights = WTSAF10YR, and nest = TRUE. Continuous normally-distributed variables (e.g., age, DI-GM) were summarized as mean ± standard error (SE), categorical variables (e.g., race, smoking) as percentages (%), with group comparisons via svyglm()-based t-tests and chi-square tests. DI-GM was categorized into quartiles (Group 1 as reference), and its association with CKM syndrome was assessed through multivariate design-adjusted logistic regression (Table 2), supplemented by restricted cubic splines (RCS) to test nonlinearity (Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses stratified by demographics, socioeconomic status (education, PIR), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol use), and dietary patterns (DII) included formal interaction testing. Missing data were primarily handled by complete case analysis (assuming MCAR), with sensitivity analysis using random forest imputation to evaluate robustness; all computations were executed in R 4.4.3.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants at baseline

The baseline characteristics of participants with and without CKM from the NHANES dataset between the years 2007 and 2016 are displayed in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, the average age was 34.28 ± 12.64 years in the participants without CKM, while the average age was over 48.81 ± 16.66 years in the participants with CKM(P < 0.001). The CKM group demonstrated significantly lower DI-GM compared to the non-CKM group (P < 0.001). Notable covariates in other sample characteristics, such as gender, marital status, education level, race, PIR, smoking habits and DII were also found between the two groups(P < 0.05).

The results from multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated a consistent negative association between DI-GM and CKM across the unadjusted model (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.95, P < 0.001), Model 1 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.92, P < 0.001), Model 2 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.84–0.93, P < 0.001), and Model 3 (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99, P < 0.05). DI-GM was divided into 4 groups based on score. When using the first group (≤ 3) as the reference, the high-score group (DI-GM ≥ 6) showed persistent negative associations in both non-adjusted model (OR = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.55–0.83, P < 0.001), Model 1 (OR = 0.60, 95%CI: 0.47–0.75, P < 0.001) and Model 2 (OR = 0.62, 95%CI: 0.49–0.78, P < 0.001) compared to the reference group. In Model3, the average prevalence of CKM syndrome in the high score group (DI-GM ≥ 6) was 0.23 units lower than in the reference group (OR = 0.77, 95%CI: 0.59–0.99, P < 0.05), and the trend test confirmed this finding (P < 0.05).

RCS analysis

We used RCS regression analysis to explore the nonlinear relationship between DI-GM and CKM (Fig. 2). The RCS analysis demonstrated a significant dose-response relationship between DI-GM and CKM (overall P < 0.001). The findings indicated a linear negative relationship between DI-GM and CKM (P = 0.071).

Subgroup analysis

We performed subgroup analyses that considered gender, age, race, marital status, education level, smoking, drinking, PIR and DII in order to better examine the relationship between DI-GM and CKM in various groups (Fig. 3). The results suggested that the protective association between DI-GM and CKM prevalence was more pronounced in females (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.95), non-Hispanic Whites (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96), participants with education above high school (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.98), and high-income individuals (PIR ≥ 3) (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.97). Significant heterogeneity (interaction P-value < 0.05) was observed in race (P = 0.027), education level (P = 0.008), and smoking (P = 0.017). Smoking has a significant effect on the association between DI-GM and CKM syndrome (P < 0.05), suggesting that smoking may weaken the protective effect of DI-GM against CKM. No significant interaction between DI-GM and DII (P = 0.451), indicating DII does not modify the association between DI-GM and CKM.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 10,786 U.S. adults from NHANES 2007–2016, elevated DI-GM levels demonstrated an independent linear dose-response association with reduced odds of CKM syndrome. Subgroup analyses revealed effect modification by smoking status, with stronger protective associations observed among non-Hispanic White individuals and those with higher educational attainment, and no interaction was detected between DI-GM and DII.

The negative correlation between DI-GM and the odds of CKM syndrome is consistent with the results of previous studies linking DI-GM to the occurrence of metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease32–35.

The DI-GM is based on a large number of research literature to construct a tool for assessing the relationship between diet and gut microbiota. Its purpose is to quantify the impact of dietary patterns on the composition and function of the gut microbiota. This study is the first to find a linear negative association between DI-GM and CKM.

The mechanisms underlying the association between DI-GM and CKM risk may be multifaceted. First, a healthy diet promotes a balanced gut microbiota that produces fewer pro-inflammatory microorganisms and inflammatory mediators14–17. Our findings are consistent with the known effects of diet on gut microbiota composition and function. Consumption of fiber-rich foods, whole grains, and fermented products promotes the growth of probiotic gut bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyric, acetic, and propionic acids13–15,36–38.

Short-chain fatty acids produced by gut bacteria have been shown to activate G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR) on immune cells and enterocytes, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines39.

DII is an assessment tool designed to quantify the impact of dietary patterns on systemic inflammation levels, based on established diet-inflammation relationships. In this study, multivariable logistic regression indicated that adjusting for DII as a covariate resulted in no significant change in the effect size of DI-GM (P > 0.05). Subgroup analysis further demonstrated no significant interaction between DI-GM and DII (P = 0.451). Although DI-GM and DII share conceptual similarities in evaluating inflammatory mechanisms, they diverge in their primary focus and mechanistic pathways. Research has confirmed significant associations between DII and multiple inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., C-reactive protein, IL-6, TNF-α)40,41. This contrasts with the anti-inflammatory mechanism of DI-GM, which mitigates systemic inflammation through increased production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Furthermore, DI-GM may exert biological effects via pathways independent of inflammatory responses.

DI-GM may improve metabolic function. Short-chain fatty acids produced by gut bacteria have also been shown to improve insulin sensitivity as well as lipid metabolism, helping to control metabolic risk factors for CKM39. Studies have shown that a low glycemic index (GI) diet can reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease by improving blood lipid levels, lowering triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and reducing inflammation42. The DI-GM scoring system includes low-GI foods such as whole grains and high-fiber foods, which are considered beneficial. Thus, a high DI-GM score may reflect a higher quality of low-GI diet, which indirectly reduces the risk of CKM. In contrast, diets high in saturated fats, red meat, and processed foods have been associated with an increase in pathogenic bacteria and a decrease in beneficial bacteria, leading to intestinal dysbiosis and low-grade inflammation, which are key factors in the pathogenesis of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases24–27. High animal fat diets disrupt intestinal microbiota homeostasis (i.e., dysbiosis), thereby increasing the production of TMAO, a metabolite produced by the gut microbiota, which has been associated with increased risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events46.

Our subgroup analyses indicated that DI-GM offered greater protection for non-Hispanic whites compared to other racial groups. This finding may be attributable to differences in genetic background, lifestyle, or dietary habits between racial groups. Genetic variations can affect how individuals metabolize certain nutrients and how their gut microbiota responds to dietary components47,48. In addition, cultural eating habits and access to healthy foods may differ between racial groups, altering the relationship between DI-GM and CKM risk. For example, non-Hispanic whites may be more likely to access and consume fermented dairy products, whole grains, and fresh produce, which are important components of a high DI-GM diet49. Further research is needed to delve into these interracial differences and to develop targeted dietary interventions for different racial populations.

Cigarette smoking was found to attenuate the protective effect of DI-GM on CKM odds. This is consistent with existing evidence that smoking can alter the composition of the gut microbiota, reduce microbial diversity, and increase the number of potentially pathogenic bacteria50–53. Smoking-induced gut dysbiosis may counteract the beneficial effects of a healthy diet on the gut microbiota and metabolic health. In addition, the negative effects of smoking, a known risk factor for cardiovascular and kidney disease, may overshadow the positive effects of a healthy diet54,55. This highlights the importance of considering smoking status when assessing the role of diet in CKM prevention and emphasizes the need for comprehensive lifestyle interventions that address both dietary habits and smoking cessation.

This study has important implications. First, it utilized a large, nationally representative sample from NHANES, ensuring the generalizability of the findings to the U.S. adult population. Second, the study utilized a comprehensive approach to defining CKM syndrome, integrating metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular systems and capturing the complex interactions of these systems. Third, the use of multivariate regression models and adjustment for potential confounders enhanced the validity of the observed associations. The DI-GM score serves as a novel predictor of CKM odds with critical clinical relevance, demonstrated through these potential applications: (1) Quantifying dietary impact on CKM odds: DI-GM objectively measures how dietary patterns influence CKM odds, enabling clinicians to assess dietary quality and predict disease probability. (2) Guiding personalized dietary interventions: Facilitates tailored nutrition plans to mitigate individual CKM risk. (3) Identifying high-risk populations: Allows early targeting of individuals for preventive strategies. (4) Evaluating intervention efficacy: Tracks dietary modification effectiveness to optimize therapeutic adjustments. (5) Enhancing public health literacy: Educates communities on diet-disease relationships to promote healthier lifestyles. (6) Informing evidence-based prevention: Provides scientific foundations for developing CKM prevention strategies. (7) Enabling early diagnosis and treatment: Supports timely clinical management to improve outcomes. (8) Advancing etiological research: Offers mechanistic insights into CKM pathogenesis.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precluded assessment of dietary changes following CKM diagnosis and limited causal inference regarding the linear relationship between DI-GM and CKM syndrome. Second, inherent limitations in NHANES self-reported data may introduce measurement error: (1) recall bias from retrospective dietary assessments (e.g., 24-hour recalls capturing only short-term intake); (2) underreporting of sensitive behaviors (e.g., smoking, heavy alcohol use) due to social desirability bias; (3) disease misclassification risk from unverified self-reported diagnoses; (4) compromised validity among non-native English speakers or less-educated individuals; and (5) cross-wave inconsistencies from 2011 questionnaire revisions.

Furthermore, the DI-GM score’s 14-component structure may oversimplify diet-microbiota interactions, while unmeasured confounders (physical activity, substance use, genetics) and memory-related data inaccuracies could persist. Regarding analytical approaches, complete case exclusion may induce selection bias if missingness correlates with CKM risk, though we mitigated this via random forest imputation sensitivity analyses. Collectively, these limitations necessitate cautious interpretation of results, particularly for clinical applications.

Future studies could incorporate measurements of the gut microbiota to verify whether DI-GM accurately reflects changes in gut microbiota. Meanwhile, we need more longitudinal studies to determine the causal relationship between DI-GM and CKM. We can also collect samples from CKM participants for metabolomic analysis to explore the specific mechanism between DI-GM and the incidence of CKM syndrome.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern, i.e., a higher DI-GM score, is associated with reduced odds of CKM syndrome. This association was more pronounced in specific ethnic groups and may be influenced by smoking status. These findings underscore the importance of diet as a modifiable factor in the prevention of CKM and suggest that personalized dietary interventions that take into account racial and lifestyle factors may help reduce the burden of CKM syndrome. Future research should focus on elucidating underlying biological mechanisms and conducting longitudinal studies to further establish causality.
