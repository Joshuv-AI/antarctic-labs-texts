Hybrid Sensor–Vision Machine Learning for Predicting Mahewu Fermentation Dynamics
Authors: Ismail Adeleke, Oluwafemi Ayodeji Adebo, Nnamdi Nwulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study developed and evaluated a laboratory‐scale hybrid sensor‐vision machine learning framework for monitoring and predicting mahewu fermentation dynamics using synchronized physicochemical sensing and surface‐image analysis. White‐ and yellow‐maize mahewu were fermented under controlled processing conditions while pH, electrical conductivity (EC), and temperature were recorded and surface images were captured under fixed LED illumination. Images were segmented and converted into color, texture, edge, and morphology descriptors, then aligned with sensor records and raw laboratory total titratable acidity (TTA) and total soluble solids (TSS) measurements using tolerance‐valid matching. Semantic segmentation was evaluated separately across 96 manually annotated images from 14 fermentation‐run groups using nested grouped validation, producing a run‐balanced mean Dice coefficient of 0.8070 and mean intersection over union of 0.7195. Primary raw laboratory prediction models used 7945 matched image‐sensor records from 12 main fermentation runs, including 71 TTA and 87 TSS reference records. Leakage‐controlled models were evaluated by fermentation run using image‐only, sensor‐context, and multimodal feature sets. Raw laboratory TTA was predicted most effectively using sensor‐context variables with Gradient Boosting (R 2 = 0.8298, MAE = 61.03 μL/g, RMSE = 96.46 μL/g). Raw laboratory TSS showed the best point‐estimate performance with a multimodal Random Forest model (R 2 = 0.5939, MAE = 0.624 , RMSE = 0.789 ). Image‐only models were weaker for both targets, indicating that surface descriptors are better interpreted as complementary rather than standalone information. Further validation across independent batches, lighting conditions, vessel geometries, and production environments is required before use beyond controlled laboratory conditions.Practical Applications Mahewu fermentation is often assessed through appearance, sourness, and producer experience. Recording pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, and surface images together provided a more objective account of within‐batch changes under laboratory conditions. After validation in practical production settings, this approach could help producers compare batches and make more consistent fermentation decisions.

Introduction

Mahewu, also referred to as amahewu in some studies, is a traditional nonalcoholic fermented cereal beverage widely consumed in Southern Africa. It is commonly produced from maize or related cereal substrates, with product quality shaped by raw material composition, inoculum source, boiling conditions, fermentation temperature, and fermentation duration (Gadaga et al. 1999; Oyeyinka et al. 2021; Daji et al. 2022). Recent studies also show that formulation, inoculum type, and cereal composition influence nutritional, physicochemical, and consumer‐relevant attributes in sorghum‐based, provitamin A‐biofortified, and Bambara‐fortified amahewu systems (Qaku et al. 2020; Awobusuyi and Siwela 2019; Awobusuyi et al. 2021; Akanni et al. 2025). Despite this research base, mahewu production still depends strongly on empirical judgment, including visual appearance, sour taste, and producer experience. This creates batch‐to‐batch variability and limits objective quality control in household, artisanal, and small‐scale production systems.

The biochemical progression of mahewu and related African cereal fermentations is mainly driven by lactic acid bacteria (LAB), although microbial communities vary with ingredients, processing environment, and inoculation practice (Pswarayi and Gänzle 2019; Kewuyemi et al. 2020; Pswarayi and Gänzle 2022). African cereal beverages often develop LAB‐dominant communities involving Lactococcus, Lactobacillus sensu lato, Weissella, Leuconostoc, and Enterococcus, depending on substrate and process conditions (Mukisa et al. 2012; Gabaza et al. 2019; Hlangwani et al. 2023). LAB metabolize fermentable carbohydrates produced through cereal starch hydrolysis, leading to organic acid accumulation and measurable acidification (Gänzle 2015; Ziarno and Cichońska 2021; Adebo et al. 2021; Kewuyemi et al. 2022). In mahewu and comparable cereal beverages, pH generally decreases as titratable acidity increases, while total soluble solids (TSS) can behave less monotonically because starch hydrolysis and sugar consumption may occur simultaneously or sequentially (Foma et al. 2012; Namugumya and Muyanja 2009; Muyanja et al. 2012; Daji et al. 2023).

Some LAB reported in mahewu and closely related emahewu fermentations, including Lactobacillus fermentum, Leuconostoc spp., and Weissella spp., are capable of heterofermentative metabolism. Through the phosphoketolase pathway, heterofermentative carbohydrate metabolism can produce lactate together with carbon dioxide and ethanol and/or acetate. Carbon dioxide produced through these pathways, together with gas that may be produced by co‐occurring fermentative yeasts, can contribute to visible bubbles or foam at the product surface. However, the present study did not measure microbial populations, gas evolution, carbon dioxide, or metabolites. Bubble‐ and foam‐related image features were therefore interpreted as indirect macroscopic indicators associated with fermentation activity rather than evidence of a specific microorganism or metabolic pathway (Fusco et al. 2015; Gänzle 2015; Pswarayi and Gänzle 2019; Simatende et al. 2019; Jeong and Kim 2025).

pH, total titratable acidity (TTA), TSS, and electrical conductivity (EC) provide complementary information about fermentation progress. pH reflects hydrogen‐ion activity, whereas TTA represents the amount of standardized base required to titrate sample acids to the defined endpoint and is often more closely linked to cumulative acid load and perceived sourness. However, TTA cannot be measured continuously by the electronic sensor array because it requires manual sampling and titration to a defined endpoint. EC was included because acidification and solute transformation alter the ionic environment of the cereal matrix, making EC a continuously measurable signal that can support calibrated acidity soft sensing when interpreted with pH, temperature, and laboratory validation. Direct mahewu‐specific evidence for EC as a standard fermentation marker remains limited, but related fermentation and food‐processing studies show that electrical properties can support quality monitoring when calibrated and validated appropriately (Zhu et al. 2019).

Digital monitoring studies show that embedded sensors, structured data logging, and machine‐learning models can support continuous or near‐continuous monitoring of fermentation and related physicochemical systems. Previous work on the Internet of Things in food fermentation identified remote monitoring, automation, real‐time sensing, and forecasting as active directions in the field (Adeleke, Nwulu, Adebo, et al. 2023). Related amasi studies demonstrated embedded sensing, machine learning, and sensor‐vision digital‐twin monitoring for acidity modeling (Adeleke et al. 2025; Adeleke et al. 2026), while low‐cost embedded sensing work outside food fermentation shows the importance of calibration, edge acquisition, and structured sensor‐data handling for reproducible measurement systems (Adeleke, Nwulu, Ogbolumani, et al. 2023). These studies support the broader feasibility of embedded sensing architectures, but mahewu‐specific validation remains necessary because cereal matrices, starch hydrolysis, and surface properties differ from dairy and environmental systems.

Sensor‐based monitoring captures bulk physicochemical changes, but it does not quantify visual surface evolution during fermentation. In mahewu, brightness variation, surface texture, bubbling, foaming, and surface heterogeneity may provide noncontact visual cues associated with fermentation progression. These cues are macroscopic descriptors rather than direct microbiological measurements. Computer vision has been investigated for food and fermentation monitoring, including image‐based quality assessment in fermented foods, bubble‐related monitoring in makgeolli fermentation, and semantic segmentation of fermentation foam using U‐Net‐type architectures (Song et al. 2025; Jeong and Kim 2025; Vihuro et al. 2026). These studies indicate that image‐derived features can support monitoring in related fermented systems, although mahewu‐specific image‐based prediction of raw laboratory TTA and TSS remains underdeveloped.

Deep learning and image segmentation methods provide a route to isolate the active food surface and extract quantitative descriptors from fermentation images. U‐Net and related convolutional neural network architectures are relevant for separating product regions from background, lighting artifacts, and vessel boundaries. However, imaging systems require careful control of camera geometry, illumination, preprocessing, segmentation quality, and validation design. This is particularly important in small laboratory datasets, where random splitting of temporally related observations can overestimate predictive performance. Validation by independent batch or fermentation run is therefore essential to reduce same‐run leakage and estimate model performance more realistically.

This study developed and evaluated a laboratory‐scale hybrid sensor‐vision framework for mahewu fermentation monitoring. The framework combines pH, EC, temperature, and process‐context variables with image‐derived visual features extracted from segmented mahewu surface images. The analysis focuses on raw laboratory TTA and TSS as reference targets and uses tolerance‐valid image‐to‐dataset matching followed by grouped validation by fermentation run. The objectives were to: (i) acquire synchronized sensor and image data during white‐ and yellow‐maize mahewu fermentation; (ii) align image records with sensor and raw laboratory measurements using a predefined tolerance‐valid rule; (iii) compare image‐only, sensor‐context, and multimodal models for raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction; and (iv) interpret the final leakage‐controlled models using permutation importance. This approach provides a cautious proof of concept for hybrid sensor‐vision monitoring of cereal fermentation while distinguishing laboratory reference measurements from sensor‐derived and image‐derived predictors.

Materials and Methods

Overview of the Laboratory‐Scale Sensor‐Vision Workflow

This study used a laboratory‐scale sensor‐vision framework to monitor and model mahewu fermentation using synchronized physicochemical sensing, image acquisition, image processing, laboratory reference measurements, and machine‐learning analysis. The workflow is summarized in Figure 1. White‐ and yellow‐maize mahewu were prepared under defined boiling‐time and fermentation‐temperature conditions, then fermented in a custom‐built chamber equipped with pH, EC, and temperature sensing, controlled stirring, temperature regulation, and fixed top‐view image acquisition.

Workflow of the laboratory‐scale sensor‐vision framework for mahewu fermentation monitoring.

During each run, the Raspberry Pi‐based platform recorded pH, EC, and temperature while also capturing surface images of the fermenting mahewu under LED illumination. Intermittent laboratory measurements of TTA and TSS were used as reference values. The image records were segmented, cropped, and converted into color, texture, and morphology descriptors. These descriptors were then aligned with sensor records and laboratory measurements using a tolerance‐valid matching procedure before model development. The analysis comprised primary raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction models and secondary soft‐sensor and reconstruction analyses. Grouped validation by fermentation run was used for the primary models.

Materials, Reagents, Sensors, and Equipment

White and yellow maize grains were obtained from the Agricultural Research Council (Pretoria, South Africa). The grains were washed, dried overnight at 38 in a laboratory drying oven (Prolab, Manama, Bahrain), milled to 1.5 mm particle size using a Perten 3600 laboratory mill (Hudding, Sweden) and sieved on an Analysette 3 Spartan (Fritsch GmbH, Idar‐Oberstein, Germany). Malted sorghum (King Korn, Tiger Consumer Brands Ltd., South Africa) served as the fermentation inoculum. Analytical‐grade sodium hydroxide (NaOH), pH calibration buffers at pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00, and EC calibration standards of 84 and 1413 µS/cm (commercial calibration standards) were used for calibration and laboratory analysis.

The fermentation platform was built around a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B microcomputer (Raspberry Pi Foundation, UK). Temperature was measured using a DS18B20 digital temperature probe (Maxim Integrated, USA). pH and ionic changes were monitored using an Atlas Scientific EZO‐pH circuit and probe and an Atlas Scientific EZO‐EC conductivity circuit and probe (Atlas Scientific, New York, USA). Timekeeping was supported by a DS3231 real‐time clock (SparkFun Electronics, CO, USA). Surface images were captured using a Raspberry Pi NoIR Camera Module V2 mounted in a fixed top‐view position above the sample vessel. LED illumination was used during image capture to reduce variation from ambient lighting. A positive‐temperature‐coefficient (PTC) fan heater, cooling fan, magnetic stirrer, and LED illumination system were controlled through a relay and ULN2003 driver arrangement (SparkFun Electronics). TSS was measured using a handheld refractometer (Hanna Instruments H196801, Woonsocket, RI, USA), calibrated with distilled water. TTA was determined manually by titration with 0.1 M NaOH to pH 8.3. All materials were handled under laboratory conditions, and sensors were maintained according to manufacturer specifications to ensure calibration stability.

Fermentation Platform, Hardware Interface, and Acquisition Architecture

The fermentation platform consisted of a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B connected to sensing, imaging, timing, and actuation modules. The system architecture and data flow are shown in Figure 3. The Raspberry Pi code used Broadcom GPIO numbering. The cooling fan relay was controlled through BCM GPIO 24, the PTC fan heater relay through BCM GPIO 18, the magnetic‐stirrer relay through BCM GPIO 25, and the LED illumination relay through BCM GPIO 23. These relays were driven through the ULN2003 relay‐driver arrangement and a 5 V relay module. The magnetic stirrer was used to maintain sample homogeneity, while the fan and PTC heater regulated chamber temperature.

System architecture and data‐flow diagram of the mahewu sensor‐vision monitoring platform.

The DS18B20 temperature sensor was connected through the 1‐Wire interface, with the data line connected to BCM GPIO 4, ground connected to a ground terminal, and power connected to 5 V. The DS3231 real‐time clock used the I2C interface, with SDA connected to Raspberry Pi physical pin 3/BCM GPIO2 and SCL connected to physical pin 5/BCM GPIO3. The pH circuit was connected to the Raspberry Pi through UART port /dev/ttyAMA4, with the UART lines connected to BCM GPIO 8 and BCM GPIO 9. The EC circuit was connected through UART port /dev/ttyAMA5, with the UART lines connected to BCM GPIO 12 and BCM GPIO 13. The camera was connected through the Raspberry Pi camera ribbon interface.

The acquisition software recorded sensor data and triggered image capture during fermentation. Surface images were acquired with rpicam‐still, and the LED illumination relay was activated during image capture. Image filenames included the timestamp, pH, EC, and temperature values, which supported later matching between image files and tabular records. pH, EC, and temperature were recorded every 30 s, and surface images were acquired every 3 min. A run‐level inventory of unique image filenames assigned to each fermentation group, together with the first and last image timestamps, is provided in Table S1. Sensor records and image files were stored for segmentation, cropping, feature extraction, tolerance‐valid matching, and model development. The detailed pin‐mapping summary is provided in Figure S1.

Surface images were captured using the Raspberry Pi NoIR Camera Module V2 under controlled LED illumination and stored at 3280 px × 2464 px in 24‐bit RGB format. The same nominal camera‐acquisition configuration was used throughout the image dataset, including ISO 200, an exposure time of 1/39 s, and automatic white‐balance mode. Because automatic white balance remained enabled, white balance gains were not fixed across images. Camera geometry and illumination arrangement were held constant between fermentation runs. No separate photometric, histogram, or color normalization was applied before feature extraction. Images were resized and converted into the color spaces required for the grayscale, RGB, HSV, and CIELAB descriptors.

Manual Laboratory Reference Measurements and Sampling Procedure

Manual TTA and TSS measurements were collected intermittently during fermentation and served as reference values for target‐specific model calibration and validation. Sampling was performed through the opening of the fermentation chamber. The chamber was opened briefly during sampling, and sterilized sampling tools were used to withdraw aliquots. The fermentation vessel remained inside the chamber during sampling, and the chamber was closed immediately after each collection to reduce temperature disturbance and contamination risk. The withdrawn aliquots were not returned to the fermentation vessel after laboratory analysis. Magnetic stirring was used during fermentation to support sample homogeneity.

TSS was measured with a handheld refractometer and reported in . The refractometer was calibrated with distilled water before use. TTA was determined by titrating the prepared sample with 0.1 M NaOH to an endpoint pH of 8.3, following the endpoint approach reported by Kewuyemi and Adebo (2024). The titrant volume was recorded in μL and normalized by sample mass as μL/g. The TTA values therefore represent manual laboratory acidity measurements, not electronic sensor readings.

The laboratory measurements were timestamped and later aligned with sensor records and image files. Because manual TTA and TSS measurements were intermittent, the number of raw laboratory reference records was smaller than the number of sensor and image records. This distinction was retained throughout the analysis: raw laboratory TTA and TSS were used as primary validation targets only when the corresponding laboratory values were available.

Quantitative Problem Formulation and Leakage‐Controlled Task Definition

The fermentation dataset was represented as a time‐indexed multimodal sequence. For each record i, the system could contain a surface image, sensor measurements, process‐context variables, and laboratory reference values where available: (1) oi=Ii,si,ci,li,ti where Ii is the surface image, si={pHi,ECi,Ti} is the sensor vector containing pH, EC, and temperature, ci represents process‐context variables such as maize type, boiling time, and fermentation‐temperature condition, li represents available laboratory reference values, and ti is the timestamp or elapsed fermentation time.

Each image was transformed into a quantitative image‐feature vector through segmentation, cropping, and feature extraction: (2) vi=ΦIi where Φ(.) represents the image‐processing function and vi contains the extracted color, texture, and morphology descriptors.

Three feature sets were defined to separate the contribution of image information from sensor‐context information: xsensor,i=pHi,ECi,Ti,ci (3) xmulti,i=vi,pHi,ECi,Ti,ci

The primary prediction targets were raw laboratory TTA and raw laboratory TSS. Primary target datasets were constructed from matched records containing laboratory TTA or TSS values: (4)

Because laboratory TTA and TSS values were not available for every image‐sensor record, TTA and TSS were evaluated as target‐specific prediction tasks using only records with the relevant non‐missing laboratory target. This avoided treating missing or proxy‐filled values as raw laboratory measurements.

For pH and EC reconstruction, the target variable was excluded from the predictor set. When pH was treated as a secondary reconstruction target, pH was excluded from the input feature set. When EC was treated as a secondary reconstruction target, EC was excluded from the input feature set. Proxy‐labeled TTA outputs generated from sensor‐derived soft sensing were also analyzed separately from raw laboratory TTA. These rules were applied so that model performance could be interpreted as prediction of the specified target rather than reconstruction of a value already present in the input variables.

Hybrid Fuzzy‐Machine‐Learning TTA Soft‐Sensor Formulation

TTA cannot be measured directly by the electronic sensor array. It requires manual titration, which is intermittent, labor‐intensive, and unsuitable for continuous inline monitoring. For this reason, a secondary TTA soft‐sensing layer was developed to estimate acidity from continuously available sensor variables. This layer was retained as part of the monitoring framework but was not treated as raw laboratory TTA validation.

The fuzzy‐logic system used pH, EC, and temperature as inputs: (5) zi=ECi,Ti,pHi

Each input variable was mapped into linguistic levels using trapezoidal membership functions. For a fuzzy set A with parameters a, b, c, and d, the membership function was defined as: (6) μAz=maxminz−ab−a,1,d−zd−c,0

EC, temperature, and pH were each assigned to Low, Medium, or High categories. The rule base therefore contained 27 possible fuzzy‐rule combinations. Each rule followed the form: (7) IFECisEjandTisTkandpHisPl,thenTTAisYjkl where Ej, Tk, and Pl represent the fuzzy sets for EC, temperature, and pH, respectively, and Yjkl represents the fuzzy acidity output associated with that rule.

The firing strength of each rule was calculated using the minimum t‐norm: (8) wjkl=minμEjECi,μTkTi,μPlpHi

The aggregated fuzzy output was converted into a crisp fuzzy TTA estimate using centroid defuzzification: (9) TTAF,i=∫yμoutydy∫μoutydy

The fuzzy TTA estimate was then combined with the original sensor variables to form the hybrid fuzzy‐machine‐learning feature vector: (10) xfuzzy,i=TTAF,i,ECi,Ti,pHi

A Random Forest regressor was used to learn the nonlinear relationship between this hybrid feature vector and laboratory‐measured TTA where laboratory TTA was available: (11) TTAhybrid,i=fRFxfuzzy,i

This hybrid formulation was used as a soft‐sensor pathway for acidity estimation. Its purpose was to support near‐real‐time acidity monitoring from continuously available pH, EC, and temperature signals. The fuzzy‐machine‐learning estimate served as a continuous soft‐sensor output, while raw laboratory TTA prediction was evaluated separately. Raw laboratory TTA prediction, proxy‐labeled TTA sensitivity analysis, and pH or EC reconstruction were reported separately to maintain clear interpretation of model performance. The secondary fuzzy‐machine‐learning soft‐sensor models were evaluated against laboratory‐measured TTA using ordinary five‐fold cross‐validation. The fuzzy‐only, machine‐learning‐only Random Forest, and hybrid fuzzy‐machine‐learning models were evaluated using the same folds. Fermentation run was not used as the grouping unit in this secondary analysis. The results are therefore treated as exploratory and do not estimate generalization to previously unseen fermentation runs.

Semantic Segmentation and Image‐Analysis Pipeline

The physicochemical and visual methods were designed as complementary measurement streams. Sensor and laboratory measurements characterized bulk acidity, ionic, thermal, and soluble‐solids changes, whereas surface imaging captured visible changes in color, brightness, texture, bubbles, foam, and surface heterogeneity. Semantic segmentation was therefore applied before feature extraction to isolate the visible mahewu region and support separate image‐only, sensor‐context, and multimodal analyses.

Surface images were processed to isolate the mahewu product region before quantitative feature extraction. Manual polygon annotations were prepared in LabelMe and used as reference masks for the semantic‐segmentation workflow. The U‐Net model separated the visible mahewu surface from the vessel wall, immersed probes, background, and other non‐product regions. The resulting masks were used to generate cropped product‐region images and binary masks for downstream feature extraction.

The segmentation task was formulated as a pixel‐level mapping from the original image to a binary product‐region mask: (12) Mi=gIi;θg where Ii is the input image, Mi is the predicted binary product‐region mask, g(.) is the segmentation model, and θg represents the segmentation‐model parameters.

Training used an equal‐weight combination of binary cross‐entropy loss and soft dice loss. The dice‐loss component was defined as: (13) LDice=1−2ΣpMpGp+εΣpMp+ΣpGp+ε where Mp is the predicted mask value at pixel p, Gp is the corresponding manual ground‐truth mask value, and ε is a small constant used to prevent division by zero.

Segmentation evaluation used 96 manually annotated images from 14 fermentation‐run groups. This evaluation was distinct from the primary raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction analyses. A custom U‐Net was used with four encoder stages containing 16, 32, 64, and 128 channels and a 256‐channel bottleneck. Images and masks were resized to 256 pixels × 256 pixels. Training used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, batch size of four, and a maximum of 30 epochs. The training objective combined binary cross‐entropy loss and soft dice loss with equal weighting.

Nested grouped validation was used, with one fermentation‐run group held out for outer testing and five‐fold GroupKFold validation within the remaining groups for epoch selection. All images from the outer held‐out group were excluded from model fitting and inner validation. The selected epoch was determined from mean inner‐validation macro‐dice, after which the outer model was trained on the corresponding outer‐training data. Binary masks were evaluated using a threshold of 0.50.

No synthetic image augmentation was used in the nested grouped segmentation evaluation. Overfitting risk was controlled using an outer run‐level hold‐out and grouped inner‐fold epoch selection. Images from each outer test group were excluded from model fitting and epoch selection, providing an out‐of‐run performance estimate. The analysis used PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128 and random seed 20260620.

After segmentation, the product‐region mask was applied to remove non‐product pixels. The retained mahewu region was cropped and used for feature extraction. The extracted descriptors included color statistics, grayscale intensity descriptors, texture measures, local binary pattern features, edge‐density features, and morphology‐related descriptors. These features quantified visible surface properties including brightness, color distribution, texture, edge structure, and surface heterogeneity. The complete feature dictionary is provided in Table S2.

Multimodal Data Fusion, Tolerance‐Valid Matching, and Grouped Validation

Sensor records, image files, and laboratory measurements were aligned before model development. Each image filename contained an acquisition timestamp and embedded pH, EC, and temperature values. These embedded values were used together with the tabular sensor records to verify image‐to‐record consistency. The matching procedure retained records only when the image timestamp and tabular timestamp were sufficiently close and when the embedded pH and EC values agreed with the corresponding tabular values within predefined tolerances.

For each candidate image‐to‐record match, the absolute timestamp difference was computed as: (14) Δtij=timage,i−tdata,j

The pH difference was computed as: (15) ΔpHij=pHimage,i−pHdata,j

The relative EC difference was computed as: (16) ΔECij%=100×ECimage,i−ECdata,jmaxECimage,i,ECdata,j

A record was retained within the tolerance‐valid dataset when Δtij ≤ 5 s, ΔpHij ≤ 0.02, and ΔECij≤ 2%. These thresholds were used only for image‐to‐dataset matching and quality control. After matching, the dataset was organized into three modeling feature sets: image‐only, sensor‐context, and multimodal. The image‐only feature set contained only image‐derived descriptors. The sensor‐context feature set contained pH, EC, temperature, maize type, boiling time, and fermentation‐temperature condition. The multimodal feature set combined image‐derived descriptors with sensor‐context variables. This structure allowed the contribution of image features to be assessed separately from the contribution of sensor and process‐context variables.

Model validation was conducted by grouping records according to fermentation run. Records from the same fermentation run were kept within the same validation fold to reduce same‐run temporal leakage. Repeat or audit runs were retained for traceability and sensitivity checks but were excluded from the primary validation set. The grouped validation structure and held‐out fermentation‐run groups are summarized in Table S3A,B.

Predictive Modeling, Model Interpretation, and Secondary Analyses

The primary predictive tasks were raw laboratory TTA prediction and raw laboratory TSS prediction. For each target, models were trained only on records with the corresponding non‐missing laboratory reference value. Image‐only, sensor‐context, and multimodal models were compared for each target. This design tested whether image‐derived descriptors could support prediction alone or add useful information beyond sensor‐context variables.

The supervised prediction problem was expressed as: (17) y^i=fxi;θf where xi is the selected feature set, f(.) is the regression model, θf represents the fitted model parameters, and y^i is the predicted target value. Separate models were developed for raw laboratory TTA and raw laboratory TSS. The evaluated regressors included Ridge Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting. Model performance was reported using mean absolute error, root mean squared error, coefficient of determination, and Pearson correlation. These metrics were reported separately for TTA and TSS to avoid combining chemically different targets into a single performance claim.

All predictor values were complete in the final analytical datasets. The model pipelines retained a median‐imputation step fitted separately within each training fold, although no values required imputation. For Ridge Regression, this was followed by standardization using StandardScaler, whereas Random Forest and Gradient Boosting were not scaled. Maize type was encoded as YM = 1 and WM = 0. Boiling time and fermentation‐temperature condition were entered as numerical process‐context variables. All preprocessing operations were fitted using training‐fold data only and subsequently applied to the corresponding held‐out fold.

Fixed model configurations were compared; no automated grid or randomized hyperparameter search was performed. Ridge Regression used α = 1.0. Random Forest used 100 trees, a minimum leaf size of two, and random_state = 42. Gradient Boosting used 100 estimators, a maximum tree depth of two, and random_state = 42. A mean‐prediction dummy regressor was included as a baseline. Parameters not explicitly specified retained the defaults provided by scikit‐learn 1.6.1.

Candidate model classes were compared using pooled out‐of‐fold RMSE from five‐fold GroupKFold validation grouped by fermentation run. The model with the lowest pooled out‐of‐fold RMSE was retained for each target and feature set. The same grouped folds were used for model‐class selection and performance reporting; therefore, the primary regression comparison did not constitute nested grouped model selection and may contain some model‐selection optimism.

The primary regression analyses were conducted in Google Colab using Python 3.12.13, NumPy 2.0.2, pandas 2.2.2, SciPy 1.16.3, scikit‐learn 1.6.1, and joblib 1.5.3. The preprocessing procedures, model configurations, random seeds, and model‐selection details are summarized in Table S9A, while the software environment is reported in Table S9B.

Uncertainty in the comparison between the selected sensor‐context and multimodal TSS models was assessed using paired out‐of‐fold predictions and a fermentation‐run cluster bootstrap. Complete fermentation‐run groups were resampled with replacement, with all records from each selected run retained together. The RMSE difference was calculated as multimodal RMSE minus sensor‐context RMSE, and the percentile bootstrap distribution was used to obtain the 95% confidence interval. The cluster bootstrap used 10,000 replicates and random seed 20260726.

Secondary analyses were separated from the primary raw laboratory validation. The fuzzy‐machine‐learning TTA soft‐sensor was used to estimate an acidity‐related signal from continuously available pH, EC, and temperature data. Proxy‐labeled TTA sensitivity analyses were reported separately and were not presented as raw laboratory TTA validation. pH and EC reconstruction analyses were also treated as secondary tasks. When pH was the target, pH was excluded from the predictors. When EC was the target, EC was excluded from the predictors. This avoided target/input leakage in the reconstruction tasks.

Permutation importance was calculated after model fitting to assess the contribution of individual predictors and feature groups within the tolerance‐valid models. Pearson correlation analysis described relationships among selected sensor, process‐context, image‐derived, and laboratory variables, while residual plots were examined to identify target‐specific prediction‐error patterns. Results and Discussion

Dataset Alignment, Target Availability, and Physicochemical Trend Context

The tolerance‐valid alignment procedure produced the dataset used for the main modeling analyses. Image records were retained only when the image timestamp, embedded image filename values, and tabular sensor records satisfied the predefined matching criteria. The final tolerance‐valid dataset contained 7945 matched image‐sensor records from 12 main fermentation runs. Raw laboratory TTA values were available for 71 matched records, while raw laboratory TSS values were available for 87 matched records. These raw laboratory subsets were used as the target‐specific validation records for the primary TTA and TSS prediction tasks.

The difference between the number of matched image‐sensor records and the number of laboratory reference records reflects the intermittent nature of laboratory sampling. pH, EC, temperature, and image records were collected more densely than TTA and TSS reference measurements. Therefore, raw laboratory TTA and TSS predictions were evaluated only where the corresponding laboratory measurement existed. This distinction is important because it prevents proxy‐filled or sensor‐derived values from being interpreted as raw laboratory measurements.

Two run groups, WM_BT19_T25b and YM_BT13_T29b, were designated repeat/audit groups and excluded from the primary raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction analyses. These exclusions did not apply to the separate semantic‐segmentation evaluation, which used 96 manually annotated images from all 14 fermentation‐run groups and is reported in Section 3.2. Records from the same fermentation run were grouped during primary model validation to reduce same‐run temporal leakage. The final primary dataset scope and target‐specific raw laboratory availability are summarized in Table 3. The grouped validation task structure and held‐out fermentation‐run groups are provided in Table S3A,B. Across the 14 fermentation‐run groups, the image‐to‐run inventory contained 9278 unique filenames. The 12 main runs accounted for 8470 files, with 60–1964 files per run, while the two repeat/audit groups accounted for 808 files. Observed image‐acquisition spans ranged from 7.13 to 88.49 h across the main runs. Table S1 provides the run‐level counts and timestamps.

Segmentation, Crop Extraction, and Representative Image Records

The image‐processing pipeline isolated the mahewu product region from the original top‐view images. Manual LabelMe annotations and U‐Net‐based semantic segmentation were used to reduce interference from the vessel wall, immersed probes, background, and other non‐product regions. The segmentation workflow is shown in Figure S4. The resulting masks were used to generate cropped product‐region images for feature extraction.

Semantic segmentation was evaluated across 96 manually annotated images from 14 fermentation‐run groups using nested grouped validation. The run‐balanced mean Dice coefficient was 0.8070, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of 0.7180–0.8827. The corresponding run‐balanced mean intersection over union was 0.7195, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of 0.6308–0.7996. Held‐out performance varied across run groups. In lower‐overlap cases, predicted masks missed parts of the manually annotated product surface or included regions near immersed probes. Two run groups contained fewer than three annotated test images; their individual fold metrics were therefore not interpreted separately. Per‐run results are provided in Table S4, and representative held‐out predictions are shown in Figure S5.

The retained image descriptors quantified surface brightness, color distribution, grayscale intensity, texture, edge structure, morphology, and surface heterogeneity. These image‐derived variables were used as predictors in image‐only and multimodal models. The full feature dictionary is provided in Table S2.

Representative tolerance‐valid cropped images are shown in Figure 4. The panel includes images selected from low, medium, and high raw laboratory TTA ranges and low, medium, and high raw laboratory TSS ranges where corresponding laboratory reference values were available. Exploratory Hybrid Fuzzy‐Machine‐Learning TTA Soft‐Sensor Analysis

The hybrid fuzzy‐machine‐learning TTA estimator was retained as an exploratory secondary soft‐sensor analysis. Manual TTA titration is intermittent and cannot provide continuous inline acidity values; therefore, the exploratory pathway used continuously available pH, EC, and temperature values to estimate an acidity‐related signal through a fuzzy‐logic layer followed by machine‐learning correction. This analysis remained separate from the primary grouped validation of raw laboratory TTA.

The fuzzy‐only model produced MAE = 84.5 μL/g, RMSE = 112.3 μL/g, and R 2 = 0.62 (Table 4). The machine‐learning‐only Random Forest produced MAE = 49.1 μL/g, RMSE = 61.7 μL/g, and R 2 = 0.84. The hybrid fuzzy‐machine‐learning model produced MAE = 31.3 μL/g, RMSE = 40.5 μL/g, and R 2 = 0.92.

Leakage‐Controlled Prediction of Raw Laboratory TTA

Raw laboratory TTA prediction was evaluated using only records with manually measured TTA values. This analysis was separated from the hybrid fuzzy‐machine‐learning soft‐sensor analysis reported in Section 3.3. Raw laboratory TTA performance was evaluated against manually measured titration records.

Under grouped validation by fermentation run, the image‐only Gradient Boosting model showed limited predictive strength for raw laboratory TTA, with R 2 = 0.1743, MAE = 155.26 μL/g, RMSE = 212.46 μL/g, and Pearson r = 0.4666 (Table 5). This result indicates that visible surface descriptors alone were not sufficient for accurate TTA prediction under the leakage‐controlled validation design. This is expected because TTA reflects cumulative acidity in the bulk mahewu matrix and cannot be inferred fully from surface appearance alone.

Leakage‐Controlled Prediction of Raw Laboratory TSS

Raw laboratory TSS prediction was evaluated using records with refractometer‐measured TSS values. TSS was analyzed separately from TTA because soluble‐solids behavior in cereal fermentation can reflect starch hydrolysis, release of soluble compounds, and microbial utilization of fermentable substrates.

The image‐only Gradient Boosting model showed weak predictive performance for raw laboratory TSS, with R 2 = 0.0532, MAE = 0.878 , RMSE = 1.204 , and Pearson r = 0.4567 (Table 5). Surface‐image descriptors therefore provided limited information about the soluble‐solids state when used without sensor or process‐context variables.

The sensor‐context Ridge Regression model improved TSS prediction, with R 2 = 0.5326, MAE = 0.701 , RMSE = 0.846 , and Pearson r = 0.7477. The multimodal Random Forest model produced the best point‐estimate performance, with R 2 = 0.5939, MAE = 0.624 , RMSE = 0.789 , and Pearson r = 0.7825. The full‐model performance output is provided in Table S5. Sensor‐context variables carried the main predictive information for TSS, while the multimodal model produced the best point estimate. The observed‐versus‐predicted TSS results are shown in Figure 5B, and the feature‐set comparison is shown in Figure 6B.

The difference between the sensor‐context and multimodal TSS models was evaluated using paired out‐of‐fold predictions and fermentation‐run cluster bootstrap resampling. The sensor‐context model had an RMSE of 0.8461 , while the multimodal model had an RMSE of 0.7887 . The RMSE difference, calculated as multimodal minus sensor‐context, was −0.0574 , with a 95% run‐cluster bootstrap confidence interval of −0.1463 to 0.0130 . Because the interval included zero, the uncertainty analysis did not establish a clear improvement of the multimodal model over the sensor‐context model. The multimodal result is therefore interpreted only as the best point‐estimate performance. Permutation‐Importance Interpretation

Permutation‐importance results for the tolerance‐valid models are summarized in Table S6 and Figure S7. For raw laboratory TTA, pH produced the largest mean decrease in R 2 after permutation (1.6067), followed by EC (0.0697), boiling time (0.0609), and temperature (0.0483). This pattern is consistent with the stronger performance of the sensor‐context TTA model.

For raw laboratory TSS, EC, and pH produced the largest importance values, with mean decreases in R 2 of 0.4933 and 0.3544, respectively. Image texture and color descriptors also contributed to the multimodal TSS model. The most influential image‐derived variables included local binary pattern texture bin 2 (0.0578), mean HSV hue (0.0172), and maximum grayscale intensity (0.0083). The contribution of these descriptors can be interpreted physically. Fermentation‐related gas accumulation can alter bubble and foam patterns, producing changes in local edges, intensity variation, and spatial texture, while changes in matrix structure and optical scattering can influence RGB, HSV, CIELAB, and grayscale descriptors. Related studies have shown fermentation‐associated changes in color or surface structure in barley tempeh and yoghurt and have used image analysis to monitor fermentation bubbles and foam in other systems (Feng et al. 2007; Jeong and Kim 2025; Chopde et al. 2025; Vihuro et al. 2026). These mechanisms provide a plausible physical basis for the complementary contribution of image texture and color features. However, permutation importance describes predictor contribution within the fitted model and does not establish that any descriptor directly measures microbial activity, carbon dioxide production, or chemical concentration.

Secondary Analyses and Implementation Relevance

Secondary analyses were conducted to support interpretation of the main raw laboratory prediction results. These analyses included pH and EC reconstruction, proxy‐labeled TTA sensitivity analysis, residual diagnostics, and image‐feature distribution assessment. Table S7 shows that reconstruction performance varied by target and dataset scope. Within the verified‐and‐probable records, the sensor‐context model with pH excluded achieved the highest reported pH reconstruction performance (R 2 = 0.8570), while the sensor‐context model with EC excluded achieved the highest reported EC reconstruction performance (R 2 = 0.7470). Table S8 shows that image‐only prediction of proxy‐labeled TTA produced R 2 values of 0.4888 for the main non‐repeat dataset and 0.3911 for the verified‐and‐probable dataset. These secondary analyses characterize model behavior on sensor‐derived targets and remain separate from the raw laboratory TTA and TSS results.

Figure S8 shows target‐specific residual patterns for the raw TTA and TSS models. TTA residuals were centered near zero across much of the observed range but included several larger deviations at mid‐to‐high observed TTA values, including one large negative residual at the upper end of the range. TSS residuals were predominantly positive at the lowest observed values and predominantly negative at the upper observed range, indicating target‐range‐dependent prediction error. These patterns complement the MAE, RMSE, R 2, and Pearson correlation values reported for the two targets.

This implementation demonstrates that synchronized pH, EC, temperature, and image acquisition can be integrated into a Raspberry Pi‐based monitoring system. The results support the feasibility of sensor‐vision monitoring for mahewu under controlled laboratory conditions. Any use beyond controlled laboratory conditions requires validation across larger batches, different vessel geometries, variable lighting conditions, different stirring regimes, and independent production environments. Endpoint forecasting is relevant to fermentation scheduling, but it requires a dedicated validation framework with explicitly defined pH and TTA thresholds, independent‐run testing, and error metrics reported in time units.

Comparative Analysis With Existing Studies

Direct mahewu and amahewu studies have identified pH, TTA, and TSS as process‐sensitive indicators affected by maize type, inoculum, fermentation temperature, and fermentation duration (Gadaga et al. 1999; Oyeyinka et al. 2021; Daji et al. 2022, 2023). Broader African cereal‐fermentation studies similarly report pH decline and TTA increase during acidification, while TSS may vary with starch hydrolysis and microbial utilization of soluble carbohydrates (Foma et al. 2012; Namugumya and Muyanja 2009; Muyanja et al. 2012). The physicochemical trajectories observed in the present study are consistent with these established fermentation patterns.

EC is less established as a mahewu fermentation endpoint than pH, TTA, or TSS. In the present study, EC was used as a continuously recorded predictor associated with changes in the ionic environment of the fermenting cereal matrix, rather than as a standalone measure of acidity or a fermentation completion criterion. This use of EC is consistent with related food‐monitoring work in which electrical properties support quality assessment when calibrated against laboratory measurements (Zhu et al. 2019).

Related digital‐fermentation studies have demonstrated the value of embedded sensing, structured data acquisition, and machine‐learning models for fermentation monitoring (Adeleke, Nwulu, Adebo, et al. 2023; Adeleke et al. 2025; Adeleke et al. 2026). Computer‐vision studies in fermented foods have also shown that segmentation, foam or bubble analysis, color variation, and texture descriptors can provide useful visual information for process monitoring (Song et al. 2025; Jeong and Kim 2025; Vihuro et al. 2026). The present study extends this direction by evaluating image‐derived descriptors against raw laboratory TTA and TSS measurements under grouped validation by fermentation run.

Table 6 summarizes the position of the present framework relative to direct mahewu physicochemical studies, broader African cereal‐fermentation research, embedded monitoring, computer vision, and soft sensing. The grouped‐validation results showed that image‐only models were insufficient for raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction. Sensor‐context variables provided the strongest TTA prediction, whereas the multimodal model produced the best point‐estimate result for TSS. Image‐derived descriptors were therefore treated as complementary inputs; however, the TSS uncertainty analysis did not establish a clear improvement over the sensor‐context model.

Implications, Limitations, and Study Boundaries

The main implication of this study is that a hybrid sensor‐vision workflow can support laboratory‐scale monitoring of mahewu fermentation when raw laboratory validation, tolerance‐valid matching, and grouped model evaluation are applied. Surface images alone were insufficient for accurate prediction of raw laboratory TTA and TSS because the camera recorded only the exposed product surface, whereas TTA and TSS were measured from the sampled bulk matrix. TTA reflects the acid‐neutralization capacity of the matrix, whereas TSS reflects the soluble‐solids state influenced by starch hydrolysis, the release of soluble compounds, and microbial utilization of fermentable substrates. Surface color, brightness, texture, bubbles, and foam can provide indirect information on fermentation progression but cannot fully represent biochemical and compositional changes throughout the stirred matrix (Daji et al. 2022). This distinction explains why sensor‐context variables performed most strongly for TTA and why the multimodal TSS model showed only a modest point‐estimate improvement over the sensor‐context model. The multimodal TSS result is therefore interpreted as the best point‐estimate performance rather than evidence that image features alone capture bulk TTA or TSS dynamics. The limited number of annotated images also restricts the precision and broader generalizability of the segmentation performance estimates, and further validation using larger independently annotated datasets is required.

The study was conducted under laboratory‐scale conditions using a fixed camera position, controlled LED illumination, and a specific fermentation chamber. The annotated segmentation dataset contained 96 images distributed unevenly across 14 fermentation‐run groups. Held‐out segmentation performance varied across groups, and two groups contained fewer than three annotated test images, limiting interpretation of their individual fold metrics. Raw laboratory TTA and TSS measurements were intermittent, resulting in fewer laboratory reference records than matched image‐sensor records. The image pipeline characterized visible product‐surface appearance; it did not measure microbial composition or bulk chemical composition. Larger independent datasets are needed before the workflow can be evaluated beyond controlled laboratory conditions.

White and yellow maize were included as process categories, but the intrinsic composition and physicochemical properties of the maize materials were not independently characterized. Consequently, differences associated with maize type cannot be separated fully from unmeasured variation in raw‐material composition. Future studies should characterize relevant compositional attributes and evaluate their effects across independently sourced maize batches.

Future work also needs to evaluate additional independent fermentation runs, vessel geometries, lighting conditions, maize sources, and inoculum conditions. Microbiological or metabolite measurements would strengthen interpretation of the visual descriptors. Endpoint‐forecasting studies require explicit pH and TTA thresholds, independent‐run validation, and prediction errors reported in time units. Sequential image or video analysis could also be evaluated to capture short‐term surface changes that are not represented by isolated still images.

Conclusion

This study shows that a laboratory‐scale hybrid sensor‐vision framework can support mahewu fermentation monitoring when synchronized pH, EC, temperature, and surface‐image records are aligned with raw laboratory reference measurements and evaluated under grouped validation by fermentation run. Separating raw laboratory TTA and TSS prediction from proxy‐labeled TTA, pH reconstruction, and EC reconstruction reduced target/input leakage and clarified the interpretation of model performance. Raw laboratory TTA was predicted most effectively by the sensor‐context Gradient Boosting model (R 2 = 0.8298, MAE = 61.03 μL/g, RMSE = 96.46 μL/g). For raw laboratory TSS, the multimodal Random Forest model produced the best point‐estimate performance (R 2 = 0.5939, MAE = 0.624 , RMSE = 0.789 ). The paired fermentation‐run cluster‐bootstrap confidence interval for the RMSE difference between the multimodal and sensor‐context models included zero; therefore, a clear multimodal advantage was not established. Image‐only models were weaker for both targets, showing that visible surface descriptors are better interpreted as complementary information rather than standalone replacements for physicochemical sensing or laboratory analysis. Further validation is required across independent fermentation batches, vessel geometries, lighting conditions, maize sources, and production environments.
