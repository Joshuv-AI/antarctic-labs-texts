Online Learning Of Expanding Graphs
Samuel Rey 1 {}^{\textbf{1}} (Member, IEEE), Bishwadeep Das 2 {}^{\textbf{2}} (Student Member, IEEE), and Elvin Isufi 2 {}^{\textbf{2}}, (Senior Member, IEEE)

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of online network topology inference for expanding graphs from a stream of spatiotemporal signals. Online algorithms for dynamic graph learning are crucial in delay-sensitive applications or when changes in topology occur rapidly. While existing works focus on inferring the connectivity within a fixed set of nodes, in practice, the graph can grow as new nodes join the network. This poses additional challenges like modeling temporal dynamics involving signals and graphs of different sizes. This growth also increases the computational complexity of the learning process, which may become prohibitive. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to tackle this setting. We propose a general online algorithm based on projected proximal gradient descent that accounts for the increasing graph size at each iteration. Recursively updating the sample covariance matrix is a key aspect of our approach. We introduce a strategy that enables different types of updates for nodes that just joined the network and for previously existing nodes. To provide further insights into the proposed method, we specialize it in Gaussian Markov random field settings, where we analyze the computational complexity and characterize the dynamic cumulative regret. Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach using both controlled experiments and real-world datasets from epidemic and financial networks.

2
Fundamentals of network topology inference

The problem of learning a graph involves inferring its topology from a set of nodal observations
[
1
]
.
Specifically, let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
denote a graph with a node set
{\mathcal{V}}
consisting of
N
nodes and an edge set
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
.
Let
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
be the graph-shift operator (GSO), a matrix representing the graph structure such as the adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
or the graph Laplacian
{\mathbf{L}}

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
Nodal observations are represented by
graph signals
, which are defined on the set
{\mathcal{V}}
and expressed as a vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
x_{i}
denotes the signal value at node
i
.

Given a matrix of
T
graph signals
{\mathbf{X}}:=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T}
, the graph learning problem comprises solving the inverse problem
{\mathbf{S}}=F^{-1}({\mathbf{X}})
, where
F(\cdot)
is a function that relates the GSO to the observed signals.
Consequently, the success of this task hinges on the dependence between
{\mathbf{X}}
and the unknown
{\mathbf{S}}
, with different methods leveraging various relations.
For instance, graphical models
[
39
]
assume
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
, where
{\mathbf{C}}
denotes the covariance of the observed data; graph stationarity
[
3
]
posits that
{\mathbf{C}}
is a polynomial of
{\mathbf{S}}
; and signal smoothness
[
2
]
requires the observed signals to be smooth over the graph.

Denoting the sample covariance matrix as
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}=\frac{1}{T}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}
, the graph topology can be recovered by solving

{\mathbf{S}}^{*}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{S}},{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t})+\lambda r({\mathbf{S}}),

(1)

where
{\mathcal{L}}
is a loss function encoding the relation between
{\mathbf{S}}
and the observed signals,
r({\mathbf{S}})
is a regularizing promoting desired properties on
{\mathbf{S}}
, and
{\mathcal{S}}
defines the set of constraints for a valid GSO, like the non-negative adjacency matrix or graph Laplacian.
Here, we focus on
r({\mathbf{S}})=\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1}
to enforce graph sparsity, but alternative penalties may be considered.

Learning graphs from streaming data.

In many applications, graph signals arrive sequentially over time.
Formally, let
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
denote the signal observed at time
t
, leading to the observation of a sequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
To mitigate memory issues associated with large values of
T
, it is common practice to recursively update the covariance matrix
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
.
Upon the arrival of a new datum
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, common methods to update
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t-1}
include the rank-one correction

{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}=\frac{t-1}{t}{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t-1}+\frac{1}{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top},

(2)

tailored to stationary settings
[
20
]
, or the exponential decay

{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}=\gamma{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma){\mathbf{x}}_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top},

(3)

employed in non-stationary settings
[
11
]
, where
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the forgetting factor.
These types of updates are prevalent in online algorithms where (
2
) is solved on the fly
[
20
,
11
]
.

3
Learning expanding graphs

Figure 1
:
Evolution of
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
in the expanding graph setting.
Green edges and blocks of the GSO denote the connectivity of previous nodes in the network while purple indicates the connectivity of incoming nodes.
Starting at
t-1
, the initial GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
is an
N_{t-1}\times N_{t-1}
matrix.
At time
t
, a new signal
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{t}}
(yellow) arrives and a new node joins the network, enlarging the size of the graph. Similarly, two nodes arrive on time
t+1
and so on.

This work addresses the problem of learning the topology of a dynamic graph whose set of nodes increases with time.
The evolving topology can be represented by the sequence
\{{\mathcal{G}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
In the context of expanding graphs, new nodes join the network over time.
Denoting the incoming nodes at time
t
as
{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
, this leads to the node sets
{\mathcal{V}}_{t}={\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
, with
N_{t}\geq N_{t-1}
.
The growing dimensionality of the node set poses additional challenges when relating graphs and data across different time instants, a critical element in learning time-varying graphs.

Regarding the unknown topology of the dynamic graph,
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
is related to its GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
, represented by an
N_{t}\times N_{t}
matrix.
Since the nodes are added sequentially over time, each
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
exhibits the following block structure

{\mathbf{S}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}&[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1},{\mathcal{I}}_{t}}\\
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{I}}_{t},{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}&[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{I}}_{t}}\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

Here, the block
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{t-1}\times N_{t-1}}
encodes the connectivity among nodes present in the network at
t-1
.
Similarly, the blocks
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1},{\mathcal{I}}_{t}}
and
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{I}}_{t},{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
respectively denotes the connectivity between incoming and previously present nodes, and
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{I}}_{t}}
encodes the connectivity among incoming nodes.
This block structure allows us to relate GSOs across different time instants by directly comparing
{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
with the block
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
.
Moreover, it helps in capturing prior information about the connectivity involving new nodes.
For instance, if nodes in
{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
can only have edges with nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
we have
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{I}}_{t}}={\mathbf{0}}
.
Similarly, if the connectivity of nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
remains unchanged we have
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}={\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
.
The sequence of expanding graphs and the resulting block structure of
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
is depicted in
Fig.

1
.

With these in place, we formalize the problem at hand.

Problem 1

Given a sequence of graph signals
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, with
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{t}}
available at time
t
, our aim is to learn the dynamic topology encoded in the sequence of GSOs
\{{\mathbf{S}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, under the assumptions:

(AS1)

The set of nodes grows with time, i.e.,
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{t}
.

(AS2)

[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
are close in some distance
d(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Here,
(AS1)
captures the key characteristic of the expanding graph scenario by accounting for new nodes joining the network over time.

(AS2)
ensures that the connectivity between nodes already in the network (modeled at time
t
by the block
[{\mathbf{S}}_{t}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
) changes smoothly.
This assumption makes the topology inference problem tractable and ensures that the main discrepancies between
{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
are primarily due to the addition of new nodes
[
16
,
13
]
.

Given
Problem

1
, the time-varying network can be estimated at each time
t
in an offline manner by solving

{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t}=\;\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{S}},{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t})+\alpha d([{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}},{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t-1})+\lambda\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1}.

(5)

Similar to the static problem in (
1
), the term
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{S}},{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t})
represents a loss function of interest relating the graph topology and the signal (statistical) properties.
Note that the dimension of
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
increases with time, so the ensuing section introduces an update strategy tailored to expanding graphs.
Next, recalling that
[{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
encodes the topology related to nodes already present at time
t-1
,
d([{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}},{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}^{*})
captures any prior knowledge about the evolution of the graph with time.
For instance, if the weights of the edges change across time but the support remains fixed, a Frobenius penalty might be adequate.
In contrast, an
\ell_{1}
norm penalty might be preferred if changes only involve rewiring a few edges.
Finally, the set of constraints
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}
reflects that the dimension of
{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t}
varies with time.

Solving the offline problem (
5
) can provide a high-quality estimate
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}
.
Nonetheless, many delay-sensitive applications where new data arrives rapidly may not allow obtaining such an estimate since it may incur excessive computational costs.
In settings where changes in the topology of
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
take place with high frequency, it might not even be prudent to solve (
5
) with high precision.
Indeed, this poses a trade-off where sub-optimal and fast solutions may be preferred over optimal but time-consuming alternatives.
Motivated by these limitations, we introduce next an online low-complexity algorithm to solve
Problem

1
.

4
Online learning of expanding graphs

Online graph learning methods are well-suited for learning dynamic graphs from streaming data
[
19
,
21
]
.
Here, we first study how to update the sample covariance matrix and then introduce an algorithm to solve (
5
) online.

4.1
Expanding covariance update

When dealing with expanding graphs the dimensions of
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
and
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t-1}
may not match due to the arrival of new nodes, hence rendering classical updates invalid.
We leverage zero-padding to keep track of
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
in an expanding fashion.

For any matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{i\times i}
, let
\underline{{\mathbf{Z}}}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{j\times j}
be given by

\underline{{\mathbf{Z}}}^{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{Z}}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix},

(6)

with
j\geq i
and each
{\mathbf{0}}
being the all zero matrix of appropriate size.
Simply put,
\underline{{\mathbf{Z}}}^{(j)}
is a matrix of size
j\times j
constructed by zero-padding
{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Then, consider the update

{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}={\mathbf{M}}_{1}\circ\underline{{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}+{\mathbf{M}}_{2}\circ{\mathbf{x}}_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top},

(7)

where
\circ
denotes the entry-wise product, and the masks
{\mathbf{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma{\mathbf{1}}&\frac{\tilde{t}-1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}\\
\frac{\tilde{t}-1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}&\frac{\tilde{t}-1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\gamma){\mathbf{1}}&\frac{1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}\\
\frac{1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}&\frac{1}{\tilde{t}}{\mathbf{1}}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

with
\tilde{t}=t-\tau
,
\tau
being the last time instance that new nodes arrived, and the top left blocks of
{\mathbf{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{2}
being matrices of size
N_{\tau}\times N_{\tau}
.
Masks
{\mathbf{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{2}
help in using different update strategies for the blocks in
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
related to new nodes and the blocks related to previously existing nodes.
The motivation for this update is twofold.
First, it allows us to keep track of the changes in
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t-1}
due to the addition of new nodes via a dynamic update for the top left block of
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
.
At the same time, the temporal stationary update of the blocks corresponding to new nodes accounts for the lack of prior observations, thereby helping to gather sufficient data from these nodes faster.

4.2
Online algorithm

We now propose an online algorithm based on PPG descent, a well-established method for minimizing non-smooth functions such as the
\ell_{1}
norm present in our objective function
[
40
,
41
]
.
For simplicity, we assume that the distance metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is differentiable and group the smooth terms of the objective function in (
5
) as

f_{t}({\mathbf{S}})={\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{S}},{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t})+\alpha d([{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}},{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t-1}).

(9)

Nonetheless, non-differentiable distance functions can also be accommodated by modifying the proximal operator.

As summarized in Algorithm
1
, our iterative algorithm performs different steps at each time
t
.
These steps involve: i) updating
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
; ii) performing a PPG step; and iii) combining the estimate from
t-1
and the result of the second step.
This process is detailed next.

Step 1.

Upon receiving a new observation
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, we update the sample covariance
{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}
according to (
7
).

Step 2.

We take a PPG step with respect to the function (
9
), given by

{\mathbf{\check{S}}}_{t}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}}\left(T_{\eta\lambda}\left(\underline{{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}-\eta\nabla f(\underline{{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1})\right)\right).

(10)

The proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
norm is the soft-thresholding operator, which can be computed entry-wise as
T_{\lambda}(Z_{ij})=\max\{|Z_{ij}|-\lambda,0\}\text{sign}(Z_{ij})
for some matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}
. The scalar
\eta>0
represents the step size,
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}}
denotes the projection onto the feasible set
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}
,
{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t-1}
is the estimated GSO at the previous iteration, and
\underline{{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}
denotes its zero-padded version as defined in (
6
).
This zero-padding strategy ensures the dimension of
{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}
grows dynamically as new nodes arrive.

Step 3.

The estimate of the GSO at time
t
is given by the convex combination

{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}=h{\mathbf{\check{S}}}_{t}+(1-h)\underline{{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1},

(11)

controlled by the scalar
h\in(0,1]
.
Values of
h
close to 0 promote solutions that vary slowly with respect to the previous estimate, while
h=1
focuses exclusively on the estimate resulting from the proximal gradient step.
Analogous to the covariance update in (
7
), different values of
h
can be applied to blocks corresponding to nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
and in
{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
.

Algorithm
1
considers a single proximal gradient iteration per time instant, a design tailored to highly delay-sensitive applications.
Additional iterations can be performed if a more accurate estimate is preferred over obtaining a fast solution.
This application-dependent trade-off is determined by the frequency of which new nodes and data arrive and is studied in detail in
Section

6
.
Finally, note that the proposed algorithm considers a general loss function
{\mathcal{L}}
linking the data to the underlying topology, with the only requirement being its differentiability.
Consequently, we can use Algorithm
1
to learn the topology of expanding graphs for different graph-data priors, such as GMRF
[
42
]
, graph stationarity
[
3
]
, or structural equation models
[
43
]
, to name a few.
The following section studies the particular case where the observed signals follow a GMRF.

5
Online expanding Gaussian graphical model

In Gaussian graphical models, each observation
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{t}}
is sampled from a zero-mean GMRF, implying
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
adheres to the zero-mean multivariate Gaussian distribution with covariance
{\mathbf{C}}_{t}={\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}
, whose dimension increases with
t
due to the growing graph size.
Here, the graph topology, or equivalently, the inverse of the covariance matrix, is learned by maximizing the (regularized) log-likelihood of the observed data
[
42
]
.

The maximum likelihood estimator of the GSO at time
t
can be computed offline by solving

\displaystyle\!{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t}=

\displaystyle\;\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\;\;\text{tr}({\mathbf{S}}{\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t})-\log\det({\mathbf{S}}+\epsilon{\mathbf{I}})+\lambda\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1}

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\alpha d([{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}},{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.\;t.}\;{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}:=\{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}|{\mathbf{S}}\succeq 0,~\|{\mathbf{S}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma\}.\;\;

Similar to existing works on graph Laplacian Gaussian graphical models
[
44
]
, the term
\epsilon{\mathbf{I}}
for
\epsilon>0
in the determinant, allows us to obtain positive semi-definite GSOs, thus enhancing the practicality of our method.
The ability to estimate rank-deficient matrices allows for disconnected graph solutions.
Moving on to the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}
, the constraint
{\mathbf{S}}\succeq 0
ensures
{\mathbf{S}}
is a positive semidefinite matrix and
||{\mathbf{S}}||_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma
imposes bounded eigenvalues, as is typically the case in real-world networks.
This set also reflects the growing size of the graph.
The distance term
d(\cdot,\cdot)
captures
(AS2)
by modeling how the connectivity of nodes previously existing in the network evolves.
When no prior information about the relation between
[{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{t-1}
is available, it suffices to set
\alpha=0
.

We solve (
12
) online using Algorithm
1
.
Let
f_{t}({\mathbf{S}})
encompass all the terms in the objective except for the
\ell_{1}
norm, resulting in the gradient

\nabla f_{t}({\mathbf{S}})={\hat{\mathbf{C}}}_{t}-\left({\mathbf{S}}+\epsilon{\mathbf{I}}\right)^{-1}+\alpha\nabla d([{\mathbf{S}}]_{{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}},{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t-1}).

(13)

Since
d(\cdot,\cdot)
only depends on the entries of
{\mathbf{S}}
related to nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
, note that
\nabla d(\cdot,\cdot)
will be an
N_{t}\times N_{t}
matrix with zeros in the entries not related nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
.
It can be shown that
f_{t}({\mathbf{S}})
is strongly convex with constant
\sigma^{-1}
and its gradient is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\epsilon^{-2}
(given that
\nabla d
is also Lipschitz)
[
45
]
.
Therefore, we select the step size such that
\eta\leq\epsilon^{2}
.
The projection onto
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}
can be computed as

\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}}({\mathbf{S}})={\mathbf{V}}\min\left\{\max\left\{\bm{\Lambda},0\right\},\sigma^{1/2}\right\}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top},

(14)

where
{\mathbf{V}}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
denote the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{S}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{V}}\bm{\Lambda}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
.
With some abuse of notation, we let
\min\{\max\{\bm{\Lambda},0\},\sigma^{1/2}\}
represent the element-wise minimum and maximum operation on the entries of
\bm{\Lambda}
.
This operation projects the eigenvalues contained in the diagonal of
\bm{\Lambda}
into the interval
[0,\sigma^{1/2}]
.

Computational complexity.

From a computational standpoint, the complexity of each iteration of Algorithm
1
is dictated by the matrix inverse in (
13
) and the eigendecomposition in (
14
).
While over-the-shelf implementations of these operations incur a computational complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(N_{t}^{3})
, efficient implementations based on fast matrix multiplications can reduce the complexity to
{\mathcal{O}}(N_{t}^{2.4})

[
46
]
.
We stress that a complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(N_{t}^{2.4})
is considerably efficient since learning the graph topology involves
{\mathcal{O}}(N_{t}^{2})
variables.

5.1
Dynamic cumulative regret analysis

An important question in online graph learning is whether Algorithm
1
closely tracks the sequence of minimizes
\{{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
for large values of
T
.
To answer this question, we analyze the dynamic cumulative regret, defined as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}\|_{F}

[
47
]
.
The expanding graph setting makes our dynamic regret analysis substantially different from classical results in conventional online learning.
The following theorem establishes a bound for the dynamic cumulative regret of the sequence provided by Algorithm
1
for the Gaussian graphical models described in
Section

5
.

Theorem 1

Let
\{{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be the sequence of estimates procured by Algorithm
1
with
\nabla f_{t}({\mathbf{S}})
and
\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{t}}}({\mathbf{S}})
as given in (
13
) and (
14
), respectively.
Let
\{{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be the sequence of minimizers of (
12
).
Setting the step size as
\eta\leq\epsilon^{2}
, the dynamic cumulative regret is upper bounded by

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}\|_{F}\leq K_{1}+K_{2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\|{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}-\underline{{\mathbf{S}}^{*}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}\|_{F},

(15)

with
\underline{{\mathbf{S}}^{*}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}
being the
N_{t}\times N_{t}
zero-padded version of
{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t-1}
as defined in (
6
), and with constants

\displaystyle K_{1}=\frac{\|{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{1}-{\mathbf{S}}_{1}^{*}\|_{F}-\rho\|{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{T}-{\mathbf{S}}_{T}^{*}\|_{F}}{1-\rho},

\displaystyle K_{2}=\frac{1}{1-\rho},

(16)

where
0\leq\rho:=(1-h\eta/\sigma)^{1/2}<1
,
\sigma^{-1}
is the strong convexity constant of
f_{t}
, and
h\in(0,1]
.

The theorem is proven in Appendix A.

Theorem

1
asserts that the ability of Algorithm
1
to track the dynamic and expanding topology depends on the evolution of the optimal solution to (
12
), given by
\sum_{t=2}^{T}\|{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}-\underline{{\mathbf{S}}^{*}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}\|_{F}
. This is also known as the path length, which, in turn, reflects the variability of the underlying graph over time.
As
T\to\infty
,
Theorem

1
guarantees that if the sum of the variations in
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}
is sub-linear in time, then
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}||{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{S}}^{*}_{t}||_{F}\to 0
.
To provide additional intuition, consider a scenario where changes in the topology are solely due to the arrival of new nodes with binary edges, while the connectivity of nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}_{t-1}
remains unchanged.
Moreover, let
|{\mathcal{I}}_{t}|
and

respectively denote the number of incoming nodes and the maximum number of edges per new node.
Then, we have

\|{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{*}-\underline{{\mathbf{S}}^{\star}}^{(N_{t})}_{t-1}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2d_{\textnormal{max}}|{\mathcal{I}}_{t}|},

meaning that the cumulative regret is determined by the total number new edges added to the network.

7
Conclusion

We addressed the relevant problem of learning the topology of expanding graphs online from streaming data.
The arrival of new nodes poses additional modeling challenges that we overcame by exploiting the block structure of
{\mathbf{S}}_{t}
and zero padding.
After designing a covariance update tailored for the arrival of new nodes, we introduced a general online algorithm based on projected proximal gradient descent.
We specialized it to deal with GMRF observations and provided a bound for the dynamic cumulative regret that reflected the impact of incoming nodes.
Finally, we demonstrated its performance using controlled, financial, and COVID-19 data.
