Variability of the Behavioral Assessment Screening Tool for Mobile Health Using Ecological Momentary Assessment in Chronic Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Lauren Terhorst, Shannon B Juengst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the variability of self-reported behavioral symptoms over a 2-week period in 44 individuals with chronic traumatic brain injury (TBI) using subscale scores from the Behavioral Assessment Screening Tool for Mobile Health (BASTmHealth) collected in a prior feasibility study.DesignSecondary analysis of data collected from a prospective, high-frequency, in situ, repeated-measures design feasibility study of the BASTmHealth.SettingCommunity.ParticipantsForty-four (N=44) community-dwelling adults with a lifetime TBI history who completed at least 2 assessments. The sample had an average age of 43 years (SD, 14.4), consisted primarily of women (69.2%), and had an average time since the last TBI of 8.6 years (SD, 10).InterventionsNot applicable.Main Outcome MeasuresSelf-reported behavioral symptoms measured with the BASTmHealth.ResultsWe found that the Every Other Day Scheduled prompts captured the most between-day variability in self-reported behavioral symptoms compared with the Daily Random and Daily Scheduled prompts, while all 3 prompting schedules captured within-person variability over a 14-day period. We identified 3 clusters of symptom reporters, which we labeled psychiatric, cognitive, and steady symptoms.ConclusionsWe were able to capture and evaluate variability in self-reported emotional and behavioral symptoms within and between individuals. Understanding individual symptom fluctuations may guide and evaluate treatment interventions. Thus, our results indicate that BASTmHealth may be a valuable, evidence-based tool and clinical monitoring system for evaluating post-TBI symptoms in both clinical and pragmatic trials.KEYWORDS: Ecological momentary assessment, Experience sampling method, Measurement, mHealth, Neurobehavioral, Rehabilitation, Traumatic brain injury

Common behavioral and emotional symptoms after traumatic brain injury (TBI), particularly neurobehavioral symptoms like poor affect, cognitive challenges, and dysregulated behaviors that fluctuate considerably from day-to-day, may develop into psychiatric disorders (eg, depression) or adverse behavioral problems (eg, substance abuse).1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Measuring behavioral symptoms in clinic-based settings under controlled conditions may not generalize to complex everyday life situations, whereas assessment in naturalistic contexts provides a more realistic picture of day-to-day functioning. Understanding individual patterns and recognizing symptom trends could lead to early identification of emerging psychiatric disorders or problematic behaviors. The growing ubiquity of smartphones and other mobile technologies makes real time in situ data collection increasingly feasible,6 presenting opportunities to improve both clinical surveillance and clinical trial design.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) is a method for collecting repeated-measures of symptoms or behaviors in real time within an individual’s natural environment.7, 8, 9 EMA is an increasingly popular means of assessing a variety of constructs across diverse community-based populations because it provides a more comprehensive picture of symptoms and behaviors.7 By capturing experiences at specific, brief moments in time, EMA reduces reporting errors that can occur as a result of recall bias or memory impairments.7, 8, 9 EMA captures day-to-day fluctuations in emotions and behaviors, providing opportunities to account for natural within-person variability that may not otherwise be identified in retrospective reporting.10,11

Though still an emerging area of research, a few recent studies have reported that within-person measures of variability may be critical for understanding how to detect meaningful changes in ratings of the same symptom across temporally proximal time points. These studies also emphasized that clinical decisions should not be based on a single measurement and stressed the need to frequently monitor outcomes that may lead to rapid changes in individual variability.12, 13, 14 In studies of individuals with TBI, participants demonstrated significant within-person variability in behavioral symptoms, with greater variability associated with more frequent experiences of symptoms.15,16 Findings from these recent studies suggest that understanding individual symptom fluctuations may guide and evaluate treatment interventions for subsets of individuals, and that EMA may be effective at capturing meaningful change in the context of clinical trials. However, this remains speculative in the absence of further evaluation.

To capitalize on the methodological advantages of EMA for assessing symptom fluctuations, assessment tools are needed that translate effectively into high-frequency, in situ measurements. One such assessment is the Behavioral Assessment Screening Tool for Mobile Health (BASTmHealth), which captures neurobehavioral symptoms across 5 domains. BASTmHealth has shown preliminary evidence of validity and feasibility for collecting self-reported neurobehavioral symptoms among community-dwelling adults with TBI.17,18 The development of BASTmHealth, a short form of the original Behavioral Assessment Screening Tool (BAST),19,20 was a critical first step toward increasing the use of remote clinical monitoring systems to identify and improve post-TBI symptoms.

There are several design considerations for EMA studies, including the use of fixed vs random prompting schedules. A recent study used a factorial design to identify optimal EMA methodological considerations, revealing no significant differences between those receiving fixed vs random prompting schedules.21 A systematic review of strategies to evaluate mood and stress in adults with various medical diagnoses, including TBI, showed that across 12 studies, the EMA prompting schedule varied between random, stratified, semirandom, and fixed.22 No study has evaluated these different prompting schedules from the perspective of capturing within-person variability.

To better understand the clinical utility of characterizing within-person variability, the current study was a secondary analysis of a feasibility study that assessed self-reported behavioral symptom variability, as measured by BASTmHealth, in individuals with chronic TBI.17 This study aimed to explore differences in within-person variability across 3 prompting schedules over a 14-day period and to exploratorily identify distinct patterns in this variability, with a description of participant characteristics for each pattern.

Methods

Design and participants

We conducted a secondary analysis of data collected from a prospective, high-frequency, in situ, repeated-measures design BASTmHealth feasibility study.17 In 2020, BASTmHealth was administered over a 14-day period to adults with a lifetime history of TBI. All parent study activities were approved by University of Texas Southwestern’s Institutional Review Board (STU-2019-0742). Participants accessed a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) link to review study information and provided consent (e-consent) to participate. Upon consent, participants completed a set of electronic questionnaires to confirm a lifetime history of TBI and to capture basic demographic information.

Fifty-two participants with TBI were assigned via stratified block randomization in REDCap to a prompting schedule group. Prompting groups were time-based and informed by prior EMA studies.21, 22, 23 The prompting groups were: (1) Daily Random: once daily, at a random hour between 9 am and 8 pm; (2) Every Other Day Scheduled: once every other day at a scheduled time of their choosing; (3) Daily Scheduled: once daily at a scheduled time of their choosing.

Participants received a link via text message to electronically self-report their neurobehavioral symptoms (BASTmHealth) based on their assigned prompting group schedule. Participants received up to 2 reminders (1h apart each) if they had not completed the assessment.

Sample size considerations

In the parent study,17 52 individuals were randomized to prompting schedules, exceeding the recommended minimum (min) sample size for feasibility studies in the literature.24 The aim of our secondary analysis was to describe the variability of self-reported behavioral symptoms, and we also explored patterns of variability using cluster analysis to determine whether subgroups of variability existed within our sample. While there are no formal methods to assess sample size adequacy for cluster analysis, a recent methodological study reported statistical power for cluster sizes of 10-20, ranging from 77% to >90% when cluster separation is large.25

Measures

Baseline data included demographic information, TBI information (most severe lifetime TBI, number of lifetime TBIs, TBI with loss of consciousness [LOC], TBI before age 15y), and neurobehavioral symptoms. A modified version of the Ohio State University TBI Identification test26,27 was used to identify the presence and characteristics of lifetime TBI, and the original, previously validated 38-item BAST18,19 instrument measured self-reported baseline neurobehavioral symptoms across the 5 BAST domains. EMA was used over a 14-day period to collect symptom data using BASTmHealth.16,17 The 10-item BASTmHealth consists of 2 items that capture symptom frequency in the past 48 hours across the 5 BAST neurobehavioral symptom subscales: Negative Affect, Fatigue, Executive Dysfunction, Impulsivity, and Substance Misuse. Each item’s response scale ranges from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). The average response of the 2 items was used as the subscale score for each daily measure.

Data analysis

We characterized our sample using descriptive statistics of baseline measures. “Completers” retained for analysis were identified as participants who responded to more than 2 daily measures.28 Average measures were calculated based on the number of participant responses over the 14-day period. The data set and coding are available on Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/q4ckb/?view_only=e78fe8e9a6b445ff91156dc6238b106d.

To identify patterns in day-to-day variability, we computed the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) for each subscale within each prompting group. The RMSSD, a nonlinear method, captured day-to-day fluctuations in symptoms and provided a measure of average variability across the subscales over the 14-day assessment period, which took place between September, 2020 and December, 2020. Larger RMSSD values corresponded to greater variability from one day to the next within a particular subscale. The RMSSD was computed using the psych R package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, version 4.3.2).29,a Next, to measure within-person variability for each subscale, we used SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute)30,b to compute coefficients of variation (CoVs), which provide an individual estimate of within-person variability for each subscale. The CoV is not sensitive to symptom fluctuations and reflects the “trend” in within-person variability over the sampling period. We examined the distributions of the RMMSDs and CoVs using the Shapiro-Wilk test, histograms, and box plots. The Substance Misuse and Executive Dysfunction distributions were positively skewed; the others were normally distributed, with no outliers. Next, to determine whether variability differed among prompting groups, we conducted one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test using trend statistics (RMSSD, CoVs) as dependent variables and the prompting group as the independent variable. We also computed correlation coefficients (Pearson r or Spearman rs) to examine the relationships between each CoV and participant characteristics.

We explored whether subgroups of individuals with distinct variability patterns could be identified in our sample, irrespective of the sampling schedule. We performed k-means clustering using PROC FASTCLUS in SAS, with the CoV for each subscale. We ran the analysis with different maximum (max) cluster counts (n=2, 3, and 4) to find the best solution. To evaluate cluster quality, we computed silhouette scores using Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity measure. A silhouette score can range from −1 to 1, with higher values indicating that individuals fit their own cluster well and differ from one another.

Once clusters were formed, we labeled symptom variability patterns and examined the characteristics of individuals within each cluster. We used ANOVA (or the Kruskal-Wallis test) and the Fisher exact test to examine cluster differences. For post hoc pairwise comparisons, we used the Bonferroni correction to adjust the alpha level.

Several characteristics were associated with CoVs. Executive Dysfunction variation was significantly correlated with the total number of lifetime TBIs with LOC (rs=0.30, p=.05); Fatigue variation was significantly correlated with total lifetime TBIs (r=−0.31, p=.04); and Substance Misuse variation was significantly correlated with having a TBI before the age of 15 years (rs=0.43, p=.01). There were also significant associations between the original BAST baseline subscale scores and the corresponding BASTmHealth CoVs for Negative Affect (r=0.31, p=.04) and Substance Misuse (rs=0.43, p=.01), and between the original BAST baseline Fatigue and the BASTmHealth Fatigue CoVs (r=−0.32, p=.03).

The cluster analysis results indicated that a 3-cluster solution provided the best fit, with an R2 of 0.39. Evaluation of clustering quality revealed that cluster 1 had a mean silhouette score of 0.317 (min=0.099, max=0.490), cluster 2 had a mean silhouette score of 0.179 (min=0.002, max=0.337), and cluster 3 had a mean silhouette score of 0.403 (min=0.185, max=0.535). Across the entire sample, the mean silhouette score was 0.302, indicating a moderate level of cluster cohesion and separation. These results suggest that individuals in clusters 1 and 3 were more appropriately grouped than those in cluster 2, although all clusters provided reasonable distinction between individuals within and between clusters.

Table 4 reports the cluster labels and characteristics of the 3-cluster solution. Cluster 1 (n=10), Psychiatric Symptom Reporters (Psychiatric), had the highest variability in Negative Affect and Substance Misuse, while cluster 2 (n=16), Cognitive Symptom Reporters (Cognitive), had the highest variability in Executive Dysfunction, Fatigue, and Impulsivity. Cluster 3 (n=18), Stable Symptoms Reporters (Stable), had the lowest variability across all 5 subscales. While there were no significant mean differences (MD) in participant characteristics across clusters, we observed significant differences in the CoVs for Executive Dysfunction (Cognitive vs Stable: MD=8.3, p=.026), Fatigue (Psychiatric vs Cognitive: MD=13.63, p=.001; Cognitive vs Stable: MD=19.34, p<.001), Impulsivity (Cognitive vs Stable: MD=11.24, p=.006), and Substance Misuse (Psychiatric vs Cognitive: MD=26.45, p<.001; Psychiatric vs Stable: MD=27.10, p<.001). We also found significant cluster differences in baseline Substance Misuse (Psychiatric vs Cognitive: MD=0.392, p=.015).

Discussion

Our study investigated day-to-day variability and within-person fluctuations in neurobehavioral symptom reporting among individuals with chronic TBI who were randomized to 3 prompting schedules. We found that all prompting schedules were able to capture fluctuations in individual neurobehavioral symptoms over the study period, with no statistically significant differences. This provides further evidence that the choice of prompting schedule may not influence results, as a prior study21 found no significant differences in adherence rates between fixed and random prompting schedules.

We found the most within-person variability in Negative Affect and Impulsivity across all 3 prompting schedules, and the least variability in Substance Misuse. Of note, 30 of the 44 participants showed no variability in Substance Misuse reporting over the study period, which explains the low overall variability in the sample. Unlike the other subscales that cover a range of symptoms that many people experience with fluctuating frequency, the Substance Misuse subscale items focus on symptoms that are typically present in the context of substance misuse. We also found that Substance Misuse variability was positively associated with having a TBI before the age of 15 years. This mirrors several prior studies indicating that those with childhood TBI are more likely to engage in risky substance use in adulthood.31, 32, 33, 34

A prior study of factors associated with greater within-person variability in affective symptoms after a TBI found that those with more overall symptoms also exhibited greater variability.15 This is consistent with the current findings, which show that Negative Affect had the highest variability and that BASTmHealth Negative Affect variability and baseline BAST Negative Affect were positively correlated, ie, participants with higher baseline symptom reporting experienced greater within-person variability in Negative Affect. Baseline Impulsivity scores were generally low, so higher within-person variability scores suggest that symptoms on this subscale may be more intermittent from day-to-day. We found that participants who experienced TBI with LOC were more likely to exhibit greater variability in Executive Dysfunction, though we did not find a significant association between baseline Executive Dysfunction and variability, as in a prior study.10 We could speculate that those with a history of TBI with LOC are more prone to Executive Dysfunction in day-to-day life, but this requires further study to best understand how and when cognitive strategies may be beneficial.

The BASTmHealth Fatigue subscale was negatively associated with baseline BAST Fatigue, indicating that those reporting less fatigue at baseline experienced greater variability in fatigue over days, whereas those reporting more fatigue at baseline remained stable over days. This suggests that those with greater fatigue at baseline were less variable in their reporting because their fatigue symptoms were persistently high, whereas those with less fatigue at baseline could still have days when they experienced fatigue, though less frequently overall. We found that participants with more total lifetime TBIs had less variability in Fatigue, consistent with findings showing that a greater number of lifetime mild TBIs is associated with more postconcussive symptoms.35 Further work is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms behind this phenomenon and how injury severity may contribute to this relationship.

Our study provides proof of concept that there is natural variability in symptom reporting among individuals with TBI, and that this variability is potentially meaningfully associated36 with clinical factors. BASTmHealth scores over a short period of time could provide a more comprehensive picture of a person’s overall functioning, and variability in a patient’s symptom reporting could inform how confident a clinician should be in the accuracy of a subsequent single-timepoint assessment (eg, a single assessment for a highly variable symptom reporter may be more questionable than a single assessment for a more stable symptom reporter). This aligns with prior research supporting frequent monitoring of outcomes that may change rapidly because of individual variability.14

Using EMA for emotional or behavioral outcomes may provide a better measure in the context of intervention trials than single-timepoint assessments, as it may better indicate clinically meaningful change at the individual level and be less prone to missing or falsely detecting a treatment response. For example, a very small change might be meaningful for a person with low symptom variability, whereas the same change could fall within the variability range for a person with high symptom variability. Additionally, more complex modeling using “lagged” observations may provide more context about individuals’ symptom reporting from one time point to the next.37,38

The BAST conceptual model emphasized the interrelationships among neurobehavioral symptom domains and the need to measure them together to best understand how to intervene.39 To explore how variability in symptom reporting across all 5 domains was interrelated, we identified 3 clusters of symptom reporters based on the stability and nature of symptoms reported. If replicated in larger studies, these clusters could provide insight into personalized interventions, such as behavioral interventions to promote positive coping or cognitive strategy training.

Study limitations

Although our study was not without limitations, it provides scientific value by demonstrating that within- and between-person variability in neurobehavioral symptoms among individuals with TBI can be captured using a validated instrument, the BASTmHealth, which can be used to inform intervention efficacy and effectiveness. A limitation of our analysis was that the sample was homogeneous, consisting primarily of women with postsecondary education. This limits the generalizability of our findings and suggests that self-reported variability in emotional and behavioral symptoms should be evaluated in a larger, more diverse sample. Although the small sample size is also a concern, particularly for the cluster analysis results, we did find moderate levels of separation between clusters. The information provided in this study on the 3 emotional and behavioral profiles could be used to generate hypotheses for larger studies of more diverse samples of individuals with TBI.

Conclusions

Rapid advances in mobile technology offer new opportunities for effective EMA among community-dwelling individuals with chronic conditions. Mobile technology use in chronic TBI, particularly in community-based assessment, is an emerging field, with growing evidence that it is a feasible approach in this population.39, 40, 41, 42, 43 Our study contributes to the field by providing evidence that variability in neurobehavioral symptoms can be captured using BASTmHealth and evaluated within and between persons. This information may be valuable for assessing the efficacy and effectiveness of interventions. Leveraging the possibilities inherent in mobile health technology (mHealth) can improve access to and delivery of health care, but the success of mHealth depends on rigorous research to develop and validate every aspect of an mHealth system.
