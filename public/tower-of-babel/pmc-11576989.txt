Association between proton pump inhibitors and dementia risk: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Kexin Xie, Jing Li, Chengwei Tang, Zhiyin Huang, Ming Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Numerous observational studies suggest associations between proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and dementia, but causal relationships remain uncertain. Using large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, we performed univariable Mendelian randomization (UVMR) analysis to assess the causality between five PPI types, and all-cause dementia and its five subtypes. Confounders were controlled through multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis to isolate PPIs’ direct effects on dementia. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments, and leave-one-out analysis, were conducted to validate the robustness of our results. Initial UVMR estimates suggested that lansoprazole (odds ratio [OR] 1.291; 95%confidence interval [CI] 1.001–1.665; p = 0.049) and pantoprazole (OR 1.118; 95% CI 1.014–1.233; p = 0.025) potentially increased VD risk, with their respective direct associations also discovered in MVMR. Additionally, FTD was found to reversely increase rabeprazole use (OR 1.086; 95% CI 1.011–1.167; p = 0.023). However, after adjustment for the false discovery rate (FDR), none of these associations remained statistically significant (pFDR> 0.05). The robustness of our results is supported by consistent estimates across complementary MR methods and the absence of pleiotropy. Our study indicates no robust causality between PPI use and increased dementia risk. Thus, it is inappropriate to restrict clinically justified PPI prescriptions merely due to potential cognitive risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79821-1.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are pivotal in the treatment of upper gastrointestinal diseases and in the prevention of drug-induced ulcers and bleeding, as they reduce excessive acid production1–4. Their proven efficacy and favorable tolerability profile have led to widespread use5, particularly in the elderly6, making PPIs among the most commonly prescribed medications worldwide7,8. However, the increase in PPI prescriptions often exceeds the actual increase in the prevalence of gastrointestinal disease, indicating a significant number of inappropriate prescriptions5,9. In both primary and secondary care, almost half of all PPI prescriptions lack a valid therapeutic rationale5,6. This trend is exacerbated by the rebound acid hypersecretion that occurs 12 to 24 h after therapy, resulting in reluctance to discontinue treatment and prolonged use, particularly in older adults3,9,10. Indiscriminate use of PPIs represents a significant economic burden and raises concerns about potential adverse effects with long-term use2–4,7,9–11.

Previous pharmacoepidemiologic studies have indicated a possible association between PPIs and an increased risk of dementia4–6,8,11, particularly in older people, who are already at higher risk of dementia and have easier access to PPIs7,9. These studies consistently suggest that PPI users have a higher risk of developing dementia compared to non-users5,6,11. However, some recent research suggests that PPI use is not associated with the development of dementia in older people3,7,8,12–14, and even a few studies even suggest they have a preventive effect on dementia12,15,16. Given the complexity of dementia, which affects around 55 million people worldwide and has an annual economic cost of over a trillion dollars, it is crucial to understand these findings thoroughly5,17.

This debate also extends to the biological mechanisms that may link PPIs to dementia, including their role in amyloid-β accumulation18, disruption of cholinergic pathways19, facilitation of N-terminal oligomerization of NOTCH320, and impairment of vitamin B12 absorption4, as well as reports of their neuroprotective properties through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions15. This ongoing controversy is further complicated by confounding factors typical of observational studies5,21,22, such as co-medication, demographic variability, and differences in study design that make causal conclusions difficult. Therefore, rigorous research is essential to determine the safety and efficacy of PPI use and provide a reference for clinical practice.

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants from genome-wide association studies as instrumental variables (IVs) to establish causal relationships between exposures and outcomes23–25. By using the random assignment of alleles, MR minimizes the confounding and reverse causality inherent to observational studies23,26–28 and provides a robust alternative to randomized controlled trials without ethical, logistical, and financial obstacles28. Therefore, MR is of great value in the rigorous assessment of drug safety. In this context, we conducted univariable MR (UVMR) and multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis to explore the causal associations between five PPI types and all-cause dementia and its five subtypes, controlling for twenty-six confounding factors. Our study relied on extensive genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from large, diverse cohorts, which greatly improved the robustness and reliability of our results. To further ensure the robustness of our results, extensive sensitivity analyses were performed, including assessments of pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and leave-one-out analysis.

Methods

Study design

To investigate whether the use of five common PPIs causally affects the risk of all-cause dementia and its five subtypes, we performed two-sample UVMR and MVMR analyses (Fig. 1). First, the UVMR analysis assessed the causal relationships between these PPIs and the dementia types. Then, a bidirectional UVMR analysis was performed to examine the effects of PPI indications on dementia risk. Finally, the MVMR analysis was adjusted for the indications of PPI use and various dementia-related confounders to isolate the direct effects of PPIs on dementia. The IVs for these analyses met three main criteria23: They were associated with exposure, not associated with confounding factors, and only influenced the outcome by exposure. All genetic data were obtained from publicly available GWAS datasets, so no additional ethical approvals or participant informed consent were required.

Data sources

PPIs and dementia

To ensure the accuracy of our analyses and avoid overlap between participants, we drew the GWAS summary statistics from separate cohorts. For PPIs, we used data from the UK Biobank (UKB), including case-control statistics for omeprazole (26,869/429,407), lansoprazole (16,241/440,035), pantoprazole (807/455,469), esomeprazole (1,520/454,756), and rabeprazole (824/455,452)29. For the dementia subtypes, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were extracted from the FinnGen (R9) database, which included all-cause dementia (17,901/359,376), Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (5,422/356,660), vascular dementia (VD) (2,335/360,778), Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD) (534/170,667), frontotemporal dementia (FTD) (111/360,134), and dementia in other diseases (1,157/356,660)30. The genetic associations were obtained from regression adjusted for age, sex, and genetic principal components. Table 1 provides detailed data sources for this MR analysis.

Adjustment factors

MVMR analysis was conducted to assess the direct effect of PPIs on dementia risk, controlling for indications for PPI use—specifically gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)—and several potential confounders that may influence the occurrence of dementia3,4,7,22,31,32. These factors now encompass parental history of Alzheimer’s dementia; six commonly used concomitant medications (cholesterol-lowering drugs, agents acting on the renin-angiotensin system, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, opioids, and vitamin B12 supplementation); two cardiovascular conditions (myocardial infarction and hypertension), as well as associated indices, specifically triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. Additionally, we controlled for educational attainment, physical activity, frequency of social contacts, loneliness, depression, obesity, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, hip circumference, and hearing loss. Detailed descriptions of each adjustment factor are provided in Table S1.

UVMR analysis

In our MR study, IVs were selected strictly based on robust associations with exposure (p < 5 × 10−6), lack of linkage disequilibrium (R2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb range), and no direct correlation with outcome. To refine IV selection, the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) test was used to identify and correct for outliers indicative of horizontal pleiotropy (p < 0.05)33. Steiger filtering ensured that only variants explaining more variance in the exposure than in the outcome were retained as ‘TRUE’ and otherwise discarded as ‘FALSE’34, effectively minimizing potential reverse causality bias. Additionally, IVs associated with GERD (“rs4676893”, “rs2523589”) and aspirin (“rs2523589”) were excluded to explore the true effect of PPIs on dementia, as identified in the GWAS catalog database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/home). IVs without sufficient statistical strength (F-statistic < 10), calculated as (beta/SE) ², were also excluded to ensure robust instrument reliability35.

Before MR analysis, the exposure and outcome datasets were harmonized by aligning effect alleles on the forward strand and excluding palindromic variants to avoid alignment errors. Our primary method, inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR36, synthesizes SNP-specific estimates from Wald ratios, assuming that SNPs do not exhibit directional pleiotropic effects36. To strengthen the robustness of our results, several complementary methods were included: weighted median37, MR-Egger38, and MR-PRESSO test33. The weighted median estimator is reliable if more than 50% of IVs are valid37. We applied MR-Egger to account for directional horizontal pleiotropy, although this results in wider confidence intervals (CIs) and lower statistical efficiency38. The MR-PRESSO test was also used to detect significant outliers and correct for horizontal pleiotropy effects by excluding them33. Based on previous findings that support the robustness of the MR-APSS method across varied IV selection thresholds39, we have applied an additional analysis with the MR-APSS method40. This analysis was necessary as we had to relax the p-value threshold (p < 5 × 10−6) for IVs given the insufficient number of SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8) associated with our exposure variable.

MVMR analysis

MVMR extends traditional MR by using IVs associated with both primary and secondary exposure, the latter being referred to as adjustment factors. This method isolates the direct effect of the primary exposure on the outcome41–43. We performed MVMR to determine the direct causal effects of PPIs on dementia, adjusting for several potential confounders which are listed in Table S1. Similar to UVMR, the IVW method was used as the primary approach for the MVMR analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted a comprehensive series of sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity was first assessed using Cochran’s Q-test; if heterogeneity was significant (Pheterogeneity < 0.05), a random-effects IVW model was used, whereas in its absence, a fixed-effects IVW model was applied44. Horizontal pleiotropy was examined using MR-Egger regression intercepts, with a significant deviation from zero indicating pleiotropy38. Then, a leave-one-out strategy was applied, where each SNP was removed in turn to assess its impact on the aggregate estimates45. Next, we calculated the statistical power of this MR study46. Moreover, we have enhanced the validation of our MR results by conducting a genetic correlation analysis using LD score regression (LDSC)47. This methodology, applied in a recent Alzheimer’s disease study48, strengthens our approach by assessing the genetic correlation between PPI use and dementia risk. Finally, adjustments for the false discovery rate (FDR) were carefully made to ensure the reliability of statistical significance statements49. All analyses were performed using R packages ‘TwoSampleMR’, ‘MRPRESSO’, ‘MendelianRandomization’, ‘ldscr’, and ‘MVMR’ in R version 4.3.0.

Result

UVMR analysis

Our UVMR analysis examines the causal relationships between the use of five PPIs, two indications for PPI use, and all-cause dementia and its five subtypes. We analyzed between 4 and 49 SNPs, detailed in Tables S2-S5. Additionally, the F-statistics consistently exceeded 10 across all traits analyzed (Table S6-S9), indicating the absence of potential weak instrumental bias.

Forward UVMR analysis

Initially, IVW estimates suggested associations for lansoprazole and pantoprazole as potential risk factors for VD, with odds ratios (ORs) of 1.291 (95% CI 1.001–1.665; p = 0.049; pFDR = 0.645) and 1.118 (95% CI 1.014–1.233; P = 0.025; pFDR = 0.572), respectively (Fig. 2, Table S6). However, these associations were not statistically significant after FDR adjustment, indicating no robust causal relationships (pFDR > 0.05). Additionally, the use of lansoprazole or pantoprazole was not associated with the risk of other dementia types, including AD, PDD, FTD, and other classified dementias. Similarly, no significant causal relationships were found for the other PPIs—omeprazole, esomeprazole, and rabeprazole—with any dementia categories. All findings were free of pleiotropy (Ppleiotropy > 0.05) and heterogeneity (Pheterogeneity > 0.05) and were consistent with results from the other three additional MR approaches (Fig. 2, Table S6). The results from the MR-APSS method are aligned with other MR methods and reinforce the robustness of our findings, as shown in Table S10. Leave-one-out analysis (Figure S1) and scatter plots (Figure S2), further reinforced the stability and reliability of our findings. Upon conducting the LDSC analysis, we found no significant genetic correlation between PPI use and dementia risk (Table S11). This finding reinforces the primary conclusions drawn from our MR analysis–the absence of a causal link between PPI use and dementia.

Evaluating the impact of GERD and NSAIDs on dementia (Figure S3, Table S7), the initial analysis indicated that NSAIDs might reduce the risk of dementia associated with diseases classified elsewhere (OR 0.652; 95% CI 0.442–0.961; p = 0.031; pFDR = 0.572). However, it was not statistically significant anymore after FDR correction (pFDR > 0.05). This finding was consistently supported by three supplementary MR methods. No other significant causal links were found between NSAID usage or GERD and the incidence of any examined dementia types. MR-Egger intercept analysis confirmed the absence of pleiotropy (Ppleiotropy > 0.05), reinforcing the stability and reliability of our results (Figure S3, Table S7). Validation through leave-one-out analysis and scatter plots corroborated these findings (Figures S4-S5).

Reverse UVMR analysis

In reverse UVMR analysis investigating the link between various dementia types and PPI use, preliminary findings suggested a potential increase in rabeprazole use among FTD patients with IVW OR of 1.086 (95% CI 1.011–1.167; p = 0.023; pFDR = 0.572) (Table S8). However, this association did not persist after FDR adjustments (pFDR > 0.05). This outcome was corroborated by three alternative MR approaches. Additionally, no significant relationships were detected between FTD and other PPI types, nor between other dementia types and any PPI types. The robustness of these results is further supported by the absence of horizontal pleiotropy (Ppleiotropy > 0.05) and heterogeneity (Pheterogeneity > 0.05). Leave-one-out analysis and visual inspection of scatter plots further supported the reliability of these findings (Figures S6-S7).

Our UVMR analysis of dementia with common indications for PPI therapy initially revealed mild associations. Specifically, VD (OR 1.033, 95%CI 1.005–1.061, p = 0.020; pFDR = 0.572), FTD (OR 0.989, 95%CI 0.978–0.999, p = 0.037; pFDR = 0.577), and other classified dementia (OR 0.964; 95%CI 0.931–0.997; p = 0.035; pFDR = 0.572) showed slight associations with the use of NSAIDs, and VD with decreased GERD risk (OR 0.971, 95%CI 0.944–0.998, p = 0.036; pFDR = 0.572). However, these associations did not remain significant after FDR adjustment (pFDR > 0.05). Our results are robust and free from pleiotropy. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and scatter plots (Figures S8-S9) validated these findings.

MVMR analysis

Our MVMR analysis evaluated the direct causal effects of five PPIs on all-cause dementia and its five subtypes (Fig. 3, Table S12). Initially, multivariable inverse-variance weighted (MV-IVW) estimates indicated suggestive associations of pantoprazole and lansoprazole with VD, consistent with UVMR results. Specifically, pantoprazole was associated with a slightly increased risk of VD, even after controlling for confounders such as hypertension (OR 1.118; 95%CI 1.014–1.233; p = 0.025; pFDR = 1.000), opioid use (OR 1.115; 95%CI 1.012–1.230; p = 0.028; pFDR = 1.000), and parental Alzheimer’s history (father: OR 1.114; 95%CI 1.010–1.228; p = 0.030; pFDR = 1.000; mother: OR 1.143; 95%CI 1.007–1.298; p = 0.039; pFDR = 1.000). Similarly, lansoprazole showed an increased risk for VD, adjusted for GERD (OR 1.340; 95%CI 1.058–1.698; p = 0.015; pFDR = 1.000). However, these associations dissipated following correction for multiple testing (PFDR > 0.05). Moreover, several sub-analyses of omeprazole and esomeprazole revealed a similar situation (Fig. 3). But these associations dissipated following correction for multiple testing (PFDR > 0.05) and were further supported by consistent estimates across complementary MR methods (Table S12). Thus, no robust direct causal links between PPIs and dementia risk were identified.

Discussion

For the first time, MR analysis was used to explore the causal nexus between PPI use and dementia risk. Both UVMR and MVMR analyses provided no strong genetic evidence linking specific PPIs to dementia across different subtypes. Initially, there was a suggested association between pantoprazole and lansoprazole use and an increased risk of VD. However, these associations were not significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons using the FDR method. Similarly, no significant reverse causality was observed. These results contrast with earlier observational studies5,6,11,12,15,16 but are consistent with other clinical research3,7,8,12–14.

Concerns about PPIs’ potentially increasing dementia risk initially stemmed from pharmacoepidemiological studies in Germany6,11 and cohort data from Denmark5. However, these observational studies only show correlation, not causation6,11. Their reliance on claims data6 and healthcare registers5, along with the failure to account for the lag time between PPI use and dementia onset6,11, raises concerns about data accuracy and introduces confounding factors like common indications and concomitant medications. Limited experimental data from cellular and animal models suggest that specific PPIs, such as lansoprazole, could induce amyloid-β accumulation by modulating γ-secretase and β-secretase activity18. However, these findings may not apply to humans due to significant interspecies differences. Additionally, recent studies suggest PPIs’ electrophilic properties might promote NOTCH3 oligomerization, impairing cognition. Still, these findings are constrained by small sample sizes and non-rigorous control selection20. Furthermore, it was hypothesized that PPIs could reduce vitamin B12 absorption, contributing to dementia onset4. However, our findings, adjusted for vitamin B12 supplementation, showed no strong causal link between PPI use and any form of dementia after FDR correction. Our study drew on extensive GWAS summary statistics from large cohorts, encompassing over 500,000 individuals, significantly enhancing the robustness and reliability of our findings. These large sample sizes and rigorous methods, including MR, allowed us to control for confounding factors effectively. Moreover, well-designed, human-based studies are essential to clarify the relationship between PPI use and dementia risk and to elucidate potential biological mechanisms.

Contrary to earlier suggestions of harm, some studies propose that PPIs might possess neuroprotective effects12,15,16, potentially due to their anti-cholinesterase, antioxidative, and anti-inflammatory properties15. A notable longitudinal study suggests that PPIs may protect against cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease12. However, like earlier research11, this study’s reliance on self-reported PPI usage introduces potential misclassification biases12, which may affect the findings. Nevertheless, a robust German population-based case-control study using prescription data confirms similar protective outcomes16. Despite these observational findings, experimental and mechanistic studies on the specific effects of different PPIs on dementia subtypes are limited and inconsistent4,15,18,20. It is also crucial to specify PPI categories and dementia types due to their distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and varying incidences50. This underscores the urgent need for more meticulously designed studies to investigate these links further and refine clinical guidelines.

In contrast to these mixed findings, a landmark prospective, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that pantoprazole is safe for prolonged use, with a slight increase in enteric infections8. Extending this finding, our UVMR results reveal that pantoprazole, omeprazole, lansoprazole, esomeprazole, and rabeprazole show no significant causal link with dementia risk across six categories after FDR correction. This conclusion holds true even after adjusting for PPI indications and twenty-four dementia-linked confounders, which are overlooked in previous observational studies showing positive associations5,6,12. Furthermore, concerns about potential reverse causality, where dementia might lead to inappropriate PPI prescription9, especially in elderly populations at higher risk for dementia and related brain pathologies51, risk for dementia and related brain pathologies. Initially, we found a slight increase in FTD risk associated with increased rabeprazole usage, but this was not significant after FDR correction. A Finnish nested case-control study corroborates our results, finding no correlation between PPI use and Alzheimer’s disease after accounting for potential reverse causality with lag periods of 3 to 5 years13. Similarly, a post hoc analysis of the international multicenter RCT, “Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly”, reaffirmed the cognitive safety of PPI use over six years7. Additional support comes from two prospective cohort studies3,14, which meticulously controlled for educational attainment, health characteristics, genetic predispositions, and shared environmental factors—elements previously neglected in epidemiological studies of cognitive decline6. Collectively, this MR analysis indicates no significant causal links between PPI usage and dementia onset, supported by multiple pieces of high-quality evidence3,7,8,12–14.

Our study has multiple strengths. Firstly, utilizing bidirectional UVMR analysis effectively overcomes the limitations of traditional observational studies23,26–28, such as susceptibility to confounders and reverse causation, providing robust causality evidence in scenarios where RCTs are impractical or unethical. Secondly, unlike prior research5,6,11, our study categorizes and examines associations between five PPIs and six dementia classifications, considering the diversity in their pathophysiological mechanisms and incidence rates22. Thirdly, the credibility of our study is strengthened through multiple MR methods and extensive sensitivity analyses, further solidified by using clinically verified dementia diagnoses and minimizing sample overlap in data sources to reduce weak instrument bias. Lastly, we conducted bidirectional UVMR to independently evaluate the associations between GERD, NSAIDs, and dementia, alongside MVMR to adjust for two PPI indications and twenty-four dementia-related confounders.

Nonetheless, our study has certain limitations. Firstly, the reliance on participants of exclusively European ancestry limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities and regions, given the variable prevalence of dementia globally22. Secondly, the lack of individual-level data precludes stratified analysis for potential confounders such as age6,22, gender22,32, histological stages22, and duration and dosage of PPI use, which is crucial given the demographic disparities22,32 in dementia incidence and potential dose-dependent risk changes associated with prescription PPI use. Thirdly, due to potential deviations in the demographic characteristics of the database participants from those of the general population, our conclusions should be cautiously extended to the general population. Nevertheless, the large magnitude, diversity, and similar minor allele frequencies (Table S13) of the biobank enable these findings to offer insights applicable to the broader European population 30,52. Fourthly, it is imperative to recognize that statistical power may be inadequate in the analysis of certain subtypes, such as omeprazole to FTD, etc. (Table S14). Especially in PDD and FTD, where case numbers are notably limited, further investigations with larger sample sizes are necessary to draw robust conclusions. Fifthly, in consideration of the limited number of SNPs and consistent with prior studies53–55, we appropriately relaxed the p-value threshold for IV selection to account for the fewer SNPs associated with exposure. This adjustment, while necessary, might reduce the strength of the association between genetic variation and exposure, potentially making these IVs suboptimal proxies. However, to ensure reliable causal estimates, we conducted supplementary analyses using the MR-APSS method, which is validated for robustness across various IV selection thresholds39. The MR-APSS results corroborate the findings observed in other MR methods, supporting the consistency and reliability of our conclusions. Lastly, pleiotropy is an inherent concern in MR studies. However, after using methods such as the MR-PRESSO test and Steiger filtering to eliminate pleiotropic SNPs, no horizontal pleiotropy was found by the MR–Egger intercept test in our UVMR and MVMR. Adjustments for twenty-six confounding factors in MVMR, along with consistent findings across supplementary MR methods and comprehensive sensitivity analyses, solidify the credibility of our conclusions.

Conclusion

Our study utilized large-scale bidirectional UVMR and MVMR analyses, effectively overcoming the limitations of traditional observational studies, such as confounding factors and reverse causation. The results showed no significant causal link between the use of five common PPIs (omeprazole, lansoprazole, esomeprazole, rabeprazole, and pantoprazole) and six types of dementia. This conclusion was further validated through multiple MR methods and extensive sensitivity analyses, eliminating concerns about weak instrument bias and horizontal pleiotropy. These results support the justification for the use of PPIs regarding cognitive health. Future research should include a more diverse population, different age groups, and individual-level data to further validate and expand these findings.
