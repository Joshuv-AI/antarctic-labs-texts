Manual therapy treatment mechanisms are complex: Challenges and a call to action
Authors: Chad E Cook, Damian Keter, Vitaly Napadow, Mary F Barbe, Stephen M Perle, Jennifer Bent, Kenneth Learman, William R Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment mechanisms reflect the underlying processes or pathways through which a treatment or intervention produces its effects on an outcome. For force-based manipulations (which include manual therapies), treatment mechanisms have been measured in multiple animal and preclinical studies. In this commentary, we argue that measuring treatment mechanisms of force-based manipulation is highly complex, most notably because these mechanisms: 1) frequently interact locally and regionally with unmeasured variables; 2) are influenced by time, clinician, environments, and a litany of other mediators; and 3) are likely variable because people respond differently to all interventions. For each challenge, we provide recommendations on how to improve study design methodology and clarity of inference. Lastly, we provide a “Call for Action” to improve manual therapy mechanistic research, because studying the mechanisms of force-based manipulations is critical for advancing both scientific knowledge and clinical practice.Keywords: Manual therapy, Physical therapy, Mechanisms, Spine manipulation musculoskeletal, manipulations

Background

Mechanism’ is a broad term used to describe the molecular, cellular, biomechanical, or whole systems processes that: a) contribute to disease development, progression, and manifestation (e.g., disease or pathological mechanisms); b) explain how therapeutic interventions exert beneficial effects on a disease or condition (e.g., treatment mechanism); or c) are involved in the pain transmission, perception, and modulation within the nervous system (sometimes referred to as ‘pain mechanisms’) (Table 1).1 Investigations of treatment mechanisms have generated much interest of late, because they have the potential to be used to tailor therapies to individual patients, potentially unlocking a whole new level of precision, innovation, and safety in medicine.2

In the realm of force-based manipulations (FBM) (which includes the heterogenous categories of manipulation, mobilization, massage, acupuncture, osteopathic treatments, and more), our team has studied potential treatment mechanisms inovled in animal or preclinical research,3 has categorized mechanisms types,1 and has been involved in a comprehensive summary of study quality and bias.3 Our work and that of others has reinforced that uncertainty remains as to ‘how’ FBM interventions work to produce positive clinical outcomes. In this commentary, we outline an interpretive perspective of four complexities with FBM treatment mechanisms and measurements and present a call to action for future investigations. To reduce heterogeneity, we will focus on thrust and non-thrust manipulation and soft tissue manipulation / massage. The findings are not to be misconstrued as a review of FBM mechanisms, but rather a list of challenges we’ve encountered. While each challenge is presented separately to facilitate clarity and analytical depth, each are likely interdependent.

Challenge 1-Treatment mechanisms are interrelated

To simplify their summary of FBM mechanisms, Keter and colleagues3 grouped treatment mechanisms into eight distinct subcategories: biomechanical, neurological, neuroimmune, neurovascular, neurotransmitter/peptide, neuroendocrine, neuromuscular, and a general “other” category. Each subcategory (i.e., biomechanical, neurological, etc.) encompasses a range of potential measures (e.g., structural/positional changes, neurophysiological activity (peripherally or centrally), or dedicated biomarker changes) at multiple different structural levels (e.g. molecular, cellular, regional, systems).4 It is important to emphasize that Keter and colleagues3 did not assume causation of responses (e.g., this treatment caused this response); they only reported findings within the exploratory or associational designs.

The biomechanical, neurological, neuroimmune, neurovascular, neurotransmitter/peptide, neuroendocrine, neuromuscular and other subcategories function within networks and do not operate in isolation when stimulated or suppressed. Many exhibit complex interactions with one another (Fig. 1) involving the countless cascades of changes that defy simplistic description and interpretation.5 For example, serotonin neurons can influence dopamine signaling through receptor-level mechanisms; some serotonergic neurons express dopamine D2 receptors, enabling dopamine to modulate serotonin activity. Substance P, a neuropeptide associated with pain and stress, interacts with dopamine pathways in the striatum. Similarly, orexin A promotes wakefulness and arousal by activating monoaminergic neurons, including those that release dopamine and norepinephrine.

Beyond intra-category interactions, there is also dynamic cross-talk between neurotransmitter and neuropeptide and other systems, including neuroendocrine, neuromuscular, and neuroimmune pathways. This underscores the complex, integrated nature of treatment mechanisms,1 while also highlighting an inherent challenge of accounting for all of the potential interactions.

Recommendations

Given this complexity of these interactions, a systems-based frame of reference should be adopted, particularly in light of contextual factors discussed further in the next section. To improve understanding of the complexities, we recommend the adoption of the 3-Dimensional (3D) Matrix Model for FBM proposed by Loghmani and colleagues.4 The tool is an interactive, integrated, and dynamic framework designed to guide both mechanistic research and clinical practice in force-based manual therapies. It helps researchers and clinicians systematically account for the many factors that influence how individuals respond to mechanical forces applied to the body. Using a tool such as this provides the flexibility to measure to scale, beyond a single or set of mechanisms. This tool also allows mechanistic and clinical outcomes to be viewed as one of many potential responses from the individual’s interactive system rather than an isolated “A causes B” interaction.6

Challenge 2-treatment mechanisms and responses are dynamic

Treatment mechanisms should not be conceptualized as static outcomes but rather as part of a highly dynamic continuum shaped by internal biological rhythms, external environmental conditions, and procedural variability. Human physiology is continuously modulated by a range of physical and psychological inputs that influence neuromuscular, neuroimmune, and neurological systems.3 Subtle contextual factors, such as ambient temperature, environmental noise, or the perceived demeanor of a healthcare provider, can exert measurable effects on these physiological systems before and after treatment or factor in as only a nuisance variable with very little influence.4,7 Moreover, molecular families often fluctuate under circadian and seasonal influences, with certain functional subgroups showing increased activity at night versus during the day, or elevated inflammatory markers in spring due to allergies.8,9 These temporal shifts mean that a therapy effective at one time of day or season may not yield the same results under different temporal conditions.

The act of measuring physiological responses, through venipuncture, imaging, or other clinical assessments, may itself alter the system being observed, complicating efforts to interpret mechanistic data. This challenges conventional assumptions about the timing and alignment of measurements, particularly when attempting to identify mediating effects. In FBM interventions, treatment delivery is inherently variable.10 A multitude of force parameters including magnitude, rate, angle, direction, contact area, frequency, pattern, and mode influence the mechanistic response.4 The size, shape, and density of the therapist’s body part or instrument influence the pressure applied, and delivery angles change throughout a session to accommodate anatomical contours. Additionally, the absorption and dissipation of force vary across tissue types. For example, skin and muscle are more malleable than tendon or bone, and therapists adjust their technique based on patient feedback, tissue injury level, and structural response, often unconsciously. These factors make it exceedingly difficult to quantify or standardize force dynamics across sessions, therapists, and diverse patient populations.10

Recommendations

It is important to realize that physiological measurements taken at isolated timepoints offer only a snapshot of transient associations and are not to be considered as an explanation for a clinical response; at best, these should be considered associated responses. In contrast, longitudinal tracking capturing data before and after interventions, and capturing potential treatment mechanisms with simultaneous clinical outcomes provides deeper insight into temporal patterns and adaptive responses. Capturing mechanisms as potential mediators requires embedding mechanistic measurements directly within the collection of clinical outcomes11 because mediation is fundamentally about linking changes in a proposed mechanism to changes in the outcome itself. Embedding these measurements within a longitudinal trial ensures temporal alignment, allows researchers to model causal pathways, and makes it possible to test whether the mechanism truly mediates the clinical effect rather than merely correlating with it. This integrated approach strengthens mechanistic inference and supports more precise, evidence-based interpretations of how and why an intervention works.

Emerging technologies like wearable biosensors offer promising solutions, enabling continuous monitoring of biomarkers such as cortisol and heart rate variability with minimal disruption to daily life; however, many key markers cannot be measured directly and easily. These tools enhance ecological validity and support a more nuanced understanding of treatment mechanisms in real-world contexts.

Challenge 3-treatments do not provide consistent mechanistic effects across all populations

For a number of reasons, FBM treatment mechanisms may demonstrate inconsistencies across different populations.3 There are many reasons, including the fact that all animal and many preclinical studies involve non-human subjects and designs that are not presumed to be transferable to clinical practice. In human subjects, individual patients vary greatly in characteristics that affect the absolute benefit they will receive from treatment,12 including physiological differences across patients and physiological adaptability (i.e., differences in mechanisms with the same manual stimulus due to differences in pain sensitivity, tissue response, and neurochemical profiles).6 For example, FBMs can influence immune signaling, including cytokine release and glial cell activation-patients with chronic inflammation or immune dysregulation may respond differently due to altered neuroimmune feedback loops.2 Individual variations in neurotransmitter levels, like dopamine, serotonin, and endocannabinoids, can modulate pain perception and emotional response to therapy.13 These molecules also influence arousal, mood, and stress, which are tightly linked to therapeutic outcomes.

Recommendations

Comparable methodological strategies can be applied, as in clinical trials where sub-analyses account for heterogeneity in treatment effects.14 Collecting biomarkers (e.g., cytokine levels, glial activation markers, serotonin/ dopamine metabolites) before and after treatment is essential, and these measures should be carefully controlled for confounding factors such as diurnal variation or circadian rhythm effects, baseline stress levels, and other physiological fluctuations. Employing adaptive trial designs15 that allow modifications mid-study (e.g., adjusting inclusion criteria, stratification, or dosing based on interim biomarker findings) remains underutilized but holds significant potential to improve mechanistic insights. Stratifying participants by key physiological and neurochemical markers (e.g., baseline inflammation levels, pain sensitivity, neurotransmitter profiles) should be prioritized to better account for interactions across body systems and treatment mechanisms.16 Integrating systems biology approaches and network modeling may help identify mechanistic subgroups and predict treatment responsiveness, thereby advancing precision in FBM research.

Challenge 4-studies often lack rigor and causality, fail to use common terminologies and measures, and fail to discern/establish therapeutic ranges based on quantifiable force parameters

Authors have noted that research on manual therapy and other FBMs often lacks methodological rigor, suffers from inconsistent terminology, and rarely incorporates standardized measurement of applied forces.1,17,18 The NIH-sponsored Force-Based Manipulation Taxonomy and Terminology Committee has emphasized that the field lacks common definitions and reporting standards, limiting reproducibility and cross-study comparisons.4 Similarly, Bialosky and colleagues19 argue that manual therapy research frequently fails to quantify key mechanical parameters, making it difficult to determine therapeutic dose–response relationships or establish mechanistic pathways. Collectively, these critiques underscore the need for standardized terminology, quantifiable force metrics, and more rigorous study designs to advance FBM science and improve study reproducibility.

Recommendations

Greater use of causal mechanistic designs that integrate multimodal outcome measures, including indices of mood and stress alongside study designs that explicitly account for mediators and moderators such as pain chronicity, psychological traits (e.g., fear-avoidance beliefs, patient expectations), and contextual influences may clarify the extent to which treatment mechanisms are shaped by external factors.3 Advancing this line of inquiry will require the application of causal mediation designs for FBM interventions, which directly link mechanistic processes to clinical outcomes.1,20 Such approaches depend on the concurrent collection of mechanistic and clinical data, adequately powered samples, sustained funding, and collaboration with statisticians experienced in the methodological demands of complex analytic models.

Consistent reporting and the use of standardized terminology should also be expected in FBM research. Because “mechanisms” vary in meaning across investigators and span multiple levels of measurement, including both direct and indirect methods, the reporting recommendations proposed by Cook and colleagues1 offer valuable guidance. Likewise, the recent review by Keter et al.3 provides a careful delineation of mechanistic domains relevant to FBM research. The 3D Matrix Model for FBMs4 may further support the field by offering a structured approach for reporting and quantifying force parameters. Universal and consistent use of standardized terms, force-parameter definitions, and categorized mechanistic measures will strengthen continuity and comparability across studies.

Summary of recommendations

This commentary highlights four major challenges in mechanistic research, each of which can distort causal inference if not addressed (Table 2). These issues threaten validity by oversimplifying complex networks, relying on single timepoints, masking subgroup effects, or using poorly standardized methods. Solutions include systems-based models, longitudinal designs, stratified or adaptive analyses, and rigorous standardization paired with adequately powered causal mediation approaches.

Call to action

We argue that the challenges outlined above are solvable, and that future studies capable of addressing them will be far better positioned to clarify how manual interventions influence physiological and psychosocial systems. Accurately identifying mechanisms not only strengthens the evidence base for FBM but also improves its ability to deliver consistent, reproducible, and patient-centered outcomes across diverse clinical populations. However, in some contexts, additional mechanistic work may offer limited value, such as when mechanisms cannot be meaningfully isolated, when comparator treatments are highly heterogeneous, when contextual factors dominate treatment effects, or when mechanistic insights fail to enhance prediction, stratification, dosing, or clinical decision-making. In these situations, continued mechanistic investigation should be reconsidered, and a shift toward our recommended alternative approaches is warranted.
