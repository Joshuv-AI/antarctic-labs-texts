A generalist precision medication framework using temporal causal inference based on treatment-free physiological profiles
Authors: Zizhen Deng, Wei Wu, Chi Zhang, Xitong Zhao, Minghao Pu, Yanbin Bu, Yanfeng Liao, Changguan Wang, Jiarui Yang, Yanni Wang, Jinzhuo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterogeneity in treatment responses to standard treatment regimens poses a critical challenge in clinical practice, compounded by the unobservability of counterfactual outcomes. To address this, we present temporal causal precision medication (TCPM), a generalist framework translating routine physiological indicators into personalized treatment strategies. Central to TCPM is the treatment-free physiological profile (TFPP), a representation of heterogeneous physiological states trained using counterfactual prediction and adversarial learning to model patient’s intrinsic, unbiased physiological state while eliminating the confounding effects of existing therapies from the routine clinical data. Validated across four public datasets and two private datasets, TCPM significantly outperformed standard clinical protocols and state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods in therapeutic efficacy and physician simulation studies. Furthermore, TCPM identified patient subgroups with divergent response patterns, and elucidated evolving physiological network dynamics to uncover mechanistic heterogeneity. By integrating data-driven precision with actionable physiological insights, TCPM establishes a clinically practical, evidence-based framework for delivering an optimal individual clinical benefit through personalized treatment allocation in acute and chronic diseases, advancing the clinical implementation of precision medication.Subject terms: Prognostic markers, Diagnosis, Therapeutics, Predictive medicine

Introduction

In clinical medical practice, a major challenge that has long existed and needs to be solved is that even when facing the same disease diagnosis and using standardized drug treatment plans, the treatment results of different patients often show significant heterogeneity - from complete recovery to partial remission, or even complete ineffectiveness. This huge difference in treatment response is essentially due to the complex heterogeneity of individual patients formed by the interaction of multi-dimensional factors such as genetic background, metabolic characteristics, pathophysiological state, and environmental exposure1–6. The traditional “one-size-fits-all” treatment model ignores this biological heterogeneity between individuals, which not only limits the maximization of treatment effects but also may cause some patients to bear unnecessary drug side effects or the risk of treatment failure. Precision medication is a solution proposed for this clinical pain point. Its core lies in systematically analyzing the heterogeneity of individual physiological characteristics by integrating multi-source data, building dynamic models, and developing intelligent algorithms, and on this basis, realizing personalized customization and dynamic optimization of treatment strategies1,2,7–11.

The rapid development of multi-omics technology in recent years has provided powerful tools for precision medication, enabling researchers to comprehensively characterize the biological characteristics of individuals from multiple dimensions, such as genome, transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome, and on this basis, build more sophisticated individualized characterization models7,12–15. However, the widespread application of these methods in clinical practice still faces significant challenges14,16–19. Therefore, although multi-omics methods can theoretically provide a more comprehensive individual characterization, their implementation in the real world is still limited by scalability, cost-effectiveness, and technology transformation. There is an urgent need to develop more efficient, economical, and easy-to-promote alternatives to fill this gap.

Conventional clinical physiological indicators—such as blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, body temperature, and electrolyte levels—are routinely collected at low cost in daily medical practice. Despite their simplicity, these signals encode rich, multidimensional information about an individual’s genetic background, metabolic profile, pathophysiological state, and environmental adaptability20–24. These indicators not only record the patient’s current physiological state but also reveal the potential pathological mechanisms and drug response patterns through their dynamic change trends. There have been many studies on precision medicine for specific diseases based on physiological indicators3,25–32.

While routine clinical data hold potential for treatment response modeling, their intrinsic biases necessitate advanced analytical methods to enable reliable precision medicine. A patient’s recorded physiology is a complex mixture of their underlying health state and the effects of treatments they have already received. This confounding makes it exceptionally difficult to discern a patient’s true condition and accurately predict their response to a new therapy. Consequently, scalable methods to translate routine data into truly personalized treatment strategies remain elusive.

In view of the current problems in precision medication, we propose Temporal Causal Precision Medication (TCPM) – a generalist framework that translates routine physiological indicators into personalized treatment strategies. Central to TCPM is the Treatment-Free Physiological Profile (TFPP), a novel representation of heterogeneous physiological states. TFPP is trained using counterfactual prediction and adversarial learning to model temporal treatment-response interactions while eliminating the confounding effects of existing treatment from the routine clinical data. (Fig. 1a, c)33,34. By leveraging the TFPP as an unbiased representation of patient states, the framework dynamically evaluates potential treatment responses across different temporal windows. The A*-powered search algorithm then identifies optimal medication sequences by simulating treatment trajectories, balancing immediate efficacy against long-term outcomes (Fig. 1d). Beyond individualized optimization, TCPM systematically characterizes interpatient variability in treatment responses. Through counterfactual-derived TFPP, it stratifies patients into subgroups with distinct treatment response patterns. Each subgroup’s unique physiological signature is decoded by constructing dynamic physiological networks—correlation networks and causal graphs that reveal how physiological systems interact differently across subgroups (Fig. 1e). This paradigm can not only improve the treatment effect of patients, but also provide a clear basis for clinicians’ decision-making and the possibility of in-depth analysis of pathology.

Evaluation of TFPP to generate unbiased representation and accurate disease outcome prediction

The key to causal inference is to answer the question “What is the causal effect of the treatment on the outcome?”4,36,37. In our scenario, this is the causal effect of medication on the outcome of the disease. In order to estimate the causal effect, we need to compare the potential outcomes (counterfactuals) of the same group when “treated” and “not treated”. But in reality, we can only observe one outcome, such as patients either take the medicine or not. Therefore, treatment-free representations of physiological profiles are necessary for making counterfactual predictions and estimating causal effects33,34,38–41. The TFPP we obtain converts the original covariates into a low-dimensional representation, which can accurately predict the outcome, but cannot predict the treatment, ensuring that the representation is balanced between different treatment groups (similar to a randomized trial) to avoid introducing selection bias or confounding bias.

In our framework, the treatment information is removed through adversarial training, so that the treatment is unbiased in the representation space of physiological profiles, that is, different treatment groups are evenly distributed in the representation space. Taking sepsis as an example, Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the uniform distribution of the five main treatment strategies in the representation space.

In the real dataset, the observed outcome represents the counterfactual under actual treatment38,42,43. To evaluate the model’s counterfactual prediction performance, we tested its ability to predict patient outcomes under real treatment across training and validation sets from multiple disease cohorts. Comparisons between actual outcome trajectories and model predictions demonstrated accurate long-term physiological outcome predictions at extended time steps. Here, the prediction step denotes the number of time steps projected from the baseline time point. As theoretically expected, prediction accuracy declined with increasing prediction steps, as no post-baseline covariate information was available—only physiological profiles captured at the baseline time point served as the input. For overall outcome prediction, we aggregated individual-level results to derive group-level outcomes for downstream analysis.

Our model demonstrated strong predictive performance across disease cohorts. In the sepsis cohort, prediction step k = 1 yielded PCC = 0.8 and R² = 0.62, while k = 4 maintained high accuracy (PCC = 0.73, R² = 0.54; Fig. 2a). Similarly, for diabetes, k = 1 achieved PCC = 0.86 and R² = 0.74, with k = 4 showing robust performance (PCC = 0.78, R² = 0.6; Fig. 2b). These results confirm the model’s reliable counterfactual prediction capability on test data. We also compared our approach with RL-based precision medication strategies and the temporal causal inference method to highlight the significant advantages of our approach. The results can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3. Prediction results for the remaining four disease cohorts are presented in Fig. 2c–f.

This approach allows objective assessment of treatment strategy efficacy through comparative analysis.

To demonstrate our model’s clinical utility, we selected four representative patient cases from the MIMIC database (chosen for their extended longitudinal records) across four distinct disease cohorts. Fig. 4 presents comparative analyses between (1) observed outcomes under actual treatment regimens and (2) predicted outcomes under TCPM-recommended strategies. For sepsis patients, TCPM’s recommendations achieved significantly lower SOFA scores compared to actual treatments (Fig. 4a). Since SOFA scores directly correlate with disease severity and mortality risk, these reductions demonstrate our model’s potential to improve clinical outcomes. Similarly, across the three additional disease cohorts, model-recommended strategies consistently maintained superior performance throughout the entire treatment course, as evidenced by persistently lower disease-specific outcome scores (Fig. 4b–d). Critically, these longitudinal case studies provide direct evidence of TCPM’s capability to deliver personalized treatment recommendations at the individual patient level, which is the central objective of our precision medicine framework. We quantitatively analyzed the relationship between treatment adherence to TCPM-recommended and clinical outcomes (Fig. 4e, f). Patients whose actual treatments closely matched the model’s recommendations demonstrated significantly better outcomes, including lower SOFA scores and reduced mortality rates. Conversely, greater deviations from recommended treatments were associated with progressively worse outcomes. Notably, dose analysis revealed that clinicians tended to administer less fluid than recommended in cases of treatment deviation. This observation underscores the critical role of appropriate fluid management in severe sepsis cases, where insufficient administration may contribute to poorer outcomes.

Clinical validation via physician simulation studies

We conducted the retrospective simulation studies on the Age-related Macular Degeneration disease cohorts to validate the feasibility of our precision medication approach. In the Age-related Macular Degeneration validation cohort, we compared the performance of the model with that of actual clinical treatment on the anti-VEGF treatment strategy for AMD using 61 patients (218 data points in total) (Fig. 5). Three groups of physicians with different levels of clinical experience were recruited: Group 1, junior physicians with 1–3 years of clinical experience; Group 2, intermediate physicians with 4–7 years of clinical experience; Group 3, senior physicians with 8–20 years of clinical experience. The physician group evaluated the treatment strategies generated by the model and the treatment strategies used in actual clinical treatment (medication records in EHR). The evaluation method also included scoring the treatment strategies separately and recommending new treatment strategies. The actual scoring results showed that physicians in Group 2 and Group 3 believed that the treatment strategy recommended by the model was almost exactly the same as clinical practice, while junior doctors in Group 1 believed that the treatment strategy recommended by the model was significantly different from actual clinical treatment.

Performance evaluation between the TCPM and actual clinical treatment in retrospective studies.

a The study design compared the strategies generated by the model with actual clinical treatment in three physician experience groups (G1: junior, 1–3 years; G2: intermediate, 4–7 years; G3: senior, 8–20 years). b The evaluation results showed consistency between the model recommendations and clinical practice: senior and intermediate physicians rated model strategies as nearly identical to standard care, while junior physicians believed that TCPM was significantly superior.

Clinical mechanism heterogeneity analysis

To investigate the underlying causes of interpatient heterogeneity in treatment response, we conducted a systematic analysis of treatment effect variation across patient subgroups. Using a unified methodological framework, we identified distinct clinical subtypes based on heterogeneous treatment effects, characterized their differentiating physiological profiles, and elucidated potential mechanistic explanations to inform precision clinical decision-making. While the main text presents detailed heterogeneity analysis for sepsis, comprehensive results for all disease cohorts are provided in the Supplementary Figs. 6–10. All analyses were consistently derived from patient-specific physiological profiles using our standardized analytical paradigm.

Subgroup identification based on treatment responses

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, characterized by significant heterogeneity in pathogens, infection sites, and organ dysfunction patterns, necessitating individualized treatment5. Mortality varies substantially depending on specific combinations of infection sites and organ dysfunction. Clinical management primarily relies on two interventions: vasoactive drugs (e.g., norepinephrine), which increase blood pressure and improve organ perfusion (benefiting cardiovascular and neurological SOFA scores) but may compromise mesenteric circulation; and fluid resuscitation, which enhances cardiac output and renal perfusion but risks pulmonary edema if excessive, potentially worsening respiratory scores44,45.

Using counterfactually derived physiological profiles, we evaluated treatment responses by comparing (high infusion, low vasopressor) and (low infusion, high vasopressor) strategies against a (low infusion, low vasopressor) reference. This revealed four distinct response patterns: Dual-low-responders (benefit from conservative therapy), Infusion-low / vasopressor-high responders, Infusion-high / vasopressor-low responders, and Dual-high-responders (require aggressive therapy).

Heterogeneity analysis of physiological indicators and interaction graphs

The analysis of physiological indicators showed that there were significant differences between different subgroups (Fig. 6a). Analyzing the differences in physiological indicators behind different subgroups can explain the physiological mechanisms of different treatment effects. Multiple physiological indicators of subgroup 4 fluctuated significantly, reflecting a state of systemic disorder. At this time, more aggressive high-dose infusion and pressor drugs may have a better effect. The deviation of renal function indicators (especially creatinine) in subgroups 1 and 2 was greater than that in subgroup 3, indicating that their renal function may be in a high-load state. Restricting fluid can avoid increased venous return and thus reduce renal venous congestion.

Beyond individual indicator distributions, we constructed physiological interaction networks to examine coordinated system-level differences across subgroups. Two complementary approaches were employed: correlation networks quantifying association strength between indicators (Fig. 6b) and causal networks identifying directed influence pathways (Fig. 6d). The causal networks proved particularly informative, eliminating spurious correlations and revealing distinct interaction patterns for each subgroup. The unique interaction patterns of each subtype, namely the unique edges in the correlation network (Fig. 6c) and causal graph (Fig. 6e), provide a glimpse into the physiological mechanisms behind its therapeutic heterogeneity.

Analysis of the correlation network revealed distinct pathophysiological patterns across subgroups. Subgroup 1 patients may have a tendency to be overloaded. Excessive infusion will increase the burden on the heart or tissue edema, while the low infusion strategy maintains hemodynamic stability by limiting the amount of fluid intake. The possible mechanism of the unique index correlation (Creatinine – Potassium, BUN -- Potassium) is: low infusion reduces venous return pressure, avoids renal venous congestion, glomerular filtration rate is maintained, creatinine and BUN levels are stable in a lower range. At the same time, the kidney’s ability to excrete potassium was not significantly affected, and the blood potassium level was positively correlated with renal function indicators (creatinine, BUN), which may reflect that the kidney’s regulation of potassium is in a compensatory state. The subgroup 4 benefits from high infusion + high vasoactive drugs, and its unique correlation edges are BUN -- Magnesium, FiO2 -- GCS. Renal function-electrolyte coupling reveals a sensitive constitution to volume overload. The strong correlation between Creatinine/BUN and blood potassium reflects that the kidney’s ability to excrete potassium is highly dependent on the glomerular filtration rate. Such patients often have a chronic heart and kidney disease foundation, and the kidney’s compensatory capacity has reached its limit. Low infusion avoids fluid overload, reduces renal venous pressure, maintains GFR stability, blocks the synchronous increase chain of creatinine/BUN-potassium. Low vasopressors prevent excessive vascular contraction, ensure renal cortical blood flow, support potassium excretion function. The core is to reveal that the strong coupling of creatinine/BUN-potassium is a sign of renal reserve depletion, and the low intervention strategy interrupts this vicious cycle by reducing the burden on the kidneys.

Next, we analyzed the mechanism behind the heterogeneity from the edges unique to different subgroups in the causal graph. The subgroup 1 benefits from low infusion + low vasoactive drugs, and its unique causal edge is Arterial_BE → HCO₃. BE directly reflects the metabolic acid-base state, and its dominant HCO₃⁻ indicates that acidosis is mainly caused by insufficient tissue perfusion or renal dysfunction, rather than respiratory problems. Such patients are usually sensitive to volume overload, and excessive infusion will dilute HCO₃⁻ and aggravate edema. Low infusion avoids fluid overload, reduces cardiac burden, improves tissue oxygenation, reduces lactate production, BE rebounds, HCO₃⁻ passively recovers. Low vasoactive drugs prevent excessive vascular contraction, ensure microcirculatory perfusion, reduce anaerobic metabolism. The core is to reveal that metabolic acidosis is the primary problem, and the low-intervention strategy reduces acid production from the source. The subgroup 2 benefits from low infusion + high vasoactive drugs, and its unique causal edge is INR, PT. INR dominates PT, indicating that coagulation factor deficiency is the core defect. High vasoactive drugs quickly correct vascular paralysis, improve liver perfusion, restore coagulation factor synthesis; low infusion avoids aggravating liver congestion/intraperitoneal hypertension, protects liver function. The core is to expose the essence of insufficient liver perfusion. vasopressors give priority to restoring liver blood flow, while limiting fluid prevents secondary damage. Subgroup 3 benefits from high infusion + low vasoactive drugs, and its unique causal edge is paCO₂ → Arterial_pH, indicating that ventilation dysfunction is the main cause of acidosis. High infusion quickly restores effective circulating blood volume, increases cardiac output; low vasoactive drugs: avoid vasopressors aggravating pulmonary vasoconstriction, improve ventilation/blood flow ratio, reduce paCO₂ → pH rises. The core is to reveal the contradiction of respiratory failure, and high infusion volume expansion + avoiding vasopressors aggravating lung injury is the key. The subgroup 4 benefits from high infusion + high vasoactive drugs, and its unique causal edges are paO₂ → PaO₂/FiO₂, Sodium → Chloride. The former indicates that oxygen transport disorder is the core contradiction, and the latter indicates that renal tubular function is actively compensated. Most of these patients are in mixed shock (low volume + vascular paralysis), and dual intervention is required to restore oxygen supply. High infusion flushes microthrombi and improves pulmonary blood flow distribution, paO₂ increases, oxygenation index improves. High vasoactive drugs constrict peripheral blood vessels, increase perfusion pressure, ensure glomerular filtration, maintain Na⁺/Cl⁻ balance. The core is that paO₂→oxygenation index reveals the nature of microcirculatory failure, Na⁺→Cl⁻ reflects the need for renal compensation, and the dual strategy simultaneously optimizes oxygen delivery and perfusion pressure. Translation of heterogeneity findings into clinical insights

Heterogeneity analysis is an important step in deriving physiological mechanisms from data and a critical step from results to clinical recommendations3,5,6,22,46. Our approach establishes a precision medication framework through systematic characterization of treatment heterogeneity. By identifying subgroups with divergent therapeutic responses, we then: (1) analyze their distinctive physiological profiles through patterns and interaction networks, (2) elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving these differential responses, and (3) map these subtypes to recognizable clinical phenotypes. This creates a closed-loop system where retrospective response patterns inform prospective treatment guidance - observed therapeutic heterogeneity is decoded into mechanistically grounded subtypes that directly correspond to optimized intervention strategies. The methodology transforms empirical clinical observations into actionable physiological insights, enabling data-driven personalization of fluid and vasopressor therapy based on each patient’s characteristic pathophysiological signature.

Discussion

This study establishes the TCPM to transform routine clinical physiological indicators into actionable precision medication strategies. By combining adversarial training and counterfactual prediction to obtain TFPP and integrating optimal search and heterogeneity analysis, our approach addresses key limitations of the current precision medicine paradigm, specifically, the high cost and technical barriers of multi-omics approaches and the static interpretation of physiological data in clinical practice.

Our framework eliminates confounding biases over time by introducing treatment-free physiological representations achieved through adversarial training, thereby achieving unbiased counterfactual estimation. This goes beyond reinforcement learning by explicitly addressing implicit biases in observational data. Counterfactual multi-outcome learning is able to capture complex disease dynamics. For instance, in diabetes, simultaneous modeling of glucose fluctuations and creatinine trends can reveal renal load status, thereby guiding fluid restriction strategies. Physiological interaction networks decode heterogeneity mechanisms, and causal graphs identify subtype-specific pathways that are overlooked by traditional correlation analysis, thereby providing mechanistic explanations for different drug responses.

Validation in six cohorts showed that the method consistently outperformed clinician protocols. At the population level, the strategy recommended by the model reduced the SOFA score of patients with sepsis and maintained low complication indicators in chronic diseases (e.g., renal failure in patients with diabetes). In terms of personalized medication decision support, case studies have confirmed that deviations from model recommendations are associated with adverse outcomes. Subgroup stratification has clinical guidance significance. For example, the four sepsis subgroups are associated with different mechanisms. Subgroup 4 with double high response accompanied by microcirculatory failure can benefit from active fluid compression therapy, while subgroup 1 driven by metabolic acidosis performs well under conservative treatment. Physician simulation studies further verified the feasibility. The reliability of the model in anti-VEGF treatment for AMD was also recognized by experts at different levels.

While our framework demonstrates strong scalability for routine clinical implementation, several important challenges require further attention. First, the accuracy of physiological characterization may be constrained by missing or irregularly sampled data points, necessitating the development of robust imputation methods in future iterations. For real-world deployment, seamless integration with electronic health records and the establishment of clinician feedback loops will be critical to enable continuous learning and system refinement. To enhance clinical utility, we envision expanding the model’s capabilities by incorporating accessible multi-omics data, which could provide valuable pharmacogenomic insights for personalized drug response prediction. Most importantly, rigorous prospective validation through large-scale randomized controlled trials will be essential to establish clinical efficacy across diverse care settings, from primary care to intensive care units.

By converting low-cost physiological indicators into dynamic physiological features and actionable treatment options, this framework bridges the gap between data-driven algorithms and clinical decision-making. It advances precision mediation from a “static genome” model to a “dynamic physiological” model, where personalized treatments evolve with the patient’s state. Future iterations may set a new standard for real-time adaptive treatment in acute and chronic disease management.

The limitation of the current work is that the action space was defined only on the primary treatments of interest for the specific disease cohort (e.g., fluids and vasopressors for sepsis). Comedications are considered part of the background, time-varying context that influences the patient’s physiological state. The framework cannot predict the effect of novel drug combinations not present in the training data. Its knowledge is constrained by the co-prescription patterns observed in the historical EHR. Developing methods to infer the therapeutic effects of medication strategies not previously observed in electronic medical records is a future research direction.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Peking University Third Hospital (IRB00006761-M20250550) and Beijing Children’s Hospital (2024-E-172-R) and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the de-identified, observational, and retrospective nature of this study, informed consent was not required.

Study design and disease cohorts

We propose a general precision medication framework and evaluate the proposed model through a retrospective study. The study involves a large real-world public dataset (MIMIC-III) and 2 private datasets (Pediatric burns and Age-related macular degeneration).

MIMIC-III is an open access, anonymized database that records 60,000+ admissions to a Boston hospital from 2001 to 2012, recording the patient’s demographics, vital signs, laboratory tests, medications, and diagnoses35. Pediatric burns is a treatment data of pediatric burns obtained from the Department of Burn and Plastic Surgery, Beijing Children’s Hospital from 2015 to 2024, recording the patient’s demographics, vital signs, laboratory tests, and medication information during the period of admission to the hospital for treatment after burns. Age-related macular degeneration is data on anti-VEGF treatment of age-related macular degeneration from 2020 to 2024 obtained from the Department of Ophthalmology of Peking University Third Hospital, recording the patient’s demographics, blood routine, eye image data, and medication information.

In order to verify the universality of the proposed precision medication framework, we extracted 6 different disease cohorts from these 3 data sets for retrospective analysis, covering a variety of chronic and acute diseases. For the MIMIC-III database, we extracted 4 disease cohorts from it, including sepsis, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease. The remaining two private data sets each constitute a disease cohort. Next, the data composition of each disease cohort will be introduced separately, including physiological indicators focusing on prognostic outcomes and treatment options.

Sepsis

We extracted 39 temporal covariates (such as vital signs and laboratory test results) and 3 static covariates (gender, age, weight). The data covered the entire period from 24 h before the estimated onset of sepsis to 48 h afterward. The outcome of the study was the SOFA value, and the treatment of interest was infusion and vasopressors. Finally, 18,909 patients were identified, and these patients generated a total of 247,501 records during the time period of interest. SOFA was chosen as the main target of interest for several reasons: first, SOFA is a time series indicator that can dynamically monitor the changes in the physiological status of patients with different treatments; second, compared with death, which may have too many inducing factors, it can more directly reflect the improvement of sepsis.

Diabetes

We extracted 37 temporal covariates and the same 3 static covariates. The data covered the time point from the time when the patient started receiving treatment. The outcomes of the study were Glucose and Creatinine, and the treatment of interest was insulin and infusion. Finally, 5762 patients were identified, and a total of 77530 records were generated. Glucose was selected as the result because blood sugar in diabetic patients is the main improvement target, and Creatinine was selected as the result because renal failure is the most important complication of diabetes and is a very important indicator in critically ill patients.

Hypertension

We extracted 36 temporal covariates and the same 3 static covariates. The data covers the time point from when the patient started treatment. The results of the study are blood pressure indicators such as diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, mean blood pressure. The treatment of interest is a variety of mainstream blood pressure control drugs, and different drugs are suitable for different constitutions and situations. Finally, 6728 patients were identified, and 103739 records were generated.

Coronary heart disease

We extracted 37 temporal covariates and the same 3 static covariates. The results of the study are HR and Shock Index. The treatment of interest is a variety of mainstream drugs for coronary heart disease, and different drugs are suitable for different constitutions and situations. Finally, 8820 patients were identified, and a total of 123104 records were generated.

Pediatric burns

We extracted 8 temporal covariates and 4 static covariates (gender, age, weight, TBSA), and the data covered the time from the beginning of the patient’s treatment. The outcomes of interest were Pulse and High Blood Pressure, and the treatments of interest were divided into crystalloids (NaCl injection) and colloids (Plasms, Dextran, Albumin). Different drugs are suitable for different burn shock conditions. Since the critical period for burn shock treatment is the first 48 h, limited by the recording frequency of the actual EHR, we took the admission time as time 0 and every 24 h as a time point, so the time series length greater than 2 was included in the study; at the same time, due to the length of the time series, the performance of the model declined to a certain extent. Finally, 720 patients were identified, and a total of 2115 records were generated.

Age-related macular degeneration

We extracted the required structured features from the optical coherence tomography (OCT) photos of the eye and obtained 20 temporal covariates and 2 static covariates (age, gender). The treatment of interest is three mainstream anti-VEGF drugs, namely conbercept, aflibercept, and ranibizumab. We regard the most recent treatment after each OCT as the corresponding treatment time, and exclude time points that are too far apart; the first OCT information and the first treatment are used as the baseline, and the second OCT information is compared to determine the effect of different drugs. Finally, 335 patients were identified, and a total of 1074 records were generated.

Data processing

For the data in MIMIC, for the missing values of physiological indicators and the processing of discrete time series, we mainly refer to the processing methods for sepsis-related indicators25. In order to deal with the impact of different indicator value ranges on model performance, we standardized all covariates and outcomes. If it is a disease cohort with multiple outcomes, the score in the treatment strategy experiment is the sum of multiple outcomes. At the same time, due to the small amount of data in the two private data, data enhancement technology was used to increase the sample size during the model training process.

Causal identification

To estimate the causal effect of treatment sequences from observational data, our TCPM framework relies on three standard causal identifiability assumptions:

Consistency

The observed outcome for a patient who received treatment sequence A¯ is equal to their potential outcome under that same sequence, YA¯.

The definition of treatments in our study to ensure the consistency assumption holds. For instance, ‘fluid’ is strictly defined as crystalloid boluses of > 500 ml within a 1 h window, as recorded in the inputevents_mv table. This precise definition minimizes variability in how the treatment is delivered and recorded.

Positivity

For any possible patient history Ht, the probability of receiving any possible treatment at is positive, i.e., P(At=at∣Ht)>0. This ensures that we have data to estimate the effect for all types of patients.

We have empirically checked the positivity assumption by analyzing the overlap in propensity scores. We trained a gradient boosting model to predict the probability of receiving vasopressors and fluid based on patient history Ht. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 12, the distributions of propensity scores for the treated (treatment = 5) and control (treatment = 0) groups exhibit substantial overlap, indicating that the positivity assumption is reasonably satisfied in our data.

Sequential Ignorability

Given the observed patient history Ht up to time t, the treatment assignment At is independent of all potential future outcomes.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis to quantify the robustness of our findings to potential unmeasured confounding. To assess TCPM’s robustness to unmeasured confounding, we generated a synthetic binary confounder U (representing unobserved factors like genetic predisposition or unrecorded comorbidities) following these steps: U ~ Bernoulli(p), where p = 0.5 (balanced distribution to avoid extreme subgroup sizes). This choice ensures U has equal prevalence across treatment groups in the absence of correlation, making its impact on treatment/outcome measurable. We quantified the strength of correlation between U and A, and U and Y, using relative risk (RR)—a clinically interpretable metric for association in observational data. RR_UY denotes the relative risk of a poor outcome (e.g., SOFA score increase) for patients with U = 1 versus U = 0. We tested 10 levels of RR_UY (1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0, 2.25, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0) to cover weak (1.0–1.5) to strong (3.0–4.0) confounding. For example, RR_UY = 2.0 means patients with U = 1 have twice the risk of a higher SOFA score than those with U = 0. RR_UA denotes the relative risk of receiving an aggressive treatment (e.g., high fluid + high vasopressors) for patients with U = 1 versus U = 0. To mimic real-world confounding (e.g., sicker patients U = 1 more likely to get aggressive treatment), we set RR_UA = RR_UY, ensuring U confounds the treatment-outcome relationship by influencing both.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, the vertical axes of both subgraphs represent clinician outcome - TCPM outcome. When the value is positive, it means that TCPM is better than the clinician strategy. The results showed that even with RR_UY = 3.0, the TCPM-recommended strategy was still significantly superior to the clinical strategy. It can be seen that the confidence interval gradually expands as the strength of the confounding factor increases.

Learning TFPP via counterfactual prediction and adversarial training

In the precision medication framework, patients’ historical data often contain a large number of time-varying confounding factors, which not only affect treatment allocation but are also affected by past treatments. Traditional causal inference methods are difficult to effectively handle this complex time-varying confounding problem. To this end, we introduce adversarial training technology to construct a covariate balanced representation to eliminate confounding bias40. In this study, we use an adversarial time series encoder to process patients’ time series data. Its dual-channel architecture design includes a main channel and an adversarial channel. The main channel uses LSTM to encode patients’ time series data (such as blood pressure, heart rate, laboratory indicators, etc.) to capture dynamic physiological patterns; while the adversarial channel predicts the current treatment allocation probability through a classifier, aiming to ensure that the encoded representation does not contain information related to treatment allocation. During the training process, we introduce a gradient reversal mechanism so that the gradient of the adversarial channel is reversed, prompting the representation generated by the main channel to gradually become “treatment-free”, that is, the representation distribution between different treatment groups tends to be consistent.

Algorithms for searching optimal treatment strategies

Based on the counterfactual representation obtained previously, we can further estimate the potential treatment effect of patients under different treatment plans. Use the optimal search strategy to find the optimal treatment sequence for each patient in multiple time steps in the future47. The A* algorithm is a heuristic - based informed search algorithm widely used in combinatorial optimization problems, and it serves as the core engine for exploring optimal longitudinal treatment strategies in personalized healthcare. Its fundamental goal is to efficiently navigate a predefined “state space”—where each state represents a patient’s clinical condition at a specific time point—to identify the shortest path from an initial state to a target state. A* uses a weighted cost function fn=gn+h(n) to prioritize promising paths: gn denotes the actual cost of reaching the current state from the initial state, while h(n) is a heuristic estimate of the remaining cost to reach the target state from n. When h(n) is admissible and consistent, A* is guaranteed to find the globally optimal path.

Here, the patient’s current physiological state and known treatment effect information are used as the initial state, and the future treatment goal is used as the target state. The evaluation function comprehensively considers multiple factors such as treatment effect, treatment risk, and treatment cost to evaluate the pros and cons of different treatment sequences. During the search process, the algorithm constructs a treatment decision tree, where each node represents a treatment decision, and each edge represents a transfer from one treatment state to another. Through the search mechanism of the A* algorithm, it is possible to efficiently find an optimal path from the initial state to the target state in the huge treatment decision tree, that is, the optimal treatment sequence. This treatment sequence not only takes into account the current treatment effect but also dynamically adjusts the treatment plan according to the changes in the patient’s physiological state and possible future treatment responses to achieve the best treatment effect.

Implementation details of reinforcement learning baseline

Our RL baseline is strictly implemented based on the sepsis RL framework proposed by Komorowski et al. (2018, Nature Medicine), which is a landmark work in RL-based critical care treatment optimization.

The baseline adopts a model-based RL approach built on a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to formalize the sequential treatment decision problem for sepsis. The MDP is defined by the tuple {S, A, T, R, γ}, where S represents the state space, A denotes the action space, T is the state transition function, R is the reward function, and γ is the discount factor. The optimal treatment policy is learned via in-place policy iteration, a standard MDP solution method that iteratively evaluates and improves a policy until convergence to the optimal strategy. For evaluating clinicians’ existing treatment patterns, temporal difference learning is used (offline sampling from EHR trajectories) as it avoids requiring explicit knowledge of the MDP transition matrix.

The state space S is designed to capture the dynamic physiological status of sepsis patients, with 48 variables extracted from EHRs covering demographics, premorbid conditions, vital signs, laboratory values, fluid balance, and treatment history; continuous variables are standardized, log-normally distributed variables are log-transformed before standardization, outliers are capped to clinically plausible ranges using Tukey’s method and frequency histograms, missing data is handled via a time-limited sample-and-hold approach for MIMIC-III with remaining gaps filled by multivariable nearest-neighbor imputation. Patient time series (4-hour time steps, up to 72 h post-sepsis onset) from the MIMIC-III training set (80% of cohort) are clustered into 750 discrete mutually exclusive states using k-means + +, with the number of clusters optimized via Bayesian Information Criterion and Akaike Information Criterion to balance granularity and representativeness; two additional absorbing states (death and discharge) are added to the space to terminate the patient’s trajectory as treatment decisions are no longer relevant.

The action space A focuses on the two most critical sepsis interventions: intravenous (IV) fluids and vasopressors, where IV fluids include crystalloids, colloids, and blood products standardized by tonicity to ensure consistency across fluid types, and vasopressors include norepinephrine, epinephrine, vasopressin, dopamine, and phenylephrine—all converted to norepinephrine-equivalent doses using published dose correspondence; each treatment is divided into 5 discrete dose levels, with Level 1 being no drug (0 dose) and Levels 2–5 being non-zero doses split into 4 quartiles based on the distribution of clinician-prescribed doses in the MIMIC-III training set; the two treatments (fluids + vasopressors) form 25 unique discrete actions, each corresponding to a specific combination of fluid and vasopressor dose bins.

The reward signal is directly tied to patient survival to align with the original study’s goal of maximizing long-term outcomes, with a terminal reward issued at the end of each patient’s trajectory: + 100 for patients who survive (90-day survival in MIMIC-III) and -100 for patients who die; the discount factor (γ) is set to 0.99, a value that prioritizes long-term survival (e.g., late deaths at 72 h post-sepsis) nearly as highly as early survival, matching clinical goals of avoiding delayed organ failure.

Tuning is performed on the MIMIC-III cohort (80% training, 20% validation) to optimize model performance and ensure consistency with the original study’s results, with the tuning objective being to maximize the 95% confidence lower bound of the AI policy’s value—chosen to prioritize statistical safety. The tuning workflow involves three key steps: first, independent RL models are built, each using a unique random sample of the MIMIC-III training set and distinct k-means + + clustering solutions; second, for each model, the value of the AI policy is estimated via weighted importance sampling—a method to correct for discrepancies between the clinician’s behavior policy and the AI’s evaluation policy—with bootstrapping used to compute 95% confidence intervals for policy values; third, the model that maximizes the 95% confidence lower bound of the AI policy is selected to ensure the chosen policy is robust to sampling variability and reliably outperforms the clinician’s policy.

Construction of physiological interaction graphs

In the framework of precision medication, physiological heterogeneity analysis is a key link in understanding individual differences among patients and optimizing treatment strategies. By classifying patient groups and constructing interaction graphs of physiological indicators, we can reveal the potential mechanisms of different treatment response subgroups and provide theoretical support for clinical decision-making. Patients were classified based on their treatment effect at each timestep to identify subgroups with similar treatment response patterns. A physiological indicator interaction graph was constructed for each subgroup to reveal the dynamic relationship between the physiological indicators.

Construct the correlation network

The correlation network was constructed to accurately calculate the correlation between physiological indicators by using the Pearson correlation coefficient or mutual information; based on this, a weighted graph was constructed, in which nodes represent physiological indicators and edges represent the strength of correlation. Based on the correlation network, we can analyze the physiological heterogeneity of different subgroups and closely link it with medication strategies. It is also possible to identify key nodes and use centrality indicators (such as degree centrality and betweenness centrality) to find key nodes in the network.

Construct the causal graph

The causal graph eliminates false correlations and gives the direction of influence between physiological indicators, revealing the unique interaction pattern of each subgroup more reliably. When constructing the causal graph, several classic and mainstream causal discovery methods were used, including PC, GES, and Notears48,49. Since different causal discovery methods apply different data assumptions, after comparing the performance of multiple methods, we selected the more robust Notears as the implementation method for obtaining the causal graph.

Temporal causal graphs

Our TCPM framework is a temporal causal inference framework that leverages temporal information. It uses information from before the baseline (for sepsis, time points 0–4) to establish an unbiased representation, which can then predict the response to different treatments at subsequent time points (e.g., time points 5–8). In the heterogeneity analysis section, we construct a causal graph, which is a temporal causal graph, but with lag = 1, so that we can obtain a causal graph at each subsequent time point (Supplementary Fig. 11). We made the specific lag choice for causal discovery for two primary reasons, grounded in both clinical and methodological considerations:

Clinical plausibility and data granularity: The physiological indicators in our datasets (e.g., MIMIC-III) were recorded at approximately 4 h intervals. At this temporal resolution, the physiological state of a critically ill patient is most directly and strongly influenced by its immediate prior state (time t-1). The influence from time points further in the past (e.g., t-2, t-3) is often mediated through the state at t-1 or is too attenuated to be reliably detected. Therefore, a lag-1 model most efficiently captures the dominant and most immediate causal pathways in the system.

Methodological robustness and parsimony: constructing causal graphs with longer lags results in a significantly larger and more complex search space. This leads to extremely sparse graphs where many edges are statistically weak or non-existent. By focusing on lag-1 dependencies, we prioritize the strongest and most robust causal relationships, which are also the most clinically interpretable for understanding the immediate physiological mechanisms driving heterogeneous treatment responses.

Mechanism interpretation of subgroup heterogeneity

Statistically significant edges in correlation/causal networks were prioritized for mechanistic hypotheses. Clinical experts annotated these edges with potential biological explanations (e.g., Renal potassium excretion depends on glomerular filtration rate). Unique edges were formalized into subgroup-specific physiological signatures (e.g., Subgroup 1: Metabolic acidosis driven by tissue hypoperfusion). Subgroup-network mappings (e.g., Fig. 5e) were translated into treatment rules (e.g., Avoid fluid overload in Subgroup 1 due to renal venous congestion risk).

Statistics & reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.10. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables or median ± interquartile range (IQR) for non-normal variables. Normality was assessed via Shapiro-Wilk tests (α = 0.05). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Cohort sizes were determined by power analysis based on pilot data, ensuring > 80% power (β = 0.2) at α = 0.05 to detect clinically relevant effect sizes. Patients were randomly partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) sets using stratified sampling by diagnosis and baseline severity to preserve outcome distribution. Counterfactual prediction accuracy was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) between predicted vs. observed outcomes and the coefficient of determination (R²) for variance explained. Treatment strategy efficacy was assessed via Relative reduction (%) in disease-specific scores (e.g., SOFA) between model-recommended vs. clinician strategies. Subgroup differences in physiological indicators were tested via One-way ANOVA (with Tukey’s post hoc) for normally distributed variables. Edges of correlation networks was retained if the coefficient r > 0.3. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
