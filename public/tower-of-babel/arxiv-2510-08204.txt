Fitting sparse high-dimensional varying-coefficient models with Bayesian regression tree ensembles
Soham Ghosh, Saloni Bhogale, Sameer K. Deshpande

Abstract
By allowing the effects of p p covariates in a linear regression model to vary as functions of R R additional effect modifiers , varying-coefficient models (VCMs) strike a compelling balance between interpretable-but-rigid parametric models popular in classical statistics and flexible-but-opaque methods popular in machine learning. But in high-dimensional settings where p p and/or R R exceed the number of observations, existing approaches to fitting VCMs fail to identify which covariates have a non-zero effect and which effect modifiers drive these effects. We propose sparseVCBART , a fully Bayesian model that approximates each coefficient function in a VCM with a regression tree ensemble and encourages sparsity with a global–local shrinkage prior on the regression tree leaf outputs and a hierarchical prior on the splitting probabilities of each tree. We show that the sparseVCBART posterior contracts at a near-minimax optimal rate, automatically adapting to the unknown sparsity structure and smoothness of the true coefficient functions. Compared to existing state-of-the-art methods, sparseVCBART achieved competitive predictive accuracy and substantially narrower and better-calibrated uncertainty intervals, especially for null covariate effects. We use sparseVCBART to investigate how the effects of interpersonal conversations on prejudice could vary according to the political and demographic characteristics of the respondents.



1
Introduction

Varying coefficient models
(
Hastie and Tibshirani, 1993
, VCMs;)
assert a linear relationship between an outcome
Y
and
p
covariates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
but allow the relationship to change with respect to
R
additional variables (known as
effect modifiers
)
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{R}
:
\mathbb{E}[Y|\bm{X},\bm{Z}]=\beta_{0}(\bm{Z})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})X_{j}}.

VCMs occupy a middle ground between inflexible-but-interpretable parametric models and flexible-but-opaque nonparametric models
(
Zhou and Hooker, 2022
;
Lengerich et al., 2023
)
.

Since their introduction, VCMs have been used extensively in statistics and econometrics.
Important examples include
Hoover et al., 1998
, which estimated how the effects of maternal health on infant weight varied over time,
Li et al., 2011
, which estimated how returns to education vary with respect to several demographic factors, and
Mu et al., 2018
, which estimated spatial variation in the effects of wind speed and air temperature on air pollution concentration; see
Fan and Zhang, 2008
and
Franco-Villoria et al., 2019
for comprehensive reviews of VCMs.
VCMs are also useful in causal inference:
Hahn et al., 2020
’s Bayesian Causal Forest (BCF) model, which has proven highly effective at uncovering treatment effect heterogeneity
(
Dorie et al., 2019
)
, is just a VCM with a single covariate (the binary treatment indicator).

Early work on VCMs largely focused on settings with a single effect modifier where it is popular either (i) to approximate each function
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
with a linear combination of basis functions
(
Huang et al., 2002
;
Wang et al., 2008
;
Wei et al., 2011
;
Bai et al., 2023
, see, e.g.,)
or (ii) to estimate the
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
’s in a fully nonparametric fashion with kernel smoothing
(
Hoover et al., 1998
;
Wu and Chiang, 2000
;
Li et al., 2002
;
Chiang et al., 2001
)
.
In more modern settings, when the
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
’s might depend on complex interactions between
R>1
modifiers, several authors have used regression trees to estimate the covariate effects in a VCM
(
Wang and Hastie, 2012
;
Bürgin and Ritschard, 2015
;
Zhou and Hooker, 2022
, see, e.g.,)
.
Generally speaking, tree-based approaches are better equipped to capture
a priori
unknown interactions and scale much more gracefully with
R
and the number of observations
N
than kernel methods like the one proposed in
Li and Racine, 2010
, which involves intensive hyperparameter tuning.

Recently,
Deshpande et al., 2024
introduced VCBART, a fully Bayesian method for fitting VCMs that exhibits superior covariate effect recovery and uncertainty quantification compared to the current state-of-the-art without requiring any hand-tuning.
VCBART approximates each
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
with its own regression tree ensemble, avoiding overfitting with a regularizing prior that encourages each tree to be a “weak learner”.
Across several synthetic and real datasets, VCBART displayed state-of-the-art empirical performance.

Deshpande et al., 2024
also derived posterior contraction rates for VCBART in the regime where
p
was fixed and all the
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
’s were non-null.

In many applications, however, only a small subset
S_{0}\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
of covariates have a non-null effect,.
Further, for each covariate
j\in S_{0}
, the function
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
often only depends on just a few modifiers
S_{0,j}\subset\{1,\dots,R\}
.
To meet the demands of such applications, we introduce
sparseVCBART
, which
simultaneously
learns covariate sparsity and per-coefficient modifier sparsity.
At the covariate level, we place global-local shrinkage priors on the output in each regression tree leaf so that the
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
functions are adaptively shrunk towards zero
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
;
Polson and Scott, 2012
;
Bhadra et al., 2016
)
.
At the modifier level, we endow each coefficient’s tree ensemble with a heavy-tailed Dirichlet prior over split variables, which concentrates mass on a small subset of modifiers relevant to
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
.

Our main theoretical results (
Theorems

1
and
2
) show that the
sparseVCBART
posterior contracts at nearly the minimax-optimal rate
r_{N}
where

r_{N}^{2}=N^{-1}{(d_{0}\log p)}+\sum_{j\in S_{0}}(\log N)N^{-{2\alpha_{j}}/{(2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j})}},

d_{0}=\lvert S_{0}\rvert
is the number of covariates with non-null effects, and
s_{0,j}=\lvert S_{0,j}\rvert
is the number of effect modifiers on which
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
depends.
Our theoretical analysis significantly extends the work of
Deshpande et al., 2024
. In the more challenging regime where
p
grows with
N,
we establish a faster contraction rate for estimating sparse VCMs than that obtained in their analysis.
We further show that the posterior over the splitting probabilities in each tree concentrates on the true modifier sets
S_{0,j}
(
Theorem

3
) and that the posteriors for the local scale parameters corresponding to null
\beta_{j}(\bm{z})
’s concentrate around zero (
Theorem

4
).
These latter results essentially imply that, at least asymptotically,
sparseVCBART
can identify the modifiers that drive the truly non-null covariate effects while simultaneously shrinking away truly null effects.

Empirically in
Section

4
, across synthetic and semi-synthetic high-dimensional VCMs,
sparseVCBART
matches or improves predictive accuracy relative to strong baselines while delivering markedly sparser models: it prunes irrelevant covariates and modifiers and provides coherent posterior uncertainty for the remaining effects.
These properties make
sparseVCBART
a practical, theoretically grounded tool for high-dimensional varying coefficient analysis well-suited to numerous applications.

2
Proposed Model

We observe
N
triplets
(\bm{x}_{1},y_{1},\bm{z}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{N},y_{N},\bm{z}_{N})
of a covariate vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
scalar output
y\in\mathbb{R},
and an effect modifier vector
1
1

1

For simplicity, we present our model in the setting with only continuous effect modifiers. In our actual implementation, we allow for both categorical and continuous modifiers. See
Deshpande, 2025
for formal definitions and priors for regression trees defined over mixed input spaces.

\bm{z}\in[0,1]^{R}
and model

y_{i}=\beta_{0}(\bm{z}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\beta_{j}(\bm{z}_{i})x_{ij}}+\sigma\epsilon_{i},

(1)

where
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{N}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
and
\beta_{0}(\bm{Z}),\ldots,\beta_{p}(\bm{Z})
are unknown functions mapping
[0,1]^{R}
to
\mathbb{R}.

Following
Deshpande et al., 2024
, we (i) represent each unknown function
\beta_{j}
in
Equation

1
as a sum of
M_{j}
regression trees; (ii) specify a regularizing prior over the regression trees that discourages overfitting; (iii) compute a posterior distribution over regression tree ensembles; and (iv) use Markov chain Monte Carlo to approximate posterior summaries.
The key difference between our proposed
sparseVCBART
and the original VCBART model is our use of a global-local prior to model the outputs of each regression tree.

Notation
.
Formally, a regression tree is a pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M})
of a (i) rooted binary decision tree
\mathcal{T}
with
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})
terminal leaf nodes and several non-terminal decision nodes and (ii) a collection of scalar jumps
\mathcal{M}=\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})}\},
one for each leaf.
Each decision node is associated with a decision rule of the form
\{Z_{r}<t\}.

Given
\mathcal{T},
we can associate every
\bm{z}\in[0,1]^{R}
with a single leaf node
\ell(\bm{z};\mathcal{T})
by tracing a path down from the root node that, upon encountering the decision rule
\{Z_{r}<t\}
, proceeds to the left (resp. right) if
z_{r}<t
(resp.
z_{r}\geq t
).
By associating each leaf
\ell
in
\mathcal{T}
with a scalar jump
\mu_{\ell},
the pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M})
represents a piecewise-constant step function.
Given a regression tree
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M})
, let
g(\bm{z};\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M})=\mu_{\ell(\bm{z};\mathcal{T})}
be the evaluation function returning the jump associated with leaf
\ell(\bm{z}).

2.1
Prior Specification

For each
j=0,\ldots,p,
we introduce an ensemble of
M_{j}
regression trees
\mathcal{E}_{j}=\{(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m},\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M_{j}}
and express

\beta_{j}(\bm{z})=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{j}}{g(\bm{z};\mathcal{T}_{m}^{(j)},\mathcal{M}_{m}^{(j)})}.

We denote the collection of all ensembles with
\bm{\mathcal{E}}.

Decision tree prior.
We model individual decision trees
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{(j)}
in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
as
a priori
i.i.d. and further model the trees as
a priori
independent across ensembles.
To sample a random decision tree from the prior, we first use a Galton-Watson branching process to generate the graphical structure (i.e., the arrangement of leaf and decision nodes) and then draw the decision rules associated to each decision node.
To ensure the tree is of finite depth, we calibrate the branching process so that the probability that a node at depth
d
is non-terminal is
0.95\times(1+d)^{-2}.

Having drawn the graphical structure, we draw a decision rule for each non-terminal node in two steps.
For a tree in the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{j},
we select the splitting variable
r\sim\textrm{Multinomial}(\theta_{j1},\ldots,\theta_{jR})
and then, conditionally on
r,
draw the cutting point
t
uniformly.
We specify independent priors for the vectors
\bm{\theta}_{j}:=(\theta_{j1},\ldots,\theta_{jR})\sim\textrm{Dirichlet}(\eta_{j}/R,\ldots,\eta_{j}/R)
with hyperpriors
\eta_{j}/(\eta_{j}+R)\sim\textrm{Beta}\left(1,0.5\right)
.
This prior hierarchy, which was first introduced by
Linero, 2018
, encourages the regression trees in each ensemble to split on only a few effect modifiers, thereby encouraging modifier sparsity.

Global–local jump prior.

The original VCBART model specified independent
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1/(4M_{j})\right)
priors on all the jumps in each ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{j},
which implies the marginal prior
\beta_{j}(\bm{z})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1/4\right).

So, the original VCBART applies the exact same amount of shrinkage to all functions
\beta_{j}(\bm{z}).

Within the Bayesian variable selection literature, it is well-established that
adaptive shrinkage
methods, which shrink smaller signals to zero more aggressively than larger signals, recover underlying sparsity patterns much more effectively than constant shrinkage methods
(
Polson and Scott, 2012
;
Bhadra et al., 2016
;
George and Ročková, 2020
)
.

On this view, to encourage selective shrinkage of the
\beta_{j}(\bm{z})
’s in our high-dimensional setting, we specify regularized horseshoe priors
(
Piironen and Vehtari, 2017b
, RHS;)
on our regression tree jumps.
Specifically, we introduce a shared global parameter
\tau>0
, a slab parameter
c>0
, and local scales
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{p}
and model

\mu^{(j)}_{m,\ell}\mid\lambda_{j},c,\tau\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,s_{j}^{2}\right),

where
s_{j}^{2}=\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\,c^{2}/(c^{2}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}).

All leaves in
\mathcal{E}_{j}
share the same local scale
\lambda_{j}
; when
\lambda_{j}
shrinks toward zero,
every
leaf in that coefficient is pulled to (near) zero, effectively switching off
\beta_{j}(\bm{z})
and yielding covariate-level sparsity.
The global scale
\tau
controls the overall amount of shrinkage across ensembles, adapting to the number of truly active coefficients.
The finite slab
c
prevents over-shrinkage of large signals and stabilizes inference relative to the plain horseshoe prior
(
Piironen and Vehtari, 2017a
;
Piironen and Vehtari, 2017b
)
, especially when signals are strong but sparse.

We endow the local and global scale parameters with independent half-Cauchy priors:
\lambda_{j}\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)
, and
\tau\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,\tau_{0})
.
The slab variance uses the conjugate prior
c^{2}\sim\mathcal{IG}(\nu_{c}/2,\nu_{c}s_{c}^{2}/2)
.
Finally, we specify the conjugate prior
\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{IG}\big({\nu}/{2},\,{\nu\lambda}/{2}\big)
, where
\nu
and
\lambda
are fixed hyperparameters.

2.2
Posterior Computation

We generate posterior draws with a high-level Gibbs sampler that sequentially updates each of
\bm{\mathcal{E}},

\bm{\Theta}\coloneqq\{\bm{\theta}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{p},

\bm{\eta}\coloneqq\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{p},

\bm{\lambda}\coloneqq\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{p},

\tau,

c,
and
\sigma^{2},
conditionally on all the others.
Here, we briefly describe each Gibbs update and defer the full sampler derivation to
Appendix

S2
.
We update each of
\bm{\mathcal{E}},\bm{\Theta},\bm{\eta},
and
\sigma^{2}
using essentially the same sampler as the one developed by
Deshpande et al., 2024
for the original VCBART model.
Compared to that sampler, each iteration of our
sparseVCBART
sampler includes additional steps to update
\bm{\lambda},\tau,
and
c.

Updating
(\mathcal{T}^{(j)},\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m}
).

We update the trees in
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
one at a time and update individual regression trees in two steps.
First, we draw a new decision tree
\mathcal{T}
with a Metropolis-Hastings step that involves randomly growing or pruning the existing tree.
Then, we draw new jumps
\mathcal{M}
conditionally on the new tree decision tree.
This sampling strategy is enabled by the fact that given
\bm{y},\bm{\lambda},c,\sigma^{2}
, and all other trees, the conditional posterior density of a single decision tree
\mathcal{T}
and the conditional distribution of its jumps
\mathcal{M}|\mathcal{T}
are available in closed form (
Equations

S2.4
and
S2.6
).

Updating
\bm{\Theta},\bm{\eta},
and
\sigma^{2}
. We respectively update each vector of splitting probabilities
\bm{\theta}_{j}
and
\sigma^{2}
with standard conjugate updates (
Equations

S2.8
and
S2.11
). We update each
\eta_{j}
with a Metropolis-Hastings step.

Updating
\bm{\lambda}
,
\tau
, and
c
.

It turns out that the conditional posterior density
\pi(\bm{\lambda},\tau,c|\ldots)
factorizes, allowing us to update each parameter separately (
Equation

S2.9
).
We specifically use one-dimensional slice sampling to update each
\lambda_{j}
’s and
\tau.

We draw a new
c^{2}
using a conjugate normal-inverse gamma update (
Equation

S2.10
).

Default Hyperparameter Settings

depends on several hyperparameters: the number of trees

in ensemble

; the scale

for the prior on the global scale

and the inverse-gamma hyperparameters

Finally, we recommend setting

and

In our experiments, we have found that these settings generally yield very accurate results; see

3
Theoretical Results

In this section, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of
sparseVCBART
in a high–dimensional regime where both
p
and
R
grow with the sample size
N
.
Under standard design and regularity conditions, we show that the posterior contracts around the truth at a rate that is nearly minimax–optimal, while adapting to unknown sparsity and smoothness.
We further establish that the posterior consistently identifies which covariates
X_{j}
have non–null effects on
Y
and, for each active covariate, which modifiers
Z_{r}
drive its effect.
Finally, we prove that the posterior distributions of the local shrinkage scales for truly null covariates collapse to zero as
N
diverges.

To study
sparseVCBART
theoretically, we assume the data were generated from a true VCM indexed by a vector of coefficient functions
\bm{\beta}_{0}=(\beta_{0,0},\dots,\beta_{0,p})
and a noise variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
. Collectively, we call
\theta_{0}\coloneqq(\bm{\beta}_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
. We assume that the set of active covariates,
S_{0}\subset\{1,\dots,p\}
, is small, with size
d_{0}:=|S_{0}|\ll p
.
For any inactive covariate
j\notin S_{0}
, we assume that the true function is null (i.e.,
\beta_{0,j}(\bm{Z})\equiv 0
).
For each active covariate
j\in S_{0}
, we assume that its coefficient
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
depends only on a small subset of modifiers
S_{0,j}\subset\{1,\ldots,R\}
of size
s_{0,j}:=|S_{0,j}|\ll R
.
Finally, we assume that for each
j\in S_{0},
the truly non-null function
\beta_{0,j}(\bm{Z})
lies in a particular Hölder class (see Assumption
(A4)
).

Notation.
Because we let the number of covariates grow with
N,
we will use the notation
p_{N}
instead of

For any index set
S,
let
\bm{z}_{S}=(z_{u})_{u\in S}.

We denote the empirical
L_{2}
norm
\|f\|_{N}:=\{N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\bm{Z}_{i})^{2}\}^{1/2}.

With
\alpha>0
, let
s=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
and
\zeta=\alpha-s

, we write

for the mixed derivative.
The Hölder ball of radius

is

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\alpha}\big([0,1]^{R}\big)=\Big\{f:[0,1]^{R}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}:\max_{|\bm{k}|\leq s}\|D^{\bm{k}}f\|_{\infty}\\
+\max_{|\bm{k}|=s}\ \sup_{\bm{x}\neq\bm{y}}\frac{|D^{\bm{k}}f(\bm{x})-D^{\bm{k}}f(\bm{y})|}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{\infty}^{\zeta}}\ \leq\ B\Big\}.\end{split}

3.1
Posterior contraction

To establish posterior contraction, we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

The number of trees per coefficient is uniform and bounded:
M_{j}\equiv M
for all
j
, with
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
2
2

2

Our theoretical results are unaffected if
M
grows slowly (e.g.,
M=\mathcal{O}(\log N)
), since the proofs depend on the total leaf budget per ensemble, not the specific number of trees. Allowing
M
to grow merely changes constants and bookkeeping.

(A2)

The sample
\{\bm{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset[0,1]^{R}
is
k-d–regular
:
there exist constants
0<c_{\mathrm{kd}}\leq C_{\mathrm{kd}}<\infty
such that for any axis-aligned rectangle
A\subset[0,1]^{R}
,

c_{\mathrm{kd}}\,\mathrm{vol}(A)\leq\frac{1}{N}\#\{i:\bm{Z}_{i}\in A\}\leq C_{\mathrm{kd}}\,\mathrm{vol}(A).

(A3)

\max_{i\leq N}\max_{1\leq j\leq p_{N}}|X_{ij}|\leq D<\infty
.

(A4)

\beta_{0,j}\equiv 0\ \ \forall j\notin S_{0},
and for each
j\in S_{0},

\exists\tilde{\beta}_{0,j}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\alpha_{j}}\left([0,1]^{s_{0,j}}\right)
with
0<\alpha_{j}\leq 1
such that
\beta_{0,j}(\bm{z})=\tilde{\beta}_{0,j}(\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}).

(A5)

There exists
0<\xi<\min_{j\in S_{0}}s_{0,j}/(2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}),
such that
\log p_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{\xi})
,
d_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\log N)
, and
R=o(\log N)
.

(A6)

There exist constants
C\geq 1
and
0<c_{\min}\leq c_{\max}<\infty
such that for any

S\subset\{1,\dots,p_{N}\}
with
|S|\leq Cd_{0}
and any arrays
u_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\begin{split}c_{\min}\sum_{j\in S}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{ij}^{2}&\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\sum_{j\in S}X_{ij}u_{ij}\Big)^{2}\\
&\leq c_{\max}\sum_{j\in S}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{ij}^{2}.\end{split}

Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
are basic BART regularity conditions:
(A1)
bounds model complexity, the k-d regularity in
(A2)
ensures good approximation properties for the tree ensembles
(
Ročková and van der Pas, 2019
)
, and
(A3)
provides numerical stability by preventing explosion of the prediction norms.
The high-dimensional regime in
(A5)
allows for sub-exponentially growing
p_{N}
, a strictly more challenging setting than the fixed-
p
regime studied in
Deshpande et al., 2024
.
Our key statistical assumption is the functional restricted-eigenvalue condition
(A6)
, a functional analogue of standard restricted eigenvalue conditions in sparse regression
(
Bickel et al., 2009
, §3)
that precludes pathological collinearity and enables the construction of powerful tests and stable identifiability: it lets us translate empirical function error
\sum_{j\in S}\|\beta_{j}-\beta_{0,j}\|_{N}^{2}
into prediction error
\|\sum_{j\in S}X_{ij}(\beta_{j}-\beta_{0,j})^{2}\|_{N}^{2},
preventing near-collinear designs from masking nonzero effects.
Finally, the smoothness constraint
\alpha_{j}\leq 1
in
(A4)
is an inherent consequence of using piecewise-constant step-functions to approximate the
\beta_{j}(\bm{Z})
’s
(
Jeong and Ročková, 2023
)
; this limitation can be relaxed by using the so-called “soft” decision trees (as in
Linero and Yang, 2018
) or replacing constant jumps with smoother functions (as in
Yee et al., 2025
).

To control model capacity while retaining covariate and modifier-specific adaptivity, we make the following prior assumptions on the tree topology, modifier-split coordinates, leaf values with covariate-level global–local shrinkage, and the noise variance.

(P1)

Each regression tree follows a depth-penalized Galton–Watson process:
a node at depth
m
splits with probability
\gamma^{m}
where
N^{-1}<\gamma<1/2
is fixed.

(P2)

We draw
d\in\{0,1,\dots,p_{N}\}
with
\pi(d)\propto 1/d!
. The global scale
\tau\mid d\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}\!\bigl(0,A_{g,d}\bigr)
with

A_{g,d}=\Theta\left(dp_{N}^{-1}(\log p_{N})^{-2}(\log N)^{-2}\right)
.

(P3)

Let
r_{N}^{2}
denote the target contraction rate (stated later). We use a mildly informative prior

\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{IG}\!\left(\frac{\nu_{N}}{2},\,\beta\right),\quad\nu_{N}=\mathcal{O}(Nr_{N}^{2}),\quad\beta>0\ \text{fixed}.

4.2
A semi-synthetic experiment

Interpersonal conversations can reduce exclusionary attitudes towards outgroups
(
Broockman and Kalla, 2016
;
Williamson et al., 2021
)
.

Kalla and Broockman, 2023
recently evaluated whether the type of conversation affects the reduction of prejudice.
Their study involved canvassers having conversations with voters who were randomly assigned to one of the three intervention arms.
In the first arm, voters were told a story about an immigrant they knew before being asked to discuss a time when they needed support.
The second arm involved only the first part of the full intervention (i.e., the story telling), but not the second.
The third arm was a placebo in which the canvasser and the voter had a short conversation about unrelated topics.

We downloaded the raw data from
Kalla and Broockman, 2023
’s study from the Harvard Dataverse Network.
The data set contains
N=4921
observations and
R=99
effect modifiers that record voter demographics and current political attitudes.
The original data also contains
p=2
binary covariates that encode whether voters received the full or partial intervention arm.
Because this was a randomized trial, the coefficients associated with these covariates in a VCM capture the conditional average treatment effects of both interventions relative to placebo.

To assess
sparseVCBART
’s ability to identify non-null covariate effects, we augmented the data with 18 additional noise covariates generated from a standard Normal distribution.
We found a clear separation between the local scales corresponding to the two original covariates and the additional noise covariates.
Specifically, the posterior median
\lambda_{j}
’s for the intervention indicators exceed 3.5 while the posterior median scales for the null covariates were all less than 1.7; see
Figure

S3.2
.
Such separation is consistent with the conclusions of
Theorem

4
and suggest that both interventions had a non-null effect on the outcome.
Examining the corresponding vectors of the split probabilities
\bm{\theta}_{j}
, we further found that only a handful of effect modifiers drove the heterogeneity of each intervention’s effect.
The most prominent were related to the voter’s support for allowing undocumented immigrants to access public schools: the more supportive the voters were for inclusive schooling, the more effective both narrative interventions were in reducing prejudice; see
Section

S3.3
for a more detailed overview of the data and analysis of effect heterogeneity.

5
Discussion

We have introduced
sparseVCBART
for fitting sparse VCMs with potentially more covariates
p
and/or effect modifiers
R
than observations
N.

Our main theoretical results show that, under mild conditions, (i) the
sparseVCBART
posterior contracts at nearly the minimax-optimal rate that adapts to the underlying sparsity structure and (ii)
sparseVCBART
consistently identifies which modifiers drive the non-null effects and shrinks null effects to zero.
Empirically, the model delivers sparse, interpretable representations and strong predictive performance with coherent posterior uncertainty.

A key practical consideration is the choice of ensemble sizes
M_{j}.

While both models generally benefit from larger ensembles, our preliminary sensitivity analyses (see
Section

S3.2
) suggest that
sparseVCBART
is more robust to a smaller ensemble size.
Beyond determining an optimal ensemble size, possible extensions include sharing variable splitting probabilities across coefficient ensembles, adapting the framework to generalized outcomes (e.g., logistic regression), and establishing formal covariate-selection consistency beyond the shrinkage results presented here.

The proof of Theorem 1 follows the general framework for posterior contraction rates established by

Rationale of sieve construction.
The construction of a sieve is a critical part of proving posterior contraction. At a high level, the sieve is a well-behaved subset of the entire parameter space. Proving that the posterior concentrates on a tiny neighborhood around the truth is too hard to do in the full, infinitely complex model space. The strategy is to first show that the posterior concentrates on the much smaller, more manageable sieve, and then work within that sieve. A good sieve must satisfy two competing goals: (i) It must be
large enough
so that the true function is inside it with very high prior probability, and (ii) it must be
small enough
so that its complexity can be controlled.

The key to
sparseVCBART
theory is adaptation: the final contraction rate depends on the unknown
\alpha_{j}
and the unknown
s_{0,j}
. However, the sieve itself must be constructed without knowing these true values. This is why we use a worst-case rate
\bar{r}_{N}
to define the complexity cap,
L_{N}
(the maximum number of leaves). We need a single leaf budget,
L_{N}
, that is guaranteed to be sufficient for any possible true function that satisfies our assumptions. The number of leaves required to approximate a function gets larger as the function becomes less smooth (smaller
\alpha
) and higher-dimensional (larger
s
). Therefore, the
hardest
possible function to approximate is one with the minimum allowed smoothness (
\alpha_{\min}
) and the maximum possible dimension (
R
).

In
Definition

1
, we ensure our sieve is large enough to contain a good approximation of the true function, no matter what its true
\alpha_{j}
and
s_{0,j}
are.

Definition 1

(Dimension–adaptive sparse sieve)
.

Let

s_{N}=\big\lceil C_{s}\,\log N\big\rceil

for a constant
C_{s}>0
chosen large enough so that
d_{0}\leq s_{N}
for all large
N
.
Fix a minimal smoothness
\alpha_{\min}\in(0,1]
and define the worst–case per–component rate

r_{N,\mathrm{wc}}^{2}\;:=\;(\log N)\,N^{-2\alpha_{\min}/(2\alpha_{\min}+R)}.

Set the envelope rate

\bar{r}_{N}^{2}\;:=\;\frac{s_{N}\log p_{N}}{N}\;+\;s_{N}\,r_{N,\mathrm{wc}}^{2},\qquad L_{N}\;:=\;C_{L}\,\frac{N\,\bar{r}_{N}^{2}}{\log N},

for a sufficiently large constant
C_{L}>0
, and the sieve inactivity threshold

\varepsilon_{N}:={C_{\varepsilon}}/{\log N},\ C_{\varepsilon}>0.

For a set
S\subset\{0,1,\dots,p_{N}\}
of size
|S|=k\leq s_{N}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{N}(S)\;:=\;\left\{(\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}):\begin{array}[]{l}\|\beta_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{N}\quad(j\notin S)\\[2.0pt]
\text{the $M$ trees forming }\beta_{j}\text{ have in total }\leq L_{N}\text{ leaves}\quad(j\in S),\\[2.0pt]
0<\sigma^{2}\leq c_{0}\end{array}\right\}.

The full sieve is the model–selection union

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\star}\;:=\;\bigcup_{k=0}^{s_{N}}\ \bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1,\dots,p_{N}\}\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}_{N}(S).

Remark S1.1
.

The specific structure of
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\star}
, is designed to discard unreasonable functions in three ways, perfectly mirroring the assumptions of our model: The most important step is taking the union over only sparse sets of predictors. This immediately throws away the vast majority of the model space (all non-sparse models). This is justified by the assumption that the true model is sparse (
d_{0}\ll p_{N}
). Next, for the covariates that are considered active, we do not allow their corresponding functions
\beta_{j}
to be infinitely complex. The cap on the total number of leaves,
L_{N}
, prevents overfitting by ensuring the functions remain reasonably simple. Finally, for predictors that are considered inactive, we do not require them to be exactly zero, which is difficult with continuous shrinkage priors. Instead, we only require that they are negligibly small (
\|\beta_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{N}
). This creates a “soft sparsity” that is compatible with the prior and is sufficient for the theoretical arguments.

Lemma S1.4

(Prior mass of the sieve)
.

Assume
(A1)
–
(A5)
and priors
(P1)
–
(P3)

By definition,

We bound

(i) Bounding

Under

\Pi\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}L_{j,m}>L_{N}\right)\ \leq\ \sum_{m=1}^{M}\Pi\!\left(L_{j,m}>\tfrac{L_{N}}{M}\right)\ \leq\ M\,\exp\!\left\{-c_{1}\,\frac{L_{N}}{M}\,\log\left(\frac{L_{N}}{M}\right)\right\},

(S1.24)

for all

Therefore,

for

(S1.29)

Fix
C_{s}>0
large so that, for all large

,

whenever

, which is possible by (

Chernoff bound.

The multiplicative Chernoff bound gives, for all

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\bigl(S_{N}>s_{N}\mid d\bigr)&\leq\ \exp\Bigl(-\frac{(s_{N}-\mu_{N}(d))^{2}}{3\mu_{N}(d)}\Bigr)\\
&\leq\ \exp\Bigl(-c\cdot\frac{s_{N}^{2}}{\mu_{N}(d)}\Bigr)\\
&\leq\exp(-c^{\prime}(\log N)(\log p_{N})),\end{split}

(S1.30)

where the last step uses

when

.

Average over

.
We split on

\Pi(E_{2,N})=\sum_{d=0}^{p_{N}}\pi(d)\mathbb{P}(S_{N}>s_{N}\mid d)\leq\underbrace{\sum_{d\leq D_{N}}\pi(d)\exp(-c^{\prime}(\log N)(\log p_{N}))}_{by\eqref{eq:chernoffbd}}+\sum_{d>D_{N}}\pi(d).

\begin{split}\sum_{d>D_{N}}\pi(d)&\leq\exp\{-D_{N}\log D_{N}+\mathcal{O}(D_{N})\}\\

&\leq\exp\{-c^{\prime\prime\prime}(\log N)(\log p_{N})\},\end{split}

for all large

\Pi(E_{2,N})\leq\exp\{-c_{2}(\log N)(\log p_{N})\}.

The following shell-partitioning approach in

Lemma S1.5

(Covering number of the sieve)

\|\Delta_{m,k}\|_{2}^{2}\ \geq\ c_{\min}N(2^{m}M_{\star}r_{N})^{2}.

Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\varphi_{N}&\leq\sum_{m\geq 0}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{m}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\phi_{m,k}\\
&\leq K_{m}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\min}}{8c_{0}}4^{m}M_{\star}^{2}Nr_{N}^{2}\right)\\
&=\sum_{m\geq 0}\ \exp\!\Big\{\,\log K_{m}-\frac{c_{\min}}{8c_{0}}\,4^{m}M_{\star}^{2}\,Nr_{N}^{2}\Big\}.\end{split}

Applying (
S1.42
),

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\varphi_{N}\ \leq\ \sum_{m\geq 0}\ \exp\!\Big\{\,C_{4}Nr_{N}^{2}+C_{\mathrm{sh}}\,m\log N-\frac{c_{\min}}{8c_{0}}\,4^{m}M_{\star}^{2}\,Nr_{N}^{2}\Big\}.

Pick
M_{\star}
large (depending only on
c_{\min},c_{0},C_{4}
) so that

({c_{\min}}/{8c_{0}})M_{\star}^{2}\ \geq\ C_{4}+2c_{5}

for some
c_{5}>0
. Since
4^{m}\geq 1
and, for
m\geq 1
,
4^{m}Nr_{N}^{2}\gg m\log N
(because
Nr_{N}^{2}\gtrsim(\log N)\log p_{N}
by
(A5)
and
\log p_{N}\to\infty
), the
m\geq 1
terms are exponentially dominated. Thus

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\varphi_{N}\ \leq\ \exp\!\big\{-(C_{4}+2c_{5}-C_{4})Nr_{N}^{2}\big\}\ +\ \sum_{m\geq 1}\exp\!\big\{-c\,4^{m}Nr_{N}^{2}\big\}\ \leq\ \exp\{-c_{5}\,Nr_{N}^{2}\}

for large
N
(absorbing the tail sum into the constant), which proves the desired Type I bound.

Type II error.

Fix any
\theta\in\Theta_{N}
and let
m\geq 0
be the shell index such that

2^{m}M_{\star}r_{N}<\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|_{N}\leq 2^{m+1}M_{\star}r_{N}
.
Choose
k
so that
d_{n}(\theta,\theta_{m,k})\leq\gamma=r_{N}/8
.
By definition of
d_{n}
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\Delta(\theta,\theta_{m,k})\|_{2}\ \leq\ \gamma\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\|\Delta(\theta,\theta_{m,k})\|_{2}\ \leq\ \gamma\,\sqrt{N}.

(S1.44)

Furthermore, by (
S1.41
) and the choice of
m
,

\|\Delta(\theta,\theta_{0})\|_{2}\ \geq\ \sqrt{c_{\min}}\,2^{m}M_{\star}r_{N}\sqrt{N}.

Therefore, by the triangle inequality and (
S1.44
),

\|\Delta_{m,k}\|_{2}\ =\ \|\Delta(\theta_{m,k},\theta_{0})\|_{2}\ \geq\ \|\Delta(\theta,\theta_{0})\|_{2}-\|\Delta(\theta,\theta_{m,k})\|_{2}\ \geq\ \Big(\sqrt{c_{\min}}\,2^{m}M_{\star}-\tfrac{1}{8}\Big)\,r_{N}\sqrt{N}.

Plugging this into (
S1.43
) gives

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}(1-\varphi_{N})\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\|\Delta_{m,k}\|_{2}^{2}}{8c_{0}}\Big)\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{8c_{0}}\,\big(\sqrt{c_{\min}}\,2^{m}M_{\star}-\tfrac{1}{8}\big)^{2}\,Nr_{N}^{2}\Big).

Enlarging
M_{\star}
if necessary so that
\frac{1}{8c_{0}}\,(\sqrt{c_{\min}}\,M_{\star}-1/8)^{2}\geq c_{5}
, and using
2^{m}\geq 1
, we obtain

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{N}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\theta}(1-\varphi_{N})\ \leq\ \exp\{-c_{5}\,Nr_{N}^{2}\}.

Combining the Type I and Type II bounds completes the proof.
∎

S1.4
Proof of near-minimax optimality of the contraction rate

Our near-minimax lower bound follows the standard information–theoretic template used for high-dimensional nonparametric models: we build a large, well-separated finite subset of the parameter space and control the pairwise Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences so that Fano and Assouad inequalities apply. This strategy goes back to classical treatments in
Tsybakov, 2009
and, in the sparse additive setting, is carried out in closely related forms by
Koltchinskii and Yuan, 2010
and
Raskutti et al., 2009
. Following a similar style of proofs as in these works, the lower bound naturally splits into a selection piece, reflecting the combinatorial difficulty of identifying an active subset among
p_{N}
candidates (giving a
d_{0}\log(p_{N}/d_{0})/N
term), and a nonparametric piece, reflecting the functional difficulty of estimating
s-
dimensional
\alpha-
Hölder components (giving
N^{-2\alpha/(2\alpha+s)}
terms, possibly heterogeneous across coordinates).

We need the following lemma to show minimax optimality of the rate
r_{N}
. It is the key local building block that lets us realize, inside the Hölder class, tiny “on/off” perturbations supported on
s_{0,j}-
dimensional cubes of side
h
, with amplitude
a\asymp h^{\alpha_{j}}
, such that their empirical
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})
energy scales like
a^{2}h^{s_{0,j}}.
This guarantees separation – neighboring hypotheses that differ by flipping a single bump are separated by
\|f_{j,1}-f_{j,0}\|_{N}^{2}\asymp h^{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}
and small KL.

Lemma S1.7

(Modifier–sparse bump construction under k-d regularity)
.

Fix an active predictor
j
with modifier set
S_{0,j}\subset\{1,\dots,R\}
of size
s:=s_{0,j}\in\{1,\dots,R\}
and smoothness
0<\alpha_{j}\leq 1
. Assume
(A2)
(k-d regularity) holds on the design
\{\bm{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset[0,1]^{R}
.
There exist constants
c,C\in(0,\infty)
(independent of
N
) and, for any bandwidth
h\in(0,h_{0}]
with
h_{0}
small enough, one can construct a pair of functions

f_{j,0}\equiv 0,\quad f_{j,1}(\bm{z})=a\,\varphi\Bigl(\frac{\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x}{h}\Bigr),\quad\bm{z}\in[0,1]^{R},

with the following properties:

(i)

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,1]^{s})
10
10

10

C^{\infty}_{c}([0,1]^{s})
denotes the class of infinitely differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
whose support lies in
(0,1)^{s}
, considered on
[0,1]^{s}
,
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and its Hölder seminorms up to order
\alpha_{j}
are bounded by a constant depending only on
\alpha_{j},s
.

(ii)

For the amplitude
a:=c\,h^{\alpha_{j}}
, one has
f_{j,1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha_{j}}_{B}([0,1]^{R})
, with Hölder ball radius
B
independent of
N
(and of
h
for
h\leq h_{0}
).

(iii)

There exists a center
x\in(0,1)^{s}
(depending on the design and
h
) such that

c\,a^{2}h^{s}\ \leq\ \|f_{j,1}-f_{j,0}\|_{N}^{2}\ \leq\ C\,a^{2}h^{s}.

Consequently, choosing
h\asymp N^{-1/(2\alpha_{j}+s)}
yields

\|f_{j,1}-f_{j,0}\|_{N}^{2}\ \asymp\ N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s}}.

Proof.

Proving (i).
Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((-1,1))
be a standard bump with
\psi\geq 0
,
\psi(0)=1
, and
\|\psi\|_{\infty}=1
. A standard one-dimensional
C^{\infty}
bump can be constructed as

\psi_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}\right),&\ |t|<1\\
0,&\ |t|\geq 1.\end{cases}

Note that
\textrm{supp}(\psi_{0})=[-1,1],
we can normalize to have a peak at
1
:
\psi(t)=\psi_{0}(t)/\psi_{0}(0)=e\psi_{0}(t),
so
\psi(0)=1
and
\|\psi\|_{\infty}=1.
We set

\varphi(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{s}\psi(u_{\ell}),

then
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
with
\textrm{supp}(\varphi)\subset[-1,1]^{s},
so after translation,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\infty}((0,1)^{s})
. Trivially, it also satisfies
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Because
\psi
is smooth with bounded derivatives on a compact support, all partial derivatives of
\varphi
are bounded. For
0<\alpha\leq 1
the
\alpha-
Hölder semi-norm of
\varphi
is finite and bounded by a constant depending only on
s
and bounds on
\psi^{\prime}:

|\varphi(\bm{u})-\varphi(\bm{v})|\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|_{2}\ \implies[\varphi]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\text{diam}([-1,1]^{s})^{1-\alpha}.

Thus
\varphi
satisfies (i) with constants depending only on
s
and
\alpha_{j}
.

Proving (ii).
Because
\varphi
has a compact support contained in
(0,1)^{s},
the function
\bm{z}\mapsto\varphi((\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x)/h)
is supported in the cube
\{\bm{z}:\|\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x\|_{\infty}<h\}\subset(0,1)^{s},
which holds since
x\in(h,1-h)^{s}.
Using
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1,

\|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\bm{z}\in[0,1]^{R}}a|\varphi((\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x)/h)|\leq a=ch^{\alpha_{j}}\leq ch_{0}^{\alpha_{j}}.

Thus, if we choose
c\leq B/h_{0}^{\alpha_{j}},
then
\|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{\infty}\leq B.
Now, for any
\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{R},
we set
\bm{u}=(\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x)/h
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{S_{0,j}}-x)/h.
Then,

\displaystyle\left|f_{j,1}^{(x)}(\bm{z})-f_{j,1}^{(x)}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=a|\varphi(\bm{u})-\varphi(\bm{v})|

\displaystyle\leq aK_{\alpha_{j}}\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|_{2}^{\alpha_{j}}

\displaystyle=aK_{\alpha_{j}}h^{-\alpha_{j}}\|\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-\bm{z}^{\prime}_{S_{0,j}}\|_{2}^{\alpha_{j}}.

Since
\|\bm{z}_{S_{0.j}}-\bm{z}^{\prime}_{S_{0,j}}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}\|_{2},

\frac{\left|f_{j,1}^{(x)}(\bm{z})-f_{j,1}^{(x)}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\right|}{\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{\alpha_{j}}}\leq aK_{\alpha_{j}}h^{-\alpha_{j}}=cK_{\alpha_{j}}.

Taking the supremum over
\bm{z}\neq\bm{z}^{\prime}
yields
\left[f_{j,1}^{(x)}\right]_{C^{0,\alpha_{j}}}\leq cK_{\alpha_{j}}.
So, if we also choose
c\leq B/K_{\alpha_{j}},
we obtain
\left[f_{j,1}^{(x)}\right]_{C^{0,\alpha_{j}}}\leq B.

Thus, for any
c\leq\min\{B/h_{0}^{\alpha_{j}},B/K_{\alpha_{j}}\}
we can ensure
\|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{\infty}\leq B
and
\left[f_{j,1}^{(x)}\right]_{C^{0,\alpha_{j}}}\leq B.
This proves (ii).

Proving (iii).
It remains to show the empirical
L_{2}
energy bound (iii). By k-d regularity
(A2)
, there exist constants
0<c_{\mathrm{kd}}\leq C_{\mathrm{kd}}<\infty
such that for any axis-aligned rectangle
A\subset[0,1]^{R}
,

c_{\mathrm{kd}}\,\mathrm{vol}(A)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{N}\#\{i:\ \bm{Z}_{i}\in A\}\ \leq\ C_{\mathrm{kd}}\,\mathrm{vol}(A).

For our
f_{j,1}^{(x)}
, the support is
\Omega_{x}:=\{\bm{z}:\ \|\bm{z}_{S_{0,j}}-x\|_{\infty}\leq h\}\times[0,1]^{R-s}
, with
\mathrm{vol}(\Omega_{x})=(2h)^{s}
. Hence

\|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a^{2}\,\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{\bm{Z}_{i,S_{0,j}}-x}{h}\right)=a^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:\ \bm{Z}_{i,S_{0,j}}\in x+[-h,h]^{s}}\ \varphi^{2}\left(\frac{\bm{Z}_{i,S_{0,j}}-x}{h}\right).

By the boundedness of
\varphi^{2}
above by
1
, and its positivity on a smaller cube (say on
[-1/2,1/2]^{s}
), one has

c^{\prime}\,a^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\#\{i:\ \bm{Z}_{i,S_{0,j}}\in[x-h/2,x+h/2]^{s}\}\ \leq\ \|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{N}^{2}\ \leq\ C^{\prime}\,a^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\#\{i:\ \bm{Z}_{i,S_{0,j}}\in[x-h,x+h]^{s}\},

for some
0<c^{\prime}\leq C^{\prime}<\infty
. Applying k-d regularity with the corresponding rectangles
A_{x}
gives

c\,a^{2}h^{s}\ \leq\ \|f_{j,1}^{(x)}\|_{N}^{2}\ \leq\ C\,a^{2}h^{s},

uniformly over
x\in\{x\in(h,1-h)^{s}:x\ \text{on a regular grid of mesh }h\}
, proving (iii). Taking
x
to be any grid point completes the construction. Finally, setting
h\asymp N^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha_{j}+s}}
yields the displayed rate.
∎

Proof overview of the near-minimax theorem.

We now move on to prove our near-minimaxity result. To that endeavor, we first split the domain into two disjoint regions via smooth windows: one used purely for selection and the other for nonparametric difficulty. On the selection region, we assign constant signals of size
a_{N}
to a Hamming-separated family of supports
T
via a Gilbert-Varshamov code. With
a_{N}^{2}\asymp\log|\mathcal{V}|/[N(d_{0}-m)],
the pairwise KL’s are small while the
L_{2}
separation is of order
(d_{0}-m)a_{N}^{2},
and Fano’s inequality yields the
d_{0}\log(p_{N}/d_{0})/N
contribution. On the nonparametric region, for a fixed subset
S^{\star}
of
m
active predictors we place multi-bump codes: each predictor
j\in S^{\star}
carries a grid of disjoint
s_{0,j}-
dimensional bumps at bandwidth
h_{j}\asymp N^{-1/(2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j})};
toggling a single bump changes the mean by size
\asymp N^{-1/2}
with uniformly small KL. Assouad’s lemma over this hypercube then yields the remaining part of the minimaxity bound.

\mathrm{KL}\big(P_{(T,\bar{\theta})},P_{(T^{\prime},\bar{\theta})}\big)\ \leq\ \frac{c_{\max}}{2\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\,N\,L_{\mathrm{sel}}\big(\bm{\beta}^{(T,\bar{\theta})},\bm{\beta}^{(T^{\prime},\bar{\theta})}\big)\ \leq\ C\,N\,(d_{0}-m)\,a_{N}^{2}.

Choose

a_{N}^{2}\ :=\ \frac{\kappa}{C}\cdot\frac{\log|\mathcal{V}|}{N\,(d_{0}-m)}\qquad\text{with }\ \kappa\in(0,1/8).

(S1.56)

Then the average pairwise KL is
\leq\kappa\log|\mathcal{V}|
.
By Fano’s lemma (e.g.,
Tsybakov, 2009
) and (
S1.53
),

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\sup_{T\in\mathcal{V}}\ \mathbb{E}L_{\mathrm{sel}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};T)\ \geq\ c\cdot\frac{\log|\mathcal{V}|}{N}\ \geq\ c\,\frac{d_{0}\log(p_{N}/d_{0})}{N},

using (
S1.50
).

Nonparametric term via Assouad.

Fix
\bar{T}\in\mathcal{V}
. Consider the hypercube indexed by all bump bits

\Theta:=\prod_{j\in S^{\star}}\{0,1\}^{M_{j}},\qquad D:=\sum_{j\in S^{\star}}M_{j}.

For neighbors
\theta
and
\theta^{(j,x)}
that differ only in bit
(j,x)
, we have

\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{(\bar{T},\theta)}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{(\bar{T},\theta^{(j,x)}}=\begin{cases}g_{j,x},&\ j^{\prime}=j,\\
0,&\ j^{\prime}\neq j.\end{cases}

Hence,

\rho_{j,x}^{2}\ :=\ L_{\mathrm{np}}\big(\bm{\beta}^{(\bar{T},\theta)},\bm{\beta}^{(\bar{T},\theta^{(j,x)})}\big)=\|g_{j,x}\|_{N,\Omega_{\mathrm{np}}}^{2}\ \asymp\ a_{j}^{2}h_{j}^{s_{0,j}}\ =\kappa_{j}^{2}h_{j}^{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}},

(S1.57)

by (
S1.51
). Moreover, by (
S1.54
),

\mathrm{KL}\big(P_{(\bar{T},\theta)},P_{(\bar{T},\theta^{(j,x)})}\big)\ \leq\ \frac{c_{\max}}{2\underline{\sigma}^{2}}\,N\,\rho_{j,x}^{2}\ \lesssim\ N\,h_{j}^{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}.

(S1.58)

Choose

h_{j}\ :=\ c_{j}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}},\qquad a_{j}:=\kappa_{j}\,h_{j}^{\alpha_{j}},

(S1.59)

with
c_{j}>0
and
\kappa_{j}\in(0,1]
small enough so that the RHS of (
S1.58
) is bounded by a fixed
\kappa_{0}<(\log 2)/8
for all
j
and
N
. Then

\rho_{j,x}^{2}\ \asymp\ h_{j}^{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}\ \asymp\ N^{-1},\qquad M_{j}\ \asymp\ h_{j}^{-s_{0,j}}\ \asymp\ N^{\frac{s_{0,j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}.

(S1.60)

By Assouad’s lemma for nonhomogeneous coordinates (see, e.g.,
Tsybakov, 2009
), the uniform neighbor KL bound implies that for any estimator,

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathbb{E}L_{\mathrm{np}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\theta)\ \geq\ c\sum_{j\in S^{\star}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{j,h_{j}}}\rho_{j,x}^{2}\ \asymp\ \sum_{j\in S^{\star}}M_{j}\,h_{j}^{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}\ \asymp\ \sum_{j\in S^{\star}}h_{j}^{2\alpha_{j}}\ \asymp\ \sum_{j\in S^{\star}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}.

Hence

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathbb{E}L_{\mathrm{np}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}};\theta)\ \geq\ c\,\sum_{j\in S^{\star}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}.

(S1.61)

Since
|S^{\star}|=m=\lfloor d_{0}/2\rfloor
, by choosing
S^{\star}
as the
m
active indices with the largest

N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}
among the
d_{0}
active predictors,

\sum_{j\in S^{\star}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}\geq\frac{m}{d_{0}}\sum_{j\in S_{0}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2d_{0}}\right)\sum_{j\in S_{0}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}.

(S1.62)

Combining (
S1.55
), the selection bound and (
S1.61
)–(
S1.62
),

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\ \sup_{(T,\theta)}\mathbb{E}L(\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\beta}^{(T,\theta)})\ \geq\ c\Bigg\{\frac{d_{0}\log(p_{N}/d_{0})}{N}+\sum_{j\in S_{0}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}\Bigg\}.

Finally, by (
S1.46
),

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\ \sup_{\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\big\|_{N}^{2}\ \geq\ c\,\inf_{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}\ \sup_{(T,\theta)}\mathbb{E}L(\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\bm{\beta}^{(T,\theta)})\ \geq\ c\Bigg\{\frac{d_{0}\log(p_{N}/d_{0})}{N}+\sum_{j\in S_{0}}N^{-\frac{2\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j}}}\Bigg\}.

Under
(A4)
,
d_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\log N)
and
\log p_{N}\to\infty
, so
\log(p_{N}/d_{0})\asymp\log p_{N}
, yielding the near-minimax statement of the theorem up to universal constants.
∎

S1.5
Proof of modifier selection consistency

Proof of Theorem 3.

Recall that we assume the split–coordinate prior

\bm{\theta}_{j}\,\sim\,\mathrm{Dir}\!\left({\eta_{j}}/{R},\dots,{\eta_{j}}/{R}\right),\quad\eta_{j}\sim\pi(\eta)\propto(R+\eta)^{-(R+1)},

independent of the other prior blocks, and we write
s:=s_{0,j}
and
\alpha:=\alpha_{j}\in(0,1]
.
We prove that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\Pi(\sum_{r\notin S_{0,j}}\theta_{jr}>\varepsilon\mid\bm{Y})\to 0

in
P_{\theta_{0}}
–probability.

Approximation budget and waste inflation.

Let
T_{j}
be the total number of internal splits across the
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
trees in the ensemble for
\beta_{j}
,
and let
n_{jr}
be the number of splits on modifier
r\in\{1,\dots,R\}
so that
\sum_{r=1}^{R}n_{jr}=T_{j}
.
Define the
waste fraction

\delta_{j}\ :=\ \frac{1}{T_{j}}\sum_{r\notin S_{0,j}}n_{jr}\in[0,1],\qquad T_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}}\ :=\ \sum_{r\in S_{0,j}}n_{jr}\ =\ (1-\delta_{j})\,T_{j}.

Only splits along
S_{0,j}
refine the function along its intrinsic
s
–dimensional argument.
Let
m_{u}:=\sum_{\text{nodes}}\mathbbm{1}\{\text{node splits on }u\}
be the number of splits placed (across the ensemble)
on coordinate
u\in S_{0,j}
, so that
\sum_{u\in S_{0,j}}m_{u}=T_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}}
.
The common refinement of all axis–aligned cuts along the
s
active coordinates induces at most

K_{\mathrm{eff}}\ \leq\ \prod_{u\in S_{0,j}}(m_{u}+1)\ \leq\ \Big(1+\tfrac{T_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}}}{s}\Big)^{s}\ \leq\ C\,(1+T_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}})^{s}

distinct cells when projected onto the intrinsic space; the inequalities follow by AM–GM.
Hence, for any partition induced by an ensemble with waste fraction
\delta_{j}
,

K_{\mathrm{eff}}\ \lesssim\ (1-\delta_{j})^{s}T_{j}^{s}

up to an absolute multiplicative constant.
For
\alpha
–Hölder functions on
[0,1]^{s}
, the best empirical
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})
step–function
approximation error with
K
cells satisfies

\inf_{\#\text{cells}\leq K}\|f-\Pi f\|_{N}\gtrsim K^{-\alpha/s}

(e.g.,
Davydov et al., 2020
).
Combining the two displays yields, for
T_{j}
large,

\inf_{\Pi:\,\text{waste}=\delta_{j}}\ \big\|\beta_{0,j}-\Pi(\beta_{0,j})\big\|_{N}\ \gtrsim\ K_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-\alpha/s}\ \gtrsim\ \big((1-\delta_{j})^{s}T_{j}^{s}\big)^{-\alpha/s}\ =\ (1-\delta_{j})^{-\alpha}\,T_{j}^{-\alpha}.

(S1.63)

Let the adaptive target rate be

r_{jN,\mathrm{ad}}:=(\log N)^{1/2}N^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+s)}.

By (
S1.63
), any ensemble achieving error
\lesssim r_{jN,\mathrm{ad}}

must satisfy

T_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}}\ =\ (1-\delta_{j})T_{j}\ \gtrsim\ r_{jN,\mathrm{ad}}^{-1/\alpha}\ =\ N^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+s}}(\log N)^{-1/(2\alpha)}.

(S1.64)

Fix a deterministic threshold

\underline{L}\ :=\ \Big\lfloor c_{L}\,N^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+s}}(\log N)^{-1/(2\alpha)}\Big\rfloor\qquad\text{with }c_{L}>0\text{ small, so that }\ \underline{L}\to\infty.

By Theorem 1, for any fixed constant
C>0
,

\Pi\big(\|\beta_{j}-\beta_{0,j}\|_{N}\leq C\,r_{jN,\mathrm{ad}}\ \bigm|\ \bm{Y}\big)\ \to\ 1\ \ \text{in $P_{\theta_{0}}$–probability.}

On that high–probability contraction event, (
S1.64
) implies

\Pi\big(T_{j}<\underline{L}\ \bigm|\ \bm{Y}\big)\ \to\ 0\qquad\text{in $P_{\theta_{0}}$–probability.}

(S1.65)

Next, (
S1.63
) quantifies
waste inflation
.
For any
\delta\in(0,1/2]
, Bernoulli’s inequality gives

(1-\delta)^{-\alpha}\geq 1+\alpha\delta.

Hence, for some
c_{\star}>0
(independent of
N
),

\inf_{\Pi:\ \text{waste}=\delta}\ \|\beta_{0,j}-\Pi(\beta_{0,j})\|_{N}\ \geq\ (1+c_{\star}\delta)\cdot\inf_{\Pi:\ \text{waste}=0}\ \|\beta_{0,j}-\Pi(\beta_{0,j})\|_{N}.

Taking
\delta=\varepsilon/2
shows that ensembles with
\delta_{j}\geq\varepsilon/2
incur at least a fixed
multiplicative loss in approximation, and therefore they cannot attain the adaptive rate
r_{jN,\mathrm{ad}}

for all large
N
. By posterior contraction, we conclude

\Pi\big(\delta_{j}>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\ \bigm|\ \bm{Y}\big)\ \to\ 0\qquad\text{in $P_{\theta_{0}}$–probability.}

(S1.66)

Posterior of
\bm{\theta}_{j}
given split counts.

Conditional on
\{n_{jr}\}
and
\eta_{j}
, the Dirichlet posterior is

\bm{\theta}_{j}\ \big|\ \{n_{jr}\},\eta_{j}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Dir}\Big(n_{j1}+\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R},\dots,n_{jR}+\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R}\Big).

Let

A\ :=\ \sum_{r\notin S_{0,j}}\Big(n_{jr}+\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R}\Big),\qquad B\ :=\ \sum_{r\in S_{0,j}}\Big(n_{jr}+\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R}\Big),\qquad A+B=T_{j}+\eta_{j},

and define the irrelevant–mass random variable

S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}:=\sum_{r\notin S_{0,j}}\theta_{jr}\mid\{n_{jr}\},\eta_{j}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(A,B).

On the event
\{\delta_{j}\leq\varepsilon/2\}
,

A\ \leq\ \frac{\eta_{j}(R-s)}{R}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,T_{j},\qquad B\ \geq\ \frac{\eta_{j}s}{R}+\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)\,T_{j}.

Hence, for
T_{j}\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}\mid\{n_{jr}\},\eta_{j}\big]=\frac{A}{A+B}\ \leq\ \frac{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}T_{j}+\eta_{j}}{T_{j}}\ =\ \frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\eta_{j}}{T_{j}},

and, using
AB\leq(A+B)^{2}/4
,

\mathrm{Var}\big[S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}\mid\{n_{jr}\},\eta_{j}\big]=\frac{AB}{(A+B)^{2}(A+B+1)}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{4(T_{j}+\eta_{j}+1)}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{4T_{j}}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. On the event
\{T_{j}\geq\max(\underline{L},\,4\eta_{j}/\varepsilon)\}
we have

\mathbb{E}[S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}\mid\cdot]\leq 3\varepsilon/4
, and Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}>\varepsilon\ \Bigm|\ \{n_{jr}\},\eta_{j}\Big)\ \leq\ \frac{\mathrm{Var}[S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}\mid\cdot]}{(\varepsilon-\mathbb{E}[S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}\mid\cdot])^{2}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{4T_{j}}\cdot\frac{1}{(\varepsilon/4)^{2}}\ \leq\ \frac{C}{\underline{L}}.

(S1.67)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pi\Big(S_{j}^{\mathrm{(irr)}}>\varepsilon\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)\

\displaystyle\leq\ \Pi\Big(\delta_{j}>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)+\Pi\Big(T_{j}<\underline{L}\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)+\Pi\Big(T_{j}<\tfrac{4\eta_{j}}{\varepsilon}\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)+\frac{C}{\underline{L}}.

By (
S1.66
) and (
S1.65
), the first two probabilities vanish in
P_{\theta_{0}}
–probability,
and the last term vanishes because
\underline{L}\to\infty
.

It remains to show that
\Pi(T_{j}<4\eta_{j}/\varepsilon\mid\bm{Y})\to 0
in
P_{\theta_{0}}
–probability.
To this end, condition on the counts
\{n_{jr}\}
(equivalently, on
T_{j}=\sum_{r}n_{jr}
) and examine the
posterior
tail of
\eta_{j}
.
Under the conditional independence of split variables across internal nodes given
(\bm{\theta}_{j},\eta_{j})
and of topology/cutpoints, the Dirichlet–multinomial marginal for the counts satisfies

p(\{n_{jr}\}\mid\eta_{j})\ \propto\ \frac{\Gamma(\eta_{j})}{\Gamma(\eta_{j}+T_{j})}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\Gamma\!\big(n_{jr}+\eta_{j}/R\big)}{\Gamma(\eta_{j}/R)}.

Using Stirling’s bounds, for large
T_{j}
there exists
C_{1}>0
such that

\frac{\Gamma(\eta_{j})}{\Gamma(\eta_{j}+T_{j})}\ \leq\ C_{1}\,T_{j}^{-\eta_{j}},\qquad\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\Gamma\!\big(n_{jr}+\eta_{j}/R\big)}{\Gamma(\eta_{j}/R)}\ \leq\ C_{1}\,(1+\eta_{j})^{C_{1}R}.

Combining with the prior density
\pi(\eta_{j})\propto(R+\eta_{j})^{-(R+1)}
, the
posterior
tail (conditional on
\{n_{jr}\}
) obeys

\pi(\eta_{j}\mid\{n_{jr}\})\ \lesssim\ T_{j}^{-\eta_{j}}\,(1+\eta_{j})^{-2},

for
\eta_{j}
large. Therefore, for any
c_{0}>0
,

\Pi\big(\eta_{j}>c_{0}\log T_{j}\ \bigm|\ \{n_{jr}\}\big)\ \leq\ \int_{c_{0}\log T_{j}}^{\infty}T_{j}^{-\eta}\,(1+\eta)^{-2}\,d\eta\ \leq\ \exp\!\big\{-c_{0}(\log T_{j})^{2}\big\}.

Since
\underline{L}\to\infty
and
T_{j}\geq\underline{L}
with probability
\to 1
by (
S1.65
), we obtain

\Pi\Big(\eta_{j}>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}\,\underline{L}\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)\ \to\ 0\quad\text{in $P_{\theta_{0}}$–probability,}

whence

\Pi(T_{j}<4\eta_{j}/\varepsilon\mid\bm{Y})\to 0
.
Putting the pieces together gives

\Pi\Big(\sum_{r\notin S_{0,j}}\theta_{jr}>\varepsilon\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)\ \longrightarrow\ 0\qquad\text{in $P_{\theta_{0}}$–probability,}

which proves the theorem.
∎

S1.6
Proof of local-scale consistency

Proof of Theorem 4.

Fix an inactive coefficient
j\notin S_{0}
, so
\beta_{0,j}\equiv 0
.
Let
L:=L_{j}
be the total number of terminal nodes across the
M
trees of
the
j
th ensemble and let
S:=S_{j}=\sum_{\ell,m}(\mu^{(j)}_{\ell,m})^{2}
be the
sum of squared leaf values. Throughout we condition on all other ensembles, and on
(\tau,c^{2})
.
By assumption, under the posterior there exist deterministic constants

0<\underline{\tau}\leq\bar{\tau}<\infty
and
0<\underline{c}^{2}\leq\bar{c}^{2}<\infty
such that

\Pi(\,\underline{\tau}\leq\tau\leq\bar{\tau},\ \underline{c}^{2}\leq c^{2}\leq\bar{c}^{2}\mid\bm{Y})\to 1
.
We work on this event, and all constants below may depend on
(\underline{\tau},\bar{\tau},\underline{c}^{2},\bar{c}^{2})
but not on
N
.

Under the regularized horseshoe leaf prior, each leaf value

\mu\equiv\mu_{\ell,m}^{(j)}
is Gaussian with variance

\text{Var}(\mu\mid\tau,\lambda,c^{2})=\frac{s^{2}(\tau,\lambda,c^{2})}{M},\quad s^{2}(\tau,\lambda,c^{2})=\frac{\tau^{2}\lambda^{2}c^{2}}{c^{2}+\tau^{2}\lambda^{2}}\in(0,c^{2}).

Hence, up to a factor independent of
\lambda
,

\prod_{\ell,m}\phi\Big(\mu_{\ell,m}^{(j)};0,\,\frac{s^{2}}{M}\Big)~\propto~\{s^{2}\}^{-L/2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{M\,S}{2s^{2}}\Big\}.

(S1.68)

Multiplying (
S1.68
) by the half–Cauchy prior

\pi(\lambda)\propto(1+\lambda^{2})^{-1}\mathbbm{1}\{\lambda>0\}
gives the conditional posterior kernel in
\lambda
.

Set
v:=s^{2}(\tau,\lambda,c^{2})\in(0,c^{2})
and compute the change of variables
\lambda\mapsto v
.
Solving

v=\dfrac{\tau^{2}\lambda^{2}c^{2}}{c^{2}+\tau^{2}\lambda^{2}}

for
\lambda
gives

\lambda^{2}=\frac{v\,c^{2}}{\tau^{2}(c^{2}-v)},\qquad\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{v}{\tau^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{c^{2}-v}}.

Differentiating,

\frac{d}{dv}\lambda^{2}=\frac{c^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\cdot\frac{c^{2}}{(c^{2}-v)^{2}},\qquad\Big|\frac{d\lambda}{dv}\Big|=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\frac{d\lambda^{2}}{dv}=\frac{c^{3}}{2\tau}\,\frac{v^{-1/2}}{(c^{2}-v)^{3/2}}.

The half–Cauchy prior can be written as

\pi(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\pi(1+\lambda^{2})}=\frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{\tau^{2}(c^{2}-v)}{\tau^{2}(c^{2}-v)+vc^{2}},

so that

\pi(\lambda)\,\Big|\frac{d\lambda}{dv}\Big|=\frac{c^{3}\tau}{\pi}\cdot\frac{v^{-1/2}}{(c^{2}-v)^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau^{2}(c^{2}-v)+vc^{2}}.

On
v\in(0,c^{2}/2]
, the factor
\big[(c^{2}-v)^{1/2}\big]^{-1}\big[\tau^{2}(c^{2}-v)+vc^{2}\big]^{-1}
is bounded above and below by finite positive constants that depend only on
(\underline{\tau},\bar{\tau},\underline{c}^{2},\bar{c}^{2})
. Consequently, there exist
0<b_{1}\leq b_{2}<\infty
such that for all
v\in(0,c^{2}/2]
,

b_{1}\,v^{-1/2}\ \leq\ \pi(\lambda)\Big|\frac{d\lambda}{dv}\Big|\ \leq\ b_{2}\,v^{-1/2}.

(S1.69)

Combining (
S1.68
) and (
S1.69
), for some
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}<\infty
,

C_{1}\,v^{-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\,\exp\!\Big\{-\frac{MS}{2v}\Big\}\ \leq\ \pi(v\mid\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot})\ \leq\ C_{2}\,v^{-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\,\exp\!\Big\{-\frac{MS}{2v}\Big\},\quad 0<v\leq\frac{c^{2}}{2}.

(S1.70)

Let
U:=MS/(2v)
(so
v=SM/(2U)
). Since
v\in(0,c^{2})
, we have
U\in\big(MS/(2c^{2}),\,\infty\big)
. Using
dv=-(SM/2)U^{-2}\,dU
, the bounds (
S1.70
) imply that, for
U\geq MS/c^{2}
(equivalently
v\leq c^{2}/2
),

\tilde{C}_{1}\,U^{k-1}e^{-U}\ \leq\ \pi(U\mid\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot})\ \leq\ \tilde{C}_{2}\,U^{k-1}e^{-U},\qquad k:=\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{2},

(S1.71)

for some finite
0<\tilde{C}_{1}\leq\tilde{C}_{2}<\infty
(depending only on
(\underline{\tau},\bar{\tau},\underline{c}^{2},\bar{c}^{2})
). Hence the conditional posterior of
U
is sandwiched by
\mathrm{Gamma}(k,1)
densities on
[MS/c^{2},\infty)
.

Fix any
K>1
. Since
v=MS/(2U)
, the event
\{v>K\,MS/L\}
is equivalent to

\{U<L/(2K)\}
.

Using a Chernoff bound for
G_{k}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(k,1)
(
\mathbb{E}G_{k}=k
) yields constants
c_{0}=c_{0}(K)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Big(G_{k}\leq\frac{L}{2K}\Big)\ \leq\ \exp\{-c_{0}\,L\},\qquad\text{for all }k=\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

(S1.72)

Because
\lambda
increases with
v
, the tail event
\{\lambda>C^{\prime}\sqrt{MS/L}\}
is implied by
\{v>KMS/L\}.
So, controlling
\Pi(v>KMS/L\mid\mu_{\cdot,\cdot}^{(j)})
gives us a uniform tail bound for
\lambda,
which then integrates to the posterior bound on
\lambda.

We now control
\Pi\big(v>K\,MS/L\mid\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}\big)
via two cases:

(1)

If
{L}/{(2K)}<{MS}/{c^{2}}
, then
U\geq MS/c^{2}>{L}/{(2K)}
almost surely, hence

\Pi\big(U<L/(2K)\mid\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}\big)=0
.

This holds since the map
v\mapsto U=MS/v
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty),
so the smallest value
U
can take occurs at the largest admissible
v=c^{2}
implying
U\geq MS/c^{2}
almost surely.

(2)

If
{L}/{(2K)}\geq{MS}/{c^{2}}
, then the Gamma bracketing (
S1.71
) applies on
[MS/c^{2},\ L/(2K)]
and, by (
S1.72
),

\Pi\Big(U<\frac{L}{2K}\,\Bigm|\,\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}\Big)\ \leq\ C\,\exp\{-c_{0}(K)\,L\}

for some constant
C<\infty
, by absorbing the sandwiching constants.

Therefore there exist positive constants
C,c
(depending only on
K
and the bounds on
(\tau,c^{2})
) such that

\Pi\Big(v>K\,\frac{MS}{L}\,\Bigm|\,\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}\Big)\leq C\,e^{-c\,L}.

(S1.73)

The map
v\mapsto\lambda(v)=\sqrt{\dfrac{v}{\tau^{2}}}\sqrt{\dfrac{c^{2}}{c^{2}-v}}
is increasing.
Moreover, since
\sqrt{\dfrac{c^{2}}{c^{2}-v}}\geq 1
, we have the simple lower bound

\lambda(v)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\bar{\tau}}\,\sqrt{v}.

(S1.74)

Hence, for any
C^{\prime}>0
, the event
\{\lambda>C^{\prime}\sqrt{MS/L}\}
implies

v\ >\ (C^{\prime}\bar{\tau})^{2}\,\frac{MS}{L}.

Taking
K:=(C^{\prime}\bar{\tau})^{2}
in (
S1.73
) gives

\Pi\Big(\lambda_{j}>C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{MS}{L}}\,\Bigm|\,\mu^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}\Big)\ \leq\ C\,e^{-c\,L}.

(S1.75)

Integrating (
S1.75
) with respect to the posterior of the leaf values yields

\Pi\Big(\lambda_{j}>C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{MS_{j}}{L_{j}}}\,\Bigm|\,\bm{Y}\Big)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\big[\,C\,e^{-c\,L_{j}}\,\big|\,\bm{Y}\big].

By
(A7(i))
,
L_{j}\geq\underline{L}_{N}\to\infty
deterministically for inactive ensembles, hence

\Pi\Big(\lambda_{j}>C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{MS_{j}}{L_{j}}}\,\Bigm|\,\bm{Y}\Big)\ \leq\ C\,e^{-c\,\underline{L}_{N}}\ \longrightarrow\ 0,

which proves the first claim:

\Pi\Big(\lambda_{j}\leq C\,\sqrt{\frac{MS_{j}}{L_{j}}}\,\Bigm|\,\bm{Y}\Big)\ \longrightarrow\ 1.

Controlling
S_{j}/L_{j}
by the empirical norm and contraction.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{L}
be the terminal cells (across all
M
trees) of the
j
th
ensemble, and let
n_{\ell}:=\#\{i:\ \bm{Z}_{i}\in A_{\ell}\}
be the empirical
counts. We assume the following
leaf–mass comparability
, which is the precise version of
(A7(ii))
used here and is implied, e.g., by k–d regularity of the design together with balanced splits in inactive ensembles:
there exist constants
0<c_{\star}\leq C_{\star}<\infty
such that

\frac{c_{\star}}{L}\ \leq\ \frac{n_{\ell}}{N}\ \leq\ \frac{C_{\star}}{L}\qquad\text{for all leaves }A_{\ell}.

(S1.76)

Since
\beta_{j}(\bm{z})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{z}\in A_{\ell}\}
,

\|\beta_{j}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{j}(\bm{Z}_{i})^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{n_{\ell}}{N}\,\mu_{\ell}^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{c_{\star}}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}^{2}\ =\ \frac{c_{\star}}{L}\,S,

hence

\frac{S_{j}}{L_{j}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{c_{\star}}\,\|\beta_{j}\|_{N}^{2}.

(S1.77)

By the functional RE condition
(A6)
, and the posterior contraction result (Theorem 1), there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

\Pi\Big(\ \sum_{u=0}^{p_{N}}\|\beta_{u}-\beta_{0,u}\|_{N}^{2}\ \leq\ C_{0}\,r_{N}^{2}\ \Bigm|\ \bm{Y}\Big)\ \longrightarrow 1.

For
j\notin S_{0}
we have
\beta_{0,j}\equiv 0
, hence
\|\beta_{j}\|_{N}^{2}\leq C_{0}r_{N}^{2}
with posterior probability
\to 1
; therefore, by (
S1.77
),

\frac{S_{j}}{L_{j}}\ =\ \mathcal{O}_{\Pi}\big(r_{N}^{2}\big).

This proves the second claim and completes the proof.
∎

Appendix S2
Additional algorithmic details

This section provides the full algorithmic details for our Metropolis-Hastings-within-Gibbs sampler. We outline conditional updates for the tree structures, modifier-sparsity parameters, global-local shrinkage scales, and noise variance.

Gibbs Sampler Derivation

We use a Metropolis-Hastings-within-Gibbs sampler to simulate draws from the joint posterior. The algorithm proceeds by iteratively updating the blocks of parameters from their full conditional distributions. We first describe the update for the tree ensembles and then detail the updates for the remaining model parameters.

Conditional Regression Tree Updates

We update the
m^{th}
regression tree
(T_{m}^{(j)},\mathcal{M}_{m}^{(j)})
in the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{j}
conditional on all other parameters. First, we define the leave-one-tree-out partial residuals
r_{i}^{(j,m)}
for each observation
i=1,\dots,N
:

r_{i}^{(j,m)}\;=\;y_{i}-\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\beta_{j^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{i})-\sum_{m^{\prime}\neq m}x_{ij}\,g(\bm{z}_{i};\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m^{\prime}},\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m^{\prime}}).

(S2.1)

Conditionally on the other trees, these residuals follow the simple regression model for the leaves of the tree
(j,m)
:

r_{i}^{(j,m)}\;=\;x_{ij}\,\mu^{(j)}_{m,\ell(\bm{z}_{i};\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m})}\;+\;\sigma\,\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(S2.2)

Following
Chipman et al., 2010
, we update the tree in a two-step process: first, we sample the tree structure
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{(j)}
using a Metropolis-Hastings step that integrates out the leaf parameters, and then we sample the leaf parameters
\mathcal{M}_{m}^{(j)}
from their full conditional given the new structure.

Metropolis–Hastings for
\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m}

Propose a local move (GROW/PRUNE) taking
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\star}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}(\mathcal{T})=\{i:\ell(\bm{z}_{i};\mathcal{T})=\ell\}
,
n_{\ell}=|\mathcal{I}_{\ell}|
, and define

\bm{x}_{j,\ell}=(x_{ij})_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}},\quad\bm{r}_{\ell}=(r_{i}^{(j,m)})_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}},\quad A_{\ell}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}x_{ij}^{2},\quad B_{\ell}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}x_{ij}r_{i}^{(j,m)}.

With the Gaussian prior
\mu_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s_{j}^{2})
and likelihood

\bm{r}_{\ell}\mid\mu_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell}\bm{x}_{j,\ell},\sigma^{2}I)
, we can integrate out
\mu_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{r}_{\ell};\mu_{\ell}\bm{x}_{j,\ell},\sigma^{2}I)\,\phi(\mu_{\ell};0,s_{j}^{2})\,d\mu_{\ell}

(S2.3)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-n_{\ell}/2}\Big(1+\tfrac{s_{j}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}A_{\ell}\Big)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left\{\frac{B_{\ell}^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}/s_{j}^{2}+A_{\ell})}-\frac{\|\bm{r}_{\ell}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

The marginal likelihood for a tree is the product over the leaves.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{marg}}(r^{(j,m)};\mathcal{T})\;=\;\prod_{\ell=1}^{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T})}\text{\eqref{eqS:leafmarg}}.

(S2.4)

The MH acceptance probability is

\alpha\;=\;1\wedge\frac{p(\mathcal{T}^{\star})}{p(\mathcal{T})}\cdot\frac{q(\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}^{\star})}{q(\mathcal{T}^{\star}\to\mathcal{T})}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{marg}}(r^{(j,m)};\mathcal{T}^{\star})}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{marg}}(r^{(j,m)};\mathcal{T})},

(S2.5)

where
p(\mathcal{T})
is the tree prior and
q(\cdot)
is the proposal kernel.

Gibbs for
\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m}\mid\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m}

Given
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m}
, leaves decouple and each
\mu_{\ell}
has a Gaussian full conditional:

\mu_{\ell}\,\big|\,\ldots\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(m_{\ell},V_{\ell}\big),\quad V_{\ell}=\Big(\frac{A_{\ell}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{s_{j}^{2}}\Big)^{-1},\quad m_{\ell}=V_{\ell}\,\frac{B_{\ell}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(S2.6)

After iterating through all tree updates, we update the remaining model parameters.

Modifier–sparsity
(\bm{\theta}_{j},\eta_{j})

Let
N_{jr}
be the number of internal nodes in ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{j}
that split on modifier
Z_{r}
and
N_{j}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}N_{jr}
.
Conditionally on
\eta_{j}
and the current trees, the splitting–probability vector has the conjugate update

\bm{\theta}_{j}\mid\eta_{j},\{\mathcal{T}_{m}^{(j)}\}~\sim~\operatorname{Dirichlet}\!\Big(\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R}+N_{j1},\ldots,\tfrac{\eta_{j}}{R}+N_{jR}\Big).

(S2.7)

Integrating out
\bm{\theta}_{j}
yields the Dirichlet–Multinomial factor

p\!\left(\{N_{jr}\}\mid\eta_{j}\right)~\propto~\frac{\Gamma(\eta_{j})}{\Gamma(\eta_{j}+N_{j})}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\eta_{j}/R+N_{jr}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\eta_{j}/R\right)}.

(S2.8)

Following the implementation in
Deshpande et al., 2024
, we reparameterize
\eta_{j}
via

u_{j}\;=\;\frac{\eta_{j}}{\eta_{j}+R}\in(0,1),\qquad\eta_{j}\;=\;\frac{R\,u_{j}}{1-u_{j}},

and place a Beta prior
u_{j}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a_{u},b_{u})
. The one–dimensional target for
u_{j}
is then

\log\pi(u_{j}\mid\{N_{jr}\},\ldots)=\log p\!\left(\{N_{jr}\}\mid\eta_{j}(u_{j})\right)+\log\!\operatorname{Beta}(u_{j}\mid a_{u},b_{u})+\text{const}.

We update
u_{j}
with a Metropolis–Hastings step on the logit scale (random–walk proposals), and map back to
\eta_{j}=Ru_{j}/(1-u_{j})
for the subsequent Dirichlet update (
S2.7
). This preserves conjugacy for
\bm{\theta}_{j}
while providing a simple 1D MH move for the concentration parameter.

Global–local scales
(\bm{\lambda},\tau,c^{2})

Let
S_{j}=\sum_{m,\ell}(\mu^{(j)}_{m,\ell})^{2}
and
L_{j}=\sum_{m}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}^{(j)}_{m})
.
Given
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M})
and
\sigma^{2}
, the joint log-target is

\log\pi(\bm{\lambda},\tau,c^{2}\mid\ldots)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\!\Big(S_{j}/s_{j}^{2}+L_{j}\log s_{j}^{2}\Big)+\log\pi(\bm{\lambda})+\log\pi(\tau)+\log\pi(c^{2}),

(S2.9)

with
s_{j}^{2}={\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}c^{2}}/{(c^{2}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2})}.
The full conditionals of
\lambda_{j}
and
\tau
are non-conjugate; we update each
\log\lambda_{j}
(independently over
j
) and
\log\tau
via univariate slice sampling targeting (
S2.9
).

For fixed
(\tau,\{\lambda_{j}\})
, the prior likelihood contribution of ensemble
j
to the kernel in
c^{2}
is

\prod_{m,\ell}\big(2\pi\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}c^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\mu^{(j)}_{m,\ell}\big)^{2}}{2\,\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\,c^{2}}\right)\ \propto\ (c^{2})^{-L_{j}/2}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{S_{j}}{2\,\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{c^{2}}\right).

Multiplying over
j=0,\dots,p
yields

\pi(\{\mu\}\mid c^{2},\tau,\{\lambda_{j}\})\ \propto\ (c^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{p}L_{j}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{p}\frac{S_{j}}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right).

With the prior
c^{2}\sim\mathcal{IG}(\nu_{c}/2,\ \nu_{c}s_{c}^{2}/2)
,

\pi(c^{2})\ \propto\ (c^{2})^{-(\nu_{c}/2+1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\nu_{c}s_{c}^{2}}{2\,c^{2}}\right),

the posterior density of
c^{2}
is

\pi(c^{2}\mid\ldots)\ \propto\ (c^{2})^{-\big(\frac{\nu_{c}}{2}+1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}L_{j}\big)}\;\exp\!\left\{-\,\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Big(\nu_{c}s_{c}^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{p}\frac{S_{j}}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\Big)\right\},

i.e.,

c^{2}\mid\ldots\ \sim\ \mathcal{IG}\!\left(\frac{\nu_{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{p}L_{j}}{2},\;\frac{\nu_{c}s_{c}^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{p}S_{j}/(\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2})}{2}\right).

(S2.10)

Noise variance
\sigma^{2}

Let
r_{i}=y_{i}-\beta_{0}(\bm{z}_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{ij}\beta_{j}(\bm{z}_{i})
be the residuals and set
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})^{\top}
.
Conditional on the trees and jumps,

\pi(\bm{y}\mid\ldots,\sigma^{2})\ \propto\ (\sigma^{2})^{-n/2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}\right).

With the prior
\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{IG}(\nu/2,\ \nu\lambda/2)
,

\pi(\sigma^{2})\ \propto\ (\sigma^{2})^{-(\nu/2+1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\nu\lambda}{2\,\sigma^{2}}\right),

the posterior is

\sigma^{2}\mid\ldots\ \sim\ \mathcal{IG}\!\left(\frac{\nu+n}{2},\;\frac{\nu\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}}{2}\right).

(S2.11)

Per-sweep complexity.

The per-sweep computational complexity of the sampler is dominated by the tree proposal and scoring steps, scaling as
\mathcal{O}(npM)
, where
n
is the number of observations and
M
is the total number of trees across all ensembles. The updates for the other model components—including the global-local shrinkage scales (
\mathcal{O}(p)
), the modifier-split probabilities (
\mathcal{O}(pR)
), and the leaf parameter draws (
\mathcal{O}(M\bar{L})
)—are substantially less costly, where
M\bar{L}=\sum_{j,m}L_{jm}
.

Heuristic for choosing
M
.

The optimal choice of
M
is problem-dependent and represents a
bias-variance tradeoff
. A larger
M
allows the model to fit more complex functions with lower variance by averaging over many simple trees, which is the core idea of ensemble methods. However, this comes at a linear increase in computational cost. A mathematically grounded heuristic can be derived from the approximation theory. The theory suggests that a total leaf budget of
K_{j}\asymp N^{s_{0,j}/(2\alpha_{j}+s_{0,j})}
is needed to approximate a function with intrinsic dimension
s_{0,j}
and smoothness
\alpha_{j}
. Since the total expected number of leaves is roughly the number of trees (
M
) times the average number of leaves per tree (
\mathbb{E}[L_{m}]
), we have
M\times\mathbb{E}[L_{m}]\approx K_{j}
. This implies that for more complex functions (smaller
\alpha_{j}
, larger
s_{0,j}
), a larger total leaf budget is required, which can be achieved by increasing
M
.

In practice, since
\alpha_{j}
and
s_{0,j}
are unknown, we recommend starting with a default like
M=50
. This is often large enough for the ensemble effect to work well, as noted by
Deshpande et al., 2024
. If we suspect the underlying functions are extremely complex or if performance is suboptimal, we might increase
M
to
100
or further, computational budget permitting. If computational cost is a major constraint,
sparseVCBART
likely allows for a smaller
M
than
vanillaVCBART
would require for the same level of regularization, as its shrinkage prior provides an additional mechanism for controlling model complexity.

S3.3
Semi-synthetic data

Exclusionary attitudes are a form of prejudice wherein individuals tend to favor their own group and oppose policies that promote well-being of individuals of the ‘outgroup’
(
Kalla and Broockman, 2023
)
. Recent work in political science
(
Broockman and Kalla, 2016
;
Williamson et al., 2021
)
shows that interpersonal conversations can reduce exclusionary attitudes towards outgroups.
Kalla and Broockman, 2023
evaluate what types of interpersonal conversations can reduce prejudice. We focus on their door-to-door canvassing experiment conducted in 2019, conducted in Michigan, Pennsylvania and North Carolina. The intervention was designed to reduce prejudice against unauthorized immigrants.

The intervention involves a canvasser knocking on doors and having conversations with 2838 voters who participated in the study. Voters were assigned to one of two treatment conditions or a placebo condition. The first treatment was a conversation that involved (what the authors call) a “full intervention” wherein the canvasser and voter would share stories about immigrants they knew, and then voters would be asked to discuss a time they needed support. The partial intervention was designed to assess whether only the first part of the intervention— here, the canvasser and voter would only share stories about immigrants they knew. A placebo arm, canvassers had short conversations about unrelated topics.

Finally, outcomes were collected 1 week, 7 weeks and 4.5 months after the intervention. The primary outcomes are a policy index, that gauges support for including unauthorized immigrants in government programs, and a prejudice index. The study finds that both the treatment arms are similarly effective, and that the effects persist upto 4.5 months after the survey. Participants exposed to the treatment arms are between 3 and 10% more likely to agree with policies that support unauthorized immigrants.

Understanding heterogeneity in this context is important for a number of reasons. First, the increase in support for immigrant policies may be stronger when contact is made with an immigrant canvasser. Second, the treatment effects may vary by partisanship, due to the polarizing nature of the topic. Finally, personal characteristics of voters like age, gender, minority status in addition to initial levels of prejudice may be possible modifiers. Thus, the average effect of the treatment may be smaller or larger for certain subgroups. As a result, practitioners working on prejudice reduction at scale may not fully understand which subgroups respond best to an intervention, and may lead to resources being allocated inefficiently.
