Doubling measures and Poincaré inequalities for sphericalizations of metric spaces
Anders Björn

Abstract




Abstract
.
The identification between the complex plane and the Riemann sphere
preserves holomorphic and harmonic functions and is a classical tool.
In this paper we consider
a similar mapping from an unbounded metric space
X
to a bounded
space and show how it preserves
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions and Poincaré inequalities.
When
X
is Ahlfors regular, this was shown in our earlier paper
(
J. Math. Anal. Appl.

474
(2019), 852–875).
Here we only require the much
weaker (and more natural) doubling property
of the measure.
Furthermore, we consider a broader class of transformed measures.
The sphericalization is then applied to
obtain new results for the Dirichlet boundary value problem in unbounded sets
and for boundary regularity at infinity for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.
Some of these results are new also for
unweighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
and
p\neq 2
.

Key words and phrases
:
Boundary regularity,
doubling measure,
Dirichlet problem,
metric space,
Perron solution,

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function,
Poincaré inequality,
sphericalization.

(2020):
Primary:
31E05; Secondary:

1
Introduction

The identification between the complex plane and the Riemann sphere
preserves
holomorphic and harmonic functions and is a classical tool with a vast
number of applications.
In this paper we consider
a similar mapping from an unbounded metric space to a bounded
space and show how it
preserves
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions, which in the metric space setting
are defined as minimizers of the
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy integral and correspond to the solutions
of the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace equation

\dvg(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=0
,
1<p<\infty
, in Euclidean spaces.

This transformation opens up for future applications.
We apply it to
obtain new results for the Dirichlet boundary value
problem and boundary regularity
for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions in unbounded open sets,
see Theorems
1.3
–
1.5

and Section
7
.
Some of these results are new also for
unweighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
and
p\neq 2
.

Assume for the rest of the introduction that

X=(X,d)
is an
unbounded
metric space equipped with a Borel measure
\mu

such that
0<\mu(B)<\infty
for all balls
B
.
We also fix a
base point

a\in X

and let

|x|:=d(x,a).

Following Bonk–Kleiner
[
22
]

we define the sphericalization of
X
as follows.
Let
{\widehat{X}}=X\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}
.
Define
d_{a},\hat{d}:{\widehat{X}}\times{\widehat{X}}\to[0,\infty)
by

d_{a}(x,y)=d_{a}(y,x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{d(x,y)}{(1+|x|)(1+|y|)},&\text{if }x,y\in X,\\[8.53581pt]
\dfrac{1}{1+|x|},&\text{if }x\in X\text{ and }y={\boldsymbol{\infty}},\\[8.53581pt]
0,&\text{if }x=y={\boldsymbol{\infty}},\end{cases}

and

\hat{d}(x,y)=\inf_{x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}=y}\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{a}(x_{j-1},x_{j}),

where the infimum is taken over all finite sequences

x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}=y
in
{\widehat{X}}
.
This makes
\hat{d}
into a metric on
{\widehat{X}}
, and
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})

is the
sphericalization
of
(X,d)
.

As in our earlier paper
[
12
]
,
we equip
{\widehat{X}}
with the
measure

d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(x)=\frac{d\mu(x)}{(1+|x|)^{q}}\quad\text{with }{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0

for suitable
q>0
.
The choice
q=2p
will preserve
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.

The standard assumptions for the theory of
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
are that
X
is complete and
\mu
is a doubling measure
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality.
To be able to exploit sphericalization for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
we need these properties
to hold also for the sphericalized space
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{2p}})
.
A large part of the paper is therefore devoted
to results showing when these
properties are preserved.

The following result shows when the doubling condition
is preserved.
(See Section
4
for the definition of
uniform perfectness.)

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
X
is uniformly perfect at the base point
a
for radii
\geq 1
,
and that
\mu
is doubling on
X
.
Then
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
is doubling on
{\widehat{X}}
if and only if there
is
0<s<q
such that

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{s}\quad\text{whenever }1\leq r\leq R<\infty.

(1.1)

For the preservation of
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequalities
we obtain the following result.
This is a special case of Theorem
5.3
,
which contains more details.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
X
is complete and
annularly connected for large radii around
a
,
as in Definition
5.2
.
Also assume that
\mu
is doubling,
satisfies (
1.1
)
for some
s>0
, and supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality with
p\geq 1
.

Then the sphericalization
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, with
q>s
, also supports a

p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality.

Metric properties of sphericalizations, such as (annular) quasiconvexity,
were studied in Bonk–Kleiner
[
22
]
, Balogh–Buckley
[
3
]

and Buckley–Herron–Xie
[
23
]
.

Preservation of the doubling property, Ahlfors regularity and Poincaré inequalities
has been studied under various assumptions by
Wildrick
[
46
]
, Li–Shanmugalingam
[
42
]

and Durand-Cartagena–Li
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
,
but with other measures on
{\widehat{X}}
,
so these results do not apply here.

Sphericalization of unbounded uniform metric spaces
(\Omega,d)
,
based on rectifiable curves as in
[
3
]
and with measures
tailored to preserve
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonicity as in
[
12
]
,
was considered in Gibara–Korte–Shanmugalingam
[
27
]

and Korte–Rogovin–Shanmugalingam–Takala
[
39
]
,
and used to solve a Dirichlet problem in
\Omega
.
However, their assumptions and results are different from ours.
In particular, the assumption that
\Omega
is uniform (which is essential for the
proofs in
[
27
]
and
[
39
]
) excludes many domains.
In our situation, the open set
\Omega
considered for the Dirichlet problem
is arbitrary, even though we impose some assumptions on the underlying space
X
.

Another useful transformation, called uniformization,
between unbounded and bounded spaces
was introduced and studied in Bonk–Heinonen–Koskela
[
21
]
.
It maps unbounded Gromov hyperbolic spaces to bounded uniform spaces.
In Björn–Björn–Shanmugalingam
[
17
, Propositions 10.4 and 10.5]
,
it was shown that, when equipped with suitable measures, it also
preserves
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions and can be used to obtain a Liouville type theorem in
Gromov hyperbolic spaces.

The main reason for our choice of the measure
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
is that with

q=2p
and
p>1
,
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions are preserved under sphericalization,
as we show in Section
7
.
When
X
is Ahlfors regular, this was shown
in our earlier paper
[
12
]
.
But even for
Ahlfors regular spaces, the assumptions
in this paper
are weaker than in
[
12
]
, see Remark
7.1
.
However, the main point is that we replace the
Ahlfors regularity assumption in
[
12
]
by the much
weaker (and more natural) doubling property.
In particular, this makes it possible to include weighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}

with
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible weights, as in
Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
31
]
, into our considerations.
The doubling
property and a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality, together with completeness,
are standard assumptions
for the theory of
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions on metric spaces,
so these are natural assumptions for the results below to hold.

In this paper, we apply sphericalization
to obtain new results on solutions of the Dirichlet boundary value problem
in unbounded open sets
and about boundary regularity at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.
We use the Perron method
and say that a boundary function

f:\widehat{\partial}\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
is
resolutive

if its lower and upper Perron solutions coincide,
in which case the common solution is denoted by
Pf
,
see Definition
7.5
.

The following resolutivity and invariance result
improves upon Propositions 11.6 and 11.7 in Björn–Björn
[
9
]
,
where a similar result was obtained under considerably
more restrictive conditions
on
h
, in particular that
h
is bounded.
In particular, the Perron solution
Pf
is the unique bounded

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function in
\Omega
satisfying both (
1.2
) and

\lim_{\Omega\ni y\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}u(y)=f({\boldsymbol{\infty}})
.

Corresponding results in
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolic spaces
(as in Definition
6.7
) were
obtained by Hansevi
[
29
, Theorem 7.8]
.
Here
{C_{p}}
denotes the Sobolev capacity.
Theorem
1.3
is a special case of Theorem
7.7

and Corollary
7.9
, which both use a more refined capacity.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that the assumptions in Theorem
1.2

are satisfied with
p>1
and
q=2p
.
Also assume that
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic

(
as in Definition
6.7
)
and
that
\Omega\subset X
is an unbounded open set.

Let
f\in C(\partial\Omega\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})
.
Assume that
h:\partial\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
vanishes

{C_{p}}
-q.e. and let
h({\boldsymbol{\infty}})=0
.
Then both
f
and
f+h
are resolutive and
Pf=P(f+h)
.

Moreover, if
u
is a bounded

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function in
\Omega
such that

\lim_{\Omega\ni y\to x}u(y)=f(x)\quad\text{for ${C_{p}}$-q.e.\ }x\in\partial\Omega

(1.2)

and

\lim_{\Omega\ni y\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}u(y)=f({\boldsymbol{\infty}})
,
then
u=Pf
.

The following theorem is a so-called trichotomy at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.
Similar results for
finite
boundary points in metric spaces were obtained in
Björn
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
(for bounded
\Omega
) and
Björn–Hansevi
[
20
]
(for unbounded
\Omega
).
In a linear axiomatic setting in unweighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, a similar characterization
is due to Lukeš–Malý
[
43
]
.

Note that
f\in C(\partial\Omega\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})
is resolutive
by Theorem
1.3
if
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic,
and by Theorem 7.8 in Hansevi
[
29
]
if
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolic.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that the assumptions in Theorem
1.2

are satisfied with
p>1
and
q=2p
.
Also assume that
\Omega\subset X
is an unbounded open set,
and that
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic or
that
{C_{p}}(X\setminus\Omega)>0
.

Then at least one of the following two situations happens at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
:

1.

The limit
\displaystyle\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}Pf(x)
exists for every

f\in C(\partial\Omega\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})
.

2.

There is a sequence
\Omega\ni x_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}Pf(x_{j})=f({\boldsymbol{\infty}})\quad\text{for every
$f\in C(\partial\Omega\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})$.
}

The main point in Theorem
1.4
is that it is
impossible for both properties
1
–
2
to fail.
Note that the sequence in
2
is independent of
f
.
If both
1
and
2
hold, then
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is
regular
.
If only
1
holds, then
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is called
semiregular
, while
if only
2
holds, then
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is called
strongly irregular
.
For finite boundary points, these latter two properties correspond
to the irregularity of
0
in the punctured ball as in Zaremba
[
47
]
,
and to the Lebesgue spine as in Lebesgue
[
41
]
, respectively.

We also show that regularity of
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
can be characterized
using barriers in the following way.
In particular, condition
3
below says that regularity at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}

is a local property.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume that the assumptions in Theorem
1.2

are satisfied with
p>1
and
q=2p
.
Also assume that
\Omega\subset X
is an unbounded open set,
and
that
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic or that

{C_{p}}(X\setminus\Omega)>0
.
Let
K\subset X
be compact.

Then the following are equivalent
:

1.

{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is regular with respect to
\Omega
,

2.

there is a barrier at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
, i.e. a superharmonic function
u
in
\Omega

such that

\lim_{\Omega\ni y\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}u(y)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\liminf_{\Omega\ni y\to x}u(y)>0\quad\text{for every }x\in\partial\Omega.

3.

{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is regular with respect to
\Omega\setminus K
.

The barrier characterization
for
finite
regular points in unbounded open sets
is due to Björn–Hansevi
[
19
, Theorem 6.2]
.
In
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic spaces all three conditions in
Theorem
1.5

are true (and thus also equivalent) by
Lemma 11.1 in Björn–Björn
[
9
]
.
On unweighted and weighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
Theorem
1.5
is due to
Kilpeläinen
[
35
, Theorem 1.5 and Remark 5.3]

and Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
31
, Theorem 9.8 and Corollary 9.15]
.

.

.

.

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonicity when

and

.

we also deduce
Theorems
1.3
–
1.5
.

2
Metric spaces and upper gradients

We assume throughout the paper
that
1\leq p<\infty
and that

X=(X,d)
is a metric space.
Except for Section
3

we also assume that
X
is
equipped with
a positive complete Borel measure
\mu

such that
0<\mu(B)<\infty
for all
balls
B\subset X
.
Additional standing
assumptions are added at the beginning of
various sections.

The assumption
0<\mu(B)<\infty
implies that

X
is separable and Lindelöf.
Proofs of the results in
this section can be found in the monographs
Björn–Björn
[
7
]
and
Heinonen–Koskela–Shanmugalingam–Tyson
[
33
]
.

The measure
\mu
is
doubling

if there is a
doubling constant

C_{\mu}>1
such that
for all balls

B=B(x_{0},r):=\{x\in X:d(x,x_{0})<r\}
in
X
,

0<\mu(2B)\leq C_{\mu}\mu(B)<\infty.

Here and elsewhere we write

\lambda B=B(x_{0},\lambda r)
.
All balls considered in this paper are assumed to be open,
unless said otherwise.

The following simple lemma
will be used several times.
A proof can be found e.g. in Björn–Björn
[
7
, Lemma 3.6]
.
Here and later
we write
a\lesssim b
and
b\gtrsim a

if there is an implicit comparison constant
C>0
such that
a\leq Cb
,
and
a\simeq b
if
a\lesssim b\lesssim a
.
The implicit comparison constants are allowed to depend on the
fixed data.
When needed, we will
explain the dependence in each case.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
\mu
is a doubling measure.
Let
B=B(x,r)
and
B^{\prime}=B(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})
be two balls such that

d(x,x^{\prime})\leq cr\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r}{c}\leq r^{\prime}\leq cr.

Then
\mu(B)\simeq\mu(B^{\prime})
with comparison constants
depending only on
c\geq 1
and
C_{\mu}
.

The measure
\mu
is
Ahlfors
Q
-regular
,
Q>0
, if

\mu(B(x,r))\simeq r^{Q}\quad\text{when }r\leq 2\diam X.

X
is
proper
if every closed bounded subset is compact.
If
\mu
is doubling, then
X
is proper
if and only if it is complete.

A
curve
is a continuous mapping from an interval,
and a
rectifiable
curve is a curve with finite length.
A rectifiable curve can be parametrized by arc length
ds
.
A property holds for
p\mspace{1mu}
-almost every curve

if the curve family
\Gamma
of rectifiable curves
for which it fails has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus,
i.e. there is
\rho\in L^{p}(X)
such that

\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds=\infty
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Following Heinonen–Koskela
[
32
]
and Koskela–MacManus
[
40
]

we define upper gradients and
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

A Borel function
g:X\to[0,\infty]
is an
upper gradient

of a function
u:X\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]

if for all nonconstant rectifiable curves

\gamma:[0,1]\to X
,

|u(\gamma(0))-u(\gamma(1))|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds,

(2.1)

where the left-hand side is interpreted as

\infty
whenever at least one of the terms therein is infinite.
If
g:X\to[0,\infty]
is measurable
and (
2.1
) holds for
p\mspace{1mu}
-almost every nonconstant rectifiable curve,
then
g
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient
of
u
.

If
g\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
,
then one can find a sequence
\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

of upper gradients of
u
such that
\|g_{j}-g\|_{L^{p}(X)}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.
If
u
has an upper gradient in
L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
, then
it has an a.e. unique
minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient

g_{u}\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)

in the sense that
g_{u}\leq g
a.e. for every
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient

g\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
of
u
.

Together with the doubling property defined above, the following
Poincaré inequality is often a standard assumption on metric spaces.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
X
(or
\mu
) supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality

if
there exist constants
C_{\rm PI}>0
and
\lambda\geq 1

such that for all balls
B\subset X
,
all integrable functions
u
on
X
and all
upper gradients
g
of
u
,

\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{B}|u-u_{B}|\,d\mu\leq C_{\rm PI}r_{B}\biggl(\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{\lambda B}g^{p}\,d\mu\biggr)^{1/p},

where
u_{B}:=\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{B}u\,d\mu:=\int_{B}u\,d\mu/\mu(B)

and
r_{B}
is the radius of the ball
B
.
The constant
\lambda
is called the
dilation constant
.

Note that one can equivalently require
that
u
is integrable on
\lambda B
and that
g

is an upper gradient (or
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient) of
u
on
\lambda B
,
see the proof of Theorem 8.1.53 in
Heinonen–Koskela–Shanmugalingam–Tyson
[
33
]
.
The constants
C_{\rm PI}

and
\lambda
remain the same.
If
X
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality then it is connected.

The space
X
is
L
-quasiconvex

if for every
x,y\in X
there is a curve

\gamma
from
x
to
u
with length
l_{\gamma}\leq Ld(x,y)
.
It is well known and was originally proved by Semmes that
a complete metric space equipped with a doubling measure
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality is
quasiconvex.
For a proof of the following statement see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 4.32]
or
[
33
, Theorem 8.3.2]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
X
is complete and
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality
and
\mu
is doubling,
then
X
is
L
-quasiconvex,
with
L
only depending on
C_{\mu}
,
C_{\rm PI}
and
\lambda
.

The Newtonian Sobolev space on a metric space
X
was
introduced as follows by
Shanmugalingam
[
44
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

For measurable
u
, let

\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}=\biggl(\int_{X}|u|^{p}\,d\mu+\inf_{g}\int_{X}g^{p}\,d\mu\biggr)^{1/p},

where the infimum is taken over all upper gradients of
u
.
The
Newtonian space
on
X
is

N^{1,p}(X)=\{u:\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}<\infty\}.

The space
N^{1,p}(X)/{\sim}
, where
u\sim v
if and only if
\|u-v\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}=0
,
is a Banach space and a lattice.
We also define the
Dirichlet space

D^{p}(X)=\{u:u\text{ is measurable, finite
a.e.\ and has an upper gradient
in }L^{p}(X)\}.

This definition deviates from the definition in e.g.
[
7
, Definition 1.54]
in that it requires the functions
to be finite a.e.,
see the comments after Theorem
2.6
.
In this paper, it is convenient to
assume that functions in
N^{1,p}
and
D^{p}

are defined everywhere (with values in
{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
),
not just up to an equivalence class in the corresponding function space.

We say that
u\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X)
if
for every
x\in X
there exists a ball
B_{x}\ni x
such that

u\in N^{1,p}(B_{x})
.
The space
D^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
is defined similarly.
If
u,v\in D^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
,
then
g_{u}=g_{v}
a.e. in
\{x\in X:u(x)=v(x)\}
.
In particular, for
c\in\mathbf{R}
we have
g_{\min\{u,c\}}=g_{u}\chi_{\{u<c\}}

a.e. in
X
,
where
\chi
denotes the characteristic function.
For a measurable set
E\subset X
, the
spaces
N^{1,p}(E)
,
N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(E)
,
D^{p}(E)
and
D^{p}_{\rm loc}(E)
are defined by
considering
(E,d|_{E},\mu|_{E})
as a metric space in its own right.

The
Sobolev capacity
of an arbitrary set
E\subset X
is

{C_{p}}(E)=\inf_{u}\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}^{p},

where the infimum is taken over all
u\in N^{1,p}(X)
such that

u\geq 1
on
E
.
It is easy to see that the Sobolev capacity is countably subadditive.
For further properties, see
e.g.
[
7
]
and
[
33
]
.

A property holds
quasieverywhere
(q.e.) if the set of points for which it fails
has Sobolev capacity zero.
The Sobolev capacity is the correct gauge
for distinguishing between two Newtonian functions, namely

v\sim u
if and only if
v=u
q.e.
Moreover, if
u,v\in N^{1,p}(X)
and
u=v
a.e., then
u=v
q.e.

A function
u:X\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
is
quasicontinuous

if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is an
open
set
G

with
{C_{p}}(G)<\varepsilon
such that
u|_{X\setminus G}
is continuous.
Here and elsewhere, continuous functions are
always assumed to be real-valued, while
quasicontinuous functions are
allowed to be
{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
-valued.

That Newtonian functions are quasicontinuous was first
shown by
Björn–Björn–Shanmugalingam
[
14
]
,
assuming that
X
is complete and that
\mu
is a doubling measure
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality.
These assumptions have later been substantially weakened, first by
Ambrosio et al.
[
1
]
,
[
2
]

and more recently by Eriksson-Bique–Poggi-Corradini
[
26
, Theorem 1.3]
,
who only require that
X
is locally complete.
The following theorem
shows how to extend the result from
[
26
]

to functions in
D^{p}
.
This will be useful
in Section
7
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
u\in D^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
.
Then the following hold
:

1.

{C_{p}}(\{x:|u(x)|=\infty\})=0
.

2.

If
X
is locally complete, then
u
is quasicontinuous.

In e.g.
[
7
, Definition 1.54]
,
functions
u\in D^{p}(X)
were not required to be finite a.e.
However, if
there are no rectifiable curves in
X
, then
such a definition would allow

u\equiv\infty\in D^{p}(X)
, which
is not a quasicontinuous function.
In fact, no function
u
with
\mu(\{x:|u(x)|=\infty\})>0
can be
quasicontinuous, so
the implicit condition that
u
is finite a.e. is essential
in Theorem
2.6
, unlike for functions in
N^{1,p}

where it is automatically satisfied.

As
X
is Lindelöf,
it can be covered by a countable collection

\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
of balls such that
u\in D^{p}(B_{j})
for each
j
.

1

It follows from Theorem 1.56 and Proposition 2.27 in
[
7
]

that

u
is absolutely continuous on
p\mspace{1mu}
-almost every rectifiable curve.
Since
u
is finite a.e., by definition,

1
now follows from Corollary 1.70 in
[
7
]
.

2

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let

u_{k}=\min\{k,\max\{u,-k\}\},\quad k=1,2,\dots,

be the truncations of
u
at the levels
\pm k
.
Then
g_{u}
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient also of
u_{k}
.
Hence
u_{k}\in N^{1,p}(B_{j})
for each
j
.
As
X
is locally complete, so is
B_{j}
and
therefore
u_{k}
is quasicontinuous in
B_{j}
,
by Theorem 1.3 in
Eriksson-Bique–Poggi-Corradini
[
26
]
,
i.e. there is an open set
G_{j,k}
such that
{C_{p}}(G_{j,k})<2^{-j-k}\varepsilon

and
u_{k}|_{B_{j}\setminus G_{j,k}}
is continuous.

Moreover, by
1
and Theorem 1.5 in
[
26
]

(which says that
{C_{p}}
is an “outer capacity”),
there is an open set
G^{\prime}\supset\{x:|u(x)|=\infty\})
such that

{C_{p}}(G^{\prime})<\varepsilon
.
Let
G=G^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{j,k=1}^{\infty}G_{j,k}
.
Then
{C_{p}}(G)<2\varepsilon
.

Next, let
x\in X\setminus G
.
Then
u(x)\in\mathbf{R}
and there are
j
and
k

such that
x\in B_{j}
and
|u(x)|<k
.
Since
u_{k}|_{B_{j}\setminus G}
is continuous at
x
, also

u|_{X\setminus G}
must be continuous at
x
.
Therefore
u|_{X\setminus G}
is continuous.
∎

3
Sphericalization

In this section we assume that
X
is an unbounded metric space.

In this section we do not need any measure on
X
.
Moreover, the few results here hold also when
X
is nonseparable.
From now on, we also fix a
base point

a\in X
.
To simplify the notation we write

|x|:=d(x,a).

Following Bonk–Kleiner
[
22
]

we define the sphericalization of
X
as follows.
Let
{\widehat{X}}=X\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}
.
Define
d_{a},\hat{d}:{\widehat{X}}\times{\widehat{X}}\to[0,\infty)
by

d_{a}(x,y)=d_{a}(y,x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{d(x,y)}{(1+|x|)(1+|y|)},&\text{if }x,y\in X,\\[8.53581pt]
\dfrac{1}{1+|x|},&\text{if }x\in X\text{ and }y={\boldsymbol{\infty}},\\[8.53581pt]
0,&\text{if }x=y={\boldsymbol{\infty}},\end{cases}

and

\hat{d}(x,y)=\inf_{x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}=y}\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{a}(x_{j-1},x_{j}),

where the infimum is taken over all finite sequences

x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}=y
.
This makes
\hat{d}
into a metric on
{\widehat{X}}
, and
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})

is the
sphericalization
of
(X,d)
.
Moreover,

\tfrac{1}{4}d_{a}(x,y)\leq\hat{d}(x,y)\leq d_{a}(x,y),

(3.1)

see
the proof of Lemma 2.2 in
[
22
]
.
The sphericalization

({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})
is compact if and only if
X
is proper,
in which case
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})
is
topologically the one-point compactification of
(X,d)
.

Note that
d_{a}
is in general not a metric since the triangle inequality
may fail for it.
However, because of (
3.1
), it is a quasimetric and
satisfies

d_{a}(x,y)\leq 4(d_{a}(x,z)+d_{a}(y,z)).

Moreover, it is easily verified using elementary calculations and
the triangle inequality for
d(x,y)
that the special triangle inequality

d_{a}(x,y)\leq d_{a}(x,z)+d_{a}(z,y)\quad\text{holds whenever }\{x,y,z\}\cap\{a,{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}\neq\varnothing.

(3.2)

Hence,
if
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}={\boldsymbol{\infty}}
, then

\displaystyle d_{a}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})

\displaystyle\leq d_{a}(x_{0},x_{1})+d_{a}(x_{1},{\boldsymbol{\infty}})

\displaystyle\leq d_{a}(x_{0},x_{1})+d_{a}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{a}(x_{2},{\boldsymbol{\infty}})\leq\dots\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{a}(x_{j-1},x_{j}),

from which it follows that
\hat{d}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})=d_{a}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})
.
Similarly
\hat{d}(x,a)=d_{a}(x,a)
.
These identities will be used extensively.

Note that (with
|x|=d(x,a)
as before)

\hat{d}(x,y)\leq d_{a}(x,y)=\frac{d(x,y)}{(1+|x|)(1+|y|)}\leq\frac{|x|+|y|}{(1+|x|)(1+|y|)}<1,\quad\text{if }x,y\in X.

Since also
\hat{d}(a,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})=d_{a}(a,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})=1
,
we conclude that the
sphericalization
{\widehat{X}}
is always bounded and

\diam_{\hat{d}}{\widehat{X}}=\diam_{d_{a}}{\widehat{X}}=1.

(3.3)

We will denote balls in
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})
by
{\widehat{B}}(x,r)
,
while balls in
(X,d)
are denoted by
B(x,r)
as before.
The “balls” in
{\widehat{X}}
with respect to
d_{a}
will be denoted

B_{a}(x,r)=\{y\in{\widehat{X}}:d_{a}(x,y)<r\}
.
Clearly, (
3.1
) implies that

{\widehat{B}}(x,\tfrac{1}{4}r)\subset B_{a}(x,r)\subset{\widehat{B}}(x,r).

(3.4)

To further simplify the notation we will also write

\hat{d}(x):=\hat{d}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})=\frac{1}{1+|x|}=1-\hat{d}(x,a).

(3.5)

In the following lemma it will be convenient to use
the closed balls
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}(x,\rho)=\{y\in X:d(y,x)\leq\rho\}

with
\rho\in\mathbf{R}
.
In other situations, radii are implicitly assumed to be positive.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},r)={\widehat{X}}\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}\biggl(a,\frac{1}{r}-1\biggr).

(3.6)

Moreover, for
x\in X
,

\displaystyle B\biggl(x,\frac{3r}{4\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\subset B_{a}(x,r)\subset B\biggl(x,\frac{3r}{2\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\biggr),

\displaystyle\text{if }r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x),

(3.7)

\displaystyle B\biggl(x,\frac{1}{4\hat{d}(x)}\biggr)

\displaystyle\subset B_{a}(x,r)\subset{\widehat{B}}(x,r)\subset{\widehat{X}}\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}\biggl(a,\frac{1}{4r}-1\biggr),

\displaystyle\text{if }\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x)\leq r\leq 1.

(3.8)

The
identity (
3.6
)
follows directly from (
3.5
).
To prove (
3.7
), let

r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x)

and assume that
y\in B_{a}(x,r)
.
Then

d(x,y)<r(1+|x|)(1+|y|)\leq r(1+|x|)(1+|x|+d(x,y))=\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}+\frac{rd(x,y)}{\hat{d}(x)},

from which we obtain

d(x,y)\biggl(1-\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)}\biggr)<\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}.

Since
1-r/\hat{d}(x)\geq\tfrac{2}{3}
, we conclude that

d(x,y)<\frac{3r}{2\hat{d}(x)^{2}},

i.e. the second inclusion in (
3.7
) holds.

Similarly, for the first inclusion, assume that
d_{a}(x,y)\geq r
.
Then

d(x,y)\geq r(1+|x|)(1+|y|)\geq r(1+|x|)(1+|x|-d(x,y))=\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}-\frac{rd(x,y)}{\hat{d}(x)},

which yields

d(x,y)\biggl(1+\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)}\biggr)\geq\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}.

Since
1+r/\hat{d}(x)\leq\tfrac{4}{3}
,
we obtain that
d(x,y)\geq\tfrac{3}{4}r/\hat{d}(x)^{2}

and the first inclusion in (
3.7
) follows.

Now assume that
r\geq\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x)
.
To prove the first inclusion in (
3.8
),
note that by (
3.7
),

B_{a}(x,r)\supset B_{a}(x,\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x))\supset B\biggl(x,\frac{1}{4\hat{d}(x)}\biggr).

The second inclusion is trivial, while the
third inclusion follows from
(
3.6
) since

{\widehat{B}}(x,r)\subset{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},4r)
.
∎

If
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
is a (not necessarily rectifiable) curve, then
it is quite easy
(cf. Li–Shanmugalingam
[
42
, (2.11)]
)
to see that the arc lengths
ds
and
d\hat{s}
with respect to
d
and

\hat{d}
, respectively,
are related by

d\hat{s}(x)=\frac{ds(x)}{(1+|x|)^{2}}=\hat{d}(x)^{2}\,ds(x).

(3.9)

As
\gamma([0,1])
is compact it follows that
\gamma
is rectifiable with
respect to
d
if and only if it is rectifiable with respect to
\hat{d}
.
The following relation between upper gradients follows directly from (
3.9
)
and (
2.1
).
(The definition of upper gradient in Definition
2.2
does not
require any measure on
X
.
Corollary
6.2
extends this identity to
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients,
which also involves the measure.)
Note that the formulation in
[
42
]
is
for Lipschitz functions on
{\widehat{X}}
,
but their proof directly covers our case.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
[
42
, Lemma 3.4]
)

Let
E\subset X
.
A Borel function
g:E\to[0,\infty]
is an
upper gradient of
u:E\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
with respect to
d
if and only if

\hat{g}(x)=g(x)(1+|x|)^{2}=\frac{g(x)}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}},\quad x\in E,

is an upper gradient of
u
with respect to
\hat{d}
.

4
Doubling measures and sphericalization

As in Section
3
, we
assume in this section that
X
is unbounded.
Also recall the standing assumptions from
the beginning of Section
2
.

In Li–Shanmugalingam
[
42
]
,
the sphericalization
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})
was equipped
with the measure

{\tilde{\mu}}
(called
\mu_{a}
in
[
42
]
)
defined through

d{\tilde{\mu}}(x)=\frac{d\mu(x)}{\mu(B(a,1+|x|))^{2}}\quad\text{with }{\tilde{\mu}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0.

It is always true that
{\tilde{\mu}}({\widehat{X}})<\infty
, by
Proposition 3.1 in Björn–Björn–Li
[
12
]
.
In this paper we instead consider

d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(x)=\frac{d\mu(x)}{(1+|x|)^{q}}=\hat{d}(x)^{q}\,d\mu(x)\quad\text{with }{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0.

(4.1)

for suitable
q>0
.
Observe that measurability is the same with respect to
\mu
and
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
.
The measure
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
was considered in
[
12
]

under more restricted assumptions than here.

Instead of connectedness it is sometimes enough to assume that
the space
X
is

uniformly perfect
,
i.e. there is a constant
\kappa>1
such that

B(x,\kappa r)\setminus B(x,r)\neq\varnothing\quad\text{whenever }x\in X\text{ and }r>0.

In this section the following pointwise version will be enough.
The space
X
is
uniformly perfect at
a
for radii
\geq 1

with constant
\kappa>1
if

B(a,\kappa r)\setminus B(a,r)\neq\varnothing\quad\text{whenever }r\geq 1.

It is easy to see that the constant
1
in
r\geq 1
can equivalently
be replaced by any other positive number, provided that
\kappa
is allowed to change.
Similarly, the condition holds equivalently at any other point

x\in X
, again provided that
\kappa
is allowed to change.
This pointwise condition may have first been used
in Björn–Björn–Korte–Rogovin–Takala
[
10
]
.
See Heinonen
[
30
, Chapter 11]

for further discussion and history
of uniform perfectness.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
X
is uniformly perfect at the base point
a
for radii
\geq 1

with constant
\kappa
.
Also assume that
\mu
is doubling on
X
and
that for some
s,C_{s}>0
,

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\geq C_{s}\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{s}\quad\text{whenever }1\leq r\leq R<\infty.

(4.2)

If
q>s
, then the measure
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
, defined by (
4.1
), satisfies
for
x\in{\widehat{X}}
,

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))\simeq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\hat{d}(x)^{q}\mu\biggl(B\biggl(x,\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\biggr)\biggr),&\text{if }r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x),\\[11.38109pt]
r^{q}\mu(B(a,1/r)),&\text{if }\tfrac{1}{6}\hat{d}(x)\leq r\leq 2.\end{cases}

(4.3)

The comparison constants in the first formula in (
4.3
)
depend only on
C_{\mu}
, while in the second formula they
depend only on
C_{\mu}
,
\kappa
,
C_{s}
and
q-s
.

It is well known that doubling measures
in uniformly perfect spaces
always satisfy (
4.2
)
with some
s,C_{s}>0
(independent of the base point
a
).

For the first formula in (
4.3
),
let
r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x)
.
Then by (
3.7
),
for all
y\in B_{a}(x,r)
,

\frac{1}{\hat{d}(y)}=1+|y|\leq 1+|x|+d(x,y)<\frac{1}{\hat{d}(x)}+\frac{3r}{2\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{2\hat{d}(x)}

and similarly

\frac{1}{\hat{d}(y)}=1+|y|\geq 1+|x|-d(x,y)>\frac{1}{\hat{d}(x)}-\frac{3r}{2\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2\hat{d}(x)},

i.e.
\hat{d}(y)\simeq\hat{d}(x)

in (
4.1
) for all
y\in B_{a}(x,r)
.
The inclusions (
3.7
) and the doubling property of
\mu

then immediately imply that

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))\simeq\hat{d}(x)^{q}\mu\biggl(B\biggl(x,\frac{r}{\hat{d}(x)^{2}}\biggr)\biggr),

which proves the first formula in (
4.3
).

For the second formula (with
\tfrac{1}{6}\hat{d}(x)\leq r\leq 2
), we
first assume that
x\in X
.
Then by (
3.8
),

\displaystyle{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1+|y|>1/4r}\frac{d\mu(y)}{(1+|y|)^{q}}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{2^{j-3}}{r}\biggr)^{-q}\mu(\{y\in X:1+|y|<2^{j}/r\}).

The assumption (
4.2
) with
r
and
R
replaced
by
2/r\geq 1
and
2^{j}/r
, respectively,
yields

\mu(\{y\in X:1+|y|<2^{j}/r\})\leq\mu(B(a,2^{j}/r))\leq\frac{2^{(j-1)s}}{C_{s}}\mu(B(a,2/r)).

As
q>s
, inserting this into (
4.4
) implies that

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))\lesssim r^{q}\mu(B(a,2/r))\simeq r^{q}\mu(B(a,1/r)).

(4.5)

For the converse inequality, the first part of the proof
and the doubling property of
\mu
yield

\displaystyle{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))

\displaystyle\geq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,\tfrac{1}{6}\hat{d}(x)))

\displaystyle\simeq\hat{d}(x)^{q}\mu(B(x,1/\hat{d}(x)))\simeq\hat{d}(x)^{q}\mu(B(a,1/\hat{d}(x))).

where the last comparison is guaranteed by Lemma
2.1
,
since

|x|\leq 1/\hat{d}(x)
.
Hence, for
r\leq 2\hat{d}(x)
, we have
r\simeq\hat{d}(x)
and
thus by the doubling property of
\mu
,

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(B_{a}(x,r))\gtrsim r^{q}\mu(B(a,1/r)),

which exactly matches the upper bound (
4.5
).

On the other hand, if
2\hat{d}(x)\leq r\leq 2

and
\hat{d}(y)<\tfrac{1}{2}r
, we have by
(
3.2
) that

d_{a}(x,y)\leq\hat{d}(y)+\hat{d}(x)<r,

and so by (
3.6
),

B_{a}(x,r)\supset B_{a}\Bigl({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\frac{r}{2}\Bigr)={\widehat{X}}\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}\biggl(a,\frac{2}{r}-1\biggr)\supset X\setminus B\biggl(a,\frac{2}{r}\biggr).

Since
X
is unbounded and uniformly perfect at
a
for radii
\geq 1
,
there is
z\in X
such that

\frac{3}{r}\leq|z|<\frac{3\kappa}{r}.

It then follows that for all

z^{\prime}\in B(z,1/r)\subset X\setminus B(a,2/r)
,
we have in (
4.1
),

.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that

is uniformly perfect at the base point

for radii
\geq 1

with constant

.
Also assume that
\mu
and
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
, with parameter

Then there are

and

such that

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\geq C_{s}\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{s}\quad\text{whenever }1\leq r\leq R<\infty.

(4.7)

Consider first
r\geq 4\kappa
.
By the uniform perfectness, there is
x_{r}
such that

\frac{r}{2\kappa}\leq|x_{r}|<\frac{r}{2}.

Hence
B(x_{r},r/4\kappa)\subset A(r):=B(a,r)\setminus B(a,r/4\kappa)
.
It thus follows from Lemma
2.1
that

\mu(B(a,r))\simeq\mu(B(x_{r},r/4\kappa)\simeq\mu(A(r))

and hence

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{r^{q}}\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(A(r)).

(4.8)

Next, we show that

{\widehat{B}}(x_{r},\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x_{r}))\subset A(r)\subset{\widehat{B}}\biggl({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\frac{4\kappa}{1+r}\biggr)\setminus{\widehat{B}}\biggl({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\frac{1}{1+r}\biggr).

Indeed, the latter inclusion follows immediately from (
3.6
),
while for

we have

|y|=\frac{1}{\hat{d}(y)}-1<\frac{1}{\tfrac{2}{3}\hat{d}(x_{r})}-1=\frac{3}{2}(1+|x_{r}|)-1=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}|x_{r}|<2|x_{r}|<r

and similarly

|y|=\frac{1}{\hat{d}(y)}-1>\frac{1}{\tfrac{4}{3}\hat{d}(x_{r})}-1=\frac{3}{4}(1+|x_{r}|)-1=\frac{3}{4}|x_{r}|-\frac{1}{4}>\frac{1}{2}|x_{r}|\geq\frac{r}{4\kappa}.

Lemma
2.1
shows that, with

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}(x_{r},\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x_{r})))\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\hat{d}(x_{r})))\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho)),

which together with (

{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},4\kappa\rho))\geq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho))+{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}(x_{r},\tfrac{1}{3}\hat{d}(x_{r})))\geq(1+\theta){\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho)).

,
we easily obtain by iteration that for some

and all

\frac{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho^{\prime}))}{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho))}\lesssim\biggl(\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}\biggr)^{\delta}.

(4.11)

From (

)–(

)
and the doubling property of

we also obtain that

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{r^{q}}\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(A(r))\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},1/(1+r)))\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},1/r)).

Applying these estimates also with

together with (

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\simeq\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{q}\frac{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},1/r)}{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},1/R)}\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{q-\delta}\quad\text{for }R\geq r\geq 4\kappa.

Observing also that

\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\simeq\frac{\mu(B(a,4\kappa r))}{\mu(B(a,4\kappa R))},

we conclude that (

) holds with

for

∎

One direction follows from

,

.

5
Preservation of the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality

The following important result guarantees that
X

with a sufficiently good Poincaré inequality is

annularly quasiconvex
,
i.e. there is a constant
A\geq 1
such that whenever
B\subset X

is a ball and
y,z\in B\setminus\tfrac{1}{2}B
, there is a rectifiable curve

\gamma\subset AB\setminus(2A)^{-1}B
,
connecting
y
to
z
, with length
l_{\gamma}\leq Ad(y,z)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

(Korte
[
38
, Theorem 3.3]
)

Let
X
be a complete metric space equipped with a doubling measure
\mu

for which
there are constants
C,\sigma>1
such that

\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,R))}\leq C\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{\sigma}\quad\text{whenever }x\in X\text{ and }0<r\leq R<\infty.

If
X
supports a
\sigma
-Poincaré inequality,
then
X
is annularly quasiconvex.

We shall use the following similar condition near
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that
X
is
annularly connected for large radii around
a

if there are constants
A,R_{A}\geq 1
such that
any two points
x
and
y
with
|x|=|y|\geq R_{A}

can be connected by a (not necessarily rectifiable) curve

\gamma\subset B(a,A|x|)\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B(a,|x|/A)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(a,|x|/A)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B(a,|x|/A)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(a,|x|/A)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B(a,|x|/A)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(a,|x|/A)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B(a,|x|/A)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(a,|x|/A)}}}
.

We do not need the curve
\gamma
to be rectifiable,
although under the additional
assumptions in Theorem
5.3

below it follows quite easily from the quasiconvexity of
X

(provided by Theorem
2.4
)
that one can equivalently require

\gamma
to be rectifiable and even of length

\simeq d(x,y)
.
Thus, in quasiconvex spaces, this condition is equivalent to
“annular quasiconvexity for large radii around
a
”,
but the current formulation is more convenient for us.
At the same time, the condition is stronger than
“sequentially annularly chainable around
a

which only requires chainability for a sequence
r_{j}\to\infty
of radii, cf. Björn–Björn–Shanmugalingam
[
16
, Definition 5.2]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume that
(X,d,\mu)
is complete, unbounded and
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality,
with dilation constant
\lambda
,
and that the measure
\mu
is doubling and satisfies (
4.2
)
for some

.
Assume in addition that

X
is annularly connected for large radii around
a
,
with constants
A,R_{A}\geq 1
.

Then the sphericalization
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, with
q>s
, also supports a

p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality, with constants depending quantitatively
on all of the constants above.
Moreover, the dilation constant
\hat{\lambda}

in the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality on
{\widehat{X}}

can be chosen as
\hat{\lambda}=100\lambda\max\{A,2R_{A}\}
.

Note that if
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d})
is
\hat{L}
-quasiconvex for some
\hat{L}\geq 1
,
then the dilation constant can be chosen to be
\hat{L}
instead,
by Corollary 4.40 in
[
7
]
.
We stress the fact that the assumption (
4.2
)
and the condition
q>s
is only needed to
guarantee that
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
is doubling.
We will not use any of the more
precise estimates from Section

The main idea of the proof, based on chains of subWhitney balls,
is relatively standard and has been used
for different measures in e.g. Hajłasz–Koskela

Heinonen–Koskela
[

]

and Li–Shanmugalingam

The main difference compared with the above papers is in Case 2

{\boldsymbol{\infty}}

Rather than estimating the measure of the set

,
we control the measure of the Whitney balls

\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}}|u|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\simeq 2^{k}
.

Then, by Lemma

24\lambda r\leq\hat{d}(x)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}(x,r)}|u-u_{B^{\prime}}|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},r)}|u|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},r))}\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}2^{k}{\hat{\mu}_{q}}(G_{k})

\displaystyle\lesssim t_{0}+\sum_{2^{k}>t_{0}}\frac{2^{k}}{2^{kp/\tau}}r^{p/\tau}\biggl(\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},3Ar)}\hat{g}^{p}\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\biggr)^{1/\tau}.

Since

the series converges and

\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},r)}|u|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\lesssim t_{0}+t_{0}^{1-p/\tau}t_{0}^{p/\tau}=2t_{0}\lesssim r\biggl(\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},3Ar)}\hat{g}^{p}\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\biggr)^{1/p}.

A standard argument using the triangle inequality
allows us to replace

.
Note that the dilation constant here is
3A
.

Case 3:

\hat{d}(x)<24\lambda r
and

We then have, using also

\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}(x,r)}|u-u_{{\widehat{B}}(x,r)}|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\leq 2\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}(x,r)}|u-u_{{\widehat{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}\\
\lesssim\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{{\widehat{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}|u-u_{{\widehat{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}|\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}.

The last integral is estimated using the

p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality
for the ball
{\widehat{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
(with dilation

) from Case 2.
Note that

.
The inclusion

3A{\widehat{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\infty}}={\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},75A\lambda r)\subset{\widehat{B}}(x,100A\lambda r)

.

6
Consequences for upper gradients and capacities

In this section we assume that the assumptions in Theorem
5.3

are satisfied.
It thus follows from Corollary
4.2
and
Theorem
5.3
that
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
is doubling and
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality on
{\widehat{X}}
.

We begin with some general results.
The following lemma replaces Lemma 3.2 in
[
12
]
, which
states that the two moduli are always equal when
q=2p
.
The equality fails when
q\neq 2p
,
but here it is enough that the zero modulus families
coincide.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a family of compact rectifiable curves in
X
.
Then
\Gamma
has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus with respect to
(X,d,\mu)

if and only if it has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus with respect to
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.

Let
\Gamma_{m}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\subset B(a,m)\}
.
As
d
and
\mu
are comparable to
\hat{d}
and
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
on
B(a,m)
,
we see that
\Gamma_{m}
has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus with respect to
(X,d,\mu)

if and only if it has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus with respect to
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.
Since this is true for all
m\in\mathbf{Z}
, the same is true for

\Gamma=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{m}
.
∎

The following relation
extends Lemma
3.2
to
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients.
Note that
g
is measurable with respect to
\mu
if and only if
it is measurable with respect to
{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
.
The proof is similar to the proof of
Lemma 3.3 in
[
12
]
, but relies
on Lemma
6.1
above
instead of Lemma 3.2 in
[
12
]
.
Also the proofs of most of the other results
in this section are similar to proofs of corresponding
results in
[
12
]
.
For the reader’s convenience we provide the short proofs.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
E\subset X
be measurable,

u:E\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
be a function,
g:E\to[0,\infty]

be measurable, and

\hat{g}(x)=g(x)(1+d(x,a))^{2},\quad x\in E.

Then
g
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
in
E
with respect to

(d,\mu)
if and only if
\hat{g}
is a

p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
in
E
with respect to

(\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.

Assume that
g
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
with respect to

(d,\mu)
.
Let
\Gamma
be the family of exceptional
nonconstant rectifiable
curves in
E
for which (
2.1
) fails.
Then
\Gamma
has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus with respect to
(X,d,\mu)
(since
g
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient)
and thus also with respect to
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, by Lemma
6.1
.
Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to E
be a nonconstant rectifiable curve not in
\Gamma
.
Then, using (
3.9
),

|u(\gamma(0))-u(\gamma(1))|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds=\int_{\gamma}\hat{g}\,d\hat{s}.

Thus

\hat{g}
is
a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of

u
with respect to

(\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.
The converse implication is shown similarly.
∎

From now on, for a function
u
we
let
g_{u}
and
\hat{g}_{u}
be the minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients with respect
to
(X,d,\mu)
and
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, respectively,
when they exist.
We also let
{C_{p}}
and
{\widehat{C}_{p}}
denote the Sobolev capacities
with respect to
(X,d,\mu)
and
({\widehat{X}},\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, respectively.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
E\subset X
be measurable.

1.

If
E
is bounded, then
N^{1,p}(E,d,\mu)=N^{1,p}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
,
as sets.

2.

N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(E,d,\mu)=N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.

The same is true for
D^{p}
.

By Corollary
6.2
,
g_{u}\simeq\hat{g}_{u}
a.e. in
E
if
E
is bounded.
As also
d\simeq\hat{d}
and
\mu\simeq{\hat{\mu}_{q}}
in

,
we immediately get

, while

2
follows directly from
1
,
since
g_{u}

only depends on the function locally.
The corresponding results for
D^{p}
are shown similarly.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
E\subset X
be measurable and
q=2p
.
If
u\in D^{p}_{\rm loc}(E,d,\mu)=D^{p}_{\rm loc}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
,
then

\hat{g}_{u}(x)=g_{u}(x)(1+d(x,a))^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{E}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu=\int_{E}\hat{g}_{u}^{p}\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}.

(6.1)

In particular,
D^{p}(E,d,\mu)=D^{p}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
.

Note first that
D^{p}_{\rm loc}(E,d,\mu)=D^{p}_{\rm loc}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})
, by
Lemma
6.3
,
and
\hat{g}_{u}(x)=g_{u}(x)(1+d(x,a))^{2}
, by Corollary

.
Therefore,

\int_{E}\hat{g}_{u}^{p}\,d{\hat{\mu}_{q}}=\int_{E}g_{u}(x)^{p}(1+d(x,a))^{2p}\frac{d\mu(x)}{(1+d(x,a))^{2p}}=\int_{E}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu,

from which it follows directly
that
u\in D^{p}(E,\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{q}})

if and only if
u\in D^{p}(E,d,\mu)
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
E\subset X
.
Then

{\widehat{C}_{p}}(E)=0

if and only if

{C_{p}}(E)=0
.

If moreover,
X
is Ahlfors
Q
-regular and
p>Q
,
then
{\widehat{C}_{p}}(E)=0
only if
E=\varnothing
.

By Lemma
6.3
1
,
the
N^{1,p}
-norms are comparable in
B(a,j)
,
j\geq 1
,
from which it follows
that
{C_{p}^{\prime}}(E\cap B(a,j))=0
if and only if

{\widehat{C}_{p}^{\prime}}(E\cap B(a,j))=0
, where
{C_{p}^{\prime}}
and
{\widehat{C}_{p}^{\prime}}

are capacities with
B(a,j)
as the underlying space.
These capacities have the same zero sets in
B(a,j)
as
{C_{p}}
and
{\widehat{C}_{p}}
,
respectively, by e.g. Lemma 2.24 in
[
7
]
.
The countable subadditivity of the capacities then
shows that
{C_{p}}(E)=0
if and only if
{\widehat{C}_{p}}(E)=0
.

As for the second part, we must have
Q\geq 1
since

is connected.
Corollary 5.39 in
[
7
]
therefore concludes the proof.
∎

The classification of unbounded metric spaces as parabolic and
hyperbolic plays an important role for the point
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.
For this we first need to define the condenser capacity
\capp_{p}
.
We only need this for bounded
E
.
(The definition for unbounded
E
is more complicated, see
Björn–Björn
[
9
]
.)

Definition 6.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset X
be a (possibly unbounded) open set.
The
condenser capacity
of a bounded set

E\subset\Omega
with respect to

\Omega
is

\capp_{p}(E,\Omega)=\inf_{u}\int_{\Omega}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all

u\in N^{1,p}(X)
such that
\chi_{E}\leq u\leq\chi_{\Omega}
.
If no such function
u
exists then
\capp_{p}(E,\Omega)=\infty
.

Definition 6.7
.

Assume that
X
is unbounded.
Then
X
is called
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic

if
\capp_{p}(K,X)>0
for some compact set
K\subset X
.
Otherwise,
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolic
.

The following
characterization of
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolicity was obtained
in Björn–Björn–Lehrbäck
[
11
]
.
(Theorem 1.0.1 in Keith–Zhong
[
34
]

provides us with the better Poincaré inequality required in
[
11
]
.)

Theorem 6.8
.

(Theorem 5.5 in
[
11
]
)

Assume that
p>1
.
Then
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolic
if and only if

\int_{1}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{r}{\mu(B(a,r))}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\,dr=\infty.

Based on this and Proposition 5.3 in
[
11
]

we obtain the following result.

Lemma 6.9
.

Assume that
p>1
.
Then
{\widehat{C}_{p}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0
if and only if

\int_{1}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{r^{q-2p+1}}{\mu(B(a,r))}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\,dr=\infty.

In particular, if
q=2p
then
{\widehat{C}_{p}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0
if and only if
X

is
p\mspace{1mu}
-parabolic.

Proposition 5.3 in Björn–Björn–Lehrbäck
[
11
]
shows that

{\widehat{C}_{p}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=0
if and only if

\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\frac{\rho}{{\hat{\mu}_{q}}({\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\rho))}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\,d\rho=\infty.

Remark
4.3

and the change of variable
r=1/\rho
then prove the first statement.

The statement for
q=2p
now
follows immediately from Theorem
6.8
.
∎

7
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
under sphericalization

In this section we assume that the assumptions in Theorem
5.3

are satisfied with
p>1
and
q=2p
.
Thus,
by Corollary
4.2
and
Theorem
5.3
,
{\hat{\mu}_{2p}}
is doubling and
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality on
{\widehat{X}}
.
We also assume
that
\Omega\subset X
is a nonempty open set.

In the rest of the paper
we apply sphericalization and the results from the earlier
sections to obtain new results about
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions and the
corresponding Dirichlet
problem on unbounded open sets,
from similar results in bounded open sets.

Recall from Lemma
4.1
that we need

q>\overline{q}_{\infty}:=\inf\biggl\{s>0:\frac{\mu(B(a,r))}{\mu(B(a,R))}\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{s}\text{ for }1\leq r\leq R<\infty\biggr\}.

Hence
our condition on
p
is that
p>\max\{1,\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{q}_{\infty}\}
,
which in Ahlfors
Q
-regular spaces means that

p>\max\{1,\tfrac{1}{2}Q\}
.
It is quite easy to see that
\overline{q}_{\infty}
is independent of the choice
of the base point
a\in X
.

Note that
\mathbf{R}^{n}
equipped with a
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible
measure
d\mu=w\,dx
, as in Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
31
]
,
is included here provided that
p>\max\{1,\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{q}_{\infty}\}
.

Remark 7.1
.

With Theorem
5.3
, we can treat more situations
than in Björn–Björn–Li
[
12
]
.
The main advantage compared with
[
12
]
is that
we do not require Ahlfors regularity.
But even for Ahlfors
Q
-regular spaces, we can treat a wider range of
p\mspace{1mu}
.
More precisely, we allow for all
p>Q
and only require
a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality for
\mu
,
while
(2-Q)p<Q
and a stronger
t_{p}
-Poincaré inequality with

t_{p}=pQ/(2p-Q)<Q
were required in
[
12
]
.
Also the case
Q=1
, which was
omitted in
[
12
]
, is included here.
The case
Q/2<p\leq Q
, with
Q>1
, was however already covered by
[
12
]
.

Even under the assumptions in
[
12
]
our results
below are more general since they use the
more refined capacity
{{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle C}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{C}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle C}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{C}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle C}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{C}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle C}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{C}}}_{p}}
and the larger space

D^{p}(\overline{\Omega},d,\mu)
(instead of
{C_{p}}
and
D^{p}(X,d,\mu)
).
Results as in Theorems
1.4
and
1.5

were not considered in
[
12
]
.

At the same time, we need to explicitly assume in addition that
X

is annularly connected for large radii around
a
,
since for
p>Q
this does not follow
from Theorem
5.1
, unlike in
[
12
]
where annular quasiconvexity
followed from the better
t_{p}
-Poincaré inequality
(and Theorem
5.1
).
In our setting, it is only guaranteed by Theorem
5.1

if the measure
\mu
grows sufficiently fast at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}

and satisfies a sufficiently good Poincaré inequality.

We start this section by some necessary preliminaries about

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions and Perron solutions.

Definition 7.2
.

A function
u\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega,d,\mu)
is a

minimizer

in
\Omega

(with respect to
(d,\mu)
) if

\int_{\varphi\neq 0}g^{p}_{u}\,d\mu\leq\int_{\varphi\neq 0}g_{u+\varphi}^{p}\,d\mu\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in{\Lip_{c}}(\Omega),

where
g_{u}
and
g_{u+\varphi}
are the minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients of
u

and
u+\varphi
with respect to
(d,\mu)
.
Here,
{\Lip_{c}}(\Omega)
denotes the space of Lipschitz functions
with compact support in
\Omega
.

Minimizers
with respect to
(\hat{d},{\hat{\mu}_{2p}})
are defined analogously.
A
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function
is a continuous minimizer.

We are primarily interested in solvability and uniqueness for
the Dirichlet (boundary value) problem
for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions in unbounded open sets,
and the associated boundary regularity.
The most general way of treating the Dirichlet problem
is to consider Perron solutions,
for which we need superharmonic functions.

Definition 7.3
.

A function
u:\Omega\to(-\infty,\infty]
is

superharmonic
in
\Omega
if

(i).

u
is lower semicontinuous;

(ii).

u
is not identically
\infty
in any component of
\Omega
;

(iii).

for every nonempty open set
G\Subset\Omega
with
{C_{p}}(X\setminus G)>0

and every function
v\in C({\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}})
which is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in
G
and such
that
v\leq u
on
\partial G
, we have
v\leq u
in
G
.

This definition of superharmonicity is the same as the one
usually used in the Euclidean literature, e.g. in
Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
31
, Section 7]
.
It is equivalent to other definitions of superharmonicity on metric spaces,
by Theorem 6.1 in Björn
[
4
]

(or
[
7
, Theorem 14.10]
).

Minimizers,
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
and superharmonic functions
were introduced in metric spaces by
Shanmugalingam
[
45
]
,
Kinnunen–Shanmugalingam
[
37
]

and Kinnunen–Martio
[
36
]
.
In
[
37
]

it was also shown that
under the assumptions of doubling and a

-Poincaré inequality,
minimizers
can be modified on sets of zero capacity to become
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic
functions.

Our choice of the sphericalization measure
{\hat{\mu}_{2p}}
leads to the
following invariance result which is crucial for the applications in this
section.
The proof in Björn–Björn–Li
[
12
]
(based on
(

)) applies verbatim here.

Theorem 7.4
.

(Theorem 6.3 in
[
12
]
)

A function

Definition 7.5
