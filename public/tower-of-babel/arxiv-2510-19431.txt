Abstract
. Gauge symmetric methods for data representation and analysis utilize tools from the differential geometry of vector bundles in order to achieve consistent data processing architectures with respect to local symmetry and equivariance. In this work, we elaborate concepts of geometric gauge theory for data science. Motivated by lattice gauge theory , we focus on discrete descriptions of vector bundles for data representation and analysis, with clear relations to the established mathematical bundle formalism. Our approach unifies various existing approaches to data processing via vector bundles, within the framework of gauge theory. We provide geometric insights into gauge symmetric heat kernel operators that are closely related to graph connection Laplacians, and into their data transformation properties in terms of the non-trivial nullspace of the corresponding gauged Laplacians . In particular, we utilize a discrete Yang-Mills energy in order to characterize significant properties of the heat kernel in terms of associated synchronization problems .

Node Features on Graphs, Gauge Invariance and Heat Kernels

,

,

We apply and interpret concepts from gauge theory in the context of data science in this paper, which requires discrete formulations of gauge theory. Specifically, node feature data on graphs are represented as

The gauged heat kernel approach establishes links between different approaches to discrete geometric modelling in data science, like the discrete bundle approaches of

Thus, gauge theory appears to provide a unifying principle for gauge invariance and vector bundle methods in data science,

Consequently, along these lines, we furthermore transfer analysis methods for gauge fields to data analysis, based on

[

Contribution

Our work extends the perspectives on gauge symmetry for data science adopted by
[
WFVW21
]
and
[
GAC+23
]
, from a complementary point of view, in that we work out the details and implications of gauge invariant methods when applied to node features over a finite graph.
In addition, we compare our discrete theory to smooth approaches to gauge invariance and specify explicitly

Our approach removes technical requirements of fiber bundle theory and differential geometry from the gauge invariant frameworks of

,

]

We identify heat kernel methods as the

[

]
,
[
WFVW21
]
and the approaches in
[

,
BS14
]
motivated by PDE methods.
This helps to interpret the

of
[

,
JW22
]
in the context of differential operators on vector bundles, so that we can connect the vector bundle PDE methods of
[
BS14
]
and the perspective on deep learning adopted in
[
WFVW21
]
.
Furthermore, our discrete gauge theoretic approach provides a perspective under which other discrete vector bundle data processing approaches may be related to the framework of
[
WFVW21
]
, such as the synchronization bundle methods of
[
GBM21
]
and the cellular sheaf methods of
[
HG21
,
BDGC+23
,
BBDB24
]
.
Thus our formalism ‘interpolates’ between discrete and smooth vector bundle approaches to data science and establishes gauge theory as the organizing first principle.

Based on these relations between data science and gauge theory, we extend the

of
[

]
to a discrete Yang-Mills analysis and show the connection to heat kernel approaches.
Additionally, we apply gauge theoretic arguments in order to analyze the data transformation properties of our heat kernel formalism. We show how the underlying graph structure shapes the nullspace of the generator of our heat kernel, in terms of a

To summarize, we complement the existing apparatus for gauge symmetry in data science with concepts from discrete and smooth gauge theory. In particular, we show how tools from Yang-Mills theory integrate into data science.

2.
Gauge Invariant Representations of Discrete Data

This section first recalls the concept of
gauge invariance
for data science as introduced in
[
Bat11
,
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
. Then we extend this perspective and provide additional interpretations for the formalism of associated vector bundles, in order to highlight its significance for data science.

We focus on gauge invariance concepts for
discrete data
. That is, we do not impose smoothness or manifold assumptions on our data. We relate our approach to smooth vector bundle theory in Appendix
A
.

2.1.
Notation

Let
\eta
be a finite,
connected
, simple, directed graph with vertex set
\nu
and directed edge relation
<
, i.e. we write
i<j
if there is a directed edge from node
i
to node
j
. We also denote by
\varepsilon
the edge set of
\eta
and by
\sim
the
symmetrization
of
<
, that is
i\sim j
if
i<j
or
j<i
for
i,j\in\nu
. We write also
i>j\in\varepsilon
and
i\sim j\in\varepsilon
to address the edges of a graph
\eta
.

The symbol
N\coloneqq|\nu|
denotes the number of graph nodes such that set
\nu
can be identified with the set
\{1,\dots,N\}
after fixing a total order of the vertices.

We consider any fixed dimension
d>0
and define the set

X=\{x:\nu\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\}=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

(2.1)

of
d
-dimensional vectors indexed by the nodes of the graph
\eta
. We equip
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Euclidean scalar product
\langle v,w\rangle=v^{\top}w
, such that
X
also becomes an inner product vector space. Elements
x\in X
are represented by tuples
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i\in\nu
. We identify
X
with the space of matrices
\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
by identifying the tuple
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\nu}
with the
N\times d
matrix whose rows are given by
x_{i}
.

We assume furthermore that the graph
\eta
comes along with a symmetric weight function

\kappa:\nu\times\nu\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\quad\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{ji},\quad\kappa_{ij}=0\quad\text{if}\quad i\not\sim j,

(2.2)

that is,
\kappa
is a positive weight function on the symmetrized (undirected) edges of the graph.

2.2.
Gauge Symmetry for Discrete Data

This subsection introduces coordinate independence for data modelling as in
[
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
, here adapted to the case of
discrete
data. Here ‘discrete’ refers to a graph
\eta
that supports the data, rather than to the range of the data that are real-valued. Suppose a data space
X
of the format (
2.1
) is given. We want to transform data samples
x\in X
in terms of a function
f:X\to X
.

Gauge invariance
entails a change of perspective on the feature vector space
X=\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
. We postulate that there exists an abstract vector space
\Gamma
, which is the natural space for representing given data and that
\Gamma\cong X
after choosing a basis of
\Gamma
.
A notable point here is that there are many choices for isomorphisms
\Gamma\cong X
but no canonical one among them. Rather, the space
\Gamma
represents all the bases of
X
simultaneously.
Elements
\phi\in\Gamma
are called
gauge invariant
or
basis independent representatives
of samples
x\in X
. We call mappings
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma

gauge invariant transformations
. They represent
basis independent transformations
of the space
\Gamma
.

The following assumption is adopted throughout.

Assumption 2.1 (gauge invariance).

Data
x\in X
must be represented in terms of gauge invariant representatives
\phi\in\Gamma
and are transformed by gauge invariant mappings
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
.

The concept of gauge invariance and its role in data processing has been motivated and discussed in the literature
[
Bat11
,
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
.
We point out that our analysis of gauge invariance is not aimed at facilitating numerical computations. Rather, we are interested in the
consistency conditions
resulting from assumptions about gauge invariance and their significance in the context of data science.

Abstract vector spaces
\Gamma
are not directly amenable for practical applications, since no representation suggests itself that is suitable for numerical computation. However, since the basis independence assumption stipulates that the abstract space
\Gamma
is the correct domain for analyzing data, we are forced to find a way of working with
\Gamma
.

A solution is provided by
gauge theory
known from differential geometry and theoretical physics
[
Ham17
]
, due to the observation that the space of all possible choices of bases
X
is given by the general linear group
\GL(X)
. Gauge theory considers a
concrete model
for the space
\Gamma
of the form

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=(\GL(X)\times X)/\GL(X)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\{[g,x]\subset\GL(X)\times X\mid[gh,x]=[g,hx],\;h\in\GL(X)\}.

We note here that the group
\GL(X)
acts naturally on
X
, since
\GL(X)
consists of linear isomorphisms of
X
. This action can be understood more concretely if we identify
X=\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
via the row stacking isomorphism
\operatorname{vec}
. The isomorphism
\operatorname{vec}
also identifies
\GL(X)
with
\GL_{Nd}(\mathbb{R})
, which we denote by
g\in\GL(X)\mapsto\tilde{g}\in\GL_{Nd}(\mathbb{R})
. The action of
\GL(X)
on
X
, denoted by
\GL(X)\curvearrowright X
, is then given by

\GL(X)\times X\to X,\quad(g,x)\mapsto gx\coloneqq\operatorname{vec}^{-1}(\tilde{g}\operatorname{vec}(x)).

(2.4)

The set
\Gamma
forms a vector space
isomorphic
but
not equal
to
X
, with respect to the operations

[g,x]+[g,x^{\prime}]=[g,x+x^{\prime}],\quad r[g,x]=[g,rx],\quad\forall[g,x],[g,x^{\prime}]\in\Gamma,\;r\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.5)

Modelling data in terms of the space
\Gamma
imposes the constraint that vectors
x\in X
must come along with an explicit choice of basis
g\in\GL(X)
in order to be well-defined. The space
\Gamma
is canonically isomorphic to
X
in terms of the isomorphism

\Gamma\to X,\quad[g,x]\mapsto gx.

(2.6)

Remark 2.2 (
\Gamma
versus
X
).

Despite the existence of this canonical isomorphism
\Gamma\cong X
, we do not identify the two spaces. The goal of gauge invariant data modelling entails to keep track of
how
to identify
\Gamma
and
X
. In particular,
two distinct roles
of
\GL(X)
in
\Gamma=(\GL(X)\times X)/\GL(X)
are considered; it appears as a group and as a
principal homogeneous space
.

Additionally, we would like to point out that
\Gamma
represents a special case of a more general modelling scheme; similar constructions can be made when replacing the group action
\GL(X)\curvearrowright X
, see also Remark
2.11
.

The concrete model (
2.3
) of
\Gamma
now allows to describe gauge invariant representatives
\phi\in\Gamma
explicitly.
The elements
[g,x]\in\Gamma
are equivalence classes of bases and vectors, identified by the relation
[gh,h^{-1}x]=[g,x]
, which means that if we transform the basis
g
along with the vector
x
, then we stay in the same equivalence class.
A data sample
x
can be promoted to a gauge invariant representation by choosing a basis
g\in\GL(X)
and setting

\phi\coloneqq[g,x].

(2.7)

By means of the concrete model (
2.3
) of
\Gamma
, we can give a complete characterization of transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
.

Proposition 2.3 (gauge invariant transformations).

The gauge invariant transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
correspond one-to-one to the functions

F:\GL(X)\times X\to X

(2.8)

with equivariance property

F(gh^{-1},hx)=hF(g,x),\quad\forall h\in\GL(X).

(2.9)

Proof.

We denote in this proof by
\Sigma_{g}
the isomorphism
\Gamma\to X,[h,x]\mapsto g^{-1}hx
, for any fixed
g\in\GL(X)
. This implies
\Sigma_{g}([g,x])=x
and
\Sigma_{gh^{-1}}=h\Sigma_{g}
, since

\Sigma_{gh^{-1}}([h^{\prime},x])=hg^{-1}h^{\prime}x=h\Sigma_{g}([h^{\prime},x])

(2.10)

for all
g,h\in\GL(X)
and
[h^{\prime},x]\in\Gamma
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a given gauge invariant transformation
\Gamma\to\Gamma
. Then define
F
by

F:\GL(X)\times X\to X;\quad F(g,x)=\Sigma_{g}(\mathcal{F}([g,x])).

(2.11)

F
defined by this rule satisfies

\displaystyle F(gh^{-1},hx)

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{gh^{-1}}(\mathcal{F}[gh^{-1},hx])

(2.12a)

\displaystyle{=}\Sigma_{gh^{-1}}(\mathcal{F}([g,x]))

(2.12b)

\displaystyle{=}h\Sigma_{g}(\mathcal{F}([g,x]))

(2.12c)

\displaystyle=hF(g,x)

(2.12d)

This implies relation (
2.9
).

As for the converse direction, assume that
F:\GL(X)\to X
satisfying (
2.9
) is given. Then define

\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma,\quad[x,g]\mapsto[g,F(g,x)].

(2.13)

We establish that
\mathcal{F}
is well-defined,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}([gh,h^{-1}x])

\displaystyle=[gh,F(gh,h^{-1}x)]\overset{\eqref{eq:consis}}{=}[gh,h^{-1}F(g,x)]\overset{\eqref{eq:smashed-prod}}{=}[g,F(g,x)]

(2.14a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}([g,x]).

(2.14b)

These two constructions are clearly inverse to each other.
∎

Corollary 2.4 (characterization of gauge invariant transformations).

The set of gauge invariant transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
corresponds one-to-one to the space of mappings
X\to X/\GL(X)
, where
X/\GL(X)\coloneqq\big\{[x]\in X\mid[x]=[gx],\;\forall g\in\GL(X)\big\}
is the space of orbits of the group action
\GL(X)\curvearrowright X
.

Proof.

The gauge invariant transformations
\mathcal{F}
correspond one-to-one to equivariant functions
F:\GL(X)\times X\to X
, due to Proposition
2.3
. Equivariant functions are fully characterized by their induced functions
(\GL(X)\times X)/\GL(X)\to X/\GL(X)
on the corresponding orbit spaces.

Consequently, since the space
\Gamma=(\GL(X)\times X)/\GL(X)
is isomorphic to
X
as argued in connection with (
2.5
), a bijection exists between the spaces of gauge invariant transformations
\Gamma\to\Gamma
and functions
X\to X/\GL(X)
.
∎

Corollary
2.4
says that
\GL(X)
-gauge invariant transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
correspond to functions
f:X\to X/\GL(X)
. The space of orbits
X/\GL(X)
contains only two elements, however: the orbit of 0 and the orbit of nonzero elements. Consequently, the transformations
\mathcal{F}
can be understood as binary classification functions
f:X\to\{0,1\}
.

This reveals that
\GL(X)
-gauge invariance imposes conditions on the space of admissible feature transformations which restrict the applicability of
\GL(X)
-gauge invariant modelling to binary classification tasks.

Remark 2.5 (subgroup-gauge invariance).

We can achieve broader applicability of the basis independence assumption by considering gauge invariance under suitable
subgroups

\Xi\subset\GL(X)
. Subgroup-gauge invariance changes the structure of the gauge-invariant feature vector representation, see (
2.19
). Yet, the statements of Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
remain valid after replacing
\GL(X)
by
\Xi
.

For instance, if we consider the subgroup
\Xi=\SO(X)\subset\GL(X)
, we can still construct the space
\Gamma=(\Xi\times X)/\Xi
, but the mappings
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
are now parametrized by the orbit space
X/\Xi=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which projects to
X
via
x\mapsto\|x\|
.

The following Definition (
2.6
) and Assumption
2.7
below prepare our choice of the subgroup
\Xi=\SO(d)^{N}
– cf. (
2.18
) – for the remainder of this paper.

2.3.
Discrete Vector Bundles

So far, we considered the feature space
X
as a vector space. We point out additional structures carried by
X
.

(i)

X=\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
is a product of
N
copies of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

X
is an inner product space through

\langle\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(x^{\top}x^{\prime}),

(2.15)

compatible with the product structure, that is
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
induces an inner product on the factors of
X
.

Now we define gauge invariant feature spaces that respect these structures of
X
.

Definition 2.6 (discrete vector bundle).

A
discrete vector bundle
is a vector space
\Gamma
with a decomposition

\Gamma=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{0}

(2.16)

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Gamma_{0}
is another vector space. For an element
\phi\in\Gamma
, we write
\phi_{i}\in\Gamma_{0}
for the projection to the
i
-th factor of
\phi
.

A
compatible metric
on
\Gamma
is a scalar product
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
that respects the product decomposition. This means that we can write

\langle\langle\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle,

(2.17)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a scalar product on
\Gamma_{0}
.

A
compatible isometry
between two discrete vector bundles
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
and compatible metrics is a vector space isometry
\Gamma\cong\Gamma^{\prime}
that induces an isometry on each factor.

Recall that
X=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
is itself a discrete vector bundle with compatible metric, with factors
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These notions lead to a modified version of the gauge invariant modelling assumption, if we impose gauge invariance only for the subgroup
\Xi\subset\GL(X)
of automorphisms of
X
compatible with the discrete vector bundle and metric structure of
X
.

Assumption 2.7 (
\Xi
-gauge invariance).

We postulate that there exists an abstract vector space
\Gamma
which admits a discrete vector bundle structure and a compatible metric, and is isomorphic to
X
via a compatible isometry. Data
x\in X
must be represented in terms of gauge invariant representatives
\phi\in\Gamma
and transformed by transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
, as characterized by Proposition
2.3
.

This modified assumption expresses that data can be modeled in a basis invariant way, via an abstract vector space
\Gamma
that respects the product decomposition of
X
and its metric structure. In view of Remark
2.5
, transformations
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
are called
\Xi
-gauge invariant transformations
.

Similar to Equation (
2.3
), we specify a concrete model for
\Gamma
by noticing that the group of factor preserving isometries of
X
is given by

\Xi\coloneqq\SO(d)^{N}\subset\GL(X),

(2.18)

which acts on
X=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
by applying factorwise rotations. The group
\Xi
can be embedded into
\GL(X)
, after identification with
\GL_{Nd}(\mathbb{R})
, via block matrices with
d\times d
rotation matrices on the main diagonal and matrices 0 elsewhere.
\Xi
represents the orthonormal bases of
X
that respect the product structure, and there is a group action
\Xi\curvearrowright X
analogous to (
2.4
).

Remark 2.8 (local symmetry properties).

This property of gauge invariant transformations is often interpreted as a
equivariance under local symmetry transformations

[
BS14
,
Ham17
,
WFVW21
,
Wip23
,
GAC+23
]
.
‘Local’ here refers to the fact that the group
\Xi=\SO(d)^{N}
acts with rotation matrices
\xi_{i}\in\SO(d)

independently
at each graph node
i\in\nu
. In other words, a group element
\xi\in\Xi
‘localizes’ at each node as
\xi_{i}
, such that the overall decomposition of
X
into factors is preserved. See Figure
2
for an example and a visualization of such a group action.

In the context of theoretical physics, this observation leads to
gauge theory
as an extension of Maxwell’s theory of electrodynamics
[
PS95
,
Gar15
,
Ham17
]
.

On the other hand, these symmetry properties were also central parts of the works
[
BS14
,
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
where they served as a motivation to introduce gauge invariance and equivariance in data science. In particular, it was recognized that local symmetries are important when we consider transformation properties of data sets, and it was demonstrated that local symmetry-aware modelling architectures can improve performance in various applications.

Figure 2
.

Local rotations applied to an array of MNIST digits. The relevant graph here is a 8
\times
8 grid graph with node feature vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{784}
(visualized by MNIST images). Thus, the relevant feature space is given by
X=\mathbb{R}^{64\times 784}
and the local rotation is performed by acting with the group
\SO(784)^{64}
, or more precisely, via a
\U(1)^{64}
subgroup of
\SO(784)^{64}
.

Definition 2.9 (discrete bundle sections).

We define the space of
discrete bundle sections
as

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=(\Xi\times X)/\Xi

(2.19)

\displaystyle=\{[\xi,x]\in\Xi\times X\mid[\xi\tau,x]=[\xi,\tau x],\;\forall\tau\in\Xi\}.

Remark 2.10 (connection to the fiber bundle formalism).

The above definition of the abstract vector space
\Gamma
connects to the general framework of
associated vector bundles
(cf., e.g.,
[
Jos17
, pp. 87-88]
).
Given a principal
G
-bundle
P\to M
with smooth manifolds
P
and
M
and structure group
G
, and a vector space
V
on which
G
acts linearly from the left, the
associated vector bundle

E\to M
with fiber
V
is constructed as follows. One has a free action of
G
on
P\times V
from the right,

P\times V\times G\to P\times V,\qquad(p,v)\cdot g=(p\cdot g,g^{-1}v).

(2.20)

Dividing out the
G
-action by identifying
(p,v)
and
(p,v)\cdot g
, the fibers of the bundle
(P\times V)/G\to M
become vector spaces isomorphic to
V
, and

E\coloneqq(P\times V)/G\to M

(2.21)

is the associated vector bundle with fiber
(G\times V)/G=V
.

The relationship between the discrete bundle sections
\Gamma
and the continuous framework is explained in Appendix
A
, where we show that
\Gamma
can be thought of as a discretization of the space of sections
\Gamma(E)
.
We point out that we do not require the graph structure so far. The constructed objects only rely on the finite set
\nu
. In the next section, however, the graph structure will be necessary in order to define graph voltages and Laplacians.

We can write
\Gamma
also in the form

\Gamma=\big((\SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})/\SO(d)\big)^{N}

(2.22)

which clarifies that
\Gamma
has indeed a discrete vector bundle structure. A compatible metric can be defined as

\langle\langle\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\xi_{i}x_{i},\tau_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad\phi=[\xi,x],\;\phi^{\prime}=[\tau,x^{\prime}].

(2.23)

Importantly, this choice is well-defined and does not depend of the representatives
x,x^{\prime}
.

By virtue of Proposition
2.3
, Corollary
2.4
, the choice (
2.18
) and the product-compatible form (
2.22
), we can completely characterize
\Xi
-gauge invariant mappings
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
as functions
f:X\to X/\Xi
. In contrast to the
\GL(X)
case, the orbit space
X/\Xi
now is bijective to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
. This means that the class of
\Xi
-gauge invariant transformations
\mathcal{F}
effectively represents the class of functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
.

Thus, by restriction to the subgroup
\Xi\subset\GL(X)
, we significantly increase the class of transformations that are representable in a gauge invariant manner, from
X\to\{0,1\}
to
X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
.

Remark 2.11 (general group representations).

We present here only a snapshot of the theory of discrete associated vector bundles, where one would consider general subgroups
\Xi\subset\GL(X)
induced by group representations

\rho:G\to\GL_{d}(\mathbb{R}).

(2.24)

Such a representation induces an abstract vector space

\Gamma=(G^{N}\times X)/G^{N},

(2.25)

which also admits a product structure compatible with the product structure of
X
.

We focus in this work on the special group representation
\SO(d)\to\GL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, since we aim for a simplified exposition in terms of more concrete arguments. Generally, the smaller the subgroup
G
, the larger is the space of data transformations compatible with gauge invariance. For a more general treatment, we refer to
[
Ham17
,
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
.

The theory that we develop in the remainder of this paper, however, holds more generally for compact, connected Lie groups
G
with
d
-dimensional orthogonal representations.

In the next section, we define
heat kernel methods
compatible with the notion of gauge invariance. This will allow us to connect the gauge invariant formalism to the theory of discrete connections
[
LMW05
,
GL10
]
and associated Laplacians
[
SW12
]
.

3.
Gauge Invariant Heat Kernel Operators for Data Science

We define in this section heat kernel methods for data transformation based on the gauge invariant modelling method presented in the preceding section. The general literature on heat kernels is vast. We refer, e.g., to
[
Dav89
,
BGV96
,
Gri09
,
Avr15
]
for general expositions.

3.1.
Heat Kernel Approaches to Data Transformation

We first collect few basic concepts about generalized heat kernels and give examples where such transformations appear in data science.
In this subsection, we assume that data samples to be transformed are elements of a Hilbert space
(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, for which the node feature space
X
is an example.

Definition 3.1 (generalized heat kernel).

A
generalized heat kernel

K^{t}:V\to V
is a one-parameter family of continuous linear operators, indexed by
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with the following properties.

(i)

There exists a continuous operator
L:V\to V
such
K^{t}=e^{-tL}
holds.
L
is called the
generator
.

(ii)

The generator
L
is positive semi-definite and self-adjoint.

The superscript
t
on
K
is suppressed in the following.

The importance of such heat kernel transformations for data science is evident.
An example is provided by the graph heat kernel
K_{\eta,\kappa}
associated to a weighted graph
(\eta,\kappa)
and acting on the space of node features
X
over the graph
[
Gri11
]
. The generator of
K_{\eta,\kappa}
is the weighted graph Laplacian matrix
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}
. The graph heat kernel is of great interest for node labelling tasks on graphs, such as spectral clustering methods
[
Chu97
,
vL07
]
and graph neural networks
[
KW17
,
BDGC+23
,
BBDB24
]
.

3.2.
Gauged Heat Kernel (GHK)

We now introduce heat kernel operators
K_{\chi}
derived from discrete gauge theory
[
GL10
,
Jia23
]
. An important property of these operators is that they are consistent with
\Xi
-gauge symmetry while acting on the abstract space
\Gamma
.
In order to define these heat kernels we need to equip
\eta
with more structure which is required to generate the relevant operators.

3.2.1.
Graph Voltages

Recall that we assume a graph
\eta
to be given with vertex set
\nu=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and directed edge relation
<
.

Definition 3.2 (graph voltages
[
GT87
]
).

A
\SO(d)
graph voltage
\alpha
assigns to each positively oriented edge
i>j
of
\eta
an element
\alpha_{ij}\in\SO(d)
and the inverse matrices
\alpha_{ij}^{-1}
to negatively oriented edges
i<j
. This results in the function

\alpha:\nu\times\nu\to\SO(d),\qquad\begin{cases}\alpha_{ij}=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d},&\text{if $i\not\sim j$,}\\
\alpha_{ij}=\alpha_{ji}^{-1},\quad&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

We denote the space of graph voltages by
\mathcal{A}
. We denote by
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}}
the voltage assigning the identity matrix to all edges.

We define additionally a group action of
\Xi
on the space of voltages in order to define gauge invariant voltages.

Definition 3.3 (gauge invariant voltages).

Given a voltage
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and a gauge transformation
\xi\in\Xi
, we can define a new voltage
\xi\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
defined by

(\xi\alpha)_{ij}=\;\xi_{i}\alpha_{ij}\xi_{j}^{-1},\qquad\forall i,j\in\nu.

(3.2)

This defines a group action of
\Xi
on
\mathcal{A}
and allows us to define the space of
gauge invariant voltage representations

\Omega\coloneqq(\Xi\times\mathcal{A})/\Xi=\{[\xi,\alpha]\subset\Xi\times\mathcal{A}\mid[\xi\tau,\alpha]=[\xi,\tau\alpha]\}

(3.3)

A triple

\chi=(\eta,\kappa,\omega)

(3.4)

consisting of a weighted graph
(\eta,\kappa)
, with a positive weight function
\kappa
supported on the edges of
\eta
, and a gauge invariant voltage
\omega\in\Omega
, is called a
gauge invariant voltage graph
.

Remark 3.4 (graph voltages in related work).

Graph voltages naturally appeared in different areas of mathematics and physics. While our exposition is inspired from algebraic graph theory
[
GT87
]
, we list here few other occurrences.

One perspective on graph voltages is given by
cellular sheaves

[
HG19
,
HG21
,
BDGC+23
]
, where one encodes sheaf structures over graphs in terms edge matrices, similar to voltages. This approach favors
flat voltages
as specified in Definition
5.11
.

Graph voltages also can be obtained from
discrete gauge theory

[
Bae96
,
GL10
,
Jia23
]
, where similar edge matrices on graphs arise from
gauge fields on a manifold
after discretization
[
LMW05
]
. This connection is further explained in Appendix
A
.

Furthermore, voltages naturally arise as a modelling scheme for
synchronization problems

[
GBM21
]
, which are encountered for instance in cryo electron microscopy
[
SZSH11
,
SS12
]
.

In the sequel, we will assume that we are given a gauge invariant graph voltage
\omega
, as a basis for defining gauge invariant heat kernels.

3.2.2.
Gauged Heat Kernel

We are now in the position to define gauge invariant heat kernels
K_{\chi}
. Consider
\Gamma
, c.f. (
2.19
), as a inner product space with product

\langle\langle\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle_{Z}=\sum_{i\in\nu}Z_{i}\langle x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad\phi=[\xi,x],\;\phi^{\prime}=[\xi,x^{\prime}].

(3.5)

This definition is well-posed and does not depend on the choice of
\xi
. Here,
Z_{i}
denotes here the weight degree of vertex
i

Z_{i}=\sum_{j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij},

(3.6)

used below for normalization.

We next define some basic linear operators.

Definition 3.5 (gauged heat kernel and Laplacian ).

Let
\chi=(\eta,\kappa,\omega)
denote a gauge invariant voltage graph. We define the
gauged Laplacian
, denoted by
L_{\chi}
, as the linear operator

L_{\chi}:\Gamma\to\Gamma;\quad\phi=[\xi,x]\mapsto[\xi,\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x],\qquad\omega=[\xi,\alpha],

(3.7)

with

(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x)_{i}=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}(\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad i\in\nu.

(3.8)

Define the
gauged heat kernel
associated to
\chi
as

K_{\chi}^{t}=e^{-tL_{\chi}}:\Gamma\to\Gamma,\qquad\chi=(\eta,\kappa,\omega).

(3.9)

The notation
\phi=[\xi,x]
,
\omega=[\xi,\alpha]
used in the definition means that both, the section
\phi
and the voltage
\omega
must be represented in terms of a
matching
gauge
\xi
, in order for equation (
3.7
) to hold.

Despite the summation over all graph nodes
\nu
,
L_{\chi}
is a
local
operator in the sense that, for a given vertex
i
, the sum only visits the first-order neighbors
j\sim i
, as
\kappa
is only supported on adjacent pairs of vertices.

Remark 3.6 (relation to connection and sheaf Laplacians).

The gauge invariant Laplacian, or rather its representation
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}
, are known concepts in the context of gauge theory and data science.

From the point of view of gauge theory, this Laplacian is derived from the Lagrangian of the scalar field after the minimal coupling procedure, as discretized differential operator which governs the kinetic part of the field
[
Ham17
]
. This connection is explained in
[
Ton18
, Section 4]
and
[
Rot12
]
.

Another way to formulate this relationship is to identify the gauge theoretic Laplacian as a
generalized/twisted Laplacian
on a vector bundle
[
BGV96
]
and treat the operator
L_{\chi}
as its discretization. This perspective is adopted in
[
GBM21
]
.

Furthermore, this Laplacian was introduced as the
discrete connection Laplacian
in the context of
vector diffusion maps

[
SW12
]
.

Yet another perspective is gained when looking at the problem through the lens of
cellular sheaf theory
, where similar operators are constructed to capture
H^{0}
-spaces of sheaf cohomology
[
HG19
,
BDGC+23
]
.

Proposition 3.7.

The operators
L_{\chi}
and
K_{\chi}
are well-defined.

Proof.

We need to check that the definition of
L_{\chi}
does not depend on the choice of the representative
[\xi,x]
. To this end, we calculate
L_{\chi}[\xi\tau^{-1},\tau x]
and verify that it is equal to
L_{\chi}[\xi,x]
.
In order to evaluate
L_{\chi}[\xi\tau^{-1},\tau x]
, we transform the gauge for
\omega
via

[\xi,\alpha]=[\xi\tau^{-1},\tau\alpha]=\omega.

(3.10)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle L_{\chi}[\xi\tau^{-1},\tau x]

\displaystyle{=}[\xi\tau^{-1},\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\tau\alpha}(\tau x)]\overset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eqref{def-action-Xi-A}\\
\eqref{eq:def-lalpha}\end{subarray}}{=}[\xi\tau^{-1},\tau\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x]=[\xi,\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x]

(3.11a)

\displaystyle{=}L_{\chi}[\xi,x].

(3.11b)

∎

The following lemma prepares the study of properties of the gauged Laplacian and heat kernel from Definition
3.5
. Similar versions appear in
[
GBM21
, Proposition 2.4]
. We give a full proof to make this paper self-contained.

Lemma 3.8 (discrete integration by parts).

We have the following relation

\langle\langle\phi^{\prime},L_{\chi}\phi\rangle\rangle_{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle\alpha_{ij}y_{j}-y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle,

(3.12)

for all
\phi=[\xi,x],\phi^{\prime}=[\xi,y]\in\Gamma
and
\omega=[\xi,\alpha]\in\Omega
.

Proof.

First write

\displaystyle\langle\langle\phi^{\prime},L_{\chi}\phi\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\nu}Z_{i}\langle y_{i},(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x)_{i}\rangle

(3.13a)

\displaystyle{=}-\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle

(3.13b)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\Big(\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle-\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle\Big).

(3.13c)

The relation
\sum_{j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}=Z_{i}
from the definition (
3.6
) then implies

\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\nu}Z_{i}\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle.

(3.14)

Furthermore, the relations
\alpha_{ij}^{-1}=\alpha_{ij}^{\top}=\alpha_{ji}
and
\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{ji}
imply

\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle\overset{i\leftrightarrow j}{=}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ji}\langle y_{j},\alpha_{ji}x_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle\alpha_{ij}y_{j},x_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle,

(3.15)

such that we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle+\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle)=\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle.

(3.16)

This last simplification can also be derived from the fact that for any matrix
O\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},N=|\nu|
, one has

\sum_{i,j\leq N}O_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\leq N}(O_{ij}+O_{ji}),

(3.17)

thus only the symmetric part of
O
contributes to the sum of all entries.

These simplifications allow us to rewrite (
3.13c
) as

\langle\langle\phi^{\prime},L_{\chi}\phi\rangle\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle+\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle)+\sum_{i\in\nu}Z_{i}\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle.

(3.18)

Putting together, we obtain for the right-hand side of (
3.12
)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle\alpha_{ij}y_{j}-y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle

(3.19a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle)

(3.19b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle\alpha_{ij}x_{j},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle)

(3.19c)

\displaystyle{=}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle)

(3.19d)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle y_{j},x_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle)

(3.19e)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}(\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}y_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle)+\sum_{i\in\nu}Z_{i}\langle y_{i},x_{i}\rangle,

(3.19f)

where the last step follows again from (
3.17
). Thus we obtained (
3.13c
) which proves Equation (
3.12
).
∎

Proposition 3.9 (
K_{\chi}
is a heat kernel).

The operator
K_{\chi}
is a heat kernel, i.e.
L_{\chi}
is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite.

Proof.

Lemma
3.8
shows that

\langle\langle\phi,L_{\chi}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle_{Z}=\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\langle\alpha_{ij}y_{j}-y_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle=\langle\langle L_{\chi}\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle_{Z}

(3.20)

for all
\phi=[\xi,x]
and
\phi^{\prime}=[\xi,y]
in
\Gamma
and

\langle\langle\phi,L_{\chi}\phi\rangle\rangle_{Z}=\sum_{i,j\in\nu}\kappa_{ij}\|\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}\geq 0.

(3.21)

∎

We provide bounds on the spectrum of the heat kernel.

Proposition 3.10 (spectrum of the GHK).

The spectra of
L_{\chi}
and
K_{\chi}^{t}
lie in
[0,2]
and
[e^{-2t},1]
respectively.

Proof.

We use an argument of Gershgorin type. Assume
\lambda,\phi=[\xi,x]
is an eigenpair of
L_{\chi}
. Then we can write

L_{\chi}\phi=[\xi,\lambda x]=[\xi,\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}x],\quad\omega=[\xi,\alpha]

(3.22)

which implies by (
3.8
)

\lambda x_{i}=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}(\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}),\quad\forall i\in\nu.

(3.23)

Taking the scalar product with
x_{i}
on both side and rearranging yields

(\lambda-1)\|x_{i}\|^{2}=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle,\quad\forall i\in\nu.

(3.24)

We pick
i
such that
\|x_{i}\|^{2}
is maximal and take absolute values.

\displaystyle|\lambda-1|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle\bigg|

(3.25)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}\left|\frac{\langle x_{i},\alpha_{ij}x_{j}\rangle}{\|x_{i}\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}=1

due to our choice of the index
i
and
\alpha_{ij}\in\SO(d)
. Thus
|\lambda-1|\leq 1
. Furthermore, we know that
\lambda
is real, since
L_{\chi}
is self-adjoint. Hence
\lambda\in[0,2]
and
e^{-\lambda t}\in[e^{-2t},1]
, which proves the claim.
∎

3.3.
Axiomatic Description of the Gauged Laplacian

In this subsection, we give an additional description of the gauged Laplacian
L_{\chi}
which distinguishes it among all other gauge invariant operators. We first introduce further notation required to state our result.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
denote a gauge invariant data transformation. For any gauge
\xi\in\Xi
, we write
\mathcal{F}([\xi,x])=[\xi,y(x)]
for some
y(x)\in X
which depends on both
\xi
and
x
. We denote by
y(x)
the value of the function
F:\Xi\times X\to X
for the argument
(\xi,x)
from Proposition
2.3
,

y(x)=F(\xi,x).

(3.26)

We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
locally Laplacian
if, for every
i\in\nu
, there is gauge
\xi^{i}\in\Xi
such that, for every
\phi\in\Gamma
, one has

\mathcal{F}([\xi^{i},x])=[\xi^{i},y^{i}(x)],\quad y^{i}(x)\coloneq F(\xi^{i},x),\quad\phi=[\xi^{i},x]

(3.27)

and

y^{i}(x)_{i}=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}(x_{j}-x_{i})\coloneq(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}x)_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.28)

The notation uses the linear operator
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, which can be identified as the
random walk normalized graph Laplacian

[
HAL07
]
associated to the weighted graph
(\eta,\kappa)
.

We set

\xi^{ij}\coloneq(\xi^{i})^{-1}\xi^{j}\in\Xi

(3.29)

for any pair
i,j\in\nu
, not necessarily adjacent in the graph
\eta
, with the property
\xi^{ij}=(\xi^{ji})^{-1}
. We call
\xi^{ij}
the
associated gauges
for a given locally Laplacian transformation
\mathcal{F}
, which satisfy the relation

\xi^{ji}\xi^{ik}=\xi^{jk},\qquad\forall i,j,k\in\nu.

(3.30)

These notions allow us to characterize the gauged Laplacians in terms of locally Laplacian transformations that satisfy a certain
integrability condition
imposed on the associated gauges.

Proposition 3.11 (consistent locally Laplacian operators are gauged Laplacians).

Let
\mathcal{F}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
be a locally Laplacian gauge invariant data transformation, such that the associated gauges
\xi^{ij}
satisfy the relations

\xi^{ij}_{i}=\xi^{ij}_{j},\qquad\forall i,j\in\nu.

(3.31)

Then there is a gauge invariant graph voltage
\omega\in\Omega
such that

\mathcal{F}=L_{\chi},\qquad\chi=(\eta,\kappa,\omega),

(3.32)

and for all
i,j,k\in\nu,j>k
, we have

\omega=[\xi^{i},\alpha^{i}],\qquad\alpha^{i}_{jk}=\xi^{ij}_{j}\xi^{ji}_{k}\in\SO(d).

(3.33)

Proof.

We show that
\omega
defines a gauge invariant voltage, which amounts to verifying that the following consistency conditions hold

\alpha_{jk}^{i}=(\alpha_{kj}^{i})^{-1},\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{jk}^{m}=\xi^{mi}_{j}\alpha^{i}_{jk}\xi^{im}_{k}

(3.34)

for all
i,j,k,m\in\nu
. The second equation one holds since

\xi^{mi}_{j}\alpha^{i}_{jk}\xi^{im}_{k}=\underbrace{\xi^{mi}_{j}\xi^{ij}_{j}}_{\overset{\eqref{eq:ass-g}}{=}\xi^{mj}_{j}}\underbrace{\xi^{ji}_{k}\xi^{im}_{k}}_{\overset{\eqref{eq:ass-g}}{=}\xi^{jm}_{k}}=\xi^{mj}_{j}\xi^{jm}_{k}\overset{\eqref{eq:def-alphai}}{=}\alpha^{m}_{jk}.

(3.35)

For the first condition, we compute

\alpha^{i}_{jk}\alpha^{i}_{kj}=\xi^{ij}_{j}\underbrace{\xi^{ji}_{k}\xi^{ik}_{k}}_{\overset{\eqref{eq:ass-g}}{=}\xi^{jk}_{k}}\xi^{ki}_{j}=\xi^{ij}_{j}\underbrace{\xi^{jk}_{k}}_{\overset{\eqref{eq:intergrability}}{=}\xi^{jk}_{j}}\xi^{ki}_{j}=\xi^{ij}_{j}\xi^{jk}_{j}\xi^{ki}_{j}\overset{\eqref{eq:ass-g}}{=}\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}.

(3.36)

This shows that the definition (
3.33
) is consistent and defines a graph voltage.

We proceed in order to prove (
3.32
).
Since both transformations are gauge invariant, we can check equality in any gauge, which we choose here to be
\xi^{i}
for a fixed choice of
i\in\nu
. Evaluating both operators on
\phi=[\xi^{i},x]
yields

\mathcal{F}([\xi^{i},x])=[\xi^{i},y^{i}(x)],\qquad L_{\chi}([\xi^{i},x])=[\xi^{i},\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha^{i}}x],

(3.37)

where
y^{i}(x)
is defined by (
3.27
).
We thus have to verify that

\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha^{i}}x=y^{i}(x).

(3.38)

We separately check equality at all nodes
j\in\nu
and first calculate
y^{i}(x)_{j}
. Due to gauge invariance, the equation

y^{i}(x)=F(\xi^{i},x)\overset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eqref{eq:consis}\\
\eqref{eq:ass-g}\end{subarray}}{=}\xi^{ij}F(\xi^{j},\xi^{ji}x)\overset{\eqref{eq:def-yi}}{=}\xi^{ij}y^{j}(\xi^{ji}x).

(3.39)

holds. In particular, we have

y^{i}(x)_{j}=\xi^{ij}_{j}y^{j}(\xi^{ji}x)_{j},\quad\forall j\in\nu.

(3.40)

On the other hand, relation (
3.28
) yields

y^{j}(\xi^{ji}x)_{j}=(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}\xi^{ji}x)_{j}.

(3.41)

By combining both equations, we obtain

y^{i}(x)_{j}=\xi^{ij}_{j}y^{j}(\xi^{ji}x)_{j}=\xi^{ij}_{j}(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}\xi^{ji}x)_{j}

(3.42)

which expands to

\xi^{ij}_{j}(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}\xi^{ji}x)_{j}\overset{\eqref{eq:def-y-diag}}{=}-\xi^{ij}_{j}\sum_{k\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{jk}}{Z_{j}}(\xi^{ji}_{k}x_{k}-\xi^{ji}_{j}x_{j})=-\sum_{k\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{jk}}{Z_{j}}(\xi^{ij}_{j}\xi^{ji}_{k}x_{k}-x_{j})

(3.43)

using
\xi^{ij}_{j}\xi^{ji}_{j}=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
in the last step. Applying the definition (
3.33
) to the last expression gives

\xi^{ij}_{j}(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}\xi^{ji}x)_{j}=-\sum_{k\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{jk}}{Z_{j}}(\alpha_{jk}^{i}x_{k}-x_{j})\overset{\eqref{eq:def-lalpha}}{=}(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha^{i}}x)_{j}.

(3.44)

Hence (
3.42
)–(
3.44
) show that

y^{i}(x)_{j}=(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha^{i}}x)_{j},\quad\forall j\in\nu.

(3.45)

We conclude that Equation (
3.38
) holds and
\mathcal{F}=L_{\chi}
with
\chi=(\eta,\kappa,\omega)
.

We finish the proof by demonstrating that the equation
\mathcal{F}=L_{\chi}
is compatible with
\mathcal{F}
being locally Laplacian, i.e. the assumptions of the Proposition are not violated. By virtue of the definitions (
3.29
) and (
3.33
), we find
\alpha^{i}_{ij}=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
for all edges
i>j
, such that

y^{i}(x)_{i}=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{i}}(\alpha^{i}_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i})=-\sum_{j\in\nu}\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{Z_{j}}(x_{j}-x_{i})=(\Delta_{\eta,\kappa}x)_{i},\quad\forall i\in\nu,

(3.46)

which is Equation (
3.28
).
∎

Remark 3.12 (associated gauges and synchronizability).

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a locally Laplacian operator and the associated gauges
\xi^{ij}
are all equal to the unit element, that is
\xi^{ij}_{k}=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
for all
i,j,k\in\nu
. Then condition (
3.31
) is directly satisfied and hence
\mathcal{F}
is equal to some operator
L_{\chi}
.
In particular, then the voltage
\omega
is
synchronizable
. This notion is defined and discussed in detail in Section
4
.

Another way of characterizing the gauged Laplacian is to analyze its
lifting properties
. For a given graph
\eta
with edge set
\varepsilon
, we can construct a family of related graphs, called the
tree blow-ups of
\eta
. We now iteratively construct such a tree blow-up of
\eta
, which we denote by
\tilde{\eta}
, with vertex set
\tilde{\nu}
and edge set
\tilde{\varepsilon}
.

Start with a spanning tree
\tilde{\eta}\subset\eta
. For every vertex
i\in\nu
, we denote with
\tilde{i}
the corresponding vertex of
\tilde{\nu}
. These two vertex sets are in one-to-one correspondence at this stage, since the spanning tree contains all the vertices of
\eta
. The edge set
\tilde{\varepsilon}
is a subset of
\varepsilon
.

Define an orientation for the edges of
\tilde{\eta}
by setting
\tilde{i}>\tilde{j}
if and only if
i>j
. Then, for every edge
i>j
in
\varepsilon\setminus\tilde{\varepsilon}
, add a new vertex
I
to
\tilde{\nu}
and a new edge to
\tilde{\varepsilon}
from
\tilde{j}
to the newly created vertex
I
with orientation
I>\tilde{j}
. The resulting graph
\tilde{\eta}
is a tree blow-up of
\eta
.

Any tree blow-up
\tilde{\eta}
has the following properties.

(i)

\tilde{\eta}
is a tree.

(ii)

There is a bijection between the edge sets
\tilde{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon
.

(iii)

There is a surjective mapping
\pi:\tilde{\nu}\to\nu
, with
\tilde{i}\in\pi^{-1}(i)
for all
i\in\nu
.

(iv)

\tilde{\eta}
inherits a symmetric edge weight function
\tilde{\kappa}
by setting
\tilde{\kappa}_{IJ}\coloneq\kappa_{\pi(I)\pi(J)}
for all
I,J\in\tilde{\nu}
.

(v)

We denote by
\tilde{\Gamma}=(\tilde{\Xi}\times\tilde{X})/\tilde{\Xi}
the space of gauge invariant node features over the graph
\tilde{\eta}
, where
\tilde{X}=\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{N}\times d}
denotes the
d
node features over
\tilde{\eta},\tilde{N}=|\tilde{\nu}|
and
\tilde{\Xi}=(\SO(d))^{\tilde{N}}
. There are mappings
\sigma_{1}:\Gamma\to\tilde{\Gamma}
and
\sigma_{2}:\tilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
, defined by

\sigma_{1}(\phi)_{I}=\phi_{\pi(I)},\qquad\forall I\in\tilde{\nu}

(3.47)

and

\sigma_{2}(\tilde{\phi})_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\tilde{\nu}\\
\pi(I)=i\end{subarray}}\tilde{\phi}_{I},\qquad\forall i\in\nu.

(3.48)

3.4.
GHK as a Machine Learning Device

We review in this subsection the role of the gauged heat kernel in machine learning.

The connection between heat kernel methods and machine learning methods has been fruitfully applied in the context of
graph neural networks

[
KW17
,
DBV17
,
BBL+17
]
, where heat kernels on graphs were recognized as basic elements of generalized wavelet filtering architectures
[
CRG+21
,
CRE+21
]
.

The main building block of generalized
message passing
network architectures is the following. Given input data
x
are repeatedly transformed by some form of aggregation transformation
\mathcal{K}
, concatenated with non-linear activation functions. The connection to heat kernel methods results from identifying heat kernels
K=e^{-tL}
as candidates for the aggregation operator
\mathcal{K}
. The further step towards machine learning entails a parametrization of a suitable subspace of potential heat kernels and learning a suitable heat kernel from given data by optimizing some loss function.

In the case of the gauged heat kernel, we can parametrize all gauge invariant Laplace operators, and thereby also all heat kernels over a given graph
\eta
, in terms of weight functions
\kappa
and voltages
\omega
.
Thus, the connection between the gauged heat kernel and machine learning is to interpret
K_{\chi}
as a parametrized data transformation architecture, whose parameters are given by graph weights
\kappa
and voltages
\alpha
, and to infer optimal weight from data.
This approach was taken in
[
BDGC+23
,
BBDB24
]
and
[
CSAS25
]
, where such parameters were estimated in the context of node classification on graphs. The authors report that the gauged heat kernel
K_{\chi}
has favorable properties for solving such tasks.

A motivation to pursue connections between gauged heat kernels and machine learning is to perform learning over geometrically meaningful parameter domains, such as the space of voltage graphs
\chi
in present case. In
[
BDGC+23
]
, voltage graphs were related to
sheaf structures
, whereas
[
GBM21
,
BBDB24
]
related them to discrete vector and principal bundles. The gauged heat kernel also resembles discretized
kernel field transforms
for coordinate independent convolutional neural networks
[
WFVW21
]
. The connection is that the gauged heat kernel may be viewed as discretization of a kernel field transform, with a trivial kernel field.

We provide additional geometric interpretation of voltage graphs in terms of
lattice gauge theory

[
GL10
]
, see the Remarks
2.11
,
3.4
,
3.6
and Appendix
A
. This relates the space of graph voltages to parallel transport operators and gauge fields on principal fiber bundles.

Building on intuitions gained from the gauge theory perspective, we introduce below novel analysis methods for the gauged heat kernel and voltage graphs with regard to their applications in data science. Specifically, we focus in Sections
4
and
5
on the confluence of
synchronization
properties of voltage graphs, heat kernel transformations and Yang Mills curvature analysis. Especially the Yang-Mills
energies
are promising in the context of machine learning, providing a scalar quantity for controlling the transformation properties of corresponding heat kernels.

Overall, this approach highlights symmetry properties inherent to the gauged heat kernel and its embedding into the coordinate/gauge invariant deep learning formalism.

4.1.
GHK as a Projection Operator

We assume that

is finite dimensional, which is the case for node feature data over finite graphs, and take a closer look to the operators introduced by Definition

Proposition 4.1 (heat kernel converges to projection operator
[
HG19
,
HG21
]
).

Let
K^{t}
by a heat kernel with generator

is self-adjoint and is positive semi-definite for all scales

\ker L
denotes the nullspace of the operator

with the corresponding orthogonal projection
\operatorname{proj}
. Then one has

\|K^{t}-\operatorname{proj}\|\leq e^{-\mu t},

(4.1)

where
\mu
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
L
. Thus, the heat kernel family converges to projection operator
\operatorname{proj}
in the operator norm on
V
.

Proof.

Since
L
is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite and

is finite dimensional, we can diagonalize

,

L=\sum_{r=1}^{\dim V}\lambda_{r}\varphi_{r}\varphi^{\top}_{r},\qquad Lv=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}\langle\varphi_{r},v\rangle\varphi_{r},

(4.2)

where
\lambda_{r}\geq 0
and
\varphi_{r}
are the eigenvectors with duals
\varphi_{r}^{\top}
.
Then we have

K^{t}=\sum_{r}e^{-\lambda_{r}t}\varphi_{r}\varphi^{\top}_{r},

(4.3)

and the properties follow directly.
∎

Proposition
4.1
says that

defines an asymptotic (in the sense of infinite time) homotopy between the identity

and the projection operator
\rm{proj}
:
V
transforms over time into

under

. The nullspace

thus defines an important analysis tool for heat kernel methods, in that if one can characterize

Definition 4.9 (synchronization for voltages).

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
be a graph voltage. We call

\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}^{-1}\xi_{j},\quad\forall i>j\in\varepsilon.

If a voltage can be synchronized, then its information is

A voltage

is synchronizable if and only if there is a gauge transformation
\xi\in\Xi
such that

denotes the voltage assigning the identity matrix to all edges. This leads to the proposition below.

\alpha^{\prime}
is synchronizable iff
\alpha
is synchronizable. This means that ‘synchronizable’ is a gauge invariant statement. The voltage
\omega
is synchronizable iff there is an element

\omega=[\xi^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}}],

, Proposition 2.1]
and
[

, Lemma 6]
.

Corollary 4.15.

\omega
is synchronizable on
\eta
if and only if
\dim\ker L_{\chi}=d
.

Proof.

Assume
\dim\ker L_{\chi}=d
and let
\varepsilon_{1}
denote again the complement of the set of edges of a spanning tree
\eta_{0}\subset\eta
, with tree gauge representation
\omega=[\xi,\alpha]
.
This implies that

on all edges of spanning tree
i>j
in
\eta_{0}
.
If

\dim\bigcap_{i>j\in\varepsilon_{1}}E_{1}(\alpha_{ij})=d.

(4.19)

Since the dimension of the intersection of the eigenspaces is

for all edges in

∎

For the next corollary, we recall our assumption that the graph

Corollary 4.16 (tensor decomposition via synchronizability).

Let
\omega=[\xi,\alpha]
denote a graph voltage and
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}
a representation of
L_{\chi}
with respect to the gauge
\xi

\omega
is synchronizable if and only if there is a gauge
\xi\in\Xi
, a matrix

and a diagonal matrix

such that

\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}=\xi^{-1}(R\Lambda R^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{d\times d})\xi,

(4.20)

as operators
X\to X
, and there is a unique

with
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\Lambda_{ii}=0
and
\lambda_{j}\coloneqq\Lambda_{jj}>0
for all

.

Proof.

First assume that
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}
admits a decomposition as defined in (
4.20
). We then prove that
\dim\ker\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}=d
, which shows synchronizability by Corollary
4.15
.
A linear isomorphism
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\ker\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}
can be specified as follows. Denote by

the
i
-th column of the matrix

. Then we consider the mapping

\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\ker\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}\;,\quad v\mapsto\xi^{-1}(r_{i}\otimes v).

(4.21)

This mapping is linear and injective. It is well-defined in the sense that it maps into the kernel of
\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\eta,\kappa,\alpha}\xi^{-1}(r_{i}\otimes v)

\displaystyle=\xi^{-1}(R\Lambda R^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{d\times d})\xi\xi^{-1}(r_{i}\otimes v)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\xi^{-1}(r_{i}\otimes v)

\displaystyle{=}0,

(4.22c)

where the second equation holds since

We first show surjectivity for the mapping

from which surjectivity of (
4.21

and the fact that

by the assumptions of the Corollary. We conclude that (

Conversely, assume that
\alpha

\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}\xi_{j}^{-1}

5.
Yang Mills Curvature Analysis

Both Theorem
4.14
and Corollary
4.16
show that the synchronizability of a voltage
\omega
is central to the transformation properties of the heat kernel
K_{\chi}
. In this section, we analyze the synchronizability of graph voltages
\omega
independently of heat kernels.
We provide here an analysis of synchronizability in terms of
Yang-Mills
energies, which are an important tool in the theory of smooth vector bundles. Our exposition closely relates to the work
[
GBM21
]
in that we also link synchronization properties to holonomy properties of graph voltages. While
[
GBM21
]
analyzes synchronizability in terms of
synchronization bundles
, we choose here geometric gauge theory as the vantage point which allows us to extend the analysis
beyond flat voltages
.

5.1.
Holonomy for Graph Voltages

We define holonomy for a discrete voltage
\alpha
on a graph
\eta
.

Definition 5.1 (loops of a graph
\eta
).

A
loop
of
\eta
is an ordered sequence
l=(i_{0},\dots,i_{m})
of vertices
i_{j}\in\nu
such that
i_{m}=i_{0}
,
i_{j}\sim i_{j+1}
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
and
i_{j}\neq i_{j^{\prime}}
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
. The vertex
i_{0}
is called the
base point
of
l
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
the set of loops of
\eta
.

We define an equivalence relation on the space of loops as follows. Two loops
l,l^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if the ordered sequence
l^{\prime}
can be obtained from the sequence
l
by applying a cyclic permutation and, if necessary, a permutation that reverses the order.

Definition 5.2 (cycles of
\eta
).

A
cycle
of
\eta
is an equivalence class of loops
\ell
under the relation defined above. We denote by
\mathcal{L}(\eta)
the set of cycles of
\eta
.

Definition 5.3 (holonomy).

The
holonomy
of a voltage
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
along a loop
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
with
l=(i_{0},\dots,i_{m})
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)\coloneqq\prod_{k=1}^{m}\alpha_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}.

(5.1)

We define the
scalar holonomy
as

\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)\coloneqq\tr(2\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{\top}).

(5.2)

The holonomy
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}:\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)\to\SO(d)
is well-defined for any loop
l
, yet depends on the choice of a base point and orientation. The scalar holonomy
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{L}(\eta)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, on the other hand, neither depends on the base point nor on the orientation of a loop
l
, hence is well-defined for any cycle
\ell
.

Proposition 5.4 (scalar holonomy does not depend on base point).

If
l,l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
denote two equivalent oriented loops, then

\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l^{\prime}).

(5.3)

The scalar holonomy is well-defined as a function
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}:\mathcal{L}(\eta)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

Proof.

The two
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l^{\prime})
can be obtained from each other by composing conjugation and inversion operations, since the same loop is traversed, but with different ordering and maybe different orientation.
A different ordering results in a conjugation operation on
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
. Reversing an orientation is equivalent to inverting
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
.
As for the scalar holonomy, we symmetrize
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
and take the trace. Due to symmetrization, the scalar holonomy becomes invariant under inversion, which amounts to transposition of
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)\in\SO(d)
. Due to taking the trace,
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)
is invariant with respect to conjugation, which results from changing the base point.

The scalar holonomy maps to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, since the matrices
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
lie in
\SO(d)
such that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)+\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{\top}
has its spectral range in
[-2,2]
. Consequently, the matrix
2\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{\top}
is symmetric and positive semi-definite with spectral range
[0,4]
, which the trace maps to the interval
[0,4d]
.
∎

Proposition 5.5 (scalar holonomy completely characterizes holonomy).

We have

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}\quad\iff\quad\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)=0.

(5.4)

Proof.

If
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
, then
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)=2d-d-d=0
.

If we have
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)=0
, then the positive-semidefinite symmetric matrix
2-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)-\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{\top}
has trace 0, which implies that the matrix itself is 0. Therefore, multiplying the equation
2=\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)+\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{\top}
from the left by
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
and rearranging gives

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)^{2}-2\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)+\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}=0.

(5.5)

Any eigenvalue
\sigma
of
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
therefore satisfies the polynomial equation
\sigma^{2}-2\sigma+1=(\sigma-1)^{2}=0
, that is
\sigma=1
must hold. Thus,
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)
is a diagonalizable matrix with all eigenvalues equal to 1 and hence
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
.
∎

Proposition 5.6 (gauge transform of holonomy).

If
\xi\in\Xi
denotes a gauge transformation, then we have the property

\mathcal{H}_{\xi\alpha}(l)=\xi_{i_{0}}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)\xi_{i_{0}},

(5.6)

where
i_{0}
is the base point for
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
and

\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathfrak{h}_{\xi\alpha}(l).

(5.7)

Proof.

Equation (
5.6
) follows from Definition
5.3
and the transformation property
(\xi\alpha)_{ij}=\xi_{i}\alpha_{ij}\xi_{j}^{-1}
. Equation (
5.7
) holds since the trace is invariant under conjugation.
∎

5.2.
Holonomy as an Obstruction

Proposition 5.7 (synchronizable voltages have trivial holonomy).

If
\alpha
is synchronizable then
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
, for every
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
.

Proof.

A synchronizable voltage
\alpha
can be written as
\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}\xi_{j}^{-1}
for some
\xi\in\Xi
. From Definition
5.3
the equation
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
follows, for every loop
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
.
∎

As a consequence of the preceding statement, a loop with non-trivial holonomy
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
defines an obstruction for
\alpha
to be synchronizable.

Theorem 5.8 (
[
GBM21
, Corollary 2.1]
).

A voltage
\alpha
is synchronizable if and only if
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
for all loops
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
.

We first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9 (path-independent net voltage).

Let
\alpha
denote a graph voltage with
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
(trivial holonomy) for all loops
l\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\eta)
. Then the net voltage on
\eta
is path-independent: If there are two paths
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
in the graph which start and end at the same vertices, then
\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma)=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma^{\prime})\in\SO(d)
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
denote two such paths as in the statement of the lemma with transport matrices
\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma)
and
\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma^{\prime})
. Then consider the loop
l
formed by composing the two paths
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, which is possible because they start and end at the same points. We can orient the paths such that we can write
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma^{\prime})^{\top}\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
, from which we conclude that
\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma^{\prime})=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.8
.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
for all loops, the net voltage
\Pi
along the graph is path-independent, by Lemma
5.9
.

The reasoning below is based on the following facts.

(i)

Let
\eta_{0}
denote a spanning tree of the graph
\eta
.

(ii)

Synchronize
\alpha
along the tree
\eta_{0}
, which is possible by Lemma
4.13
. This defines a synchronization
\xi
on all graph vertices.

(iii)

This synchronization
\xi
also synchronizes the edges of
\eta\setminus\eta_{0}
since net voltage is path-independent.

The spanning tree
\eta_{0}
contains all vertices of the graph such that the partial synchronization found on the tree
\eta_{0}
defines a synchronization matrix
\xi_{i}
, for each vertex
i\in\nu
.
The claim is that these matrices satisfy

\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}\xi_{j}^{-1}

(5.8)

also for the edges
i>j
in the complement
\eta\setminus\eta_{0}
of the spanning tree.

Let
i>j
be such an edge. Both endpoints
i
and
j
are connected to the root node
i_{0}
of the spanning tree via unique paths
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
. Hence we can form a closed loop
l
by composing the paths
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
and the edge
i>j
. By the trivial holonomy assumption, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma_{i})\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma_{j})^{-1}\alpha_{ij}=\mathbf{I}_{d\times d},

(5.9)

which implies

\alpha_{ij}=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma_{i})^{-1}\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma_{j}).

(5.10)

But by the construction of the tree gauge in Lemma
4.11
, we know that
\xi_{i}=\Pi_{\alpha}(\gamma_{i})^{-1}
holds for all
i\in\nu
. Comparing this with the above equation shows that
\alpha_{ij}=\xi_{i}\xi_{j}^{-1}
and verifies (
5.8
).
∎

5.3.
Yang-Mills Energy

We now characterize the Yang-Mills energy as a
local
measure of holonomy. It has the advantage that it can be computed by only considering the 3-cliques which form a subspace of the space of all loops
\mathcal{L}(\eta)
, which is often too large to be checked for holonomy. In the general geometric context, the Yang-Mills energy measures the
curvature
of connections on principal bundles. See, e.g.,
[
Pet16
, Section 10]
for a brief overview of curvature and holonomy and
[
Ber03
, Sections 12,13]
for an extended overview including the topological viewpoint.

Definition 5.10 (3-cliques (triangles)).

The
3-cliques
\mathcal{L}^{3}(\eta)
of
\eta
is the set of cycles in
\mathcal{L}(\eta)
of length 3.

Definition 5.11 (Yang-Mills energy, flat voltages).

The
discrete Yang-Mills energy
is the function

\mathcal{YM}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad\omega=[\xi,\alpha]\mapsto\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{3}(\eta)}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\ell).

(5.11)

A voltage
\omega
is called
flat
if
\mathcal{YM}(\omega)=0
.

This functional is well-defined in that it does not depend on the choice of representative
[\xi,\alpha]
for
\omega
, by Proposition
5.6
.
By the non-negativity of
\mathfrak{h}
, a voltage
\omega=[\xi,\alpha]
is flat if and only if
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\delta)=0
for all 3-cliques
\delta\in\mathcal{L}^{3}(\eta)
.

Proposition 5.12 (synchronizable voltages are flat).

Let
\omega
be a synchronizable voltage, then
\mathcal{YM}(\omega)=0
.

Proof.

The statement follows from Proposition
5.7
.
∎

Remark 5.13 (motivation from discrete gauge theory).

Definition
5.11
of the Yang-Mills energy is motivated by
lattice gauge theory

[
GL10
]
and
gauge theory on graphs

[
Bae96
,
Jia23
]
. In lattice gauge theory one usually works with cubical meshes where the smallest cycles have length 4, from which a slight mismatch originates between our set-up and the convention of lattice gauge theory.

Figure 6
.
Regular 2 dimensional torus grid graph with flat but non-synchronizable voltage. Periodic boundary edges are marked with blue.

Example 5.14 (flat but non-synchronizable voltage).

Consider the regular 2 dimensional torus grid graph with additional diagonal edges as depicted by Figure
6
. This graph contains
non-contractible
cycles, which are cycles which wrap across the periodic boundaries.

Consider the orientation of the edges depicted in Figure
6
and define a voltage
\alpha
that evaluates to a fixed matrix
\alpha_{0}
for non-diagonal grid edges and to
\alpha_{0}^{2}
on all diagonal edges, see Figure
6
. Then
\alpha
is flat, that is
\mathcal{YM}(\alpha)=0
, but there are still loops
l
such that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)\neq\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
and hence
\alpha
is not synchronizable, in general.
For instance, following a loop
l
along the straight vertical path across the periodic boundary of the depicted graph, we obtain
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(l)=\alpha_{0}^{3}
, which need not be equal to
\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}
.

This example demonstrates that flatness is a
local
measure of obstruction for voltages to be synchronizable. In other words, even if
\alpha
is flat, there may still exist a loop along which there is non-vanishing curvature such that
\alpha
cannot be synchronizable.

Remark 5.15 (general theory of flat bundles).

The notion of flatness is well known in the context of vector bundles theory, see e.g.
[
Mil58
]
for an early account. Notably, flat
G
-bundles can be classified in terms of equivalence classes of
G
-representations of the fundamental group (the non-contractible loops) of the underlying base manifold
[
Bau14
]
.

Flatness of voltages is connected to flatness of principal fiber bundles via lattice gauge theory; a flat principal bundle induces a flat voltage through discretization
[
Rot12
]
.

It was shown in
[
GBM21
]
that a flat voltage
\alpha
enables to construct a flat principal bundle over geometric realizations of the clique complex of
\eta
. Synchronizability of the voltage is equivalent to the triviality of the corresponding principal bundle.

The above statements show that the Yang-Mills energy can only capture information about the flatness of voltages, whereas
topological
effects are ignored, so far. We now turn to a modified version of the Yang-Mills energy, augmented by topological information, such that it also reflects topological properties of the underlying graph.

5.4.
Topological Obstructions to Synchronizability

The Yang-Mills energy is a
local measure of non-synchronizability
for voltages.
‘Locality’ here means that we only consider the holonomy across the smallest loops, the 3-cliques. Accordingly, the Yang-Mills energy can only account for obstructions that arises from cycles which are boundaries
of subgraphs which can be built from triangles, cf. Corollary
5.17
.
There are, of course, more complex cycles in graphs, in general.
Therefore, we focus below on
non-local
obstructions
not
captured by the Yang-Mills energy. The main result of this section, the more powerful
extended
Yang-Mills energy, is stated as Theorem
5.19
.

5.4.1.
Graph Homology

In order to characterize non-local obstructions, we recall some facts about simplicial homology on graphs
[
JLYY11
,
GLMY15
]
; see
[
Hat02
]
for the general background.

We denote by
C_{*}(\eta)
the set of induced subgraphs of
\eta
. This set becomes an abelian group under the
symmetric difference
operation, denoted here by
+
, where
0\in C_{*}(\eta)
is the empty set. According to the symmetric difference operation, every element of
C_{*}(\eta)
has order 2, i.e. it holds
x+x=0
for all
x\in C_{*}(\eta)
, which implies that
C_{*}(\eta)
is also a vector space over the field
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Next, let
C_{0}(\eta)
denote the subgroup of
C_{*}(\eta)
generated by subgraphs consisting only of vertices of
\eta
, by
C_{1}(\eta)
the subgroup generated by edges of
\eta
and by
C_{2}(\eta)
the subgroup generated by 3-cliques of
\eta
. The group
C_{1}(\eta)
contains all subgraphs of
\eta
that do not contain isolated vertices. Elements of
\Sigma\in C_{2}(\eta)
can be represented as finite sums
\Sigma=\sum_{r}\delta_{r}
where
\delta_{r}\in\mathcal{L}^{3}(\eta)
are 3-cliques. Both
C_{1}(\eta)
and
C_{2}(\eta)
are vector subspaces of
C_{*}(\eta)
and the 3-cliques are a basis for
C_{2}(\eta)
, such that representations of the form
\Sigma=\sum_{r}\delta_{r}
are unique.

We define the linear maps
\partial_{1}:C_{1}(\eta)\to C_{0}(\eta)
and
\partial_{2}:C_{2}(\eta)\to C_{1}(\eta)
:
\partial_{1}
sends a subgraph
\sigma\in C_{1}(\eta)
to its vertices which are contained in an odd number of edges and
\partial_{2}
is defined on 3-cliques
\mathcal{L}^{3}(\eta)
by sending a triangle to the sum of its boundary edges. The mapping can then be linearly extended to
C_{2}(\eta)
by

\partial_{2}:C_{2}(\eta)\to C_{1}(\eta),\quad\Sigma=\sum_{r}\delta_{r}\mapsto\sum_{r}\partial_{1}\delta_{r}.

(5.12)

Equivalently,
\partial_{2}
sends
\Sigma\in C_{2}(\eta)
to the sum of its edges which are contained in an odd number of triangles.
One can check that
\partial_{1}\circ\partial_{2}=0
and
\ker\partial_{1}
are the Eulerian subgraphs of
\eta
, while
\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}
are the Eulerian subgraphs that can be represented as the boundary of subgraphs
\Sigma\in C_{2}(\eta)
additively built from triangles.

The
first homology group
of
\eta
is defined as the quotient group

H_{1}(\eta)=\ker\partial_{1}/\operatorname{im}\partial_{2}.

(5.13)

This group contains the Eulerian subgraphs modulo boundaries of subgraphs
\Sigma\in C_{2}(\eta)
. It is also a vector space over the field
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
with dimension denoted by

Theorem 5.19 (extended Yang-Mills energy).

Let
\{\ell_{1},\dotsc,\ell_{h_{1}(\eta)}\}\in\mathcal{L}(\eta)
denote a cycle basis of
H_{1}(\eta)
. The function

\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\qquad\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}(\omega)=\mathcal{YM}(\omega)+\sum_{k=1}^{h_{1}(\eta)}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\ell_{k})

(5.26)

establies the equivalence of the relations

\omega\in\Omega\text{ is synchronizable}\quad\iff\quad\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}(\omega)=0.

(5.27)

Proof.

If
\omega
synchronizable, then
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\ell)=0
for all loops
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(\eta)
. Then
\mathcal{YM}(\omega)=0
by Proposition
5.12
and consequently
\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}(\omega)=0
.

It remains to show that
\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}(\omega)=0
implies that
\omega
is synchronizable. Thus, we assume that
\mathcal{YM}_{\operatorname{e}}(\omega)=0
holds. Recall that the scalar holonomy is non-negative on every loop

, such that both terms of the extended Yang-Mills energy are non-negative. Therefore,
\mathcal{YM}_{\rm{e}}=0
implies that
\omega
is flat and
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\ell_{k})=0
for all

. The claim is that
\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha}(\ell)=0
holds for all loops of the graph
\eta
.

First, we remark that there are basis loops
\ell_{k_{j}},k_{j}\in\{1,\dots,h_{1}(\eta)\}\}
such that
[\ell]=\sum_{j}[\ell_{k_{j}}]=\left[\sum_{j}\ell_{k_{j}}\right]
, because the

\ell=\sum_{j}\ell_{k_{j}}+\sum_{r}\delta_{r},

where the

are triangles. Now we distribute the triangles

of the second sum into the first sum, by adding the triangles
\delta_{r}
to the cycles

until all triangles are absorbed into one of the cycles. This yields a new set of cycles, denoted by
\tilde{\ell}_{k_{j}}
, and

\ell=\sum_{j}\tilde{\ell}_{k_{j}}.

(5.29)

The relation between

and

(5.30)

6.
Conclusions

In this paper, we extended of the available formalism of gauge invariant data representation for machine learning
[
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
using methods from lattice gauge theory. Specifically, we introduced a gauge invariant heat kernel formalism and elaborated the connection to the discrete Yang-Mills energy from lattice gauge theory.
In addition to the connection to discrete gauge theories, our results reinforce from a common viewpoint the links between equivariant deep learning
[
WFVW21
,
GAC+23
]
, classical data science of vector bundles
[
SW12
,
Bat11
,
BS14
,
Bat25
]
and the discrete vector bundle/cellular sheaf formalism from
[
HG19
,
BDGC+23
,
BBDB24
]
.

Motivated by other approaches to discrete fiber bundles
[
LMW05
,
GL10
,
SW12
,
GBM21
]
, we emphasized a framework of discrete associated vector bundles which provides a proper representation of vector bundle concepts from differential geometry that is amenable to computational realizations. The relations of our discrete approach and the continuous vector bundle formalism are discussed in some detail in Appendix
A
.

Our discrete approach has the advantage of being formulated entirely in terms of concepts from finite graph theory, that are convenient for numerical applications. It also removes some part of the theoretical overhead incurred by established mathematical vector bundle descriptions, while preserving the main features of gauge symmetry properties.

We equipped our discrete model with suitable Laplacians and associated heat kernels and analyzed them using gauge theoretic methods. We showed that our gauged Laplacian is distinguished by the property of being the only gauge invariant operator which satisfies a
local Laplacian
and a
tree blow-up lifting
property, formulated as Theorem
3.14
.
Furthermore, we analyzed the interplay of our discrete bundle methods and the
Yang-Mills formalism
from high energy physics, showing that Yang-Mills analysis provides a useful tool for
synchronization analysis
, as shown by Theorem
5.19
.

Future directions for research may further extend our approach towards machine learning, discrete gauge theory and topological graph theory.
As explained in the Section
3
, the heat kernels that we studied in this work appeared in several works on machine learning on graphs
[
BDGC+23
,
BBDB24
,
CSAS25
]
.
In such scenarios, one is often confronted with loosely structured data such that one cannot rely on structures such as graph voltages as part of given data. Thus, a natural research task is to
learn the required structures from data
. This approach amounts to interpreting the geometric structures of discrete vector bundles (in the present case, graph voltages
\omega
) as a parameter of a machine learning architecture, to be estimated by minimizing a suitable loss function.
The resulting approaches are promising candidates for removing persistent limitations of graph neural networks, e.g. in connection with
node classification on heterophilic graphs
.

In particular, the recent work
[
BDGC+23
]
showed that the kernel of the associated Laplacian is a crucial quantity for understanding the characteristics of vector bundle heat kernel methods in the context of machine learning.
Our work shows that the
Yang-Mills energy
could serve as a
gauge invariant regularizing term
in learning scenarios, that preserves gauge invariance but also controls the kernel of the relevant Laplacian operator. Exploring these connections will also be part of our future work.

Furthermore, we next aim to analyze how discrete vector bundle concepts may profit from insights from topological graph theory about voltage graphs. In particular, we wish to connect our present analysis of the synchronizability of graph voltages to the theory of
derived graphs
, which is an established concept of topological graph theory
[
GT87
]
.

A.1.
Discrete Bundles from Smooth Bundles

We now describe the relationship between smooth associated vector bundles
E\to M
and the discrete bundle sections
\Gamma
introduced by Definition

The following result shows that discrete vector bundles arise indeed from smooth associated vector bundles through a pullback mapping.

Proposition A.3 (discrete pullback bundles).

Let
P\to M
be a principal
\SO(d)
bundle over
M
and
E\coloneqq P\times_{\rho}\mathbb{R}^{d}
the associated vector bundle for the representation
\rho
of
\SO(d)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\SO(d)
-rotation matrix acts on an
\mathbb{R}^{d}

E_{p}
the fiber of
E
at the point
p
.
Assume that the graph
\eta
is embedded into
M
, with embedding
\iota:\eta\hookrightarrow M
.
Then one has an isomorphism

\iota^{*}E\coloneqq\mathop{\dot{\bigcup}}_{i\in\nu}E_{p_{i}}\cong\nu\times Y,

(A.3)

where
Y\coloneqq(\SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})/\SO(d)
. The set
\iota^{*}E
is a vector bundle over the zero dimensional base manifold
\nu
and its smooth sections are given by

\Gamma(\iota^{*}E)\cong Y^{N},\qquad N=|\nu|.

(A.4)

Proof.

We note that the fibers are given by

E_{p_{i}}=(P_{p_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})/\SO(d),

where

.
Now choose for every

\mathop{\dot{\bigcup}}_{i\in\nu}E_{p_{i}}\cong\nu\times Y.

The sections of the bundle

\Gamma(\iota^{*}E)\cong\left\{\phi:\nu\to\nu\times Y\mid\phi\in C^{\infty},\;\exists v:\nu\to Y\text{ s.t. }\phi=\mathrm{id}\times v\right\}.

Remark A.8 (relationship between gauge fields and parallel transport).

The common way to parametrize parallel transport operators in gauge theory is to specify
gauge fields
, which is the term in physics used for the notion of
connections
from differential geometry
[
Ham17
]
. A gauge field
B
is a Lie algebra valued one-form, with additional properties. The relationship to parallel transport operators
\Pi^{E}
is given by
[
GL10
]

\Pi^{E}(\gamma)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(\int_{\gamma}B\right),

(A.12)

where
\mathcal{P}\exp
denotes the
path ordered exponential
,
\int_{\gamma}B
integrates the pullback of the gauge field along
\gamma
, and the full expression is the solution to the parallel transport ODE associated to
B
. This fact is explained in detail in
[
Ham17
, Section 5.10]
.

A.3.
Discrete Laplacians from Covariant Derivatives

We extend the relationship between the discrete and continuous theories by stating an approximation result that connects respective energy functions. We assume in this subsection that we are given an embedded graph
\iota:\eta\to M
, an
\SO(d)
-associated bundle
E\to M
with parallel transport operator
\Pi^{E}
and a Riemannian metric
\mathfrak{g}
on
M
.

In order to state our result, we recall few more notions that are established in the smooth case. Recall the bundle metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{p}
on
E
. This bundle metric also defines an inner product on the
E
-valued one-forms
\Omega^{1}(M,E)
, together with the Riemannian metric
\mathfrak{g}
, through

\langle\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\rangle_{p}:=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(p)\langle\sigma_{p}(\partial_{a}),\sigma^{\prime}_{p}(\partial_{b})\rangle_{p},\quad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega^{1}(M,E),

(A.13)

with
\sigma_{p},\sigma^{\prime}_{p}\in\mathrm{Hom}(T_{p}M,E_{p})
and basis vectors
\{\partial_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}\subset T_{p}M
and
\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(p)
the inverse matrix to
\mathfrak{g}_{ab}(p)
, which is the representing matrix of the metric
\mathfrak{g}
in the basis
\{\partial_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
.

This expression can be simplified in terms of
local orthonormal frame fields
. A local frame field at a point
p\in M
is a set of vector fields
e_{A},A\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, all defined on an open neighborhood
U
of
p
, with component functions
e^{a}_{A},\,a\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
such that

\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\mathfrak{g}_{ab}(p)e_{A}^{a}(p)e^{b}_{B}(p)=\delta_{AB},\quad\forall A,B\in\{1,\dotsc,n\},\quad\forall p\in U.

(A.14)

For any given point
p
, we can find a small neighborhood around it, such that a local orthonormal frame field exists. The vectors
e_{A}
form an orthonormal frame for the bilinear form
\mathfrak{g}_{ab}(p)
at every
p\in U
. We can infer, that the associated Gramian matrix is the inverse bilinear form

\sum_{A}^{n}e^{a}_{A}(p)e^{b}_{A}(p)=\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(p),\quad\forall a,b\in\{1,\dots,n\},p\in U.

(A.15)

This enables to rewrite (
A.13
) in the form

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\mathfrak{g}^{ab}(p)\langle\sigma_{p}(\partial_{a}),\sigma_{p}(\partial_{b})\rangle_{p}

(A.16a)

\displaystyle{=}\sum_{a,b,A}^{n}e^{a}_{A}e^{b}_{A}\langle\sigma_{p}(\partial_{a}),\sigma_{p}(\partial_{b})\rangle_{p}

(A.16b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b,A=1}^{n}\langle\sigma_{p}(e^{a}_{A}\partial_{a}),\sigma_{p}(e^{b}_{A}\partial_{b})\rangle_{p}

(A.16c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A=1}^{n}\Big\langle\sigma_{p}\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{n}e^{a}_{A}\partial_{a}\Big),\sigma_{p}\Big(\sum_{b=1}^{n}e^{b}_{A}\partial_{b}\Big)\Big\rangle_{p}

(A.16d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A=1}^{n}\langle\sigma_{p}(e_{A}),\sigma^{\prime}_{p}(e_{A})\rangle_{p},\quad\forall p\in U.

(A.16e)

where the third step follows by linearity of
\sigma_{p}
and the last step from the definition of the component functions
e_{A}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}e^{a}_{A}\partial_{A}
.

Given a parallel transport operator
\Pi^{E}
on
E
, one defines a
covariant derivative for bundle sections
as follows
[
Ham17
]
. In order to compute the derivative
\nabla_{v}\Phi(p)
for
v\in T_{p}M
and
\Phi\in\Gamma(E)
, first take any curve
\gamma:[0,\varepsilon)\to M
with
\gamma(0)=p,\dot{\gamma}(0)=v
. Then define the function

S_{\Phi}:[0,\varepsilon)\to E_{p},\quad S_{\Phi}(t)=\Pi^{E}(\gamma|_{[0,t)})^{-1}\Phi(\gamma(t))\in E_{p},

(A.17)

where
\gamma|_{[0,t)}
is the curve
\gamma
restricted to the interval
[0,t)
. Then define

\nabla_{v}\Phi(p)\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{S_{\Phi}(t)-S_{\Phi}(0)}{t}\in E_{p}.

(A.18)

This defines a distinguished mapping

\nabla:\Gamma(E)\to\Omega^{1}(M,E),\quad\Phi\mapsto\nabla\Phi.

(A.19)

We associate the
Laplacian

\Delta_{E}
with the covariant derivative
\nabla
by

\Delta_{E}:\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(E),\qquad\Delta_{E}=\nabla^{*}\circ\nabla,

(A.20)

where
\nabla^{*}:\Omega^{1}(M,E)\to\Gamma(E)
is the adjoint operator to
\nabla
with respect to scalar products

\langle\langle\Phi,\Phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle_{E}\coloneq\int_{M}\langle\Phi(p),\Phi^{\prime}(p)\rangle_{p}\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}(p)}\dd^{n}p,\quad\Phi,\Phi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(E)

(A.21)

and

\langle\langle\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\rangle_{E}\coloneq\int_{M}\langle\sigma(p),\sigma^{\prime}(p)\rangle_{p}\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}(p)}\dd^{n}p,\quad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega^{1}(M,E)

(A.22)

respectively. The adjoint
\nabla^{*}
is defined by

\langle\langle\nabla\Phi,\sigma\rangle\rangle_{E}=\langle\langle\Phi,\nabla^{*}\sigma\rangle\rangle_{E},\quad\forall\Phi\in\Gamma(E),\quad\sigma\in\Omega^{1}(M,E)

(A.23)

implying

\langle\langle\Phi,\Delta_{E}\Phi\rangle\rangle_{E}=\langle\langle\Phi,\nabla^{*}\nabla\Phi\rangle\rangle_{E}=\langle\langle\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi\rangle\rangle_{E}\geq 0

(A.24)

assuming the manifold
M
is compact and without boundary.

Proposition A.9 (discrete and smooth Dirichlet energy
[
GL10
]
).

Let
\iota:\eta\to M
be an embedded graph and
E\to M
an
\SO(d)
-associated vector bundle with parallel transport operator
\Pi^{E}
. Assume that the manifold
M
is endowed with the Riemannian metric
\mathfrak{g}
and that the following criteria are fulfilled.

(i)

\eta
is is a regular graph with equal degree
2\dim M
at every node
i\in\nu
, oriented such that every node has the equal number of in- and out-edges.

(ii)

M
is covered by open sets
U_{i},i\in\nu
, such that
\cup_{i\in\nu}U_{i}=M
and
\iota(i)=p_{i}\in U_{i}
and
\iota(j)=p_{j}\not\in U_{i}
for all
j\neq i
.

(iii)

Let
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote a partition of unity with respect to this covering. We have the volume normalization condition

\int_{U_{i}}\beta_{i}\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}}=\operatorname{vol}_{\mathfrak{g}}(U_{i})=1,\quad\forall i\in\nu.

(A.25)

(iv)

Let
e_{A}
denote a local orthonormal frame for the metric
\mathfrak{g}
, defined in all points
p_{i},i\in\nu
. For all
i\in\nu
and
A\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there is a unique neighbor
j\in\nu
and
j>i
with path
\gamma_{ij}:p_{i}\to p_{j}
such that
\dot{\gamma}_{ij}(0)=e_{A}(p_{i})
; see Figure
13
for an illustration.

Then, for any section
\Phi\in\Gamma(E)
, one has

\langle\langle\Phi,\Delta_{E}\Phi\rangle\rangle_{E}=\langle\langle\phi,L_{\chi}\phi\rangle\rangle_{Z}+\mathcal{O}(C)+\mathcal{O}(C^{\prime}),

(A.26)

where
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle_{Z}
was defined in (
3.5
), (
3.6
) here with
\kappa_{ij}=\lambda_{ij}^{-2},\lambda_{ij}=\operatorname{len}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\gamma_{ij})
, and (recall (
A.2
) and (
A.10
))

\chi=(\eta,\iota^{*}\mathfrak{g},\iota^{*}\Pi^{E}),\quad\phi=\iota^{*}\Phi,

(A.27)

after a choice of trivialization
\zeta:\iota^{*}E\to\nu\times Y
. The constants
C,C^{\prime}
are given by

C=\max_{i\in\nu}\sup_{p\in U_{i}}\left|\|\nabla\Phi(p)\|_{p}^{2}-\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}\right|,\qquad C^{\prime}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\nu\\
i\sim j\end{subarray}}\lambda_{ij}^{2}.

(A.28)

Remark A.10 (proper discretization of
M
).

Assumption (i) specifies the uniform degree of
\eta
that matches the dimension of
M
, for a suitable discretization.

Assumption (iii) is a volume normalization condition that can be easily satisfied by a conformal rescaling of the metric with a suitable function.

Assumption (iv) entails that the paths
\gamma_{ij}
must be compatible with the metric
\mathfrak{g}
in the sense that they must be generated by a local frame vector field.

In summary, the stated assumptions enforce compatibility relations between the embedded graph
\iota(\eta)
and
M
in the metric and topological sense.

Proof of Proposition
A.9
.

We first evaluate the integral over the manifold

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Phi,\Delta_{E}\Phi\rangle\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\|\nabla\Phi(p)\|_{p}^{2}\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}(p)}\;d^{n}p

(A.29)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\nu}\int_{U_{i}}\beta_{i}(p)\|\nabla\Phi(p)\|_{p}^{2}\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}(p)}\;d^{n}p

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\nu}\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}\underbrace{\int_{U_{i}}\beta_{i}(p)\sqrt{\det\mathfrak{g}(p)}\;d^{n}p}_{\overset{\eqref{eq:norm}}{=}1}+\;\mathcal{O}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\nu}\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(C),

where

C=\max_{i\in\nu}\sup_{p\in U_{i}}\left|\|\nabla\Phi(p)\|_{p}^{2}-\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}\right|.

(A.30)

To proceed, we employ the definition of the bundle metric

\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}=\langle\nabla\Phi(p_{i}),\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\rangle_{p_{i}}\overset{\eqref{eq:sigma-times}}{=}\sum_{A=1}^{n}\langle\nabla_{A}\Phi(p_{i}),\nabla_{A}\Phi(p_{i})\rangle_{p_{i}},\quad\nabla_{A}\coloneq\nabla_{e_{A}}

(A.31)

and approximate the covariant derivatives, as defined in (
A.18
), by choosing an explicit curve
\gamma
and a finite time
t>0
. For some given
A
and
p_{i}
, there is a unique pair of indices
i,j
such that
\gamma_{ij}(0)=p_{i}
,
\gamma_{ij}(\lambda_{ij})=p_{j}
and
\dot{\gamma}_{ij}(0)=e_{A}(p_{i})
, by assumption, where
\lambda_{ij}=\operatorname{len}(\gamma_{ij})
.

Approximating the derivative then yields

\nabla_{A}\Phi(p_{i})=\frac{\Pi^{E}(\gamma_{ij})^{-1}\Phi(p_{j})-\Phi(p_{i})}{\lambda_{ij}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda_{ij}).

(A.32)

Now we can choose a trivialization
\iota^{*}E\cong\nu\times Y
which generates isometries
E_{p_{i}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
, under which
\Phi(p_{i})\in E_{p_{i}}
is sent to some
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i\in\nu
. Furthermore, the operator
\Pi^{E}(\gamma_{ij})
is identified with a matrix
\alpha_{ij}\in\SO(d)
under these isometries, which allows us to evaluate the squared norm of the first term of the above derivative approximation to

\left\|\frac{\Pi^{E}(\gamma_{ij})^{-1}\Phi(p_{j})-\Phi(p_{i})}{\lambda_{ij}}\right\|^{2}_{p_{i}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{ij}^{2}}\left\|\alpha_{ij}x_{i}-x_{j}\right\|^{2}.

(A.33)

Substituting into (
A.31
) gives

\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in\nu,j>i}\frac{\|\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{ij}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda_{ij}^{2})

(A.34)

and consequently

\sum_{i\in\nu}\|\nabla\Phi(p_{i})\|_{p_{i}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(C)=\sum_{i\in\nu}\sum_{j>i}\frac{\|\alpha_{ij}x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{ij}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(C^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}(C)

(A.35)
