Health-related quality of life among urban children living in poverty
Authors: Naffisah Mohd Hassan, Erne Suzila Kassim, Noor’ain Mohamad Yunus, Syukrina Alini Mat Ali, Yahya Mahyuddin Utoh Said
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Poverty is one of the challenges and barriers to children’s well-being. Although studies have sought to understand many aspects of children’s quality of life, there is still an urgency to comprehend Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) among children of the urban poor. Therefore, concentrating on urban poverty in Malaysia, the study aims to investigate the issue by comprehensively analysing how gender, age conceptualised as adolescent category, number of siblings, internet access, and the extent of family healthy living style affect the children’s HRQoL measured as psychological, physical, friends, family and school relationship. Data was collected by surveying 855 children aged 7 to 17 under parental supervision. The findings suggest that boys scored lower than girls in psychological, family, peer, and school domains compared to girls. Age-related variations emerged in physical, friends, and school dimensions. In terms of the number of siblings, differences were evident between 0 and 2 siblings and more than six siblings’ groups, impacting HRQoL across all HRQoL domains. Internet access and a healthy family setting are related to enhanced HRQoL. The study and its findings enrich theoretical discourse that aligns with established child development theories. Likewise, practical intervention strategies could be developed to benefit urban children navigating poverty-associated challenges. Future research may compare the study settings of urban and rural differences or evaluate policy intervention effectiveness.

Introduction

In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of children living in urban poverty, as this population faces heightened risks of compromised physical, psychological, and social well-being [1–3]. Poverty is a multidimensional phenomenon that restricts access to nutritious food, safe housing, education, and healthcare, ultimately undermining children’s development trajectories [4–6]. These disadvantages accumulate over time and can have enduring effects into adulthood, perpetuating intergenerational cycles of deprivation.

International evidence consistently demonstrates that children from low-income households score lower on HRQoL assessments than their more affluent peers [7, 8]. Gender and age-related differences are also evident: boys and older adolescents often show lower HRQoL outcomes, particularly in emotional well-being and self-esteem domains [9, 10]. It is essential, however, to interpret these differences as associations rather than causal mechanisms, since they are shaped by broader socioeconomic and environmental contexts [11].

Various validated instruments are available to measure HRQoL among children, including the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), and the KINDL-R (Questionnaire for Measuring Health-Related Quality of Life in Children and Adolescents – Revised Version) [12–14]. The KINDL-R, in particular, captures multiple domains of well-being, physical, emotional, self-esteem, family, friends, and school and has demonstrated strong psychometric reliability in both clinical and community settings [15]. Its adoption in this study enables robust and internationally comparable assessments of HRQoL.

Despite extensive research globally, the HRQoL of Malaysian urban poor children remains underexplored. Rapid urbanisation has deepened socio-economic inequalities in Malaysia, with low-income families disproportionately affected by overcrowding, unhealthy environments, and limited access to services [16]. While Malaysian research has examined HRQoL primarily among children with chronic illnesses such as asthma or diabetes [17], little attention has been paid to socioeconomically disadvantaged but otherwise healthy children. This gap is critical, as it leaves policymakers without evidence to design interventions tailored for vulnerable urban populations.

Therefore, the present study investigates HRQoL among Malaysian children living in urban poverty. Specifically, we examine the influence of gender, adolescence category, number of siblings, internet access, and family healthy living style on HRQoL outcomes. By situating these findings within the context of Malaysia’s urban poor, this study contributes to child health literature in middle-income settings. It informs interventions aimed at reducing inequalities and promoting equitable development.

Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of understanding and addressing the HRQoL challenges children face [7, 8]. Research findings suggest these children are more likely to experience physical health problems, such as malnutrition [1], chronic illnesses, and inadequate healthcare access [9]. Furthermore, the emotional well-being of children in poverty is often affected by increased stress, anxiety, and a higher risk of mental health disorders [4]. Socially, they may encounter difficulties in building positive relationships and face stigmatisation and discrimination, which can further impede their HRQoL [10]. Therefore, assessing the HRQoL of children living in poverty is crucial for understanding the full extent of their well-being and implementing effective interventions.

Various indicators and measurement tools have been developed to assess children’s health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including those living in poverty. One widely used instrument is the KINDL-R questionnaire, created by Ravens-Sieberer and Bullinger [11], which is designed to capture the multidimensional aspects of HRQoL in children and adolescents. It covers physical well-being, emotional well-being, self-esteem, family, friends, and school functioning. Another valuable measurement tool is the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [12]. The SDQ assesses emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behaviour.

The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), developed by Varni [13], is commonly used to assess HRQoL across physical, emotional, social, and school functioning domains. This tool provides a broader understanding of the different aspects of HRQoL and enables researchers to examine the impact of poverty on specific domains of well-being. In addition, to further explore protective factors and positive outcomes, researchers have utilised instruments such as the Children’s Hope Scale developed by Snyder [14]. This scale measures children’s level of hope and optimism, crucial in promoting resilience and positive outcomes, even in challenging circumstances.

Additionally, the Personal Well-Being Index–School Children [15] offers a valuable assessment of subjective well-being across various life domains, including school, friends, and living environment. These additional measures enhance our understanding of the factors contributing to HRQoL and provide valuable insights into the protective factors that can improve the well-being of children living in poverty.

In the context of the Malaysian urban poor, there is a need for comprehensive research within the area. Therefore, this study aims to examine the HRQoL among urban children living in poverty, utilising the KINDL-R questionnaire as a reliable and validated tool. Specifically, this study aims to conduct a comparative analysis of various factors, including gender, adolescence category, number of siblings, internet connection availability, family healthy living style, and their impacts on children’s HRQoL. The findings are essential for informing evidence-based interventions and policies that can address their specific needs. By identifying the factors that influence HRQoL, policymakers and practitioners can develop targeted strategies to enhance the children’s well-being. Additionally, the findings of this study can contribute to existing literature on children living in poverty and the nature of life that will provide insights for similar research efforts in other urban settings globally.

Review of literature

Research consistently shows that children from low-income households experience diminished HRQoL compared with their wealthier counterparts [18, 19]. Poverty exerts its effects through multiple pathways, including food insecurity, inadequate healthcare, limited educational opportunities, and exposure to unsafe environments [20]. For example, Rigolon et al. [21] observed that children from underprivileged neighbourhoods in Denver reported lower life satisfaction and reduced overall well-being. At the same time, Adams and Savahl [22] found similar patterns in South Africa, where disadvantaged children reported poorer outcomes in physical, emotional, and social domains. Such evidence highlights the universality of poverty’s impact on HRQoL across different cultural and geographical contexts.

Sociodemographic factors, including age, gender, and parental education, further shape children’s HRQoL. Studies in Europe and Asia have shown that older adolescents often report lower HRQoL than younger children, reflecting developmental and psychosocial challenges during adolescence [17, 23]. Gender disparities are also prominent. Coppo et al. [24] found that girls from disadvantaged backgrounds reported lower HRQoL across physical, psychological, and social dimensions compared to boys, while Bhana et al. [25] highlighted greater vulnerability among girls with chronic illnesses such as asthma. These findings underscore the need for a nuanced analysis of sociodemographic determinants within specific contexts.

Another important determinant is family structure. Evidence from India and China suggests that larger family size, particularly households with more than five or six children, is associated with significantly lower HRQoL scores [26, 27]. This supports the resource dilution model, which posits that parental time, attention, and material resources become more limited as the number of siblings increases. In contrast, smaller families are often better able to provide supportive environments conducive to well-being.

Digital access is increasingly recognised as a key determinant of child well-being in urban contexts. Children with internet access benefit from enhanced educational opportunities and social inclusion, while a lack of access exacerbates existing inequalities [28]. Similarly, family lifestyle practices such as healthy diets, non-smoking environments, and hygiene routines play protective roles in supporting children’s HRQoL [29, 30]. Conversely, unhealthy family habits can significantly undermine both physical and psychological well-being.

Despite the growing global evidence base, few studies have examined HRQoL among Malaysian urban poor children. Existing research has primarily focused on clinical populations, overlooking the socioeconomically disadvantaged but otherwise healthy child population [17]. This study, therefore, contributes by synthesising global knowledge and situating it within Malaysia’s urban context, addressing a critical gap in the literature and offering evidence to inform policy and practice.

Sociodemographic factors and HRQoL

Several studies have examined the influence of sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, education, and income on children’s HRQoL. Gutiérrez-Pérez [20] and Gu [31] both established that variables like age, gender, parents’ education, and greater income are associated with enhanced HRQoL. This alignment is further substantiated by Kumar, Kroon, and Lalloo’s [22] findings, which revealed a positive correlation between higher parental education and HRQoL. This association was reinforced by a meta-analysis encompassing fifteen studies. Similarly, a cross-sectional survey conducted by KA and Cheah [17] among schoolchildren underscored the influence of gender, ethnicity, religion, monthly household income, and perceived social support on HRQoL. Collectively, these investigations highlight the significant impact of sociodemographic factors on children’s HRQoL. Children raised in impoverished conditions confront various challenges that can compromise their HRQoL. Prior research [23, 24] notably underscores the pivotal role of social support, particularly for children facing poverty. Moreover, psychological aspects such as self-esteem, resilience, and coping strategies, as highlighted by Wang [25], can significantly impact the quality of life. These dynamic factors also present opportunities for positive change over time, with interventions potentially leading to improved HRQoL for economically disadvantaged children [24].

Gender and HRQoL

Numerous investigations have studied the impact of gender on HRQoL [26]. Gender plays a role in shaping HRQoL patterns. In a study conducted by Bisegger et al. [27]. It was found “gender differences can already be found in childhood, with lower values for girls” [27]. Similarly, an analysis centred on urban adolescents with asthma highlighted that females exhibited diminished HRQoL in both physical and social realms when compared to males, with more pronounced disparities among those facing severe asthma [28]. In addition, existing research has underscored gender-based inequality in mental health and overall well-being, with adolescent girls exhibiting heightened vulnerability to conditions like anxiety and depression [29]. These inquiries underscore the potential significance of gender in shaping HRQoL among children. However, a comprehensive understanding of these gender-related variations’ mechanisms warrants further exploration.

HRQoL among different age categories of adolescents

Research into HRQoL among teenagers has encompassed diverse age brackets, ranging from kindergarten to middle and high school students. Notably, within this spectrum, an apparent trend emerges wherein younger adolescents exhibit elevated HRQoL levels when contrasted with their older counterparts [25]. As children transition through adolescence, their bodies and minds undergo transformative changes that inherently influence their HRQoL. For instance, alterations in physical appearance, shifts in social relationships, and evolving cognitive capacities can collectively impact aspects like self-esteem and overall wellness [25]. This inherent instability can present challenges in effectively managing their health, particularly regarding mental well-being.

Number of siblings and HRQoL

An often overlooked yet significant factor influencing children’s HRQoL is the number of siblings within the family. Recent research conducted in India has revealed a noteworthy trend: children with more siblings tend to report lower HRQoL levels. This pattern persists even after considering parental affluence and educational backgrounds [30]. Similarly, a study conducted in China echoed these findings, indicating that adolescents with a higher count of siblings reported diminished scores in both physical and psychosocial health aspects compared to their peers with fewer siblings [32]. These observations resonate with the resource dilution model, a theoretical framework positing that children from larger families may encounter reduced parental attention and available resources, ultimately translating into compromised health outcomes and a reduced sense of well-being. As we delve further into this realm, it becomes pertinent for future research to delve into the nuanced influence of siblings on diverse dimensions of children’s health, encompassing physical well-being, mental health, and overall quality of life, especially those living in urban areas.

Internet access and HRQoL

Likewise, digital connectivity has rapidly emerged as a pivotal facet of contemporary life, profoundly impacting urban childhood experiences. Particularly significant in urban settings, digital connectivity offers many advantages encompassing information access, entertainment, and communication. Nevertheless, its influence is not confined solely to rural or urban distinctions. It also extends to intra-city dynamics. This phenomenon is notably conspicuous within low-income urban areas, where broadband adoption rates remain lower [33]. Internet access is central in shaping social inclusion and exclusion dynamics within these contexts. It is imperative to recognise that while digital access can be transformative, disparities in its availability can exacerbate existing inequalities, especially in economically marginalised urban sectors.

Family living style and HRQoL

The existing body of literature provides valuable insights into the intricate relationship between family dynamics and children’s quality of life, particularly concerning elements such as extreme smoking habits, dietary patterns, hygiene practices, and the extent of freedom afforded within the family context. Research has consistently demonstrated that excessive smoking within the household can significantly compromise children’s well-being. Exposure to second-hand smoke has been associated with a range of health issues, including respiratory problems, cardiovascular risks, and impaired cognitive development [34]. Similarly, dietary habits within the family unit are pivotal in shaping children’s overall health. Unhealthy eating patterns marked by high consumption of sugary, processed, and fast foods have been linked to increased risks of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases among children [35]. Furthermore, inadequate hygiene practices within the family environment can adversely affect children’s physical well-being. Poor hygiene and unsanitary living conditions can elevate susceptibility to infections, gastrointestinal disorders, and other preventable health issues [36].

Research methodology

Population, sample and sampling design

The research targeted a population of children aged between 7 and 17 years old, with further classification based on the American Academy of Paediatrics guidelines into three distinct categories: childhood (7–10 years old), early adolescence (11–14 years old), and middle adolescence (15–17 years old). To ensure a comprehensive sample, assistance from authorised institutions was sought, providing financial support and catering to the basic living needs of the participants’ families. Subsequently, a screening process was conducted to evaluate the household income of potential participants. Only children from households with income falling within the lowest income bracket were deemed eligible to participate in the study. Data was collected based on a purposive sampling design.

Instrument design

This study’s primary instrument was the KINDL-R questionnaire, a 24-item measure designed to assess health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among children and adolescents [11]. The KINDL-R has been widely validated and adapted in various cultural contexts, including Malaysia. In addition to the KINDL-R, selected items from the KIDSCREEN-27 Health Questionnaire for Children and Young People (Ravens-Sieberer & KIDSCREEN Group Europe, 2006) were incorporated to complement domains not fully captured by the KINDL-R. Several of the items presented in Table 1 (e.g., “In general, how would you say your health is?”; “Have you felt fit and well?”; “Have you been physically active?”) originate from the KIDSCREEN-27. The rationale for integrating these items was to enrich the assessment framework by ensuring broader coverage of HRQoL domains relevant to the Malaysian context. KINDL-R and KIDSCREEN-27 are internationally recognised instruments with overlapping but distinct emphases, and their combined use provided a more comprehensive assessment tool for this study. All items were translated into Malay using a back-translation procedure, and content validation was carried out by language and subject matter experts to ensure conceptual equivalence and cultural appropriateness.

Data collection

Data collection spanned from December 2021 to December 2022, encompassing a year-long period. The extended duration was necessary due to the intricate process of collecting data from minors. Initial approval of the research ethics was obtained from the researchers’ institution, Universiti Teknologi MARA Shah Alam (REC/09/2021 (MR/805), followed by a series of comprehensive training provided to enumerators tasked with data collection. Given that data had to be gathered from children as young as 7, utmost care was taken to ensure a comfortable setting that would put them at ease. Moreover, the enumerators were trained to convey and explain questionnaire statements using simple and accessible language. Parental and guardian involvement was limited to providing informed consent and supporting the children during data collection. The children completed the questionnaires, with enumerators assisting younger participants by explaining items in simple language. The research sample encompassed four major Malaysian cities: Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Johor Bharu, and Shah Alam. It is important to note that this study does not account for potential differences in poverty incidence or depth across the four cities, which may influence results, for example, access to the internet, household size, or gender distribution. A total of 1020 data points were collected for the study. After rigorous evaluation, 855 data points were analysed, excluding missing data, incomplete responses, and those not meeting the sample characteristics. Missing data were addressed through listwise deletion, with incomplete responses excluded from the final analysis.

The respondents provided information on their gender, age, number of siblings, availability of internet access, and family healthy living style, including factors such as extreme smoking habits, eating habits, hygiene and excessive freedom granted within the family. Based on the age, we categorised them as childhood (7 to 10 years old), early adolescence (11 to 14 years old) and middle adolescence (15 to 17 years old). To ensure the reliability and validity of the instrument, we examined its psychometric properties. Previous studies have assessed these properties in samples of school children [37]. The detailed results can be found in Table 3. Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70 indicate acceptable reliability for most subscales. The composite reliability, greater than 0.70 and AVE, greater than 0.50, further support construct validity, although the spiritual subscale showed weaker reliability. Therefore, our analysis revealed a satisfactory level of reliability and validity, providing evidence for the robustness and accuracy of the questionnaire as a measurement tool for assessing HRQoL in children living in poverty.

Discussion and research implications

This research compares HRQoL scores among impoverished urban children based on gender, adolescent category, internet availability, number of siblings and family healthy lifestyle. The findings present significant differences based on the factors. Consistent with prior findings [27], girls reported higher HRQoL in family, friends, and school domains, while boys scored slightly higher in physical well-being. Although these differences were minor, they highlight meaningful gender disparities that justify gender-sensitive school and community programs in line with Malaysia’s National Policy on Children (2019).

Next, significant age-related differences emerged in the friends domain: younger children (7–10 years) reported higher scores than middle adolescents (15–17 years), indicating a decline in peer-related HRQoL as children grow older. This supports previous findings about the fragility of adolescent peer relationships and emphasises the significance of peer-support programs, such as the Healthy Mind Program proposed by the Ministry of Education Malaysia in 2024.

Significant differences were observed across sibling groups, with children from larger families (six or more siblings) reporting notably lower HRQoL scores in all domains than those with fewer siblings. The most significant gap was in school-related HRQoL, indicating a moderate effect. This pattern reflects the ‘resource dilution’ perspective noted in prior studies [30, 32] and highlights the importance of strengthening parental support and family-based programs under Malaysia’s Dasar Keluarga Negara (National Family Policy).

Children from families practising healthy lifestyles scored higher in physical health (a moderate effect), family and school domains, compared to households with less healthy practices. These results demonstrate the protective role of household habits on children’s well-being, particularly in mitigating risks of obesity and safety concerns. Policy implications align with Malaysia’s Kempen Cara Hidup Sihat and the National Strategic Plan for Non-Communicable Diseases (2016–2025), which aim to foster healthier environments at the family and community levels.

Finally, the research findings reveal important insights into the relationship between internet access and HRQoL scores among children. Children with internet access reported significantly higher HRQoL in physical, psychological, family, and school domains than those without access, although no difference emerged in the friends domain. These findings highlight the role of digital inclusion in promoting academic engagement and broader well-being. They are particularly relevant to Malaysia’s MyDIGITAL Blueprint (2021–2030), which prioritises universal access to digital infrastructure. However, as this study only measured access and not patterns of use, future research should explore whether the quality of internet use (e.g., educational versus recreational) shapes HRQoL outcomes.

Theoretical implications

The findings of this research have significant theoretical implications, as they align with established theories on child development and well-being. The gender-based differences in HRQoL scores support the notion that gender roles can influence how boys and girls perceive and experience their health and well-being. Moreover, the age-related differences in HRQoL scores support developmental theories, emphasising that children undergo distinct physical, emotional, and social changes as they transition through different age categories, influencing their perceptions of well-being. Additionally, the significant impacts of healthy family lifestyles and access to the internet enhance the literature by demonstrating that daily habits and digital inclusion serve as structural determinants of child well-being. Together, these findings expand existing Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) models by incorporating sociocultural, familial, and technological factors particularly relevant in urban poverty contexts.

Managerial implications

From a managerial perspective, the study offers several insights for educators, healthcare providers, and policymakers. First, gender- and age-sensitive interventions are needed in schools, such as peer-support programs and targeted mental health services, to address specific vulnerabilities among boys, girls, and adolescents. Second, family-based programs should strengthen parenting skills and promote healthy routines, aligning with national initiatives such as the Dasar Keluarga Negara and Kempen Cara Hidup Sihat. Third, interventions must consider family size, ensuring that children from larger households receive adequate social and academic support. Finally, bridging the digital divide is crucial. The findings highlight the importance of expanding internet accessibility under Malaysia’s MyDIGITAL Blueprint (2021–2030) to enhance school engagement and equitable opportunities for children in low-income families. By integrating these measures into existing community and national programs, stakeholders can simultaneously address multiple dimensions of children’s well-being.

Conclusion

In conclusion, examining the HRQoL of urban children living in poverty is vital to understanding and addressing their challenges. Researchers can obtain a comprehensive picture of the multidimensional aspects of their well-being by utilising validated measurement tools like the KINDL-R questionnaire. The study conducted in the Malaysian context aims to contribute valuable insights that can inform interventions and policies to improve the HRQoL of these vulnerable children.

The findings reveal gender-based, age-related, and family-related differences in HRQoL scores, shedding light on the unique challenges faced by these children. By understanding these factors and their impact on HRQoL, policymakers, educators, and healthcare providers can develop targeted interventions and programs to improve the well-being and prospects of these vulnerable populations. Additionally, this research contributes to the growing body of literature on children’s quality of life and provides a foundation for similar studies in other urban settings worldwide. Addressing the HRQoL challenges of children living in poverty is crucial for building a more equitable and inclusive society that fosters the well-being and potential of all its members.

While interpreting these findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study did not examine whether the poverty incidence or depth of the four central urban locations (Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Johor Bahru, and Shah Alam) differ. Variations in socio-economic conditions across these cities may have influenced the distribution of demographic factors such as gender composition, number of siblings, and access to the internet. If a town has more girls living in larger households without internet access, this might help explain the differences in their HRQoL scores. Future studies should consider analysing data by city or including measures of poverty incidence and severity to isolate better the impacts of individual and family factors from regional disparities. In addition, the study only measured internet access availability, not usage patterns, which limits the interpretation of digital engagement as a factor in HRQoL.
