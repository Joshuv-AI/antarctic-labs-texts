Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in non-cirrhotic portal hypertension secondary to primary myelofibrosis: A rare case report
Authors: Febrian Ramadhan Pradana, Lady Sherly Nuramalia, Ummi Maimunah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is a life-threatening infection of ascitic fluid, typically associated with cirrhosis and advanced portal hypertension, while its occurrence in non-cirrhotic portal hypertension (NCPH) is rare and often underrecognized. Primary myelofibrosis (PMF), a myeloproliferative neoplasm, may lead to NCPH through mechanisms such as hypercoagulability, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and splenomegaly-related hemodynamic changes; however, SBP in this setting is extremely uncommon. A 60-year-old woman who presented to Dr. Soetomo General Academic Hospital with progressive painful abdominal distension, fever, and bilateral lower limb edema. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis, anemia, and elevated inflammatory markers, with preserved renal and liver function. Ascitic fluid analysis demonstrated a polymorphonuclear leukocyte count of 1676 cells/mm3. Serologic tests for hepatitis B and C were negative, and the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index score was 0.4. Imaging studies showed splenomegaly and features of portal hypertension without evidence of cirrhosis or splanchnic thrombosis. PMF was confirmed by detection of the Janus kinase 2 mutation and bone marrow biopsy findings. The patient was diagnosed with SBP in the setting of NCPH secondary to PMF and was treated with intravenous cefotaxime, resulting in clinical improvement. This case highlights that SBP can occur in non-cirrhotic portal hypertension due to PMF and underscores the importance of early diagnostic paracentesis, prompt antibiotic therapy, and increased clinical vigilance in atypical presentations.

Introduction

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is a serious and potentially life-threatening infection of ascitic fluid occurring without an identifiable intra-abdominal source, most commonly in patients with cirrhosis and advanced portal hypertension. Impaired immune function and low ascitic protein levels facilitate bacterial translocation and infection. Early recognition and prompt antibiotic therapy are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality.1

SBP is rarely reported in non-cirrhotic portal hypertension (NCPH), defined by increased portal pressure without cirrhosis.2 Primary myelofibrosis (PMF), a myeloproliferative neoplasm, is an uncommon cause of portal hypertension, driven by hypercoagulability, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and splenomegaly-related hemodynamic changes.3, 4, 5

Given the rarity of SBP in NCPH, especially in the PMF, it is important to enhance clinical awareness and guide management strategies. We present a rare case of SBP in NCPH secondary to PMF, highlighting its clinical presentation, diagnostic considerations, and therapeutic implications.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 60-year-old woman presented to Dr. Soetomo General Academic Hospital with a one-year history of progressive, painful abdominal distension, accompanied by bilateral lower extremity edema, fever and generalized weakness. Her medical history included hematemesis, melena, and multiple prior blood transfusions.(Table 1)

Clinical finding

On physical examination, the patient was febrile (38.3 °C) and had marked abdominal distension and bilateral lower extremity edema. She was hemodynamically stable.

Diagnostic assesment

Laboratory evaluation showed hemoglobin 6.5 g/dL, leukocyte 23,610/μL, platelet 113,000/μL, neutrophils 88.3%, and reticulocytes 3.5%. Coagulation parameters were within normal limits (prothrombin time: 12 s, activated partial thromboplastin time: 27 s, international normalized ratio: 1.07). Liver and renal function tests were within normal limits (alanine aminotransferase: 13 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase: 20 U/L, total bilirubin: 1.16 mg/dL; blood urea nitrogen: 12.4 mg/dL, creatinine: 0.6 mg/dL). D-dimer and procalcitonin was elevated (9610 ng/mL; 1.23 ng/mL). Serum albumin was 2.51 g/dL. Serologic tests for hepatitis B and C were negative, and the APRI score was 0.4. Molecular analysis detected the Janus kinase 2 valine 617 mutated to phenylalanine mutation, and bone marrow biopsy confirmed PMF with grade 2 osteosclerosis.

Therapeutic paracentesis yielded approximately 4.5 L of turbid yellow, odorless ascitic fluid with a liquid consistency. Analysis showed a polymorphonuclear (PMN) count of 1676 cells/mm3, albumin 1.23 g/dL, total protein 1.89 g/dL, glucose 118 mg/dL, and lactate dehydrogenase: 143 U/L, with a serum–ascites albumin gradient of 1.3 g/dL. Ascitic fluid culture was not performed due to limited resources.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient received intravenous cefotaxime 2 g every 8 hours for 7 days. She was subsequently started on ruxolitinib 5 mg once daily for management of portal hypertension secondary to PMF, aiming to prevent further complications and reduce the risk of readmission.

Follow-up and outcomes

After 6 days of treatment, the leukocyte decreased (11,270/μL), with clinical improvement characterized by reduced abdominal pain and resolution of fever.

Discussion

SBP is a well-recognized and life-threatening complication of ascites, most commonly associated with liver cirrhosis, primarily attributed to bacterial translocation from the intestinal lumen to mesenteric lymph nodes, followed by bacteremia and seeding of ascitic fluid.1,6 However, SBP in NCPH remains rare, likely due to relatively preserved hepatic synthetic function and higher ascitic protein levels, which enhances opsonic activity and reduces infection risk.1,7

SBP can still develop in NCPH under certain conditions. Portal hypertension can lead to intestinal congestion and increased mucosal permeability, facilitating bacterial translocation, while ascites provides a favorable medium for bacterial growth. Thus, although the risk is lower than in cirrhosis, patients with NCPH remain susceptible, particularly in the presence of systemic inflammation or impaired immunity.1

In this case, NCPH was attributed to PMF, a myeloproliferative neoplasm that alters portal hemodynamics through multiple mechanisms. PMF is associated with a hypercoagulable state predisposing to splanchnic venous thrombosis, while extramedullary hematopoiesis in the liver and spleen may cause sinusoidal infiltration and increased intrahepatic vascular resistance. In addition, massive splenomegaly can increase portal venous inflow, further contributing to portal hypertension. Together, these mechanisms may lead to NCPH even in the absence of cirrhosis.7

Although splanchnic thrombosis is a well-described complication in PMF, no evidence of portal, mesenteric, or splenic vein thrombosis was identified in this patient. This suggests that non-thrombotic mechanisms-particularly extramedullary hematopoiesis and increased portal flow due to splenomegaly-predominated in the development of portal hypertension.2,8

Although SBP has been reported in patients with NCPH, there are no previous reports describing its occurrence in NCPH secondary to PMF. This underscores the rarity and clinical significance of this case. Despite relatively preserved liver function and higher ascitic protein levels, SBP may still occur under certain conditions, particularly in the presence of additional risk factors such as systemic inflammation, leukocytosis, or impaired immune response. Therefore, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for SBP in patients with NCPH presenting with fever, abdominal pain, or leukocytosis.

SBP was diagnosed based on an ascitic PMN count >250 cells/μL, the gold standard criterion.1,6 Although cultures were not performed due to limited resources, marked clinical and laboratory improvement after empirical antibiotics supports the diagnosis.

Clinically, this case highlights the importance of early diagnostic paracentesis in all patients with ascites and suspected infection, regardless of cirrhosis status. Empirical third-generation cephalosporins remain the cornerstone of SBP management and should be initiated promptly. Patients with underlying conditions such as PMF require close monitoring for complications of portal hypertension, including ascites and SBP. Secondary prophylaxis may be considered in high-risk patients, although evidence in non-cirrhotic populations remains limited. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Ascitic fluid culture was not performed due to limited resources, preventing microbiological confirmation. Transient elastography (Fibroscan) could not be conducted in the presence of ascites, and liver biopsy was not performed as the patient declined the procedure.

This case highlights that SBP, although uncommon, can occur in NCPH secondary to PMF. Early recognition and timely management are crucial to reduce morbidity and improve outcomes. This case has several limitation.

Conclusion

SBP is a well-recognized complication of ascites most commonly associated with liver cirrhosis, but it may rarely occur in NCPH. This case shows that PMF can cause portal hypertension via extramedullary hematopoiesis, splenomegaly-induced increased portal flow, and hypercoagulability, predisposing to ascites and SBP even without cirrhosis.
