Recent advances and expanding applications of organoid models in unveiling drug ADME profiles
Authors: Guolei Zhang, Ruifan Zhang, Zihang Shu, Jing Zhao, Lei Yin, Meiyun Shi, Yanshuo Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) in vitro models generated from stem or progenitor cells that closely replicate the structural and functional complexity of human organs. These systems are emerging as powerful tools in pharmacokinetic research, addressing key limitations of traditional models such as two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures and animal experiments, which are often constrained by physiological mismatch and species-specific differences. This review summarizes recent advances in the development of organoid models, with a focus on hepatic and intestinal systems, and highlights their application in studying drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). We discuss how organoids improve predictions of oral bioavailability, facilitate assessment of drug-drug interactions (DDI), and help interpret interindividual pharmacokinetic variability. Current challenges including incomplete functional maturation, lack of standardization, and limited vascularization are also considered. Finally, we explore future directions involving the integration of organoids with advanced microphysiological systems (such as organ-on-a-chip (OoC)) and artificial intelligence (AI). With ongoing refinement, organoid-based approaches are expected to significantly improve the efficiency of drug development and the accuracy of clinical translation, paving the way for advances in precision pharmacology and personalized medicine.

Introduction

Pharmacokinetics (PK), the study of how organisms process drugs, examines the dynamic sequences of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [1]. Traditional PK research has long depended on two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures and animal models. Although 2D monocultures are operationally simple, they cannot replicate the intricate tissue architecture and cell-to-cell communication present in vivo, which often results in the unstable expression of crucial drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. Animal models offer a whole-organism context but are limited by substantial species differences in drug processing pathways, reducing their predictive accuracy for human outcomes [2]. Ethical issues and the considerable expense of animal testing, especially with non-human primates, have also motivated a regulatory movement toward alternative strategies consistent with the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) [3]. These inherent constraints of conventional models contribute to high failure rates in drug development, creating a pressing need for more human-relevant and predictive in vitro systems (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Comparative analysis of the strengths and limitations of organoid models versus mouse models, two-dimensional (2D) cell models, and non-human primate models. 3R: replacement, reduction, refinement; ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.

Organoid technology has arisen as a transformative solution to these challenges [4]. Following the pioneering generation of intestinal organoids in 2009 [5], self-organizing 3D structures derived from stem or progenitor cells have been successfully established for major metabolic and barrier organs such as the liver, kidney, and blood-brain barrier (BBB). These models closely emulate the cellular diversity, spatial arrangement, and functional characteristics of native tissues [6]. For example, liver organoids sustain stable expression and activity of cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and phase II metabolic pathways [7], while intestinal organoids retain the polarized distribution of efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) [8]. Owing to this high physiological fidelity, organoids provide a powerful human-relevant tool for accurately modeling essential absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) processes, including hepatic metabolism, intestinal absorption, and biliary and renal excretion, thereby improving the prediction of drug disposition, drug-drug interactions (DDI), and interindividual variability [9].

Notwithstanding their considerable potential, the use of organoids in PK research faces several obstacles. Current constraints include incomplete functional maturation relative to adult human tissues [10], batch-to-batch variability [11], a lack of uniform culture protocols [12], and the frequent absence of functional vascularization [13] and immune components [14]. These issues affect the reproducibility and physiological comprehensiveness of organoid models. This review systematically summarizes recent progress in applying organoid technology to drug ADME studies. It critically assesses the deployment of major organoid models across the key PK stages just like absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion and highlights both technological advances and enduring challenges. The article also discusses promising future research directions, particularly the integration of organoids with advanced bioengineering approaches such as microphysiological systems (MPS) and artificial intelligence (AI). By outlining paths to overcome existing limitations, this review emphasizes the potential of organoid technology to reshape drug development and improve clinical translation success.

Technical basis of organoid models

Fundamental principles of organoid generation and validation

Cellular sources for organoid generation

Organoid technology has evolved through three primary cellular sourcing strategies: adult stem cells (ASCs), pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), and patient-derived tissues (PDOs). Each approach offers distinct advantages for pharmacological applications. ASC-derived organoids, sourced from tissue-resident progenitors, effectively model adult tissue physiology and regeneration, though they typically produce epithelial-dominated structures lacking stromal and vascular components [15]. PSC-derived systems, including both induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and embryonic stem cells (ESCs), provide access to developmental processes and challenging tissue types, particularly neuroectodermal structures like cerebral and retinal organoids [16]. PDOs maintain exceptional fidelity to donor genetic and histological profiles, making them invaluable for modeling disease heterogeneity [17], especially in oncology where tumor organoids from biopsies preserve original drug response patterns for therapeutic stratification [18].

Culture systems and microenvironmental cues

The physiological relevance of organoid models depends on advanced culture systems that replicate native microenvironments. Organoids are typically embedded in three-dimensional extracellular matrices, such as matrigel or synthetic hydrogels, which provide essential biomechanical support and biochemical cues [19]. These are combined with precisely formulated media containing stage-specific combinations of growth factors, Wnt agonists, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) inhibitors, and other morphogens that guide self-organization and differentiation [20]. For instance, hepatic organoid maturation requires Notch inhibition with fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) and BMP7 supplementation to drive hepatocyte differentiation from Lgr5+ progenitors [21]. Similarly, intestinal organoids from PSCs undergo sequential induction through activin-mediated definitive endoderm formation followed by Wnt and FGF4-driven hindgut patterning [22]. To enhance physiological complexity, co-culture systems incorporating endothelial, immune, or stromal cells are increasingly implemented, introducing vascularization and immune modulation capabilities.

Validation of organoid fidelity

Comprehensive validation using multimodal approaches is essential for establishing organoid fidelity. Standard assessment includes high-resolution imaging [23] and histology [24] to verify structural organization, transcriptomic and proteomic profiling to confirm cell-type composition [25], and functional assays measuring metabolic activity [26], transporter function [27], and electrophysiological properties [28]. Since the groundbreaking establishment of intestinal organoids from single Lgr5+ stem cells in 2009, the field has expanded to include sophisticated models of liver, kidney, brain, and retina, many recapitulating functional zonation and multicellular interactions.

Current challenges and integrative developments

Current research focuses on overcoming challenges in full maturation, scalability, and standardization across lineages [29]. The integration of organoids with OoC technology [30], advanced imaging [31], and AI-assisted analysis [32] represents a promising direction, establishing organoid platforms as reproducible, human-relevant systems that will significantly advance drug discovery and personalized therapeutic development (Fig. 2).Fig. 2The development process of organoid model. Key organoid models relevant to ADME research

Hepatic organoids

The liver exhibits complex cellular architecture comprising both parenchymal cells (hepatocytes and cholangiocytes) and multiple non-parenchymal components, including hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, Kupffer cells, and hepatic stellate cells [33]. This intricate cellular ecosystem operates within a precisely regulated microenvironment where paracrine signaling and extracellular matrix interactions coordinately control metabolic functions [34]. Conventional 2D hepatocyte cultures fundamentally fail to replicate this physiological complexity, demonstrating rapid decline in CYP450 enzyme activity, absence of critical cell-cell junctions, and inadequate representation of non-parenchymal cell interactions [35]. These limitations substantially restrict the utility of 2D systems for predicting human drug metabolism and toxicity. In contrast, hepatic organoids recapitulate the liver's multicellular architecture and maintain stable, long-term expression of key drug-metabolizing enzymes, particularly CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, at levels approaching those observed in primary human hepatocytes [36]. This enhanced metabolic competence enables more accurate prediction of PK parameters such as metabolic stability and DDI potential.

Substantial evidence supports the physiological relevance of hepatic organoid systems. A established mouse liver organoid model incorporating hepatic progenitors and endothelial cells demonstrated complete expression of Phase I and II metabolic enzymes and significantly enhanced metabolic capacity for endocrine-disrupting chemicals compared to conventional rat liver S9 fractions, as shown in Fig. 3A [37]. Co-culturing hepatic stem/progenitor cells with liver endothelial cells yields organoids that retain stable morphology, liver-specific markers (Lgr5, EpCAM), and key drug-metabolizing enzyme (CYP450, UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT)) activity during long-term culture. This robust platform has thus been utilized to evaluate the activity changes of various endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) following metabolism [38]. Models derived from human fetal hepatocytes or iPSCs further recapitulate mature hepatic characteristics, including serum protein secretion, mitochondrial metabolic activity, and inflammatory responses. These advanced systems provide standardized, human-relevant platforms that effectively reduce reliance on animal models while improving predictive accuracy for drug metabolism and toxicity assessment. Intestinal organoids

Intestinal organoids serve as advanced in vitro models that faithfully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of the human intestinal epithelium. Unlike conventional 2D intestinal models, these 3D systems maintain the crypt-villus architecture and contain the complete repertoire of intestinal epithelial cells, including enterocytes, goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, and Paneth cells [39]. This cellular diversity enables organoids to accurately mimic the physiological environment of the intestinal mucosa, including stem cell niches and differentiation gradients. The preservation of native stem cell populations allows for continuous self-renewal and proper cellular turnover, providing a robust platform for investigating intestinal development, barrier function, and response to pharmaceutical compounds [40]. Particularly significant is their capacity to model the intricate interplay between drug absorption and metabolism, addressing critical limitations of traditional systems such as Caco-2 monolayers [41].

Substantial progress has been demonstrated in establishing functionally competent intestinal models, as evidenced by the iPSC-derived intestinal organoid system developed by Saito et al. (Fig. 3B) [42]. This approach successfully generates monolayers derived from intestinal organoids exhibiting enhanced expression of key metabolic enzymes including CYP3A4, UGT1A1, and carboxylesterase 2 (CES2), along with functional P-gp efflux activity. The model's physiological relevance is confirmed by its strong correlation (R2 = 0.8633) between measured drug permeability and human oral bioavailability, outperforming traditional Caco-2 models in predicting absorption outcomes. However, challenges remain in maintaining metabolic stability beyond 18 days of culture, highlighting the need for improved maturation protocols. Future developments should focus on extending functional longevity, incorporating microbial components, and integrating with OoC technologies to better simulate intestinal motility and fluid flow. These advancements will further establish intestinal organoids as indispensable tools for reliable prediction of drug absorption and first-pass metabolism in pharmaceutical development.

Kidney organoids

The kidney plays an essential role in PK as a primary organ for drug elimination, responsible for the excretion of many pharmaceuticals and their metabolites through glomerular filtration and tubular secretion. Renal organoids have consequently emerged as valuable 3D in vitro models that recapitulate key aspects of kidney development and function. These complex microtissues are typically generated from human pluripotent stem cells through stepwise differentiation protocols that mimic embryonic kidney development. The resulting structures contain multiple renal cell types, including podocytes, proximal and distal tubular epithelial cells, and stromal components, organized into nephron-like arrangements [43]. This cellular complexity enables renal organoids to approximate the functional characteristics of native kidney tissue, providing a human-relevant platform for studying drug-induced nephrotoxicity, renal clearance mechanisms, and developmental biology.

Significant progress has been made in establishing physiologically relevant kidney models, exemplified by the ureteric bud/collecting duct organoid system developed by Osafune's team (Fig. 3C) [44]. Their protocol employs sequential activation of key developmental pathways beginning with mesoderm induction using activin A and CHIR99021, followed by BMP and FGF signaling to generate nephric progenitors, and culminating in glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and Wnt activation to promote ureteric bud formation. These progenitor cells subsequently self-organize into three-dimensional structures exhibiting characteristic collecting duct epithelium when cultured in appropriate extracellular matrices with epidermal growth factor (EGF) and FGF supplementation. Future advancements should focus on enhancing functional transport capabilities, incorporating perfusion systems, and establishing patient-specific models for personalized nephrotoxicity testing. Such developments will solidify the position of renal organoids as indispensable tools for pharmaceutical research and safety assessment.

Other relevant organoid models

Beyond the primary metabolic organs, several specialized organoid systems provide unique platforms for studying distinct pharmacological barriers. BBB organoids represent advanced 3D models engineered to replicate the complex neurovascular unit through co-culture of brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and neurons [45]. These systems are typically established by directing pluripotent stem cell differentiation through Wnt/β-catenin activation using agonists like CHIR99021, followed by FGF supplementation to promote endothelial maturation. The resulting constructs demonstrate characteristic BBB features including tight junction proteins (Claudin-5, ZO-1) and functional efflux transporters (P-gp), enabling sophisticated investigation of drug penetration across this critical barrier. Similarly, pancreatic organoids derived from either adult stem cells or pluripotent precursors replicate both exocrine and endocrine functions through staged differentiation protocols employing Activin A, FGF, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) inhibitors to generate definitive endoderm, followed by pancreatic progenitor specification and final maturation into insulin-secreting islet-like structures or enzyme-producing acinar organoids (Fig. 3D) [46].

These specialized organoid systems significantly expand the toolbox for PK research by modeling tissue-specific barriers and metabolic functions. BBB organoids provide unprecedented opportunities for evaluating central nervous system (CNS) drug delivery strategies and investigating pathological mechanisms in neurological disorders, while pancreatic organoids offer human-relevant platforms for diabetes research and drug screening. Both systems demonstrate superior physiological relevance compared to traditional monolayer cultures, particularly in preserving tissue-specific cellular interactions and functional properties. The continued refinement of these specialized organoid platforms will substantially advance our capability to predict tissue-specific drug distribution and metabolism, ultimately strengthening the drug development pipeline for neurological and metabolic disorders.

The development of physiologically relevant hepatic, intestinal, renal, and specialized organoid models, provides a robust in vitro toolkit that closely recapitulates the structural and functional complexity of human tissues. These models not only overcome key limitations of traditional 2D cultures and animal systems but also establish a human-relevant platform capable of simulating organ-specific environments essential for PK studies. With their demonstrated fidelity in cellular composition, metabolic activity, and barrier functions, organoids are now poised to bridge the gap between mechanistic understanding and predictive modeling of drug behavior in vivo.

Applications of organoids in pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Intestinal absorption represents the crucial initial phase in determining oral drug bioavailability, involving complex interactions between physicochemical properties and biological barriers. While the Caco-2 monolayer system has served as the conventional gold standard for predicting drug absorption, it exhibits significant limitations including essentially lacks functional CYP3A4 expression, and cannot simulate intestinal first-pass metabolism, inadequate cellular diversity, and non-physiological transporter profiles [47]. These constraints substantially compromise its predictive accuracy for human absorption characteristics. Intestinal organoids have emerged as transformative tools that overcome these limitations by recapitulating the native tissue architecture and microenvironmental features of the human intestinal epithelium. Their 3D organization preserves the crypt-villus axis and contains the complete spectrum of intestinal epithelial cell types, enabling more accurate modeling of the complex processes governing drug absorption.

Recent advances in intestinal organoid technology have demonstrated remarkable progress in predicting human drug absorption. Seminal studies have established human monolayer models derived from intestinal organoids that closely mirror primary enterocytes in gene expression profiles, barrier integrity, and functional activity of drug transporters and metabolic enzymes [48]. These systems exhibit well-developed tight junctions, functional P-gp and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) transporters, and active CYP3A4 metabolism, with impressively low batch-to-batch variability (8.6% coefficient of variation). Validation studies using 19 reference drugs revealed R2 as high as 0.88 (based on 19 reference drugs) between model permeability and human absorbed fraction, with 7 of 9 compounds achieving predicted intestinal availability within 1.5-fold of clinical values, as shown in Fig. 4A [49]. Particularly noteworthy are models specifically designed to study dual P-gp/CYP3A substrates, which successfully capture the synergistic interplay between efflux transport and intestinal metabolism [50]. Furthermore, iPSC-derived intestinal epithelial cells demonstrate high expression of key transporters and metabolic enzymes, enabling simultaneous prediction of intestinal permeability and first-pass metabolism. While these systems represent substantial improvements over conventional models, challenges remain in maintaining metabolic stability beyond 18 days and standardizing differentiation protocols across different stem cell sources. Distribution

Organoid technology has revolutionized drug distribution studies by enabling precise modeling of compound transport across specialized biological barriers. BBB organoids exemplify this capability through their incorporation of endothelial cells, astrocytes, and pericytes in three-dimensional configurations that replicate critical barrier properties. These systems demonstrate functional tight junctions, specific transporter expression patterns, and physiologically relevant transendothelial electrical resistance, providing accurate assessment of drug penetration into the central nervous system [51]. Similarly, placental organoids derived from first-trimester villi form hollow structures expressing key ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters including P-gp, BCRP, multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1), and MRP2 with native-like localization. Validation studies using fluorescent substrates and specific inhibitors have confirmed their functional efflux activity, enabling evaluation of therapeutic agents such as antihypertensive and antipsychotic drugs for fetal exposure risk assessment, as shown in Fig. 4B [27].

Patient-derived tumor organoids further extend distribution modeling capabilities by preserving original tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental features [52]. These models maintain the tumor-specific extracellular matrix network, including components such as collagen type I and fibronectin, which is remodeled from the patient's own tumor. This preserved extracellular matrix (ECM) acts as a physical barrier that directly impedes drug diffusion, especially for larger therapeutic molecules, and modulates the cellular microenvironment through integrin-mediated signaling pathways [53]. Furthermore, the self-assembled, 3D architecture of tumor organoids exhibits high cellular density and a tightly packed structure. This configuration accurately recapitulates the elevated interstitial pressure and restricted extracellular space observed in solid tumors in vivo [54]. Consequently, it physically limits drug convection and diffusion, establishing a concentration gradient from the tissue periphery toward the core, and may also activate pro-survival signaling through enhanced cell-cell contacts, thereby indirectly influencing drug sensitivity [55]. In addition to these structural barriers, tumor organoids maintain the clonal heterogeneity of the original tumor. They encompass diverse cellular subpopulations that differentially express drug efflux pumps (e.g., P-gp), metabolizing enzymes, or drug targets. This cellular diversity leads to selective drug metabolism, efflux, or binding within distinct micro-regions, generating localized “hotspots” and “blind spots” of drug distribution [56]. Together, the preserved ECM, the dense 3D architecture, and the cellular heterogeneity provide a unique in vitro platform that recapitulates key determinants of intratumoral drug distribution. This enables more accurate investigation of drug penetration, diffusion barriers, and the resulting heterogeneous drug exposure within the tumor microenvironment. Studies involving gastric and colorectal cancer organoids have demonstrated enhanced sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents including oxaliplatin, gemcitabine, 5-fluorouracil, and paclitaxel, with the 3D architecture enabling more accurate simulation of intratumoral drug distribution patterns [57]. The integration of these specialized models into multi-organ platforms represents another significant advancement, as demonstrated by microfluidic platforms connecting liver, intestine, kidney, and bone marrow tissues that successfully predict systemic distribution kinetics for compounds like nicotine and cisplatin, as shown in Fig. 4C [58].

The correlation between organoid-based distribution patterns and clinical pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data is increasingly evident. The distribution pattern of drug enrichment at the periphery coupled with insufficient core exposure observed in organoids can provide a PK-based explanation for the clinically common phenomenon of resistance in the tumor core. If a compound exhibits a significant penetration gradient in PDO. For example, a core concentration only 10% of that at the periphery-and this gradient is highly correlated with the spatial distribution of apoptosis or proliferation inhibition, then this in vitro model directly reveals a potential clinical efficacy bottleneck for that drug. Moreover, the clonal heterogeneity preserved in tumor organoids enables correlation of specific genotypes with distribution phenotypes. For instance, if an organoid model contains tumor cell subpopulations with both high and low expression of P-gp, enhanced efflux and reduced drug accumulation can be directly observed in the P-gp-high region following drug exposure. This in vitro phenotype can be directly compared with clinically measured P-gp expression levels in the same patient's tumor biopsy tissue and their corresponding response data to P-gp substrate drugs. If the sensitivity predicted by the organoid aligns closely with the patient's clinical response (e.g., radiographic response or progression-free survival), then such an organoid platform holds potential as a companion diagnostic for prospectively identifying patient populations likely to benefit from specific drugs. While most current research focuses on overall efficacy rather than detailed distribution kinetics, with advancements in higher-resolution imaging technologies and quantitative analytical methods, tumor organoids will be capable of more precisely quantifying the “intratumoral drug exposure-effect” relationship, enabling them to play an indispensable role in drug development for precision oncology and individualized therapeutic decision-making.

The collective evidence from these organoid platforms demonstrates their transformative potential in drug distribution research. BBB models provide unprecedented insight into neurotherapeutic delivery challenges, while placental organoids offer human-relevant assessment of medication safety during pregnancy. Tumor organoids enable personalized evaluation of chemotherapeutic distribution and efficacy, addressing critical challenges in cancer treatment optimization, as shown in Fig. 5A [59]. Multi-organ platforms further enhance predictive capabilities by modeling complex inter-organ distribution dynamics and metabolite-mediated effects [60]. However, several challenges require attention, particularly regarding vascular integration, functional stability maintenance, and standardization across different organoid types. Future development should focus on incorporating physiological flow conditions, establishing immune components, and creating standardized protocols for multi-organ platform construction. The integration of advanced technologies such as microfluidic systems, real-time monitoring capabilities, and computational modeling will further strengthen the predictive accuracy of organoid-based distribution studies. Metabolism

Drug metabolism represents a pivotal component of PK, primarily occurring in the liver through enzymatic transformations mediated by CYP450 systems and other metabolic pathways [61]. These biochemical processes critically influence both therapeutic efficacy and potential toxicity. Traditional 2D hepatocyte cultures, while widely implemented, suffer from rapid dedifferentiation and unstable expression of metabolic enzymes, failing to maintain the complex cellular interactions essential for physiologically relevant drug metabolism [62]. Hepatic organoids have emerged as transformative models that address these limitations through their 3D architecture and multicellular composition, enabling more accurate prediction of metabolic pathways and DDI.

Functional maturation and metabolic stability

Recent advances in organoid technology have significantly enhanced our capacity to model human drug metabolism. Hepatic organoids show remarkable long-term stability in the expression and function of key CYP450 enzymes, such as CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6. For example, they can maintain stable CYP450 enzyme activity (e.g., CYP3A4 for over 30 days) while preserving both Phase I and II metabolic activities at levels comparable to primary human hepatocytes [63]. Under optimized conditions, activity of some CYP enzymes in liver organoids can reach 75%–90% of that in primary hepatocytes. In contrast, liver fractions/microsome or S9 fractions possess enzyme activity but lack complete cellular architecture and cofactor regeneration systems, unable to simulate long-term metabolism or enzyme induction/inhibition. Methodological innovations have further strengthened their application, such as the development of a sophisticated cocktail assay utilizing liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to simultaneously quantify five major CYP450 enzyme activities in adult stem cell-derived liver organoids, as shown in Fig. 5B [64]. This approach, employing multiple reaction monitoring in a single 6-min analysis, provides comprehensive metabolic phenotyping capability. Another innovative strategy involves the construction of zone-specific liver organoids that recapitulate the metabolic zonation of hepatic lobules, creating biomimetic models with physiological gradients from periportal to pericentral regions, as shown in Fig. 5C [65].

Biotransformation pathways and metabolite identification

Beyond assessing metabolic enzyme activity, organoids offer a unique platform for studying complex biotransformation pathways and identifying metabolites. Their physiological architecture supports the complete network of Phase I (e.g., oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis by CYPs, dehydrogenases) and Phase II (e.g., glucuronidation by UGTs, sulfation by sulfation by sulfotransferases (SULTs)) conjugation reactions under near-physiological cofactor balance and subcellular organization. This enables the generation of metabolite profiles more reflective of in vivo conditions compared to subcellular fractions like liver microsomes or S9 [66]. The integration of organoid culture systems with advanced analytical techniques, particularly high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) and LC-MS/MS, allows for the direct identification and quantification of primary and secondary metabolites from the culture medium or lysates [67]. For instance, the aforementioned cocktail assay using liver organoids not only quantifies enzyme activity but also facilitates the tracking of specific metabolite formation for each CYP450 isoform. This capability is crucial for early detection of reactive or toxic metabolites and for elucidating interspecies differences in metabolic pathways. Intestinal organoids also contribute to this area by modeling first-pass metabolism, where the intestinal transformation of prodrugs or the generation of specific metabolites can be directly analyzed, as demonstrated in microfluidic chip models integrating intestinal organoids.

Drug-drug interactions and integrated models

Beyond hepatic systems, intestinal metabolism models have also seen substantial improvement. Lee et al. [68] developed a 3D-printed microfluidic chip integrating small intestinal organoids that successfully simulates first-pass metabolism, demonstrating how intestinal transformation of docetaxel significantly reduces its cytotoxic effects on colorectal cancer spheroids. The evolution towards integrated multi-organ platforms, such as gut-liver chips, further allows for the study of sequential metabolism and organ-cross talk in metabolite formation and handling.

These developments collectively establish organoids as superior platforms for metabolic studies, though several challenges require resolution. Current hepatic organoids typically achieve only 60%–80% of in vivo CYP450 activity levels, indicating incomplete functional maturation [69]. Additional limitations include insufficient vascularization and batch-to-batch variability that affect reproducibility. Future progress depends on integrating engineering approaches such as microfluidic OoC systems, advanced co-culture protocols, and dynamic perfusion cultures to enhance metabolic stability and predictive accuracy. The convergence of these technologies with standardized differentiation protocols and analytical methods will accelerate the implementation of organoid platforms in drug discovery and development, ultimately providing more reliable prediction of human drug metabolism and supporting the advancement of personalized medicine.

Excretion

Drug excretion represents the terminal phase in PK, with renal and biliary pathways serving as the primary elimination routes for pharmaceuticals and their metabolites [70]. The accurate prediction of excretion patterns remains challenging due to species-specific differences in transporter expression and clearance mechanisms. Organoid technology has emerged as a transformative approach in this domain, enabling the development of human-relevant models that faithfully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of excretory organs. These advanced systems provide unprecedented opportunities to study drug elimination processes under physiologically relevant conditions, addressing critical limitations of traditional animal models and conventional cell cultures in predicting human excretion profiles.

Significant progress has been achieved in developing organoid models for both renal and biliary excretion studies. Renal organoids generated from patient-derived tissues demonstrate remarkable fidelity in reproducing kidney architecture and function. One established methodology enables the simultaneous establishment of normal and cancerous renal organoids from the same patient, preserving original pathological features and genetic heterogeneity [71]. This dual-model system permits concurrent evaluation of drug efficacy against tumor cells and toxicity toward normal renal tissues, offering a powerful platform for personalized therapy development. In biliary excretion research, cholangiocyte organoids have been successfully differentiated into polarized monolayers that replicate the structural and functional characteristics of native bile ducts, as shown in Fig. 5D [72]. These models express crucial drug transporters including MRP2 and multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1), enabling precise quantification of hepatobiliary transport kinetics. As the first in vitro system permitting dynamic monitoring of biliary excretion under near-physiological conditions, this platform represents a substantial advancement in predicting drug clearance and assessing hepatobiliary toxicity.

The implementation of organoid technology in excretion studies marks a paradigm shift in PK research. Renal organoids provide clinically relevant insights into drug-induced nephrotoxicity and renal clearance mechanisms, while cholangiocyte models offer unprecedented capabilities for studying hepatobiliary elimination. However, challenges remain in achieving complete functional maturation, particularly regarding the establishment of fully integrated vascular networks and the replication of in vivo pressure gradients that influence excretory functions [73]. Future advancements should focus on enhancing model complexity through the incorporation of flow dynamics, improving long-term functional stability, and developing standardized protocols for quantitative excretion assessment. The continued refinement of these organoid platforms will significantly improve the prediction of drug excretion profiles in humans, paving the way for more predictive and individualized drug development while reducing reliance on animal testing. Toxicology

Traditional drug toxicity assessment faces significant limitations due to the physiological disparities of animal models and the structural simplicity of 2D cell cultures. Organoid technology has emerged as a transformative approach in toxicological evaluation by reproducing human organ complexity in 3D architectures [74]. These advanced models maintain tissue-specific cellular interactions and physiological functions that are crucial for accurate toxicity prediction. The capacity of organoids to recapitulate human tissue responses positions them as invaluable platforms for detecting organ-specific toxicities, thereby addressing critical gaps in conventional preclinical safety assessment methods.

Organoid systems have demonstrated substantial utility across multiple toxicity testing applications. Hepatic organoids exhibit enhanced sensitivity in detecting drug-induced liver injury through stable expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters, enabling faithful reproduction of toxicity mechanisms observed with hepatotoxic agents like acetaminophen, as shown in Fig. 6A [75]. These models successfully mimic crucial injury pathways including oxidative stress (Fig. 6B) [76], mitochondrial impairment, and programmed cell death [77]. Renal organoids containing structured nephron components enable assessment of drug-induced kidney damage, with patient-specific iPSC-derived models revealing individual susceptibility variations to nephrotoxic compounds such as cisplatin [78]. Cardiac organoids with spontaneous contractile activity and comprehensive ion channel expression provide integrated evaluation of pro-arrhythmic risks, surpassing the limitations of single-channel assays, as shown in Fig. 6C [79]. Immuno-pharmacokinetics: the role of immune-competent organoids

The immune system, as a key modulator of drug PK and pharmacodynamics, is attracting increasing attention for its regulatory roles. Immune cells and the cytokines they secrete can directly or indirectly regulate the expression and activity of key drug-metabolizing enzymes such as CYP450 enzymes and transporters including P-gp, thereby influencing the processes of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. Conversely, drugs and their metabolites can modulate the function of immune cells, leading to therapeutic effects or adverse reactions [84]. Conventional ADME models largely lack functional immune components, which results in significant limitations in predicting immune-related drug responses. The technological advancement of co-culturing parenchymal cells with tissue-resident or circulating immune cells to generate immune-competent organoids has opened up new avenues for the research of immunopharmacokinetics.

In the liver field, the co-culture of hepatic organoids with Kupffer cells (liver-resident macrophages) enables the investigation of the mechanisms underlying drug-induced liver injury (DILI) involving immune activation. Such models can recapitulate inflammatory responses that suppress CYP450 enzyme activity and alter metabolic profiles, and can also be used to evaluate the hepatotoxicity of immunomodulatory drugs [84]. In the intestinal organoid systems, the incorporation of innate immune cells (e.g., macrophages) or adaptive immune cells allows for the exploration of the mechanisms by which drugs affect the immune homeostasis of the intestinal barrier. This research is of crucial significance for assessing the intestinal toxicity of drugs such as chemotherapeutics and biologics, as well as for investigating the first-pass metabolism of orally administered immunotherapies [85]. In the tumor microenvironment field, the co-culture of patient-derived tumor organoids with autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) provides a powerful experimental platform for evaluating the penetration, distribution characteristics and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapies in a personalized tumor-immune microenvironment [86]. The integration of immune cells into microfluidic multi-organ chips (e.g., chip systems connecting intestinal, hepatic and lymphoid tissue mimics) offers a systemic approach to studying the PK of biologics, vaccines and immunotherapies, enabling the analysis of the distribution patterns of such drugs in immune organs and their potential to trigger systemic cytokine release.

In the future, immune-competent organoids hold enormous potential for personalized immunopharmacology research, the prediction of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), and the development of drugs targeting the immune-metabolic axis. The key challenges in this field include maintaining the long-term viability and normal phenotypic states of diverse immune cell subsets in 3D culture systems, scaling up these complex co-culture systems, and establishing standardized protocols for functional readouts. Overcoming these challenges will further solidify the central role of immune-competent organoids, making them indispensable tools for comprehensive ADME research and drug safety evaluation in the era of immunotherapy and precision medicine. Organoids in clinical trials

Organoid technology is progressively advancing from fundamental research toward clinical translation, establishing its pivotal role as an in vitro patient avatar in clinical trial contexts [87]. By accurately simulating individual patient characteristics, these models allow for the prediction of individual treatment outcomes prior to therapeutic initiation. This capability offers unprecedented insights for the design and implementation of clinical trials, while also serving as a powerful tool for investigating individualized PK. Key applications include elucidating interindividual PK variability [88], forecasting DDI [89], and optimizing clinical sampling strategies [90].

Elucidating the sources and consequences of individualized PK variability

While population PK studies identify general trends influenced by factors such as age and body weight, they frequently fail to predict individual patient responses with sufficient accuracy [91]. PDO have emerged as powerful tools to dissect the mechanistic basis of interindividual PK variability at the tissue and cellular level. These models retain donor-specific expression and functional profiles of key drug-processing proteins, enabling direct investigation of how individual differences in transporter and enzyme activity ultimately influence drug exposure and response.

Substantial heterogeneity in drug transporter function has been documented using intestinal organoid models. Research demonstrates considerable variation in the expression and activity of efflux transporters such as P-gp and BCRP among organoids derived from different individuals. A representative clinical-translation-focused study employed an advanced imaging-based assay using Rhodamine 123 as a fluorescent P-gp substrate [92]. The study design involved establishing intestinal organoids from a cohort of patients scheduled to receive oral P-gp substrate drugs (e.g., digoxin or tacrolimus). The efflux activity in each patient's organoids was precisely quantified by comparing Rhodamine 123 accumulation rates in the presence and absence of the specific inhibitor verapamil. Key results confirmed not only the stability of this P-gp function through cryopreservation and passaging but also a strong correlation (e.g., R2 > 0.7) between high in vitro efflux activity in organoids and subsequently measured low systemic exposure to the corresponding P-gp substrate drug in the matched patients. This approach allows the pre-therapeutic identification of individuals with high intestinal efflux capacity, who are at risk of subtherapeutic drug exposure, thereby potentially guiding dose adjustments or the use of efflux inhibitors to improve efficacy. Similarly, patient-derived liver organoids reveal significant interindividual differences in metabolic capacity, particularly in the activity of CYP450 enzymes such as CYP3A4. These models accurately reflect donor-specific metabolic phenotypes, effectively replicating “rapid” or “slow” metabolizer status observed in clinical settings.

The capacity of organoids to mirror interindividual PK variability represents a significant advancement toward personalized medicine. High-throughput platforms, including microfluidic systems, now enable scalable functional characterization of transporter and enzyme activities [93]. The resulting data provide crucial evidence for clinical decision-making, informing strategies such as dose adjustment, concomitant use of transporter inhibitors, or alternative administration routes for patients with extreme phenotypic profiles. Furthermore, standardized culture protocols continue to improve the functional maturity and reproducibility of organoid models, establishing a robust foundation for metabolic phenotyping [94]. As these technologies mature, PDO will play an increasingly vital role in predicting individual drug exposure, optimizing therapeutic outcomes, and minimizing adverse reactions in precision pharmacotherapy.

Predicting DDI at the individual level

The concurrent administration of multiple medications during clinical trials frequently raises concerns about pharmacokinetic DDIs. While conventional population-based approaches provide generalized risk assessments, they often fail to capture individual susceptibility variations [95]. Organoid technology presents a transformative strategy for personalized DDI prediction by preserving patient-specific metabolic and transport characteristics, thereby enabling more accurate evaluation of individual interaction risks that transcend population-level estimations [96].

Substantial evidence supports the application of organoid models in individualized DDI assessment. Studies utilizing iPSC-derived liver organoids demonstrate marked variations in metabolic inhibition responses when exposed to enzyme inhibitors [97]. When organoids derived from different donors were treated with a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor alongside a substrate drug, the degree of metabolic inhibition (measured by increased substrate exposure) varied between organoid lines. This differential susceptibility pattern directly correlated with the donors' genetically inferred CYP3A4 activity status, enabling the in vitro prediction of which patients would be at highest risk for a clinically significant DDI. Furthermore, co-culture systems incorporating patient-derived liver organoids with investigational drugs permit direct quantification of metabolic inhibition magnitude in the presence of interacting medications [98]. Technological advancements have progressed from single-organ models to integrated multi-organ platforms, such as gut-liver chip systems that simulate complete first-pass metabolism pathways. When DDI perpetrators are introduced into these sophisticated platforms, researchers can simultaneously monitor their impact on both intestinal absorption and hepatic metabolism, providing comprehensive prediction of individualized effects on overall drug bioavailability.

The evolution of organoid-based DDI prediction represents a paradigm shift in clinical pharmacology. These patient-specific models overcome the limitations of population-average predictions by accounting for individual genetic and phenotypic variations in drug processing pathways [99]. The integration of multi-organ systems further enhances physiological relevance by capturing the complex interplay between different metabolic and transport processes [100]. Current developments focus on standardizing these platforms for reliable clinical translation, establishing quantitative frameworks for DDI risk stratification, and validating predictions against clinical outcomes. As these technologies mature, organoid-based DDI assessment will increasingly inform clinical trial design and therapeutic decision-making, ultimately contributing to safer medication regimens and improved patient outcomes in polypharmacy scenarios.

Optimizing bioanalytical strategies in clinical trials

The integration of organoid technology into clinical trial design offers transformative potential for optimizing bioanalytical strategies. Organoids function as predictive functional biomarkers that can identify patients likely to demonstrate atypical PK profiles before trial initiation [101]. This proactive approach enables researchers to design more efficient sampling protocols and allocate analytical resources more effectively, moving beyond the conventional one-size-fits-all methodology in clinical pharmacology.

A primary application lies in refining PK sampling strategies. Organoids derived from prospective trial participants can detect extreme metabolic phenotypes, such as exceptionally high or low enzyme activities, during patient screening. These functional data can be incorporated into physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models to generate preliminary PK simulations and perform risk stratification before drug administration. This predictive capability enables implementation of a stratified sampling strategy: participants identified as “outliers” through organoid screening undergo intensive PK sampling to fully characterize their unique profiles, while those with “normal” metabolic phenotypes undergo validated sparse sampling. This targeted approach maximizes information yield while optimizing resource utilization in clinical trials.

Beyond optimizing existing trial operations, organoids are increasingly acting as engines for therapeutic discovery, directly shaping clinical trial hypotheses. Confronted with high clinical trial failure rates in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a multi-center study employed a PDO biobank to screen for chemosensitizers [102]. Testing approved drugs on resistant PDOs revealed hyperactivity in the mevalonate and mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathways, identifying statins as promising agents. This organoid-derived rationale directly informed a Phase II trial (NCT06241352), which evaluates atorvastatin combined with chemotherapy in patients with stabilized or progressive disease. Promising early results show a significant carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) decline in 70.3% of patients, demonstrating that PDOs can successfully translate in vitro discoveries into clinical trials and act as engines for therapeutic discovery.

The adoption of organoid-informed bioanalytical strategies represents a significant advancement in precision clinical pharmacology. This approach enhances trial efficiency by focusing intensive sampling on the most informative participants, thereby reducing overall burden while maintaining scientific rigor. Furthermore, it improves patient safety by identifying individuals at risk of abnormal drug exposure before therapeutic intervention [103]. As validation of organoid-based predictions continues to accumulate, these systems are poised to become integral components of clinical development programs. Future implementation should focus on standardizing organoid-based phenotypic screening protocols, establishing quantitative thresholds for metabolic classification, and validating these approaches across diverse therapeutic areas. This evolution in clinical trial methodology promises to accelerate drug development while advancing the principles of personalized medicine.

Potential and challenges of organoid models in PK studies for special populations

Translating drug efficacy and safety data from general adult populations to individuals with distinct physiological or pathological conditions remains a central challenge in clinical pharmacology. This is particularly true for pediatric [104] and elderly patients [105], as well as for individuals with compromised hepatic or renal function [106]. Conventional PK models frequently lack the resolution to capture the significant alterations in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion that define these groups. Organoid technology, especially in the form of PDO, presents a transformative strategy to address this gap. These in vitro avatars preserve donor-specific genetic, developmental, and disease-associated phenotypes, thereby enabling both mechanistic studies and personalized predictions of drug disposition in scenarios where traditional models are inadequate.

Pediatric and geriatric populations: Modeling age-specific physiology

The PK profiles of drugs in pediatric and elderly patients differ significantly from those in healthy adults due to ongoing organ development or age-related functional decline.

Regarding pediatric populations, the development of reliable PK models is complicated by both biological and practical factors. Chief among these is the difficulty of obtaining tissue from children, whether healthy or unwell, which severely restricts the ability to create pediatric organoids via standard methods. To address this limitation, researchers are pursuing two main approaches. One involves optimizing techniques to grow stable organoid lines from the minute tissue samples collected during pediatric surgeries or endoscopic procedures [107]. The other, increasingly important strategy employs induced pluripotent stem cell technology. This method avoids the need for direct organ tissue by reprogramming easily sourced pediatric cells, such as skin fibroblasts or urine derived cells, into iPSCs [108]. These stem cells are subsequently differentiated into functional organoids of key organs like the liver, kidney, or intestine. These pediatric specific models are critical for studying developmental changes in drug processing mechanisms, for instance the shift from fetal CYP3A7 to mature CYP3A4 enzyme activity. As such, they provide an essential in vitro tool for assessing drug metabolism, toxicity, and appropriate dosing for children, helping to build a foundation for safer pediatric pharmacotherapy.

For geriatric populations, cellular replicative senescence and altered metabolic homeostasis present distinct challenges for organoid culture. Successfully established models must account for donor age. These “aged” organoids can simulate hallmarks of aging [109], such as chronic low-grade inflammation (“inflammaging”), increased oxidative stress, and tissue fibrosis [110]. They serve as critical platforms for investigating the heightened susceptibility to adverse drug reactions and the complex PK interactions arising from polypharmacy, which is prevalent in elderly patients. By capturing age-associated molecular and functional changes, geriatric organoids can help identify drugs with unfavorable safety profiles in this vulnerable demographic before clinical use.

Patients with organ dysfunction: Disease modeling and personalized therapeutic guidance

For patients with hepatic or renal insufficiency, the predictive failure of standard PK models can lead to severe toxicity or therapeutic inefficacy. Organoid technology provides a dual-value proposition here: serving as precise models for disease mechanism investigation and as tools for prospective, personalized therapy optimization.

In disease modeling, organoids can be directly generated from patient biopsy tissues. These disease-specific organoids retain critical pathological features of the source tissue, including lipid accumulation, inflammatory signaling, fibrosis, or cyst formation [111]. They enable the direct study of how disease pathophysiology alters the expression and function of drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., downregulated CYP450s in cirrhosis) and transporters (e.g., impaired renal tubular secretion), providing mechanistic insights into altered drug PK in these conditions.

More critically, for personalized therapeutic guidance, PDOs act as in vitro avatars for pre-treatment testing. Prior to administering a drug with known hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic risk (e.g., chemotherapeutics like cisplatin or tyrosine kinase inhibitors), organoids derived from the patient's own cells can be exposed to the drug. The subsequent analysis of drug and metabolite accumulation, cellular toxicity pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, apoptosis), and functional impairment provides direct, actionable data [112]. This “avatar-guided” approach can inform clinicians on whether to proceed with a standard regimen, adjust the dose, or select an alternative agent, thereby personalizing therapy and mitigating the risk of drug-induced organ injury.

Pregnant individuals: Assessing fetal exposure and placental toxicology

Pregnant individuals represent another special population with a distinct, dynamic, and dual-patient (mother and fetus) PK environment. Predicting drug and metabolite transfer across the placenta is essential for managing maternal conditions while minimizing fetal risk. Placental organoids, derived from first-trimester or term villi, have emerged as a groundbreaking model. These 3D structures self-organize into syncytiotrophoblast- and cytotrophoblast-containing units that express a full complement of ABC efflux transporters (e.g., P-gp, BCRP) and metabolic enzymes at physiologically relevant levels and localization. They enable functional studies of transporter-mediated efflux, which serves as a protective barrier for the fetus. By using these organoids, researchers can quantitatively assess the placental permeability of therapeutic agents (e.g., antivirals, antidepressants) and environmental toxins, greatly improving the prediction of fetal exposure compared to static cell lines or animal models with divergent placental biology.

Challenges and technological advances

The past decade has witnessed remarkable progress in the development and application of organoid models across biomedical research. Concurrently, standardized resources and validation frameworks have emerged to systematically characterize the structural and functional complexity of these systems. As organoid technology continues to evolve toward greater physiological relevance and functional sophistication, it remains essential to proactively address the accompanying ethical considerations and safety implications.

Main challenges currently facing organoid technology

While organoid technology demonstrates significant potential across biomedical applications, several substantial limitations impede its full implementation. A primary constraint involves inadequate vascularization, which frequently induces central necrosis in larger structures and limits long-term culture maintenance [113]. Functional immaturity presents another major challenge, as evidenced by subphysiological enzymatic activity levels (hepatic organoids typically achieve only 60%–80% of native CYP3A4 activity) and incomplete cellular differentiation. Hepatic and renal organoids often fail to achieve full maturation primarily due to the absence of critical in vivo microenvironmental cues and systemic physiological signals. For liver organoids, this deficit includes a lack of physiological oxygen gradients, hormonal cycles, and the complete paracrine signaling network normally supplied by non-parenchymal cells such as Kupffer cells and stellate cells. This results in insufficient expression of key metabolic enzymes like CYP3A4. For kidney organoids, incomplete vascularization and the absence of hemodynamic pressure hinder the proper development of polarized tubular transport systems and the glomerular filtration barrier. At the molecular level, this stagnation often correlates with the insufficient activation of key signaling pathways, including those involving hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α) and WNT/β-catenin, which are crucial for driving terminal differentiation and functional maturation in native tissues [114].

Standardization remains problematic due to substantial batch-to-batch variability and reliance on biologically-derived matrices. Scaling production encounters economic and technical barriers, while regulatory frameworks lack specific guidelines for organoid quality control. Additional concerns encompass ethical considerations regarding genetic manipulation and neural organoid development [115]. Finally, integrating multiple organ systems faces technical obstacles including insufficient physiological connectivity and limited capacity for monitoring inter-organ interactions.

Technological advances

To address the aforementioned challenges and enhance the predictive power of organoids in ADME research, several cutting-edge technologies are being integrated into advanced in vitro platforms, collectively known as MPS [116]. MPS are bioengineered platforms designed to recapitulate functional units of human organs and their interactions under dynamic, physiologically relevant conditions. The most prominent implementations of MPS include OoC platforms, complex co-culture systems, and vascularized organoid models [117]. The following sections highlight how these specific MPS technologies tackle existing limitations, advancing organoids toward more predictive PK models.

Organ-on-a-chip

Organ-on-a-chip technology represents a bioengineered platform that integrates microfluidic systems with three-dimensional cellular models to create physiologically relevant organ mimics [118]. By combining organoids with precisely controlled microenvironments, these platforms enable the reconstruction of human tissue structures and functions with unprecedented fidelity. The technology's capacity to simulate mechanical cues, fluid shear stress, and multicellular interactions addresses fundamental limitations of conventional static cultures. This convergence of engineering and biology offers transformative opportunities for investigating organ development, disease mechanisms, and drug responses, positioning OoC systems as essential tools in pharmaceutical research and development. Several innovative OoC platforms demonstrate the technology's advancing capabilities in modeling organ-level functions. The 3D BBB organoid model has been successfully utilized to evaluate the therapeutic effects of “Guanxinning Injection”, a traditional Chinese medicine formulation [119]. This approach aided in deciphering its comprehensive protective profile, which includes improving barrier permeability, preserving tight junction integrity, restoring P-gp efflux function, and mitigating oxidative stress. These findings demonstrate that the 3D organoid model can accurately simulate the complex in vivo physiological and pathological milieu. It thus provides a powerful in vitro tool for holistically assessing a drug's protective efficacy on the BBB, elucidating its multi-target mechanisms, and identifying active constituents. This model is particularly instrumental for drug discovery involving complex systems like traditional Chinese medicine formulas. A personalized vascularized tumor OoC platform has been developed, which creates a hierarchically structured, tumor-specific microvascular network to provide a highly biomimetic system for drug screening and efficacy evaluation, as shown in Fig. 7A [120]. This chip successfully mimics the patient-specific vascular tumor microenvironment, enabling real-time observation of tumor-vascular interactions and the assessment of anti-angiogenic drugs like apatinib, a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) inhibitor. The platform revealed that such treatment effectively suppressed tumor-induced neovascularization while exhibiting minimal impact on mature vessels, faithfully recapitulating the clinical response. These platforms collectively demonstrate how engineered microenvironments can restore and maintain tissue-specific functions that are typically lost in conventional culture systems. The integration of organoid technology with OoC platforms marks a significant milestone in predictive drug development. These systems overcome critical limitations of traditional models by providing dynamic control over microenvironmental parameters, thereby enhancing the expression and activity of metabolic enzymes and transporters. While challenges remain in standardization, cost reduction, and regulatory acceptance, continuous technological innovations are steadily addressing these barriers. The ability of OoC systems to simulate human physiology with increasing accuracy supports their growing implementation in drug metabolism and PK studies [121]. Co-culture systems

Co-culture systems represent a significant advancement in organoid technology by incorporating multiple cell types including immune cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts into 3D microenvironments [122]. This approach substantially enhances physiological relevance by reconstructing crucial cellular interactions that occur in native tissues. The integration of diverse cell populations enables more accurate modeling of inter-organ communication, disease-specific microenvironments, and comprehensive drug response mechanisms. By bridging the gap between simplified monocultures and complex in vivo conditions, co-culture systems have established themselves as indispensable platforms for studying systemic physiological and pathological processes. The application of multi-organoid co-culture platforms has yielded particularly valuable insights into metabolic cross-talk between tissues. Tao et al. developed an innovative microfluidic system that maintains functional human liver and islet organoids in continuous communication, effectively replicating the liver-islet axis under both physiological and pathological conditions, as shown in Fig. 7B [123]. Transcriptomic analysis of islet organoids within this co-culture system revealed significant upregulation of metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and protein digestion, demonstrating successful recapitulation of inter-organ signaling. When exposed to hyperglycemic conditions, this platform effectively modeled metabolic dysfunction, and subsequent metformin treatment partially restored mitochondrial function and glucose transport activity. This system not only provides a human-relevant model for studying type 2 diabetes pathophysiology but also serves as a valuable platform for evaluating novel therapeutic compounds. Similar approaches have been applied to study gut-liver axis interactions [124], neurovascular unit function [125], and tumor-immune microenvironment dynamics [126].

Co-culture systems substantially expand the experimental capabilities of organoid technology by introducing critical physiological components such as vascular networks, immune modulation, and stromal support [127]. While these systems offer unprecedented opportunities for modeling human physiology and disease, they also present technical challenges related to maintaining cellular ratio stability, ensuring long-term culture viability, and achieving reproducible system assembly. Future developments should focus on standardizing co-culture protocols, establishing quality control metrics for multi-cellular systems, and integrating real-time monitoring capabilities. As these technical hurdles are addressed, co-culture platforms are poised to become increasingly valuable for pharmaceutical research, particularly in the realms of immunotherapy assessment, metabolic disease modeling, and complex toxicity evaluation, ultimately enhancing the predictive power of in vitro systems for clinical translation.

Vascularization

The absence of functional vascular networks represents a fundamental limitation in conventional organoid models, restricting their physiological relevance and experimental utility [128]. Vascularization is crucial not only for adequate oxygen and nutrient delivery but also for waste removal, enabling organoids to develop beyond the oxygen diffusion limit and achieve enhanced structural and functional maturity. The incorporation of vascular components more accurately recapitulates the in vivo microenvironment where drug distribution occurs primarily through blood perfusion rather than simple diffusion [129]. This advancement is particularly vital for modeling systemic drug responses and tissue-specific targeting, addressing a critical gap in current organoid technology. Multiple innovative strategies have emerged to address the vascularization challenge. A study developed a visualized vascular organoid model based on a dual-reporter human pluripotent stem cell line [130]. By coupling platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) expression with secreted luciferase activity, the researchers established a high-throughput drug screening platform that enables 3D morphological observation and quantitative bioluminescence detection in the supernatant. Using this platform, the study not only validated the anti-angiogenic effects of VEGFR inhibitors but also identified novel modulators targeting multiple pathways such as TGF-β, Wnt, and Yes-associated protein (YAP). Lead compounds including Repsox (pro-angiogenic) and c-Met inhibitors (anti-angiogenic) were successfully recognized, providing an efficient and quantitative screening tool for the development of angiogenesis-related drugs. The vascularized tumor OoC model, developed in a recent study, has showcased significant value for drug development. This platform not only enabled the specific assessment of anti-angiogenic drugs like the VEGFR2 inhibitor apatinib but also uncovered, via integrated transcriptomic analysis, the critical role of the Notch signaling pathway in mediating tumor migration. This model thus serves as a powerful preclinical tool for screening anti-angiogenic compounds, predicting individual patient responses, and investigating metastasis mechanisms. Genetic engineering approaches have also shown promise, with Velazquez et al. identifying PROX1 overexpression as a key regulator of angiogenic factor expression in liver organoids, enabling the formation of functional human-mouse vascular connections upon implantation, as shown in Fig. 7C [131]. These diverse strategies collectively demonstrate the feasibility of creating vascularized organoids through complementary biological and engineering approaches.

The development of vascularized organoid systems marks substantial progress toward physiologically relevant in vitro models. These advanced platforms demonstrate improved culture viability, enhanced functional maturation, and more accurate simulation of drug distribution patterns compared to their non-vascularized counterparts [132]. However, challenges remain in achieving complete vascular network integration, establishing physiological flow conditions, and maintaining long-term stability. Future research directions should focus on optimizing the spatial organization of vascular networks, developing biomimetic perfusion systems, and creating standardized protocols for vascularized organoid generation across different tissue types [133]. As these technical hurdles are overcome, vascularized organoids will increasingly bridge the gap between conventional in vitro systems and in vivo conditions, ultimately enhancing their predictive value in drug development and disease modeling applications.

Integration with AI

The integration of artificial intelligence with organoid technology is transforming the field from an experience-driven research paradigm into a data- and algorithm-driven precision science. AI serves not only as a powerful analytical tool but also, increasingly, as a central driving force for enhancing PK predictive accuracy, optimizing model construction, and elucidating underlying biological mechanisms [134].

Core AI algorithmic strategies for enhancing ADME prediction

The deep integration of artificial intelligence and organoid technology is fundamentally reshaping the paradigm of drug ADME research. The core of this transformation lies in a series of advanced computational strategies designed to address the inherent challenge of data scarcity in organoid models, while significantly enhancing predictive reliability and extrapolation capability. Currently, this field primarily adopts two core AI strategies: first, hybrid modeling, which dynamically couples data-driven deep learning models (for predicting PK parameters) with mechanism-driven PBPK models. This approach rigorously constrains the prediction process within established biophysical frameworks, thereby greatly improving model reliability and generalizability in scenarios with limited samples or when extrapolating [135]. Second, multi-task learning and transfer learning; the former efficiently uncovers common patterns from limited data by sharing an underlying network architecture to simultaneously predict multiple related ADME attributes [136], while the latter enables the rapid construction of high-performance specialized models by first pre-training on large, general biological datasets and then fine-tuning on smaller, organoid-specific datasets. These methodological advances have catalyzed a fundamental shift in the role of AI in ADME research, from an “analytical tool” to a “predictive engine.” For instance, the recently proposed “AI virtual organoid” framework creates a digital twin of an organoid by synergistically integrating mechanism-based models grounded in biophysical principles with deep learning algorithms trained on multi-omics data. This system can quantitatively predict drug metabolic flux and enzyme kinetics within a simulated tissue microenvironment, thereby enabling reliable estimation of key parameters like hepatic clearance under data-sparse experimental conditions. Furthermore, the deep integration of AI with classical mechanistic PK models constitutes another critical dimension of its predictive power. Taking the hybrid modeling strategy as an example, AI models (such as deep neural networks) trained on organoid data can first accurately predict functional parameters like intestinal drug permeability or intrinsic metabolic clearance. These parameters, possessing high human relevance, are then dynamically input into PBPK models. Consequently, functional validation data derived from human organoids, including active transport and metabolism data obtained from intestinal organoid monolayers, can directly drive PBPK models to simulate the complex disposition of drugs throughout the body, such as oral bioavailability and tissue distribution kinetics. This approach transcends the traditional reliance on animal data or static in vitro experiments, allowing for a more accurate simulation of non-linear first-pass effects and prediction of in vivo exposure levels.

Practical AI empowerment in key ADME research areas

AI has permeated multiple aspects of organoid-based ADME research, enabling a systematic transformation from descriptive phenotyping to predictive mechanistic analysis.

In model construction and optimization, reinforcement learning or Bayesian optimization algorithms can intelligently explore the high-dimensional parameter space of culture conditions and biomaterial compositions [137]. In a goal-oriented manner, they autonomously search for protocols that maximize the drug disposition functions of organoids, thereby promoting standardization and functional enhancement.

For phenotypic analysis and quality control, computer vision systems based on convolutional neural networks enable automated, high-content image analysis. These systems classify organoid morphology, detect developmental abnormalities, and perform quantitative assessment of structural features (e.g., crypt-villus architecture in intestinal organoids) with human-level accuracy, as shown in Fig. 7D [138], which is crucial for ensuring batch-to-batch reproducibility. Crucially, for toxicity assessment, such imaging analysis systems enable the dynamic, quantitative identification and risk stratification of early subcellular toxicological features in organoids, providing more sensitive alerts than traditional methods.

In functional prediction and mechanistic insight, a study developed a deep learning model that enables automated, label-free classification of drug-induced liver injury levels [139]. The model extracts morphodynamic signatures associated with cellular health and accurately maps them to clinically relevant toxicity risks. This approach allows for the high-throughput and cost-effective early-stage screening of compound hepatotoxicity. The proposed Artificial Intelligence Virtual Organoid framework introduces an innovative paradigm for predicting drug metabolism and permeability. It creates a digital twin of organoids by integrating mechanism-based models founded on biophysical principles with artificial intelligence algorithms trained on multi-omics data. For drug metabolism, this system can simulate metabolic network flux and enzymatic reaction kinetics of compounds within virtual tissues. Regarding permeability, it calculates the processes of drug diffusion and transport across complex biological barriers. This approach enables the high-throughput, cost-effective virtual screening of key ADME properties for drug candidates and facilitates the exploration of underlying mechanisms, thereby significantly enhancing the predictive power of preclinical research [140]. Graph neural networks, by integrating multi-omics data (transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic), can not only predict endpoints like metabolic stability and toxicity but also infer key biological pathways by correlating gene expression profiles with functional outputs [141]. This enables a capability leap from phenotypic description to mechanistic prediction and forecasting of individualized metabolic phenotypes.

Challenges and future perspectives towards intelligent organoid platforms

Despite these promising prospects, the application of AI in this field is confronted with significant challenges that must be addressed to realize its full potential. The foremost bottleneck is data sparsity. Generating high-quality, well-characterized functional data from organoids, such as accurate kinetic parameters for specific CYP enzymes or transporter-mediated efflux ratios, is resource-intensive and inherently low-throughput. This results in typically small “gold standard” datasets available for training robust AI models, which readily leads to overfitting. In such cases, a model may perform excellently on its training data but its predictive performance deteriorates sharply when generalizing to new chemical entities or different batches of organoids. Closely related is the issue of data bias. Existing organoid studies are often concentrated on specific classes of drugs (e.g., a limited set of CYP substrates) or particular culture conditions. AI models trained on such skewed datasets may fail to accurately predict the ADME properties of structurally novel compounds or those measured under different experimental paradigms, thereby limiting their broader applicability. Furthermore, the insufficient interpretability of many advanced AI models, particularly deep neural networks, poses an additional obstacle. These models are frequently regarded as “black boxes”, which can limit researchers' trust in the predictions and hinder the extraction of biologically meaningful insights into the underlying mechanisms.

The convergence of organoid technology with artificial intelligence represents a transformative approach to addressing key challenges in model development and data interpretation. This synergy holds substantial promise for advancing pharmaceutical research and personalized medicine. Current integrations demonstrate significant improvements in standardization, predictive modeling, and high-throughput screening capabilities. However, to fully realize this potential, several critical challenges require resolution. These include the need for standardized data formats to enable algorithm training and comparison, improved interpretability of complex AI models to foster trust and biological discovery, and more accessible computational infrastructure. Future developments should therefore focus on creating validated, purpose-built AI tools for organoid analysis, establishing open, shared databases for model training and benchmarking to broaden adoption across the research community. As these computational methods mature, AI-enhanced organoid platforms are expected to become indispensable tools for drug discovery and clinical decision-making, ultimately enabling more accurate prediction of human drug responses and advancing precision medicine initiatives.

Standardization and safety considerations

Standardization and ethical oversight are critical components for the responsible development and application of organoid technology. Establishing uniform protocols across all stages including tissue sourcing, 3D culture, passaging, cryopreservation, and quality control is essential to ensure reproducibility and reliability of organoid models. Concurrently, comprehensive ethical frameworks must govern the use of human-derived materials, particularly regarding donor consent and appropriate oversight mechanisms [142]. These parallel efforts in standardization and ethics form the foundation for translating organoid technology from research laboratories to industrial and clinical settings.

Building upon this foundation, significant progress has been made in developing standardized approaches for organoid culture and characterization. Protocols now encompass the complete workflow from initial tissue acquisition through final molecular characterization, incorporating rigorous quality control measures at each stage. Currently, a preliminary operational consensus has been established across three core dimensions of organoid standardization: the clear definition and quality control of starting cell materials (such as specific stem cells or biopsy samples); the promotion of standardized core culture protocols, including the use of chemically defined synthetic hydrogels and the sharing of standardized medium formulations; and the establishment of a multi-tiered quality assessment system [143]. This system encompasses morphological uniformity, quantitative analysis of specific cell lineages, and critical functional benchmarks (e.g., baseline CYP450 enzyme activity in hepatic organoids and transepithelial electrical resistance in intestinal organoids). The next phase of standardization work must focus on three key frontiers. The primary task is to develop and extensively validate fully defined, xeno-free culture systems to fundamentally address the “black box” problem [144]. Subsequently, there is a need to construct and openly share organoid molecular and functional reference atlases based on single-cell multi-omics technologies, thereby providing an objective “gold standard” for quality assessment [145]. Finally, it is imperative to actively promote the harmonization and development of international ethical and regulatory science frameworks [146].

To achieve objective assessment and standardization of functional maturity in organoid models, it is imperative to establish a benchmark system encompassing multiple dimensions and quantifiable metrics. At the metabolic level, thresholds for CYP450 enzyme activities must be defined [147]. For instance, the activities of key isoforms such as CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 in liver organoids should be systematically benchmarked against reference values from primary human hepatocytes. It is recommended that an acceptable benchmark be set as achieving 60%–80% of the mean activity of key CYP enzymes observed in primary human hepatocytes under optimized culture conditions, with stability maintained over a defined culture period (e.g., 14–21 days). Regarding transporter function, quantitative assessment of the apical membrane localization of efflux transporters such as P-gp and BCRP should be performed using immunofluorescence combined with confocal microscopy. Functional uptake/efflux assays using standard substrates such as digoxin and fluorescein-methotrexate should be conducted, with benchmarks defined by efflux ratios consistent with human tissue data and reversible upon treatment with specific inhibitors. Structurally, morphometric parameters such as villus height-to-crypt depth ratio in intestinal organoids [148] and glomerular diameter and lumen formation in kidney organoids [149] must be quantified and compared with human histological reference data. Moreover, transcriptomic and proteomic profiles of organoids should be systematically compared with human tissue reference databases, such as those from the Genotype-Tissue Expression project, to comprehensively assess the model's resemblance to the adult human phenotype.

Concurrently, the ethical dimension has similarly evolved, with requirements for ethics committee approval and detailed informed consent that explicitly addresses sample preservation, research use, drug testing, and potential commercial applications [150]. The recent U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Modernization Act provides regulatory impetus for adopting organoid models that align with 3R principles, though important limitations remain. Particularly for cerebral organoids, ongoing ethical concerns regarding potential consciousness emergence have prompted collaborative frameworks between neuroscientists and bioethicists to establish responsible research guidelines [151].

Currently, major regulatory agencies such as the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have not yet established dedicated approval pathways specifically for organoid-based ADME models. The core principle underlying their regulatory acceptance follows the “application-specific scientific validation” approach. This means that the evaluation of such models does not depend on their attributes as technological platforms, but rather on the evidence submitted for a specific application context (such as metabolic prediction or toxicity assessment), demonstrating a reliable and robust correlation between the model's predictions and human clinical data. This concept aligns closely with the “fit-for-purpose” principle applied to traditional in vitro models. Specifically, to utilize organoid data in support of an Investigational New Drug (IND) application, validation must strictly adhere to this principle, providing a complete chain of evidence tailored to the specific application: demonstrating model reproducibility and control of batch-to-batch variability through Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs); establishing functional physiological relevance by confirming the expression and activity of key enzymes and transporters; and substantiating predictive accuracy through strong correlations with known clinical data. Notably, in 2025, the US FDA achieved a landmark approval of the first IND solely based on patient-derived organoid drug efficacy data, establishing a critical practical benchmark for the adequacy of organoid data validation. Despite the absence of specific guidelines, organoid models are progressively being integrated into international regulatory harmonization frameworks. Examples include encouragement from the US FDA Modernization Act, efforts by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) towards standardizing liver and kidney organoids for specific toxicity endpoints, and the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) M12 guideline providing an international pathway for harmonizing data from DDI studies submitted for adequately validated models. In summary, from a regulatory perspective, ADME data derived from organoids are currently regarded as valuable complements to, rather than replacements for, traditional animal studies in IND-enabling research. The central tenet for their regulatory acceptance is the principle of “context-specific scientific validation”, which requires applicants to construct a comprehensive chain of evidence encompassing reproducibility, physiological relevance, and predictive accuracy for the specific scientific question under investigation. The US FDA's groundbreaking 2025 approval, coupled with the development of standards and guideline harmonization by international bodies like the OECD and ICH, not only confirms the trend of integrating organoid models into the regulatory science mainstream but also clarifies for researchers in this field the optimization directions and multidimensional quality control objectives for functionally defined models [152].

The maturation of organoid technology necessitates continued attention to both standardization and ethical considerations. While substantial advances have been achieved in protocol development and ethical governance, challenges persist in achieving complete standardization across different organoid types and addressing the unique ethical questions posed by certain models. Future efforts should focus on establishing international standards for organoid quality assessment, developing consensus guidelines for ethical review processes, and creating transparent mechanisms for public engagement [153]. As the field progresses, maintaining this dual focus on technical standardization and ethical responsibility will be crucial for realizing the full potential of organoid technology while maintaining public trust and scientific integrity. Cost-benefit analysis: comparison of organoids with existing models

Organoid models occupy a distinctive cost-benefit niche in the selection of drug discovery tools. They are positioned between 2D cell models, which offer high throughput but limited physiological relevance, and animal models, which provide intact systemic physiology but are associated with high costs, low throughput, and significant species-specific discrepancies. A systematic comparison across key dimensions is as follows.

Cost analysis

The cost structure of organoid models assumes a unique intermediate position within the spectrum of preclinical tools. The per-experiment material and culture costs are significantly higher than those of highly standardized, low-consumable 2D cell models, primarily due to expenses for specialized extracellular matrices, complex growth factor cocktails, and longer culture periods. However, when evaluated from a project-wide or long-term perspective, the comprehensive cost of organoids remains substantially lower than that of traditional animal models [154]. Animal experiments entail not only high expenses for animal procurement, specialized facility maintenance, and long-term husbandry but also considerable implicit costs associated with ethical review processes, regulatory compliance, and specialized personnel. Consequently, organoids enable access to 3D human tissue complexity while representing a relatively economical investment.

Operational complexity and throughput

Operationally, organoid models present a specific balance between complexity and throughput. 2D cell cultures, with their highly standardized and easily automatable protocols, possess unparalleled ultra-high-throughput screening capability [155]. In contrast, organoid culture is more demanding, requiring maintenance of 3D architecture, timed supplementation of factors, and control of batch-to-batch variability, which inherently challenges its throughput [156]. Nevertheless, through protocol optimization and the integration of automated analysis platforms, organoids can achieve a medium-throughput level that far exceeds the capacity of animal experiments [157]. Animal models, constrained by breeding cycles, individual variability, and low parallel processing capability, offer very limited throughput [158]. Thus, organoids substantially surpass animal models in throughput while preserving the physiological complexity absent in 2D systems.

Predictive value and translational potential

The core value of organoid models lies in their superior predictive accuracy and translational potential, which directly defines their cost-effectiveness. 2D cell models, due to their lack of tissue architecture and cell-cell interactions, suffer from a high failure rate in translating discoveries to the clinic. Although animal models provide systemic pharmacological data, interspecies differences frequently lead to inaccurate predictions of human efficacy and toxicity. The advantage of organoids is that they offer a platform that closely mimics human organ physiology and pathology, at a fraction of the cost and with higher throughput than animal studies. They are particularly suited for investigating human-specific disease mechanisms, assessing interpatient heterogeneity, and elucidating the true distribution and metabolic behavior of drugs within complex tissue microenvironments. This positions organoids as a more human-relevant decision-making tool at the critical preclinical translation stage [159].

Conclusion

Organoid technology has fundamentally transformed PK research by providing physiologically relevant human models that bridge critical gaps between conventional preclinical systems and clinical outcomes. These 3D structures recapitulate essential aspects of human organ physiology, enabling more accurate prediction of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion processes. The demonstrated superiority of intestinal organoids in modeling oral bioavailability, hepatic organoids in maintaining metabolic competence, and renal models in simulating excretion pathways collectively establishes organoids as indispensable tools for mechanistic ADME investigation. Furthermore, PDO offer unprecedented opportunities for understanding interindividual variability and advancing personalized medicine approaches. Despite these significant advances, challenges persist in achieving full functional maturation, standardizing culture protocols, and incorporating vascular and immune components. The absence of standardized quality control metrics and regulatory frameworks also presents hurdles to widespread adoption. However, emerging technologies are progressively addressing these limitations. Advanced microphysiological systems, including OoC platforms, introduce physiological flow conditions and multi-organ interactions, while co-culture strategies enhance cellular complexity. AI applications accelerate quality control and data analysis, and vascularization techniques improve nutrient delivery and structural organization. Future development should focus on establishing integrated multi-organ platforms that simulate systemic drug disposition, creating comprehensive biobanks representing diverse genetic backgrounds, and expanding applications to novel therapeutic modalities. The convergence of these technologies will further enhance the predictive power of organoid systems, ultimately reducing reliance on animal models and improving clinical translation success. As standardization improves and ethical frameworks evolve, organoid technology is poised to become a cornerstone of pharmaceutical development, enabling more efficient drug screening and personalized treatment strategies that maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.
