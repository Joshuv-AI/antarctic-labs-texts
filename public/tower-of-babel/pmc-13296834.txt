Advising on Antidepressant Use in Pregnancy: Are We All on the Same Page?
Authors: Lucy Charlotte Broughton, Natalie Hughes, Jiaxu Zeng, Alesha Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposePerinatal mood disorders are associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes and affect 15%–20% of pregnant people. Guidelines recommend SSRIs when non‐pharmacological therapies are ineffective, yet antenatal depression remains undertreated. This study aimed to assess the attitudes, knowledge, and practices of general practitioners (GPs) and midwives on SSRI use in pregnancy.MethodsA knowledge, attitudes, and practice survey was developed in QualtricsXM and distributed to New Zealand‐registered GPs and midwives between May 2023 and October 2024 using convenience sampling.ResultsResponses from 119 GPs and 62 midwives were analyzed. Discomfort managing antenatal depression was reported by 11% of GPs and 36% of midwives. Knowledge of SSRI risks in pregnancy varied among respondents. Average concern regarding the use of SSRIs in pregnancy and the risk of adverse outcomes was low, but there was considerable variation. In the case vignette, recommended actions differed: 53% advised maintaining treatment, 13% suggested dose reduction, and 24% recommended switching antidepressants. Patient counseling was limited, and appropriate written patient information was rarely provided.ConclusionsThis study highlights variability in knowledge, attitudes, and practices among GP's and midwives regarding SSRIs during pregnancy. Further training, improved access to patient information, and decision aids could enhance patient‐centered care and ensure pregnant people receive balanced, evidence‐based guidance on SSRI use.

Introduction

Perinatal mood disorders affect 15%–20% of pregnant people and are associated with adverse outcomes including spontaneous abortion (SA), preterm birth, low birthweight (LBW), and neurodevelopmental disorders (Jahan et al. 2021).

Prescribing antidepressants during pregnancy requires collaborative decision‐making, balancing risks of untreated depression against risks of fetal medication exposure (RANZCOG 2018). Meta‐analyses suggest SSRIs are relatively safe (Desaunay et al. 2023) but are associated with neonatal adaptation syndrome (NAS) (Grigoriadis et al. 2013) and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) (Skalkidou et al. 2020), with conflicting evidence for SA, preterm birth, LBW, malformations, and neurodevelopment (Fabiano et al. 2025; Uguz 2020).

Given the risks of antenatal depression, guidelines recommend SSRIs when non‐pharmacological options are unsuitable. They also advise continuing pre‐existing antidepressants and discourage switching or routine discontinuation without discussion of risks and benefits (RANZCOG 2018).

Despite these guidelines, a recent study found 12% of pregnant people had untreated depression (Svardal et al. 2021). Additionally, two‐thirds of pregnant people on pre‐existing treatment discontinue treatment (Broughton et al. 2023), potentially causing withdrawal reactions and increasing relapse risk (Cohen et al. 2006). In one study, 70% of those who stopped medication experienced adverse effects including suicidal ideation (Einarson et al. 2001).

Understanding the views of healthcare practitioners (HCP) regarding antidepressants and pregnancy is important because HCP opinions strongly influence patients' decision‐making (Mulder et al. 2012). International evidence shows HCPs give inconsistent advice on antidepressant use in pregnancy, often overestimating teratogenic risk (Eakley and Lyndon 2022). In New Zealand (NZ), midwives are primary maternity care providers, with general practitioners (GPs) providing support for concurrent medical conditions; access to specialized perinatal mental health services is limited. To our knowledge, no studies have explored midwives' perspectives, despite their key role in pregnancy care, or examined the views of GPs within NZ.

The objectives of this study were to explore knowledge and attitudes regarding SSRI use and untreated depression during pregnancy, and clinical practices from NZ‐registered GPs and midwives.

Methods

Reporting adheres to CHERRIES guidelines (Eysenbach 2004) (Supporting Information Appendix A).

Study Design and Participants

A cross‐sectional online survey was conducted using QualtricsXM. Eligible participants were practicing midwives registered with the NZ Midwifery Council and GPs registered with the NZ Medical Council. Secondary‐care specialists were excluded as were those not directly involved in patient care. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Otago Ethics Committee (Health) H23/032.

Survey Instrument

The survey was developed following guidance on designing knowledge, attitudes, and practice surveys in psychiatry (Andrade et al. 2020). Content was adapted from previous surveys (Bilszta et al. 2011; Taouk et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2020), tailoring items to NZ context. Content validity was checked through piloting with three GPs and two midwives. The survey consisted of mostly multiple‐choice questions, although a 0–10 sliding scale (0 being least concerned, 10 being most concerned) was used to assess concerns. Recruitment

Convenience sampling was used. The Royal NZ College of General Practitioners (RNZCGP) has 5728 registered GPs. They included the survey link in two e‐mail newsletters (RNZCGP 2024). The survey link was also posted on a NZ GP social media page. Additionally, primary health organizations (PHOs) were emailed via their website and invited to distribute the link. Canterbury PHO agreed to distribute the link directly to all regional members.

NZ has 3298 practicing midwives registered with the NZ college of midwives (Midwifery Council 2023). The survey link was promoted on two NZ midwifery social media pages, and birthing units throughout NZ were emailed via their websites and invited to share the link with their midwifery staff.

The survey ran between October 2023 and May 2024. All participants were invited to enter a draw to win 1 of 4 $50 gift vouchers.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were: (1) knowledge of risks of SSRI use and untreated depression; (2) concern ratings regarding adverse outcomes; and (3) treatment recommendations in the vignette. Secondary outcomes explored associations between evidence interpretation and concern ratings.

Analysis

Responses were included if participants met inclusion criteria and completed at least one survey item beyond consent. Partially complete questionnaires were analyzed on a per‐question basis, using all available data.

Knowledge of risks of SSRI use and untreated depression was assessed using predefined response options (associated, not associated, conflicting, no evidence, don't know). The distribution of all response options was reported as proportions. “Correct” interpretations were defined a priori based on current peer reviewed literature (Table 2). The proportion of respondents selecting the correct interpretation for each outcome was also reported.

Results

Of 203 who consented, 181 were eligible (119 GPs, 62 midwives).

Demographics

GPs were predominantly female and European/Pākehā, with respondents spread across NZ. Midwives were all female, predominantly European/Pākehā, and nearly half were from Canterbury. Participants represented a range of practice durations (Table 3).

Untreated Antenatal Depression

Most participants recognized associations between untreated antenatal depression and adverse outcomes, although 21% of GPs and 29% of midwives were unsure of associations with SA, preterm birth, or LBW; 29% of midwives were unsure of associations with antenatal depression and neurodevelopmental problems (Figure 1a).

SSRI Exposure During Pregnancy

Interpretation of SSRI risks varied (Figure 1b):

40% of GPs and 33% of midwives recognized that evidence was conflicting regarding SSRI exposure during pregnancy and the risk of congenital malformations (approximately a quarter of GPs thought they were associated); less than 20% of both professions recognized evidence was conflicting for SSRI exposure and the risk of adverse effects on neurodevelopment. Less than 10% of GPs and midwives recognized SSRI associations with PPH; approximately 50% knew of NAS.

SSRI Efficacy

Nearly all respondents (98%) endorsed SSRI efficacy for moderately severe depression.

Attitudes

Concerns

Average concerns regarding untreated antenatal depression were moderate‐high, particularly regarding risk of postnatal depression, but varied widely among both professions (Figure 2a). Average concerns regarding SSRI exposure during pregnancy were low, except for midwives' moderate concern about NAS, but again with wide variation overall (Figure 2b).

Respondents who viewed evidence as “not associated”; “conflicting,” or “don't know” generally expressed significantly lower concerns about SSRI exposure in pregnancy than those endorsing associations for most adverse outcomes (see Table S1).

Comfort Managing Conditions in Pregnancy

Among GPs (n = 116), 11% reported discomfort in managing depression during pregnancy compared with 4% for asthma, and 31% for diabetes; among midwives (n = 62), discomfort was 36% for depression, 18% for asthma, and 8% for diabetes.

Practices

Case Vignette

In the case vignette, all GPs and most midwives (84%) stated they would provide counseling on the risks and benefits of fluoxetine; 16% of midwives indicated they would be unable to provide information.

In terms of giving a recommendation on whether to continue with fluoxetine, almost all GPs (94%) and a minority of midwives (10%) would give their recommendation, the remainder would refer. Of HCPs providing recommendations, 53% recommended maintaining current therapy, 13% suggested dose reduction, and 26% advised switching antidepressants (Figure 3).

Responses to the case vignette. GP, general practitioner.

Prescribing and Avoidance

All GPs reported they would prescribe antidepressants during pregnancy for patients with suicidal ideation or nonresponse to non‐pharmacological therapies. Other reasons included functional impairment (99%); pre‐existing treatment (93%); inaccessibility of non‐pharmacological therapy (90%); patient request (84%); and prior history of depression (75%).

Reasons for avoiding antidepressants included patient refusal (96%), mild depression (89%), preference for non‐pharmacological therapy (85%), safety concerns (35%), and neurodevelopmental concerns (8%).

SSRI Preferences for First‐Episode Antenatal Depression

A total of 105 GPs who answered this question. Sertraline was preferred (65%) followed by escitalopram (10.5% each), fluoxetine (9.5%), paroxetine (1%), “other” (1.9%), and avoid all antidepressants (1.9%). Sertraline was favored for familiarity, safety, and breastfeeding suitability.

All GPs endorsed providing counseling on SSRI safety in pregnancy; 80% claiming the discussion took less than 10 min. Results were disparate for midwives; 29% of midwives did not provide counseling, 37% spent over 10 min counseling the patient, and 35% spent less than 10 min.

In addition to verbal counseling, respondents were asked about providing written information on SSRI safety. Among GPs, 19% provided none and 60% relied on potentially unsuitable sources (e.g., leaflets with little pregnancy‐specific advice, or clinician‐oriented materials); 22% used pregnancy‐specific patient leaflets. Among midwives, 39% provided no written information and 8% used pregnancy‐specific leaflets (see Table S2).

Discussion

Knowledge and Attitudes

Previous literature suggests that HCPs may underestimate the risks of untreated antenatal depression (Eakley and Lyndon 2022). Our study found that most GPs and midwives were aware of the risks and expressed moderate to high concern about possible adverse outcomes. However, we did identify that there could be greater awareness regarding the risks of antenatal depression on SA, preterm birth, or LBW (almost one‐third of providers unaware), and the risks to neurodevelopment (29% midwives unaware) (Figure 1a).

There was disparity in evidence interpretation about SSRI risks in pregnancy. Few GPs and midwives were aware that evidence on SA, preterm birth, or LBW, and congenital malformations is conflicting. Specifically, 14.4% of midwives and 10.9% of GPs believed SSRIs were associated with SA, preterm birth or LBW, and 27% of GPs believed SSRIs were associated with congenital malformations (Figure 1b). These views likely stem from studies that did not control confounding factors. A meta‐analysis that controlled for confounding by indication did not find these associations (Gao et al. 2018).

Conversely, awareness of established SSRI risks was low. Less than 20% of GPs and midwives were unsure or unaware of the link between SSRIs and NAS, which affects about 30% of infants exposed in the third trimester (Grigoriadis et al. 2013). Although symptoms are often mild and self‐limiting, awareness is key to ensuring maternal and newborn well‐being. Educating parents about NAS may reduce anxiety, prepare them for postdelivery expectations, and support timely intervention.

Despite warnings from regulatory authorities (Medsafe 2021) PPH was another under‐recognized SSRI‐related risk. Although the absolute risk is small (one excess case of PPH for every 80–100 pregnancies), awareness is important for optimizing labor management and ensuring timely access to uterotonic agents if needed.

Average concern ratings regarding the risk of SSRI exposure during pregnancy and adverse outcomes were low; however, the wide variation demonstrates disparate attitudes/opinions among both professions.

The variation in evidence interpretation observed in this study is consistent with findings by Taouk et al. among U.S. obstetricians and gynecologists (Taouk et al. 2018) and is unsurprising given the vast body of contradictory literature. Importantly, HCPs who believed SSRIs were associated with adverse outcomes expressed higher concern across most outcomes than those who viewed the evidence as conflicting, were uncertain, or were not associated (Table S1). Such misinterpretation may amplify patient anxiety, as negative information from HCPs is recalled more vividly than reassuring information (Mulder et al. 2012), increasing the risk of unsafe treatment discontinuation. Conversely, inadequate recognition of established risks may leave patients insufficiently informed and unprepared for potential adverse outcomes, ultimately increasing the risk of clinical harm. These findings highlight the importance of accurate evidence interpretation in clinical decision‐making and counseling. Unfortunately, the small sample size limited assessment of whether concern levels influenced prescribing recommendations.

Among GPs and midwives, 10.9% and 35.5% respectively, felt uncomfortable managing depression during pregnancy. Clinician discomfort in providing pharmacotherapy during pregnancy to mentally ill patients has also been reported in the qualitative literature (Eakley et al. 2025). Given the prevalence and risks of antenatal depression, this lack of confidence is concerning, especially now that routine antenatal depression screening is recommended (Mellor et al. 2019; RANZCOG 2018). An integrative review found that patients perceive HCP discomfort, and feel that this results in HCPs being reluctant to discuss treatment (Eakley and Lyndon 2022). The reasons behind HCP discomfort require further exploration; however, a US study among psychiatric mental health practitioners identified that lack of training, inadequate knowledge, medication safety concerns, and fear of personal consequences were possible underlying factors contributing to reluctance to treat depression during pregnancy (Eakley et al. 2025). Limited mental health referral pathways or resources have also been identified as a barrier to providing mental health care among NZ midwives (Mellor et al. 2019). The unexpected finding that 31% of GPs were uncomfortable managing pregnant people with diabetes also merits further investigation.

Practices

Pre‐Existing Antidepressant Treatment (Case‐Vignette)

Dose reduction was advised by 13% (despite fluoxetine being at the low end of the therapeutic range) while 26% recommended switching antidepressants.

Guidelines discourage dose reduction due to relapse risk, which could expose the fetus to both medication risks and untreated depression, with no evidence that dose reduction mitigates harm. Switching antidepressant treatment is also discouraged due to relapse risk and fetal exposure to multiple medications (RANZCOG 2018).

Two‐thirds of pregnant patients discontinue antidepressants (Broughton et al. 2023). In this survey, no HCP recommended discontinuing pre‐existing treatment possibly suggesting that patients could be stopping treatment independently of HCP advice. However, given the limited response rate, and potential selection bias (98% of respondents viewed SSRIs as effective for moderate depression suggesting a generally positive attitude to antidepressants) and that only midwives and GPs were surveyed (e.g., not specialists, nurses, or pharmacists), we cannot rule out that there may be HCPs advising pregnant patients to cease treatment.

Initiating Treatment During Pregnancy

All GPs reported that they would prescribe an antidepressant during pregnancy for suicidal ideation or nonresponse to non‐pharmacological therapy. Conversely, nearly all would refrain if the patient did not want an antidepressant. While the final decision should rest with the patient, it should be informed by a clear understanding of not just antidepressant risks, but also risks of no treatment to mother and infant, as well as the risk of relapse (RANZCOG 2018). However, GPs endorsed that discussions were often brief (< 10 min), and suitable written information was rarely provided, potentially leaving patients under‐informed.

Additionally, about one‐third of GPs endorsed “antidepressants are unsafe during pregnancy” as a reason for avoiding antidepressants in pregnancy, which may also contribute to the undertreatment of perinatal depression (Svardal et al. 2021).

Patient Advice

In the case vignette, 17% of midwives said they would not provide a risk–benefit discussion on antidepressant use. Furthermore, 28.8% of midwives reported never discussing this in their practice. This may stem from lack of confidence or a belief that these discussions fall within the GP's role. However, the NZ Midwifery College website advises people to consult their midwives about medication use during pregnancy: “The use of any medicine during pregnancy requires consideration of both the risks and benefits to you and your baby, so it is important to discuss with your midwife what you are taking or considering taking” (New Zealand College of Midwives 2024). To support midwives in providing this advice, additional education and resources, including written patient information, would be beneficial.

Written patient information was seldom provided, and when given, often lacked details on pregnancy‐specific benefits and risks or was not tailored to patient health literacy. Taken together, these findings indicate that only a small minority of patients are likely to receive both detailed counseling and appropriate written information, leaving many under‐informed. These findings are consistent with findings in studies from women's perspectives in which women report receiving limited information about antidepressant safety from HCPs (Eakley et al. 2025; Kothari et al. 2019).

Awareness of leaflets on medicine safety in pregnancy is lacking. BUMPS (UK) leaflets were rarely used, and Mother to Baby (US) leaflets were not used at all. This gap is significant as qualitative research shows pregnant people struggle to find quality safety information, contributing to decisional conflict (Westgate et al. 2023). Written materials are crucial for informed decision‐making, especially as depression can impair concentration, making verbal discussions harder to retain.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has some limitations. Convenience sampling was necessary because both the GP and midwifery colleges declined to distribute the survey via their national databases, citing concerns about over‐surveying amid workforce shortages. As a result, response rates were low (2.1% and 1.9% of registered GPs and midwives, respectively). Canterbury and female GPs were over‐represented, and Asian and Pacific HCPs under‐represented, limiting generalisability. However, responses were obtained from across NZ and included a HCPs with a range of practice durations. Selection bias is also possible, as participants may have been more knowledgeable about maternal mental health, though less knowledgeable HCPs may have produced even greater variability. A modest decline in completion rates (consistent with survey fatigue) further reduces sample size from later sections. Other HCPs that may be involved in decision making regarding antidepressant use in pregnancy (e.g., pharmacists, specialists) were not included.

The survey utilized a quantitative design to establish variation in knowledge, concerns, and counseling practices, rather than exploring the underlying reasoning shaping individual decisions. As a result, important contextual detail may not have been captured, including how respondents interpreted key terms within fixed‐response items (e.g., what constituted “functional impairment”) and the factors influencing their responses. Future research should broaden professional inclusion and apply qualitative methods to deepen understanding. In addition, findings were based on HCP self‐report and could not be directly compared with patient‐reported experiences of information provision, which may differ from HCP perceptions. While patient reports from international studies suggest similar gaps in information provision, further research incorporating patient perspectives in NZ would also complement these findings. Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first to survey midwives' knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding antidepressant use in pregnancy, adding a novel perspective to the literature. We also improved on prior surveys by including questions on HCP beliefs about SSRI efficacy (key to risk–benefit assessment) and by specifying moderate depression to reduce ambiguity around illness severity. These refinements enhance the validity of our findings. Importantly, our results align with international surveys (Taouk et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2020) showing inconsistent knowledge, attitudes, and practices across Western countries, while uniquely highlighting the midwifery perspective.

Conclusion

There is variability in knowledge, attitudes, and practices among NZ‐registered GPs and midwives regarding SSRIs during pregnancy. Interpretation of evidence was varied with under‐recognition of established risks (NAS and PPH). There were disparities in recommendations for pre‐existing SSRI treatment and a lack of appropriate written patient information.

Differing opinions may contribute to decisional conflict, potentially delaying decision‐making and leading to unsafe treatment discontinuation. Most respondents expressed interest in further training on perinatal mood disorders, access to patient information, and collaborative decision aids. These would help HCPs to give accurate, balanced information on SSRI use and support safe management of antenatal depression.
