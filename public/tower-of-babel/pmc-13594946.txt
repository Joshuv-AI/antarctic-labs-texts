Ultrasound-assisted green hydrophobic deep eutectic solvents extraction of capsanthin from red peppers: optimization, kinetics, stability, and insights into extraction mechanisms
Authors: Zhongxu Li, Caihong Cheng, Jialin Xue, Qingyang Sun, Lixiang Huai, Yuting Xiao, Xianjin Zhou, Yaya Yao, Ruiguo Cui, Lijun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCapsanthin is a widely used natural pigment, but its extraction with traditional solvents suffers from low efficiency and environmental pollution. To address these issues, this study employed ultrasonic-assisted hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent (HDES) to extract capsanthin. The thymol: menthol (molar ratio 1:2) (HDES-3) was selected as the optimum solvent. The extraction process of capsanthin conformed to Fick’s second law. FTIR and SEM analyses confirmed that hydrogen bond interactions between HDES components led to its formation. HDES-3 had the most obvious destructive effect on the raw material structure and showed a stronger extraction ability. Density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to further explore the interaction between HDES-3 and capsanthin. The main interactions between capsanthin and HDESs were van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds with van der Waals forces playing the dominant role. Meanwhile, the more stable hydrogen bond between HDES-3 and capsanthin enhances the stability of capsanthin. This study provides an efficient and green alternative to the use of traditional solvents in the extraction of capsanthin.Keywords: Hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent, Capsanthin, Red peppers, Density functional theory, Molecular dynamics simulations

Introduction

The color of food products serves as a key indicator of quality and plays a significant role in consumer acceptance. Although synthetic pigments are widely used in industry for their vibrant colors and stability, growing safety concerns have increased scrutiny regarding their application [1], [2]. In contrast, natural pigments have gradually gained popularity among consumers due to their higher safety and favorable coloring properties [3]. Capsanthin is a lipid-soluble compound. The main components in the crude extract of dried red peppers are capsanthin, capsorubin, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin and zeaxanthin, among which capsanthin is the most abundant, followed by capsorubin [4], [5]. Studies have shown that capsanthin constitutes 92.51% of the crude extract of dried red peppers [6]. Its red and red–orange color arises from conjugated polyene chromophore and ketone groups in the molecular structure [7]. Notably, capsanthin has been recognized by the FAO and WHO as a Class A pigment component with outstanding safety properties [8]. This has promoted the wide application of capsanthin in the food, dye and cosmetic industries. Moreover, capsanthin also showed antioxidant antioxidant [9], anti-obesity [4], anti-inflammatory [7], anti-tumor [10], and other biological functions. These beneficial functions highlight its promising potential in functional foods and pharmaceuticals.

Organic solvents like n-hexane and petroleum ether are currently the dominant media for capsanthin extraction but pose environmental and human health risks with long-term use [7], [10], [11], [12], [13]. Furthermore, these conventional methods afford low yields and produce pigments with limited stability [8], [14]. This has driven the urgent search for efficient and eco-friendly alternatives. Ionic liquids (ILs) and supercritical fluids have been proposed as green alternatives for natural products extraction. Some researchers have already applied ILs to the extraction of chestnut shell pigments [15] and carotenoids [16]. However, the shortcomings of IL, including high preparation cost, toxicity, and a complex production process, have become more prominent [17]. Supercritical fluid extraction also exhibited high efficiency for the extraction of natural products. For active components including polyphenols, capsanthin, alkaloids, and flavonoids, its extraction yield is superior to that achieved using conventional solvents [18], [19], [20]. However, its practical application is limited by expensive equipment, and complicated operation [21]. DESs have recently attracted significant research attention as viable alternatives to both ILs and conventional solvents [22], [23]. It forms through hydrogen bonding between hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) and donors (HBD) [24]. Compared with ILs, DESs possess lower production cost, simpler preparation, stronger biodegradability and better stability [17], [25], [26]. Additionally, DESs are less toxic and more environmentally friendly due to their low-toxicity, biodegradable components, with some formulations entirely made from food-grade materials [27].

Nevertheless, most DESs are hydrophilic, limiting their use in lipophilic samples. Vash Osch et al. (2015) firstly prepared hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent (HDES) composed of decanoic acid (HBD) and quaternary ammonium salts (HBA) [28]. The hydrophobic nature of HDES compared to hydrophilic DESs allows it to be used in lipid soluble samples [17]. HDESs have been shown to exhibit high efficiency and stability in lipid-soluble pigment extraction. For example, curcumin content extracted by HDES (anethole-n-nonanoic acid) reached 115.64 mg/g, higher than that with methanol [29]. The yield of astaxanthin obtained from algae by menthol: octanoic acid was about 2.58-fold higher than that of conventional solvents [30]. Similarly, lycopene was recovered through thymol-menthol with higher stability compared to conventional solvents [31]. Meanwhile, comparable results have been reported for carotenoid recovery from mango peel [32], canistel (Lucuma nervosa) pulps [33], orange peel [34], and pumpkin [35]. Satisfactorily, HDESs can be made from low-cost, readily available natural materials like menthol, thymol, and octanoic acid, aligning with green economy principles [36]. These successes with diverse hydrophobic pigments underscore the potential of HDESs for capsanthin extraction. Research on the extraction of capsanthin using HDESs is scarce, and the interaction mechanism has remained largely unexplored up to now.

Conventional techniques (maceration, digestion, heating, stirring, Soxhlet, etc.) can extract bioactives yet are hindered by long durations and low efficiency, undermining green and sustainable objectives [37]. Newer physical-assisted techniques such as microwave-assisted extraction, infrared-assisted extraction, pressurized hot-water extraction, and induction heating can significantly improve DES extraction efficiency. But they are limited by thermal degradation, poor penetration, high solvent use, low selectivity, and high energy consumption [38]. Ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE) is widely employed for natural bioactives because it is simple and cost-effective. High-frequency ultrasonic waves generate cavitation bubbles whose formation and rupture effectively disrupt plant cell walls of plants and promote the penetration of the solvent into the cell matrix [39]. Ultrasonic technology offers a greener alternative to conventional methods by markedly reducing extraction time and energy consumption [40]. Interestingly, HDES themselves also have destructive effects on plant cell walls. Therefore, the combination of HDES and ultrasonic irradiation effectively disrupts cell walls and increases target compound solubility and yield in solvents [41], [42]. This provides a feasible alternative to the traditional methods. UAE-HDES has been widely used to extract compounds from natural plants [40], but so far few studies have applied it to the extraction of capsanthin.

In this study, red pepper was used as the raw material, and ultrasound-assisted green HDESs were employed for capsanthin extraction. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the extraction processes, and the stability and extraction kinetics of capsanthin was assessed in comparison with that obtained using traditional solvents. The interaction mechanism between capsanthin and HDESs was further elucidated through the density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The study will provide a novel, efficient, and environmentally sustainable alternative method for capsanthin extraction.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

The red peppers (Capsicum annuum 'Honglong 25′) were collected from Korla City, Xinjiang Province. The red peppers were washed and dried at 45 °C using an oven (DHG-9245A, Shanghai Huitai Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), then ground using a high-speed mill (CS-700, Wuyi Haina Electrical Appliance Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China). Subsequently, the paprika through a 60-mesh sieve, place into a hermetic bag, and store at −20 °C for further analysis.

The thymol, menthol, and octanoic acid were all purchased from Damas Beta company (Shanghai Titan Science Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The capsanthin standard was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other analytically pure reagents were purchased from Hengrui Tongxiang Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Preparation and physicochemical properties of HDESs

Preparation of HDESs

According to previous methods [43], HBA and HBD were combined in a certain molar ratio (Table S1) and placed in a round-bottom flask. The flask was sealed with plastic wrap to prevent moisture from entering. The mixture was stirred magnetically until a transparent liquid formed at 60 °C (Fig. S1). Then the mixture was cooled to room temperature (25 °C) and allowed to equilibrate for 24 h to assess its stability. The absence of crystallization confirmed the successful synthesis of the HDES. The validated HDES was then stored in a desiccator for subsequent experiments.

The formation and eutectic characteristics of the thymol:menthol (1:2) HDES system have been systematically verified via DSC analysis in previous reports [44], and no additional DSC test was conducted in this study.

Physicochemical properties determination

The viscosity, pH, and density of DES were measured at 25 °C using a rotary viscometer (DV–1, Yueping Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), a pH meter (PHS–3C, Ohaus Instruments Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China), and a density flask, respectively.

Extraction of capsanthin using different HDESs

In order to screen for the best extraction solvent, pepper powder (5 g) was mixed with different HDESs separately (1:20, m/v) (Table S1) in a conical flask. Then the mixture was extracted by ultrasound (KQ–500 DE, 40 kHz, 500 W, Jiangsu Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments co., Ltd., China) at 60 °C for 45 min. After extraction, centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, then the supernatants were filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. The filtrates were stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Determination of capsanthin

The method adopted by Li et al. (2024) was modified [8]. In brief, 100 µL of the capsanthin solution was diluted 100-fold and scanned employing a UV-1780 spectrophotometer over a wavelength range of 250 to 600 nm to measure and compare absorption characteristics. The UV spectrum (Fig. 1) revealed that the maximum absorption peak of the capsanthin solution occurred at 470 nm. Capsanthin solutions were prepared with methanol as the solvent at concentrations of 0.03, 0.04, 0.05, 0.06, and 0.07 mM, respectively. Ultraviolet absorbance values at 470 nm were recorded, plotting concentration C (mM) on the abscissa and absorbance (A) on the ordinate. The resulting standard curve for capsanthin was described by the equation: A = 6.7295C − 0.0453 (R2 = 0.9953). Capsanthin concentrations were expressed as mg per mL of extract (mg/mL), calculated from the standard curve using pure capsanthin (MW = 584.86 g/mol).

The effects of extraction temperature (30–70 °C), extraction time (15–75 min), and ultrasonic power (100–500 W) on capsanthin yield were examined. The parameter range was set based on the experience of predecessors [45], [46], [47]. During these experiments, only one variable was altered at a time. To elucidate the mechanism by which HDESs extract capsanthin, the Fick's second law was employed to model the HDES-capsanthin extraction kinetics [48]. Studies have shown that mass transfer in HDES is barely affected and target molecules can diffuse freely when the viscosity is below 100 mPa·s [50]. The maximum viscosity of the HDES used in this study is 27.81 mPa·s, which is far below the critical value. No obvious aggregation of solid matrix particles was observed during extraction, ensuring uniform particle size and independent diffusion behavior. Capsanthin molecules can move freely in the solvent, and the mass transfer process is hardly affected. Therefore, the kinetic model based on Fick’s second law and the idealized particle behavior is suitable to describe the extraction process.

FTIR

FTIR was determined using an FT-IR spectroscopy (BRUKER TENSOR 27, GER). The capsanthin extract and HDESs were respectively dropped into potassium bromide powder, compressed into a disc, and scanned within a wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm⁻1.

SEM

The microstructure of residue after extracting capsanthin was observed according to the method of Li et al. (2025) [40]. Briefly, the residue was washed three times with absolute ethanol. After centrifugation, the powder was placed in an oven and dried at 35 °C until it reached a constant weight. And the dried samples were affixed to aluminum stubs for gold spraying and observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan).

Density functional theory (DFT)

Gauss View 6.0 was used to build the initial structures for visualization of ethanol, HDES-3 (thymol: menthol, 1:2), and capsanthin. Geometric optimization and frequency validation of the solvent and solute molecular structures were performed at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6–31 + G(d,p) level using Gauss 09 W software [48]. The BSSE-corrected interaction energy was calculated at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP level. Electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis of the extraction-system molecules was carried out with Multiwfn and VMD [51]. Scatter plots for the interaction region indicator (IRI) analysis were generated using Multiwfn and GNUPLOT software [29].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analysis

Ethanol and HDES-3 systems were established using the Packmol program in a 9 nm × 9 nm × 9 nm cubic box [52]. The number of molecules in each box was determined by their molar ratios. The HDES-3 box contained 828 thymol molecules, 1656 menthol molecules, and 9 capsanthin molecules. The ethanol box contained 7,272 ethanol molecules and 9 capsanthin molecules. MD simulation was then performed using GROMACS (2023.4). The GAFF force field and RESP charges were assigned according to the previous method [53]. All system was energy-minimized using the steepest descent method, and the simulation temperature was maintained at 333 K over 500 ps. MD simulation was performed for 100 ns at 333 K. The GROMACS tool was used for the analysis of MD simulation.

Statistical analysis

All data were measured in triplicate and expressed as mean ± SD. Graphs were generated using Origin 2021pro software (OriginLab Corporation, USA). One-way and Type I analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed using SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM, USA). Statistical significance among different groups was assessed at the 5% significance level.

Result and discussion

Screening of DES

The UV–Vis spectrals of the 5 capsanthin extracts are shown in Fig. 1a. As same as the capsanthin standard, all samples exhibited the same maximum absorption wavelength at 470 nm, indicating that the main constituent of the extracts was capsanthin. Based on the result, we further investigated the effect of different HDESs on the extraction yield of capsanthin (Fig. 1b). As shown in Fig. 1b, HDESs exhibited significantly higher capsanthin yields compared to ethanol. Among the 5 extraction solvents, HDES-3 achieved the highest extraction yield (14.06 mg/mL), followed by HDES-2 (12.90 mg/mL), HDES-4 (12.51 mg/mL), HDES-1 (11.73 mg/mL), and ethanol (10.03 mg/mL). Based on the results in Table S1, it can be seen that the yield of capsanthin is closely associated with the physicochemical properties of HDESs, such as viscosity, pH, and density [54]. As shown in Table S1, the density of HDESs ranged from 0.88 to 0.93 g/cm3, which is lower than that of water. This indicates that will become concentrated in the upper layer following the mixing with water. Four types of HDES were evaluated according to the standard proposed by Van Osch et al. (2019), which requires a density difference between the extractant and water of ≥ 0.05 g/cm3 [55]. All HDESs exceeded this threshold (0.07–0.12), confirming their suitability for extracting non-polar capsanthin. These findings align with previous studies [36].

It should be noted that viscosity is a key property of DESs, which is closely related to the extraction efficiency. Most hydrophilic DESs exhibit high viscosity, which impedes solvent flow and restricts mass transfer, thereby reducing the extraction efficiency of target compounds [17]. In contrast, the viscosity of HDES (2.31–1807 mPa·s) is generally lower than that of hydrophilic DES, and it has a better mass transfer effect [17]. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that mass transfer is minimally restricted when the viscosity of HDES is lower than 100 mPa·s [50]. The viscosity values (2.35–27.81 mPa·s) obtained in this study are far below this critical threshold. This result indicated that the diffusion of the target compound during the extraction process is minimally influenced by viscosity. Meanwhile, the research conducted by Serna-Vázquez et al. (2021) indicated that low-viscosity HDES does not encounter any Coulombic force obstruction, and mass transfer is not limited by viscosity [56]. Therefore, no obvious mass transfer limitation was imposed by the viscosity level in the present extraction system. In addition, the localized high temperature generated by ultrasonic cavitation further reduced the viscosity of HDES–3 and promoted mass transfer, which compensated for any possible mass transfer resistance. Although HDES–3 had the highest viscosity among the screened solvents, it exhibited superior hydrogen–bonding capability and solubilization capacity toward capsanthin. A good balance between viscosity, mass transfer, and solubilization performance was achieved.

Notably, HDES with relatively higher viscosity typically possess stronger hydrogen bonding capacity, which enhances the solubility of capsanthin through intermolecular interactions and stabilizes its conjugated structure through a protective hydrogen bond network, ultimately improving extraction efficiency and stability [27]. This finding also explains why HDES-3, with the highest viscosity (27.81 mPa·s) among all tested solvents, achieved the optimal extraction performance.

Capsanthin decomposes in acidic environments but remains stable in neutral or alkaline conditions [8]. Since HDES-1 and HDES-4 exhibited acidic pH values, this explains their relatively low extraction efficiencies. The main interactions between non-polar compounds and HDESs are the van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds, with van der Waals forces playing the dominant role [40], [57]. A suitable hydrogen bonding capability of the solvent improves the solubility of non-polar compounds and increases the extraction yield [27], [58]. As shown in Section 3.5, HDES-3 forms more hydrogen bonds than HDES-1 and HDES-4, which endows HDES-3 with higher solubility for capsanthin and a greater extraction yield. However, hydrogen bonds exhibit a dual effect. Excessive hydrogen bonds increase solvent polarity, which reduces capsanthin yield based on the principle of “like dissolves like” [59], [60]. This provides an explanation for the poor extraction rate of HDES-2. Additionally, the weakly acidic environment (pH = 5.48) of HDES-2 likely promoted capsanthin decomposition. Hence, HDES-3 (pH = 7.87) was selected as the optimal extraction solvent for subsequent applications. Consistent with our results, Van Osch et al. (2019) also identified that thymol:menthol (molar ratio 1:2) was evaluated as a relatively sustainable and ideal hydrophobic solvent.

The gradual increase in capsanthin yield with temperature is due to enhanced molecular motion and diffusion at higher temperatures. Additionally, high temperature conditions diminished viscosity to facilitate capsanthin mass transfer and increase solubility [61]. But the stability of capsanthin can be compromised at too high a temperature leading to its decomposition and lower production [8].

Furthermore, with the increase of ultrasonic power, the yield of capsanthin showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing (Fig. S3). Previous study has showed that pressure fluctuations at the vapor–liquid interface generate cavitation bubbles during sonication, and these bubbles collapse rapidly under alternating acoustic stress [38]. Higher power favors the formation of larger bubbles and more intense implosion. The violent implosion effect releases high-intensity micro-jets and shockwaves, which induce cell wall rupture and facilitate the release of capsanthin [62]. Moreover, US significantly enhances the penetration, mixing and diffusion of solvents through non-cavitation effects such as acoustic streaming, microstreaming and pressure gradients, thereby improving the extraction efficiency [38]. Notably, the localized high temperature generated by cavitation during sonication reduces the viscosity of HDES-3 and thereby facilitates mass transfer. This effect may represent the key mechanism by which ultrasound compensates for the mass transfer limitation imposed by the relatively viscous HDES system. However, when the ultrasonic power exceeds a certain critical threshold, cavitation reaches saturation. Further increasing the power will lead to the formation of ineffective bubbles, thereby enhancing scattering attenuation and reducing cavitation intensity [39]. Fig. S3d displays a clear elliptical projection, providing further evidence of a significant interaction between factors A and C (p < 0.05). Despite this, the high energy consumption associated with large–scale ultrasonic irradiation in practical production may represent a limiting factor. Therefore, designing efficient industrial ultrasonic reactors with uniform sound field distribution is crucial to balance extraction efficiency and energy input, thereby promoting the industrial application of HDES for capsanthin extraction.

Validation

RSM optimized extraction conditions for capsanthin: 61 °C (predicted to be 61.080 °C), 417 W (estimated to be 417.192 W), and 46 min (predicted to be 46.081 min). The model-predicted concentration under these conditions was 14.48 mg/mL. Three parallel experiments were carried out under these conditions and the concentration of capsanthin was 14.58 ± 0.25 mg/mL. The close agreement between the experimental results and the model predictions demonstrated the high accuracy and reliability of the parameters optimized by RSM.

Extraction kinetics model

The investigation of kinetic models plays a crucial role in controlling extraction conditions and predicting the extraction process [63]. The extraction of natural active ingredients is considered a heterogeneous and unsteady diffusion process, which characterized by continuous changes in solute concentration [49]. Regrettably, so far, there is no reports on the kinetic mechanism of the extraction processes during UAE- HDESs. This study focused on the extraction of capsanthin from red peppers. The kinetic model was established based on the Fick's second law. The extraction process of capsanthin primarily consists of three stages: (1) Due to the added energy under ultrasound condition, when HDES fused with red pepper powder, the solvent rapidly penetrated the interior of the red pepper particles. (2) Due to the interaction between capsanthin and HDES, capsanthin molecules transferred from the interior to the surface of red pepper particles (solid–liquid interface). (3) The capsanthin molecules then diffuses into the des solution [48], [49].

The results of extraction kinetics models were shown in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2a and e the extraction process of capsanthin at different temperatures (303–343 K) with time (0–––60 min). It can be seen that the interaction between red pepper powder and HDES was enhanced with the increase of temperature in the preliminary stage of extraction. This stage falls within the domain of extraction kinetics, and the mass transfer effect is dominant [64]. As the heating time is extended to 20 min, the extraction process entered the second stage. At this point, the extraction rate of capsanthin reached a stable level, and the concentration of capsanthin reached equilibrium. These findings indicate that time and extraction rate are not necessarily proportional, which aligns with previous studies [65], [66].

This indicated that under the identical temperature conditions, HDES-3 exhibits a higher extraction rate and requires less energy. Correspondingly, previous studies on pectin extraction have also drawn this conclusion [67]. Meanwhile, considering cost-effectiveness and energy-saving, the appropriate temperature maintaining high extraction efficiency while possessing a low Ea value is deemed a critical factor for HDES-UAE extraction.

Stability analysis

Thermal stability

To investigate the thermal stability of capsanthin extracts, the changes in ΔE after heating treatment under various temperatures were studied and the results were displayed in Fig. 3a-3c. The findings demonstrated significant differences in the stability of capsanthin extracted using different solvents (p < 0.05). The stability of capsanthin extracted by HDESs is superior to that of ethanol. With increasing heating time at different temperatures, the ΔE of capsanthin showed an upward trend. The rate of increase varies among the solvents, following the order: Ethanol > HDES-1 > HDES-4 > HDES-2 > HDES-3. Remarkably, HDES-3 exhibited the slowest rising frequency. After heating at 100 °C for 10 h, the ΔE of capsanthin extracted by HDES-3, HDES-2, HDES-4, HDES-1, and ethanol were 6.72, 9.11, 10.36, 14.72, and 16.67, respectively. Ethanol showed the most prominent changes across all temperatures, followed by HDES-1, HDES-4, and HDES-2, while HDES-3 displayed the highest stability. This phenomenon can be explained by the following factors. Firstly, in the HDES system composed of thymol and menthol, thymol is a phenolic compound. Combined with menthol, the mixture formed stronger hydrogen-bond interactions, markedly enhancing capsanthin stability in the solvent [69], [70]. Meanwhile, the aromatic ring of thymol undergoes π–π stacked with the conjugated polyene backbone of capsanthin, intensifying van der Waals forces and firmly anchoring the pigment within the HDES phase [36], [63]. This also explained why HDES-2 and HDES-3 have higher stability. Moreover, as described in Section 3.1, capsanthin can maintain better stability under neutral or alkaline conditions.

Color stability of capsanthin extracts at different temperatures (a: 25 °C, b: 60 °C, c: 80 °C) and natural light conditions (d) (HDES-1: Menthol: octanoic acid, HDES-2: Menthol: thymol, HDES-3: Thymol: menthol, HDES-4: Thymol: octanoic acid).

Light stability

The stability of capsanthin exposed to natural light conditions is presented in Fig. 3d. The ΔE values of all samples exhibited an upward trend with increasing light exposure time. During the first 6 days, the increasing rate of ΔE for capsanthin extracted using HDES-1 and HDES-4 was higher than that of other samples. At 6 d, the ΔE of capsanthin extracted using HDE-1 and HDES-4 were 1.66 and 1.17, respectively. The ΔE of other samples ranged from 0.25 to 0.73. Notably, the ΔE of ethanol-extracted capsanthin rose rapidly after 6 d. However, capsanthin extracted employing HDESs either rose slowly or even tended to remain stable. By day 12, the ΔE values for capsanthin extracted using ethanol, HDES-1, HDES-2, HDES-3, and HDES-4 were 4.21, 2.46, 1.09, 0.61, and 2.41, respectively. Overall, HDESs enhanced the stability of capsanthin under light conditions.

These results may originate from the fact that capsanthin is relatively sensitive and prone to decomposition under acidic and light conditions [71], [72]. As shown in Table S1, HDES-1 (pH = -1.28) and HDES-4 (pH = -0.62) represented strongly acidic environments, which can reduce the decomposition stability of capsanthin. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to light accelerated the oxidation and degradation of capsanthin due to extended contact with air [35]. However, oxidative degradation is minimized due to hydrogen bonding between the solvent and the target compound, which limited solute mobility and oxygen exposure at the DES-air interface [32], [34]. Simultaneously, Pitacco's et al. (2022) studied the application of thymol-based HDES on astaxanthin extraction [73]. The results demonstrated that thymol has superior antioxidant properties, which effectively inhibited the oxidation of astaxanthin, thereby enhancing its stability. Above all, compared with ethanol, HDESs have improved the stability of capsanthin under different temperature and thermal conditions.

FTIR

The functional groups in diverse samples were gauged by FTIR, and the results were showed in Fig. 4.

Fourier transform infrared scanning spectrum. (a: Pure reagents, b-c: Different HDESs and extracts). (HDES-1: Menthol: octanoic acid, HDES-2: Menthol: thymol, HDES-3: Thymol: menthol, HDES-4: Thymol: octanoic acid).

As shown in Fig. 4a, The C–H stretching vibrations of all pure reagents were observed within the range of 2958–2865 cm⁻1. The stretching vibrations of –OH were observed in pure thymol (3237 cm⁻1) and pure menthol (3319 cm⁻1). The stretching vibrations of C-O showed in pure menthol (1178 cm⁻1). Additionally, the carboxylic acid group in pure octanoic acid generated a C=O band at 1706 cm⁻1 [36], [74], [75].

As depicted in Fig. 4b and Fig. 4c, no new peaks were observed in the capsanthin extract compared with HDESs, indicating the absence of ionic or covalent interactions between HDESs and capsanthin. This signifies that the interaction between HDESs and capsanthin is mainly through physical interactions (such as hydrogen bonds, the van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, etc.) rather than chemical bonds [65]. The characteristic bands changes of the HDESs and ethanol at 3660–3195 cm−1 were compared. The results showed that the wavenumber variation of HDESs is greater than that of ethanol, implying that the number of hydrogen bonds in HDESs are higher [74].

As shown in Fig. 4, the characteristic C=O peak of octanoic acid in HDES-1 and HDES-4 has shifted to 1710 cm⁻1, aligning with previous results [76]. The C=O stretching vibration occurred at 1924 cm⁻1 in ethanol, while it was transferred to 1874 cm⁻1 in HDESs. This result indicated that there was an increased formation of hydrogen bonds. Furthermore, both HDES-2 and HDES-3 experienced additional –OH vibrations at 3531 cm−1, indicating the formation of hydrogen bonds. This reflected a higher hydrogen bond density. It is worth noting that the stretching vibration of HDES-2 was additionally detected with the stretching vibration of OH at 3053 cm⁻1. This may be due to the presence of hydrogen bonds [77]. Similarly, previous studies using HDESs have also obtained the same results [74], [78]. These results indicated that hydrogen bonds exist between the HDES components, and HDES-2 has the most hydrogen bonds. The formation of HDESs is supported by these results.

Meanwhile, Bergua et al. (2022) verified the formation of HDES prepared with thymol: menthol (molar ratio 1:2). DSC analysis in their study reveals a glass transition (Tg) rather than a distinct eutectic melting peak, attributable to strong hydrogen bond interactions that suppress crystallization and promote metastable glass formation [44]. The experimental phase diagram shows a significant negative deviation from ideal behavior, with the melting point being approximately 40 K lower than that of the ideal system [44]. This confirms that the thymol and menthol mixture conforms to the characteristics of a deep eutectic solvent (DES).

SEM

In order to further investigate the interaction between the solvent and raw materials, the microstructural changes of the untreated raw material and the residue after extraction were observed using SEM, as shown in Fig. 5. The untreated sample was found to have smooth and flat surfaces, with intact cell wall structures and almost no signs of damage (Fig. 5 a). As shown in Fig. 5b, the surface of the residue after ethanol extraction is relatively rough with slight wrinkling changes. In contrast, the surface of the residue extracted by HDES presented a rough and porous texture structure (Fig. 5c-f). In particular, it can be seen from Fig. 5e that the residue after HDES-3 extraction exhibited the most apparent effect. A similar conclusion was reached in a study on the extraction of active components from Verbena officinalis [79].

SEM of the raw material residuals after extraction with different solvents (a: Raw powder, b: Ethanol, c: HDES-1, d: HDES-2, e: HDES-3, f: HDES-4).

This phenomenon is attributed to acoustic cavitation generated during sonication, where violent bubble collapse produces high-velocity microjets and shock waves that rupture plant cell walls, enhancing porosity and solvent penetration [38]. Concurrently, the superior solubility and penetration capacity of HDES-3 facilitate the disruption of red peppers powder surface structures, significantly increasing surface roughness and creating additional pores [70], [80]. Porous morphology increases the powder–solvent interfacial area enabling faster capsanthin release [40], [81]. Moreover, HDES extraction cleaved lignin–hemicellulose ether bonds, selectively stripping lignin from the middle lamella and cell corners to boost porosity thus promoting capsanthin release [82], [83]. This comprehensive and profound structural disruption is regarded as the key mechanism for the significantly higher extraction efficiency of capsanthin from HDES compared to untreated materials and traditional solvents (ethanol).

ESP analysis

The electrostatic potential map visually shows how charge is distributed on the molecular surface, helping identify reaction sites and intermolecular interaction areas [36]. As shown in Fig. 6a, the blue and red represent the negative and positive potential, respectively. The thymol exhibits its minimum negative potential on the O atom of the –OH group, with a value of −28.03 kcal/mol. The maximum positive potential is primarily localized on the H atom of OH group, with a magnitude of 43.91 kcal/mol. The minimum negative potential of menthol is −35.73 kcal/mol on the oxygen atom of the –OH group, and the maximum positive potential is 39.33 kcal/mol on the hydrogen atom of the –OH group.

Theoretical calculation diagram of DFT (a: Electrostatic potential diagrams of HDES and its components, b: Structural optimization and interaction energy diagram of HDES-capsanthin, c: Weak interaction diagram between HDES-3 and capsanthin).

The electrostatic potential (ESP) map of HDES-3 revealed a hydrogen bond between the hydroxyl hydrogen of menthol and the hydroxyl oxygen of thymol, confirming donor–acceptor interactions [79]. The results indicated that hydrogen bonds exist in HDES, and their formation is primarily driven by non-covalent interactions between donors and acceptors [36]. This further confirms the results in Section 3.5. In HDES-3, the most negative potential is located on the –OH oxygen (38.86 kcal/mol), whereas the most positive potential resides on its hydrogen. For capsanthin, the minimum negative potential is on the O atom of the C=O group (−41.46 kcal/mol). And the maximum positive potential is on the H atom of the –OH group (42.34 kcal/mol). To establish a more stable “solvent–solute structure”, interactions typically occur at these extreme potential sites [29]. Consequently, during extraction, the positively charged hydrogen of the –OH group in HDES-3 pairs with the carbonyl oxygen of capsanthin to form an interaction.

Mechanism of HDESs extraction

To further clarify the extraction mechanism based on HDESs. The DFT was employed to calculate the intermolecular interactions and optimize the molecular structure to evaluate the binding energy. The key binding sites was identified using capsanthin as the solute model, and the result was showed in Fig. 6. As illustrated in Fig. 6b, the binding energy between HDESs and capsanthin is lower than that observed between ethanol and capsanthin. Moreover, the combined energy follows the sequence from small to large: HDES-3 < HDES-2 < HDES-4 < HDES-1 < Ethanol. This sequence is consistent with the experimentally observed trend in extraction efficiency (Fig. 1). When HDES-3 was employed as the extractant, the binding energy between the solvent and capsanthin (−42.42 kcal/mol) was the most negative, corresponding to the highest extraction efficiency. These observations can be attributed to the fact that the binding energy magnitude reflects the strength of interaction between capsanthin and HDESs. The more negative the binding energy between capsanthin and HDES, the stronger the interaction force between them [84]. The more readily the capsanthin molecules are attracted into the solvent, the higher the solubility and the extraction efficiency. During HDES-3 extraction, the π-π stacking of the benzene ring (existed in thymol), and the polyene chain (existed in capsanthin), as well as the hydrophobic embedding of the menthol alkyl chain, jointly driven the dominant the van der Waals interaction, thereby forming a low-polarity microenvironment [85], [86]. Notably, the fixed-oriented hydroxyl groups within the cyclic structure of menthol can enhance molecular stability through electronic delocalization. This, in turn, raised the energy barrier for breaking its hydrogen bonds, and forming interactions with capsanthin. These results illustrated the competitive interaction between HDES components and capsanthin molecules during the extraction process [36], [59], [87].

Interaction force analysis

To further investigate the weak interaction between HDES-3 and capsanthin, we conducted an in-depth analysis using the interaction region index (IRI) method, and the result was showed in Fig. 6c. Fig. 6c presents the distribution patterns of weak interactions between HDES-3 and capsanthin. In this isoplanar graph generated by IRI analysis, different colors correspond to different types of intermolecular interactions. Blue regions denote hydrogen bonding interactions, green regions represent the van der Waals forces, and red regions indicate repulsive interactions [29], [52], [88]. From Fig. 6c, it can be seen that the sign (λ2) ρ values for the interactions of solvent and capsanthin exhibited significant peaks (ranging from 0.04 to 0.01). The distribution of the green peaks is particularly concentrated. This result indicated that the van der Waals forces dominated the interaction between the two components, but hydrogen bonding also existed simultaneously [89]. Furthermore, the blue and green areas circled in the 3D non-covalent interaction diagram (Fig. 6c) further confirmed the conclusion of Section 3.1. That is, the main interaction forces between HDES-3 and capsanthin are hydrogen bonds and the van der Waals forces. This result was also confirmed in the study by Wang et al. (2025) on extracting curcumin using anisyl alcohol and n-nonanoic acid. The efficient interaction between capsanthin molecules and HDES is achieved through the van der Waals forces acting on the non-polar sites of HDES [90]. Based on the above results, we can infer as follows: the van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds provided intermolecular attraction. This enabled capsanthin molecules to overcome their aggregation tendency, disperse, and embed into the liquid structure. These voids were formed by HDES components through hydrogen bonds and other interactions. This interaction did not disrupt the hydrogen bonds within HDESs and thus showed minimal impact on the properties of HDES. This observation elucidates the primary mechanism underlying capsanthin extraction from HDES-3.

MD simulation

To further explore the interaction mechanism between HDES-3 and capsanthin, the extraction system was simulated from a microscopic perspective using molecular dynamics. MD simulation was performed with capsanthin as the target compound and ethanol and HDES-3 as the respective extraction solvents. The results were shown in Fig. 7.

Molecular dynamics simulation results (a: Intuitive dissolution behavior, b-c: The temperature–time profile of the extraction systems, d: Density variation, e: Number of hydrogen bonds, f: Average hydrogen bond lifetime, g: Average interaction energies).

Fig. 7a shows the intuitive dissolution behavior of capsanthin in ethanol and HDES-3 systems. As shown in Fig. 7a, the capsanthin molecules were close to each other and formed clusters during the period of 0–100 ns in ethanol solution. In HDES-3, these capsanthin molecules were uniformly distributed, which implied that the capsanthin is more soluble in HDES-3 than that in ethanol. This may be also the reason for the higher extraction efficiency in the HDES-3 system [48].

Fig. 7b −d show the temperature–time profile of the extraction systems. As shown in Fig. 7b − Fig. 7d, the temperature of the two extraction systems remained constant between 0 and 100,000 ps. Moreover, the density profiles of capsanthin molecules in both extracts exhibited an approximately linear trend and remained relatively unchanged. These observations confirmed the reliability of the model [48].

The average number and lifetime of hydrogen bonds between the solvent and capsanthin are shown in Fig. 7e and Fig. 7f, respectively. The number of hydrogen bonds between HDES-3 and capsanthin is only 1/3 of that in the ethanol system. But its average lifespan is 211.80 ps, indicating that the menthol hydroxyl group formed a strongly oriented hydrogen bond with the capsanthin ketone group. The existence of hydrogen bond interactions between HDES-3 and capsanthin was further confirmed [91]. This is consistent with the result in Section 3.7. These phenomena can be explained as the formation of hydrophobic cavities by the low-polarity mixture of thymol and menthol in HDES-3. Capsanthin is embedded via the van der Waals forces (as shown in Fig. 7a), and it collaborated with a small amount of hydrogen bonds (e.g., between the menthol –OH and capsanthin ketone groups) anchoring the solute [31], [92]. Although the number of hydrogen bonds is small, their average lifetime is long enough. The longer the average lifetime of the hydrogen bonds, the more stable they are [93], [94].

As shown in Fig. 7f, the average lifetime of the hydrogen bonds between HDES-3 and capsanthin is 211.80 ps, which is much longer than that in ethanol (53.92 ps). Therefore, the formation of the highly stable bond between HDES-3 and capsanthin may account for the higher extraction yield of HDES-3 system [95]. On the other hand, ethanol, due to its high polarity, formed a transient hydrogen bond network (numerous but short-lived). This hydrogen bond network disrupted the hydrophobic environment of capsanthin [96]. Similarly, excessive hydrogen bonds may create steric hindrance due to increased system polarity, thereby hindering the dissolution of non-polar compounds (such as capsanthin in this study). This phenomenon further explained the low efficiency of ethanol extraction [97].

Additionally, the interaction energy between capsanthin and the two solvents was calculated, and the results were showed in Fig. 7g. The interaction energy represents the strength of the interaction force between solvent and solute molecules, primarily comprising electrostatic and the van der Waals energies. Negative values denote attractive forces; the more negative the value, the stronger the attraction and the more stable the resulting complex [29], [53]. As shown in Fig. 7g, HDES-3 exhibits the most negative average interaction energy with capsanthin, with the van der Waals term outweighing the electrostatic term. This indicates that the binding degree of HDES-3 to capsanthin is the strongest. Van der Waals force is the main driving force, and it extracts capsanthin together with the assistance of hydrogen bond, in agreement with Section 3.7.3. This further explains the high extraction rate and strong stability of HDES-3.

Conclusion

In this study, a new green and efficient HDES (thymol: menthol = 1:2, HDES-3), was selected for the extraction of capsanthin. RSM was employed for process optimization. Under the optimal extraction conditions of 61 °C, 417 W, and 46 min, the yield of capsanthin reached a maximum of 14.58 mg/mL. The extraction kinetics study showed that the process conforms to Fick’s second law. Compared to ethanol and other HDESs, capsanthin extracted using HDES-3 exhibited the best stability. FTIR and SEM analyses demonstrated the existence of hydrogen bonds among HDES components, thereby confirming the successful formation of HDES. Moreover, HDES-3 has a stronger erosive ability, which significantly improves the dissolution efficiency of capsanthin. Furthermore, the extraction and stability mechanisms of capsanthin were further elucidated through DFT and MD simulation. The results show that the main binding forces between capsanthin and HDES-3 are the van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds, with the van der Waals forces playing a dominant role. Moreover, the hydrogen bonds between HDES-3 and capsanthin are markedly more stable, thereby enhancing capsanthin stability. The results confirmed that the thymol-menthol system may be a green and efficient alternative to organic solvents. This research was conducted solely at the laboratory level. Future efforts should focus on optimizing the recovery and purification of thymol:menthol HDES using cost-effective and efficient separation technologies to reduce production costs and improve environmental sustainability, while exploring its extraction compatibility with existing industrial capsanthin production lines to minimize equipment transformation costs. Resolving these challenges will lay a solid foundation for the large-scale industrial application of green HDES-based extraction technology in the capsanthin industry.
