Whole genome sequencing approach for the detection of rare protein-altering variants associated with familial male infertility
Authors: Muhammad Ajmal, Muhammad Riaz Khan, Arvand Akbari, Helen Castillo-Madeen, Jasvinder S Ahuja, Taqweem Ul Haq, Donald F Conrad, Aftab Ali Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhole-genome sequencing (WGS) is an efficient, accurate, and high-throughput technique used for the discovery of exonic as well as non-exonic variants causing different genetic anomalies, improving clinical management for the disease. This study was designed to evaluate pathogenic genetic variants related to male infertility in male infertile families in remote rural areas of Pakistan.MethodsFor the five families recruited, WGS was conducted on 14 patients and 6healthy controls. WGS was performed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA Nano Library Prep workflow protocol. DNA was assessed with TapeStation and sequenced using the TruSeq kit (Illumina). Bioinformatics tools were then used to identify potential causative variants, including indels and single-nucleotide variants.ResultsIn family 1, five homozygous nonsynonymous SNVs were identified in FMR1, PHF8, TOPBP1, and DAB2IP. These X-linked or recessive variants cause amino acid changes that affect key cellular pathways. In family 10, homozygous nonsynonymous SNVs were detected in FOS, MIR452, PDHA1, and SERPINB1, implicating roles in structural, transcriptional, metabolic, and synaptic processes. In family 11, a frameshift deletion in PRDM9 and nonsynonymous SNVs in KIAA1210, suggesting possible disruption of gene regulation and protein stability. In family 13, multiple nonsynonymous variants were identified in SPATA20, and G6PD,, with implications in reproductive function, angiogenesis, enzymatic activity, and neuromuscular disorders. SNV Variant was detected in SLC12A4 in Family 15.ConclusionThe study contributes to a systematic understanding of the problem and provides a basis for identifying causal variations in idiopathic cases of male infertility, setting targets for future therapeutic development.Keywords: Familial infertility, Genotyping, Next-generation sequencing, Whole genome sequencing

Introduction

In human life, reproduction is one of the central events linked with the pivotal aim of delight, totality, and family consolidation. A significant measure of effective reproduction in humans is fertility outcome [1]. Related to the human civilization, infertility is a key health problem that extremely impacts the psychological, social, and economic well-being of those experiencing it and their families as well [2]. Failure of vibrant couple to achieve pregnancy after twelve months of unprotected sexual intercourse is termed as infertility according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [3] and is documented as a disorder of the reproductive system by the International Committee for Monitoring Assisted Reproductive Technology (ICMART), as well as by the World Health Organization (WHO). Across the globe, almost 50 million couples are reported as victims of infertility. Infertility prevalence is very high, worldwide, affecting 8–12% of couples of reproductive age.

Pakistan, being the fifth-most populous country in the world, has a population of more than 249 million having trend of consanguineous marriages between first or second cousins due to local norms and traditions. Conservative estimates report that about 70% of marriages in Pakistan are consanguineous [4]. These marriages enhance the probability of homozygous variants in the offspring as compared to marriages between non-relatives. Offspring resulting from consanguineous marriages show about 10% higher incidence of inbred deformities, instigating multiple disorders. In Pakistan, the prevalence of primary infertility accounts for 4% of the total 22% reported previously [5]. However, this frequency may be higher in the local population, equating the total population and global prevalence (50 million couples) of infertility [6]. Both male and female factors are attributed to infertility, adversely distressing reproductive ability (WHO). Both partners equally contribute to the etiology of infertility [7, 8]. In seeking male infertility, half of the infertility cases are idiopathic.

Male infertility is also subject to several environmental factors like stress, lifestyle, eating habits, alcohol abuse, and smoking [9]. Previous studies have identified several candidate genes for male infertility [10]. The complete and accurate genetic information required for fertility maintenance is not yet determined. Recent genomic screening and homozygosity mapping were used for the detection of variants in inherited retinal disease in the same study population [11]. Functional studies will be required to validate the role of these detected variants.

A complex network of genes regulates all biological functions in a coordinated way [12]. Spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis are also tackled in this way; more than two thousand genes are reported to be entangled in the spermatogenesis process, and a huge number of genes are assumed to be required for its maintenance [13]. Sperm production from primordial germ cells is called spermatogenesis. Abnormal spermatogenesis results due to irregularity in the complex multi-step process of spermatogenesis [14]. Certain genes present on unrelated chromosomes can regulate gene expression on remote loci. Whole genome sequencing aids in discovering regulatory elements present on unrelated chromosomes that yet control the expression of genes involved in causing infertility [4]. Certain disrupted genes on autosomes can be enriched for undescribed human “knockouts” and are yet to be linked to the infertility phenotype. Tuba3a/3b knock-out mice completely lost germ cells at the early postnatal stages [15]. The current WGS study identifies a candidate variant (different genes) in related men suffering from infertility. This claim is further substantiated by an analysis of data from single-cell expression data from HISTA (https://conradlab.shinyapps.io/HISTA/). An online database of human testicular gene expression [16].

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria, recruitment of the families, and clinical characteristics

The Departmental Bioethical Committee (DBC), University of Malakand, has reviewed and approved the current study vide notification No. UoM/BT/BC/2019/20 dated July 4, 2019. Furthermore, the DBC was endorsed by the Advanced Studies and Research Board (ASRB) of the University of Malakand, Pakistan (Ref. No. UOM/Admin/2022/112 dated February 02, 2022). Written informed consent was taken from all patients and healthy controls of selected families according to the ethical standard outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [17]. Probands from each family were first identified through preclinical data collected in the local community of Malakand Division, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. Family members with a history of infertility were examined by an Andrologist and were recruited as shown in Fig. 1. The semen analysis was conducted in a healthcare laboratory as per World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. The semen examination was performed immediately after the liquefaction of semen and no longer than 60 min after ejaculation. The semen was examined microscopically, and the reports were generated accordingly. Blood sample collection and genomic DNA extraction

A total of 3–5 mL whole blood samples were collected from patients and healthy controls aseptically in an EDTA tube. Genomic DNA was extracted through the Phenol/Chloroform method [18], and the extracted DNA was confirmed through gel electrophoresis (Clever Scientific Ltd, UK) as reported previously [19].

Library preparation and sequencing for NGS

The Illumina TruSeq DNA Nano Library Prep workflow protocol was used for WGS. After checking the qualitiy of the extracted DNA, the samples were subjected to library preparation using the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Sonicator was used for the fragmentation of DNA using the S220 sonicator (Covaris). The fragmented DNA was subjected to end-repair and size selection (approximately 200–700 bp), followed by 3′-end adenylation and ligation of indexed sequencing adapters. The adapter-ligated fragments were subsequently amplified through a limited number of PCR cycles. The quality and quantity of the resulting libraries were then assessed before sequencing by the Bioanalyzer TapStation (Agilent). The libraries were normalized, pooled, and sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw base call files were then converted into FASTQ format using bcl2fastq (Illumina).

GATK pipelines for NGS data analysis

Raw FASTQ files were uploaded to the cloud environment, where they were processed into genomic Variant Call Format (gVCF) files. Briefly, this workflow follows steps analogous to the pipeline, encompassing read mapping and alignment, sorting and duplicate marking, variant calling, and variant quality score recalibration (VQSR). Single-family Variant Call Format (VCF) files containing SNVs and insertions/deletions (indels) were subsequently generated through joint genotyping. Furthermore, CNVs were detected using smoove (https://github.com/brentp/smoove).

Variant filtering and annotation

As previously reported [4], whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed according to the approved protocol [19]. The NGS data were filtered for rare recessive variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of < 0.01, CADD Score of > 20, Genotype; Homo, Disease Model; REC-Hom, Variant Location; Exonic, Gene Expression; Expressed in Testis and Literature; PubMed. The target regions were amplified in the candidate genes using conventional PCR [20]. Investigating the expression of candidate genes using the human infertility single-cell testis atlas (HISTA). It is an interactive web tool for a better understanding of human spermatogenesis through scRNA-Seq analysis. It represents the expression level and location of candidate biomarkers in different cells during spermatogenesis. With 26,093 high-quality cells taken from testis biopsies, the human infertility single-cell testis atlas (HISTA) web portal (2022 data release) (https://conradlab.shinyapps.io/HISTA/) [21] is an interactive web tool for understanding human spermatogenesis through scRNA-Seq analysis. HISTA also comprises machine-learning-derived gene modules that capture the entire transcriptional perspective of the testis tissue. We show how the use of these gene modules/components can simplify the highly complex data in HISTA, assist with the interpretation of genes with unknown functions, and allow for hypothesis testing. In the present study, the expression of different genes was investigated using HISTA as reported previously [19].

Investigation of nonsynonymous SNV in SPATA20, and G6PD

The SPATA20 is involved in spermatogenesis and reproductive function. Our study investigated a nonsynonymous SNV in the Spermatogenesis Associated 20 (SPATA20) gene, located on chromosome 17 (17q21.33, Genomic coordinates (GRCh38): 17:50,547,174 − 50,555,852) at position 50,551,552, with a total of 6 exons. The transcript is ENST00000297213.8. In this variant, guanine (G) is replaced by adenine (A) (G > A). This mutation is homozygous recessive and occurs in the exonic region, causes an amino acid alteration, and leads to a truncated protein.

In this analysis, we found a nonsynonymous variant (SNV) in the Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) gene, located on chromosome X (Xq28, Genomic coordinates (GRCh38): X:154,531,390 − 154,547,569) at position 154,534,362 A > G. It contains 13 exons. This exonic variant may potentially impact the function of the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) enzyme. This variant is hemizygous and follows an X-linked inheritance pattern.

Detection of the nonsynonymous variant in the SLC12A4

We identified a nonsynonymous SNV in the SLC12A4 gene, located on chromosome 16 (16q22.1, Genomic coordinates (GRCh38): 16:67,943,474 − 67,968,694) at position 67,946,459, characterized by G > A. This gene comprises 24 exons. This mutation leads to an amino acid alteration that may impact the function of the K-Cl cotransporter encoded by SLC12A4. This is a homozygous recessive variant. This variant could have pathogenic relevance, particularly in disorders related to ion transport or cellular osmoregulation.

Discussion

In the current study, we employed WGS to investigate genetic variants that are responsible for familial male infertility. By using comprehensive variant annotation, stringent bioinformatic tools, several novel, rare hemizygous and homozygous SNVs are detected. Several of the identified variants have high CADD scores ≥ 20, indicating that these changes may potentially affect the protein structure and function. CAVIN 2 may play a pivotal role in testosterone biosynthesis by activating protein kinase C PKC by Ca2 + influx into the cytoplasm [22]. In spermatogenesis and hormone production, Ca and intra-cellular Ca 2 + binding proteins are involved in the rat testis. Calbindin D-28 K and the calmodulin superfamily were localized in the Leydig cells, which are sites of testosterone synthesis. At all stages of development, in fetal-type Leydig cells and adult-type Leydig cells, Calbindin D-28 K-immunoreactivity was recorded. In developing spermatocytes and maturing spermatids, the presence of Calmodulin is evidenced by Calmodulin staining. The localization and appearance of these proteins point towards their role in Leydig cell function and spermatogenesis [23]. CALB 2 Knock-out mice show vulnerable fertility [22]. In humans, testicular cell biopsies CALB 2 is detected in immature Sertoli cells, which shows its involvement in spermatogenesis [24]. In a study where testis tissue samples were taken for localization of c-fos, phosphorylated c-fos. Immunohistochemistry showed its expression and localization in spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids, and in Sertoli cells [25].

The microRNA gene miR-452, like other microRNAs, may have a regulatory function with various genes responsible for spermatogenesis. A defective miRNA-mRNA complex leads to defective maturation and production of sperm, eventually leading to infertility [26]. A study designed to assess gene expression of miRNA in infertile males shows significantly high expression for miRNA-122 in azoospermia patients and oligospermia patients. In the mouse model, double knock-out of miR-34b/c and miR-449 results in severe disorder in spermatogenesis. Another study reveals disturbed spermatozoa maturation resulting in asthenospermia due to deletion of miR34b/c and miR-449 in mice (Papaioannou and Nef 2010). miRNA-191–5p is associated with sperm differentiation.

Our study has revealed several rare hemizygous variants in X-linked genes, including PDHA1 (19359639 A > C, G6PD (154534362 A > G) All these X-linked variants play a pivotal role in reproduction and fertility.

TJP2 stands for “Tight junction protein” and encodes zonula occludens, which is a member of the membrane–associated guanylate kinase homolog family. TJP2 is an integral protein bound to the actin cytoskeleton by adaptor proteins. Most prominent of which are claudins and occludins, mainly zonula occludens proteins ZO-1, ZO-2, and ZO-3, which are associated with the cytoplasmic surface of the tight junctions and organize a complex. Loss of tight junction integrity in Sertoli cells could disrupt the BTB, disrupting spermatogenesis, which may lead to infertility. These proteins are localized to the blood-testis barrier region, so ZO-2 plays a key role in blood-testis barrier integrity and male fertility [27]. Tight junctions between Sertoli cells make up the major component of the blood-testis barrier in the seminiferous epithelium. In CIS disturbed distribution of these junction proteins ZO-1 and ZO-2 in Sertoli cells reveals loss of function in the blood-testis barrier [28].

Drug interaction study on male mice reveals regulatory role of SLC12A4 and Mt-nd6 in sperm mitochondria along with Catsperb, Greb1, Mospd2, Esrrb, Specc1, and Sohlh1 linkage to estrogen response and sperm penetration function, so it can be concluded that some drugs alter the expression of mRNA in the reproductive system [29]. The RT-nested PCR indicates the presence of KCC3 SLC12A6, and KCC4 SLC12A7 in the mRNA of sperm, which strongly supports the role of these transporter/carrier proteins in RVD, thus aiding in the penetration of sperm into the egg [30].Furthermore, SNVs were also detected in genes such asSERPINB1. To summarize, WGS effectively identified novel and potentially pathogenic variants that are linked with male infertility in Pakistani families. Furthermore, the recurrence of shared variants across multiple families and their implication in cellular and reproductive processes support potential etiologies in idiopathic male infertility.

Despite the use of advanced NGS technology for discovering new and novel variants in the study population, several limitations were also noted. Limitations of the study includes a smaller sample size and a lack of functional validation of the detected variants. That makes it difficult to make a decisive conclusion about the genotype-phenotype correlations. Our analysis disclosed a set of rare and novel variants in both autosomal and X-linked genes. The findings further improve our understanding of the pathophysiology of male infertility and underscore novel gene-disease associations. Replication of the same experimental model on larger cohorts in vitro assays followed by validation through animal models, is required to elucidate the pivotal role of the identified variants and to establish their clinical significance.
