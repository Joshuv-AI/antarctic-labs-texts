The Interstellar Mapping And Acceleration Probe High Energy (IMAP-Hi) Neutral Atom Imager

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe IMAP-Hi Energetic Neutral Atom (ENA) Imager on NASA’s Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe (IMAP) mission (McComas et al. 2018a, 2025) is designed to measure ENAs from the global interaction between the heliosphere and the local interstellar medium (LISM). These ENAs are initially plasma ions of solar wind origin that are neutralized by charge exchange with the cold neutral atoms of LISM that freely flow through the heliosphere-LISM interaction region. IMAP-Hi consists of two identical single-pixel sensors, each covering the ENA spectral range from 0.44 keV to 15.6 keV over nine contiguous energy passbands and having an approximately conical field-of-view (FOV) of 4.1o full width at half maximum (FWHM). The Hi-45 sensor points 45o relative to the spacecraft spin axis from the antisunward direction; each spacecraft spin, it measures ENA intensity over a circular swath with half-cone angle 45o centered on the ecliptic plane. The Hi-90 sensor points 90o relative to the spin axis; each spacecraft spin, it measures ENA intensity over a great circle in the sky, sampling both the north and south ecliptic poles. As the IMAP spin vector is re-pointed daily toward the Sun, the ecliptic longitude of the swaths moves daily by ∼1o such that a full sky map is acquired by Hi-90 every six months and a complete low latitude (−45o to +45o) map is acquired by Hi-45 annually. The IMAP-Hi sensor design has direct heritage from the IBEX-Hi imager on the Interstellar Boundary Explorer (IBEX) mission, with substantial improvements in energy range, energy resolution, angular resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and, for ecliptic latitudes within ±45o, temporal resolution and exposure time. The global ENA maps acquired by IMAP-Hi partially overlap in energy and viewing with the ENA maps acquired by the IMAP-Lo and IMAP-Ultra ENA imagers, which we combine to answer fundamental questions about the structure and dynamics of the interaction of the heliosphere and the LISM.Keywords: Heliosphere, Space weather, Interstellar medium, ENAs, Solar wind, Plasma, IBEX, IMAP

Introduction

Complex and dynamic plasma domains are formed by the collision of the continuously outflowing solar wind with the cold plasma of the local interstellar medium (LISM) through which the Sun is moving. The scientific objective of the Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe (IMAP) mission is to discover the global interaction between the heliosphere and the LISM (McComas et al. 2018a, 2025) by addressing the following core scientific objectives:

1. Improve understanding of the composition and properties of the LISM;

2. Advance understanding of the temporal and spatial evolution of the boundary region in which the solar wind and the interstellar medium interact;

3. Identify and advance the understanding of processes related to the interactions of the magnetic field of the Sun and the LISM;

4. Identify and advance understanding of particle injection and acceleration processes near the Sun, in the heliosphere, and the heliosheath

A key signature that enables these discoveries is the population of energetic neutral atoms (ENAs), which are formed by charge exchange of hot plasma ions (mostly H+) of solar wind origin with cold neutral atoms of the LISM that are inert to electric and magnetic fields and thus flow into the heliosphere due to the Sun’s relative motion through the LISM at a speed of 26 km/s (Bzowski et al. 2014; McComas et al. 2015; Wood et al. 2015). ENAs, which follow ballistic trajectories and can be detected in the inner heliosphere by IMAP, retain unique information about the properties of their source plasma. ENAs are therefore crucial messengers used to remotely map the plasma structures of the outer heliosphere and nearby regions of the LISM. The time evolution of ENA emissions provides insight into dynamics of these structures over the solar cycle in response to dynamic changes in the solar wind, and in response to powerful, dynamic structures embedded in the solar wind such as coronal mass ejections (CMEs) and stream interactions (e.g., McComas et al. 2020, 2024).

The neutral atoms of the LISM permeate the inner heliosphere, where some are ionized and entrained in the expanding solar wind, forming a suprathermal pickup ion (PUI) population. At the termination shock located at 94 AU and 84 AU along the Voyager 1 and 2 trajectories, respectively (Stone et al. 2005, 2008), the core and suprathermal solar wind, as well as plasma associated with any transients such as CMEs and merged interaction regions (MIRs), are slowed and heated. Between the termination shock and the heliopause (located ∼122 AU and ∼119 AU from the Sun along the Voyager 1 & 2 trajectories, respectively, during each spacecraft’s passage through the heliopause (Stone et al. 2013, 2019)) lies the heliosheath, a layer rich with dynamic plasma structures that evolve over space, time, and energy. Major plasma structures (Figs. 1, 2) discovered so far by the IBEX-Hi ENA imager (Funsten et al. 2009b) on the IBEX mission (McComas et al. 2009a) include the heliospheric nose (McComas et al. 2009b, 2020), heliotail (Zirnstein et al. 2017a), and ribbon (McComas et al. 2009b; Funsten et al. 2009a).

The incredibly circular ribbon of enhanced ENA emission discovered by the IBEX mission (McComas et al. 2009a) is believed to be centered on the direction of the interstellar magnetic field (McComas et al. 2009a; Schwadron et al. 2009; Funsten et al. 2009a). The superior spatial resolution and signal-to-noise performance of IMAP-Hi will enable clear understanding of the sharp intensity gradients of the ribbon to test hypotheses of its origin. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the ribbon intensity distribution changes dramatically over the five energy passbands of the IBEX-Hi ENA imager (Funsten et al. 2009b). Notably, the origin of the ribbon has yet to be definitively identified; Imaging the ribbon over the energy ranges of the suite of IMAP ENA imagers and with higher accuracy and precision than IBEX will enable understanding of how the ribbon structure changes over energy and whether it is correlated with the putative belt of ENA emission observed by Cassini/INCA (Krimigis et al. 2009; Dialynas et al. 2017).

IBEX-Hi also demonstrated that the outer heliosphere is highly sensitive to solar wind variations, such as observing a strong correlation of the ENA intensity with the solar cycle, in both the globally distributed flux (GDF) and the ribbon (e.g., McComas et al. 2020, 2024; Reisenfeld et al. 2016, 2019; Zirnstein et al. 2017b; Noh et al. 2025). The responsiveness of the heliosheath to solar modulation was dramatically demonstrated when a strong increase in the outgoing solar wind dynamic pressure observed at 1 AU in 2014 collided with the heliosheath, resulting in a rapid rise of ENA intensity in 2016 (Fig. 2, left panel; McComas et al. 2020; Walia et al. 2025) and tracked from the heliospheric nose (closest to the Sun), to the heliospheric flanks (further away), and toward the tail regions (farthest away). Indeed, the delayed correlation between time variations in the outgoing solar wind flow and the return ENA signal (McComas et al. 2018b) provides a method for “sounding” the distance to ENA emission source region, enabling the construction of a 3D model of the heliospheric boundary (Reisenfeld et al. 2021). See Reisenfeld et al. (2026) for a full discussion of the outer heliosphere science IMAP achieves through the observation of ENAs.

The suite of ENA imagers on IMAP will provide exquisite global ENA intensity maps with unprecedented angular resolution, energy range, energy resolution, time resolution, sensitivity, and signal-to-noise ratio, the last of which is of intense consideration for instrument design due to the extremely dim ENA intensity at 1 AU. The differences in energy ranges covered by IMAP-Lo (Schwadron et al. 2025), IMAP-Hi, and IMAP-Ultra (Gkioulidou et al. 2026) are prescribed by the most effective and efficient methods for detection and measurement of the dim ENA intensity. By acquiring global maps of the spectral distribution of ENA intensities emitted from the outer heliosphere and nearby LISM, the combined ENA imagers of IMAP collectively span 0.01 to 300 keV.

The IMAP-Hi imager measures ENAs with intrinsic angular resolution 4.1o FWHM across nine contiguous energy passbands over an energy range 0.44–15.5 keV, which covers the energy range of the key components of the solar wind that inflate the outer heliosphere: the core solar wind typically ∼440 km/s (∼1 keV for protons, PUIs at up to twice the solar wind speed and are the seed population for nonlinear acceleration processes at the termination shock and beyond, and the bulk plasma of solar wind transients. All these plasma populations are heated by the termination shock and by dynamic processes across the heliosheath. IMAP-Hi provides superior performance relative to IBEX-Hi, enabling deep understanding of the major plasma structures of the outer heliosphere and their dynamics, as well as providing crucial context for measurements of interstellar material (Szalay et al. 2025).

IMAP-Hi Overview and Measurement Requirements

The design of the IMAP-Hi ENA imager is optimized for the energy range that covers both the core and suprathermal components of the solar wind and is thus central to answering key science objectives of IMAP. IMAP-Hi consists of two identical sensors, each a single pixel camera with 4.1o (FWHM) field-of-view (FOV). This FOV enables precision measurement across the narrow and comparatively intense ENA emission of the circular ribbon. Similar to IBEX-Hi viewing, the boresight FOV of the Hi-90 sensor is oriented 90o with respect to the spacecraft spin axis, thus sampling a great circle swath of 4.1 over each spacecraft spin. In contrast, the Hi-45 sensor is oriented 45o from the anti-sunward direction of the spacecraft spin axis, thus sampling a conical swath of width 4.1o FWHM over a 45o cone angle over each spacecraft spin. As the IMAP spin vector is re-pointed daily back toward the Sun, the ecliptic longitude of the Hi-45 and Hi-90 swaths move daily by ∼1o.

Every six months, Hi-90 acquires a full sky map in which half the map is from events detected in the ram direction (within 90o of the IMAP spacecraft velocity vector) and the other half is from events detected in the anti-ram direction. Hi-45 annually acquires a complete sky map between ecliptic latitudes −45o to +45o. The additional Hi-45 sensor for IMAP-Hi provides key improvements over the single IBEX-Hi sensor: Hi-45 and Hi-90 combine to double the single-sensor geometric factor; Hi-45 provides additional viewing at lower ecliptic latitudes (within ±45o) at which key features such as the nose, tail, and a majority of the ribbon are observed, but which were significantly undersampled by IBEX-Hi relative to the poles; and Hi-45 provides additional temporal viewing at lower latitudes facilitating the observation of temporal variation of plasma structures over finer time scales than achievable by only Hi-90. The next section describes in more detail the IMAP-Hi imager subsystems and their unique designs that maximize sensitivity and minimize noise and background to levels sufficient to view the faint ENA signal.

The IMAP-Hi Imager

IMAP-Hi consists of two identical single pixel sensors with large annular apertures pointed in different directions relative to the spacecraft spin axis. Figure 4 shows a cross-sectional illustration of an IMAP-Hi sensor, which is divided into five subsystems that are sequentially encountered by an ENA as it traverses the sensor. Each sensor has a separate Ebox Subsystem that includes low-voltage and high-voltage power supplies, event processing and classification electronics, command and data handling (CDH) electronics, and the electrical interface with the spacecraft. IMAP-Hi draws extensive heritage from the IBEX-Hi imager (Funsten et al. 2009b), although transformational modifications and lessons learned from IBEX-Hi have enabled major advances in IMAP-Hi performance, as shown in Table 1.

Cross sectional illustration of one of two identical sensors of the IMAP-Hi ENA Imager. The sensor consists of five subsystems sequentially encountered by an ENA that is successfully detected

Following an ENA as it transits the sensor, it first encounters the Deflector Subsystem, whose sole purpose is to reject ambient charged particles from entering the Collimator System within the sensor FOV. This is performed by electrostatic deflection of charged particles into pocketed sidewalls. Next, the Collimator Subsystem consists of stacked, co-aligned plates, each with an identical, dense array of hexagonal apertures, forming channels that define the approximately conical FOV with 4.1o FWHM. The ENA then traverses the Charge Conversion Subsystem, which utilizes ultrathin carbon foils mounted on high transmission Ni grids to ionize a fraction of ENAs. Positively ionized ENAs subsequently enter the Electrostatic Energy Analysis (ESA) Subsystem, which consists of nested toroidal analyzer plates, whose geometries and biases define the energy passband of ENAs that can enter the Detector Subsystem.

The Detector Subsystem consists of two sequential drift regions (Stage 1 and Stage 2) for two independent measurements of an ENA’s speed via time-of-flight (TOF); in practice, up to four independent events are detected, and their time differences correlate with the speed of an ionized ENA in Stages 1 and 2. Stage 1, biased to −8 kV to accelerate ionized ENAs and thus increase detection efficiency, has an entrance foil (Foil 1, “Start”), an exit foil (Foil 2, “Stop”), and two channel electron multiplier (CEM) detectors to detect secondary electrons (SEs) emitted from these two foils. The SEs are electrostatically collected using a cross-trajectory electric field geometry (Funsten et al. 2013b) so that SEs from the exit surface of Foil 1 are uniquely detected by CEM A, and SEs emitted from the entrance surface of Foil 2 are uniquely detected by CEM B. Stage 2 has a microchannel plate (MCP) detector that detect SEs emitted by the exit surface of Foil 2 and also detects the ENA after it has traversed the drift region of Stage 2. The complexity of the Detector Subsystem is driven by the need to unambiguously detect the extremely dim ENA intensity within a large, persistent background of penetrating cosmic rays. These subsystems are now described in detail.

Deflector Subsystem: Rejecting Ambient Charged Particles

The Deflector Subsystem is designed to block ENAs with trajectories significantly outside of the Hi single pixel FOV as well as to prevent ambient charged particles with energy-per-charge below 18 keV/q from reaching the Collimator Subsystem within its FOV. As shown in Fig. 5, the subsystem consists of two cylindrically symmetric electrodes immediately inside and outside of the annular FOV defined by the Collimator Subsystem.

Cross section of the Deflector, Collimator, and Charge Conversion Subsystems. The fins of the Deflector Subsystem lie 1 mm from a 5.3o acceptance zone defined at the inner and outer edges of the annular collimator aperture

An important objective for highly efficient rejection of off-angle ENAs and ambient charged particles is their removal from the FOV without direct scattering from a subsystem surface into the collimator. Thus, the electrodes each incorporate nine fins of thickness 1.27 mm that form deep pockets for trapping these particles and for minimizing the surface area for direct scattering into the collimator. The fins, whose lengths decrease with increasing distance from the collimator, extend within 1 mm of a 5.3o acceptance zone from the inner and outer edges of the annular collimator aperture as shown in Fig. 5. This provides sufficient margin to not obstruct the collimator FOV and to block incoming particle trajectories outside of the acceptance zone. Using a 3D model of the Deflector Subsystem, the fin lengths (and thus pocket depths) and their separations were optimized by simulation of a uniform 2π sr intensity of ions and electrons. The ends of the fins were machined to 0.127 mm radius and tapered at 14o on the side facing space to minimize the amount of scattering surface. The fins were nickel plated (∼10 μm) followed by a ∼1 μm layer of gold plating.

For ambient charged particle deflection, the inner and outer deflector electrodes are operated at −6.9 kV and +6.1 kV, respectively, for which no ions or electrons with energy-per-charge E/q≤ 18 keV reach the collimator with trajectories that lie within the collimator FOV. The unbalanced biases are optimized to minimize the electric field projected outside the instrument aperture and to ensure the net projected field is slightly positive to repel ionized outgassing species in the vicinity of the aperture. The inner deflector electrode stack is 3.0 mm shorter than the outer electrode stack to minimize the risk of field emission of electrons from the edge of the inner electrode fin closest to the collimator. The unbalanced electrode biases as well as of the different inner and outer deflection plate radii and lengths result in a small difference in the maximum rejection energies of positive ions and electrons.

The Deflector Subsystem represents a major improvement over IBEX-Hi, which incorporated ambient electron rejection (E/q≤ 120 eV) by biasing inner and outer ring electrodes at the sensor entrance to −1.4 kV followed by ambient ion rejection (E/q≤ 7 keV) by biasing the entire Collimator Subsystem to +7 kV. These voltages were reduced during IBEX commissioning from initial, higher design values to reduce both the deflection of solar wind ions into the sensor and ion contamination in the highest energy passbands, while remaining sufficiently high to minimize the “ion gun” background, in which ambient electrons ionize atomic and molecular volatiles and adsorbates around the collimator, that are then accelerated toward the conversion foils and masquerade as heliospheric ENAs (McComas et al. 2014). The IMAP-Hi design, with separate Deflector and Collimator Subsystems, mitigates both the solar wind ion background and the ion gun background.

Additionally, a large sunshade, located on IMAP’s sun-facing spacecraft deck, shields the sensor from solar wind over a large range of incoming angles to further limit backgrounds from deflected solar wind. The shade extends beyond the Hi-90 bay such that the angle from the tip of the shade to the most anti-sunward edge of the sensor is 12o. Because the spin axis is pointed into the aberrated mean solar wind direction (∼4o off-sun pointing) with a 3-σ margin of ±3.5o, solar wind incident up to ±8.5o from the mean solar wind direction is shielded from impinging on the Hi-90 aperture.

Collimator Subsystem: Defining the Field-of-View

The Collimator Subsystem defines the sensor FOV and derives direct heritage from the designs of IBEX-Hi (Funsten et al. 2009b), IBEX-Lo (Fuselier et al. 2009), and IMAP-Lo (Schwadron et al. 2025). It uses a stacked set of 21 plates, each 0.05 mm thick with an array of identical close-packed hexagonal holes with 2.70 mm center-to-center cell spacing (pitch). The plates are separated by controlled gap distances to prevent off-angle ENA trajectories from traversing adjacent hex cell apertures and entering the Charge Conversion Subsystem. The Collimator Subsystem also limits access into the sensor of UV light, to which the detectors are sensitive.

The collimator has been optimized to maximize the ENA transmission. In comparison to round or square aperture arrays, the hexagonal aperture array geometry maximizes the collimator transmission and thus the ENA throughput. The open hexagonal cell geometry is w=2.25 mm between opposite sides and 2.60 mm between opposite vertices, with a line thickness d=0.45 mm between adjacent hex cells. The FOV, designed for ∼4o FWHM, is determined solely by the hexagon geometry and the distance h=26.35 mm between the first and last collimator plates. An open hexagonal cell has an area 4.384 mm2 and solid angle FOV of 0.0063 sr. With this geometry, the maximum polar angle of the FOV relative to the boresight direction is 5.64o, which corresponds to an ENA trajectory traversing opposite hexagonal vertices of the first and last plates of the collimator stack. The azimuthal-averaged (over 2π sr) polar angle probability distribution PPSF(θ) of the point spread function, normalized to unit at its boresight direction, is shown in Fig. 6(b). The resulting FWHM of the FOV as defined by PPSF(θ) is 4.05o.

Immediately in front of the first collimator plate encountered by an ENA is the precision-milled “pre-collimator” with trapezoid-shaped ribs around the perimeter of the hexagonal apertures. With a height of 1.85 mm, these ribs block all ENAs and charged particles incident on the collimator at angles θ>57o relative to the central boresight (θ=0o) of the collimator FOV. The hexagonal holes of the pre-collimator are larger than the collimator plates and thus do not contribute to defining the collimator FOV. The pre-collimator surfaces have no line-of-sight through the collimator, so particles scattered from the pre-collimator cannot enter the foil subsystem.

ENAs and charged particles incident on the collimator completely outside the FOV (tan(θ) ≥d/h) but not blocked by the pre-collimator could traverse adjacent hexagonal columns and, possibly, enter the next subsystem. These trajectories have been eliminated by careful spacing of the plates in a specific geometric sequence, in which the largest plate separations are at the exit and entry of the collimator, and separation distances decrease toward the center of the stack, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The 8 plates with the smallest separation of h1=0.4 mm are placed at the center of the collimator, where h1<d/ tanθmax and θmax is the largest angle of incidence of ENAs that must be rejected. From this set of interior plates, additional plates are alternately stacked toward the entrance and exit of the collimator with increasing plate spacing according to hi+1 = hi(w+d)/w, with the largest spacing of 4 mm at the exit plate.

With a fixed angular acceptance and the annular aperture area defined by the carbon foil panes in the Charge Conversion Subsystem, the annular acceptance area linearly decreases from the sensor entrance to the conversion foils. This change is clear for the Deflector Subsystem (Fig. 5) and is also incorporated into the Collimator Subsystem: the hexagonal array of first 19 plates extends between inner radius 141.0 mm and outer radius 169.0 mm, whereas the hex array in the second-to-last and last plates lie between inner radii 142.8 and 143.2 mm and outer radii 167.2 and 166.8 mm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 6.

Precision alignment of the collimator and pre-collimator plates during assembly was achieved using alignment pins together with in situ digital microscope imaging. Optical comparator measurements were taken immediately after collimator assembly to assess performance. Measurements of the Hi-45 collimator boresight accuracy resulted in θx=−0.0057 0.016o and θy=+0.002o± 0.052o relative to the zˆ axis, which is perpendicular to the mounting surface of the collimator to the Hi baseplate. As shown in Fig. 7(b), θx is the offset angle in direction xˆ towards Quadrant 3 that bisects hexagonal cells through the cell sides, and θy is the offset angle in the direction yˆ (orthogonal to xˆ and zˆ) that bisects hexagonal cells at two opposing vertices. Measurements of the Hi-90 boresight accuracy are θx=−0.105 0.049o and θy=+0.061o± 0.039o.

Optical tests of the Hi-45 and Hi-90 collimators resulted in FWHM of 4.14o (+0.04o/-0.06o). Because of the slight FOV asymmetry and extended tails of the PSF, both due to the hexagonal cell geometry, we use 4.1o FWHM to characterize the average FOV width. This result is a significant advance over the 6.5o FWHM imaging resolution of IBEX-Hi and will enable spatially resolving the sharp features of key plasma structures observed by IBEX-Hi, particularly within the ribbon.

The geometrical transparency T0 of the collimator is determined using T0 = (1 + d/w)−2, which yields T0=69.44%+0.74%. Here, the uncertainties are found from the etching tolerance (± 0.01 mm) and alignment tolerance (± 0.01 mm). T0 is slightly reduced by 0.53% due to microscopically jagged edges around the perimeter of the hexagonal holes, yielding a net collimator transparency TC=68.92%+0.74%. The geometric factor of the collimator is 1.18 (+0.02/-0.03) cm2sr, which accounts for collimator transmission, losses due to quadrant spokes, incomplete hexagons at the inner and outer edges of the annular hexagonal array, and shadowing at the edges.

Charge Conversion Subsystem: ENA Ionization

ENAs that successfully transit the Collimator Subsystem enter the Charge Conversion Subsystem, which converts ENAs to positive ions by transmission through an ultrathin carbon foil. A total of 15 foil frames, each having two panes of foils mounted on a Ni support grid, are distributed in a circular array as shown in Fig. 7. The open panes have inner and outer radii of 144.3 mm and 165.7 mm, respectively. The center rib between the two panes is 1.78 mm wide, and the two outer pane walls subtend 22.5o. The open area of each pane is 5.604 cm2, which corresponds to a total aperture area of 168.1 cm2 over the 30 panes of a sensor. Including the 70% transmission of the Ni support grids, the unobstructed sensor aperture area of the Charge Conversion Subsystem is therefore 117.7 cm2.

The carbon foils, acquired from ACF Metals, are nominal 0.5-0.8 μg cm−2 and mounted on 333 line-per-inch (lpi) pure Ni grids acquired from Thin Metal Parts. The support grids have nominal 12 μm linewidth, ∼12 μm thickness, and 70% transmission. The Ni grid was glued with Ablestik 967-1 silver epoxy onto a stainless steel frame, and the carbon foil floated onto the grid.

The probability of ENAs exiting the foil as H+ is shown in Fig. 8 based on measurements for IBEX-Hi foils (Funsten et al. 2009b) and from Funsten et al. (1993). The ionization probability ranges from 0.020 at 0.4 keV to 0.35 at 20 keV. The discrepancy of the data sets is not relevant to determination of the IMAP-Hi geometric factor, which is derived through lab calibration of the flight instruments using a well-characterized H0 beam. The significant increase in the probability of ionization with increasing incident energy is fortuitous, because the ENA intensity from the outer heliosphere significantly decreases with increasing energy. Energy loss of H in carbon foils, which was a factor used to optimize the energy passband voltages, has been characterized by Allegrini et al. (2006, 2015). The dashed line of Fig. 8 is a 4th order polynomial fit to all data for E0≥ 0.9 keV, with fit parameters (of decreasing power) 2.2141 × 10−6, −1.2638 × 10−4, 2.0389 × 10−3, 6.5251 × 10−3, 5.7847 × 10−2. This fit enables estimation of the probability of incident H0 exiting the foil as H+ over the incident H0 energy range 0.9-25 keV. For E0<0.9 keV, the ionization probability increases linearly with increasing energy (Funsten et al. 2009b).

This ESA geometry azimuthally focuses ionized ENAs throughout the first half of deflection, followed by azimuthal defocusing throughout the second half of deflection.

The radius of the electro-optic surface of the inner (outer) ESA plate is 5.32 cm (8.34 cm), with the center of this radius located 88.3 mm (87.6 mm) from the central symmetry axis of the ESA plates and 8.17 cm (11.53 cm) below the plane of the conversion foils. The interior surfaces of the ESA plates are serrated with hemispherical troughs of radius 0.76 mm with depth 0.38 mm. The serrations are concentric relative to the symmetry axis of the sensor and are thus perpendicular to the path of ionized ENAs as they traverse the ESA. The serrations, as well as the Ebonol C coating of the surfaces of the ESA plates (Hooks et al. 2024), minimize scattering into the Detector Subsystem of (i) UV and soft X-rays to which the detectors are sensitive, (ii) ENAs that traverse the foils but are not ionized, and (iii) ionized ENAs that lie outside the energy passband.

The inner and outer ESA plates can be independently biased up to −9.0 kV and +5.0 kV, respectively, which allows flexibility in defining both the central energy and width of the energy passband. The plate voltages are set for adjacent passbands to be contiguous, with matching response at their FWHMs. Because of the higher voltages applied to the IMAP-Hi ESA plates relative to the IBEX-Hi design, and thus their potential susceptibility to electrostatic discharge from high electric fields, all edges of the ESA plates were machined with teardrop-shaped 1.5-mm radius.

Detector Subsystem: Robust ENA Detection

As shown in Fig. 10(a), the Detector Subsystem consists of two drift regions, each of which can measure the speed of an ionized ENA via TOF. Stage 1 has an entrance foil (Foil 1) and an exit foil (Foil 2) from which secondary electrons (SEs) are generated by passage of an ionized ENA (Ritzau and Baragiola 1998; Allegrini et al. 2003; McComas et al. 2004). SEs emitted from the back surface of Foil 1 are electrostatically focused onto the entrance funnel of the CEM detector A (CEM A); an event detected by CEM A registers a Start pulse A for the Stage 1 TOF. SEs emitted from the front surface of Foil 2 are focused onto CEM B and register a “Stop” pulse B, such that the Stage 1 TOF is the time difference between A and B events. Foils 1 and 2 were nominal ∼1 μg/cm2 with measured foil constant kF=16.1 keV deg for Hi-90 and 18.2 keV deg for Hi-45. The foil coverage was >99.4% for Hi-90 and >99.6% for Hi-45.

(a) The Detector Subsystem consists of two independent drift regions (Stage 1 and Stage 2) for up to four independent, time-correlated events from a single ionized ENA. (b) Cross-sectional view of an electro-optical simulation of secondary electron trajectories in Stages 1 and 2. In Stage 1, SEs are accelerated from the large Foil 1 area (56 mm diameter) to the small (10 × 20 mm) input area of CEM A (similarly for SEs from Foil 2 to CEM B). These simulations were used to optimize the Stage 1 drift region geometry, yielding a SE collection probability of >97% on each CEM detector

As illustrated in Fig. 10(b), Stage 1 incorporates an electro-optical design that focuses SEs emitted from either end of a drift region onto a small detector area using crossing trajectories, which was previously developed and used on the HOPE mass spectrometer on the Van Allen Probes (Funsten et al. 2013b). Critical to this design is the “U”-shaped sidewalls of the drift region that efficiently extract and project SEs onto the CEM detectors, which have a rectangular funnel entrance area of 10 × 20 mm (Sjuts Model KBL A2010; a 70 lpi Ni grid with 90% transmission was clamped within a faceplate assembly directly in front of the CEM funnel to optimize its response). The entrance and exit of the Stage 1 drift region are 56.0-mm-diameter carbon foils mounted on 333 lpi Ni grids (identical to the grids used for the conversion foils). The round portion of the “U” can is 58.2- mm-diameter, and the distance between the entrance and exit foils is 52.0 mm. From SIMION (Dahl 2000) simulations, this geometry enables collection efficiency of SEs of ∼98.7% from Foil 1 to CEM A and ∼97.8% from Foil 2 to CEM B. The simulations also provided transit times for SEs to travel from the emission point on Foil 1 (or Foil 2) to the entrance of CEM A (or CEM B); the time lag and time dispersion associated with electron travel are estimated to be 9.2 ± 5.2 ns for A events and 8.2 ± 4.2 ns for B events.

Stage 1 is biased to −8 kV for three reasons. First, it generates a sufficiently strong electric field between the drift region and CEM detectors to efficiently and quickly extract and focus SEs into the CEMs, whose entrances are nominally biased to −4.5 kV. Second, it accelerates positively ionized ENAs to higher energies, resulting in less angular scattering in the foils (Funsten et al. 1993) and higher secondary electron emission yield from the foils (Ritzau and Baragiola 1998; Allegrini et al. 2003) over the entire IMAP-Hi energy range. Third, the acceleration of ionized ENAs into the Detector Subsystem acts as electrostatic proximity focusing to (a) counteract azimuthal defocusing by the toroidal ESA and (b) align ionized ENA trajectories more perpendicular to the foil mounting grid, which reduces vignetting due to the high aspect ratio of the grid pane thickness-to-width ratio (both 12 μm).

For reference, after this acceleration, the TOF of H+ travelling straight through the Stage 1 drift region is 40.7, 37.6, and 28.0 ns for incident ENA energies 0.5, 1, and 10 keV, respectively. Because of variation of H+ trajectories (and thus variation in path length) in Stages 1 and 2 as well as the time dispersion of SEs from their emission location to the CEM detector, the TOF measurements do not improve knowledge of ENA energy that is provided by the ESA passband setting.

An additional feature incorporated into Stage 1 is a circular disk held by four radial arms of width 1.0 mm, centered on the symmetry axis of the drift region, 1.7 mm from Foil 1, and located immediately above the spindle of the outer ESA. With a diameter of 24.0 mm, it mitigates the potential “ion gun” background generated from emission of SEs from the front surface of Foil 1, their acceleration to the spindle of the outer ESA, and their ionization of ambient volatiles species or adsorbates on the spindle. These ions are subsequently accelerated into Stage 1 and can masquerade as a robust and indistinguishable ENA background. The blocking disk, which results in a 3% loss of transmission of ionized ENAs into Stage 1 due to its radial arms, was incorporated specifically to block this background.

The Stage 2 drift region spans the back surface of Foil 2 to the MCP detector entrance surface, which is nominally biased to −3 kV. An ENA traversing Foil 2 can generate SEs from its back surface; the SEs are accelerated to the MCP detector and register a first event C1. The ENA continues to the MCP, registering a second event C2. The distance between Foil 2 and the input surface of the MCP detector is 25.9 mm, half the drift distance of Stage 1. The MCPs are from Photonis with a chevron configuration, 86.6-mm OD, 75-mm active diameter, 0.61-mm thickness, 10- μm pore diameter, 12- μm pitch, and 12o channel bias.

Stage 2 has subtle features that are important for interpreting the data. First, a C1 event generated by an SE from the back surface of Foil 2 occurs simultaneously with a B event registered by SEs produced at the Foil 2 front surface. However, because the travel time to the MCP by SEs associated with C1 events is ∼1.1 ns, a C1 event is detected before the B event. Furthermore, the avalanche propagation time is much longer in CEMs than for MCPs, resulting in a time delay of a detected event of ∼26 ns for the Stage 1 CEMs but only ∼1 ns for the Stage 2 MCP (Fraser 1990; Henkel et al. 2010; Gys 2015). These intrinsic timing features are accommodated in the event processing to ensure proper event timing for meaningful TOF measurements. Additionally, an ENA can exit Foil 2 as an ion or neutral, resulting in a charge dependence of the TOF in Stage 2 due to the 5 kV difference across the drift region. This dependence on exit charge state is considered in analysis of the detected events.

The voltages across each detector (CEM A, CEM B, and MCP C) are independently commandable. The rear voltages of each detector can be commanded independently of the front voltages so that the electro-optics of the Detector Subsystem are independent of detector biases. CEMs A and B share a common front voltage.

The Direct Events (DEs) from an ENA traversing the Detector Subsystem cover a range of combinations of A, B, C1, and C2 events, with different combinations considered valid if their TOF lies within a range defined in a look up table (LUT). Generally, more combinations provide higher quality data; for example, DEs with three events (triples) generally have considerably lower backgrounds than DEs with only two events (doubles). Additionally, combinations that include an A event span the longest drift region (Stage 1) and thus have longer and better-ordered TOF values. Therefore, valid DE events are classified based on quality of information and can be placed in the telemetry stream in prioritized order. The three classes of DEs defined in the default onboard look-up table (LUT) are Gold (ABCC, ABC, ACC, AB), Silver (BCC, AC, CC), Bronze (BC), and Other, which includes any DE events that do not pass the onboard qualification scheme. Note that “CC” is shorthand for two events (C1 and C2) detected by the MCP detector, and a single event C1 with no subsequent C2 event is denoted as a solitary “C”. Bronze events typically have the highest background from random stimulation of an MCP channel, which (i) generates a C1 event, (ii) can ionize adsorbates or volatiles in a channel, such that ions are accelerated to Foil 2 (ion feedback), which (iii) can subsequently generate SEs from Foil 2 and generate C2 and B events. Bronze and Silver events are useful as instrument performance diagnostics and are not currently baselined for use in primary scientific data products.

In summary, the Detector Subsystem generates up to four events (A, B, C1, C2), for which the time difference between A and B is the TOF in Stage 1, and the time difference between C1 and C2 is the TOF in Stage 2. These four time-correlated events, for which a subset of two (“doubles”) or three (“triples”) detected events still provides unique and rich information, represents an enormous improvement over the IBEX-Hi triple coincidence scheme that did not incorporate TOF beyond simple short and long TOF windows and could not eliminate a significant, ubiquitous background due to penetrating cosmic rays.

Front End Electronics

The Front-End Electronics (FEE) board is housed in its own enclosure within the Detector Subsystem. As illustrated in Fig. 11, the FEE receives detected event signals from the CEM A (A event), CEM B (B event) and the MCP (C1, C2 events) detectors, measures the relative time-of-arrival between detected events, and reports these values via DE data packets to the Ebox for further processing and packetization. Seven TOF combinations can be measured: A-to-B, B-to-A, A-to-C1, C1-to-A, B-to-C1, C1-to-B and C1-to-C2. The first six are measured to a resolution of 1 ns, and the last to 0.5 ns. An adjustable programmable coincidence window, nominally 1 μs, is used to group the TOF measurements as a valid DE. The FEE is capable of computing TOF for a given DE within 2 μs. All detected events and TOF combinations are individually counted in the FEE and reported to the Ebox for logical event analysis and performance monitoring.

FEE block diagram. The FEE accepts detector signals and measures time differences between the detected events arriving within a nominal 1 μs window

The FEE is powered by +3.3V, +5V, −5V and +12V secondary power from the Low-Voltage Power System (LVPS) in the Ebox. An incoming command channel operates at 115.2 kbps and carries control messages from the CDH to the FEE. The three event signals are each shaped via constant-fraction discriminator (CFD) circuits, with commandable thresholds using digital-to-analog converters (DACs). Each of these three analog chains of circuits is implemented by back-to-back diodes to limit the amplitude of the incoming signal (and thus protect the circuits from high voltage transients), followed by two AD8001 amplifiers and a AD8561 comparator with threshold, to a 2N2222 transistor that converts the comparator output to 2.2 V C-level logic, with that final output signal for the signal chain input into a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA). Using the single MCP signal chain as input, the FPGA uses logic to distinguish consecutive pulses to internally generate separate MCP C1 and C2 signals intended to correspond to secondary electron and H ion/neutral atom arrival, respectively.

The timing relationship of the detector signals is measured within the FPGA, which uses logic to determine which of the A, B, or C1 signals is the first to arrive, and then measures TOF between the first detected event and later-arriving events within the coincidence window opened by the first detected event. The FPGA then reports three of the seven possible TOF combinations, thus fully defining the timing relationships as a record of a Direct Event: a CEM A start yields TOF values of A-to-B, A-to-C1, and C1-to-C2; a CEM B start yields B-to-A, B-to-C1 and C1-to-C2; and an MCP C1 start yields C1-to-A, C1-to-B and C1-to-C2. A valid DE record is generated if at least one TOF pair can be measured within the coincidence window. A DE record, which consists of the start identifier (A, B or C1) and corresponding three TOF values is then transmitted from the FEE to the CDH on a dedicated DE low voltage differential signaling (LVDS) channel at 6.25 Mbps. To manage the output data flow to accommodate short bursts of consecutive DEs, the FPGA has an eight-record first-in-first-out (FIFO) transmit buffer.

The FPGA also periodically reports seven logical counters, four of which use the information determined from a given DE: A-first-hit only; B-first-hit only; C-first-hit only, and valid DE count. The “first-hit-only” coincidence counters account for events in which the named signal was detected, but none of the other signals were subsequently received within the same coincidence window. The remaining three of the seven counters are the A, B and C raw counts that measure the activity of the original incoming signals into the FEE, independent of the coincidence window. These seven values, along with a diagnostic FPGA register readback field, are reported in a 24-byte packet to the CDH on a second LVDS channel, transmitted at 115.2 kbps. The output of this packet is coordinated via an incoming sync pulse LVDS channel from the CDH, which marks the passage of 4o spin increments that assist in organizing the FEE data output into histogram spin-bins.

For diagnostic checks, the FEE can be commanded to produce stimulation pulse (“stim”) signals or pre-defined data packets. The stim function can pulse any one or more of the incoming signals and generate relative TOF values between them in integer increments of 20 ns in any hit order. The stim repetition can be programmed to rates of 1 Hz, 10 Hz, 100 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz or 100 kHz. Pre-defined outputs simulating DE or counter data can also be programmed via FPGA diagnostic registers to any operator-defined numerical value in order to check LVDS communications and downstream logic with the Ebox.

Electronics Box (EBox) Subsystem

The IMAP-Hi Ebox shares many design features with similar subsystems of three other IMAP instruments (IMAP-Lo, CoDICE, and SWE), all of which are part of the Instrument Common Electronics (ICE) program that was implemented for efficient design across these IMAP instruments. The elements that share a common architecture are the LVPS and CDH boards and the electrical bus, as well as the overall mechanical Ebox design.

As shown in Fig. 3, each IMAP-Hi Ebox Subsystem is separate from its sensor. It provides conditioned power, high voltages, event scalars, look-up tables (LUTs), housekeeping information, fault management, and communications with the spacecraft. The Ebox receives spacecraft primary power, nominally 31 V, into its LVPS, and communicates with the spacecraft via 115.2 kbps LVDS command input and 115.2 kbps LVDS telemetry output via its CDH board. The Ebox front panel has three connections used for ground testing – a high-voltage safe-arm plug connector, an RS-422 test UART, and a digital debug port. The LVPS, high voltage power system (HVPS), and CDH reside within the Ebox. Power, control, and feedback signals are distributed between the boards via a backplane board.

Low Voltage Power System

The LVPS converts incoming spacecraft primary power into secondary power, utilizing three DC-DC converters (+3.3V, ±5V and ±12V), required by the boards in the Ebox and in the FEE. FEE power can be turned on or off via commands to switches residing on the LVPS. The LVPS provides voltage, current, and temperature measurements to the CDH for housekeeping reporting.

Command and Data Handling (CDH) Board

The CDH board acts as the information center for the sensor, having control and status interfaces to all other electronics boards. A Microsemi ProAsic RT3PE3000L FPGA and Atmel AT697 processor running Flight Software (FSW) complement each other for the control and status responsibilities. Other major components within the CDH include: a 128kB PROM for storing the Boot FSW image; 2 MB MRAM for storing two sets of Application (App) flight software (FSW) images and lookup tables (LUTs); 256 MB SDRAM for working processor memory; FPGA watchdog timer; FPGA support timers; HVPS and FEE communications interfaces; and an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) system capable of acquiring 49 channels of analog data from the sensor. A pass-through port for the FEE Direct Event output was used for high-rate signals during ground calibration.

For incoming spacecraft commands and telemetry output to the spacecraft, the CDH provides 8 kB command FIFO data buffer and 8 kB telemetry FIFO data buffer. The FPGA maintains time and spacecraft spin status via the incoming Time and Status message that arrives every second from the spacecraft. The FPGA creates an instrument-internal virtual spin pulse based on that message, and hence a reference to spacecraft spin. Time intervals corresponding to 4o spin bins referenced to the virtual spin pulse are generated by the FPGA, with each 4o spin bin generating a sync signal to the FEE, which provides a means to organize the science-based cadence of data acquisition.

The CDH FPGA receives FEE packets containing seven counters every spin bin on the 115.2 kbps LVDS channel and provides them in seven FPGA registers for the App FSW to read and accumulate the science histogram packet. The CDH FPGA also receives FEE packets containing DE data on the 6.25 Mbps LVDS channel. Each DE data packet is time-tagged relative to the spacecraft virtual spin pulse, typically to a resolution of 2 ms, and is stored in a 256-DE packet FIFO data buffer. The time tags are ultimately used in ground processing to generate the IMAP-Hi sky maps of ENA intensity. The FPGA also creates DE-data-derived counters for eight Qualified Spin Histograms, named SH0 through SH7; and eight Long Qualified Spin Histograms, named LSH0 through LSH7. The DE data criteria for a counter to be incremented is programmable via FPGA registers, in which a minimum-maximum range of DE TOF fields is specified. These 16 counters provide the remaining counter fields in the science histogram packet. Time-tagged DE record data are transmitted via a separate science telemetry packet, which is generated from the 256 DE FIFO and processed and packetized by the App FSW.

Calibration Facility

Hi-45 and Hi-90 were calibrated (and IMAP-Lo and IMAP-Ultra were cross calibrated with IMAP-Hi) in the Los Alamos Space Plasma Instrument Calibration Facility, described in detail in Funsten et al. (2013b). The microwave ion source output has energy dispersion < 2 eV (Chamberlin et al. 1987) and the beam flux is highly stable over time. A 2-mm diameter beam-defining aperture was located at the entrance of the calibration chamber, ensuring a narrow, well-collimated beam incident on the sensor. The H0 beam flux was measured using the same absolute beam-flux monitor (ABM, see Funsten et al. (2005)) that was used for IBEX-Hi calibration. The ABM was intermittently moved into the beam to measure and ensure beam stability over time. Calibration was performed under the condition that the incident H0 beam flux changed <5% between ABM measurements. The incident beam entered the chamber at a fixed location; the sensors being calibrated were thus moved relative to the fixed beam trajectory using one linear motion stage and three rotational stages.

An H0 beam was obtained by charge exchange of the primary ion beam in the beamline between the analyzing magnet of the accelerator and the calibration chamber, and residual ions in the beam were deflected using a permanent magnet immediately upstream of the calibration chamber. Because neutralization by charge exchange of the H+ beam occurs with minimal scattering or energy loss, high beam quality (energy dispersion <2 eV and measured beam divergence <0.1o) is maintained. To ensure sufficiently high beam flux at lower energies in which the intrinsic beam intensity decreases (< 1 keV), the ambient pressure in this beamline section was increased from a baseline value of ∼3×10−6 torr to as much as ∼2×10−5 torr using a precision leak valve to admit room air, increasing the charge exchange probability and thus the H0 beam flux. The 2-mm aperture at the entrance of the calibration chamber enabled the chamber pressure to remain <10−7 torr when charge exchange gas was admitted into the upstream beamline.

Energy Response

The energy response of IMAP-Hi is primarily governed by the geometry of the ESA Subsystem with small contributions due to angular scattering and energy loss in the charge conversion foils. Two approaches were used to evaluate ESA performance. First, the ESA electro-optic model was validated using a fixed incident beam energy and stepping the inner and outer ESA plate voltages to modify the energy passband in small steps. This method is fast (the incident H0 beam energy is not changed) and effective for model validation. Second, and more importantly, the sensor performance at nominal flight energy passbands was acquired for different H0 beam energies across the passbands.

Background and Noise

Detection and measurement of ENAs from the outer heliosphere is challenging due to their low intensity and the numerous sources of background (other particles that masquerade as heliospheric ENAs in the Detector Subsystem) and noise (other sources that may result in a false valid event). With an anticipated maximum count rate of 1 s−1 from heliospheric ENAs, exceptional care is required to minimize sources of background and noise.

Because the CEM and MCP detectors are sensitive to UV light, ambient UV must be minimized. With an estimated count rate from direct solar radiation is 0.3 Hz, the Hi-90 sunshade, added to the sun-pointing spacecraft deck to minimize solar wind background, ensures Hi-90 remains in shadow during nominal operations and throughout nominal spacecraft maneuvers. Measurement for sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) light was performed by exposing the sensor directly to a 15 W mercury lamp at 254 nm; based on these tests, it was determined that ambient, indirect UV from “helioglow” is predicted to produce <10−7 cts/s in the sensors.

Ion scattering from the Deflector Subsystem was tested using under worst-case conditions. Because electro-optics are linearly scalable and lower energy protons scatter more easily, the Deflector Subsystem was tested at lower than nominal voltage (±3.5 kV) using an intense, 6 × 104 H+ s−1 beam at 9 keV at a worst-case angle ∼50° from the center of the sensor FOV. At energy passband ESA 8, no detected events were observed above a noise floor of 4 mHz. This yields a suppression ratio ≥1.5 × 107:1, which exceeds 106:1 requirement by >10x. These results apply at nominal deflector biases of ±6.3 kV for incident 18 keV H+.

The background count rates for all TOF combinations as well as (exclusive) singles rates for Hi-45 and Hi-90 were tested over extended periods (13,580 s for Hi-45, 14,910 s for Hi-90) without an input H0 beam. The count rates, in units of counts per 1000 s, are shown in Table 6 and illustrate less background for increasing number of TOF combinations. Specific TOF combinations can be exploited to monitor the background and maximize signal-to-noise for the ENA intensity map products. As shown in Table 6 and previously discussed, the Bronze event combinations BC and CC have higher background than other combinations and are not baselined for inclusion in science data products but can be used as a performance diagnostic.

Cross-Calibration of IMAP-Hi with IMAP-Lo and IMAP-Ultra

Hi-45 was cross-calibrated with IMAP-Lo, and Hi-90 was cross-calibrated with IMAP-Ultra at the Los Alamos Space Plasma Instrument Calibration Facility (see Sect. 3.1 and Funsten et al. (2013b)). The cross-calibrations used a narrow H0 beam, and the same ABM used for IMAP-Hi calibrations was also used for cross-calibration. The configurations of the sensors on the rotational and linear stages in the calibration chamber are illustrated in Fig. 15.

Configurations of Hi-45 and IMAP-Lo (left panel) and Hi-90 and IMAP-Ultra in the calibration chamber for cross-calibration

The goal of cross-calibration is to directly compare the instrument response at a subset of H0 energies and input aperture locations. These measurements serve as critical tie-points for the integrated sensor responses, which are developed through (1) individual sensor calibrations at multiple energies, aperture locations, and angles of incidence as well as (2) instrument performance models that enable interpolation of calibration and cross-calibration results into validated, quantitative, integrated response functions, including the geometric factor. For cross-calibration, the ABM provides a measurement of the rate R (ENAs /s) of H0 striking a sensor’s entrance aperture over the cross-sectional area of the H0 beam. The instrument provides a resulting count rate Ci (counts/s) of, for example, different TOF combinations i (such as ABCC, ABC, ACC for Hi). The probability Pi of detecting a single H0 at the specific aperture location, energy, and angle of incidence of the cross-calibration is simply Pi=R/Ci.

For both Hi-45 and Hi-90 cross calibrations, the H0 beam was directed onto the foil in frame location 3b. For the Hi-45 calibration, the entire foil pane was snaked by the beam. For IMAP-Lo, the H0 beam was incident on the conversion surface in Quadrant 0 (Schwadron et al. 2025). The ABM measured the beam flux before and after each calibration measurement and was required to vary <4% across calibration measurements.

Sensor Environmental Testing: Lessons Learned

As a hermetic membrane, carbon foils on Ni support grids are susceptible to acoustic damage. Pressure oscillations from acoustic waves <140 dB are typically below 200 Pa, which the carbon can withstand (tests have shown that nominal 0.5 μg/cm2 carbon foil can tolerate more than 5000 Pa of static pressure differential (Allegrini and Dayeh 2014)). However, acoustics can drive a grid to fatigue failure before any carbon foil is destroyed. Acoustics testing was performed on the foil assemblies (5 different designs) used in the Charge Conversion Subsystem during early development of IMAP-Hi. However, the testing was not sufficiently flight-like, and the acoustic test facilities could not reach the maximum specifications for the Falcon 9 launch environment. Both of these contributed to foil support grid failure during Phase D of IMAP-Hi.

Vibration testing after initial assembly and test of the first IMAP-Hi Flight Model assembly resulted in large tears and missing sections of the support grids of multiple foil assemblies in the Charge Conversion Subsystem and Foil 1 of the Detector Subsystem. These failures were attributed to the more severe launch environment of the Falcon 9 launch vehicle used for the IMAP mission compared to the Pegasus rocket used for the IBEX mission.

After failure during vibration testing, the Ni support grids were subsequently evaluated for tensile strength and survival in a high acoustic environment. It was found through tensile testing that the 333 lpi (∼13.1 lines per mm) Ni support grids from Precision E-Forming, specifically MN37 (64% transmission) for conversion foils and MN38 (70% transmission) for Foils 1 & 2 in the Detector Subsystem, had tensile strengths of 3.3 ± 0.2 Lbf (4.5 ± 0.3 N⋅m) and 1.2 ± 0.3 Lbf (1.6 ± 0.4 N⋅m), respectively, for 2-in-wide test specimens. These Ni grids have direct heritage from IBEX and other missions and were baselined for IMAP-Hi. A similar 333 lpi Ni grid was obtained Thin Metal Parts, which could manufacture thicker grids; these had tensile strength 15.9 ± 2.3 Lbf (21.6 ± 3.1 N⋅m), much higher than the MN38 and MN37 grids. An additional feature of the Thin Metal Parts grid was an increase of ∼6% in grid transmission for ENAs at normal incidence compared to the MN37 grids.

Acoustic survival tests were performed on the Stage 1 drift region assembly with different support grid types with mounted carbon foils. Each acoustic test evaluated a single grid type for both Foil 1 and Foil 2 locations. The test followed a flat acoustic profile from 31.5-630 Hz (envelopes the primary drum mode of both the sensor and, as determined from laser vibrometry, the detector foils) from 121.5 dB to 142.5 dB Overall Sound Pressure Level (OASPL) at 3 dB increments and 30 s duration at each level. The final test was 120 s at 145.5 dB OASPL. Grid types MN37 and MN38 ripped at 133.5 dB and 127.5 dB OASPL, respectively, and completely failed at 136.5 dB and 139.5 dB OASPL, respectively. The foil from Thin Metal Parts experienced no failure and no major loss of carbon foil for the entire test.

Several key modifications were made to successfully qualify for the IMAP launch. First, the Ni support grids from Thin Metal Parts were incorporated into the foil assemblies in the Charge Conversion Subsystem and detector foil assemblies in the Detector Subsystem for both Hi-45 and Hi-90. For future missions, tensile testing of Ni support grids for carbon foils is strongly recommended for lot screening and to ensure grid integrity and survival.

Second, the method of attachment of conversion foil grids to the frames was changed from the heritage spot-welded shim design that clamps a grid to a frame to bonding the grids with Ablestik 967-1 Silver Epoxy. Testing under the launch environment conditions showed the spot welds did not provide uniform clamping of a grid around the pane perimeter, resulting in stress concentration points that likely initiated grid failure. The bonded grids demonstrated uniform clamping of the grid around the pane perimeter.

Third, IMAP-Hi incorporates an ESA cover (IBEX had no ESA cover), and the outer ESA plate is mounted directly to the cover for quick removal to inspect foils and grids in the Charge Conversion and Detector Subsystems. Through vibro-acoustic simulations, it was shown that the combination of the outer ESA plate and the ESA cover was likely acting as an acoustic energy source induced by random vibration. To prevent this potential acoustic excitation, the outer ESA plate was mechanically attached by a PEEK ring to the ESA cover near the point of closest distance between the outer plate toroid and the ESA cover (see Fig. 4). The insulating PEEK ring was epoxied to the outer ESA plate using Loctite EA-9394 and mechanically attached to the ESA cover with screws. The mating surfaces in the epoxy bond area were pre-conditioned: the outer ESA was abraded to the base aluminum, and the PEEK ring was acid-etched on the bond surface with 99% sulfuric acid. To accommodate this change and ensure the desired rigidity, the design of the ESA cover was modified for increased stiffness. This change resulted in decreased ESA resonant motion as predicted by random vibration analysis. Acoustic modeling of the configuration before these changes indicated high acoustic energies at the foils (and thus likely failure); acoustic modeling after design changes showed between 0.5 and 5 orders of magnitude reduction in acoustic energy at the foils.

Fourth, venting channels were incorporated into the frames of the charge conversion foils and Foil 1 to mitigate pressure differences at several locations around the frame perimeter. For the charge conversion foil frames, 12 channels were machined into each frame at its mating surface with the baseplate, with the channels having 1.52 mm width, 0.25 mm depth, and 5.3 mm length. Ten venting channels were added to the Foil 1 frame, each having 0.76 mm depth, 1.59-mm radius, and 3.8 mm length. In IBEX-Hi, the conversion foils and frames formed a hermetic seal between the ESA and Collimator Subsystems to minimize outgassing into the collimator area and thus minimize the ion gun background; because IMAP-Hi has no ion gun effect at this location, a hermetic seal at the conversion foils and frames is not required.

Fifth, the original grid design of Foil 1 in the Detector Subsystem had an integrated disk at the center of the grid to prevent ion feedback from the spindle region of the outer ESA as described in Sect. 2.5. The additional disk mass as well as different mechanical properties of the solid Ni disk and adjacent high-transmission Ni grid were likely factors in grid failure during vibration. The disk was removed from the grid design, and a separate disk on four support arms was added 1.7 mm in front of Foil 1 grid, resulting in a grid with homogeneous structure and mechanical properties.

A telecentric imaging system was developed to optically evaluate conversion foil grids throughout environmental testing. Optical imaging is challenging due to the restricted visibility because of the hexagonal collimator grids, the small area of the collimator hexagonal holes, and the large working distance between foils and imaging system due to the Collimator and Deflector Subsystems. A telecentric lens forms an image using only rays perpendicular to the object and is therefore able to peer through multiple collimator channels to a conversion foil and its support. This system, developed to inspect the integrity of the IMAP-Hi conversion foils and their mounting grids throughout environmental testing, used an f/6 telecentric lens with 0.5x magnification and 173 mm working distance (Edmund Optics 62-932). Coupled with a 2/3” format CCD sensor (Sony ICX655ALA), each image provided a clear view of 17 × 13 mm area of foil. In order to inspect the entire annular area of the foils, the camera system was mounted on a motorized XYZ gantry stage (Zaber LC040 series). Crucially, the lens’ long working distance allowed the imager to operate outside the Deflector Subsystem to eliminate possibility of mechanical collision. IMAP-Hi Sensor Operations and Data Products

Sensor Operation

Figure 17 illustrates IMAP-Hi’s operation modes with a brief description of each mode on the right. After turn-on and flight software boot, the instrument will enter the low voltage engineering mode (LVENG) where no high voltage commanding is possible but from which the instrument can be configured to enter two other operating modes: low voltage science (LVSCI) and high voltage engineering (HVENG). In LVSCI the instrument performs end-to-end science acquisition generating science data products but with no high voltage enabled. The FEE FPGA can be configured to stimulate the FEE CFD inputs producing expected rates in the different data products and verifying that the FEE performs nominally. In HVENG, the high voltages can be enabled, and manual commanding of each individual voltage is possible while raw data is collected at up to 1 s cadence. For example, this is the operating mode where the detector high voltages are ramped up (or down) before entering the other two high voltage modes: high voltage standby (HVSTANDBY) and high voltage science (HVSCI). In HVSTANDBY, some high voltages are lower compared to the nominal values (typically for observatory maneuvers) and science data is not collected.

IMAP-Hi Instrument Modes

During the daily spacecraft repoint maneuver, IMAP-Hi remains with high voltages at nominal science levels except ESA voltages set to 0 V in order to minimize the stress on optocouplers in the bulk HV power supplies that may result from cycling the HV power supplies of the deflectors, detectors, and Stage 1 drift region. With ESA plates set at 0 V, IMAP-Hi performs a daily background test for the duration of the maneuver.

SAFE mode can be entered from any other mode as triggered by certain rules, such as out-of-limit violations or other unallowed operations (such as trying to run two macros at the same time). The two types of safing actions are Soft Safe and, for conditions in which instrument safety is of critical concern, Urgent Safe. A Soft Safe action, e.g., for a temperature out-of-limit (OOL), ramps down HVs (if enabled) in ∼4 minutes and leaves the sensor in SAFE Mode. An Urgent Safe action, such as an OOL detector current, instantaneously disables power to the HV boards and the sensor is placed into LVENG Mode.

IMAP-Hi operates most of the time in HVSCI where no commanding is needed for nominal operations once the instrument is in this mode. In science mode, all high voltages are at nominal operating levels, and the ESA voltages are stepped once every eight spacecraft spins according to a configurable ESA stepping table, which nominally contains nine entries corresponding to Table 5, but which can include an additional step such as a background measurement step or can contain fewer steps if the need arises. Figure 18 illustrates on the top the ESA high voltages sequence and on the bottom how the histogram data is collected and transmitted to the CDH. A complete energy sweep over all nine nominal passbands is therefore completed every 72 spins, which corresponds to 18 min based on a 15 s nominal spin period. The detailed timing of the science data acquisition is shown in Fig. 19. The histogram counts are accumulated in each 4°-bin (every ∼167 ms) for eight spins. After an ESA step is complete, a new set of ESA voltages is commanded and the histogram data from the previous ESA step is transmitted from the CDH to the spacecraft. The last histogram bin is shortened by 40 ms to gather the histogram and DE data before awaiting the spin pulse.

IMAP-Hi HVSCI science data acquisition cycle

Detailed timing for science data acquisition

Macros – executed by time-tagged commands – are used to transition the instrument state to HVSCI mode from turn-on, to transition the instrument state back to LVENG mode from science mode, and for all standard tests (such as gain and background tests). The macros and on-board tables can be updated as necessary. Other special operations, such as uploading new macros or changing a table, and contingency operations require individual commands and likely real time commanding.

Gain tests and background tests are run nominally every six months. The gain tests of three-hour duration ensure that the voltages on the CEM and MCP detectors are on the gain plateau such that the pulse magnitudes of almost all detected ENAs are above the discriminator threshold. Defining V0 as a nominal operating detector voltage, a gain test on the MCP detector steps to V0± 50 V and subsequently at steps in multiples of 75 V. A gain test of each CEM detector steps to V0± 100 V and subsequently at steps in multiples of 125 V. If V0 is no longer on the gain plateau, the detector voltage can be increased to a voltage that is on the gain plateau.

The background test consists of measuring the count rates of the TOF combinations as well as singles events when both ESA plate voltages are set to 0 V. The test is operated as a mode where an additional 10th step is added to the ESA sequence, extending the ESA stepping sequence to 20 minutes for a nominal 15 s spin period. Thus, normal scientific data collection still occurs during the background test. The duration of this background mode is nominally a week and is adjustable. The noise floor/crosstalk tests assess the health of the amplification and signal processing electronics in the FEE. They are run infrequently during HVSTANDBY or LVENG via a macro such that they do not impact science data acquisition time.

Data Products and Algorithms

In science mode, IMAP-Hi reports three data products: (1) Direct Event (DE) data consisting of individual coincidence events selected using a prioritization scheme and reported with high time (and therefore angular) resolution, (2) histograms of accumulated counts as a function of angle (90 × 4°-bins), energy step (nine), and event type at every energy step, and (3) housekeeping data. The average telemetry rate is 480 bps for DE data, 171 bps for histogram data, and ∼68 bps for housekeeping. Additional telemetry (25 bps) is allocated for spin timing information at 1 ms resolution, checksum, and header information.

Eight TOF combinations are recorded as Direct Events: AB, AC, BC, CC, ABC, ACC, BCC, and ABCC. Each event is considered valid (qualified) based on time offsets between A/B/C1/C2. The time offsets are adjustable in the CDH via command with defaults stored in a lookup table. This qualification scheme can be independently selected for each type of coincidence. DEs are recorded at higher time resolution (2 ms, or ∼0.1o of spin angle) than used for acquisition of the histograms. IMAP-Hi’s data allocation allows a DE event rate up to 10 s−1, as well as sending up to ∼600 Direct Events per DE packet, which are nominally created every two or four spins during an 8-spin cycle or ESA step.

A prioritization scheme favors transmission of DE data for qualified quadruple, triple, and double coincidences. If there is insufficient room in the telemetry to send all Direct Events, then the events are ranked Gold/Silver/Bronze/Other and selected accordingly. Priority is given to the coincidence types that represent the measurements used for primary science data products. The high priority events (Gold) are stored up to the limit of ∼600 events per DE packet. If unused data volume remains, the second priority (Silver), third priority (Bronze), and fourth priority (Other) events are included in the DE data in order of priority. Because Direct Events are recorded at greater spin time resolution (and thus greater angular resolution) and with precise TOF resolution than the histograms, they represent the key science data for production of the ENA intensity maps for scientific analysis and interpretation.

The on-board TOF ranges used for qualification of DEs are broader than the ranges that will be used in ground processing at the SDC for science. This provides flexibility to better identify and assess backgrounds to facilitate their removal and to record events signal associated with heavier ENAs (e.g., He0) at longer TOFs than those for H0. A second, narrower qualification scheme is used in ground processing and ENA map construction.

Science processing also defines specific event combinations spanning the gold, silver, and bronze prioritization scheme. The intent is to separate DEs into as few categories as possible by their relative backgrounds, to allow science products to be made with lower-background, lower-statistics products or higher-background but better-statistics products. For the best tradeoff between highest signal rate and lowest background rate, the expected primary DE types for scientific data products are AB, ABC, and ABCC, followed by AC and ACC.

Two types of histograms are accumulated every eight spins (120 s): “qualified” histograms constructed from the Direct Event data that provide redundancy and “long” histograms, which have event TOF values outside the valid ranges. The long histograms, which are used as a diagnostic for instrument performance and backgrounds, also include singles (non-coincidence) histograms for each detector to monitor their detection efficiencies over time (Funsten et al. 2005). Each histogram is a 9 × 90 array of counts for the nine energy passbands and 90 angular bins around the spin axis, each 4o wide. The counter for each angular bin is 12 bit and remains uncompressed.

The following is a summary list of the IMAP-Hi data products:

Level 1A Data Product: Decommuted Level 0 CCSDS packets produced by Hi-90 and Hi-45, repackaged into CDFs per each APID. This also includes the Instrument Status Summary.

Level 1B Data Product: Comprised of i) annotated DEs, which are DEs with timing, attitude, and ephemerides information for each event as provided by the spacecraft, ii) a “bad times” list, identifying time intervals over which the data is likely to be unsuitable for heliospheric imaging, either because of the specific configuration of the instrument (e.g., during detector gain tests) or because of spacecraft activities such as the daily repoint, iii) spacecraft “spin pulse” times as well as ground-reconstructed, validated phase-0 spin times, and iv) diagnostic histogram plots of count rates and event TOFs, created both from onboard histograms and from DE data.

Level 1C Data Product: This consists of Pointing sets, which are the fundamental building blocks for maps. They are angular 1-D bins of counts and aperture exposure times at 0.1° resolution in the “Despun Pointing Frame”, a fixed frame defined by the average spacecraft spin axis for a given day between spacecraft repoint maneuvers. The Pointing Set bins follow the circle in the sky traced out by the boresight directions of Hi-90 and Hi-45, respectively, over the course of a complete spacecraft spin period. Pointing Sets are produced for each TOF combination type at each energy step. They also include backgrounds and uncertainties (both statistical and non-statistical) for each angular bin.

Level 2 Data Product: Pointing sets are combined to produce ENA intensity maps, with the Hi spectral response model appropriately adjusted for the spectral index (Fig. 12(b)). These maps are accumulated over 3, 6, and 12 months and at both 4o and 6o grid resolution. Maps are made in the spacecraft frame, and, after applying the Compton-Getting corrections (McComas et al. 2010; Zirnstein et al. 2013), in the heliospheric frame (i.e. in a frame at rest with respect to the Sun). To avoid potential data quality issues for ENAs viewed in the anti-ram direction, maps are built not only from full Pointing Sets (full-spin maps), but separate “ram” and anti-ram” maps are generated from the ram and anti-ram halves of the Pointing sets, respectively. In addition to ENA intensity maps, uncertainty, exposure time, observation date, and background rate maps are generated.

Level 3 Data Product: Used for scientific analysis and interpretation, this consists of i) ENA intensity maps corrected for ENA survival probability between the termination shock and their arrival at IMAP, which considers ENA loss due to charge exchange with the solar wind, photoionization, and electron impact ionization, ii) spectral index maps, and iii) combined Hi-90 and Hi-45 maps.

Summary

The MAP-Hi ENA imager consists of a pair of highly sensitive, single pixel ENA sensors to remotely observe from near-Earth orbit the structure and dynamics of the distant plasmas of the outer heliosphere. Each sensor has a FOV of 4.1o FWHM and nine contiguous energy passbands spanning an energy range of 0.44–15.6 keV, which covers the core and PUI populations of the solar wind. IMAP-Hi incorporates numerous improvements based on lessons learned from the IBEX-Hi ENA imager, which itself made fundamental discoveries; the IMAP-Hi imager will far exceed the IBEX-Hi performance, including imaging resolution, energy range, energy resolution, and rejection of background and noise. IMAP-Hi has been fully calibrated, and it has been cross-calibrated at key parameter tie-points (H0 beam energies and angles of incidence) with IMAP-Lo and IMAP-Ultra. The integrity of these measurements spanning 0.04 to 300 keV will lead to a plethora of discoveries and deep understanding about how the Sun interacts with the nearby interstellar medium and how these interactions influence the space environment of the inner heliosphere over the solar cycle, including cosmic ray modulation.
