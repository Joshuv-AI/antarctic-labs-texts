Psychometric properties of the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire version A among Chinese expectant fathers
Authors: Dang Ma, Bing Xue, Xin Wang, Wei Wei, Sharon R Redding, Huijun Chen, Yan-Qiong Ouyang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on the mental status of expectant fathers indicates that their fears of childbirth (FOC) are of great concern. In consideration of expectant fathers’ increasing involvement during pregnancy and childbirth, it is necessary to provide a reliable instrument to measure the psychometric properties of expectant fathers. The purpose of this study was to revise the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire version A (W-DEQ A) for expectant fathers, analyze the psychometric properties and explore its applicability to the Chinese population.MethodsThe W-DEQ A was revised to apply to Chinese men following the completion of a cross-sectional study. Content validity was assessed by an expert panel of specialists in obstetrics. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. Reliability was assessed by the internal consistency coefficient and retest reliability.ResultsA total of 741 expectant fathers participated in this study. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was greater than 0.8, while the scale-level CVI (S-CVI) was 0.92, indicating satisfactory content validity. Exploratory factor analysis revealed eight factors, accounting for 60.97% of the cumulative variance. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed acceptable structural validity of the father’s version of the W-DEQ A (W-DEQ AF), demonstrating a good model fit. The Cronbach’s alpha for the W-DEQ AF was 0.843, indicating good reliability of the scale.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the W-DEQ-AF is reliable and could be considered for use with expectant fathers in China to assess their psychological status and fear of childbirth during pregnancy of their spouse. Keywords: Fear of childbirth, Psychometric properties, Expectant fathers, W-DEQ

Background

Childbirth is a process during which the role change for both husband and wife occurred [1]. Due to the unpredictability of the process and results of childbirth, fear of childbirth (FOC) has become a common psychological problem for both pregnant women and their spouses [2]. Expectant fathers may experience negative psychological experiences such as uncertainty, helplessness, depression, anxiety and fear [3, 4], which may lead to some negative impacts. Studies have reported that men with FOC were found to be in poorer health and may compromise their preparation to become fathers, even affecting the emotional and cognitive development of their children [5, 6]. Expectant fathers having FOC are more likely to advocate for cesarean birth as a preferred mode of delivery for pregnant women, which may influence their partners’ decision-making. The expectant fathers’ poor mood may affect emotional and psychological support for pregnant women, making them less confident about childbirth [6].

European studies found that the prevalence of FOC among expectant fathers ranged from 13.6% to 54.3% [7, 8]. A cross-sectional study reported that the prevalence of FOC among expectant fathers was 32.1% in China [9]. The prevalence of expectant fathers’ FOC showed that it was becoming a health concern. However, most studies on fathers’ FOC have been carried out in Western societies and research on the FOC among pregnant spouses in Asia is limited, and it was crucial to further explore the prevalence of FOC among expectant fathers in China [10].

The prevalence and severity of fathers’ FOC depend to some extent on various instruments that measure the fear of expectant fathers in different social cultures. In recent years, scholars have developed a variety of assessment tools related to FOC. However, those tools were developed for women and there is a lack of assessment tools applicable to expectant fathers’ FOC. The Fathers’ Fear of Childbirth Scale (FFCS) was the first scale to measure expectant fathers’ FOC specifically [3], which was developed in 2021 and introduced to Chinese expectant fathers in 2023 [11]. However, the FFCS has not been utilized in many countries and its dimensions are limited, which included only two factors, namely fear of childbirth process and fear of hospital [3]. What’s more, the Wijma Delivery Expectation/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) [12], being widely used in pregnant women [13], has a higher visibility and popularity. Given expectant fathers experienced FOC which was found to be affecting their health and may compromise their preparation to become fathers [6]. Therefore, it is significant to explore an appropriate measurement tool applicable for expectant fathers. The W-DEQ has been previously used to screen for FOC in men, but those items considering not relevant to males were omitted [5, 14], may resulting in bias. Therefore, the reliability and validity of this screening tool in men require further investigation.

Existing research on FOC has mainly focused on women during pregnancy, and there is a lack of studies related to FOC in expectant fathers and a lack of an instrument for expectant fathers in China. It is necessary to provide a reliable instrument to measure the FOC of expectant fathers. The purpose of this study was to revise the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire version A (W-DEQ A) for expectant fathers, analyze the psychometric properties and explore its applicability to the Chinese expectant father population.

Methods

Setting and sampling

A cross-sectional psychometric study was conducted from October 2022 to January 2023. The convenience sampling method was used in this study. Expectant fathers who accompanied their pregnant partners to attend routine prenatal care were invited to participate in this study at the obstetric department of two hospitals in Wuhan, China. The inclusion criteria were: expectant fathers who are over 22 years. Exclusion criteria were: 1) having a mental or psychiatric disorder; 2) participation in special prenatal education programs such as cognitive behavioral treatment and psychological counselling. The psychometric analysis includes exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Following DeVellis’ guidelines for scale development [15], the EFA is generally 5–10 times the number of items on the scale, meanwhile the CFA requires that the sample size should be greater than 200 cases. Therefore, considering a 10% rate of invalid questionnaires, the total sample size required for this study should be greater than 402 cases. There are nearly 780 expectant fathers were invited, and 741 participants eventually completed this study, yielding to a response rate of 95.0%.

The researchers were trained to be familiar with the uniform measurement procedures and the criteria for correctly completing the questionnaires. They distributed informational flyers to expectant fathers at the antenatal clinic after explaining the purpose and content of the investigation. Written informed consent was obtained from fathers who agreed to participate. Participants were informed that the questionnaires would remain anonymous and stored in a secure location. Data were stored on a disk to which only the researchers had access. This study was approved by Wuhan University Institutional Review Board of the authors’ institutions (Authorized number: IRB 2022021).

Instruments

Socio-demographic questionnaire

A researcher-designed socio-demographic questionnaire was developed following a review of the literature. It included age, gestation week (partner), occupation, education level, monthly income, gravidity (partner), parity (partner), the preferred mode of birth and previous birth experiences.

Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) Version A

The original W-DEQ was developed to measure the FOC by Wijma, which included two versions, version A and version B, to assess FOC during pregnancy and after childbirth separately [12]. The Chinese version of the W-DEQ A was translated by Liu’s team [16]. It consists of 33 items, with each item using a six-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Item numbers 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 19, 20, 24, 25, 27, and 31 use reverse scoring. The total score ranges from 0 to 165 with higher scores indicating more severe FOC. Existing studies of women usually use ≥ 85 points as the cut-off value for determining whether there is FOC. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for women in China was 0.928 [16]. A Swedish researcher excluded eight items that were not relevant to men and used the revised scale for expectant fathers, and Cronbach’s alpha for this revised scale was above recommendations for a well-functional scale of 0.89 [5].

Procedure

The Chinese version of the W-DEQ A has been widely used and validated in women. After asking for permission from the original author of the Chinese version of the scale, the father’s version of W-DEQ A (W-DEQ AF) was developed through group discussion, expert consultation, cognitive interviews, a pilot study, and reliability and validity assessment.

With the permission of the authors of Chinese version, two of research team members revised the Chinese female version of the W-DEQ A to make it applicable to expectant fathers. Then, they invited an expert panel to discuss item by item and reached a consensus. A new father’s version of W-DEQ A (W-DEQ AF) was sent to the original authors to review to eliminate any discrepancy of idioms and semantics between W-DEQ A and W-DEQ AF. Statements such as “during your partner’s labor” were included to make items more appropriate for expectant fathers. Content validity was assessed by an expert panel consisting of two obstetricians and three clinical nurse specialists in obstetrics. They were included in the study to review and adjust the first version of the W-DEQ AF and evaluate the content validity of the initial items using a content validity index (CVI). Items rated below 0.80 were deleted or modified based on expert opinions. Two rounds of expert consultations were conducted. The Kendall’s W coefficients of the expert panel were 0.413 and 0.441 (p < 0.05), respectively, which indicated that the expert consistency was acceptable [17].

Two rounds of cognitive interviews involving six fathers for each round were conducted by purposive sampling. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as those of the cross-sectional psychometric study. The age of participants in cognitive interviews was 30.33 years (SD = 6.59), with close to half having college or above degrees. The face-to-face interviews consisted of the fathers completing a questionnaire and an assessment of their understanding of the content. The researcher observed the expectant fathers as they completed the questionnaire and recorded if they did not complete an item, changed their answer, chose multiple responses or hesitated to answer. Feedback was obtained about the fathers’ understanding of the scale and any problems with items/responses. Scale items were then modified based on the fathers’ comments. Content from these cognitive interviews was used only for scale revision and the questionnaires were not included in the final data analysis of this study.

Some items were modified by comparing the results derived from the interview data. “How do you imagine it will feel the very moment you deliver the baby?” was modified to “What is your perception at the moment your spouse delivers”. The word “self-confidence” in Item 22 was changed to “very confident in my own emotions and performance”; in Item 31, “dangerous” was changed to “my partner will be in danger” and so on.

A pilot study was conducted in September 2022. A total of 30 expectant fathers were asked to complete the modified questionnaire to determine the time spent for its completion and to identify any items that were difficult to understand. These 30 fathers did not participate in the final cross-sectional study. They indicated that the questionnaire was straightforward to understand and that the completion time was acceptable. No changes were made to the questionnaire based on the pilot study.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA) and AMOS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, US) were used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. Regarding the socio-demographic characteristics, number, frequency (%), mean and standard deviation were calculated. Missing data was substituted with the mean values of scores in the scales only in cases where 10% or a lesser proportion of the data were missing in a scale [18].

The content validity, construct validity and convergent validity were used to test validity. And internal consistency coefficient and retest reliability were used to test reliability.

Content validity was determined by an expert panel who used a consultation form to rate the importance of the items on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = inappropriate, 4 = appropriate). The content validity index of each item (I-CVI) and the scale-level CVI (S-CVI) were calculated to evaluate the content validity. The I-CVI value should be greater than 0.78 and the entire S-CVI needed to be greater than 0.80 [19].

Construct validity was tested through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), which was used to confirm the latent structure of the instrument. The final sample was randomly divided into two data sets, with data A for EFA and data B for CFA, and both data sets were used in reliability analysis. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett Sphericity Test were used to confirm the suitability of the data for EFA. Before EFA was conducted, the KMO test was applied for sampling adequacy and for the criteria of principal component analysis (PCA) requirements, with the KMO value being greater than 0.60 [20]. If factors with an eigenvalue were greater than 1.0 and the factor loading coefficient of the item on the corresponding factor were greater than 0.40, they were considered acceptable for its factors [21]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using data set B to assess the goodness of fit of the model by calculating the Chi-square/degree-of-freedom ratio (χ2/df), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). The IFI, CFI and TLI being greater than 0.80, the RMSEA less than 0.06 and χ2/df less than 3.0 were considered as a good fit [22, 23].

Convergent validity indicated the level of correlation of multiple items of the same factor that were in agreement. A composite reliability (CR) of more than 0.70 and an average variance extracted estimate (AVE) of more than 0.50 were considered, and each factor was measured consistently [24]. Modification indices (MI) were also evaluated as they provide detailed information regarding model misfits. Modification indices over 10 were considered large enough to require model modification or item deletion [25].

The Cronbach’s alpha was used to evaluate internal consistency, and its coefficient greater than 0.70 was considered acceptable [26]. To test the retest reliability, at least 17 participants should be selected in the second week after completing the questionnaire for the first time to determine the Inter-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC), and a value of greater than 0.08 was considered to indicate good agreement [22].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 741 participants were included in this study, with 413 in Data Set A for exploratory factor analysis and 328 in Data Set B for confirmatory factor analysis. The mean age of the participants was 31.37 years (SD = 4.31). The mean gestational week of their partners was 20.89 weeks (SD = 7.51). Two-thirds of the participants had an education level of a college graduate. Most participants had a monthly income of more than 5000 Chinese Yuan (CNY), and nearly half had an income between 5001 and 10,000 CNY. Most partners of the expectant fathers were primigravida or nulliparae and did not experience adverse birth outcomes. More than three out of four participants preferred vaginal delivery (74.2%) to cesarean delivery (25.8%). Socio-demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Validity and reliability

Item analysis

The revised item and correlation coefficient of the 33 items ranged from 0.335 to 0.495.

Content validity test

The final I-CVI of the items was higher than 0.80. The S-CVI/AVE value was 0.92 based on expert consultation. Complex sentences were modified according to the experts’ opinions. Item 3, “Lonely”, was revised to “Powerlessness”. The experts suggested that during childbirth, the feeling of expectant fathers may not be lonely, but rather that they received insufficient support and may feel unsure of what was happening and what to do. The lack of full participation and attention from the expectant father could result in frustration and distress. In item 15, “Abandon” was revised to “Ignored”, and in item 10 “Independent” was changed to “Be prepared”. Item 32, “Child will die”, was revised because the experts suggested that Chinese people express more implicitly about death, and the word “death” was too straightforward. The experts recommended to be changed to “something bad happened”. Participants indicated that these questionnaires were easy to understand and took an average of seven minutes to complete.

Construct validity test

Exploratory factor analysis

The EFA was conducted on the data set (n = 413). The KMO value was 0.842, and Bartlett’s test value was 5056.614 (p < 0.001), which indicated that the variables were suitable for the factor analysis. The eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were extracted, accounting for 60.97% of the cumulative variance. All items had a loading of more than 0.4 on their single factor. The eight factors were: Attitudes toward the process of childbirth of their wife, Feeling of powerlessness, Self-efficacy, Feeling of helplessness, Positive emotions, Feeling of fear and reactions, Cognition of the childbirth and newborn, and Worry about the newborn, respectively (see Table 2).

Confirmatory factor analysis

A CFA was conducted on another data set (n = 328) to cross-validate the fit of the data to the factorial structure. The eight-factor model showed a poor fit with χ2/df (4.035) and the applicable index of CFI, TLI and IFI (p < 0.001). After adjusting the original model, the model (χ2/df = 2.982, RMSEA = 0.076, IFI = 0.921, CFI = 0.903 and TLI = 0.917) was generated according to the modification indices, which showed a good fit (see Table 3 and Fig. 1). The convergent validity results showed that the AVE values ranged from 0.422 to 0.782, and the CR values ranged from 0.729 to 0.899 (See Table 4).

Reliability test

The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the adapted W-DEQ AF was 0.843, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the sub-factors ranged from 0.635 to 0.845. When removing one item at a time, Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranged from 0.832 to 0.848 (Table 2). The sample size for test–retest reliability was 45, reaching 10% of the exploratory factor analysis sample size. The retest reliability was 0.917 (p < 0.01). Overall, the internal consistency and test–retest reliability indicated that the tool is reliable and consistent.

Discussion

The W-DEQ AF is a self-report measurement instrument designed to assess the FOC of expectant fathers. The revised questionnaire tested the psychometric properties with good reliability and validity. The scale covers a wide range of content that is valid in the Chinese cultural context. Findings suggested that the W-DEQ AF is a credible and valid tool for assessing FOC among Chinese expectant fathers. The content of the W-DEQ AF is easily understood by the target population and takes less than 10 min to complete. The use of validated, specific scales to assess the FOC in expectant fathers is of great value in clinical practice and research.

Although a Chinese fathers’ version of the Fear of Childbirth Scale (C-FFCS) was introduced in 2023 [11], this original scale has not been utilized in many countries. The original FFCS was constructed by Ghaffari et al. to investigate the levels of FOC among expectant fathers, which included 17 items and divided into 2 domains entitled “fear of childbirth” and “fear of hospital” [3]. However, the revised version of the C-FFCS has 16 items grouped into 3 factors [11], which was a bit different from the original FFCS. It was essential to further verify the C-FFCS in a larger sample before widespread use. Compared to the C-FFCS, the revised W-DEQ AF retained all items of original questionnaire within the acceptable range, which included 33 items and divided into eight factors. Similarly, the Chinese female version of W-DEQ A retains all the items, with five factors [27]. Actually, the number of items loaded to each factor and the number of items retained with adequate psychometric validity in the revised versions of the W-DEQ A varied across studies [28, 29]. The W-DEQ AF was a multidimensional tool to measure multiple dimensions of FOC, which was consistent with the Chinese version of W-DEQ A [27].

In this study, the findings identified eight factors in this scale. The first factor is “Attitudes toward the process of childbirth”. Thoughts of Chinese expectant fathers could be fantastical, frightening, panic-driven, and painful when thinking about their spouse and feeling afraid. This factor was in line with the scale in the women’s version of W-DEQ A [27, 29]. Their emotions can be influenced by attitudes [30]. The second factor is the “Feeling of powerlessness”, including items such as feeling powerlessness, tension and weakness. The third factor is “Self-efficacy” and it consists of a robust and confident belief that their wife will be safe and prepared well during labor. This factor is the same as in another study regarding the women’s version [27, 29]. The fourth factor was the “Feeling of helplessness”, which was similar to the factor 3 (“Isolation”) in the Chinese version of W-DEQ A [27]. Some expectant fathers often described feeling helpless, powerless, anxious, and distressed during their partner's pregnancy and birth [30]. The fifth factor is “Positive emotions”, such as gladness, composition and relaxation, which focused on expectations and experiences of childbirth as a happy and cheerful event. This factor aligns with other studies [29, 31, 32]. The sixth factor is “Fear and reaction”, including hopelessness, behaving badly, fear of danger to their wife and losing control. Fear appears to be associated with feelings about the outcome of childbirth and their partner’s health status [4]. The seventh factor is “Cognition of childbirth and newborn”. It includes emotions such as the thought that everything is natural, trusting their wife and healthcare workers, being proud of their wife and happiness and longing for the baby. During the process of childbirth, expectant fathers may have a new understanding of childbirth, and this is similar to those of pregnant women and related to the experience of childbirth [33, 34]. The last factor is “Worry about the newborn”. Referring to the Chinese women version of W-DEQ A [27], its factor 4 (“Concern for the child”) only included item 32 and 33, which was consistent with our results. It is common for expectant fathers to be concerned about the newborn’s health, including fetal deformities or even death [10]. Factors 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 are the same as the original version for women in previous studies [16, 35]. These items relate to the expectations of the expectant fathers about childbirth.

Due to the lack of previous research instruments to assess fathers’ FOC, comparing items is difficult. In a Swedish study, eight items irrelevant to men were removed [5], and the scale was used to evaluate expectant fathers’ FOC and the impact on the birth experience [36]. In terms of reliability, the internal consistency of the W-DEQ AF, as indicated by Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale and each factor, was acceptable, though lower than the Swedish study with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 [5] and lower than the version for Chinese women [13, 37]. The test–retest reliability of the W-DEQ AF was high, indicating good stability in measurement.

Results of the CFA indicated that the CFA was only barely acceptable. The modified parameters met the required thresholds and fit the W-DEQ AF well. Regarding the modifications, part of the reason may be that there may be a more complex structure or relationship behind the W-DEQ A, particularly between the modified variables. Another explanation is bias due to using a convenience sampling method. The CR value was good in the convergent validity of the W-DEQ AF, but the AVE values for Factor 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7 were not satisfactory. However, it cannot be said that convergent validity was not achieved. It could be that CR, the indicator of convergent validity, was satisfied. To improve convergent validity, further research is needed to refine the scale by removing items that are not highly relevant. Regarding inter-factor discriminant validity, it is difficult to say that there is absolutely no correlation between elements within a concept because the measurement instrument is designed to measure a concept [22]. In this respect, the results of construct validity were reasonable.

The W-DEQ A is currently the most frequently used assessment tool for FOC internationally and has a high application value due to its multidimensional properties [38]. The W-DEQ AF has these same characteristics. It can assist healthcare professionals to clinically identify the current status of expectant fathers’ FOC and plan interventions to address these issues during the prenatal and postpartum periods. In addition, this study enriches the research related to expectant fathers and provides other researchers with tools to promote further research on the FOC in expectant fathers.

There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, this cross-sectional study was conducted from only two hospitals in Wuhan. Population-based longitudinal studies are needed to further validate the adverse outcomes of FOC in Chinese expectant fathers and the cut-off points for the scale. Secondly, most of the indicators assessed in this study met the criteria, but the RMSEA (0.076) was still slightly higher than the 0.06 criterion. However, the values were very similar. Further research is recommended to determine if a more stable factor structure is available. In addition, the lack of criterion validity of the W-DEQ AF may be one of limitations of this current study, suggesting further diverse population need to be investigated to establish a norm.

Conclusions

The current study demonstrated that the Chinese version of the W-DEQ-A is a valid and reliable tool for measuring the FOC in men. Assessment of FOC could help to explore men’s psychological status and monitor their birth experience during pregnancy among expectant fathers in China. It is recommended for healthcare professionals, especially obstetric healthcare workers, to pay a close attention to expectant fathers’ fears and negative attitudes towards childbirth so that effective personalized support can be provided in clinical care settings.
