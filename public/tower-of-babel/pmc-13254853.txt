Dramatic Increase of Congenital Syphilis in Hungary—Imperative National Public Health and Laboratory-Based Actions are Under Implementation
Authors: Eszter Balla, Fruzsina Petrovay, Tímea Tisza, Magnus Unemo
Published: 2026

Congenital syphilis (CS) can result in serious adverse fetal/neonatal outcomes, including stillbirth and postnatal death. In the EU/EEA, confirmed CS cases are surveyed using EU case definitions.1 In 2020–2024, the CS incidence in 28 EU/EEA countries increased from 1.3 (n = 46) to 4.7 (n = 140) cases/100,000 livebirths (Appendix p 2).2 This substantial increase necessitates detailed investigations of national antenatal care (ANC) systems and their syphilis screening, to inform evidence-based actions to control CS. In 2024, Hungary reported 30% (42/140) of all confirmed CS cases in the EU/EEA (Appendix p 2).2

We report the confirmed CS cases in Hungary, 2012–2025, and imperative public health and laboratory-based actions implemented to reduce the CS incidence.

In 2012–2020, 0–5 annual CS cases were confirmed in Hungary (Fig. 1). A dramatic increase in CS cases, including fatal cases, was observed from 2021 (12 cases, 12.9/100,000 livebirths) to 2025 (n = 91, 126.4/100,000 livebirths).3,4 From 2021, the syphilis incidence also in adult females and males has sharply increased (Appendix p 3). Among the 91 CS cases in 2025, 19 caused intrauterine fetal losses and seven early postnatal deaths (Fig. 1).

Confirmed congenital syphilis (CS) cases (black line) and fatal CS cases (stillbirths and neonatal deaths; black dashed line) in Hungary, 2012–2025.3,4

Public health and laboratory-based actions to reduce the CS incidence in Hungary are under implementation since 2022, which likely partly explain the high incidence in 2024–2025 (more cases were detected). The following implemented actions will hopefully reduce the CS incidence:

Firstly, in 2022, a detailed national CS register was established at the National Center for Public Health and Pharmacy (NCPHP), Budapest. This CS register contains and identifies all confirmed CS cases and intrauterine infections, including fatal cases, linked to sociodemographic, epidemiological and clinical data, allowing identification of most affected regions and CS risk factors that inform actions to implement.

Secondly, in 2024, the National Guideline for Management of Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) was optimised.5 For increased diagnostic sensitivity, a Treponema pallidum (TP)-PCR test,6 when required confirmed with another TP-PCR test,7 has replaced dark-field microscopy and TP direct fluorescent antibody test (DFA-TP), which are no longer recommended for routine diagnosis in Europe.8 Sensitive TP-PCR tests6,7 and/or TP-IgM detecting serological assays,9 supported by non-treponemal tests (VDRL/RPR), are now primary methods for CS confirmation (Appendix pp 4–5).5 Accordingly, the identification of suspicious CS cases across Hungary has improved because of broader appropriate sampling, diagnostics, and regular follow-up.

Thirdly, NCPHP collects and analyses detailed data of all suspicious CS cases, confirms CS cases (in vivo and postmortem), and provides training/collaborations with diagnostic laboratories, and regular consultations with clinicians.

Fourthly, the national ANC system has included a free-of-charge syphilis (and HBV) screening routinely offered in the 1st trimester. However, the 2024 national STI guideline5 recommends syphilis screening also in the 2nd and 3rd trimester for females at higher risks, which has been implemented in several regions with higher CS incidence. Furthermore, a free-of-charge 2nd syphilis screening in weeks 24–28 of gestation is planned to be universally offered nationally (likely from July, 2026) and, since December, 2025 a free-of-charge syphilis screening using a rapid diagnostic treponemal test is routinely offered at maternity wards nationally (to capture hard-to-reach females that have avoided previous syphilis screening and females (re)-infected shortly before delivery).

Fifthly, all syphilis-seropositive females are provided laboratory testing, venerological check-up, and treatment including follow-up, and neonates of syphilis-seropositive mothers are treated, as required. Work to improve assessments of coverage of ANC visits, syphilis screening of pregnant females attending ANC, and appropriate treatment of syphilis-seropositive pregnant females is on-going.

Finally, current serious CS situation and essential actions are continuously emphasized in trainings and consultations with ANC professionals, neonatologists, laboratory professionals and pathologists, and communicated in national media.

In conclusion, for future control of CS in Hungary public health and laboratory-based actions are continuously implemented to ensure sensitive detection and appropriate timely treatment of all syphilis-infected pregnant females (plus their partner(s)) and neonates. In Hungary and several additional EU/EEA countries, the 1st-trimester syphilis screening may not be sufficient. In Hungary, syphilis screening also in the 2nd and/or 3rd trimester, and at delivery, is considered required. Furthermore, to avoid underestimating the CS incidence in the EU/EEA, the EU CS case definition (Appendix p 4)1 needs revision: appropriate diagnostic methods should be recommended for CS confirmation (e.g., TP-PCR test instead of dark-field microscopy or DFA-TP),8 and stillbirths reported (Appendix pp 4–5), as in the WHO and US CDC CS case definitions.10 The increasing CS incidence in Hungary and EU/EEA aligns with substantial increases in syphilis incidence among females of reproductive age since 2021–2022.2,3 Improved prevention, diagnosis and treatment of syphilis among adult females and males in the general population remain imperative.
