Eigenvalue bounds for Schrödinger operators with complex potentials on compact manifolds Thanks: Support through the Engineering & Physical Sciences Research Council (EP/X011488/1) is acknowledged.
Jean-Claude Cuenin

Abstract
. We prove eigenvalue bounds for Schrödinger operator − Δ g + V -\Delta_{g}+V on compact manifolds with complex potentials V V . The bounds depend only on an L q L^{q} -norm of the potential, and they are shown to be optimal, in a certain sense, on the round sphere and more general Zoll manifolds. These bounds are natural analogues of Frank’s [ 9 ] results in the Euclidean case.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Spectral inclusion

Let
\Delta_{g}
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a closed (i.e., compact boundaryless) Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n\geq 2
.
We consider Schrödinger operators

(1)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{g}+V\quad\mbox{on }L^{2}(M)

with a complex-valued potential
V
. The unperturbed operator
-\Delta_{g}
is a
non-negative, unbounded self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(M)
, defined with respect to the Riemannian volume measure
{\rm d}x
. Since
-\Delta_{g}
has compact resolvent, its spectrum consists of nonnegative eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\leq\ldots
with
\lambda_{k}^{2}\to\infty
, and the set of corresponding eigenfunctions
(e_{j})
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)
.
By standard perturbation theory, if
V\in L^{\infty}(M)
, then the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}+V
are contained in a union of closed disks,

(2)

\displaystyle\spec(-\Delta_{g}+V)\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(\lambda_{k}^{2},r),\quad r=\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}.

In this paper, we deal with unbounded potentials, i.e.,
V\in L^{q}(M)
for some
q<\infty
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Delta_{g}
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a closed Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n\geq 2
, and let
q>n/2
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(M,g,q)
such that for all
V\in L^{q}(M)
,

(3)

\displaystyle\spec(-\Delta_{g}+V)\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(\lambda_{k}^{2},Cr_{k})\cup\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|z|)^{-\sigma(q)}\leq C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}\},

where
\lambda_{k}^{2}
are the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}
,

r_{k}:=\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}(1+\lambda_{k})^{2\sigma(q)}
,
and

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma(q):=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{2q}-\frac{1}{2}\quad&\mbox{if }\frac{n}{2}\leq q\leq\frac{n+1}{2},\\
\frac{n-1}{4q}\quad&\mbox{if }\frac{n+1}{2}\leq q\leq\infty.\end{cases}

If
n\geq 3
and
q=n/2
, then there exist constants
C,c>0
, depending on
M,g,q
, such that for all
V\in L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)
satisfying
\|V\|_{L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)}\leq c
, we have

(5)

\displaystyle\spec(-\Delta_{g}+V)\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(\lambda_{k}^{2},Cr_{k}),\quad r_{k}:=\|V\|_{L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)}(1+\lambda_{k}).

We emphasize that the constant in (
3
) is
independent of
V
. In particular,
V
is allowed to be
frequency-dependent
; cf. Theorem
1.3
below. Estimates of a similar type were established in
[
6
]
for the Landau Hamiltonian (that is, the Schrödinger operator with constant magnetic field) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(
n
even). In contrast to the situation here, the radii of the disks tend to zero (i.e., the powers of
\lambda_{k}
are negative) in this case.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the operator
-\Delta_{g}+V
can be defined as an
m
-sectorial operator by standard quadratic form methods; see, e.g.,
[
14
]
, or more specifically,
[
10
, Appendix B]
. The proof uses Sobolev embedding, as in the case of real-valued
V
, considered in
[
2
, Section 6]
.

Remark 1.2
.

(i) We will refer to the union of disks
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(\lambda_{k}^{2},Cr_{k})
as “the first set” in (
3
) and to
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|z|)^{-\sigma(q)}\leq C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}\}
as “the second set”. By distinguishing the cases
|z|\leq 1
or
|z|>1
, it is easy to see that the second set is contained in the disk

(6)

\displaystyle\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|\leq\max((2^{\sigma(q)}C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)})^{2},(2^{\sigma(q)}C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)})^{\frac{1}{1/2-\sigma(q)}})\}

centered at the origin. Since
\lambda_{0}=0
, the disk
D(\lambda_{0}^{2},Cr_{0})
is also centered at the origin and could be absorbed in (
6
) by including the term
C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}
in the maximum. However, the decomposition (
3
) arises naturally in the proof.

(ii) Asymptotically, as
k\to\infty
, the disks in the first set are contained inside the region

(7)

\displaystyle\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|\mathrm{Im}\,z|\leq C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}(\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{\sigma(q)}\}.

Note that
\sigma(q)<1/2
for
q>n/2
and
\sigma(q)=1/2
for
q=n/2
. Hence, for the latter case, the region (
7
) is the interior of a parabola around the positive
\mathrm{Re}\,z
axis, while for
q>n/2
, it is much smaller, approaching a strip
|\mathrm{Im}\,z|\leq C\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}
as
q\to\infty
.

1.2.
Comparison with the Euclidean case

We recall the bounds for the Schrödinger operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
that we wish to compare our results to: For
n/2<q\leq(n+1)/2
,

(8)

\displaystyle\spec(-\Delta+V)\subset[0,\infty)\cup\Big\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|^{q-\frac{n}{2}}\leq C_{n,q}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|V(x)|^{q}{\rm d}x\Big\}.

Moreover, if
n\geq 3
and
q=n/2
, then there exists a constant
c_{n}
such that there are no eigenvalues outside
[0,\infty)
if
\|V\|_{L^{n/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq c_{n}
.
The one-dimensional bound is due to Abramov–Aslanyan–Davies
[
1
]
, and the higher dimensional bounds are due to Frank
[
9
]
. It is known
[
3
]
that the inequality (
8
) fails for
q>(n+1)/2
, whereas Laptev and Safronov
[
16
]
had conjectured its validity up to
q<n
. The limitation to
q\leq(n+1)/2
essentially comes from the Knapp example in harmonic analysis. It requires that
V
decay sufficiently fast at infinity in an average sense. On a compact manifold, such a condition is of course meaningless.
However, if we consider the
n
-dimensional flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and rescale by a factor
L>0
, we obtain

(9)

\displaystyle\spec_{L^{2}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})}(-\Delta+L^{-2}V(\cdot/L))=L^{-2}\spec_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}(-\Delta+V).

Hence, for the rescaled operator, the decay of the potential is now measured relative to the scale
L
. According to Theorem
1.1
, its spectrum is contained in the
L^{-2}
dilation of the set on the right-hand side of (
3
). In Section
4
, we will show that, as
L\to\infty
, all limit points of this set lie in the right-hand side of (
8
), provided that
n/2<q\leq(n+1)/2
. More precisely, it follows from the proof of Lemma
4.1
that limit points of the first set in (
3
) (the union of disks) are contained in
[0,\infty)
, while limit points of the second set of (
3
) are contained in the second set on the right-hand side of (
8
).

We could also use (
3
) to prove (
8
), but since the latter is already known, we will not pursue this direction. More importantly, the argument shows that since (
8
) is known to be optimal
[
3
]
, the second set on the right-hand side of (
3
) is also optimal in the sense that, for large
|z|
, the power
|z|^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma(q)}
cannot be improved on a torus, in the regime where the sidelength
L
tends to infinity.

1.3.
Saturation on the sphere and Zoll manifolds

We turn to the optimality of the first set on the right-hand side of (
3
) when
M=S^{n}
, the round sphere, or more generally, a Zoll manifold. These are manifolds whose geodesics are periodic with a common minimal period. Without loss of generality after rescaling the metric, we may assume that
the common minimal period is
2\pi
. By a theorem of Weinstein

[
23
]
, there is a constant
\alpha=\alpha_{M}\geq 0

so that all the nonzero eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}
cluster around the values
(k+\alpha)^{2}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. More precisely,
there is a constant
A=A_{M}
such that each non-zero eigenvalue is contained in a cluster
[(k+\alpha-A/k)^{2},(k+\alpha+A/k)^{2}]
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and the number of eigenvalues in this cluster, counted with multiplicity, is
d_{k}\approx k^{n-1}
. We label the eigenvalues in the
k
-th cluster by
\lambda_{k,j}^{2}
,
j=1,\ldots,d_{k}
.

In the case of the sphere
S^{n}
, the eigenvalues of the Laplacian are given explicitly by
\lambda_{k,j}^{2}=k(k+n-1)
, and thus the first set in (
3
) is a union of disks of the form

(10)

\displaystyle D_{k}:=D(k(k+n-1),Ck^{2\sigma(q)}\|V\|_{L^{q}(S^{n})}),

when
k\geq 1
. We will consider the asymptotic regime
k\to\infty
here and allow
V
to depend on
k
. For general Zoll manifolds, we will additionally assume that

(11)

\displaystyle k^{2\sigma(q)}\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}\gtrsim 1,

which ensures that all the disks
D(\lambda_{k,j}^{2},Cr_{k,j})
corresponding to
k
-th cluster are contained in the larger disk

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{k}:=D((k+\alpha)^{2},Ck^{2\sigma(q)}\|V\|_{L^{q}(S^{n})}),

for some larger constant
C
, depending on
A
and the lower bound in (
11
).

Theorem 1.3
.

i) Let
n\geq 2
and
q>n/2
.
For any
\rho>0
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
k\gg 1+\rho^{1/(1-2\sigma(q))}
,
there is a potential
V=V_{k,\theta,\rho}\in L^{q}(S^{n})
,
\|V\|_{L^{q}(S^{n})}\approx\rho
, such that

(13)

\displaystyle k(k+n-1)+\rho{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(1+\mathcal{O}(\rho k^{2\sigma(q)-1}))k^{2\sigma(q)}\in\spec(-\Delta_{S^{n}}+V)\cap D_{k}.

If
n\geq 3
and
q=n/2
, then (
13
) holds provided
\rho\ll 1
.

ii) If
M
is a general Zoll manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
,
q>n/2
,
\rho>0
,
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
k\gg 1
, and if we assume in addition that
k^{2\sigma(q)}\rho\gg 1
, then there is a potential
V=V_{k,\theta,\rho}\in L^{q}(M)
,
\|V\|_{L^{q}(S^{n})}\approx\rho
, such that

(14)

\displaystyle(k+\alpha)^{2}+\rho{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(1+\mathcal{O}(\rho k^{2\sigma(q)-1}))k^{2\sigma(q)}\in\spec(-\Delta_{g}+V)\cap\tilde{D}_{k}.

If
n\geq 3
and
q=n/2
, then (
14
) holds provided
\rho\ll 1
.

Remark 1.4
.

(i) The case
q=\infty
is included but is not particularly interesting, as it is easily seen that (
2
) is sharp when
V
is taken to be a constant potential.

(ii) Theorem
1.3
shows that, on the sphere or more general Zoll manifolds, the radius of the disks in Theorem
1.1
,
r_{k}=\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}(1+\lambda_{k})^{2\sigma(q)}
, cannot be decreased (up to the value of the constant). More precisely, (
3
) cannot hold if
r_{k}
there is replaced by
\epsilon(\lambda)r_{k}
, where
\epsilon:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is any decreasing function satisfying
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\epsilon(\lambda)=0
.

1.4.
Resolvent estimates

Frank
[
9
]
proved the Euclidean bound (
8
) by combining uniform
L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}
resolvent bounds, established by Kenig, Ruiz, and Sogge
[
15
]
in 1987, with the well-known Birman–Schwinger principle. The latter stipulates that
z
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta+V
if and only if
-1
is an eigenvalue of the compact operator
|V|^{1/2}(-\Delta-z)^{-1}V^{1/2}
. Here and in the following,
V^{1/2}:=V/|V|^{1/2}
. We will follow the same strategy for proving Theorem
1.1
. Compared to the Euclidean case,
L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}
resolvent bounds for the Laplace–Beltrami operator on compact manifolds have a comparatively short history. To make a direct connection to Theorem
1.1
we shall adopt the following convention throughout this paper,

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}.

Dos Santos Ferreira, Kenig, and Salo
[
8
]
proved the uniform bound

(16)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim|z|^{\sigma(q)-\frac{1}{2}},\quad|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1,

for
p=2n/(n+2)
and
n\geq 3
, in which case
\sigma(q)-\frac{1}{2}=0
. The latter improves earlier work of Shen
[
17
]
for the flat torus. Here and in the following, we are using the principal branch of the square root on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
with
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}\geq 0
. Frank and Schimmer
[
12
]
, and, independently, Burq, Dos Santos Ferreira, and Krupchyk
[
5
]
, proved (
16
) for the endpoint
p=2(n+1)/(n+3)
and
n\geq 2
. A straightforward interpolation argument, combined with some additional considerations specific to the case
n=2
, will yield the estimates for the intermediate values of
p
in the region where
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1
(see also
[
12
, Remark 9]
). Note that the estimates are uniform in this region, as
\sigma(q)-\frac{1}{2}\leq 0
. We will see that these uniform resolvent estimates account for the second set on the right-hand side of (
3
). In contrast, the first set, consisting of a union of disks, corresponds to non-uniform resolvent estimates in the complementary region:

(17)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim\mathrm{dist}(z,\spec(-\Delta_{g}))^{-1}(1+|z|)^{\sigma(q)},\quad

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|<1.

These estimates are new in this generality. They are proved by using Sogge’s spectral cluster bounds
[
20
]
, in the spirit of Bourgain, Shao, Sogge, and Yao
[
4
]
and the author’s own work
[
7
]
. The “universal” bounds of Sogge
[
20
]
, valid for any closed manifold
M
, state that

(18)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\lambda-1/2,\lambda+1/2])\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim(1+\lambda)^{\sigma(q)}.

Sogge’s bounds hold for the larger range
2\leq p^{\prime}\leq\infty
(see (
20
), (
34
)) than indicated by (
4
), (
15
). However, when using (
18
) to prove resolvent estimates, the latter restrictions are imposed by Sobolev embedding.

We remark that, in the special case
M=S^{n}
,
n\geq 3
and
p=2n/(n+2)
, (
17
) was proved in
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
.
In fact, they showed that (
17
) is an approximate equality in this case. This shows that the region of
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which (
16
) holds for
n\geq 3
and
p=2n/(n+2)
, is essentially optimal on the sphere and, more generally, on any Zoll manifold.
Similarly, optimality of the region
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|>1
in (
16
) for Zoll manifolds, in the case
p=2(n+1)/(n+3)
and
n\geq 2
, was proved in
[
12
]
.

Notation

We write
A\lesssim B
for two non-negative quantities
A,B\geq 0
to indicate
that there is a constant
C>0
such that
A\leq CB
. If
C
depends on a list of parameters
\ell
, we sometimes emphasize this by writing
A\lesssim_{\ell}B
. However, the dependence of
C
on fixed parameters like the manifold
(M,g)
, the dimension
n
and the Lebesgue exponent
q
is usually omitted. The notation
A\approx B
means
A\lesssim B\lesssim A
. We will often consider the frequency
\lambda_{k}
as an asymptotic parameter,
\lambda_{k}\to\infty
(thus also
k\to\infty
). The notation
A\ll B
means that
A\leq cB
, where
c>0
is suitably small but fixed (in particular, independent of
k
). Sometimes, this notation is used to shorten a “there exists…” statement, e.g. in Theorem
1.3

k\gg 1
means “there exists
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
, we have…”.
We denote the
L^{p}
norm of a function
f
on
M
, with respect to the Riemannian measure
{\rm d}x
, by
\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}
. If
T:L^{s}(M)\to L^{r}(M)
is a bounded linear operator, we denote its operator norm by
\|T\|_{L^{s}(M)\to L^{r}(M)}
. If
r=s=2
, we sometimes write
\|T\|:=\|T\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}
.
For a subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we define
\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in\Omega)
by functional calculus. Explicitly, if we denote the orthogonal projection onto the eigenfunction
e_{j}
by
E_{j}
, that is
E_{j}f=\langle e_{j},f\rangle e_{j}
, then
\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in\Omega)f:=\sum_{\lambda_{j}\in\Omega}E_{j}f
. The inner product is defined as
\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{M}\overline{u}v\,{\rm d}x
.
The closed disk centered at
r\in{\mathbb{C}}
, with radius
r>0
, is denoted by
D(z,r)\subset{\mathbb{C}}
.

2.
Resolvent estimates

In the following, we will use the abbreviation

d(z):=\mathrm{dist}(z,\spec(-\Delta_{g})).

Since
\Delta_{g}
is self-adjoint, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}=d(z)^{-1}.

Note that
p=2
corresponds to
q=\infty
and
\sigma(\infty)=0
. Since
q
will not play any role in this section (except in the proof of Theorem
1.1
), we will use the notation
\nu(p^{\prime}):=\sigma(q)
when
1/q=1/p-1/p^{\prime}
. Explicitly,

(20)

\displaystyle\nu(p^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})\quad&\mbox{if }2\leq p^{\prime}\leq\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1},\\
n(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})-\frac{1}{2}\quad&\mbox{if }\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}\leq p^{\prime}\leq\infty.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Delta_{g}
be the Laplace–Beltrami operator on an
n
-dimensional closed Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
. Let
2n/(n+2)\leq p\leq 2
if
n\geq 3
and
1<p\leq 2
if
n=2
.
Then there is a constant
C=C(M,g,p)
such that for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\spec(-\Delta_{g})
,

(21)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq C|z|^{\nu(p^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}},\quad|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1,

(22)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq Cd(z)^{-1}(1+|z|)^{\nu(p^{\prime})},\quad|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|<1.

Remark 2.2
.

(i) The region
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1
corresponds to the set

(23)

\displaystyle\Xi:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{2}\geq 4(\mathrm{Re}\,z+1)\},

which is the exterior of a parabola.

(ii) The new result here is (
22
), whereas (
21
) is essentially known.

Proof of (
21
).

If
p=2n/(n+2)
and
n\geq 3
, or if
2n/(n+2)<p\leq 2(n+1)/(n+3)
and
n\geq 2
, then (
21
) follows from
[
8
]
, or from
[
12
, Theorem 1 and Remark 9]
, respectively. For
\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq 0
, we have
d(z)=|z|
, so (
19
) is stronger than (
21
) when
p=2
. For
\mathrm{Re}\,z\geq 0
,
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}\geq 1
, we have
|z|\lesssim d(z)^{2}
, hence (
19
) implies (
21
) in this region as well when
p=2
. The Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem between
p=2n/(n+2)
,
p=2(n+1)/(n+3)
and
p=2
then yields (
21
) for
2n/(n+2)\leq p\leq 2
and
n\geq 3
. For
n=2
, one interpolates with some
p>1
arbitrarily close to
p=1
.
∎

Before proceeding with the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we record the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
A\geq 1
there exists a constant
C_{A}
(also depending on
n,q
) such that

(24)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\leq C_{A}d(z)^{-1},\quad\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq A.

Proof.

We employ a spectral decomposition into frequencies
\lambda_{j}\leq\sqrt{2A}
and
\lambda_{j}>\sqrt{2A}
. For the latter, we have a uniform bound

(25)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(-\Delta_{g}>2A)(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\leq C_{A},\quad\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq A.

Indeed, since for
\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq A
,

(26)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(-\Delta_{g}>2A)(-\Delta_{g}+1)(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}\leq\sup_{\tau>2A}\frac{\tau+1}{|\tau-z|}\lesssim 1,

it follows from Sobolev embedding (and its dual)
that if
f
has frequencies
\lambda_{j}>\sqrt{2A}
(i.e.,
f
is in the range of
\mathbf{1}(-\Delta_{g}>2A)
), then

(27)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(-\Delta_{g}+1)^{\frac{n}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}

(28)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(-\Delta_{g}+1)^{\frac{n}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})-1}(-\Delta_{g}-z)f\|_{L^{2}(M)}

(29)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)f\|_{L^{p}(M)}.

Next, if
f
has frequencies
\lambda_{j}\leq\sqrt{2A}
, then
by Young’s inequality,
\|f\|_{L^{r}(M)}\lesssim_{A}\|f\|_{L^{s}(M)}
for all
1\leq s\leq r\leq\infty
(see e.g.
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
). In particular, if
(e_{j})
is an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of
-\Delta_{g}
in
L^{2}(M)
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{2}
and
E_{j}
denotes the orthogonal projection
E_{j}f=\langle e_{j},f\rangle e_{j}
, then

(30)

\displaystyle\|E_{j}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\leq\|e_{j}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}^{2}\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}

for
\lambda_{j}\leq\sqrt{2A}
and
E_{j}f=0
for
\lambda_{j}>\sqrt{2A}
.
Thus,

(31)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\leq\sum_{\lambda_{j}\leq\sqrt{2A}}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}^{2}-z|}\|E_{j}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim_{A}d(z)^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}.

In the last inequality, we also used that
\#\{j:\lambda_{j}\leq\sqrt{2A}\}\lesssim_{A}1
since the spectrum is discrete.
∎

We now turn to the proof of (
22
).

Proof of (
22
).

If
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}\leq 10
, then (
22
) follows from Lemma
2.3
, so it remains to consider the case
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}>10
.
We recall that we are also assuming
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|<1
.
We will split the resolvent into two pieces, one involving frequencies close to
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
and the other involving frequencies at least a unit distance from
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
, i.e.
|\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|\leq\delta
for the first piece and
|\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|>\delta
for the second piece. Here,
0<\delta\leq 1
is fixed, and we will take
\delta=1/2
throughout (any other choice can be reduced to this one by scaling the metric).
We will prove

(32)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim d(z)^{-1}|z|^{\nu(p^{\prime})},

(33)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim|z|^{\nu(p^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining these estimates yields the claimed bound (
22
). Note that (
33
) is stronger since
|z|^{-1/2}\lesssim d(z)^{-1}
for
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}<1
,
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}>10
.

The proof of (
32
) uses Sogge’s spectral cluster bounds
[
20
]
,

(34)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\lambda-1/2,\lambda+1/2])\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim(1+\lambda)^{\nu(p^{\prime})},

(35)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\lambda-1/2,\lambda+1/2])\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}\lesssim(1+\lambda)^{\nu(p^{\prime})},

with
\lambda=\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
.
Thus, by (

), (
35
), (

),

(36)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

(37)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{\nu(p^{\prime})}\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}

(38)

\displaystyle\leq d(z)^{-1}(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{\nu(p^{\prime})}\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])f\|_{L^{2}(M)}

(39)

\displaystyle\lesssim d(z)^{-1}(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)},

which proves (
32
) since
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}\leq|z|^{1/2}
. A more general version of the above argument can be found in
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
and
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
.

The proof of (
33
) is reduced to the case
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1
by the following argument (similar to the proof of
[
4
, (2.33)]
: Choose
z_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}=\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
and
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}|=1
. Then

(40)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

(41)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

(42)

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])[(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}-(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}]f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}=I+II.

For the first term
I
, we have

(43)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\leq\|(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}+\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{II}.

Since
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}=1
, it follows from (
21
) that
\mathcal{I}
is bounded by the right-hand side of (
33
). For
II
and
\mathcal{II}
, we will again use Sogge’s bounds (
34
), (
35
) to show that

(45)

\displaystyle II+\mathcal{II}\lesssim|z|^{\nu(p^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}.

The proof of the bound for
\mathcal{II}
is similar to that of (
32
). The only difference is that the
L^{2}
bound in the third line of (

) is replaced by

(46)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\in[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}\lesssim|z|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(M)},

where we used that, for
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}=1
,
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}>10
,

(47)

\displaystyle|\tau^{2}-z_{1}|=|\tau-\sqrt{z_{1}}||\tau+\sqrt{z_{1}}|\geq(\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{1}})(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z_{1}})\geq\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}\gtrsim|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Given the resolvent identity

(48)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}-(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}=(z-z_{1})(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}

and the fact that
|z-z_{1}|\lesssim\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
, the bound for
II
in (
45
) would follow from

(49)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\lesssim|z|^{\nu(p^{\prime})-1}.

Since the left-hand side is dominated by

(50)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)}\|(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{2}(M)},

and since the
L^{2}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)
norm equals the
L^{p}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
norm of the adjoint operator, it suffices to prove

(51)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{g}}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2])(-\Delta_{g}-\overline{z})^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{\nu(p^{\prime})}{2}-\frac{1}{2}},

(52)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{2}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{\nu(p^{\prime})}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}.

We start with the proof of (
52
). Since
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}=1
and
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z_{1}}=\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
, we have the lower bound

(53)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{j}^{2}-z_{1}|=|\lambda_{j}+\sqrt{z_{1}}||\lambda_{j}-\sqrt{z_{1}}|\gtrsim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})(|\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1),

Thus, by
L^{2}
orthogonality,

(54)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z_{1})^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}^{2}-z_{1}|^{-2}\|E_{j}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

(55)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(|\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1)^{-2}\|E_{j}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

(56)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sup_{\tau\in[k-1,k)}(|\tau-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1)^{-2}\|\sum_{\lambda_{j}\in[k-1,k)}E_{j}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

(57)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{-2}\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1)^{-2}k^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}.

In the last line, we used Sogge’s spectral cluster bound (
35
).
We split the sum into two pieces, one with
|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
and the other with
|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|>\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
. For the first piece, we use
k\leq\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
to estimate

(58)

\displaystyle\sum_{|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}}(|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1)^{-2}k^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}.

For the second piece, we use that
|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1>\frac{1}{2}|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
, leading to the better estimate

(59)

\displaystyle\sum_{|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|>\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}}(|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1)^{-2}k^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}

(60)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|k-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{-2}k^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}{\rm d}k\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})^{2\nu(p^{\prime})-1}.

Altogether, we obtain (
52
).

For the proof of (
51
), we use that
\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{\overline{z}}=\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}
and
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\overline{z}}=-\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}
. Moreover, for
\lambda_{j}\notin[\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}-1/2,\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}+1/2]
,

(61)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{j}^{2}-\overline{z}|=|\lambda_{j}+\sqrt{\overline{z}}||\lambda_{j}-\sqrt{\overline{z}}|\gtrsim(\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z})(|\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{Re}\,\sqrt{z}|+1).

Thus, (
51
) follows by repeating the proof of (
52
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
. For the proof, we will revert to the notation
\sigma(q)
, recalling that
\nu(p^{\prime})=\sigma(q)
when
1/q=1/p-1/p^{\prime}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By the Birman–Schwinger principle,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\spec(-\Delta_{g})
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{g}+V
if and only if
-1
is an eigenvalue of the (compact) Birman–Schwinger operator
K(z):=|V|^{1/2}(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}V^{1/2}
. In particular, if
z
is an eigenvalue, then
K(z)
has norm at least one, which implies that

(62)

\displaystyle 1\leq\|K(z)\|\leq\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{p}(M)\to L^{p^{\prime}}(M)},

where
1/q=1/p-1/p^{\prime}
. Thus, by (
62
) and the resolvent estimates (
21
), (
22
), if
z\in\spec(-\Delta_{g}+V)
, then either

(63)

\displaystyle|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|z|)^{-\sigma(q)}\lesssim\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}\quad\mbox{or}\quad d(z)\lesssim(1+|z|)^{\sigma(q)}\|V\|_{L^{q}(M)}.

In the first case, (
3
) trivially follows.
The second case in (
63
) occurs for
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|<1
, or equivalently,
z\in\Xi^{c}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{2}<4(\mathrm{Re}\,z+1)\}
, see (
23
). The set
\Xi^{c}
is contained in the union of a neighborhood of the origin and a parabolic region around
(1,\infty)
,

(64)

\displaystyle\Xi^{c}\subset\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|<\sqrt{8}\}\cup\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathrm{Re}\,z>1,\,(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{2}<8\mathrm{Re}\,z\}.

If
|z|<\sqrt{8}
, then the second inequality in (
63
) implies that
z\in D(\lambda_{0}^{2},Cr_{0})
(recalling that
\lambda_{0}=0
). If
\mathrm{Re}\,z>1,\,(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{2}<8\mathrm{Re}\,z
, then there exists a unique
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathrm{Re}\,z\in[\lambda_{k-1}^{2},\lambda_{k}^{2})
. The second inequality in (
63
) then implies that
d(z)\leq Cr_{k}
. Since
d(z)=\min\{|z-\lambda_{k-1}^{2}|,|z-\lambda_{k}^{2}|\}
and since
\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j-1}}=1
by Weyl’s asymptotics, it follows that
z\in D(\lambda_{k-1}^{2},r_{k-1})\cup D(\lambda_{k}^{2},r_{k})
. This completes the proof of (
3
), and hence of Theorem
1.1
in the case
q>n/2
.

If
n\geq 3
and
q=n/2
, then (
21
) yields a uniform bound, i.e.

(65)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{g}-z)^{-1}\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n+2}}(M)\to L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0},\quad|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1.

Therefore, (
62
) implies that

\|V\|_{L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)}\geq C_{0}^{-1}
. Put differently,
z
cannot be an eigenvalue if
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|\geq 1
and
\|V\|_{L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)}<C_{0}^{-1}
. Thus, if we set
c:=C_{0}^{-1}/2
, then all eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}+V
,
\|V\|_{L^{\frac{n}{2}}(M)}\leq c
, are contained in the region
|\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|<1
. Using (
22
), we obtain the refinement stated in (
5
).
∎

3.
Optimality of eigenvalue bounds

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
. We will seek the potential in the form

(66)

\displaystyle V=\kappa\chi^{2},\quad 0\leq\chi=\chi_{k}\in L^{2q}(S^{n}),\quad\|\chi\|_{L^{2q}(S^{n})}=1,\quad\kappa=\kappa_{k,\theta,\rho}\in{\mathbb{C}},\quad 0\leq|\kappa|\lesssim\rho.

3.1.
Optimality on
S^{n}

We recall that

(67)

\displaystyle\spec(-\Delta_{S^{n}})=\{\lambda_{k}^{2}=k(k+n-1):k=0,1,2,\ldots\}

and that the eigenspace
H_{k}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
k(k+n-1)
, the space of spherical harmonics of degree
k
, has dimension

(68)

\displaystyle d_{k}:=\dim H_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}n+k\\
n\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}n+k-2\\
n\end{pmatrix}\approx k^{n-1},

see e.g.
[
18
, Section 22.3]
or
[
21
, Section 3.4]
.

Step 1:
By the Birman–Schwinger principle (see the first paragraph in the proof of Theorem
1.1
), it suffices to find
\chi,\kappa
as above such that
-\kappa^{-1}
is an eigenvalue of the compact operator
\chi(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z_{k,\theta,\rho})^{-1}\chi
, where

(69)

\displaystyle z_{k,\theta,\rho}=k(k+n-1)+\rho{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(1+\mathcal{O}(\rho k^{2\sigma(q)-1}))k^{2\sigma(q)}.

In other words, we seek a zero
(z,\kappa)=(z_{k,\theta,\rho},\kappa_{k,\theta,\rho})
of the operator-valued function

(70)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(z,\kappa):=I+\kappa\chi(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z)^{-1}\chi,\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\spec(-\Delta_{S^{n}}),\quad|\kappa|\lesssim\rho.

For fixed
\kappa
, we denote

(71)

\displaystyle\spec{\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\kappa)}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathcal{A}(z,\kappa)\mbox{ is not invertible in }L^{2}(S^{n})\}.

For
z\in D_{k}
(defined in (
10
)), we decompose
\chi(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z)^{-1}\chi
into a ‘resonant’ part and a ‘nonresonant’ part,

(72)

\displaystyle K_{\rm r}(z):=\frac{\chi P_{k}\chi}{k(k+n-1)-z},\quad K_{\rm nr}(z):=\chi(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z)^{-1}\chi-K_{\rm r}(z),

where
P_{k}
is the orthogonal projection onto
H_{k}
.
Note that

(73)

\displaystyle|\sqrt{z}-\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}|\lesssim\rho k^{2\sigma(q)-1}\quad\mbox{for all }z\in D_{k}.

Thus, if
q>n/2
and
k\gg 1+\rho^{1/(1-2\sigma(q))}
, or if
q=n/2
and
\rho
is sufficiently small, we have

(74)

\displaystyle|\sqrt{z}-\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}|<1/2,\quad\min_{j\neq k}|\sqrt{z}-\sqrt{j(j+n-1)}|\geq 1/2,

for all
z\in D_{k}
; the second inequality also uses that
|\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}-\sqrt{j(j+n-1)}|\gtrsim 1
for
j\neq k
. Then (
73
) implies that we may write

(75)

\displaystyle K_{\rm r}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{S^{n}}}\in[\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}-1/2,\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}+1/2])(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z)^{-1},

(76)

\displaystyle K_{\rm nr}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{S^{n}}}\notin[\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}-1/2,\sqrt{k(k+n-1)}+1/2])(-\Delta_{S^{n}}-z)^{-1},

and hence, by (
32
), (
33
), together with Hölder’s inequality and the normalization condition on
\chi
in (
66
), we have the bounds

(77)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in D_{k}}\|K_{\rm r}(z)\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})\to L^{2}(S^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim k^{2\sigma(q)}|k(k+n-1)-z|^{-1},

(78)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in D_{k}}\|K_{\rm nr}(z)\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})\to L^{2}(S^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim k^{2\sigma(q)-1}.

We will choose
\chi
so that (
77
) becomes an approximate equality.

Step 2:
Since
P_{k}
has rank
d_{k}
and
\chi
is bounded,
\chi P_{k}\chi
has rank at most
d_{k}
; in particular, it is a compact operator. Moreover, for any
f\in L^{2}(S^{n})
,
\langle\chi P_{k}\chi f,f\rangle=\|P_{k}\chi f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(S^{n})}\geq 0
, thus
\chi P_{k}\chi
is self-adjoint and non-negative,
having at most
d_{k}
nonzero eigenvalues
a_{j}(k)
(counted with multiplicity),

(79)

\displaystyle\|\chi P_{k}\chi\|=a_{0}(k)\geq a_{1}(k)\geq\ldots\geq a_{d_{k}}(k)\geq 0.

By the spectral theorem for compact, self-adjoint operators,
\chi P_{k}\chi
has an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
\{f_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{k}}
. If
\{e_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{k}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathop{\rm Ran}P_{k}
(thus,
e_{k,i}
are eigenfuncitons of
-\Delta_{g}
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{2}
), then the functions
f_{k,j}
lie in the linear span of
\{\chi e_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{k}}
. Moreover, the eigenvalues
a_{j}(k)
can be computed by diagonalizing the Gram matrix
G=(G_{ij})
, with
G_{ij}=\langle\chi e_{k,i},\chi e_{k,j}\rangle
,
1\leq i,j\leq d_{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
q>n/2
or
n\geq 3
,
q\geq n/2
. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
sufficiently large, there exists a nonnegative function
\chi=\chi_{k}\in L^{2q}(S^{n})
,
\|\chi\|_{L^{2q}(S^{n})}=1
, and a constant
C_{a_{0}}>0
, such that

(80)

\displaystyle C_{a_{0}}^{-1}k^{2\sigma(q)}\leq a_{0}(k)\leq C_{a_{0}}k^{2\sigma(q)}.

Proof.

Sogge proved
[
19
, Theorem 4.1]
that

(81)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})\to L^{2}(S^{n})}\approx k^{\nu(p^{\prime})},

where we recall that
\nu(p^{\prime})=\sigma(q)
when
1/q=1/p-1/p^{\prime}
. By a
TT^{*}
argument, we also have

(82)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})\to L^{p^{\prime}}(S^{n})}\approx k^{2\nu(p^{\prime})}.

The upper bound in (
82
) and Hölder’s inequality imply that for any nonnegative function
\chi
with
\|\chi\|_{L^{2q}(S^{n})}=1
,

(83)

\displaystyle\|\chi P_{k}\chi\|=\sup_{\|f\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})}=1}\langle f,\chi P_{k}\chi f\rangle\leq\|P_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})\to L^{p^{\prime}}(S^{n})}\sup_{\|f\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})}=1}\|\chi f\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})}\lesssim k^{2\sigma(q)}

since
1/p=1/(2q)+1/2
.

If we take the supremum of (
83
) over all normalized
\chi\in L^{2q}(S^{n})
, we obtain

(84)

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\chi\|_{L^{2q}(S^{n})}=1}\|\chi P_{k}\chi\|=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\chi\|_{L^{2q}(S^{n})}=1\\
\|f\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})}=1\end{subarray}}\langle\chi f,P_{k}\chi f\rangle\geq\sup_{\|g\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})}=1}\langle g,P_{k}g\rangle=\|P_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})\to L^{p^{\prime}}(S^{n})},

where the inequality follows from the fact that any normalized
g\in L^{p}(S^{n})
can be factored as
g=\chi f
, where
\chi
and
f
are normalized functions in
L^{2q}(S^{n})
and
L^{2}(S^{n})
, respectively. Indeed, if we write
g={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\arg g}|g|
, then we can take
\chi=|g|^{\frac{p}{2q}}
and
f={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\arg g}|g|^{\frac{p}{2}}
. It follows from (
82
) that there exists a normalized function
\chi\in L^{2q}(S^{n})
such that we have
\|\chi P_{k}\chi\|\gtrsim k^{2\sigma(q)}
. The lemma is proved since the supremum can be taken over nonnegative functions
\chi
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

(i) The function
\chi
can be constructed as follows.
By (
81
), there exists
\psi_{k}\in L^{p}(S^{n})
such that

(85)

\displaystyle\frac{\|P_{k}\psi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})}}{\|\psi_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})}}\approx k^{\sigma(q)},

and we normalize
\psi_{k}
so that
\|\psi_{k}\|_{L^{p}(S^{n})}=1
.
With this choice, we have

(86)

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{k},P_{k}\psi_{k}\rangle=\|P_{k}\psi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(S^{n})}^{2}\approx k^{2\sigma(q)}.

Similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, setting
\chi_{k}=|\psi_{k}|^{\frac{p}{2q}}
and
f_{k}={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\arg\psi_{k}}|\psi_{k}|^{\frac{p}{2}}
, we then have
\psi_{k}=\chi_{k}f_{k}
,

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho\epsilon^{-1}(\max_{w\in\gamma}|(k+\alpha)^{2}-w|^{-2}k^{2\sigma(q)}+k^{2\sigma(q)-1})

(155)

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}k^{-2\sigma(q)}+\rho\epsilon^{-1}k^{2\sigma(q)-1}\ll 1,

where the last inequality holds for
k\gg 1
if
q>n/2
and for
\rho\ll 1
,
k^{2\sigma(q)}\rho\gg 1
for
q=n/2
,
n\geq 3
. The latter condition is satisfied by assumption.

Thus, Lemma
3.3
is also proved for the Zoll case, and the second part of Theorem
1.3
follows in the same way as the first.

4.
Scaling considerations

Consider
M=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the
n
-dimensional flat torus, viewed as
(-1/2,1/2]^{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For
L>0
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, the space
L^{p}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})
consists of
p
-integrable functions on
Q_{L}:=(-L/2,L/2]^{n}
satisfying periodic boundary conditions. Extension by zero yields an embedding

(156)

\displaystyle L^{p}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})\subset L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Conversely, multiplication with the characteristic function of
Q_{L}
defines a projection

\mathbf{1}_{Q_{L}}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to L^{p}(L\mathbb{T}^{n}).

We define the unitary transformation

(157)

\displaystyle U_{L}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\to L^{2}(L\mathbb{T}^{n}),\quad(U_{L}f)(x):=L^{-\frac{n}{2}}f(x/L).

Then

(158)

\displaystyle L^{-2}U_{L}(-\Delta+V)U_{L}^{-1}=-\Delta+L^{-2}V(\cdot/L),

which implies (
9
). We will consider
L^{2}V(L\cdot)
in place of
V
, in which case (
9
) becomes

(159)

\displaystyle\spec_{L^{2}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})}(-\Delta+V)=L^{-2}\spec_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}(-\Delta+L^{2}V(L\cdot)).

We assume
V\in L^{q}(LM)
for some
q\in(n/2,\infty)
; the case
q=n/2
can be handled similarly (under an additional smallness condition), but we will not consider it here. Theorem
1.1
, and more specifically (
3
), implies that the spectrum of
-\Delta+V
is contained in the set

(160)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(L^{-2}\lambda_{k}^{2},CL^{-2}r_{k})\cup\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:L|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+L^{2}|z|)^{-\sigma(q)}\leq CL^{2-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})}\},

where we used the identity
L^{2}\|V(L\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}=L^{2-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})}
, and where

(161)

\displaystyle r_{0}=L^{2-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})},\quad r_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{2\sigma(q)}L^{2-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(L\mathbb{T}^{n})},\quad k\geq 1.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
M=\mathbb{T}^{n}
be the flat torus. Assume that
(z_{L})_{L\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a convergent sequence (as
L\to\infty
).

1. If
q\in(n/2,\infty)
and
z_{L}\in\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}D(L^{-2}\lambda_{k}^{2},CL^{-2}r_{k})
, then

\lim_{L\to\infty}z_{L}\in[0,\infty)
.

2. If
q\in(n/2,(n+1)/2)
and

(162)

\displaystyle L|z_{L}|^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+L^{2}|z_{L}|)^{-\sigma(q)}\leq CL^{2-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

then
z:=\lim_{L\to\infty}z_{L}
satisfies

(163)

\displaystyle|z|^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma(q)}\leq C\|V\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

with the same constant
C
as in (
162
).

Proof.

1. For
\epsilon>0
to be chosen sufficiently small, we split the union of disks in (
160
) into two sets (strictly speaking, two sequences of sets, depending on
L
): the first set
A_{L}
contains all disks with
\lambda_{k}\leq L^{1+\epsilon}
, and the second set
B_{L}
contains all disks with
\lambda_{k}>L^{1+\epsilon}
. The first set satisfies

(164)

\displaystyle A_{L}:=\bigcup_{\lambda_{k}\leq L^{1+\epsilon}}^{\infty}D(L^{-2}\lambda_{k}^{2},CL^{-2}r_{k})\subset[0,L^{2\epsilon}]+D(0,CL^{2(1+\epsilon)\sigma(q)-\frac{n}{q}}\|V\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}),

where we used the embedding (
156
) to identify the
L^{q}
norm of
V
on
L\mathbb{T}^{n}
with that on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Since
2\sigma(q)-n/q<0
for all
q\in(n/2,\infty)
, we may choose
\epsilon
so small (depending on
q
) that
2(1+\epsilon)\sigma(q)-\frac{n}{q}<0
. Hence, if there exists
L_{0}>1
such that
z_{L}\in A_{L}
for
L\geq L_{0}
, then

(165)

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}z_{L}\in[0,\infty).

The alternative is that for all
L_{0}>1
, there exists
L\geq L_{0}
such that
z_{L}\in B_{L}
. Then we can write
z_{L}=L^{-2}(\lambda_{k}^{2}+Cr_{k}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})
for some
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and some
k
satisfying
\lambda_{k}>L^{1+\epsilon}
. Therefore, we have

(166)

\displaystyle|z_{L}|\geq L^{-2}\lambda_{k}^{2}(1-Cr_{k}\lambda_{k}^{-2})\geq L^{2\epsilon}(1-o(1)).

This shows that
(z_{L})
does not converge, contradicting our assumption. Hence, the alternative cannot hold, and we are done.

2. Fix
R>0
. If there is a subsequence, again denoted by
(z_{L})
, such that
|z_{L}|\leq RL^{-2}
, then
\lim_{L\to\infty}z_{L}=0
, so (
163
) holds trivially. Otherwise,
|z_{L}|>RL^{-2}
for
L
sufficiently large, and then (
162
) implies

(167)

\displaystyle|z_{L}|^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma(q)}\leq C(1+R^{-1})^{\sigma(q)}L^{1-\frac{n}{q}+2\sigma(q)}\|V\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Since
1-\frac{n}{q}+2\sigma(q)=0
for
q\leq(n+1)/2
and
R
is arbitrarily large, the claim follows.
∎
