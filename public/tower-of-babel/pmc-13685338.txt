Behavioural influences on oral health status among individuals with schizophrenia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Harsika Kesavan, Sibyl Siluvai, Indumathi K P, Krishnaprakash G, Arul Saravanan Ramachandran, A V Saravanan, Raghul Gandhi Venkatesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of the study was to evaluate the behavioural assessment using the schizophrenia oral health profile (SOHP) scale and examine the oral health status using Oral Hygiene Index-Simplified (OHI-S), Decayed, Missing and Filled teeth (DMFT), Community Periodontal Index (CPI) and Loss of attachment (LOA) indices, and correlate the behaviour with oral health and determine an association between behavioural assessment and oral health status.MethodsThis hospital-based cross-sectional study included 74 patients with schizophrenia (SCZ), comprising both inpatients and outpatients receiving treatment at the Department of Psychiatry in a randomly selected hospital in Tamil Nadu, India. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling method through the lottery technique for data collection. Demographic data were collected using a structured form. The Schizophrenia–Oral Health Profile (SOHP) questionnaire was administered to all participants to determine their self-perceived oral health status. The participants’ dental (OHI-S, DMFT, CPI, LOA) status was subsequently analysed in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests.ResultsAmong the 74 participants, the majority demonstrated fair oral hygiene habits (83.8%). The experience of dental caries was low to moderate overall, while periodontal disease showed a high prevalence of calculus and periodontal pockets. In this regard, a significant number of patients had a probing depth of ≥ 4 mm and attachment loss of ≥ 4 mm. No significant gender differences were observed in the oral health indices or SOHP scores (p > 0.05). However, significant differences were found across age groups for the OHI-S, DMFT, CPI, LOA, and SOHP scores (p < 0.05). SOHP scores showed a significant positive correlation with DMFT (r = 0.326, p = 0.005) and LOA (r = 0.411, p < 0.001), and a negative correlation with CPI (r = − 0.465, p < 0.001).ConclusionIndividuals with schizophrenia exhibited a considerable burden of dental caries and periodontal disease, with behavioural determinants significantly associated with oral health outcomes. These findings highlight incorporating routine oral health screening and validated behavioural tools such as SOHP into psychiatric care protocols to enable earlier identification of high-risk patients and timely dental referral.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Oral health, Mental health, Questionnaire, Psychiatric patients

Background

Mental health is a crucial element of general health. Mental disorders are progressively assuming an overwhelming burden on global health. They include depressive, bipolar, and anxiety disorders, eating disorders, schizophrenia (SCZ), self-harm, and others, which contribute substantially to poor quality of life [1].

SCZ is a complex mental disorder with a lifetime prevalence of 0.45% among adults, characterised by chronic hallucinations, delusions, psychosis, social withdrawal, agitation, disorganized behaviour, etc [2, 3]. Factors contributing to this are poor oral hygiene habits resulting from a lack of motivation, fewer dental visits, and side effects of medications [4]. Discrimination and stigma are often experienced by patients with SCZ, which contributes to their withdrawal from society and their inability to perform basic routine tasks [5]. According to a recent meta-analysis, 24.2% of patients with SCZ regained premorbid functioning after treatment. It is also estimated that the risk of relapse is 3.5% per month in SCZ patients.

People with schizophrenia face a range of health problems in addition to mental illness. Diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, and other psychiatric disorders are the main causes of morbidity and mortality in this population [6, 7]. In addition, these people invariably have their oral health substantially compromised [8]. Studies observe that those who have schizophrenia experience the severity of dental pathology regarding caries, periodontal disease, and tooth loss higher than that of the general population [9]. Oral health is one of those basic bodily functions related to eating and speaking that plays its part in social interaction, self-esteem, and confidence [10].

Oral and mental health are closely interrelated, with complex interactions [11]. Maintaining daily oral hygiene can be particularly challenging for people with schizophrenia, and regular dental visits are also less common among these patients. Dry mouth from decreased saliva production is one of the side effects of many medications used to treat schizophrenia [12], including mood stabilisers, antipsychotics, and anticholinergic drugs [13]. Dental decay and other oral health issues may worsen more quickly due to this deficiency, along with difficulty in maintaining oral hygiene [14]. Therefore, there is an increasing need to put forth initiatives aimed at recognizing specific psychiatric patient groups and their concerns, owing to the suboptimal dental healthcare that they receive [15].

The WHO’s Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan (2013–2030) emphasizes the importance of integrated mental and physical health care services. This study fulfils its purpose and will aid in the growth of our knowledge, shedding light on the correlation between mental and dental aspects for better treatment and directing attention towards patients who require special care.

The rationale for this particular study was based on the well-established correlation and cohesiveness between mental health and oral health. Schizophrenic patients often exhibit poor oral hygiene due to multiple factors. While all this poses a significant concern in this population, it remains underdiagnosed and undertreated. Not many studies/research have been carried out in this field to find the association of mental health with oral health-based behaviour. While existing literature documents poor oral health outcomes in schizophrenia, few studies have applied a validated, multidimensional behavioural tool to directly correlate psychiatric-behavioural patterns with objectively measured clinical oral health indices. The present study addresses this gap by integrating the Schizophrenia Oral Health Profile (SOHP) which captures emotional, social, physical, and self-perception domains related to oral health, with standard clinical indices (OHI-S, DMFT, CPI, LOA) within a single cohort, enabling a more comprehensive, behaviourally informed assessment of oral health status than has typically been reported in this population. This study aimed to assess oral and periodontal health along with behavioural factors using the SOHP scale to highlight the need for integrated dental and mental health care, ultimately improving their overall well-being.

Materials and methods

Study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria

This cross-sectional study was carried out at the Department of Psychiatry, of a hospital in Tamil Nadu, India, which was randomly selected. Study participants included individuals diagnosed with SCZ, receiving outpatient or inpatient care at the hospital. The diagnosis was based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) of the American Psychiatric Association (APA) [16], which specifies the positive, negative symptoms, as well as the social or occupational dysfunction over a period of time.

Patients diagnosed with SCZ were selected using a simple random sampling method through the lottery technique from the psychiatric ward, irrespective of age and sex. It strictly included those patients with schizophrenia, irrespective of medication status, those who gave their consent and were willing to participate in the study, those who were cooperative for the study, and if the patients were below 18 years of age, parental consent was taken. There were also some criteria for exclusion. The study excluded those patients with any other psychiatric disorder other than schizophrenia, uncooperative individuals, and those who did not give their consent. Completely edentulous individuals were not included in the analysis of periodontal status parameters. Disease severity was not used as a stratification variable during sampling, participants were included based on confirmed SCZ diagnosis and eligibility criteria alone, without formal stratification by illness severity.

Procedure and evaluation of oral health status

After taking prior permission, participants were recruited from the hospital based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, and informed consent was obtained from the patients or their legal guardians.

The general demographic data included information on demographic characteristics and lifestyle habits of the participants, such as age, sex, medical history, deleterious habits, medication history, and allergies. Following this, the study proceeded to a behavioural assessment using the Schizophrenia Oral Health Profile (SOHP) [17], a 53-item tool comprising 42 core items spanning nine theoretical dimensions- emotional well-being, social well-being, physical activity, sleep, pain, dysfunction, self-consciousness, social support, autonomy, and time management/perspective, along with an 11-item supplementary module assessing treatment-related side effects and their impact on daily functioning. Respondents indicated their answers on a Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” As the original validation study [17] did not establish a standardised numerical scoring range or cut-off values for score interpretation, responses in the present study were categorised into three response groups: agree, neutral, and disagree based on the number of items falling into each category per respondent, providing a pragmatic and transparent framework for behavioural classification consistent with the ordinal nature of the original tool. The SOHP was administered in its original English format through investigator-led, face-to-face questioning rather than self-administration; the investigator read each item aloud to the participant and provided clarification or informal explanation in the local language for participants who had difficulty understanding the English items. Prior to the main study, the questionnaire and administration approach were pilot tested and found to be within an acceptable range of comprehension, based on which the same tool and administration format were retained for the main study.

During the intraoral examination, the following indices were calculated: the level of oral hygiene was measured using the Oral Hygiene Index-Simplified (OHI-S), dental caries experience was recorded using the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) Index, and periodontal status was evaluated using the Community Periodontal Index (CPI).

The clinical oral examinations were conducted in a comfortable space. The oral health status assessment of the SCZ patients was carried out under the supervision of a psychiatric care physician. The oral cavity was illuminated with the light from the dental chair. The oral cavity was examined visually using appropriate dental armamentarium. The procedure did not involve any other investigations apart from the examination. All the dental examinations were performed by a single examiner. After completing the oral examination of the subjects, instructions for maintaining oral hygiene were given by the operator.

Statistical analysis

The data was entered into Microsoft Excel. The data was further analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software. The demographic data and oral health parameters were assessed by descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented by the use of frequencies and percentages. Given the ordinal and skewed nature of the oral health indices (OHI-S, DMFT, CPI, LOA) and SOHP scores, non-parametric tests were considered the more conservative and appropriate approach for this dataset. A formal Shapiro-Wilk test of normality was not conducted in the original analysis and non-parametric methods were retained as the statistically sound choice given the observed data characteristics. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare oral health parameters and SOHP scores between males and females. The Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to distinguish the oral health parameters and the SOHP scores in different age groups. The mean ranks were used to establish a clear difference between groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to verify the relationship between the SOHP scores and oral health parameters, including OHI-S, DMFT, CPI, and LOA. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and followed national ethical standards. The ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, SRM Medical College and Hospital, Tamil Nadu, India (Reference Number: SRMIEC-ST0525-2568). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants involved in this study or their legal guardians. During data collection, the anonymity of the patients was preserved.

Discussion

The current study assessed the associations between behavioural variables and the status of oral health in patients with schizophrenia, utilising the Schizophrenia Oral Health Profile (SOHP) questionnaire, in combination with conventional measures of oral health. The results clearly indicate a high prevalence of dental caries and periodontal disease in this group and the impact of behavioural variables on oral health.

The predominance of patients with fair oral hygiene habits in the current study is in line with previous studies that identified poor oral hygiene habits in patients with schizophrenia. The lack of motivation, cognitive impairment, and inability to perform self-care activities daily are some of the barriers that have been identified to prevent patients with schizophrenia from practicing good oral hygiene habits [18]. Additionally, the xerostomotic effects of antipsychotic medications may further contribute to plaque accumulation and oral disease progression.

The distribution of DMFT scores indicated that a considerable proportion of participants experienced low to moderate caries levels, with a smaller subset demonstrating high caries experience. Such findings are in line with the results of a previous study, which indicated a greater frequency of dental caries among schizophrenia patients compared to the general population [19]. However, the study by Arnaiz et al. [19] employed a matched case-control design comparing schizophrenic outpatients with a psychiatrically healthy control group in Spain. In contrast, the present study was cross-sectional without a comparator group, limiting the extent to which our findings can be directly benchmarked against the general population. Dental visit irregularity, as well as the impact of the drug-induced modification of saliva secretion, could be considered as factors that contribute to the development of dental caries among such patients [20].

The test results indicate a high level of periodontal pockets and loss of periodontal attachment, which shows the poor periodontal condition of the subjects. This is reflected in the literature, which identifies schizophrenia as a major periodontal risk factor [21]. Notably, Agarwal et al. [21] also employed a hospital-based cross-sectional design in Rohtak, Haryana, India, similar in setting and methodology to the present study, lending contextual relevance to the comparability of our findings within the Indian population. However, their study included a larger sample of 111 subjects and additionally incorporated a modified Dental Coping Belief Scale alongside a structured questionnaire and clinical examination, offering a broader behavioural assessment framework. Inadequate oral hygiene practices and limited access to preventive dental care have been implicated in the progression of periodontal disease in individuals with severe mental illness [22].

The absence of statistically significant gender differences in oral health indices suggests that schizophrenia-related behavioural and functional impairments may affect oral health similarly in both males and females. While sex-specific influences on dental caries have been reported in patients with schizophrenia [23], that study was conducted among 496 Chinese inpatients using a multidimensional model incorporating cognitive impairment, symptom severity, and medication data alongside DMFT, a substantially larger analytical scope than the present study. The present study, by contrast, did not demonstrate significant gender differences, which may reflect either a genuinely more uniform oral health risk profile within our study population or the more limited set of variables examined here relative to Yang et al.‘s multidimensional model. Significant differences across age categories indicate that oral health deterioration may be influenced by cumulative exposure to risk factors over time, including prolonged disease duration and sustained behavioural challenges [10].

A key finding of the present study is the significant association between SOHP scores and clinical indices of dental and periodontal disease. The observed correlations with DMFT, CPI, and LOA support a close relationship between patients’ self-reported behaviours and their objectively measured disease burden. The inverse correlation between SOHP and CPI may reflect that patients with more favourable behavioural profiles, as indicated by higher SOHP scores, engage more consistently in oral hygiene practices and are more likely to seek preventive dental care, thereby reducing periodontal disease severity. Conversely, patients with lower behavioural engagement may experience delayed care-seeking and poorer plaque control, contributing to higher CPI scores. These results reinforce the broader link between mental and dental wellbeing, underscoring the value of incorporating behavioural screening into psychiatric evaluation protocols [24]. However, as this study did not include a matched non-schizophrenia control group, it cannot be determined whether these behaviour-oral health associations are unique to schizophrenia or reflect broader patterns seen across populations with similar behavioural profiles.

These findings underscore the need for integrated dental and mental health care services. Combining clinical screening, targeted behavioural intervention, and patient education within a unified management approach could meaningfully improve overall wellbeing in this population [25].

Limitations

Despite the valuable insights that this study provides, there are several limitations to be declared. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing a causal relationship between behavioural factors and oral health status. Second, the study was conducted only on schizophrenia patients without a healthy control group, limiting direct comparison with the general population. Third, the sample size was relatively small and recruited from a single tertiary care centre, which may limit generalizability. Fourth, potential confounding variables such as antipsychotic medication type and dosage, duration of illness, smoking status, socioeconomic factors, xerostomia, and frequency of dental visits were not systematically recorded or adjusted for in the analysis. Fifth, all clinical examinations were performed by a single trained examiner; while this eliminated inter-examiner variability by design, intra-examiner reliability was not formally tested and could not be established retrospectively. Sixth, a formally translated and culturally validated local-language version was not available, and formal internal consistency testing (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not performed for this population. Finally, the SOHP questionnaire relies on self-reported data, which may be subject to response bias, particularly given the cognitive symptoms associated with schizophrenia.

Future recommendations

Future studies should also take longitudinal study designs into consideration to further clarify the causal relationship between behavioural factors and the deterioration of oral health in patients with schizophrenia. Larger multicentre studies would also increase the external validity of the particular study. In addition, a control group is also needed to look at schizophrenia patients in relation to the normal population. Given the sample size and predictor-to-outcome ratio in the present study, multivariable regression analysis was not performed, as it would have fallen below accepted events-per-variable thresholds and risked unreliable estimates; future studies with larger samples and structured confounder data should incorporate multivariable regression models to identify independent predictors of oral health after adjusting for age, sex, behavioural factors, and other relevant confounders. Stratification by disease severity and structured classification of antipsychotic medication type, dosage, and duration of illness, etc. would further improve the ability to isolate the independent contribution of behavioural and clinical factors to oral health outcomes.

Future studies should also assess the impact of different types of antipsychotic medication, treatment period, disease state, substance use (smoking included), and xerostomia on overall oral health. Interventional studies on the implementation of extensive oral health educational programs, as well as the integration and participation of caregivers and dental/psychiatric team members, are strongly recommended. The routine use of SOHP in psychiatric settings would also help in the early identification of high-risk patients and the provision of preventive dental care.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study suggest several practical implications for integrated psychiatric and dental care. First, routine incorporation of a brief behavioural oral health screening tool, such as the SOHP, into psychiatric intake or periodic review protocols could help clinicians identify patients at higher risk of poor oral health before physical symptoms become severe. Second, psychiatric care teams could benefit from establishing structured referral pathways to dental services, particularly for patients showing lower behavioural engagement scores. Third, caregiver and nursing staff training in basic oral hygiene support may help offset the motivational and cognitive barriers commonly observed in this population. Finally, dental professionals treating psychiatric patients should be sensitized to the behavioural and communication challenges specific to schizophrenia, enabling more patient-centred and effective care delivery.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrates a high prevalence of dental caries and periodontal disease in patients with schizophrenia and highlights the significant role of behavioural factors in oral health outcomes. These findings support incorporating routine oral health screening and validated behavioural tools such as the SOHP into standard psychiatric care, and suggest piloting integrated dental–psychiatric referral pathways as a practical next step toward improving oral health outcomes in this population.
