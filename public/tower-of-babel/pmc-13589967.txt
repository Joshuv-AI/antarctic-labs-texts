Depression and Longitudinal Frailty Trajectories in Middle-Aged and Older Adults with Chronic Pain: A 8-Year National Cohort Study in China
Authors: Haotian Zhang, Aiqi Wang, Shun Wang, Guoyan Feng, Lu Wang, Liqin Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal population aging is accelerating, and pain and depression frequently co-occur in middle-aged and older adults. Frailty, an age-related clinical syndrome, markedly elevates the risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality. This study investigated whether comorbid depression affects long-term frailty trajectories in patients with chronic pain, to help identify high-risk populations and guide comprehensive interventions.MethodsBased on 8 years of follow-up (2011–2018) from the nationally representative multi-stage probability sample of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), 4,778 participants aged 45 years and older with chronic pain were enrolled. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), with a total score ≥10 indicating depressive symptoms, and frailty was quantified by a 32-item Frailty Index (FI). Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) identified FI developmental trajectories, and multinomial logistic regression examined the association between depression and trajectory group membership.ResultsThree frailty trajectories were identified: Low Frailty Group, Moderate Frailty Group, and High Frailty Group. Overall, 61.3% of participants had comorbid depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms were associated with significantly higher odds of the moderate (OR = 2.82, 95% CI 2.42–3.29) and high trajectories (OR = 6.57, 95% CI 5.18–8.34). Each 1-point CES-D-10 increment increased the odds by 11% and 19%, respectively. These associations remained stable across subgroups.ConclusionIn middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, depression was associated with worsening frailty trajectories. Routine depression screening integrated with mental health care for chronic pain and frailty may help slow frailty progression and reduce disability burden.Keywords: depression, frailty, CHARLS, pain, GBTM

Introduction

With the accelerating global population aging, maintaining the functional capacity required by older adults to safeguard their quality of life and achieve healthy aging has emerged as a critical public health challenge. Frailty is an aging-related clinical syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserve and reduced resistance to stressors, resulting in increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes. Clinically, frailty can be conceptualized either as a physical phenotype (eg, unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow walking speed, and reduced grip strength) or as the accumulation of multiple deficits across physical, psychological, and functional domains. Frailty significantly increases the risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality among older adults. This not only severely impairs their quality of life but also imposes substantial healthcare burdens on families and societies.1–4 Furthermore, due to the convergence of various risks, the benefits derived from necessary medical interventions are considerably diminished.5 Given the multifaceted negative impacts of frailty, early identification of frailty status and prevention of its progression are of paramount importance.

Pain and depression represent two of the most prevalent health issues among middle-aged and older adults, frequently occurring concomitantly. Multiple large-scale studies and Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data demonstrate that both conditions are extremely common in this population, interact reciprocally, and exacerbate one another. This comorbidity not only markedly elevates the risk of disability in older adults but also substantially increases individual and societal healthcare expenditures.

Globally, the problem of geriatric depression is becoming increasingly severe. In 2021, approximately 93.1 million individuals aged 55 years and older worldwide suffered from depressive disorders, representing a 136% increase compared to 1990. This resulted in approximately 14.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).6 According to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease report, the annual incidence rate, total prevalence proportion, and disability-related health loss rate of major depressive disorder among adults aged 60 years and older have maintained a steady annual increase of 0.24%–0.25% since 1990.7 Concurrently, the age-standardized incidence and health loss rates of geriatric depressive disorders globally have also exhibited an overall sustained upward trend. Additionally, a meta-analysis revealed that approximately 35% of community-dwelling older adults present with depression-related symptoms.8

Meanwhile, chronic pain—commonly defined as persistent or recurrent pain lasting three months or longer—has become one of the leading causes of disability worldwide. Among individuals aged 50 years and older in Europe, the prevalence of pain ranges from 30% to 60%, with an increasing temporal trend.9 In the United States, the prevalence of chronic pain among adults ranges from 11% to 40%, with higher rates observed among older adults.10 In China, the prevalence of pain among individuals aged 45 years and older is approximately 30%, while the prevalence of chronic pain among those aged 60 years and older reaches as high as 55%.11

More importantly, a robust bidirectional association exists between pain and depression. Among adults with chronic pain, the prevalence of clinically significant depressive symptoms is approximately 39%, with approximately 37% of participants meeting diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder.12 Multiple international studies have confirmed this association: across six low- and middle-income countries, the prevalence of major depressive disorder was 14.5% among individuals aged 50 years and older with back pain, significantly higher than the 4.5% among those without back pain. Individuals with back pain exhibited an approximately 2.4-fold increased risk of depression, with greater pain severity and longer duration associated with higher depression risk. Among adults aged 60 years and older in India, nearly 40% reported pain, and 8.7% suffered from major depression. After multivariable adjustment, individuals with frequent pain demonstrated an approximately 1.7-fold increased depression risk.13 Among disabled older adults in Japan, the association between chronic pain and depression was even more pronounced, with severe pain associated with an approximately 3.7-fold increased depression risk.14 Two major international aging cohorts—the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS)—further confirmed that chronic pain predicts subsequent exacerbation of depressive symptoms, while depressive symptoms predict future intensification of pain. This bidirectional association, although of moderate-to-small effect size, persisted over 12 years of follow-up.15 A study encompassing approximately 270,000 adults across 47 low- and middle-income countries also demonstrated that any degree of depression (including subclinical states) was significantly associated with severe pain, with individuals experiencing major depression exhibiting an approximately 3.9-fold increased risk of severe pain compared to those without depression.16 In summary, with the accelerating global population aging, the prevalence of pain-depression comorbidity among middle-aged and older adults is continuously rising, representing an unignorable public health issue.

In recent years, a growing body of research has focused on the relationships among pain, depression, and frailty. Existing evidence indicates that both pain and depression are independent risk factors for the development and progression of frailty, and the two factors may exert interactive and mediating effects during frailty pathogenesis.17 However, previous studies have predominantly employed cross-sectional designs, focusing primarily on associations at a single time point. This approach limits the ability to reveal dynamic change patterns and neglects the heterogeneity of frailty development trajectories across different individuals. Frailty is not a static state but a dynamic process, with different individuals potentially exhibiting distinct developmental trajectories. Identifying these distinct trajectory patterns and exploring their influencing factors is crucial for developing targeted frailty prevention and intervention strategies. Nevertheless, whether depression comorbid with chronic pain affects frailty progression remains to be elucidated.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative cohort study of middle-aged and older adults in China. Initiated in 2011, it has completed multiple follow-up waves, providing valuable data resources for investigating dynamic changes in health issues among China’s middle-aged and older population. Based on longitudinal data from the CHARLS database spanning 2011–2018, this study employed trajectory analysis to explore the impact of comorbid depression in participants with pain on long-term frailty development trajectories. Compared with single-time-point or mean-change approaches, trajectory analysis has the advantage of capturing the heterogeneity of frailty development: it identifies heterogeneous subgroups with distinct frailty developmental patterns, thereby enabling more targeted prevention and intervention strategies. This research aims to provide a scientific basis for identifying high-risk populations for frailty and developing targeted comprehensive intervention measures.

Methods

Study Population

Data used in this study were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort study that used a multi-stage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size cluster sampling strategy covering 450 communities/villages across 28 provinces (municipalities/autonomous regions) of China, with all non-institutionalized adults aged 45 years and older eligible for participation. Detailed information regarding this cohort can be found in relevant literature.18 The Peking University Institutional Review Board approved the CHARLS study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent (approval number: IRB00001052-11015). This study used CHARLS 2011 survey data as baseline and incorporated data from three subsequent follow-up waves (2013, 2015, and 2018). The inclusion criteria were: (i) age ≥45 years at the 2011 baseline; (ii) self-reported chronic pain at baseline, defined as being often troubled by body pain in accordance with the CHARLS pain item, consistent with previous CHARLS-based studies;19 (iii) complete data on the Frailty Index and depression at baseline; and (iv) at least one valid Frailty Index measurement during follow-up (2013, 2015, or 2018). Participants without pain, those younger than 45 years, and those with missing information on the Frailty Index or depression at baseline or with no valid follow-up Frailty Index measurement were excluded. Ultimately, 4,778 participants were included in the final analysis, with detailed information presented in Figure 1.

Flow chart of inclusion of this study population.

Depressive Symptom Assessment

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) in CHARLS. Each item was scored on a 3-point scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Consistent with previous research, depressive symptoms were defined as a score ≥ 10.20

Frailty Index

In this study, we computed the Frailty Index (FI) following established standardized protocols, capturing a comprehensive range of health dimensions. The 32-item FI was constructed using indicators spanning comorbidities, physical function, disability, depression, and cognitive status. With the exception of item 32—a continuous measure of cognition where higher scores denote greater impairment—all components were coded dichotomously (0 = absence, 1 = presence). Detailed specifications of these items are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

For each participant, the 32-FI score was calculated as the proportion of accumulated deficits relative to the total number of items assessed (range: 0–1), with higher values reflecting more severe frailty. Consistent with prior literature, a cutoff of ≥0.25 was used to define the frail state.21 To optimize data utilization while minimizing bias, we adopted a standard missing-data imputation rule, permitting up to 20% (ie, 6 out of 32) of variables to be missing. Participants exceeding this threshold were excluded from the analysis (FI treated as missing), whereas those within the threshold were assigned an FI based on the mean of their available non-missing items.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level, marital status, and residential location), lifestyle factors (smoking status, drinking status, and body mass index [BMI]), and physical health indicators. Educational level was categorized as “no formal education,” “primary school,” “middle school,” and “high school or above”; marital status as “married” and “single”; and residential location as “rural” and “urban.” Smoking and drinking status were defined based on current status at the time of interview. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and categorized according to the World Health Organization criteria (<18.5, 18.5–23.9, 24.0–27.9, and ≥28.0 kg/m2). Chronic diseases included hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, heart disease, stroke, chronic lung disease, and arthritis, defined on the basis of physician diagnoses, blood pressure, laboratory measures, or medication use as appropriate. Based on the cumulative number of these eight chronic diseases, comorbidity burden was categorized into 0, 1, and ≥2 conditions.

Statistical Methods

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was employed to determine the optimal number and shape of latent frailty trajectories by fitting models with up to six classes using linear, quadratic, or cubic growth parameters. The optimal model was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), average posterior probability (≥0.70), the requirement that each trajectory subgroup comprised >5% of the sample, and clinical interpretability. After identifying distinct frailty index trajectories, chi-square tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to examine differences in individual characteristics across trajectory groups. Multinomial logistic regression models were then used to evaluate associations between frailty index trajectories and depression, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). To visualize the association between depressive symptoms (measured by CES-D scores) and predicted probabilities of belonging to different FI trajectory groups, we utilized estimated parameters from the multinomial logistic regression model. Finally, subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted, stratified by age (<65 years, ≥65 years), sex, marital status, smoking status, and drinking status, to evaluate associations between frailty index trajectories and depression. All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2, using the “gbmt” package for GBTM; two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

This study was conducted based on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database. A total of 4,778 participants with chronic pain aged 45 years and older were included, among whom 1,851 (38.7%) were without depressive symptoms and 2,927 (61.3%) had comorbid depressive symptoms. Compared with participants without depressive symptoms, those with comorbid depressive symptoms were slightly older (mean age 59.9 vs 58.4 years), more likely to be female (65.6%), to have no formal education (61.0%), to be single (16.5%), and to live in rural areas (73.1%); they also had a higher prevalence of two or more comorbid chronic diseases (54.6%) and a higher baseline Frailty Index. In contrast, participants without depression had higher rates of smoking and drinking and higher proportions of overweight and obesity (Supplementary Table 2).

Frailty Index Trajectories

To determine the optimal grouping pattern for assessing changes in frailty status among participants with chronic pain, we evaluated the fit quality of various trajectory models, as shown in Table 1. The study employed a group-based trajectory model to identify the developmental trajectories of frailty indices in middle-aged and elderly populations. By comparing the fit quality of models 1–4 and comprehensively considering the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), mean posterior probability, group sample proportions, and clinical interpretability, three cubic trajectory models were ultimately selected. The BIC value for this model was −31,669.32; the mean posterior probabilities for all groups exceeded 0.9, with sample proportions of 29.33%,45.87%, and 24.81%, respectively. The trajectory exhibited a clear hierarchical pattern of “low–medium–high,” demonstrating both robust statistical performance and clinical relevance.

As shown in Figure 2, these three subgroups exhibited clear stratified characteristics in their frailty evolution trajectories over eight years: The first group (Low Frailty Group) consistently maintained good physical status throughout the observation period. This group had the lowest baseline frailty level among the entire sample, and its frailty index remained relatively stable during subsequent follow-up, showing only a slight upward trend. The second group (Moderate Frailty Group) exhibited characteristics of progressive decline. This population started at an intermediate level of frailty, and its change curve subsequently showed a gentle and continuous upward trend. The third group (High Frailty Group) demonstrated a marked rapid deterioration trend. This population not only had the highest baseline frailty level but also exhibited the steepest upward slope during the observation period, particularly with significantly accelerated growth in the later stages. This trajectory indicates that this subgroup faces more severe and rapidly progressing health risks.

To further explore differences in depressive symptom levels among the three trajectory groups, we analyzed baseline depressive symptom (CES-D score) levels across the three trajectory populations (Figure 3). Results showed: The median CES-D score for the Low Frailty Group was 8 points, with the lowest overall depressive symptom level, concentrated distribution, and small dispersion. The median CES-D score for the Moderate Frailty Group was 12 points, with intermediate depressive symptom levels. The median CES-D score for the High Frailty Group was 16 points, with significantly higher depressive symptom levels than the other two groups and an overall upward distribution shift. Association analysis between trajectory membership probability and CES-D scores revealed (Figure 4) that as CES-D scores increased, the probability of individuals belonging to the low-level trajectory group significantly decreased, while the probability of belonging to the high-level trajectory group significantly increased. The membership probability for the moderate-level trajectory group exhibited an inverted U-shaped trend, first increasing then decreasing. This indicates that higher depressive symptom levels are associated with greater likelihood of individuals entering frailty trajectories with higher severity and faster progression.

Regarding lifestyle factors, as frailty trajectory level increased, smoking and drinking rates in the population showed decreasing trends. Regarding weight distribution, the High Frailty Group had the highest proportion of underweight individuals (10.2%) and the lowest proportion of normal weight individuals (56.2%). Regarding health status, comorbidity was closely associated with frailty trajectories. Only 24.3% of individuals in the Low Frailty Group had 2 or more chronic diseases, while this proportion rose to 55.6% in the Moderate Frailty Group and reached 74.1% in the High Frailty Group. Particularly noteworthy, the prevalence of depressive symptoms showed a significant stepwise upward trend with increasing frailty trajectory: 39.2% in the Low Frailty Group, 65.0% in the Moderate Frailty Group, and as high as 81.3% in the High Frailty Group.

Association Between Depression and Frailty Trajectories in Chronic Pain Participants

To clarify the relationship between depression and frailty trajectories in middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, three sequential multinomial logistic regression models were constructed, with the low-stable frailty trajectory group as the reference outcome category and non-depressed participants as the reference category for depression. Model 1 was the crude model without adjustment; Model 2 further adjusted for demographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level, marital status, and residential location); Model 3 additionally adjusted for smoking, drinking, chronic disease status, and BMI on the basis of Model 2. As shown in Table 3, all models confirmed a significant positive association between depression and adverse frailty trajectories, with a clear dose–response relationship. In Model 1, compared with non-depressed participants, participants with depression had significantly higher odds of the moderate (vs low) trajectory (OR = 2.88, 95% CI: 2.51–3.30, P<0.001) and the high trajectory (OR = 6.72, 95% CI: 5.59–8.06, P<0.001). These associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for demographic characteristics (Model 2: OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 2.50–3.33; and OR = 7.05, 95% CI: 5.73–8.68, respectively) and after further adjustment for lifestyle and health-related factors (Model 3: OR = 2.82, 95% CI: 2.42–3.29; and OR = 6.57, 95% CI: 5.18–8.34, respectively).

Subgroup Analysis

To further clarify the association between depression and frailty trajectories across different populations, this study conducted stratified subgroup analyses by sex, age, marital status, residential location, smoking status, drinking status, and comorbidity status. Subgroup analysis results are presented in Figure 5. Using the low frailty trajectory group as reference, depression remained an important risk factor for moderate and severe frailty trajectories among middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, and this association remained consistent across all subgroups. Interaction test results revealed no significant interactions between the aforementioned stratification factors and depression (all interaction P-values > 0.05), indicating that none of the evaluated demographic and health-related factors significantly altered the strength of the association between depression and frailty trajectories. This study demonstrates that the association between depression and frailty trajectories in middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain exhibits broad population robustness.

Sensitivity Analysis

To verify the robustness of the association between depression and frailty trajectories in older adults with chronic pain, this study used continuous CES-D scores as an alternative indicator of depression and repeated the aforementioned multinomial logistic regression analysis. Still using the low frailty trajectory group as reference, three sequential models consistent with the main analysis adjustment strategy were constructed: Model 1 was the crude model without adjustment for confounding factors; Model 2 further adjusted for demographic characteristics including age, sex, educational level, marital status, and residential location; Model 3 was the fully adjusted model, which further corrected for smoking, drinking, chronic disease status, and BMI on the basis of Model 2.

Sensitivity analysis results are presented in Table 4. All models showed that CES-D scores were significantly and positively associated with adverse frailty trajectories in older adults with chronic pain, and the association strength remained highly stable across all models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each 1-point increase in CES-D score was associated with an 11% increased risk of moderate frailty trajectories (OR=1.11, 95% CI: 1.09–1.12, P<0.001) and a 19% increased risk of severe frailty trajectories (OR=1.19, 95% CI: 1.17–1.21, P<0.001). These results are completely consistent with the conclusions obtained using binary depression variables in the main analysis, further confirming the good robustness of the association between depression and adverse frailty trajectories in middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain.

Discussion

With the intensifying global population aging trend, the psychological and physical health problems faced by middle-aged and older adults have become major challenges in the field of public health. As one of the countries with the fastest aging rate, China is projected to have more than 400 million people aged 60 years and older by 2040, accounting for 28% of the national population.22,23 Under these circumstances, issues such as depressive symptoms, chronic pain, and their coexistence are increasingly affecting the quality of life of middle-aged and older adults. By analyzing large cohort data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) spanning 2011–2018, this study identified three distinct types of frailty trajectories. Study results showed that among middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, depression was a significantly associated factor for frailty trajectory deterioration. In this population of middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain, individuals with depression had a significantly increased likelihood of entering higher-risk frailty trajectories. Furthermore, we evaluated the relationship between CES-D scores and frailty trajectories and found that as CES-D scores increased, the risk of middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain entering higher-risk frailty trajectories also gradually increased.

In this study, three distinct longitudinal frailty trajectories—low, moderate, and high—were identified among middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain over the 8-year follow-up, and baseline depression was associated with a substantially higher likelihood of belonging to the moderate and high trajectories in a dose-dependent manner. These trajectory-based findings extend previous cross-sectional evidence by showing that depression does not simply correlate with the current level of frailty, but predicts its progression over time. Importantly, our analyses were conducted specifically in the chronic pain population, a group in which the interplay between pain and depression is particularly pronounced. Pain is a long-term problem commonly faced by middle-aged and older adults that significantly affects quality of life and activities of daily living; persistent pain constitutes a sustained physiological stress that readily induces emotional disorders. Multiple multinational studies have shown that patients with chronic pain have substantially higher risks of concurrent depressive or anxiety symptoms.24–26 Consistent with the broader literature, meta-analyses have shown that approximately 40% of depressed patients have comorbid frailty and that depression significantly increases frailty risk in older adults,27–29 and regional studies have reached similar conclusions.30–32 Our results extend this evidence to the chronic pain population and, more importantly, demonstrate that depression predicts distinct longitudinal frailty trajectories rather than only a single-time-point frailty status.

When pain coexists with depression, the two conditions may act synergistically to accelerate physical decline and frailty progression through shared inflammatory pathways, reduced physical activity, and functional limitation, which may explain why depression predicted a faster worsening of frailty trajectories in our chronic pain population. Depressive symptoms directly trigger a series of physiological and behavioral changes that subsequently increase the risk of physical frailty. At the cognitive and behavioral level, depression leads to cognitive function declines in attention and memory, reduced mental flexibility, and simultaneously significantly reduces participants’ social activities and physical activity levels.33–35 Physiologically, depression is frequently accompanied by reduced appetite and weight loss, slower walking speed, limited activities of daily living, and persistent fatigue and low energy.36 These changes progressively weaken overall bodily function, making individuals more susceptible to frailty. From the perspective of lifestyle and health behaviors, depressed participants exhibit multiple habits detrimental to physical health. Compared with peers without depressive symptoms, they have significantly lower levels of physical activity, are more prone to excessive alcohol consumption and unbalanced diets, and simultaneously have poorer self-care awareness and significantly reduced adherence to health management regimens and treatment plans prescribed by physicians.37,38 These factors not only directly lead to deteriorating physical conditions but also increase the risk of adverse events such as falls and malnutrition, further promoting frailty development. Regarding pharmacotherapy, the use of certain antidepressant medications is also associated with physical frailty, potentially increasing the risks of osteoporosis, fractures, and falls.39–41 Additionally, depression is frequently accompanied by sleep disturbances, emotional dysregulation, and cognitive impairment, which interact to form a vicious cycle, severely impairing participants’ overall health status.42–44 Notably, depression and frailty may share common pathogenic mechanisms, with both closely related to elevated systemic inflammatory levels, including abnormalities in inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, as well as white blood cells and neutrophils.45 Simultaneously, chronic pain itself triggers systemic inflammatory responses, and when pain participants develop comorbid depression, this inflammatory state is further exacerbated, thereby significantly increasing the risk of physical frailty.

This study investigated the impact of depressive symptoms on frailty trajectories among middle-aged and older Chinese individuals with chronic pain. It provides new evidence for research in this population and offers valuable references for the development of clinical intervention strategies. However, this study has certain limitations: (1) Regarding data, the CHARLS questionnaire may not have covered all relevant factors, affecting the generalizability of results. (2) Regarding study design, although confounding factors were controlled for, this observational study cannot definitively establish a causal relationship between depressive symptoms and frailty; future intervention studies are therefore needed to determine whether treating depression can slow frailty progression and reduce the risk of entering high-risk frailty trajectories. (3) Due to the lack of objective indicators such as biomarkers, this study was unable to explore potential biological mechanisms in greater depth, which requires further validation in future studies by incorporating more biomarkers. (4) Participant dropout and death during the 8-year follow-up may have introduced attrition bias, although GBTM uses all available follow-up measurements; future studies should also account for competing risks and attrition.(5) The 32-item frailty index includes a depression-related deficit item, which presents conceptual overlap with the baseline CES-D-10 measure of depressive symptoms and may theoretically slightly overestimate the observed association. Nevertheless, this item is an inherent component of the standard deficit-accumulation frailty framework, accounting for only approximately 3% of total items, and the overlap is restricted to the baseline wave only, limiting the magnitude of potential bias.

Conclusion

Using large-scale cohort data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this study confirms that comorbid depression is significantly associated with worsening frailty trajectories among middle-aged and older adults with chronic pain. These findings underscore the importance of early screening for depressive symptoms (eg, using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, CES-D-10) and of integrating mental health management into chronic pain care—such as collaborative care combining pain management with psychological treatment—which may help to delay frailty progression and improve long-term health outcomes in this high-risk population.
