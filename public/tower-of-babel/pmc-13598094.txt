Low-gas-flow strategy boosts hierarchical porosity of ZIF-8 single-atom catalysts for efficient adsorption-driven degradation
Authors: Guiwei Liang, Shuyi Yang, Yaping Xie, Tingran Liu, Rongbing Fu, Bingcai Pan, Yuping Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHierarchical porosity amplifies single-atom catalyst (SAC) performance by enhancing mass transport and site accessibility, yet conventional fabrication typically relies on sacrificial-templating or post-etching protocols. Here, we construct well-connected pore networks via minimalistic gas-flow control. Lowering the N2 flow rate during pyrolysis of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks preserves furnace humidity, triggering sequential framework Zn species transformation and volatilization to refine hierarchical porosity. This additive-free strategy yields Co-SACs with a 2.7-fold mesopore and 2.6-fold micropore expansion. The optimized catalyst shows exceptional bisphenol A adsorption and complete degradation within 5 min, achieving a 20-fold turnover frequency increase. Experimental and theoretical studies reveal Co-sites become electron-enriched by extracting electrons from adsorbed pollutants. This transfer promotes peroxymonosulfate activation, elucidating the core electronic driver of adsorption-driven catalysis. Furthermore, this versatile low-flow protocol successfully produces enhanced Fe- and Cu-based analogues. Our findings demonstrate that sophisticated pore engineering can be realized via simple gas-flow regulation, fundamentally deepening adsorption-catalysis synergy.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Metal-organic frameworks, Heterogeneous catalysis

Introduction

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) hold immense promise for Fenton-like water purification reactions, offering well-defined active sites and maximal atom-utilization efficiency1–5. However, their practical deployment is constrained by sluggish mass transport, limited accessibility to isolated metal centers, and vulnerability to deactivation in complex aqueous systems2,6,7. Engineering hierarchical pore architectures presents a potent solution to these challenges8–13. While micropores provide anchoring sites for metal atoms and deliver high surface areas, the integration of meso- and macropores substantially enhances molecular diffusion and facilitates efficient reactant–active site interactions8–11,14. Furthermore, the spatial confinement within hierarchical pores enables precise manipulation of reaction activity and pathways, thereby optimizing the catalytic performance of SACs, making such architectures particularly effective for Fenton-like oxidation2,12,15,16.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), especially zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), serve as attractive precursors for SACs synthesis due to their well-defined porosity, high surface areas, and uniform coordination environments2,17–19. Yet, direct pyrolysis of MOFs typically yields microporous carbons, which impede aqueous-phase molecular diffusion and restrict access to active sites18. To overcome this limitation, various strategies have been developed to introduce hierarchical porosity into MOF-derived SACs9,18,20–22, such as sacrificial templates21 and etching-based approaches20. Despite their efficacy, these methods necessitate multistep procedures, extensive chemical additives, and intricate process control. Their complexity elevates manufacturing challenges and impedes scalable production, underscoring the urgent need for more straightforward and efficient techniques for regulating porosity in SACs. Furthermore, extensive studies have demonstrated that the confined environment within porous structures can induce a synergy between adsorption and catalysis to enhance the catalytic performance of single atom catalysts15,23,24. Although the prevailing understanding typically attributes this phenomenon to pollutant enrichment and shortened diffusion distances between reactive species and pollutants23,24, the underlying mechanisms at the electronic level structure remain elusive.

Here, we introduce a streamlined strategy for regulating hierarchical porosity in ZIF-8-derived SACs through precise modulation of pyrolysis gas flow rate. Reducing the N2 flow preserves high furnace humidity, triggering a self-propagating cascade: framework Zn2+ transforms into ZnO nanoparticles, which undergo carbothermal reduction to form metallic Zn. Subsequent volatilization of Zn species generates an interconnected network of micropores and mesopores without disrupting the local coordination and electronic structure of catalytic sites. The resulting Co-N3C1 catalyst exhibits an exceptional bisphenol A (BPA) adsorption capacity, while its confined nanospaces intensify adsorption–catalysis synergy, enabling rapid BPA degradation with a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 0.608 min–1. Within these confined domains, the Co single-atom sites directly extract electrons from BPA molecules adsorbed on adjacent nitrogen sites via the carbon matrix, elevating local electron density and enhancing the adsorption affinity for peroxymonosulfate (PMS) and the number of available electrons. The strategy’s versatility is demonstrated by its successful extension to other metal-based SACs. Crucially, this study offers a simple and scalable approach to manufacturing high-performance environmental catalysts without the need for multistep templating or etching agents. It also deepens the fundamental understanding of the synergy between adsorption and catalysis at the electronic level.

Results

Hierarchically structural evolution of Co-ZIF-8 porosity driven by reduced N2 flow

A series of single-atom cobalt catalysts featuring hierarchical porous structures, designated as Co-N3C1-X (where X indicates the flow rate), were synthesized through controlled N2 flow modulation during Co-ZIF-8 pyrolysis. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3, all Co-N3C1 materials exhibited high BET surface areas (SBET) and a micro–mesoporous architecture dominated by micropores. Systematic reduction of the N2 flow rate from 180 to 10 mL min–1 induced marked structural improvements: a significant rise in SBET from 940.4 to 1243.5 m2 g–1, an expansion of mesopore area from 42.5 to 112.7 m2 g–1, a growth in 0.9–2 nm micropore volume from 0.0209 to 0.0534 cm3 g–1, and the average pore diameter increased from 1.74 to 2.03 nm. These results suggest that simply reducing the N2 flow rate, without altering other reaction parameters, effectively enhances the multiscale pore organization. However, the underlying mechanism required deeper exploration.

The Co-ZlF-8 precursor contains 27.58 wt% zinc, which volatilizes readily at high temperatures given its relatively low boiling point (907 °C)21,24,25, suggesting a key role in pore development. As the N2 flow decreased from 180 to 10 mL min–1, the residual Zn content on Co-N3C1 decreased from 5.83 to 1.65 wt% (Fig. 1i), and the volatilization rate increased from 78.9% to 94.0% (Supplementary Fig. 4). Correlation analysis further revealed a strong negative relationship between the Zn content and both mesopore area (R2 = 95.8%) and 0.9–2 nm micropore volume (R2 = 95.0%) (Fig. 1b), confirming that zinc loss fundamentally drives pore formation.

The continuous supply of N2 influences the atmosphere composition inside the tube furnace. Oxygen was initially excluded as a contributing factor, since its concentration dropped rapidly to zero under all flow conditions (Supplementary Table 2). Instead, the concentration of water vapor (H2O(g)) emerged as crucial. The furnace initially held a relative humidity of ~70%. Following nitrogen gas introduction, humidity fell markedly, with greater reductions observed at higher flow rates. At 10 mL min–1, relative humidity settled at 18.0%, while increasing the rate to 180 mL min–1 caused a sharp decline to 9.3% (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 3). Previous studies have demonstrated that H2O(g) can react with Zn2+ in ZIF-8 frameworks to form ZnO nanoparticles (nZnO), which are subsequently reduced to metallic Zn at temperatures near 700 °C and ultimately volatilized26. Consequently, the water vapor concentration, modulated by N2 flow rate, directly influences the extent of zinc volatilization from Co-N3C1 materials during pyrolysis.

To verify this hypothesis, we prepared a series of samples labeled Co-N3C1-X-T, where T is the pyrolysis temperature, to track the evolution of zinc species under different pyrolysis temperatures (600, 700, 800, and 908 °C) and nitrogen flow rates (10, 40, 70, and 180 mL min–1). X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 1d) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, Fig. 1e) revealed abundant nZnO, with lattice spacings of 0.164, 0.247, 0.260, and 0.281 nm in Co-N3C1-10-60019,26–28. At higher flow rates (40–180 mL min–1), nZnO quantity diminished (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 5) and crystallite size decreased, eventually becoming undetectable by XRD (Fig. 1d). High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) analyses of samples pyrolyzed at 600 °C further reflected that diminishing N2 flow rates correlated with progressive enrichment of nZnO phases. As shown in Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 6, the Zn–O species fraction in O 1s increased from 23.4% to 35.7%, the Zn2+ contribution in the Zn LMM Auger spectrum rose from 61.3% to 70.3%, and the percentage of metal–N bonding decreased26,27,29,30. Collectively, these results confirm that a lower N2 flow rate promotes the formation of more nZnO.

When the temperature reached 700 °C, nZnO diffraction features disappeared and were replaced by lattice fringes corresponding to the (100) and (002) planes of metallic Zn26 (Fig. 1eIV and Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating carbothermal reduction of nZnO to Zn nanoparticles. Zn K-edge Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) analysis further elucidated the temperature-dependent evolution of zinc species. The spectrum of Co-N3C1-10-600 exhibited a distinct Zn–O–Zn coordination peak characteristic of ZnO nanoparticles, which vanished at 700 °C and was replaced by a prominent Zn–Zn peak, confirming reduction to metallic Zn26 (Fig. 1f). As the temperature further increased to 800 and 908 °C (exceeding zinc’s boiling point), the Zn–Zn peak gradually weakened, suggesting progressive volatilization of Zn nanoparticles (Fig. 1f). Figure 1h and Supplementary Fig. 8 showed a strong negative correlation between the residual zinc content in Co-N3C1 at 950 °C and three critical parameters linked to the increased nZnO content in materials pyrolyzed at 600 °C: the O–Zn ratio in O 1s (R2 = 99.3%), the Zn2+ ratio in the Zn LMM Auger spectrum (R2 = 91.6%), and the metal–N ratio in N 1s (R2 = 97.8%). This correlation directly links initial ZnO content to subsequent zinc loss during pyrolysis. Quantitative analysis confirmed zinc content remained stable below 700 °C, decreased notably at 800 °C, and further dropped at higher temperatures, with lower N2 flow rates consistently promoting more complete volatilization and lower residual zinc content (Fig. 1i).

These findings support a mechanistic chain for pore-structure evolution of Co-ZIF-8: reduced N2 flow preserves high water vapor levels, promoting ZnO formation; these nanoparticles are then reduced to metallic Zn, whose volatilization during pyrolysis generates a well-developed hierarchical pore network (Fig. 1j). This work demonstrates that rational tuning of single process parameter, carrier gas flow, can replace complex chemical strategies for designing hierarchically porous SACs, offering a scalable and efficient route to advanced functional materials.

Flow-tunable microenvironment of Co-SACs in hierarchical carbon frameworks

All Co-N3C1 catalysts retained the characteristic dodecahedral morphology of the ZIF-8 precursor, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Supplementary Fig. 9). Their XRD patterns showed only two broad peaks (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating a graphitic carbon structure without detectable Co nanoparticles31,32. Atomic dispersion of cobalt was unambiguously demonstrated by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM)31,33, where numerous uniformly distributed bright spots were observed in Co-N3C1-10, supported by corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Fig. 2a).

Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra revealed that the oxidation state of Co in Co-N3C1-10 was close to +2, slightly lower than that in Co-N4 (Supplementary Fig. 11), which can be attributed to the asymmetric nitrogen coordination around the Co centers31,34. Based on the linear relationship between the Co absorption edge (E0) and the valence states established using reference standards, the average Co oxidation state in Co-N3C1-10 was calculated to be +1.807. Notably, the Co-N3C1 samples prepared under varying N2 flow rates exhibited nearly identical Co valence states (Fig. 2b). Co K-edge FT-EXAFS spectra displayed a prominent Co-N/C coordination peak near 1.3 Å in Co-N3C1 SACs (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 12), with no Co–Co scattering path observed around 2.17 Å, further supporting the atomic dispersion of Co31,32,34. This conclusion was reinforced by wavelet transform EXAFS analysis, wherein the Co-N/C signals in Co-N3C1-10 closely matched those of CoPc. (Supplementary Fig. 13)32,34. Quantitative EXAFS fitting results (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Tables 4, 5) indicated that the average coordination number around Co in Co-N3C1-10 was 3.04 for nitrogen and 1.35 for carbon, confirming a Co-N3C1 coordination environment32,34. Notably, the Co-N3C1 coordination structure was maintained in all SACs regardless of the N2 flow rate applied during synthesis. Meanwhile, XPS spectra revealed the similar elemental compositions and chemical environment among the series of Co-N3C1 catalysts19,26,35,36 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Figs. 15–17).

Raman spectra revealed that as the N2 flow rate decreased from 180 to 10 mL min–1, the ID/IG ratio reduced from 1.36 to 1.19 (Fig. 2e), suggesting a slight increase in graphitization degree37. The trend was corroborated by N 1s XPS analyses, which showed a progressive increase in the proportion of graphitic nitrogen from 26.44% to 36.21% with decreasing flow rates (Fig. 2g). Such improvements are probably attributed to the relatively high humidity levels under reduced gas flow conditions, which promote selective removal of amorphous carbon through H2O(g) etching during high-temperature pyrolysis, while preserving more thermodynamically stable graphitic structures38. Additionally, lowering the N2 flow rate substantially increased the Co site density in Co-N3C1 catalysts. As shown in Fig. 2f, the Co content in Co-N3C1 rised from 1.50 to 2.13 wt% when flow rates reduced from 180 to 10 mL min–1. This phenomenon arises from two factors: the intensified mass loss of Co-ZIF-8 resulting from enhanced Zn volatilization and amorphous carbon etching, and the stability of high-boiling-point Co (2927 °C) during pyrolysis, both of which facilitate spatial concentration of atomically dispersed Co sites (Supplementary Table 6).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the low gas flow strategy simultaneously enhances the graphitization degree and active site density in Co-N3C1 catalysts, while maintaining highly consistent physicochemical properties and Co coordination environment. Traditional steam activation relies on vigorous chemical reactions induced by exogenous H2O vapor to aggressively etch carbon structures, which can readily disrupt delicate catalytic sites38. In contrast, our low gas flow strategy optimizes the hierarchical porosity while perfectly preserving the local electronic structures of the single atom sites.

Low N2 flow-induced hierarchical porosity enabling maximized contaminant adsorption

To evaluate the effect of N2 flow rate on adsorption performance, BPA was employed as a representative pollutant to systematically compare the adsorption behaviors of Co-N3C1 materials synthesized under different N2 flow rates. The results revealed a significant dependency flow-rate: as the flow rate decreased from 180 to 10 mL min–1, BPA adsorption efficiency rose from 6% to 89%, and the adsorption capacity increased from 15.03 to 217.08 mg g–1 (Fig. 3a). These improvements were closely linked to changes in SBET and hierarchical porosity induced by varying N2 flow rates.

Although Co-N3C1-10 exhibited the highest BET surface area, the weak correlation (R2 = 81.4%) between SBET and adsorption capacity indicates that this parameter alone does not govern adsorption performance (Fig. 3bI). Similarly, the poor correlation with total micropore volume (R2 = 86.7%, Supplementary Fig. 18) suggests limited contribution from general microporosity. Previous studies indicate that effective molecular capture requires micropores exceeding 1.7 times the secondary molecular dimension of the adsorbate39–44. Given the molecular size of BPA (0.94 nm × 0.53 nm × 0.43 nm)45, micropores in the 0.9–2 nm range are ideally suited for BPA uptake via pore-filling43,44. This was experimentally verified by the strong positive correlation between adsorption capacity and the volume of these specific micropores (R2 = 99.4%, Fig. 3bIII). Nevertheless, variations in normalized adsorption capacity among samples with 0.9–2 nm micropore volumes (Supplementary Table 7) suggest the involvement of additional structural factors46.

Importantly, the BPA adsorption strongly correlated with the mesopore area (R2 = 99.9%, Fig. 3bII), along with the significant variations in the normalized adsorption capacities based on mesopore areas (Supplementary Table 7). These results highlight the dual role of mesopores in providing supplementary adsorption sites and facilitating molecular transport to micropores47. Furthermore, a significant negative correlation between zinc content and adsorption capacity (R2 = 95.7%, Fig. 3bIV) underscores the critical role of zinc volatilization in generating the hierarchical pores responsible for adsorption performance.

Adsorption-driven catalytic process for efficient BPA degradation by Co-N3C1-10

To determine whether a hierarchical porous structure improves BPA degradation in conjunction with adsorption, we compared the BPA removal efficiency of Co-N3C1 prepared under varying N2 flow rates. Upon achieving adsorption equilibrium (30 min), PMS was introduced to initiate degradation. The Co-N3C1 exhibited progressively improved BPA removal, with efficiency increasing from 18% for Co-N3C1-180 to 100% for Co-N3C1-10 within 5 min, along with the pseudo-first-order rate constant (kobs) rising from 0.013 to 0.43 min–1 (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 19). The catalysts were collected after adsorption and degradation stage and subjected to methanol desorption, with residual BPA quantified by ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)20. UPLC analysis showed that catalysts undergoing adsorption alone released BPA amounts matching their adsorption capacities. In contrast, under PMS oxidation, the BPA signal in desorption solutions decreased markedly as the N2 flow rate reduced during catalyst synthesis (Fig. 3d). Notably, for Co-N3C1-10, the material with the strongest adsorption capacity, the BPA peak entirely disappeared, confirming complete degradation of adsorbed BPA and suggesting adsorption-enhanced catalysis. While the Co content and surface area of the Co-N3C1 catalysts increased significantly at lower N2 flow rates (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Tables 1 and 8), the pronounced variations in their normalized kobs confirm that these physical expansions do not solely govern the enhanced performance. As shown in Fig. 3e, decreasing the flow rate increased the TOF from 0.03 to 0.608 min–1, proving that pore structure optimization directly augments the intrinsic activity of the catalytic sites. Consequently, Co-N3C1-10 achieves a higher TOF for BPA degradation than most reported cobalt single-atom catalysts (Supplementary Table 9). Additionally, the BPA degradation rate over Co-N3C1 increased at higher reaction temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 20). Crucially, analyzing the Arrhenius plots and apparent activation energies (Ea)27,48 of the samples synthesized at low versus high flow rates (Fig. 3f) reveals a fundamental mechanistic shift from diffusion control to chemical reaction control. Unlike Co-N3C1-180, the Co-N3C1-10 catalyst exhibited a robust linear correlation between lnkobs and T–1, producing an Ea roughly twice as high49. This difference proves that the optimized hierarchical porosity in Co-N3C1-10 effectively eliminates the constraints imposed by micropores and minimizes mass transfer resistance, fundamentally boosting catalytic efficiency.

We further examined Co-N3C1-10 to understand how adsorption governs catalytic performance by systematically varying the pre-adsorption time to control BPA loading. As pre-adsorption time increased from 1 to 30 min, BPA adsorption capacity rose from 128 to 205 mg g–1. Upon PMS introduction, degradation efficiency increased from 70% to 100%, and the kobs sharply rose from 0.013 to 0.43 min–1, showing a 21.5-fold enhancement (Fig. 3g). This result underscores the crucial role of high adsorption capacity in enabling rapid and complete BPA degradation.

The hierarchical pores in Co-N3C1-10 create confined spaces that enrich reactants and elevate their contact frequency with active sites22,23,50. Consistent with this view, Co-N3C1-10 displayed a higher PMS consumption rate than materials prepared at higher N2 flow rates (Supplementary Fig. 21). Moreover, extending the pre-adsorption time from 1 to 30 min increased the PMS consumption rate of Co-N3C1-10 from 10.2% to 35.2% (Fig. 3h), indicating that greater BPA adsorption significantly promotes the catalytic activity of the Co-N3C1 sites.

Control experiments unequivocally established the necessity of Co atomic sites for catalytic functionality. Though Co-free N3C1-10 material strongly adsorbed substrates, it barely degraded them (Supplementary Fig. 22). Similarly, neither homogeneous Co2+ ions (22.8 μg L–1, matching post-reaction dissolved Co levels) nor the Co-N4-10 control achieved appreciable BPA removal. These results confirm that asymmetrically coordinated Co single atoms are extremely important catalytic sites; thus, the increase of Co atom density in Co-N3C1 is conducive to enhancing catalytic reaction. However, the significant differences of Co content normalized kobs (Supplementary Table 8), the adsorption-controlled pollutant degradation and PMS consumption behaviors (Fig. 3g, h), clearly demonstrate that the high adsorption capacity of catalysts synthesized at low flow rate plays a more decisive role than Co site density in the superior Fenton-like catalytic activity of Co-N3C1-10/PMS system.

Rapid and complete BPA degradation by Co-N3C1-10 via reinforced electron-transfer

The degradation of adsorbed BPA proceeds through a near-complete electron-transfer route, as evidenced by multiple experimental evidences. Scavenger experiments (Supplementary Fig. 23) preliminarily excluded significant contributions from hydroxyl radicals (•OH), sulfate radicals (SO4•–), singlet oxygen (1O2), and high-valent Co–oxo species50–53. Consistent with these findings, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra revealed no appreciable enhancement in DMPO-•OH, DMPO-SO4•–, or TEMP-1O2 signals across both Co-N3C1-10/PMS and Co-N3C1-10/BPA/PMS systems, even after prolonged reaction times50–53 (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 24). Meanwhile, only trace amounts of methyl phenyl sulfone (PMSO2) were detected, comparable to the PMS-only background (Supplementary Fig. 25). The results further confirm the negligible contributions of reactive oxygen species in this process52,53 In situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 3j) provided critical insights into the activation mechanism: the introduction of Co-N3C1-10 induced a blue shift of the HSO5– peak from 1061 to 1078 cm–1, signaling electron donation from the catalyst to PMS and subsequent activation53–55. Additionally, a transient peak emerged near 838 cm–1 and vanished upon BPA addition, indicating the formation and consumption of the activated PMS species (PMS*)56. Galvanic oxidation process (GOP) tests were conducted using a dual-chamber reactor to isolate PMS and BPA. The system displayed a highly pronounced current response upon the introduction of PMS (Supplementary Fig. 26), directly evidencing Co-N3C1-10 mediated electron transfer as the principal degradation pathway2.

Electrochemical measurements further supported this interpretation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) revealed markedly reduced charge-transfer resistance for Co-N3C1-10 compared to its higher-flow counterparts57 (Supplementary Fig. 27), correlating with enhanced graphitization. The open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements exhibited an immediate increase upon PMS addition, followed by a gradual decline with BPA introduction (Supplementary Fig. 28), reflecting real-time generation and consumption of PMS* species2,19. Amperometric i–t curves showed a clear response in current density upon sequential addition of PMS and BPA (Supplementary Fig. 29). Additionally, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) analyses revealed significantly higher current densities in the Co-N3C1/PMS/BPA systems than in other systems (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31), reflecting electron transfer and redistribution between the catalyst and reactants19,57,58. Crucially, pre-adsorbed BPA prior to PMS introduction generated substantially greater current in i–t curves than the reverse sequence (Fig. 3k), providing compelling evidence for adsorption-facilitated electron transfer. Most importantly, diminishing N2 flow rate progressively enhanced electrochemical responses of synthesized catalysts across all tests, with Co-N3C1-10 exhibiting optimal activity due to the structure optimization of SACs under low flow conditions.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations rationalized these observations energetically. Despite the large energy gap between the LUMO of PMS (–2.723 eV) and the HOMO of BPA (–4.892 eV) impeding direct electron transfer from BPA to PMS55,58,59 (Fig. 3l), BPA adsorbed on Co-N3C1 to form a complex that elevated its HOMO level to –1.716 eV, exceeding the LUMO of PMS and making electron transfer through the catalyst energetically feasible.

Overall, the low-flow-rate-induced optimization of hierarchical porosity and micro-environment in Co-N3C1 SACs creates dual advantages: accelerated electron transfer and enhanced adsorption-driven catalytic performance, collectively enabling rapid and efficient pollutant degradation.

Mechanistic insight into BPA adsorption-mediated activation of PMS

To comprehend the fundamental mechanism of adsorption-driven catalysis under pore confinement, we employed DFT computations, incorporating a curved surface and an implicit solvation model, alongside in situ XANES spectroscopy to examine how BPA adsorption on Co-N3C1 influences its catalytic performance. Since the Co-free N3C1-10 material still exhibited substantial BPA uptake (Supplementary Fig. 22), nitrogen functional groups are deemed the primary adsorption sites. DFT modeling revealed that BPA preferentially adsorbed onto graphitic nitrogen sites (–0.662 eV) rather than pyridinic (–0.571 eV) and pyrrolic nitrogen (–0.548 eV). This energetic hierarchy establishes graphitic nitrogen as the primary anchoring locus for BPA molecules20,60 (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). Subsequent modeling, therefore, focused on the Co-N3C1 graphitic nitrogen structure.

Further investigations were carried out to elucidate how pre-adsorbed BPA influences PMS adsorption at cobalt sites. Computational analyses indicated that PMS coordinates to cobalt through its Oα, Oβ, or Oγ atoms20,52 (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35), with Oα binding being energetically favored in both the presence and absence of BPA (Fig. 4a). Significantly, BPA pre-adsorption markedly reduced the adsorption energy of Oα-HSO4 from –2.218 to –2.685 eV, consequently strengthening PMS binding affinity toward cobalt centers. Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 36) further revealed a stronger overlap between the Co 3d and O 2p orbitals. Additionally, the system containing BPA and PMS (Co-N3C1/BPA/PMS) exhibited a much higher d-band center (–1.004 eV) than the system without BPA (–1.212 eV). These findings rigorously confirm that BPA adsorption amplifies PMS binding on the catalyst surface and facilitates subsequent activation19,20.

Charge distribution analysis coupled with Bader charge calculations offered deeper insights into the electronic modulations induced by adsorbed BPA. Both Co-N3C1/PMS and Co-N3C1/BPA/PMS systems showed electron transfer from catalyst to PMS (Fig. 4c), visualized through yellow regions denoting accumulation and blue zones signaling depletion, consistent with electrochemical observations. The Bader charge transfer number rose from 0.83 e– in the Co-N3C1/PMS system to 0.98 e– upon BPA introduction (Supplementary Table 10), while the O–O bond length of PMS increased from 1.534 to 1.618 Å (Fig. 4c). These results suggest that adsorbed BPA enhances electron supply to PMS, facilitating O–O bond cleavage2,20,52. Crucially, although more electrons were transferred to PMS in the BPA-containing system, the Co atom lost fewer electrons (1.15 e–) compared to the BPA-free case (1.19 e–). This indicates that adsorbed BPA supplies electrons to the Co site via the carbon matrix.

In situ XANES directly tracks the evolution of the Co oxidation state during BPA adsorption and PMS activation. BPA adsorption resulted in a shift of the Co absorption edge to lower energy (Fig. 4d), indicating Co reduction and a rise in electron density26,61,62, in agreement with DFT predictions. The subsequent introduction of PMS shifted the absorption edge to higher energy, reflecting electron transfer from Co to PMS and an increase in the Co oxidation state26,61,62. Gibbs free energy calculations for the PMS activation pathway showed that pre-adsorbed BPA optimized the adsorption of reaction intermediates and lowered the energy barrier of the rate-determining step. Specifically, this barrier corresponding to the *PMS to *SO4–OH transformation decreased from 0.53 to 0.40 eV (Fig. 4e), providing thermodynamic evidence for BPA-enhanced PMS activation.

In summary, both experimental and theoretical results confirm that BPA adsorbed on graphitic nitrogen sites donates electrons to Co active sites via the carbon substrate. This elevates the electron density at Co, improves PMS adsorption, reduces the activation barrier, accelerates electron transfer, and ultimately enhances the adsorption-driven catalytic efficiency.

Evaluating the applicability of Co-N3C1-10 for water remediation

The practical potential of the Co-N3C1-10/PMS system was systematically evaluated with respect to degradation pathways, ecotoxicity, reaction efficiency and selectivity, environmental adaptability, and catalyst stability.

The ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time of flight (UPLC-Q-TOF) analysis identified 12 intermediates during BPA degradation (Supplementary Fig. 37 and Supplementary Table 11), enabling the proposal of two plausible degradation pathways. Ecological structure activity relationships (ECOSAR)-based toxicity assessment52,63 (Fig. 5a) revealed that most intermediates exhibited lower acute and chronic toxicity to aquatic organisms (fish, daphnia, and green algae) than BPA, underscoring the system’s capacity to reduce ecological risk. Experiments evaluating the effects of PMS dosage and initial solution pH demonstrated that Co-N3C1-10 achieved complete BPA degradation with a PMS dosage of only 0.3 mM and a broad pH range (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39), highlighting its favorable process economy and robust stability. The system also exhibited distinct degradation selectivity (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Tables 12 and 13): electron-rich pollutants (low ionization potential) underwent rapid degradation; moderately electron-rich contaminants (medium ionization potential) were removed via adsorption and degradation; and electron-deficient compounds (high ionization potential) were only adsorbed. This trend aligns with the electron-transfer mechanism, as electron-deficient pollutants cannot readily donate electrons to Co sites, preventing PMS activation. Under diverse water matrices (tap water, urban river water, and Yangtze River water), the system maintained complete BPA removal within 30 min, despite the reduced kobs being observed (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 40), demonstrating strong anti-interference capability. Moreover, the catalyst preserved 100% BPA removal over five consecutive cycles (Fig. 5e), highlighting its stability and reusability.

Overall, the Co-N3C1-10/PMS system combines high degradation efficiency, effective toxicity reduction, notable environmental robustness, and excellent stability, showcasing strong promise for practical wastewater treatment.

Minimalist gas flow-directed synthesis of Fe-N3C1 and Cu-N3C1 single-atom catalysts

To evaluate the generality of the low-gas-flow strategy in synthesizing ZIF-8-derived SACs, we extended this approach for fabricating Fe- and Cu-based materials. XRD patterns showed no detectable metal nanoparticle phases (Supplementary Fig. 41), while XANES spectra at the Fe and Cu K-edges indicated oxidation states were close to +2 (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43a). FT-EXAFS analysis validated their atomic-level dispersion of both metal species (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43b). EXAFS fitting results further substantiated an average N/C coordination number of 3.4/1.4 for Fe and 2.8/1.1 for Cu (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Figs. 42, 43c–e and Supplementary Tables 14, 15), supporting a local coordination environment analogous to Co-N3C1.

The low-gas-flow approach also markedly enhanced the adsorption and catalytic properties of Fe-N3C1 and Cu-N3C1. Reducing the N2 flow rate from 180 to 10 mL min–1 increased BPA adsorption capacity from 16.61 to 234.53 mg g–1 for Fe-N3C1 and from 70.44 to 246.01 mg g–1 for Cu-N3C1 (Supplementary Fig. 44), mirroring the trend observed for Co-N3C1. A strong negative correlation was noted between adsorption capacity and residual Zn content (R2 = 95.8% for Fe-N3C1; R2 = 98.8% for Cu-N3C1; Fig. 5g, h), emphasizing the key role of Zn volatilization in generating a hierarchical porosity for enhanced adsorption. Notably, this adsorption improvement directly correlated with superior catalytic performance. Under the same gas-flow reduction from 180 to 10 mL min–1, BPA degradation efficiency rose sharply from 15% to 100% for Fe-N3C1 and from 34% to 100% for Cu-N3C1 (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 45). These consistent outcomes highlight the broad applicability of the low-gas-flow strategy in tailoring the hierarchical pore architecture of ZIF-8-derived SACs, thereby concurrently enhancing both adsorption and catalytic performance.

Discussion

This study develops a simple yet effective low-gas-flow strategy for optimizing the synthesis of ZIF-8-derived SACs. This method triggers a cascade effect that creates a hierarchical pore structure, thereby boosting the adsorption-driven catalysis for the degradation of electron-rich pollutants. The key mechanistic insight is that the high-humidity environment under low nitrogen flow promotes abundant in situ formation of nZnO. The subsequent carbothermal reduction and volatilization of Zn nanoparticles are critical for constructing the resulting porous network. Systematic variation of the N2 flow rate from 180 to 10 mL min–1 reveals that lower flow rates progressively increase the volume of mesopores and 0.9–2 nm micropores, thereby enhancing adsorption capacity.

Crucially, this hierarchical porous architecture enables a superior catalytic degradation efficiency through a well-defined mechanism. Within confined microenvironments, adsorbed electron-rich pollutants such as BPA donate electrons to adjacent cobalt sites via the conductive carbon matrix, thereby elevating their Co local electron density. Meanwhile, the low gas flow strategy increases both the graphitization degree and the active site density, significantly improving the electron transfer ability. These synergistic effects empower the Co centers to rapidly activate PMS. Consequently, the Co-N3C1‑10 catalyst, synthesized at the minimal flow rate (10 mL min–1), exhibits optimized hierarchical porosity, exceptional adsorption affinity, and outstanding catalytic activity. Notably, in the Co-N3C1‑10/PMS system, complete BPA degradation is achieved via an electron-transfer pathway, even at low PMS doses (0.3 mM) and in complex real water matrices. This simple, parameter-controlled method offers a versatile platform for optimizing other MOF-derived SACs. Overall, our work establishes a minimalist synthesis paradigm that precisely controls the hierarchical porous structure and functionality of the catalyst through simple flow rate regulation. Furthermore, it provides new insights into the underlying electronic mechanisms governing adsorption-driven catalysis.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Reagents used in this study are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Catalyst preparation

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 8 mmol) and cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.8 mmol) were dissolved in 60 mL of methanol via ultrasonication for 10 min to yield Solution A. Separately, 2-methylimidazole (32 mmol) was dissolved in 40 mL of methanol to prepare Solution B. Subsequently, Solution B was poured into Solution A, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting purple precipitate, designated as Co-ZIF-8, was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with methanol, and dried under vacuum.

For pyrolysis, Co-ZIF-8 powder was loaded into an open porcelain boat positioned at the center of a tube furnace (Supplementary Fig. 46). A steady stream of N2 gas was maintained throughout the thermal treatment. The furnace followed a multi-stage heating protocol: heated from 35 to 45 °C at 0.067 °C min–1, then to 100 °C at 2 °C min–1, and finally to 950 °C at 5 °C min–1. After holding at 950 °C for 3 h, the system was allowed to cool naturally to ambient temperature, yielding the final Co-N3C1 material. The deliberate slow ramping rate between 35–45 °C facilitated partial moisture removal from the furnace atmosphere via the sweeping N2 flow. By exclusively modulating the N2 flow rate during pyrolysis, the intra-furnace water vapor content at critical temperature intervals was precisely controlled, triggering cascade transformations that engineered the hierarchical porosity of the Co-N3C1 SACs. Catalysts synthesized under N2 flow rates of 180, 130, 100, 70, 40, and 10 mL min–1 were labeled Co-N3C1-180, Co-N3C1-130, Co-N3C1−100, Co-N3C1-70, Co-N3C1-40, and Co-N3C1-10, respectively. Analogous procedures were employed to synthesize Fe-N3C1, Cu-N3C1 SACs, N3C1-10, and Co-N4-10, with detailed parameters provided in the Supplementary Information.

Experimental procedure

For adsorption experiments, 2.8 mg of catalyst was dispersed in 70 mL BPA solution of 10 mg L–1 and continuously stirred on a magnetic stirrer at 25 ± 1 °C. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn and filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters for analysis.

In degradation experiments, a 70 mL mixture solution containing 0.04 g L–1 catalyst and 10 mg L–1 BPA was stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, 0.5 mL PMS (100 mM) was injected to initiate the catalytic reaction. At specific time intervals, 0.5 mL aliquots were collected, immediately filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, and quenched with 0.5 mL of sodium thiosulfate (100 mM) to halt reactive species generation. All collected samples were stored at 4 °C prior to UPLC analysis. No pH adjustments were performed in any adsorption or catalytic experiments. To specifically quantify degradation of pre-adsorbed BPA, post-reacted catalysts were centrifuged, resuspended in methanol, and subjected to 1 h sonication to desorb residual BPA. The supernatant was then filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters and analyzed by UPLC.

Sample characterization

Crystal structure was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation. Surface morphology and elemental distribution were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Nova Nano SEM 450) equipped coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) imaging was conducted on a Thermo Fisher Titan G2 60-300 microscope. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images and corresponding elemental mapping of Co-N3C1-10 were acquired using a JEOL-2100F field emission transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV. Chemical states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha system, which also enabled semi-quantitative determination of ZnO content in samples pyrolyzed at 600 °C. All binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Defect characterizations were conducted on a Raman microscope (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution, Japan). Metal contents (Co and Zn) in the prepared materials and cobalt leaching after reaction were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5800) and mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800). Nitrogen sorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a Quantachrome Instruments Analyzer to determine specific surface areas (Brunauer-Emmett-Teller method), total pore volumes, and pore size distributions. Mesopore and micropore analyses were performed via Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) and Horvath-Kawazoe (HK) methods, respectively. X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra at the Co K-edge, Fe K-edge, Cu K-edge, and Zn K-edge were collected at the BL13SSW beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Additionally, in situ XAFS measurements at the Co K-edge during BPA adsorption and PMS activation were conducted at the BL11B beamline of SSRF to monitor changes in the cobalt oxidation state and local coordination environment. Detailed XAFS analysis protocols and electrochemical measurements are described in the Supplementary Information.

Analytical methods

Residual pollutant concentrations were quantified using UPLC (ACQUITY Premier, USA) equipped with a BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm). Mobile phase composition and detection wavelength for each analyte are listed in Supplementary Table 9. The residual concentration of PMS in the solution post-reaction was determined using a colorimetric method. Specifically, 0.1 mL of sample solution was injected into the 5 mL of 0.5 M KI containing 0.048 mol L–1 NaHCO3, and measured at 352 nm after stabilizing 15 min. Degradation intermediates of BPA were identified using UPLC-Q-TOF (Waters Acquity UPLC I-Class PLUS/Bruker Impact II, Germany). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were trapped with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) or 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) and detected by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy on a Bruker ER200-SRC spectrometer. In situ Raman spectra of reaction systems were acquired using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution microscope (Japan) at 532 nm excitation. The Autodesk 3ds Max 2022 and Microsoft PowerPoint 2016 were used to draw all schematic diagrams.

Theoretical calculations

All DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio Simulation program (VASP 6.3.0). The details are shown in the Supplementary Information.
